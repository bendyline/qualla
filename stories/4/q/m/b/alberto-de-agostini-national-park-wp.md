# Alberto de Agostini National Park

Where the Andes finally drown in the sea, a roadless wilderness of tidewater glaciers and fjords bears the name of a priest who climbed its mountains in his cassock.

**Location:** -54.7491, -70.4397

**Categories:** Protected areas of Magallanes Region, National parks of Chile, Tierra del Fuego, Protected areas established in 1965, Environment of Chile, Biosphere reserves of Chile, Magellanic subpolar forests

## Introduction

He climbed in a cassock. Father Alberto Maria De Agostini, an Italian Salesian missionary who arrived in Tierra del Fuego in 1910, spent decades photographing, mapping, and ascending the wildest mountains at the bottom of the Americas. At seventy-three he organized the first successful ascent of Monte Sarmiento, a pyramid of ice he had dreamed of for decades — his climbers Carlo Mauri and Clemente Maffei finally reached its summit on March 7, 1956. The national park created on January 22, 1965, carries his name, and it guards a landscape as severe and beautiful as any on Earth: a maze of fjords where the Andes make their last stand before sinking beneath the Southern Ocean.

## Where the Mountains Drown

There are no roads here. There never have been. The park sprawls across the southern islands of the Chilean archipelago, taking in Gordon, Cook, and Londonderry islands and part of Hoste, its coastline so deeply gnawed by fjords that the land seems to be coming apart. At its heart rises the Cordillera Darwin, the southernmost range of the Andes, where peaks drop straight into the water with no shoreline to soften the fall. The range is crowned by Monte Sarmiento, a glacier-sheathed pyramid of roughly 2,235 meters, and by Monte Darwin nearby. Glaciers fill the valleys the sea has not yet claimed and spill across small high plateaus. To reach the park you come by boat from Punta Arenas, roughly 148 kilometers to the northeast, or by small plane from Puerto Williams. Either way, you arrive on the ocean's terms.

## The Avenue of the Glaciers

Along the northwest arm of the Beagle Channel, six glaciers spill toward the water within sight of one another, named for the European nations whose ships once probed these waters: España, Romanche, Italia, Francia, Holanda, and Alemania. Sailors call this stretch the Avenue of the Glaciers, though there is nothing tame or boulevard-like about it. The grandest single glacier, the Marinelli, has become a marker of a changing world. It is the fastest-retreating glacier in the Cordillera Darwin, having pulled back roughly thirteen kilometers between 1960 and 2005, baring rock that had been buried in ice for millennia.

## Darwin's Crucible

In December 1833, the Beagle threaded these channels, and a young naturalist aboard met the Fuegian peoples for the first time. Charles Darwin saw the Yaghan, the Selkʼnam, the Haush, and the Kawésqar living through a cold he could scarcely fathom, and the encounter unsettled him in ways that would echo through his later writing on human origins. These were not curiosities to be catalogued. They were people who had endured at the edge of the inhabitable world for thousands of years, the Yaghan settling these channels roughly ten millennia ago. Darwin's discomfort says more about him than about them.

## Life at the Edge

This is the realm of the Magellanic subpolar forest, where the evergreen coihue and the aromatic canelo cling to slopes battered by wind and rain. The climate is subpolar oceanic: wet, cold, and relentlessly gusty, even in the brief southern summer. Yet life crowds the cold water. South American sea lions haul out on the rocks; southern elephant seals and leopard seals patrol the shallows; humpback whales surface in the channels; and the Chilean dolphin, found nowhere else on Earth, cuts through the swell alongside Peale's and Burmeister's porpoise. On land, the culpeo and the Patagonian gray fox hunt, and the guanaco grazes the open ground. Overhead, the southern royal albatross rides the wind, and the great Magellanic woodpecker hammers at the forest's edge. In 2005, UNESCO folded the park, together with neighboring Cabo de Hornos National Park, into the Cape Horn World Biosphere Reserve.

## Flight Context

Centered near 54.75°S, 70.44°W in Chile's Magallanes y la Antártica Chilena region, the park has no airfields and no roads. The Cordillera Darwin's glaciated peaks make dramatic visual landmarks from cruising altitude in rare clear weather, but cloud, rain, and fierce williwaw winds dominate. The nearest paved runway lies at Puerto Williams (SCGZ) to the southeast; Punta Arenas (Carlos Ibáñez del Campo, SCCI) sits roughly 148 km northeast. Expect low ceilings and turbulent conditions year-round.
