# St. Elizabeth's Church, Marburg

Germany's earliest purely Gothic church was raised over the grave of a widowed landgravine who gave away a fortune, nursed the sick herself, and died at twenty-four.

**Location:** 50.8150, 8.7697

**Categories:** Churches, Gothic architecture, Medieval, Pilgrimage, Germany

## Introduction

The church was built for a woman who would probably have objected to it. Elizabeth of Hungary spent the last three years of her life in Marburg giving away the wealth she had married into - she founded a hospital beside the Lahn, dedicated it to Francis of Assisi, and nursed in it herself until she died there on 17 November 1231, aged twenty-four. Four years later Pope Gregory IX canonised her. In the same year the Teutonic Knights laid a cornerstone directly over her grave and began raising eighty metres of sandstone above the spot where she had been buried in a plain tomb in a modest chapel.

## The Landgravine Who Gave It Away

Elizabeth was born in 1207, a daughter of King Andrew II of Hungary, and married at fourteen to Louis IV, Landgrave of Thuringia. By all accounts the marriage was a happy one, and it was short. Louis died of fever at Otranto on 11 September 1227, on his way to crusade, leaving Elizabeth twenty years old and pregnant. Disputes over her dowry pushed her out of Thuringia and she came to Marburg, where she fell under the direction of her confessor Konrad von Marburg. He held her to standards almost nobody could have met, ordered her beaten, and had her three children sent away from her. What she built with what remained of her dowry was a hospital for the sick and the destitute, in 1228, and she worked in it. Within three years she was dead.

## Gothic Before Germany Knew the Word

Gregory IX granted the new church the right to issue indulgences in a letter dated 30 May 1235, and the cornerstone went down on 14 August. What rose over the next decades is one of the earliest purely Gothic buildings in the German-speaking world, and a structure architectural historians treat as a model for Cologne Cathedral. It is sandstone, cruciform, an early hall church with a three-lobed choir at its east end: the Elisabeth Choir to the north, the High Choir in the middle, the Landgrave Choir to the south. The nave vaults rise more than 20 metres. Two towers reach roughly 80 metres, one crowned with a star, the other with a knight. Elizabeth's grave was never moved - the north apse was simply built over the old hospital chapel that held it.

## The Shrine, and the Graves Under the Floor

The Gothic shrine of St. Elizabeth is the church's great treasure. Begun in 1235 and finished in 1249, when her relics were carried into the new choir, it is oak sheathed in gilded silver and copper, studded with pearls and Mediterranean gems, including three dozen engraved stones in the original scheme. Many of those are gone, prised out by thieves over the centuries, most recently in a burglary in 1920; the shrine now sits behind glass. Around it stand five Gothic altars, the finest of them the polychromed High Altar of 1290, and six stained glass windows in the High Choir from the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries. One of them tells Elizabeth's own life, though its panels sat in the wrong order for two hundred years after the Seven Years War until a restoration in the 1970s put the story straight. Excavations beneath the nave have turned up hundreds of pilgrims' graves.

## A Descendant Comes for the Bones

In 1539 Landgrave Philip I of Hesse - a leader of the Reformation, and one of Elizabeth's own descendants - came to the church and demanded her bones, intending to end the pilgrimage traffic she had drawn for three centuries. The building was already turning Protestant that year, though a quirk of imperial law kept it officially Catholic until 1570. In 1605 Landgrave Moritz converted to Calvinism and ordered every image west of the choir screen destroyed. The Seven Years War brought worse: the church served as a powder magazine and warehouse, and the medieval glass came out of it in pieces. Elizabeth's skull reliquary had already gone, looted from the Marienberg Fortress at Würzburg; it is in Stockholm now, in the Swedish History Museum.

## What the Twentieth Century Left Behind

For the 700th anniversary of Elizabeth's death in 1931, the church commissioned a crucifix from Ernst Barlach for the Altar of the Cross. The Nazis removed it in 1936 as degenerate art. It survived, and it went back on the altar after they fell. The altarpieces and medieval windows spent the Second World War at Haina Abbey; the building came through nearly intact, though shrapnel from a 1944 air raid struck the north wall of the nave. In the summer of 1945 American Monuments Men brought here the sarcophagi of Frederick the Great, Frederick William I and Paul and Gertrud von Hindenburg, found hidden from the advancing Soviets in a Thuringian salt mine. The Prussian kings left for Hohenzollern Castle in 1951. The Hindenburgs stayed, in the northern tower chapel. A four-year restoration finished in 2024 returned the interior to its original paint scheme.

## Flight Context

St. Elizabeth's stands at 50.815°N, 8.770°E, at the northern edge of Marburg's old town where the slope flattens toward the Lahn. Its two towers, at about 80 metres, are the tallest structures in the town and the easiest visual fix in the valley - look for the twin spires below and north of the long ridge-line of the Landgrafenschloss, with the river running past to the east. Best viewed at 1,500-3,000 ft AGL. Marburg has no airfield; Frankfurt/Main (EDDF) lies roughly 90 km south and Kassel-Calden (EDVK) about 80 km north, and the Lahn valley funnels traffic and weather along a northeast-southwest axis. Autumn and winter mornings frequently fill the valley floor with fog while the surrounding hills stay clear.
