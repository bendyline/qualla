# The Vertical Earth Kilometer

In 1977 Walter De Maria sank eighteen tons of solid brass a kilometre into the ground beneath a Kassel square, leaving a five-centimetre circle as the only part anyone will ever see.

**Location:** 51.3132, 9.4967

**Categories:** Public art in Germany, 1977 sculptures, Land art, Conceptual art, Buildings and structures in Kassel

## Introduction

Everything you can see of it is a brass circle five centimetres across, set flush into a square of red sandstone in a Kassel park. Step on that circle and you have stood on the entire visible surface of a sculpture that continues, straight down, for one kilometre. Walter De Maria's The Vertical Earth Kilometer has been in the ground on Friedrichsplatz since 1977, when it was installed for documenta 6, and it is not coming out. Eighteen tons of solid brass, driven through six geological layers beneath a city square, of which the public is shown roughly the area of a coin. It is an artwork you have to take on trust, and it knows exactly what it is asking.

## Seventy-Nine Days of Drilling

The borehole went down a thousand metres, cutting through six distinct geological layers, and the drilling took seventy-nine days. Kassel had to listen to every one of them. Into the finished shaft went 167 brass rods, screwed together end to end to form a single continuous rod five centimetres in diameter, one kilometre long and weighing eighteen tons. The top face was set flush with the surface of the ground and framed in a plate of red sandstone two metres square. Technical direction fell to the engineering firm of Dr Hans Jürgen Pickel of Kassel; the work was realised through documenta 6 under its director Manfred Schneckenburger, with the support of the Dia Art Foundation. It is a piece of civil engineering that produced no building, no shaft head, no visible infrastructure of any kind - only a sealed line of metal and the noise of putting it there.

## Three-Quarters of a Million Marks for a Disc

The people of Kassel were not charmed. The bill came to 750,000 Deutsche Marks, something on the order of two million US dollars in 2016 values, and what the city got for it could not be seen. Weeks of drilling noise, and then a small brass circle in the pavement. The objection is entirely reasonable and entirely beside the point, because the invisibility is the work. What the money bought was not an object to look at but a fact to carry around: that directly beneath a particular patch of ground, a solid line of metal runs a kilometre down into the crust of the planet. De Maria did not build something to be admired. He altered what is underneath a place where people walk their dogs.

## Minimal, Conceptual, Land

Documenta describes the piece as sitting inside all three of the significant artistic strategies of its period at once. It has the stripped geometry of Minimal art, the primacy of idea over object that defines Conceptual art, and the literal engagement with the earth that made Land art. Most works of the era settle for one. Ken Jennings, writing for Condé Nast Traveler, pointed out that the visible result closely resembles a kilometre-zero marker - the small metal plate European cities set into the ground to mark the point from which road distances are measured. That likeness is a good joke and probably not an accident. It looks exactly like a marker for a distance, and the distance it marks runs the wrong way.

## The Companion Piece

Two years later, in 1979, De Maria built the counterpart in a street-level storefront in SoHo, New York. The Broken Kilometer uses the same brass, the same diameter and the same total length, but cut into 500 rods of two metres each and laid out on the floor in five rows of a hundred, under stadium lights, with the gaps between rods widening by five millimetres as the eye travels toward the back of the room. In Kassel the kilometre is completely hidden and completely intact. In New York it is completely visible and completely broken. The same quantity of metal, the same measurement, two opposite answers to the question of what you are allowed to see.

## Taking It on Faith

The Dia Art Foundation maintains The Vertical Earth Kilometer as one of the twelve locations and sites in its care, and it has been on permanent view - a phrase that asks for some generosity - since 1977. No visitor can verify any of it. You cannot see the rod, measure it, or watch it descend. What you can do is stand on Friedrichsplatz in front of the Fridericianum, where documenta has opened every five years since 1955, look down at a warm circle of metal in a square of red stone, and decide whether to believe that it continues for a thousand metres. Nearly everybody does. That decision, made quickly and without evidence by every person who walks across it, is the part of the sculpture that is actually above ground.

## Flight Context

The Vertical Earth Kilometer is at 51.3132N, 9.4967E, in Friedrichsplatz immediately in front of the Fridericianum in central Kassel, northern Hesse. The work itself is invisible from any altitude - the visible element is five centimetres across - so navigate to the square instead: from 2,000-4,000 ft, look for the large open rectangle of Friedrichsplatz with the long neoclassical Fridericianum along its western side, the Fulda river to the east, and the baroque Karlsaue park running southeast toward the water. The Hercules monument and cascade in the Bergpark Wilhelmshöhe, on the wooded hill west of the city, form a straight axis aimed at the centre of Kassel and make the best long-range reference. Kassel-Calden (EDVK) is the local airport just northwest; Hanover (EDDV) and Frankfurt (EDDF) are the larger fields north and south. Hilly terrain surrounds the Fulda valley, and low cloud on the ridges is common outside summer.
