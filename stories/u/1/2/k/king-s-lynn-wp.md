# King's Lynn

England's most important port in the 14th century, the first English town bombed from the air, and the place where Margery Kempe wrote the first autobiography in English.

**Location:** 52.7543, 0.3976

**Categories:** towns, ports, medieval, hanseatic, history, norfolk, kings-lynn

## Introduction

On the night of 19 January 1915, a German Zeppelin called L 4 slid over the North Sea coast and dropped eleven bombs on King's Lynn. It was one of the very first aerial attacks on civilians in history. Two residents died of shock: Alice Gazely, a 26-year-old war widow, and Percy Goate, who was 14 years old. About thirteen others were injured. The town that absorbed this strange new violence was not a random pick. King's Lynn had been a target for seven centuries by then - as England's most important port in the 14th century, as a royal prize King John designated a Royal Port, as a Hanseatic trading hub whose 1475 warehouse is one of only two such buildings left in England. The Zeppelin found Lynn because Lynn had always been findable.

## Bishop's Lynn to King's Lynn

In 1101, Herbert de Losinga, Bishop of Thetford, laid out a new town between the rivers Purfleet and Mill Fleet, commissioned St Margaret's Church, and authorised a Saturday market. The settlement grew along the waterways that drained the Fens. By the 14th century, Lynn ranked as England's most important port, as vital to medieval England as Liverpool would become during the Industrial Revolution. Trade was dominated by the Hanseatic League. The Trinity Guildhall was rebuilt in 1421 after a fire; two former Hanseatic warehouses survive - Hanse House of 1475 and Marriott's Warehouse. The town was called Bishop's Lynn until 1537, when Henry VIII closed the Benedictine priory and the three friaries, took the town from episcopal control, and renamed it King's Lynn. The town's medieval Jewish community had been wiped out centuries earlier in the widespread massacres of 1189.

## Margery Kempe's Loud Tears

Around 1438, in Bishop's Lynn as it then was, an illiterate mother of fourteen began dictating her life story to a series of clerical scribes. Margery Kempe described visions, pilgrimages to Jerusalem and Rome and Compostela, conversations with Christ, and the loud weeping fits that infuriated her neighbours and several archbishops. The book sat unread for almost five hundred years until a manuscript was rediscovered in a Derbyshire country house in 1934. It is now recognised as the first autobiography in English. Margery worshipped at St Margaret's Church, which still stands and is now King's Lynn Minster. The medieval streets she walked - the Saturday Market Place, Queen Street, King Street - survive in unusual completeness, including the chequered flint-and-stone Trinity Guildhall and the St George's Guildhall of 1406. The University of East Anglia has confirmed it is highly probable that William Shakespeare performed in St George's in 1593, making it the only working theatre in the world that can credibly claim that distinction.

## Loaves and Witches and Sir Robert

King's Lynn's history is unusually frank about the cruelties of early modern justice. A carved heart on the wall of the Tuesday Market Place is traditionally associated with the execution of a woman accused of witchcraft - some accounts name Margaret Read, burned at the stake in 1590; others name Mary Smith, hanged in 1616. The square was a regular site of hangings and burnings for women convicted of witchcraft, a routine of judicial cruelty. In September 1708, two children - seven-year-old Michael Hammond and his eleven-year-old sister Ann - were convicted of stealing a loaf of bread and publicly hanged near the South Gate. The local Member of Parliament at the time was Sir Robert Walpole, who would become Britain's first de facto Prime Minister and the longest-serving in British history. The Custom House on Purfleet Quay, designed in 1683 by the architect-mayor Henry Bell, is the prettiest building in town. The history beneath the prettiness is honest about how brutal the law could be.

## Trade, Decline, Rebirth

King's Lynn declined as transatlantic trade favoured west coast ports. The railways arrived in 1847 and revived the town's fortunes. By the 20th century, Campbell's was canning soup here, and in 1962 Lynn was designated a London overspill town, with new estates added at the Woottons and Gaywood. The 1960s town-centre redevelopment demolished much that was old. In 2006, King's Lynn became the United Kingdom's first member of The Hanse, the revived European network of former Hanseatic ports. The 17-hectare park called the Walks, the only 18th-century town walk surviving in Norfolk, was restored with £4.3 million from the Heritage Lottery Fund. The cobbled streets still funnel down to the Custom House and the River Great Ouse, still tidal, still flowing out into the dangerous fourfold estuary of The Wash.

## Flight Context

Located at 52.7543 degrees N, 0.3976 degrees E on the east bank of the Great Ouse at the head of The Wash. From cruising altitude, the medieval town centre shows as a dense, narrow grid against the river; the docks are immediately north, the broader 20th-century estates spread east and south. Best viewing 2,000-5,000 feet AGL. Nearest airport: RAF Marham (EGYM) approximately 10 nm south-southeast. Norwich International (EGSH) 40 nm east.
