# Norfolk

The English county where Boudica raised her revolt, where the wool trade built medieval cathedrals, and where the USAAF Eighth Air Force fought half its war from quiet farms.

**Location:** 52.6700, 0.9500

**Categories:** county, england, history, boudica, wwii, broads, agriculture

## Introduction

The Angles who arrived in the 5th century divided themselves simply: the people in the north became the north folk, the people in the south became the south folk. Fifteen centuries later, those two place names still mark the county boundaries: Norfolk, Suffolk. The names are blunt and practical. So is the landscape they cover. Norfolk is a low, flat ceremonial county on England's eastern bulge, jutting into the North Sea between The Wash and the Suffolk border. It produced Boudica, Horatio Nelson, Edith Cavell, and Thomas Paine. It hosted more American bomber crews per square mile than perhaps anywhere on earth during the Second World War. It contains the Broads, England's nearest equivalent to a national park, and a coast eroding into the sea at one of the fastest rates in the country.

## Boudica's Iceni

Norfolk's first famous inhabitants were the Iceni, a Brittonic tribe whose territory covered roughly the area of the modern county. They revolted against the Roman occupation in AD 47. Thirteen years later, in AD 60, they revolted again under their queen Boudica, sacking Camulodunum, Londinium, and Verulamium before being crushed by Roman regulars in a single decisive battle. Boudica's name has survived two thousand years; the names of her Roman opponents have not. After the crushing, the area opened to Roman roads, ports, and farms. When the Romans left, the coast became vulnerable to Saxon raiders, and a Germanic migration replaced the older population so thoroughly that the Brittonic language largely disappeared from this corner of England.

## Wool, Cathedrals, and the Black Death

The medieval centuries were Norfolk's prosperous era. The wool trade poured money into towns and villages, and the surplus paid for the construction of an extraordinary number of churches, many of them disproportionately large for the villages they served. Norwich Cathedral, begun in 1096, is one of the finest in England. By the late 16th century, Norwich was England's second-largest city. Then came catastrophe: over one-third of Norwich's population died in the plague of 1579, and another third in the Great Plague of 1665. Kett's Rebellion of 1549, led by the yeoman farmer Robert Kett, protested the enclosure of common land that left peasants with nowhere to graze their animals. The rebels took Norwich. They lost the Battle of Dussindale to John Dudley, 1st Duke of Northumberland. Kett was hanged from the walls of Norwich Castle, three thousand rebels dead beside him.

## The Mighty Eighth's County

Norfolk had its biggest geopolitical moment in the 20th century, when the flat fields became some of the most strategically important real estate in the European war. The American USAAF Eighth Air Force established dozens of heavy bomber bases across the county during 1942-1945. From RAF Hardwick, RAF Deopham Green, RAF North Pickenham, RAF Horsham St Faith, RAF Old Buckenham, RAF Snetterton Heath, and many others, B-17 Flying Fortresses and B-24 Liberators flew daylight missions over Germany. Tens of thousands of young Americans lived in Norfolk farmhouses and Nissen huts; hundreds did not come home. The flat agricultural geography that made Norfolk ideal for air bases is the same geography that lets you still trace the runways from the air today, ghostly geometric patterns under the wheat.

## Eroding Coast, Living Broads

Most of Norfolk's coast is chalk and clay, soft material that the North Sea has been carving away for centuries. The 1953 North Sea flood was the most recent catastrophe; villages disappear gradually in lesser ones. Around 200 settlements have been lost in Norfolk since the medieval period, taken by coastal erosion, agricultural enclosure, depopulation, and, in 1940, the establishment of the Stanford Training Area, which simply requisitioned villages for the war effort and never returned them. To the east, the Norfolk Broads, a network of rivers and lakes formed by medieval peat cutting, have national-park-equivalent status. The county is birdwatcher and walker country: Cromer for crabs, Sandringham for the royal residence, Wells-next-the-Sea for the beach, the Pedders Way for the long walk.

## Norfolk Notables

The county's people have ranged across many lives. Horatio Nelson, victor of Trafalgar, was born in the rectory at Burnham Thorpe. Edith Cavell, the nurse executed by the Germans in 1915 for helping prisoners escape occupied Belgium, came from Swardeston. Thomas Paine, the political pamphleteer whose Common Sense helped argue America into revolution, was born in Thetford. Edward Coke, the 17th-century jurist who wrote the Petition of Right, came from Mileham. Howard Carter spent his childhood in Swaffham before going on to find Tutankhamun's tomb. Diana, Princess of Wales, grew up at Park House near Sandringham. Sir James Dyson, the inventor, was born in Cromer. The local nickname for Norfolk people, 'Norfolk Dumplings,' refers to the flour dumplings that were a staple of the local diet, and not, despite the alleged medical slang, to anything else.

## Flight Context

Norfolk occupies the East of England between roughly 52.4 and 53.0 degrees north, 0.3 and 1.7 degrees east. The county's airspace is anchored by Norwich International Airport (EGSH), with RAF Marham (EGYM), RAF Honington (EGXH), and the former bases of Old Buckenham, Horsham St Faith, and others scattered across the agricultural interior. Recommended viewing altitude is 4,000 to 7,000 feet AGL: high enough to take in the geometry of the Broads and the eroding coast, low enough to pick out individual former airfields and the church towers that mark the medieval wool villages.
