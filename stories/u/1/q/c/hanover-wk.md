# Hanover

A city that lost ninety per cent of its centre to bombing, rebuilt without nostalgia, and settled its loudest argument about art with a public tug-of-war.

**Location:** 52.3744, 9.7386

**Categories:** city, urban-history, world-war-ii, public-art, germany

## Introduction

Twenty thousand letters of complaint reached Hanover's city offices in 1974, and most of them were about three women. The women were fibreglass - outsized, round, painted in loud stripes and dots - and the French artist Niki de Saint Phalle had set them down on the bank of the Leine where nobody could avoid them. Hanoverians were furious. The argument ran on until someone proposed the only resolution the two camps could agree to, which was a public tug-of-war. The Nanas stayed. They are called Sophie, Charlotte and Caroline now, after three women out of local history, and Saint Phalle was eventually made an honorary citizen of the city that had sent her all that mail. The underground passage running from the main station to Kröpcke carries her name too.

## Eighty-Eight Raids

Hanover was bombed eighty-eight times in the Second World War. The heaviest attack began on the night of 8 October 1943 and killed 1,245 people in a few hours; across the whole war the raids killed 6,782, nearly all of them civilians who lived here, and ninety per cent of the city centre was destroyed. When the counting was done, roughly forty half-timbered houses were still standing anywhere in Hanover, scattered across a landscape of rubble. Rather than leave them where they were, the rebuilders moved them - façades dismantled, carried across the city, and reassembled around a handful of survivors in one small quarter. The Altstadt you walk through today is genuinely old and genuinely relocated, a district made of the parts that came through. Around it stand the 1950s and 1960s blocks that went up fast, because people needed roofs. It is not a pretty city in the way that nearby Celle and Hamelin are pretty, and Hanoverians know it.

## The Crown That Came With the Title

For 123 years Hanover and Britain shared a monarch and almost nothing else. Sophia, granddaughter of James I and the designated Protestant heir under English law, died weeks too early to wear the crown; her son George Louis, Elector of Hanover, inherited it instead in 1714 and became King George I. What followed was a personal union rather than a political one - two states, two governments, one head wearing both hats. Hanoverian soldiers who emigrated to Britain formed the King's German Legion, the only German army to fight through the entirety of the Napoleonic Wars, and it was in the line at Waterloo in 1815. The arrangement ended in 1837. Britain's crown passed to Victoria, while Hanover's succession law barred a woman from the throne and sent it to her uncle instead, and the two kingdoms went their separate ways.

## The Business of Showing Things

Hanover's modern fortune was made by exhibitions. The fairground on the southeastern edge of the city is the largest exhibition ground in the world by most measures, and the calendar built around it turned a wrecked provincial capital into the place where Germany does business with everyone else. CeBIT set the computer industry's year here for decades. In 2000 the city staged the only world exposition Germany has held since the war, and it is still living with the results. Hanover became the capital of Lower Saxony after 1945, a state assembled from disparate pieces, and the northern end of Germany's first high-speed rail line, opened in 1991 between Würzburg and Hanover and still the country's longest. Try to book a hotel room here during a big trade fair and you will learn exactly how this economy works.

## The Lake and the Green

The landscape is flat, the Leine runs through it, and Hanover carries more forest and park than a city its size usually manages. South of the centre lies the Maschsee, a lake dug by hand in the 1930s and now the city's summer living room: joggers on a marked six-kilometre circuit, electric boats, and in a hard winter, once the ice passes thirteen centimetres, ten thousand people out on the surface at once. Herrenhausen supplies the formal counterweight a few kilometres northwest, and every summer six nations' pyrotechnicians compete over its baroque parterres across six Saturday nights. Hanover's Schützenfest claims to be the largest marksmen's fair of its kind, and its Oktoberfest claims second place worldwide - which is, in its way, a very Hanoverian pair of claims to make.

## Meet Me at the Clock

Kröpcke is where the city gathers. Three subway tunnels cross beneath it in independent alignments, the opera house stands at one edge, and a large green clock does the job a station concourse does elsewhere: meet me at the clock, and everybody knows. The neighbourhoods that escaped the worst of the bombing - Oststadt, List, Linden - still carry their late-nineteenth-century housing stock with historicist and Jugendstil ornament, and they are where you go to see what the whole city once looked like. If someone hands you two glasses, take them both in one hand. A Lüttje Lage is beer and korn drunk simultaneously, the two glasses tipped together in a single motion, and managing it without soaking your shirt is the local test of belonging.

## Flight Context

Hanover sits at 52.3744N, 9.7386E on the flat Leine valley in Lower Saxony, roughly 250 feet above sea level. From the air the city reads through three features: the green copper dome of the New Town Hall, the dark rectangle of the Maschsee immediately south of it, and the vast pale roofs of the Messe exhibition halls on the southeastern edge near Laatzen. The Leine curves through the western side of the centre; the A2 (east-west) and A7 (north-south) autobahns cross northeast of town. Hannover-Langenhagen (EDDV) lies about 11 km north of the centre, so the city core sits under its approach and departure paths - expect controlled airspace and coordinate before overflying at low level. Bremen (EDDW) is roughly 100 km northwest and Braunschweig-Wolfsburg (EDVE) about 60 km east. An overview altitude of 2,500 to 3,500 feet AGL frames the centre, the lake and the fairground together. On clear days the Harz massif is visible to the southeast as a long-range reference; the North German Plain here is frequently hazy in summer and low-stratus prone in winter.
