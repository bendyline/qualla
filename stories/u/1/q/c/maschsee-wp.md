# Maschsee

Designed to stop floods, dug by unemployed men for wages barely above the dole, opened by the Nazi Party in 1936, and hidden under canvas six years later.

**Location:** 52.3531, 9.7447

**Categories:** lake, urban-history, engineering, third-reich, germany

## Introduction

During the war, Hanover tried to hide its lake. Bomber crews navigating at night looked for water, and 78 hectares of it lying flat and reflective just south of the city centre amounted to a signpost, so canvas was stretched across the surface and fake landscapes were built on floating islands to break up the shape. The Maschsee was less than a decade old at the time. It had been designed by hydraulic engineers to solve a flooding problem, dug out of a swamp by unemployed men for wages barely above the dole, and opened in 1936 by a party that presented it as proof of what it could deliver. Today it is where Hanover swims, sails, jogs and drinks beer in the sun. All of that is true at once.

## A Lake Built Above a Swamp

The Leine and the Ihme flooded Hanover whenever the snow melted in the Harz, and a lake in the wide river valley had been discussed since the late nineteenth century as a way of taking the pressure off. In September 1925 the newly elected mayor, Arthur Menge, put a water engineer from the Hanover Technical College - the institution that is now Leibniz University - in charge of the hydraulics, while the city building department under Karl Elkart handled everything above ground. The clever move was counterintuitive. Rather than dig down into the marsh and let the Leine fill the hole, the final design raised the lake above the swamp in a shallow bowl, sitting higher than the river and filled by pumps, so that flood water and the mud it carries could never get in. In January 1926 the city granted 14,000 Reichsmarks toward sealing the basin. Then the plan sat untouched for eight years.

## Fifty-Eight Thousand Out of Work

What eventually moved it was the Depression. Hanover counted 58,000 unemployed residents in 1932 and had no money to build anything. The Nazi Party, in power from January 1933, had made the elimination of unemployment central to its propaganda and understood exactly what a shovel-ready public work was worth; the council approved the project on 28 November 1933, and the first ground was broken in the Leine swamp on 21 March 1934. The men who took the work took it because there was nothing else - pay barely above unemployment support, hard conditions, and many of them supplying their own tools. Fourteen locomotives hauled 365 tipping wagons along fifteen kilometres of temporary track, alongside diggers and five crawler tractors. The workforce began at a hundred. By the time the basin was finished, 1,650 people had worked on it.

## The Tower in the Middle

One obstacle stood literally in the way. A Bismarck Tower nearly twenty metres tall occupied the centre of the planned lake, and it was not a neutral piece of masonry: paramilitary formations had marched at its foot, and in 1933 it was the site of a book burning. It came down in 1935, in the course of the excavation, and the water closed over the ground it had stood on. Nothing about the demolition was ideological - the tower was simply in the wrong place for a lake - but the sequence is a fair picture of how quickly the landscape south of Hanover was rewritten in those two years.

## 21 May 1936

The basin was complete by early 1936 and the lake was opened on 21 May. Hundreds of thousands of Hanoverians and visitors lined the banks. The ceremony began with the trooping of the Nazi Party that had become standard for any public occasion, and some 6,000 sportspeople paraded afterwards alongside the armed forces and city officials. It made precisely the picture the regime wanted: a great civic amenity, delivered on schedule, with swastikas over it. Six years later the same water was being camouflaged against Allied bombers, and the sculptures already installed along its banks included a bronze Olympic torchbearer on an 18.5-metre column. The awkwardness has never fully dissolved. In 2005 the Spanish artist Santiago Sierra made the point by filling a walk-in room at the Kestner Society gallery with mud, so that anyone who wanted to think about where the Maschsee came from could go and stand in it.

## What the Lake Is Now

The Maschsee is the largest body of water in Hanover, kept topped up by three pumps that push groundwater from ponds at Ricklingen along an 800-metre pipe; from November to February the pumps rest and the level drops around 45 centimetres. In summer the Maschseefest runs for weeks along the shore and draws about two million people, a tradition begun in 1986 for the lake's fiftieth birthday. Electric boats work the water, rowing and sailing clubs occupy the western bank, and a six-kilometre circuit was measured out along the footpath in 2005. When the ice passes thirteen centimetres the city hoists a flag on the northern shore and the surface becomes public space - more than ten thousand people a day in a good freeze, and in 2003, when it reached twenty centimetres, market stalls out on the ice. The sculpture along the banks spans the whole strange history: Georg Kolbe's Human Couple from 1936-37, two lions by Arno Breker in 1938, a swimmer unveiled in 1948, and Alexander Calder's six-metre red steel Halberdier from 1972, striding in from a completely different world.

## Flight Context

The Maschsee lies at 52.3531N, 9.7447E, immediately south of Hanover's city centre in Lower Saxony, and is one of the best visual anchors in the region: a dark, sharp-edged rectangle roughly 2.4 km long on a north-south axis, 78 hectares, set in an otherwise flat green floodplain. The green copper dome of the New Town Hall sits at its northern tip, the Niedersachsenstadion and the Sprengel Museum on its western side, and the Leine runs parallel to the west. Because the lake was engineered to sit above the surrounding marsh, its banks read as raised embankments from low altitude. Hannover-Langenhagen (EDDV) is about 13 km north, and the lake sits under the approach corridor - expect controlled airspace and coordinate before low-level overflight. A viewing altitude of 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL frames the lake, the town hall and the stadium in one pass. In hard winters the frozen surface turns bright white against the dark city; in summer, sailing regattas and dragon-boat racing put heavy small-craft traffic on the water.
