# Town Musicians of Bremen

Four discarded animals set out to become musicians in Bremen, never get there, and end up as the city's best-loved bronze anyway.

**Location:** 53.0762, 8.8075

**Categories:** folklore, fairy-tale, sculpture, city-landmark, germany

## Introduction

Four animals set out for Bremen. Not one of them arrives. That joke sits at the centre of the most famous story the city has ever been attached to: a donkey, a dog, a cat and a rooster walk toward a town they have heard will pay them to make music, get distracted by a lit window in the woods, scare off a gang of robbers, and settle into the stolen house so comfortably that Bremen drops out of the tale altogether. The Brothers Grimm printed it in 1819. The city has spent the two centuries since embracing a legend in which it functions mainly as a place nobody reaches. Its bronze proof stands against the western wall of the town hall, where the donkey's front hooves have been rubbed to a bright polish by several generations of hands.

## Four Retirements, One Road

The story opens as an accounting problem. A donkey has hauled sacks to the mill for years and can no longer manage the load; his master begins calculating the cost of feed against the value of an old animal. The donkey does not wait for the arithmetic to finish. He leaves for Bremen, having decided that his bray qualifies him as a town musician. On the road he collects three more: a hunting dog too slow to keep up with the hunt, a cat whose teeth have gone blunt and who has heard talk of drowning, a rooster who has worked out what Sunday's soup will be made from. Each has served a household faithfully. Each is about to be thrown away for the crime of getting old. Each is recruited with the same argument - you have a voice, come with us - and that is the whole moral engine of the thing. It is a story about being written off, told by people who knew exactly how that felt.

## The Racket at the Window

They cannot reach Bremen in a single day. Night finds them in a forest, and the rooster, roosting highest, spots a distant light. The light belongs to a robbers' house with a laid table inside. What follows is the image the whole tale is remembered for: the donkey props his forefeet on the window ledge, the dog climbs onto his back, the cat onto the dog, the rooster onto the cat's head, and on a signal they all sound at once. Bray, bark, meow, crow - then the crash of glass. The robbers run into the woods. The animals eat, put out the light, and sleep. When one robber creeps back later to investigate, he is clawed, bitten, kicked and shouted at in the dark, and returns to his gang with an account of a witch, a man with a knife, a monster in the yard and a judge bellowing from the roof. The gang never comes back.

## The Town They Never Reached

The house suited the four musicians so well, the Grimms wrote, that they did not care to leave it. And so the tale ends in a forest clearing, with Bremen still somewhere out past the trees - a rumour, an ambition, a destination that turned out not to be the point. Folklorists index the plot as ATU 130, "The Animals in Night Quarters," and it entered the Grimms' collection as number 27 in the second edition of Kinder- und Hausmarchen, published in 1819. Their source was Dorothea Viehmann (1755-1815), a storyteller from near Kassel whose repertoire supplied a substantial share of the brothers' book. She died four years before this particular telling reached print.

## The Same Story, Everywhere

The tale type travels. Antti Aarne proposed an Asian origin for it. The French folklorist Paul Delarue sorted the surviving versions into two branches - a western one, in which the exiles are always domestic animals, which is the shape the Grimms recorded, and an eastern one built around different creatures, popular across Japan, China, Korea, Melanesia and Indonesia. Cousins of the story turn up as the Norwegian "The Sheep and the Pig Who Set Up House," the Finnish "The Animals and the Devil," the Flemish "The Choristers of St. Gudule," the Irish "Jack and His Comrades," the South African "The World's Reward." The twentieth century did what the twentieth century does with public-domain charm: Disney animated it in 1922, Ub Iwerks made a ComiColor version in 1935, a 1969 Soviet musical cartoon turned the animals into rock-and-roll vagabonds and became a national fixture, and the Muppets eventually took their turn.

## Bronze, and the Hands That Wear It

Gerhard Marcks cast the four of them in 1953 and set them on the western flank of Bremen's town hall, a few steps from the Marktplatz where the medieval Roland statue stands and the Rathaus itself carries UNESCO World Heritage protection. The sculpture is roughly two metres tall and reads as a single vertical column of animals: the donkey braced and patient underneath, the dog on his back, the cat above, the rooster crowning it. Most of the bronze has weathered to a dark, quiet green. The donkey's two front hooves have not. Visitors take hold of both at once, make a wish, and move on, and the accumulated friction of all that hoping has burnished the metal to the colour of new brass. It is the most honest monument in the city - not a hero on a horse, but four worn-out workers who refused to be disposable, kept polished by strangers who understood the reference.

## Flight Context

The sculpture stands at approximately 53.0762N, 8.8075E, on the western side of Bremen's Rathaus in the Altstadt, immediately north of the Weser. For an overview of the old town, 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL gives the best read on the Marktplatz, the twin towers of St. Petri Cathedral and the tight curve of the river around the medieval core. Bremen Airport (EDDW) lies roughly 4 km south of the city centre, making the Altstadt a natural feature on approach; Hannover-Langenhagen (EDDV) is about 100 km southeast. North German coastal weather brings frequent low stratus and autumn fog along the Weser valley, so plan for reduced visibility outside high summer. The sculpture itself is a street-level, hand-on-the-hoof landmark rather than an aerial one.
