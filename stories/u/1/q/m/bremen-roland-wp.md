# Bremen Roland

An archbishop's soldiers burned Bremen's wooden Roland in 1366, so the city carved a ten-metre replacement in stone and pointed its inscription straight at the cathedral.

**Location:** 53.0759, 8.8073

**Categories:** monument, medieval, world-heritage, city-landmark, germany

## Introduction

On the night of 28 May 1366, soldiers of Prince-Archbishop Albert II pulled down the wooden statue standing in Bremen's market square and burned it. Everyone who saw the ashes understood the message: the market square belonged to the archbishop's town, and the citizens who had put a figure of Charlemagne's paladin there to say otherwise had been corrected. Bremen took thirty-eight years to answer. In 1404, before work had even begun on the new town hall behind it, the council hired two stonemasons named Claws Zeelleyher and Jacob Olde, paid them 170 Bremen marks, and had them carve the thing again - this time in limestone, at a scale nobody could quietly remove in the dark. It is still standing in the same spot, six hundred years and two world wars later, and it is the oldest surviving Roland statue anywhere.

## A Legal Document, Carved as a Knight

Rolands went up in market squares across the old Holy Roman Empire, and they were never decoration. Each one was a claim - that this town held market rights, that its courts were its own, that it answered to the emperor rather than to whichever bishop or duke lived nearby. Bremen's is the ancestor of the type and the largest free-standing statue to survive from the German Middle Ages. The figure alone rises 5.47 metres and stands on a rostrum of sixty centimetres; add the supporting column and the Gothic canopy above it and the whole assembly reaches 10.21 metres. The knight came out of Elm limestone, quarried from the hills east of Braunschweig, and the pillar beneath him from Obernkirchen sandstone. His sword is drawn - Durendal, in the old chivalric romances - and his shield carries the double-headed imperial eagle, which in this square was less a heraldic flourish than a jurisdictional argument.

## The Sentence on the Shield

Read the shield and the argument stops being implicit. In Middle Low German it says: vryheit do ik yu openbar / de karl und mennich vorst vorwar / desser stede ghegheven hat / des dankt gode is min radt. Freedom I make plain to you, which Charlemagne and many princes indeed have given to this place; thank God for it, that is my advice. Note who is being addressed and where the statue is looking. Roland faces the cathedral. The city was informing the prince-archbishop, in stone, in the vernacular any Bremen fishwife could read aloud, that its liberties came from the emperor and were not his to withdraw. The claim was partly a fiction - Charlemagne granted Bremen nothing of the sort - but it was a useful one, and repeating it in ten metres of limestone for six centuries has a way of making a thing true.

## The Spare in the Cellar

The city grew superstitious about its own monument. Bremen remains free and independent, the story goes, only so long as Roland keeps watch over the market square, and from that grew the rumour that a second Roland lies hidden in the vaults beneath the town hall, ready to be swapped in should the first one ever fall. It is almost true, which is the best kind of legend. What actually sat in the cellar between the world wars was the Iron Roland, a wooden figure of the sort into which citizens hammered nails in exchange for war donations, and the presence of a spare Roland downstairs was apparently too good a coincidence for anyone to leave alone. No stone twin has ever been found. The insurance policy is a story, which is roughly how civic liberty works anyway.

## The Little One Across the River

In 1737 the citizens of Neustadt, on the far bank of the Weser, put up a Roland of their own. They had reason to feel the point needed making: since 1642 their rights of citizenship had been sharply limited compared with those of the old town, and the first Neustadt citizens' company built their small copy of the 1404 original as a response. The inscription they gave it is one of the more disarming things in Bremen. You have the big one standing there, it reads, we stand before the little one; we are not the big city, we are the little one and the appendage - but we are as much city as you are. Six hundred years of Rolands, and the finest sentence attached to any of them belongs to the suburb that felt left out.

## Sandbags, and a New Head

The twentieth century came close to ending the run. In 1939 the statue was boxed inside a timber casing packed with sand, and later given masonry splinter protection, while the city around it burned; the bombing that destroyed much of Bremen left Roland encased and standing. Age did what the RAF did not. During the thorough restoration of 1983 and 1984 the weathered head was lifted off and replaced with a faithful copy, and the original now sits indoors at the Focke-Museum, out of the weather at last. Since 1973 the statue has been protected under the Monument Protection Act, and in July 2004 UNESCO inscribed it on the World Heritage list together with the town hall behind it - a stone knight put up to defy one authority, now guarded by an international one.

## Flight Context

The Bremen Roland stands at approximately 53.0759N, 8.8073E, in the centre of the Marktplatz in Bremen's Altstadt, directly in front of the Rathaus and facing St. Petri Cathedral. The statue itself is far too small to resolve from the air; navigate to the Altstadt instead, an unmistakable oval of dense old-town roofs inside the ring of the former ramparts, with the Weser curving along its southern edge and the cathedral's twin towers marking the square. Recommended overview altitude is 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL. Bremen Airport (EDDW) lies about 4 km south of the centre, putting the old town squarely under the approach path; Hamburg (EDDH) is roughly 100 km northeast and Hannover-Langenhagen (EDDV) about 100 km southeast. The Weser valley produces low cloud and river fog readily from October through March.
