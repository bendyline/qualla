# Bremen

A twelve-hundred-year-old Hanseatic port on the Weser that governed itself for most of its history, and put a ten-metre stone knight in the market square to make the point.

**Location:** 53.0758, 8.8072

**Categories:** Cities in Germany, Hanseatic League, Port cities, World Heritage Sites in Germany, Lower Saxony and Bremen

## Introduction

Roland has stood in Bremen's market square since 1404, and he is not decoration. Rolands were erected in the market squares of medieval Central Europe as legal instruments carved in stone: this town holds market rights, this town answers to the emperor and not to the local lord. Bremen's is the largest free-standing statue to survive from the German Middle Ages - the figure alone is five and a half metres, 10.21 metres with its base and canopy - and he faces the cathedral across the square, which is precisely where the archbishop whose authority the city spent centuries refusing happened to sit. In 2004 UNESCO listed him together with the Gothic town hall behind him. The bronze donkey, dog, cat and cockerel stacked up beside the town hall collect more photographs, and they have their own story, but Roland is why the square is World Heritage.

## A Republic on the Weser

Bremen has more than twelve hundred years of history and spent most of it governing itself. It was a member of the medieval Hanseatic League, the trading confederation that ran the northern seas, and it kept the habit of independence long after the Hanse dissolved. The formal name is still the Free Hanseatic City of Bremen.

It is also, technically, a country in miniature. Together with Bremerhaven at the mouth of the Weser it forms Germany's smallest federal state - two separate enclaves surrounded by Lower Saxony, joined by a river. The city itself is an odd shape: about ten kilometres long along the Weser and only two or so across, laid out flat on the river plain, so that walking away from the water in either direction gets you out of town surprisingly fast. Around 586,000 people live here.

## The Schnoor

A few minutes southeast of the market square the streets suddenly shrink. The Schnoor is Bremen's oldest surviving quarter, first mentioned in writing in the thirteenth century, and its little houses - most of them built between the fifteenth and eighteenth centuries - are packed along lanes narrow enough to touch both walls at once.

The name is Low German for "string", and locals explain it two ways: the houses are threaded along the lanes like beads, and the people who lived here twisted rope and cordage for the ships in the port. River fishermen, craftsmen and traders made their lives in these rooms. The lanes now hold jewellers, paper-model shops, a year-round Christmas shop and a great deal of souvenir trade, but the buildings are the real thing, and their scale tells you more about medieval Bremen than any museum case.

## The Street That Coffee Built

Böttcherstraße runs 108 metres from the market square down toward the Weser, and it is the strangest thing in the city. Between 1922 and 1931, Ludwig Roselius - a Bremen coffee merchant who had made a fortune from decaffeinated coffee, sold as Kaffee HAG - bought up the lane and rebuilt it as a single work of art, with the sculptor Bernhard Hoetger directing the design.

What came out is Expressionist brick: dark, sculpted, angular, more grown than built, and completely unlike the merchant gables a hundred metres away. One building carries a carillon of thirty Meissen porcelain bells that chime several times a day. The street now holds galleries, museums, upmarket shops and a couple of fish restaurants: 108 metres that took nine years and one man's coffee money.

## The River Side

Bremen is a port city sixty kilometres from the sea, which is a very Hanseatic thing to be: far enough up the Weser to be defensible, close enough to load ships. The Schlachte, the old riverbank quay, is now a promenade of bars and restaurants where the city eats outdoors from the first warm evening onward. Downstream lies the Überseestadt - "overseas city" - the answer to what happens when a working port moves on and leaves its warehouses standing; Speicher XI, an enormous brick shed of a building, now houses a university of the arts and a museum of the port.

On the other side of the centre, the Osterdeich embankment fills up on any decent evening with groups on the grass, a grill and a crate of beer between them. Werder Bremen play at the Weserstadion on the north bank, a kilometre east of the centre. And the Bürgerpark, a big immaculate green in the middle of the city, has a rowing pond, mini-golf and a petting zoo.

## Kluten, Kohl and a Tailor on the Ice

Every 6 January a group of men in black walk down to the Punkendeich accompanied by a tailor who is required to weigh ninety-nine pounds, and the tailor tests the Weser with a hot iron to establish whether the river is going or standing. The Eiswette started as a genuine wager in November 1828 - eighteen Bremen gentlemen betting a communal cabbage dinner on whether the Weser would freeze - and it has been repeated every year since. The river has not frozen in decades. The dinner now raises several hundred thousand euros annually for the German maritime rescue service.

The rest of the calendar is committed in the same spirit. The Freimarkt in the second half of October is among the oldest and largest fairgrounds in Germany. Beck's is brewed here and runs tours. The Ratskeller beneath the town hall keeps the oldest wines in the country. Eat Labskaus, Knipp and green cabbage in winter; buy Kluten, cubes of peppermint half-dipped in chocolate, by the bag.

## Flight Context

Bremen sits at 53.08 degrees N, 8.81 degrees E, on the River Weser about 60 km upstream from the North Sea. From the air the city reads as a long, narrow band following the meandering Weser on flat river plain - roughly 10 km along the northwest-southeast axis and only about 2 km across - with the port basins and Überseestadt warehouses on the northwest side and the green rectangle of the Bürgerpark just north of the main station. Bremen Airport (EDDW / BRE) lies about 3.5 km south of the centre, with tram 6 running into town in ten minutes; Hamburg (EDDH) is roughly 100 km northeast and Hannover (EDDV) around 100 km southeast. Best viewing altitude 2,000-4,000 feet for the old town, higher for the river's full course to Bremerhaven. Low cloud and sea haze off the North Sea are common in winter.
