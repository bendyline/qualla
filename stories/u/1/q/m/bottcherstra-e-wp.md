# Böttcherstraße

One hundred metres of expressionist brick in Bremen, built on a decaffeinated-coffee fortune by a patron who courted the Nazis - and whose street they condemned as degenerate.

**Location:** 53.0750, 8.8056

**Categories:** architecture, expressionism, museum, city-landmark, germany

## Introduction

One hundred metres of brick, paid for with coffee that had the caffeine taken out of it. Böttcherstraße runs from Bremen's market square down toward the Weser, a lane short enough to walk in ninety seconds and strange enough that almost nobody manages it that fast. Ludwig Roselius patented a commercial decaffeination process in 1906, sold the result as Kaffee HAG, and spent the 1920s buying the street building by building. He handed the artistic direction to the sculptor Bernhard Hoetger, and between 1922 and 1931 the two of them produced something with no real parallel in Germany: an entire streetscape built in a variant of expressionism, several of its houses textbook Brick Expressionism, all of it squeezed into a medieval alley. The regime Roselius twice applied to join would later file the whole street under degenerate art. It is still standing.

## Coopers, Then Coffee

The street is named for the trade that worked it. Böttcher means cooper, and for centuries the barrel-makers lived along this connection between the Bremer Marktplatz and the river - a short, practical route between the merchants and the water that fed them. The whole logic of the lane depended on that geography, and when the harbour was relocated in the mid-nineteenth century, the geography stopped mattering. Böttcherstraße faded into a back alley. That is roughly the state Roselius found it in when he bought the house at number 6 in 1902, under pressure from the previous owners, and made it the headquarters of the firm that would become Kaffee HAG. The building is now the Ludwig Roselius Museum. He kept buying, lot by lot, until he owned enough of the street to rebuild it as an idea.

## Hoetger's Hundred Metres

The first wave went up after the First World War in the local materials - brick and sandstone - and produced the HAG-Haus, the Haus St. Petrus, and the House of the Seven Lazy Brothers. Then the street began arguing with itself. In 1926 Roselius commissioned the Paula Modersohn-Becker House, its outer walls worked into relief, its interior following principles of organic architecture; it opened in 1927 as the first museum anywhere in the world devoted to the work of a woman painter. In 1931 came Haus Atlantis, built in glass, steel and concrete, a deliberate collision with everything around it, along with the Robinson Crusoe House. Above the entrance to the Glockenspiel House hangs the detail visitors remember longest: a carillon of thirty Meissen porcelain bells, installed in 1934, which chimes three times a day while carved panels rotate past showing pioneers of ocean and air. Porcelain bells in North German weather should not work. They ring anyway.

## An Attempt to Think German

Roselius was a sympathiser of National Socialism, and the street was never politically innocent. He followed the völkisch-Nordic ideas of the ideologists Julius Langbehn and Herman Wirth, including their belief in the irreplaceable value of a Nordic race, and he intended Böttcherstraße to make those ideas physical. "The re-erection of Böttcherstraße is an attempt to think German," he said. He wanted, he wrote elsewhere, to break a spell he believed the Romans had cast on his people. In 1936 he and Hoetger mounted a gilded relief at the street's entrance - Der Lichtbringer, the Bringer of Light, showing the archangel Michael fighting the powers of darkness - and dedicated it to Hitler. In October of that year Roselius increased his stake in the Focke-Wulf aircraft company to 46 percent and held its board meetings on the street. He applied for NSDAP membership twice. He was refused twice.

## Listed as Degenerate

The rejection was aesthetic before it was anything else. In early 1934 Roselius was summoned to a meeting with Hitler and Otto Wagener, at which Hitler criticised him for letting Hoetger run loose with expressionism. Roselius defended his architect, then changed the subject. It did not help. On 9 September 1936, in a speech on cultural matters at the Nuremberg party congress, Hitler dismissed this whole strand of völkisch art and coined a sneer for it - Boettcher-Straßen-Kultur, culture in the style of the Böttcherstraße. The party denounced the street as reflecting a divergent view of culture. Then, on 7 May 1937, it was formally listed as an architectural monument, with the listing describing it as an example of the degenerate art of the Weimar period. A man who wanted to build the regime's aesthetic in brick had instead built the thing it used as a warning label. Seven years later the RAF finished what the ideology had only sneered at, and much of the street was destroyed in the bombing of 1944.

## Rebuilt, Sold, Bought Back

Kaffee HAG restored most of the façades to their original state by 1954. In 1973 the ensemble came under the protection of the Monument Protection Act - guarded now by a democratic state, catalogued once as decadence. Ownership then wandered: in 1979 Ludwig Roselius Jr. sold the coffee company and the street together to General Foods, and two years later bought the street back. When serious structural decay surfaced in 1989, the Sparkasse Bremen bought the whole lane, every building except Haus Atlantis, and finished restoring it in 1999. Since 2004 it has belonged to a foundation. Today the hundred metres hold the Museen Böttcherstraße, craft workshops, bars, restaurants, shops and a hotel, and the bells still ring at their appointed hours - a street built as an argument, now visited mostly by people who came for the porcelain.

## Flight Context

Böttcherstraße lies at approximately 53.0750N, 8.8056E, in Bremen's Altstadt, running roughly 100 m south-west from the Marktplatz toward the Weser. At that width it is invisible from the air; navigate instead to the Marktplatz, marked by the Rathaus and the twin towers of St. Petri Cathedral, with the Weser bending immediately south. Recommended overview altitude for the old town is 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL. Bremen Airport (EDDW) sits about 4 km south of the centre and the Altstadt is a clear landmark on approach; Hannover-Langenhagen (EDDV) lies roughly 100 km southeast. Expect low cloud and river fog in the Weser valley through autumn and winter.
