# Bremen City Hall

Bremen's merchants built their town hall a few inches larger than the archbishop's palace, and then spent six centuries making sure the point was not missed.

**Location:** 53.0759, 8.8076

**Categories:** architecture, unesco-world-heritage, civic-history, medieval, germany

## Introduction

The insult was measured in inches. When Bremen's councilmen built their new town hall around 1400, they made both of its great halls slightly longer and slightly wider than the great hall in the archbishop's palace across the way - not enough to be crude about it, exactly enough that anyone who cared could check. The market square had been finished a century earlier and had been the archbishop's stage. Now a two-storey brick hall, 41.5 metres by 15.8, faced onto it and took the view. In front of it stood a stone knight, the Roland, holding a sword and the claim that went with it: this is a free city, answerable to the emperor and not to the man in the palace. Six centuries later the building is still the seat of Bremen's mayor. The archbishop's palace is gone.

## A Building That Argued

Burgomaster Johann Hemeling and the councilmen Friedrich Wagner and Hinrich von der Trupe drove the project through, and almost every choice in it was a statement. Sixteen large sculptures of emperors and prince-electors ran across the façade, asserting Bremen's claim to be an imperial city, with four ancient philosophers added for weight. The entrances went at the sides rather than facing the square - the same arrangement the palace used. It was also, quietly, a fortress: two wall-walks, one above the market-front gallery and one running around the gutters of the hip roof, reached from the upper hall by four small stair towers. The ground-floor gallery that now looks like shelter for market traders was nothing of the kind for its first few centuries. Trials were held there. Old Saxon law forbade justice being done behind closed doors, which is probably also why Bremen's earlier town hall - recorded in 1229 as a domus theatralis, a show house - seems to have been built with an open hall.

## The Face Lüder von Bentheim Gave It

By the end of the sixteenth century Bremen was rich again, and the town hall got a new front. The architect and mason Lüder von Bentheim worked in two passes. Between 1595 and 1596 he replaced the ten pointed-arch windows of the upper hall with large rectangles. Then, from 1608 to 1612, he did something considerably bolder: the two middle windows and the proclamation door between them came out, and in their place went a huge avant-corps of slim pillars, columns and glass, crowned with an ornamented gable in the Flemish Renaissance manner and flanked by two more. Reliefs and balustrades filled in the rest. Von Bentheim was working from printed pattern books - Hans Vredeman de Vries, Hendrik Goltzius, Jacob Floris - and the result is the Weser Renaissance front that made the building famous: Dutch ornament applied to a Gothic brick box, and somehow the better for the collision.

## The Wine Underneath

Wine has been stored and sold beneath the halls since the building went up. The Ratskeller has been in business since 1405, and for four centuries no citizen of Bremen could sell wine at all without the council's permission - a monopoly that survived until 1815. What accumulated down there is remarkable even now. The cellar holds the oldest wine barrel in Germany, a Rüdesheimer dated 1653, and something on the order of 650 varieties of German wine, the largest selection of exclusively German wines anywhere. Directly above it, the Lower Hall keeps its original unadorned form: stone floor, exposed timber beams, whitewashed walls. It served as a market once, for spices and cloth, the expensive goods that came off Bremen's ships. It holds exhibitions now, and it is still the plainest room in the building.

## Boards, and a Fire Brigade

The complex kept growing. In 1803 the neighbouring archbishop's palace finally passed to the city; by 1818 it was being partly dismantled and rebuilt as a neoclassical office block, the Stadthaus. That in turn came down in 1909, and the excavation for its replacement turned up far more Gothic material than historians had expected. The New Town Hall built on the site between 1909 and 1913, by the Munich architect Gabriel von Seidl, is roughly twice the size of the old building, and Seidl's real achievement was making something that large defer to something that small. Then came the war. More than sixty per cent of Bremen was destroyed by air raids, and the town hall came through with little damage - because its outer walls had been boarded up, and because the fire brigade fought for it. Among the great civic buildings of northern Germany, it is one of the few that is not a reconstruction.

## The Gold Room

Off the Upper Hall, still used for the city's ceremonial occasions and hung with a painting of Charlemagne and Bishop Ansgar dated 1532, there is a small chamber added in 1608 and known as the Güldenkammer. In 1905 the artist Heinrich Vogeler was handed the room and redecorated the entire thing in Art Nouveau - every door handle, the fireguard, the chandeliers, and gilded leather wallpaper of the kind called cuir de Cordoue. It is a jewel box set into a fortress, five centuries of taste compressed into a few square metres. In July 2004 UNESCO listed the town hall together with the Roland standing outside, not only for the architecture but as evidence of how civic self-government developed inside the Holy Roman Empire. The listing describes a political idea. The building has been making that argument in brick since 1400, and it has not stopped.

## Flight Context

Bremen City Hall stands at 53.0759N, 8.8076E on the northeastern side of the Marktplatz in the old town, about 500 m north of the Weser. From the air the market square reads as a small irregular clearing in dense roofline, framed by four landmarks that identify it unambiguously: the twin towers of Bremen Cathedral immediately east, the single spire of the Church of Our Lady to the north, the gabled Schütting opposite the town hall, and the Bürgerschaft's modern glass front on the southeastern side. The Roland statue is a pale point in the open square in front of the hall. The Weser curves west and south of the old town, with the Schnoor quarter's tight roofs between the square and the river. Bremen Airport (EDDW) lies about 4 km south of the centre, so the old town sits under its approach corridor and inside controlled airspace - coordinate before low-level overflight. Hannover-Langenhagen (EDDV) is roughly 100 km southeast. An overview altitude of 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL frames the market square and the Weser bend together; low sun makes the Weser Renaissance façade legible from the southwest.
