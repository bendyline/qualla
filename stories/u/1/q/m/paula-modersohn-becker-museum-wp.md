# Paula Modersohn-Becker Museum

The first museum anywhere built for a woman painter, opened twenty years after she died at thirty-one having sold almost nothing.

**Location:** 53.0751, 8.8060

**Categories:** museum, art, expressionism, architecture, germany

## Introduction

She dated the painting 25 May 1906 - her wedding anniversary, spent in Paris, three months after leaving her husband. In it she stands stripped to the hips, an amber necklace at her throat, a white cloth low across her body, both hands cupped beneath a rounded belly. She was not pregnant. No woman in the recorded history of European painting had put herself on a canvas naked before, and Paula Modersohn-Becker did it in a rented Paris studio at the age of thirty, with almost nothing sold, almost nothing exhibited, and eighteen months to live. The picture hangs today in a dark brick building on a short lane in Bremen, in the first museum in the world devoted to a woman artist. It opened in 1927. She had been dead for twenty years.

## Seven Hundred Paintings and Almost No Buyers

The output is the part that stops people. In an active painting life of barely a decade Modersohn-Becker produced more than seven hundred paintings and over a thousand drawings, working through Worpswede winters and four separate stints in Paris, pushing toward flattened, weighted, unsentimental figures that nobody in Germany was making yet and that read now as a door swinging open into modernism. She sold a handful. She died on 20 November 1907, aged thirty-one, of a pulmonary embolism eighteen days after giving birth to her daughter, during the long bed rest her doctors had prescribed. The nude self-portraits had never been shown publicly; like most of the work, they became visible only once she was gone. Rilke, who had known her in Worpswede, sat down in Paris the following autumn and wrote Requiem for a Friend about it.

## The Building Hoetger Made Her

The museum exists because two men who had known Paris in her lifetime decided it should. Ludwig Roselius, the Bremen merchant who had patented a commercial coffee decaffeination process and grown rich on it, commissioned the building; the sculptor and architect Bernhard Hoetger, who had met Modersohn-Becker in Paris, designed it. What he produced on Böttcherstraße in 1926 and 1927 is not a neutral container. The brick is worked into relief across the outer walls, the interior follows principles of organic architecture rather than the rectilinear logic of a gallery, and the whole thing behaves less like a display case than like a piece of sculpture that visitors happen to walk through. It counts among the key works of expressionist architecture in Germany, and since 1973 it has carried monument protection of its own.

## Condemned Twice Over

In 1935 local Nazis attacked the art and the architecture together, and the following September, in a speech on culture at the Nuremberg party congress, Hitler dismissed the entire street and everything on it. The museum survived that judgement without being demolished - officially catalogued instead as an example of the degenerate art of the Weimar period, preserved as a warning label. Her paintings elsewhere fared worse. In 1937 seventy of her works were purged from German public collections, either destroyed or hung in the degenerate art exhibitions to be jeered at. A woman who had sold almost nothing while alive was, thirty years after her death, considered dangerous enough to remove from the walls of a country that had barely begun to look at her.

## What Hangs There Now

The collection follows the whole arc - student pictures from her training years in Berlin, the Worpswede work, and the Paris paintings of 1906 and 1907 in which everything she had been reaching for finally arrives. Roselius's own holdings form the core, supplemented by loans from the Paula Modersohn-Becker Foundation. The museum also holds the most extensive collection anywhere of Hoetger's work, and the rooms he designed now take temporary exhibitions of classical modern art. Since 2005 there has been one more voice in the building: Jenny Holzer's electronic homage For Paula Modersohn-Becker, installed permanently in the stairwell, text moving in the dark on the way up to the paintings. Roughly a century after a woman who could not sell her work painted herself as no one had, the sentences run past on the wall as you climb.

## A Hundred Metres of Bremen

The address matters more than most museum addresses do. Böttcherstraße is a hundred metres of expressionist brick running from Bremen's market square down toward the Weser, a lane that a coffee fortune rebuilt in the 1920s and that now holds workshops, bars, a carillon of porcelain bells and the Museen Böttcherstraße, of which this is one half - the Ludwig Roselius Museum, in the old merchant house at number 6 that Roselius bought in 1902, is the other. The company that manages it is named for the street. That is a modest arrangement for the artist involved, and Bremen knows it: the city's most valuable painter is the one whose canvases nobody wanted, filed under the name of the alley where they finally found a wall.

## Flight Context

The museum sits at approximately 53.0751N, 8.8060E, on Böttcherstraße in Bremen's Altstadt, roughly 100 m southwest of the Marktplatz. At street scale it is invisible from the air; navigate to the old town instead - a compact oval of dense roofs bounded by the ring of the former ramparts, with the Weser along its southern edge and the twin towers of St. Petri Cathedral marking the market square. Recommended overview altitude is 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL. Bremen Airport (EDDW) lies about 4 km south of the centre and the Altstadt sits close to the approach; Hannover-Langenhagen (EDDV) is roughly 100 km southeast and Hamburg (EDDH) about 100 km northeast. Expect low stratus and river fog in the Weser valley through autumn and winter.
