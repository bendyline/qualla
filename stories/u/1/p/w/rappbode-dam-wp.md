# Rappbode Dam

Germany's highest dam holds back 106 metres of water by weight alone, was begun under one German state and finished under another, and now has a 483-metre footbridge strung across the gorge below it.

**Location:** 51.7400, 10.8934

**Categories:** Dams in the Harz, Dams in Saxony-Anhalt, Dams completed in 1959, Engineering, Water supply

## Introduction

Two inscriptions used to greet drivers coming out of the tunnel at the end of the dam road. They credited the wall of concrete behind them to socialism, to the workers' and farmers' state, and to the tenth anniversary of the German Democratic Republic. When the crest was reconstructed between 2000 and 2003, the national emblems came off the plaques. The dam itself did not change at all. It is still 106 metres of grey concrete wedged across a Harz valley, still the highest dam in Germany, and still supplying drinking water to a string of cities that stretches as far east as Leipzig — a piece of infrastructure that outlived the political system that finished it and the one that started it.

## Begun by One Germany, Finished by Another

The argument for damming the Bode began with disaster. Floods at the start of the twentieth century sent engineers looking at the river's tributaries, and the first serious proposal called for a single enormous barrier that would have drowned several villages. That plan was abandoned. The scheme completed in 1938 did the same job with a chain of smaller structures instead, and not one settlement had to be given up for it. Construction started that year and stopped in 1942, when the war took the labour and the materials. It resumed after 1945 under East Germany, with the original layout kept and the technology brought up to date, and the brief expanded to include drinking-water treatment. The foundation stone was laid on 1 September 1952. On 7 October 1959 the dam went into service.

## Held Back by Weight Alone

The barrier is a straight gravity dam, which is the least subtle design in the engineer's catalogue and among the most reliable. There is no arch transferring load into the valley walls and no clever geometry. The concrete simply outweighs the water. It stands 106 metres high and 415 metres long, built in sections and completed in 1959, and holds a reservoir of roughly 109 million cubic metres. By the turn of the century the crest had aged badly: the roadway and its concrete had weathered to the point where they could no longer take the traffic crossing them, and both were renewed before 2002. Behind the wall, the water is unusually good. Hardness averages three degrees on the German scale, soft enough to be ideal for steam raising and for washing.

## A Reservoir That Comes Out of Taps

The lake's first job is not power. It is a reserve drinking-water store for the towns east of the Harz, and pressure pipes run out of it to Aschersleben, Halberstadt, Bernburg on the Saale, Halle and Leipzig. Hydroelectric generation happens, but only in a small way. What makes the system work is the chain of structures around the main dam. Upstream at Königshütte, a diversionary dam catches the Bode just before the Warme and Kalte Bode meet and shunts part of the flow down a channel toward the Rappbode reservoir. The Mandelholz dam on the Kalte Bode, a river that rises at the foot of the Brocken, holds back rock and nutrients before they reach the main barrier, as do auxiliary dams on the Hassel and the Rappbode. Downstream, the Wendefurth dam adds flood protection and stores water for a pumped-storage power station. Each piece is unremarkable on its own. Together they run the eastern Harz's water.

## The Red Rock and the Drowned Bridge

There is a viewpoint above the reservoir called the Rotestein, meaning red rock, named for the colour of the diabase it is made of. Before the valley filled, these rocks stood over the old road between Hasselfelde and Rübeland, at the point where it crossed the Rappbode on a stone bridge. The bridge is still down there, under the water. Information boards at the viewpoint explain the reservoir and the country around it, and the spot is checkpoint 54 in the Harzer Wandernadel, the hiking network that stamps its walkers' passports across the whole range. A quieter option is the Rappbodeblick, on cliffs about three kilometres north of Hasselfelde as the crow flies, checkpoint 56 in the same system. Neither takes long to reach. Both give you the scale of what was put here, which the road across the crest does not.

## Walking Above It

In 2017 an adventure operator strung a footbridge across the gorge below the dam. The Titan RT runs 483 metres, most of it in a single freely hanging span, and it bounces underfoot in a way that stops conversation. From it, and from the crest, people now jump on bungee cords from a hundred metres above the water. A 39-metre observation tower, the Solitair, opened in spring 2022, and a zipline about a kilometre long crosses the valley. It is an odd afterlife for a piece of flood-control infrastructure conceived in response to drowned villages and built as a monument to a state that no longer exists. The dam takes no notice. It goes on doing the two things it was designed for: stopping the winter water, and sending the summer water east.

## Flight Context

The dam sits at 51.7400°N, 10.8934°E, in the eastern Harz between Wendefurth and Hasselfelde. From the air the reservoir is the clearest landmark for miles: a long, dark, many-fingered lake filling a set of forested valleys, with the pale straight line of the dam crest closing its northern end. The Titan RT footbridge shows as a thin thread across the gorge just downstream. The Brocken dome lies about 20 km northwest and makes a reliable orientation point. Sightseeing altitudes of 2,500-4,000 ft AGL give the best sense of the wall's height against the valley; higher than that and 106 metres of concrete flattens into a grey stripe. Nearby airports include EDVE (Braunschweig-Wolfsburg) to the north, EDDV (Hannover) to the northwest, EDDP (Leipzig/Halle) to the southeast and EDVK (Kassel-Calden) to the southwest. Morning valley fog is common over the reservoir in autumn; the water surface also produces glassy, reflective conditions that can confuse height judgement at low level.
