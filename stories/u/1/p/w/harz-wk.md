# Harz

Germany's northernmost mountain range made its fortune on silver, its reputation on a songbird, and spent forty years cut in half by the Iron Curtain. It now markets itself as the Kingdom of the Lynx.

**Location:** 51.7500, 10.6333

**Categories:** Mountain ranges, Regions, Mining history, National parks, Germany

## Introduction

Sometime around 1730, miners arriving from the Tyrol carried canaries north with them into the Upper Harz, and the birds stayed. Over the following century the villages between Lautenthal and Sankt Andreasberg bred them into something new: a canary that sang with its beak closed, in soft rolling trills, and that sold across Europe under the name Harzer Roller. Raising the birds and building their cages became a second income for mining families whose first income came out of the ground. Surplus hens went underground as well, carried into the workings as living detectors of a gas that men could not smell. A range that made its money on silver and lead also made a small fortune on song.

## A Range That Behaves Like an Island

The Harz runs a hundred kilometres from northwest to southeast and thirty across, lying between the Elbe and the Weser over parts of Lower Saxony, Saxony-Anhalt and a corner of Thuringia. It is not high by Alpine standards. What it is, is alone. Terraced plateaus of limestone, sandstone and slate rise abruptly out of the North German Plain, cut through by narrow, deep valleys. The granite Brocken tops out at 1,141 metres and the Wurmberg at 971. In the west, the Upper Harz plateau falls from around a thousand metres to 485 in the centre and takes the full weight of the Atlantic westerlies, which keep it cold and damp even in July. Southeast of there, the Lower Harz gets an easier climate, gentle enough for grain and cattle. That difference in weather became a difference in livelihoods, and the two halves of the range have never entirely been the same place.

## Ore, Water, Wood

Between the tenth and sixteenth centuries the Harz became one of the most valuable pieces of ground in Europe. Silver, lead, iron, zinc and copper came out of it in quantity, and the wealth flowed downhill to the Electorate of Hanover. What made the mining possible was not just the ore but the two things stacked on top of it: water to drive the wheels and timber to fire the smelters. Both were here in abundance, and both were used until they nearly ran out. The water is now managed rather than merely exploited. A system of dams holds back the winter floods, generates a modest amount of hydroelectricity and supplies drinking water across the region. Quarrying for marble, granite and gypsum continues, along with wood processing for paper and cardboard, but tourism has long since become the thing the Harz lives on.

## The Line Down the Middle

Until 1990, the boundary between Lower Saxony and Saxony-Anhalt was also the boundary between two countries, complete with fence and watchtower. On the eastern side, access to whole villages was restricted, and roads and railway lines were closed or pulled up entirely. Forty years of enforced neglect left the forest largely undisturbed, which is precisely why a national park could be declared so quickly afterwards: the Saxony-Anhalt portion in October 1990, the Lower Saxon portion in January 1994, the two merged into a single 247-square-kilometre park on 1 January 2006. The division left other marks. Unemployment stayed high in the east after the industrial combines collapsed, and Goslar and its neighbours still compete with the Wernigerode, Quedlinburg and Blankenburg cluster over which side offers the authentic Harz. Ask a local where to go and you may learn more about where they grew up than about the mountains.

## Kingdom of the Lynx

The last lynx in the Harz was killed in 1818. Wolves and brown bears went the same way, hunted out of a landscape that had been full of them. Reintroduction began in 1999, and between 2000 and 2004 nineteen lynx raised in zoo breeding programmes were released into the hills. Kittens have been born in the wild here since 2002, and the region now sells itself as the Kingdom of the Lynx. The forest they came back to is having a harder time. Roughly four-fifths of it is spruce, planted as a nineteenth-century monoculture to feed the mines and mills, and spruce planted in rows is exactly what bark beetles want. Beetle outbreaks have surged since 2006, the storm Kyrill flattened stands in 2007, and warmer, drier summers have finished the job on many slopes. Whole hillsides now stand grey and bare. Park policy is to leave the deadwood where it falls and let a different forest work out what comes next.

## Sour Cheese, Clear Korn, a Waterfall Built to Order

Harzer cheese is made from low-fat curd and contains about one percent fat, which does nothing to soften its smell or its taste. Nordhausen, on the southern edge, is known for Doppelkorn, a rye spirit drunk neat, and its distilling tradition runs deep enough that a tax on locally produced liquor appears in a document from 1507. Altenau has brewed beer by hand since 1617. In the Oker valley the river runs below its dam past the Rabenklippen, crags named for a legend in which a starving missionary was kept alive by a flock of ravens; a lynx enclosure now sits nearby. A little further on at Romkerhalle, an innkeeper opened a guesthouse in 1863 and had a waterfall built to match, diverting a stream through a 350-metre channel so that water would drop past his terrace. Since 1988 the handful of buildings there has advertised itself as the smallest kingdom in the world, on the technicality that they sit in unincorporated land belonging to no municipality at all.

## Flight Context

The Harz centres near 51.75°N, 10.63°E. From the air it is unmistakable: an isolated block of forested upland roughly 100 km by 30 km, rising out of flat farmland with no foothills to soften the transition. The Brocken dome at 1,141 m carries a distinctive round transmitter tower and is the highest ground for hundreds of kilometres in any direction; it is under cloud on most days of the year, so plan on seeing it only in a clear, stable airmass. Useful visual anchors are the reservoirs, which read as dark forked shapes against the forest: the Oker reservoir in the northwest and the Rappbode reservoir in the east. The pale ribbon of the Teufelsmauer marks the northern edge. Sightseeing altitudes of 4,500-6,500 ft AMSL keep you comfortably above the terrain while holding detail. Nearby airports include EDDV (Hannover) to the north, EDVE (Braunschweig-Wolfsburg) to the northeast, EDDP (Leipzig/Halle) to the east and EDVK (Kassel-Calden) to the southwest. Expect low cloud, rime ice in winter and strong westerly turbulence on the lee side of the ridges.
