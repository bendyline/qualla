# Brocken Railway

No rack, no cable, no cog — just steel wheels on steel rails, climbing 582 metres in nineteen kilometres by wrapping itself one and a half times around the mountain. For twenty-seven years it carried no passengers to the summit at all — only fuel and building materials for a Cold War garrison.

**Location:** 51.7704, 10.7269

**Categories:** Railways, Engineering, Cold War, Mountains, Harz, Germany

## Introduction

Every mountain railway you have heard of cheats a little. The Swiss ones use racks; the funiculars use cable. The Brocken Railway does not. It leaves Drei Annen Hohne at 543 metres, arrives at Brocken station at 1,125, and covers the 582 metres of climb on adhesion alone — steel wheel gripping steel rail on gradients of up to 33 per mille, the whole thing hauled by a steam locomotive on metre-gauge track. To make the numbers work the engineers did the only thing available to them. They wrapped the last stretch of line around the mountain in a spiral, one and a half times, so the train climbs the Brocken by going around it.

## Permission, Eventually

A railway to the Brocken was drawn up as early as 1869 and turned down. The resubmission in 1895 succeeded, and on 30 May 1896 the construction permit was issued — but only once Prince Otto of Stolberg-Wernigerode had agreed to release the land, because the summit and its approaches were his.

The first section, from Drei Annen Hohne up to Schierke at 688 metres, opened on 20 June 1898, and the line reached the top later the same year, with Brocken station accepted on 4 October. For its first half-century it was a summer proposition: trains ran to the summit only between 30 April and 15 October, and through the winter they terminated at Schierke. That changed almost by accident. The German winter sports championships were held at Schierke in 1950, a temporary station was built at Eckerloch for the crowds, and only afterwards did winter services start running the whole way up.

## The Ride

The route is a lesson in how to gain height in a small space. Out of Drei Annen Hohne the line swings south-west, crosses the Schierke road and enters the Harz National Park, then works west to Schierke — where a siding once ran to the Knaupsholz granite quarry until 1963, about halfway along. Beyond that it runs a long way along the flank above the valley of the Cold Bode, which drops away below on the southern side.

The 971-metre Wurmberg comes up on the left, the train crosses the Brocken Road, and after a tight left-hander before the Eckerloch bridge and a right to answer it, the line reaches Goetheweg at 956 metres — a station now used only as a locomotive depot. Then the spiral. The train crosses the Brocken Road a second time, curls around the summit, and finishes at 18.9 kilometres. The fastest booked run takes 49 minutes; nobody is in a hurry. Top speed on the line is 40 km/h.

## Coal for the Antennas

The Second World War left the track badly damaged by bombing and shellfire — the Harz had been declared a fortress — and the section to the Brocken did not reopen until 1949. Ownership passed from the Nordhausen-Wernigerode Railway Company to public hands in 1948, and to the East German Deutsche Reichsbahn on 11 April 1949.

Then the border closed. From 14 August 1961, the day after work began on the Berlin Wall, the Brocken became a restricted military zone and the summit was shut to the public. The passenger service stopped at Schierke, and even that needed a special pass, because Schierke itself lay inside the border strip. Freight kept climbing. Right up to the late 1980s, steam locomotives hauled coal, oil and building materials to the top for the East German border troops and Soviet soldiers stationed there — a nineteenth-century tourist railway spending three decades as a supply line to a listening post.

## Two Locomotives, September 1991

After reunification the line's future was genuinely in doubt. Running steam trains through a newly designated national park to a summit that had just been handed back to the public struck a good many people as a bad idea, and the case for closure was not frivolous.

What saved it was an unlikely coalition of railway enthusiasts, regional politicians led by the state economics minister Horst Rehberger, and the Bundeswehr — which needed the railway, because the obsolete military installations on the Brocken had to come down and there was no other sensible way to carry the wreckage off the mountain. The line was renovated and reopened ceremonially on 15 September 1991 with two steam-hauled trains. The locomotives chosen said everything: 99 5903, a Mallet built for the original company in 1897 or 1898, and 99 6001, a one-off prototype the Krupp works turned out in 1939.

## Eleven Pairs a Day

Since the narrow-gauge lines were privatised in 1993 the Brocken Railway has been run by the Harzer Schmalspurbahnen, alongside the Harz Railway and the Selke Valley Railway. It is the only line in the network whose scheduled services are worked exclusively by steam — the Class 99 tanks do the job every day, with diesel railbuses kept for specials and the Class 199.8 machines held back for clearing snow.

Six pairs of trains a day run to the summit in winter, four of them starting from Wernigerode; in summer that rises to eleven. Around half a million people a year make the trip. It is not a preserved line playing at being a railway. It is a working railway that never stopped being one, hauling passengers up through spruce forest and above the treeline behind a locomotive design older than almost everyone aboard, and arriving at the highest station in Germany reached without a rack rail.

## Flight Context

The Brocken Railway climbs from Drei Annen Hohne (51.7550°N, 10.7000°E, 543 m) to Brocken station (1,125 m) on the summit at 51.7991°N, 10.6156°E, with the mid-point at Schierke near 51.7704°N, 10.7269°E. From 2,500–4,500 ft the whole line is traceable in clear air: a single thread of track cutting through dark Harz spruce, much of it storm- and beetle-damaged, tightening into an unmistakable spiral that wraps the bald summit dome one and a half times before reaching the cluster of white buildings and the antenna mast on top. On a still day the steam plume gives the train away from a long way off. Wernigerode lies 8–11 km north-east, the Oderteich 12 km south, Goslar 25 km north-west. Nearest airfields: Ballenstedt (EDCB) about 36 km east, Magdeburg-Cochstedt (EDBC) about 49 km east-north-east, Braunschweig-Wolfsburg (EDVE) about 62 km north, Hildesheim (EDVM) about 70 km north-west, Erfurt-Weimar (EDDE) about 89 km south. The massif rises roughly 900 m above the surrounding plain in a few kilometres, so allow generous terrain clearance; the summit is inside Harz National Park, sits in cloud on something like 300 days a year, and produces severe rime icing in winter.
