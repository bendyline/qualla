# Erfurt Latrine Disaster

In 1184 the floor gave way beneath a royal court session in Erfurt, dropping some sixty German nobles into the cesspit underneath.

**Location:** 50.9781, 11.0289

**Categories:** History, Medieval, Disasters, Folklore, Germany

## Introduction

Henry VI, king of Germany and heir to Frederick Barbarossa, spent part of the afternoon of 26 July 1184 clinging to a stone window ledge in Erfurt, waiting for somebody to bring a ladder. Below him, where the floor of the assembly hall had been a moment earlier, there was a hole. Below that there was a cesspit. Roughly sixty of the noblemen he had come to preside over were in it. Two months and six days earlier, Henry had knelt at Mainz in front of thousands of knights and received the accolade from his father at one of the great court festivals of the medieval empire. Chivalry has rarely been handed a sharper lesson in humility.

## A Quarrel Worth a King's Detour

The trouble was a land dispute, the ordinary currency of German politics in the twelfth century. Landgrave Louis III of Thuringia and Archbishop Conrad of Mainz had been at odds since the fall of Henry the Lion redistributed power across the north, and by 1184 the argument had grown loud enough that the imperial family had to intervene. Barbarossa was not free to do it himself. His eighteen-year-old son was on campaign, heading toward Poland, and on his father's orders Henry turned aside and rode to Erfurt to mediate. He called a Hoftag - a court day - and the Thuringian and Saxon nobility came in. The chronicles agree the session met on the upper floor of a two-storey building near Erfurt Cathedral. They disagree about which one: the provost's house, or the bishop's residence next door.

## What Was Under the Floor

Medieval towns had to put their waste somewhere, and the usual answer was directly underneath. A pit was dug beneath the ground floor, the privies emptied into it, and everyone learned to live with the arrangement. It worked well enough as long as the timber above stayed sound. In Erfurt it had not. The wooden beams carrying the upper hall were rotten, and they had been asked to hold a room's worth of armoured men who had all arrived on the same day. The floor let go, dropped through the storey beneath it, and delivered the assembly into the cesspit. Some of the dead were killed in the fall. Others were not, which was worse: they drowned in human waste, in the dark, under the weight of the men who had landed on top of them.

## The Ledge

Henry survived by geography. He and Archbishop Conrad had been seated in a stone window alcove built into the outer wall, and stone does not fall when timber does. The two of them held on to the masonry until rescuers arrived with ladders and brought them down. Landgrave Louis went into the pit with everyone else and was pulled out alive. Around sixty men were not, and the chronicles preserve some of their names - Heinrich of Schwarzburg, Friedrich of Abenberg, Gozmar of Ziegenhain, Burchard of Wartburg, whose estate passed that day to his son Ludwig. They were the men who governed this corner of the empire, and they died at a committee meeting.

## The Count's Proverb

A death this undignified was never going to stay in the chronicles. It drifted into Thuringian folklore, and Ludwig Bechstein set down a version of it in his Deutsches Sagenbuch of 1853. In his telling the story belongs to Heinrich of Schwarzburg, a count with an unfortunate turn of phrase: whenever he wanted to swear he had done nothing wrong, he would say Tue ich das, so müsse ich im Abtritt ersaufen - if I did that, may I drown in the privy. He did, of course. Folklore is not fussy about the details, and Bechstein's version scrambles a good many: it turns the Hoftag into a Reichstag, moves it to the Benedictine monastery of Saints Peter and Paul, gives Heinrich the wrong regnal number, relocates Friedrich of Abenberg to Arnsberg, splits Gozmar of Ziegenhain into two separate men, and swaps the cesspit for a sewer.

## Still the Benchmark

Erfurt itself came through the Middle Ages remarkably intact and is now one of the best-preserved medieval city centres in Germany, with the cathedral and the church of St. Severus rising together above the Domplatz and the shop-lined Krämerbrücke crossing the Gera a few hundred metres away. Nothing marks the building; nobody is even certain which one it was. What survives instead is the reputation. The Latrinensturz has become the fixed point against which other bad ways to die are measured, turning up again and again on lists of history's worst deaths alongside contenders like the Great Molasses Flood. The joke is durable because the horror is real, and it is worth remembering that the men in the pit were not a punchline to themselves.

## Flight Context

Erfurt Cathedral, beside which the collapse occurred, stands at 50.978°N, 11.029°E in central Thuringia. From the air the target is the Domplatz group: the cathedral and St. Severus side by side on their raised terrace at the western edge of the old town, with the star-shaped bastions of the Petersberg Citadel immediately northwest - the most distinctive shape in the city and an easy fix. The Gera river threads through the medieval core, and the Thuringian Forest ridges run along the horizon about 30 km to the southwest. Best viewed at 1,500-3,000 ft AGL, where the cathedral steps and the citadel outline read clearly. Erfurt-Weimar Airport (EDDE) lies about 5 km west of the city centre, so expect controlled airspace and traffic; Leipzig/Halle (EDDP) is roughly 100 km northeast. Winter inversions in the Thuringian basin can hold haze over the city well past mid-morning.
