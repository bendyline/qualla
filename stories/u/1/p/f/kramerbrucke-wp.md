# Krämerbrücke

A medieval bridge in Erfurt so completely built over that you can walk its length without noticing the river, and people have lived on it without a break for more than five hundred years.

**Location:** 50.9787, 11.0308

**Categories:** Bridges, Medieval, Architecture, Trade, Germany

## Introduction

The houses on the Krämerbrücke have street numbers and doorbells, and the river underneath them is invisible from the road between. Walk the 125 metres from Benediktsplatz to Wenigemarkt and it reads as an ordinary lane in an old German town - cobbles underfoot, half-timbered fronts leaning in on both sides, five and a half metres of daylight overhead. The Gera is down there, though, running through six barrel arches of limestone and sandstone laid in 1325. People have lived on top of them continuously for more than five hundred years, longer than on any other bridge in Europe.

## Seven Fires

There has been a crossing at this ford since somewhere between the eighth and eleventh centuries, and there were merchants selling from stalls on it almost from the start. The trouble was that it kept burning. The wooden bridge went up in flames in 1175, again in 1178, then 1213, 1222, 1245, 1265 and 1293 - seven fires in a little over a century, each one taking the traders' stock with it. After the fire of 1265 the city began seriously discussing stone. In 1293 the municipal administration bought out the monasteries that held the trading rights on the bridge, which settled the question of who would pay. Even so, the stone bridge was not finished until 1325.

## Building a Street Over Water

Stone did not end the problem, only relocated it. In 1472 a fire destroyed nearly half of Erfurt, including the wooden market stalls standing on the new bridge. What went up in their place was permanent: rows of three-storey houses, 13 to 15 metres tall, completed in 1486 and still the longest run of inhabited buildings on any bridge in Europe. Making them fit took some engineering. The builders widened the deck with timber Sprengwerke - braced frames set alongside the stone vaults - so the houses could overhang the bridge itself. The finished structure is 26 metres wide with a lane of 5.5 metres running down the middle. From below, the effect is peculiar: a stone bridge wearing a village.

## The Gate You Walk Through

Both ends were once closed by churches with gated archways - St. Benedict's to the west, St. Giles' to the east. Only St. Giles' remains, and it was there long before the stone bridge; as a bridge chapel it appears in the record in 1110. Its archway is the eastern entrance still, 3.75 metres wide and 3.25 metres high, and stepping through it out of the enclosed lane into the open square of the Wenigemarkt is the moment most visitors remember. Pilgrims felt something similar. The bridge lies on a main route of the Camino de Santiago, and every one of them walked through that same gap in the wall.

## Toymakers and Tanners

Krämer means small trader, and the name has been in ordinary use since 1510. Through the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries the workshops behind these doors belonged to toymakers, furriers, makers of passementerie - the braid and trim that decorated expensive clothing - and leather tanners, whose presence beside a river was not an accident. The trades on the bridge today are a deliberate echo: craft workshops, a puppetmaker, chocolatiers, small galleries, each with a carved or painted house sign above the door in the old manner. The whole structure, arches included, was extensively restored by the East German government in 1985 and 1986, an unusually generous piece of conservation for a state with other priorities.

## The Third Week of June

Once a year the bridge stops being a street and becomes a stage. The Krämerbrückenfest has run for three days in the third week of June since 1975 and draws around 130,000 people into a city centre built for a fraction of that. It is a medieval festival in the full sense - music, costume, craft, food, crowds pressed into a lane five and a half metres across. Proceedings open with an actor playing Till Eulenspiegel, the trickster of German folklore, who according to legend came to Erfurt and convinced the university's professors that he had taught a donkey to read. The city has never entirely stopped enjoying the story.

## Flight Context

The Krämerbrücke crosses the Breitstrom arm of the Gera at 50.979°N, 11.031°E, in the middle of Erfurt's old town. It is a difficult target from the air precisely because it does not look like a bridge - the roofline is continuous with the surrounding streets, and the giveaway is a narrow block of buildings with water emerging at both ends. Navigate instead to the Domplatz group a few hundred metres west, where the cathedral and St. Severus stand together on their terrace with the star-shaped Petersberg Citadel behind them, then look east along the river. Best viewed at 1,000-2,000 ft AGL; higher and the bridge merges into the medieval core. Erfurt-Weimar Airport (EDDE) is about 5 km west of the centre, with controlled airspace over the city; Leipzig/Halle (EDDP) lies roughly 100 km northeast. Winter haze settles in the Thuringian basin and can persist through the morning.
