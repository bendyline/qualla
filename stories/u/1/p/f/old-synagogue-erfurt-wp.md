# Old Synagogue (Erfurt)

A synagogue whose oldest timbers were cut in 1094 survived into the twenty-first century because, after Erfurt's Jewish community was massacred in 1349, the building was turned into a warehouse and its origins forgotten for six hundred years.

**Location:** 50.9786, 11.0293

**Categories:** Jewish heritage, Medieval history, World Heritage Sites, Museums, Thuringia, Synagogues

## Introduction

For six centuries nobody in Erfurt knew what the building behind the Waagegasse had been. It served as a storehouse, then a restaurant, then a ballroom. Its walls were thick, its roof was sound, and its origins had been so completely forgotten that when the Nazi regime burned and demolished Erfurt's other synagogues, this one was left standing — because nobody recognised it. Serious interest in its history only revived in the late 1980s. The oldest timbers in the structure have been dated by dendrochronology to 1094, and because the roof was never lost, it is thought to be the oldest intact synagogue building in the world.

## A Community With an Address

Most of what stands today was built between about 1250 and 1320, on top of that eleventh-century core. Part of the western wall survives from a second construction phase in the twelfth century, pierced by a sandstone double-arched window that is the building's oldest visible face.

What makes the site unusual is not the synagogue alone but the set of things around it. A mikveh — a ritual bath, fed by groundwater near the Gera — was uncovered by archaeologists in 2007 and has been open to visitors on guided tours since September 2011. A few streets away stands the Stone House, a substantial secular building of 1250 that belonged to Jewish owners. Prayer hall, bathhouse, private residence: the ordinary architecture of a community that lived in the middle of the Christian town rather than at its edge. The Center for Jewish Art at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem rates the synagogue at its highest level of significance, 4 (International).

## March 1349

Plague was moving across Europe, and across Europe Jews were falsely accused of causing it. On 21 March 1349 the Jewish community of Erfurt was destroyed. Estimates of how many people were killed range from around a hundred to as many as three thousand; the chronicles disagree, and one account claims that of a community of three thousand souls not a single person survived. Some families set fire to their own houses and died inside rather than be taken in the street. Those who lived through it were expelled.

What they left behind passed to the city council — their houses, their books, their synagogue. The building was stripped of what made it a synagogue and put to work as a warehouse. Within a generation Jews had resettled in Erfurt and formed a second community, which lasted until 1458. The plague reached the city anyway: more than 16,000 residents died in a ten-week period in 1350.

## What Kalman Buried

In 1998, work near the Michaelisstrasse turned up a hoard hidden in a wall: medieval coins, goldsmiths' work, jewellery. It had probably belonged to a merchant named Kalman of Wiehe, who put it into the masonry at some point around the massacre and never came back for it.

The Erfurt Treasure is now on permanent display inside the building whose congregation Kalman would have belonged to. The two facts sit awkwardly together and are better for it. A hoard is evidence of wealth, but it is also evidence of terror — nobody bricks up their family's silver on an ordinary afternoon. The objects are extraordinarily fine, and they were buried by someone who expected to need them again.

## The Books That Were Sold

The community's manuscripts had a longer and stranger journey. The Erfurt Hebrew Manuscripts, religious texts from the twelfth to fourteenth centuries, came into the possession of the city council after the massacre. By the late seventeenth century they had ended up in the library of the Lutheran Evangelical Ministry, housed in Erfurt's former Augustinian Monastery, where they sat among Protestant books for two hundred years. In 1880 the Ministry sold them to the Royal Library in Berlin, and the originals remain there in what is now the Berlin State Library.

One of them matters far beyond Erfurt. The Tosefta, a companion to the Mishnah, was rarely copied; only three manuscripts of it are known. The Erfurt Tosefta, written in the twelfth century, is the oldest of the three and the second most complete. Facsimiles are on display in the museum, a few metres from where the originals once lived.

## Four Buildings, One Congregation

Erfurt's later synagogues tell the rest of the story in fast forward. The Kleine Synagoge went up in 1840, classically fronted, and served until 1884; restored in 1998, it is now an events venue. In 1884 the community built the Grosse Synagoge, a confident Moorish Revival building, and the regime destroyed it during the Kristallnacht riots on the night of 9–10 November 1938. The confiscated site was returned to the Jewish community by the city council in 1947, and the Neue Synagoge opened there on 31 August 1952 — funded by the East German government and the only entirely new synagogue built in that state. It is where Erfurt's congregation worships today. In April 2000 a group of neo-Nazis set it on fire.

The old building opened as a museum on 27 October 2009. In 2023 it was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List together with the mikveh and the Stone House.

## Flight Context

The Old Synagogue stands at 50.9786°N, 11.0293°E, in the dense medieval core of Erfurt between the Fischmarkt and the Krämerbrücke — far too small to pick out from the air on its own. Navigate instead by the two features that make Erfurt unmistakable from 2,000–4,000 ft: the twin churches on the Domberg terrace above the Domplatz, the Mariendom and St Severi standing shoulder to shoulder at the west end of the old town, and the star-shaped Petersberg citadel immediately behind them with its distinctive bastioned ramparts. The synagogue lies a few hundred metres east of the Domplatz, inside the tight tangle of red roofs bounded by the arms of the Gera. Nearest airfields: Erfurt-Weimar (EDDE) about 5 km west of the city, Jena-Schöngleina (EDBJ) about 45 km east, Eisenach-Kindel (EDGE) about 55 km west, Leipzig/Halle (EDDP) about 110 km north-east. The Thuringian Basin traps haze and holds winter inversions that can leave the city flat and grey while the Harz and Thuringian Forest ridges either side stay clear.
