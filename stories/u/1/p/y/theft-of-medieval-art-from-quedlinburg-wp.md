# Theft of Medieval Art from Quedlinburg

An American lieutenant mailed a thousand-year-old church treasure home to small-town Texas, where it sat in a bank vault for four decades before the world found out.

**Location:** 51.7858, 11.1369

**Categories:** History, World War II, Art and Culture, Crime, Germany, Medieval

## Introduction

For most of the second half of the twentieth century, a ninth-century gospel book bound in gold, jewels, and Byzantine ivory sat in a safe deposit box in Whitewright, Texas, a farming town north of Dallas. The bank staff had no idea. Neither did the congregation of St. Servatius in Quedlinburg, Germany, half a world away, who knew only that their treasury had been robbed in the last chaotic weeks of the Second World War and that eight of its greatest objects had simply evaporated. The two facts would not meet for more than forty years.

## A Cave in the Harz

Quedlinburg sits at the northern edge of the Harz mountains, a town of timbered houses beneath a sandstone ridge where the collegiate church of the old abbey has stood since the Ottonian centuries. Its treasury held objects of extraordinary rarity: the Samuhel Gospel, an illuminated manuscript from the ninth century; a printed evangeliary of 1513, the Evangelistar aus St. Wiperti; reliquaries; an ivory liturgical comb used in the liturgy; a flask of carved rock crystal meant to hold perfume. As Allied bombers ranged deeper into Germany, the church moved the collection out of town and into a mine shaft in the hills for safekeeping. On 19 April 1945, a unit of the advancing U.S. Army, the 87th Armored Field Artillery Battalion, found the cache and posted a guard over it. That should have been the end of the danger.

## The Lieutenant Who Knew What He Was Looking At

By June, church authorities were reporting that eight of the finest pieces were gone. The man responsible was Lieutenant Joe T. Meador of the 87th, who had studied art in college and understood perfectly well what he was handling. He did not smash a case or run a black-market ring. He simply mailed the objects home to his family in Whitewright, one parcel at a time, through the ordinary Army postal system. There was no fence, no buyer, no plan that anyone could later identify. The treasure of a European cathedral went home to Texas the way a soldier's souvenir spoons and helmets went home, and then it stopped moving. Meador died in 1980, leaving his estate to his brother Jack and his sister Jane. The objects stayed in the vault.

## A Gospel Book Comes Up for Sale

In 1987 the Samuhel Gospel surfaced quietly in the European art market, and the tremor it caused ran in both directions. In May 1990 it was sold to a German cultural foundation for a large fee, which raised the obvious question: sold by whom? Two people would not let it go. Willi Korte, an art researcher with a talent for archives, worked backward through Pentagon records toward a unit and then a name. Friedemann Goßlau, the pastor in Quedlinburg, kept the claim alive from the church's side. William Honan, a journalist at The New York Times, published the story in June 1990 under a headline that said what everyone had begun to suspect: a trove of medieval art had turned up in Texas.

## Home, By Way of the Courts

What followed was years of litigation and negotiation with Meador's heirs, and it ended not in a verdict but in a bargain. An out-of-court settlement paid the family millions and sent the surviving objects back across the Atlantic. Before they left, they went on display in Dallas, and then in Berlin, drawing crowds who came to see medieval Saxony rendered in gold leaf and rock crystal. In September 1993 the treasure returned to Quedlinburg itself, to the church it had been made for. One famous manuscript associated with the town had never been at risk at all: the fifth-century Quedlinburg Itala fragment, oldest of them all, had been moved to a Berlin museum years earlier and so escaped the theft entirely.

## What the Law Could Not Reach

The criminal case dissolved. On 22 October 1996 a district court dismissed the charges because the statute of limitations had run out long before anyone in Germany learned where the objects were. The Internal Revenue Service then took its own approach, arguing that the family had received unreported income, and initially sought more than $50 million in taxes, penalties, and interest. On 20 April 2000 the IRS settled for $135,000. Honan published a book about the hunt in 1997. And the story is still not closed: two of the eight objects taken in 1945, including a twelfth-century reliquary in the form of a crucifix, have never been recovered, and researchers believe they remain somewhere in Texas, in hands nobody has yet identified.

## Flight Context

Quedlinburg lies at 51.786°N, 11.137°E, on the northeastern edge of the Harz mountains in Saxony-Anhalt. The sandstone castle hill with the twin towers of the collegiate church of St. Servatius is the town's unmistakable marker from the air, rising directly above a dense medieval core of red-tiled roofs. The Harz massif rises immediately southwest, with the 1,141-metre Brocken about 32 km due west, giving an easy orientation cue. Best viewed at 2,000-4,000 ft AGL; the town core is compact and the castle hill can be lost against the surrounding ridge from higher up. Nearest field is Magdeburg-Cochstedt (EDBC), about 20 km north; Leipzig/Halle (EDDP) lies roughly 80 km southeast and Hannover (EDDV) about 120 km northwest. Harz weather is changeable and low cloud can hang on the northern slopes even when the plain is clear.
