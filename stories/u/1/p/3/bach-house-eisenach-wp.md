# Bach House (Eisenach)

The world's first Bach museum stands in a house where Bach was never born - and the plaque that says otherwise has been put back on the wall on purpose.

**Location:** 50.9715, 10.3226

**Categories:** Museums, Classical music, Historic houses, Musical instruments, Thuringia, Germany

## Introduction

The plaque above the door is wrong, and the museum knows it, and it is back on the wall deliberately. In 1868 a local music society fixed it to the house at Frauenplan 21 to mark the birthplace of Johann Sebastian Bach. Sixty years later an Eisenach hobby historian named Fritz Rollberg went through the city's tax records and established that Bach's father had paid tax on a different building entirely. By then the address was famous across the world. The plaque came down during a restoration in the early 1970s; visitors went on believing anyway; and in 2007 the museum put it back and explained it, with one of Rollberg's tax files displayed nearby. Some errors become part of the record they got wrong.

## Frauenplan 21

The building is old enough to be interesting on its own. Its eastern half went up in 1456 and its western half in 1458, and the two were joined around 1611, making it one of the oldest houses in Eisenach.

It was an Ackerbürgerhaus, a farming burgher's house: the hall that now holds the instrument collection may well have been a barn, with cattle and horses stabled beside the stairs. A cowbell from 1688, dug up in the garden, makes the point. Upstairs, a Renaissance window frame and carved panelling mark the status of the people who lived here. At the time of Bach's birth the owner was Heinrich Börstelmann, headmaster of the Latin School. Bachs did eventually move in - Caroline Amalie Rausch, born a Bach, lived here from 1746 to 1779 - which is very likely how the confusion started.

## How the Error Was Made

The nineteenth century wanted Bach back. Mendelssohn, Schumann and others had revived his music, and revivals send people looking for relics.

In 1857 the biographer Karl Hermann Bitter interviewed the surviving descendants of Johann Bernhard Bach and came away convinced that the composer had been born at Frauenplan 21. No supporting evidence has surfaced since. The oral tradition Bitter trusted had probably simply slipped, remembering a house Bachs occupied sixty years after the birth as the house of the birth itself.

## A Ten-Year-Old Leaves Town

Wherever exactly the room was, the childhood is documented and it was short. Johann Ambrosius Bach came to Eisenach in 1671 as the council's Haußmann, its director of town music. The family first rented rooms in the Rittergasse, immediately south of today's museum garden, and in 1674 bought a house a hundred metres north - property being the price of citizenship. That house is long gone.

Johann Sebastian was born on 21 March 1685 and baptised two days later in St George's Church, where his cousin Johann Christoph Bach had been organist since 1665 and where the boy first heard the instrument that became his own. He attended the Latin school from 1692, singing in its chorus musicus. Then his mother Elisabeth died on 1 May 1694 and his father followed on 20 February 1695, and in July that year Johann Sebastian and his brother Johann Jacob left for Ohrdruf to live with their eldest brother. He was ten years old and would not live in Eisenach again.

## How the Instruments Arrived

The New Bach Society bought the house on 15 May 1905 and opened the world's first Bach museum on 27 May 1907, with the St Thomas choir from Leipzig singing. The collection began with four instruments from the Dutch collector Paul de Wit, and grew by another 164 from the estate of the conductor Aloys Obrist - a bequest that exists because of a killing. In June 1910 Obrist shot Anna Sutter, the Swiss soprano who had sung at the Stuttgart opera since 1895 and who had ended their relationship, and then killed himself.

Five baroque keyboards have been played hourly for visitors since 1973, among them a spinet built in Strasbourg in 1765 by Johann Heinrich Silbermann, nephew of the Gottfried Silbermann whose instruments Bach helped sell in Leipzig. The Thuringian positive organ has the closest brush with him. It served a village church near Weimar while Bach was court organist there, was repaired twice by his friend the organ builder Heinrich Nicolaus Trebs, and was tested three times by his own pupil - and the museum concedes there is no record that Bach ever touched it.

## Three Hands on One Sheet

The exhibition begins at a door. When Leipzig demolished the St Thomas School in 1902, a music teacher there saved the door to Bach's own rooms, and it now marks the entrance to the display.

The manuscript at the centre of the collection is a continuo part from the cantata Alles nur nach Gottes Willen, and reading it is like watching a household at work: one side copied by Bach's nephew Johann Heinrich, part of it in his wife Anna Magdalena's hand, and the final chorale, titles and corrections written by Bach himself as far as the word Fine. Anna Magdalena is remembered with a bouquet of yellow carnations, kept fresh - she was described in her own lifetime as a great lover of gardening, and those were her favourites. War came close twice, in the air raid of 23 November 1944 and under American artillery on the night of 5 April 1945; both times it took the roof. On 29 April 1945 the American commander of the city ordered the museum repaired immediately.

## Flight Context

The Bachhaus stands at 50.971°N, 10.323°E on the Frauenplan, in the centre of Eisenach in western Thuringia - a modest half-timbered building attached to a curved modern extension, essentially invisible from the air among the roofs of the old town. Navigate to the town rather than the house. Eisenach sits in the Hörsel valley, which runs east-west and carries both the historic Via Regia and the A4 autobahn, with the Thuringian Forest rising steeply to the south; the unmistakable landmark is the Wartburg on its wooded ridge at 410 m, roughly 2 km south-west of the museum. Best flown at 1,500-3,000 ft, approaching along the valley. Erfurt-Weimar (EDDE) lies about 45 km east, Kassel-Calden (EDVK) about 80 km north-west and Frankfurt (EDDF) roughly 160 km south-west. Autumn brings dense morning fog to the Hörsel that can bury the town while the Wartburg ridge stays clear above it.
