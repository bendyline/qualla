# Nyborg Slot

The moated castle at the centre of Denmark, where a cornered king signed away his own power in 1282 and the realm's medieval parliament met for more than a century.

**Location:** 55.3127, 10.7867

**Categories:** Castles and manor houses on Funen, Historic house museums in Denmark, Medieval history, Danish history

## Introduction

In 1282, King Eric V of Denmark, known as Erik Klipping, put his seal to a document that took power away from him. He had offended the nobility so thoroughly that they were in a position to demand it. The charter he signed, the håndfæstning of 1282, is remembered in Denmark as the country's first constitution, and the genre it launched has often been called a Scandinavian parallel to England's Magna Carta. He signed it at Nyborg, in a moated castle in the middle of Funen, which is to say in the middle of the kingdom.

## Where the Realm Met

Nyborg was chosen because of where it is. Medieval Denmark was an archipelago of a kingdom, its power scattered across Jutland, Funen, Zealand and Scania, and the Danehof — the Danish medieval parliament, which mattered from roughly 1250 until 1413 — needed a site that no faction could claim as home ground. It had to gather somewhere central, and that was most often Nyborg Castle, sitting on Funen at the geographic heart of the Lands of Denmark. The palatium, the castle's residential range, was the assembly's principal venue until 1413. The 1282 charter set firmer rules around how the Danehof worked, turning what had been an occasional gathering of magnates into something closer to an institution — a place where the king had to appear, and answer.

## Stone Around Water

The castle was established around 1170 by Knud Prislavsen, a nephew of Valdemar the Great, and was in royal hands by 1193. It is Denmark's oldest preserved royal residence. The original plan was severe and functional: a square, moated enclosure with corner towers linked by a curtain wall, the ring wall standing close to ten metres high, with projecting flanking towers and a heavy fortification tower. Comfort arrived later. A residential palatium was raised against the west wall in the mid-thirteenth century and a strong defensive tower went up on the east wall. Christian II was born here in 1481. Christian III, who stayed in the building often, added a new banqueting hall and a tower in the 1540s, and under Christian IV the Italian architect Domenicus Badiaz carried out further work in 1607, giving the main tower, Knudstårnet, a spire modelled on the Blåtårn in Copenhagen. Until 1560 Nyborg functioned as the nation's capital.

## Quarry

War ended it. The castle was badly damaged in the Dano-Swedish War of 1657–58, and afterwards most of the buildings were pulled down. From 1722 the stone was carted off to build Odense Palace — a medieval royal seat dismantled to furnish a baroque one. What survived spent roughly two and a half centuries in military hands, useful but unloved, and when the garrison finally left Nyborg in 1913 the remaining fabric was in a sorry state. Almost nowhere else in Denmark is the sequence so bare: the building where the crown's power was first written down became a building site, then a barracks, then a ruin.

## Rebuilt, and Rebuilt Again

Local interest saved it. Encouraged by the town, the National Museum of Denmark took on an extensive restoration under Mogens Clemmensen, working from careful archaeological investigation. The project ran about ten years before the money ran out in 1925, leaving much of the north wing and a good deal of interior detail unfinished. Clemmensen worked mainly from the castle's appearance under Christian III, but he also folded in details he found in other sources, and he did not always document which was which — so the Nyborg Slot a visitor walks through today is partly a building and partly an interpretation of one. Afterwards the rooms were furnished with pieces bought from historic manor houses. Three banqueting halls each stand for a different Danish century: the Danehofsalen, where the parliamentary meetings were held; Margrethe's Hall, where court representatives of the Kalmar Union met; and Christian III's Hall, which carries a display on the period when Nyborg was the capital.

## The Museum and the Unfinished Plan

Nyborg Slot is run as part of Østfyns Museer, formed in 2009 by the merger of Kerteminde Museum and Nyborg Museum, an organisation whose reach traces the eastern half of Funen: the castle itself along with Borgmestergården in Mads Lerches Gård, the Johannes Larsen Museum at Kerteminde, and the Viking Museum at Ladby, where Denmark's only Viking Age ship burial lies in the ground. The most recent chapter has been harder than the medieval one. A major restoration began in the autumn of 2017, aiming to complete the surviving royal wing and add some two thousand square metres of new exhibition space by 2023. On 17 December 2020 the Environmental and Food Appeals Board ruled against the scheme, finding that the planned construction could not go ahead as designed, and on 8 February 2022 the A.P. Møller Foundation withdrew its funding, citing the project's postponed timetable. Eight centuries after Erik Klipping was made to sign, the castle is still arguing with the authorities.

## Flight Context

Nyborg Slot stands at 55.3127°N, 10.7867°E, in the town of Nyborg on the east coast of Funen, roughly five kilometres west of the Funen landfall of the Great Belt Fixed Link. From 1,500–2,500 ft the castle reads as a compact rectangular complex with a distinctive square tower, set inside the surviving earthworks and water features of the old town defences — an unusually legible medieval footprint inside a modern Danish town. The best orientation cue on approach is the Great Belt crossing itself: follow the bridge west to the Funen shore, then look inland and slightly south. Nyborg Fjord and the harbour lie immediately east of the castle. Odense Airport (EKOD) is about 30 km north-west; Copenhagen (EKCH) roughly 95 km east. Low winter sun from the south picks the ring wall out sharply against the town.
