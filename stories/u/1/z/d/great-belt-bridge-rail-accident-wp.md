# Great Belt Bridge Rail Accident

On the second morning of 2019, a semi-trailer came loose from a freight train crossing the Great Belt and struck a passing InterCity express, killing eight passengers — Denmark's deadliest rail accident in three decades.

**Location:** 55.3013, 10.8611

**Categories:** Railway accidents, Transport in Denmark, 2019 in Denmark, Disasters

## Introduction

It was the second morning of the year, the first ordinary commute of 2019. InterCity express ICL 210 left Odense at 07:11 with 131 passengers and three crew aboard, bound for Copenhagen — people going back to work after New Year, reading, dozing, watching the flat Funen countryside give way to open water. Twenty-four minutes later, out on the low West Bridge of the Great Belt Fixed Link, eight of them were dead.

## Closed to Cars, Open to Trains

Storm Alfrida reached Denmark on 1 January 2019, and overnight the Danish Road Directorate closed the Great Belt Bridge to all road traffic, expecting to reopen at eleven the next morning. Rail traffic was not restricted, and the reasoning was sound. The eighteen-kilometre link between Zealand and Funen is not one crossing but two. Road and rail share the West Bridge, a low box-girder structure close to the water, while the East Bridge — the great suspension span that appears in every photograph of Denmark — carries only cars, because trains cross that stretch through a tunnel beneath the seabed. The trains were on the sheltered side. Nobody had reason to stop them.

## Shortly Before 07:35

Coming the other way was a DB Cargo freight train, crewed by one man, loaded with semi-trailers full of empty Carlsberg bottle crates. The two met between the islet of Sprogø and the Funen shore. The driver of the passenger train said afterwards that he had seen sparks in the wrong place — lower than they should have been, not up at the overhead catenary — and that something big was hanging from the freight train coming toward him. The object was initially thought to be a tarpaulin. It was a semi-trailer, swinging free from its wagon, and with two trains closing at line speed there was no interval in which anyone could act.

## Eight People

Eight passengers were killed: five women and three men, between twenty-seven and sixty years old, all Danish citizens, all identified by 4 January. Sixteen others were injured, none critically — fourteen slightly, two moderately. The storm and the setting made the response brutally difficult; the trains were stopped mid-span on an exposed bridge in a gale, with the road shut. Six deaths were confirmed while the trains were still out there. After they had been moved to a secluded area, two more bodies were found in the passenger carriages. The National Operative Staff, the government's crisis coordination body, was convened. The bridge reopened to road traffic at around 12:20 that same day, and the westbound carriageway had to be closed again briefly that afternoon because drivers were slowing to look. Rail traffic resumed at 10:40 on 3 January on a single track, and ran normally by that evening. It was Denmark's deadliest rail accident since 1988.

## It Was Not the Wind

The Accident Investigation Board Denmark took the case. On the day, investigator Bo Haaning said the crash was a collision between the passenger train and an empty semi-trailer from the freight train, while staying careful about the sequence: it either hit the train, or the train drove into it. Public attention went first to the weather. Gusts of up to 20.9 m/s — about 47 mph — had been measured, with sustained winds lower, blowing from the north, straight across the line of the bridge. Banedanmark, which runs the railway, and Sund & Bælt, which runs the crossing, both stated that every procedure had been followed. They were telling the truth, and that was the uncomfortable part. Attention shifted to the wagon itself. On a pocket wagon, the front of a semi-trailer is held by nothing but its kingpin; the rear stays put only because the wheels sit down into a recess. DB Cargo suspended its bottle-crate services. The accident resembled an incident at Hamburg, where an inadequately secured semi-trailer had come loose and struck a bridge pier. The European Union Agency for Railways is supposed to circulate information about serious incidents. Danish authorities had never been told.

## A Question of Grease

The measures came quickly. On 5 January the Danish Transport Authority issued a warning about pocket wagons on the investigation board's recommendation, stating that the locking mechanism securing a trailer could pose a safety risk, and asked the Joint Network Secretariat to open a formal procedure. On 7 January a test at DB Cargo found that a semi-trailer could still be lifted off a wagon after being loaded and secured — and this train had been checked before departure and had appeared properly secured. The following day the authority temporarily banned semi-trailers on pocket wagons until additional locking procedures were in place, and tightened the wind limits for freight crossing the Great Belt. Comparable measures soon spread across the continent. The final report came on 18 December 2019. The semi-trailer had not been locked in place, and the reason the lock failed was improper maintenance: the mechanism requires regular lubrication, the official guidelines for doing so were incomplete, and parts of it went insufficiently greased. Underneath that lay a question nobody had answered: whether maintaining it fell to DB, which leased and operated the wagon, or VTG Rail, which owned it. Had the lock worked, the wind could not have pulled the trailer free. One smaller, sadder postscript: a DB Cargo union representative crossed out the name of the colleague who had inspected the loaded freight train before departure. He received a 20-day suspended sentence for evidence tampering, upheld on appeal.

## Flight Context

The accident site is on the West Bridge of the Great Belt Fixed Link at approximately 55.30°N, 10.86°E, between the islet of Sprogø and the Funen shore. From 2,000–4,000 ft the geometry that mattered on 2 January 2019 is immediately legible: the West Bridge runs low and straight across the shallow western channel carrying both the motorway and the twin-track railway, while the East Bridge — the long suspension span — leaps the deep eastern channel to Zealand carrying road traffic only, with the rail tunnel running unseen beneath it. Sprogø sits between the two as the hinge of the whole crossing. Odense Airport (EKOD) is about 30 km west; Copenhagen (EKCH) is roughly 90 km east. Winds across the Belt are frequently strong and almost always perpendicular to the crossing, which is exactly the condition that closed the bridge to cars that night. This is a place where eight people died on an ordinary commute; it is worth flying past it quietly.
