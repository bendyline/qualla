# Odense

Denmark's third city spent eleven years cutting a motorway through its medieval heart, then spent a fortune taking it out again.

**Location:** 55.4000, 10.3833

**Categories:** Cities, Denmark, Funen, Urban planning, Maritime history, Robotics

## Introduction

In 1959 Odense began cutting a four-lane road through the middle of its own medieval centre. The work took eleven years. Then, on 28 June 2014, the city closed the road it had built. Thomas B. Thriges Gade had been carrying roughly 25,000 vehicles a day straight through the oldest quarter of Funen's capital, and Odense decided the bargain was a bad one. Realdania put up 255 million kroner, the municipality 250 million, and the asphalt came up. The first residents of the new quarter built on top of it moved in during June 2017. Not many cities admit a mistake that expensive, and fewer still pay to undo it.

## Odin's Sanctuary

The name is a thousand years older than the road. Óðins vé means Odin's sanctuary — ground set aside for worship of the god — and people had been living here for some four thousand years before anyone bothered to write it down. The first written mention is a letter of 18 March 988, in which the German emperor Otto III granted rights to Odense and its neighbouring settlements. By 1070 it was already a city of standing. In 1247 it burned, caught in the war between King Erik IV and his brother Abel. Rebuilt, it kept growing: 3,808 people in 1670, 8,709 in 1834, 30,268 by 1890, and 40,138 at the census of 1901. As of 1 January 2026 the city proper holds 187,558 residents and the municipality 213,168, which puts Odense behind only Copenhagen and Aarhus, and makes it, comfortably, the capital of Funen.

## A Canal Instead of a Coast

Odense sits about thirteen metres above sea level and — awkwardly, for a trading town — not on the sea at all. The Odense Å slides through the city just south of the main shopping streets, a river far too modest to carry freight. So between 1796 and 1806 the town dug its way out to the water: an eight-kilometre canal, seven and a half metres deep, running north from Odense Harbour to Odense Fjord. Rail came next. A line across Funen opened in 1865 and made Odense one of Denmark's busiest junctions, a role the Great Belt Bridge only reinforced when it took trains in 1997 and road traffic in 1998. Copenhagen is now about an hour and a quarter away; Aarhus, an hour and a half.

## Steel, Then Robots

A.P. Møller founded the Odense Steel Shipyard in 1917, and from the late 1950s the work moved to a vast new yard at Lindø, across the fjord at Munkebo. From 2006 it turned out the Mærsk E-class container ships, 15,550 TEU apiece and the largest afloat at the time. Then the yard lost the next generation to a Korean competitor, Maersk announced closure in 2009, and on 12 January 2012 Denmark's biggest shipbuilder stopped building ships. The site did not die. More than a million square metres of it became the Lindø Industrial Park, fabricating and storing components for the offshore industry. Meanwhile the city grew a second specialism nobody had planned for: robotics. Universal Robots and Mobile Industrial Robots both started here, and the Odense Robotics cluster now counts more than 300 robot, drone and automation companies employing close to 20,000 people, with a particular reputation for collaborative robots — the kind built to work beside a human rather than behind a cage.

## From Street to City

The project that closed Thomas B. Thriges Gade was called, plainly enough, Fra Gade til By — from street to city. Where four lanes had split the old core in two, there are now apartments, squares and paving. It arrived alongside the other half of the plan: Odense Letbane, the light rail that opened on 28 May 2022 and runs 14.5 kilometres across 26 stops from Tarup in the north-west to Hjallese in the south, threading past the main station and through the reclaimed centre. The shopping did not vanish; it simply sorted itself out. Kongensgade, Vestergade and the pedestrian lanes off them hold the city-centre trade, while Rosengårdcentret in the south-east — open since 1971, 144,000 square metres, over 150 shops — remains the largest shopping centre in Denmark.

## The Shoemaker's Son, and the Fiddler's

Odense has never been shy about its most famous child. Hans Christian Andersen was born here on 2 April 1805, and characters out of his tales stand around the city in bronze and stone — the Steadfast Tin Soldier, the Shepherdess and the Chimney Sweep, the Emperor's New Clothes, the Paper Boat, the Wild Swans. Andersen himself sits in Eventyrhaven, the Fairy Tale Park beside the cathedral, sculpted by Louis Hasselriis in 1888 with a book open in his hand. But the other great Funen export was a musician. Carl Nielsen, born in 1865 at Sortelung south of the city, went from village fiddler to the composer of six symphonies, and Odense keeps a museum to him and to his wife, the sculptor Anne Marie Carl-Nielsen. The Odense Symphony Orchestra plays in a hall named after him.

## Flight Context

Odense lies at 55.40°N, 10.38°E on the island of Funen, roughly 13 m above sea level and about 8 km inland from Odense Fjord. From the air the city reads as a compact grey-and-red mass on a broad green plain, with the arrow-straight Odense Canal running north-east to the fjord and the Great Belt shining to the east. St Canute's Cathedral tower and the tall stack at Fyn Power Station are the clearest fixes. Hans Christian Andersen Airport (EKOD) sits about 10 km north-west at Beldringe; Billund (EKBI) is roughly 70 km west and Copenhagen Kastrup (EKCH) about 140 km east. Best viewed from 2,500–4,000 ft. Maritime climate, frequent low cloud and haze; the clearest air usually follows a northwesterly airflow.
