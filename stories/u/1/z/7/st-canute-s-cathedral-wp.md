# St. Canute's Cathedral

A king was killed at an altar in Odense in 1086; nine centuries later his bones are still on public view a few hundred metres away.

**Location:** 55.3954, 10.3890

**Categories:** Cathedrals, Medieval history, Denmark, Odense, Gothic architecture

## Introduction

On 10 July 1086 a crowd tore at the timber wall of a small priory church beside the Odense river, trying to get at the king inside. Canute IV and his brother Benedict had fled there for sanctuary and the rebels refused to grant it. Benedict held the choir door until he was hacked down. Canute had taken communion; tradition says he did not resist. A 2008 forensic examination of his remains found a violent thrust from the front through the lower abdomen toward the base of the spine, and a skull fracture likely caused by a sword blow to the cheek or a blow from a blunt weapon — a picture that contradicts Aelnoth's account of a lance wound to the flank. Seventeen of his housecarls were killed with him. St Canute's Cathedral is not that church — but it exists entirely because of what happened in it.

## The King They Would Not Carry

Canute was a son of Sven Estridsen and had ruled since 1080. He wanted a stronger monarchy and a stronger church, and paid for both with tithes and a new poll tax that fell hardest on people who were already struggling to eat. In 1085 he assembled a fleet at Struer to invade England, then kept it waiting so long that hungry crews sailed home; he answered by fining them ruinously. Vendsyssel rose first, and the rising spread. The grievances shouted through the church windows at Odense were not abstract — the chronicles record "This is for stealing my cow! This is for taking my horses!" Afterwards the Benedictine monks buried Canute and Benedict before the priory's high altar, and when his widow Adela of Flanders came to remove the body to Flanders she was persuaded to leave it. Reports of healings at the grave began almost at once, and seven years of famine that followed his death — which earned his successor Olaf the nickname Hunger — were read as confirmation.

## A Church Built for a Body

Aelnoth of Canterbury, an English Benedictine at St Alban's Priory, recorded a new church under construction in 1095, a little farther from the river than the murder site. It was built of travertine in the Romanesque manner, semicircular arches under a flat timber ceiling, and it went up specifically to house Canute's remains. Its foundations can still be seen in the crypt of the present cathedral. Rome supplied the rest: Pope Paschal II canonised Canute in 1101, and Denmark's newly Christian population acquired a saint of its own. When the first St Canute's was finished, a three-day fast was proclaimed across the kingdom and the bodies were moved. The old test was applied — the shroud was set alight, and the fire is said to have left the king unmarked.

## Two Hundred Years of Brick

In 1247 Odense and its cathedral burned in the civil war between Eric IV and his brother Abel. Rebuilding began around 1300 under Bishop Gisico, and the new church went up in Gothic style — pointed arches, high vaults — in oversized red brick, which was cheaper and far easier to work than the porous local stone. The old building was dismantled piece by piece as the new one grew around it. It took roughly two centuries; the cathedral was finally dedicated on 30 April 1499, cruciform and, for the moment, towerless. The crypt was deliberately enlarged so that pilgrims could reach the saint's reliquary beneath the raised choir without disturbing the canons at their hourly offices overhead. The single west tower, 63 metres tall, was completed in 1586.

## Claus Berg's Wall of Oak

The cathedral's other treasure arrived by demolition. Claus Berg, a sculptor from Lübeck who ran a workshop of about a dozen assistants in Odense from around 1504, was commissioned by Queen Christina of Saxony to make an altarpiece for the Franciscan friary church, which she intended as a burial chapel for herself and King Hans, who died in 1513. She died in 1521. The carved oak triptych stands 3.75 metres high, gilded and painted, its panels crowded with figures working through the Crucifixion, the Passion and the Coronation of the Virgin. It survived the iconoclasm of the Reformation, quite possibly because of the royal graves it stood over. In 1807 the friary church was pulled down, and the altarpiece and four royal bodies were moved to St Canute's; Christian II, his wife Isabella of Austria and their son Hans were brought back from Ghent and reinterred in the royal chapel in 1883.

## Down the Steps

The crypt was shut at the Reformation and stayed shut for three centuries. During the restoration work of the 1870s it was refurbished, opened as a chapel, and Canute went back on display alongside his brother Benedict — which is why the most-visited part of an otherwise austere Lutheran cathedral is the room under the choir where two eleventh-century men lie in the open. Upstairs, the building keeps accumulating: an organ installed in 1752, whose facade survives inside a much-rebuilt instrument, and the memory of Thomas Kingo, bishop of Funen from 1677 until his death in 1703 and the greatest hymn-writer Denmark has produced, whose 1699 hymnal carried eighty-five of his own texts. A Renaissance chapel added in 1633 was less lucky; it was dismantled in the great restoration of 1868 and is gone.

## Flight Context

St Canute's Cathedral stands at 55.395°N, 10.389°E on Abbey Hill in the centre of Odense, just north of the Odense Å and beside Eventyrhaven. Its 63-metre west tower is the single most useful visual fix in the city — a plain brick shaft with no spire, rising clear of the surrounding red-tiled roofs, with the darker mass of the city hall immediately to its west. Hans Christian Andersen Airport (EKOD) is about 10 km north-west; Billund (EKBI) roughly 70 km west, Copenhagen Kastrup (EKCH) about 140 km east. Best viewed at 1,500–3,000 ft. Danish maritime haze often softens the brickwork; morning light from the east gives the strongest contrast on the tower face.
