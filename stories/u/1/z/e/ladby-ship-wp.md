# Ladby Ship

Denmark's only Viking Age ship burial, where the vessel itself has rotted entirely away and what remains is its outline in the soil, drawn by two thousand iron rivets.

**Location:** 55.4454, 10.6152

**Categories:** Viking ships, Viking ship burials, Archaeological discoveries in Denmark, Kerteminde Municipality, Archaeology

## Introduction

There is no ship at Ladby. Walk down into the dim vault under the mound near Kerteminde and what lies in front of you is a shape — a long, narrow, unmistakably boat-shaped stain in the sandy soil, its overlapping clinker planking still legible in the discolouration, its hull traced out by roughly two thousand iron rivets that stayed exactly where the wood around them dissolved. The vessel is gone. Its shadow is what survived, and its shadow is precise enough to measure: about twenty-two metres from stem to stern, three metres at the widest, a metre deep amidships and drawing only half a metre of water. A fast, shallow, coastal thing, built to be pulled up on a beach.

## What Is Still Down There

The rivets are the ship. Everything organic — oak planking, mast, rigging, the wooden burial chamber set between the stern and the mast — has been gone for a thousand years, but iron endures in this soil, and the rivets held their positions as the timber rotted from around them. In the bow lies an anchor of about twenty kilograms with its iron chain. Ornamental fittings from the stem survive. And in the bottom of the hull, still visible, are the bones of the animals killed at the funeral: eleven horses, small by modern standards at roughly 1.25 metres at the shoulder, and at least four dogs. The chieftain who owned them is not there. That absence is not an accident of preservation.

## Someone Got Here First

The grave was extensively damaged in antiquity, and only a few small human bones were ever recovered. One reading is that the site was a translation — a body moved from a heathen grave to a Christian one as Denmark converted. The other is violence. The struggle for dominance between Harald Bluetooth and his heir Sweyn Forkbeard may have brought raiders to this mound. A buried ship was a monument, plainly visible to everyone who lived in the district or sailed past it up the Kerteminde inlet, and its whole function was to glorify the minor king lying inside it. By removing the dead man and chopping his grave goods into hundreds of pieces within a few years of the funeral, whoever came here struck directly at the prestige of his heirs. It worked well enough that we do not know his name. Forty-five arrowheads with fragments of their shafts were found outside the rail, and a shield boss survived, but the weapons that should have been in a burial like this are simply not there.

## Dated by a Dog's Harness

The burial has been dated to the first half of the tenth century — between roughly 900 and 950 — and the evidence for that is almost comically small: a gilded bronze link from a harness, decorated in the Jelling style, whose ornament places it firmly in that window. This is the only ship burial from the Viking Age ever found in Denmark. It belongs to a small northern family of graves that includes the boat chamber grave at Hedeby, which was Danish territory when it was dug and is in Germany now, and the great Norwegian ship burials at Oseberg, Borre, Gokstad and Tune. Ladby is the most thoroughly wrecked of them, and in one respect the most affecting, because the others were lifted out of the ground and put on display. Ladby stayed where it was put.

## February 1935

The find belongs to an amateur. Poul Helweg Mikkelsen was a pharmacist with an interest in archaeology, and on or around 28 February 1935 he located the grave in an otherwise unremarkable Viking Age burial ground near Kerteminde in north-eastern Funen. The drawings he made, together with those of the National Museum conservator Gustav Rosenberg, remain the primary source material for everything known about the find — a reminder of how much early excavation depended on a steady hand and a pencil. Mikkelsen then paid, out of his own pocket, for a vaulted chamber to be built over the site in 1937, which was covered again with earth and grass so that the mound would look much as it always had. He handed the ship to the National Museum, which held responsibility until 1994, when it passed to the archaeologists at the Viking Museum at Ladby.

## The Museum Over the Grave

The Viking Museum at Ladby is now part of the Museums of Eastern Funen, and it does two things at once. Below ground, the grave is left exactly as it was found: a stain, a scatter of rivets, an anchor, bones. Above ground, in a building opened in 2007, the original finds are displayed alongside a full-scale reconstruction of the burial as it may have looked on the day it was closed — the dead chieftain laid on a bed in a replica of his ship, his grave goods around him, the dogs and the eleven horses at hand. An interpretive film draws on Norse myth and the imagery of the Gotlandic picture stones to set out what the people at that funeral may have believed about the journey into the realm of the dead. The reconstruction is the ship you can see. The other one, twenty metres away and a thousand years older, is the one that is actually there.

## Flight Context

The Ladby ship lies at 55.4454°N, 10.6152°E, on the south shore of Kertinge Nor — the long, narrow inlet running inland from Kerteminde on the north-east coast of Funen. From 1,000–2,000 ft the setting explains the burial: the mound sits just above a sheltered waterway with direct access to the Great Belt, exactly the kind of protected anchorage a tenth-century chieftain would control. Look for the town of Kerteminde at the mouth of the inlet and follow the water south-west; the museum building and its low grass-covered mound sit on the shoreline. Odense Airport (EKOD) is very close, about 15 km west-south-west, which makes this an easy diversion on any Funen circuit; Copenhagen (EKCH) is roughly 110 km east. The site is small and unspectacular from the air — it is meant to be, since Mikkelsen deliberately reburied the vault under turf in 1937.
