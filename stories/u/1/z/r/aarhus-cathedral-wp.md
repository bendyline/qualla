# Aarhus Cathedral

Denmark's longest and tallest church holds more medieval fresco than any other building in the country, a Lübeck master's folding altarpiece, and a model warship that washed ashore after a storm.

**Location:** 56.1569, 10.2106

**Categories:** Cathedrals, Medieval architecture, Brick Gothic, Denmark, Aarhus, Church art, Reformation

## Introduction

In the great east window at Aarhus Cathedral, the crucified Christ lets his head fall to the left. Almost every other crucifixion in European art tilts it to the right, and nobody has explained why the Norwegian artist Emanuel Vigeland broke the convention in 1926. His window is fourteen metres tall, the largest stained glass in Denmark — and among the newest things in a building that has been under revision for eight centuries. Ninety-three metres long, ninety-six to the top of the tower, this is the longest and tallest church in the country. It began as an act of shrewd ecclesiastical politics.

## A Saint With an Anchor Round His Neck

Clement was an early bishop of Rome who, in a later and largely invented biography, was martyred with an anchor tied to his neck and thrown into the Black Sea. That made him the patron saint of sailors, and sailors made him enormously popular in Scandinavia. Bishop Ulfketil built a timber church near this spot in 1102 to house his relics; it burned some time before the 1190s. By then the site had a second and more local draw. St Niels of Aarhus, a younger son of King Canute V, had left court for the village of Skibby, built a church with his own hands, and asked on his deathbed in 1180 to be buried in "the little church by the sea." So when Bishop Peder Vognsen of the powerful Hvide family began the cathedral in the decade after 1190, he built it around St Clement's, enclosing a devotion the townspeople already had.

## Fire, and the Long Rebuild

The church Vognsen started was completed in 1300: Romanesque, half-rounded arches, a flat timber ceiling, built of large red bricks — then a new material that would spread across Scandinavia and northern Germany. Thirty years later it burned along with most of the town, and for well over a century nothing happened. The cathedral stood abandoned until 1449. By the time work resumed, Gothic building had reached Denmark, and the rebuilding ran in stages until about 1500. The nave was stretched to ninety-three metres, the transept widened, and vaulting lifted the ceilings high enough for tall windows, which is why a church of this bulk feels so full of light. The tower reached its present ninety-six-metre form only in 1931; lightning had set it ablaze in 1642 and destroyed some of the historic bells, though the interior came through almost untouched. The building seats roughly 1,200 and was formally listed on 1 February 2012.

## Paint That Survived the Whitewash

Before the Reformation nearly every wall here was painted. Most of that work is gone, but 220 square metres survive — more medieval fresco than any other church in Denmark can show, dating from 1470 to 1520. The figures of St Christopher and St Clement are the tallest painted figures in the country; St Michael appears, and St George with his dragon. In the northwest corner is something older, a painting from around 1300 saved from the first Romanesque cathedral and known as the Lazarus Window. Tradition holds that the opening beneath it was used to hand food out to people with leprosy, who were not permitted inside.

## Gifts From a Bishop

Luther's ideas reached Aarhus through Hans Tausen, a Danish friar whose Good Friday sermon in 1524 got him locked in a monastery cell in Viborg. He kept teaching from the cell, ordinary people responded, and a near-riot forced his release; within a year he was chaplain to the king. Aarhus townspeople began demanding the liturgy in Danish, and neither bishop nor canons could stop them. Frederik I died in 1533, the civil war known as the Count's Feud followed, and in the summer of 1536 the last Catholic bishop of Aarhus, Ove Bille, was arrested. Tausen had argued throughout that tearing apart ancient churches was wrong. Whatever the reason, Aarhus kept its furniture. The altarpiece carved by the Lübeck master Bernt Notke was dedicated on Easter Sunday 1479 and is still in place, built with movable sections so the scene changes with the liturgical year. Two years after giving it, Bishop Jens Iversen Lange commissioned the copper font, cast in 1481 by the bell-maker Peter Hansen of Flensborg. It stands on the heads of the four evangelists as men with animal faces — eagle for John, lion for Mark, ox for Luke, and Matthew alone with a human head.

## The Ship and the Organ

A model ship hangs at the crossing, as one does in churches all over Denmark: a reminder of the sea and of everyone the sea has taken. This one is called Enigheden, dated 1720, and at 2.65 metres long and 3.50 high it is the largest church ship in the country. Peter the Great, the story goes, ordered warships from Dutch builders who sent a scale model rather than drawings; the model's transport sank in a storm off Skagen, the model washed ashore nearly intact, and fishermen from Aarhus bought it and gave it to the cathedral. It is read here as a figure for the voyage from cradle to grave. Above it is the organ. Lambert Daniel Kastens, a pupil of the Hamburg builder Arp Schnitger, made the original in 1730, and the baroque facade is still his. A restoration by Marcussen & Søn finished in 2020 left the instrument with eighty-nine stops, the largest church organ in Denmark. Eight bells hang in the tower, the oldest cast in the thirteenth century by a founder named Martinus.

## Flight Context

Aarhus Cathedral stands at 56.157°N, 10.211°E, on the north side of Store Torv in the medieval core of Aarhus, roughly 400 m inland from the harbour front. The 96 m tower is the tallest church tower in Denmark and the most reliable visual fix over the old city; from 2,000-3,000 ft AGL it reads clearly against the tight grid of the Latin Quarter, with the harbour cranes and Dokk1 to the southeast and the Aarhus Bay opening to the east. Aarhus Airport (EKAH) at Tirstrup lies about 35 km northeast on Djursland; Karup (EKKA) is 80 km west and Billund (EKBI) 90 km southwest. Coastal Jutland weather brings frequent low stratus and drizzle from October to March; the clearest light over the bay is generally May to early September, and long summer evenings give the brick a deep red cast.
