# Dokk1

Scandinavia's largest public library sits on the Aarhus waterfront under a hovering metal disc, and rings a three-tonne bronze gong every time a baby is born in the city.

**Location:** 56.1535, 10.2142

**Categories:** Libraries, Modern architecture, Denmark, Aarhus, Public buildings, Urban renewal, Public art

## Introduction

In the maternity ward at Aarhus University Hospital there is a button. When new parents press it, a bronze tube seven and a half metres long and weighing close to three tonnes begins to sound above the main staircase of the city's public library, several kilometres away on the waterfront. Readers look up. Someone has just been born in Aarhus. The gong is a work by the artist Kirstine Roepstorff, and it is the single most quoted thing about Dokk1 — which is fair, because it tells you exactly what kind of building this is trying to be.

## A Building Named by Competition

The name came out of a public contest held in the autumn of 2012, after construction had already started. Dokk1 can be said as Dokken, Dok-Ét or Dok-Een — the Dock, or Dock One — and it points at what used to be here: working industrial harbour at the mouth of the Aarhus River. Ground was broken on 8 June 2011 and the building was inaugurated four years later, on 20 June 2015. Schmidt Hammer Lassen Architects designed it with landscape architect Kristine Jensen, NCC managed the construction, and the whole thing sits inside a much larger scheme called Urban Mediaspace Aarhus, financed jointly by Aarhus Municipality and the philanthropic association Realdania at a cost of 2.1 billion Danish kroner. That budget also covered uncovering the river, rebuilding Europa Plads, digging an underground rainwater reservoir against future flooding, and laying out two new public squares.

## A Disc Above a Glass Box

The form is unusual and reads clearly even from the water: a heptagonal metal disc appearing to hover above a glass prism, the prism itself resting on wide, irregular flights of steps that spill outward on every side. Offices and municipal administration occupy the disc. The library fills the glass, deliberately transparent so that everything happening inside is visible from outside — and so that readers get the harbour, the bay, the forest and the city all at once. Sixty thousand square metres of floorspace in total, of which 17,500 belong to the library and 10,500 are let to businesses. Most of the rest is an automated underground car park with twenty lifts and space for a thousand vehicles. Aarhus's light rail runs through and under the building, with its own station at the lower entrance.

## The Largest Library in Scandinavia

Aarhus Main Library lives here — the largest library in the municipality, the central book repository and the administrative hub for every branch in the area, and the biggest public library in Scandinavia. It spreads across two levels: literary fiction, professional literature, magazines and newspapers, media, exhibition space, a café, lounges, and children's sections graded by age, with a robotic delivery system moving books and media behind the scenes. The municipal public services department shares the building, so a library visit and a trip to the council counter are the same errand. Accessibility was treated as a design problem rather than a compliance exercise — inclines instead of steps where possible, adaptable counters, hearing loop systems, pictogram signage, lifts and escalators throughout. The International Federation of Library Associations and Institutions named Dokk1 Public Library of the Year in 2016, a year after it took a commendation for cultural regeneration at the MIPIM AR Future Projects Awards.

## One Percent for Art

A full percent of the project cost was reserved for art and decoration, which is how a library ends up with a birth gong. Below the central staircase, in the ceiling of the underground car park, hangs Magic Mushrooms by the Berlin-based duo Elmgreen & Dragset: three hundred square metres of imaginary city, modelled at 1:100 and turned upside down, so drivers park beneath an inverted skyline. Outdoors, the broad concrete deck that encircles the building carries a four-part playground called Kloden — the Globe. The southern section stands for Africa, with a jungle, a large ape and drawings that echo hieroglyphs. The east has an Asian dragon and a six-metre bear for Russia and Eastern Europe. In the north, white wooden platforms mounted on heavy springs pitch underfoot like Arctic ice floes. The west gets a wooden eagle perched on an erupting volcano, standing in for Iceland and the Americas.

## The Boards Behind the Facade

Denmark's weather is a design constraint, not a footnote: long stretches of driven rain and sustained coastal wind. In June 2016, less than a year after opening, it emerged that the 6,400 square metres of magnesium oxide boards installed as a wind barrier behind Dokk1's outer facade had absorbed water. The boards were rusting the fixings behind them, and left alone the damage would eventually become structural. Replacement work began that September. A leading Danish building expert put the repair cost at 19 to 26 million kroner, and the question of who pays has never been fully settled — which in practice means taxpayers. Dokk1 was not alone. Since 2014, MgO boards have caused expensive damage in buildings across Denmark, and this was only one of the costlier cases. The library that Aarhus built to look permanent had one of the most modern materials in it quietly falling apart.

## Flight Context

Dokk1 stands at 56.154°N, 10.214°E on Hack Kampmanns Plads, at the mouth of the Aarhus River on the central waterfront, immediately southeast of the old town and next to the Custom House. From 1,500-3,000 ft the heptagonal roof disc is unmistakable — a pale seven-sided plate set against rectangular quays, with the container terminal to the north and the Marselisborg shoreline running south. The cathedral tower lies about 700 m northwest. Aarhus Airport (EKAH) at Tirstrup is roughly 35 km northeast; Karup (EKKA) 80 km west and Billund (EKBI) 90 km southwest. Onshore wind and low stratus are common from October to March; the harbour front is at its clearest on high-pressure days from May to September.
