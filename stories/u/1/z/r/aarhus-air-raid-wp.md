# Aarhus Air Raid

On 31 October 1944 the Danish resistance asked Britain to bomb the building where its own people were being interrogated — and twenty-five Mosquitoes flew from Norfolk to do it.

**Location:** 56.1670, 10.1970

**Categories:** World War II, Denmark, Aarhus, Military history, Royal Air Force, Resistance movements

## Introduction

The telegram left Jutland in October 1944 and it asked for something terrible. "Jeg beder indtrængende om, at kollegium 4 og 5, må blive ødelagt ved luftangreb" — I urgently request that dormitories 4 and 5 be destroyed by air attack. Vagn Bennike, chief of resistance operations in Jutland, was asking the Royal Air Force to bomb the student halls of the University of Aarhus, because the Gestapo had taken them over, filled them with the files that were dismantling his organisation, and was at that moment holding and interrogating his people inside them. He sent it after the arrest of a courier named Ruth Philipsen on 7 October. An assault on the ground had already been discussed and judged impossible.

## A Bad Year in Jutland

The resistance in Jutland was collapsing from the inside. In December 1943 the Gestapo caught a British-trained paratrooper, Jakob Jensen, in Aarhus; under interrogation he gave up the supply networks across the peninsula, and 145 people were arrested as a result — among them the Hvidsten Group, taken on 11 March 1944. Then, in the spring, the Gestapo recruited Grethe Bartram from communist and resistance circles in the city as an informant. She named some fifty people. Group after group came apart, including the Samsing Group in June. Weapons stopped arriving, and the men who knew where the remaining weapons were kept were sitting in dormitories 4 and 5, in offices the Germans had installed in rooms built for students, next to the archives that recorded everything they had said.

## Thirty-Three Aircraft from Norfolk

The force lifted off from Swanton Morley in Norfolk on the morning of 31 October, the first aircraft rolling at 8:40 and all thirty-three airborne by 9:20. It comprised twenty-four Mosquito Mk.VI fighter-bombers from three squadrons of 140 Wing, one Mosquito Mk.IV of the RAF Film Production Unit to record what happened, and an escort from 315 (Polish) Squadron — twelve Mustang Mk.IIIs on paper, eight in practice, after four were found that morning with broken tail wheels. The Mosquito was made largely of wood and was fast enough at low level to arrive before the sirens did. The timing was deliberate in another way too: mid-morning meant Gestapo staff would be at their desks, while most Danish prisoners were expected to be elsewhere at lunch.

## 11:41

The first bombs went off at 11:41. The attack came in waves, one after another: the leading aircraft carried high explosive to break the buildings open, the last two waves incendiaries to set the paper alight. The bombs were fused with an eleven-second delay so the aircraft that dropped them would be gone before they detonated. Dormitories 4 and 5 were hit. The Langelandsgade barracks, a secondary target, were badly damaged. One bomb went into the university's main building, which was still under construction, and there it killed men who had nothing to do with the war on either side. Most of the Gestapo archive burned, including much of the file on the Danish resistance. Nobody has ever established exactly how much was lost.

## Who Died

A German internal report counted 39 dead in the dormitory offices: 27 SS officers working for the Gestapo, among them the Aarhus commander Eugen Schwitzgebel, and 12 from other German police departments, most of them clerical staff. Another twenty were killed at the Langelandsgade barracks, eighteen of them soldiers. About ten Danish civilians died as well. Several were builders working on the unfinished main building; the chief architect, C.F. Møller, was injured. One Danish prisoner was killed while under interrogation. Ruth Philipsen, whose arrest had prompted the telegram, came through it almost unhurt. So did the resistance leader Harald Sandbæk, who was being interrogated at the moment the bombs arrived — badly injured, treated in secret, and eventually got out to Sweden. One Mosquito was hit by flak from the German light cruiser Nürnberg and was too damaged to cross the North Sea; escorted over the Kattegat, it landed safely in Sweden, where the crew destroyed the aircraft before Swedish authorities reached them. They spent the rest of the war interned.

## What It Bought

The RAF afterwards called it the most successful operation of its kind in the war, and by the standard applied to it — records destroyed, headquarters wrecked, precision achieved from rooftop height in daylight over a friendly city — the claim is hard to argue with. The Germans responded by reinforcing, nearly doubling the number of Gestapo agents in Denmark. Aarhus was left with a more complicated inheritance. The raid was requested by Danes, aimed at a Danish university, and it killed Danes: workmen on a building site, a prisoner in a cell. Bennike knew that would happen when he sent the telegram, which is the part of the story that does not resolve. The university buildings were repaired and remain in use.

## Flight Context

56.167 N, 10.197 E — the University of Aarhus campus on the northern edge of the city centre, immediately north of the botanical gardens. The campus is unmistakable from the air: a cluster of yellow-brick, red-tiled buildings by C.F. Møller set around the University Park, a broad open green with an oak-lined ravine running through it. Overfly at 1,500-2,500 ft AGL for the layout. Aarhus Cathedral and the harbour lie south-east; Den Gamle By is just south-west across the gardens; the Langelandsgade barracks site is a few hundred metres west. Aarhus lies under controlled airspace — check current charts and coordinate before descending. Nearest airports: Aarhus Airport (EKAH) at Tirstrup about 19 nm north-east, Karup (EKKA) about 40 nm west, Billund (EKBI) about 45 nm south-west. Low cloud off Aarhus Bay is common; the 1944 crews needed clear weather at rooftop height and got it.
