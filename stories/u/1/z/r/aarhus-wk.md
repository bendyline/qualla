# Aarhus

Denmark's second city grew from a Viking harbour called Aros into a young, student-heavy port that has spent two decades converting its container quays into a new district.

**Location:** 56.1564, 10.2097

**Categories:** Cities, Denmark, Jutland, Ports, University towns, Urban renewal, Viking Age

## Introduction

Smilets By — the City of Smiles — was supposed to be a temporary idea. Aarhus adopted it in 1938 as a tourist-board campaign and shut the campaign down by 1948, but nobody told the residents, who kept saying it, printing it and painting it on things. Nearly eighty years after it was officially retired, the slogan is still how Denmark's second city introduces itself. That stubbornness is characteristic. Aarhus is a place that fought to get its old spelling back, and that has spent the last twenty years rebuilding its entire waterfront.

## The Mouth of the River

The old name was Aros: ār, meaning river, plus os, meaning mouth. A harbour settlement stood here by the late 700s, at the point where the Aarhus River empties into the bay, and excavation has dated the first earthen rampart to around 934 — built fast, probably under military pressure. Six runestones have been found in and around the city, all from roughly the year 1000, and since only prominent men raised runestones, six of them says something about how much traffic and money passed through this river mouth. The town received its market-town privileges on 2 July 1441. The spelling drifted for centuries — Aros, Arus, Aars, and in the 1449 charter "Aarss" — and settled on Aarhus after the Reformation. The Danish spelling reform of 1948 then turned every "Aa" into "Å," and the city became Århus — until the council voted in 2010 to change it back, with effect from 1 January 2011. The double A is a small, deliberate act of local memory.

## What 1928 Did

Aarhus University opened in 1928, the first university in Jutland, and the yellow-brick campus north of the centre has been reshaping the city ever since. Today the median age here is 37.5 years, the lowest of any Danish city, and more than forty percent of residents hold an academic degree. You feel it in the café density, in the number of bicycles, and in mid-July, when the students leave and the centre goes strangely quiet — restaurants close for weeks at a stretch and visitors briefly outnumber locals. Things restart in mid-August, in time for Aarhus Festuge, a ten-day festival at the end of the month that is one of the largest cultural events in Northern Europe. In August 2012 Aarhus was named European Capital of Culture for 2017, an honour it shared with the whole of the Central Denmark Region, and the building boom that followed is still visible on the skyline.

## Fifty Percent of Denmark's Containers

None of this is a museum town. The Port of Aarhus runs the largest container terminal in the country and handles more than half of all Danish container traffic, moving millions of tonnes of cargo a year across quays that sit within walking distance of the cathedral. Cranes are part of the skyline and always have been. What changed is where the cargo goes: as the container operation consolidated northward and eastward, the older inner harbour was left with room to spare, and the city decided to give the water back to the people who live beside it.

## The New Ground

Construction on Aarhus Ø, the redeveloped northern docklands, began in 2008 on ground that had been a container port. Built out, it is intended to hold ten to twelve thousand residents and about ten thousand jobs, and most of the first generation of buildings was finished by 2018. The one everyone photographs is Isbjerget — the Iceberg — a white housing block by JDS Architects and CEBRA whose stepped, jagged roofline was arranged so that units behind the waterfront still get a view; it won a MIPIM award in 2013. Above it stands the Lighthouse tower, 142 metres, finished in 2023 to designs by 3XN and UNStudio after the original scheme was abandoned over unstable ground and revived in modified form in 2018. Closer in, the Dokk1 library anchors the central stretch of waterfront where the river reaches the sea.

## Where People Actually Live

The medieval core, Indre By, was walled and gated until 1857 and is now largely pedestrianised; inside it the Latin Quarter keeps its cobbles, low houses and narrow lines. Northwest of that, Øgade-kvarteret is a grid of colourful townhouses from the 1850s to the 1920s with every street named after a Danish island. Frederiksbjerg, south of the broad railway yard, went up in the late 1800s and kept most of its original buildings — small shops, small parks, a lot of street life. Trøjborg, north of the centre, is four and five storeys of brick from the early 1900s, once working-class, now quiet. Then the seasons do their work. The beech trees come into leaf around the first of May, and within days the parks fill; by October the city forests turn, and by November the light is gone by mid-afternoon and everyone moves indoors to the cafés that gave Aarhus its other nickname.

## Flight Context

Aarhus lies at 56.156°N, 10.210°E on the east coast of Jutland, at the head of Aarhus Bay opening onto the Kattegat. From 3,000-5,000 ft the city reads as a dense grey-brown core wrapped around a river mouth, with the 96 m cathedral tower and the 142 m Lighthouse tower on Aarhus Ø as the two clearest verticals and the container quays and Marselisborg Forests bracketing the waterfront north and south. Aarhus Airport (EKAH) at Tirstrup is about 35 km northeast on the Djursland peninsula; Karup (EKKA) lies 80 km west, Billund (EKBI) 90 km southwest, and Aalborg (EKYT) 110 km north. Expect frequent low cloud, drizzle and onshore winds from October through March, and long, clear evenings from late May to August when the sun sets after 22:00.
