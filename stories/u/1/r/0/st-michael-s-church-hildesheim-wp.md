# St. Michael's Church, Hildesheim

An Ottonian abbey church laid out entirely from the square of its own crossing — bombed flat in 1945, but its painted ceiling had been carried out two years earlier.

**Location:** 52.1529, 9.9435

**Categories:** Religious sites, Romanesque architecture, UNESCO World Heritage Sites, Medieval art, Lower Saxony, World War II

## Introduction

On 29 September 1022, the feast of the archangel Michael, Bishop Bernward of Hildesheim consecrated a church that was not finished. He had begun it twelve years earlier on a hill half a kilometre north of his city walls, and he had shaped almost everything about it — the plan, the proportions, the fittings. He was also running out of time. Weeks after the consecration he was dead, and his successor inherited a building site. What Bernward got to see was enough to change how churches were built in Germany.

## Everything From One Square

Stand in the crossing, where nave and transept meet, and you are standing inside the unit that generates the entire building. The square of that crossing is the module: every other dimension in the ground plan is a multiple of it. This was not a new idea in the abstract, but nobody had carried it through with such discipline.

What makes the system visible is the alternation of supports. Instead of a uniform march of columns down the nave, piers and columns take turns — pier, column, column, pier — so that the eye reads the nave as a series of squares rather than a continuous tunnel. Nikolaus Pevsner called it a more thorough metrical system than anything in Romanesque architecture before it. The numbers are modest by cathedral standards: about 75 metres end to end, a nave 27 metres long and 8.6 metres wide between the arcades, rising 16.7 metres, inside walls more than a metre and a half thick. The effect is not size. It is legibility.

## A Bishop Who Knew How to Cast Bronze

Bernward came to this with an unusual training. As private tutor to the young Otto III he learned bronze casting, painting and metalwork himself, and he lived in Rome for a period during that service, where the early Christian basilicas gave him a model of severe, unornamented interiors to work against.

The abbey he founded was Benedictine, part of a small complex: two further sanctuaries dedicated to Martin and to the Holy Cross stood in the cloister extending north, while the church presented its south flank to the city like a facade. Bernward's successor Godehard carried the work through to completion in the 1030s and had it reconsecrated to Michael on the archangel's feast day. In 1033 he moved Bernward's remains into the crypt, where they still lie. In 1186, after a fire and a rebuilding, Bishop Adelog of Dorstedt consecrated the church again — with Tammo, Prince-Bishop of Verden, assisting.

## Thirteen Hundred Oak Boards

The ceiling is the reason many visitors come. Around 1130 someone assembled more than 1,300 oak planks into a flat wooden field 27.6 metres long and 8.7 metres wide, and painted across it the Tree of Jesse — the ancestral line of Jesus, growing out of the sleeping figure of Jesse and branching upward through kings and prophets.

Painted panel ceilings of that age almost never survive. Fire takes them, or damp, or a later generation's taste for vaulting. The Hildesheim ceiling is one of a tiny group left in Europe, alongside those at Zillis in Switzerland and Dädesjö in Sweden. It was heavily restored to its present state in 2010, and it is not comfortable viewing — the neck gives out long before the eye does.

## Two Congregations, One Building

The Reformation reached Hildesheim in force around 1542. With the backing of the authorities governing the city, newly empowered Lutherans took over the bulk of the church and left the monastic community with very little. Structural fabric suffered under the new arrangement, less through malice than through decades of nobody being clearly responsible for the roof.

What came out of it is a simultaneum, a shared church, and it has held ever since: the main body of the building is Lutheran, the crypt beneath the eastern choir remains Roman Catholic. Bernward's grave is in the Catholic half. The service he founded the place to house goes on in the Protestant one. Half a kilometre southeast, across the city, the cathedral holds the bronze doors he commissioned; scholars have argued for a century about whether they were cast for St Michael's and moved later, and excavations during the 2006 renovation quietly demolished one leading version of the argument by proving the church never had the westwork that theory required.

## Carried Out in Time

On 22 March 1945 the RAF raid that flattened central Hildesheim destroyed St Michael's. High explosives and incendiaries took the building down to its walls, the last and worst of a series of attacks that month.

The ceiling was not in it. On the initiative of the provincial conservator Hermann Deckert, the painted boards had been dismantled in 1943 and dispersed to storage, along with the church's other treasures; the angel choir screen was walled in behind a protective barrier and came through untouched. Reconstruction under the architect August Albert Steinborn began in 1947, paid for in part by the pastor Kurt Degener and by Bernard R. Armour, a Jewish businessman in New York — a donation whose date and circumstances are worth pausing over. The nave and western transept were reconsecrated on 20 August 1950, and the work was finished by 1960. In 1985 UNESCO listed the church together with the cathedral. The oak boards went back up over a rebuilt nave, older than everything holding them.

## Flight Context

St Michael's stands at 52.1529°N, 9.9435°E on the Michaelishügel, a low rise at the western rim of the Hildesheim city centre, with the Innerste river beyond it to the west and the baroque Magdalenengarten park immediately west of the cloister. From 2,000-3,500 ft the church reads as a long pale block with towers at both ends, distinctly separate from the cathedral on its own hill about 1 km southeast — the two hills and the two churches between them define the UNESCO site and the shape of the old town. The Gymnasium Andreanum lies just north. Hildesheim sits beneath the approach and departure corridors for Hannover (EDDV), 28 km north, so check controlled airspace; the local field at Hildesheim-Drispenstedt (EDVM) is on the northeastern edge of the city and Braunschweig-Wolfsburg (EDVE) is about 45 km east. Fog forms readily in the Innerste valley from autumn through early spring and can sit below the hilltops well into the day.
