# Celle Castle

A Lutheran court chapel packed with seventy-eight paintings, a Venetian façade grafted onto a Saxon water castle, and a court theatre still working three and a half centuries after it was built for an audience of one household.

**Location:** 52.6239, 10.0775

**Categories:** Castles, Celle, Lower Saxony, Renaissance architecture, Baroque, Theatres, Museums

## Introduction

The chapel is roughly the size of a large drawing room, and there is almost nowhere in it to rest your eye. Between 1565 and 1576 the Antwerp painter Marten de Vos and his workshop filled every available surface: seventy-eight paintings, fifty sandstone reliefs, more than a hundred and twenty biblical inscriptions worked into the panelling and galleries. It is a Lutheran chapel decorated to a density that would embarrass a cathedral, and it has come through four and a half centuries essentially as it was finished — the single most surprising room in Lower Saxony's largest castle.

## A Room That Refuses to Stop

Consecrated in 1485 and reworked after the Reformation, the chapel kept its Renaissance fittings while almost every comparable interior in northern Germany was stripped, burned or modernised. Whitewash was the fashionable answer to Protestant scruples about images. Celle took the opposite view and commissioned more of them.

The result is dense but not chaotic. Painted panels run in registers, the reliefs mark the structural bays, and the texts thread between them so that the whole room reads as a single argument rather than a gallery. Restoration between 1978 and 1981 stabilised what four hundred years of candle smoke had dimmed. The historic state rooms and the chapel can be seen on guided tours only, which is less a limitation than a kindness — you want somebody telling you where to look.

## From Ford to Fortress

The site began as a job, not a residence. Around 980 a Brunonen count raised a walled tower called Kellu to watch a ford across the River Aller — a water castle in character, wet-footed and defensive. Otto the Strict extended it in 1292; the cellar vaulting and the lower stages of that watchtower still survive, buried beneath the castle theatre. Castrum Celle is first recorded around 1315.

Then came the promotion. After the War of the Lüneburg Succession, the dukes of Brunswick-Lüneburg moved their residence from Lüneburg to Celle in 1378 and began turning a fort into a seat. Frederick the Pious enlarged it substantially between 1471 and 1478. From 1530, Ernest the Confessor brought the Renaissance indoors, while between 1520 and 1560 the ramparts and bastions were pushed outward. What emerged was a textbook building of its moment: a quadrangle around a rectangular courtyard, massive corner towers, and the stepped, ornamented detail of the Weser Renaissance.

## Venice on the Aller

Duke George William had spent time in Italy before he came to power, and from 1670 he set about rebuilding the place in the light of what he had seen there. The façades were reworked on Venetian models and are essentially what you see today; the roofline gained its ring of gables, and the corner towers their oddly bulbous domes, which look less Saxon the longer you study them.

Inside, he added baroque state rooms. Outside the old defensive belt he laid out the French Garden on French principles, later softened into an English landscape park, though the baroque bones still show in places. When he died in 1705 the absolute rule of the Celle dukes ended with him, and the castle passed with the principality toward Hanover — and, eventually, toward a family that would find itself running Britain.

## A Theatre Built for One Household

Among what George William brought back from Venice was a taste for Italian opera, and he acted on it. Work on the court theatre began in 1670 to designs by the Italian architect Giuseppe Arighini and was largely complete by 1675. He then imported companies to fill it — from France, from Italy, from Hanover down the road.

It was never meant for the public. The theatre served the court, and only at the end of the eighteenth century were ordinary townspeople admitted to plays in any real numbers. After the duke's death it went quiet, and was woken up briefly for Caroline Matilda, the young British-born queen of Denmark sent to Celle in 1772 after the collapse of her marriage and her affair with Johann Friedrich Struensee. A second gallery was built for her. She died of scarlet fever in 1775, aged twenty-three. The theatre carried on until 1890, closed, was thoroughly renovated in 1935, and today is one of the very few surviving baroque theatres in northern Germany — with a resident company still working in it.

## The Moat That Ate the Ramparts

Because the castle was never actually fought over, its defences became surplus. Between 1785 and 1802 the outer bastions were dismantled and shovelled into the deep, wide moats they had once protected. From 1826 the levelled embankments were planted with trees and shrubs and laid to lawn, and a landscape garden grew up around the walls. Some of the park went for housing around 1900; about seven hectares remain, and the castle still sits on its island, ringed by water.

In the grounds stands a bronze of a stallion called Wohlklang, shown performing at liberty. It was made in 1985 for the 250th anniversary of the Lower Saxon State Stud a short walk south, and it is a fair emblem of the town: a ducal seat that lost its dukes, kept its rooms, and went on being famous for horses.

## Flight Context

Celle Castle stands at 52.6239°N, 10.0775°E, on the southwest edge of the old town of Celle in Lower Saxony, about 35 km northeast of Hannover. From 2,000–3,500 ft it is unmistakable: a pale quadrangular block with four corner towers, sitting on a moated island in a roughly seven-hectare green park, immediately west of the dense red-roofed grid of half-timbered streets, with the loops of the River Aller to the north and the wedge of the French Garden just to the south. Celle Air Base (ETHC) lies about 4 km southwest at Wietzenbruch and is an active German Army aviation training field — expect rotary traffic in the circuit and coordinate before transiting. Hannover (EDDV) is roughly 35 km southwest. The Bergen-Hohne training area to the north-northwest carries restricted airspace and live firing; check NOTAMs. Morning fog forms along the Aller on still autumn days, and heath haze is common in high summer.
