# Phaeno Science Center

Zaha Hadid's concrete science centre in Wolfsburg, propped on ten hollow cones so that the city can walk underneath it.

**Location:** 52.4289, 10.7900

**Categories:** museum, science, architecture, germany, lower-saxony, design, modern

## Introduction

Twenty-seven thousand cubic metres of concrete went into this building, and almost none of it touches the ground. Zaha Hadid's Phaeno stands on ten conical legs, several of them wide enough to hold rooms inside, and the whole grey mass hangs above a shaded public space that anyone can wander through without buying a ticket. Wolfsburg had asked for a science centre. What arrived, after just over four years of construction and 67 million euros, was a 170-metre slab of engineering that behaves as though it weighed nothing. It opened on 24 November 2005, collected a RIBA European Award and an Institution of Structural Engineers award the following year, and has been frustrating photographers ever since - there is no angle from which the building resolves into a shape you can describe to someone else.

## Ten Legs, Most of Them Hollow

The cones are the structural trick and the architectural argument at the same time. Ten of them, up to seven metres across, carry the load down to the ground and leave a cave-like undercroft between them that gets used for outdoor events. They are not solid. Several have been hollowed out and given work to do - one holds the science theatre, another the ideas forum - so visitors climb up through the supports into the exhibition floor. At night the stilts and the underside are lit, and the building appears to float above its own shadow. A metal bridge with escalators and stairs at either end crosses to the Autostadt on the far side of the Mittelland Canal, which means foot traffic between the two attractions passes through Phaeno's legs without ever going inside it.

## Hadid's Argument About Minds

Preliminary planning began in November 1998. An architectural competition followed in January 2000, and Hadid won it, working with the structural engineers Adams Kara Taylor and, in project partnership, the Lörrach practice Mayer Bährle. Inside, nothing repeats. Walls slope. The kinked steel girder roof is left exposed overhead. Long cursive viewing slits cut across solid exposed concrete, and niches lean at angles that make the floor read differently depending on which way you happen to be facing. Hadid was explicit that this was not decoration: the building's restlessness was meant to encourage mental openness and mobility in the people moving through it. Whether that lands on a Tuesday morning full of school groups is arguable, but the effect is real. Perspectives change with every step, and you never quite settle.

## Three Hundred and Fifty Ways to Get It Wrong

The exhibition covers roughly 9,000 square metres and offers more than 350 interactive stations, about forty of them made by artists. They fall under six headings - Life, Vision, Energy, Dynamics, Sense and Maths - but there is deliberately no prescribed route. The educational premise is that playing with a phenomenon teaches it better than being told about it, so the arrangements are simple enough to invite tinkering and stubborn enough to require thought. Panels in German and English explain what is happening, usually after you have already got it wrong a few times. The science theatre runs live demonstrations built around single subjects - laser, air, mechanics, water, fire - alongside talks and science slams.

## The Quiet Floor

The children's area occupies three levels and is open to visitors from around the age of three. The upper two are for exploring and setting things off, which accounts for most of the noise audible anywhere in the building. The lowest level is the opposite: a calm room where the materials are corks and clothes pegs and other ordinary objects, and the object is to make something rather than trigger something. It is a small piece of design wisdom tucked inside a building famous for grand gestures. Around 228,000 people came through in 2022 - roughly 38 per cent families, 35 per cent groups and individuals, 27 per cent schools and nurseries - and about nine in ten had travelled less than 150 kilometres. Phaeno is a recognised extracurricular place of learning, which in practice means a great many buses.

## A Building That Plays Other Buildings

Film crews found it quickly. In The International (2009) it stands in for the headquarters of an Italian arms company, digitally relocated to a cliff above Lake Iseo. The 2013 German disaster film Helden - Wenn dein Land dich braucht casts it as a Large Hadron Collider, which is barely a stretch. It appears again in the Belgian-Dutch dystopian series Arcadia in 2023. The pattern holds: directors reach for Phaeno when they need somewhere that looks like the near future and looks like nowhere in particular. That was more or less the commission. One critic called it a hypnotic work of architecture, the kind of building that utterly transforms our vision of the future, and the Financialist later put it on a list of seven wonders of the modern world.

## Flight Context

Phaeno sits at 52.429 degrees N, 10.790 degrees E, on the south bank of the Mittelland Canal in Wolfsburg, Lower Saxony, directly across the water from the Autostadt and its two 60-metre glass car towers. From the air the canal is the navigation line - dead straight, running east to west through the city - with the vast grey roofs of the Volkswagen plant and its four tall power station chimneys on the north side. Phaeno reads as an angular pale mass on the south bank, distinct from everything around it because it casts a shadow underneath itself. Nearest airports: EDVE (Braunschweig-Wolfsburg) about 20 km southwest, EDDV (Hannover) about 75 km west, EDDB (Berlin Brandenburg) about 185 km east. Recommended viewing altitude 2,000 to 4,000 feet. Overcast northern German skies flatten the building badly; low sun is what brings the folds out.
