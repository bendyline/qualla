# Gottorf Castle

An island seat on the Schlei that grew from a bishop's manor into the residence of the Holstein-Gottorp dukes - a line that would supply Swedish kings and Russian emperors - and now houses two of Schleswig-Holstein's state museums.

**Location:** 54.5117, 9.5414

**Categories:** Castles, Museums, History, Germany, Schleswig-Holstein

## Introduction

In 1544 the duchies of Schleswig and Holstein were cut into three. Adolf, third son of King Frederick I, took one of the pieces and made his residence on a small island in the Schlei, and the state he founded took its name from the house he lived in: Schleswig-Holstein-Gottorp. It was not a large inheritance. Within two centuries the Gottorp line had put four kings on the throne of Sweden and put its blood into the Russian imperial family, and the dukes themselves had been driven out of the castle that gave them the name. Gottorf today is a museum, and one of the most important secular buildings in northern Germany.

## Eight Hundred Years of Landlords

The island had been a residence since 1161, when Bishop Occo of Schleswig moved onto it after his own house was destroyed - though the oldest surviving structures date from the 13th century. After that it changed hands the way border property does. The Danish Duke of Schleswig bought it in 1268. In 1340 it went to the Schauenburg Count of Holstein at Rendsburg. In 1459, by maternal inheritance, it passed to Christian I of Denmark, the first Danish king of the House of Oldenburg. Each owner added to it, and the additions never quite agreed with one another: a medieval castle became a Renaissance fortress, and the Renaissance fortress became a Baroque palace, without any of them entirely erasing what came before. It sits on the Schlei, the long narrow inlet that runs some 40 km inland from the Baltic.

## First Renaissance North of the Elbe

Frederick I, Christian's younger son, made Gottorf his principal residence and around 1530 had the west wing rebuilt in the Nordic early Renaissance manner. It was the first Renaissance building anywhere north of the Elbe - an architectural frontier crossed in a provincial duchy rather than a capital. The castle chapel followed in 1590, and in 1612 a private prayer box was fitted above the altar so that the ducal family could hear a service without being in the congregation. The chapel is one of the rooms where the building's layered centuries are easiest to read at a glance.

## Frederick III's Terraces

The court of Frederick III, who ruled from 1616 to 1659, turned Gottorf into a European cultural centre. Its most visible monument is the Neuwerkgarten, laid out from 1637 in the manner of Roman terrace gardens - a hillside stepped and levelled south of the castle, running down through terraces to a pond, with a figure of Hercules at its focus. The garden went the way of the dynasty. Its reconstruction did not begin until 1984, and the Hercules group was not put back until 1997, three and a half centuries after the duke first stood on his terrace and looked down the axis he had made.

## The Wing That Finished Nothing

In 1697 the Swedish architect Nicodemus Tessin the Younger began remodelling and enlarging the castle in the Baroque style. Only the vast south wing was finished. Duke Frederick IV was killed in battle before the rest could be built, and the great scheme stopped where it stood - which is why the courtyard still shows a Baroque facade on one side and older work on the others. The ducal line was forced out in 1702, and after Sweden's defeat in 1713 the Gottorp dukes were stripped of power by the Danish crown. Under Frederick IV of Denmark the palace slid into neglect. Furniture, paintings and fittings were carried off piece by piece, and in the 19th century the shell served as barracks, first Danish and then Prussian. After the war of 1848 the Danes ran a military hospital in it before converting it back to barracks.

## The Museum Island

In the last months of the war, in early 1945, Gottorf became a reception camp for people displaced from the eastern territories of the Reich, and it continued to house the displaced afterwards. Restoration began in 1947. From 1948 the buildings were handed to the state museums of Schleswig-Holstein, and the long repair of eight centuries of alteration was not considered finished until 1996. The castle now belongs to a foundation of the state and holds two collections: the State Museum of Art and Cultural History, and the State Archaeological Museum, whose holdings include the Iron Age bog finds from the Thorsberg moor a short distance north. The dukes who left in 1702 would recognise the building. They would not recognise what it is for.

## Flight Context

54.51 N, 9.54 E, on an island at the western head of the Schlei on the southern edge of the town of Schleswig. The Schlei itself is the navigational feature - a narrow, branching inlet reaching roughly 40 km inland from the Baltic at Schleimünde - and Gottorf sits where it finally narrows to nothing. From 1,500-3,000 ft the layout reads clearly: the long ochre south wing facing the water, the courtyard behind it, and the stepped Neuwerkgarten terraces on the rising ground to the south. The Danevirke earthworks and the Viking site of Haithabu lie just to the southeast across the water. Schleswig-Jagel (ETNS) is 12 km southwest, Flensburg-Schäferhaus (EDXF) 35 km north, Kiel-Holtenau (EDHK) 45 km southeast. Coastal haze is common; low cloud frequently caps the inlet in autumn and winter.
