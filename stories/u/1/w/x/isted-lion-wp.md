# Isted Lion

A Danish victory monument that stood in Flensburg for twenty months, spent 147 years being carried between Berlin and Copenhagen, and came home in 2011.

**Location:** 54.7853, 9.4295

**Categories:** Buildings and structures in Flensburg, History of Berlin, 1862 in Denmark, 1862 sculptures, First Schleswig War memorials in Denmark, Sculptures by Herman Wilhelm Bissen, Monuments and memorials in Germany, Danish military memorials and cemeteries, Sculptures of lions, Outdoor sculptures in Germany

## Introduction

Most monuments mean whatever they were carved to mean. The Isted Lion means where it happens to be standing. Herman Wilhelm Bissen's bronze lion has been unveiled in triumph, toppled and had its tail broken off, shipped to Berlin, installed in an arsenal, moved to a Prussian cadet school, copied with its meaning deliberately inverted, handed back to Denmark by the United States Army, received by a king, restored, unveiled a second time in Copenhagen, and finally — in September 2011 — set back down on the exact patch of Flensburg cemetery it left in 1864. No other statue in northern Europe has had its address changed so often, or by so many governments who understood precisely what they were saying by moving it.

## A Lion Built from Two Coats of Arms

On 25 July 1850, Danish forces met Schleswig-Holstein insurgents at Isted, south of Flensburg, in what remains the largest battle ever fought in Scandinavia. The First Schleswig War was in substance a civil war inside the Danish realm, though Prussian troops had backed the insurgents. Denmark won. Bissen had already made *Landsoldaten*, the Foot Soldier, unveiled at Fredericia in 1858 to honour the ordinary conscript rather than any general. At the banquet afterwards, subscribers began raising money for a second monument; some wanted a statue of General Bülow, who had commanded at Fredericia during the siege. The politician Orla Lehmann steered the money toward Isted instead, and the commission went to Bissen again. He chose a lion, drawn from the heraldry of both Denmark and Schleswig — three lions in one coat of arms, two in the other. To get the animal right he went to Paris and studied a live lion in the Jardin des Plantes, then built a full-size model before casting.

## Twenty Months in Flensburg

The lion was unveiled on 25 July 1862, twelve years to the day after the battle, in Flensburg's old cemetery among the graves of the men it commemorated. Four portrait medallions were set into the granite base: the generals Schleppegrell, Helgesen and Krogh, and Colonel Læssøe. Danes read it as victory; many also read it, more quietly, as a memorial to their dead. German-minded Schleswig-Holsteiners read it as an occupier's boast planted in their churchyard. It stood for twenty months. On 28 February 1864, during the Second Schleswig War, it was pulled off its plinth by Schleswig-Holsteiners and badly damaged — the lion lost its tail.

## Berlin Takes the Trophy

Prussia won that war and took Schleswig, and in 1867 the lion went to Berlin. On 9 February 1868 it was installed in the Zeughaus, the old arsenal on Unter den Linden, among captured guns and colours: a war trophy, displayed as one. In April 1878 it moved again, out to the cadet academy at Berlin-Lichterfelde where Prussian officers were trained. A zinc copy made in 1874 completed the inversion. The four medallions of the Danish commanders were stripped off and replaced with a single portrait of Prince Friedrich Karl of Prussia — the same monument, the same animal, now saluting the other side. Denmark never stopped noticing. "The removal of this sepulchral monument, which in this country is considered a national sanctuary, and its erection in a German military academy," the Danish foreign minister John Christmas Møller said, "caused a resentment which till this very day is still alive in wide circles of the Danish people."

## Copenhagen, October 1945

The lion outlasted the Kaiser, the Weimar Republic and the Third Reich in the grounds of a military school. When the war ended, the United States Army recovered it and gave it back — formally, as a gift. It reached Copenhagen on 20 October 1945, where King Christian X received it in the courtyard of the Tøjhusmuseet, the royal arsenal museum. There it sat for half a century, an awkward guest: a monument about a border, kept far from the border.

## Two Cities Want It Back

By the 1990s the lion had become an argument. Fredericia, home to Bissen's Foot Soldier, campaigned to bring it there and complete the pair. Others thought it belonged where it had been made to stand. In 2000 it was restored and moved to Søren Kierkegaards Plads in Copenhagen, given a substantial brick plinth in place of the old wooden one and reunited with its four officers' reliefs for the first time in well over a century. The unveiling was staged on 25 July 2000, the 150th anniversary of the battle. The Danish culture minister Elsebeth Gerner Nielsen used her speech to say what the ceremony was quietly about: she hoped the lion would go back to Flensburg.

## Home, and the Meaning Changes Again

Eleven years later it did — and the invitation came from the German side. Flensburg's city council asked for it, and the Danish government agreed. On 10 September 2011 the Isted Lion was set back in the old cemetery where it had first been unveiled in 1862, with Prince Joachim of Denmark attending. Nothing about the bronze had changed. Everything about the reading had. A monument raised to mark one nation's victory, seized as a trophy and re-carved to celebrate the victors, was returned by Danes to Germans at German request — and now stands in a border region where each country's minority lives on the other's side. The lion is the same lion. It just finally arrived somewhere that could hold it.

## Flight Context

The Isted Lion stands at 54.7853°N, 9.4295°E in Flensburg's Alter Friedhof (Old Cemetery), a short distance southwest of the head of the Flensburg Firth and only a few hundred metres from the Flensburger Brauerei at Munketoft. From the air the cemetery reads as a dark green rectangle of mature trees inside the city grid; the firth itself, cutting northeast toward the Baltic with the Danish border on its far shore, is the primary navigation feature. Flensburg-Schäferhaus (EDXF) is immediately west of town, Sønderborg (EKSB) lies north in Denmark, and Schleswig-Jagel (ETNS) south. The statue is small — plan on 1,000–1,500 ft and good visibility to make out the cemetery, and expect the usual Baltic-coast low cloud.
