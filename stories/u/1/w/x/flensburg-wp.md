# Flensburg

Germany's far-northern border city: built on herring and rum, half-Danish by inclination, briefly the capital of the Third Reich, and permanently famous as the place that keeps the country's traffic points.

**Location:** 54.7819, 9.4367

**Categories:** cities, border towns, maritime history, world war ii, denmark, germany, schleswig-holstein

## Introduction

Ask a German driver what Flensburg means and you will not hear about the harbour. You will hear about points. Since 2 January 1958 the federal motor transport authority has kept the national register of traffic offences here — the Verkehrszentralregister, renamed the Fahreignungsregister in 2014, and known to everyone as the Verkehrssünderkartei, the traffic sinners' file. One to three points per offence; at eight, the licence goes. Around 10.1 million people were on it at the start of 2017. A Baltic town of 96,326 people, seven kilometres from the Danish border, has become a synonym for having been caught.

## Herring Money

Danish settlers founded Flensburg at the innermost tip of the Flensburg Firth by 1200 at the latest, and German merchants followed. King Erik Glipping confirmed its town rights in 1284. Because Schleswig lay outside the Holy Roman Empire, Flensburg never joined the Hanseatic League, though it traded with the Hanse constantly. What made it rich first was herring — kippered, barrelled and shipped inland and to nearly every country in Europe. The town that money built was not a comfortable one. The main streets were neither paved nor lit; when the muck became impassable citizens laid down wooden walkways over it. Every household kept livestock in the yard. Plague and dysentery arrived at intervals, and lepers were isolated at the St Jürgen hospital well beyond the gates. On 28 October 1412, Queen Margaret I of Denmark died aboard a ship in this harbour after a sudden illness — plague is the most-cited theory, though historians have never settled on a cause.

## The Rum Houses

After the Hanseatic decline, Flensburg's merchants ranged to the Mediterranean, to Greenland for whale oil, and to the Caribbean. That last route made the eighteenth century. Cane sugar came in from the Danish West Indies — today the US Virgin Islands — grown and cut by enslaved Africans on the plantations there, and Flensburg refined it, distilled it, and sent rum back out along the same sea lanes. When the town passed to Prussia in 1864 the supply shifted to British Jamaica: imported, blended in Flensburg cellars, sold across Europe. There were once well over twenty rum houses in the town. One distillery, A. H. Johannsen, is still working. The rest survives as a museum, a regatta each spring, and the merchants' warehouses that still line the Holm and the Norderstraße.

## The Border That Moved

Between 1460 and 1864 Flensburg was Denmark's second port after Copenhagen. The Second Schleswig War ended that. The town went to Prussia, and the slow business of changing its language began. There was still a Danish majority at the North German Reichstag election of 1867, and it held until around 1880, after which incoming workers from elsewhere in Germany and officials brought up from the south tipped the balance. When the League of Nations settled the frontier by plebiscite in 1920, Flensburg voted decisively to remain German, and some of its northern neighbourhoods went to Denmark. In return the city was given a hall, the Deutsches Haus, endowed as thanks for German loyalty. The line drawn that year is the one still there.

## Three Weeks of the Reich

Flensburg came through the Second World War almost untouched by bombing, though twenty children died when a nursery school was hit in 1943, and an explosion at a munitions store shortly after the war ended killed many more. Then, at the beginning of May 1945, the last German government arrived. Hitler had named Grand Admiral Karl Dönitz his successor and shot himself; Dönitz and what remained of the Reich leadership set up at the Naval Academy at Mürwik. From the Flensburg transmitter, on 8 May, Germany's surrender was announced to the country. The government went on issuing orders to nobody for another fortnight, until British troops walked into Mürwik on 23 May and arrested the lot of them; the Berlin Declaration of 5 June made the dissolution formal. For those few weeks the capital of the Third Reich was a Baltic port of shipyards and rum warehouses.

## Two Languages, One Firth

Flensburg is the organisational centre of the Danish minority of South Schleswig, and the arrangement is remarkably undramatic. There are Danish schools, Danish libraries, Danish Lutheran congregations, a Danish consulate-general, and a bilingual daily, Flensborg Avis. Flensborghus, a former orphanage on the Norderstraße, houses the minority's institutions; the Duborg-Skolen is its Gymnasium. The SSW, the minority's party, regularly takes a fifth to a quarter of the local vote, and in 2010 Simon Faber became the first Lord Mayor from the Danish minority since the war. Traffic runs the other way too: Danes cross for the border shops, where lower VAT and duty make drink and sweets cheap. On the ferries you can still hear Petuh, a spliced Danish-German patois belonging nowhere else. Above the old town stands the Nordertor, the gate that every postcard uses; below it lies the firth, where the saloon steamer Alexandra, built in 1908, still takes passengers out on the water.

## Flight Context

54.7819° N, 9.4367° E, at the head of the Flensburg Firth in the far north of Schleswig-Holstein, with the Danish border about seven kilometres away. The firth itself is the navigation feature — a long, narrow Baltic inlet running northeast, with the Angeln peninsula on its southern shore and Denmark on the northern. The old town and harbour sit at the very end of the water; the seventeen-storey town hall of 1964 is the tallest thing in the skyline, and the Naval Academy at Mürwik stands on the shore to the northeast. Best at 1,500–3,000 ft AGL. Nearby airfields: Flensburg-Schäferhaus (EDXF) just west of the city, Sønderborg (EKSB) across the border in Denmark, Schleswig-Jagel (ETNS) to the south. Baltic haze, sea fog and low stratus are common; northerly winds bring the clearest visibility.
