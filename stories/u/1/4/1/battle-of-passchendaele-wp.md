# Battle of Passchendaele

A four-month campaign through the mud of Flanders that became a byword for the futility and horror of the First World War.

**Location:** 50.9003, 3.0211

**Categories:** military, world-war-i, battlefield, memorial, belgium

## Introduction

Nineteen mines detonated simultaneously beneath Messines Ridge at 3:10 a.m. on June 7, 1917. The explosion — nearly a million pounds of explosives packed into tunnels dug over two years — was heard in London, 140 miles away. It was supposed to be the beginning of a swift campaign to drive the Germans from the Belgian coast. Instead, it was the overture to four months of fighting through mud so deep it swallowed men whole. The Third Battle of Ypres, which history remembers as Passchendaele, would produce somewhere between 475,000 and 850,000 casualties on both sides — the exact numbers are still disputed a century later, which tells you something about the chaos of what happened here.

## The Ridgeline Nobody Could Take

Passchendaele sits on the last ridge east of Ypres, barely 160 feet above the surrounding plain — a rise so gentle you might not notice it on a clear day. But in trench warfare, even fifty feet of elevation meant the difference between observing and being observed, between firing down and being fired upon. The Germans had held these heights since 1914, and from their positions they could see every movement in the Ypres salient below.

Field Marshal Douglas Haig's plan was ambitious: break through the German lines, capture the ridges, and push on to the Belgian ports of Bruges and Ostend, where German submarines threatened Allied shipping. The attack opened on July 31 with the Battle of Pilckem Ridge, and the initial advance went reasonably well. Then the rain began.

## Drowning on Dry Land

August 1917 brought 127 millimeters of rain to Flanders. That number sounds manageable until you understand the terrain: this was reclaimed marshland, and three years of shelling had destroyed the drainage systems that kept it habitable. The craters filled with water. The soil became a soupy grey-brown porridge that could pull a man under if he stepped off the duckboard tracks.

Artillery pieces sank. Mules drowned in shell holes. The wounded who fell from stretchers disappeared into the mire. Advancing troops moved at a crawl, visible to German machine gunners on the heights above. The overcast, windless weather reduced evaporation, and the ground never dried. Haig's timetable, which had assumed a swift push to the coast, collapsed into a series of grinding, blood-soaked set-piece attacks measured in hundreds of yards.

## October 12: New Zealand's Darkest Day

The First Battle of Passchendaele on October 12 remains one of the worst days in New Zealand military history. Allied troops were exhausted, morale had cratered, and the rain had made it nearly impossible to move artillery forward. The New Zealand Division attacked through bottomless mud toward the village of Passchendaele itself. German counterattacks recovered most of the ground gained. The cost: 2,735 New Zealand casualties in a single day, 845 of them killed or lost in the mud of no-man's-land, never recovered.

It fell to the Canadian Corps, transferred from Lens, to finish the job. In three carefully planned assaults between October 26 and November 6, the Canadians captured what remained of Passchendaele village — by then little more than a smear of brick dust on a churned landscape. The ridge was taken. The Belgian coast was not.

## The Argument That Never Ended

No battle of the First World War has produced more historical acrimony than Passchendaele. The casualty figures alone remain contested. The official British historian, James Edmonds, put British losses at 244,897 and estimated German casualties at 400,000. A. J. P. Taylor called Edmonds' calculations "farcical" and reversed the ratio: 300,000 British to 200,000 German. German medical records show 217,194 casualties from June through November, but that figure excludes soldiers treated at regimental aid posts and returned to duty — men who, by British counting standards, would have been listed as casualties.

Lloyd George later wrote that Passchendaele was "one of the greatest disasters of the war" and that "no soldier of any intelligence now defends this senseless campaign." German staff officers, paradoxically, told a different story: a postwar General Staff publication stated that "Germany had been brought near to certain destruction" by the Flanders battle of 1917. Both assessments contain truth. Both are incomplete.

## What Remains

The Menin Gate in Ypres bears the names of 54,000 Commonwealth soldiers who died in the salient and have no known grave — and it ran out of room. Those who fell after August 16, 1917, are commemorated instead at Tyne Cot Cemetery, the largest Commonwealth war cemetery in the world, where nearly 12,000 men are buried and another 35,000 names line the memorial walls. A New Zealand memorial stands on Gravenstafel Ridge, marking where their troops fought on October 4.

The landscape has healed. The fields around Passendale — as the village is now spelled — are green and flat and prosperous. Farmers still turn up shells and bones with their plows, a harvest the Belgians call the "iron harvest." Every evening at eight o'clock, traffic stops beneath the Menin Gate and buglers from the local fire brigade play the Last Post, as they have done every night since 1928, interrupted only by the German occupation. It is the longest-running daily act of remembrance in the world.

## Flight Context

Located at 50.90°N, 3.02°E in West Flanders, Belgium. The Ypres salient lies south and east of the city of Ypres (Ieper). From the air, Tyne Cot Cemetery is the most visible landmark — a vast field of white headstones on the slope leading to the Passchendaele ridgeline. The Menin Gate is in the center of Ypres itself. The terrain is flat to gently rolling farmland, and the subtle ridgelines that cost so many lives are barely perceptible from altitude. Nearest airports: Kortrijk-Wevelgem (EBKT), 20 nm south; Ostend-Bruges (EBOS), 25 nm northwest. Expect maritime weather — low clouds, rain, and reduced visibility are common, particularly in autumn. Recommended viewing altitude: 2,000-3,000 ft AGL to appreciate how slight the elevation changes are.
