# Great Fire of Hamburg

For three days in May 1842 a cigar-factory fire ate a third of Hamburg's old town — and the reckoning afterwards invented modern reinsurance.

**Location:** 53.5510, 9.9940

**Categories:** History of Hamburg, Disasters, 19th century, Fires, Urban history

## Introduction

At about one in the morning on 5 May 1842, a neighbour on Deichstraße woke the night watch. A cigar factory at number 42 — or 44; the records disagree — had caught fire. Hamburg had a system for exactly this. Lookouts called Türmer kept watch from the church towers, watchmen carried horns, and a code of bells told the city where a fire was burning and how badly. Volunteer companies raced one another to the scene, since the first crew to arrive collected a premium. None of it made any difference. The spring had been unusually dry, the wind was strong and kept shifting, and by daybreak much of the Altstadt was alight.

## A City Built to Burn

Within hours the fire had crossed to number 25 on the far side of the street, and after that it had everything it needed. Hamburg's old town was packed with wooden and half-timbered houses, tall and narrow because the building plots were tall and narrow. Merchants ran their businesses out of their homes, which meant a great many of those houses were also warehouses, and the warehouses held rubber and shellac. Collapsed buildings dropped into the canals and blocked the fireboats. Nobody had built a system for coordinating the firefighting companies of different parishes, so the parishes fought separately. Crews came from as far away as Lübeck and Kiel. The Hamburg–Bergedorf railway had been due to open on 7 May; it was put into service two days early instead, carrying firefighters in and survivors out.

## Ascension Day

5 May was Ascension Day. A few hours after the midday feast service, despite everything the crews could do, the spire of the Nikolaikirche came down and the church burned. Buildings were blown up to make firebreaks, among them the old Rathaus, first built in 1290 — eighteen wagonloads of records were hauled out before the charges went in. Its wine cellar partly collapsed, and a figure of Bacchus salvaged from that rubble still stands in the city hall built on the site later. The militia helped fight the fire until looting got bad enough that it had to police the city instead. The flames burned until the morning of 8 May. Fifty-one people were killed, twenty-two of them firefighters. Around 20,000 were left homeless, four senators among them. Seventeen hundred houses were gone, along with seven churches, two synagogues, sixty schools and the Bank of Hamburg.

## The First News Photographs

The world read about it fast. The first issue of the Illustrated London News carried eye-witness reports and an engraving of the burning city. Hermann Biow, a pioneering photographer, climbed onto the roof of the new Stock Exchange — the only building in the district the fire spared — and made daguerreotypes of the ruins. They are the first photographs of Hamburg, and possibly the first news photographs ever taken. Painted dioramas of the catastrophe toured later. Elise Averdieck came into the city from the suburb of St Georg while it was still burning and wrote down what she saw in the streets; she used the scenes again in Roland und Elisabeth, part of a set of autobiographical tales for children. Almost seven million marks arrived through an international appeal, the largest gifts from Tsar Nicholas I of Russia and King Louis Philippe of France, with civic donations from Antwerp, London, Riga, Rotterdam and St Petersburg. Sixty German writers contributed work to a fundraising Hansa-Album.

## Lindley's Hamburg

A British engineer named William Lindley proposed a plan for the rebuilding, and the city adopted it with modifications — property lines were kept rather than the streets straightened, which is why the Altstadt still bends where it always bent. Lindley also designed a scheme to drain and develop Hammerbrook, and Hammerbrook was built up out of the fire's own rubble. The new city went up quickly, in brick, with a brief fashion for classical white public buildings. Merchants stopped living over their stock: warehouses moved to the docks, families moved to the suburbs, and from the 1880s the firms got purpose-built offices called Kontorhäuser. Lindley pushed through sewers and a piped water supply. What the city would not pay for was sand filtration, so Hamburg drank unfiltered river water, notorious for what was living and dead in it, and that economy came back as the cholera epidemic of 1892. The firefighting was rebuilt too: by 1866 a survey found two fire districts, a high-pressure supply and almost 2,000 hydrants, and by 1870 the city had gas mains and street lighting.

## What the Fire Invented

The losses came to 100 million marks, and they broke the arithmetic of the insurance business. The municipal Hamburger Feuerkasse, membership in which had been compulsory for most residents since 1817, was liable for 38 million — roughly a billion euros in 2006 money. It raised a bond of 34.4 million marks, with the banker Salomon Heine putting up much of the collateral, and spent the next forty-six years paying it off. The private Gothaer Feuer had to ship 250,000 thalers in coin to Hamburg because claimants insisted on being paid in Prussian currency. British underwriters, badly stung, turned their attention increasingly toward the Empire. And out of the wreckage came an idea: that insurers themselves needed insuring. Discussion of the first reinsurance company began later in 1842, and the Kölnische Rückversicherungs-Gesellschaft was chartered in 1846 — ancestor of Gen Re, and of an entire industry. Hamburg itself did not finish replacing its city hall until 1897.

## Flight Context

53.551°N, 9.994°E, over Hamburg's Altstadt. The burned district is the ground between the Binnenalster and the Elbe: at 2,000–3,000 ft AGL you can trace it by the 19th-century brick and the Nikolaikirche ruin, whose blackened tower was left standing as a memorial and is still one of the tallest things in the old town. Hamburg Airport (EDDH) lies 8 km north; Hamburg Finkenwerder (EDHI) sits across the Elbe to the west. Low cloud and haze off the river are common; morning light from the east picks out the Alster and the canal network that the fireboats could not use.
