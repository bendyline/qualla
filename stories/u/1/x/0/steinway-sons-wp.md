# Steinway & Sons

A charcoal burner's son from the Harz built his first piano as a wedding present, emigrated to New York, and thirty years later his family shipped the company back to Hamburg to get around a tariff.

**Location:** 53.5743, 9.9241

**Categories:** Manufacturing, Music history, Hamburg, Pianos, German-American history, Industrial heritage

## Introduction

In 1825, in the small Harz town of Seesen, a joiner named Heinrich Engelhard Steinweg finished a square piano and gave it to his bride Juliane as a wedding present. He was twenty-eight, the son of a charcoal master from Wolfshagen, apprenticed to carpentry at fifteen and trained in instruments at Goslar, and he had already fought against Napoleon with the Schwarze Schar. He built guitars, zithers and mandolins before he built keyboards. His first grand piano came in 1836. By the time he left Germany he had made 482 instruments - and the numbering of those pianos would follow him across an ocean and eventually back again, to a factory in Hamburg that most people assume came first.

## Number 483

Steinweg sailed for New York on 28 May 1850 with his wife and seven of his nine children. The eldest, C. F. Theodor, stayed behind to keep the Steinweg name in business in Germany, partnering with the dealer Friedrich Grotrian from 1856. In March 1853 the father and three sons went into business for themselves as Steinway & Sons, working out of a loft at the back of 85 Varick Street in Manhattan. The first piano they finished was not given serial number 1. It was given 483, because 482 already existed in Germany. That instrument sold to a New York family for $500 and now sits in the Stadtisches Museum in Seesen, back in the town where the run began. The founder anglicised himself to Henry E. Steinway and died in New York on 7 February 1871.

## A Tariff, and a Factory

The Hamburg works exist for a commercial reason, not a sentimental one. By 1880 there were European customers who wanted Steinway pianos and European import duties that made shipping them from Queens expensive. William Steinway and C. F. Theodore solved the problem by building inside the tariff wall, in the free city of Hamburg, at an address on Schanzenstrasse in the western part of the city. C. F. Theodore took charge of the German factory - his close associate Arthur von Holwede served as its first director - while William returned to New York. Theodore was the family's most productive inventor: roughly a third of the company's patented improvements carry his name. He died in Braunschweig in 1889, having spent his last decade competing against the Steinweg brand he had left behind, which by then was Grotrian-Steinweg.

## Part Laboratory, Part Factory

Hamburg was never simply an overseas assembly line. The two factories traded techniques and patents constantly across the Atlantic and still do, and for years much of the precision cutting and drilling machinery destined for New York was tried out in Hamburg first - the German plant functioned as the company's testbed. The operation outgrew Schanzenstrasse and opened an expanded facility in Bahrenfeld, west of the city centre, in 1928. That is where Steinway still builds pianos today. The two houses also settled into a permanent division of the world: New York supplies the Americas, Hamburg supplies everywhere else.

## The Wood They Took Away

The Second World War nearly ended the Hamburg factory. Sales collapsed to no more than a hundred instruments a year, and in the later years of the war the company was ordered to surrender the seasoned, dried timber stacked in its lumber yard for war production - for a piano maker, the loss of decades of patience. Then Allied bombs hit the buildings and came close to destroying them outright. Across the Atlantic the New York factory spent the same years building wooden troop-carrying gliders and 2,436 Victory Verticals: small olive-drab uprights light enough for four men to lift, built to be shipped or parachuted to soldiers. Hamburg rebuilt with Marshall Plan help and had pianos moving again by 1948. By the 1960s it was producing well over a thousand grands a year.

## Two Rooms, One Name

Pianists argue about which factory makes the better instrument, and they have been arguing for a century. Some prefer the Hamburg sound, some New York; the reviewer Larry Fine has pointed out that the differences owe more to individual instruments than to which side of the Atlantic they came from. Enough people care that Carnegie Hall keeps model D-274 concert grands from both factories on hand. The name itself has been defended in court: Steinway's countersuit against Grotrian-Steinweg, won on appeal in 1975, restricted the German rival to the name Grotrian in the United States and established the doctrine of initial interest confusion in American trademark law. Two brothers, two factories, two countries, one surname - and a dispute that outlived every one of them.

## Flight Context

53.5743 N, 9.9241 E, in Bahrenfeld in western Hamburg, roughly 3 nm west-northwest of the inner city. The site is an industrial block rather than a landmark - orient instead on the Altona rail yards to the southeast and the green expanse of Volkspark and the Volksparkstadion to the northwest, with the Elbe about 1.5 nm to the south. Useful from 1,500 to 3,000 ft AGL on a westbound run along the river. Hamburg Airport (EDDH) is about 6 nm northeast, Hamburg Finkenwerder (EDHI) about 3 nm south-southwest across the Elbe. Bahrenfeld sits under the Hamburg terminal area, so expect controlled airspace and traffic on the EDDH approach.
