# St. Nicholas Church, Hamburg

For two years it was the tallest building on Earth; today the scorched tower stands alone above an empty floor, kept as a memorial to the victims of war and tyranny.

**Location:** 53.5475, 9.9906

**Categories:** Memorials, Churches, Hamburg, World War II sites, Gothic Revival architecture, Ruins

## Introduction

The tower has nothing to hold up. It rises 147.3 metres out of a rectangle of open ground in the middle of Hamburg, blackened at the edges, its Gothic tracery ending in air where the nave used to begin. Everything that made this a church was cleared away in 1951, deliberately, and what was left was left on purpose. Between 1874 and 1876, this spire was the tallest built structure in the world. It is now the second-tallest thing in Hamburg after the television tower, and it is not a church at all. It is a memorial to the victims of war and tyranny between 1933 and 1945.

## A Chapel for Sailors

Nicholas is the patron saint of sailors, which is why a settlement growing around a harbour on the Alster in the twelfth century dedicated its chapel to him. That first building, finished in 1195, was wood, and it was only the second church in Hamburg after St. Mary's Cathedral. In 1335, a few years before the Black Death reached northern Europe, work began on a three-naved brick hall church in the North German Gothic that the Hanseatic cities made their own. The nave lasted five hundred years. The towers did not. One went up in 1517 and burned down in 1589; its replacement collapsed in 1644. Peter Marquardt's third attempt, 122 metres and domed, finally held, and became the jewel of the skyline.

## The Englishman's Spire

On 5 May 1842, the midday service had to be cut short with a prayer for the building's survival. St. Nicholas was the first large public building to catch in the Great Fire of Hamburg, and its burning told the city how bad the following days would be; the tower went up around four in the afternoon, and almost none of its art was saved. A shilling collection began in 1843, and a competition the next year was won by Gottfried Semper, from neighbouring Altona, with a domed Romanesque design. It was never built - too foreign for the skyline, the city decided. Cologne Cathedral's resumed construction had made Gothic the national style, so Hamburg turned to the English architect George Gilbert Scott. He gave them an 86-metre nave under a 28-metre vault, French and English in its bones with a pointed German spire. Construction started in 1846, the church was consecrated on 27 September 1863, and the tower was finished in 1874. For two years, until Rouen Cathedral overtook it in 1876, nothing built by human beings stood higher.

## Visible From Above

That was precisely the problem in the summer of 1943. A spire that could be seen from any direction made an excellent orientation point, and Allied aircrews used it as one. On 28 July 1943 the church took heavy bomb damage: the roof came down, the interior of the nave was gutted, cracks opened in the walls. Neither the walls nor the tower fell. After the war, engineers judged the Gothic structure sound enough to restore, and the city decided against it. The surrounding streets were no longer residential, a replacement Hauptkirche was built out in Harvestehude, and against the Michel this church had never quite ranked among Hamburg's essential landmarks. In 1951 the nave was demolished. Its rubble went into reinforcing the banks of the Elbe.

## What Was Kept On Purpose

The ruin was put under monument protection in 1960 and formally made a memorial in 1971, then left largely to weather for years. In December 1987, an association formed to rescue it - Rettet die Nikolaikirche, later the Forderkreis Mahnmal St. Nikolai - and began stabilising the fabric and fishing fragments of the destroyed nave out of the Elbe, where some were recovered in November 2000. Nobody intends to rebuild it as Dresden rebuilt the Frauenkirche. Instead the site accumulated other kinds of answer. A carillon of 51 bells, thirteen tonnes of bronze cast by the Dutch foundry Eijsbouts, was installed in July 1993; the same year the memorial joined the Coventry Cross of Nails community, and a cross of nails from that bombed English cathedral now hangs in the tower hall.

## Prufung, and the Crypt

In the apse of a vanished side chapel sits Edith Breckwoldt's sculpture Prufung, placed in 2004 and dedicated to the prisoners of the Sandbostel camp sixty kilometres west of the city, with words attributed to Dietrich Bonhoeffer cut beside it: no one on Earth can change the truth. Down in the crypt, the exhibition Gomorrha 1943 opened in September 2013 across 450 square metres, taking its name from the operation that destroyed the city around this church. Since 1 September 2005 a glass lift has carried visitors up the inside of the tower to a platform at about 75 metres, where documentation panels sit against a panoramic view of the Speicherstadt and the harbour. You look out over a rebuilt city from inside the one thing that was not rebuilt.

## Flight Context

53.5475 N, 9.9906 E, in Hamburg-Mitte a short distance northwest of the Speicherstadt. The isolated 147.3-metre spire with no nave beneath it is unmistakable from the air and sits roughly 800 m east of St. Michael's, giving an easy two-spire fix on the inner city. Best seen from 2,000 to 4,000 ft AGL, where the empty footprint of the demolished nave reads clearly as a gap in the block.
