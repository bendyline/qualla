# Altona Bloody Sunday

On 17 July 1932 an SA march through working-class Altona ended with eighteen dead - sixteen of them residents shot by police - and gave Franz von Papen his pretext to destroy the government of Prussia three days later.

**Location:** 53.5517, 9.9530

**Categories:** History, Weimar Republic, Germany, Hamburg, Political Violence

## Introduction

At seven in the evening the Altona police reported that "calm was restored." The phrase went into the file and stayed there for sixty years. Behind it lay a Sunday afternoon in which eighteen people were killed in a few narrow streets near the Altona railway station - sixteen of them residents who had no part in the fighting and who died from police bullets. The police wrote the day up as a Communist ambush from the rooftops. No sniper was ever produced, no weapon ever found, and not one policeman was so much as injured. Three days later the national government used the massacre to justify deposing the government of Prussia; a year after that, the courts of the new regime used it to kill four more men.

## Red Altona

Altona in 1932 was not yet part of Hamburg. It belonged to the Prussian province of Schleswig-Holstein - a working-class town of shipyard hands and dockers wedged against its larger neighbour, voting Communist and Social Democrat in numbers that earned it the names Red Altona and Little Moscow. That summer the streets of Germany had turned lethal. On 16 June the Papen government lifted the ban on the SS and SA that Heinrich Brüning had imposed in April - a favour to the Nazi Party for tolerating Papen's minority cabinet. Within a month ninety-nine people were dead and 1,125 injured across the country, and in Schleswig-Holstein alone Nazis had killed two Social Democrats and two Communists in the first days of July. Into that, Altona's Social Democratic police chief Otto Eggerstedt authorised a recruitment march: seven thousand uniformed SA men from across the province, routed through the tangled old town where the Communists lived. Eggerstedt was not in Altona that day. Neither was his deputy.

## Ninety Minutes

The marchers assembled at half past twelve between the station and the city hall. At three the column set off towards Ottensen and Bahrenfeld, reaching the old town by half past four. Just before five, near what is now Walter-Möller-Park, people in the crowd threw objects at the procession. Two SA units broke ranks into Große Johannisstraße and began beating people. Police could not separate the two sides even after reinforcements arrived from Hamburg. As the SA re-formed, shots were fired and two of them fell dead. From that moment the police fought an enemy that existed mainly in their own reports: convinced they were being shot at from roofs and upper windows, they cleared the streets and fired at buildings. The SA had already been pushed back towards the station. At 5:40 the house searches began and about ninety people were arrested. More shooting came at 6:45. By seven it was over, and sixteen residents of the quarter were dead.

## Three Days Later

On 20 July, Reich Chancellor Franz von Papen cited Altona as proof that Prussia could no longer keep order and carried out the Preußenschlag, the coup that removed the caretaker Prussian government and suspended the democratic constitution of Germany's largest state. The last serious institutional obstacle to a Nazi takeover was gone six months before Hitler became chancellor. Not everyone accepted the official account. Altona's Lutheran pastors answered with a declaration drafted largely by Hans Asmussen - the Message and Confession of Altona Pastors in the Distress and Confusion of Public Life - read from the town's pulpits on 11 January 1933. It insisted the church enter no alliance in the political struggle, and is now read as a precursor of the Barmen Declaration of 1934.

## A Courtyard Behind the Court

The investigations that followed were directed exclusively at Communists and produced almost nothing. After the Nazi seizure of power the state reopened the case as the Bloody Sunday trials, built on those one-sided investigations and on evidence, expert opinion and testimony that was in part falsified. Johannes Block chaired the special court. Four men - August Lütgens, Walter Möller, Karl Wolff and Bruno Tesch - were condemned to death, and on 1 August 1933 they were beheaded with a hand axe in the courtyard of the building that is now the Altona District Court. They were the first politically motivated executions of the Third Reich. Eleven other defendants went to prison. Emil Wendt drew ten years; after serving his sentence he was moved to Waldheim prison in Saxony and murdered there on 26 October 1944. Six trials in all grew out of that Sunday, running to 1935.

## The File Reopened

The trial records sat in the court archive at Schleswig until the French resistance veteran Léon Schirmann worked through them in 1992. What he established was almost entirely negative, and devastating for that reason. Nobody had fired from a roof or an upper floor, no Communist gunman had been arrested, no weapon had turned up in any searched flat, and no policeman had been killed or hurt. There were no snipers among the dead; all sixteen were Altona residents uninvolved in the demonstration, and all sixteen had been shot by police. In November 1992 the Hamburg Regional Court acknowledged that evidence had been falsified, overturned the four death sentences, and recognised Lütgens, Möller, Wolff and Tesch as victims of the Nazi regime. No one could say by then which officers had fired, or who had killed the two SA men at the start of it. Three later verdicts still stand. A memorial stone in the park and a plaque behind the district court mark the two ends of the story.

## Flight Context

53.55 N, 9.95 E, in the Altona district on the north bank of the Elbe, roughly 3 km west of Hamburg's centre. From 2,000-4,000 ft the landmarks are unmistakable: the Elbe swinging west past the container terminals, the long Altona waterfront and Fischmarkt, and the Altona railway station just inland. The old-town streets where the shooting happened were largely destroyed in 1943 and rebuilt, so the ground plan visible today is postwar. Hamburg Airport (EDDH) lies about 12 km north-northeast; Hamburg-Finkenwerder (EDHI) sits directly across the river to the southwest. The maritime climate brings frequent low stratus and haze, with the clearest light in late spring and early autumn.
