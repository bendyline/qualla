# Chilehaus

Fritz Hoger's 1924 brick office block in Hamburg, built on saltpetre money, whose knife-edge corner turns four point eight million clinker bricks into a ship's prow.

**Location:** 53.5482, 10.0016

**Categories:** Architecture, UNESCO World Heritage Sites, Hamburg, Expressionism, Office buildings

## Introduction

Stand at the corner of Pumpenstrasse and Niedernstrasse and the building comes at you. The two brick facades converge at an angle so acute that the Chilehaus stops behaving like an office block and starts behaving like a ship - a dark clinker prow driving east through the Hamburg street grid, upper storeys stepped back like decks, the whole mass apparently under way. Fritz Hoger finished it in 1924, and a century later nobody has found a better description than the one every visitor arrives at independently within about four seconds.

## Saltpetre Money

The man who paid for it, Henry Brarens Sloman, made his fortune in Chilean saltpetre - mining it and trading it - and by 1912 he was reckoned to be worth 60 million marks. That is where the name comes from, and Chile is written all over the building for anyone who looks: the country's coat of arms sits above the gates at Entrance A, and a condor, Chile's national bird, is worked into the facade. Construction ran from May 1922 to February 1924, straight through the German hyperinflation, which is why nobody can say with confidence what it cost. The usual estimate is upwards of ten million reichsmark, a figure that meant something quite different at the start of the build than at the end of it.

## Four Point Eight Million Bricks

The Chilehaus occupies a site of roughly 5,950 square metres and packs about 36,000 square metres of floor space onto it across nine to ten storeys, spanning the Fischertwiete street rather than stopping at it. The skin is dark Oldenburg clinker - Bockhorn brick, some 4.8 million of them, deliberately chosen for irregularity. Hoger credited a large part of the building's effect to the roughness of misshapen bricks. Behind that skin is a reinforced concrete frame sunk on piles driven sixteen metres into difficult ground. There are around 2,800 windows, near-identical, and the risk was monotony; Hoger beat it with strong vertical emphasis, a long curved facade along Pumpenstrasse and those setback upper floors, so that a very large building reads as surprisingly light. The sculptor Richard Kuohl supplied the ceramic ornament on the facades and in the stairwells.

## The Water Below

The Elbe is close, and the water table is closer. The cellar had to be specially sealed, and the heating plant was set inside a caisson designed to float within the building if the ground floods - a piece of engineering pragmatism from a city that has always known the river will eventually try the door handles. For decades two sets of paternoster lifts, those doorless chains of open cabins that never stop moving, carried clerks between floors. They went in the 1991 modernisation, which is the sort of loss Hamburg office workers still grumble about.

## Kontorhaus Country

The Chilehaus is not a one-off but the loudest voice in a chorus. It anchors the Kontorhausviertel, the merchant-office quarter of vast brick commercial buildings that Hamburg raised in the 1920s, and in 2015 UNESCO inscribed the district together with the Speicherstadt warehouses as a single World Heritage Site. Brick Expressionism is the label; what it means on the ground is dark, textured, handmade-looking surfaces pushed to sculptural extremes at exactly the moment most of Europe was heading toward white walls and flat glass. The building still works for a living. It belongs to the real-estate company Union Investment Real Estate AG, its offices are let, and the Spanish language school Instituto Cervantes is among its tenants.

## Where to Stand

The best view is from the east, looking back along the converging walls toward the point. Come in low sun and the clinker turns from near-black to a deep liver red, and the shadow lines in the brickwork - the projecting courses, the vertical ribs, Kuohl's ceramics - resolve into the pattern that photographs never quite capture. Walk into the courtyard and the ship dissolves; you are simply inside a very good 1920s office building, with tiled halls and a lot of daylight. Then go back out to the corner, and the prow reassembles itself.

## Flight Context

The Chilehaus stands at 53.5482 N, 10.0016 E in Hamburg-Altstadt, a few hundred metres north-east of the Speicherstadt and roughly a kilometre east of the Rathaus. From above, look for the wedge: a dark, sharply pointed block among the rectangles of the Kontorhausviertel, with the red-brick canal warehouses of the Speicherstadt and the wave-topped Elbphilharmonie just downstream to the south-west. Hamburg Airport (EDDH) is about 9 km north, Hamburg Finkenwerder (EDHI) about 11 km west. Best appreciated at 1,000-2,000 ft AGL on a low-sun morning or evening, when the brickwork's relief throws shadow; overcast North Sea days flatten it to a grey slab.
