# HafenCity

Rather than wall a new district off from the Elbe, Hamburg raised the ground eight metres and let the floods pass underneath.

**Location:** 53.5417, 9.9933

**Categories:** Urban planning, Waterfronts, Modern architecture, Hamburg, Ports

## Introduction

Hamburg decided not to build a dike. Storm surges come up the Elbe, the risk is worsening rather than easing, and the obvious protection for a new quarter built on old harbour ground was a wall — but a wall would have cost a fortune and taken away the one thing the district was selling, which is the water. So the planners raised the ground instead. Roads and public squares in HafenCity sit on artificial sand terraces about eight metres up, more than twenty-five feet above the normal high-tide line. Buildings stay at the original level with their lower floors waterproofed, and every one of them is required to provide a way up to the raised road. When the surge arrives, the district does not keep the water out. It stands above it.

## Where the Pirates Were Hanged

Until the early nineteenth century this was all outside the walls. Hamburg's fortifications ran along what is now Am Sandtorkai; beyond them the Elbe island of Grasbrook was boggy meadow, and its western end was where the city carried out executions — the pirate Klaus Störtebeker and the Victual Brothers were put to death here. Shipyards and port businesses worked the island through the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries. In 1844 Hamburg's first gasworks went up on the northern end. The residential quarters of Kehrwieder and Wandrahm, inside the walls, occupied ground that is now part of HafenCity.

## The Port Takes the Island

By the end of the nineteenth century the Binnenhafen and Niederhafen were full. The walls came down and the harbour expanded onto Grasbrook. The first artificial basin opened at Sandtorhafen in 1868, the Grasbrookhafen followed in 1881, and then came the Magdeburger, Brooktor and Baakenhafen basins, the last with a rail link to the Elbbrücken. By 1886 the whole island was docks and installations. Part of the price was paid by people who lived there: Kehrwieder and Wandrahm were demolished in 1883 to clear ground for the customs-free zone, putting some 20,000 residents out of their homes, and the Speicherstadt warehouses rose on top of those neighbourhoods from 1888. Kaispeicher B had gone up in 1878 and the old port authority building in 1885; Fritz Schumacher designed a refrigerated herring warehouse here in 1928. Since 2015 the Speicherstadt has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site together with the Kontorhausviertel.

## Undoing the Free Port

What finished the free port was ordinary progress. European Union free trade made customs zones largely pointless, container ships wanted deeper water further downstream, and border security tightened. On 1 January 2003 the area was released from the duty-free zone so that people could live in it. Ground had already been broken, on 20 June 2001; the first quarter, Am Dalmannkai/Sandtorkai, was completed in 2009; and on 1 March 2008 HafenCity was constituted as a district in its own right. At roughly 2.2 square kilometres it is the largest urban redevelopment project in Europe by area. The U4 underground line reached it in 2012 with three new stations — Überseequartier, HafenCity Universität and Elbbrücken.

## The Concert Hall on the Warehouse

The landmark is the Elbphilharmonie, and it is essentially a warehouse with a building landed on top. Herzog & de Meuron kept the brick bulk of Kaispeicher A, built in 1963 to Werner Kallmorgen's design, and set a rippling glass superstructure above it, reaching 108 metres. A public plaza occupies the join, on the eighth floor where brick becomes glass; the Grand Hall seats about 2,100 in a vineyard arrangement wrapped around the orchestra. It opened on 11 January 2017, six years behind schedule, at a final cost of €866 million against an early estimate of around €241 million. Hamburg spent a decade making jokes about it and then filled it.

## Counting the Residents

Projections for the completed district have run from about 12,000 residents up to 16,000, with somewhere between 40,000 and 45,000 jobs. At the end of 2024, 8,062 people actually lived there. Kühne + Nagel, Unilever and Der Spiegel have headquarters in the quarter, Greenpeace arrived in 2011, cruise ships berth at Strandkai, and the district supports a primary school and a university devoted to architecture and urban planning. It is also still a building site, and that carries a cost the brochures leave out. More than a thousand people have worked the construction here since 2016, many employed through layers of subcontractors, many of them from southeastern and eastern Europe. On 30 October 2023, scaffolding running up eight floors collapsed in the Überseequartier, and five of those workers were killed.

## Flight Context

53.5417°N, 9.9933°E, on the north bank of the Norderelbe immediately south of Hamburg's Altstadt. From 2,000–3,000 ft the quarter is unmistakable: the wave-crested glass roof of the Elbphilharmonie at the western tip, the red-brick grid of the Speicherstadt behind it, and a comb of finger basins — Sandtorhafen, Grasbrookhafen, Baakenhafen — running east. Hamburg Airport (EDDH) is 10 km north; Hamburg Finkenwerder (EDHI) is 7 km west along the river. Expect river haze in the mornings and busy low-level helicopter and drone activity over the cruise terminals.
