# Reeperbahn

Hamburg's most notorious street is long and straight for an industrial reason: a Reeperbahn is a ropewalk, and rope for a sailing ship needs a clear run.

**Location:** 53.5494, 9.9623

**Categories:** Streets in Hamburg, Red-light districts in Germany, Prostitution in Germany, Tourist attractions in Hamburg, St. Pauli

## Introduction

The street is long and straight because rope has to be. A Reeperbahn is a ropewalk — Reep is Low German for a heavy ship's cable — and twisting one for a large sailing vessel needed an unobstructed run of some four hundred metres, up to a thousand for the biggest. Hamburg's ropemakers once worked in what is now the Neustadt, but the growing city built over them, and from 1626 they were pushed out beyond the walls onto the country road toward Altona. A map of 1791 still shows the Reepschläger Bahn and its huts out there in the fields. The rope trade left this ground in the 1880s. The straight line stayed, and everything since has been built along it.

## The Gap Between Two Cities

The Reeperbahn runs about 930 metres west from the Millerntor to the Nobistor, where it becomes the Königstraße. Those two gateposts are the whole point. The Millerntor marked Hamburg's boundary and the Nobistor marked Altona's, and in the gap between them lay the suburb of Hamburger Berg, later renamed St. Pauli. People and trades unwelcome in either city could settle in that gap and still do business with both. The ropewalks came first, occupying a broad belt north of the modern street from 1626 until 1883. Then came the amusements: houses along the north side from 1826, the St. Pauli Theater in 1841, beer halls and dance halls after 1860, cinemas at the turn of the century, variety theatres in the 1920s, the Café Keese in 1953, and large brothels such as the Eros-Center in the 1960s.

## What the Kiez Actually Contains

Locals call it the Kiez; the marketing calls it die sündige Meile, the sinful mile. In practice it is a dense entertainment district that happens to contain a sex trade. Theatres cluster along the parallel Spielbudenplatz — the Operettenhaus, which gave Germany its premieres of Cats and Mamma Mia!, plus the St. Pauli Theater, the Schmidt Theater, Schmidts Tivoli and the Panoptikum waxworks. On the corner stands the Davidwache, Germany's best-known police station, designed by Fritz Schumacher, finished in 1914 and dressed in ceramic ornament by Richard Kuöhl; its beat covers 0.92 square kilometres and about 14,000 residents. The Reeperbahn Festival has filled the clubs every September since 2006. After violent crime climbed, the city banned weapons in 2007 and glass bottles on weekend nights in 2009, with fines up to 5,000 euros. Neither measure produced a measurable drop.

## Herbertstraße

A hundred metres long and less than seven metres wide, Herbertstraße links Davidstraße to Gerhardstraße, and around 250 women work there, sitting at the windows. It was Heinrichstraße until 1922 — one of a run of side streets given ascending male first names, Antoni, Bernhard, Carl, David, Erich, Friedrich, Gerhard, Heinrich — and the renaming was a failed attempt to make it residential and shed the reputation. The screens at both ends date from 1933. The Nazi state banned prostitution outright, could not enforce that in St. Pauli, and tolerated the trade here on the condition that no passer-by could see it. Signs at the barriers since the 1970s declare entry forbidden to women and to men under eighteen; those were put up at the sex workers' own request and expense, and lawyers still argue over whether a public road can exclude anyone. On 9 August 2024 a memorial stone was set into the kerb outside the gate for the sex workers persecuted under the Nazis.

## Work, and the Difference Between Work and Coercion

Sex work in Germany is a regulated occupation. The Prostitution Act of 1 January 2002 made it a service like others: sex workers can sue for unpaid fees and enrol in the statutory health, unemployment and pension schemes, and providing decent working conditions stopped being a crime. The Prostitutes Protection Act of 2017 added licensing for premises and registration for workers. Street prostitution is legal on Davidstraße during set hours. None of that makes the district safe by itself. At a trial running through 2006 and 2007, ten men who ran brothels on and around the Reeperbahn were charged with pimping; the judge rejected the charge of forming a criminal gang and handed down suspended sentences. They had begun relationships with young women in local discos in order to recruit them, which is illegal where the women are under twenty-one, and some had abused the women who worked for them. The law draws its line at coercion. Enforcing that line is the harder part.

## Selling the Mile

Hamburg has never decided whether to clean the street up or sell it as it is. Hans Albers, playing a Reeperbahn singer in the 1944 film Große Freiheit Nr. 7, gave the district its anthem, Auf der Reeperbahn nachts um halb eins; Jörg Immendorff's 1986 statue on Hans-Albers-Platz shows him with accordion and seaman's cap. Udo Lindenberg has the only star set into this pavement. Tom Waits and The Police both wrote the place into songs. A 9.7-million-euro rebuild opened in June 2006 and gave the Spielbudenplatz two facing mobile stages, though the western one burned down in September 2015 and has not been replaced. The Tanzende Türme went up at the eastern end, the Mojo Club reopened beneath them in 2013, and the St Pauli Preservation Society has objected to the gentrification throughout. Covid closed almost everything in April 2020, and the street only came fully back in 2022.

## Flight Context

53.5494° N, 9.9623° E, running east–west through St. Pauli about 800 m north of the Elbe. From the air the Reeperbahn is genuinely easy to identify: an unusually long, perfectly straight boulevard cutting west from the Millerntor, with the open green of the Heiligengeistfeld and the bowl of the Millerntor-Stadion at its eastern end and the twisted glass Tanzende Türme beside them. The Landungsbrücken piers and the copper spire of St. Michael's lie a short way south. Best viewed from 1,500–2,500 ft AGL. Hamburg Airport (EDDH) is roughly 6 nm north-northeast; Hamburg-Finkenwerder (EDHI) about 4 nm west-southwest across the river. Low stratus and drizzle are common; the street's own character is best judged after dark, when the strip reads as a bright line in an otherwise ordinary grid.
