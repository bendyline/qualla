# Hamburg City Hall

A city republic that never had a king built itself a palace on four thousand oak trunks and wrote the word freedom across the front.

**Location:** 53.5502, 9.9928

**Categories:** Government buildings, Architecture, History of Hamburg, Renaissance Revival architecture, Tourist attractions in Hamburg

## Introduction

In 1971 someone in the Hamburg Rathaus lost a document behind a filing cabinet. Going after it, they found a room in the tower that nobody had counted. The official total is 647 — a great many rooms for a building with no monarch in it, and still fewer than Buckingham Palace's 775 — but since that afternoon nobody in the building will swear the number is final. That is the kind of building it is: enormous, deliberately overwhelming, and put up by a city republic that wanted the world to understand it had never needed a king in order to become rich.

## Forty-Four Years of Rubble

The Rathaus that stood here before was begun in 1290, and it was destroyed on purpose. During the great fire of May 1842 it was blown up to make a firebreak, after eighteen wagonloads of records had been dragged clear; its wine cellar partly collapsed, and a figure of Bacchus salvaged from that debris is still displayed in the building that replaced it. Replacing it took a very long time. Construction did not begin until 1886, forty-four years after the fire, and the new city hall was not inaugurated until 1897. It cost 11 million gold marks, something like €80 million in today's money. On 26 October 1897, at the opening ceremony, the First Mayor, Johannes Versmann, was handed the key.

## Four Thousand Oak Trunks

Hamburg's centre is soft, wet ground, and the answer was timber: more than 4,000 oak piles were driven into the marsh to carry the load. What they carry amounts to 7,840 square metres of footprint, a facade 111 metres long and a tower rising 112 metres. The roof is copper, long since turned green by a century of reaction with water and carbon dioxide, which makes it the most honest clock on the building. A consortium of architects under Martin Haller produced the design. The whole thing came through the bombing of the Second World War very nearly intact, with only minor damage to the tower — remarkable in a city where so little else did.

## A Republic Dressed as a Palace

Outside, the style is neo-Renaissance, and this is one of the few completely preserved works of historicism left in Hamburg; inside, that discipline gives way to whatever historical mode a given room seemed to call for. It went up in the flush years after Prussia's armies defeated France and the German Empire was proclaimed, and it was certainly meant to display money. But it was also meant to display something Berlin did not have. Above the balcony sits a mosaic of Hammonia, the city's patron figure, with the coat of arms and a line of Latin: Libertatem quam peperere maiores digne studeat servare posteritas — may posterity strive worthily to preserve the freedom won by the forefathers. A city that had governed itself for centuries wrote the word freedom across the front of its town hall at the precise moment Germany was consolidating into an empire, and meant it as an argument.

## Hygieia and the Water

In the courtyard stands a fountain crowned by Hygieia, the Greek goddess of health, treading on a dragon. Joseph von Kramer sculpted it in 1895 and 1896, three years after cholera swept the city in 1892 — an epidemic fed by Hamburg's earlier refusal to pay for filtering the river water its new supply system delivered. The dragon is the epidemic. The figure's practical job, at the time, was rather different: the fountain's base housed the inlets for the building's air-cooling system. Hamburg built a monument to clean water that also worked as ventilation, which is about as Hanseatic as civic sculpture gets.

## Where the City Actually Governs

The left wing holds the chamber of the Hamburgische Bürgerschaft, where 121 representatives sit in a room renovated during the Nazi era; only three fields of the ceiling still carry the original decoration. The Senate and the office of the First Mayor are housed in the same building. Behind it, sharing the courtyard known as the Ehrenhof, stands the Stock Exchange — occupied in December 1841, the only building in the district that the great fire spared, and the roof from which Hermann Biow photographed the ruined city. In front lies the Rathausmarkt, where Hamburg holds its festivals and, in 1962, held a service of remembrance for the victims of the North Sea flood. Haile Selassie came here; so did Mohammad Reza Pahlavi in 1955 and Elizabeth II in 1965. Hamburger SV celebrated a German championship on the balcony, most recently in 1983. Mönckebergstraße runs from the square to the main station, the Binnenalster lies just to the north, and once a year, for the Long Night of Museums, a building that is not a museum opens its doors under the motto "long night in the centre of power".

## Flight Context

53.5502°N, 9.9928°E, in Hamburg-Altstadt between the Binnenalster and the Elbe. The 112 m green-copper tower is the easiest fix in the old town from 2,000–3,000 ft, standing over the open rectangle of the Rathausmarkt with the Binnenalster's fountain 300 m north. Hamburg Airport (EDDH) is 8 km north-northeast, Hamburg Finkenwerder (EDHI) 9 km southwest, Lübeck (EDHL) 55 km northeast. Afternoon light from the southwest brings out the green of the roof against the surrounding brick.
