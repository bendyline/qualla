# Hafenstraße

A dozen condemned tenements above the Elbe became the most famous squat in the world, and after fourteen years of standoff their occupants bought them.

**Location:** 53.5464, 9.9594

**Categories:** History of Hamburg, Squats, Housing, Protest movements, Streets in Hamburg

## Introduction

In November 1987 the residents of a dozen tenements above the Elbe built barricades across their own street and waited. The city had run out of patience, the interior senator had mobilised the police, and everyone in Hamburg understood that this was going to be the day. It was not. A contract was signed at the last minute, the barricades came down, and the standoff on the Hafenstraße went unresolved for another eight years. Few political conflicts in postwar Germany ran as long, or ended as oddly.

## Twelve Buildings

The houses were apartment blocks put up in 1900 on Hafenstraße and Bernhard-Nocht-Straße, badly in need of renovation and slated for demolition. In 1981 twelve of them were squatted: students already living in the buildings joined forces with autonomists who wanted them saved. Nobody in authority noticed for a year. When the owner — SAGA, the municipal housing company — worked out what had happened in 1982, it reported the trespass at once and the police cleared the buildings. They were reoccupied almost immediately. What followed was a three-year temporary-use contract, with the residents arguing that they should be allowed to carry out the renovations themselves.

## A Fortress with a Kitchen

They fortified the buildings against future evictions and they also ran them. A pirate radio station broadcast from inside. A Volxküche — a cheap communal kitchen — opened in 1982 and is still there. Alongside the activists, the houses took in homeless people, teenagers and refugees. Politically the place became a centre of autonomist and anti-imperialist organising: two currents that disagreed about most things and agreed about defending the houses. They publicised the 1984 hunger strike by imprisoned Red Army Faction members, protested the death of the antifascist Günter Sare in 1985, and hosted Danish squatters from the BZ movement at the Hafentage over New Year 1986. There was also a night the movement has never been able to explain away. In June 1984 a woman was raped and tortured by three men living in the houses. The response inside was summary — the three were beaten, their heads shaved, and they were expelled — and one apartment was afterwards kept as a women-only space; the condemnation from outside was general, and deserved. When the city evicted several apartments, autonomists answered by setting fires in thirteen department stores, causing more than ten million dollars in damage. In 1986, as the contract expired, 12,000 people marched to defend the squats.

## The Mayor Who Would Not Storm It

The 1987 confrontation set two defensible positions against one another. The city owned the buildings, was responsible for public order, and had been openly defied for six years. The residents had spent those six years making condemned housing habitable and had nowhere else that would take them. Mayor Klaus von Dohnanyi kept pressing for a negotiated end, against pressure from within his own party; the interior senator, Alfons Pawelczyk, put the police on the street. The barricades went up, the contract was signed at the last possible moment, and neither side got the resolution it had come for.

## Bought

In 1993 the Hamburg Higher Regional Court declared the contract void, which put everything back in play. Henning Voscherau, mayor by then, offered terms: settle the dispute, accept the developments planned nearby, and build social housing. In February 1995 the Senate sold the remaining houses to the people living in them for around two million Deutsche Marks. The buyer was a self-organised cooperative, Alternativen am Elbufer — Alternatives beside the Elbe — which owns them still. Fourteen years of eviction orders, street battles and marches ended in a property transaction.

## Paint and Afterlife

The gable ends facing the empty lots have always been painted. In 1987 the squatters put up a slogan reading "Boycott 'Israel'! Goods, kibbutzim, and beaches", the country's name in quotation marks to deny its legitimacy; it belonged to a wider Hamburg boycott campaign that drew on the apartheid comparison associated with activists such as Uri Davis. In 2018 a new mural went up on one building in support of the Kurdish Women's Protection Units. The Independent called Hafenstraße "the most famous squat in the world" in 1998. The band Die Goldenen Zitronen emerged from the scene around it, and the filmmaker Rasmus Gerlach has documented the street twice, in Die Hafentreppe in 1991 and Hafenstraße im Fluss in 2010. "It is the only attempt at utopia by the German left that has survived to this day," he said of it.

## Flight Context

53.5464°N, 9.9594°E, on the bluff above the Elbe in St Pauli, about 700 m west of the Landungsbrücken piers. From 1,500–2,500 ft the terrace of painted apartment blocks stands out as a short row facing the river, with the Bernhard-Nocht-Straße running behind it and the harbour cranes of Steinwerder directly across the water. Hamburg Airport (EDDH) lies 9 km north-northeast; Hamburg Finkenwerder (EDHI) is 6 km southwest. River fog and low stratus are common in autumn and winter.
