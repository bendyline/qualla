# Hamburg

Charlemagne built a fortress on the one dry rock in a marsh, and twelve centuries later the marsh is Germany's second city, its largest port, and a republic that still governs itself.

**Location:** 53.5500, 10.0000

**Categories:** Cities, Port cities, Hanseatic League, German history, City-states, Maritime

## Introduction

Charlemagne's engineers had one patch of firm ground to work with: a rise of rock in a marsh where the Alster runs down into the Elbe. Around AD 808 they raised a fortress on it against incursions from the Slavic east and called it the Hammaburg. What the burg meant is obvious enough. What the hamma meant, nobody has ever established. Twelve hundred years later the castle is gone, the marsh has been drained and dredged and crossed by something like 2,500 bridges, and the settlement that grew out of it is the second-largest city in Germany and one of only three that governs itself as a federal state.

## The Castle in the Marsh

In 834 the fortress became a bishop's seat under Ansgar, the missionary charged with converting the Danes and the Swedes, which turned a swampy frontier post into the ecclesiastical launching point for the whole European north. It did not go smoothly. In 845 a fleet of 600 Viking ships came up the Elbe and destroyed the town. Hamburg's position explains both the ambition and the vulnerability: this is the last place a tidal river is still deep enough for seagoing ships and nearly the first place it can be crossed, and a spot that useful attracts everybody, including the people you would rather not meet. The town rebuilt after each catastrophe, and it would get a great deal of practice.

## Free Passage to the Sea

In 1189 Frederick Barbarossa granted Hamburg the status of a free imperial city, and with it tax-free passage down the Lower Elbe to the North Sea. Nearly everything the place later became is downstream of that single exemption. In 1241 Hamburg allied with Lübeck, and historians treat that agreement as the founding moment of the Hanseatic League, though the formal documentation only surfaces in 1266. The city adopted Lutheranism in 1529 and then took in Reformed refugees driven out of the Netherlands and France, a pattern it would repeat for centuries. In the seventeenth century Jan van Valckenborgh threw a second ring of fortifications around the town against the Thirty Years' War, pulling the Neustadt inside the walls. When the Holy Roman Empire dissolved in 1806, Hamburg simply carried on as a sovereign state under a title it still uses: the Free and Hanseatic City of Hamburg. The French occupied it anyway. Russian forces under General Bennigsen freed the city in 1814.

## Fire, Bombs, Water

Twice the city has been largely destroyed, and once it was drowned. A fire that broke out on 4 May 1842 burned for four days and took about a quarter of the inner city; fifty-one people died, some 20,000 were left homeless, and the rebuilding ran more than forty years. Recovery was ferocious: in the second half of the nineteenth century the population more than quadrupled to 800,000 as Atlantic trade made Hamburg the second-largest port in Europe. Then, on 23 July 1943, British and American firebombing raised a firestorm that spread out from the Hauptbahnhof and consumed entire boroughs. Operation Gomorrah killed at least 42,600 civilians. Of the nearly 17,000 Jews living in Hamburg before 1933, about 7,800 were murdered in the Holocaust. And in February 1962 a North Sea storm surge pushed the Elbe to its highest recorded level, flooding a fifth of the city and killing more than 300 people. A city built on a floodplain is not permitted to forget the floodplain.

## A Republic of Merchants

Hamburg is a city and a federal state at once, one of three in Germany alongside Berlin and Bremen, spread across 755 square kilometres, seven boroughs and 104 quarters. The habit of self-government is old and you can read it in the streets: no royal avenue, no prince's palace axis, just a merchants' city hall and a great many company names on brass plates. The port is still the engine. It is Germany's largest and Europe's third, after Rotterdam and Antwerp, and it lies 110 kilometres up the Elbe from open water, inland by any map yet deep enough to take ocean-going vessels, which is why so many countries keep a consulate here. Heavy industry followed the water: steel, aluminium, copper, and shipyards including Blohm + Voss. So did aerospace. Airbus employs more than 13,000 people at its Finkenwerder plant, which puts Hamburg alongside Toulouse and Seattle as one of the places civil airliners are actually built.

## Counted in Bridges

No city in Europe has more bridges: roughly 2,500 of them, over the Elbe, over the Alster, and over the dense grid of canals the merchants cut so cargo could reach their own back doors. The Alster spreads into two lakes in the middle of town, the small Binnenalster ringed by shopfronts and the broad Außenalster scattered with dinghies all summer. The land barely rises at all. The highest ground in the entire city-state is the Hasselbrack, out in Neugraben-Fischbek, at 116.2 metres. Nearly two million people live here and more than five million in the wider metropolitan region; roughly a third of Hamburg residents have a migrant background, with immigrants from 200 countries. The largest Portuguese community in Germany, around 30,000 people, is one of the older strands. Portuguese sailors and merchants have been coming to this port since the fifteenth century, and since the 1970s a stretch of the Neustadt near the harbour has been known as the Portuguese Quarter. Its residents are the reason a statue of Vasco da Gama stands on the Kornhaus bridge. They asked for it.

## Flight Context

Hamburg sits at 53.55 N, 10.00 E, on the Elbe some 110 km upstream from the North Sea. From 3,000-5,000 ft AGL the city reads instantly: the twin Alster lakes as a bright rectangle and oval north of the centre, the Elbe splitting around the harbour islands to the south, and the container terminals and the Köhlbrand bridge west of downtown. The Elbphilharmonie's wave-topped glass and the copper spire of the Michel are the two best fixed references over the old town. Terrain is negligible, with the highest point in the state at 116.2 m. Hamburg Airport / Helmut Schmidt (EDDH) lies about 8 km north-northeast of the centre; Hamburg-Finkenwerder (EDHI), Airbus's own field, is on the south bank of the Elbe west of the city; Lübeck (EDHL) is roughly 55 km northeast. Expect maritime weather: low stratus, frequent drizzle, and rapid visibility changes off the North Sea, with the clearest air usually behind a cold front.
