# Dialogue in the Dark

In a Speicherstadt warehouse, blind and partially sighted guides lead sighted visitors through a world with the lights switched off - and the usual hierarchy of competence quietly inverted.

**Location:** 53.5462, 10.0016

**Categories:** Museums, Hamburg, Accessibility, Social enterprise, Attractions

## Introduction

Andreas Heinecke was a journalist at a German radio station in the 1980s when he was handed an unusual assignment: help a colleague who had lost his sight return to work. Working through the practicalities of that, Heinecke arrived at a question that turned into a career. What happens if you switch off the light entirely - darken a room completely, and put blind people and sighted people into it together with their roles reversed? He built the answer in 1988, and it has since taken more than nine million people through the dark.

## Role Reversal, Literally

The premise of Dialogue in the Dark is exactly as simple as it sounds and considerably stranger to experience. Small groups are led through a series of environments in absolute darkness - not gloom, not dim red safety lighting, but the complete absence of light. The guides are blind or partially sighted. Inside the exhibition they are the competent ones: they know the space, they navigate it fluently, they keep track of where everyone is. The visitors, who arrived with what they assumed was the advantage, spend the next hour or so being talked through doorways. It is not a simulation exercise dressed up as empathy training. It is a straightforward transfer of expertise from the people who have it to the people who suddenly need it.

## Learning to Use the Rest of Yourself

The exhibition is built out of ordinary settings reconstructed for the ear, the nose, the skin and the hands: a park, a street in a city, a boat, a bar. Wind moves. Temperature changes. Smells arrive before you can place them, and sounds come from directions you have never had to think about. Most visitors discover within minutes how much information they had been discarding - how loud a room is, how a surface underfoot changes, how close another person is standing. During and after the tour there is time to ask the guide questions, and the questions people ask at the end are generally not the ones they had ready at the start.

## The Warehouse in the Speicherstadt

The travelling version came first. For more than a decade Dialogue in the Dark moved between museums, fairs and festivals, opening in Frankfurt and touring widely before anyone tried to give it a permanent address. That happened in Hamburg in 2000, in the Speicherstadt, the nineteenth-century red-brick warehouse district on the canals just south of the old city. Some 90,000 people a year go through it. There is a certain rightness to the location: the Speicherstadt was built as a place where goods were stored out of sight, and the district's tall brick blocks have always been more about interiors than views.

## A Business, Not a Charity Appeal

What Heinecke built is a social enterprise, and the structure is deliberate. Dialogue Social Enterprise owns the brand and licenses it as a franchise, selling both the public exhibition and corporate workshops run in the dark - leadership, communication and teamwork sessions where the facilitator is the only person in the room who can operate. That revenue is the point. The organisation's stated second goal, alongside raising awareness, is to create work for disadvantaged people by turning a perceived deficit into a marketable skill, and thousands of blind and partially sighted guides and facilitators have been employed through it.

## Thirty Countries Later

Since 1988 the exhibition has appeared in more than 130 cities across over thirty countries, from Japan and South Korea to Israel, Italy, Greece, Russia, Singapore and the United States, and it currently operates in around twenty-one countries in various formats. The Hamburg site remains the flagship and the oldest permanent installation. Book ahead, and expect the disorientation to last a few minutes past the exit - the light on the way out is harder to handle than the darkness on the way in.

## Flight Context

The Hamburg exhibition sits at 53.5462 N, 10.0016 E in the Speicherstadt, the warehouse district on the canals between Hamburg's old city and the harbour. From the air the Speicherstadt is unmistakable: a long, dense block of red-brick gabled warehouses laced with waterways, running roughly east-west, with the glass-and-wave roof of the Elbphilharmonie at its western end and the Chilehaus a few hundred metres north-east. Hamburg Airport (EDDH) is about 9 km north; Hamburg Finkenwerder (EDHI) about 11 km west. Fly it at 1,000-2,000 ft AGL along the Elbe. The brick reads best in low sun; the exhibition itself, appropriately, has nothing to see from outside.
