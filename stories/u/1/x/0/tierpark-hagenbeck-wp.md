# Tierpark Hagenbeck

The Hamburg zoo that taught the world to replace cages with moats was built by a man who also put human beings on display - eight of whom, brought from Labrador in 1880, died within four months.

**Location:** 53.5964, 9.9378

**Categories:** Zoos, Hamburg, Human zoos, Inuit history, Animal trade, Colonial history

## Introduction

On 24 September 1880, a schooner called the Eisbar tied up in Hamburg carrying eight people from Hebron, on the coast of Labrador. Abraham Ulrikab was thirty-five. His wife Ulrike was twenty-four; their daughters Sara and Maria were three and a half and ten months old. Tobias, unmarried, was twenty. With them came Tigianniak, Paingu and their daughter Nuggasak. Carl Hagenbeck's agent had recruited them to be looked at. Within four months every one of them was dead of smallpox, a disease they had not been vaccinated against, and Abraham had left a diary in Inuktitut describing what was happening to his family. The zoo that stands in Stellingen today grew out of the same business.

## From the Fish Shop to the Docks

Carl Hagenbeck Sr., born in 1810, sold fish in Hamburg and kept exotic animals as a sideline - seals, and once a polar bear, arriving on ships from everywhere the port reached. His son took over that side of the trade at fourteen. By the 1870s the animals were worth far more than the fish, and Carl Hagenbeck the younger, born in Hamburg on 10 June 1844, was among Europe's most prominent exotic animal dealers. He travelled the world buying stock in 1874 and opened an animal park in Hamburg the same year. When an exhibition left him holding a herd of unsellable elephants, he started a circus rather than take the loss.

## The People He Displayed

Alongside the animals, Hagenbeck exhibited human beings. From 1874 he showed Samoan and Sami people, the Sami staged with their tents, weapons, sledges and a group of reindeer, presented to paying crowds as specimens of the natural world. From 1876 he toured a Nubian group across the continent. In 1880 he sent Johan Adrian Jacobsen to Labrador for the party that included Abraham Ulrikab. People were recruited under false promises, and in some cases apparently taken without meaningful consent. They were not exhibits. They were families, with names, who signed on believing something other than what they got. The Labrador group was shown in Hamburg from 2 October, then Berlin, Prague, Frankfurt, Darmstadt, Krefeld and Paris. Nuggasak died on 14 December 1880, Paingu on 27 December, Sara on New Year's Eve, Maria on 10 January 1881, Tigianniak on the 11th, Tobias and Abraham together on the 13th, and Ulrike on the 16th. Their remains ended up in museum collections in Paris and Berlin, where they still are, while the Nunatsiavut government works to bring them home.

## Hiding the Ditch

The shows lost their pull as photography spread and his tableaux began to look staged by comparison, so he set about making his displays more convincing - and that pursuit of realism is what changed zoos. In the 1890s he built the Northern Panorama: seals and walruses in a pool at the front, a moat concealed behind them, reindeer beyond it, a second hidden moat, and polar bears beyond that. To a visitor at the rail it read as one continuous Arctic landscape. He patented the idea in 1896. The engineering came from the circus: he had years of measurements on how high and how far different species could jump, so he knew exactly how wide a ditch had to be.

## Stellingen, 1907

On 7 May 1907 Hagenbeck opened a whole park built on the principle, at Stellingen beyond the city limits, where the Tierpark still stands. Nothing was behind bars. He argued that animals from warm climates did not need the expensive, stifling glasshouses zoos had been putting them in. He called it an animal paradise where creatures would live beside one another in harmony and the struggle for survival would be abolished. The design travelled: he laid out Rome's zoo the same way in 1911, and in 1913 built the first monkey rock, an artificial crag ringed by a moat and populated with some two hundred hamadryas baboons. He died on 14 April 1913 of a snake bite, probably from a boomslang.

## Two Wars and a Requisition

The First World War took the keepers into the army. With the military requisitioning civilian horses for the front, Hagenbeck's animals were hired out as draught power - elephants and trained bears hitched to heavy wagons, hauling coal and firewood through Hamburg. The zoo shut for two years afterwards as the German economy collapsed. In 1943 the bombing of Hamburg destroyed roughly seventy per cent of the park and killed nine members of staff along with many of the animals. In 1947 the British occupation authorities requisitioned a number of survivors as a forced loan to London Zoo. The family rebuilt anyway, and still owns and runs the place.

## Antje, and the Pavilion

Post-war Stellingen produced its own small legends. In July 1956, forty-five rhesus monkeys got out and spent a memorable day in Hamburg's bathrooms and treetops before police, firemen, keepers and schoolchildren rounded them up. A Pacific walrus named Antje arrived in 1976, became the on-air mascot of the northern German broadcaster NDR from 1980 to 2001, and died on 17 July 2003 at twenty-seven; she is mounted and on display at the city's Zoological Museum. Near the centre of the park stands the Sala Thai, a traditional pavilion by the Thai National Artist Pinyo Suwankiri, built in Thailand with joinery that uses no nails, then shipped and reassembled by twenty Thai craftsmen - a gift from the Thai government in 2002. The Tierpark today covers nineteen hectares and holds around 1,400 animals of 140 species, with some 14,300 more in the Tropen-Aquarium.

## Flight Context

53.5964 N, 9.9378 E, at Stellingen in the Eimsbuttel district of northwestern Hamburg, about 4 nm northwest of the inner city. From the air the park reads as an irregular green pocket of water bodies and rockwork wedged between the A7 autobahn to the west and dense housing on the other three sides; the Volkspark lies about 1.5 nm southwest. Best distinguished from 1,500 to 3,000 ft AGL, where the artificial crags and moated pools are visible as landscaped shapes rather than ordinary parkland. Hamburg Airport (EDDH) sits about 4 nm east-northeast, so the area lies squarely under its approach and departure paths; Hamburg Finkenwerder (EDHI) is roughly 5 nm south-southwest.
