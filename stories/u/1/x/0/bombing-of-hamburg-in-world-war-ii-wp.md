# Bombing of Hamburg in World War II

In one week of July 1943, Operation Gomorrah raised a firestorm over Hamburg that killed tens of thousands of civilians, drove a million people out of the city, and left a death toll that has never been fixed with certainty.

**Location:** 53.5506, 9.9933

**Categories:** History, World War II, Germany, Hamburg, Aviation

## Introduction

In some of Hamburg's cellars, the recovery teams could not count the dead. The cellars had been converted into air protection rooms, families had gone down into them when the sirens went, and what the teams found afterwards was ash on the floor. The number of people who had been there was estimated from how much of it there was. By 1 December 1943, four months after the raids, the city had 31,647 confirmed deaths on its books, but only 15,802 of those rested on the identification of an actual body. The rest were arithmetic. That gap is the honest starting point for anything said about the bombing of Hamburg: a week of raids in July 1943 killed more people than anyone was afterwards able to name.

## The Week the Radar Went Blind

Hamburg had been a target since the start of the war - a port city with shipyards, U-boat pens and, out at Harburg, oil refineries. What began on the night of 24 July 1943 was something else in scale. Seven hundred and ninety-one RAF bombers took off; 728 of them dropped their loads over the city in fifty minutes. On that raid the RAF used Window for the first time, strips of foil cut to the wavelength of German radar and tipped out of the bomb bays in bundles, and the defences filled with false returns. American daylight raids followed: 90 of 127 B-17s reached the city at 16:40 on 25 July, and 54 aircraft bombed the next day. Hamburg was already burning, its firefighting stretched, when 787 RAF aircraft came back on the night of 27 July.

## The Second Night

The fires that night joined into one. A firestorm is a fire large enough to make its own weather, drawing air in at ground level to feed itself, and the Hamburg firestorm pulled winds of up to 240 km/h through streets where temperatures reached 800 degrees Celsius. Flame rose more than 300 metres; witnesses described a tornado of fire some 460 metres high. Half an hour after it started, four square miles were alight. Asphalt appeared to burn - it was the phosphorus from the incendiaries - and fuel oil from ruptured ships, barges and storage tanks spread across the canals and the harbour and caught alight on the water. More than 21 square kilometres of Hamburg were incinerated. The people who died did not, for the most part, die in the flames. They had gone into cellars and shelters, and the fire above consumed the oxygen while carbon monoxide filled the spaces below. The winds in the streets were strong enough to lift a person off the ground. An estimated 18,474 people were killed that night.

## What the Raids Were For

Two more raids followed - 777 bombers on 29 July, of which 707 reached the city, and 737 on 2 August, scattered by a storm so violent that only 400 got through. On the third night the Pathfinders missed the aiming point by two miles and a four-mile creepback carried the bombing into Barmbek and Wandsbek; 370 people died of carbon monoxide poisoning in a single public shelter. Across the whole operation the Allies flew about 3,000 sorties and dropped 9,000 tons of bombs. The pattern of the destruction says plainly what was being aimed at. Of 414,500 dwellings in Hamburg, 214,350 were destroyed, some 60 per cent of the city's houses. This was area bombing, directed at the built-up city itself rather than at individual works. Hamburg was a Reich port building U-boats for a regime at war. The people who suffocated in its cellars were overwhelmingly civilians.

## A Number Nobody Can Fix

Police records gave about 34,000 dead; the figure most often accepted now is 37,000, within a range running to 40,000. Around 2.4 per cent of everyone living in Hamburg was killed in a week. Wildly higher numbers circulated at the time and long afterwards - a Swiss dispatch in November 1943 reported 152,000; the US Strategic Bombing Survey, published in October 1945, relayed German estimates of 60,000 to 100,000. In the first week after the raids close to a million people left the city. Sixty-one per cent of the housing stock was destroyed or damaged, the labour force shrank by a tenth, and 183 of the city's 524 large factories were gone. Hamburg was bombed another 69 times before the war ended; the RAF dropped 22,580 long tons on it in all.

## What Hamburg Kept

Hammerbrook, where dock and port workers had lived, was destroyed so completely that it was never rebuilt as housing; it came back as a commercial district, and neighbouring Rothenburgsort largely shared its fate. The U-Bahn line that had linked both to the central station was never relaid. Elsewhere, houses were put up across the street from where they had stood, so the old connected blocks never reformed. The hills in Öjendorfer Park are made of the rubble. The dead were buried in mass graves at Ohlsdorf Cemetery, where Gerhard Marcks's memorial Passage over the Styx shows Charon ferrying a young couple, a mother with her child, a man, and a figure in despair. A plaque on Hamburger Strasse marks a shelter beneath a department store, hit on the night of 29 July, where everyone below was killed by heat and carbon monoxide. On rebuilt houses across the city a small formula recurs: Destroyed 1943, Rebuilt - and then a year.

## Flight Context

53.55 N, 9.99 E. The target area was the inner city and the working-class districts east and southeast of it - Hammerbrook, Rothenburgsort, Barmbek, Wandsbek - laid out along the Elbe and its harbour basins. From 3,000-5,000 ft the geography that shaped the raids is legible at once: the Elbe splitting around the port, the Alster lakes as the Pathfinders' visual reference, and the abrupt change in street pattern where the postwar rebuild begins. Hamburg Airport (EDDH) is 8 km north-northeast of the centre, Hamburg-Finkenwerder (EDHI) on the south bank. Approaches often run over the harbour. Low cloud and haze are common year-round in this maritime climate; clearest air usually follows a northwesterly airstream.
