# Krameramtsstuben

Twenty rent-free apartments built in 1676 for the widows of Hamburg's shopkeepers — a charity that was also, quite openly, a way of getting them out of their husbands' shops.

**Location:** 53.5480, 9.9802

**Categories:** Historical sites, Architecture, Museums, Hamburg, Social history

## Introduction

The kindness had a purpose, and the guild did not pretend otherwise. When Hamburg's Krameramt built twenty small apartments off the Krayenkamp in 1676, they were free, they came with fuel and a modest pension, and they were offered to the widows of deceased members on an understood condition: that the widow give up her late husband's shop so a new man could take it. A roof for a livelihood. Three and a half centuries later the courtyard is still there, in the shadow of the Michel's tower, the last enclosed courtyard of its kind left in Hamburg.

## A Summerhouse Among the Gardens

The oldest houses on the site were not built for widows at all. Around 1615 to 1625, when this stretch of the Neustadt had only just been brought inside Hamburg's new ramparts and was still mostly ornamental gardens, someone with money put up a country house and summerhouse here — cantilevered upper floors, ornamental cut cleats, painted ceilings. Those ceilings survive, exposed, and they are the giveaway: nobody paints a ceiling for a servant. The buildings at Krayenkamp 10 and 11 are now the oldest surviving residential structures in central Hamburg, a fact they owe less to luck than to being tucked into a back plot where nothing much needed to be knocked down.

## Twenty Doors

The Krameramt was old and rich. Established by 1375 as a guild-style association of small retailers — grocers, silk and iron merchants — it offered its members protection in trade and, when they died, something for the families they left. In 1676 it bought the grounds and put up twenty apartments in two parallel rows of five houses each, facing one another across a courtyard barely wider than a cart. Each house originally held two dwellings, one at ground level and one in the attic, and a dwelling meant a chamber, a hall and somewhere to cook. Later they were consolidated into single units. Around 1700 a further house went up to close the courtyard off from the street, which is why the courtyard is entered through a passage rather than a front door.

## Life in the Yard

Look up in the courtyard and the details are all practical ones. Twisted brick chimneys. Wooden racks bolted outside the windows for drying laundry, because there was nowhere else to put it. A well, which is where every household here drew its water until a mains supply arrived shortly before 1900 — more than two hundred years of carrying buckets across the yard. These were not spacious lives. They were, however, secure ones, in a port city where a widow without a trade and without a pension had very few places to go.

## After the Guild

Free-trade legislation, passed in 1864, took effect on 1 February 1865 and ended the old system; Hamburg's guilds, the Krameramt included, were formally dissolved in 1866, when the city took over the buildings and kept using them for much the same purpose, housing elderly single women in place of grocers' widows. The apartments were placed under a preservation order in 1933, and they came through the bombing of the Second World War largely intact — a survival worth pausing on, given how much of central Hamburg did not. They stood empty from 1970. Restoration began in 1972 at a cost of 1.6 million marks, and in June 1974 the courtyard reopened as a cultural quarter of small shops, galleries and restaurants.

## One Door Left as It Was

One apartment was not converted. It is kept as a museum, a branch of the Hamburg Museum, furnished in the style of about 1850 to 1860 to show how the people who lived here actually lived: the scale of the rooms, the low ceilings, the bed and stove and table in what is essentially one space. On display with the furniture are the two instruments that defined the trade the whole place was built on — a set of balance scales and an ell, the cubit-length measuring stick, both dating from 1800. They appear on the grocers' guild sign as well. Weights and measures: the entire business of a Kramer, in two objects, in a room a widow was given because she agreed to stop using them.

## Flight Context

The Krameramtsstuben stand at 53.5480 N, 9.9802 E, on the Krayenkamp in Hamburg's Neustadt, immediately southeast of St. Michael's Church and about 300 m north of the Elbe. From 1,500–2,500 ft AGL the courtyard itself is too small to resolve, but the Michel's copper spire directly beside it is one of the most conspicuous landmarks on the north bank; the site lies between that spire and the river. Other references: the St. Pauli Landungsbrücken piers to the west, the Elbphilharmonie and Speicherstadt to the east. Hamburg Airport / Helmut Schmidt (EDDH) is about 9 km north-northeast; Hamburg-Finkenwerder (EDHI) sits across the Elbe to the west; Lübeck (EDHL) is roughly 58 km northeast. Central Hamburg is controlled airspace. Low maritime cloud is common; the spire is often the last thing visible in reduced visibility.
