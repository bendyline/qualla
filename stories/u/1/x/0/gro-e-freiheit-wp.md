# Große Freiheit

The street called Great Freedom was named for the right to pray and to work outside the guilds — and it has been trading on the word ever since.

**Location:** 53.5506, 9.9575

**Categories:** Streets in Hamburg, History of Hamburg, Religious freedom, Nightlife, Red-light districts in Germany

## Introduction

Halfway along a short street running north off the Reeperbahn, wedged between neon signs and doorways, there is a Catholic church. St Joseph has a baroque facade and it stood at Große Freiheit 41 long before any of its present neighbours arrived. The street's name means Great Freedom, and the freedom in question was originally the freedom to worship. That a strip now known for sex shows and rock clubs began as a haven for religious dissenters is not something the street works hard to explain to visitors. It is, however, the reason the street exists at all.

## A Loophole Outside Hamburg

Lutheran Hamburg did not allow non-Lutherans to practise their religion in public. A short walk west, in Altona, the rules were different. In 1601 Count Ernst von Schauenburg granted a privilege to the Mennonite merchant François Noë and others, creating what amounted to a special economic and religious zone; the street was laid out in 1610 and took its name from the freedoms attached to it. Mennonites and Roman Catholics could worship here. Craftsmen shut out by the guilds that governed work elsewhere could trade here. At the time this ground belonged to Altona in the county of Holstein-Pinneberg and had nothing to do with Hamburg. When the Duchy of Holstein-Glückstadt annexed Holstein-Pinneberg in 1640 the old comital freedoms were confirmed, and from that year until 1864 Altona belonged to Denmark. Tolerance, on this street, was somebody else's foreign policy.

## The Congregations That Stayed, and the One That Left

St Joseph's, rebuilt in 1721, still holds services among businesses of an entirely different character. The Mennonite congregation established in 1611 lasted three centuries here: its buildings survive at number 73, put up in 1850, and number 75, built in 1772 and used as the parsonage, though the congregation itself moved to another neighbourhood in 1915. The street stayed foreign to Hamburg longer than either congregation stayed put. Only on 1 April 1938, when the Nazi government redrew the city boundaries under the Greater Hamburg Act, did Große Freiheit cease to be part of Altona and become part of St Pauli.

## A Name Worth Borrowing

By then the name had become a property in its own right. The 1944 film Große Freiheit Nr. 7 took its title from an address on the road; Hans Albers plays a singer working a Reeperbahn club who falls for a young woman played by Ilse Werner. In 2003 the Kurdish-German director Yüksel Yavuz made Kleine Freiheit — Little Freedom — about the friendship between two teenage boys living in Germany without papers, set here, the title turning the street's name into the film's central irony. The rock band Randy Pie put the street on both sides of their 1975 album Kitsch: the same view at night on the front cover, in daylight on the back. Music venues took root at several addresses from the 1960s onward, and Große Freiheit 36 and Grünspan still operate.

## The Other Trade

The street is also part of Hamburg's red-light district and has never pretended otherwise. In the 1970s four theatres — the Salambo, Regina, Colibri and Safari — sold live sex acts on stage. The Safari outlasted the rest and was the last live sex theatre in Germany when it closed in 2013; the table-dance club Dollhouse now occupies the Salambo's old premises. The Hotel Luxor, Hamburg's oldest brothel, had traded on the street for sixty years when it shut in 2008 — the same year the city laid out Beatles-Platz at the Reeperbahn end, and three years after the street acquired its only hotel, the St Joseph, named for the church. What has come and gone here has mostly been business. The people conducting it, whether preaching without a licence in 1610 or working the doorways in 2013, were generally people the respectable city preferred to keep on the far side of a boundary line. The boundary moved in 1938. The street kept its name.

## Flight Context

53.5506°N, 9.9575°E, in St Pauli, roughly 1.5 km west-northwest of Hamburg's Altstadt and 400 m north of the Elbe. From 2,000 ft the street reads as a short spur running north from the bright east–west line of the Reeperbahn, with the green-copper spire of St Joseph's the only vertical marker. Hamburg Airport (EDDH) is 9 km north-northeast; Hamburg Finkenwerder (EDHI) is 6 km southwest across the river. Best identified after dark, when the district is the brightest thing on the north bank.
