# Miniatur Wunderland

Two brick warehouses in Hamburg's Speicherstadt hold more than sixteen kilometres of model railway, roughly 290,000 tiny people, and a night that falls every fifteen minutes.

**Location:** 53.5438, 9.9889

**Categories:** Tourist attractions in Hamburg, Model railroads, Museums in Hamburg, Miniature parks, Speicherstadt

## Introduction

Every fifteen minutes, night falls on four continents at once. Streetlights come up in Las Vegas, the Venetian lagoon goes dark, a fire brigade turns out in a town called Knuffingen that exists nowhere else, and then dawn arrives again. All of it happens inside two brick warehouses in Hamburg's Speicherstadt, across roughly 1,700 square metres of model. Guinness World Records certifies Miniatur Wunderland as the largest model railway in the world. What a certificate cannot convey is the obsessiveness of the thing — the sense of a machine built by adults who simply never agreed to stop.

## Two Pages and a Zurich Model Shop

The idea arrived on holiday. In the summer of 2000 Frederik Braun wandered into a model train shop in Zurich and came out thinking about the largest model railway on Earth. Back in Hamburg he ran an email survey on the popularity of the city's attractions, real and invented, and the world's largest model railway — which did not exist — came third among male respondents. That was encouragement enough. He sold his discotheque and his record label, and in 2001 founded the company with his father Jochen W. Braun, his twin brother Gerrit and Stephan Hertz. The brothers say the business plan and its financing ran to two pages. Hamburger Sparkasse turned them down before lending two million Deutschmarks against personal guarantees; no public money went in. Construction began in December 2000, and the first three sections opened on 16 August 2001.

## Sixteen Kilometres of H0

The numbers keep moving because the layout keeps growing. Track length passed 13,000 metres, then reached 15,400 in September 2015 when the Switzerland and Italy sections were joined in front of a Guinness adjudicator, and it now runs past 16,000 metres of H0 gauge — around 1,400 kilometres at full size. Some 1,200 digitally controlled trains haul more than 12,000 wagons through 3,600 sets of points. Around them stand roughly 5,300 houses and bridges, more than 11,000 road vehicles of which some 285 drive themselves, and about 290,000 figures, lit by over half a million LEDs. Twelve sections were complete by 2024: the Harz, Knuffingen, Austria, Hamburg, America, Scandinavia, Switzerland, Knuffingen Airport, Italy, Rio de Janeiro, Patagonia, and Monaco with Provence. Total build cost to that point was about 45 million euros. The whole apparatus runs on software written in Delphi.

## The Airport That Files a Flight Plan

Knuffingen Airport took six years and roughly 3.5 million euros, and opened on 4 May 2011. On a fourteen-metre runway, aircraft accelerate on a sled hidden beneath the tarmac, then rise on guide rods that tilt them convincingly before they vanish into a wall. The fleet ranges from 747s and A380s through the liveries of long-dead airlines to a British Airways Concorde, a Space Shuttle, a bee called Bummel and the Millennium Falcon. Ground movements are choreographed too: vehicles refuel, load and unload around each stand in small coordinated routines. Screens display a live timetable synchronised with real scheduled arrivals and departures. Here, for once, the railway is nearly invisible — there is only an underground station beneath the terminal.

## Push the Button, Get the Chocolate

About two hundred push-buttons hand control to the visitor. One starts a mine train. Others spin the wind turbines, score a goal in the football stadium, launch a helicopter, or lengthen Pinocchio's nose. One runs a miniature chocolate factory through its entire production cycle and then delivers a real bar of Lindt to whoever pressed it. The detail rewards staring: a Jet filling station displays the actual current fuel prices of its real counterpart on Hamburg's Amsinckstraße, a lorry has shed its load of cheese wheels across a road, and Knuffingen's fire brigade turns out roughly every quarter of an hour. In the Scandinavian section the sea is real water, a 30,000-litre basin with simulated tides.

## Twenty-Five Million People

The ten-millionth visitor arrived on 5 December 2012, the fifteen-millionth on 2 December 2016, the twenty-millionth on 23 August 2021, and the twenty-five-millionth on 7 March 2025. In 2019 around 1.4 million people came, roughly a third of them from outside Germany, before the pandemic cost the place most of two years. Foreign visitors have repeatedly voted it their favourite attraction in Germany. In 2010 Frederik and Gerrit Braun and Stephan Hertz received the Cross of Merit on Ribbon for their social work, and on 5 June 2026, for the Wunderland's twenty-fifth anniversary, an ICE 4 trainset was christened Knuffingen at Hamburg-Altona station. A British section is due to begin construction in 2028.

## Flight Context

53.5438° N, 9.9889° E, in the Speicherstadt warehouse blocks at Kehrwieder, on the northern edge of the Hamburg port. From the air the giveaway is the Speicherstadt itself — a grid of red-brick warehouses standing on oak piles between narrow canals, wedged between the Elbe and HafenCity, with the wave-crest roof of the Elbphilharmonie about half a kilometre to the southwest. Best seen from 1,500–2,500 ft AGL. Hamburg Airport (EDDH) lies roughly 5 nm north; Hamburg-Finkenwerder (EDHI) is about 5 nm west across the river. Note that the Speicherstadt sits under Hamburg's inner-city airspace and adjacent to a working container port. Hamburg's maritime climate brings frequent low stratus and drizzle; the brick reads best under flat overcast, which is most of the year.
