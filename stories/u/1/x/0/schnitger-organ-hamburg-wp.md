# Schnitger Organ, St. Jacobi

The largest organ surviving from before 1700 outlived the destruction of Hamburg because somebody took it out of the church in 1942.

**Location:** 53.5504, 10.0003

**Categories:** Culture in Hamburg, Individual pipe organs, Baroque music, Churches in Hamburg, Arp Schnitger

## Introduction

In 1942 the windchests, the pipework and the carvings came out of St. Jacobi and went into storage. It was ordinary wartime caution, and it rescued one of the great instruments of Europe. When the church burned out, Arp Schnitger's case was destroyed along with the bellows housing and the console of 1774 — but nearly everything that actually makes sound had already been carried away. What came back is the largest organ to survive from anywhere before 1700: four manuals and pedal, sixty stops, fifteen of them reeds, and close to four thousand sounding pipes.

## Meister Rudolf's Successors

Nobody wrote down when St. Jacobi first had an organ, though a "Meister Rudolf" is attested as organist around 1300. Between 1512 and 1516 Jacob Iversand and Harmen Stüven built a two-manual instrument, and a Rückpositiv was added before 1543. After that the church simply kept hiring builders: Jacob Scherer from 1551, his son-in-law Dirk Hoyer in 1577–78 for a new Rückpositiv and two pedal towers, Hans Bockelmann and Hans Scherer the Elder between 1588 and 1592 for a new Oberwerk, then Scherer's sons in 1606–07. Michael Praetorius recorded the 1592 specification in his Syntagma musicum: fifty-three stops across three manuals, which left Hieronymus Praetorius playing one of the largest organs anywhere in Europe. Gottfried Fritzsche rebuilt it to four manuals in 1635–36, and his son renovated it two decades later for the incoming organist Matthias Weckmann.

## Against Reincken's Advice

Arp Schnitger, the most celebrated organ builder of his generation, took the instrument over in 1689 and finished in 1693. He kept twenty-seven existing stops — the oldest pipes among them going back to 1516 — and built the rest himself, bringing the total to sixty. Johann Adam Reincken, organist at St. Katharinen and a formidable voice in the city, advised against it, but Schnitger installed both a Principal 32′ and a Posaune 32′ in the pedal. Two thirty-two-foot ranks were a statement of civic wealth as much as of music. The bill came to 29,108 marks. The façade is the largest surviving example of the "Hamburg prospect" developed by the Scherer family around 1600: symmetrical, with huge pedal towers at either flank and the manual divisions stacked in tiers, the Oberpositiv hidden behind the Werck. Christian Precht carved the figures, among the last works we have from him.

## Bach Did Not Get the Job

In 1720 Johann Sebastian Bach applied to be organist here, and was passed over. The post went to Johann Joachim Heitmann, who could pay 4,000 marks into the church treasury and marry the head pastor's daughter. Johann Mattheson preserved the sermon that followed, in which the head pastor Erdmann Neumeister told his congregation he was quite certain that if one of the angels of Bethlehem came down from heaven and played divinely and wished to be organist of St. Jacobi, but had no money, he might as well fly away again. Bach did play the great organ next door at St. Katharinen. The Jacobi instrument happened to be in poor condition just then, and he left the city before the official audition.

## A Walk in 1919

The first real wound came in 1917, when the tin façade pipes were requisitioned for the war effort. Two years later the writer Hans Henny Jahnn was out walking with his friend Gottlieb Friedrich Harms, the two of them stepped into the church, and there stood the organ, in Jahnn's phrase, desolately stripped. Harms sat down and played it. That was enough. Learning that the parish meant to demolish the thing and buy a new instrument, they wrote at once to the church council and alerted the Hamburg senate. Jahnn campaigned for repair and replacement of the missing pipes, won approval, and from 1922 raised the money through the Ugrino benefit concerts, for which the church lent itself free of charge. He recruited Günther Ramin of the Leipzig Thomaskirche, who put seventeenth-century Hamburg organ music back into the room alongside Buxtehude and Bach. At the organ conference Jahnn convened in July 1925, St. Jacobi became the model instrument for the entire northern European revival.

## Three Hundred Years Later

Rebuilding took decades and two false starts. The Lübeck workshop Kemper set the organ up provisionally in the surviving south aisle in 1950 and returned it to the west end of the nave by 1961, with a new case, a new console and an action so stiff it fought the player. The sound was uneven: pipes had been shortened across two centuries to chase modern pitch, and the wind pressures matched no seventeenth-century practice. In 1982 Rudolf Kelber arrived as church musician and pressed for a proper restoration. The consensus was to go back to the late eighteenth century, keeping Johann Jacob Lehnert's registers of 1761 and returning to Schnitger's short octave, modelled on a surviving Schnitger console from Lübeck cathedral. Jürgen Ahrend did the work; Cornelius H. Edskes documented it in more than 60,000 individual measurements. Three hundred years after Schnitger finished, the organ went back into use in 1993, tuned in modified meantone at a1 = 495.45 Hz — a temperament read off the pipe lengths inside the pedal Principal 32′.

## Flight Context

53.5504° N, 10.0003° E, in the Hamburg city centre on Steinstraße, between the Mönckebergstraße shopping street and the Hauptbahnhof. St. Jacobi is one of Hamburg's five principal Lutheran churches, and from the air its dark green copper spire is the marker; the taller, better-known spire of St. Petri stands a few hundred metres west, and the ruined tower of St. Nikolai — deliberately left standing — is south of both, so the three read as a line across the Altstadt. The Binnenalster lake lies northwest, the Speicherstadt about a kilometre south. Best viewed from 1,500–2,500 ft AGL. Hamburg Airport (EDDH) is roughly 5 nm north; Hamburg-Finkenwerder (EDHI) about 7 nm west-southwest. Busy inner-city airspace with frequent low stratus off the Elbe.
