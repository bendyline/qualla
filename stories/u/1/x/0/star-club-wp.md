# Star-Club

A Hamburg cinema bought to satisfy a fire regulation became the loudest room in Europe - and the stage where the Beatles finished their apprenticeship.

**Location:** 53.5511, 9.9578

**Categories:** Music history, Hamburg, Nightclubs, The Beatles, Rock and roll, Lost buildings

## Introduction

The most famous rock club in Europe began as a paperwork problem. Manfred Weissleder, a former AEG technician who had become an electrician and then a businessman on the Kiez, owned several premises on the Paradieshof in St. Pauli. Building authorities told him he needed an emergency exit. The cheapest way to get one was to buy the property behind it - the old Stern-Kino, a cinema he had run himself - and knock through. Horst Fascher, a promoter who knew what young Hamburg wanted to hear, suggested doing something more interesting with the space than leaving it as a fire escape. On Friday 13 April 1962, the Star-Club opened at Grosse Freiheit 39.

## The Cinema They Barely Touched

They hardly bothered to convert it. The cinema's entrance, its balcony and most of its rooms stayed as they were, which is why the Star-Club held two thousand people in rows of cinema seating - a rock venue with the floor plan of a picture house. The programming was relentless. Through the club's first phase, until June 1964, seven bands played every night, one hour each, straight through. Fascher started as a waiter and ended as manager. For young British musicians the arrangement was brutal and educational in equal measure: hours on stage, night after night, in front of an audience that had heard the same songs from six other bands already and would not applaud out of politeness.

## Three Residencies in One Year

The Beatles opened the club and came back twice more inside the same year - 13 April to 31 May 1962, then 1 to 14 November for twenty-eight concerts, then 18 to 31 December. They arrived as a bar band still finding a drummer and left with the stamina that made the following decade possible. Somebody in the audience during the December run pointed an amateur tape machine at the stage. The results, rough and half-drunk and entirely alive, surfaced fifteen years later as Live! at the Star-Club in Hamburg, Germany; 1962. It remains the best surviving evidence of what the group sounded like before the studio got hold of them, and it exists only because the recording was never meant to be a record.

## Everyone Came Through Grosse Freiheit

The roster reads like a syllabus of early rock and roll. Ray Charles played here. So did Fats Domino, Bo Diddley, Bill Haley, Brenda Lee, the Everly Brothers, and Little Richard with a young Billy Preston in his band. Jerry Lee Lewis recorded a live album at the club in 1964 that is still argued about by people who care about such things. The Searchers came, and Cream, and Chicken Shack with Christine McVie, and the Liverbirds, one of the very few all-female rock bands of the era. In September 1966 an unknown amateur group from Hannover called the Scorpions turned up. The Jimi Hendrix Experience played in March 1967.

## The Label in the Room

In October 1962, Siegfried Loch came to the Star-Club to see Fats Domino. Loch was a label manager at Philips, and he left having talked Weissleder into letting him install recording equipment in the building. Star-Club Records followed as a Philips subsidiary, operating from 1964 to 1966 and often cutting records with the acts already booked into the room. It was an unusually direct arrangement: the club was the venue, the talent pool and the studio at once, and the tapes went out under the name over the door.

## Hardin & York, Midnight, 1969

The end was quiet. By 1969 the music had moved on and weeknight audiences were thin enough that the club could not cover the Groundhogs' fee. Weissleder closed it on 31 December 1969; the last band on the stage was Hardin & York. The building had a squalid afterlife as an erotic venue called the Salambo, then a fire in 1983 left it derelict, and it was pulled down in 1987. Weissleder himself did not see that - he died of heart failure in Hamburg-Altona in February 1980, fifty-two years old. What stands at Grosse Freiheit 39 today is a memorial stone listing the bands that played there, put up by the photographer Gunter Zint and his St. Pauli Museum. The first version had names wrong on it and had to be corrected, which is about right for a club whose greatest recording was made by accident.

## Flight Context

53.5511 N, 9.9578 E, on Grosse Freiheit off the Reeperbahn in St. Pauli, about a kilometre and a half northwest of the Landungsbrucken piers. There is nothing to see from altitude - the building is gone - but the street grid is easy to trace: the Reeperbahn runs east-west as a broad bright corridor just inland of the Elbe, with Grosse Freiheit branching north from its western end. Best oriented from 1,500 to 2,500 ft AGL on a river approach. Hamburg Airport (EDDH) is about 8 nm north-northeast; Hamburg Finkenwerder (EDHI) about 4 nm southwest across the water.
