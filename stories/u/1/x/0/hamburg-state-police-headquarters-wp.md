# Hamburg State Police Headquarters

For a decade the Gestapo ran northern Germany out of a police building on the Stadthausbrücke, and Hamburg is still arguing about how much of it should be given back to the people who were held in its cellars.

**Location:** 53.5531, 9.9859

**Categories:** Historical sites, World War II, Memorials, Nazi Germany, Hamburg

## Introduction

On 26 March 1933, in a building on the Stadthausbrücke that had housed Hamburg's police since 1814, a Communist member of the city parliament named Etkar André was asked whether he was willing to talk. He said no. Five other prisoners were made to watch what followed: a squad of men set on him with rubber truncheons and beat him until he could no longer speak, and when water was offered it was thrown in his face. Hamburg's Gauleiter, Karl Kaufmann, was in the room. André was executed in 1936. The details of that afternoon survive only because they were later read into an indictment.

## The Force Changes Hands

The takeover was almost instant. Immediately after the Reichstag election of 5 March 1933 the National Socialists seized control of the Hamburg police, and the force passed to a new police senator, Alfred Richter, who took office on 6 March and brought it into line at once. Little new law was needed: the Reichstag Fire Decree of 28 February had already stripped citizens of their basic liberties and allowed anyone deemed an opponent to be taken into “protective custody” indefinitely and without charge. Officers thought politically unreliable were dismissed or shunted aside, and their places went to Nazi transfers and unemployed SA and SS men. The force numbered 70 officers in March 1933 and 151 by the start of 1934. A core of long-serving detectives stayed on, which mattered: the expertise was inherited rather than invented, and it was turned on the city's own people.

## The Cellars

The political police, officially the Secret State Police from December 1935, worked out of the annexe at Stadthausbrücke 8 alongside ordinary police departments. Prisoners were beaten during interrogation to force confessions out of them. The cellars served as holding rooms, where people were kept and tortured, and where some were driven to their deaths. Between March 1933 and October 1934 alone, Hamburg state police arrested more than 5,000 Communists. Where do you put that many people? First the remand prison and an unused wing at Fuhlsbüttel, then, as the number in protective custody passed 1,750 by May 1933, a new camp at Wittmoor, and after its closure back to Fuhlsbüttel — a concentration camp from September 1933, run from 1936 as a police prison staffed by Gestapo officers. Prisoners were tortured there too. Hamburgers came to know the place by a contraction of its name: Kola-Fu.

## What the Departments Were For

The 1936 reorganisation made Hamburg a Staatspolizeileitstelle, a regional headquarters directing branch offices across northern Germany, and the division of labour inside it names its targets. The Jewish Department, independent from 1938, watched Jewish institutions, raided them, and compelled staff of the Jewish Religious Association to draw up deportation lists — forcing a community to write out its own. Between October 1941 and February 1945, 5,848 Jews were deported from the Hannoverscher Bahnhof in seventeen transports, and more than 5,000 of them were murdered. The Foreigners Department, some forty-five staff under Albert Schweim, supervised the more than 400,000 people brought to Hamburg from occupied countries as forced labourers, running informers through over 1,200 camps; a reported infraction could end in execution. One department went after teenagers. Of up to 1,500 Hamburg swing youths, whose offence was liking American dance music, more than 400 were arrested and as many as seventy sent to the camps.

## The Decree of 12 June 1942

That summer Heinrich Müller, head of Office IV of the Reich Security Main Office, issued a decree on “intensified interrogation” authorising officers to mistreat suspects, explicitly to the point of death. Hamburg set up a special department for it the following month under Detective Inspector Fritz Knuth. In October 1942 the RSHA sent two investigators from the Red Orchestra commission, Horst Kopkow and Walter Habecker, who arrived carrying arm and calf clamps. One Hamburg officer, criminal secretary Henry Helms, told a colleague the clamps were “a joy” and that it was a pleasure to watch “how people hop and jump”. From autumn 1943 the department went after the Hamburg White Rose: thirty arrested by March 1944, of whom eight did not live to see the end of the war. In spring 1945, with the British approaching, senior officers drew up three lists of Fuhlsbüttel prisoners — those to be released, those to be marched to Kiel, and those to be killed. The third list held seventy-one names. They were murdered at Neuengamme.

## The Argument Over the Room

By 1946 some 340 members of the Hamburg Gestapo were in custody and about forty still at large, but the reckoning was uneven. British military trials sent two former heads of the office, Blomberg and Kuhl, to their executions, and hanged the Fuhlsbüttel commandant Willi Tessmann. Bruno Streckenbach, who had run the Hamburg state police from 1933, came home from Soviet captivity in 1955 and lived out his life in his home city untroubled by the courts. Remembrance came slowly: a plaque at the main entrance in 1984, and in 2008 and 2009 three Stolpersteine set into the pavement for Gustav Schönherr, Carl Burmester and Wilhelm Prüll, three men who died inside the building. Then the city sold it. The 2008 agreement with the investor required a memorial of 750 square metres; what opened in 2018 was a bookshop with a café, with roughly fifty square metres left for remembrance. Survivors' organisations protested under a blunt slogan: consumption instead of memory, never. They were not being sentimental — the cellars are directly below. After the bookshop's insolvency in 2022 the Senate took a twenty-year lease and the memorial passed to the Hamburg memorials foundation. What the building was is still contested, which is better than the decades of silence that preceded it.

## Flight Context

Stadthausbrücke 8 stands at 53.5531 N, 9.9859 E, in Hamburg's Neustadt just west of the Rathausmarkt and a few hundred metres north of the Elbe. From 2,000-3,000 ft AGL the block is picked out by its round corner tower and cupola, rebuilt during the 2010s restoration, on the north bank of the Herrengrabenfleet between the city hall and the Michel's copper spire. Nearby references: the Alster lakes to the northeast, the Speicherstadt warehouses to the southeast. Hamburg Airport / Helmut Schmidt (EDDH) is about 8 km north-northeast; Hamburg-Finkenwerder (EDHI) lies across the Elbe to the west; Lübeck (EDHL) is roughly 55 km northeast. Note that central Hamburg is inside controlled airspace with noise-sensitive overflight restrictions. Maritime weather brings frequent low cloud and drizzle; best visibility usually follows a cold front.
