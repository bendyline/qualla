# St. Michael's Church, Hamburg

Hamburg's baroque Michel has burned, been struck by lightning, and been bombed - and a trumpeter still climbs its 132-metre tower twice a day to play a chorale into the wind.

**Location:** 53.5483, 9.9789

**Categories:** Churches, Baroque architecture, Hamburg, Landmarks, Music history, Rebuilt buildings

## Introduction

At ten in the morning, a man climbs to a small platform 83 metres above the Neustadt, opens four little windows, and plays a chorale on a trumpet - once toward the north, once east, once south, once west. He does it again at nine at night. On Sundays and church holidays he plays only at noon. The custom is roughly three centuries old, and it is one of the few things about St. Michael's Church that has never had to be rebuilt. Nearly everything else has. The 132-metre copper spire above him is the crown of the third church to stand on this ground, and even this one has been reconstructed twice.

## Struck at Eleven in the Morning

Hamburgers call it the Michel, and the bronze archangel over the portal - Michael with the devil beneath him - tells you which Michael. The first church here went up between 1647 and 1669, serving the Neustadt, the new town laid out inside the city walls in 1625 and filling steadily ever since. In 1687 the parish was formalised and the Michel became Hamburg's fifth Hauptkirche. It lasted sixty-three years. On 10 March 1750, around eleven in the morning, lightning found the tower. The fire smouldered unnoticed for about two hours before anyone understood what was happening, by which point nothing could be done. What the city had built in twenty-two years came down in an afternoon.

## Sonnin Finishes the Job

The foundation stone of the replacement was laid in 1751, to a design by Johann Leonhard Prey and Ernst Georg Sonnin. Prey died in 1757; Sonnin carried the work alone. The building was inaugurated in 1762 with an oratorio by Georg Philipp Telemann, who had run the city's music since 1721 and wrote for all five main churches. The tower was not topped out until 1786. What Sonnin left is the largest church in Hamburg - 2,500 seats, a Latin cross 52 metres long and 44 wide, vaulting 27 metres overhead - and, unusually, a Protestant building designed as one from the start, rather than a medieval Catholic church repurposed at the Reformation. The pulpit stands in the centre of the room, where the sermon rather than the altar sets the geometry. Sonnin himself is buried in the crypt below.

## Falling Objects and a Baptism

The tower has been useful for things other than worship. In 1802 the astronomer Johann Benzenberg used its interior shaft to test whether the Earth turns, dropping metal balls from a height and measuring how far east of the vertical they landed. On 26 May 1833, a Hamburg couple brought their infant son here to be baptised; the boy was Johannes Brahms, and he would be confirmed in the same church at fifteen by the pastor who had married his parents. Out on the Elbe, the spire did plainer work. Sheathed entirely in copper, it was the landfall mark for ships coming upriver, the first solid piece of Hamburg a crew would pick out. The four clock faces are eight metres across - the largest in Germany - with minute hands of 4.91 metres.

## The Nineteenth of October, Twice

On 3 July 1906, workers soldering damaged copper on the roof set the church alight. Reconstruction took six years, guided in part by the photographs Wilhelm Weimar had made of the interior, and the Michel reopened on 19 October 1912. Three decades later the nave was hit again, in 1944 and again in 1945, incendiaries punching through the roof. Repairs ran through the ruined postwar years, and when the church was reconsecrated it was on 19 October 1952 - the same calendar date, forty years on. Renovation has never really stopped: work on the spire began in 1983, then moved to the roof. A church made of copper, wood and marble in a wet North Sea climate is less a finished object than a standing obligation.

## Two Thousand Four Hundred and Twenty-Five Names

Beneath the floor lies the older Hamburg. The crypt holds 2,425 recorded burials, in chambers dug deep enough to stack four coffins vertically - merchant families, guilds, government offices, anyone whose burial fund reached far enough. The composers Johann Mattheson and Carl Philipp Emanuel Bach are down there. Burials stopped in 1813, when the French occupation banned interments inside the city. Coffin carts still stand where they were left. During the Second World War the crypt served as an air-raid shelter, and in 2004 researchers opened some of the graves and found intact coffins with preserved textiles and upholstery. Renovated in the early 2000s, the space now hosts services and concerts, and since October 2008 an organ named for Felix Mendelssohn Bartholdy - one of five in the building, alongside a Steinmeyer of 6,674 pipes.

## Flight Context

53.5483 N, 9.9789 E, on the northern bank of the Elbe in Hamburg-Neustadt. The 132-metre copper spire is the classic visual fix for the inner city - approach from the southwest along the river and the Michel appears just inland of the Landungsbrucken piers, with the Speicherstadt warehouses and the Elbphilharmonie to its southeast. Best appreciated from 1,500 to 3,000 ft AGL; below that the spire competes with the harbour cranes, above it the copper green flattens against the roofscape. Hamburg Airport (EDDH) lies about 8 nm north-northeast; Hamburg Finkenwerder (EDHI) is 5 nm west across the river. Low stratus and drizzle are common year-round off the North Sea - the clearest air usually follows a cold front out of the northwest.
