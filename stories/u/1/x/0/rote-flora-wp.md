# Rote Flora

Hamburg tried to put The Phantom of the Opera into a derelict Schanzenviertel theatre; the neighbourhood took the building instead, and has held it since 1989.

**Location:** 53.5620, 9.9615

**Categories:** Squats in Germany, Autonomism, Counterculture communities, Former theatres in Germany, History of Hamburg, Politics of Hamburg, Infoshops

## Introduction

The plan was Andrew Lloyd Webber. Cats had been running at the Operettenhaus on the Reeperbahn since April 1986, and the city agreed to hand its producers a second house for Das Phantom der Oper — the derelict Flora theatre at Schulterblatt 71, which had spent decades as a discount store. Residents of the Schanzenviertel wanted a community centre there and said so, loudly. The city ignored them and demolished the rear of the building in April 1988. Sabotage followed on the construction site, and that September the producers announced they had found somewhere better; the Phantom eventually opened in a purpose-built theatre over in Altona-Nord. The old Flora was occupied instead, and it has stayed occupied ever since.

## A Theatre, a Cinema, a Thousand Pots

The building went up in 1888 and 1889 for the merchants Theodor Mutzenbecher and Lerch, opening as the Flora-Theater on 2 June 1889. It spent its first decades as a concert hall and theatre, then took whatever the market offered: operetta, revue, variety, boxing matches. After the war it became a cinema, reopening in 1953 as the Flora-Filmpalast. From 1964 it sold cheap housewares under the name 1000 Töpfe — a thousand pots. By the time Friedrich Kurz's musical scheme arrived in 1987, the place had been a shop for more than twenty years and was visibly failing. What made it worth fighting over was never the architecture. It was the address, at the centre of a working-class quarter that could feel what a West End musical would do to its rents.

## 1 November 1989

Under local pressure, the community was granted a temporary lease to use the building. It expired on 1 November 1989 — eight days before the Berlin Wall opened — and the occupiers simply did not leave. The Rote Flora has run as a self-managed social centre ever since, housing a music venue, an infoshop, a bar, a café, rehearsal rooms, a bicycle workshop, and, since 1998, an archive of social movements. Hamburg's government offered the project a formal contract in the early 1990s and was refused. The collective explained itself in 2001 with a line it has never bettered: "We are the 'UFO in the neighbourhood.' The black hole in public space. The City won't get rid of us because we are a part of what life is."

## 370,000 Marks and a Buyback

In 2001 the city sold the building to a property dealer, Klausmartin Kretschmer, for 370,000 Deutschmarks, on condition that it remain a social centre. The occupiers declined to acknowledge him at all: "We neither asked Kretschmer to buy the Flora almost nine years ago, nor are we in the slightest interested in his opinions about the political ideologies and the work of the Rote Flora." Hamburg later offered 1.1 million euros to buy it back, and Kretschmer asked for five million. In December 2013 the city's redevelopment plans set off large and sometimes violent demonstrations; the following January the Altona borough reversed itself and the building was taken off the demolition list. That November, under mayor Olaf Scholz, the city bought it back for 820,000 euros through the Lawaetz foundation. Two security guards afterwards swore under oath that Kretschmer had wanted to pay them to burn the place down.

## G20

In July 2017 the G20 summit came to Hamburg and the Schanzenviertel saw a night of rioting and looting a few hundred metres from the Flora's door. The building immediately became the target of political demands for its closure. The collective distanced itself from the violence in unusually plain language: "a form of militancy was brought onto the streets which was intoxicated with itself, and we find that both politically and in terms of content wrong." Whether that separation is genuine or convenient is the argument Hamburg has been having about this address for more than thirty years, and neither side has ever quite won it.

## Achidi-John-Platz 1

The collective gave itself its own address: Achidi-John-Platz 1, for Achidi John, who died in 2001 after Hamburg police forced him to swallow an emetic. The rules posted at the door in 2018 are equally direct — no sexism, racism, anti-Semitism, ableism, homophobia, transphobia, lookism, or boundary-crossing behaviour of any kind. Outside, the neighbourhood has turned into exactly what the 1988 protesters predicted. The Schanzenviertel is now one of Hamburg's most thoroughly gentrified quarters, and the Rote Flora has become a fixture on alternative-tourism itineraries, its graffitied façade photographed by people who then eat somewhere expensive. A former police officer who studies left-wing politics has noted the quieter cost: the hours activists spend keeping a large, old, half-ruined building standing are hours not spent on anything else.

## Flight Context

53.5620° N, 9.9615° E, at Schulterblatt 71 in the Sternschanze quarter, roughly 1.5 km north of the Reeperbahn. From the air the landmark is not the Flora itself — a single low, heavily painted building in a dense block — but the Schanzenturm, the tall brick water tower rising out of Sternschanzenpark just to the north, now a hotel. The Messehallen exhibition halls and the green of Planten un Blomen lie immediately east, and the Heiligengeistfeld and Millerntor-Stadion south of those. Best viewed from 1,500–2,000 ft AGL. Hamburg Airport (EDDH) is about 4 nm north-northeast, Hamburg-Finkenwerder (EDHI) about 5 nm southwest. Inner-city airspace, frequent low cloud, and dense low-rise roofscape that makes individual buildings hard to pick out without the water tower as a reference.
