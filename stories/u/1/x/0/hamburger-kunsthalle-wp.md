# Hamburger Kunsthalle

Three buildings a century apart, seven centuries of European art, and the most famous back in painting — Friedrich's Wanderer hangs here, along with questions the museum has not finished answering.

**Location:** 53.5550, 10.0031

**Categories:** Museums, Art, Architecture, Hamburg, Culture

## Introduction

The most famous back in European painting is in Hamburg. A man in a green coat stands on a rock with his weight on one leg, looking away from us into a sea of fog, and because Caspar David Friedrich never shows his face, two centuries of viewers have been able to climb into the coat themselves. Wanderer above the Sea of Fog, painted in 1818, has ended up on more book jackets and record sleeves than almost any picture of its century. It hangs, without any particular ceremony, in a red-brick building wedged between Hamburg's main railway station and the Alster.

## Three Buildings, One Museum

The Kunsthalle is not a building but three of them, joined together with roughly a century between each seam. It began as a collection rather than a house. The Hamburg Kunstverein, founded in 1817, started assembling works in 1849, and in 1850 they went on public view as the Städtische Galerie. The name Kunsthalle — art hall — is a fossil of that moment. The collection outgrew its rooms fast, and between 1863 and 1869 Georg Theodor Schirrmacher and Hermann von der Hude put up the red-brick original, paid for largely by private donation, which in a merchants' republic was simply how civic things got done. The first director was the art historian and educator Alfred Lichtwark (1852–1914); his successor between the wars, Gustav Pauli, saw Fritz Schumacher's domed Kuppelsaal finished between 1914 and 1921. Then, in 1997, the Cologne architect Oswald Mathias Ungers added the Galerie der Gegenwart: 5,600 square metres of contemporary art in a pale cube set on a monolithic base near the Binnenalster, about as unlike its neighbour as a building can be while still shaking its hand.

## Seven Centuries Under One Roof

Few German collections cover as much ground. The Kunsthalle runs from the Middle Ages to the present across four galleries — Old Masters, nineteenth century, classical modernism, contemporary. Its oldest strength is North German panel painting: the gold-ground altarpieces of Master Bertram and Master Francke, made for Hamburg churches by artists who worked in Hamburg. Beyond them come the Dutch, Flemish and Italian pictures of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, with Rembrandt, Rubens, Ruisdael, Tiepolo and Goya among them. The nineteenth-century rooms are the heart of the place. Friedrich's Wanderer is here, and so is his Sea of Ice of 1824, where slabs of ice have shoved themselves into a pyramid over a crushed ship. Manet's Nana of 1877 hangs a few rooms away, alongside Courbet, Monet, Degas, Menzel, Liebermann and Runge, and Makart's vast Entry of Charles V into Antwerp of 1878. Then Munch's Madonna, Kirchner, Klee, Beckmann, Nolde, Picasso; then Beuys, Warhol, Serra, Rebecca Horn, Hockney, On Kawara. Roughly twenty temporary exhibitions open each year.

## The Painting That Had to Be Bought Back

In 1994 the Kunsthalle lent a Friedrich, Nebelschwaden, to the Schirn Kunsthalle in Frankfurt, and it was stolen there in what became known as the Frankfurt art theft. Recovery took an unusual route. After negotiations with the thieves, a lawyer bought the painting back. What happened next is the part nobody puts on the wall label: the Kunsthalle declined to pay him the consideration it had agreed, and the lawyer sued the museum and won. The picture is in Hamburg. The bill was settled in court.

## Whose Picture Is It

Provenance is the unfinished business of every major German collection, and the Kunsthalle's is not settled. In December 2023 the museum drew public criticism over its handling of a restitution claim brought by the family of Robert Graetz, a Jewish collector who was murdered in the Holocaust, concerning Paula Modersohn-Becker's Girl's Head. The painting had been listed on the German Lost Art Foundation's database for more than twenty years. It passed through the hands of the Nazi-era dealer Konrad Doebbeke and reached the museum as a gift from Doebbeke's widow. The director, Alexander Klar, rejected the claim, saying it was simply not clear whether this work was the one that had hung in the Graetz collection. The family reads the same evidence differently. Cases like this do not close with a press statement, and this one has not.

## Flight Context

The Kunsthalle sits at 53.5550 N, 10.0031 E, in Hamburg's Altstadt, immediately northwest of the Hauptbahnhof and just south of the Außenalster. From 2,000–3,000 ft AGL, look for the rail yards and the great arched train shed of the Hauptbahnhof, then the two joined brick blocks and Schumacher's dome directly north of the tracks; Ungers's pale cube stands slightly north again, closest to the Binnenalster. The Alster lakes are the dominant visual anchor for the whole quarter, with the Heinrich-Hertz-Turm to the west and the Michel's copper spire southwest. Hamburg Airport / Helmut Schmidt (EDDH) lies about 8 km north-northeast; Hamburg-Finkenwerder (EDHI) is on the south bank of the Elbe to the west; Lübeck (EDHL) is roughly 55 km northeast. Central Hamburg is inside controlled airspace. Expect maritime cloud and drizzle, with best light on clear days behind a cold front.
