# Hanseatic League

A confederation with no capital, no army and no written constitution ran the trade of northern Europe for three centuries, then held a final meeting in 1669 that hardly anyone bothered to attend.

**Location:** 53.8697, 10.6864

**Categories:** History, Trade, Medieval Europe, Baltic Sea, Germany, Lübeck, Hanseatic League

## Introduction

In 1669 the manager of the Hanseatic trading post in London wrote home asking for money. The Great Fire had taken the Steelyard three years earlier - warehouses, weigh house, church, the houses the merchants lived in - and rebuilding it would cost more than the post could raise. Hamburg, Bremen and Lübeck called a Hanseatic Day to discuss the problem. Only a few cities sent anyone, and those who came were reluctant to pay. The delegates went home. Nobody in the room understood that they had just held the last diet of the organisation that had run the commerce of the North and Baltic seas for three centuries.

## A League With No Capital

The Hanseatic League was never a country, and it is easier to say what it lacked than what it was. No standing army. No treasury. No written constitution. What it had was a network of merchant guilds and market towns that grew out of Lübeck and other north German ports in the late twelfth century and, across the thirteenth to fifteenth, drew in close to two hundred settlements spread over what are now eight countries - from Estonia and Russia west to the Netherlands. The word Hanse turns up in a document in 1267. Its single central institution was the Hansetag, the diet, which probably began meeting around 1300 and was formalised by 1358; it worked by consensus, and its decisions bound only those cities whose delegates had been in the room. The seed was planted in 1241, when Lübeck and Hamburg allied and between them took the salt-fish trade. Cologne joined their diet in 1260.

## Four Doors Into Four Kingdoms

Where the league did put down roots, it built Kontore - fortified merchant colonies inside foreign cities, living under their own rules. Four mattered. At Novgorod, on the Volkhov, the Peterhof was running by the first half of the thirteenth century, on ground where Gotland merchants had kept a post called Gutagard since about 1080. In London the Steelyard stood west of London Bridge and grew into a walled town of its own, with warehouses, a weigh house, a church, offices and homes. Bruges hosted a Kontor that, unusually, owned no buildings until the fifteenth century. And at Bergen the wooden wharf of Bryggen housed the stockfish trade, sending dried cod south and taking grain in return. Bryggen outlasted the rest, running until 1754, and its buildings are the only Kontor structures still standing.

## What the Cogs Carried

The cargo lists explain the map. Eastward from Flanders and England came cloth - broadcloth above all - and, as the centuries went on, manufactured goods. Westward came the produce of forest and field: beeswax, furs, timber, resin and tar, flax, honey, wheat and rye. Metal ore travelled from Swedish mines and the Carpathians. Herring came out of the north in barrels, and the salt to pack it came overland from Lüneburg or by ship from France and Portugal. Moving all of it was the cog, the fat, high-sided workhorse of the medieval Baltic. None of this was glamorous freight. It was food, fuel, cloth and preservative - the things a northern winter demands - and the league's power came from being the only organisation that could guarantee their delivery.

## Stralsund, and Everything After

Trade that valuable eventually attracts a king. Between 1361 and 1370 the league fought Denmark, and in 1368 towns from Prussia and the Netherlands joined the Wendish cities in the Confederation of Cologne. They sacked Copenhagen and Helsingborg, and in the peace signed at Stralsund in 1370 King Valdemar IV and his son-in-law Haakon VI of Norway granted the league tax exemptions and control over the Øresund fortresses for fifteen years - control, in other words, of the gate between the Baltic and the world. Historians have called the league a northern European great power for this period, and it is the only stretch where the phrase sits comfortably. The unravelling began almost immediately. When Albert of Sweden was taken prisoner in 1389 he hired privateers, the Victual Brothers, who seized Bornholm and Visby in his name and then went into business for themselves, preying on Baltic shipping from 1392 into the 1430s and holding Gotland until the Teutonic Order dislodged them in 1398.

## Four Closed Doors

The rest of the decline was a matter of doors shutting one by one. Flanders and Holland passed to Burgundy and began excluding Lübeck from the grain trade; from the late fifteenth century Amsterdam, no Hanseatic city, was the leading port for Polish and Baltic grain. In 1494 Ivan III closed the Novgorod Kontor - only forty-nine traders were there to be deported to Moscow. It reopened in 1514, but Novgorod was no longer a hub. The Zwin inlet silted and Bruges strangled, its Kontor moving to Antwerp in 1520 and closing in 1593. Elizabeth I expelled the league from London in 1597 and the Steelyard was sequestered the following year; it returned under James I in 1606 and never recovered. Which is how a request for rebuilding money in 1669 came to be the league's last act. By the formal end in 1862 only Hamburg, Bremen and Lübeck remained. Until reunification those three alone carried Hanseatic City in their official German names; since 1990 another twenty-four German towns have claimed the title back. The New Hanse, whose Hanseatic Days began at Zwolle in 1980, still gathers them each summer. A festival now, not a trading bloc - but roughly the same guest list.

## Flight Context

The league had no single address, so its nominal coordinates are Lübeck's: 53.8697 N, 10.6864 E. To fly the Hansa is to fly a coastline. From Lübeck the Wendish arc runs east along the German Baltic shore through Wismar, Rostock and Stralsund, then to Gdańsk, Kaliningrad, Riga and Tallinn - roughly 700 nm end to end, all of it low country where the brick church towers are the tallest things for miles and make excellent visual waypoints. The four Kontore lie at the compass points: Bergen (ENBR) in Norway, where the Bryggen wharf still fronts the Vågen harbour; Bruges, inland from Ostend (EBOS) and now cut off from the sea by the silted Zwin; London, where the Steelyard stood on the north bank west of London Bridge, under the City approach to London City (EGLC); and Veliky Novgorod, southeast of St Petersburg (ULLI). Lübeck's own field is EDHL, 8 km southwest of the old town, with Hamburg (EDDH) 55 km further southwest; Riga is EVRA and Tallinn EETN. Fly the German coast at 2,000-3,000 ft AGL. Baltic summer haze softens the horizon; the crispest air comes with a cold winter high.
