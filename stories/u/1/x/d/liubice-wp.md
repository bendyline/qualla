# Liubice

Four kilometres downstream from Lübeck's spires, an Obotrite prince held a fortified harbour called Liubice - the lovely place - until a Rani fleet burned its church in 1138 and left the ground clear for somebody else's city.

**Location:** 53.9078, 10.7144

**Categories:** Archaeology, History, Medieval Europe, Slavic History, Germany, Schleswig-Holstein, Lübeck

## Introduction

The oldest timbers in the rampart were felled in 819. That single date, recovered by archaeologists in the 1970s from wood buried at the confluence of the Schwartau and the Trave, pushed the story of this place back two centuries beyond what anyone had assumed. There is not much to see now - low ground four kilometres north of Lübeck's old town, across from Teerhof island, the sort of field you would fly over without a second glance. For roughly a hundred years it was the seat of the princes of the Obotrites, and it had a name of its own long before it had a German one. Liubice. It means lovely.

## The People Who Were Already Here

West Slavic groups moved into the Bay of Lübeck from the seventh century onward, as Germanic populations migrated away. The Wagrians and the Polabians built villages and strongholds across the region - Starigard, Plune, Racisburg, Liubice among them - and they were not a scattering of hamlets waiting for history to arrive. The Obotrite confederacy was a polity: it had princes and dynasties, tribute, fleets to fight and fleets to fear, treaties with Saxon counts, and by the eleventh century a Christian ruling house. Liubice itself sat in the borderland where Wagrian, Polabian and Obotrite territory met, which is precisely what made it a useful place for a prince to sit. It stayed thinly populated through the ninth and tenth centuries. In the middle of the eleventh it began to grow.

## Gottschalk, Kruto, Henry

The century that followed reads as a family quarrel conducted with armies. From 1055 the old castle was rebuilt under Gottschalk, a Christian prince of the Obotrite confederacy. He was killed in an uprising in 1066 and succeeded by Kruto, who was not a Christian. The fortifications were reworked a second time in 1087, near the end of Kruto's reign: because the site occupied a peninsula between the two rivers, the builders cut a trench twelve metres wide across the neck and turned Liubice into an island. In 1093 Gottschalk's son Henry killed Kruto, avenged his father, and took the title the chroniclers render as King of the Slavs. He made this harbour his residence, and for a generation it was the political centre of the southwestern Baltic.

## A Small Fortress, a Mixed Town

Henry's castle was not large - its walls enclosed a circle roughly seventy-five to a hundred metres across - but it was densely used. Around 1100 he built a stone church inside it, the earliest identifiable stone construction anywhere in the region, and members of the princely family were buried within its walls. The hall probably stood northwest of the church, a granary east of it, cattle stalls southeast against the ramparts, with houses for the garrison and, on the evidence, workshops for goldsmiths or a mint. A wooden earthwork ringed the whole thing, pierced by a gate to the south. Outside the walls the settlement spread by rank: craftsmen immediately southwest under the castle's protection, a poorer quarter across the trench to the northwest that was probably where servants lived, and southwest across the Trave a colony of foreign merchants, mostly Saxons, who were permitted a church of their own. A Slavic prince, a Christian chapel, German traders and a harbour, all within sight of each other.

## Burned Twice

Around 1100 the fleet of the Rani, the pagan Slavs of Rügen, laid siege to Liubice. Henry called in Saxons from Holstein and forced the Rani to pay tribute instead. It was a reprieve rather than a settlement. Henry died in 1127; the Nakonid dynasty ended with him and the Obotrite state came apart, his lands divided between Niklot and Pribislav. The Rani came back the following year and sacked the place. Pribislav was defeated by Henry of Badewide in the late 1130s and reduced to a Saxon vassal in Wagria, and in 1138 a further Rani campaign ravaged Liubice and the Oldenburg region and destroyed the castle church - the stone building that had been the region's first. Nobody rebuilt it. When the church was rediscovered in 1852 it had been in the ground for seven centuries, and excavation later showed that a wooden church had stood there before it.

## Why Lübeck Sits Where It Does

In 1143 Duke Henry the Lion granted Wagria and Segeberg to Count Adolf II of Holstein, who founded a new German settlement four kilometres upstream on a peninsula called Bucu, where the Wakenitz joins the Trave. That is the site of Lübeck. The choice was not arbitrary: Adolf was taking over a river system whose value the Obotrites had established, and moving to a defensible island a little further from the open water. The older place did not simply vanish from the new one. Slavic inhabitants of the region continued to hold their assemblies at Lübeck's Marienkirche into the thirteenth century, and elements of older Slavic law were folded into Lübeck's own city code - the Lübeck law that would eventually be adopted by around a hundred towns around the Baltic. The archaeology keeps adding depth. Dendrochronology has dated repairs and activity inside the castle to 1002 and 1035, and wall sections to 1055 and 1087, layered over that first rampart of 819.

## Flight Context

Liubice lay at 53.9078 N, 10.7144 E, where the Schwartau meets the Trave opposite Teerhof island, about 4 km north of Lübeck's old town and roughly 10 km inland from the Baltic at Travemünde. There is no standing structure - the site is a protected archaeological ground, so what you are looking for is topography rather than architecture: the low tongue of land in the fork of the two rivers, with the Trave widening downstream toward the industrial quays of Kücknitz and Herrenwyk. The relationship is the point, and it only reads from the air. Fly the Trave upstream at 1,000-1,500 ft AGL and you can hold both places in one view - the empty confluence, then four kilometres later the seven brick spires on their island. Nearest airport is Lübeck (EDHL), 12 km south-southwest, with Hamburg (EDDH) 60 km southwest; EDHL's airspace covers the corridor, so call ahead. Low sun in spring or autumn brings out crop marks and the faint ring of the old rampart better than midday light does.
