# Lübeck

Six of Lübeck's church towers stand over a hundred metres high - more than any other city on earth - and on one night in 1942 the RAF set out to prove that the medieval town beneath them would burn.

**Location:** 53.8697, 10.6864

**Categories:** Cities, History, Germany, Schleswig-Holstein, UNESCO World Heritage, Brick Gothic, Hanseatic League, Literature

## Introduction

No city in the world has more church towers above a hundred metres than Lübeck. Six clear that mark, on an island barely two kilometres long, and the seven spires that give the place its old nickname - die Stadt der sieben Türme - are visible from the Baltic before the town itself resolves. They were built from clay, because northern Germany has no building stone worth quarrying. What the merchants of the Hanseatic League had instead was mud, fire and money - and with all three they raised a skyline that a hundred other Baltic towns spent three centuries copying.

## Königin der Hanse

Count Adolf II of Schauenburg and Holstein founded a settlement on the river island of Bucu in 1143. Fire destroyed it in 1157; Henry the Lion took the site in 1159, rebuilt it, and moved the bishopric from Oldenburg a year later. After Henry's fall in 1181 Frederick Barbarossa confirmed the city's privileges, and in 1226 Frederick II granted imperial immediacy - Lübeck answered to the emperor and nobody nearer. From the mid-thirteenth century it led the Hanseatic League, and after Visby fell in 1361 it became the league's principal seat - the Königin der Hanse. Its greatest export was not salt or herring but a legal code: Lübeck law was adopted by around a hundred Baltic towns, so a merchant from Reval and a merchant from Wismar argued their disputes under the same rules. The crown slipped in the 1530s, when Lübeck's intervention in the Count's Feud failed, and with it the city's last bid to control Baltic trade.

## Everything Made of Clay

Brick Gothic is Lübeck's contribution to architecture, and the cathedral is where it starts: begun in 1173, finished in 1335, the first large brick church in the Baltic region. St Mary's, raised between 1265 and 1351, became the model most Brick Gothic churches in the Hanseatic sphere were built against. Over eighty per cent of the old town still holds its medieval pattern of narrow lanes and courtyards, and two of the four town gates remain: the Holstentor of 1478 and the Burgtor of 1444. Along the Trave stand the Salzspeicher, gabled warehouses where salt hauled overland from Lüneburg waited for ships - salt being what turned perishable herring into a commodity sellable a thousand miles inland. At Koberg sits the Hospital of the Holy Spirit of 1286, one of the oldest surviving social institutions in the world, still a home for the elderly seven centuries on.

## The Night of 28 March 1942

Lübeck was chosen because it would burn: a cultural centre rather than an industrial one, lightly defended, its timber-framed old town a suitable place to test incendiary tactics under the Area Bombing Directive. On the night of 28 March 1942, 234 Wellingtons and Stirlings dropped some 400 tons of bombs, including 25,000 incendiaries and a number of 1.8-tonne landmines. A corridor of destruction roughly 300 metres wide ran from the cathedral to St Peter's. Three main churches were gutted; the bells of St Mary's fell and melted where they landed. In all 1,468 buildings were destroyed and more than 11,000 damaged. Police recorded 301 dead, three missing, 783 injured; over 15,000 lost their homes. Germany answered with the Baedeker raids on English cathedral towns, starting at Exeter on 23 April. Three years later, on 3 May 1945, RAF aircraft attacked ships in the Bay of Lübeck carrying prisoners evacuated from Neuengamme concentration camp. The Cap Arcona and the Thielbek burned and capsized, and more than 7,000 people who had already survived the camps died within sight of shore, days before the war ended.

## The Organ Loft and the Novel

Franz Tunder, organist at St Mary's, began the weekly Abendmusiken. In 1668 his daughter Anna Margarethe married Dieterich Buxtehude, who took over the post; Handel and Mattheson came to hear him play in 1703, Bach in 1705. Two and a half centuries later Thomas Mann, born into a Lübeck merchant family, published Buddenbrooks in 1901 and gave German readers - and then, in translation, the world - an unsparing portrait of the nineteenth-century Lübeck bourgeoisie he came from. The Nobel followed in 1929. His brother Heinrich wrote novels too. Willy Brandt, later Chancellor, was born here in 1913. Günter Grass, another Nobel laureate, lived at nearby Behlendorf from 1995 until his burial there in 2015, and put one of the city's stranger episodes into his 1986 novel The Rat: Lothar Malskat, hired to conserve medieval frescoes the bombing had uncovered at St Mary's, painted new ones instead, passed them off as recoveries, fooled the experts, then exposed the fraud himself.

## Almonds, and What Was Rebuilt

Lübeck's other famous export is marzipan. Local legend holds it was invented here during a siege or famine, when the city had nothing left but stored almonds and sugar and baked loaves of sweet paste instead of bread - probably apocryphal, since marzipan appears to have reached Europe from Persia centuries earlier, and told anyway. Niederegger on Breite Strasse is the best-known maker, with a museum of wooden moulds above its café. Rotspon is older still - French wine shipped north in barrel, then aged and bottled here, a Hanseatic habit that never stopped. Reconstruction began in 1949 and has never entirely stopped. In 1987 UNESCO inscribed the historic centre - 81.1 hectares, with a 693.8-hectare buffer - as a World Heritage Site, the most extensive medieval urban ensemble in Germany. Around 217,000 people live here, and in 2019 the city logged two million overnight stays.

## Flight Context

Lübeck sits at 53.8697 N, 10.6864 E, about 50 km northeast of Hamburg on the river Trave, some 15 km inland from the Baltic at Travemünde. From the air the old town is unmistakable: an almond-shaped island ringed by water, the Trave to the west and the Wakenitz to the east, with seven green copper spires clustered on it and nothing else nearby that reaches anything like their height. The Holstentor guards the western bridge, the Burgtor the northern tip; the Salzspeicher line the Trave bank in a row of stepped gables. Fly at 2,000-3,000 ft AGL to hold the whole island in one frame, higher if you want Travemünde and the Bay of Lübeck in the same picture. Nearest airport is Lübeck (EDHL), 8 km southwest of the centre, with controlled airspace over the approach - get clearance before overflying the Altstadt. Hamburg (EDDH) is 55 km southwest. Baltic haze is common in summer; the copper roofs read greenest under overcast, while low winter sun off the water is the best light for the brick.
