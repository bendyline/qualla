# Hindenburgdamm

An eleven-kilometre causeway across the Wadden Sea with rails and no road — which is why every car going to Sylt has to ride a train.

**Location:** 54.8836, 8.5244

**Categories:** Railways, Causeways, Engineering, Schleswig-Holstein, North Sea, Islands, Transport

## Introduction

There is no road to Sylt. If you want to take your car to Germany's most fashionable island, you drive to a terminal at Niebüll, queue with several hundred other drivers, and are waved up a ramp onto an open flatcar. Then you sit in your own vehicle, engine off, while a diesel locomotive hauls the whole train eleven kilometres across the open tidal flats of the Wadden Sea. The crossing takes about ten minutes. More than 100 trains use the Hindenburgdamm every day, around half of them carrying cars, and something over 450,000 vehicles a year make the trip this way. It is one of the strangest routine journeys in Europe, and it exists because of a border.

## A Line Moved, an Island Cut Off

Before the causeway, getting to Sylt meant a paddlesteamer. The west coast railway ran north from Altona through Husum and Niebüll to Tondern, and from there tracks continued to the port at Hoyerschleuse, where boats crossed to Munkmarsch on Sylt. The service depended entirely on the tide, and in hard winters the ice in the Wadden Sea shut it down completely. Westerland had become a town in 1905 and was growing into a serious seaside resort, so by 1910 the authorities were planning a rail causeway in earnest. The war stopped everything. Then the peace made it urgent: under the postwar settlement, Tønder and Hoyerschleuse went to Denmark. Sylt stayed German, but the only practical route to it now ran through another country, and a short trip to the beach meant applying for a Danish visa.

## Building on Water

Construction began in 1923 and ran for four years. Philipp Holzmann AG of Frankfurt worked outward from the mainland; Peter Fix Söhne of Duisburg worked back from Sylt. Early on, a storm surge simply erased what had been built, and the engineers responded by shifting the alignment further north and driving a trench-like cofferdam ahead of the works. Between 1,000 and 1,500 men were employed. They moved more than three million cubic metres of sand and Klei — the heavy grey clay of the tidal flats — and dumped 120,000 tonnes of stone to armour the flanks. On 1 June 1927 the causeway opened. Reichspräsident Paul von Hindenburg came for the ceremony and rode the first train across; at the breakfast afterwards, the railway official Julius Dorpmüller gave the structure his name.

## Diesel and Dune Grass

The Hindenburgdamm carries the Marschbahn, the Marsh Railway, double-tracked for most of its length though single-tracked in places, with a signal box standing alone out on the causeway. The line is not electrified, so trains from Hamburg and further south swap their electric locomotive for a diesel at Itzehoe before continuing north. Two operators run the car shuttles: DB's Sylt Shuttle in red and RDC's Autozug Sylt in blue, both loading at Niebüll and unloading at Westerland thirty minutes later. Riding it is not scenic in the conventional sense. On one side and then the other there is water, or mud, or a sheet of something in between, depending on the hour — and then dune grass, and then Sylt.

## The Sea Keeps Score

Before 1927 the tide moved freely through the channel between Sylt and the mainland. The causeway put a wall across it, and the water has been rearranging itself ever since. The interruption to tidal flow is understood to be part of why Sylt keeps losing ground at its southern tip, at Hörnum Odde, where the shoreline has been in retreat for decades. The causeway itself now lies inside Zone I of the Schleswig-Holstein Wadden Sea National Park, the most strictly protected category, which is why the mudflat walks that are a regional institution everywhere else along this coast are forbidden here. Not everything about the crossing has been peaceful either: on 3 September 2009 a squall blew a truck off a flatcar mid-causeway, throwing the driver from the cab and killing him.

## A Name Nobody Agrees On

By the 1960s the name had become a problem. Hindenburg was the Reichspräsident who appointed Hitler chancellor, and Germany spent the postwar decades reconsidering what it wanted written on its infrastructure. Alternatives were proposed and argued over — Sylt-Damm, Friedens-Damm for peace, Friesen-Damm for the Frisians whose coast this is — and none of them ever gathered a majority. Deutsche Bahn has since floated Syltdamm again. The debate has now outlasted several generations of locomotive, which may be the most German outcome available. In the meantime the trains keep running, roughly every hour, plus the shuttles, plus the intercity services, and nearly a century of cars have crossed the Wadden Sea sitting perfectly still.

## Flight Context

The Hindenburgdamm runs east-west at approximately 54.8836°N, 8.5244°E, joining Klanxbüll on the mainland to Morsum on Sylt across roughly 11 km of the North Frisian Wadden Sea. From the air it is a razor-straight artificial line drawn across an otherwise organic landscape of tidal channels, sandbanks and mudflats — the geometry gives it away instantly, and at low tide the surrounding flats read as a braided silver-brown maze. Best viewed from 2,000-3,000 ft AGL on a north-south track; time it near low water for the strongest contrast. Nearest airfields: Sylt (EDXW) at Westerland, about 15 km northwest; Flensburg-Schäferhaus (EDXF) roughly 55 km east. The area lies within a strictly protected national park zone, so respect minimum-altitude and bird-sanctuary restrictions. North Sea coastal weather is changeable; strong westerlies and low cloud are common, and the same squalls that trouble the trains make for turbulent low-level flying.
