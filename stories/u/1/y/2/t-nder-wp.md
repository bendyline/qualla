# Tønder

Denmark's oldest market town lost its harbour to a dyke, replaced it with twenty thousand lacemakers, hosted the target of the first carrier air raid in history, and voted to stay German in 1920 anyway.

**Location:** 54.9428, 8.8639

**Categories:** town, Denmark, lace, border history, aviation history, music festival

## Introduction

In 1801, twenty thousand people in Tønder and the villages around it made lace by hand. The town itself held a few thousand. Bobbins clattered in cottages across the whole southwestern corner of Jutland, turning out a point-ground lace with honeycomb fillings and small square tallies worked into the mesh — tønderkniplinger, a pattern specific enough to this one border town that it still carries its name. The tall gabled houses along the main street are what that work bought. Then machines learned to do it, and an industry that had employed a district became a hobby.

## Twenty Thousand Pairs of Hands

The trade did not grow here by accident. In 1647 a merchant brought lacemakers from Westphalia to Tønder to teach the craft, and it took hold in a place that badly needed something to sell. Tønder lace is bobbin lace of the point-ground type, painstaking work done at a pillow with dozens of threads in play at once, and for a century and a half it was the region's cash crop. Peak employment came around 1801. Machine-made lace arrived in the late nineteenth century and the economics collapsed almost immediately — no hand can compete with a loom on price. The craft never actually died, though. It is still worked in Tønder today, mostly by hobbyists, and the town produced its own authority on it: Hansigne Lorenzen, writer and lace expert, born in 1870 and buried in 1952.

## A Market Town Without a Harbour

Tønder is Denmark's oldest privileged market town, granted Lübeck market rights in 1243, and for three centuries it was a port. The sea reminded the town of the arrangement in 1532, when a storm surge pushed water 1.8 metres deep into the church — 5.3 metres above normal level. The dykes that followed solved the flooding and destroyed the trade. Built west of town in the 1550s at the direction of Duke Hans the Elder of Schleswig-Holstein-Haderslev, son of Frederick I of Denmark, they cut Tønder's harbour off from open water for good. A market town stranded inland has to find another product, which is roughly when the lacemakers turned up. Kristkirken, the town's main church, dates from around 1592, built in the middle of that reinvention.

## Seven Camels off HMS Furious

The Imperial German Navy put an airship base at Tønder during the First World War, and on 19 July 1918 the Royal Navy came for it. Seven Sopwith Camels launched from the carrier HMS Furious; one turned back with engine trouble and six pressed on. They hit two of the three airship hangars, destroying the Zeppelins L 54 and L 60 inside one and a captive balloon in the other. It was the first attack in history carried out by aircraft flown from an aircraft carrier — an entire century of naval aviation begins with six biplanes over a North Frisian marsh. One pilot was lost and presumed drowned; three landed in neutral Denmark and were interned. Tønder was finished as an active airship station after that. A wartime aircraft hangar and some ancillary buildings survive, but the great airship sheds are down to their foundations, and the site now holds the Zeppelin and Garrison Museum.

## The Town That Voted the Other Way

Before 1864 Tønder sat inside the Duchy of Schleswig, which is to say inside the most tangled sovereignty question in nineteenth-century Europe. When the Schleswig plebiscite came in 1920, Tønder's own voters were emphatic: 76.5 per cent chose Germany, 23.5 per cent chose Denmark. It made no difference. The vote was counted by zone rather than by town, and Zone I as a whole went strongly Danish, so Tønder became Danish along with it. The town did not simply convert. German parties held majorities on the town council for years afterwards, and Tønder was officially bilingual until 1945. Only after the German occupation of Denmark ended did German political influence fall away. The border remained an economic problem long after it stopped being a political one; sitting at the edge of a country is a poor position from which to build industry, and growth stayed slow through the late twentieth century.

## Chairs, Folk Music, and Weddings

What the border cost the factories it has partly returned in other ways. Because Tønder is the first Danish town north of Germany and Danish marriage law is far quicker than German — a week of formalities rather than three months' residence, fewer documents, and vows permitted in languages other than Danish — the town hall has been an elopement destination since the 1960s. In 2007 it married more than 2,500 non-resident couples against 150 local ones. Every August the Tønder Festival, running since 1974 on the last full weekend of the month, fills the town with folk, roots and blues; by 2006 it took some 1,900 volunteers to stage. The old water tower has held Museum Sønderjylland's Hans J. Wegner collection since August 1995 — thirty-six chairs the designer himself picked as his best work, shown in the town where he was born on 2 April 1914. Det Gamle Apotek puts on Scandinavia's largest Christmas exhibition. The town counted 7,477 residents at the start of 2025.

## Flight Context

54.9428N, 8.8639E, four kilometres north of the German border in the flat marshland of southwest Jutland. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet Tønder reads as a compact grey-roofed town set in an otherwise empty green plain of reclaimed polder, drainage ditches and dead-straight field boundaries; the water tower on the north side and the Kristkirken tower are the two vertical marks. The Zeppelin base site lies just north of town. Møgeltønder and the Schackenborg estate avenue are four kilometres west, the Gallehus memorial stones just beyond, and the Wadden Sea coast about fifteen kilometres further on. Nearest airports are Flensburg-Schäferhaus (EDXF) 35 km southeast, Sønderborg (EKSB) 45 km northeast, and Westerland/Sylt (EDXW) 35 km west; Billund (EKBI) is 113 km north. Terrain is near sea level with no obstacles beyond wind turbines, which are numerous — the constraint is North Sea weather, with frequent low cloud, drizzle and marsh haze.
