# Rømø

A Danish Wadden Sea island with a beach wide enough to drive on, a fence made of whale jawbones, and the whaling-captain fortunes that built both.

**Location:** 55.1333, 8.5167

**Categories:** island, Wadden Sea, Denmark, whaling history, beach, Tønder Municipality

## Introduction

Around 1720, Rømø held roughly sixteen hundred people and forty commanders — men qualified to take a ship north to the whaling grounds and bring it home again. Every February they crossed to Hamburg or Amsterdam to join their vessels, and the island ran itself until the fleets came back. What that trade paid for is still standing. There is a farmhouse with a sperm whale in the barn, a church hung with model ships, and at Juvre a fence whose posts are the jawbones of whales.

## Where the Land Runs Out Slowly

Drive west across the island and the road more or less continues onto the beach. At Lakolk and Sønderstrand the western foreshore runs two to three kilometres from dune line to waterline — flat, pale, firm enough that cars park on it in ranks and still look stranded. This is Wadden Sea coast, where the tide withdraws over sand rather than rock and the horizon relocates by kilometres twice a day. Kite buggies work the southern beach, small rigs hauled at speed behind traction kites, and on the first weekend of September Rømø holds one of Europe's largest kite festivals. Not all of it is open to traffic now; a stretch was closed to cars in October 2020 to protect nesting and feeding birds. Rømø is the southernmost of Denmark's Wadden Sea islands, a title it inherited only when the small uninhabited island of Jordsand finally went under in 1999.

## Kommandør

The Danish word kommandør is the old term for the captain of a whaler, and on Rømø it was almost a local profession. The Kommandørgården is an eighteenth-century whaling captain's farm, now run by the National Museum, preserved as the home of a household that made its living in the Arctic. Part of the manor served as a school between 1784 and 1874. In the barn hangs the skeleton of a sperm whale that stranded on the island in the late 1990s — a reminder that the trade's raw material still occasionally washes up on its own. At Juvre, on the northeastern side, stands the island's last surviving hvalkæbehegn: a fence built from whale jawbones set upright in the ground, the leftovers of butchering turned into something useful because nothing was wasted.

## Ships Inside the Church

St Clement's, the island's main church, was built around 1200 and enlarged through the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries as the whaling money arrived. Votive ships hang from the ceiling, models given by men who had reason to bargain with the weather. In the nineteenth century the parish let people buy nameplates for the benches, which is how the pews became a directory of the island's families. One name on a front bench is H.P.P. Møller. His son, Peter Mærsk Møller, was born on Rømø in 1836 and went to sea as a captain; Peter's son, Arnold Peter Møller, founded the shipping company that still carries the family's middle name across every ocean the whalers ever worked.

## Borders, Rails, and a Road Over the Mud

Rømø belongs to Southern Jutland, and Southern Jutland has changed hands. The Second Schleswig War of 1864 put the island inside Prussia and then the German Empire, as part of the Province of Schleswig-Holstein; it returned to Denmark in 1920 after the Schleswig plebiscite. Getting to it was another problem. A narrow-gauge line ran from Kongsmark inland to the beach at Lakolk, and the plan for a steam locomotive failed because the sand would not carry the weight, so two horses pulled the excursion wagons instead until services stopped in 1940 and the track was lifted the year after. The permanent fix came later: Rømødæmningen, a 9.2-kilometre causeway across the tidal flats, opened on 18 December 1948 and badly damaged by storms in 1976, 1981 and 1999. From Havneby a car ferry crosses to the German island of Sylt, three kilometres away, roughly every hour and in about forty minutes. Fewer than six hundred people live on the island's 129 square kilometres today, in Kongsmark, Østerby, Havneby and a handful of other settlements, all of it part of Tønder Municipality since 2007.

## Flight Context

55.1333N, 8.5167E. Rømø is unmistakable from the air: a rectangular sand island roughly 17 km long north to south, with a dune-backed western beach so broad that at low water the pale foreshore is wider than many of the island's fields. Best viewed at 2,000 to 4,000 feet, ideally within two hours of low tide, when the Wadden Sea east of the island drains into a brown-and-silver braid of tidal channels between Rømø and the Jutland mainland. The 9.2 km causeway runs dead straight east to Skærbæk. Sylt lies 3 km south across the Lister Dyb channel; Mandø and Fanø step away to the north. Nearest airports are Westerland/Sylt (EDXW) 20 km south and Esbjerg (EKEB) 55 km north, and a small airfield on the island itself. Expect strong westerlies, sea haze, and low cloud coming off the North Sea; terrain is essentially at sea level.
