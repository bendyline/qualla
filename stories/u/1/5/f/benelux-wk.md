# Benelux

Three small kingdoms, three official languages too few for the region's many tongues, and one shared instinct - learned the hard way - to build something larger than themselves.

**Location:** 51.1173, 5.4053

**Categories:** Travel regions, Europe, Benelux

## Introduction

The name is an accident of typography. In 1944, while their countries were still under German occupation, the exiled governments of Belgium, the Netherlands, and Luxembourg signed a customs convention in London and needed a shorthand for the new arrangement. They took the first letters - Be, Ne, Lux - and joined them. Benelux. It looked like a word and so it became one. What it stood for, three small monarchies wedged between France and Germany who had decided to stop being trampled, turned out to be the seed of the European Union itself.

## Why the Three Belong Together

Geography drew the outline. The Benelux sits where four rivers - Scheldt, Meuse, Rhine, Ems - empty into the North Sea, and most of it is part of a great low-lying delta that was, for centuries, more water than land. The Dutch coined a word for the work of pushing the sea back: polder. Parts of the Netherlands lie meters below the ocean, kept dry only by dikes and pumps that never stop running. Inland, in Belgium's south and across Luxembourg, the land lifts into the Ardennes, hilly enough that white-water canoeing is one of the regional pastimes. The climate is uniformly grey and uniformly mild, and the cuisine - fish, potatoes, cheese, and rivers of beer - testifies to a shared culinary heritage that the political borders never quite managed to divide.

## A Land Always Divided

Yet division has shaped this region as deeply as union. The Netherlands is majority-Protestant, Belgium and Luxembourg historically Catholic. Northern Belgium - Flanders - speaks Dutch. Southern Belgium - Wallonia - speaks French. Brussels, the capital, is officially bilingual, though French dominates in practice. Luxembourg juggles three official languages - Luxembourgish, French, and German - and is the only country in the EU where everyday speech still uses a Germanic language that almost no foreigner has heard. The linguistic split has caused real political tension, especially in Belgium, where most Flemish speakers can manage French but few francophones bother with Dutch. Travelers usually escape the whole problem: English fluency in Flanders and the Netherlands is among the highest in non-English-speaking Europe, partly because TV is subtitled rather than dubbed.

## The Cities You Came For

If you want canals and the cheerful disorder of seventeenth-century town houses leaning on each other for support, you want Amsterdam. If you want a single cathedral the size of a small village and a port that handles a respectable fraction of Europe's trade, you want Antwerp. Bruges has kept its pre-Renaissance walls and the medieval street plan that made it one of Europe's richest cities in the 1300s - so well preserved that the city itself feels like an exhibit. The Hague is where governments meet and international courts pronounce; Rotterdam is what Amsterdam might look like if you flattened it in 1940 and rebuilt it as a sculpture park of modern architecture, which is more or less what happened. Brussels is the unofficial capital of the European Union, with the European Quarter's glass slabs sitting incongruously beside the chocolate shops and beer cafes of the old town. Luxembourg City rises improbably on a cliff above two river gorges, looking like a fortress drawn by a child who wanted one to look like a fortress should.

## Getting Around the Easy Way

The Benelux is the easiest part of Continental Europe to travel. Schiphol in Amsterdam and Zaventem in Brussels are the major airports, with smaller ones at Eindhoven, Charleroi, Maastricht, Liege, Rotterdam, and Luxembourg picking up the no-frills traffic. The high-speed rail is some of the densest in the world: Eurostar (the successor to Thalys, which rebranded in 2023) connects Paris to Brussels and onward to Amsterdam in about three and a half hours, Eurostar runs through the Channel Tunnel to London, ICE trains reach Frankfurt and Cologne. Within the region, train tickets are remarkably cheap, especially in Belgium, where keeping rail affordable is officially government policy. Then there are the bikes. The Netherlands owns more bicycles than people. Every town of any size has a network of bike paths used by everyone from primary-school children to bankers in suits. Renting a bike in Amsterdam is less a tourist experience than a basic act of competent travel.

## Honest Brokers in a Difficult Neighborhood

There is one more thing the three countries share - a shared 20th-century memory of being overrun. The German invasions of 1914 and 1940 swept through all three, and the postwar instinct to build something that would make such invasions unthinkable came out of that shared injury. The Benelux Economic Union, signed in 1958, was the practice run for the European Economic Community, which was being negotiated in the same years. Today the region acts as a sort of neutral capital district for the continent: the International Court of Justice in The Hague, the EU institutions split between Brussels and Luxembourg City, the headquarters of NATO in Brussels, the European Investment Bank in Luxembourg. It is a strange thing to walk through Amsterdam or Bruges and remember that these compact, walkable, almost playful places were once the bargaining chip Napoleon and Hitler thought worth invading the world to take. The bicycles in the morning streets are the answer the region eventually chose.

## Flight Context

The Benelux centroid is roughly 51.12 N, 5.41 E - this story coordinate sits in Belgian Limburg near the Dutch border, almost on the line between Belgium and the Netherlands. To take in the whole region cruise at 8,000 to 12,000 feet: the polders of the western Netherlands are flat and patterned like a circuit board, the Ardennes rise to the south, and the Rhine, Meuse, and Scheldt deltas braid into the North Sea. Major airports: Schiphol (EHAM) Amsterdam, Brussels (EBBR), Luxembourg-Findel (ELLX), Rotterdam (EHRD), Eindhoven (EHEH), Liege (EBLG), Antwerp (EBAW). Some of the busiest controlled airspace in Europe - expect heavy IFR traffic and many restricted areas.
