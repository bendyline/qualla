# Taganrog Round House

The first round apartment house in the Soviet Union curls into a broken ring in Taganrog, an experiment in utopian architecture that its own residents came to resent.

**Location:** 47.2175, 38.9132

**Categories:** Architecture, History, Soviet, Taganrog

## Introduction

From the street it reads as an ordinary brick apartment block, until you notice the wall curving away and refusing to stop. The Taganrog Round House bends into a great broken ring, its facade wrapping around a hidden, perfectly circular courtyard so acoustically alive that a whisper carries clear across it. Built in 1929, it was the first round residential house in the Soviet Union, conceived as a symbol of a new architecture: minimum material, maximum strength, the future poured into a shape the past had never tried. Nearly a century later, its curve still stops passersby cold on Alexandrovskaya Street.

## The Idea in a Ring

The house was raised for the workers of the Krasny Kotelshchik boiler factory, and for years its designer was misremembered. Only through the research of a Rostov architectural historian did the true author emerge: Ivan Taranov-Belozerov, an engineer who had trained in Kharkiv and worked in the trust that oversaw the Taganrog plant, where the project was drawn up and published for debate in a construction journal in 1929. The building embodies the bold optimism of early Soviet design. Its brick walls run in a torn ring, its long horizontal windows banded by dark piers, its entrance turned pointedly to the north. The enclosed courtyard was a stage, an experiment, a manifesto in masonry, meant to prove that a workers' home could be modern, efficient, and unlike anything before it.

## Life Inside the Circle

The reality of living in a manifesto was harder than the drawings suggested. Doors on the second and third floors opened onto a shared balcony that ringed the whole curve, and the utopian efficiency had unglamorous limits: bathrooms were designed to serve seven families at a time. When residents finally moved in by late 1932, the shared toilet stood outside, some twenty meters from the building, and running water and sewerage did not reach the apartments until the early 1960s. The concept never caught on; a few more round houses went up elsewhere, but the form never spread. What had looked like the shape of the future turned out to be a one-of-a-kind curiosity, admired from outside far more easily than lived in.

## A Monument Nobody Asked For

The strangest chapter came in December 2016, when the round house was declared a cultural heritage object of the Rostov region, a designation championed by an international architectural preservation body. The residents, to the public's surprise, met the honor with dismay rather than pride. Russian law, it turned out, placed the full burden of maintaining a protected monument on the building's owners, meaning the very people who lived there. For families of modest means, that was a weight they had never sought. The round house had come full circle in a way its designer never intended: an experiment in housing the worker had become a landmark whose upkeep now fell on the workers' descendants, a reminder that even the boldest architecture must eventually be lived in and paid for.

## Flight Context

The Round House stands at 47.2175°N, 38.9132°E on Alexandrovskaya Street in Taganrog, near the Sea of Azov. From above, its distinctive ring shape set within the old-town grid is itself the landmark. Nearest airports are Taganrog Yuzhny (URRT) and Platov International at Rostov-on-Don (URRP), roughly 90 km east.
