# Taganrog Museum of Art

In a merchant's mansion in Taganrog hangs a collection of Russian masters that survived a wartime plundering, its courtyard now a refuge for rescued stone angels.

**Location:** 47.2122, 38.9214

**Categories:** Museum, Art, History, Taganrog

## Introduction

In the inner courtyard of a Taganrog mansion, a stone angel keeps watch over the graves of people it was never carved for. The figures gathered here were rescued from an abandoned cemetery across town, saved from the wrecking ball and set among the flowerbeds of the Taganrog Museum of Art. Inside the house hang canvases by some of the greatest names in Russian painting. But the courtyard tells the museum's truest story: this is a place devoted to saving beautiful things from disappearing, a mission it learned the hard way when war emptied its walls.

## A Merchant's House of Masters

Though the museum was formally inaugurated in 1968, its collection reaches back to the end of the nineteenth century, when the local city museum first opened an art department. Since 1975 it has occupied the former mansion of the merchant Anton Handrin on Alexandrovskaya Street. The walls hold icons from the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries and a deep gallery of Russian realism from the late nineteenth: portraits and genre scenes by Konstantin Makovsky and Konstantin Savitsky, luminous landscapes by Isaac Levitan, Alexei Savrasov, and Arkhip Kuindzhi, and studies by Viktor Vasnetsov, Vasily Surikov, and Vasily Vereshchagin. A second wing traces Soviet art across generations, from the paintings of Igor Grabar to the sculpture of Yevgeny Vuchetich and the graphic work of Vladimir Favorsky.

## The Empty Walls

The museum carries a wound from the Second World War. During the occupation of Taganrog, between 1941 and 1943, its collection was looted, hundreds of works carried off, a cultural theft catalogued afterward among Russia's lost heritage. To stand in the galleries now is to see a collection that was rebuilt around an absence, its survivors made more precious by the ones that never returned. The plunder was not unique to Taganrog; across the occupied territories, museums were stripped of the art their communities had gathered over lifetimes. What hangs here today is both a display and a quiet act of recovery, a refusal to let the theft be the final word.

## Angels in the Yard

The museum's most affecting works may be the ones outdoors. Beginning in 1976, sepulchral monuments of real artistic value were carried from the abandoned Taganrog Old Cemetery into the museum's inner courtyard, sparing them from destruction. Among them stands the weathered stone angel, wings folded, that now greets visitors from the yard. There is something fitting in it: a museum that lost so much to war became a sanctuary for funerary art that would otherwise have crumbled to nothing. Between the rescued paintings on the walls and the rescued angels in the garden, the Taganrog Museum of Art makes the same steady promise, that the things people made to be remembered will not be allowed to vanish.

## Flight Context

The museum stands at 47.2122°N, 38.9214°E on Alexandrovskaya Street in central Taganrog, near the Sea of Azov. From above, the old-town grid descending toward the harbor is the guiding landmark. Nearest airports are Taganrog Yuzhny (URRT) and Platov International at Rostov-on-Don (URRP), roughly 90 km east.
