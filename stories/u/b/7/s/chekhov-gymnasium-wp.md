# Chekhov Gymnasium

The oldest school in southern Russia kept a jail cell for misbehaving boys - and its most famous inmate grew up to write The Cherry Orchard.

**Location:** 47.2150, 38.9214

**Categories:** Historic schools, Literary landmarks, Anton Chekhov, Taganrog

## Introduction

Among the exhibits in this old Taganrog schoolhouse, one stops visitors cold: a small, bare punishment cell where unruly pupils were once locked away to reflect on their sins. According to the museum, a schoolboy named Anton Chekhov visited it more than once. That detail says much about the place - the oldest gymnasium in the south of Russia, a school of stern discipline and classical rigor that shaped, and sometimes chafed, the boy who became one of the world's greatest playwrights. He spent eleven years within these walls. Today they bear his name and preserve the very desk where he sat, a temple of learning turned into a monument to its most reluctant star.

## The Oldest School in the South

Founded in 1809, the boys' gymnasium was the first institution of its kind in southern Russia, and it moved into its stately classical building in Taganrog in 1843. For over a century it educated the sons of the region, offering grants to promising students and running the demanding curriculum of Greek, Latin, and mathematics that defined a nineteenth-century Russian secondary education. A chapel was added to the building in the 1870s, its cross visible on old postcards. The school outlasted empires: after the 1917 revolution and the civil war that followed, the building was pressed into service as a cavalry college, and later still it endured the Second World War. Through every transformation, its dignified colonnaded facade remained a landmark of the town.

## An Average Pupil

Anton Chekhov entered the gymnasium around the age of eight, after a stint at a local school for Greek boys, and by his teachers' reckoning he was an unremarkable student. What he lacked in grades he made up for in observation. As an adolescent he was already writing - short comic anecdotes to amuse his classmates, and at least one serious long play, an early work he later destroyed. His schooling carried a poignant subsidy: an annual grant of 300 rubles, established by the Taganrog City Council after a failed assassination attempt on Tsar Alexander II. History pressed close on the ordinary life of a schoolboy here, and Chekhov, ever alert, was quietly absorbing all of it for the fiction to come.

## Left Behind to Finish

The defining ordeal of Chekhov's school years came when his family collapsed financially. His father's business failed, and in the mid-1870s the whole family fled to Moscow to escape their debts - leaving the teenaged Anton alone in Taganrog to fend for himself and complete his education. For roughly three years he lived on his own, tutoring younger pupils to earn his keep, learning a self-reliance and quiet resolve that would run through both his character and his writing. In 1879 he passed his final exams at last and rejoined his family in Moscow, having secured a scholarship to study medicine at the university. The boy who had endured the punishment cell left the gymnasium a doctor in the making - and a writer already forming.

## A School of Remarkable Sons

Chekhov was the most famous, but he was far from the only distinguished graduate. The gymnasium's alumni read like a cross-section of Russian achievement: the painter Konstantin Savitsky, the bridge engineer Nikolai Belelyubsky, the general Leonid Gobyato, credited with inventing the first modern mortar, the anthropologist Vladimir Bogoraz, the composer Samuel Maykapar, and Valentin Parnakh, a founder of Soviet jazz. Even Chekhov's own brothers Alexander and Nikolai studied here. In 1954 the school was formally named after Anton Chekhov, and in 1975 it reopened as a literary museum. After the Pushkin House in Saint Petersburg, it is the second-largest literary museum in Russia, its halls holding period books, Chekhov's belongings, and his own school-leaving certificate - the record of eleven hard-won years.

## Flight Context

The Chekhov Gymnasium stands in central Taganrog near 47.22°N, 38.92°E, best appreciated from low altitude. From the air, orient on the old-town grid of Taganrog on its low cape jutting into the shallow Sea of Azov. The nearest major airport is Rostov-on-Don's Platov International (URRP), about 40 kilometers to the northeast.
