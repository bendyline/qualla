# Taganrog Fortress

To build Russia's first navy base, Peter the Great chose a windswept cape on the Sea of Azov and did something no one had done before: he built a harbor out in the open sea.

**Location:** 47.2035, 38.9415

**Categories:** History, Fortress, Peter the Great, Taganrog

## Introduction

On the night of 27 July 1696, a Russian tsar slept on a bare cape jutting into the Sea of Azov and decided to build a navy there. Peter the Great had just taken the Turkish fortress of Azov, but the shallow mouth of the Don could never shelter a fleet. So he sailed the coast looking for deep water, and at a windswept spur of land called Tagan-Rog he found it: sea deep enough for warships, firm stone soil for a harbor, and a small freshwater spring. From that decision came Taganrog, Russia's first purpose-built naval base, and one of the boldest feats of engineering the young empire ever attempted.

## A Fortress by Design

Founded formally in September 1698, Taganrog was among the first Russian cities laid out to a detailed advance plan, its streets radiating from a central plaza in the geometry of a star fort. An Austrian engineer, Baron Ernst Friedrich von Borgsdorf, drew up the works on Peter's instructions. Earthen ramparts rose about eight meters high above a moat five meters deep that ran for some two miles, studded with bastions, ravelins, and demi-bastions bristling with cannon. Inside stood the monarch's house, a Saint Trinity cathedral, warehouses, ovens, and wells. By 1711 the fortress held more than two hundred stone buildings and was defended by nearly three hundred cannon. Vice-Admiral Cornelis Cruys, the Dutch-born father of the Russian Navy, served as the town's first head and charted the first maps of the Azov and the Don.

## A Harbor in Open Water

The most audacious stroke was the harbor itself. Rather than tucking his fleet into a sheltered bay, Peter's engineers built one where none existed, driving some thirty thousand oak piles into the seabed and sinking nearly two hundred stone-filled boxes between them to raise breakwaters out of open water. Taganrog's haven is considered the first port in the world created in the open sea rather than a natural inlet. Two kilometers offshore they went further still, raising an entirely artificial island called Cherepakha, a rock-fill citadel held by oak piles and armed with more than a hundred guns to guard the approach. The island endures in ghostly form: even now, when the tide runs low and an east wind blows, Cherepakha rises briefly from the water, and divers in 1981 still found the old oak piles beneath the waves.

## Ruin and Revival

The triumph was short-lived. After a Russian defeat by the Ottomans on the Pruth River, Peter was forced by treaty to return Azov to Turkey and to destroy the very base he had built. In September 1711 he ordered Taganrog demolished, and by early 1712 his troops had marched away, leaving the fortress and its remarkable harbor to lie in ruins for half a century. Revival came under Catherine the Great, whose forces reclaimed the cape in 1769; Turkey ceded it for good in 1774. But history had moved on. Once Russia annexed Crimea in 1783, Taganrog's fortress lost its purpose, and its guns and ships went to the new base at Sevastopol. The fortifications were abolished, the buildings slowly dismantled, and Taganrog settled into a long, prosperous second life as a merchant town by the sea.

## Flight Context

The fortress site occupies the cape at 47.2035°N, 38.9415°E, where Taganrog's headland reaches into the Sea of Azov. From above, the low peninsula and the shallow harbor mark the historic anchorage; when the water is low, the remnants of Cherepakha islet may show offshore. Nearest airports are Taganrog Yuzhny (URRT) and Platov International at Rostov-on-Don (URRP), about 90 km east.
