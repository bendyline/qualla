# The Durov Museum

A small Art Nouveau mansion in Taganrog holds the arena, the animals, and the legend of a clown whose family remade the Russian circus.

**Location:** 47.2089, 38.9225

**Categories:** Museums, Circus History, Art Nouveau, Taganrog

## Introduction

Step into the central hall and there is a circus arena, small and stylized, ringed with performers frozen mid-act: a crane reading, a monkey with a violin, a parrot, animals caught in the moment before applause. None of them are alive. They are the stuffed remnants of Anatoly Anatolyevich Durov's famous menagerie, arranged as he once presented them, and standing at the rail of that little arena is the strangest sensation this museum offers. The show goes on in a house on Shevchenko Street, in a city where the great clown chose to spend the last two years of his life.

## A Clown Comes to the Azov

By 1926 Anatoly Anatolyevich Durov was already famous in Russia and abroad, an animal trainer of a kind the circus had rarely seen. He belonged to a remarkable dynasty. The Durov family had spent decades raising the prestige of the Russian circus, and their bloodline stretched back to Nadezhda Durova, celebrated as the first woman to serve as an officer in the Russian army during the Napoleonic Wars. That year Anatoly moved to Taganrog with his whole household, wife, mother, two sisters and their families, and the animals that traveled with him. He bought two mansions on Shevchenko Street and founded the Taganrog Animal Theater. The move looked like the start of a long chapter. It was nearly the end of one. In 1928 he died, killed in a hunting accident near Izhevsk, at the age of forty-one. His family stayed on in Taganrog for another dozen years.

## The House Itself

The mansion that holds the museum was built in 1900, an Art Nouveau design that keeps slipping toward something older and more Russian. A low arched entrance, set with barrel-shaped columns of the kind you find on Orthodox churches, divides the facade in two. A tall pediment rises above, a trapezoidal crest crowns the metal roof, and the corner is rusticated in white and red brick. For all its modern styling, the building is so compact and its columns so church-like that it reads, in the end, as a house in the traditional Russian manner. It had a full life before the circus. A priest owned it until 1915; then it passed to the wife of a minor official, and before the revolution its mistress was Olga Mironosnichenko, sister to Sasha Selivanova, an old friend of Anton Chekhov. The state nationalized it in 1920.

## The Arena That Doesn't Close

The exhibition traces the whole arc of Durov's craft, the training methods, the world tours, the pupils and followers who carried his art forward. His nephews would grow up to become People's Artists of the USSR. Beyond the stylized arena at the museum's heart, one hall recreates a fragment of the railway coach a touring performer of the early twentieth century lived in, laid out with Durov's own belongings. Another room, called "Kino," plays rare footage of his 1920s attraction "Cabaret." More than forty stuffed exotic animals and birds survive here, alongside the original posters that once shouted his acts across Russia, "Kangaroo Boxer," "Dog's Wedding," "Jazz Band," "Dog Jockeys." Letters, reviews, and old photographs fill the glass cases. The exhibition opened in 1987, and the mansion was restored the following year.

## Flight Context

Located at 47.2089 N, 38.9225 W, on Shevchenko Street in central Taganrog, on the north shore of the Sea of Azov. The mansion is a small target; use the Taganrog peninsula and the broad Gulf of Taganrog as your orientation. Best viewed at low altitude in clear weather.
