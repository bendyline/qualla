# Taganrog Aviation Museum

Southern Russia's only aviation museum exists because two officers refused to scrap every jet on the chopping block, saving one of each type from the cutting torch.

**Location:** 47.2400, 38.8500

**Categories:** Museum, Aviation, History, Taganrog

## Introduction

In the early 1990s, a repair plant at the edge of Taganrog was handed a grim assignment: dismantle warplanes. Under a landmark arms-control treaty, hundreds of Soviet combat aircraft were to be pulled apart and reduced to scrap, and this base was designated as one of the places where frontline jets would go to die. But two officers looked at the fighters rolling in for destruction and had a different idea. Save one of each, they proposed. Strip the engines, pull the systems, but keep a single example intact. That quiet act of preservation is why Southern Russia's only aviation museum exists today.

## The Cutting Torch

Since 1948 the 325th Aviation Repair Plant has worked at the Taganrog-Central air base, overhauling transport aircraft and helicopters. Then came the Treaty on Conventional Armed Forces in Europe, signed in 1990, which capped the arsenals of the Cold War's two blocs and required vast numbers of tanks, artillery, and aircraft to be destroyed. Many combat planes of the Soviet and later Russian air forces were sent for disposal, and the Taganrog plant became a base for scrapping frontline aviation. It was the kind of order that erases machines by the hundred, each one a product of enormous engineering effort, bound for the breaker's yard.

## Two Officers and an Idea

Lieutenant Colonel Vladimir Stoyanov and the base commander, Colonel Alexander Kornienko, appealed up the chain to the 4th Air Army. Their request was modest and stubborn: let us keep at least one specimen of every aircraft type coming through for dismantlement, engines and avionics removed, airframes preserved. The army's commander, Vladimir Mikhaylov, approved. On 23 April 1995 he ordered four aircraft spared from the scrapping program, a MiG-21bis, a MiG-23M, a MiG-25BM, and a Su-22. Others soon followed, among them a pair of Czechoslovak trainers, the L-39 Albatros and L-29 Delfin, the vertical-takeoff Yak-38, and the humble biplane An-2. From a demolition base grew a collection.

## The Last of Its Kind

Today the museum holds a dozen types of aircraft, and visitors are allowed to climb inside each one, an intimacy most air museums never permit. The rarest resident is that MiG-25BM, a specialized variant of the fearsome high-speed interceptor, of which only around forty were ever built. The one at Taganrog appears to be the sole survivor, a machine that would have been cut into scrap had two officers not spoken up. As of 2022 the museum's keepers were campaigning for official city recognition of their collection. It is an unusual monument: not to a battle or a leader, but to the impulse to preserve, to the idea that even the tools of war are worth saving for the people who come after.

## Flight Context

The museum sits at the Taganrog-Central air base, near 47.24°N, 38.85°E, northwest of the city center on the Sea of Azov. From the air the airfield's runways and the shoreline beyond make the clearest landmarks. Nearest airports are Taganrog Yuzhny (URRT) and Platov International at Rostov-on-Don (URRP), roughly 90 km east.
