# Greek Church and Greek School

For over 150 years a Greek Orthodox church and its little school stood at the heart of Taganrog's merchant colony, teaching a boy named Chekhov before both church and empire vanished.

**Location:** 47.2118, 38.9385

**Categories:** History, Religion, Anton Chekhov, Taganrog

## Introduction

Before the boy became the writer, he was a small pupil in a wooden schoolhouse behind a Greek church, learning his letters in a language not his own. Taganrog in the nineteenth century was a Greek town as much as a Russian one, its harbor thick with merchants from the Aegean who had made the Sea of Azov their second home. At the center of that colony rose the Church of Sts Constantine and Helena, consecrated in 1782, and behind it the parish school where, from 1866 to 1868, a grocer's son named Anton Chekhov sat among the choristers' benches.

## A Colony by the Sea

Construction of the Greek Church began in 1781, and by March of the following year the priest Nikita Aguros held the first service. This was the spiritual anchor of Taganrog's Greek merchants, and the empire took notice of it. When Empress Elizabeth Alexeievna, the wife of Alexander I and born Louise of Baden, passed through Taganrog in 1826, she left the congregation money for its upkeep. By 1830 a new stone church had replaced the original wooden one, and worship there rang out in both Russian and Greek, the two languages of a port that faced the Mediterranean as much as it faced Moscow. The church would even see gunfire: in 1855, during the Crimean War, its grounds became a defensive position when British and French landing parties struck the town.

## The Choristers' School

Behind the church stood a modest building, wooden at first and later faced with brick, that served the Greek community in shifting ways. Early in the nineteenth century it housed the Greek city council, which coexisted with the town's municipal government. In 1862 it opened as a parochial school to train choristers, funded by the church and by the wealthy Greek merchants whose fortunes had been built on grain and trade. Pupils studied Greek, theology, mathematics, history, calligraphy, and singing. Among them, from 1866 to 1868, was young Anton Chekhov, sent here until at the age of eight he moved on to the Gymnasium for Boys. The rote lessons and the strict discipline of these early classrooms would surface later, gently mocked, in his stories of provincial life.

## What the Century Took

The church did not survive the twentieth century. In 1938 Soviet authorities closed it, and it was demolished soon after; by the 1940s an apartment block stood on its site, the congregation and its bells simply erased from the streetscape. The old schoolhouse fared a little better, though barely recognizably. After the October Revolution it was handed to the families of factory workers, and its facade and layout were altered beyond their original form. What remains is memory and a single surviving building, standing where a Greek colony once prayed and where a future master of the short story first learned to read. In a town that lost so much, the ghost of that classroom lingers in every page Chekhov wrote.

## Flight Context

The former church site lies at 47.2118°N, 38.9385°E, on Greek Street in the historic core of Taganrog, close to the Sea of Azov. From above, the tight grid of the old town descending toward the harbor is the key landmark. Nearest airports are Taganrog Yuzhny (URRT) and Platov International at Rostov-on-Don (URRP), roughly 90 km east.
