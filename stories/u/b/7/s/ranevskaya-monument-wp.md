# Ranevskaya Monument

A bronze statue outside a Taganrog townhouse honors Faina Ranevskaya, the merchant's daughter who stuttered as a child and grew into the sharpest comic voice of the Soviet stage.

**Location:** 47.2106, 38.9336

**Categories:** Monument, Theater, History, Taganrog

## Introduction

She leans against her own front door in bronze, purse in hand, mid-thought, as if she has just delivered a line too wicked to repeat. This is Faina Ranevskaya, cast outside the very house in Taganrog where she was born in 1896, and the sculptor David Begalov caught her in the pose the whole Soviet Union knew: the raised eyebrow, the ready barb, the weary glamour of a woman who could steal a scene with a single look. The statue was unveiled in 2008. It stands where her improbable life began, on a street that has since taken a new name.

## The Girl Who Stuttered

Faina Feldman was born into a wealthy Jewish family in Taganrog, the daughter of a factory owner who kept his own steamboat, the Saint Nicholas, on the Sea of Azov. She should have grown up in comfort and ease, but a childhood stutter left her painfully shy, so uneasy among the girls at her prestigious school that her parents moved her to lessons at home. Her mother loved literature and music, and above all loved the stories of Anton Chekhov, Taganrog's own. In that household of books and art, the awkward, stammering girl found the one place her voice could soar: the imagined worlds of the stage. She would spend the rest of her life proving that the shyest child in the room can become the loudest presence on it.

## A Name from a Cherry Orchard

When she left for the theater, she left her birth name behind and took a new one that could only have come from her hometown's presiding genius. She called herself Ranevskaya, after Lyubov Ranevskaya, the tender, ruined landowner at the heart of Chekhov's The Cherry Orchard. It was a perfect closing of a circle: a girl raised in Chekhov's town, in a house that revered him, borrowing her stage identity from his most heartbreaking heroine. On stage and screen she became a master of both comedy and tragedy, her lines quoted like proverbs across the country, and in 1961 she was named a People's Artist of the USSR, the highest honor the Soviet arts could bestow.

## Coming Home

Recognition of Ranevskaya's roots came slowly to Taganrog. In 1986, two years after her death, a memorial plaque was fixed to her birth house on what is now Frunze Street. The monument itself arrived in May 2008, unveiled during the first International Ranevskaya Drama Festival, a gathering named The Great Province in tribute to the provincial city that made her. There she stands today, leaning on the doorframe of the house she was born in, purse over her arm, forever poised to say something unforgettable. Passersby stop to photograph her, and the joke of it is entirely hers: the girl who was afraid to speak now holds court on the sidewalk, in bronze, without a stammer in the world.

## Flight Context

The monument stands at 47.2106°N, 38.9336°E on Frunze Street in central Taganrog, near the Sea of Azov. From above, the dense old-town grid sloping toward the harbor is the guiding landmark. Nearest airports are Taganrog Yuzhny (URRT) and Platov International at Rostov-on-Don (URRP), roughly 90 km east.
