# Peter I Monument

The bronze tsar who founded Taganrog has stood, been buried in a box, and been raised again by three different regimes, a statue whose wanderings mirror a century of upheaval.

**Location:** 47.2050, 38.9464

**Categories:** Monument, History, Peter the Great, Taganrog

## Introduction

The bronze Peter the Great gazes out over the Sea of Azov toward the water he came here to command, one hand resting easy, the other on his sword. He founded this town in 1698, and it took Taganrog two centuries to raise him in bronze. But the strange part of the story is what came after the unveiling: over the next fifty years the statue would be torn down, crated in a wooden box, hidden in a museum courtyard, and re-inaugurated no fewer than three times, by tsarist officials, then by wartime occupiers, then by the Soviets who had once removed him.

## A Writer's Errand

The idea belonged to Achilles Alferaki, mayor of Taganrog in the 1880s, and it carried imperial blessing: Alexander III granted permission in 1893. To sculpt the founder, the city turned to Mark Antokolsky, one of the most celebrated sculptors of the age. The commission had an unlikely go-between. In April 1898, Anton Chekhov, Taganrog's most famous son, met Antokolsky in Paris to help arrange the bronze. The statue was cast in the Parisian atelier of the Thibaut Brothers, its pedestal carved in Odessa, and in 1901 the whole work sailed from Marseille to Taganrog aboard the steamer Despino. On 13 May 1903 it was unveiled with ceremony, its pedestal inscribed simply: To the Emperor Peter I, Taganrog 1698 to 1898.

## Into the Box

The revolution changed everything a monument could mean. On 25 January 1924 the Soviets dismantled Peter and moved him into the Chekhov Museum, then housed in the town library. By 1933 the tsar had been sealed inside a wooden crate in the museum courtyard, a founder boxed away like surplus furniture. In 1940 the regional government resolved to raise him again, this time near the seaport rather than his original street, but the German invasion of the Soviet Union the following year shattered those plans. During the occupation, on 18 July 1943, the statue was re-inaugurated by the occupying authorities before the entrance to the city park, a founder's image pressed into service by an army that would soon be driven out.

## Home at Last

Liberation came, and the statue outlasted the war as it had outlasted everything else. In September 1948, during celebrations of Taganrog's 250th anniversary, Peter was placed at last on Komsomolsky Boulevard near the harbor, where he stands today. From there the bronze tsar looks out over the deep-water anchorage he personally chose, the reason this town exists at all. Antokolsky's Peter is a familiar figure across Russia; versions of the same commanding pose stand in other cities. But the Taganrog casting carries a biography no replica can match. It is a statue that was hidden and hunted and repeatedly reborn, and its survival is, in its own quiet way, the town's survival too.

## Flight Context

The statue stands at 47.2050°N, 38.9464°E on Komsomolsky Boulevard, on the Sea of Azov near the Taganrog seaport. From the air, the harbor and the headland reaching into the water frame the view. Nearest airports are Taganrog Yuzhny (URRT) and Platov International at Rostov-on-Don (URRP), about 90 km east.
