# Aeroflot Flight 6515

On a freezing October night in 1978, a twin-engine airliner iced over the shallow waters of the Sivash and fell in ninety seconds, taking all twenty-six aboard.

**Location:** 45.4333, 35.1667

**Categories:** aviation history, disaster, Crimea, Soviet history

## Introduction

The water beneath the Arabat Spit is so shallow that a person could almost wade across it, a vast silvery marsh of brackish lagoons called the Sivash, the Rotten Sea. On the night of 23 October 1978, this unlikely stretch of not-quite-sea became the resting place of Aeroflot Flight 6515. The Antonov An-24 had left Stavropol earlier that evening and was nearing Simferopol when ice built silently on its wings and engines. Ninety seconds after both engines fell quiet, the aircraft struck the marsh. All twenty-six people aboard were killed. They came to rest just two and a half meters below the surface, in water too shallow to hide them and too soft to give them up easily.

## Ice in the Dark

The trouble was the weather, and it crept up quietly. Over Stavropol the sky had been merely partly cloudy, visibility better than ten kilometers. But past Kerch the clouds closed into an unbroken deck, and as the airliner bored toward Simferopol at 2,450 meters, the icing grew steadily worse. At 19:39 the crew radioed the Simferopol dispatcher. Flying in clouds, experiencing icing, they reported, and then, with an edge of concern, the icing is quite substantial. A minute later came word that the left engine had failed. Fourteen seconds after that, they clarified the worst: both engines were gone. They turned toward land and, after that, fell silent. The investigation would later show that for most of the flight the crew had not switched on the anti-icing system, leaving ice free to accumulate and, when heat was finally applied, to shed suddenly into the engines.

## Ninety Seconds

What followed happened almost too fast to grasp. At 19:40 both engines quit within three and a half seconds of each other and the propellers feathered automatically. The crew hauled the aircraft into a sharp left turn toward land, but an attempt to restart the left engine produced reverse thrust instead, and the airliner rolled hard and slowed until it stalled. It fell into a tightening spiral, then rolled a full 360 degrees around its own axis at about 500 meters. A minute and a half after silence fell over the engines, at 19:41, the An-24 struck the Sivash at nearly 490 kilometers per hour, nose and left wingtip first. The whole descent, from the first stumble to the water, lasted roughly ninety seconds. It is a span of time shorter than a held breath, and inside it twenty-six lives ended.

## Raising the Wreck from the Rotten Sea

Finding the aircraft was easy; recovering it was not. The wreck lay 24 kilometers southeast of the village of Yemelyanovka, submerged in barely two and a half meters of water. Beneath that thin layer, however, lay four and a half to five meters of soft silt, a swallowing muck that made the shallow marsh far more treacherous than it looked. Divers worked in nearly zero visibility for weeks. Not until 18 November, almost a month later, was the left engine finally brought up, along with the last major pieces of the airframe and the flight recorders that would tell the story. The recorders confirmed the chain of failures and noted, too, that the crew had never been trained for a double-engine flameout in cloud and ice at night, an emergency the flight manual itself did not address.

## What the Cold Taught

Every accident investigation is, in part, an attempt to make sense of loss, and this one carried a hard lesson. Investigators ran their own tests, deliberately icing engines and watching how the ice, once heat was applied too late, sheared off and choked the machinery. The pattern matched what had happened over the Sivash exactly. But the report reached beyond the failures of a single crew on a single freezing night. It faulted the system that had sent them aloft, the missing procedures, the absent training for the very scenario that killed them. That is the quiet tragedy behind the numbers. Twenty-six people, crew and passengers alike, did not simply fall victim to bad weather; they fell into a gap in the knowledge and preparation of their time. The marsh keeps its silence now, but the improvements written in the wake of such disasters are the reason later travelers fly a little safer through the same cold skies.

## Flight Context

The crash site lies in the Sivash lagoons at roughly 45.43°N, 35.17°E, southwest of the Arabat Spit and about 24 km from Yemelyanovka. From 5,000 feet the Sivash reads as a maze of pale, mirror-still shallows unlike any open sea. Nearest major airfield is Simferopol (UKFF), some 60 km to the southwest, the destination the flight never reached.
