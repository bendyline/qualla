# Kimmerikon

On the flank of a lonely Crimean mountain stood a Greek fortress city with walls thick enough to stop the Scythians, until Goths, pirates, and time reduced it to stones on a hillside.

**Location:** 45.0434, 36.2313

**Categories:** ancient history, archaeology, Greek colonies, Crimea

## Introduction

The walls tell you what the people here feared. At Kimmerikon, the city ramparts were built two and a half meters thick, and the walls of the acropolis a full three and a half, stone piled deep against an enemy the townsfolk knew could come at any time. This ancient Greek city clung to the western slope of Opuk mountain on the southern shore of the Kerch Peninsula, about forty kilometers southwest of modern Kerch, its citadel perched on the hills where it could watch the steppe. Founded by colonists from Miletus in the fifth century BC, Kimmerikon was not built for beauty. It was built to hold a line.

## The Shield of a Kingdom

Kimmerikon guarded something precious. It stood as an important stronghold defending the heartland of the Bosporan Kingdom, its center and its capital, from the Scythians who ranged the plains beyond. That is why its walls were so thick, why its acropolis crowned a defensible height. The city may even have inherited its name and its purpose from an earlier settlement of the Cimmerians, the people who gave their name to the Cimmerian Bosporus, so that the fortress guarding the frontier carried an echo of those who had held this ground before the Greeks arrived. For centuries the strategy worked. Kimmerikon flourished, reaching its height around the beginning of the Christian era, a bulwark on the kingdom's exposed southern edge.

## Goths, Pirates, and Fire

No wall holds forever. In the middle of the third century AD, the Goths swept down and sacked Kimmerikon, breaking the long peace the fortress had kept. The city did not die at once; some urban life clung on for a few more decades. But near the end of that century the end came violently, as the town was laid waste and burnt by pirate raiders who left it in ruins. Even then, a fortress lingered on the site, until it too was destroyed in the first third of the sixth century AD. When the Emperor Justinian re-established Byzantine authority over the Cimmerian Bosporus around the middle of that century, he chose not to rebuild Kimmerikon. Its long watch was over; the frontier it had guarded no longer needed it.

## Reading the Stones

What remains today is a hillside of ruin, and the patient work of those who study it. Soviet archaeologists excavated Kimmerikon in 1927 and again in the late 1940s and early 1950s, unearthing the traces of the city's long life. Much of what they found now rests in the Kerch Museum, where fragments of a fortress town speak for the people who once manned these walls. To stand on the western slope of Opuk today, among the tumbled stones and the wide silence of the steppe, is to sense how exposed this place always was, a Greek city facing outward into a vast and dangerous land, trusting to thick walls and high ground to keep the world at bay for as long as it could.

## A Name That Outlived Its City

Kimmerikon is easy to confuse, and the confusion is instructive. It should not be mistaken for the later Tatar town of Kirim, founded roughly a thousand years after Kimmerikon perished and lying some eighty-eight kilometers due west. The two share the accident of similar-sounding names but belong to entirely different worlds and eras, separated by a gulf of centuries. That such a mix-up is even possible tells you how completely Kimmerikon vanished from living memory, surviving only in scholarly notes and museum drawers until archaeologists brought it back to light. The Greek city that once shielded a kingdom left almost nothing to the surface, its ramparts robbed and weathered to low ridges of stone. Yet the setting endures: the same mountain, the same sweep of steppe and sea, the same commanding view that made a handful of Milesian colonists decide, twenty-five centuries ago, that this was a place worth walling.

## Flight Context

Kimmerikon lies on the western slope of Opuk mountain at roughly 45.04°N, 36.23°E, on the southern coast of the Kerch Peninsula about 40 km southwest of Kerch. From 4,000 feet the flat-topped Opuk massif is the key landmark, with the ancient site on its western flank and the pink Koyashskoye Salt Lake nearby. Nearest major airfield is Simferopol (UKFF), roughly 120 km west.
