# Opuk (mountain)

A flat-topped limestone mountain that once guarded a Byzantine frontier became, in our own time, the site of one of archaeology's more entertaining hoaxes: a runic stone that was never ancient at all.

**Location:** 45.0423, 36.2257

**Categories:** mountain, Crimea, archaeology, geology

## Introduction

In 1996, in a grotto beneath the old citadel on Opuk mountain, a researcher found a stone carved with four runic signs. It looked genuinely weathered, genuinely old, and it was hailed as a sensational discovery, tentatively dated to the middle of the fourth century. It was also completely fake. A Kerch resident with a fondness for the esoteric had carved the runes in 1994 as an artistic experiment and left the stone to age; two years of weather did the rest, and the fresh forgery fooled the experts. That is the kind of surprise Opuk keeps. Rising roughly 183 meters on a cape at the southern tip of the Kerch Peninsula, the highest point in its corner of Crimea, this flat-topped mountain has been fooling and fascinating people for a very long time.

## A Fold in the Earth

Opuk is a geologist's puzzle made visible. The whole massif is a single isolated synclinal fold, a great downward buckle of rock, built of Meotian limestones resting on older Sarmatian clays and sliced through by faults. Those faults, sometimes called seismogenic ditches, hint at the restless ground beneath. The result is a mountain whose slopes are anything but gentle: a jumble of stepped ledges, sheer precipices, chasms, and stone placers, fields of loose rock tumbled down the flanks. It stands alone above the surrounding steppe, a hard limestone island in a soft, flat sea of land, and that isolation is exactly what made it valuable to everyone who came here, from ancient settlers to modern scientists.

## A Frontier on the Summit

People have been drawn to Opuk's heights for nearly twenty-six centuries. The earliest ancient Greek settlements on the mountain date to the turn of the sixth and fifth centuries BC. Later, a fortress rose on the eastern summit, first built in the first century BC and perhaps linked to the ancient defensive line known as the Cimmerian shaft. The stones of that citadel hold layers of history: rich deposits from the late second and third centuries AD, and then a newer citadel built between the fourth and sixth centuries. In those late centuries the mountain marked a boundary, a borderland between the territory of Chersonesus and the Bosporan Kingdom. To hold Opuk was to hold the line where two ancient powers met.

## Two Centuries of Digging

The modern study of Opuk began with a single determined explorer. The Russian researcher Paul Du Brux was the first to investigate the site, working here between 1817 and 1830, long before archaeology was a formal science. More than a century and a half later, in 1989, the South Bosporus Archaeological Expedition under V. K. Golenko took up the work in earnest. It was this expedition that stumbled into the runic-stone affair, a cautionary tale that would eventually spawn a whole boom of supposed runic stones across the Kerch Peninsula.

## A Mountain That Invites Belief

There is something about Opuk that makes people want it to be more than rock. The runic hoax is only the most famous example; the mountain's caves, quarries, and grottoes have long drawn the imaginative, and the forged stele now sits, ironically, in a museum in Simferopol as an artifact of its own peculiar kind. Perhaps this pull is fitting for a place that has worn so many identities. Opuk has been an ancient Greek foothold, a Roman-era stronghold, a late-antique citadel on the frontier between the lands of Chersonesus and the Bosporan Kingdom, and finally, since 1998, the protected heart of a nature reserve. It is a hard, dramatic landmark of ledges and precipices rising alone from the plain, and it seems always to promise a little more than it plainly shows, a mountain that has been guarding secrets, real and invented, for a very long time.

## Flight Context

Opuk mountain rises at 45.04°N, 36.23°E, on Opuk cape at the southern tip of the Kerch Peninsula, within the Opuk Nature Reserve. From 4,000 feet the flat-topped, cliff-sided massif is a striking landmark against the level steppe, with the pink Koyashskoye Salt Lake immediately to the west and stone islets offshore. Nearest major airfield is Simferopol (UKFF), roughly 120 km west.
