# Mount Mithridat

The hill at the heart of Kerch, named for a king who defied Rome and died on its slopes, crowned by a grand stone staircase and a memorial to the soldiers of a later war.

**Location:** 45.3483, 36.4672

**Categories:** History, Landmark, Ancient History, Crimea

## Introduction

The hill is not tall - just over ninety meters - but it carries the whole memory of Kerch on its back. Climb the Great Mithridates Staircase, terrace by terrace, and the modern city falls away below you until the Kerch Strait opens flat and silver to the horizon. On the clearest days you can make out the far Caucasus shore. This is Mount Mithridat, and it is named for a man who chose to die here rather than kneel: Mithridates VI of Pontus, one of the most relentless enemies Rome ever faced.

## The King Who Would Not Bow

Mithridates VI ruled the Kingdom of Pontus and spent much of his life at war with the Roman Republic through the long series of conflicts that bear his name, the Mithridatic Wars. He was a formidable adversary - said to have hardened himself against poison by taking it in small doses for years - but in the end it was betrayal, not Rome, that undid him. After a bitter siege of Panticapaion, the Greek city that once covered this hill, his own son turned against him. Cornered, the old king tried to take his own life with poison and found, cruelly, that his years of self-dosing had left him immune. He was finally killed by the commander of his own guard. The hill has borne his name ever since, a monument to a king who lost everything except his defiance.

## A Staircase Fit for a Legend

In the 1830s, as Kerch grew into a prosperous port, the Italian architect Alexander Digbi gave the hill a proper approach. Built between 1833 and 1840, the Great Mithridates Staircase rises in a sweep of flights and balustraded terraces, a piece of grand classical theater climbing the slope where an ancient acropolis once stood. Stone griffins and urns punctuate the ascent; the balustrades frame the strait like a series of paintings. A road now reaches the summit too, but the staircase remains the way the hill wants to be climbed - slowly, with the view widening at every landing. It is the signature image of Kerch, so beloved that the mountain was once nominated among the Seven Wonders of Ukraine.

## What the Summit Remembers

In the nineteenth century a small museum shaped like a Greek temple crowned the hill, a nod to the classical city buried beneath. War took it. During the Second World War, Kerch changed hands repeatedly in some of the fiercest fighting on the Eastern Front, and the temple-museum was destroyed. In 1944 a memorial obelisk rose in its place, raised to honor the soldiers who defended the city. So the summit holds two memories at once: an ancient king who died defending his last stronghold, and the modern defenders who fought over the same ground more than two thousand years later. Stand at the top, with the obelisk behind you and the strait ahead, and the distance between those two struggles seems to collapse.

## Flight Context

Mount Mithridat rises at 45.348 N, 36.467 E in the center of Kerch, elevation about 91 meters. From 1,500-2,500 feet the summit obelisk and the pale sweep of the Great Staircase down the seaward slope make it easy to identify above the old town and the ferry harbor. The nearest airfield is Kerch; Anapa (URKA) lies across the strait to the east.
