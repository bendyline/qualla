# Church of Saint John the Baptist, Kerch

A candy-striped Byzantine church at the foot of a 432-step staircase has stood in the heart of Kerch for some thirteen centuries, one of the oldest churches in all of Eastern Europe.

**Location:** 45.3514, 36.4758

**Categories:** historic architecture, Crimea, Byzantine, religious site

## Introduction

Carved into the wall of an old church in Kerch, an epitaph waits for anyone who can read it: "Here lies the servant of God Kyriakos, a son of George, a grandson of Vindira. He died on the month of June 3, induction 10th, in the year 6265 from Adam." That way of counting time, from the creation of the world rather than the birth of Christ, tells you at once how ancient this place is. The Church of St John the Baptist has stood in the center of Kerch since around 717 AD, its walls striped like candy in alternating bands of pale stone and red brick. It is said to be one of the oldest churches in all of Eastern Europe, a survivor from a world most of us can scarcely imagine.

## The Candy-Striped Church

The first thing you notice is the pattern. The church's façade is banded, courses of light stone alternating with courses of red brick, giving the whole building the look of something layered and striped, almost sweet. This is Byzantine architecture at work, a style born of the great Christian empire centered on Constantinople, where builders used exactly this alternation of materials to strengthen walls and delight the eye. On the Kerch Peninsula, at the edge of that Byzantine world, the technique produced a small masterpiece. Thirteen centuries later the stripes still catch the light, marking the church out from everything around it as unmistakably, gloriously old.

## Built in the Age of the Khazars

The church rose in a border age. It was built in the eighth century, during the era when the Khazars, a powerful steppe people, held sway over this region, and its dedication to St John the Baptist, also known as St John the Precursor, marks it firmly as a Christian outpost in a contested land. To have endured from that time to this is remarkable. Empires and rulers have come and gone across Crimea many times over, yet this modest striped church kept its place in the heart of Kerch, its oldest sections still bearing the carved words of people who lived when the building was new. Few structures anywhere in Eastern Europe can claim so long an unbroken presence.

## At the Foot of the Great Stairs

The church sits at the bottom of a climb. Directly behind it rises the Mitridatskaya staircase, all 432 steps of it, ascending to the summit of Mitridat Hill, where a scatter of ancient sites crowns the city. The hill takes its name from Mithridates, a name that reaches back to the classical age of this coast, and the steps have long been one of the defining sights of Kerch. Together, the striped church below and the long stair above draw a line straight through the city's history, from a Byzantine sanctuary at street level to the older ruins on the heights. To stand at the foot of the stairs, beside walls raised more than a thousand years ago, is to feel the full depth of time this small city holds.

## A Survivor in an Ancient City

Kerch is one of the oldest continuously inhabited places on the Black Sea, its story reaching back to the Greek city of Panticapaeum that once crowned Mitridat Hill. Against that vast backdrop, the survival of a single eighth-century church is its own small miracle. Wars have rolled through Kerch again and again over the centuries, and the twentieth century in particular left the city scarred by some of the fiercest fighting of its age. Yet the Church of St John the Baptist kept its footing at the city's heart, its candy-striped walls enduring where so much else was lost. That endurance is precisely what makes it precious. It is not merely old; it is a living thread of continuity, a place where worshippers may still gather within walls that were already ancient when much of the modern world was young, and where the carved name of Kyriakos still waits, patient as stone, for the next reader to find it.

## Flight Context

The Church of St John the Baptist stands in central Kerch at roughly 45.35°N, 36.48°E, on the eastern shore of the Kerch Peninsula by the Strait of Kerch. From low altitude the city center and the prominent Mitridat Hill, topped with ancient sites and reached by the 432-step staircase, are the key landmarks. Nearest major airfield is Simferopol (UKFF), roughly 190 km west.
