# Pantikapaion

The greatest Greek city of the Black Sea's far shore, whose name meant 'fish-path' and whose gold coins and grain harbors made it the capital of a kingdom between two worlds.

**Location:** 45.3508, 36.4686

**Categories:** Ancient History, Archaeology, Greek Colony, Crimea

## Introduction

The name comes from a word for fish-path, and that tells you what the first Greeks saw when they arrived here in the late seventh or early sixth century BC: a strait alive with fish, a channel their nets could feed a city on. Colonists from Miletus, a wealthy port on the coast of what is now Turkey, chose a hill above the water and called their new home Pantikapaion. It would grow into the largest Greek city on the northern shore of the Black Sea - a capital, a mint, a granary for distant Athens - and its ruins lie today beneath the streets of Kerch and the slopes of Mount Mithridat.

## Greeks at the Edge of the Map

To the Greeks this was Taurica, a far frontier on the western side of the Cimmerian Bosporus, the strait we now call Kerch. It was the edge of the known world, where Hellenic cities met the vast grasslands of the Scythian nomads. For a colony so remote, Pantikapaion stayed remarkably connected to home. In its early centuries, imported Greek goods filled its markets - pottery, terracotta figurines, and metalwork carried by ship from Rhodes, Corinth, Samos, and Athens. Athenian potters even made a special line of painted bowls for the northern trade, wares so associated with this coast that scholars still call the style Kerch ware. Local craftsmen learned from the imports and made their own versions, so that Greek taste took root and flowered on a shore a thousand miles from the Aegean.

## The Coins of a Capital

A city that mints its own money is a city that matters. Pantikapaion struck silver coins from the fifth century BC and added gold and bronze in the fourth, and its gold staters - many stamped with the head of a satyr and a griffin gripping a spear - are among the most beautiful coins the ancient world produced. They were more than currency; they were a statement. By then Pantikapaion had become the capital of the Bosporan Kingdom, a realm that stitched together the Greek cities of the strait under a single dynasty. Its rulers grew rich as middlemen, funneling the grain of the Scythian steppe to the hungry cities of Greece and taking wine, oil, and fine craftsmanship in return. At its height the city sprawled across a hundred hectares, its acropolis crowning the hill that would later be named for King Mithridates.

## A City Beneath a City

Empires passed, and Pantikapaion passed with them, its glory fading over the centuries until the name itself slipped out of use. But it never truly vanished. The modern city of Kerch grew directly on top of it, so that the ancient capital survives as a layer in the ground - column drums, coin hoards, and house foundations surfacing whenever the earth is disturbed. Climb Mount Mithridat and you walk over the old acropolis; the ruins exposed along the slope let you stand where the kings of the Bosporus once looked out over their strait. Few cities have been so thoroughly buried by their own successors, and few reward the digging so richly. Every excavation here is a conversation across twenty-six centuries.

## Flight Context

Pantikapaion's excavated ruins lie at 45.351 N, 36.469 E on and around Mount Mithridat in central Kerch. From 1,500-2,500 feet, the hill with its summit obelisk and the grand staircase marks the site above the old town and ferry harbor. The nearest airfield is Kerch; Anapa (URKA) sits across the strait to the east.
