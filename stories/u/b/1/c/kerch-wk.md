# Kerch

One of the oldest cities in Europe, guarding the strait between two seas, where a Greek capital, a Byzantine citadel, and one of the Second World War's most harrowing sieges all left their mark.

**Location:** 45.3386, 36.4681

**Categories:** history, ancient, military-history, coastal

## Introduction

Climb the 428 steps of the Great Mithridates Staircase in Kerch and you rise through more than 2,500 years of history in a few minutes' breath. At the top stands the Monument of Glory, raised in 1944, looking out over a city that Greeks, Byzantines, Khazars, Genoese, Ottomans, and Russians each claimed in turn. Below and around it lie the scattered stones of Panticapaeum, a capital that minted its own coins when Rome was still a village. Kerch sits at the pinch point between the Black Sea and the Sea of Azov, and for that reason it has almost never been left in peace.

## The Capital on the Hill

The city's recorded story begins in the seventh century BC, when Greek colonists from Miletus founded Panticapaeum on Mount Mithridates near the mouth of the little Melek-Chesme River. By 480 BC it had become the capital of the Kingdom of Bosporus, and for a time, under Mithradates VI Eupator, the seat of the far larger Kingdom of Pontus. Sitting where the steppe met the sea routes to Europe, Panticapaeum grew rich exporting grain, salted fish, and wine, and the river, now shallow, once ran deep enough for sea galleys to sail inland. Gothic raiders struck in the 250s AD, and the Huns devastated the kingdom in 375. The ruins that survive are modest today, their finest treasures long carried off to museums in Moscow, Saint Petersburg, London, Paris, and Berlin, but the hill still holds them.

## A Name for Every Empire

Few cities have been renamed so relentlessly. Under Byzantine control from the sixth century, Emperor Justinian I ordered a citadel called Bospor built here. When the Turkic Khazars took it in the seventh century, they renamed it Karcha, from a word meaning "opposite" or "facing." To the Rus who followed, it was Korchev, a trading hub between Rus, Crimea, the Caucasus, and the East. The Genoese made it a colony called Cerco in 1318; the Ottomans took it in 1475, when it declined and, grimly, served as a slave market. Traces of each layer remain: the Church of St. John the Baptist, built in 717 and among the oldest in Eastern Europe, and the ruined Ottoman fortress of Yenikale on the strait.

## Hero City

Kerch wears the Soviet title of "Hero City," and it was earned in blood. From 1941 to 1945 the strait city was fought over again and again. The Germans took it in November 1941; the Soviets retook it by naval landing that December, lost it once more, and would not hold it securely until April 1944. In the Battle of the Kerch Peninsula, the Red Army lost more than 160,000 men killed or captured. When defenders were cut off, some withdrew into the Adzhimushkay quarries and fought on underground for months, enduring darkness, thirst, and gas, a story now preserved in a memorial to that catacomb resistance. Each May, a torchlight procession climbs Mount Mithridates on Victory Day, carrying flame up the same staircase the ancients once climbed.

## Flight Context

Kerch lies at 45.34 degrees north, 36.47 degrees east, at the eastern tip of Crimea overlooking the Kerch Strait. From 4,000 to 7,000 feet the standout landmarks are Mount Mithridates with its Monument of Glory above the city and, across the water, the long span of the Crimean Bridge linking the peninsula to the mainland. The main regional airport is Simferopol (UKFF), roughly 220 kilometers to the west.
