# Tyritake

A small Greek town on the Kerch shore that lived on wine and salted fish for a thousand years, weathering the Goths and the Huns before slipping quietly into the Middle Ages.

**Location:** 45.3508, 36.4686

**Categories:** Ancient History, Archaeology, Greek Colony, Crimea

## Introduction

Some ancient cities left libraries; Tyritake left fish bones and wine vats. The classical writers barely noticed this modest town on the eastern shore of Crimea - a line in Ptolemy, a passing mention by Pliny, who knew it by the name Dia. But the earth around Kerch has been more generous than the historians. Dig here, eleven kilometers south of the old Bosporan capital, and you find the machinery of an ordinary working town: presses for treading grapes, deep vats for salting fish, the patient industry of people who lived by the sea and made a good living from it for more than a thousand years.

## Wine, Salt, and the Sea

Tyritake was founded around the middle of the sixth century BC, one of the cluster of Greek colonies that grew up along the Kerch Strait under the Bosporan Kingdom. It never became grand, but it became productive. In its early centuries the town specialized in crafts and viticulture, and by the first centuries AD its economy rested squarely on two pillars: wine and fish. The strait teemed with fish, and Tyritake's people built stone vats to salt and preserve their catch on a scale that reached far beyond local appetite - salted fish and fish sauce were staples traded across the ancient world. Between the vineyards on the hills and the salting works by the shore, this was a town that fed people, a working port that turned the gifts of land and sea into cargo.

## The Centuries of Ruin

Prosperity on this frontier was never guaranteed. In the third century AD the Goths swept down and sacked the town; in the fourth, the Huns came and did it again. These were the great migrations that battered the whole late-antique world, waves of horsemen breaking against the settled coast. And yet Tyritake refused to die. Where many towns were simply abandoned, a settlement clung to this site through the upheavals and carried on into the Middle Ages - poorer, smaller, but stubbornly alive. There is something quietly moving in that persistence: generation after generation choosing to rebuild on the same salt-scented shore, long after the kingdom that founded them had faded into memory.

## Uncovering an Ordinary Town

Because it was never a capital, Tyritake tells us what most ancient histories leave out - how regular people actually lived. Serious excavation began in 1932, and the work has drawn scholars for the better part of a century since, from the great Bosporan expeditions of the mid-twentieth century to a Polish archaeological mission from the National Museum in Warsaw that joined the project in 2008. Their trenches have exposed the town's defensive walls, its houses, its vineyards and salting vats, layer upon layer of daily life pressed into the ground. Tyritake will never be famous. But for anyone curious about the texture of the ancient everyday - what people ate, how they worked, how they endured - this humble town on the strait is one of the richest storytellers of all.

## Flight Context

Tyritake lies at 45.351 N, 36.469 E, on the Crimean shore of the Kerch Strait south of central Kerch, near the Kamysh-Burun area. The low excavated site is hard to spot from altitude; approach at 1,500-2,500 feet using the Kerch shoreline and Mount Mithridat to the north as references. The nearest airfield is Kerch, with Anapa (URKA) across the strait to the east.
