# Genoese Gazaria

For two centuries a merchant republic ruled a scattered empire of Black Sea ports from a single Crimean capital, governed by law codes and a bank rather than a king.

**Location:** 45.0333, 35.3667

**Categories:** History, Medieval History, Trade, Crimea

## Introduction

There was once an empire on the Black Sea ruled not by a king but by merchants, and in the end by a bank. It was called Gazaria, the far-flung colonial domain of the Republic of Genoa, and from the mid-thirteenth century to the late fifteenth it strung a necklace of trading ports around the Black Sea, from Crimea to the coasts of what are now Ukraine, Romania, Russia, Georgia, and Abkhazia. Its capital was Caffa, present-day Feodosia, a Crimean harbor that swelled into one of the busiest markets in the medieval world. The name itself is a fossil: Gazaria comes from Khazaria, after the Khazars who had once ruled these lands and vanished long before the Genoese ever arrived, leaving behind only their name for others to borrow. It is one of history's stranger states, a commercial republic's overseas dominion run like a business venture.

## An Empire by Treaty

Gazaria was born from a bargain. In 1261 the Treaty of Nymphaeum, struck with the Emperor of Nicaea, granted Genoa the exclusive right to trade in the Mare Maius, the Great Sea, as the Genoese called the Black Sea. Five years later, in 1266, the Golden Horde granted them Caffa. From that single foothold the republic spread outward across the water. Beyond Crimea, where it held Caffa, Cembalo, Soldaia, and other towns, Genoese castles and colonies dotted an enormous arc of coastline: Maurocastro at the mouth of the Dniester, outposts near Odesa and along the Danube at Kiliya and Galati, Tana at the far end of the Sea of Azov, and a chain of harbors running down the Caucasus coast through Abkhazia to Batumi in Georgia. It was a maritime empire in the truest sense, held together not by contiguous territory but by ships, harbors, and the relentless, profitable movement of trade between continents.

## Governed by a Book

What makes Gazaria remarkable is how carefully it was run. After regaining Caffa in 1313, following a siege by the Mongol khan Toqta, Genoa built a formal apparatus of government. Legislative power lay with the Officium Gazarie, eight magistrates serving six-month terms who chose their own successors. A Consul of Caffa, appointed for a year and aided by a chancellor, held executive authority, advised by an elected council of twenty-four, half nobles and half merchants and artisans, a few of them local residents granted Genoese citizenship. In 1341 the colony's laws were gathered into the Liber Gazarie, the Book of Gazaria, still preserved in the State Archives of Genoa and updated a century later. Few medieval possessions so distant from home were governed with such deliberate, documented order.

## The Bank That Inherited an Empire

Gazaria's final chapter belongs, fittingly, to finance. The cost of defending these scattered colonies had been advanced by a syndicate of state creditors, and that syndicate belonged to the Bank of Saint George, one of the earliest and most powerful banks in Europe, which came to manage Gazaria's taxes outright. When Constantinople fell to the Ottomans in 1453, Genoa made an extraordinary decision: it handed sovereignty over the entire colonial empire to the bank, judging it the only institution with the resources and organization to mount a defense against the advancing Turks. It was a telling choice, a state entrusting its overseas dominion not to a general or a prince but to its bankers. The gambit failed. In 1475 the Ottomans conquered the Genoese possessions, and Gazaria passed into history. Its heraldry and law codes survive in archives and museums, the paper trail of a merchant empire that governed a sea by contract and ledger for more than two hundred years. Little of Gazaria's political shape is remembered today outside specialist histories, yet its physical traces remain scattered around the Black Sea, in the stout towers and walls the Genoese raised at Caffa and elsewhere, silent testimony to the improbable reach of a single Italian city that once turned the whole Great Sea into its marketplace.

## Flight Context

Gazaria's capital, Caffa, sits at 45.03°N, 35.37°E on Crimea's eastern coast; the wider domain ringed the whole Black Sea. From 3,000 to 6,000 feet over Feodosia, the surviving Genoese fortress and towers still mark the old capital above the bay. Look for Feodosia Bay opening onto the Black Sea. Nearest airport is Simferopol (UKFF) to the west.
