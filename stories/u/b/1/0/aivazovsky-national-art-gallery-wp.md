# Aivazovsky National Art Gallery

The seaside house where one of history's greatest painters of the sea lived, worked, and left his life's collection to the town that raised him.

**Location:** 45.0323, 35.3828

**Categories:** Art Museums, Culture, Feodosia, Maritime Art

## Introduction

The man who painted the sea better than almost anyone lived within sight of it, in a house in Feodosia on the Crimean coast. Ivan Aivazovsky was born here in 1817, baptized Hovhannes Aivazian to an Armenian family, and over a career spanning nearly six decades he produced something on the order of six thousand paintings, most of them water and light and the endlessly shifting moods of the sea. He knew this coast in his bones; he had grown up beside it, and he returned to it to work and, in time, to give. In 1845 he began showing his canvases in his own home, forty-nine of them to start, and that private display grew into one of the oldest art museums in all of Ukraine. When he died in 1900, he left the whole gallery to Feodosia in his will. It has carried his name ever since, a rare case of a museum that is also a monument to the man who filled it.

## A House Full of Waves

Aivazovsky's masterpiece, The Ninth Wave of 1850, shows survivors clinging to a mast as an enormous swell rears over them at dawn, terror and hope caught in the same glowing light. It is a painting about the sea's power and human fragility at once, and it made him famous across Europe and Russia alike. His gallery grew to match the reputation. By 1880 he had added a purpose-built exhibition hall to the house, and the collection became the third museum in the entire Russian Empire, following only the Hermitage in St. Petersburg and the Tretyakov in Moscow, remarkable company for a museum in a small provincial port. Today it holds around twelve thousand works on nautical themes, among them 417 paintings by Aivazovsky himself, the largest gathering of his art anywhere in the world. To walk its rooms is to move through storm and calm, shipwreck and sunrise, moonlit harbor and open ocean, all rendered by a single restless hand that never seemed to tire of water.

## The Cimmerian School

Aivazovsky was not the only artist Feodosia and its luminous coast inspired. A separate building, once the home of the painter's sister, gathers work beyond his own: mythological and biblical scenes, foreign marine paintings of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, and the canvases of the Cimmerian school, a circle of artists who took their name from the ancient people said to have lived on these shores. Their number included the poet-painter Maximilian Voloshin, Konstantin Bogaevsky, Lev Lagorio, and Aivazovsky's own grandson Mikhail Latri, alongside guests to the region such as Isaac Levitan and Arkhip Kuindzhi. Together they turned the dry, amber light of eastern Crimea into a recognizable school of landscape, a regional vision of sea and stone and haze that outlasted the master who drew them all here.

## Survival and Homage

The gallery has weathered hard years. Late in 1920, during the upheavals of the Russian Civil War, the local Cheka occupied Aivazovsky's house, and several paintings were damaged in the process, a reminder of how narrowly such collections survive the turmoil around them. In 1922 it became a state museum under the Soviet Union, a status that carried it safely through the decades that followed. In 1930 the town returned the artist's gift in kind, unveiling a monument to him by the sculptor Ilya Ginzburg in front of the main building, bearing a simple, grateful inscription: Feodosia to Aivazovsky. Painter and town remain bound together across more than a century. He gave the place his life's work and even his name; the place gave his art a permanent home and its most treasured door. To this day visitors come to Feodosia chiefly to stand where the great painter of the sea once stood, and to see the water through his eyes.

## Flight Context

The gallery sits in central Feodosia at 45.03°N, 35.38°E, on the Black Sea coast of Crimea. From 2,000 to 5,000 feet the town reads as a compact seaside grid wrapped around its harbor and bay. Look for the curve of Feodosia Bay and the port. Nearest airport is Simferopol (UKFF) to the west.
