# Siege of Caffa

The medieval siege of a Crimean port that gave the world one of its earliest recorded acts of biological warfare, and perhaps the opening act of the Black Death.

**Location:** 45.0333, 35.3667

**Categories:** History, Medieval History, Military History, Crimea

## Introduction

It may be the most consequential siege you have never heard of. In the 1340s a Crimean harbor called Caffa, the Genoese trading capital that is now Feodosia, was surrounded by the armies of the Golden Horde under the khan Jani Beg. It was one of seven Genoese ports in the Crimean domain called Gazaria, and its fall or survival mattered far beyond its walls. What happened there would echo across an entire continent. According to a famous account, when plague broke out among the besiegers, they hurled infected corpses over the city walls, an early and monstrous instance of biological warfare, and the disease that followed may have been the Black Death itself, setting out from this very port to kill a third or more of all Europe. Whether it happened in exactly that way is still debated by historians. That the pestilence passed through here on its way west is not.

## The Prize on the Black Sea

Caffa had grown rich beyond its size. Founded by Genoese merchants in 1266 through a purchase from the Golden Horde, it sat at a crossroads of trade between Europe and Asia, its wharves handling spices, silk, and precious metals bound for Mediterranean markets. It was also a genuine crossroads of peoples: Genoese, Greeks, Armenians, Jews, and Muslims lived and traded within its walls, making it one of the medieval world's more cosmopolitan places, a small polyglot city on the edge of the steppe. That wealth made it a target. Relations between the Genoese and the Mongol rulers had long swung between cooperation and conflict; an earlier khan, Toqta, had besieged the city in 1308 over the Italian trade in Turkic captives sold into military slavery far to the south, and the Genoese had burned their own town and fled. His successor mended the breach, and Caffa thrived again. But when tensions flared once more under Jani Beg in the 1340s, the great port found itself besieged all over again.

## Behind the Walls

The defense of Caffa was fierce and drawn out. Jani Beg's forces first struck in 1343, but the siege was broken in February 1344 when an Italian relief force shattered the Mongol siege engines. He renewed the assault in 1345, cutting off supplies by land and reducing the city to misery. Yet Caffa held. Its fortifications were strong and constantly reinforced, and because the Genoese could still be supplied by sea, they endured what should have starved them out. The defenders met bombardments and direct assaults with disciplined patrols, repairs, and Greek fire, the flammable weapon that could repel men swarming the walls. Month after month the stalemate ground on, both sides bleeding, neither able to force a decision, until something arrived that no wall could keep out.

## The Disease That Escaped

Plague broke out in the Mongol camp, likely carried by the rodents and fleas that thrived in the filth of a long encampment, and it devastated Jani Beg's army. By 1347 he was forced to lift the siege. The Italian notary Gabriel de Mussis left a vivid account of infected corpses flung over the walls and the terror that spread among Caffa's people, and for centuries this stood as the story of how the Black Death entered Europe. Modern scholars are more cautious: de Mussis was not there, and the pandemic more plausibly traveled west in grain ships once the wartime embargoes lifted. However it spread, the human toll defies comprehension. Across the next few years the Black Death killed perhaps a third to three-fifths of Europe's population, unmaking economies, emptying villages, and hastening the end of feudalism. Standing in quiet Feodosia today, it is sobering to think a catastrophe of that scale may have begun on this shore.

## Flight Context

The siege took place at Caffa, modern Feodosia, at 45.03°N, 35.37°E on Crimea's eastern coast. From 3,000 to 6,000 feet the town resolves around Feodosia Bay, where the surviving Genoese fortress and towers still mark the walls that held against Jani Beg. Look for the fortress on the shore and the arc of the bay. Nearest airport is Simferopol (UKFF) to the west.
