# Mufti-Jami Mosque

A four-century-old Ottoman mosque in Feodosia that survived conquest and exile by spending much of its life as a Christian church.

**Location:** 45.0219, 35.3918

**Categories:** Historic Sites, Religion, Ottoman Architecture, Feodosia

## Introduction

The Mufti-Jami Mosque survives today largely because, for a long stretch of its life, it stopped being a mosque. Built in the 1620s in a corner of Feodosia's old town once so densely Ottoman that people called it Little Istanbul, this square, domed building outlasted nearly every other mosque in the city as they fell to conquest and time. It endured by becoming a church, twice over, and the paint beneath its plaster still carries the memory of those years. In a Crimean port that has changed hands and faiths again and again, and where whole architectural eras have been swept away, the Mufti-Jami is a genuinely rare thing: an Ottoman house of worship still standing on its original ground, and once more open to prayer, after four turbulent centuries.

## The Shape of the Building

The mosque is a study in Ottoman restraint. Its core is a square roughly sixteen meters on each side, capped by a single dome, with the slender minaret rising at the northwestern corner. The main entrance faces north through a portal framed by windows and mihrab niches, and a second door opens to the west. Inside, a large rectangular mihrab, some seven meters by three, marks the direction of Mecca opposite the main door. A man named Musa commissioned the mosque in 1623, and it was finished by 1639, a substantial undertaking for a provincial port. In the courtyard stand the ruins of a small tomb, possibly one of two pilgrimage sites the great Ottoman traveler Evliya Çelebi recorded here in the seventeenth century, those of Sehid Baba and Seyh Ebubekir. A few broken gravestones still remain nearby; others have been moved to the city museum for safekeeping, small survivors of the community that once gathered around these walls.

## Saved by Conversion

When the Russian Empire took Crimea in 1783, most of Feodosia's Ottoman-era buildings were destroyed. The Mufti-Jami was spared, and given a new purpose: first as a Russian Orthodox church, then as an Armenian Catholic one. That second life proved its salvation once more. In 1944, when the Soviet authorities carried out the mass deportation of the Crimean Tatars, expelling an entire people from their homeland in one of the twentieth century's great acts of collective punishment, mosques across Crimea fell into ruin or worse. But the Mufti-Jami was not being used as a mosque at the time, and so it survived where others did not. Its history holds a quiet irony: the building outlasted catastrophe precisely by not being, for a while, what it was built to be. The change of faith that might have erased it instead preserved it, and gave the Crimean Tatars, when they began to return, one of the few Ottoman sanctuaries left to reclaim.

## The Paint Beneath the Plaster

The mosque's return began late in the twentieth century. Restoration work touched the building through the 1960s and 1970s; the minaret was rebuilt on its old base in 1976, and hand-painted designs added during its years as a church were plastered over in 1975. Then, in 1995, the Mufti-Jami reopened for Muslim worship, and those hidden church paintings were uncovered once again, layers of two faiths now visible side by side on the same walls. Regular prayer has continued here since 1998. To step inside is to stand within a rare survivor, a place that has been Muslim and Orthodox and Catholic in turn, and that carries all of those lives openly rather than hiding any of them, a single small building holding the whole tangled religious history of Crimea within its walls. Few structures anywhere wear their contradictions so plainly. The dome that shelters Muslim prayer today once sheltered Christian worship; the painted saints and the mihrab share the same air. In a peninsula where so much has been demolished or renamed, the Mufti-Jami simply kept adding layers, and survived by refusing to be only one thing.

## Flight Context

The mosque stands in Feodosia's old town at 45.02°N, 35.39°E, near the Black Sea shore. From 2,000 to 4,000 feet the town resolves around Feodosia Bay; look for the compact old quarter and the harbor, with the Genoese fortress nearby. Nearest airport is Simferopol (UKFF) to the west.
