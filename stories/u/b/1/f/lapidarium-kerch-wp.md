# Lapidarium, Kerch

One of the world's great collections of ancient carved stone, where more than two thousand Bosporan inscriptions, statues, and gravestones preserve the voices of a vanished kingdom.

**Location:** 45.3637, 36.5318

**Categories:** Museum, Archaeology, Ancient History, Crimea

## Introduction

A lapidarium is a museum of stone, and few places on earth have more of it to show than this one. In Kerch, in a building set aside for the purpose, stand more than two thousand carved slabs, statues, and gravestones gathered from the ruins of the Bosporan Kingdom - a hoard of worked marble and limestone so rich that scholars rank it among the leading collections of classical inscriptions anywhere in the world. Walk its rooms and you are surrounded by the handwriting of antiquity, chiseled into stone by people who wanted, above all, to be remembered.

## The Collector Who Started It All

The collection traces back to a single obsessed man. In the 1810s and 1820s, Paul Du Brux - a Frenchman who had settled in Kerch and served as its customs officer - began gathering the ancient objects turning up all around the city, and gave his growing hoard a Russian name meaning, roughly, ancient repository. He was one of the founders of Kerch's museum of antiquities, and the same tireless curiosity that drove him to record the plan of nearby Myrmekion drove him to rescue the carved stones that others treated as building material. His personal collection became the seed. Over the following century it swelled from many sources, most of it assembled before 1917, though along the way notable pieces were carried off to the British Museum, the Hermitage, and Moscow's Pushkin Museum.

## A Kingdom Carved in Stone

What survives here is the physical memory of the Bosporan world. There are portrait sculptures of its people and grave stelae raised over its dead; there are architectural fragments, fountains, tables, and the facades of temples; there are Greek inscriptions cut into marble alongside later Hebrew and Turkish gravestones, each a different community leaving its mark on the same shore. Together they illustrate the ordinary and the sacred life of the ancient cities of the strait - who these people worshipped, how they honored their dead, what they wanted carved above their names. During the Soviet years part of the collection sheltered inside the Church of St. John the Baptist, one of the oldest churches in the region; today it occupies its own building, with rooms set aside for restoration and study.

## A Collection Still Growing

Unlike most museums, this one is not finished. The ancient sites around Kerch are still being excavated, and every season's digging sends new stones to the lapidarium - fresh inscriptions, unearthed statues, gravestones pulled from the ground after two thousand years. In 2002 an international school for the conservation of stone was founded here, drawing specialists to develop the painstaking craft of preserving weathered marble and limestone. The work is slow and exacting, the kind that measures progress in decades rather than seasons. But that is fitting for a place whose whole purpose is endurance: a repository built to keep the carved voices of the Bosporan Kingdom legible for the people who come after.

## Flight Context

The Kerch Lapidarium lies at 45.364 N, 36.532 E, in the eastern part of Kerch near the Adzhimushkay area. The museum building itself is hard to distinguish from altitude; approach at 1,500-2,500 feet using central Kerch, Mount Mithridat, and the strait shoreline as references. The nearest airfield is Kerch, with Anapa (URKA) across the strait to the east.
