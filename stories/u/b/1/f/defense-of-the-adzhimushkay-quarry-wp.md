# Defense of the Adzhimushkay Quarry

For 170 days in 1942, thousands of Soviet soldiers and civilians held out in the flooded darkness of a limestone quarry near Kerch, dying of thirst rather than surrender.

**Location:** 45.3812, 36.5235

**Categories:** World War II, Military History, Memorial, Crimea

## Introduction

The men who defended Adzhimushkay left their words on the walls. Scratched into the cold limestone of the catacombs, in the flickering light of dwindling lamps, they wrote diaries and farewells that would be found decades later - the last testimony of people who knew they were unlikely to see daylight again. For five months in 1942, thousands of Soviet soldiers and ordinary civilians lived and died in the darkness beneath a small mining suburb near Kerch, waging one of the most desperate underground stands of the Second World War.

## Into the Dark

Adzhimushkay sat five kilometers from Kerch, above a warren of limestone tunnels quarried since 1830 - the Great and Small catacombs, miles of passages carved out for building stone. In May 1942, a German counteroffensive shattered the Red Army's foothold on the Kerch Peninsula. As the front collapsed and the evacuation across the strait began, a rear guard was left at Adzhimushkay to cover the retreat. It quickly became something larger. Retreating soldiers and civilians fleeing the city gathered around it, until the group swelled to many thousands. When it grew clear the shore could not be held, they went underground: more than ten thousand into the Great catacombs, another three thousand into the Small. The larger garrison was led by officers named Yagunov, Parakhin, and Burmin; the smaller by Yermakov, Povazhny, and Karpekin. Above them, the enemy closed in.

## The War for Water

The catacombs had never been meant for a siege. No supplies were stockpiled; worse, every well lay outside, in the open, under enemy guns. To drink was to fight. Reaching water meant mounting an armed sortie into the daylight, and men died for every bucketful. When the wells became impossible, the defenders licked moisture that condensed on the mine ceilings and dug their own shafts down through the rock - as deep as fourteen meters - hunting for the water table below. They butchered livestock that had died in the entrances. They fought back too, and hard: on the night of 8 July they overran the German garrison in Adzhimushkay itself, an assault in which Colonel Yagunov was killed. The Germans answered by ringing the quarries with barbed wire, sealing the entrances, and bombing and shelling the ground above their heads. Slowly, hunger and thirst did what the shells could not.

## What the Walls Remember

The siege lasted about 170 days. On 30 October 1942, German forces finally entered the tunnels and took the last survivors. Of the roughly thirteen thousand who had gone underground, estimates of those who lived to be captured range only from 48 to 300 - and captivity brought its own cruelty; Ivan Parakhin and other defenders were later executed in the Simferopol prison. It is a toll almost too large to feel, and yet the diaries pull it back to human scale: these were named men, with families and fears, writing to be remembered. Their words survived them. A museum opened in the quarry in 1966, and a full memorial complex followed in 1982, so that visitors could descend into the same darkness and read, on the living rock, the last messages of people who chose not to surrender.

## Flight Context

The Adzhimushkay catacombs and memorial lie at 45.381 N, 36.524 E, in a northeastern suburb of Kerch. From 1,500-2,500 feet, look for the memorial complex on the flat quarry ground north of the old town; Mount Mithridat and the Kerch Strait lie to the south. The nearest airfield is Kerch, with Anapa (URKA) across the strait to the east.
