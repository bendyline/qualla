# Battle of the Kerch Peninsula

Across five brutal months in 1941 and 1942, three Soviet armies bled to death on a narrow Crimean peninsula, in a campaign whose ferocity and human cost still haunt the flat land where it was fought.

**Location:** 45.2500, 35.9833

**Categories:** World War II, military history, Crimea, memorial

## Introduction

On the night after Christmas in 1941, Soviet soldiers crowded into fishing trawlers on the Taman Peninsula and pushed out into a freezing winter sea. They had no proper landing craft, so they made do with whaleboats and whatever floated. Many drowned before they reached the shore; others staggered onto the beaches near Kerch already half-dead from hypothermia. This was the opening of the Battle of the Kerch Peninsula, a five-month struggle at the eastern edge of Crimea meant to relieve the besieged fortress-city of Sevastopol. It would become one of the great Soviet catastrophes of the war, a place where more than a third of a million men were lost on the flat, exposed ground where they had nowhere to hide.

## A Landing in the Ice

The plan was audacious and, as it turned out, beyond what the men could achieve. Beginning on 26 December 1941, two Soviet armies were to storm ashore in the German rear, retake the peninsula, and break through to Sevastopol. The first major amphibious operation in Soviet history, it was scattered across many small landings at once, on beaches north and south of Kerch and at the port of Feodosia. Winter storms, a lack of radios, and the improvised boats made a hard task nearly impossible. Yet the sheer boldness bought a real prize: the landings prevented Sevastopol from falling and seized the initiative for a moment. That moment did not last. As the Soviets dug in along the peninsula, the German 11th Army under Erich von Manstein gathered itself for a response that would prove merciless.

## The Long Winter of Failed Offensives

From January through April 1942, the Soviet Crimean Front, commanded by Dmitry Kozlov, hurled itself again and again at the German lines across the Parpach Narrows, the pinched waist of the peninsula. Each offensive failed, and each failed at terrible cost. The terrain gave no cover. Warm spells turned the ground to a sea of mud that swallowed tanks and forced men to carry artillery shells by hand. German gunners, dug into fortified strongpoints, cut down attacking infantry in the open. Superior German artillery firepower, the sources agree, was largely responsible for the debacle. Across those months the Red Army lost roughly 352,000 men in the attacks, while Axis casualties numbered around 24,120, an unbalanced ledger of suffering that speaks to how completely the offensives broke against the German defense.

## Bustard Hunt

The end came fast and violent. On 8 May 1942 the Germans launched Operation Trappenjagd, a name that means bustard hunt, and it lived up to its grim implication. Manstein struck the southern sector with concentrated infantry, armor, and artillery, and above it all flew Wolfram von Richthofen's VIII. Fliegerkorps, some 800 aircraft flying an average of 1,500 sorties a day. The Soviet second line broke in barely 210 minutes. A panzer division swung north, trapped the Soviet 51st Army against the Sea of Azov, and by 11 May its divisions were surrendering. Kerch itself fell on 15 May, much of it burned by relentless bombing. In eleven days, three Soviet armies were destroyed or shattered. One historian wrote that at Kerch the Soviet command threw away twenty-one divisions in a nightmare of confusion and mismanagement.

## The Ground Remembers

The battle's cruelty did not end with the fighting. When Soviet defenders and civilians refused to surrender and took refuge in the limestone catacombs beneath the region, the Germans deployed poison gas against them, a horror endured by soldiers and sheltering families alike. And the peninsula had already witnessed atrocity: during the offensive, the Soviet photojournalist Dmitri Baltermants documented the aftermath of a mass execution near Kerch, where some seven thousand people, among them Jewish victims of the Holocaust, had been shot and left in anti-tank trenches. These were not statistics but neighbors, parents, and children, murdered on ground that would soon be fought over by armies. To stand on this quiet, flat land today is to stand above a great deal of grief, and to owe it a moment's remembrance.

## Flight Context

The battle was fought across the Kerch Peninsula, centered around 45.25°N, 35.98°E, with major action at the Parpach Narrows, near Feodosia to the west, and at Kerch on the strait to the east. From 6,000 feet the peninsula's flat plains, the pinch of land between the Black Sea and the Sea of Azov, and the port of Kerch are all easy to identify. Nearest major airfield is Simferopol (UKFF), roughly 110 km west; Kerch lies at the peninsula's eastern tip.
