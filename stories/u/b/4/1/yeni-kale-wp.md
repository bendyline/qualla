# Yeni-Kale

An Ottoman fortress designed to lock the Kerch Strait against the Russian navy, betrayed in the end by its single unsolvable weakness: it had no water of its own.

**Location:** 45.3494, 36.6046

**Categories:** Fortifications, Ottoman History, Military History, Black Sea

## Introduction

The fortress had cannons enough to command a strait, walls enclosing a mosque and a bath-house and powder magazines, a garrison of more than a thousand men, and one problem no engineer could fully solve. Yeni-Kale had no water. It stood on a bare shoulder of the Kerch peninsula overlooking the strait that connects the Black Sea to the Sea of Azov, and the Ottoman Turks who built it between 1699 and 1706 had to run an underground pipe for kilometers just to bring drinking water to the men behind its walls. The name means simply "New Castle," and for a few decades it was the padlock the Ottomans hung on the gateway to the Sea of Azov.

## An Italian Convert and His French Engineers

The man who directed the building of Yeni-Kale was known as Goloppo, an Italian who had converted to Islam and entered Ottoman service, and several French engineers worked alongside him. Together they raised a stronghold of some twenty-five thousand square meters on ground that had once held the ancient Greek town of Myrmekion, whose remains still surface in the area. The fortress was armed with powerful cannons and positioned to dominate the narrowest, most strategic stretch of the Kerch Strait, where any fleet passing between the two seas came within range of its guns. Inside its walls stood everything a besieged garrison might need to hold out: two powder magazines, an arsenal, a water reservoir, living quarters, a bath-house, and a mosque. It also served as the residence of the pasha, making it a seat of Ottoman authority as much as a military post.

## The Guns Fall Silent

The lock did not hold. During the Russo-Turkish War of 1768 to 1774, the Russian Army swept into Crimea in the summer of 1771. Reinforcements had reached Yeni-Kale from the Ottoman Empire beforehand, but the defenders judged the position untenable and chose to abandon it rather than fight. Russian units under General Nikolay Borzov marched into the empty fortress on 21 June 1771 without a battle. For the fortress's commandant, Abaza Muhammad Pasha, the retreat proved fatal in a different way. He fled across the Black Sea to Sinop, and the sultan, tallying his failures, sentenced him to death. When the Treaty of Küçük Kaynarca ended the war in 1774, Kerch and Yeni-Kale passed to Russia, absorbed into the Kerch-Yenikale municipality of the new Taurida Governorate.

## From Battlements to Ruins

A fortress without a war has to find another purpose, and in the nineteenth century Yeni-Kale became a Russian military hospital, its wards filling rooms once meant for gunners and guards. Even that role faded. From the 1880s the place stood completely deserted, its walls left to the wind off the strait and the slow work of decay. Today the ruins draw visitors who climb among the crumbling gates and ramparts, looking out over the same shipping lane the fortress was built to control, and the neighboring district of Kerch still carries the name Yeni-Kale. What remains is a skeleton of empire, an eighteenth-century puzzle in stone whose most stubborn flaw was never the enemy but the simple absence of a spring.

## Flight Context

Yeni-Kale sits at 45.35°N, 36.60°E on the northeastern edge of Kerch, on the Crimean shore of the Kerch Strait at its narrowest point. From the air, orient on the strait itself and the long Crimean Bridge that spans it nearby to the south; the fortress is a low stone ruin on the coastline best seen from lower altitudes in clear weather. Nearest airport is Kerch's airfield, with Anapa-Vityazevo (URKA) across the strait on the Russian mainland.
