# Tmutarakan

A medieval trading city so remote its name became Russian slang for the middle of nowhere, Tmutarakan once controlled the gateway between the Black Sea and the Sea of Azov.

**Location:** 45.2191, 36.7141

**Categories:** Ancient History, Medieval History, Archaeology, Black Sea

## Introduction

In Russian, to say something is happening in "Tmutarakan" is to say it is happening in the back of beyond, the last godforsaken place on earth. The poet Vladimir Mayakovsky used the word exactly that way. What most Russians never realize is that Tmutarakan was real, a genuine medieval city that once controlled one of the most valuable chokepoints in the known world: the Cimmerian Bosporus, the narrow passage where the Black Sea drains into the Sea of Azov. For a century straddling the year 1000, this was a wealthy principality of Kievan Rus, ruled by princes who minted their own coins, on the Taman Peninsula directly opposite the Crimean city of Kerch.

## A City of Many Names, a City of Many Peoples

Long before the Rus arrived, Greeks from Mytilene on the island of Lesbos founded a colony here in the mid-sixth century BCE and called it Hermonassa. It flourished for centuries, a center of the Bosporan cult of Aphrodite, its soil so rich with the past that excavators later pulled a hoard of fourth-century golden coins from the ground. After the collapse of Roman authority and the storms of the Hunnic and Bulgar invasions, the Khazars rebuilt the town as a fortress in the late seventh century and renamed it Tamatarkha. Arabic writers knew it as Samkarsh al-Yahud, "Samkarsh of the Jews," because so much of its commerce ran through Jewish merchants. Behind its strong brick walls and fine harbor lived Greeks, Armenians, Rus, Jews, Ossetians, Lezgins, Georgians, and Circassians, a single crowded port speaking a dozen languages and trading toward Byzantium, Armenia, and the distant Silk Road.

## Princes, Poison, and a Fire That Would Not Die

When the city came under Kievan Rus in the late tenth century, it entered a violent age of ambitious princes. Vladimir the Great gave it to one of his sons, and his grandson Mstislav raised its first stone church, dedicated to the Mother of God and built by Byzantine hands. What followed was a carousel of coups. Gleb Svyatoslavich held the city, was thrown out, and was thrown out again; the rival prince Rostislav seized it, subdued the local Circassians, and grew powerful enough to alarm the Byzantines across the water at Chersonesos. In 1066 their envoy poisoned him. The empire's deep interest here had a chemical explanation: naphtha deposits lay in the ground nearby, and naphtha was an essential ingredient of Greek Fire, the terrifying incendiary weapon of the Byzantine navy. Guarding those deposits, Constantinople long forbade even its Genoese trading partners from entering the town they called Matracha.

## Twelve Meters of Buried Time

Empires faded and the city faded with them. In the thirteenth century it passed to the Empire of Trebizond, then to Genoa, then to the Crimean Khanate in 1482, and finally to Russia in 1791. Its last written mention comes from a scroll of 1378, after which Tmutarakan sank into legend. Then, in 1792, a local peasant turned up a remarkable stone. Carved into it was an inscription recording that Prince Gleb had measured the frozen sea from here to Kerch in 1068, a medieval survey preserved by chance in the soil. The Stone of Tmutarakan reawakened the ruins to scholars, and archaeologists have dug here ever since. In some places the layers of habitation run more than twelve meters deep, city stacked upon city, the accumulated weight of three thousand years pressed into the earth beneath the wind and the Cossack town of Taman that grew up beside the ruins.

## Flight Context

Tmutarakan lies at 45.22°N, 36.71°E on the Taman Peninsula, beneath the modern village of Taman on the coast of Taman Bay. From the air, orient on the Kerch Strait separating the peninsula from Crimea and the city of Kerch directly across the water; the ruins are low coastal ground with no dramatic relief. Nearest airports are Anapa-Vityazevo (URKA) to the southeast and Krasnodar (URKK) inland.
