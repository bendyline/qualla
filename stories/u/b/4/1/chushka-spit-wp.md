# Chushka Spit

An eighteen-kilometer arm of sand reaching into the Kerch Strait, carrying a lighthouse, a railway, and a busy port, and once the anchor of a doomed wartime bridge.

**Location:** 45.3500, 36.6947

**Categories:** Geography, Coastal, Maritime, Krasnodar Krai

## Introduction

Look at a map of the Kerch Strait and one feature seems almost drawn by hand: a long, impossibly thin finger of sand curving out from the Taman shore toward the open water, reaching nearly eighteen kilometers into the channel that joins the Sea of Azov to the Black Sea. This is the Chushka Spit, on the Russian side of the strait in Krasnodar Krai. It is a landform in constant negotiation with the sea - built of sand the currents pile up and could just as easily carry away - and along its narrow spine runs a surprising amount of the modern world.

## A Road Built on Sand

The Chushka Spit forms the northern shore of Taman Bay, throwing off long branches to the south as the currents rework it. For all its fragility, people have laid serious infrastructure along its length. A lighthouse went up in 1914, showing a fixed red light from a height of about seventy-five feet to guide ships through the tricky strait. A railway line and a highway run the length of the spit out to Port Kavkaz, near its far tip - a working freight harbor that also served as the terminal of the ferry line linking the Taman Peninsula to Crimea across the water. To travel the spit is to ride a ribbon of rail and asphalt with the sea pressing close on both sides, the land beneath you no wider in places than the road itself.

## The Bridge That Ice Destroyed

The spit once anchored one of the boldest and most ill-fated engineering efforts of its century. In 1944, as the Second World War ground on, Soviet engineers ran a railway bridge from Chushka clear across the strait to the Kerch Peninsula, using materials the retreating German forces had left behind. It was, briefly, the longest bridge in the Soviet Union. But the Kerch Strait freezes, and in the winter that followed, great floes of drifting ice slammed into the piers. Artillery and blasting could not break the ice up fast enough; icebreakers could not reach the spans. By early 1945 the bridge was crippled beyond saving and had to be dismantled. Chushka went back to being what it had been - a spit with a lighthouse, a railhead, and the memory of an ambition the sea refused to allow.

## When the Sea Turned Black

The same waters that make the spit useful make it vulnerable. In November 2007, a fierce storm tore through the strait and damaged a fuel tanker off the Chushka Spit, spilling more than two thousand metric tons of heavy fuel oil into the sea. The slick coated the shallows where the currents run strong, and marine life paid the price - mussels and other invertebrates along the shore were badly hit. The spit's recovery was quicker than feared; by the following summer the mussel beds had begun to bounce back. It was a reminder of the double life this thin strip of sand leads: a highway for cargo and ferries and, at the same time, a shore where the sea's dangers wash up first. Chushka endures, low and narrow, holding its ground between the two seas.

## Flight Context

The Chushka Spit runs from roughly 45.35 N, 36.695 E on the Taman shore of the Kerch Strait, in Krasnodar Krai. From 2,000-3,000 feet its long, curving sand finger and the port facilities near its tip are unmistakable; the great arc of the Crimean Bridge crosses the strait to the south. The nearest airfields are Anapa (URKA) to the southeast and Kerch across the strait to the west.
