# Yeni Kale Lighthouse

A lighthouse on Crimea's easternmost point that has been carried off, blown apart, and rebuilt from rubble, keeping alive a beam that ancient Greek sailors first lit here more than two thousand years ago.

**Location:** 45.3856, 36.6389

**Categories:** Lighthouses, Maritime History, Military History, Black Sea

## Introduction

Long before there was a lighthouse, there was a fire. The Greek sailor's manual known as the Periplus of Scylax, written around 350 BCE, already noted the navigation beacons burning on this coast, guiding ships from the Sea of Azov down into the Kerch Strait. More than two thousand years later, the beam still shines from the same headland, Cape Fonar, the easternmost point of Crimea, just below the old Ottoman fortress of Yeni-Kale. The Yeni Kale Lighthouse is the living heir of those ancient fires, and its history is a story of a light that refused to go out, no matter how many times it was carried away or knocked down.

## From Oil Flame to Fresnel Glass

The proper tower rose in 1820, built to shepherd vessels through the tricky passage where the sea narrows toward Kerch. In the beginning its light came from simple oil lamps, a flame in a lantern room doing the same job the old cressets had done, only steadier. Then in 1861 came a leap in technology: a Fresnel lens, that marvel of nineteenth-century optics whose concentric rings of glass gather a modest flame and throw it far out to sea, paired here with brighter kerosene lamps. For generations this was the oldest lighthouse in all of Crimea, a fixed and trusted point on the mariner's chart, and the keepers who tended it lived by the discipline of the light, keeping it burning night after night above the restless strait.

## The Keeper Who Saved the Light

War nearly ended the tower's long vigil. In 1941, as the fighting closed in, the lighthouse keeper Mikhail Egorov did a quietly heroic thing: he evacuated the lighthouse equipment across the water to the Taman coast, saving the precious optics from destruction. The tower itself was not so lucky. In May 1942, a battle raged around Cape Fonar between the retreating Red Army and German forces. When enemy tanks reached the lighthouse, a flag was raised on the tower to mark it as a target for Soviet guns firing from the Chushka Spit, and the shells came. The tower was destroyed, and the hydrographers who had held out inside it were killed. The light that had guided sailors for over a century went dark, its lantern room reduced to rubble on the headland.

## Rebuilt from the Rubble

But the beam came back, as it always had. Even in 1943, with the tower in ruins, a temporary light was set up on the wreckage, because ships still needed guiding through the strait. When Kerch was liberated in April 1944, Egorov's rescued equipment was returned to the cape it belonged to. A makeshift wooden tower went up in 1946, and in 1953 a proper stone lighthouse rose again on Cape Fonar, electric-powered and modern. New optics arrived in 1957, and in 2002 the ancient beacon joined the space age with a satellite navigation system linking it to GLONASS and GPS. Two and a half millennia after the first Greek fires flickered on this shore, the light still turns above the water, unbroken across empires and wars, still keeping its old promise to everyone who sails the strait.

## Flight Context

The Yeni Kale Lighthouse stands at 45.39°N, 36.64°E on Cape Fonar, the easternmost point of Crimea, on the western shore of the Kerch Strait near the ruins of Yeni-Kale fortress. From the air, orient on the strait and the tower itself on the point, with the Chushka Spit reaching across from the mainland. Nearest airport is Kerch's airfield, with Anapa-Vityazevo (URKA) across the strait.
