# Cape Fonar

The easternmost point of Crimea takes its name from the fires that have burned here for millennia to guide ships, a wild rocky headland where seabirds wheel and a lighthouse still keeps the ancient promise of light.

**Location:** 45.3828, 36.6453

**Categories:** Geography, History, Maritime, Black Sea

## Introduction

The name is a promise: Fonar means lamp, or light. For as long as sailors have threaded the narrow gap between the Sea of Azov and the Black Sea, someone has kept a fire burning on this rocky headland, the easternmost point of the entire Crimean peninsula. First they were open cressets, iron baskets of flame; today the Yeni Kale Lighthouse crowns the cape and does the same work with electricity and satellites. Between the fires and the sea, the coast stays wild. Seabirds crowd the unpeopled cliffs in great numbers, dice snakes bask on the rocks, and only the occasional fisherman or curious tourist wanders out to where the land finally runs out.

## The Oldest Crossroads

People have lived at Cape Fonar since the Bronze Age, and archaeologists have found the traces of their settlements scattered near the point. In the age of the Bosporan Kingdom, a Greek town called Parthenion stood here, guarding the strait; it earned a mention in the Periplus of Scylax, a sailor's guide to these waters dated to around 350 BCE. That is the enduring truth of this cape. It is not a destination but a threshold, the last scrap of Crimea before the water, and everyone who has ever tried to pass between the two seas has had to reckon with it. The lights that gave the headland its name were never decoration. They marked the doorway.

## A Flag Raised to Call Down Fire

The cape's quiet was shattered in the Second World War, when it became a battleground between the retreating Red Army and the advancing German forces. One episode captures the desperate courage of those days. In May 1942, as German tanks closed in around the lighthouse, a Soviet mariner named Alexander Filimonov directed the aim of Soviet artillery firing from the Chushka Spit across the strait. When the tanks appeared beneath the tower, a flag was raised on the lighthouse itself, deliberately marking the position so that the guns could target it, even though hydrographers were still besieged inside. The tower was destroyed and those men died at their posts. A year later, in 1943, the cape served as a launching point for the Kerch-Eltigen amphibious operation, one of the war's costliest landings on this coast.

## Wrecks and the Restless Strait

The same waters the lights were meant to guard have always been treacherous. On 23 July 1995, a bulk carrier named Doja, sailing under a Syrian owner, capsized in a storm near Cape Fonar when her cargo shifted; by the locals' account every crew member escaped in the ship's own lifesaving gear. The vessel had lived many lives before that final one, built in Britain in 1962 and renamed again and again across the decades. The strait's strategic pull never faded either. In 2011, planners eyed Cape Fonar as a possible anchor for a great bridge across the Kerch Strait, before the route was ultimately drawn elsewhere. Through storms and wars and grand engineering schemes, the cape endures as it always has: a rocky finger of Crimea, loud with seabirds, marked by a light.

## Flight Context

Cape Fonar sits at 45.38°N, 36.65°E, the easternmost tip of the Crimean peninsula on the western shore of the Kerch Strait near its opening to the Sea of Azov. From the air, orient on the strait and the Yeni Kale Lighthouse crowning the point, with the Chushka Spit reaching across the water from the east. Nearest airport is Kerch's airfield, with Anapa-Vityazevo (URKA) across the strait on the mainland.
