# Syvash

A vast, foul-smelling, pink-tinged maze of salt lagoons, so shallow armies have twice waded across it, that nearly severs Crimea from the mainland.

**Location:** 46.0833, 34.3333

**Categories:** Lagoons, Wetlands, Geography, Sea of Azov

## Introduction

Locals gave it a blunt name: the Rotten Sea. In the heat of summer the Syvash, a sprawling tangle of lagoons on the western edge of the Sea of Azov, warms to bathwater and gives off a putrid, sulphurous reek that carries for miles on the wind. The water is so shallow you could walk for kilometers and never wet your chest, and so charged with salt that in places it glows a surreal, cosmetic pink. This is no ordinary bay. The Syvash very nearly cuts Crimea from the mainland altogether, leaving only the thin Isthmus of Perekop and the long Arabat Spit to bridge the gap, and for centuries that geography has made it both a natural wonder and a natural battlefield. Registered as a wetland of international importance under the Ramsar Convention, it is one of the largest and strangest coastal lagoon systems in Europe.

## The Shallowest Sea

Nothing about the Syvash behaves like open ocean. Its deepest point reaches barely three meters; most of it is far shallower, its bed carpeted in thick silt. Trapped between the Arabat Spit to the east and the mainland steppe, connected to the Azov only through the narrow Henichesk Strait beside the port of Henichesk, the water has almost nowhere to go. So it evaporates, and evaporates again, concentrating salt to extraordinary levels, an estimated 200 million metric tons of it dissolved across the lagoons. Industrial plants have long harvested this mineral wealth. The system is sometimes split in two, the Western and Eastern Syvash, joined by the Chongar Strait. When the water pulls back in summer it exposes barren, salt-blistered flats that locals call syvashes, the same word that named the whole strange sea. It is a place that punishes life and, in a few tough and specialized species, richly rewards it, its low swampy shores home to plants and creatures found almost nowhere else.

## The Color of Salt

For all its harshness, the Syvash can be beautiful in a way that stops travelers cold. Under the right conditions the lagoons flush rose and crimson, a color produced by Dunaliella salina, a microalga that thrives in punishing brine and turns red under sun and stress. One body of water here even carries the name Lake Lemuria for its otherworldly tint, and from the air the whole system can resemble a scattering of spilled paint. Where the salt eases, in the eastern reaches, reeds and wetland plants take hold. The larger islands of the Central Syvash wear a coat of true steppe: feather grass, wild tulips flowering briefly in spring, tauric wormwood, sage, crested wheatgrass, and fescue. Along the shores grow the specialists of salt, glasswort and sea lavender and saltbush, halophytes that have made peace with ground where almost nothing else will grow. It is a botany of extremity, each plant a small solution to the same harsh problem.

## Where Armies Waded

The Syvash's shallowness has repeatedly turned it into a door. During the Russian Civil War, in November 1920, the Red Army stunned its opponents by wading straight across the lagoons during the Perekop-Chongar Operation, outflanking defenses that assumed the water was a barrier. Two decades later, in the closing years of the Second World War, Soviet soldiers made a similar crossing, pushing through the mud and brine toward Crimea. These were brutal passages, men and equipment struggling through knee-deep silt and stinging salt under fire, and they left the Rotten Sea a fixture in the region's military memory. Since the 2022 invasion of Ukraine, the entire Syvash has been under Russian occupation, the latest chapter for a place that history keeps insisting on crossing. Time and again, the Rotten Sea has proved that its greatest defense, its shallowness, is also its greatest vulnerability: too deep to walk without effort, too shallow to stop an army determined to try. The lagoons that reek and shimmer in the summer heat have witnessed more marching men than most proper seas ever will.

## Flight Context

The Syvash spreads across roughly 46.08°N, 34.33°E. From 6,000 to 12,000 feet its full labyrinth resolves, and in summer the lagoons may show unmistakable pinks and reds. Look for the Arabat Spit walling it off from the darker Azov to the east, and the neck of the Isthmus of Perekop to the west. Nearest airports are Simferopol (URFF) and Berdiansk (UKDB).
