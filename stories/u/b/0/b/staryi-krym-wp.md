# Staryi Krym

The quiet Crimean town that once gave the entire peninsula its name, a former capital of the Golden Horde now known mostly for gardens and a beloved writer's grave.

**Location:** 45.0292, 35.0886

**Categories:** History, Crimea, Cityscapes, Literary heritage

## Introduction

An entire peninsula is named after this modest town. When people say Crimea, they are echoing the name of a small city in the eastern hills, once called Qrım, that lent its name first to the Crimean Khanate and then to the whole land the khanate ruled. Today the place is called Staryi Krym, which simply means Old Crimea, a name that quietly acknowledges how far its fortunes have fallen from the days when it was the capital of a Mongol realm. Walk its streets now and you find gardens, orchards, and the grave of a dreamer, but beneath them lies the layered history of a town that named a world.

## The Town of Many Names

Few places carry such a tangle of names. In the late thirteenth century the town appears as either Solkhat, thought to be the older name, or Qrım, and for a while the two coexisted before Qrım won out by the fifteenth century. Where the names came from is genuinely uncertain, and scholars have argued for centuries. Some trace Solkhat to the Greek Colchis. The name Qrım may descend from Cimmerium, after the ancient Cimmerians who gave the nearby Strait of Kerch its Roman name of Bosporus Cimmerius; others suggest the Greek Cremnoi, meaning the cliffs. When Catherine the Great's Russia annexed Crimea in 1783, the town was officially rechristened Levkopol, after the Greek Leukopolis, or White City, but the name never stuck. It reverted to Staryi Krym, and Staryi Krym it has remained.

## Capital of the Horde

Before it was old, Crimea was new and powerful. Under the Golden Horde the town flourished through the fourteenth century as an economic and cultural center, and it held its place as capital of the newly established Crimean Khanate into the sixteenth. Coins were still being struck here as late as 1517. But its prominence did not survive the turbulence of the age. The town was completely destroyed during the civil strife under Khan Mengli Giray in the later fifteenth century, and in 1532 the capital of the Ottoman vassal state was moved to Bakhchisaray. From that point Qrım slipped into relative obscurity, its grand days behind it. By the late nineteenth century its people lived, modestly and peacefully, by gardening, tobacco cultivation, and farming, the quiet occupations of a town that had outlived its own importance.

## A Dreamer's Resting Place

In its later, gentler incarnation, Staryi Krym became a place people came to for its air and its calm. The celebrated Russian writer Alexander Grin, author of luminous romantic fables, lived out his final years and died here, and the house where he spent them is now a museum dedicated to his memory. The town also became home to an important cardiac sanatorium, once directed by the renowned heart surgeon Nikolai Amosov, a place where the mountain air was thought to mend ailing hearts. Not all of its modern history is gentle: during the Second World War, German occupiers ran a Sicherheitsdienst prison in the town, and its Jewish community suffered the horrors of the Holocaust. Today Staryi Krym remains what it long ago became, a small, green, historic town, remembered less for the empire it once anchored than for the writer who chose to die among its gardens.

## Flight Context

Staryi Krym sits at 45.03°N, 35.09°E in the eastern Crimean Peninsula, roughly 25 km west of Feodosia. From about 2,500 to 3,000 feet the town nestles among orchards and low wooded hills; the medieval Ozbek Han Mosque and, to the south, the Surp Khach Monastery are useful landmarks. Nearest airports are Feodosia (UKFF) to the east and Simferopol (URFF) to the west.
