# Bohorodytska Fortress

A turtle-shaped fort of earth and timber where the Dnieper meets the Samara, built to project Moscow's power over the free-roaming Cossacks, now a field of buried treasure and revived Cossack festivals.

**Location:** 48.5007, 35.1634

**Categories:** fortress, Cossack history, archaeology, Dnipro

## Introduction

Seen from above, it looked like a turtle. The Bohorodytska Fortress, thrown up where the Samara River joins the Dnieper near modern Dnipro, was earth and timber shaped into something so distinctive that later historians would call it a marvel of Ukrainian fortification art, its bastions splayed like a turtle's limbs around a hunkered shell. But its purpose was anything but gentle. Raised in the late 1680s as Moscow pushed south toward the Black and Azov Seas, the fort was built to fight the Crimean Khanate, to hold newly seized land, and to keep watch on the fiercely independent Cossacks of the lower Dnieper. It was a stone-and-earth statement of who now claimed this frontier.

## A Fort on Old Ground

The builders did not start from nothing. The fortress rose on the site of Stara Samar, an old Cossack town, and experts believe the settlement here stretches back centuries before the fort's walls went up. Construction began in 1687 on orders from the Moscow government, during the Russian campaigns against the Crimean Khanate, and the design fell to a foreign engineer-colonel in Russian service, William von Zalen. The defenses were formidable for their kind: an earthen rampart some four meters high, a wide ditch, and a timber palisade bristling with seventeen cannons. Inside the walls stood a wooden chapel dedicated to the Intercession of the Most Holy Theotokos, which gave the fortress its name, Bohorodytska, from the Ukrainian word for the Mother of God. It was a garrison and a church, a spearhead and a shrine.

## Mazepa and the Watched Frontier

The fortress sat at the center of the great power struggle over the Ukrainian steppe. It served as a base for soldiers marching against the Crimean Khanate, a lever of Moscow's control over the borderland, and a standing warning to the Cossacks whose loyalty Moscow never trusted. The famous Cossack hetman Ivan Mazepa was drawn into its story: his forces joined the Russian campaigns launched from this region, and in the winter of 1690, facing the threat of an invasion by the Crimean khan Selim I Giray, Mazepa gathered his troops and dispatched a corps to defend the citadel. For a few decades the turtle-shaped fort was a genuine hinge of empire, a place where the ambitions of Moscow, the Crimean Tatars, and the Cossack Hetmanate all pressed against one another along the Samara.

## Buried Treasure in a Garden

The fortress did not last. In 1798 its Intercession Church was taken apart and carried across the Samara to the settlement of Odinkovka, and the town and stronghold faded from the map. In the quiet that followed, German colonists plowed the old ramparts flat and turned the ground into vegetable gardens for the villagers of what became Shevchenka. The soil, though, kept its secrets. From 2001 onward archaeologists cataloged more than two thousand finds here: seals and trade seals, small crosses, buttons, plates and household goods, jewelry, coins spanning the 14th to 18th centuries, and weapons, a buried record of the many lives that passed through this contested place. Today the site hosts archaeological digs and lively Cossack festivals, the old frontier fort reborn as a gathering place for the descendants of the very people it was built to control.

## Flight Context

The Bohorodytska Fortress site lies at about 48.501 N, 35.163 E, on the right bank of the Samara River near its confluence with the Dnieper, on the northeastern edge of Dnipro. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL the meeting of the two rivers is the key landmark; the fort itself is a low earthwork best seen against the surrounding fields. Nearest airport is Dnipro International (UKDD) to the southwest.
