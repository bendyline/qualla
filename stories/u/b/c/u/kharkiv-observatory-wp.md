# Kharkiv Observatory

One of Ukraine's oldest astronomical institutions has watched the sky over Kharkiv for more than two centuries, chasing the Moon's surface, distant asteroids, and the bending of light itself.

**Location:** 50.0026, 36.2309

**Categories:** astronomy, science, history, Ukraine

## Introduction

The city lights of Kharkiv eventually did what no cloud could: they blotted out the stars. For more than a century, astronomers had turned their telescopes upward from the university grounds, but by the middle of the twentieth century the growing city glowed too brightly for careful work. So the observatory did something quietly remarkable - it kept its historic home in the city and sent its serious observing eastward, out to darker skies in the Chuhuiv district. The story of the Kharkiv Observatory is the story of astronomers refusing to let their sky be taken from them, first by electric light and later by something far worse.

## Two Centuries of Watching

Astronomy at Kharkiv University began early. An astronomy office was established in 1808, only a few years after the university itself opened, making it one of the oldest centers of the science in what is now Ukraine. For decades the astronomers worked from cramped university rooms, but in 1883, largely through the determined efforts of Grigori Levitski, a proper observatory was built in a separate building of its own. Levitski became its first director and served until 1894. The line of directors that followed reads like a quiet chronicle of the science in this part of the world - Ludwig von Struve, member of an astronomical dynasty; Nikolai Yevdokimov; and then, for more than four decades, Nikolai Barabashov, a planetary astronomer who would help produce the first atlas of the Moon's far side and whose name a Martian crater now carries.

## Light of the Moon, Light of the Stars

The observatory carved out a distinctive scientific niche. Rather than simply cataloging positions, its astronomers became specialists in how light scatters off the surfaces of worlds - the subtle brightening and darkening of the Moon, of asteroids, of the small bodies drifting through the Solar System. This is painstaking, patient work, the kind that turns a smudge of reflected sunlight into a map of dust and rock across millions of miles. Alongside this, the observatory built expertise in star catalogs, in the slow gravitational dance of stellar dynamics, and in gravitational lenses - those places in the sky where the mass of distant galaxies bends light like a cosmic magnifying glass. From a single building in an eastern Ukrainian city, its researchers reached across the Solar System and beyond.

## A Darker Sky, and a Darker Chapter

By 1962, light pollution over Kharkiv had made high-quality observation nearly impossible from the city, so an observing station was founded in the Chuhuiv district to the southeast. In 1963, astronomers installed an AZT-8 telescope with a 70-centimeter mirror and an AFR-2 solar instrument, giving the station real reach into the sky. For six decades it did faithful work. Then, during the Russian invasion of Ukraine, the Chuhuiv station fell under occupation from March to September 2022. When the astronomers finally returned, they found their equipment badly damaged and looted - instruments smashed, records lost, years of careful observation interrupted by the crudest of forces. It was not the first war this observatory had weathered; it had also endured occupation during the Second World War.

## The Watch Continues

For all it has lost, the observatory endures as the Institute of Astronomy of Kharkiv's V. N. Karazin National University. It still oversees the Chuhuiv station and maintains a Museum of Astronomy that carries more than two hundred years of the discipline's local history - old instruments, records, the accumulated memory of generations who leaned over eyepieces in the Kharkiv dark. Astronomers here still study the scattering of light off distant worlds, work that connects a nineteenth-century building to the frontiers of planetary science. To keep watching the sky through electric glare, through occupation, through the destruction of your own instruments, and to rebuild each time - that persistence is its own kind of light.

## Flight Context

The observatory's historic main building stands at 50.00°N, 36.23°E within the V. N. Karazin University grounds in central Kharkiv, close to the university's landmark high-rise blocks; its dark-sky observing station lies southeast in the Chuhuiv district. From 2,500-4,000 feet the dense city center and the Lopan and Kharkiv rivers frame the university district. Nearest airport is Kharkiv International (UKHH).
