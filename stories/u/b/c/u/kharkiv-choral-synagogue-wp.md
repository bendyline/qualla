# Kharkiv Choral Synagogue

One of the largest synagogues in Ukraine, a soaring Moorish-Gothic landmark that was seized, gutted, and repurposed for decades before Jewish life returned to fill it again.

**Location:** 49.9924, 36.2349

**Categories:** Architecture, Religion, Ukraine, History

## Introduction

The dome rises 138 feet above Pushkinska Street, and by law it had to stand back from the road, set apart from the churches around it as the city's ordinances required of a house of worship that was not Christian. Completed in 1913, the Kharkiv Choral Synagogue is one of the largest synagogues in Ukraine, a striking fusion of Romano-Gothic, Gothic Revival, and Moorish Revival styles that announces, in stone and arch, the confidence of a community that had put down deep roots in this city. Its long life since has traced the fortunes of Kharkiv's Jews through some of the twentieth century's darkest passages, and its survival into the present is, in its way, a quiet triumph.

## Seized and Silenced

In June 1923, the Soviet state closed the synagogue and took it for its own. The manner of the taking was pointed: the Yevsektsiya, the Jewish section of the Communist Party, declared a holiday and organized a march, and a group of Jewish workers carried a red flag into the sanctuary. A Moscow newspaper branded the building a counterrevolutionary nest, alleging that Torah scrolls inside had been dedicated to the Tsar, the era's language of denunciation turned against a house of prayer. Stripped of its purpose, the great building was put to a succession of secular uses over the decades that followed, serving as a workers club, a cinema, and a sports complex. For most of the Soviet period, the synagogue stood as a monument to a community pushed to the margins of public life.

## A Contested Homecoming

When the Soviet Union fell, the synagogue's future became a matter of struggle. Rival Jewish groups laid claim to the building, and for a time competing organizations operated within it, an awkward coexistence born of decades in which no single community had been allowed to steward it openly. The years of contested control produced their share of strange chapters before order returned. A fire gutted the building in 1998, and in its aftermath the synagogue passed fully into the hands of Chabad, the Hasidic movement that undertook extensive renovations completed in 2003. After eight decades of closure, confiscation, and dispute, the synagogue was whole again and, at last, unambiguously a place of Jewish worship and community.

## A Living Center

Today the synagogue is once more the heart of Jewish life in Kharkiv, and an important city landmark whose holidays draw Jews and non-Jews alike; Hanukkah and Purim celebrations here have brought mayors, governors, and national media through its doors. Run by Chabad as its Kharkiv headquarters, the complex holds far more than a sanctuary: a mikveh, a yeshiva, a school of some 500 Jewish children in grades one through eleven, and a kindergarten of seventy more, all under the city's chief rabbi, Moshe Moskovitz. It has also become a place of remembrance, hosting tributes to Kharkiv's Jewish veterans of the Second World War. When Russian bombing partially damaged the building in March 2022, it wounded a structure that had already outlived confiscation, fire, and a century of Kharkiv's turbulent history, and had, each time, endured.

## Flight Context

The synagogue stands at roughly 49.99 degrees north, 36.23 degrees east, on Pushkinska Street in central Kharkiv, set back from the road behind its forecourt. From the air, look for its large dome among the historic downtown near Freedom Square. The nearest major airport is Kharkiv (UKHH) to the south.
