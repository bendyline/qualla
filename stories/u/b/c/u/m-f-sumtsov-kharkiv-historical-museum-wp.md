# M. F. Sumtsov Kharkiv Historical Museum

Weathered stone women from the medieval steppe stand guard outside a Kharkiv museum whose collection reaches from Cossack banners to a Repin portrait - and which has already survived losing much of itself to war once before.

**Location:** 49.9925, 36.2306

**Categories:** museums, history, culture, Ukraine

## Introduction

Outside the museum stand four stone figures, weathered almost featureless by eight centuries of wind and rain. They are Polovtsian statues - balbals, the nomads called them - carved in the twelfth century and set up across the open steppe to watch over the graves of the dead. One was found before the revolution, two in the 1930s, and the last was pulled from the bottom of the Britai River by the museum's own archaeologists in 1980. Together they mark the entrance to the M. F. Sumtsov Kharkiv Historical Museum, where the deep past of this borderland between forest and steppe is gathered under one roof.

## A Scholar's Museum

The museum was founded in 1920 by Mykola Sumtsov, a scholar of the region's folklore and history, who called it the Museum of the Sloboda Ukraine - a name honoring the frontier land that Cossacks and settlers had made their home. Sumtsov did not build his collection from nothing. He drew the best of it from two older institutions: the Kharkiv University Museum, whose objects had been accumulating since 1804 as teaching material and formed one of the earliest museum collections anywhere in Ukraine, and the city's Art and Industry Museum of 1886, whose ethnographic holdings had been among the finest in the entire Russian Empire. A great national Archaeological Congress held in Kharkiv in 1902 had already gathered a wealth of artifacts, and many of those, too, eventually found their way into Sumtsov's museum.

## What Was Lost, What Remains

The museum's history is not one of steady accumulation. The Second World War fell heavily on its collections, and a significant part of what generations had gathered was lost - artifacts destroyed or scattered in the chaos of occupation and fighting. It is a loss that shadows any visit: the knowledge that what survives is only a fraction of what once existed. Yet what remains is remarkable in its span. The exhibitions trace the Kharkiv region from its archaeology through the Middle Ages and the Cossack era, through the nineteenth century, the World Wars, and Soviet times. Among the treasures is a portrait painted in 1906 by Ilya Repin, one of the greatest realist painters of the age, depicting the historian Dmytro Bahalii - a work by a master, hanging in the city that shaped so much of the story it tells.

## Naming the Founder

For most of its life the museum bore institutional titles rather than a person's name. That changed on 18 June 2015, when it was formally named after its founder and first director, Mykola Sumtsov - a belated but fitting tribute to the scholar who had assembled it nearly a century earlier. The renaming did more than honor one man. It reasserted a particular idea of the museum's purpose: to hold and interpret the history and culture not of an empire or a single people, but of everyone who has lived in this crossroads region, the many ethnicities whose lives overlapped on the Sloboda Ukraine frontier. In a place fought over for centuries, that mission of remembrance carries weight.

## Keeper of the Crossroads

The Kharkiv region has always been a meeting place - of forest and steppe, of settled farmers and mounted nomads, of empires pressing in from different directions. The museum's collection embodies that meeting: the silent Polovtsian statues from the age of the nomads, the relics of the Cossack Hetmanate, the ethnographic objects of village life, the paintings and portraits of the modern city. To walk through it is to move across a thousand years of a single landscape, told through the things people made and left behind. As the leading historical and cultural center of the Kharkiv region, it remains what Sumtsov intended it to be: a place where a much-contested land keeps hold of its own memory.

## Flight Context

The museum stands in central Kharkiv near 49.99°N, 36.23°E, close to Constitution Square where its Cuman stone statues are displayed and a short distance from the great open expanse of Freedom Square, the clearest landmark from above. From 2,500-4,000 feet the downtown grid and the Lopan River orient you. Nearest airport is Kharkiv International (UKHH).
