# Kharkiv Art Museum

A brewer's mansion that became one of Ukraine's great art collections, guarding the largest hoard of Ilya Repin's work in the country, including a version of his beloved Cossacks.

**Location:** 49.9991, 36.2385

**Categories:** Museums, Art, Ukraine, Culture

## Introduction

In a hall of this Kharkiv museum hangs a room-filling canvas of laughing, jeering Cossacks crowded around a scribe, composing an insult-laden letter to the Ottoman Sultan. It is a version of Ilya Repin's Reply of the Zaporozhian Cossacks, one of the most beloved paintings in the entire Slavic world, and the Kharkiv Art Museum holds it along with the largest collection of Repin's work anywhere in Ukraine, eleven paintings and eight works on paper. That the museum owns such treasures is fitting, for its story begins with the artist himself: in 1907, at the personal request of the Kharkiv historian Dmytro Bahalii, Repin donated one of his portraits to the fledgling museum, an early gift that set the tone for everything that followed.

## A Museum That Kept Changing Its Name

Few institutions have worn as many names as this one. Founded in 1905 as the City Art and Industry Museum, it became the Church-Historical Museum in 1920, absorbing artworks from diocesan repositories and the collection of Kharkiv University. In 1922 it was reborn as the Museum of Ukrainian Art, organized into departments of painting, sculpture, and architecture, its holdings enriched with icons from the sixteenth to nineteenth centuries and portrait, landscape, and genre works. Closed to the public in the 1930s, it reopened in 1944, cycled through yet another name as the State Museum of Fine Arts, and only settled into its present identity in the 1960s. Behind the shifting titles lay a single constant: a steadily growing collection that now numbers some 25,000 works across twenty-five halls.

## The Brewer's Mansion

The museum's home is itself a work of art. The building at 11 Zhon Myronosyts Street was raised in 1912 by the celebrated Ukrainian architect and academician Oleksiy Beketov, the same designer responsible for the Alchevsky mansion a short walk down the street. He built it as a private residence for the industrialist Ignatishchev, owner of the Kharkiv Ivanovo brewery, in an elegant Neoclassical style laced with Baroque and Art Nouveau flourishes. After the revolution the mansion was nationalized, and for six years it served as the seat of Soviet Ukraine's Council of People's Commissars, the republic's government, before the art museum eventually took up residence in the graceful rooms a brewing magnate had built for his family.

## The Cossacks Who Came to Kharkiv

The museum's Repin Cossacks have a journey of their own. Repin painted several versions of the Reply of the Zaporozhian Cossacks, that raucous scene of warriors dictating a mocking letter to the Sultan, and the one in Kharkiv is the second version, worked on between 1889 and 1893. It did not begin here. The canvas was transferred to the Kharkiv museum from Moscow's famed State Tretyakov Gallery in 1932, one of the Soviet-era redistributions of art that scattered masterworks across the union's museums. For the artist, the subject was personal: Repin had deep roots in Ukraine, and the Zaporozhian Cossacks were figures of his own heritage. The painting's arrival gave Kharkiv a version of one of the most celebrated images in Slavic art, a work whose subjects, defiant and unbowed, resonate in the city that now keeps them.

## Masters on the Walls

The collection Beketov's mansion now shelters ranges across centuries and borders. Alongside its famed Repin holdings hang works by other great nineteenth-century painters, including the seascapes of Ivan Aivazovsky, the forests of Ivan Shishkin, and canvases by Karl Bryullov and Vasily Surikov. Ukrainian art is richly represented too, from the poet-painter Taras Shevchenko to Serhii Vasylkivsky and the luminous landscapes of the Kharkiv school. Western European painting and decorative arts from the sixteenth to twentieth centuries fill out the galleries. In 2022 the building itself joined the ranks of Kharkiv's wounded landmarks when shelling damaged its facade, windows, and stained glass. A far graver blow came on 14 June 2026, when a Russian Shahed drone strike set the museum ablaze, injuring five people including an infant, as staff, rescuers, and volunteers raced to pull paintings from the smoke and evacuate the museum's exhibits. It is a house that has survived revolution, occupation, and a century of upheaval, tested again while quietly guarding some of Ukraine's most treasured art.

## Flight Context

The museum stands at roughly 50.00 degrees north, 36.24 degrees east, on Zhon Myronosyts Street in central Kharkiv, near the Alchevsky mansion. From the air, the dense historic core and nearby Freedom Square aid orientation; the museum itself is a low landmark best seen at street level. The nearest major airport is Kharkiv (UKHH) to the south.
