# Alchevsky Mansion

A Renaissance villa built for a family of Kharkiv educators, where Ukraine's first monument to the poet Shevchenko rose in defiance of a tsarist ban.

**Location:** 49.9990, 36.2399

**Categories:** Architecture, History, Ukraine, Culture

## Introduction

In 1900, in the garden of a graceful villa in the center of Kharkiv, a family of educators did something the tsarist authorities had expressly forbidden: they raised the first monument in Ukraine to the poet Taras Shevchenko. A white marble bust of the national bard appeared among the terraces and roses of the Alchevsky estate, and around it gathered students, Sunday-school pupils, aspiring poets, and reformers, drawn to a place where the Ukrainian language and its greatest voice were openly honored. The mansion still stands at 13 Zhon Myronosyts Street, an Italianate palace in miniature, but the story of the family who built it, and of what became of their house, is one of Kharkiv's most poignant.

## A Marriage of Reformers

The Alchevsky family made their fortune in banking, but they spent their lives on education. Oleksiy Alchevsky, an industrialist and philanthropist, founded Kharkiv's commercial and land banks; in 1862 he married Khrystyna Zhuravlyova, a teacher who would become one of the most important figures in Ukrainian public education. She founded the Kharkiv Women's Sunday School and devoted herself to teaching working women and girls to read. When their daughter married the talented Kharkiv architect Oleksiy Beketov in 1889, the family gained its own designer, and in 1893 Beketov built for them a luxurious Renaissance-style mansion wrapped in a garden. The house belonged, fittingly, to Khrystyna. Around it the family assembled a small campus of purpose: a Sunday-school building in 1896, and Beketov's own mansion across the street in 1897.

## The Poet in the Garden

The Shevchenko bust the Alchevskys placed in their garden was no casual ornament. Under imperial policy, public commemoration of the Ukrainian poet was banned, and to erect his likeness was a quiet act of cultural defiance. The marble was carved by the sculptor Volodymyr Beklemishev, who, like Beketov, had trained at Maria Raevska-Ivanova's drawing school in Kharkiv before studying at the St. Petersburg Academy of Arts. The monument became a gathering point for a rising generation, among them the political figure Mykola Mikhnovsky, the actor Severyn Pankivskyi, and Levko Matsievich, remembered as the first aviator of Ukrainian origin. Then tragedy struck the family. In 1901 Oleksiy Alchevsky died at a railway station in St. Petersburg, and the house, the school, and Beketov's mansion were all sold to cover the family's debts.

## The House That Watched History Turn

The mansion's later chapters darkened. After the 1917 revolution the house was nationalized, and in 1921, on the order of Felix Dzerzhinsky, one of the architects of the Red Terror, the building that had belonged to educators and philanthropists became a club of the Cheka, the Soviet secret police whose Kharkiv branch was notorious for its brutality. The garden where the Shevchenko monument had stood did not survive; the bust itself was later restored and sent to the Shevchenko museum in Kyiv, though its original pedestal was lost. In time the estate softened again toward culture. A chapel to the Archangel Michael was built on the grounds in the 1990s, and today the mansion houses the Palace of Culture of the regional police, its hall once more filled with concerts and creative gatherings.

## Caryatids and Careful Beauty

For all it has endured, the mansion remains a jewel of Beketov's art. He modeled it on the suburban villas of the late Italian Renaissance, a small, light, welcoming palace once ringed on every side by open terraces. What survives of the original facade shows the restrained harmony of late classicism: bas-reliefs of plants and children, slender pilasters, delicate cornices. Between the second-floor windows stand caryatids, sculpted female figures crowned with Ionic capitals, bearing the eye upward as they seem to bear the roofline. Soviet-era additions in the 1940s and 1950s destroyed the courtyard facade, and the garden is now an ordinary square of modern plantings. Yet the front of the house still speaks in the language of its first owners: a family who believed, and built as though they believed, in beauty, progress, and the power of teaching people to read.

## Flight Context

The mansion stands at roughly 50.00 degrees north, 36.24 degrees east, in central Kharkiv on Zhon Myronosyts Street near the Lopan River. From the air the dense historic core of the city, its squares and church domes, provides orientation; the villa itself is a low landmark best appreciated from street level. The nearest major airport is Kharkiv (UKHH) to the south.
