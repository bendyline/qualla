# Freedom Square, Kharkiv

One of Europe's largest city-center squares, whose changing names, from Veterinary to Dzerzhinsky to Freedom, trace a century of who ruled Kharkiv.

**Location:** 50.0044, 36.2342

**Categories:** Landmarks, History, Ukraine, Architecture

## Introduction

You can read the twentieth century in the names of this one enormous plaza. It began, unglamorously, as Veterinary Square. In 1926 the new Soviet authorities renamed it for Felix Dzerzhinsky, founder of the Bolshevik secret police. Under German occupation it was rechristened twice in two years, first German Army Square, then, in 1943, named for an SS division. Only when Ukraine became independent did it receive the name it carries now: Freedom Square. At roughly twelve hectares, it is the sixth-largest city-center square in Europe, a vast open expanse in the heart of Kharkiv where, more than once, the question of who governed the city was decided in the open air.

## A Square Built for a Century of Concrete

Freedom Square is inseparable from the architecture that frames it, all of it a product of the brief, heady years when Kharkiv was the capital of Soviet Ukraine. Its defining landmark is Derzhprom, the Constructivist skyscraper that was the first of its kind in the Soviet Union, a cluster of concrete towers whose symmetry snaps into focus only from the square's exact center. Two other great buildings, the House of Projects and the House of Cooperation, began as Constructivist works before being rebuilt in the heavier Stalinist style after the Second World War. At one end sits the regional state administration building. Together they turn the square into an open-air museum of a single, revolutionary decade of design, the moment a provincial city briefly imagined itself the capital of the future.

## Where the Statues Fall

For a public space this large, Freedom Square has an unusual talent for becoming the stage of history. A monumental statue of Lenin, erected in 1964, stood at its western edge for half a century. In 2014, amid demonstrations that pitted pro-Ukrainian and pro-Russian crowds against one another, protesters tore the statue down on the night of September 28, and the vacant plinth became, for a time, a symbol in itself. The site was paved over in 2016, and in 2020 a fountain opened where Lenin had stood. The square has hosted gentler spectacles too: in September 2008 the band Queen, with Paul Rodgers, opened their Rock the Cosmos Tour here before a crowd reported at 350,000, a concert filmed and released as Live in Ukraine.

## A Stage for Everyday Life

Between its moments of upheaval, the square is simply the great gathering ground of Kharkiv, a space too large to fill except on the biggest occasions. Its northwestern corner holds a circular park, the Round Square, that softens the vast paved expanse with trees and benches; a reconstruction begun in 2019 added new paths, lighting, and a dry fountain to the space. Roughly twelve hectares in all, the plaza has hosted New Year celebrations, civic festivals, and, for several years, annual Victory Day military parades. Its sheer scale makes it a natural venue for spectacle, which is precisely why a rock band chose it to launch a world tour, and why crowds of hundreds of thousands can assemble here without crowding. On an ordinary afternoon it is quieter, a place to cross, to sit, to feel the openness at the center of a dense city.

## Under Fire

On March 1, 2022, the square that had witnessed so much of Kharkiv's history became a target. During the battle for the city, Russian missiles struck the square and its surroundings, and the explosion severely damaged the regional state administration building and others nearby. It was a violent chapter for a plaza whose very name, chosen at independence, spoke of a different aspiration. Yet the square endures as it always has, its Constructivist towers still ringing the open expanse, its fountain still playing where a statue once stood. Freedom Square has changed its name with every power that has claimed Kharkiv, and it wears its current name pointedly, a wide public ground that has repeatedly proven it belongs, in the end, to the people who gather on it.

## Flight Context

Freedom Square lies at roughly 50.00 degrees north, 36.23 degrees east, in central Kharkiv. From the air it is unmistakable: an immense open plaza, one of the largest in Europe, bordered on its northwestern edge by a distinctive circular park and anchored by the towers of the Derzhprom building. The nearest major airport is Kharkiv (UKHH) to the south.
