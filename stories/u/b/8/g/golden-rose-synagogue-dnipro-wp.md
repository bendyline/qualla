# Golden Rose Synagogue

A synagogue that lost its Star of David to a Soviet hammer and sickle, then reclaimed it decades later to become the heart of one of Europe's most remarkable Jewish revivals.

**Location:** 48.4639, 35.0531

**Categories:** synagogue, Jewish heritage, Dnipro, religious architecture

## Introduction

For most of the twentieth century, the emblem above this building's portico was wrong. Where a Star of David had crowned the entrance since 1868, Soviet authorities mounted the hammer and sickle of the USSR, and turned the sanctuary into a workers' club and a warehouse. The congregation that had prayed here in the city then called Yekaterinoslav was scattered, silenced, or worse. That the Golden Rose Synagogue stands today with its Star restored, its prayer hall alive, and a soaring Jewish community center rising beside it is not a story of preservation. It is a story of return.

## A Star Replaced, A Star Reclaimed

The synagogue rose in 1868, in the imperial provincial capital of Yekaterinoslav, part of a Jewish community that had put down roots along the Dnieper generations earlier. Then came the Soviet years. In 1924 the state seized the building, stripped its religious identity, and installed the emblem of the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics in place of the Magen David above the entrance. For decades the sanctuary held crates and machinery, not worshippers. It was not until 1996, five years after Ukraine's independence, that the building was returned to the Jewish community. The moment carried the weight of everything the intervening century had taken. Reconstruction began in 1999, following a design by a local Jewish architect known as A. Dolnik, and the work of restoration doubled as an act of remembrance.

## The Hands of Frank Meisler

Much of what a visitor sees inside came from the Israeli sculptor Frank Meisler, a Danzig-born artist whose own family perished in the Holocaust and who devoted his career to memorializing Jewish life and loss. Meisler designed the foyer, the prayer hall, and the Holy Ark. Above the sanctuary entrance he set a representation of golden roses, the flourish that gives the synagogue its name. His most quietly brilliant touch waits in a small rotunda between lobby and sanctuary, inscribed with the opening line of the Sh'ma Israel prayer. The Hebrew words begin at the left; the Russian words begin at the right. The two languages travel around the curved wall toward each other until their final words meet on the opposite side, a design that reads like a reconciliation between the community's ancient faith and the tongue history had forced upon it.

## The Menorah Rising Beside It

In October 2012, a new landmark opened next to the old synagogue: the Menorah Center, its seven glass-clad towers arranged like the arms of a menorah, one of the largest Jewish community centers anywhere in the world. Inside are a library, a soup kitchen, classrooms, offices, and a museum named Tkumah, a Hebrew word meaning rebirth. The name is not accidental. The museum tells the story of Ukrainian Jewry, including the Holocaust that emptied so many of the region's communities. Under Chief Rabbi Shmuel Kaminetsky and community director Vyacheslav Brez, the congregation became a leading member of the Federation of the Jewish Communities of Ukraine. The old synagogue and the towering new center now stand side by side, small stone and tall glass, the past and the promise that it would not be the end.

## Flight Context

The Golden Rose Synagogue sits at roughly 48.464 N, 35.053 E in central Dnipro. From about 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL, the seven gleaming glass towers of the adjacent Menorah Center are the clearest visual marker, with the historic synagogue tucked at their base and the broad sweep of the Dnieper River just to the west. The nearest major airport is Dnipro International (UKDD), a short distance southeast of the city center.
