# Dmytro Yavornytsky National Historical Museum of Dnipro

From a Bronze Age idol to relics of the Cossacks, one of Ukraine's oldest museums has spent nearly two centuries gathering the memory of the steppe.

**Location:** 48.4558, 35.0639

**Categories:** Museums, Archaeology, Ukraine, History

## Introduction

In the courtyard stand the stone people. They are kurgan stelae, weathered figures carved by nomads and set atop burial mounds across the steppe, hands folded over their bellies, faces smoothed by a thousand years of wind, gazing out over a city that did not yet exist when they were made. Among the treasures inside is the Kernosivsky idol, one of the most remarkable carved stones of the Bronze Age. This is the Dmytro Yavornytsky National Historical Museum of Dnipro, founded in 1848, one of the oldest museums in Ukraine, and its collection of some two hundred and eighty-three thousand objects runs from Paleolithic tools all the way to the wreckage of the Second World War.

## A Governor's Idea

The museum was born in February 1848 as the Katerinoslav Public Museum, founded on the initiative of Yakiv Grakhov, a local gymnasium principal, and Andriy Fabr, the governor of Katerinoslav, the city now called Dnipro. It began in the Potemkin Palace and displayed a scholar's magpie hoard: archaeological finds, stone statues, coins, rocks and fossils, and relics said to come from ancient Egypt and Greece. Its strangest treasure arrived during the Crimean War, when Fabr is said to have brought a mummified woman and child from Odessa, an object the museum keeps to this day. The first decade was its golden age, when the novelty of a museum stirred local antiquarians to generous donations. But without steady government support, the collection slid into decline through the late nineteenth century.

## The Collectors Who Saved It

What revived the museum was the passion of private collectors. In 1887 a Katerinoslav freeman named Alexander Pol opened the region's first private museum in his own four-room house on Soborna Square, filling it with nearly five thousand objects, many of them dug from the kurgans and burials of the province he loved. He valued the collection at two hundred thousand silver karbovanets and even employed a keeper-guide to show it. When Pol died in 1890, the community resolved to build a proper museum in his name. The Alexander Pol Province Museum opened in 1902, drew together the older collections, and rose into a purpose-built home in 1905, its design modeled on a Berlin townhouse. Within a decade its holdings grew from a few hundred objects to ten thousand.

## Rescued from the Rising Water

The museum's next great expansion came from a race against the river. When the Dnieper Hydroelectric Station was planned in the late 1920s, an enormous stretch of the Dnieper valley was destined to vanish beneath the reservoir, and with it centuries of buried history. The historian Dmytro Yavornytsky led the Dnieprobud archaeological expedition from 1927 to 1932 to excavate that doomed ground, and thousands upon thousands of artifacts were lifted from the earth before the water arrived. Those finds poured into the museum. When Yavornytsky died in 1940, the institution took his name; his tomb lies near its walls. The Second World War then nearly undid everything: an occupying commandant seized the building for his headquarters, and much of the evacuated collection was lost, though a pair of technical staff, Bilyi and Duz, managed to hide some pieces to safety.

## Nine Halls of Memory

Restored in the 1970s under the director Horpyna Vatchenko, who secured the Kernosivsky idol for the collection in 1973, the museum reopened with a sweeping new exhibition, and in 2010 it was granted national status. Today its nine halls carry the visitor through the whole arc of the region's past: the ancient steppe, the Zaporozhian Cossacks, the industrial awakening of the nineteenth century, the upheavals of civil war and the twentieth century, and the war years. One hall, titled "Never to be Repeated Again," confronts the Stalinist terror that gripped Dnipropetrovsk in the 1930s and after, honoring those it consumed. A donated "Shevchenkoviana" collection preserves rare editions of the poet Taras Shevchenko, including a Kobzar printed in his own lifetime. It is, in the end, a museum about a place remembering itself, from stone idols to living memory.
