# Diorama "Battle of the Dnieper"

Inside a painted canvas the size of a house, one of the deadliest river crossings in history unfolds around the viewer in Dnipro.

**Location:** 48.4561, 35.0642

**Categories:** Museums, Military History, World War II, Ukraine

## Introduction

Step into the darkened hall and the river surrounds you. The horizon curves nearly all the way around, up to two hundred and thirty degrees of it, so wide that peripheral vision surrenders and the eye can find no edge to the scene. Sound and light complete the illusion; at the base of the painting, real debris of war, broken defenses, weapons, and the improvised gear of a river assault, blur the line between canvas and floor. This is the Diorama "Battle of the Dnieper" in Dnipro, the largest diorama in Ukraine and among the largest anywhere, and it exists to place you, for a few minutes, inside one of the bloodiest battles of the Second World War.

## The Crossing It Remembers

The Battle of the Dnieper was fought in the autumn of 1943, a connected sequence of assaults along the river's banks that ran from late August into the fall. It was one of the largest operations of the entire war. Nearly four million soldiers were drawn into a struggle stretching some fourteen hundred kilometers along the front, and the Red Army forced its way across the wide river in crossing after crossing, seizing footholds on the western bank. The cost was staggering. Estimates of the dead and wounded on both sides run from roughly 1.7 to 2.7 million, with Soviet casualties alone reaching about 1.6 million, among them more than four hundred thousand killed or missing. Behind those numbers were young men wading and paddling into fire on a cold river, and the diorama was built so their ordeal would not be reduced to a statistic.

## Painted from Memory

The diorama opened on May 7, 1975, timed to the thirtieth anniversary of the end of the war in Europe. Two masters of battle painting created it, Nikolai But and Mykola Ovechkin, both from the Grekov military art studio that specialized in such work, and the architect Vladimir Zuyev designed the structure that houses it. The canvas itself is enormous, eight hundred and forty square meters of solid-woven cloth, curved to wrap the viewer in the scene. To get the details right, the artists did not simply imagine the battle. They studied the reports, orders, letters, and memoirs of the soldiers who had actually made the crossing, reconstructing both the sweep of the action and specific, human moments within it, so that what hangs on the wall is stitched together from the testimony of the people who lived it.

## A Museum That Kept Growing

The city around the diorama carries its own layered history in its very names: founded as Katerynoslav, renamed Dnipropetrovsk in 1926, and known since 2016 simply as Dnipro. The grounds outside the diorama have grown into an open-air collection of their own. Soviet armor stands sentinel there, a T-34-85, a smaller T-70, an S-125 missile system, machines of the era the painting depicts. In more recent years the site has taken on newer chapters, with outdoor exhibits gathering the remnants of events since 2014 and, in 2016, the opening here of the country's first museum devoted to them. Fifty-two kilometers away lies the Dnipro Bridgehead Memorial, where visitors can stand at the actual place the river was stormed, the ground itself the final panel of the story the diorama begins.

## Flight Context

Located at 48.4561 N, 35.0642 W, in the city of Dnipro on the Dnieper River in central-eastern Ukraine. From the air the broad Dnieper curving through the city is the primary landmark. Note that Ukrainian airspace is currently closed to civil aviation. The city's airport is Dnipropetrovsk International (UKDD).
