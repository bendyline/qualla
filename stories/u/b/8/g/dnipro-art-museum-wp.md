# Dnipro Art Museum

Opened on a poet's hundredth birthday, one of Ukraine's oldest art museums has survived two world wars to keep its walls of icons, portraits, and dreamlike symbolism.

**Location:** 48.4571, 35.0552

**Categories:** Art Museums, Culture, Ukraine, History

## Introduction

A young woman stands at her easel, brush in hand, caught in the concentration of work. The painting is called In the Studio, and it was made by Marie Bashkirtseff, the Ukrainian-born artist and diarist who died at twenty-five and became famous the world over for the journal she left behind. Her canvas hangs in the Dnipro Art Museum, one of the oldest art museums in Ukraine, alongside some eight and a half thousand works spanning the sixteenth century to the twenty-first. The museum opened its doors in April 1914, deliberately, on the hundredth birthday of the poet Taras Shevchenko, and it has been gathering art through war and upheaval ever since.

## Born for a Centenary

The idea took shape in 1906, when the Katerynoslav Scientific Society set out to give the city a museum of fine art. It came together on donations, gifts from artists and collectors, from the Saint Petersburg Academy of Arts, from the local Museum of Antiquities. The city council bought and restored buildings in the City Park, now Lazar Hloba Park, to house the first exhibition, finishing the work the very night before the opening. The timing was no accident: the Katerynoslav City Art Museum opened in April 1914 to mark the centennial of Taras Shevchenko's birth, and it was conceived not only as a gallery but as a place of public education for the whole city. Within months the First World War began, and the turbulent years of 1917 to 1921 would test the young institution hard.

## A Museum That Kept Moving

For a time the collection had no settled home. From 1922 it occupied the second floor of Khrinnikov's grand residence, the building that is now the Hotel Ukraine. By 1937 the holdings had outgrown that space, and the museum moved into a four-story building at 21 Shevchenko Street, where it has remained ever since. The Second World War brought occupation and damage, and only after the city was retaken could the grounds be repaired; the museum reopened in 1947 and began once more to host exhibitions and events. In the early 1960s the Dnipropetrovsk Art School moved into the building, and by the mid-1970s the school and the museum were working side by side, the making of art and the keeping of it under a single roof.

## Walls of Many Worlds

The collection reaches across centuries and borders. There are icons and folk paintings, portraits and landscapes and genre scenes, art from Western Europe and from across the region. Among its treasures are Mykola Pymonenko's charged canvas A Victim of Fanaticism, Valentin Serov's portrait of Margarita Morozova, a study for The Last Supper by Nikolai Ge, and Johann Rombauer's portrait of Praskovya Kutaisova. Some works arrived from nationalized private collections; others came by way of the National Museum of the History of Ukraine. But the collection's beating heart, by many accounts, is the work of Mikhail Sapozhnikov, the symbolist painter born in this very city, whose dreamlike visions give the museum a voice that is unmistakably its own. A plaque on the facade honors him still.

## Flight Context

Located at 48.4571 N, 35.0552 E, on Shevchenko Street in central Dnipro, close to Lazar Hloba Park and the Dnieper River in eastern Ukraine.
