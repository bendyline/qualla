# Merheleva Ridge

A group of schoolchildren digging on a marlstone ridge in eastern Ukraine uncovered a temple older than the pyramids of Egypt - a stepped sacred hill where people worshipped and were sacrificed five thousand years ago.

**Location:** 48.4036, 38.9389

**Categories:** archaeology, history, prehistory, Ukraine

## Introduction

The archaeologists who found one of Eastern Europe's most remarkable ancient sites were children. In 2004, on a marlstone ridge near Perevalsk in eastern Ukraine, schoolchildren at a summer archaeology camp began uncovering something no one expected: a complex of stone mounds and stepped terraces that turned out to be a temple and burial ground roughly five thousand years old - older than the Great Pyramid of Giza. Their teacher, Vladimir Paramonov of Alchevsk, had been bringing students to dig on these hills since 1995, patient year after patient year. What his young diggers had stumbled onto would, when announced in 2006, force scholars to reconsider what the herders and farmers of this steppe were capable of building.

## A Hill Shaped by Human Hands

Merheleva Ridge takes its name from merhel, the local word for marlstone, a soft limestone that runs through the region. But this was no ordinary hill. Its builders had sculpted the slopes into steps and crowned it with a complex of what archaeologists interpret as temples and sacrificial altars - four great stone mounds, or kurgans, rising above the surrounding steppe. The base of the complex spreads across roughly three-quarters of a square mile. Much of the construction is thought to date to around 3000 BC, in the early Bronze Age, though the site may have origins as far back as 4000 BC. This was monumental sacred architecture raised by people we usually imagine as simple pastoralists, and its scale and deliberate shaping suggest a society far more organized than that image allows.

## Layers of the Dead

The ridge is a place of graves, but the archaeologists came to believe most of them were not ordinary burials - the evidence pointed instead to human sacrifice. In the mound called Kurgan No. 4, excavators found a striking vertical history: three graves of the Late Bronze Age Srubna culture lay directly above three graves of the earlier Catacomb culture, thirteen burials in all, together with sacrificial pits. One skull was dated to around 3000 BC. Four stone statues stood near the graves. According to reporting in The Guardian, the site yielded the remains of sacrificial victims, ashes, and ceramics - but no jewelry or treasure. These were people caught up in the rites of their age, their lives given to beliefs we can now only partly reconstruct. The ridge holds them still, layered generation over generation across two millennia of use.

## Not a Pyramid, and Better For It

When news of the discovery broke, the world's press reached for a familiar and irresistible word: pyramid. Headlines announced an ancient pyramid found in Ukraine, and the comparison to Egypt took on a life of its own. The archaeologist in charge, Viktor Klochko, spent considerable effort setting the record straight. "I'm not sure where the pyramid idea came from - the media got it wrong," he said. "We didn't find anything like an Egyptian pyramid. Though the site is on a hill. But it's interesting enough in its own right." He was correct on both counts. Merheleva Ridge is not a lost Egyptian monument transplanted to the steppe. It is something rarer and more revealing - a homegrown sacred complex of the peoples who lived here millennia ago, remarkable precisely because it is their own.

## Why It Changes the Story

Klochko called the find internationally significant, the first monument of its kind discovered in Eastern Europe, and explained why it mattered: it changes our whole conception of the social structure and level of development of the cattle breeders and farmers who were the direct ancestors of most European peoples. The cultures tied to this ground - the Dnieper-Donets, the early Yamna, the Catacomb and Srubna traditions - belong to the deep prehistory of the Eurasian steppe, the world from which much of European ancestry and language would eventually spring. The site remained in use for perhaps two thousand years, through the Bronze Age and into the era of the Iron Age Sarmatians. That so much of this history was recovered by schoolchildren with trowels, guided by one persistent teacher, only adds to its wonder.

## Flight Context

Merheleva Ridge lies at 48.40°N, 38.94°E near Perevalsk in Luhansk Oblast, eastern Ukraine, about 30 km west-southwest of the city of Luhansk. From 2,500-4,500 feet the low marlstone ridges rise above the open, rolling steppe of the Donbas interior. Note that Ukrainian airspace is closed to civil aviation.
