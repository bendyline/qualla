# Second Battle of Kharkov

A Soviet spring offensive meant to keep the Germans off balance instead collapsed into one of the most lopsided encirclements of World War II.

**Location:** 49.2300, 37.1281

**Categories:** World War II, Military History, Ukraine, Eastern Front

## Introduction

In the spring of 1942, the mud came before the guns. Melting snow turned the black earth of eastern Ukraine into a bog that swallowed boots, wheels, and timetables alike, and it was into this quagmire that Marshal Semyon Timoshenko sent hundreds of thousands of men. Stalin was convinced the Germans were finished, that one more push would shatter them. His generals were not so sure. What happened over sixteen days in May, in the fields and ravines southeast of Kharkov, would prove them right in the cruelest way imaginable.

## The Confidence of Winter

The Red Army had reasons to feel bold. Only months earlier it had driven German forces back from the gates of Moscow, the first great reversal of the war in the east. Stalin read that success as prophecy: the enemy was spent, and a spring of local offensives would keep them reeling before they could strike at Moscow again. His most capable advisers disagreed. Zhukov, Vasilevsky, and the aging chief of staff Boris Shaposhnikov all urged caution and a defensive posture, arguing the army was not yet ready for major offensive operations. Vasilevsky later summed up the gap between hope and reality: "Yes, we were hoping for it, but the reality was more harsh than that." Stalin overruled them. He ordered seven local offensives from the Baltic to the Black Sea, and among the chosen targets was the industrial city of Kharkov, where a bridgehead near Izium promised a springboard toward Poltava and beyond.

## A Trap No One Saw

The offensive opened at dawn on 12 May with an hour of artillery and a final rush of aircraft, and for three days it seemed to work. Soviet pincers ground forward from the Volchansk and Barvenkovo salients, some units pushing to within nineteen kilometers of Kharkov. But the Germans had plans of their own. Unknown to Timoshenko, General Friedrich Paulus's Sixth Army had already been ordered to eliminate the Izium bulge in an operation code-named Fredericus, and the Wehrmacht had quietly reinforced the region for its own summer campaign. When Kleist's panzers counterattacked from the south on 17 May, the Soviet advance became a liability. The very salient the Red Army had pushed into now formed the walls of a cage. By 22 May three Soviet armies were cut off from the rest of the front, hemmed into a shrinking pocket of open ground.

## The Barvenkovo Mousetrap

What the Germans called the Kessel, the encircled soldiers would remember as the Barvenkovo mousetrap. With their rail-heads a hundred kilometers to the rear and almost no anti-aircraft guns, roughly a quarter-million men were exposed on flat, treeless terrain that offered German observers a clear field of fire. Over the sixteen-day battle the Luftwaffe flew more than fifteen thousand sorties and dropped some 7,700 tonnes of bombs. When the trapped soldiers charged the encircling lines to break out, they did so en masse, arms linked, and the losses were catastrophic. Less than one man in ten escaped. The final tallies remain grim in any accounting: historians cite roughly 75,000 Soviet dead and some 240,000 taken prisoner, against German losses estimated near 20,000. These were conscripts, most with little training, sent into a fight their own commanders had warned against.

## The Road to Stalingrad

The defeat did more than end the winter's momentum. It stripped away the reserves and the confidence that had carried the Red Army since Moscow, and it opened the door for Case Blue, the great German drive toward the Caucasus oil fields and a city on the Volga named Stalingrad. Paulus, the general whose Sixth Army closed the trap at Kharkov, would lead that same army into Stalingrad within months, where it would be encircled and destroyed in a mirror image of what happened here. The fields southeast of Kharkov look ordinary today, gentle folds of farmland along the Seversky Donets. But this quiet ground absorbed one of the war's most one-sided catastrophes, a place where overconfidence and mud and air power combined to erase an army in little more than two weeks.
