# Boris and Gleb Cathedral, Chernihiv

A pre-Mongol cathedral in the heart of Chernihiv, named for two murdered brothers who became the first saints of the Rus' and holding the tomb of the prince who built it.

**Location:** 51.4892, 31.3069

**Categories:** Religion, History, Architecture, Kievan Rus'

## Introduction

The cathedral carries the names of two brothers who chose not to fight back. When Prince Vladimir of Kievan Rus' died in 1015, a scramble for his throne turned deadly, and two of his sons, Boris and Gleb, were murdered rather than take up arms against their own family. Boris was stabbed as he slept in his tent; Gleb had his throat cut by a servant. Their refusal to answer violence with violence made them the first saints the Rus' ever canonized, remembered as Passion-bearers. A century later, on the heights of Chernihiv, a prince raised this cathedral in their honor.

## A Prince's Church and Tomb

The Boris and Gleb Cathedral was ordered built in 1120 by Davyd Sviatoslavich, a prince of Chernihiv, on the site of an older structure whose foundations still lie beneath it. He did not have long to enjoy it. Davyd was buried within its walls in 1123, just three years after construction began, making the church both his monument and his grave. It rose in the Dytynets, the fortified core of the city, right beside the older Transfiguration Cathedral, at the very center of one of the great capitals of the medieval Rus'. This is a building of the pre-Mongol era, from the age before the invasions of the thirteenth century swept across these lands, and it has stood as a landmark of Chernihiv for nine hundred years.

## The Doors of Silver

The cathedral's most dazzling treasure came centuries after its founding. In the early eighteenth century, by order of Hetman Ivan Mazepa, the great patron of Ukrainian art and architecture, a set of silver royal doors was made for the church, crafted by the master goldsmith Philipp Jacob Drentwett of Augsburg. Royal doors stand at the center of an Orthodox iconostasis, opening onto the sanctuary, and these gleaming silver gates turned a solemn pre-Mongol church into a showcase of Baroque splendor. It is a fitting layering: a building begun by a twelfth-century prince, adorned in the eighteenth by a hetman whose name is stamped across the golden age of Chernihiv's art, each leaving his mark on the same sacred space.

## Broken and Made Whole

The cathedral's long life has been anything but serene. Over the centuries the building changed hands and forms with the powers that ruled the region, and the Second World War left it severely damaged, its ancient walls battered and its interior gutted. Between 1952 and 1958, restorers led by M. V. Kholostenko carefully returned the cathedral to its original pre-Mongol appearance, undoing later alterations to recover the shape it had worn in the twelfth century. Little of the old painted decoration came through the centuries intact; the frescoes that once covered the walls have barely survived. What endures is the powerful, austere form of the architecture itself, the bones of a Rus' cathedral rebuilt to stand again.

## A Church That Became a Museum

Today the Boris and Gleb Cathedral no longer holds regular services. Instead it serves as part of the Ancient Chernihiv reserve, housing a museum within its historic walls, so that the building itself has become an exhibit of the city's deep past. Standing in the Dytynets alongside the Transfiguration Cathedral, it anchors the ensemble of monuments that together make up one of the oldest urban landscapes in Ukraine. To step inside is to enter a space shaped by a murdered prince turned saint, built by a prince who lies buried in its floor, and adorned by a hetman's silver, all gathered under one pre-Mongol roof that has weathered nine centuries of the city's history.

## Flight Context

The Boris and Gleb Cathedral stands at 51.49 N, 31.31 E in the Dytynets at the historic heart of Chernihiv, beside the Transfiguration Cathedral above the Desna River. View from 4,000 to 6,000 feet in clear weather; the paired domes of the two cathedrals in the central park are the clearest landmark. Nearest airport is Chernihiv Shestovytsia (UKRR); Kyiv Boryspil (UKBB) lies to the southwest.
