# Catherine's Church, Chernihiv

A five-domed Baroque church at the gateway to Chernihiv, raised as a memorial to the Cossack regiment that stormed the Ottoman fortress of Azov.

**Location:** 51.4871, 31.3054

**Categories:** Religion, History, Cossack heritage, Ukrainian Baroque

## Introduction

This church is a war memorial in the shape of a prayer. Its five gilded domes rise at the very entrance to Chernihiv, greeting travelers who approach the city, and they were raised to honor a specific act of Cossack courage. In 1696, the regiment of Chernihiv joined the storming of Azov, the great Ottoman fortress at the mouth of the Don, under their colonel Yakiv Lyzohub. To commemorate the bravery of those Cossacks, the city built St. Catherine's Church, dedicated in 1715, a monument not to a battle in the abstract but to the men of Chernihiv who fought it.

## A Monument to the Regiment

The storming of Azov in 1696 was a hard-won prize, and the Chernihiv regiment earned its glory there. When the fighting was done, the city chose to remember its soldiers not with a statue but with a cathedral, dedicated to St. Catherine, a saint long revered in Ukraine. In this it followed a deep Cossack instinct: to bind faith and valor together, to make a house of worship into a record of sacrifice. St. Catherine's has changed remarkably little in the centuries since. A narthex added to its western front in 1837 and a bell tower raised in 1908 were both later removed to return the church to its original form, so that what stands today is close to what the Cossacks' descendants first raised in the early eighteenth century.

## A Wooden Church Built in Brick

St. Catherine's is considered the finest surviving monument of the Ukrainian Cossack renaissance of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, and its beauty lies in a clever translation. The church is built of brick and shaped like a cross, its composition rising in a pyramid: a tall central dome flanked by four lower ones over the faceted arms of the plan. Yet the whole design deliberately echoes a traditional wooden Ukrainian church, with five octagonal chambers each crowned by a dome, as if a carpenter's vision had been rebuilt in masonry. There is almost no molded ornament. Instead the facades speak through their proportions and their crisp details, triangular pediments and shallow cornices framing portals and windows. The walls are whitewashed, the domes and crosses gilded, and the effect is at once solemn, elegant, and tender.

## Silenced and Restored

The church's story runs through the upheavals of the twentieth century. It survived the Second World War, though the fighting of 1941 to 1943 burned its roofs and the tops of its domes, and it was painstakingly restored across two long campaigns, from 1947 to 1955 and again from 1975 to 1980. Its Cossack community had a harder road. Suppressed by the Soviet authorities in 1933, the tradition lay dormant for decades until a group of Chernihiv Cossacks revived their activity in the spring of 1990. In 2008, worship resumed within the church's walls after a prohibition of some seventy-five years, and St. Catherine's took on its role as the Cossacks' cathedral once more, its congregation gathered again beneath the domes their forebears had raised.

## The Gateway Church Endures

Standing at the approach to Chernihiv and included within the historic reserve of Ancient Chernihiv, St. Catherine's remains one of the defining landmarks of the city, its five golden domes a first and lasting impression for anyone arriving. The building has kept its role as a shaper of the cityscape for three centuries. It has not been spared the present. During the siege of Chernihiv in 2022, the church was damaged in a rocket attack, a fresh wound on a building already marked by earlier wars. Yet like so much in this ancient city, it stands still, its gilded crosses catching the light above the road, a Cossack memorial that has outlasted every force that tried to silence or destroy it.

## Flight Context

St. Catherine's Church stands at 51.49 N, 31.31 E at the eastern approach to Chernihiv, near the Dytynets and the Desna River. View from 4,000 to 6,000 feet in clear weather; its five gilded domes rising at the city's edge are a distinctive landmark, set apart from the cluster of the Dytynets cathedrals. Nearest airport is Chernihiv Shestovytsia (UKRR); Kyiv Boryspil (UKBB) lies to the southwest.
