# Chernihiv Collegium

The Baroque school in the heart of Chernihiv once called the 'Chernigov Athens,' where Cossack-era students learned Latin, rhetoric, and philosophy beneath a soaring bell tower.

**Location:** 51.4892, 31.3061

**Categories:** History, Education, Architecture, Cossack heritage

## Introduction

They called it the Chernigov Athens. At the height of its fame, this school on the ramparts of Chernihiv drew a reputation for learning that reached far across the Cossack lands and into Russia beyond. Founded in 1700, the Chernihiv Collegium was one of the first institutions on the left bank of the Dnieper to offer a full secondary and higher education, and it became a genuine center of thought, its students trained in Latin and rhetoric, poetry and philosophy. Its ornate Baroque tower still rises beside the old cathedrals, a monument not to a saint or a battle, but to the life of the mind.

## A School Born of a Hetman's Gift

The Collegium owed its existence to power and patronage. It was founded in 1700 by Ioan Maksimovich, the archbishop of Chernihiv who would later become metropolitan of distant Tobolsk, established at the expense of the hetman's office of Ivan Mazepa and in accordance with a decree of Tsar Peter the Great on the founding of collegiums for education. It grew from older roots, the Slavic-Latin and Slavic-mathematical schools of Lazar Baranovych, transferred here from Novhorod-Siverskyi in 1689. The new school took over the refectory of the Boris and Gleb Monastery in the Dytynets, the fortified heart of the city, and built upward and outward from it. Mazepa's own coat of arms still adorns the building, a reminder that the golden age of Chernihiv's learning was bound up with the great patron of Ukrainian art.

## The Chernigov Athens

What made the Collegium remarkable was the breadth of what it taught and whom it taught. Considered one of the first offspring of the celebrated Kyiv-Mohyla Academy, it opened its doors to all classes: not only the children of clergy, but nobles, townspeople, and Cossacks studied side by side. The full course ran six years. Students began with grammar and syntax in Latin and Polish, then advanced to Latin courses in rhetoric, poetics, and philosophy, learning to compose verse and craft and deliver speeches. Following the tradition of Kyiv-Mohyla, each teacher of the higher subjects had to write his own course of lectures and dictate them to his students. Over the eighteenth century the curriculum widened further, adding German, Greek, and French, arithmetic and geometry, and a full class of philosophy opened in 1749. For a provincial city, it was an astonishing engine of knowledge.

## Books From the Ramparts

The Collegium was a place that produced, not just consumed, learning. In 1705 it published a collection of syllabic verse titled Mirror from Divine Scripture. In 1716 its teachers translated the great Roman history of Livy into Church Slavonic, carrying the classical world into the language of the Orthodox church. The following year, a teacher of the Collegium, the archimandrite German Kononovych, printed the New Testament in Chernihiv for the first time, adding commentary to help readers through its more difficult passages. These were the works of scholars who read the ancients and the scriptures and labored to pass them on. In 1784 the institution was reorganized into the Chernihiv Theological Seminary, which carried on the tradition of research, founding a museum of church antiquities and publishing scholarly journals well into the modern era.

## Stone, Tower, and Survival

The Collegium is also a masterpiece of Cossack Baroque, a monument of national importance in its own right. The building stretches some forty-eight meters in an elongated rectangle, its western end crowned by a bell tower rising to about forty meters, its lower stage built for bells and its upper stage housing the Church of St. John the Theologian, with a Church of All Saints in the eastern wing. Built of grooved brick on a foundation of great boulders, its facade was once richly worked with pilasters, columns, niches, ceramic tile inserts, and decorative kokoshnik window frames. Much of that ornament was stripped away during nineteenth-century rebuilding, when the walls were smoothed and plastered over, but restoration campaigns in the twentieth century recovered the building's character. Today the Chernihiv Collegium stands within the reserve of Ancient Chernihiv, its tower still marking the skyline where the Chernigov Athens once taught its students to read the world.

## Flight Context

The Chernihiv Collegium stands at 51.49 N, 31.31 E in the Dytynets at the center of Chernihiv, beside the Boris and Gleb and Transfiguration cathedrals above the Desna. View from 4,000 to 6,000 feet in clear weather; the Collegium's tall 40-meter bell tower alongside the cathedral domes is the key landmark. Nearest airport is Chernihiv Shestovytsia (UKRR); Kyiv Boryspil (UKBB) lies to the southwest.
