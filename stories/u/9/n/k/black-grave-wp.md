# Black Grave

A towering tenth-century burial mound in Chernihiv where two Norse warriors were cremated with their armor, their idol of Thor, and their ornamented drinking horns.

**Location:** 51.4875, 31.2940

**Categories:** Archaeology, History, Kievan Rus', Viking age

## Introduction

In the late tenth century, a great fire burned on the heights above Chernihiv. Two Norse warriors, likely a father and his son, were laid on a pyre with their weapons and their gods, and when the flames died the people raised an enormous mound of earth over their ashes and held a funeral feast atop it. That mound still stands, more than ten meters high and 170 meters around, one of the largest burial barrows anywhere in the lands of the early Rus'. It is called the Black Grave, and it opens a rare window onto the moment when Viking chieftains ruled from this Slavic river town.

## The Warriors on the Pyre

When the archaeologist Dmitry Samokvasov opened the mound in 1872 and 1873, he found the remains of two cremated men, probably father and son, and dated the burial to the late tenth century, the age when Vladimir I ruled Kievan Rus'. Their status was unmistakable. They were most likely two princes, knyazes, of Chernihiv, though the old chronicles name no local ruler here before Mstislav, a son of Vladimir. Around the warriors lay the grim furnishings of a high pagan burial: sacrificial animals and, in the custom of the age, enslaved people killed to accompany their masters into death. It is a hard detail, and worth pausing on. These were human beings, their lives ended to serve a funeral rite, and the mound that honors two princes is also a grave for the nameless people buried at their sides.

## A Hoard Fit for a Chieftain

The objects laid in the Black Grave are among the treasures of the Viking-age east. Beside the bodies rested two helmets and two knee-length coats of chain mail, apparently pulled from the pyre itself, along with a great cauldron full of ram bones from the feast. There were two ritual knives, two golden Byzantine coins, and an imported sabre, tokens of trade routes stretching from Constantinople to the steppe. Most striking of all were two silver-bound aurochs horns, drinking horns from the wild ox, engraved with flowers, fabulous beasts, and the figures of a man and a woman shooting at a bird. And there was a small dark-red bronze idol of Thor, the thunder god, carried by warriors who still kept the old Norse faith even as Christianity rose around them.

## Between Two Worlds

The Black Grave captures a civilization at a hinge point. Its dead were Norsemen, followers of Thor, buried by cremation in the ancient northern way, yet they ruled a Slavic town and traded for Byzantine gold. Scholars compare the mound to the great barrows of Gnyozdovo near Smolensk, another site where Scandinavian and Slavic worlds fused into the culture that would become the Rus'. Within a generation or two, this land would be Christian, its princes building stone cathedrals instead of raising earthen mounds. The Black Grave belongs to the last age before that turn, when a chieftain could still be sent to the afterlife with his sword, his mail, his drinking horns, and a bronze god held in his hand.

## The Mound That Remains

The treasures that made the Black Grave famous no longer rest where they were buried. Excavated in the nineteenth century, the helmets, the mail, the golden coins, the drinking horns, and the little idol of Thor are now displayed far away, in the State Historical Museum in Moscow. What stays in Chernihiv is the mound itself, still swelling from the earth on the historic heights, crowned once by a stone stele. Today it stands protected within the reserve of Ancient Chernihiv, among the churches and cathedrals of later, Christian centuries. To walk up to the grassy barrow is to stand at the base of a monument older than any church in the city, a great heap of earth that has kept its shape and its silence for a thousand years.

## Flight Context

The Black Grave rises at 51.49 N, 31.29 E on the historic heights of Chernihiv, within the Ancient Chernihiv reserve near the Desna River. View from 4,000 to 6,000 feet in clear weather; the large grassy burial mound sits near the golden domes of the Dytynets cathedrals, which serve as the easiest orientation points. Nearest airport is Chernihiv Shestovytsia (UKRR); Kyiv Boryspil (UKBB) lies to the southwest.
