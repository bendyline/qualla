# Boldyni Hory

A green ridge above the Desna in Chernihiv where pagan burial mounds, a hermit's caves, and the graves of Ukraine's writers layer a thousand years of the dead into one hillside.

**Location:** 51.4789, 31.2844

**Categories:** History, Archaeology, Parks, Monuments

## Introduction

For more than a thousand years, people have been burying their dead on these hills. Walk the wooded slopes of Boldyni Hory, on the right bank of the Desna at the edge of Chernihiv, and you are walking across one of the largest early necropolises in Ukraine: some 230 earthen burial mounds, raised between the ninth and eleventh centuries, when this was a pagan land of warriors and traders. Then came the monks, who dug their caves into the same ground. Then came the writers, who chose to be buried here too. Few hillsides hold their history so densely, or so quietly.

## The Mounds of the Old Gods

Before the churches, there were the kurgans. Spread across roughly a hectare of the ridge, the burial complex of Boldyni Hory is among the largest necropolises of its era known in Ukraine, six clusters of mounds that together mark out an ancient inhabited land. The greatest of them, the Hulbyshche kurgan in the northeast, still rises six meters and once stood nearly nine, some twenty-two meters across, a grave built on the scale of a hero. Beside it sits the Bezimennyi, the Nameless mound, which the archaeologist Boris Rybakov dated to the first half of the tenth century. Excavators probed these hills in 1872 and again in 1908 and 1965, reading the story of a people who marked death with earth and height, and whose barrows still swell beneath the trees like the slow breathing of the ground.

## The Hermit and His Caves

In the middle of the eleventh century, a monk remembered as Anthony the Reverend came to Boldyna Hill and began to dig. What he started grew into the Saint Anthony Caves, a network of underground passages carved into the soft hillside and later linked to the Church of St. Elijah, raised in the twelfth century. Where a pagan people had once piled earth over their dead, Christian monks now burrowed into it to pray, turning the hill of the old gods into a place of the new faith. The caves gathered their own history over the centuries: in 1654, a colleague of Hetman Bohdan Khmelnytsky, the Chernihiv colonel Stefan Podobail, was laid to rest within them, a Cossack soldier buried in a hermit's tunnels.

## A Cemetery of Storytellers

By the seventeenth century the hills carried a crowning ornament, the Trinity Monastery, built in the late 1600s and completed with a printing house whose books helped spread learning across the region. In the tombs of its cathedral lie Chernihiv bishops and historians. But the most poignant company rests in the cemetery beside the monastery, where some of Ukraine's most beloved writers are buried, their graves carefully preserved. Here lies the fabulist and lyre player Leonid Glibov, and the great writer Mykhailo Kotsiubynsky alongside his wife, mother, and sister. Here too rests Opanas Markevych, the folklorist and ethnographer who was husband to the writer Marko Vovchok. To visit is to stand among the people who gave Ukraine its stories, gathered on a hill that had been telling its own for a millennium.

## Where Memory Meets the Present

The hills wear their modern layer too. In 1986, on the eve of Victory Day, a Glory Memorial was unveiled in the lower reaches of Boldyni Hory: four bronze figures on a granite pedestal, among them an ancient Rus' warrior bearing a shield with Chernihiv's old coat of arms, standing beside more recent defenders. Since 1972 the whole ridge has been protected as a monument of landscape art, and it remains a favorite green refuge for the city, its paths beautiful in autumn gold and winter white. The recent war has reached even here; a park gazebo was destroyed by Russian bombing. Yet the hill endures as it always has, absorbing each new chapter of grief and remembrance into the long, layered story of its slopes.

## Flight Context

Boldyni Hory rises at 51.48 N, 31.28 E, a wooded ridge on the right bank of the Desna about two kilometers from the historic center of Chernihiv. View from 4,000 to 7,000 feet in clear weather; the golden domes of the Trinity Monastery on the heights and the winding Desna are the surest landmarks. Nearest airport is Chernihiv Shestovytsia (UKRR); Kyiv Boryspil (UKBB) lies to the southwest.
