# Malyn

A paper-making town in the Polissya woods that wears a sheet of paper on its coat of arms, listens for secret nuclear tests, and may carry the name of a rebellious Drevlian prince.

**Location:** 50.7689, 29.2700

**Categories:** Cities, History, Industry, Ukraine

## Introduction

Most cities put a lion or a fortress on their coat of arms. Malyn chose a sheet of paper. It is a fitting emblem for a town whose identity has been bound up with papermaking for generations, even naming its football club Papirnyk - the papermaker. But this quiet city in the wooded Polissya region of northern Ukraine holds a stranger distinction too: hidden here is a seismic station, part of a global network that listens through the earth itself for the tremor of clandestine nuclear tests. Paper and seismographs, deep forest and deep history - Malyn is a place of unexpected layers, sitting on the banks of the Irsha River about a hundred kilometers northwest of Kyiv.

## The Rebel Prince's Name

Malyn's name reaches back into the founding legends of the Rus. It is traditionally linked to Prince Mal of the Drevlians, the East Slavic tribe of these forests, whose people rose up in the year 945 against Igor, the Grand Prince of Kyiv, when he came to collect tribute a second time. That uprising ended with Igor's death and set in motion one of the most dramatic revenge tales in early Slavic history. Whether Mal truly gave his name to this town or whether the connection is later folklore, the association places Malyn among the ancient Rus settlements - a town whose roots reach into the same fierce, chronicled world that shaped its neighbors Korosten and Ovruch across the Polissya woodland.

## Nobles, Empires, and a River

The centuries passed Malyn through many hands. It appears in the record around the turn of the 15th and 16th centuries as the possession of a nobleman named Hryńko Wnuczkiewicz, during the reign of Alexander Jagiellon. From there it moved among noble families - the Jelec, then, after the Second Partition of Poland brought the town under Russian rule in 1793, the Morzkowski and finally the great Radziwiłł family. Through every change of owner, the geography stayed constant: the town sat on the Irsha, a left tributary of the Teteriv, in a landscape whose very name, Polesia, means woodland. That forest and water shaped what Malyn would become. In an age of industry, the abundant timber and rivers of the region made paper a natural calling.

## Paper Town

The paper factory became Malyn's defining institution, so central to civic identity that a single sheet of paper found its way onto the city's coat of arms. The connection runs deep enough that the local football team took the name FC Papirnyk Malyn - literally, the papermaker. It is the kind of honest, industrial pride you find in mill towns everywhere, where the thing a place makes becomes the thing a place is. Malyn's location reinforced its importance: through the city runs the vital Kyiv-Korosten railway and the highway linking Kyiv toward Kovel and Warsaw, threading the town into the arteries of trade and travel that cross northern Ukraine toward the Polish frontier.

## Listening to the Earth

Perhaps the most surprising thing about Malyn hums away quietly beneath the surface. The town hosts a seismic monitoring station, designated PS-45, part of an international network built to verify the Comprehensive Test Ban Treaty. These stations exist to catch what nations might try to hide - the distinctive underground shudder of a secret nuclear detonation, detectable through the planet's crust from thousands of kilometers away. It is a strange and sobering role for a paper town in the woods: to serve as one of the world's ears against nuclear testing. Malyn has known hardship in recent years, its homes and a church struck during the fighting of 2022. Yet it endures - a small city of some 27,000 people, still making paper, still listening, still rooted in its ancient ground.

## Flight Context

Malyn sits at about 50.769 N, 29.270 E in Zhytomyr Oblast, roughly 100 km northwest of Kyiv, in the forested Polissya region on the Irsha River. From the air, look for the river and the Kyiv-Korosten rail line running through the wooded town; the railway station is a notable landmark. A viewing altitude of 3,000-4,000 feet AGL suits the low, tree-covered terrain. The nearest major airports are in Kyiv - Zhuliany (UKKK) and Boryspil (UKBB) - to the east and southeast.
