# Slavutych

The last city the Soviet Union ever built, raised in a hurry to rehouse the families of Chernobyl and shaped like a mosaic of eight republics.

**Location:** 51.5206, 30.7569

**Categories:** Cities, Chernobyl, Planned communities, History

## Introduction

When the reactor at Chernobyl exploded in 1986, it did not only poison a landscape; it erased a town. Pripyat, the model city that had housed the plant's workers, was evacuated in hours and never reoccupied. But the plant still needed running, which meant its people needed somewhere to live. So the Soviet Union did something it had rarely done: it built an entire city from nothing, fast, on clean ground safely east of the ruin. They called it Slavutych, and it would turn out to be the last city the USSR ever founded.

## A City Built by a Union

Slavutych rose on the left bank of the Dnieper, about 45 kilometers from Pripyat and 50 from Chernobyl itself, on land the fallout had spared. It was meant to be a showcase, a self-declared "21st century city" with modern architecture, wide green surroundings, and a standard of living far above the Soviet norm. What makes it unlike any other city in Ukraine is how it was built. Workers and architects from eight Soviet republics each raised their own quarter, and the districts were named for their capitals: Yerevan, Baku, Tallinn, Tbilisi, Riga, Vilnius, Moscow, and Kyiv. Each neighborhood carries its own style and atmosphere, an Estonian street giving way to a Georgian one, so that walking across Slavutych is a little like walking across a vanished country. Some of those names have since changed under Ukraine's decommunization and derussification laws.

## The Youngest City in Ukraine

For a place born out of catastrophe, Slavutych is strikingly full of life. It has the lowest average age of any city in Ukraine, with more than a third of its residents children under eighteen, an unusually high birth rate, and surprisingly low mortality. The reasons trace back to its founding: it was populated all at once, in the late 1980s, largely by young plant workers and their families starting over together. The 2001 census counted around 24,500 people, mostly ethnic Ukrainians with significant Russian and Belarusian minorities, speaking a mix of Ukrainian and Russian. The city that Chernobyl made necessary became, improbably, one of the most youthful and hopeful communities in the country, its playgrounds fuller than its retirement homes.

## A Company Town Without Its Company

Slavutych was built around a single employer, and that has been its enduring vulnerability. Until 2001, roughly 9,000 people commuted by rail into the exclusion zone to work at the plant, and around 85 percent of the city budget came from the company that ran it. Then Chernobyl's last reactor was shut down. Employment at the plant fell toward 3,000, most of them now monitoring and maintaining a facility that no longer generates power. Ukraine declared the city a Special Economic Zone and poured resources into retraining programs, trying to give a workforce built for one purpose a second one. The struggle continues. People have left, and the question that shadows every company town without its company hangs over Slavutych: what is a city for, once the reason it was built is gone?

## The Town That Refused to Be Taken

In late February 2022, during the Russian invasion of Ukraine, the siege of nearby Chernihiv cut Slavutych off from the rest of the country, and food and medicine ran short. On 26 March, after days of shelling, Russian troops entered the city, seized a hospital, and detained the mayor, Yuriy Fomichev. What happened next became one of the small, defining images of that spring. Residents poured into the streets in open protest, unarmed, standing their ground even as soldiers answered with flashbang grenades and warning shots. The occupiers ultimately agreed to withdraw and release the mayor. Slavutych, a city that already carried the memory of one disaster, had faced down another, and its people had answered with their bodies in the square.

## Flight Context

Slavutych sits at 51.52 N, 30.76 E on the east bank of the Dnieper, an isolated urban grid amid forest and floodplain. View from 6,000 to 9,000 feet; the compact, planned city with its distinct neighborhood clusters and the rail line running west toward the Chernobyl zone are the key landmarks. Nearest airports are Chernihiv Shestovytsia (UKRR) to the east and the Kyiv-area fields, including Boryspil (UKBB), about 200 km south.
