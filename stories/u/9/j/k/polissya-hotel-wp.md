# Polissya Hotel

One of the tallest buildings in the ghost city of Pripyat, whose rooftop became a command post directing the helicopters that battled the burning reactor.

**Location:** 51.4072, 30.0582

**Categories:** History, Chernobyl, Architecture, Ukraine

## Introduction

In the last days of April 1986, men stood on the roof of the Polissya Hotel and watched a reactor burn. From that high vantage over Pripyat, spotters directed the Soviet military helicopters that thundered overhead, guiding them as they dumped sand, lead, and boric acid onto the open, blazing core of Chernobyl's reactor number four. The hotel had been built as a place of welcome - a modern high-rise to greet visiting delegations to the atomic city. For a few desperate weeks it became something else entirely: a lookout over one of the worst disasters of the 20th century. Then it, too, was abandoned. Today the Polissya Hotel stands empty, one of the most recognizable ruins in a city the world left behind.

## A Welcome to the Atomic City

The Polissya Hotel rose in the mid-1970s, built to house the delegations and guests who came to visit the Chernobyl Nuclear Power Plant. Pripyat was a showcase - a young, purpose-built Soviet town of engineers and their families, laid out to embody the promise of nuclear power. As one of its tallest buildings, the hotel was a natural landmark, looming over the central square where residents shopped, gathered, and walked beneath the trees. For just over a decade it did the ordinary work of a hotel, receiving officials and travelers to a city that considered itself a model of the modern age. No one staying there in early 1986 could have known how little time the city had left, or that the building's height would soon give it a role no hotel was ever meant to play.

## The Roof Over the Catastrophe

On 26 April 1986, the reactor exploded, and the hotel's last ordinary guests fled as the mass evacuation of Pripyat began. But the Polissya did not empty completely. For weeks after the townspeople were gone, it kept its lights on for a different purpose, housing nuclear engineers who had come from across the Soviet Union to observe the burning reactor and reckon with what it meant. And from its roof, spotters carried out one of the most dangerous jobs of the entire response: directing the helicopters that flew again and again over the exposed core, releasing thousands of tonnes of material to smother the fire and the radiation pouring from it. To stand on that roof was to stand within sight of the most hazardous place on Earth, doing essential, perilous work in the open air.

## Reclaimed by the Forest

When the initial emergency response wound down, the Polissya Hotel was left to the elements, and it has now stood abandoned for close to four decades. Photographs taken thirty years after the evacuation show the transformation: vegetation surging up around the building, trees and undergrowth reclaiming the pavement, though the hotel and its walkways remain unmistakable beneath the green. Inside, the story is one of slow ruin - the interior in poor condition, stripped of nearly all its fittings and furniture, floors and stairwells decaying with time and weather. As of 2021, a section of the roof from which those spotters once worked has grown noticeably unstable, raising fears that part of the building could one day collapse. Nature and time are finishing what the disaster began.

## A Ruin the World Recognizes

Few abandoned buildings are as widely seen as the Polissya Hotel, even by people who will never set foot in the exclusion zone. It served as a primary setting for the acclaimed 2019 miniseries Chernobyl, which brought the disaster to a vast new audience. It has appeared across popular video games as well - a crucial location in S.T.A.L.K.E.R.: Shadow of Chernobyl, a sniper's perch in Call of Duty 4: Modern Warfare, and an explorable, photogrammetry-scanned ruin in Chernobylite - and it features in the music video for the Suede song Life Is Golden. Through all these images, the hotel has become a kind of shorthand for Pripyat itself: a tall, silent shell above an empty city, holding within its concrete the memory of the days when its roof looked out over a reactor on fire.

## Flight Context

The Polissya Hotel stands at about 51.407 N, 30.058 E in the abandoned city of Pripyat, within the Chernobyl Exclusion Zone in northern Ukraine near the Belarusian border. From the air it is one of the tallest structures in the overgrown cityscape, near the central square, with the Chernobyl plant a few kilometers to the southeast. This is restricted airspace over a nuclear exclusion zone; no real overflight should be planned. The nearest airports are in Kyiv - Boryspil (UKBB) and Zhuliany (UKKK) - well to the south.
