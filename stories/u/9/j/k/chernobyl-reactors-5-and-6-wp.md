# Chernobyl Reactors 5 and 6

Two nuclear reactors that were never finished, frozen in half-built silence beside the world's most infamous power plant, their cranes still standing where workers dropped their tools in 1986.

**Location:** 51.3892, 30.0994

**Categories:** History, Chernobyl, Nuclear, Technology

## Introduction

On the morning of 26 April 1986, while reactor number four burned barely a kilometer away and radioactive smoke drifted over the plant, 286 construction workers kept building. They were pouring concrete and raising steel for two new reactors, units 5 and 6, and they did not know the world had just changed - the glow of the fire was not visible from where they worked. They labored on through that first terrible day, unaware. Those reactors were never completed. Today they stand exactly as they were left, a vast unfinished skeleton beside the Chernobyl Nuclear Power Plant: giant portal cranes rusting in place, foundations flooded, an enormous half-built structure abandoned mid-motion. They are among the most haunting things in a landscape already full of ghosts.

## The Plant That Was Meant to Grow

Chernobyl was never supposed to stop at four reactors. The original Soviet plan imagined a colossal complex of twelve units, and reactors 5 and 6 were to be its third phase. Like the four already running, they were RBMK-1000 designs, each meant to generate around 1,000 megawatts of electricity. They were laid out side by side, mirroring reactors 3 and 4, and were to share a water chemistry plant, a gas circuit plant, and an engine hall. Because the existing cooling pond was too small and could not be enlarged, engineers planned two great cooling towers to the southeast. Construction of unit 5 began on 1 July 1981; unit 6 followed exactly two years later. Unit 5 was scheduled to come online in November 1986. It was a plan of enormous ambition, racing toward a future that would never arrive.

## Warnings in the Concrete

Even before the catastrophe, the project bore signs of trouble. In May 1984, the fire department of Kyiv Oblast criticized the fire safety of the two units, warning that it did not meet regulatory standards - and though corrections were ordered, the designer, Gidroproject, left the deficiencies unaddressed. Then, in February 1985, a serious accident struck Block 5 itself: a roof slab two meters thick and weighing 49 tonnes tore loose from its anchorage and crashed down, the result of a miscalculation. These were the ordinary hazards of building something huge and complex at speed. In hindsight, they read as small tremors before a far larger reckoning. The workers who returned each day to raise these reactors were, like their colleagues at unit 4, trusting a system whose margins for error were thinner than anyone was told.

## The Decision to Stop

After the disaster, the future of units 5 and 6 hung in doubt. Work halted, then resumed that October, then stopped again in April 1987. The turning point came in an extraordinary public hearing that same month, when more than 60 experts and scientists from the Ukrainian Academy of Science signed a petition opposing the completion of the two reactors. They feared that continuing to build on this poisoned ground would spread contamination even further. It was a remarkable act of public dissent for the Soviet era. On 23 May 1989, the decision became final: construction was discontinued, and 500 million rubles were written off. Reactor 5 was by then about 70 percent complete; reactor 6 only 20 percent. The dismantling was rushed, because radiation levels were rising - and in the haste, no record was even kept of whether unit 5's holding pools ever received their uranium fuel.

## Frozen in Place

For decades the unfinished reactors have simply waited, mothballed since 1989, their cranes standing sentinel over a project time forgot. That uncertainty about the fuel finally prompted action: in 2021, a research team flew a small drone - a Flyability Elios 2, equipped with thermal and bright LED cameras - into spaces too high and hazardous to reach on foot, to check the holding pools for leftover uranium. The drone found none, and the recording was shared with international authorities, closing a question left open since 1986. The plant itself was fully decommissioned in 2000, ending any dream of reviving units 5 and 6. On the 30th anniversary of the disaster, the Australian artist Guido van Helten transformed one of the coolant towers into a canvas, painting onto it a 1986 photograph by the photojournalist Igor Kostin - turning a monument of abandoned ambition into a place of remembrance.

## Flight Context

The unfinished reactors 5 and 6 stand at about 51.389 N, 30.099 E within the Chernobyl Nuclear Power Plant complex, just southeast of the main reactor buildings in northern Ukraine near the Belarusian border. From the air the site is marked by two enormous cooling towers - one bearing van Helten's mural - and a large half-built reactor hall with stationary portal cranes. This is restricted airspace over an active nuclear exclusion and decommissioning zone; no real overflight should be planned. The nearest airports are in Kyiv - Boryspil (UKBB) and Zhuliany (UKKK) - well to the south.
