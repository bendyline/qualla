# Kochubey House

The oldest building in Baturyn holds a courtroom, a torture cellar, and a love story that ended in betrayal and beheading.

**Location:** 51.3364, 32.8780

**Categories:** History, Ukraine, Museums, Cossack heritage

## Introduction

Descend into the basement of the Kochubey House and the air turns cool and close, the way old stone always feels. Down here, wax figures freeze a scene that once played out for real: a court clerk at his table, a prisoner in irons, the instruments of Cossack punishment arranged nearby. This cellar has not been rebuilt in centuries. Above it stands a low, whitewashed brick house from the second half of the seventeenth century, the oldest surviving building in Baturyn, and one that carried in its walls both the machinery of justice and one of Ukrainian history's most famous doomed romances.

## The Judge's House

The house was built in the style of Ukrainian, or Cossack, Baroque, a single story of brick over a deep basement. From 1687 to 1708 it served as the working residence of Vasyl Kochubey, the general judge of the Cossack Hetmanate, one of the most powerful men in the country after the hetman himself. This was where the legal business of a self-governing Cossack state was conducted, complete with its clerks, its records, and its cellar cells. The building you see is not quite the original: it was badly damaged in the Second World War, when only the walls survived, and its present form is the result of restoration. But those walls are genuinely old, and they have witnessed more than most buildings twice their size.

## A Love That Became a Scandal

One museum hall is given over to a story that Ukrainians have retold in poems and paintings for three hundred years: the attachment between Vasyl Kochubey's daughter, Motrona, and Hetman Ivan Mazepa, her much older godfather. The relationship, impossible by the customs of the church, has come down through history in the surviving letters Mazepa wrote to her, which the museum places at the center of the exhibit. Those letters, tender and anguished by turns, are the intimate heart of a tale that would end in blood. Kochubey, embittered and politically opposed to Mazepa, denounced the hetman to Tsar Peter I. The tsar chose to believe Mazepa instead. In 1708, Vasyl Kochubey was beheaded for his accusation, only months before Mazepa's own break with Moscow would set fire to Baturyn itself, a bitter irony that the town's fall would follow so soon after the execution of the man who had tried to warn the tsar against its hetman. The exhibit lets visitors sit with that irony, and with the human cost woven through it.

## From Beekeepers to Reserve

The Kochubey family held the house until 1917. Its uses since then read like a compressed history of the region. In 1925 a society of beekeepers opened a museum inside it, named for the pioneering apiarist Petro Prokopovych. The war reduced it to bare walls; restoration began in the early 1970s, and by 1975 it housed a museum of local history. In 1993 that collection became the seed of the Hetman's Capital reserve. A thorough restoration in 2003 to 2005 was followed by a new exhibition in 2007. Today its four halls trace the Kochubey family, the Cossack chronicler Samiilo Velychko, and the Mazepa romance, while the unrenovated cellar keeps its unsettling silence below.

## Flight Context

The house stands at 51.34°N, 32.88°E within the Hetman's Capital reserve at Baturyn, on the bluffs above the Seym River in Chernihiv Oblast. From about 3,000 feet look for the pale rectangular buildings of the reserve near the river's wooded bend; the palace and reconstructed citadel are the largest nearby landmarks. Nearest major airports are Boryspil (UKBB) and Kyiv-Zhuliany (UKKK) in the Kyiv region, roughly 200 km southwest.
