# Baturyn

The lost capital of the Cossack Hetmanate on the Seym River, razed to a ghost town in 1708 and reborn around a palace designed by a Scottish architect for Catherine the Great's court.

**Location:** 51.3333, 32.8833

**Categories:** Historic cities, Cossacks, Chernihiv Oblast, Ukrainian history

## Introduction

In 1708 Baturyn was a capital of twenty thousand people, crowned with forty churches and two monasteries, the seat of a Cossack state. By 1726 it was a ghost town. Between those two dates lies one of the great catastrophes of Ukrainian history and one of its strangest recoveries - for the same town that Russian troops burned to the ground would rise again a generation later around a neoclassical palace built by a Scotsman, and would one day become, quite improbably, a twin of a small parish in the English county of Kent.

## The Making of a Capital

Baturyn began modestly. A settlement stood on the Seym as far back as the 9th and 10th centuries, and a hilltop stronghold followed, until the Mongol invasion of 1239 destroyed it. The town proper was founded in 1625, under royal permission of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, on land granted to a nobleman named Piaseczyński. Cossack forces wrested it away during the Khmelnytsky Uprising, and its fortunes rose with the Cossacks' own. From 1669 the town became the capital of the Cossack Hetmanate, the autonomous Cossack republic of Left-Bank Ukraine. Here sat the General Military Chancellery and the General Military Court, the twin institutions that governed the Hetmanate. Under Hetman Ivan Mazepa, who ruled from 1687, Baturyn reached its height - a flourishing capital of some twenty thousand souls, ringed with churches, with Mazepa's own fortified palace at nearby Honcharivka.

## The Razing

In 1708, during the Great Northern War, Hetman Mazepa concluded that the tsar meant to strip away Ukraine's autonomy, and he switched his allegiance to Sweden. The response was annihilating. On 13 November 1708 a Russian army under Alexander Menshikov sacked and razed Baturyn and killed its inhabitants in a punitive strike; the commanding officer of the garrison was executed on the breaking wheel. The historian Serhiy Pavlenko estimates that Menshikov's men killed six to seven and a half thousand civilians and five to six and a half thousand soldiers - the population of a whole city, cut down in a day. The capital that had held twenty thousand people became, within two decades, an empty ruin, and the seat of the Hetmanate was moved away to Hlukhiv.

## A Palace, a Poet, and a Slow Return

Baturyn's second life came with Hetman Kyrylo Razumovsky, who moved the capital back here in the 1750s and set about rebuilding, reviving crafts and even planning a university. After he was stripped of office, he returned in his old age determined to raise a palace worthy of the place, and for it he engaged Charles Cameron, the Scottish architect whose designs Catherine the Great prized - the only work Cameron ever built in Ukraine. Constructed between 1799 and 1803 in the neoclassical style, the palace was left unfinished at Razumovsky's death and gutted by fire, and it stood damaged through two centuries of war and revolution. In 1843 the poet Taras Shevchenko came and painted the town's ruined splendours. Restoration finally succeeded in our own time: in 2009, after years of work under President Viktor Yushchenko, the Hetmans' Capital complex and the renovated Razumovsky Palace were opened to the public, and Baturyn - once a ghost town - became again a place people come to remember.

## Flight Context

Baturyn lies at 51.3333 N, 32.8833 E on the banks of the Seym River in Chernihiv Oblast, northern Ukraine. From 2,500 to 4,000 feet, look for the pale neoclassical Razumovsky Palace in its park and the reconstructed fortress citadel on the bluff above the river. Nearest major airport is Kyiv Boryspil (UKBB) well to the west; Chernihiv Shestovytsia (UKRR) lies to the northwest.
