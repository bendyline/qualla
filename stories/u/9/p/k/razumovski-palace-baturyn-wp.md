# Razumovski Palace

The last hetman of Ukraine commissioned a Scottish architect to build a palace fit for a monarchy that no longer existed, and against every odds it still stands.

**Location:** 51.3345, 32.8937

**Categories:** Architecture, History, Ukraine, Cossack heritage

## Introduction

By the time Kyrylo Rozumovsky settled in Baturyn in 1794 and dreamed up a great palace, the office that had made him powerful was already gone. He had been the last hetman of Ukraine, deposed by Catherine the Great three decades earlier when the empire simply abolished the position. And yet here, on the ruined seat of Cossack power, he set about building a mansion grand enough for a court, hiring one of the most celebrated architects working in the Russian Empire to design it. The result is the only building Charles Cameron ever created in Ukraine, and the sole hetman's palace anywhere in the country to survive to the present day.

## A Palace for a Closed Chapter

Rozumovsky ruled as hetman from 1750 to 1764, the years when Baturyn once again became the Cossack capital. Deposed but enormously wealthy, he returned late in life to the town of his old power and commissioned a palace and park ensemble to match his station. Between 1799 and 1803 the Scottish-born architect Charles Cameron, famous for the neoclassical interiors he built for the imperial family near St. Petersburg, raised a stately central palace flanked by two outbuildings and set within a formal park. Cameron's cool neoclassicism, all balance and restraint, was an odd and beautiful thing to plant on this frontier of burned Cossack glory. Rozumovsky did not enjoy it long; he died the same year it was completed, and it passed to his son Andriy, who held it until 1836.

## Two Centuries of Slow Ruin

What Cameron built, time nearly undid. A fire in 1824 gutted almost all of the palace's interiors. The ensemble became state property in 1856 and drifted into neglect; by the early twentieth century it stood abandoned. The park was destroyed and the outbuildings dismantled. Another severe fire broke out in 1924, and the facade and its ornament suffered further damage during the Second World War. Through the second half of the twentieth century, several restoration attempts kept the shell from collapsing but none were ever finished. Somehow, through fire and war and decades of decay, the central palace refused to fall. Of the whole grand ensemble Rozumovsky had imagined, this one weathered building remained standing when Ukraine finally turned its attention back to Baturyn.

## Brought Back to Life

Between 2003 and 2008, under the patronage of President Viktor Yushchenko and with money from Ukrainian philanthropists, the palace was at last fully restored. It reopened to visitors on 22 August 2009. Inside its 2,483 square meters, the ground floor now tells the story of Hetman Baturyn through Rozumovsky's statecraft and the palace's own long ordeal, while the second-floor ceremonial halls, reconstructed in the spirit of Cameron's surviving work elsewhere, evoke the age of the hetmanate. Descendants of the family have returned pieces of its history: a basket-hilted eighteenth-century sword that once belonged to Rozumovsky, kept for generations in Vienna, was presented on opening day by his direct descendant Gregor Rozumovsky. Fragments of the hetman's tombstone, carved by the sculptor Ivan Martos in 1805, are on display as well.

## Flight Context

The palace stands at 51.33°N, 32.89°E within the Hetman's Capital reserve at Baturyn, Chernihiv Oblast, on the high ground above the Seym River. From roughly 3,000 to 4,000 feet its long pale neoclassical block is the most conspicuous structure in the reserve, set among lawns near the reconstructed fortress citadel. Nearest major airports are Boryspil (UKBB) and Kyiv-Zhuliany (UKKK), about 200 km to the southwest.
