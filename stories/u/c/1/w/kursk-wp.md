# Kursk

An ancient fortress city that never fell to its besiegers sits atop the world's largest iron-ore reserve, its very ground pulling compass needles askew.

**Location:** 51.7372, 36.1872

**Categories:** Cities, History, Culture, Russia

## Introduction

Somewhere beneath the streets of Kursk, a compass needle would rather not point north. The city sits atop the Kursk Magnetic Anomaly, the largest known iron-ore reserve on Earth, a buried mass so rich in metal that it tugs at the planet's magnetic field and drew scientists here to puzzle over it long before anyone could mine it. It is a fitting secret for a place this old. Kursk is the senior city of its region, first written into the record in 1032 as a town of Kievan Rus, a fortress on a hill above the rivers that has watched nearly a thousand years pass, and whose deepest wealth lay unseen underfoot the whole time.

## The Fortress That Held

Kursk was born to be defended. It rose as a fortress city on a hill dominating the plain, ringed on three sides by the Kur and Tuskar rivers and closed off by forest thickets to the north. Destroyed by the Mongols under Batu Khan around 1237, it was rebuilt, passed to Lithuanian rule, then joined the centralized Russian state in 1508 as its southern border province. That frontier position made it a target. Through the seventeenth century the Crimean Khanate and the Nogai Horde swept north on slave raids, crossing the nearby Seym on the old Muravsky Trail, and Polish-Lithuanian forces struck at the city again and again, in 1612, 1616, 1617, and 1634. Through it all, one fact held firm: the Kursk fortress was never taken. Its garrison, sixteen hundred strong in 1616, kept the walls.

## A City on the Black Earth

Around the impregnable center, Kursk grew into a merchant town living off the fabulous fertility of the surrounding Black Earth country. It stood on the shortest roads from Moscow to the Crimea and to Kyiv, and it thrived on the grain trade, hosting a great annual fair beneath the walls of its monastery. When Catherine the Great's era brought a formal city plan in 1782, Kursk was laid out on a crisp rectangular grid of streets converging on Red Square, a design so durable it governed the city for roughly a century and a half. Its economy still reflects that inheritance, iron from the anomaly, chemicals, and the food industry drawn from one of the richest agricultural regions on the continent.

## The Boy Who Fell From the Bell Tower

Kursk's most beloved son is Seraphim of Sarov, one of the most venerated saints of the Russian Orthodox Church, born in the city in the eighteenth century. His father took part in building the great city cathedral, and the story goes that the young Seraphim, at the age of seven, fell from the top floor of its three-story bell tower and survived unharmed, an early sign, the faithful believed, of a life under heaven's protection. That cathedral, raised between 1752 and 1778, is so sumptuous a piece of Elizabethan Baroque that art historians once tried to credit it to the great Rastrelli himself. Kursk gave rise to other remarkable figures too, from the painter Kazimir Malevich, who spent formative years here, to the composer Georgy Sviridov.

## Painted and Named Among the Stars

The soul of old Kursk was caught most vividly by an outsider's brush. In the early 1880s the painter Ilya Repin created his celebrated canvas Religious Procession in Kursk Province, a teeming portrait of one of the city's great icon processions that hangs among the treasures of Russian art. The city's name has traveled far beyond the province ever since. A minor planet discovered in 1979, 3073 Kursk, carries its name through the solar system, and a Russian submarine bore it across the seas. And just outside the city lies the Central Black Earth reserve, a stretch of steppe soil that has never once felt a plow, a patch of the ancient grassland preserved as it was before the fortress on the hill was ever built.

## Flight Context

Kursk lies at about 51.74 degrees N, 36.19 degrees E, at the confluence of the Kur, Tuskar, and Seym rivers in the Black Earth region of southwestern Russia. From altitude, look for the rectangular street grid on its two hills above the river valleys and the domes of the cathedrals near Red Square. The Kursk Vostochny Airport (ICAO: UUOK) serves the city.
