# HMS Defence (1763)

A 74-gun veteran of Trafalgar and the Nile, lost on the Jutland sand on Christmas Eve 1811 because her captain would not leave his admiral.

**Location:** 56.3583, 8.1000

**Categories:** maritime-history, shipwrecks, royal-navy, napoleonic-wars, denmark

## Introduction

By the winter of 1811 there was almost no famous action of the age that HMS Defence had missed. She had been dismasted at the Glorious First of June, fought at the Nile, taken a Spanish two-decker at Trafalgar. She was forty-eight years old, a long working life for a ship of the line, and she was doing the dull job the Navy spent most of its time on: shepherding merchantmen home through the Baltic before the ice closed. On Christmas Eve she was driven onto the sand off the west coast of Jutland with 597 men and boys aboard. Fourteen came off her alive. The question that has followed her since is not how she was lost, but why she was still there to be lost.

## Built for the Long War

Israel Pownoll launched her at Plymouth Dockyard on 31 March 1763, a Bellona-class third rate: 168 feet on the gundeck, 46 feet 9 inches in the beam, 1,603 tons burthen, seventy-four guns. The 74 was the workhorse of the sailing navy, heavy enough to stand in the line and fast enough to be useful, and the Bellonas were among the best of them. Her first war was the American Revolutionary War: the Channel Fleet, then the Battle of Cape St Vincent in 1780, then east in early 1782 with Commodore Sir Richard Bickerton's squadron of five ships — too late for that year's fighting in India, but in time for Cuddalore in 1783, the last battle of the war. She came home at the end of 1785 and was laid up through the peace.

## Twenty Years in the Line

The French Revolutionary Wars brought her out again under Captain James Gambier. At the Glorious First of June in 1794 she went in hard against Mucius and Tourville and was one of only two British ships completely dismasted in the battle. Repaired, she joined Admiral William Hotham in the Mediterranean in time for the Battle of Hyères that July, and in 1798, under Captain John Peyton, she was back there for the Nile. Then came the boat actions, the unglamorous and murderous work of cutting out enemy shipping. On 1 July 1800, in Bourneuf Bay, boats from Defence, Fisgard, Renown and the hired armed cutter Lord Nelson pulled in against a French convoy at the Île de Noirmoutier, destroying a 20-gun vessel, a lugger, two schooners and a cutter and burning some fifteen merchantmen loaded with corn for the fleet at Brest. Ninety-two of the 192 men who went in did not come back; they were taken prisoner when their boats grounded.

## Trafalgar and After

In 1801 she went to the Baltic under Captain Lord Henry Paulet with Hyde Parker's fleet and was present at Copenhagen, held in the reserve and never engaged. Four years later, off Cape Trafalgar under Captain George Johnstone Hope, she had a very different afternoon: she captured the Spanish San Ildefonso and fought the Berwick, losing 36 men killed and wounded. In 1809 she supported the Walcheren campaign, the disastrous attempt on Vlissingen and Antwerp that killed far more men by fever than by fighting. By 1811 she was a Baltic convoy escort in the Gunboat War against Denmark-Norway, and in November she joined the last homeward convoy of the year out of Hanöbukten — 129 merchant ships under Vice-Admiral James Saumarez in Victory, with Rear-Admiral Robert Carthew Reynolds flying his flag in the 98-gun St George.

## "I Will Never Desert My Admiral"

St George had already been half-wrecked once. She had grounded at Rødsand in November, lost every mast and her rudder, and was crossing the North Sea on a jury rig with a makeshift rudder lashed on with chain. Defence and the 74-gun Cressy were detailed to stay with her. On 23 December a violent northwesterly caught them off the Jutland coast, and it became clear that all three were being set down onto a lee shore. Cressy hauled off and survived. Defence, under Captain David Atkins, tried to take St George in tow; the hawser parted. Then Atkins is said to have asked whether the admiral had signalled him leave to part company, and on being told no, answered: "I will never desert my Admiral in the hour of danger and distress." That version comes from Brenton's naval history of 1837, and it is the one that stuck. The Annual Register told it the other way round — Defence struck first, and St George, anchoring, swung on her cable and went ashore after her — and Gentleman's Magazine agreed. The accounts have never reconciled.

## Fourteen Men

Defence grounded on 24 December 1811 and the sea broke her open. Of 597 men and boys, 583 died, Atkins among them; fourteen survived, and even that count varies between sources by a man or two. A few miles away St George was going to pieces with nearly 740 aboard — the two wrecks together put roughly 1,400 bodies on the beach over the following fortnight, along a strip of shore where almost nobody lived. Denmark was at war with Britain. The Danes buried the dead anyway, in the sand dunes at Thorsminde that have been called Dead Men's Dunes ever since, and gave Captain Atkins full military honours. Atkins had been at sea since boyhood: midshipman under Erasmus Gower in Edgar in 1780–81, third lieutenant in Lion, a navigator whose skill with lunar observations John Barrow thought remarkable in a young man. Whatever the truth of the signal, he was the one who stayed.

## Flight Context

56.3583°N, 8.1000°E — the wreck site lies off the low, straight west Jutland coast near Thorsminde, where the narrow barrier of dune between the North Sea and Nissum Fjord is only a few hundred metres wide. Fly it at 1,000–2,000 ft AGL: the defining feature is the sluice and harbour at Thorsminde cutting through the dune barrier, with Nissum Fjord's pale shallow water inland and open North Sea to the west. The offshore sandbars that killed both ships are visible as parallel lines of surf running the length of the coast — from the air the geometry of the disaster is obvious in a way it never was from a deck. The Strandingsmuseum St George stands at Thorsminde beside the burial dunes. Nearest airports: Karup (EKKA) about 60 km east, Billund (EKBI) about 95 km southeast, Aalborg (EKYT) about 120 km northeast. Expect strong onshore winds and low cloud; this coast has no shelter of any kind.
