# 1944 Ålvand RAF Lancaster crash

Seven Pathfinder airmen died beside a small heath lake in Thy on the night of 29 August 1944; the craters their engines made are still in the ground, and their graves are in the churchyard at Vorupør.

**Location:** 56.9442, 8.4183

**Categories:** Aviation accidents and incidents in Denmark, Accidents and incidents involving Royal Air Force aircraft, World War II sites in Denmark, Aviation accidents and incidents in 1944, Denmark

## Introduction

People in Nørre Vorupør remembered the light before they remembered the noise. An enormous ball of light broke over the district, so brilliant, locals said, that "you could see a matchstick in the gravel." That was the photoflash bombs going down over farmland ten kilometres to the south, jettisoned from an aircraft that was already burning. A few minutes later the aircraft itself came over low, clipped a dune, and went into the ground beside a small lake called Ålvand. It was Avro Lancaster PB202 of No. 582 Squadron, and all seven men aboard were killed on impact. Five large depressions still mark the heath there — four engines and a fuselage, pressed into the sand on the night of 29 August 1944.

## Four Hundred and Two Aircraft

At 2110 British time that evening — 2210 in Denmark — 402 RAF aircraft lifted off for a double operation against Stettin and Königsberg. The route ran north-east across northern Denmark, on over Sweden and into Germany, and home again the same way. Twenty-three of those aircraft did not come back. PB202 belonged to No. 582 Squadron, part of No. 8 Group, the Pathfinder Force, flying from RAF Little Staughton. Pathfinders went in ahead of the main stream and marked the aiming point with flares and incendiaries so the bombers behind them had something to aim at. It was skilled, dangerous work that put the most experienced crews over the target first, and it is why this particular Lancaster was carrying photoflash bombs.

## The Fighter Over Lodbjerg

For years the story ran that the bomber had been hit by flak from the Lyngby Battery on the coast during its return leg. Later investigation moved the first blow much earlier. PB202 was struck outbound, over Lodbjerg further south along the west coast, by a radar-equipped German night fighter. German radar had been tracking it: a small Wassermann-S set at Thyborøn to the south picked it up that night, as did an installation inland at Hjardemål. The Lancaster was in the middle of a turn from north-east to east-south-east, lining up for the run past Kullen in Scania, when the fighter found it. At the controls of the Junkers Ju 88 was Unteroffizier Bruno Rupp of 4./Nachtjagdgeschwader 3. It was his eleventh confirmed kill. He would end the war with sixteen.

## Ninety Windows at Koustrup Møllegaard

Hit and losing height, the crew got rid of what they were carrying. Three bombs — almost certainly the photoflashes — went down over the farm meadows at Koustrup Møllegaard near Sønderhå, about ten kilometres south-south-east of Ålvand. Dropping ordnance over sparsely populated ground kept it away from villages and away from the crew if they went in. Agnes Møller, born in 1909, was living on the farm and later wrote about that night in her memoirs: the family went down to the shelter, and the damage kept turning up for days afterwards. Ninety panes of glass were broken. The compensation came to 1,700 kroner. The photoflashes left craters in the fields, and for summers afterwards the farm hands were sent out to fill them in whenever there was nothing else to do.

## The Last Two Minutes

After the fighter's burst the Lancaster banked, and the turn probably carried it over the Lyngby Battery, which added its own damage. It was on fire now and coming down fast. It passed low over the farm Udemark by Førby Lake, two kilometres east of Nørre Vorupør. Ahead lay Ålvand, a small lake in open heath — flat water, the best thing available to a crew with no height left, and it is believed that is what they were trying to reach. The aircraft glanced off a dune first. It struck the ground hard beside the lake, and everyone aboard died there.

## Seven Men, and an Eighth

They were not a British crew so much as a Commonwealth one. Squadron Leader Allan L. Farrington, the pilot, was twenty-nine and Australian. His two navigators, Flight Lieutenant Alfred C. Strout and Flight Lieutenant Lorne V. Tyndale, were Canadian. Flying Officer Charles F. Stewart, a gunner, was twenty-two and from Northern Ireland. The three Englishmen were Pilot Officer Henry Silverwood, the wireless operator and gunner, aged twenty-four; Flying Officer George R. Bradley, the flight engineer, twenty-three; and Flight Sergeant Douglas E. J. Stevens, a gunner, who was twenty. German soldiers buried them where they fell and set a white wooden cross over the grave reading "Hier ruhen 7 unbekannte anglo amerik. Flieger 29.8.1944" — here lie seven unknown Anglo-American airmen. On 3 February 1947 British authorities lifted them, coffined them, and reburied them at Vorupør cemetery. They share the plot with an unidentified British soldier who washed ashore near Nørre Vorupør in the autumn of 1944; the Wehrmacht buried him on the beach, and after the surrender the local people dug him up and carried him to the churchyard on 14 June 1945, so that he would not be left on the sand.

## Flight Context

The crash site is at 56.944°N, 8.418°E, on open heath about 2 km east of Nørre Vorupør in Thy, north-west Jutland — dune-heath country of the kind protected in nearby Thy National Park. Ålvand itself is a small pool, best picked out from 1,000–2,000 ft AGL; the five impact depressions left by the four engines and the fuselage are still in the ground beside it. Førby Lake and the farm Udemark lie just to the west, the North Sea coast and Nørre Vorupør 2 km beyond, and Vorupør cemetery, where the crew are buried, is in the village. Nearest airfields: Thisted (EKTS) about 15 km north-east, Karup (EKKA) about 65 km south-east, Lemvig grass strip (EKLV) about 45 km south. This coast is often overcast with low stratus and strong onshore wind; light is best in late afternoon, when the heath goes copper.
