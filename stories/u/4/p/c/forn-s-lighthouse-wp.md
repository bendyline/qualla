# Fornæs Lighthouse

At the last point of Jutland facing east, a granite tower with eighty-four stone steps has been marking the corner of Denmark since 1839.

**Location:** 56.4434, 10.9573

**Categories:** Grenaa, Lighthouses completed in 1839, Buildings and structures in Norddjurs Municipality, Lighthouses in Denmark, Djursland, Maritime history

## Introduction

Walk east from the North Sea surf at Bovbjerg and you will cross 174 kilometres of Jutland — heath, moraine, market towns, motorway — before the land simply stops. It stops at Fornæs, a low cape at the outer end of the Djursland peninsula, a few kilometres north-east of Grenaa. This is the easternmost point of Jutland, which is to say the easternmost point of the Danish mainland, and standing on it there is nothing ahead but the Kattegat and, a long way beyond the horizon, Sweden. Somebody has been keeping a light burning here since 1839.

## Holgreen's Tower, and the One That Replaced It

The first lighthouse at Fornæs went up in 1839 to drawings by Georg Holgreen, built in the old manner with the tower integrated directly into the keeper's house — light and household as one structure, so that the man responsible never had far to go. It was fitted with rotating mirrors. Fifty-three years later, in 1892, the present tower replaced it: granite, plain, and considerably more serious about the weather. The keeper's house survived alongside. That pairing — nineteenth-century domestic brickwork attached to a stump of quarried stone — is still what a visitor finds at the end of the road.

## Eighty-Four Steps

The engineering deserves a closer look than most lighthouses get, because the specification is unusually well recorded. The tower rests on a concrete foundation three metres deep and 8.8 metres across, poured onto solid limestone bedrock — the same Danian chalk that stands up as cliffs a few kilometres north at Sangstrup. Above that sits a half-metre granite base, and above that twenty-two metres of granite tower, carrying the light thirty-two metres above the sea. Inside, eighty-four granite steps spiral upward, each one built with a single end mortised into the outer wall so that the stair is cantilevered from the tower itself rather than resting on any inner column. The woodwork is teak. The lantern room at the top is a cylinder 3.7 metres in diameter, its glazing 2.8 metres high and divided by twelve iron bars. Nothing about it is decorative. Every dimension is an answer to wind.

## Acetylene, Electricity, and a Screen in Copenhagen

The light kept modernising underneath the same stonework. An acetylene lamp arrived in 1902. In 1924 the station was electrified using British lighthouse technology, and the era of tending flames ended. Then, in 1976 and 1977, something larger was built next door: a 365-square-metre, two-storey control centre from which a whole network of automated Danish lighthouses could be run remotely. For a couple of decades, this windswept cape was a nerve centre. On 1 January 2000 the control centre closed and its functions moved to Copenhagen, and Fornæs went from supervising other lighthouses to being supervised itself, from an office on the other side of the country. There is a particular kind of quiet that settles on a place after its job is transferred to a capital city.

## What the Cape Attracts

A headland that far out draws things toward it. Fornæs is a significant staging point for migrating birds, funnelled along the coast and out to the tip, and the ground behind the tower is protected — an 85-hectare stretch of beach and coastal field at Stensmark Strand was placed under conservation in 1969, running roughly ten kilometres north and south of the light. Offshore, the limestone continues as stone and chalk reef, which is why anglers and divers turn up in numbers on the gravel car park. There is public access from the parking area onto the beach and the coastal fields either side. And since 2011 the keeper's house has been let as a holiday rental, so it is now possible to sleep in the building whose entire purpose was to keep somebody awake.

## Flight Context

Fornæs Lighthouse stands at 56.4434°N, 10.9573°E, on the eastern tip of the Djursland peninsula in Norddjurs Municipality. From the air the cape is one of the cleanest navigational features on the Danish east coast: the land comes to a distinct blunt point, with Grenaa and its squared harbour basin about six kilometres to the south-west and the pale line of the Sangstrup and Karlby cliffs beginning some ten kilometres north. The 22-metre granite tower and its attached keeper's house sit almost on the shoreline with open coastal fields behind, and the reef shows as darker water offshore. A viewing altitude of 1,000–2,000 ft AGL keeps tower, cape and reef in one frame. Nearest airport is Aarhus (EKAH) at Tirstrup, roughly 35 km south-west; Aalborg (EKYT) is about 80 km north-west. This is an exposed east-facing headland — expect strong onshore wind, and sea fog off the Kattegat in spring and early summer.
