# Hirsholmene

A scatter of low islets off Frederikshavn where you may sail close but not land — forty-five hectares of gull colony, a 1640s church, and a community that finally ended in the spring of 2024.

**Location:** 57.4856, 10.6244

**Categories:** Islands, Bird Sanctuaries, Ramsar Sites, Maritime History, Denmark, Nature Reserves

## Introduction

Fifty metres. That is as close as you are permitted to come to most of Hirsholmene, and the rule is not decorative. The uninhabited islets of this small archipelago in the northern Kattegat, seven kilometres out from Frederikshavn, have been handed over to the birds: some nine thousand black-headed gulls and close to four thousand Sandwich terns crowd onto forty-five hectares of low grass, shingle and salt meadow in the breeding season. Approach too near in June and the colony comes off the ground in a single sheet. You can look. Mostly, you cannot land.

## An Inventory of Holms

The names read like a list somebody was working through and gave up on: Hirsholm, Græsholm, Tyvholm, Lilleholm, Møgholm, Pikkerholm, Kovsholm, Kølpen, Deget, Hjellen. None of them is large. Only Hirsholm, the biggest, was ever properly lived on, and everything human about the group — the church, the school, the harbour, the light — is concentrated there. The rest are strips of shingle and turf just high enough to keep their feet dry, shaped and reshaped by sand and clay that the currents deposit and take away again. Standing on Hirsholm you can see the whole archipelago at once, which is one way of understanding how small it is.

## Pilots, Stone and Thirty People

The crown held these islands until 1583. They passed through private hands after that, and the state took them back in 1883 — under the defence ministry first, transferred to the environment ministry in 2003. In between, people made livings here in ways that no longer exist anywhere. A pilot station opened in 1733 and guided shipping through the Kattegat approaches until it was closed in 1916. Men cut and shipped stone off the holms. The surrounding water was fished hard. A boat harbour was built in 1860, and a ferry link to Frederikshavn began in 1872 — it still runs, about three times a week. As the stone-cutting and the fishing dwindled, so did everything else: by 1921 there were thirty inhabitants left. Hirsholm had kept both a priest and a schoolmaster for three hundred years. The school closed in 1970. One resident remained until the spring of 2024.

## The Church, the Light, the Bunkers

The church on Hirsholm is a small half-timbered Renaissance building put up around 1640, and it is improbably still in use — services are held there several times a year for a congregation that has to arrive by boat. The lighthouse came much later: a 27-metre tower erected in 1886, automated in 1996, retiring the last job that genuinely required somebody to live out here year-round. Concrete of a very different vintage is scattered across Hirsholm and Deget. Eight German bunker positions survive from the 1940–45 occupation, including a searchlight battery built in 1944, when this stretch of water mattered a great deal to people who had no interest in the birds.

## The Colony

Hirsholmene has been a scientific nature reserve since 1938, one of the earliest in Denmark, with the marine protections widened in 1981; it joined the Ramsar list in September 1977 as site number 147, covering 3,714 hectares. The bird list is long and emphatically northern. Eider, common scoter and velvet scoter work the water offshore. Kittiwakes, razorbills and guillemots come through, along with divers and grebes, and gulls of nearly every European kind — herring, lesser black-backed, common, great black-backed. The most interesting residents are the least conspicuous. Six to seven hundred pairs of black guillemots nest at the southern end of the main island, small dark seabirds with white wing patches and startling scarlet feet, tucked into crevices and under stones where nothing much can reach them.

## Sky, Sea and an Island

For a while the place had a chronicler. Dines Skafte Jespersen, the Danish author best known for the Troldepus children's books, spent time on Hirsholm and wrote a memoir of a year living and working there, titled Himmel, hav og en ø — Sky, Sea and an Island. As an inventory of the local resources, the title is hard to improve on. Around seven thousand people a year make the crossing now. They walk the marked paths on Hirsholm, read the information boards, and look across fifty metres of open water at Græsholm, where they are not going. Deget is the single exception: it can be visited from August through February, once the nesting is finished and the birds have gone.

## Flight Context

Hirsholmene lies at 57.4856°N, 10.6244°E, about 7 km northeast of Frederikshavn in the northern Kattegat. From the air the group is easy to miss and easy to identify once found: a cluster of low, pale holms totalling only 45 hectares, strung loosely northeast of the Frederikshavn harbour approaches, with the 27-metre white lighthouse on Hirsholm the single vertical feature in the frame. In the breeding season the gull and tern colonies show as visible pale masses on the turf of Græsholm. Keep well above the islands — this is a nature reserve where surface access within 50 metres of the uninhabited holms is prohibited, and low overflight disturbs nesting birds; 2,000 ft AGL or higher is appropriate from spring through midsummer. Nearest airfields: Sindal (EKSN) roughly 15 nm west-southwest, Aalborg (EKYT) about 40 nm southwest, and Læsø (EKLS) 20 nm southeast across the water. Visibility is generally good, but haze and low stratus off the Kattegat are common in spring.
