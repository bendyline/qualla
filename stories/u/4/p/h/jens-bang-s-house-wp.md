# Jens Bang's House

A carved face pulls a rude expression from the wall of Aalborg's grandest Renaissance house — and the merchant who built it was, in documented fact, never let into the city's government.

**Location:** 57.0485, 9.9211

**Categories:** Buildings and structures in Aalborg, Renaissance architecture in Denmark, History of Aalborg, Pharmacy museums, Tourist attractions in Aalborg

## Introduction

High on the sandstone dressing of a house on Østerågade, a carved man's face sticks its tongue out at the street. It has been doing that since 1624. Aalborg will tell you whose face it is and who it is aimed at: Jens Bang, the richest merchant in town, gurning permanently across the square at the town hall of the council that never let him in. It is a very good story and it should be treated as a story — the carving is documented, the grudge behind it is tradition rather than record. What can be verified is stranger in its way. Bang really was the wealthiest man in Aalborg, he really did enjoy the favour of the king, and he really never held office in the city magistrate.

## The Outsider from Horsens

Bang was born around 1570 in Horsens, on the other side of Jutland, the son of Sidsel Jørgensdatter by her second marriage to Oluf Bang. He came to Aalborg as a young man and joined the merchants' guild, Guds Legems Lav, taking licensed merchant status in 1605. Over the next two decades his trade expanded until he was among the leading merchants of the entire kingdom and unquestionably the biggest in Aalborg. He also had a head start he rarely gets credit for: his half-brother Jørgen Olufsen was the mayor, and had already built himself a fine house further up the same street. None of it bought Bang a seat. Unlike other great merchants of his generation he took no office in the magistrate, and the sources agree he was not much liked — a formidable operator who ended up in physical fights with his contemporaries. Whether he was excluded or simply never wanted the job is not recorded. In 1637 he and his wife funded a poorhouse with twenty-six beds and a garden, which suggests a man with something to prove or something to atone for, and probably both.

## What Money Could Buy in 1624

In 1623 Bang inherited Københavns gård, an old property at the corner of Østerå and Adelsgade. He knocked most of it down and put up a residence that has embarrassed every other private house in Denmark ever since, finishing it in 1624. Five storeys, four main floors and a habitable loft, three gables in Dutch Renaissance style with sandstone auricular ornament curling around the windows like something anatomical, gargoyles projecting from the eaves. The only comparison anyone offers is Mattias Hansen's House on Amagertorv in Copenhagen, which has three floors and two gables and loses. The stonework matches the quality of Christian IV's own building projects, which is not a coincidence — Bang's standing with the king gave him access to the best designers and cutters available, and Hans van Steenwinckel the Younger has been suggested as the architect, though no document names one. Jørgen Olufsen's house up the street, once the pride of the family, was instantly outclassed.

## The Face on the Wall

So what about the tongue? The carving exists, unambiguously: among the gargoyles and grotesques there is a human face with its tongue out, and Aalborg has read it for generations as Bang's own portrait, aimed at the seat of civic authority. The reading is satisfying because the biography supports it. Here was the town's richest man, half-brother to a mayor, on excellent terms with the crown, and shut out of the magistrate all the same, building the most conspicuous house in Jutland directly on the town's main street. What is missing is any contemporary source saying so. Neither the Danish nor the English record of the building attaches the grudge to the carving; that connection lives in local tradition, guidebooks and tour patter. Treat it as folklore about a real man, which is generally how the best local stories work — the grievance is documented, the gesture is decoration, and somewhere along the line the town decided the two belonged together.

## The Swan and the Attic

Bang did not enjoy the house long. He died in 1644, and within a generation the ground floor had become a pharmacy — Svaneapoteket, the Swan Pharmacy, opened by the apothecary Johannes Friederich in the second half of the 1600s and handed down from one pharmacist to the next for more than three centuries. It is the oldest pharmacy in the city and it still trades. Aalborg's cathedral got something out of the arrangement too: in 1692 the house's first apothecary and his wife Magdalena Calow paid for the pulpit that stands in Budolfi to this day. Up under the roof, the Apotekersamling displays what the trade left behind, the jars and mortars and drug runs of three hundred years of compounding, administered since 2004 by the Historical Museum of Northern Jutland. The building itself is not municipal and never has been. It belongs to a private foundation, Apoteker S. C. Strøybergs Fond, created for the single purpose of keeping Jens Bang's stone house standing — which means the merchant the council would not admit is still, four centuries on, in private hands and making a face at them.

## Flight Context

Jens Bang's House stands at 57.0485 N, 9.9211 E, on Østerågade at Nytorv in central Aalborg, roughly 400 metres south of the Limfjord waterfront and 100 metres east of Budolfi Church. It is a single building on a dense street and will not be identified from altitude; navigate to the old-town block bounded by the fjord to the north and the cathedral spire to the west, and pick out the three stepped gables from a low pass or from the water. Useful altitude over central Aalborg is 1,000 to 2,000 feet AGL. Aalborg Airport (EKYT) is 6 km northwest across the fjord, Sindal (EKSN) 55 km north, Aarhus (EKAH) 100 km south. North Jutland gets frequent low maritime cloud; clear, low sun in the afternoon rakes the sandstone carving and is the only time the ornament reads properly.
