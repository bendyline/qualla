# Danish Distillers

For fifty years one Aalborg company made every legal drop of aquavit in Denmark; the plant fell silent in 2015 and the site is now becoming a neighbourhood.

**Location:** 57.0540, 9.9109

**Categories:** Companies based in Aalborg, Danish distilled drinks, Industrial heritage, History of Aalborg, Architecture in Denmark

## Introduction

Between 1923 and 1973 there was exactly one lawful source of aquavit in Denmark, and its address was Aalborg. De Danske Spritfabrikker — Danish Distillers — held the exclusive right to produce spirits, aquavit and yeast for the entire kingdom, a monopoly that ended only when Danish membership of the European Community forced the market open. That is an unusual thing for a city to have been. Not the home of a famous distillery, but the home of the distillery, the single set of copper stills behind every schnapps poured at every Danish Christmas lunch for half a century.

## Tietgen Puts the Trade in One Box

The company was assembled in 1881 by C. F. Tietgen, the financier whose speciality was gathering scattered Danish industries into single national champions, working with the distillers Isidor Henius and C. A. Olesen. Henius, remembered as the pioneer of modern Danish spirit production, took the director's chair. Brøndum and Harald Jensen Akvavit came into the fold by 1884, and acquisition followed acquisition until by 1923 the firm controlled every distillery in the country. His son had already gone the other way. Max Henius, born in Aalborg in 1859, took a chemistry doctorate at Marburg and emigrated to Chicago at twenty-two, where he became a leading biochemist of fermentation and co-founded the American Brewing Academy in 1891. In 1912 he handed King Christian X the deed to a stretch of Himmerland heath bought by Danish Americans, which became Rebild — where they have marked the Fourth of July every year since. One family, two continents, one subject.

## The Handsomest Factory in Denmark

Alf Cock-Clausen built the Aalborg works between 1929 and 1931, on the water west of the Limfjord bridge, and it does not look like a factory so much as a civic institution that happens to make alcohol. Long symmetrical brick ranges face the fjord in a stripped neoclassical manner with functionalist bones, and the company's Maltese cross is worked into the facades. Denmark's national heritage authority listed the complex in 2015. The architect had a second Aalborg commission from the same client in the same years: the pedestal for the Cimbrian Bull, unveiled on Vesterbro in 1937 and paid for by the distillery. In the interwar decades this company was building the city's landmarks as a matter of course.

## Sold, and Sold Again

Monopoly ended in 1973, and after that the corporate history reads like a relay race. The firm merged with De Danske Sukkerfabrikker and became part of Danisco in 1989. Sweden's V and S Vin and Sprit took it in 1999. France's Pernod Ricard held it from 2008. In 2012 the Danish brands went north to Norway's Arcus group, and the sale came with a condition: to keep Arcus from dominating the Danish market, competition regulators required it to give up Brøndums Snaps, which went to the Finnish producer Altia. There is a small joke buried in what happened next. When Arcus and Altia merged in 2021 to form Anora, the two halves of the old monopoly did not come back together, because Altia had sold Brøndums on to Galatea before the merger closed.

## April 2015

Production stopped in Aalborg in April 2015 and moved to Norway. Aalborg Akvavit is still made, still sold, still poured at julefrokost, and it is no longer made in Aalborg — the name on the bottle now describes a place the liquid has never been. A hundred and thirty-four years of distilling in one city ended on a corporate spreadsheet, as these things usually do, and the people who lost their jobs did so quietly. Aalborg also lost Gammel Dansk, the bitter that shares the company's history and, in Danish domestic mythology, its breakfast slot. The brands live on under Anora, headquartered in Helsinki. The stills do not.

## Spritten

What remains is the architecture, and the architecture turned out to be the durable asset. Protected in the same year that production ceased, the fjordside complex is being converted under the name Spritten into a mixed quarter of six to eight hundred homes with cultural venues and commercial space threaded through the listed halls. The company's Roskilde plant went the same route, into youth housing and a climbing club. There is a logic to it that the founders would probably have recognised: Tietgen's whole method was to see an underused asset and put it to work. The difference is that the asset is now the building rather than the business, and what it produces is a view of the Limfjord.

## Flight Context

The Spritfabrikken complex sits at 57.0540 N, 9.9109 E, on the south bank of the Limfjord roughly 1.5 km west of the Limfjord bridge and about 800 metres northwest of Aalborg's old town. From the air it is one of the easiest landmarks in the city: a long pale rectilinear block set hard against the waterfront, distinctly larger and more regular than anything around it, with the fjord curving past its northern face. Good look-down altitude is 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL on a westbound run along the water. Aalborg Airport (EKYT) lies 5 km northwest on the opposite bank, which puts the site almost directly under the approach corridor. Sindal (EKSN) is 55 km north. Expect maritime haze and frequent low cloud off the Skagerrak in autumn and winter.
