# Jomfru Ane Gade

Denmark's most concentrated stretch of bars and discotheques is named after a sixteenth-century nun.

**Location:** 57.0501, 9.9189

**Categories:** Restaurant districts and streets, Streets in Denmark, Nightlife, Tourist attractions in Aalborg

## Introduction

The street is named after a nun. Ane Madsdatter Viffert was a former nun of Ø Kloster on the Limfjord; by 1568 she was living in nearby Skavegade, and she was buried at Hals Church in 1572. Sometime around the end of the 1500s her name attached itself to the lane — Jomfru Ane Gade, Virgin Anne's Street. Four and a half centuries later, on a Friday in July, it is the loudest stretch of pavement in North Jutland. Locals do not bother with the full name. They just say Gaden. The street.

## Two Hundred Years of Merchants

For most of its life the street was ordinary and prosperous. After Ane Viffert's time it filled up with the houses of Aalborg merchants and stayed that way for roughly two centuries, a respectable address in a trading town on the fjord. Some of those half-timbered buildings are still standing, dark beams and pale infill panels, now with a sound system behind the windows. It is worth looking up when you walk it. Below eye level the street is neon and chalkboards and doorway bouncers; above the first floor, the same façades an eighteenth-century merchant would have recognised are still there, doing nothing in particular except outlasting everything installed underneath them.

## 5 May 1967

The change is precisely dated. On 5 May 1967 the street's first licence was granted and a discotheque called Gaslight opened its doors. Jørgen Steffensen was behind it, having come home from a trip to Italy where he had seen a dance hall and understood what could be done with one. At the same moment the Jomfru Ane Teatret was set up on the floor above — a theatre and a nightclub sharing a staircase, which tells you something about the street's tolerance for mixed company. Gaslight worked, so Fyrtøjet opened the following year. By the end of the 1970s another ten pubs and restaurants had followed. By 1992 there were twenty-six.

## The Economics of Gaden

Today more than twenty restaurants, pubs and discotheques line both sides, employing somewhere between two and three hundred people, and as of 2012 the street was turning over around 300 million Danish kroner a year. That makes it one of Denmark's longest restaurant streets and one of its densest concentrations of nightlife — a claim worth being precise about, because it is often overstated. Gaden is not the longest bar street in the country outright; areas such as Åboulevarden in Aarhus and Pisserenden in Copenhagen exceed it. What it does have is total commitment. There is nothing else on the street. On warm summer evenings the terraces fill first, and when the long northern dusk finally gives out, the crowd shifts indoors and the discotheques take over until morning.

## The Beer Falls in November

The street's biggest night is not in summer. J-dag falls on the first Friday in November, at 8:59 in the evening, when the Tuborg brewery releases its Christmas beer, Julebryg, and Jomfru Ane Gade is one of the places in Denmark where the occasion is taken most seriously. Tuborg's blue-clad elves hand out thousands of free bottles and blue Santa hats, and a street built for July nights gets used at its full capacity in the dark and the cold. It is a strange, specific piece of Danish ritual: a commercial holiday launched in 1990 around a seasonal product, and a street that treats it as a civic event.

## Looking After the Street

A place where thousands of young people drink in a confined space every weekend has predictable problems, and Aalborg has spent two decades working on them. Concerns rose after a run of crimes including a stabbing on the street in 2007, and North Jutland police began deploying facial recognition technology there the same year. The more interesting response came later. In 2022 a volunteer-run safe house opened on the street — somewhere to sit down, sober up, get a glass of water, and be looked after by people who are not police and not door staff. A TV2 Nord documentary followed it in 2024. That same year, bollards went in along the street as a precaution against vehicle attacks. The nun's name is still on it, and the street has quietly acquired a duty of care.

## Flight Context

57.0501°N, 9.9189°E, in central Aalborg one block back from the Limfjord's south bank, running between Vesterå and the waterfront. From the air the street itself is too narrow to pick out; navigate instead on the Limfjord waterfront and the Limfjord Bridge just to the north, with Budolfi Church's spire a few hundred metres south-east. Recommended viewing altitude 1,000–1,500 ft AGL over the fjord. Aalborg Airport (EKYT) is roughly 3 nm west-north-west and shares the field with Aalborg Air Base, so the city centre lies inside controlled airspace beneath the approach — expect ATC clearance requirements and military traffic. On a clear summer night the street is visible from above as a single unbroken line of light in an otherwise dim quarter of the old town.
