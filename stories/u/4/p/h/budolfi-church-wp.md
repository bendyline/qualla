# Budolfi Church

Aalborg's landmark spire was bought with the wills of a brother and sister who never saw it built, on ground where people have worshipped since at least 1132.

**Location:** 57.0479, 9.9196

**Categories:** Cathedrals in Denmark, Gothic architecture, History of Aalborg, Religion in Aalborg, Tourist attractions in Aalborg

## Introduction

Jacob and Elisabeth Himmerig died within a year of each other, leaving their money in 1773 and 1774 to the church at the head of Aalborg's market street. Five years later that money went straight up into the air. The tall spire finished in 1779 has been the city's landmark ever since, the thing you steer by from the Limfjord bridges and the thing that appears on the postcards. Their epitaphs still hang inside, among a good deal of other people's generosity. Budolfi is a church assembled out of last wishes: an altarpiece from one couple, a pulpit from an apothecary, a marble font from a widow, and a spire from a brother and sister who are remembered for nothing else.

## Stones in the Crypt

Before the brick there was timber, and before the timber there was only the fjord. Aalborg was a town by the middle of the 10th century, well placed on the Limfjord, the east-west waterway that carried trade between the North Sea and the Baltic until sand drifts closed its western end in the 12th century. Christian missionaries had probably reached this stretch of Jutland in the 800s, since Viborg and Aarhus nearby show early Christian activity, and in 948 Denmark was carved into dioceses, with Aalborg falling in time under the See of Viborg. Of the earliest churches here nothing survives above ground. They were small and built of wood, and wood does not wait around. What does survive is underfoot: in the crypt you can see the heavy foundation stones of the church raised at the direction of Bishop Eskil of Viborg no later than 1132. That building was a modest Romanesque parish church, a short nave and choir with half-round arches under a flat timber ceiling, and you could set the whole of it down inside the present church with room left over.

## A Saint for Farmers and Sailors

The name comes from St Botolph, an Anglo-Saxon abbot whose reputation for learning and holiness travelled far beyond England. He was the patron of farmers and of sailors, a useful pairing in a fjord town that lived off the water and the fields behind it, and he became genuinely popular in Denmark before the Reformation. His remains were venerated in England, at Westminster Abbey and at Ely. No relic of him is recorded at Aalborg. Churches were often named for the most famous saint whose bones they held, so the dedication here seems to have arrived without the usual reason for it. Other churches in Denmark and southern Sweden carry versions of the name as well, among them St Bodil's Church on Bornholm. Danish mouths turned Botolph into Budolf, and Budolf into Budolfi.

## Built in the Country's Cheapest Stone

Denmark has very little building stone and a great deal of clay, so Danes built in brick, and Budolfi is brick Gothic at civic scale. In the last decades of the 14th century the town raised the present church over and around the older one, the standard medieval trick of keeping the congregation dry inside while the new walls climb outside. The finished building appears in the record for the first time in 1399. Nave and choir now measure 56 metres long and 22 metres wide, excluding the porch and the additions, and it took centuries to reach that size: a sacristy went onto the north aisle in 1899, and during 1942 and 1943 the choir was pushed out a further fourteen metres, the vaulting lifted, and a chapel added to the south, toward Algade. Hack Kampmann handled the work at the turn of the 20th century; Einar Packness took on the wartime enlargement.

## The Year the Bishop Moved In

For most of its life this was a parish church answering to a diocese run from somewhere else. A royal decision changed that. In 1553 the king determined that the bishop should live in Aalborg, and in 1554 the district was separated from Viborg to form the diocese of Aalborg, which made Budolfi a cathedral. It has been one ever since. Becoming the city's church also involved a blunter kind of luck. After the Reformation Aalborg found itself holding three large churches with no monastic institutions left to maintain them, and the townspeople had no appetite for the bills. Two of the three came down. Budolfi is the one they kept.

## An Interior Bought by Strangers

Read the interior as a donor list and the town's history reads back at you. Niels Jespersen and his wife Margareta Erichsdatter gave the main altarpiece in 1684, carved by Lauridtz Jensen of Essenbæk Abbey near Randers and restored in 1980. The same hand cut the pulpit of 1692, paid for by the first apothecary at Jens Bang's House and his wife Magdalena Calow. Jens Christensen and Mette Christensdatter supplied the altar candelabras in 1686. A widow, Maren Grotum von Pentz, gave the black-and-white marble font in 1728. An altar carving from around 1450 stands in the north transept, brought in from the estate chapel of Holckershavn in southern Jutland. The Baroque organ facade of 1749 was built for Hartvig Jochum Müller's instrument and has been rebuilt behind repeatedly, most recently by Th. Frobenius and Sons in 1959. Epitaphs line the walls where once there were many more: the Renaissance memorial to Karine Hansdatter from 1583, and the neoclassical pair for the Himmerig siblings, who bought the spire.

## Flight Context

Budolfi Church stands at 57.0479 N, 9.9196 E, in the centre of Aalborg roughly 700 metres south of the Limfjord. The spire is the tallest thing in the old town and reads clearly from 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL, sitting just south of the twin crossings of the fjord: the Limfjord bridge and the Limfjord tunnel. Aalborg Airport (EKYT) lies about 6 km northwest on the far side of the water, so most approaches bring the old town into view on one side or the other. Sindal (EKSN) is 55 km north and Aarhus (EKAH) 100 km south. North Jutland weather is maritime and changeable; low stratus off the Skagerrak can hide the spire on otherwise decent days, and the best light on the copper is late afternoon in summer, when the fjord behind it goes flat and bright.
