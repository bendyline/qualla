# Cimbrian Bull

Anders Bundgaard unveiled his bronze bull on Vesterbro in April 1937 and was dead by September, leaving Himmerland the emblem it still uses.

**Location:** 57.0497, 9.9164

**Categories:** 1937 sculptures, Bronze sculptures in Denmark, Outdoor sculptures in Denmark, Sculptures by Anders Bundgaard, Tourist attractions in Aalborg

## Introduction

On 14 April 1937 a bronze bull was unveiled on Vesterbro in Aalborg, and the man who made it had five months to live. Anders Bundgaard was seventy-two. He had been born in Ersted, a village a few kilometres from Thingbæk in the Himmerland countryside south of the Limfjord, the son of a smallholder who also made wooden shoes, and he died in Copenhagen that September. The Cimbrertyren was the last major public work of a farmer's boy who had spent his career decorating the capital, and it went up in the regional capital of the district he came from. Himmerland has been putting it on shields and letterheads ever since.

## From Wooden Shoes to Christiansborg

Bundgaard's route out of Ersted ran through the workshop of the sculptor F. E. Ring and then the Royal Danish Academy of Fine Arts, where he studied from 1885 to 1887, followed by more than a decade of study trips to Paris, Italy, Egypt and Greece between 1889 and 1900. He came home to the sort of career that fills a capital city: carved and cast decoration on Christiansborg Palace and Copenhagen City Hall, and above all the Gefion Fountain, the great oxen-and-goddess group that has been throwing water at Copenhagen since 1908. Set the two beside each other and the contrast is the point. Gefion is all motion, four straining beasts under the whip of a goddess turning an island out of the earth. The Aalborg bull does nothing at all. It stands, head lowered, entirely still, and the stillness is what people remember.

## Bought with Schnapps Money

The bull did not come out of the city treasury. De Danske Spritfabrikker, the Aalborg distiller that then held the whole Danish spirits trade in one hand, paid for it, and the pedestal was designed not by Bundgaard but by Alf Cock-Clausen, the architect who had recently completed the company's monumental new distillery on the western edge of town. The site carried the company's fingerprints too. The bull stands where the firm's older Aalborg factory had stood until it closed in 1931, on the stretch of Vesterbro near where the pedestrian street Bispensgade comes in. A distillery cleared its own obsolete plant and put an ancient tribe on the empty ground, which tells you something about how confident Danish industry felt in the 1930s.

## The Poem Cut into the Pedestal

Johannes V. Jensen came to the unveiling, and a dedication he wrote to the Cimbri was cut into the pedestal. He was the obvious choice. Born in Farsø in 1873, deep in Himmerland, he had made the district into literature with the Himmerland Stories he published between 1898 and 1910, and in 1920 he had set in motion the museum centre at Aars a short drive south. In April 1937 he was simply the region's most celebrated son. The Nobel Prize in Literature would not reach him until 1944, seven years after he stood in the crowd at Vesterbro, and he lived until 1950. The pedestal therefore carries the words of a future laureate written when nobody knew he would become one.

## A Tribe Argued Over

Who were the Cimbri, and were they ever here? Ptolemy, writing in the 2nd century, placed the Kimbroi at the northernmost end of the Jutland peninsula, and the name Himmerland is generally taken to descend from theirs. What happened afterwards belongs to Roman history. The Cimbri came south, destroyed a Roman army at Arausio in 105 BC in a defeat Livy put as high as eighty thousand dead, and left Rome in the panic remembered as the terror cimbricus, before being annihilated at Vercellae in 101 BC, where the chieftains Boiorix and Lugius died on the field and Caesorix and Claodicus were taken alive. The beginning is the shaky part. Scholars place the tribe's origin in Jutland on the strength of the ancient texts, but archaeologists have found no clear sign of any mass migration out of the peninsula in the early Iron Age. Nor does the bull have an ancient warrant: what the classical sources describe of Cimbrian religion is priestesses, sacrifice and divination, not cattle. The animal is a modern choice, strength and obstinacy cast in bronze for a region that liked the fit.

## The Missing Sword

Part of the artwork was a sword fixed to the pedestal. In 2000 it vanished, and nobody noticed. Seventeen years passed before the sword resurfaced in 2017 and was handed back to the mayor of Aalborg, a theft solved less by police work than by someone's change of heart. The bull itself has had a busier public life. It was wrapped in red and white when Aalborg's football club played in the Champions League in 2008, and it has been reproduced far beyond the city: in the arms of Aars Municipality, in those of the former Aalborg Amt, and in the logo of Himmerland Forsikring. A sculpture bought by a liquor company to fill an empty lot has ended up as the closest thing a Danish region has to a national animal.

## Flight Context

The Cimbrian Bull stands at 57.0497 N, 9.9164 E, on Vesterbro in central Aalborg about 400 metres south of the Limfjord waterfront and 200 metres northwest of Budolfi Church. It is too small to identify from the air on its own, so navigate to the block instead: Vesterbro is the broad north-south artery that feeds the Limfjord bridge, and the bull sits at its junction with Bispensgade. Recommended look-down altitude over the old town is 1,200 to 2,000 feet AGL. Aalborg Airport (EKYT) is 6 km northwest across the fjord, Sindal (EKSN) 55 km north. Winter light in North Jutland is low and short; the bronze reads best under overcast, when the patina stops glaring.
