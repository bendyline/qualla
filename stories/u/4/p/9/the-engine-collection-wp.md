# The Engine Collection

In a shed in Grenaa, volunteers keep more than 450 stationary engines — northern Europe's largest such collection — not merely preserved but running, including one built in 1868.

**Location:** 56.4372, 10.8268

**Categories:** Denmark, Djursland, Museums, Industrial heritage, Engines, Technology

## Introduction

There is a particular noise a hot-bulb engine makes: a slow, unhurried thump with a long gap between each beat, like something large breathing in its sleep. In a set of sheds on Kristiansmindevej in Grenaa, at the eastern tip of the Djursland peninsula, several hundred machines that make that noise stand in rows — and most of them still work. Dansk Motor- og Maskinsamling, known in English simply as the Engine Collection, holds more than 450 stationary engines, the largest gathering of them in northern Europe. Its volunteers did not restore these machines to be looked at. They restored them to be started.

## The Oldest Thing in the Room

At one end of the exhibition stands an Otto & Langen atmospheric engine built in 1868, among the oldest surviving internal combustion engines anywhere in the world. It runs on a principle that feels almost like a joke at the expense of everything that came after it. Town gas ignites at the base of a tall vertical cylinder and throws a free piston straight up. Nothing useful happens on that stroke. The work comes on the way down: as the exhaust gases cool and contract, atmospheric pressure and gravity haul the piston back into the vacuum, and that descent — transmitted through hand-cut cogged wheels, its speed governed by an exhaust valve — is what drives the machine. There is no camshaft anywhere in it. The engine is on loan from the Danish Technical Museum, and the Grenaa volunteers are the ones who got it running again. Nikolaus August Otto (1832–1891) would not work out the four-stroke cycle that carries his name for nearly another decade.

## Two Hundred Foundries

Denmark built engines out of all proportion to its size. Between 1890 and 1930, more than two hundred domestic manufacturers produced stationary engines, many of them casting their own iron in their own foundries, and the museum's Danish emphasis is a straightforward reflection of that industry. Towering over the rest was Burmeister & Wain of Copenhagen, which by the late 1920s held close to half the world market for marine diesel engines. B&W's big diesels also worked ashore, in factories and electricity plants — turning line shafts by belt at first, and later spinning generators that sent the power out on cables instead. That shift, from belt to cable, is the whole twentieth century in miniature.

## The Engine in the Cow Shed

The small machines carry the more intimate story. A cast-iron Danish B.L.A. motor of a few horsepower — under ten, often nearer two — lived in a purpose-built shed on a farm and was started at least twice a day to run the compressor for the milking machines. Between milkings it drove the thresher, the grinding mill, whatever needed turning. Denmark was shipping bacon and butter to England in serious volume in those decades, and a good deal of that trade ran on exactly this: a heavy flywheel, a lamp warming the hot bulb, and a farmer with the knack of swinging it into life. Electric motors were smaller and needed far less nursing. Between roughly 1920 and 1950 they emptied the sheds, one farm at a time.

## Salvage

Getting the exhibits to Grenaa is half the museum's history. In 2002 the collection recovered three Burmeister & Wain DM 220 diesels of 1914 from the bunkers of Flakfortet, an abandoned artificial fortress island in the Øresund — corroded, vandalised and immense. The retrieval took a donated tug, cranes and a flatbed trailer; a single flywheel from one of those engines weighs about 1.5 tonnes. Once a machine arrives, the workshop takes over. Cylinders, pistons and glow heads are milled from scratch when the originals are past saving, the new profiles read off the eaten-away contours of the old ones. What that amounts to, quite often, is reverse-engineering a Danish foundry that shut before any of the volunteers were born.

## Kept Running

Some of what stands here may be the last of its kind. A Rudolf Kramper engine of 1927 is thought to be the only survivor anywhere. A Danish Eickhoff of 1887 was finished in 2014 after a long restoration. Outside, a band saw sawmill is driven by a Bukh engine housed indoors, connected across the yard by a belt run — because a museum of working machinery ought to demonstrate the awkward, ingenious business of moving power from where it is made to where it is wanted. The collection is a volunteer-run foundation, kept going by private sponsors and by foundations with an interest in industrial history, and staffed by people whose working lives were spent in engine building, metalwork and the trades around them. It opens daily from the start of April to the end of October, and the engines are run at public events, at demonstrations, and for groups who book ahead. Come on the right day and you will hear all of it at once.

## Flight Context

56.437°N, 10.827°E, on the outskirts of Grenaa at the eastern end of the Djursland peninsula in Jutland. The town is easy to find from 2,000–4,000 ft: it sits where the flat, canal-ruled trough of drained Kolindsund meets the Kattegat, with a large ferry harbour on the coast. The museum itself is an unremarkable cluster of industrial sheds inland of the port — look for the harbour first. Aarhus Airport (EKAH) at Tirstrup lies about 20 km south-west, with Aalborg (EKYT) and Karup (EKKA) as alternates. The Kattegat crossing to Anholt and on to Sweden runs east from here; visibility is usually good, with occasional sea haze in summer.
