# Kalø Castle

A royal fortress built by forced labour to crush a rebellion, later the prison from which Gustav Vasa walked away to become king of Sweden.

**Location:** 56.2745, 10.4671

**Categories:** castles, ruins, history, medieval, national parks

## Introduction

In September 1519 a man in peasant clothes arrived in Lübeck. He had given his word of honour that he would not run, and he had broken it. His name was Gustav Eriksson Vasa, he had spent a year as King Christian II's hostage on a small island in a Danish bay, and within four years he would be king of Sweden and the Danish crown's most consequential enemy. The island he escaped from is still there, at the head of Kalø Vig on the Djursland peninsula, and so is the castle - or what is left of it. A brick tower stump and a ring of broken walls sit on grass cropped short by cattle, reached by a cobbled causeway that runs several hundred metres out from the shore. It is one of the most photographed ruins in Denmark and one of the most misunderstood, because it was never built to be beautiful. It was built to hurt.

## Built to Break a Rebellion

King Erik Menved - Erik VI - raised Kalø in 1313, and he did not raise it alone. It was one of at least four strongholds he planted across Jutland in response to sustained revolt by the Jutlandic nobility and peasantry against the Crown. The others stood at Borgvold in Viborg, at Bygholm in Horsens, and at Ulstrup east of Struer. All four were built by local peasants under forced labour, and that was part of the point: the men made to dig the moats and heap the earth ramparts were the same men whose rebellious spirit the castles were meant to break. At Kalø the labour was brutal even by the standards of the age. Workers cobbled a causeway across open water to the island, dug deep moats, threw up mounds, and built outer ringwalls and a landing place by hand. In the early fourteenth century this was a colossal undertaking.

## Demolished by Contract

The fortress worked. It also lasted barely seven years in its intended role. When Christoffer II came to the throne in 1320, the Danish nobility made him sign a coronation charter - a contract binding the king to his subjects' terms - and one of those terms was that the Crown's Jutland fortresses had to come down. Kalø was on the list. Exactly how much of it was actually pulled down is unclear, but the Crown's grip on the place was finished; it was mortgaged away. From the fifteenth century onward Kalø settled into a quieter and more bureaucratic life as a regional administrative seat and a state prison. That combination - remote, walled, surrounded by water, and run by a royal official - was what made it useful to Christian II two centuries later.

## The Prisoner Who Kept His Freedom of the Yard

Gustav Vasa was taken as a Swedish hostage and delivered to Kalø in 1518. His keeper was the lensman Erik Eriksen Banner, and the arrangement was the one commonly offered to a captive of rank: parole. Vasa gave his word not to flee, and in return he was allowed to move about the castle grounds rather than sit in a cell. He kept the bargain for roughly a year. Then he broke it, slipped away in the dress of a common peasant, and made for Lübeck, reaching the city in September 1519. What followed - the revolt, the war, the founding of a Swedish royal dynasty - reshaped northern Europe. Denmark noticed. In 1877 the novelist Carit Etlar built a book around the episode, Fangen på Kalø, and Steen Steensen Blicher had already woven the place into The Rector of Veilbye in 1829. A ruin most Danes could not date has been kept alive in the national imagination largely by fiction.

## Shipped to Copenhagen, Stone by Stone

Absolutism finished Kalø as an institution. When Frederik III converted Denmark's elective monarchy into an absolute one in 1660, the castle's administrative function evaporated, and the buildings were already decaying after the Swedish occupation of the war years of 1643 to 1645. The ruin was granted to Ulrik Frederik Gyldenløve, Frederik III's son, who saw in it not a monument but a quarry. Kalø was dismantled and its usable brick and stone shipped to Copenhagen, where they went into the palace Gyldenløve was building for himself - the building known today as Charlottenborg, home of the Royal Danish Academy of Fine Arts. Denmark's most photogenic medieval ruin is missing most of itself because it is standing, reassembled, on Kongens Nytorv.

## Target Practice and a National Park

Protection came early. Kalø was designated as a protected site in the 1800s, and in 1903 the architect C. M. Smidt of the National Museum of Denmark began the excavation and restoration that saved what survived; a memorial stone carrying his name stands near the riding ground. The work was still going during the Second World War, when German naval crews used the ruin as a target for gunboat practice - an indignity that, remarkably, left it only lightly damaged. Today the site belongs to the Danish state, and in 2009 it was absorbed into Mols Bjerge National Park. In 2016 a removable staircase was installed inside the main tower. The name is gentler than the history: Kalø probably comes from kalv, calf, a Danish habit of naming a small island as the offspring of the larger land beside it. Whether peasants once pastured cattle there or someone simply saw a calf lying beside its mother, nobody can now say.

## Flight Context

Located at 56.275N, 10.467E at the head of Kalø Vig, on the south coast of the Djursland peninsula about 20 km northeast of Aarhus. From the air the site is unmistakable: a small green island tied to the mainland by a long straight cobbled causeway, with a single square brick tower stump at its centre and the pale water of the bight on three sides. Aarhus Airport (EKAH) at Tirstrup lies roughly 15 km east-northeast; Aarhus city and its harbour are visible to the southwest. Best viewed at 1,500-2,500 feet AGL, where the causeway, the moat lines and the outer ringwall footprint all read clearly against the grass. Low sun in the morning or late afternoon throws the earthworks into relief; the surrounding Mols Bjerge hills are hummocky glacial terrain and can generate low cloud on humid days.
