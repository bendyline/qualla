# Dronninglund Castle

A Calvinist queen bought a Benedictine convent in 1690, and her title has been attached to the building, the town and the whole district ever since.

**Location:** 57.1553, 10.2617

**Categories:** Castles in Denmark, Historic houses, Former monasteries, Listed buildings and structures in Brønderslev Municipality, Hotels in Denmark

## Introduction

In 1690 a Calvinist queen bought a former Benedictine convent in the north of Jutland, and the place has carried her title ever since. Charlotte Amalie of Hesse-Kassel was queen consort of Denmark and Norway, married to Christian V, and she is the reason the estate stopped being called Hundslund and became Dronninglund — dronning for queen, lund for grove. The queen's grove. She never lived there in any settled way; it was property, one item in the portfolio a consort accumulated. But the name stuck to the house, and the house then gave its name to the town that grew up around it, so that today several thousand people live in a place named after a seventeenth-century German princess's real-estate purchase.

## The Nuns of Hundslund

It began as a religious house. Hundslund Kloster was a Benedictine foundation of the 1200s, and the settlement outside its walls existed to serve it, as villages around monasteries generally did. The Reformation reached Denmark in 1536 and the crown took the property — but the women living there were not turned out at once. The last of them left in 1581, forty-five years later, having spent their lives in a house that legally no longer belonged to their order. In that same year the crown granted the estate to the nobleman Hans Johansen Lindenow.

## What Lindenow Left Standing

Lindenow rebuilt. The corner towers at the western end are probably his, added not long after he took possession, and the arrangement he settled on is the one you see today: three whitewashed wings opening to the north, enclosed by a wall, the main range facing south with the church running east from it. Medieval masonry survives inside those walls, and so does his sixteenth-century work — though not untouched. The building burned in 1777 and again in 1881, so a good deal of what stands now is nineteenth-century reconstruction wearing considerably older bones.

## The Queen Who Would Not Convert

Charlotte Amalie is worth knowing about for her own sake. Born in Kassel in 1650 and married to the future Christian V in 1667, she became queen in 1670 — a Reformed Protestant in a kingdom where Lutheranism was not merely the state religion but a condition of public life. The Danish clergy objected to anointing her, since the ceremony required Lutheran communion and she would not take it. She had anticipated exactly that: her marriage contract secured her right to keep her faith, and she never gave it up. In 1700, when Charles XII of Sweden landed troops on Zealand, she is credited with holding Copenhagen's nerve, talking the city commandant into releasing cannon and keeping the garrison loyal. She died of scarlet fever in 1714. Her name is easier to find on a world map than on a Danish one: Charlotte Amalie, capital of the U.S. Virgin Islands, was named for her in 1691, when the islands were Danish.

## The Hands It Passed Through

After the crown, the estate went the way most Danish manors went — sold, inherited, sold again. Erik Skeel bought it in 1844 and became both a well-regarded landlord in the district and, later, a government minister. In the twentieth century the castle came into the hands of a preservation foundation, the Marie E. and Harald Høgsbro fund, established expressly to keep Dronninglund Slot standing and running it as an independent, self-governing institution. Then in 2017 Nanna Henriette Høgsbro and Carl Christian Pedersen bought the castle back from the foundation, returning it to private family ownership after decades held in trust.

## A House That Has to Earn Its Roof

Dronninglund Slot is neither a ruin nor a museum. For decades under the Høgsbro foundation it operated as a hotel and restaurant, taking bookings for conferences, weddings and the sort of large official occasions that want a whitewashed manor with a park behind it, with a kitchen that ran a catering business on the side. Since the 2017 sale returned it to the Høgsbro-Pedersen family, it has gone back to being a private home rather than a public venue. That is an unromantic pair of fates for a building that has been a convent, crown property and a queen's possession — but each in turn is why the roof stayed sound. A house this old survives by being useful, whether that means letting people in or, eventually, just being lived in again.

## Flight Context

57.1553°N, 10.2617°E, in eastern Vendsyssel between Aalborg and the Kattegat coast. Best viewed from 1,500–2,500 ft: look for a compact whitewashed three-winged complex with a church attached on its east side, set in mature parkland and woodland at the edge of the town of Dronninglund — the trees make it stand out sharply against surrounding open farmland. Aalborg Airport (EKYT) lies about 26 km southwest and is the primary field for the area; Sindal (EKSN) is roughly 40 km north. The terrain is low and rolling with no obstacles of note, and conditions inland here are usually better than on either coast, though winter brings persistent low cloud.
