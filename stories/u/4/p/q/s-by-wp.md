# Sæby

A Kattegat harbour town whose monks gave up in 1536, whose harbour has been silting up ever since, and whose centre survived because nobody ever got rich enough to replace it.

**Location:** 57.3327, 10.5267

**Categories:** Port cities and towns in Denmark, Cities and towns in the North Jutland Region, Historic towns, Vendsyssel, Frederikshavn Municipality

## Introduction

In 1536 the prior of Sæby's Carmelite house did something unusual for a man in his position. He handed the monastery buildings over to the town and explained that the brothers could no longer support themselves. The Reformation was reaching Denmark that year and religious houses everywhere were being seized; Sæby's simply surrendered first. Everything went except the church, and the church stayed because it had never really belonged to the monks — they had built their house by enlarging a parish church the townspeople already had. Five centuries later it still stands on Strandgade, fifty-four metres from end to end, easily the largest structure in a small town that has kept the shape of its old streets.

## Maristed

Before it was Sæby it was Maristed, named for the monastery of Our Lady, and before that it was a landing place and a fishing camp on land belonging to the Bishop of Børglum. The promotion arrived on 10 August 1524, when King Frederik I granted the settlement market-town rights — the same rights Viborg held — and the local bishop confirmed them the following year. Market rights meant the legal right to trade, and for a town sitting on a coast with no natural harbour that was the entire business plan. It worked, but slowly. There were 670 people here in 1672. By 1890 there were about 1,800, and by 1930, 2,525.

## The Sand Never Stopped

The harbour was Sæby's permanent problem. Sand travels relentlessly along this coast, and the entrance filled almost as fast as it could be cleared, so the town spent centuries paying to maintain what other ports were simply given. What it got in return was flatfish. Sæby's boats worked plaice above all, along with cod and saithe, and in the 1897–98 season they landed over 20,900 dozen plaice — a quarter of a million fish out of one small town. Inland, the Sæby watermill, built as royal property in 1640, ground grain until the 1920s. The town's prosperity was never spectacular, and that is precisely why so much of it survived: nobody here ever had enough money to knock the old streets down and start again.

## The Dates Are on the Buildings

Walk Algade and the century of each house is usually written on it. Number 3 is Clasens Hotel, a timber-framed building of 1750 that acquired a neoclassical face in the early 1800s and, later, a clientele of writers — Herman Bang stayed here, and so did Gustav Wied. Number 7, Den gamle Dommergaard, is an 1848 merchant's house that lodged Adda Ravnkilde, born in 1862, dead in 1883 at twenty-one, her writing published only after she was gone. Down at Strandgade 2 the old hospital dates from 1675, with a forecourt from 1565, older than the building it fronts. None of this is a reconstructed heritage quarter. It is an ordinary town centre that happens to be three hundred years old and still in daily use.

## The Greenland Monopoly

One Sæby name travelled a great deal further than the rest. Jacob Severin, born here in 1691, was a merchant who held the Danish trade monopoly on Greenland from 1733 to 1749 — sixteen years in which a man from a silting flatfish port on the Kattegat controlled all licensed commerce with an Arctic colony. He died in 1753, a wealthy man. It should be said plainly that the wealth came from a trade the Greenlandic Inuit, whose hunting and labour produced the goods it moved, had no voice in setting the terms of.

## The Woman from the Sea

At the entrance to the harbour stands a figure six and a quarter metres tall, facing outward. Fruen fra Havet — the Woman from the Sea — is a ship's figurehead scaled up to the height of a small lighthouse, made by the artist Marit Benthe Norheim and installed in 2001. She has become the town's emblem, which is a revealing choice for a place with five centuries of history to draw on. The older answer sits inland at Sæbygaard, a three-winged Renaissance manor with octagonal corner towers and a museum of seventeenth- and eighteenth-century interiors. Sæby's population is 9,012, and since 2007 it has been part of Frederikshavn Municipality rather than the seat of its own.

## Flight Context

57.3327°N, 10.5267°E, on the Kattegat coast of Vendsyssel, roughly 12 km south of Frederikshavn. Best viewed from 1,500–3,000 ft. The town reads as a tight cluster of red roofs immediately behind a small harbour, with the long single tower of Sæby Kirke the tallest thing in it and the coast road running dead straight north–south past the western edge. Frederikshavn's much larger port to the north is the primary navigation reference. Sindal Airport (EKSN) lies about 26 km northwest; Aalborg (EKYT) is roughly 55 km southwest. The Kattegat side is generally clearer than the Skagerrak coast opposite, but haze builds in settled summer weather and the coastal strip can be gusty when the wind swings east off the water.
