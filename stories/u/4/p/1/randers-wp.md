# Randers

Denmark's only natural river harbour, a medieval market town burned three times in one century, and for three hundred years the place Europe came to buy its gloves.

**Location:** 56.4569, 10.0392

**Categories:** Randers, Municipal seats of Denmark, Cities and towns in the Central Denmark Region, Randers Municipality

## Introduction

For most of three centuries, the thing Randers sold to the world was hands. Not the labour of hands - the shape of them, in kidskin, stitched to fit. Glovemaking in this town can be traced back to the 1200s, and by the 1600s it was the chief occupation of the place; by the 1700s Randers gloves were going out to Sweden, Germany, Russia, France and England. A merchant in St Petersburg or Paris could reasonably have known the name of this Jutland market town without having the faintest idea where it was. That is a strange kind of fame for a settlement of a few thousand people at the mouth of a river, and it is only one of several strange things about Randers.

## Town on the Hillside by the River Mouth

The name is older than most Danish place names on record, and it has been spelled with remarkable inconsistency. Coins minted here from the reign of Canute the Holy (1080-86) through that of Svend Grathe (1146-57) carry Ranrosia, Ransias, Radrusia, Rand and Randrusia. Saxo Grammaticus wrote it in Latin as Randrusium; Snorri Sturluson's Heimskringla of 1230 gives the Icelandic Randrosi; Valdemar's Census Book of 1231 records Rondrus and Randrøs. Strip the variants back and you get two elements - rand, a hillside, and aros, a river mouth. Town on the hillside by the river mouth. The modern spelling only settled at the end of the 17th century. The description remains exact: Randers stands on the banks of the Gudenå about ten kilometres above its mouth in Randers Fjord, and it is Denmark's only natural river harbour.

## Burned Three Times, Rebuilt Three Times

The town was formally established around the 12th century, on ground already used in Viking times, and Canute IV minted his coins here while assembling men for an invasion of William the Conqueror's England. The 13th century treated the place badly: destroyed and rebuilt three times, including a burning in 1246 by the troops of Abel of Denmark during the uprising against his brother Eric IV. Market town privileges arrived in 1302 and trade followed. You can still trace the medieval defences without seeing a single stone of them - Østervold, Vestervold, Nørreport and Lille Voldgade curve through the centre in a ring, street names holding the line the walls once held. Some 170 merchants' estates stood here at the peak, and enough half-timbered building survives to make the old core legible: Sankt Mortens Kirke from the 15th century, Påskesønnernes Gård dating to 1460, and a neoclassical town hall finished in 1780.

## One April Morning in 1340

Denmark in 1340 had no king. The whole country had been pledged to German counts, and the most powerful of them, Count Gerhard III of Holstein, was quartered in Randers. On 1 April, according to the story the town has told ever since, the nobleman Niels Ebbesen walked into a house in the town centre and killed him. The killing detonated a wider insurrection against German control. Ebbesen did not live to see how it ended - he died that December in a large battle at Skanderborg. His statue stands in front of the old town hall, which means that the figure presiding over the civic square of Randers is not a king, a bishop or a merchant, but a man remembered for an assassination.

## Gloves, Salmon, Beer and Trains

Salmon fishing fed the medieval economy; the harbour and a competent shipbuilding and craft trade carried the town through prosperous 15th and 16th centuries. Then came the harder years - Swedish wars, plague and repeated fires through the second half of the 17th century - before the economy recovered, the harbour was extended, and by 1800 Randers was Jutland's largest town with 4,500 inhabitants. The gloves industrialised: Randers Handskefabrik was founded in 1811, among the oldest glove factories anywhere in the world, modernised under the Parisian glovemaker Charles Mattat, and by 1892 selling from shops in London and New York. The Vejrum family has owned it since 1927. Randers Handsker still keeps its headquarters and a shop in town, though production has long since moved abroad. Around the gloves grew heavier things: the Thor breweries from 1856, closed in 2003 with the brand now brewed elsewhere; the Hvide Mølle railway works of 1861, renamed Scandia in 1876, which built the IC3 intercity trains for Danish State Railways before Bombardier bought it in 2001.

## Four Thousand Paintings in a Modernist Box

Randers Kunstmuseum was founded in 1887 and now holds over 4,000 paintings, concentrated on Danish art of the 19th and 20th centuries. The Golden Age is well represented - Eckersberg, Christen Købke - and so is the quieter, stranger end of Danish painting, with Vilhelm Hammershøi's Interior with Young Woman Seen from the Back and several works by L.A. Ring, including Sommerdag ved Roskilde Fjord. The collection lives in Kulturhuset, a modernist building by the architect Flemming Lassen that also houses the cultural history museum and the city library. It was very nearly something else: in 2009 the practice 3XN won a competition for new premises of 7,550 square metres, and in September 2012 the project was abandoned after the money failed to materialise. So the paintings stayed where they were. There is something appropriate in a town that made its name on gloves keeping its Hammershøi in a well-built modernist box rather than a signature landmark - Randers has never much traded on spectacle. Today about 64,764 people live here, and the biggest draw is a rainforest under glass.

## Flight Context

56.4569 N, 10.0392 E, in eastern Jutland, 38.5 km north of Aarhus, 43.8 km east of Viborg, 80.2 km south of Aalborg and 224 km northwest of Copenhagen. Approach from the Kattegat up Randers Fjord for the clearest read on the town's geography - the fjord narrows into the Gudenå and delivers you to the harbour and the ring of the old defensive line. Best at 1,500-3,000 ft AGL, where the medieval street pattern, Sankt Mortens Kirke and the three domes of Randers Regnskov all resolve at once. Randers Airport (EKRD) sits about 5 km north near Borup and handles general aviation; Aarhus Airport (EKAH) at Tirstrup lies roughly 40 km southeast and Aalborg (EKYT) about 71 km north-northwest. The E45 motorway runs north-south past the western edge of the city as a reliable line feature. Expect frequent low stratus in winter and long, luminous evenings in June.
