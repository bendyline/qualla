# Randers Tropical Zoo

Three glass domes in Jutland where a computer decides when the sun goes down, because Denmark's daylight would otherwise wreck the internal clocks of a rainforest.

**Location:** 56.4569, 10.0325

**Categories:** Zoos in Denmark, Randers, Indoor zoos, Buildings and structures in the Central Denmark Region, Tourist attractions in the Central Denmark Region, Zoos established in 1996

## Introduction

Somewhere in the plant room under Randers Regnskov, a computer decides when the sun sets. It has to. Randers sits at 56 degrees north, where midsummer days run to around seventeen hours of light and midwinter days barely clear seven - and the animals inside the domes come from latitudes where the difference between the longest and shortest day is measured in minutes, not hours. A tropical rainforest does all of this without thinking. Here, every part of it has to be manufactured: the day length, the humidity, the heat, the light. What visitors walk into feels like weather. It is actually a very carefully maintained argument with the Danish climate.

## Africa, Asia, and the Latecomer

The zoo opened on 13 June 1996 with two domes. The African dome covers 500 square metres and rises twelve metres; the Asian dome is larger at 700 square metres and fourteen metres high. Then, in 2003, came the one that changed the scale of the place. The South America dome encloses 2,000 square metres under a roof twenty-five metres up, with a fifteen-metre waterfall inside it - big enough that the canopy has real vertical structure rather than the potted-palm compromise most indoor tropical houses settle for. Add the three together and the covered area runs to roughly 3,600 square metres. A saltwater aquarium followed in 2005.

## Manufacturing a Sunrise

Climate control is the whole engineering problem, and it is handled by computer for a specific reason: the swing in Danish daylight hours is enormous compared with anything a tropical species has evolved to expect. Get it wrong and you disrupt breeding cycles, feeding behaviour, plant growth, and moulting - the entire biological calendar of everything under the glass. So the domes run on their own imported year. Temperature and humidity are held where the biome demands rather than where Jutland suggests, which in February means walking out of a grey Danish afternoon into air thick enough to fog your glasses in about four seconds.

## Not Much Glass Between You and the Animals

The defining decision at Randers Regnskov is that most of the animals live freely in the domes rather than behind barriers. You are inside the enclosure. Lemurs occupy the African dome; the Asian dome holds pileated gibbons and Komodo dragons; South America has tapirs, manatees, callitrichid monkeys and macaws. There is a snakeyard stocked with free-ranging harmless species, a substantial reptile collection, and the saltwater aquarium with its tropical fish. Among the longer-term residents is Henriette, an albino tiger python measuring four and a half metres - the kind of animal that makes a corridor feel narrower than it did on the way in.

## The First Cats Outside

In 2015 the zoo opened an enclosure for jaguars, notable because it was the first exhibit sited outside the domes rather than within them - a deliberate step beyond the original three-biome concept. Alongside it sits Danmarksparken, or Denmark Park, with farm animals and playgrounds, which functions as the deliberate counterweight to everything else on the site: after the manatees and the macaws, a pig. It is a reminder that this is a Danish institution as much as a tropical one, and that most of its visitors arrive with children who will happily spend as long with a goat as with a Komodo dragon.

## By the Count

The numbers are worth spelling out because they explain why a provincial Jutland city is on anybody's itinerary. Roughly 275 animal species live here - about 200 of them in the domes and another 75 in the saltwater aquarium - alongside some 350 species of plant, which is what makes the rainforest read as a rainforest rather than a warm room with animals in it. The complex is described as the largest artificial rainforest in Northern Europe. In 2012 it recorded 314,643 visitors, comfortably more than four times the population of the city it stands in. Nothing about this location suggests a tropical zoo. That is more or less the point.

## Flight Context

56.4569 N, 10.0325 E, at the western edge of central Randers, close to the Gudenå waterfront. The three glazed domes are the most distinctive roofline in the city and are readily identified from 1,000-2,500 ft AGL, especially in low sun when the glass catches the light. Randers Airport (EKRD) is about 5 km north; Aarhus Airport (EKAH) at Tirstrup lies roughly 41 km southeast, and Aalborg Airport (EKYT) some 72 km north-northwest. Follow Randers Fjord inland from the Kattegat for the simplest visual arrival - the fjord narrows into the river and delivers you straight to the domes. Winter overcast is common; clearest conditions usually follow a cold northwesterly.
