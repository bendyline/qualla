# Vendsyssel

Denmark's northernmost district is an island that nobody treats as one — a raised seabed still rising, with a dune walking across it at eighteen metres a year.

**Location:** 57.2878, 10.0456

**Categories:** Traditional districts of Denmark, Regions of Denmark, North Jutland Region, Geography of Denmark

## Introduction

Ask anyone in Hjørring whether they live on an island and you will get a strange look. Technically they do. The Limfjord cuts clean across Jutland, and everything north of it — Vendsyssel, together with Hanherred and Thy — sits on what geographers call the North Jutlandic Island. Three bridges and a tunnel have made the distinction academic. But the water is real, and so is the older fact underneath it: most of this land was seabed not very long ago, and it is still coming up out of the water.

## Land That Is Still Rising

Eighteen thousand years ago Vendsyssel lay entirely under ice. When the glaciers withdrew, the crust they had been pressing down began to rebound, and it rose faster than the melting ice could raise the sea. In the Stone Age the water stood roughly thirteen metres above its present level, so the cliffs it cut are stranded well inland — old shorelines with no shore, running through farmland. The southern part of the district is a wide plain laid down by that ancient sea, interrupted here and there by isolated moraine hills that were once islands in it. Down the middle runs the Jyske Ås, an end moraine pushed up by the ice. Around 3,000 square kilometres in all, between the Skagerrak on one side and the Kattegat on the other — and the rebound has not stopped.

## Where Two Seas Argue

At the very tip, past Skagen, the land narrows to a spit of sand called Grenen, and this is where the Skagerrak meets the Kattegat. It is not a subtle meeting: two current systems collide head-on, visible from the beach as a seam of broken water running out from the point. The reef beneath it is still building: over the last century Grenen has grown about a kilometre north-east toward Sweden, roughly ten metres a year, so the northernmost point of Denmark keeps moving. People wade out to stand with a foot in each sea. Nobody swims — the currents there can kill, and swimming is prohibited.

## Sand on the Move

Vendsyssel's other great story is what the wind does with all that sand. Råbjerg Mile, on the Skagen headland, is a migrating dune covering some two square kilometres and standing forty metres high — four million cubic metres of sand that formed more than three hundred years ago and has been walking north-east ever since at up to eighteen metres a year, leaving a damp flat behind it as it goes. Historically this was catastrophe rather than spectacle. Drifting sand buried farms and fields across western Vendsyssel, and dunes could travel kilometres inland; prohibitions achieved nothing until an ordinance of 1792 shifted the strategy from banning things to planting marram grass. The most dramatic casualty is still standing, barely. Rubjerg Knude lighthouse was first lit on 27 December 1900 and switched off on 1 August 1968, beaten by sand and by a coast eroding about a metre and a half a year. On 22 October 2019 the 23-metre, 720-tonne tower was rolled 70 metres inland on rails, which should keep it out of the sea until roughly 2060.

## Vandals, Beheadings, and a Frozen Fjord

The name is old and disputed. Adam of Bremen called the place Wendila around 1075, Ælnoth wrote Wendel around 1100, and by about 1231 the Danish census book has Wændlesysæl — a syssel being an early administrative district. Some historians and linguists trace the root to the Germanic tribe of the Vandals; others to a word meaning to turn, which may first have described the winding Limfjord rather than the land beside it. The people here are still vendelboer. What followed is a run of bad centuries. In 1085 Canute the Holy raised an army for England and never sailed, then withdrew to his royal estate at Børglum here in Vendsyssel; the revolt that broke out against him ended with the king cut down at the altar of a church in Odense in 1086. During the Count's Feud, peasants under Skipper Clement burned the bishop's stronghold at Voergaard in 1534, were crushed by the nobility, and lost their leader to the axe at Viborg in 1536 — after which almost every free peasant here lost his land. In the 1640s a Swedish army under Wrangel crossed the frozen Limfjord and met the local peasant levy at Nørresundby; six to seven hundred were killed, and roughly ninety-five per cent of that army did not come home.

## The Way Out Is North

For a place that looks like a dead end on the map, Vendsyssel is unusually busy, because traffic between the European continent and Norway or western Sweden funnels through it. The E39 and E45 cross it as motorways. Ferries run from Hirtshals to Kristiansand, Larvik, Stavanger, Bergen, Oslo and Langesund, and from Frederikshavn to Gothenburg and Oslo, some carrying rail freight wagons. Hourly trains work the line from Skagen through Frederikshavn, Hjørring and Brønderslev to Aalborg. The habit is old: coastal Vendsyssel traded directly with Norway from the Viking Age onward, and the skudehandel of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries thrived partly because it slipped around various taxes. Jacob Sewerin of Sæby held the Greenland trade monopoly from 1733 to 1753 and built a winter harbour for his ships at Hals. The Vendsysselbanen railway opened in 1871, and industry followed it north. The largest towns today are Hjørring, Frederikshavn, Brønderslev, Sæby, Hirtshals, Løkken and, out at the point, Skagen — though the real centre of gravity is Aalborg, sitting mostly on the wrong side of the fjord with Nørresundby as its northern half.

## Flight Context

Centred near 57.288°N, 10.046°E; the district spans roughly 57.0–57.75°N and 9.6–10.7°E, bounded by the Skagerrak to the north-west, the Kattegat to the east, and the Limfjord to the south. Recommended transit altitude 3,000–5,000 ft for the whole shape at once: the wedge tapering to the sand hook at Grenen is unmistakable, with the pale scar of Råbjerg Mile inland of Skagen and the Rubjerg Knude dune field on the west coast between Løkken and Lønstrup. The Jyske Ås ridge runs north-east to south-west through the interior. Aalborg Airport (EKYT) at the southern edge is the main field, shared with Aalborg Air Base — controlled airspace and military traffic. Sindal (EKSN) serves the north of the district, and Skagen lies about 45 nm north-north-east of Aalborg. Expect strong westerlies off the Skagerrak, frequent sea fog on the Kattegat coast in spring, and rapidly shifting visibility where the two seas meet.
