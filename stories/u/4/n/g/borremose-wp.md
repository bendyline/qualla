# Borremose

A Himmerland bog that held both an Iron Age fortress and three people who were put into the water and never came out.

**Location:** 56.7889, 9.5736

**Categories:** Archaeology, History, Nature, Prehistory, Denmark, Wetlands

## Introduction

In three successive postwar years, peat cutters working the Borremose bog turned up a human being. One in 1946, one in 1947, one in 1948 — each about half a metre down, each preserved by the acid and the cold and the absence of oxygen that make a raised bog into an accidental archive. They had lain there for well over two thousand years. And they were not the bog's first surprise: seventeen years earlier, when the same wetland was being drained for farmland, someone had found the levelled remains of one of the largest fortified settlements in Northern Europe. Borremose keeps both stories. It is the rare Danish bog where the fortress and the dead sit within sight of one another.

## The Fortress on the Gravel Bank

The name may say it outright. Borremose translates as 'Borre'-bog, and Borre is plausibly derived from the old word burgh, a fortified place — a memory of function carried in a place-name long after the place stopped functioning. What the 1929 rediscovery exposed was a settlement built in the fourth century BC on a gravel bank measuring 140 by 90 metres, ringed by a moat 450 metres long, four metres wide and a metre and a half deep with a flat bottom, with earth mounds thrown up on the inner side. A cobbled causeway 150 metres long was the only way in from dry land. Inside stood ordinary longhouses — up to about twenty at any one time — but without byres, which is odd for an Iron Age farming community. Then, in the second century BC, the houses were burned and the whole site levelled to the ground.

## Three People, Three Years

The man found in May 1946 had a rope thirty-six centimetres long knotted around his neck with a slipknot when he came out of the peat. He was wearing two sheepskin capes and a woven cap, and examination found injuries to his skull and femur. Radiocarbon places him, and the other two, somewhere between 483 and 95 BC — the Pre-Roman Iron Age, contemporary with the fortress on the gravel bank rather than with the Bronze Age. The body found in 1947, about a kilometre away, was badly preserved; it was first identified as male, though that has since been questioned. With it lay a woman's skirt and a fringed shawl, one garment showing signs of earlier use as a man's cape, and two bones from a newborn infant. The 1948 find was a woman between twenty and thirty-five, lying face down under a large woollen skirt.

## What Can and Cannot Be Said

The rope is the hardest evidence, and it is hard to read as anything but a killing. Beyond that, honesty requires restraint. The Borremose woman's skull was extensively damaged, and forensic examination later found no evidence that the damage happened while she was alive — peat is heavy, and machinery is heavier. The infant bones beside the 1947 body are a fact without a story attached. What the bog preserves best are the ordinary things: a cap someone wove, a cape worn until it was reassigned to a different person, a skirt laid over a body. These were members of a farming community in Himmerland who died violently or were placed in water after dying, and the difference between those two sentences is the whole of the argument. They are not artefacts. They are the reason the artefacts are interesting.

## The Cauldron in the Next Bog Over

Borremose was once far larger than the fragment that survives, and two neighbouring bogs were part of the same wetland. On 28 May 1891, peat cutters in Rævemose, less than a kilometre north near the hamlet of Gundestrup, lifted out a silver vessel that had been taken completely apart and stacked — five long plates, seven short ones, a base plate, and two lengths of tubing. Reassembled, the Gundestrup cauldron stands 42 centimetres high and 69 across, weighs just under nine kilograms, and is gilded, soldered with tin, and set with glass eyes. It is one of the most argued-over objects in European prehistory, dated variously between about 150 BC and the early Roman Iron Age, and it now sits in the National Museum of Denmark in Copenhagen. In 1875 a bronze kettle made by Etruscans around 300 BC came out of the nearby bog of Mosbæk. Whatever people were doing at Borremose, they were doing it with things from very far away.

## Trees, Lakes, and What Is Left

The bog has not been kind to itself since. Industrial turf cutting during and after the Second World War hollowed out large sections, and the pits filled: much of Borremose is open water now. The northern part has grown over so densely with trees and scrub that it is impassable in most places, which is precisely why archaeologists expect it still holds finds. What can be visited is the fortress. After excavation, the outlines of the house sites were marked out with turf walls, the moats emptied and the ramparts rebuilt, so the whole settlement plan is legible on the ground. For a long time it was the only Iron Age fortified settlement known in Scandinavia — until a comparable site turned up at Lyngsmose near Ringkøbing in 1999. The type is now called a Borremose-fortification wherever it is found. Finds from the site are shown at the Museum Centre in nearby Aars, and a few kilometres southeast the Iron Age village of Østerbølle, with nine longhouses, has been restored.

## Flight Context

Borremose lies at 56.7889°N, 9.5736°E in central Himmerland, southeast of the town of Aars. From the air it reads as a mosaic of dark peat, open scrub-covered woodland, and a scatter of rectangular water-filled cutting pits — the signature of industrial turf extraction — set in an otherwise tidy agricultural landscape. The reconstructed fortress shows as a distinct rectangular earthwork with a moat, best picked out at 800–1,500 ft AGL in low sun. Rævemose, source of the Gundestrup cauldron, lies less than a kilometre to the north. Aalborg Airport (EKYT) is roughly 40 km northeast; Karup (EKKA) is about 60 km south-southwest. Bog and low-lying farmland here generate persistent ground fog on still autumn and winter mornings, and the site is often clearest in mid-afternoon.
