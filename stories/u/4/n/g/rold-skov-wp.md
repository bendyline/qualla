# Rold Skov

Denmark's second-largest forest, notorious for centuries as robber country, hiding chalk springs, five thousand species of fungi, and a Cold War government bunker.

**Location:** 56.8000, 9.8167

**Categories:** Nature, Forests, Wildlife, History, Denmark, Hiking

## Introduction

Denmark is, by most measures, a very safe place to walk. Rold Skov spent several centuries being the exception. Travellers crossing this forest in Himmerland reckoned with robbery and violent assault as an ordinary hazard of the route, and the reputation did not fade quickly, because the criminals were real and organised. In the 1830s the authorities finally moved on what may have been the largest gang of all — more than a hundred robbers, whose court cases dragged on for seven years, from 1837 to 1844. One of the convicted men gave Steen Steensen Blicher the raw material for his novella Bettefanden. The forest still trades on the story. Under the reputation lies something stranger: 8,000 hectares of spring-fed hills that hold some of the richest biology in the country.

## Water Coming Out of the Ground

Rold Skov is not flat, which in Denmark is worth saying. The terrain rolls hard enough to drive an unusual concentration of springs: Ravnkilde, Gravlevkilden, Egebækkilden, and Lille Blåkilde, whose name means 'little blue spring' and which pushes out roughly 90 litres a second — a rate that makes the diminutive look like a joke. That water fills a chain of lakes across the woods. Madum Sø is the largest and the one people swim in; Store Økssø and Mossø sit deeper in the trees; Gravlev Sø, drained long ago, has been restored to open water. The forest takes its name from the village of Rold on its southern fringe, and other settlements — Arden, Skørping, Rebild — sit inside or against it. At its northern edge, the heather-covered slopes of Rebild Bakker open out of the trees.

## Orchids on Chalk, Troll Butter on Wood

Much of Rold Skov is planted conifer, and under conifer the forest floor is usually poor in species. The exception comes where chalk surfaces near ground level, and there the plant list turns remarkable: the rare lady's-slipper orchid growing alongside red helleborine, pennywort and wood anemone. Every orchid in Denmark is protected, and picking any of them is illegal. The fungi are the real headline. More than five thousand species have been recorded here and the count is still rising — edible mushrooms, the tinder fungus once used to carry fire, the beautiful and poisonous fly agaric, the deceptive bitter bolete, and the thing Danes call Troldsmør, troll butter, which is not really a fungus at all but the slime mould Fuligo septica, capable of creeping slowly across a log while you are not looking. Red deer and roe deer move through the woods, along with badger, fox, otter, stoat, squirrels and a great many bats.

## The Rooms Under the Forest

East of Rebild, the abandoned limestone workings of Thingbæk Kalkminer run into the hillside. They have a second life as an underground art gallery, and a third as one of the country's more important winter roosts: five species of bat hibernate in the tunnels. Rebildcentret administers the mines along with museums and conservation work in the surrounding area. A short distance away, and far more deeply buried, sits Regan Vest — the bunker built during the Cold War to house the Danish government and monarch in the event of nuclear war. It was kept secret for decades, decommissioned in 2012, and opened to the public as a museum in 2023. A forest chosen for centuries by people who did not want to be found turned out to suit the state for exactly the same reason.

## Who Owns a Forest

Three-quarters of Rold Skov is privately held, mostly under the manors of Lindenborg, Nørlund and Willestrup; the Danish state owns and manages the remainder. That split makes the forest a patchwork of management styles rather than a single unified holding, which is part of why it retains such varied habitat. The whole complex forms Natura 2000 site 18 — Rold Skov, Lindenborg Ådal og Madum Sø — with status as both an EU habitat area and a Ramsar wetland of international importance. South of Skørping, the Jyske Skovhave has been quietly accumulating trees since 1886, an open arboretum of Northern Hemisphere species that reads as a nineteenth-century catalogue you can walk through. At 8,000 hectares — a little under 20,000 acres — Rold Skov is Denmark's second-largest contiguous forest, behind only the woods around Silkeborg.

## Flight Context

Rold Skov lies at 56.80°N, 9.82°E in eastern Himmerland, between Skørping and Arden and roughly 30 km south of Aalborg. From the air it is one of the largest unbroken dark-green masses in Jutland, an emphatic block of woodland in a landscape otherwise given over to fields — the single most useful visual landmark in the region. Look for Madum Sø and Store Økssø as bright openings inside the canopy, and for the pale heather slopes of Rebild Bakker breaking out of the trees on the northern edge. The E45 motorway and the Aalborg–Aarhus railway run along the western side. Good viewing from 1,500–4,000 ft AGL; autumn brings the strongest colour contrast between the conifer stands and the beech. Aalborg Airport (EKYT) is about 33 km north, Aarhus (EKAH) roughly 70 km south. The hilly terrain and lake basins favour valley fog on calm mornings.
