# Mammen

A farmer's spade in 1868 opened a Viking chamber grave here, and the silver-inlaid axe inside gave its name to an entire style of Scandinavian art.

**Location:** 56.4103, 9.6133

**Categories:** Villages in the Central Denmark Region, Towns and settlements in Viborg Municipality, Viking Age, Archaeological sites in Denmark, Viking art

## Introduction

In 1868 a farmer near the village of Mammen started digging into a mound called Bjerringhøj, probably for the soil. What he opened was a wooden burial chamber that had been sealed since the winter of 970-71, holding a man laid out on a down cushion in clothing trimmed with silk and fur, with two axes at his feet. Around the same time a hoard turned up in a gravel pit close by. Between them, these two finds gave their name to an entire phase of Viking art — and the village, which now makes cheese, is the reason art historians have a term called the Mammen style at all.

## What Was in the Chamber

The burial was not a warrior thrown into the ground with his gear. It was a staging. The dead man lay in a timber chamber on a bed of down, dressed in costly clothing decorated with purple and red silk and embroidered in red and blue — textiles so fine that they are among the best-preserved Viking-age garments anyone has recovered. Two large wax candles were placed with him, one set on the coffin lid. A bronze bucket and two wooden buckets stood on the lid as well. At his feet were two axes. The chamber's timbers can be dated by their growth rings to the winter of 970-71, which puts this burial squarely in the reign of Harald Bluetooth, and the man is thought to have belonged to the circle around that king.

## The Axe

One of the two axes is the reason the rest matters. Its iron head is inlaid with silver in patterns of extraordinary density and confidence, work far too fine for anything meant to split a shield. It is a parade weapon, made to be seen and probably carried on ceremonial occasions by a man of near-princely rank. The decoration reads two ways at once. Some see a Christian scheme in it, others a pagan one, and the argument has never been settled — which is itself the point, since this is precisely the generation in which Denmark was changing religion. The wax candles in the grave point toward Christian practice; the rest of the burial does not obviously agree.

## How a Village Names a Style

Archaeologists needed a label for the ornament on these objects, and the axe from Mammen became the type specimen — so the whole phase, dated to around 970, is now called the Mammen style. Its signature is a large bird with a pelleted body, a crest and a round eye, its hip marked by a shell-spiral and its long thin wings looping back to interlace with its own neck and body. Look for the small semi-circular nick cut into the outer edge of the wing; that detail is diagnostic. The tail splits into three tendrils with open, hooked ends. Alongside the bird run interlaced knots, spreading foliate designs with pelleted tendrils, and a triangular human mask with a spiralling beard carried over from earlier Scandinavian work.

## Where the Grave Goods Went

The contents of the chamber and the hoard both went to Copenhagen, and the objects are now held by the National Museum of Denmark, where the textiles in particular have been reconstructed and displayed to show what the man actually looked like. Almost nothing of the find stayed in Jutland. The mound itself is what remains at Mammen — a low rise in the farmland north of Bjerringbro, unremarkable to look at, which is roughly how it looked to the farmer who put a spade into it.

## The Village That Makes Cheese

Modern Mammen sits about 7 kilometres north of Bjerringbro in Viborg Municipality, and if the name is familiar to Danes it is usually not because of Viking metalwork. Mammen is where Mammen cheese is made, a well-known Danish brand, and the dairy is the village's working identity. Two kinds of fame, then, a thousand years apart, attached to one small place in central Jutland — and one of them is on the labels in every Danish supermarket while the other sits in a museum case in Copenhagen.

## Flight Context

56.4103°N, 9.6133°E, in the farmland of central Jutland roughly 7 km north of Bjerringbro and 15 km east-south-east of Viborg. Low and slow is the only way to see anything here: from 1,500-2,500 ft AGL look for the village church among a scatter of farmsteads on gently rolling arable land, with the Gudenå river valley — Denmark's longest river — running east-west to the south past Bjerringbro. The Bjerringhøj mound is a modest swell in a field and will not read from altitude. Nearest airport is Midtjyllands Lufthavn / Karup (EKKA), 40 km west; Aarhus (EKAH) is 45 km south-east. Best visibility in early summer when the fields are green and field boundaries are sharp.
