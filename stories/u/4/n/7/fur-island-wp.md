# Fur

An island of 732 people in the Limfjord that receives a quarter of a million visitors a year, sells its own bedrock by the tonne, and was once voted the loveliest place in Denmark.

**Location:** 56.8317, 9.0242

**Categories:** Islands, Limfjord, Denmark, Coastal, Geology, Small towns

## Introduction

The ferry from Branden takes three or four minutes. You board, the ramp clangs, the Fur Strait slides past, and before you have properly settled into the crossing you are on the other side. Seventy-two departures a day, around the clock, run by the local authority. It is less a voyage than a hyphen. On the far side lies an island of twenty-two square kilometres and about 732 people that somehow absorbs a quarter of a million visitors a year, and whose main export is the ground it stands on.

## Nederby and the Southern Sprawl

Fur sits in the Limfjord just north of the Salling peninsula, in Skive municipality, 8.2 kilometres long and 5.3 wide. Most of the population lives along the south coast, where Nederby, Stenøre, Debel and Søndergårde have grown into each other until the boundaries are administrative rather than visible. Nederby is the largest by a wide margin. The church stands apart from all of it, up on the east side, perched on the edge of an ancient sea cliff that the water abandoned long ago. The municipal school closed in 2014; islanders opened a private one rather than send their children across the strait every morning, which tells you most of what you need to know about how Fur regards the mainland.

## Selling the Bedrock

The north coast is where the island becomes strange. Cliffs of moler rise white and soft above the water, banded with dark layers of volcanic ash, and streaked in places with the iron-red sandstone the islanders call Rødsten. Erosion has carved a set of gullies known as the Langsted Huller. Moler is diatomite, two-thirds the microscopic silica shells of algae and one-third clay, and it is genuinely useful: fired into insulating brick for ovens and chimneys, crushed for cat litter, used in filtering and in explosives manufacture. Two firms quarry it here and on neighbouring Mors. It is an unusual arrangement, a small community with a heavy extractive industry cut into the same cliffs that everybody comes to look at, and the two have coexisted for generations.

## Fifty-Five Million Years, Open to the Public

What comes out of those cliffs is not only building material. The moler is packed with fossils about fifty-five million years old, from insects and fish to birds and plants, preserved with a completeness that has made the western Limfjord internationally famous among palaeontologists. The Fur Museum opened in 1953 to hold and explain them, and it remains the place to start: fossils, moler, and the natural history of the island under one roof. Knudeklint, the headland at the northwest point, gives its name to the lower half of the geological formation itself. In 2026 the diatomite cliffs were inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Fossil hunting is a normal thing to do here on an afternoon, and people find things.

## The Forest That Died and Came Back

Fur was once thick with Scots pine. Farming ate into it steadily, field by field, until in 1627 the last remnant of the original forest died, and for more than two centuries the island stood essentially bare. Replanting began only at the end of the 1800s. Today woodland covers fourteen percent of Fur, concentrated in the north. Something similar happened with the deer. Seven roe deer were brought over from Egeskov Castle on Funen and released in 1965 and 1966; there are now roughly two hundred of them on an island you can walk across in an afternoon. Rabbits, hedgehogs and stoats fill in around them. An island is a small, closed experiment, and Fur has run two of them within living memory.

## Denmark's Loveliest Island

In 2010 a Danish newspaper poll put the question to its readers and Fur came out on top, ahead of Læsø and Ærø, as the country's most wonderful island. The verdict has held up commercially. Around 250,000 people come each year for the cliffs, the fossils, the beaches and the campsite, and for Fur Bryghus, the island brewery that has done as much as anything to put the name in front of Danes who will never take the ferry. That is roughly 340 visitors for every resident. Most of them cross in four minutes, spend a day, and go home again with a piece of moler in their pocket.

## Flight Context

Fur lies at 56.832°N, 9.024°E in the Limfjord, immediately north of the Salling peninsula. Fly at 1,000-2,000 feet: the island is small enough to circle in a few minutes, and the contrast is the point. The south coast is low, green and settled; the north coast is a wall of white moler cliffs with visible dark ash banding and bright open quarry floors set back behind them. Knudeklint marks the northwest point. The narrow Fur Strait separates the island from Branden on the mainland, with the ferry usually visible on it. Nearby airports: Skive (EKSV), Thisted (EKTS), Karup (EKKA) and Aalborg (EKYT). Low sun from the west lights the cliffs best; expect brisk westerly wind and occasional North Sea stratus.
