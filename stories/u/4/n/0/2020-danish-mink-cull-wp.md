# 2020 Danish Mink Cull

Denmark ordered every farmed mink in the country killed — some 15 million animals — and then had to admit that the order had no legal basis, ending an industry, a minister's career, and eventually the ground's ability to hold what had been buried in it.

**Location:** 56.3144, 8.6014

**Categories:** COVID-19 pandemic in Denmark, Political scandals in Denmark, Animal culling, Fur trade, 2020 in Denmark

## Introduction

The order came on a Wednesday. On 4 November 2020, Prime Minister Mette Frederiksen told a press conference that it was necessary to cull all mink in Denmark, and that this applied to the breeding animals as well. Denmark was the largest mink fur producer in the world, supplying some 40 percent of global pelts from roughly 1,100 farms, most of them family businesses in Jutland. Within a day the responsible minister had been told the order might have no legal basis. Within four days the prime minister knew too. By 19 November about 15.5 million animals were dead, and the country was two years from the end of the argument about it.

## Cluster 5

Mink are unusually susceptible to SARS-CoV-2, and they are farmed in long sheds holding thousands of animals in close rows — conditions in which a respiratory virus moves fast and, more worryingly, moves back into the people who work there. Infection was first documented on Danish farms on 15 June 2020, in Vendsyssel, in North Jutland.

What turned an animal-health problem into a national emergency was a variant. Statens Serum Institut identified a strain carrying mutations in the spike protein, designated Cluster 5. On 13 October, SSI chief physician Anders Fomsgaard warned that the mutation could potentially reduce the efficacy of COVID-19 vaccines — and in October 2020 the vaccines did not yet exist. They were the plan the entire world was waiting on. On 2 November, SSI's executive president Kåre Mølbak warned that, in a worst-case scenario, Denmark risked becoming a new Wuhan. The risk assessment delivered the following day concluded that continued mink breeding posed a significant risk to public health.

## The Fourth of November

The industry the government was about to end was worth DKK 2.5 billion in 2019, with exports of roughly €1.1 billion a year. It was also, for well over a thousand families, the thing their parents and grandparents had built. The clause about breeding animals mattered most to them. Officials had weighed a softer option — culling most of the herd but sparing the breeding stock so the industry could be revived after the pandemic — and this was not it. Breeding lines are the accumulated work of decades and cannot simply be repurchased.

The cull ran from 30 October to 19 November 2020 across 1,182 herds. Farmers killed animals they had raised, on holdings with no future once the sheds were empty, often before anyone had explained what compensation would follow.

## No Law Behind It

Danish law allowed culling inside infection zones. It did not allow the state to order the killing of healthy mink outside them, and the Veterinary and Food Agency had raised the problem shortly before the press conference. Minister of Food Mogens Jensen was told on 5 November. Frederiksen was informed on 8 November. On 10 November the authorities went back to breeders to say that culling outside the infection zones was a recommendation rather than an order — by which point most of the animals were already dead.

Jensen resigned on 18 November 2020. Frederiksen apologised repeatedly for the process while maintaining that culling all mink had been justified on health grounds. In August 2022 she said she did not necessarily see reason to call the affair a scandal; six days later, after criticism, she called it a political scandal.

## Trenches in the Training Ground

Fifteen million carcasses had to go somewhere, and the speed of the operation outran the incinerators. Between 5 and 19 November roughly three million mink were buried in trenches in military training areas — at Nørre Felding, just southwest of Holstebro, and at Kølvrå near Karup.

Decomposition produces gas. The gas expanded the carcasses, and they pushed back up through the soil at both sites, which also sat where groundwater contamination was a real concern rather than a theoretical one. The legal scholar Peter Pagh argued there had been no authority for the mass graves either; the ministry disagreed. On 21 December 2020 it was decided to dig them all up again. Exhumation began in May 2021, and the carcasses went to 13 incineration plants around the country. Food minister Rasmus Prehn called it a "troublesome and regrettable" case.

## The Commission and the Bill

The Mink Commission was established on 23 April 2021 and reported on 30 June 2022. It found that what was said at the press conference had been objectively grossly misleading, but that Frederiksen neither knew this nor intended it, and that ministers learned of the legal problem only after the decision was announced. The commission found that ten officials had committed official misconduct serious enough that the authorities could seek to hold them accountable, among them Barbara Bertelsen, head of department at the Prime Minister's Office, and national police commissioner Thorkild Fogde. Most of the resulting sanctions were later withdrawn: Fogde, relieved of duty in August 2022, was cleared in a separate inquiry and returned to office in February 2023, and Bertelsen's warning was withdrawn in March 2023. Separately, on 1 November 2021 it emerged that text messages from Frederiksen and her advisers had been automatically deleted after 30 days; the police could not recreate them.

The money outlasted the politics. A compensation agreement adopted in January 2021 set aside DKK 1.8-2.8 billion for the animals, DKK 8.9 billion for lost income and the residual value of the farms, and DKK 3-4 billion for feed centres, auction houses and hauliers. The projected total of around DKK 20 billion was revised upward to nearly 24 billion in early 2024, with payments possibly running to 2027. When breeding was permitted again from 1 January 2023, 1,223 breeders had applied for shutdown compensation and 14 for the option of restarting. The first mink returned on 14 January 2023, imported from Norway to a farm on Thyholm.

## Flight Context

The coordinates 56.3144°N, 8.6014°E fall on the military training area at Nørre Felding, southwest of Holstebro in west Jutland — one of the two sites where roughly three million culled mink were buried in November 2020 and exhumed again from May 2021. From 2,000-4,000 ft AGL the ground reads as open heath and plantation inside a large restricted military parcel, bounded by farmland and the town of Holstebro to the northeast. The second burial site was at Kølvrå, beside the Karup military area about 30 km southeast. Nearest airfields: Karup (EKKA) roughly 30 km east-southeast, a joint civil-military field and the obvious diversion; Stauning (EKVJ) about 45 km south; Billund (EKBI) about 90 km southeast. Note that both training areas carry active restricted airspace and live-firing activity — check NOTAMs before routing overhead. Terrain is flat to gently rolling, generally below 250 ft; the usual hazards here are low stratus and rain off the North Sea rather than obstacles.
