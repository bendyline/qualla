# Battle of Hohenfriedberg

On 4 June 1745, in thirty minutes that decided a war, the Bayreuth Dragoons charged through a gap in the Prussian line, captured sixty-seven enemy colours, and made Frederick II 'the Great'.

**Location:** 50.9590, 16.2942

**Categories:** Battles of the War of the Austrian Succession, Battles involving the Kingdom of Prussia, Battles involving Austria, Battles involving Saxony, Battles of Frederick the Great

## Introduction

Thirty minutes is all it took the Bayreuth Dragoons to charge sixty-seven enemy colours into history. By nine in the morning on 4 June 1745, near a Silesian village whose German name was Hohenfriedeberg and whose Polish name today is Dobromierz, an Austrian-Saxon army of fifty-nine thousand had been broken in pieces by a Prussian force of fifty-nine thousand under a thirty-three-year-old king who had been waiting all spring to fight. Frederick II of Prussia had marched his army into position by night through difficult country, opened the battle at first light against forces still half-camped, and won what he called in his own letter to Berlin 'a complete victory over the enemy'. Within weeks his contemporaries were calling him Frederick the Great. The Austrians lost roughly five thousand killed and wounded, the Saxons about three thousand four hundred, and several thousand more were captured or deserted in the days that followed. Empress Maria Theresa would still spend the next decade trying to take Silesia back.

## The empress and the king

The First Silesian War had ended in 1742 with the Treaty of Breslau and the loss of most of Silesia, a wealthy Habsburg province, to Frederick's Prussia. Maria Theresa, twenty-five at the time, had inherited a Habsburg crisis and not handed it to anyone; she had also never accepted the loss of Silesia, the richest of the lands she had been forced to cede. By 1744 she was rebuilding alliances. Frederick, suspecting Austria would come for Silesia again, launched his own preemptive invasion of Bohemia in August 1744; it ended badly, with Field Marshal Traun's Austrians refusing to give battle and grinding the Prussians back into Silesia by the year's end. By spring 1745 Austria had brought Saxony into the alliance through the Warsaw treaty. The Austro-Saxon army of fifty-nine thousand crossed the border into Silesia under Prince Charles Alexander of Lorraine in late May. Frederick, encamped near Glatz with forty-two thousand infantry, seventeen thousand cavalry and 192 guns, knew exactly what he intended to do.

## A march in the dark

Prince Charles, on the night of 3 June, did not expect to be attacked. His scouts reported Frederick was withdrawing toward Liegnitz; he had set up a new camp near Strzegom and his troops were resting outdoors, ready to march. Around ten that night a report did reach him that enemy troops were moving toward the Saxon left. He passed the warning on to the Duke of Saxony-Weissenfels, who saw nothing in his own sector and reported as much. Meanwhile Frederick was marching his army toward them in silence, with strict orders to keep quiet, baggage sent back to Schweidnitz. The forward Prussian corps under Lieutenant General Dumoulin took heights north of Striegau and refrained from attacking the Saxon vanguard. By two in the morning the Prussian main force had reached its starting positions. The Austrians and Saxons were still asleep in tents.

## The Bayreuth Dragoons

At dawn the Prussians discovered they had misjudged the Austrian-Saxon position; the camps extended further east than Frederick expected. He hastened his main column across the Striegauer Wasser river into a meeting engagement with Saxon and Austrian cavalry. The cavalry fight on the right was brutal and personal, squadrons locked in hand-to-hand combat with sabres at close range. Leopold of Anhalt-Dessau pulled nine Prussian battalions out of the marching column and led them, building eventually to twenty-one battalions, into the Saxon infantry on the allied left. The Saxons stood for nearly two hours before they began to give ground; their Austrian allies, slower to assemble, never reached them in time. By seven the Saxons were retreating. Frederick now pivoted his uncommitted main body left to attack the Austrian infantry, which had finally formed up. A bridge collapsed and cut off ten squadrons of Prussian cuirassiers from the rest of the army; the cavalry general Hans Joachim von Zieten found a ford with his hussars and crossed in time to relieve them. With the Austrian horse pushed back, only the Austrian infantry remained. They exchanged musketry at lethally close range with the advancing Prussians until the Bayreuth Dragoons, the 5th regiment, found the gap. They charged a formation of Austrian grenadiers who could not form square in time; they overran them; they crashed into the main Austrian line; they broke it. In thirty minutes they captured sixty-seven enemy colours and 2,500 prisoners and lost fewer than a hundred of their own. By nine the battle was effectively over.

## Aftermath

Frederick wrote to Berlin announcing 'a complete victory'. To his old mentor Leopold of Anhalt-Dessau he wrote that the army had surpassed itself. Prince Charles, on the night of his defeat, wrote to Grand Duke Francis that the misfortune was all the more painful in that 'our troops have behaved like idiots. Forgive this expression, but I am furious.' Frederick officially counted 1,200 Prussian killed and wounded, but historians estimate the real cost to his army at as high as 4,700. The Saxons lost some 3,400; the Austrians close to 5,000; another 500 Austrians and 1,500 Saxons, including four generals, were taken prisoner. So many deserters arrived in the Prussian lines over the following days that Frederick estimated the total Austrian losses at 25,000. Maria Theresa, true to herself, kept fighting; the Second Silesian War would drag on until late 1745 and the Third, the Seven Years' War, would erupt eleven years later. But after Hohenfriedberg, Friedrich II was Friedrich der Große, a name that has stuck for nearly three centuries. The dragoons' charge is still studied in war colleges; the regimental march, the Hohenfriedberger Marsch, is still played on parade.

## Flight Context

The battlefield lies at 50.96°N, 16.29°E in Lower Silesia, between the modern Polish towns of Dobromierz (the historical Hohenfriedeberg) and Strzegom (the historical Striegau), in rolling country on the edge of the Sudetes foothills. EPWR (Wrocław–Strachowice) lies about 60 km northeast. From cruising altitude the long ridge of the Karkonosze mountains makes the Czech border to the south unmistakable; the Strzegomka river (the historical Striegauer Wasser) winds across the field northward toward the Bystrzyca.
