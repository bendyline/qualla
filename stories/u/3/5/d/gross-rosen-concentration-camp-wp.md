# Gross-Rosen Concentration Camp

Built around a granite quarry in Lower Silesia, Gross-Rosen processed 125,000 prisoners between 1940 and 1945; about 40,000 died there or on the death marches that ended its existence.

**Location:** 50.9982, 16.2777

**Categories:** Holocaust memorials, Concentration camps, World War II sites, Polish history, Memorial sites

## Introduction

The granite at Gross-Rosen is a hard, gray stone, the kind of stone Albert Speer wanted for the monumental architecture he and Hitler had imagined for postwar Berlin. The camp was built in summer 1940 next to the SS-owned quarry, originally as a satellite of Sachsenhausen. Prisoners arrived from Dachau and Sachsenhausen and were put to work breaking and hauling rock. The work killed quickly. By 1941 Gross-Rosen had become an independent camp; by 1944 it was the hub of a network of more than a hundred sub-camps spread across Lower Silesia, the Sudetenland, and what is now western Poland. About 125,000 prisoners passed through the system. About 40,000 died.

## The Quarry and the System

Gross-Rosen sits in the village now called Rogoznica in the Lower Silesian Voivodeship of Poland; in 1940 it was in German Silesia. The first prisoners arrived in August 1940 from Sachsenhausen. They were used as forced labor in the granite quarry operated by the SS-owned Deutsche Erd- und Steinwerke, the company set up to provide stone for Nazi monumental buildings. The work shifts were twelve hours; the food was a thin soup, a hundred and fifty grams of bread, a pint of ersatz coffee. Prisoners were beaten if they slowed. Those who became too weak to work were sent to Dachau on what the SS called 'invalid transports'; most were killed there. As the war turned against Germany, Gross-Rosen's role shifted from quarrying to munitions and aircraft production, with sub-camps attached to factories across the region.

## Who Was Sent Here

The largest group of inmates was Jewish, deported from across Nazi-occupied Europe: Poland, Hungary, Belgium, France, the Netherlands, Greece, Yugoslavia, Slovakia, Italy. After the failure of the Warsaw Uprising in autumn 1944, the Germans deported 3,000 Polish civilians from the Dulag 121 transit camp in Pruszkow to Gross-Rosen. Most were people in their twenties and thirties. Soviet prisoners of war made up another large group, treated with particular brutality. The camp held political prisoners from across Europe: Czech resistance fighters, French communists, Belgian socialists. Among the named survivors are Simon Wiesenthal, the future Nazi hunter, who described prisoners being used to break in new Wehrmacht boots on twenty-mile forced marches that few survived more than two weeks. The Polish mathematician Wladyslaw Slebodzinski secretly taught fellow prisoners. The Hungarian writer Jozsef Debreczeni survived to write Cold Crematorium, one of the great Holocaust memoirs.

## The Women's Sub-Camps

Gross-Rosen had multiple sub-camps for women, staffed by more than five hundred trained female SS guards. Brunnlitz, in what is now the Czech Republic, held the Jewish workers Oskar Schindler had bribed and maneuvered to keep alive in his enamelware factory; the Brunnlitz prisoners survived the war and became the basis of the Schindler's List story. Other women's sub-camps included Christianstadt, where the Polish historian and countess Karolina Lanckoronska was held; she had been arrested in 1942 for resistance work in Lwow and survived the war to spend the rest of her life teaching and writing in Rome. The Gabersdorf sub-camp was a textile mill where Jewish women worked spinning fabric for German uniforms in machinery that had been seized from a Jewish company in Vienna in 1939. Conditions in the women's sub-camps varied wildly; some had survival rates close to those of the men's main camp, others were essentially slow-killing operations.

## The Death Marches

In early February 1945, with the Red Army approaching, the SS evacuated Gross-Rosen and most of its sub-camps. Prisoners were marched west on foot in subzero temperatures with little food. Many were sent toward Buchenwald and Flossenburg in central Germany; others toward Bergen-Belsen. Thousands died on the roads from exposure, exhaustion, and shootings by guards who killed those who fell behind. The Schlesiersee-to-Volary march, documented by Yad Vashem, took weeks; many of the women on it had been in the women's sub-camps. The Red Army reached the empty main camp at Gross-Rosen on February 14, 1945. Some prisoners on the marches were liberated by Soviet or American forces; others died days before liberation, having walked hundreds of kilometers since leaving the camp.

## The Memorial Today

After the war, the camp site was under Soviet control until April 1947, when Poland took it over. A memorial was unveiled in 1953, designed by Adam Procki. The Gross-Rosen State Museum opened in 1983, after years of work by survivors based in Warsaw and Wroclaw. The site preserves the foundations of the prisoner barracks, the SS villa, the quarry, and the crematorium. In 1948 the Soviet Military Court tried three camp officials, including commandant Johannes Hassebroek; all were convicted of war crimes. Hassebroek was sentenced to death, then commuted to life in prison. He was released in 1954. He gave interviews in his retirement in West Germany expressing no regret. In 2002, ambassadors of the United Kingdom, Canada, and France attended a ceremony at Gross-Rosen commemorating nineteen Special Operations Executive officers murdered there in 1944, decades after their deaths. The visitors today are mostly Polish and German. The memorial does what the Treblinka monument and the Auschwitz museum and the Bergen-Belsen markers all do: it makes the place itself the witness.

## Flight Context

Located at 51.00 degrees N, 16.28 degrees E in Lower Silesia, southwestern Poland, about 60 km southwest of Wroclaw. Wroclaw Airport (EPWR) is the nearest major field. The site sits in gently rolling hills near the Czech border; the granite quarry that gave the camp its purpose is still visible from the air as a gray-white scar in the green countryside. Berlin (EDDB) lies 320 km northwest; Prague (LKPR) 200 km southwest.
