# Gross-Rosen Concentration Camp

Built around a granite quarry in Lower Silesia, the Gross-Rosen camp system imprisoned about 125,000 people between 1940 and 1945; an estimated 40,000 died there or in the evacuations that ended its existence.

**Location:** 50.9982, 16.2777

**Categories:** Holocaust memorials, Concentration camps, World War II sites, Polish history, Memorial sites

## Introduction

The granite at Gross-Rosen is a hard, gray stone, the kind of stone Albert Speer wanted for the monumental architecture he and Hitler had imagined for postwar Berlin. The camp was built in summer 1940 next to the SS-owned quarry, originally as a satellite of Sachsenhausen. Prisoners arrived from Dachau and Sachsenhausen and were put to work breaking and hauling rock. The work killed quickly. By 1941 Gross-Rosen had become an independent camp; by 1944 it was the hub of a network of about a hundred sub-camps spread across Lower Silesia, the Sudetenland, and what is now western Poland. About 125,000 prisoners passed through the system. An estimated 40,000 died.

## The Quarry and the System

Gross-Rosen sits in the village now called Rogoznica in the Lower Silesian Voivodeship of Poland; in 1940 it was in German Silesia. The first prisoners arrived in August 1940 from Sachsenhausen. They were used as forced labor in the granite quarry operated by the SS-owned Deutsche Erd- und Steinwerke, the company set up to provide stone for Nazi monumental buildings. The work shifts were twelve hours; the food was a starvation ration, at one sub-camp a pint of soup, a hundred and fifty grams of bread and black ersatz coffee. Prisoners were beaten if they slowed. Those who became too weak to work were sent away to Dachau on what the SS called 'invalid transports'. As the war turned against Germany, Gross-Rosen's role shifted from quarrying to munitions and aircraft production, with sub-camps attached to factories across the region.

## Who Was Sent Here

The largest group of prisoners was Jewish: as many as 60,000 Jewish men and women arrived from late 1943, most of them from Poland and Hungary, others from western and southern Europe. After the failure of the Warsaw Uprising in autumn 1944, the Germans deported 3,000 Polish civilians from the Dulag 121 transit camp in Pruszkow to Gross-Rosen. Most were people in their twenties and thirties. Soviet prisoners of war made up another large group, treated with particular brutality. The camp also held political prisoners from across Europe. Among the named survivors are Simon Wiesenthal, the future Nazi hunter, who wrote that healthy-looking prisoners were picked to break in new boots for soldiers on daily twenty-mile marches. The Polish mathematician Wladyslaw Slebodzinski secretly taught fellow prisoners. The Hungarian writer Jozsef Debreczeni survived to write Cold Crematorium, one of the great Holocaust memoirs.

## The Women's Sub-Camps

Gross-Rosen had multiple sub-camps for women, staffed by more than five hundred trained female SS guards. Brunnlitz, in what is now the Czech Republic, held the Jewish workers Oskar Schindler had bribed and maneuvered to keep alive after they had worked for him in Kraków; the Brunnlitz prisoners survived the war and became the basis of the Schindler's List story. Other women's sub-camps included Christianstadt. The Gabersdorf sub-camp was a spinning mill, 'Aryanized' in 1939 by a Vienna-based firm, where Jewish women were put to work. By January 1945 nearly 26,000 of the camp system's prisoners were women, most of them Jewish.

## The Death Marches

In early February 1945, with the Red Army approaching, the SS evacuated Gross-Rosen and most of its sub-camps. Prisoners were moved west on foot or in freight trains in subzero temperatures with little food. At least 44,000 were sent to camps further west, among them Bergen-Belsen, Buchenwald, Dachau, Flossenbürg and Mauthausen. Thousands died on the roads from exposure, exhaustion, and shootings by guards who killed those who fell behind. The march of more than a thousand Jewish women from the Schlesiersee sub-camp to Volary, documented by Yad Vashem, lasted 106 days and covered more than 800 kilometres; about 350 survived. The Red Army reached the empty main camp at Gross-Rosen in mid-February 1945. Some prisoners on the marches were liberated by Soviet or American forces; others died days before liberation, having walked hundreds of kilometers since leaving the camp.

## The Memorial Today

After the war, the camp site was under Soviet control until April 1947, when Poland took it over. A memorial was unveiled in 1953, designed by Adam Procki. The Gross-Rosen State Museum opened in 1983, after years of work by survivors based in Warsaw and Wroclaw. The site preserves the foundations of the prisoner barracks, the SS villa, the quarry, and the crematorium. In 1948 a military court tried three camp officials, including commandant Johannes Hassebroek; all were convicted of war crimes. Hassebroek was sentenced to death, then commuted to life in prison. He was released in 1954, was acquitted by a West German court in a later trial, and never admitted any part in the killings. In 2002 a ceremony at Gross-Rosen commemorated nineteen Special Operations Executive officers murdered there in 1944. The memorial does what the Treblinka monument and the Auschwitz museum and the Bergen-Belsen markers all do: it makes the place itself the witness.

## Flight Context

Located at 51.00 degrees N, 16.28 degrees E in Lower Silesia, southwestern Poland, about 55 km west-southwest of Wroclaw. Wroclaw Airport (EPWR), about 45 km east, is the nearest major field. The site sits in gently rolling hills north of the Sudetes; the granite quarry that gave the camp its purpose is still visible from the air as a gray-white scar in the green countryside. Berlin (EDDB) lies about 245 km northwest; Prague (LKPR) about 175 km west-southwest.
