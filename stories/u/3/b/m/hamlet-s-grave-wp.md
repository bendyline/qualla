# Hamlet's Grave

Denmark has three monuments called Hamlet's Grave. One is Neolithic, one is Bronze Age, one is a Victorian hotelier's pile of stones - and none of them has any connection to Hamlet, historical or literary.

**Location:** 55.8182, 11.6279

**Categories:** archaeology, folklore, denmark, zealand, odsherred, hamlet, tourism, disputed tombs

## Introduction

Start with the arithmetic, because it settles the question fast. The mound in Odsherred that carries the name Hamlets Grav is a Neolithic long barrow with standing stones, raised somewhere in the fourth millennium BC. Amleth, the Jutish chieftain of legend, belongs - if he belongs anywhere - to the early medieval world Saxo Grammaticus was writing down around 1200. Shakespeare's prince arrived around 1600. So the grave predates its supposed occupant by something like four thousand years, which is not a margin of error. Denmark has three such graves, in three different places, and the interesting story is not who is buried in them. It is how each one got its name.

## What Saxo Actually Wrote

The literary Hamlet descends from Amlethus, whose story fills part of the third and fourth books of Saxo Grammaticus's Gesta Danorum: the murdered father, the feigned madness, the long patient revenge. Saxo also disposes of him. Amlethus dies in battle against Wiglek, and is buried on a plain in Jutland that afterwards took his name - Insignis eius sepultura ac nomine campus apud Iutiam exstat, in Oliver Elton's translation, "A plain in Jutland is to be found, famous for his name and burial-place." Note the geography. The one early source that says anything at all about a grave points to Jutland, not Zealand, and it points to a plain rather than a specific mound. It also describes a figure for whom there is no independent historical evidence whatsoever.

## Elsinore Sells a Sepulchre

Helsingør had Shakespeare's castle and, from at least the late eighteenth century, a spot north of what is now Højstrup halt that tradition called Hamlet's grave. Tourism arrived in the 1850s, by which time an obelisk stood on an old fortification in the park of Marienlyst Castle. An American magazine writer in 1856 found it thoroughly annexed: the grave marked by a single stone obelisk, "surrounded by tables and chairs, where citizens from Copenhagen consume beer and coffee and defile with their heeltaps the sepulchre of a king." Relic hunters carried off two obelisks in succession. So in 1879 J. S. Nathanson, who rented Marienlyst and ran a luxury hotel there, marked the grave with a large heap of stones and simply restocked it as visitors pocketed them. Contemporaries were scathing about a display staged for "the credulous and sentimental" and about the hotel's aggressive use of the name.

## The Honest Stone

In 1926, for Helsingør's five-hundredth anniversary, a granite stone shaped like a sarcophagus replaced the heap, carved in relief by the sculptor Einar Utzon-Frank - a lion-like creature on one side for Hamlet, a unicorn on the other for Ophelia. This time nobody pretended. Postcards of the period call it Hamlets mindesten, Hamlet's memorial stone, and a bronze plaque added later describes it as a mindegrav, a memorial grave, bearing symbols of virile strength and virginity. That is the correct word for all of this. A memorial is a thing the living make; a grave is a thing the dead are in. Marienlyst spent a century and a half getting from one to the other.

## Randers Takes a Swing

On 27 April 1932, a pharmacist in Randers named O. Løye published a newspaper article claiming he could prove that a burial mound called Kongshøj, in a Jutland pasture named Ammelhede, was the tomb of Hamlet. His argument was largely toponymic: Ammelhede read as Amleds hede, Amleth's heath, which at least matched Saxo's plain in Jutland. The debate that followed was loud and mostly unkind, with scathing criticism from Fyns Venstreblad. On 30 April, Politiken ran a sarcastic piece suggesting the people of Randers were welcome to exploit the theory and raise a stone on the mound to draw tourists away from the "fraudulent" monument in Marienlyst park. The joke landed as an instruction. On 3 September 1933 the newly founded Randers tourist board raised exactly such a stone, with support from the folklore curator Jørgen Olrik and the archivist Hans Ellekilde, inscribed with an alliterating verse: Amled the chieftain, craftiest of old, who played the fool until the hour of vengeance, chosen king at the Jutes' thing, high-seated he rests on Amled's heath. Excavations in 1950 showed the mound is Bronze Age.

## What Is Actually There

So Denmark is furnished with three Hamlet's graves and no Hamlet. That could read as a failure, but it is worth turning around. The Odsherred barrow marked on this map is a genuinely extraordinary thing on its own terms - a long mound edged with standing stones, built by some of the first farming communities in Scandinavia, older than Stonehenge's sarsens and older than the pyramids at Giza, a monument raised by people whose names, unlike Amleth's, were never written down at all. It has never been marketed as Hamlet's grave, unlike its two rivals; the name simply attached itself. Standing at these places, the honest pleasure is not communion with a prince who never lived. It is watching how badly a country can want a story to have a location, and how obligingly the landscape supplies one.

## Flight Context

The Odsherred site given the name Hamlets Grav lies at 55.8182 degrees N, 11.6279 degrees E, in northwest Zealand - not, despite frequent confusion, near Lejre, which is 30 km southeast. Odsherred is the hooked peninsula at Zealand's northwest corner: Sejerø Bay to the west, the Kattegat north, and the unmistakable flat drained bed of the Lammefjord to the south. The barrow is a small feature in farmland, unlikely to be identified from the air; navigate by the town of Vig and the lake Skarresø to the south. The other two Hamlet's graves are far apart - Marienlyst Castle at Helsingør on the Øresund, 56.0423 N 12.6005 E, and the Kongshøj mound at Ammelhede in eastern Jutland, 56.434 N 10.221 E - so a comparative flight covers about 170 km. Nearest airports here: EKRK (Roskilde) about 41 km southeast, EKCH (Copenhagen-Kastrup) about 68 km east-southeast. Recommended viewing altitude 1,000 to 2,000 feet, with low sun to bring out mounds and field boundaries. The Trundholm sun chariot find site is 9 km north-northwest.
