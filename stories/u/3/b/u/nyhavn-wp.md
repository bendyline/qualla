# Nyhavn

Denmark's most photographed row of houses spent three centuries as a working sailors' canal that respectable Copenhagen preferred to walk around.

**Location:** 55.6799, 12.5904

**Categories:** Canals, Copenhagen, Maritime history, Hans Christian Andersen, Historic districts

## Introduction

Strip the cafe tables off the quay and Nyhavn is a ditch. That is what it was built to be - a 450-metre cut of water driven into the side of Copenhagen in the early 1670s so that cargo could be unloaded at the foot of Kongens Nytorv instead of out in the harbour. Fish came up it, and timber, and coal, and sailors, and what grew along the waterfront to meet the sailors was beer and brothels. The row of ochre, oxblood and mustard townhouses that now sells more postcards than any other view in Denmark was, for most of its life, an address you explained away.

## Dug by Prisoners of War

Christian V ordered the canal cut, and the digging was done by Danish soldiers alongside Swedish prisoners taken in the Dano-Swedish War of 1658-1660 - men shovelling out a harbour for the city they had recently besieged. Work ran through the first half of the 1670s. The result was a gateway from the sea to the old inner city, shallow enough for the small craft that carried Denmark's coastal trade and short enough to walk end to end in five minutes. Houses went up along both banks almost immediately; the oldest still standing, No. 9, dates from 1681. The two sides never quite matched. The southern bank drew the money, with substantial mansions running down to Charlottenborg Palace on the Kongens Nytorv corner. The northern bank, which gets the sun, got the taverns. There was no bridge across the water at all until 1874, and that first one was a temporary wooden footbridge, replaced by the present bridge only in 1912.

## Andersen's Three Addresses

Hans Christian Andersen lived on this canal, on and off, for eighteen years - at three separate addresses, each a little further along the quay. He was at No. 20 in 1834, before the fairy tales had made him famous abroad. He spent most of two decades in the middle of the century at No. 67, where a memorial plaque now marks the door. And from 1871 until his death in 1875 he lived at No. 18, a building that today houses an Andersen-themed souvenir shop, which he would probably have enjoyed more than his critics would like. The detail worth holding onto is what he was looking at from those windows: not a heritage harbour, but a working one, loud and dirty and full of men off ships. The writer of "The Little Mermaid" wrote it above a quay where sailors were fleeced.

## The Ships That Stopped Coming

As ocean-going vessels grew, Nyhavn shrank in usefulness, and it settled into a life handling small Danish coastal freight. After the Second World War even that went - road transport took the traffic, small-vessel trade drained out of the Port of Copenhagen, and the canal was left with empty water and a car park on the quay. The rescue came from a residents' and enthusiasts' group, the Nyhavn Society, founded in the mid-1960s to revive the place. In 1977 Copenhagen's Lord Mayor Egon Weidekamp formally inaugurated Nyhavn as a veteran ship and museum harbour, with the south side reserved for restored vessels of the Danish National Museum and the north side for privately owned wooden ships still capable of sailing. The lightvessel Gedser Rev, built in Odense in 1895, worked until 1972 before becoming a museum ship. The schooner Mira, built at Faaborg in 1898, spent years hauling chalk from Stevns. Best of all is MA-RI, purpose-built in 1920 to smuggle, boarded off Elsinore with contraband aboard in 1923, confiscated, auctioned, put to work as a fishing boat and then as a Poland-Bornholm ferry - and then used for smuggling again.

## The Anchor at the Head of the Canal

Where Nyhavn meets Kongens Nytorv sits a single enormous anchor. The Mindeankeret commemorates more than 1,700 Danish officers and sailors of the navy, the merchant fleet and the Allied forces who died in the Second World War - a toll that falls heavily on a small seafaring country whose ships kept sailing after its government had surrendered. A temporary wooden cross went up on the spot in 1945; the anchor replaced it in 1951. The anchor itself is older than the memorial, cast in 1872 and carried by the frigate Fyn, which lay at the Holmen naval base through the war. Every 5 May, Denmark's liberation day, an official ceremony is held here, a few metres from where the tour boats load.

## How the Postcard Was Made

Nyhavn's transformation has a precise date: 1980, when the quay was pedestrianised and the parked cars went. The architects Jan Gehl and Lars Gemzoe later described what the space then became - not a street but a square, a place people go in order to be there rather than to pass through. The sunny north side filled with bars and restaurants, the canal became the hub for the city's boat tours, and the coloured facades became shorthand for Denmark itself. The area kept growing outward. In 2016 the Inner Harbour Bridge opened after years of delay, a 180-metre cycling and walking drawbridge linking Nyhavn to Christianshavn; Copenhageners named it the Kissing Bridge, because when the two halves close the contour looks like a pair of tongues meeting. Kongens Nytorv metro station, served by all four lines, sits at the head of the canal. It is worth remembering, over a very expensive beer on the north quay, that the beer is the one thing about Nyhavn that has never changed.

## Flight Context

Nyhavn lies at 55.680 degrees north, 12.590 degrees east, cutting west to east into the Inner Harbour just south of the Royal Playhouse. From 1,500-3,000 ft it is unmistakable: a short straight slot of water at right angles to the harbour channel, with the open expanse of Kongens Nytorv at its landward end and the Opera House directly across the water. Use the harbour itself as the primary line feature - Nyhavn is the only canal on that stretch of the western bank. Copenhagen Kastrup (EKCH) is 7 km southeast on Amager and its approach and departure paths cross the harbour, so the city centre sits inside busy controlled airspace; Roskilde (EKRK) lies about 30 km west. Best light is late afternoon, when the low sun hits the northern row of facades and the colours read clearly from the air. Summer haze over the Oresund can soften contrast; winter afternoons give the sharpest definition.
