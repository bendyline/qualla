# Kastellet, Copenhagen

A five-pointed star fort north of Copenhagen's harbour that has been a garrison since 1626 and a public park for a century - complete with a working windmill, a church wired for prisoners, and an army still on the payroll.

**Location:** 55.6916, 12.5936

## Introduction

Someone cut holes through the wall between the church and the prison cells. They were not decorative. On the far side sat men who were not permitted to attend the service, and the openings let them hear it anyway - a mercy engineered into masonry, or a cruelty, depending which side of the wall you stood on. That wall is still there, inside the Citadel Church at Kastellet, the most human detail in a fortress otherwise built for arithmetic: five bastions, two gates, a moat, clean lines of fire. Kastellet is among the best-preserved fortresses in Northern Europe. It is also, on any decent afternoon, full of joggers.

## A King's Unfinished Idea

Christian IV began it in 1626, though not as a citadel. What he built was an advanced post on the coast north of the city, St. Anne's Redoubt, paired with a blockhouse across the water north of Christianshavn to guard the harbour entrance. At that point Copenhagen's defences reached no further north than present-day Norreport station. His larger plan was to abandon the old East Rampart and run the wall straight north to the redoubt, enlarging the fortified city in one stroke. He did not live to see it. The work was finished in the mid-1640s, shortly after Frederick III succeeded him. Then the Swedes besieged Copenhagen from 1658 to 1660, the defences were found wanting, and the Dutch engineer Henrik Ruse was called in to rebuild and extend what stood. His fortification was formally named Citadellet Frederikshavn. Almost nobody has ever called it that. It has always been Kastellet - the Citadel.

## Five Bastions, Two Gates

The plan is a pentagon with a bastion at each corner, and the Danes named them for the court: the King's, the Queen's, the Count's, the Princess's and the Prince's. Both gates date from 1663 and belong to Ruse's original scheme. King's Gate faces the city, dressed in Dutch Baroque with garlands, pilasters and a bust of Frederik III; the clock and two bells on its inner face were salvaged from the Central Guard House at Kongens Nytorv and hung here in 1874, when the guard itself moved in. Two caponiers still stand before it, low works from which defenders could rake attacking troops with fire. Norway Gate, on the north side, once looked out on open countryside and was built plainly to match its audience. Beyond the inner moat runs the Blacksmith's Line, four ravelins and three counter guards strung together by long earthworks.

## Four by Four Metres

Soldiers lived in the Rows - six two-storey terraces Ruse raised as barracks. A dorm measured four metres by four and held two triple beds, a small table and two benches - the entire domestic world of the men inside it. Over time each terrace acquired a name: General Stock, where commanders slept until the Commander's House was built, then Artillery, Star, Elephant, Swan and Fortuna. The mansard roofs came with an alteration in 1768. Two storehouses from the founding held everything a siege would demand - the Southern Magasin as arsenal, the Northern as granary - and full, they could feed the garrison's 1,800 men and their families for four years. The Commander's House went up in 1725 in Baroque yellow masonry, designed by Elias Hauser, who also built the first Christiansborg Palace. It served as the official residence of Denmark's Chief of Defence until 2008.

## Powder, and the Man in the Cage

Domenico Pelli built two identical powder houses in 1712, one at the Count's Bastion and one at the Queen's. Only the second survives. Its walls are massive and its ceiling slightly vaulted, an arrangement meant to send any explosion upward rather than outward. The logic was sound: when a powder house on the East Rampart went up in 1779, it damaged buildings through Nyboder and as far as Bredgade, and storing explosives in the ramparts was judged indefensible. The powder houses here became a jailhouse instead. Prisoners had been held at Kastellet since 1725, in a complex behind the church. Johann Friedrich Struensee waited in it for his execution. The longest-serving inmate was an English explorer and pirate named John Norcross, who spent thirty-two years here - sixteen of them confined in a wooden cage. That is a very long time to be kept in a box a few metres from a church he could hear but not enter.

## Flour for an Empress, Herons on the Rampart

On the King's Bastion stands a windmill, built in 1847 to replace a post mill of 1718 that a storm had destroyed the year before. A besieged city needs flour, so mills once crowded the ramparts - sixteen of them ringed Copenhagen in 1800. This is the last one still working. Its most distinguished customer was Maria Feodorovna, daughter of Christian IX and Empress Consort of Russia; the Army's Bread Factory shipped her rye flour to Saint Petersburg so she could be served ollebrod each morning at the Anichkov Palace. The painter Christen Kobke grew up inside these walls and painted them again and again. In April 1940 German troops landed at the neighbouring harbour and took the Citadel without a shot. Restored between 1989 and 1999, it is still Ministry of Defence property - the Home Guard, the Defence Intelligence Service and the Royal Garrison Library all work here. Everyone else comes for the grass. Herons and mute swans work the moat, the guard changes at noon, and on 28 October the Citadel marks its birthday with a concert and the wings of the windmill.

## Flight Context

Kastellet sits at 55.6916 N, 12.5936 E, on the harbour's northern edge beside Langelinie, the Little Mermaid and the Gefion Fountain. From 1,500-3,000 ft AGL the five-pointed star of grass ramparts and water is unmistakable against the dense city grid - one of the clearest geometric shapes on the Danish coastline, best read in low morning or evening light when the earthworks throw shadow. Copenhagen Kastrup (EKCH) lies about 8 km southeast across the Oresund shoreline; Roskilde (EKRK) is roughly 30 km west. Note that Kastellet is inside Copenhagen's controlled airspace and directly under approach paths - this is a landmark for the airline window seat, not for low-level flying. Coastal haze and Baltic low cloud are common; the fort reads best on crisp northerly days.
