# The Copenhagen Hypnosis Murders

Two bank employees were shot dead on Nørrebrogade in 1951, and the case that followed convicted a second man of committing the murders through another man's mind.

**Location:** 55.6900, 12.5556

**Categories:** crime, legal history, psychology, Copenhagen, Denmark, 1951

## Introduction

On the morning of 29 March 1951, a man walked into the Landmandsbanken branch on Nørrebrogade in Copenhagen with a pistol. He shot one of the cashiers, then shot the branch manager. Both men died. He took no money. He fled on a bicycle to a nearby street, went into a building, and hid in a stairwell, where several people who had followed him from the bank directed the police. He surrendered without resistance. His name was Palle Hardrup, he was twenty-eight, and within days he would begin telling investigators that he had not decided to do any of it.

## The Two Men Who Died

The record of this case is unusually thin on the point that should matter most. The Danish and English accounts alike identify the victims only by their jobs: a cashier and the branch manager of an ordinary neighbourhood bank on a busy Copenhagen shopping street. They were at work on a Thursday morning. Their names do not appear in the standard summaries, which is its own kind of injustice — seventy-five years of books, appeals, expert testimony and a feature film have gone to the question of what was happening inside the mind of the man who killed them, and almost none to the two men themselves. Whatever else this story is, it begins with two people who went to work and did not come home.

## The Bicycle

The bicycle Hardrup had used to escape belonged to someone else: Bjørn Schouw Nielsen. A few days after the robbery Nielsen went to the police of his own accord, explaining that he wanted to make sure he was not suspected merely because his bicycle had been involved. It was the single worst decision he could have made.

Investigators quickly established that the two men knew each other well. Both had served sentences for treason after the German occupation — Hardrup in the Schalburg Corps and the HIPO auxiliary police, Nielsen in Frikorps Danmark and the SS — and both had been held at Horsens State Prison, where for a time they shared a cell. In prison they had developed a shared enthusiasm for Asian philosophy, yoga, and hypnosis.

## Forty Statements

The theory that Nielsen had used hypnosis to direct Hardrup did not originate with Hardrup's defence. It came out of the investigation, and it was built on roughly forty statements from prisoners and guards at Horsens. Those witnesses described a relationship that had unsettled them at the time: Nielsen manipulating Hardrup like a puppet, able to make him behave as though in a trance. Hardrup had told several inmates outright that Nielsen hypnotised him regularly.

The case was led by the criminal investigator Roland Olsen, working with Max Schmidt, chief psychiatrist to the police department, and Dr Paul Reiter. Reiter had spent years researching whether hypnosis could produce criminal behaviour and believed it could. He and Schmidt used suggestion and hypnosis on Hardrup himself, and Reiter interviewed the family at length, building the psychological portrait that became the prosecution's evidence.

## The Argument the Court Accepted

Reiter's position, put to the court, was narrower than the popular version of it. He did not claim that a hypnotist can make anyone do anything. He argued that a person can be brought to do what they would not ordinarily do provided the act appears, to that person, morally justified — and that Nielsen had spent the prison years reshaping Hardrup's moral world until murder felt not merely permissible but inevitable, with repeated hypnotic suggestion reinforcing the work.

The proceedings ran until 1955. Hardrup was committed indefinitely to the institution at Herstedvester; Nielsen was sentenced to life imprisonment, and the Supreme Court upheld it and refused to reopen the case. The Danish Medico-Legal Council said at the time that it could neither confirm nor dispute Reiter's theory, but in December 1956, after working through the full file, it concluded that Hardrup's mental state had been significantly disordered by the influence of another person, and that this influence had involved hypnosis. A subcommittee in Strasbourg found in 1960 that Denmark had convicted Nielsen properly, and the European Court of Human Rights upheld that in 1961.

## What Was Never Settled

The verdict has never fully closed the question. A later Danish appellate review did not find it fully proven that Hardrup had been under hypnotic influence when he fired, and the Medico-Legal Council's first position — that it could neither confirm nor dispute Reiter's theory — is arguably the most honest thing said about the case. Reiter was not a neutral observer. He was both an investigator in the proceedings and a scientist who arrived already convinced that hypnotically induced crime was possible, examining the man who would prove it.

Perhaps Nielsen did exercise extraordinary control over a damaged and suggestible man. Perhaps a court reached for an explanation that was scientifically fashionable and narratively satisfying. What Denmark convicted Nielsen of, in the end, was something he had allegedly done inside another person's head.

## Afterward

Both men were released in 1967. Bjørn Schouw Nielsen killed himself with potassium cyanide in his flat on Amager on 26 May 1974, aged fifty-nine. Palle Hardrup changed his surname to Wischmann, lived quietly, kept his distance from his past, and died on 6 March 2012.

The Finnish director Arto Halonen met Hardrup in 1997 and was one of very few people ever to interview him. His film about the case — released as The Guardian Angel, and as Murderous Trance in some markets — premiered in 2018, shot in Denmark and Croatia. Pilou Asbæk played the investigator, renamed Anders Olsen for the film, opposite Josh Lucas as Nielsen and Cyron Melville as Hardrup.

## Flight Context

The site lies at 55.6900 degrees north, 12.5556 degrees east, on Nørrebrogade in the Nørrebro district of Copenhagen, about 1.5 km northwest of the city centre. There is nothing to see from the air — this is an ordinary commercial street in a dense nineteenth-century quarter, running northwest from Dronning Louises Bro across the Peblinge Lake, From 1,500 feet the lakes are the landmark: a chain of three curved bodies of water separating central Copenhagen from Nørrebro and Østerbro.
