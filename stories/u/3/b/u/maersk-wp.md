# Maersk

A Danish shipping house that began with a seven-pointed star painted on a funnel in 1886 and now moves the world's cargo from a Copenhagen headquarters - second in a trade it once led outright.

**Location:** 55.6874, 12.5981

**Categories:** Maersk, Shipping companies of Denmark, Logistics companies of Denmark, Shipping companies based in Copenhagen, Transport companies based in Copenhagen, Danish brands

## Introduction

On 6 October 1886, a Danish sea captain wrote his wife a letter about a star. Anna Moller had been travelling with her husband when she fell seriously ill, and Peter Maersk Moller - a devout man - had prayed for a sign. "The little star on the funnel is a reminder of the evening I prayed for you so dejectedly and anxiously," he wrote, "asking for the sign that I might see a star in the grey, overcast sky, a reminder that the Lord hears our prayers." She recovered. The white seven-pointed star he had painted on the funnel of his steamer SS Laura is now on the side of ships in every major port on Earth.

## Svendborg, April 1904

The company proper began in the small Funen port of Svendborg, when Peter Maersk Moller (1836-1927) and his son Arnold Peter Moller (1876-1965) founded Dampskibsselskabet Svendborg. The star came with them and became the house flag; when the Steamship Company of 1912 was established, it took the same emblem. The mark has barely changed in more than a century - the last real revision came in the early 1970s, when the Danish designer and architect Acton Bjorn was brought in to modernise it. What did change was scale. A.P. Moller - Maersk now runs port terminals, supply chains, warehousing and air freight through subsidiaries in some 130 countries, employed 108,160 people in 2024, and posted revenue of about US$55.5 billion that year.

## Ships Built to Break Records

In 2006 the Odense Steel Shipyard delivered Emma Maersk, at the time the largest container ship in the world; seven sister ships followed. Then in February 2011 Maersk ordered ten larger vessels still from Daewoo - the Triple E class, each rated at 18,000 containers, the first arriving in 2013. A second batch of ten followed that June. An option on a third batch was cancelled, which turned out to matter, because the era of building ever-bigger boxes to win a capacity race was already reaching its limits. The Triple E name was a statement of intent as much as engineering: economy of scale, energy efficiency, environmental improvement. At 399 metres long and nearly 59 wide, they are too broad for the Panama Canal, and when the first were ordered no American port could handle them at all.

## The Number Two Problem

For years the shorthand was simple - Maersk was the world's largest container line. That has not been true for some time. Alphaliner's April 2026 figures put Mediterranean Shipping Company first with roughly 7.3 million TEU across about 1,000 vessels and a 21.6 percent share of global capacity. Maersk sits second: around 4.65 million TEU across 735 ships, 13.7 percent of the market, with CMA CGM close behind in third at 12.6 percent. Rather than chase tonnage, Maersk has been reorganising around alliances and integrated logistics. In January 2024 it announced the Gemini Cooperation with Hapag-Lloyd, launched in February 2025 - roughly 340 ships and 3.7 million TEU of combined capacity, sixty percent of it Maersk's.

## Ten Days in the Dark

On 27 June 2017, Maersk's IT systems were hit by NotPetya. The malware was dressed up as ransomware, but it was not designed to make money; it was a Russian GRU cyberweapon aimed at Ukraine that escaped and kept going. Wired later called it the most devastating cyberattack in history. It nearly destroyed the shipping business. Terminals stopped, bookings vanished, and the company was forced to rebuild its entire IT infrastructure from scratch in ten days - a feat that cost it more than US$300 million in losses. There is an uncomfortable coda: in March 2020, Maersk announced it would terminate the employment of the UK-based IT team that had led that recovery, and outsource the work.

## Methanol, Emissions and Protest

Maersk decided in 2016 to separate its oil and gas businesses and concentrate on container logistics - Maersk Oil went to Total in 2018, Maersk Drilling was listed separately in 2019. The company has committed to being carbon neutral by 2040, and in September 2023 unveiled Laura Maersk, a 2,100-TEU Baltic feeder running on methanol; a November 2023 agreement with the Chinese developer Goldwind targets 500,000 tonnes a year of bio- and e-methanol produced with wind energy. The scale of the problem is large: A.P. Moller - Maersk alone accounts for more than 80 percent of the operational carbon dioxide emitted by Denmark's thirty biggest companies, some 37 million tonnes in 2021. Its cargo has also drawn political pressure. Since 2024 the Palestinian Youth Movement and allied groups have campaigned against its transport of military equipment to Israel; protesters occupied the Copenhagen headquarters in February 2025, dockworkers in Tangier refused to load F-35 parts that April, and in June 2025 Maersk said it had aligned its screening with the OHCHR database of enterprises operating in Israeli settlements. It became the first logistics company to divest from transporting settlement goods while continuing, as of July 2025, to carry military cargo for the Israeli Ministry of Defense.

## Flight Context

A.P. Moller - Maersk's headquarters stands at 55.6874 N, 12.5981 E on Esplanaden, immediately southwest of Kastellet and a short walk from the Copenhagen waterfront. From 2,000-4,000 ft AGL the more instructive sight is not the office but the traffic: the Oresund below is one of the busiest shipping approaches in northern Europe, with container and feeder vessels queueing for the Baltic. For working terminals, look south to Copenhagen Malmo Port and across the strait to the Swedish shore. Copenhagen Kastrup (EKCH) is roughly 8 km southeast and its approaches cross the strait directly; Roskilde (EKRK) lies about 30 km west. Best light is a clear morning, when ship wakes read sharply against the water.
