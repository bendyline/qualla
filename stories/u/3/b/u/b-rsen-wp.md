# Børsen

Christian IV's 17th-century bourse, whose spire of four twisted dragon tails was meant to guard it against fire — until 16 April 2024, when the roof burned and the spire came down.

**Location:** 55.6755, 12.5841

**Categories:** Renaissance architecture in Copenhagen, Stock exchange buildings, Buildings and structures in Copenhagen, Slotsholmen, Financial history of Denmark, Burned buildings and structures in Europe

## Introduction

The dragons were supposed to prevent exactly this. Four of them, cast head-down with their tails braided together into a spire fifty-six metres high, put there in 1625 by Christian IV's fireworks master to guard the building beneath from enemies and from fire, with three crowns at the summit for Denmark, Norway and Sweden. For nearly four hundred years the Dragespir was the most recognisable silhouette on Slotsholmen. On the morning of 16 April 2024, with the copper roof under renovation and scaffolding wrapped around the whole length of the building, the roof caught. Copenhageners came out into the streets to watch the spire lean and fall. By the time the fire was out, roughly half of Børsen was gone.

## A king who wanted a trading floor

Christian IV built Børsen to make Copenhagen matter commercially, not just politically. He chose a site on the north side of the embankment that carried traffic between the old city and Christianshavn, his new merchant town rising on reclaimed ground off Amager, and he had to wait for the made land to settle before anyone could dig. The design went to Lorenz van Steenwinckel, who died soon after taking it on; his brother Hans van Steenwinckel the Younger carried it through. Building started in 1620 and the shell was substantially finished by 1624, with the spire raised in 1625 and the last details of the east gable not completed until 1640. Inside were forty trading offices along the ground floor and one long hall above them. It is the outstanding piece of Dutch Renaissance architecture in Denmark — stepped gables, red brick, a long low mass lying along the water like a docked ship. It has long been listed for conservation.

## Sold, resold, and outlived by its own function

The building changed hands more often than its permanence suggests, and even the famous spire is not quite the original: by 1775 the first one was judged at risk of collapse and a replacement was raised in its place. In 1647 Christian IV sold it to the merchant Jacob Madsen for 50,000 rigsdaler; Frederick III later bought it back from Madsen's widow, who could no longer afford the upkeep. The interior was overhauled in 1855 under the architect Harald Conrad Stilling, and in 1857 Frederick VII sold Børsen to Grosserer-Societetet, the wholesalers' guild. Denmark's stock market traded here until 1974, after which the exchange moved on and the house stayed. By 2024 it belonged to Dansk Erhverv, the Danish Chamber of Commerce, and had become that rare thing: a monument still being used as an office, its great hall hung with the portraits of four centuries of merchants.

## Sixteenth of April

The fire began in the roof during renovation work — the copper was being replaced ahead of the building's four hundredth anniversary. Everything about the structure worked against the firefighters. The scaffolding caught and burned, blocking access to the walls. The copper sheeting held the heat in and kept water out, so crews had to bring in machinery to tear the roof off before they could reach the flames; soldiers were brought in to help. Beneath the copper, Børsen is very largely timber. Nobody was killed. On 18 April, two days after the fire started, part of the north façade and part of the west gable collapsed. In November 2024 the police concluded that no criminal act had caused the fire and that the exact cause could not be established.

## What people carried out

The most extraordinary hour of the fire happened at ground level. Employees, emergency workers and passers-by went into a burning building and brought out several hundred historic artworks and pieces of furniture, handing them along the pavement. The National Museum sent twenty-five staff to help. Among the rescued was P. S. Krøyer's vast 1895 group portrait From Copenhagen Stock Exchange, one of the defining Danish paintings of its century, and Thomas Kluge's modern counterpart showing the chamber's own committee. Not everything came out: a two-tonne bust of Christian IV, the king who built the place, was lost. The salvaged collection was taken to the parliament building next door and to the Danish National Archives. Troels Lund Poulsen, then deputy prime minister, called it "our own Notre Dame moment" — a comparison that came easily, since the Paris fire had happened five years and one day earlier, also during a roof renovation.

## Eight hundred thousand bricks

Denmark decided almost immediately to rebuild, and to rebuild the way the thing was made. King Frederik X laid a foundation stone on 26 September 2024, five months after the fire. The reconstruction calls for more than 800,000 handmade bricks fired in Germany and Poland, close to 900 pine trees felled in Denmark and Sweden for the timber frame, and recycled Finnish copper for the roof and the spire. Completion is targeted for 2029. The one thing craftsmanship cannot supply is age: new copper is the colour of a coin, and it may take something like seventy years of Baltic weather to bring the new roof to the green that everyone in Copenhagen pictures when they think of this building. The dragons will be back long before the colour is. For now the visitor sees scaffolding, a wounded gable, and the flat sky where the spire used to interrupt it.

## Flight Context

Børsen stands at 55.6755°N, 12.5841°E on Slotsholmen, the small island at the heart of Copenhagen that also carries Christiansborg and the Danish parliament. From 1,500–3,000 ft the island is easy to pick out — a compact block ringed by canals where the inner harbour narrows — but the landmark itself is currently altered: the long red-brick roofline is under reconstruction and the 56 m dragon spire is absent from the skyline until the rebuild completes, targeted for 2029. Use Christiansborg's square tower immediately to the west and the black glass of the Royal Library (the Black Diamond) on the harbour front to the south as your fixes instead. EKCH (Copenhagen Kastrup) lies 8 km south-southeast on Amager; EKRK (Roskilde) is 30 km west. This is central controlled airspace over the seat of government — observe from published routes only. Winter haze off the Sound frequently reduces slant visibility over Slotsholmen to a few kilometres.
