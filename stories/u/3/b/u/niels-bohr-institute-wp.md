# Niels Bohr Institute

The Copenhagen institute where quantum mechanics was argued into its modern shape, where hafnium was discovered, and from which its founder escaped occupied Denmark in the bomb bay of a Mosquito.

**Location:** 55.6969, 12.5717

**Categories:** University of Copenhagen, Research institutes in Denmark, Physics research institutes, Niels Bohr, Theoretical physics institutes

## Introduction

In September 1941 two physicists stepped outside to talk where nobody could hear them. One was Niels Bohr, in a Copenhagen under German occupation. The other was Werner Heisenberg, his former student and collaborator, now leading Germany's nuclear energy project. Whatever passed between them on that walk has never been settled. Heisenberg said afterwards that he had tried to raise the moral weight of atomic weapons; Bohr came away believing he had been sounded out about cooperating with the Nazi programme, and was appalled. The conversation ended abruptly. What is not disputed is where the two men had learned to argue like that - in the building behind them, which for twenty years had been the loudest room in physics.

## A Building for Arguing In

Bohr joined the University of Copenhagen's staff in 1914 and, from his appointment as professor in 1916, began pressing for an institute of his own. He campaigned from April 1917; legislation passed in November 1918; the Institute for Theoretical Physics opened its doors on 3 March 1921 with Bohr as director. Much of the money came from the charitable foundation of the Carlsberg brewery, with later support from the Rockefeller Foundation, industry and private donors. In 1922 Bohr received the Nobel Prize in Physics for his work on atomic structure and atomic radiation. He had a building, a prize and a method, and the method mattered most: bring in the best young theorists available, put them in a room, and let them fight.

## Copenhagen Becomes a Noun

Through the 1920s and 1930s the institute was the centre of atomic and quantum physics, and physicists crossed Europe to test their ideas against Bohr's objections. Hans Kramers came from the Netherlands, Oskar Klein from Sweden, George de Hevesy from Hungary, Wojciech Rubinowicz from Poland, Svein Rosseland from Norway. Paul Dirac spent six months here from September 1926; Erwin Schrodinger visited the same year; Heisenberg worked at the institute from 1926 to 1927, and it was in February 1927 that he formulated the uncertainty principle - which Bohr promptly disagreed with, insisting the limits followed from classical arguments about the resolving power of instruments. Out of that friction came complementarity, Bohr's principle that a thing may possess apparently contradictory properties, wave or particle, depending on how you interrogate it. The package became known as the Copenhagen interpretation, named for the work done in this building. Einstein spent decades arguing against it, and Bohr spent decades answering him.

## The Element Named for the City

Not everything here was philosophy. Bohr predicted that element 72 would not be a rare earth, as the French chemist Georges Urbain claimed with his supposed "celtium," but chemically close to zirconium. If he was right, it should be hiding in zirconium ores. Dirk Coster and George de Hevesy went looking through the Museum of Mineralogy's collections and found it. They named it hafnium, after the Latin for Copenhagen. It proved to be more common in the Earth's crust than gold.

## A Door That Stayed Open

When the Nazis came to power in 1933, Bohr turned his institute into a way station. He met the Rockefeller Foundation's president Max Mason in May of that year to arrange support, then offered dismissed and endangered scientists temporary positions, money, fellowships and onward placements abroad. The list of people who passed through on that arrangement is a roll call of twentieth-century physics: James Franck, Lise Meitner, Otto Frisch, Felix Bloch, Victor Weisskopf, George Placzek, Edward Teller, Guido Beck, Hilde Levi, Stefan Rozental and others. They were refugees before they were names. When Germany occupied Denmark in April 1940, the foreign scholars left.

## The Bomb Bay

Bohr's own turn came in September 1943, when he learned the occupation authorities regarded his family as Jewish - his mother was - and that arrest was imminent. The Danish resistance moved him and his wife across to Sweden by sea on 29 September. He used the moment: the next day he pressed King Gustaf V to declare publicly that Sweden would take Jewish refugees, and on 2 October Swedish radio broadcast the offer. More than 7,000 Danish Jews eventually reached safety. On 6 October Bohr flew to Scotland lying in the bomb bay of an unarmed BOAC Mosquito. The flying helmet was too small for his head, so he never heard the pilot's instruction to switch on his oxygen as they climbed over Norway, and he passed out. He came round only as the aircraft descended over the North Sea. Heisenberg's version of their 1941 walk was published by Robert Jungk in the 1950s; Bohr, reading it, drafted letters of rebuttal he never sent. The Niels Bohr Archive released them in 2002, and historians have been arguing over the walk ever since.

## Still Running

On 7 October 1965, the eightieth anniversary of Bohr's birth, the institute formally took his name. His son Aage Bohr had succeeded him as director in 1962 and held the post until 1970, remaining active until he retired in 1992. On 1 January 1993 the institute absorbed the Astronomical Observatory, the Orsted Laboratory and the Geophysical Institute, keeping the Bohr name. In 2024 it moved into a new building on Jagtvej, sharing it with the Department of Chemistry. Research now runs from quantum optics and condensed matter to ice cores and dark energy, and includes the Cosmic Dawn Center, a joint venture with DTU Space led by Sune Toft, chasing the first stars and galaxies to form 300 to 600 million years after the Big Bang.

## Flight Context

The historic institute site lies at 55.6969 N, 12.5717 E in Norrebro, north of the Copenhagen lakes and roughly 2 km northwest of the city centre; since 2024 the institute occupies a new building on Jagtvej nearby. From 1,500-3,000 ft AGL the useful reference is the chain of rectangular lakes curving around the old city, with Faelledparken's green expanse to the east and Assistens Cemetery's tree block to the west - the institute district sits between them. Copenhagen Kastrup (EKCH) is about 10 km southeast; Roskilde (EKRK) roughly 28 km southwest. This is dense controlled airspace beneath Kastrup's approaches. Danish overcast is the norm; clear high-pressure days in spring give the sharpest view of the lake geometry.
