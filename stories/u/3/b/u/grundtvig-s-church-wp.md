# Grundtvig's Church

An engineer turned architect spent his last years designing a church that looks like the pipes of an organ, built from five million yellow bricks - and died a decade before it was finished.

**Location:** 55.7166, 12.5336

**Categories:** Brick Expressionism, Churches completed in 1940, Churches in Bispebjerg, Landmarks in Copenhagen, N. F. S. Grundtvig

## Introduction

Stand at the bottom of Pa Bjerget and look up, and the west front of Grundtvig's Church resolves into something you have seen before but never at this size: the ranked pipes of a church organ, stood on end and rendered in brick. The verticals rise and fall across the facade in stepped ridges, forty-nine metres to the top of the bell tower, and the eye is dragged upward whether or not it consents. It is one of the strangest buildings in Copenhagen and one of the very few 20th-century churches anywhere that people travel to see for its own sake. It sits not downtown but out in Bispebjerg, at the end of a long avenue of trees.

## A Church for a Hymn-Writer

N. F. S. Grundtvig (8 September 1783 - 2 September 1872) is not easily translated into another country's terms. He was a pastor, poet, historian, teacher and politician, he wrote or translated something like 1,500 hymns, and he is credited more than anyone with shaping how modern Danes think about being Danish. He is the ideological father of the folk high school, the institution that put adult education within reach of farmers' children. His hymns changed the sound of Danish church services outright, replacing the slow measures of orthodox Lutheranism with the work of national poets. Denmark decided he deserved a church, and settled the design by competition. Peder Vilhelm Jensen-Klint won it in 1913.

## The Late Architect

Jensen-Klint (21 June 1853 - 1 December 1930) came to architecture the long way round. He qualified as a building engineer in 1877, studied painting at the Royal Danish Academy without finishing, and worked as a mathematics teacher and a city engineer before committing to buildings full-time. What he brought to the commission was an obsessive study of ordinary Danish village churches - particularly the stepped-gable churches of Zealand - and of the brick trade that built them. The foundation was laid on 8 September 1921, Grundtvig's birthday. Construction ran mainly from 1921 to 1926, and the completed tower section was inaugurated in 1927 as the Tower Church while the rest waited. Jensen-Klint died in 1930. His son Kaare Klint, an architect himself, carried the work through to completion in 1940 - twenty-seven years after his father won the drawing.

## Gothic Bones, No Ornament

Inside, the plan is straightforwardly Gothic: a long narrow nave, two side aisles, a small transept, columns rising to pointed arches, ribbed groin vaults overhead. The proportions are Gothic too - the triple-aisled hall church runs 76 metres end to end and 35 metres wide, with the nave ceiling 22 metres up. It seats 1,440, which puts it in the same class as Copenhagen Cathedral. What is not Gothic is everything else. There is no carving, no colour, no statuary, no gilding. There is only brick: some five million yellow bricks, the standard Danish building material, laid with a precision that the absence of decoration makes impossible to hide. Jensen-Klint took the stepped gable off the village churches and doubled its apex, and left it at that. The verticality that a medieval mason would have achieved with tracery is achieved here by stacking.

## On the Hill

Jensen-Klint did not design a church so much as a setting for one. Working with Vilhelm Wittrup, Charles I. Schou and Georg Gossel, he laid out a group of buildings on either side called Pa Bjerget - On the Hill - containing the parish hall and apartments, built between 1924 and 1926 in the same yellow brick and the same idiom. They frame the church symmetrically and hold the eye on it. The approach completes the effect: a long tree-lined road runs up through Bispebjerg cemetery straight at the west front, a viewing axis of the sort the Baroque used to stage palaces. Arriving on foot along it, the facade grows in front of you for several hundred metres before you reach it.

## Still in Use

For all its severity this remains a working parish church rather than a monument. It is open to visitors throughout the year, not merely at service times, and it is wheelchair accessible via a stair lift. The organ that the building resembles is really there, and in fact there are two: a Marcussen instrument of 14 stops installed on the north side in 1940, and a much larger western organ added in 1965 with 55 stops, whose biggest pipe is 32 feet long and weighs 425 kilograms. Recitals on it are regular, and hearing an organ inside a building shaped like an organ, in a hall with no soft surfaces anywhere, is the reason many people make the trip out to Bispebjerg. Reykjavik's Hallgrimskirkja, begun some years later, reaches for a comparable fusion of Gothic and modern - but Jensen-Klint got there first.

## Flight Context

Grundtvig's Church stands at 55.7166 N, 12.5336 E in the Bispebjerg district, roughly 5 km northwest of Copenhagen city centre. From 1,500-2,500 ft it is unmistakable: a single pale yellow mass, far taller than everything around it, sitting at the head of a dark green rectangle - Bispebjerg cemetery - with the tree-lined approach axis running away to the south. The symmetrical Pa Bjerget housing blocks frame it on both sides. Copenhagen Airport (EKCH) is 13 km southeast; Roskilde (EKRK) 28 km southwest. The area is inside Copenhagen's controlled airspace, so coordinate before descending. Terrain is flat with a slight rise at Bispebjerg, and the 49-metre tower reads clearly across the northern suburbs from well out. Morning light from the southeast picks out the ridged relief of the west facade best.
