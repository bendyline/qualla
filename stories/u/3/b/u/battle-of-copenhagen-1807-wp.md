# Battle of Copenhagen (1807)

For three nights in September 1807, the Royal Navy bombarded the neutral Danish capital with cannon fire and Congreve rockets to seize the Danish fleet — an act of preemptive war that killed roughly 195 civilians and was condemned even at home.

**Location:** 55.6667, 12.5667

**Categories:** Naval battles of the Napoleonic Wars, Battles involving the United Kingdom, Battles involving Denmark-Norway, 1807 in Denmark, Sieges of Copenhagen

## Introduction

Lord Erskine said it best, in the House of Lords. 'If hell did not exist before,' he told Parliament, speaking of his own government's decision to bombard a neutral city, 'Providence would create it now to punish ministers for that damnable measure.' Between 2 and 5 September 1807, the British fleet under Admiral James Gambier and a land army of twenty-five thousand under Lord Cathcart shelled Copenhagen with three hundred Congreve rockets and conventional artillery. The Danish capital had not declared war on Britain, was not at war with Britain, and had been negotiating in good faith. About 195 civilians died in the bombardment and 768 were injured; the fires that the rockets started, with most of the firefighting force evacuated and unavailable, burned more than a thousand buildings to the ground. On 7 September, Denmark surrendered its entire fleet. The war that the bombardment was meant to prevent began the moment it ended.

## The fear that drove the cabinet

It is worth understanding what George Canning and Spencer Perceval thought they were doing, because the moral cost is impossible to weigh otherwise. Napoleon had crushed Prussia at Jena in 1806 and signed the Treaty of Tilsit with Russia in July 1807. British intelligence, some of it reliable and some not, suggested Napoleon was pressing Denmark to enter the Continental System and pledge its considerable navy to France. Hilary Barnes, writing later, called Canning's response 'rash, calamitous, and lacking in understanding of the Danes and of Danish foreign policy', because the Danes did not in fact intend to hand their fleet to Napoleon and the Cabinet had no knowledge of the secret articles of Tilsit they were so afraid of. Yet the fear, that the Royal Navy might one day face a combined fleet of French, Spanish, Russian and Danish ships, was real enough to push Britain into bombarding a neutral capital. Even at the time, it was understood as a moral compromise. Britain did it anyway.

## The city in the dark

Copenhagen had about five thousand regular Danish troops and a similar number of militia inside the walls. Most civilians were evacuated in the few days before the ring closed, but most was not all; thousands stayed, and many of those who left came back when they had nowhere else to go. On 26 August General Sir Arthur Wellesley, the future Duke of Wellington, was detached with a reserve to disperse a Danish relief force at Køge; he did so on the 29th. The Danes refused British demands to surrender the fleet. Beginning on the night of 2 September, the Royal Navy fleet under Gambier opened a sustained bombardment that lasted three days. The new Congreve rockets, William Congreve's recent invention, were inaccurate but extremely effective at starting fires; the city's normal firefighting arrangements were paralysed by the evacuation. The Church of Our Lady, the medieval cathedral at the heart of the city, burned. Christian Wilhelm Eckersberg, the great Danish painter, would record the destruction in canvases that hang today in Statens Museum for Kunst. C. A. Lorentzen called his painting of the Kongens Nytorv during the bombardment Den frygteligste Nat, The Most Terrible Night.

## Fleet, scholar, horse

Denmark sued for peace on 5 September; the capitulation was signed on 7 September. Ernst Peymann, the Danish commander, had been ordered by the Crown Prince to burn the fleet rather than surrender it; he did not, and historians have argued ever since about why. Eighteen ships of the line, eleven frigates, and a long catalogue of smaller vessels were handed to the British. Most were absorbed into the Royal Navy. The bombardment also incinerated the twenty years of work of the Icelandic-Danish scholar Grímur Jónsson Thorkelin, who had been preparing the first modern edition of the Old English poem Beowulf; two of his manuscripts survived the fire and the poem appeared in 1815. A horse foaled in 1808 was named Copenhagen in commemoration of the operation; Wellesley later bought him, and rode him at Waterloo. The peace, of course, did not last. King Christian VII's government issued letters of marque on Danish privateers within a week of the British departure. Denmark joined Napoleon's Continental System anyway, though now without a fleet; the Anglo-Danish war ground on until the 1814 Treaty of Kiel.

## Copenhagenize

Britain's foreign opposition called the act vile and condemned the country's national character. Britain's foreign secretary George Canning replied to a censure motion in March 1808 with a three-hour speech that Lord Palmerston, then a young MP, called 'so powerful that it gave a decisive turn to the debate'; the censure failed 224 votes to 64. The English language acquired a new verb. To copenhagenize, the OED records it, means to seize a foreign nation's fleet preemptively, while it sits at anchor. The horror that prompted the coinage faded; the verb stayed. In Copenhagen the bombardment is part of the city's memory in a way it has never been in London. Walk through the inner city today and you can still trace the line of the great fire; the rebuilt parts of the Old Town date almost without exception from the years immediately after 1807. Nearly two hundred ordinary citizens of a neutral capital were killed in their own homes, as a precaution against a war their government had no intention of joining.

## Flight Context

Copenhagen sits at 55.667°N, 12.567°E on the eastern coast of Zealand, opposite the Swedish coast across the Øresund. The historic city, where most of the 1807 bombardment fell, is roughly bounded by the Tivoli Gardens, Kongens Nytorv and the harbour. EKCH (Copenhagen Kastrup) lies on the island of Amager just south of the city; ESMS (Malmö Sturup) sits across the Øresund Bridge in Sweden. From cruising altitude the long curved harbour and the green expanse of the Kastellet citadel are easy reference points.
