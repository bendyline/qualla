# Christiansborg Palace

The only building on Earth that houses all three branches of a country's government, standing on 850 years of its own rubble after burning down twice.

**Location:** 55.6758, 12.5789

**Categories:** palace, government, architecture, medieval ruins, Copenhagen, Denmark

## Introduction

Denmark has burned its seat of government to the ground twice and rebuilt it both times on exactly the same spot. That spot is a low islet in central Copenhagen called Slotsholmen, and the building standing on it now — Christiansborg Slot, the third palace of that name — is the only building in the world that houses all three branches of a country's government. Parliament sits in the southern wing; the Supreme Court, the Prime Minister's Office and the monarch's reception rooms occupy the northern one. Danes rarely use the full name. They call it Borgen, the Castle, and the word has come to mean the government itself.

## Absalon's Island

The chronicler Saxo Grammaticus records that in 1167 Bishop Absalon of Roskilde built a castle on a small island outside Copenhagen's harbour: a curtain wall enclosing a courtyard, a bishop's palace, a chapel, and a scatter of smaller buildings. When Absalon died in 1201 the castle and the city passed to the bishops of Roskilde, and a bitter feud soon broke out between crown and church over who owned them. That argument ran for nearly two centuries.

The castle was also a target. Wendish pirates raided it, the Hanseatic cities attacked it, and between 1249 and 1259 it was occupied and plundered. The trouble was commercial: a fortress commanding the Sound was a nuisance to Hanseatic trade. In 1370, after King Valdemar IV lost a war against the League, the victors ordered the castle taken apart. They sent forty stonemasons to do it, block by block.

## Copenhagen Castle and the Baroque Ambition

Earthworks were thrown over the ruins, and by the end of the fourteenth century a new stronghold, Copenhagen Castle, stood on top of them. It served the crown for more than three hundred years, until Christian VI decided it was beneath him and commissioned Elias David Häusser. Work began in 1733 on a baroque palace of enormous ambition, and most of the complex was finished by 1745; at the time it was the largest palace in northern Europe.

It lasted forty-nine years. In 1794 fire destroyed both palace and church. Christian Frederik Hansen was called in to raise a replacement, starting the second Christiansborg in 1803 in the French Empire style and finishing in 1828. Its chapel, completed in 1826, still stands and is still at the monarch's disposal, used for royal baptisms, confirmations, and lyings in state. The rest of Hansen's palace burned in 1884.

## Third Time, in Concrete

The third palace went up between 1907 and 1928 in a historicist neo-baroque style, and the Danes hedged their bets this time: reinforced concrete behind granite-clad façades. Fragments of Hansen's ruined palace were salvaged and set into the north façade facing Prince George's Yard, so that all three eras remain visible in one building. The original roof tiles were replaced with copper in 1937–38, paid for by a national collection. The tower reaches 106 metres, the tallest in the city, and since June 2014 a lift has carried the public to a viewing platform near the top. Admission is free; you pass through a security check, because people are working downstairs.

## Rooms Where the State Happens

Visitors reach the Royal Reception Rooms through the Queen's Gate, the Hall of the Halberdiers, and the King's Stairway. At the foot of those stairs are the two rooms closed to the public: the Audience Chamber and the State Council Room, where the King holds audience every other Monday and signs acts passed by parliament.

Upstairs, the oval Throne Room faces the Palace Square. Foreign ambassadors present their credentials there under Kræsten Iversen's ceiling painting of the Dannebrog falling out of the sky over Estonia in 1219, and the room opens onto the balcony where Danish monarchs are proclaimed. The Great Hall runs forty metres long under a ten-metre ceiling and seats four hundred; Bjørn Nørgaard's seventeen tapestries telling the history of Denmark were a gift from Danish business for Queen Margrethe II's fiftieth birthday and were hung for her sixtieth. Next door, the Alexander Hall takes its name from Bertel Thorvaldsen's marble frieze of Alexander entering Babylon — made for the second palace, partly rescued from its ashes, and mounted again.

## The Castle Under the Castle

While the foundations of the present palace were being poured, workers hit stone. What they had found were the remains of Absalon's castle and Copenhagen Castle, lying directly beneath the inner palace yard. The National Museum sent experts, the ruins were excavated, and public interest was intense enough that covering them over again became politically impossible.

The reinforced concrete vault built in 1908 to protect them was the largest of its kind in Denmark at the time. A second set of ruins under the palace square was dug out in 1917 and roofed the same way. The exhibition opened in 1924. You walk down into it from the palace above: brick and fieldstone from 1167, damp and unlit, under the floor of a working parliament.

## The Riding Ground

The showgrounds south of the palace are the oldest thing above ground here, built between 1738 and 1746 and the only substantial part of the first Christiansborg to survive both fires. Nicolai Eigtved and Lauritz de Thurah designed the Marble Bridge and its two rococo pavilions, finished in 1744 and paved with Norwegian marble, which is where the name comes from. Above the stables sits the Court Theatre, inaugurated by Christian VII and Queen Caroline Matilda in January 1767 and home to the Theatre Museum since 1922. In the middle of the ground stands Anne Marie Carl-Nielsen's equestrian statue of Christian IX, unveiled in 1927.

## Flight Context

Christiansborg stands at 55.6758 degrees north, 12.5789 degrees east, on Slotsholmen in the Indre By district, elevation roughly 11 metres. The 106-metre tower with its two-crown weather vane is the tallest in Copenhagen and the easiest fix in the old city from 1,500 to 3,000 feet; look for the green copper roof and the rectangular complex hemmed in by Frederiksholms Kanal on the west and the harbour channel on the east, with the Marble Bridge crossing the canal to the riding ground. Copenhagen Airport (EKCH) is 5 nm to the southeast, Roskilde (EKRK) 17 nm west. The palace sits inside Copenhagen's controlled airspace and drone-restricted zone. Clear northerly air gives the sharpest definition; summer haze over the Øresund can wash out the granite façades.
