# Copenhagen

A merchants' harbour that burned, starved and was bombarded into rubble, then rebuilt itself so deliberately that its citizens now swim in the middle of it.

**Location:** 55.6761, 12.5689

**Categories:** Copenhagen, Capitals in Europe, Cities and towns in the Capital Region of Denmark, Port cities and towns of the Oresund, Denmark

## Introduction

There is an element on the periodic table named after this city. Hafnium, number 72, takes its name from Hafnia, the Latin form of Copenhagen - which is itself a scholar's dressing-up of a much plainer word. Kobenhavn means merchants' harbour, and everything the city became grew out of exactly that: sheltered water on the Oresund, reliable herring, and traders who found it worth their while to stop. Some 671,000 people live in the municipality today, 1.4 million in the urban area and 2.1 million across the wider metropolitan region. It is not a large capital. It has simply been consequential for a very long time.

## Merchants' Harbour

Flint tools scattered through the area put people here in the Stone Age, but the town proper begins with fish. The natural harbour and good herring stocks drew fishermen and merchants seasonally from the 11th century and permanently by the 13th, clustered around Gammel Strand - literally old shore. Saxo Grammaticus, writing in the 12th century, called the place Portus Mercatorum. Tradition dates the founding to 1167, when Bishop Absalon built a modest fortress on the islet of Slotsholmen, where Christiansborg Palace stands today. The fortress was an answer to Wendish pirates who worked the coast throughout that century; they eventually destroyed it, and the islanders built Copenhagen Castle in its place. By about 1416 Eric of Pomerania had moved his seat here and the harbour town had become the capital of Denmark. The University of Copenhagen followed on 1 June 1479, the oldest in the country.

## The Century That Burned

The 18th century very nearly erased Copenhagen. Plague arrived in 1711 and killed roughly 22,000 people out of a population of 65,000 - one in three. Seventeen years later came the fire. It caught on the evening of 20 October 1728 and burned until the morning of the 23rd, taking about 28 per cent of the city and leaving a fifth of the population without homes. Within the medieval quarter the loss was close to half. Another great fire followed in 1795, and in 1807 a British fleet bombarded the city during the Napoleonic Wars. This is why so little of old Copenhagen survives. The elegant streets visitors walk today are, almost without exception, the rebuild - which is its own kind of achievement, and which the Danish Golden Age turned into a coherent neoclassical city.

## The Money Behind the Marble

Rebuilding needed capital, and a great deal of it came from the Danish slave trade. Between 1660 and 1806, merchants - many of them based in Copenhagen - carried approximately 120,000 enslaved Africans to the Danish West Indies. The Danish West India Company and the Danish Asiatic Company both had their headquarters in this city. The profits are still legible in the streetscape: the Moltke Mansion, the Yellow Palace and the Vestindisk Pakhus were all built with that money. Frederiksstaden, the district begun in 1749 to Nicolai Eigtved's Rococo plan and centred on the mansions that now form Amalienborg Palace, is the loveliest quarter in Copenhagen and among the loveliest in Europe. It was paid for in part by people who were bought and sold, and whose names the ledgers mostly did not bother to record.

## Five Fingers and a Low Skyline

In 1947, with the war barely over, planners drew the Finger Plan: housing and business would grow outward along five suburban rail corridors radiating from the centre, with green wedges kept open between them. It is the reason Copenhagen sprawls in stripes rather than blots. Trams gave way to buses in 1972, but from the 1960s the young architect Jan Gehl was arguing the opposite case, and pedestrian streets and cycle tracks began appearing in the centre. The Oresund Bridge opened in the summer of 2000, putting Malmo within commuting distance by road and rail. The Metro followed in 2002. Throughout, a political majority has kept high-rise building out of the historic core, so the horizon still belongs to spires - Christiansborg, the City Hall, Rosenborg, and the dragon spire of Christian IV's old stock exchange, four intertwined tails twisted into a point.

## Water You Can Swim In

For most of the 20th century Copenhagen's harbour was working water and nothing else. Years of investment in sewage treatment changed that so thoroughly that the inner harbour is now a public swimming venue, with thirteen official swimming zones and three harbour baths; the first and most popular sits at Islands Brygge and has won international awards for its design. Swim outside the marked zones and the police will fine you. Out at sea, the Middelgrunden wind farm has turned since 2001 and supplies around four per cent of the city's power. Amager Strandpark, a two-kilometre artificial island with 4.6 kilometres of beach, opened in 2005 fifteen minutes from the centre by bicycle. The city set itself the target of having three-quarters of all journeys made on foot, by bicycle or by public transport, and built accordingly.

## Flight Context

Copenhagen sits at 55.6761 N, 12.5689 E on the eastern shore of Zealand, spilling across bridges onto Amager. Approach from the east over the Oresund and the city reads instantly: a low, flat skyline broken only by green copper spires, the Oresund Bridge running southeast toward Malmo, and the artificial island of Amager Strandpark as a pale finger along the coast. Copenhagen Airport (EKCH) lies at Kastrup on Amager, about 8 km southeast of the centre and the busiest airport in the Nordic countries; Roskilde (EKRK) is 30 km west for general aviation. Best viewing altitude is 2,000-4,000 ft, low enough to pick out the star-shaped ramparts of Kastellet and the canals of Christianshavn. Terrain is negligible - the highest natural point in the municipality is 91 m. Expect changeable maritime weather off the Atlantic year-round, with the best light in June and July when the sun is up for more than seventeen hours.
