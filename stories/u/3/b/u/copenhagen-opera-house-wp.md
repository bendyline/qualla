# Copenhagen Opera House

Henning Larsen designed Denmark's national opera house, then wrote a book afterward calling it a mausoleum for the shipping magnate who had paid for it.

**Location:** 55.6819, 12.6006

**Categories:** Copenhagen, Opera houses, Henning Larsen, 21st-century architecture, Denmark

## Introduction

Mærsk Mc-Kinney Møller, the head of the Mærsk shipping line and one of the wealthiest men in Denmark, gave his country an opera house in August 2000. The donation, made through the A.P. Møller Foundation, was worth roughly 2.5 billion Danish kroner — enough for it to be often described as one of the most expensive opera houses ever built. The Folketing accepted the gift, somewhat awkwardly, in the autumn of that year; some politicians objected that since the cost was tax-deductible, the government had effectively been forced to buy a building it had not asked for. The architect Henning Larsen designed it. Mærsk Mc-Kinney Møller, who took a close hand in the design, changed it. After it opened on 15 January 2005, Larsen took the unusual step of publishing a book denouncing his own building, calling it a mausoleum for the shipowner and describing the project as the most owner-infected disaster of his fifty-year career.

## A Building in a Sightline

The opera sits on Dokøen — Dock Island — on Holmen, in central Copenhagen's inner harbour. It was placed deliberately on the axis that runs from the Royal Family's Amalienborg Palace, through the rococo dome of Frederik's Church (the Marble Church), straight across the water. Stand in the main entrance of the opera and you can look back through that sightline to the dome on the far side of the harbour. The bridges built to access the building were made from oak trees originally planted in the 19th century to replace the Danish navy's lost fleet, after the British bombardment of Copenhagen in September 1807. Larsen's original plans called for a vast glazed front, allowing visitors and passers-by to see the auditorium's outer shell from the harbour. Mærsk objected: glass, he argued, does not age gracefully. The façade was changed to a metal grid, designed and built by the Austrian specialists Waagner-Biro. The dispute over that one decision became emblematic of the architect's relationship with the patron.

## The Cathedral and the Pumpkin

Inside, the foyer is paved in Sicilian Perlatino marble. Three spherical chandeliers by the Icelandic-Danish artist Olafur Eliasson hang in the central foyer; each is built from semi-permeable glass that lets some light pass and reflects the rest, with patterns that change as you move around them. The auditorium is the building's quiet showpiece. The maple wood walls and balcony fronts curve around the audience in warm tones — the result of staining techniques meant to imitate the colour of an old violin. The shape and the colour led Copenhageners to nickname the auditorium pumpkin. Above it, the ceiling is gilded with about 105,000 sheets of nearly pure gold leaf. The hall seats between 1,492 and 1,703 people depending on the orchestra size. The orchestra pit holds 110 musicians, one of the largest in any opera house. Per Kirkeby created four bronze reliefs for the wall to the auditorium. Per Arnoldi designed the front curtain.

## Backstage and the Black Box

The technical brief was extraordinary. The building totals 41,000 square metres, with fourteen floors of which five are below ground, and more than a thousand rooms. There are six main stages: one visible to the audience and five for rehearsal and set preparation, all connected so large productions can move setups in and out continuously. A second, smaller venue called Takkelloftet — the black box — handles experimental theatre. Its name borrows from an older Takkelloftet, a 280-metre rope-storage building constructed for the navy between 1767 and 1772, anchoring the new opera to the maritime history of its site. Some of the walls in the small theatre are clad in the same Jura Gelb limestone used outside, mounted in a way that lets visitors play them like an instrument by knocking with bare hands. Plácido Domingo sang Sigmund in Wagner's Die Walküre at a gala in April 2006, attended by Queen Margrethe II from her own box on the left of the auditorium.

## An Architect's Public Verdict

What separates the Copenhagen Opera House from most expensive new buildings of its era is what its architect did afterward. In 2009, Henning Larsen published a book, De skal sige tak! — a rare thing in any architectural culture — that took apart his own creation. He called it a mausoleum for Mærsk Mc-Kinney Møller. He described the project as without comparison the most owner-infected worst-case in his fifty years as an independent architect — squeezed, as he put it, between the Phantom of the Opera himself and shipping magnates and lawyers. Henning Larsen died in 2013. Mærsk Mc-Kinney Møller had died the year before, in 2012, at the age of 98. The opera they fought through still operates as the national opera of Denmark, run by the Royal Danish Theatre, and is often described as one of the most modern opera houses in the world. Whether it is a great building, an owner-infected mausoleum, or both at once — that argument continues, and the building itself, regardless, will keep producing opera for as long as Denmark wants opera produced.

## Flight Context

The Copenhagen Opera House sits at 55.682°N, 12.601°E, on Dokøen on the Holmen islands in Copenhagen's Inner Harbour. The building is on the eastern shore of the harbour, directly across the water from Amalienborg Palace and Frederik's Church (the Marble Church). Copenhagen Airport (EKCH) is about 8 km south-southeast on Amager. From altitude the harbour cuts a sinuous north–south channel through central Copenhagen, with the opera house's pale Jura limestone façade and projecting flat roof distinctively visible on the harbour's eastern bank.
