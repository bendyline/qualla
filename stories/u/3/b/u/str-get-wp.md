# Strøget

The man who ordered the cars off Copenhagen's main shopping street received death threats for it. Sixty years later, half the world's cities have copied him.

**Location:** 55.6787, 12.5781

**Categories:** Shopping streets in Copenhagen, Pedestrian streets in Copenhagen, Tourist attractions in Copenhagen, Urban planning

## Introduction

On 17 November 1962, the man who had just ordered the cars off Copenhagen's busiest shopping street needed police protection to walk down it. Alfred Wassard was the city's planning chief, and for months he had been absorbing the argument that Danes were constitutionally unsuited to strolling — that the sort of public life such a street imagined belonged to Italians, to people who lived somewhere the sun came out in November. Merchants at the expensive eastern end were certain they would be ruined. On the day the barriers went up, drivers on the surrounding streets leaned on their horns in protest, and Wassard walked the length of his experiment under guard. The closure was meant to be temporary. It was made permanent in 1964, and no car has driven Strøget since.

## Ruten, and the Fire That Drew It

Strøget is not a street. It is five of them, laid end to end: Frederiksberggade, Nygade, Vimmelskaftet, Amagertorv and Østergade, running 1.1 kilometres from City Hall Square in the west to Kongens Nytorv — the King's New Square — in the east, with Gammeltorv and Nytorv opening off the middle like a pair of lungs. Copenhageners called the chain Ruten until the late nineteenth century. The line it follows is older than the name. Frederiksberggade was laid out in 1728, after a fire cleared the medieval tangle that stood there, and the geometry has not moved since. Most of the buildings along it date from the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, though the oldest survivor was raised in 1616. What the fire drew, the twentieth century argued over.

## The Man With the Counting Sheet

Jan Gehl took his architecture degree from the Royal Danish Academy of Fine Arts in 1960, practised for six years, then won a research grant to study something the profession had barely bothered to measure: the form and use of public space. Strøget became his laboratory. He and his colleagues counted. How many people passed, at what hour, at what speed; how long they stayed; where they chose to sit and where they never did. The findings became *Life Between Buildings*, published in Danish in 1971 and in English only in 1987 — a book arguing that architects had spent a century designing buildings and almost no time designing the space between them. Gehl was not the author of the 1962 closure. He was the person who proved, with clipboards and tally marks, that it had worked.

## Fifteen Thousand Square Metres, Then a Hundred Thousand

The trick of Copenhagen was patience. Having taken 15,800 square metres of the old centre away from cars in 1962, the city did not stop, and it did not hurry. Another street and a few more squares were emptied in 1968. More followed in 1973 and 1992. Nyhavn, the harbour arm with the painted houses, went pedestrian in 1980. City Hall Square was half-freed in 1996, when Copenhagen was European Capital of Culture — the through road removed, the buses kept. In 1993 the artist Bjørn Nørgaard resurfaced Amagertorv in a pattern of granite. By the time the process had run four decades, the car-free centre had grown to roughly 100,000 square metres, more than six times what the first trial claimed, and no single step of it had been large enough to frighten anyone.

## The Claims It Cannot Keep

Strøget is routinely described as the oldest and longest pedestrian street in the world. Neither is true. Rotterdam's Lijnbaan was pedestrianised in 1953, nine years earlier. Rue Sainte-Catherine in Bordeaux is longer; so is Laisvės Alėja in Kaunas at 1.6 kilometres; so is Sydney's George Street at two. The honest version of the claim is narrower and more interesting: at the moment of its conversion in 1962, Strøget was the longest such street anywhere, which is why the world came to look. It is still busy enough to strain. Roughly 80,000 people use it on a summer day and 48,000 in midwinter, and on the Sundays before Christmas the figure can reach 120,000. Gehl put the summer flow at around 145 people a minute along a street only ten to twelve metres wide.

## What Copenhagen Exported

The counting method travelled further than the street ever could. Gehl and his colleagues took the *Public Spaces, Public Life* survey — first applied to Copenhagen and published in 1996 — to Melbourne in 1994 and again in 2004, to London in 2004, to Perth, Sydney, Adelaide, Auckland, Wellington and Christchurch, and to New York City in 2007 and 2008, where the results helped clear Times Square of traffic. Gehl Architects was founded in 2000. The irony is that the street itself now looks like everywhere else: Lonely Planet's 2014 verdict was that Strøget offers "the same old international brand names," and advised turning off it to find anything independent. The idea outgrew its birthplace. Copenhageners, told they lacked the temperament for public life, exported the proof that nobody does.

## Flight Context

Strøget runs east–west through the medieval core of Copenhagen at 55.6787°N, 12.5781°E, between City Hall Square and Kongens Nytorv. From 2,000–3,000 feet the street reads as a pale seam through the dense red-tiled roofs of the old city, with the green copper spires of the Church of the Holy Ghost and the Round Tower flanking it to the north and the rectangular void of Kongens Nytorv closing its eastern end. Copenhagen Airport (EKCH) lies 8 km southeast across the Amager flats; Roskilde (EKRK) is 30 km west. Approaches over the Øresund give the cleanest view of the old city grid. Low winter sun and frequent maritime haze can flatten the street detail; midday summer light is best.
