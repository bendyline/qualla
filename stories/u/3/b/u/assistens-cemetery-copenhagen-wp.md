# Assistens Cemetery

A pauper's burial ground of 1760 that became the most fashionable address in the Danish Golden Age — and is now Nørrebro's favourite park, where people sunbathe a few metres from Kierkegaard.

**Location:** 55.6911, 12.5489

**Categories:** Cemeteries in Copenhagen, 1760 establishments in Denmark, Parks in Copenhagen, Nørrebro, Tourist attractions in Copenhagen

## Introduction

In 1805 a commission sat down and wrote a rule against cheerfulness. No food, no drink, no music, and no cheerful behaviour of any kind inside Assistens Cemetery — the gravediggers, who lived on the premises, were to enforce it. They enforced it so loosely that a second law had to be passed in 1813 forbidding them to sell alcohol to visitors. Crowds still came. At a grand funeral, spectators would climb the walls for a better view, and officials seriously discussed charging admission before deciding against it. Two centuries later the picnickers have simply won. On a warm July afternoon in Nørrebro, people lie out on the grass between the headstones with books and beer, and nobody writes a rule about it any more.

## Somewhere to put the poor

The cemetery opened on 6 November 1760, and it was not built out of sentiment. Copenhagen's churchyards inside the ramparts had been overwhelmed for decades — the plague of 1711 alone killed something on the order of 23,000 people in a city that had nowhere to put them — and by the 1750s the pressure was intolerable. In 1757 the City Council proposed a large new burial ground out beyond the Northern gate, on open land where nobody yet lived. An assistenskirkegård was exactly what the name says: an assisting cemetery, an overflow, and in practice a place for the city's poor, who were buried there because the parishes could not or would not find room for them closer in. For its first quarter-century that is what it was.

## How a pauper's field became an address

The turn came around 1785, when a high-ranking official chose to be buried out at Assistens rather than in a fashionable church, and the rest of educated Copenhagen followed him. By the Danish Golden Age the cemetery had become the place to be laid to rest, and — because the graves had trees around them rather than flagstones over them — a place to spend an afternoon among the dead. A Swedish visitor in 1827 called it one of the most beautiful graveyards in Europe, describing leafy trees and dark paths opening into bright flowery expanses, a little paradise. Families brought tea baskets. That is the odd, persistent thing about Assistens: the sociable use of it is not a modern irreverence imposed on a solemn place. It is nearly as old as the place itself, and the authorities were losing the argument about it by 1805.

## Section A, and the philosopher in it

The oldest ground is Section A, and it holds Søren Kierkegaard, whose funeral in 1855 turned into a public quarrel when his nephew interrupted the graveside service to protest at the state church burying a man who had spent his last years attacking it. The painter Christen Købke, whose luminous small views of Copenhagen's ramparts define Golden Age painting for most people who have seen them, lies in the same section, as does Jens Juel, the portraitist of the generation before. Section D was set aside for religious minorities, with Roman Catholic, Reformed and Russian graves; Section E originally served the Church of Our Lady. Hans Christian Andersen was buried here in 1875 in the plot of the Collin family, who had taken him in as a young man; when the Collins moved their own stone to Frederiksberg Ældre Kirkegård in 1914, his grave stayed where it was, and it is still one of the most-visited spots in the cemetery.

## Three Bohrs and two jazzmen

The scientific dynasty is here in full: Niels Bohr, his mathematician brother Harald, and their father Christian Bohr the physiologist, three generations of a family that shaped how the world understands the atom and the body. Martin Andersen Nexø, who wrote about Danish rural poverty from the inside, is buried at Assistens. So is the sculptor H. W. Bissen, and Peter von Scholten, the last governor-general of the Danish West Indies, who on 3 July 1848 declared the emancipation of every enslaved person in the colony. He did it because the enslaved people of St. Croix had risen and left him no other choice, and Denmark repaid him with a trial for treason; he was exonerated only shortly before he died. The twentieth century added the poet Michael Strunge and the writer Dan Turèll, a Nørrebro figure whose grave draws its own steady traffic, and the singer Natasja Saad. Two American jazz musicians who made their later lives in Copenhagen are buried here as well: the tenor saxophonist Ben Webster and the pianist Kenny Drew.

## Still burying, still a park

None of this is a museum. Assistens remains an active cemetery, taking new burials, while also serving as the largest and most important green space in inner Nørrebro — a wedge of old trees in one of the densest districts in Denmark. In summer people sunbathe both inside the walls and in Hans Tavsens Park along the southwestern edge. In 2003 an old horse stable on the grounds was converted into a small museum for the local artist Herman Stilling, with a children's workshop and a café. In 2019 the Nørrebros Runddel metro station opened inside Section G, which means that thousands of Copenhageners now arrive at work each morning by surfacing in the middle of a graveyard. The city has stopped pretending these two uses are in conflict.

## Flight Context

Assistens Cemetery lies at 55.6911°N, 12.5489°E in Nørrebro, about 2 km northwest of Copenhagen's medieval core. From 1,500–3,000 ft it appears as a rectangular block of dense mature canopy — a dark green island in an otherwise unbroken grid of five-storey blocks — bisected by the pale line of Kapelvej and bordered on the southwest by the open lawns of Hans Tavsens Park. The Lakes (Søerne) curve just southeast of it and make a reliable pointer. EKCH (Copenhagen Kastrup) is 11 km south-southeast; EKRK (Roskilde) is 28 km west-southwest. The canopy reads best in late spring and early autumn; from December to March the bare trees expose the grid of paths and the cemetery loses its distinctness from the surrounding roofs. Copenhagen's inner airspace is busy and controlled — plan any overflight through Kastrup's TMA procedures.
