# Rosenborg Castle

Christian IV built Rosenborg as a summerhouse, kept enlarging it for thirty years, and asked to be carried across Zealand so he could die in it.

**Location:** 55.6857, 12.5775

**Categories:** Castles, Copenhagen, Museums, Royal history, Renaissance architecture

## Introduction

On 21 February 1648 a dying Christian IV asked to be moved. He was at Frederiksborg, twenty-odd miles north of Copenhagen, and he wanted to go home - not to the working royal seat at Christiansborg but to the small brick house with copper spires that he had begun forty-two years earlier as a place to escape to in summer. He was carried there in a litter. A week later, on 28 February, he died at Rosenborg, aged seventy. Of the enormous quantity of building he inflicted on Denmark - a naval town, an observatory, an exchange, whole districts of Copenhagen - this was the one he had made for himself.

## A Summerhouse That Would Not Stop Growing

It started small. Christian IV bought the ground in 1606 and put up a modest country retreat outside the ramparts, well clear of the noise and smell of the city. Then he added to it. Then he added again. Four building phases across roughly three decades turned the retreat into the tall, gabled, three-towered structure standing today, worked out in the Dutch Renaissance idiom that defined ambitious Danish building of the period - red brick banded with pale sandstone, tall windows, steep copper roofs gone soft green. Bertel Lange and Hans van Steenwinckel the Younger are the architects associated with its planning, though as with most of the king's projects it is hard to separate their hands from his; Christian IV was an enthusiastic and interfering client who sketched his own ideas. The result reads less like a fortress than like a very confident merchant's house scaled up until it became a palace.

## The Long Hall

Run the length of the third floor and you are in the Long Hall, finished in 1624 and built as a ballroom. It changed jobs repeatedly. Around 1700 it was serving as the royal reception room and banqueting hall, and only in the later nineteenth century did anyone start calling it the Knight's Hall. Christian V hung twelve tapestries along it showing his victories in the Scanian War of 1675-1679, which is one way of decorating a room and also one way of settling an argument with Sweden. The stucco ceiling dates from the early eighteenth century: the Danish coat of arms at the centre, ringed by the collars of the Orders of the Elephant and the Dannebrog, with side reliefs recording events from Frederik IV's early reign - the ending of serfdom, the founding of the dragoons and the land militia. Hendrick Krock's frescos above represent the regalia. At one end stand the coronation chair of the absolutist kings and the queens' throne, with three silver lions life-size on guard in front of them, and along the walls a great deal of seventeenth-century silver furniture.

## What Is Kept in the Cellar

The crown jewels are downstairs. Denmark's crown regalia live in a vaulted treasury beneath the castle that has been their home for generations. Their working life ended with the 1849 constitution, which abolished the anointing of monarchs; since then the regalia have been brought out only when a Danish sovereign lies in state. Christian IV's own crown is here, dense with enamel and small figures, along with the globus cruciger, the coronation carpet, the throne chair of Denmark, and the Oldenburg horn. The rooms above hold the rest of the royal collections: furniture, portraits, glass, weapons, and personal objects running from the late sixteenth century through to the nineteenth, some of them originally the property of noble families rather than kings.

## From Residence to Museum

Danish monarchs used Rosenborg as a residence until about 1710, when Frederik IV moved on to newer and more fashionable palaces and the castle quietly became a storehouse for the collections instead. After that it was lived in only twice, both times because something had gone wrong. The first was in 1794, after Christiansborg burned down and the royal family needed somewhere to sleep. The second was in 1801, during the British attack on Copenhagen. Otherwise it sat, filling up, until the decision was taken to show it: Rosenborg opened to the public in 1838, arranged as a chronological sequence of period rooms, each furnished to its own reign. The castle is state property now. Walking it in order is an odd sensation - you are moving through time at the pace of a slow corridor.

## The King's Garden

Rosenborg sits inside Kongens Have, the King's Garden, which is older than the castle it surrounds - Christian IV had it laid out in Renaissance style shortly before he began building. It is the oldest royal garden in Denmark, and it long ago stopped belonging to royalty in any practical sense. Something like 2.5 million people a year come through it: office workers with lunch, students on the grass, summer concerts, tourists working out where the crown jewels are. Rosenborg Barracks stands at the corner of Gothersgade and Oster Voldgade; the Royal Life Guards moved their main base out to Hovelte in 1985, but the guards on duty in Copenhagen are still quartered here, so the park keeps a soundtrack of boots. Christian IV wanted a place to get away from Copenhagen. Copenhagen came and found him.

## Flight Context

Rosenborg stands at 55.686 degrees north, 12.577 degrees east, in the northern part of Copenhagen's Indre By. From 1,500-3,000 ft it is easy: look for the largest green rectangle in the old city - Kongens Have - with a compact three-towered red-brick block set at its southwestern corner and the copper-green spires catching light. The Botanical Garden's glasshouses lie immediately northwest, the Nyboder rows northeast, and the Lakes curve around to the west. Copenhagen Kastrup (EKCH) is 9 km southeast, Roskilde (EKRK) about 30 km west, and the city centre sits inside busy controlled airspace with Kastrup traffic overhead. Clear low-sun conditions in spring and autumn give the best separation between the red brick and the surrounding grey rooftops; in high summer the tree canopy in Kongens Have partly screens the castle from directly above.
