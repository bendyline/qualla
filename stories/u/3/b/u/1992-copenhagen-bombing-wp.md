# 1992 Copenhagen Bombing

A bomb in the offices of a small Danish socialist organization killed 29-year-old Henrik Christensen — a murder that, more than thirty years later, remains officially unsolved.

**Location:** 55.6713, 12.5909

**Categories:** bombing, Copenhagen, Denmark, unsolved, neo-Nazi-violence, 1992

## Introduction

Henrik Christensen was twenty-nine years old. He worked from a small office on Søllerødgade in the Nørrebro district of Copenhagen, one of about twenty members of a Danish far-left organization called International Socialists who used the rooms there. On the morning of 16 March 1992 he came to the office, ran into a fellow member named Jørgen Lund out on the street, and went back inside. At around 11:30 the building blew up. The blast killed Christensen and started a fire that destroyed almost everything in the rooms — including, the investigators later realized, much of the physical evidence about the bomb itself. Lund survived because he was outside. The Søllerødgade bombing is still, as of this writing, the only post-war political assassination on Danish soil. No one has ever been charged.

## Who He Was

What gets recorded about murder victims tends to be how they died, not who they were before they died. Christensen was a young political organizer, active in a small Trotskyist organization that probably had fewer than a hundred members nationwide and that mostly published a magazine and held meetings. He had presumably gone to school, had family, had friends, had ordinary plans for ordinary days. He happened to be one of the people who came in to the office on Søllerødgade most regularly, which is why he was inside when the bomb went off and Jørgen Lund was outside. Lund would carry that distinction the rest of his life. People who knew Christensen described him afterward in the Danish press as committed, gentle, the sort of person who showed up reliably and did the unglamorous work of running a small left-wing organization. He was, by any measure, the wrong person to be where he was that morning.

## What the Bomb Was

Detectives believed almost immediately that the device had been a letter bomb, delivered to the Søllerødgade office and triggered when Christensen opened the day's mail. The British Scotland Yard was brought in to assist with forensic analysis and could only confirm that the explosive had been TNT. Beyond that, the case was hampered from the start by the fire and by the water used to extinguish it, both of which destroyed traces a meticulous post-blast examination would normally have used. An organization calling itself Free Denmark K12 (Frit Danmark K12) sent a letter claiming responsibility, and also sent threatening letters to five other Danes. Whether the organization actually existed beyond the letters has never been established. The Danish Security and Intelligence Service investigated; the case stalled.

## The Names That Came Up Later

The political context made one suspect group obvious. Months before the bombing, Danish and Swedish anti-racist activists had clashed with neo-Nazis at a demonstration in Lund. The International Socialists' members had been part of those anti-fascist mobilizations. The IS itself believed from the beginning that neo-Nazis were responsible. In 1998, a Danish journalist named Frede Farmand claimed on television that he had warned the Danish intelligence service about a bomb plot in 1992 and that the plot involved a Dane named Albert Larsen, a member of the neo-Nazi Partiet de Nationale. Larsen, questioned by PET, admitted infiltrating left-wing groups but denied any knowledge of the bomb. In April 2013, a former neo-Nazi named Kim Fredriksson told the tabloid Ekstra Bladet that he had information pointing to a Danish-German member of the Blood & Honour network — a man identified only as MS — as the likely organizer. MS had been arrested in 1994 with TATP explosives in his apartment and had died in 2001 from cystic fibrosis. The Copenhagen police reopened the case based on Fredriksson's testimony. They have not closed it again with an answer.

## What Remains Open

Søllerødgade is a quiet residential street in Nørrebro today, lined with apartment blocks and small shops. There is a memorial plaque at the bombing site. In 2019 Danmarks Radio broadcast a five-part radio documentary investigating the case, presented by a journalist who had grown up nearby and had her own theories. The documentary explored the various hypotheses — neo-Nazi attack, internal far-left dispute, accidental detonation by the victim himself — and reached no firm conclusion. The retired police officer who had led the original investigation insisted to the producers that there had never been a fixed three-point hypothesis, that the case had been kept open to all possibilities, and that it had simply ended blindly. As an unresolved murder it remains officially sealed, the files inaccessible. Henrik Christensen would now be in his sixties. The people who knew him are still alive. The person or people who killed him have, as of 2026, never been named in court.

## Flight Context

1992 Copenhagen Bombing (historical site): 55.6713 N, 12.5909 E, on Søllerødgade in the Nørrebro district of central Copenhagen, Denmark. Best viewed below 2500 feet. The site is a normal residential block in a dense urban neighborhood; nothing distinguishes the building from the air. Use the broader pattern of Nørrebro's grid streets and the nearby Assistens Cemetery as visual landmarks. Copenhagen Airport (EKCH) is about 12 km southeast on Amager Island. Class C airspace around EKCH; expect heavy traffic.
