# 1992 Copenhagen Bombing

A bomb in the offices of a small Danish socialist organization killed 29-year-old Henrik Christensen — a murder that, more than thirty years later, remains officially unsolved.

**Location:** 55.6713, 12.5909

**Categories:** bombing, Copenhagen, Denmark, unsolved, neo-Nazi-violence, 1992

## Introduction

Henrik Christensen was twenty-nine years old. He worked from a small office on Søllerødgade in the Nørrebro district of Copenhagen, one of about twenty members of a Danish far-left organization called International Socialists who used the rooms there. On the morning of 16 March 1992, shortly before the blast, he met a fellow member named Jørgen Lund out on the street, apparently after collecting the mail, and went back inside. At around 11:30 the building blew up. The blast killed Christensen and started a fire that destroyed almost everything in the rooms — including, the investigators later realized, much of the physical evidence about the bomb itself. Lund survived because he was outside. The Søllerødgade bombing has never been solved. No one has ever been convicted.

## Who He Was

What gets recorded about murder victims tends to be how they died, not who they were before they died. Christensen was a young political organizer, active in a small Trotskyist organization. He was inside when the bomb went off, and Jørgen Lund was outside. Public accounts of the case say little more about him than his age, his politics and the place where he died.

## What the Bomb Was

Detectives thought the device may have been a letter bomb, delivered to the Søllerødgade office with the day's mail. Scotland Yard was brought in to assist with forensic analysis and could only establish that the explosive had contained TNT. Beyond that, the case was hampered from the start by the fire and by the water used to extinguish it, both of which destroyed traces a meticulous post-blast examination would normally have used. An organization calling itself Free Denmark K12 (Frit Danmark K12) sent a letter claiming responsibility, and also sent threatening letters to five other Danes. Whether the organization actually existed beyond the letters has never been established. The Danish Security and Intelligence Service investigated; the case stalled.

## The Names That Came Up Later

The political context made one suspect group obvious. Months before the bombing, Danish and Swedish anti-racist activists had clashed with neo-Nazis at a demonstration in Lund. One police theory was that the bomb was revenge for that clash. The IS itself believed from the beginning that neo-Nazis were responsible. In 1998, a Danish journalist named Frede Farmand claimed on television that he had warned the Danish intelligence service about a bomb plot in 1992 and he linked it to a member of the neo-Nazi Partiet de Nationale. That man, questioned by PET, admitted infiltrating left-wing groups but denied any knowledge of the bomb. In April 2013, a former neo-Nazi named Kim Fredriksson told the tabloid Ekstra Bladet that he had information pointing to a Danish-German member of the Blood & Honour network — a man identified only as MS — as the likely organizer. MS had been arrested in 1994 with TATP explosives in his apartment and had died in 2001 from cystic fibrosis. The Copenhagen police reopened the case based on Fredriksson's testimony. They have not closed it again with an answer.

## What Remains Open

Søllerødgade is a quiet residential street in Nørrebro today, lined with apartment blocks and small shops. In 2019 Danmarks Radio broadcast a five-part radio documentary investigating the case, presented by a journalist who had known of the case since childhood and had long assumed right-wing extremists were responsible. The documentary explored the various hypotheses — neo-Nazi attack, internal far-left dispute, accidental detonation by the victim himself — and reached no firm conclusion. The retired police officer who had led the original investigation insisted to the producers that there had never been a fixed three-point hypothesis, that the case had been kept open to all possibilities, and that it had simply ended blindly. As an unresolved murder it remains officially sealed; the documentary's reporter was refused access to the files. Henrik Christensen would now be in his sixties. The person or people who killed him have, as of 2026, never been named in court.
