# Amalienborg

Four identical rococo palaces built for four noble families around an octagonal square — and the home the Danish royal family moved into only because their own palace burned down.

**Location:** 55.6840, 12.5933

**Categories:** Palaces in Denmark, Official residences in Copenhagen, Rococo architecture in Copenhagen, Nicolai Eigtved buildings, Tourist attractions in Copenhagen, 1760 establishments in Denmark

## Introduction

Stand in the middle of the octagon and turn slowly through a full circle. The same façade passes you four times: the same rusticated ground floor, the same pilasters, the same restrained grey-and-gold rhythm. It looks like one enormous building with a hole cut through the middle. It is nothing of the kind. These are four separate palaces, commissioned in the 1750s by four separate aristocratic families who each wanted a mansion in the smartest new district in the kingdom, and who were required as a condition of the land grant to build behind an identical face. The Danish monarch lives here now — but only because, forty years after the last of them was finished, the royal family had nowhere else to go.

## The theatre that burned with the audience inside

The ground already had a name and a catastrophe attached to it. Queen Sophie Amalie, consort of Frederick III, built a pleasure palace here between 1669 and 1673 on land her father-in-law Christian IV had bought outside the old walled city; she called it Sophie Amalienborg and lived there as dowager until her death in 1685. On 15 April 1689 her son Christian V marked his forty-fourth birthday at the palace with a German opera staged in a temporary wooden theatre — very likely the first opera performed in Denmark. It went so well that they repeated it on the 19th. Shortly after that second performance began, a piece of stage scenery caught fire. The theatre and the palace burned to the ground, and about 180 people died in them: courtiers, servants, musicians, people who had come out for an evening's entertainment. The name Amalienborg survived. Nothing else did.

## Four families, one face

Sixty years later, Frederick V laid out Frederiksstaden across the same ground — a grid of straight streets and a monumental cross-axis, designed by the court architect Nicolai Eigtved. At the crossing he placed an octagonal square and offered the four corner plots to noblemen who could afford to build on them. Lord High Steward Adam Gottlob Moltke took the southwest and spent the most: his Great Hall, the Riddersalen, was fitted with woodcarving by Louis August le Clerc, paintings by François Boucher and stucco by Giovanni Battista Fossati, and is still regarded as perhaps the finest rococo interior in Denmark. It opened on 30 March 1754, on the eve of the King's birthday. Eigtved died a few months later; Lauritz de Thurah and Nicolas-Henri Jardin finished what he had started. Count Christian Frederik Levetzau built the northwest palace, and when his estate later sold it, the family attached one condition — that the Count's coat of arms must never be taken down. It is still there on the wall, beside the monarch's.

## How the crown moved in

In February 1794 Christiansborg Palace burned, and the court became, abruptly, homeless. The royal family bought Moltke's palace first and had Caspar Frederik Harsdorff convert it; they were living in it by December of that year. Harsdorff also threw a colonnade of eight Ionic columns across Amaliegade to tie two of the palaces together, which is why the square reads as a single composition rather than four rich men's houses. One by one the others were acquired. The buildings are known today by the kings who lived in them rather than the counts who paid for them: Christian VII's Palace in the southwest, Christian VIII's in the northwest, Frederik VIII's in the northeast, Christian IX's in the southeast. At the centre stands Jacques Saly's equestrian bronze of Frederick V, commissioned in 1753 by Moltke in his capacity as director of the Danish Asiatic Company, which paid for a great deal of it. The foundation stone went down in 1760. The statue was not unveiled until 1771, five years after the King it honours had died.

## Which windows are lit

It remains a working residence, not a monument. King Frederik X and Queen Mary live in Frederik VIII's Palace, restored for them in the 2000s when they were still crown prince and princess. Margrethe II has been in Christian IX's Palace since 1967, five years before she became queen. Christian VIII's Palace holds the Amalienborg Museum, which preserves the private apartments of monarchs from 1863 to 1947 — bedrooms and studies left as their occupants used them. Christian VII's Palace has been the state guest house since 1885, used for receptions and visiting heads of state and opened to the public when nobody important is staying.

## Noon, and what the guard tells you

The Royal Life Guards — Den Kongelige Livgarde — hold Amalienborg day and night in scarlet tunics, blue trousers and navy bearskin caps. They leave Rosenborg at half past eleven each morning and march across the city, arriving to change the guard in the square at noon; sentries are relieved every two hours around the clock. There are three grades of watch, and reading them is a small Copenhagen skill. A King's Watch means the monarch is in residence in Christian IX's Palace, and the band comes with the guard. A Lieutenant Watch means a regent is standing in. A Palace Watch — the smallest — means nobody is home at all.

## Flight Context

Amalienborg sits at 55.6840°N, 12.5933°E, one block back from Copenhagen's inner harbour on the Zealand side. From 1,500–3,000 ft the octagon is unmistakable: four matching grey roofs enclosing a paved eight-sided void, with the great green copper dome of Frederik's Church (the Marble Church) directly inland on the short axis and the Copenhagen Opera House, finished in 2004, directly across the water on the same line — the three buildings make a ruler-straight sightline that is the easiest visual fix in the city. The star-shaped ramparts of Kastellet lie about 1 km north along the same waterfront. EKCH (Copenhagen Kastrup) is 8 km south-southeast on Amager; EKRK (Roskilde) is 30 km west. This is dense controlled airspace over a head-of-state residence — treat it as a look from established routes, not a target to orbit. Best light is late afternoon, when the low sun rakes across the four identical façades and separates them.
