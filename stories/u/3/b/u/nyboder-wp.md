# Nyboder

Christian IV built rows of small yellow houses for the sailors who crewed his navy - and nearly four centuries later, Danish service families still live in them.

**Location:** 55.6889, 12.5875

**Categories:** Historic districts, Copenhagen, Naval history, Architecture, Christian IV

## Introduction

Danish has a word for one particular shade of yellow. Nyboder-gul - Nyboder yellow - is the ochre wash on a grid of long, low terraced houses just inside the old ramparts north of central Copenhagen, and Danes use the term generically, the way English speakers say royal blue. The joke is that the colour is an afterthought. When Christian IV built these rows for the crews of his navy, he had them painted red and white, the national colours. The yellow came later, and stuck so hard that it took the district's name with it.

## Houses for the Men Below Decks

The name means new houses, which implies old ones, and there were: Bremerholm already had accommodation for naval officers. Nyboder was for everybody else - common sailors and other ranks, and their families, who until then had found lodging where they could in a walled city with no room to spare. The Royal Danish Navy was expanding fast under Christian IV, and a fleet is only as reliable as its ability to find its crews when it needs them. The king laid the district out in 1631 on land he had bought outside the fortifications, intending eventually to push the city walls north; Saint Anne's Post, which would grow into the Kastellet, already stood a little further on. Hans van Steenwinckel the Younger and later Leonhard Blasius did the design work. Two main streets were meant to radiate from a central square that was never actually built, with the rows running perpendicular to them. By about 1641 there were some two hundred dwellings of roughly forty square metres each: a living room of nineteen, a chamber, an entrance hall, and a kitchen shared with the family next door.

## Elephant Alley, Hedgehog Alley

Whoever named these streets was enjoying it. The historic names run in two registers - spices and herbs on one hand, Tulipanstraedet, Timianstraedet, Lavendelstraedet; animals on the other, Elefantstraedet, Harestraedet, Lovestraedet, Pindsvinestraedet. Tulip Alley and Hedgehog Alley, for barracks. The house numbering is odder still: it ran boustrophedon, up one side of a row and back down the other, the way an ox turns at the end of a furrow. And the properties never received cadastral numbers at all, most likely because the navy owned and administered the entire district. Nyboder was not quite part of the city. It was a piece of the fleet that happened to be on land.

## Rebuilt, Raised, Extended

The district kept outgrowing itself. In 1647, a year before Christian IV's death, the moving of the Eastern City Gate finally brought Nyboder inside the fortifications. By 1695 a commission was considering shipping naval personnel off to the island of Mon for lack of space; nothing came of it, and nothing came of a later plan to build fresh barracks on the reclaimed Frederiksholm islet either. In the end the navy simply rebuilt where it stood. Philip de Lange contributed twenty-four two-storey houses in 1756, and architects who followed him kept working to his design. Anthon and Harsdorff added a storey to some of Christian IV's original rows in 1771. Between 1781 and 1796 roughly 150 more houses went up, along with a guard house in 1787 and five officers' houses. Almost everything a visitor sees today therefore postdates 1757. One fragment of the original survives, in Sankt Pauls Gade, dating from 1635 and protected since 1918 - it is now the Nyboders Mindestuer, a small museum of how these rooms were actually lived in.

## Still a Garrison

Nyboder never stopped doing its job. The houses remain in the hands of the Danish defence ministry, and they are still allocated to service personnel, with priority to those stationed in Copenhagen; until 2006 the navy held exclusive rights, and since then all three services have shared them equally. Selling the district off has been proposed more than once and rejected each time. In 2009 the A. P. Moller Foundation donated 50 million kroner to refurbish part of the stock. The neighbourhood has its own landmarks: St Paul's Church, built in red brick between 1872 and 1877 to Johannes Emil Gnudtzmann's design and known to everyone as Nyboder's Church; a bronze Christian IV by Vilhelm Bissen, unveiled in 1900 at the corner of Kronprinsessegade and Oster Voldgade; and out on Store Kongensgade, a memorial to Vice Admiral Edouard Suenson, who commanded the Danish ships at the Battle of Heligoland on 9 May 1864.

## The Nyboder of Danish Letters

For a district of naval housing, Nyboder has an outsized literary shadow. Ludvig Holberg used it in Den Stundeslose to mock the local habit of marrying young and having far too many children. Soren Kierkegaard, in Either/Or, throws out the line "Why was I not born in Nyboder, why did I not die as a baby?" - the district standing in for an ordinary life he could imagine but not have. Hans Christian Andersen opens "The Elder-Tree Mother" with an elder tree in a poor Nyboder yard, and beneath it an old sailor and his very old wife; elder grew everywhere here, and the superstitions attached to it grew with it. In Henrik Pontoppidan's Lykke-Per the young protagonist rents a room from a boatman in Hjertensfrydsgade. Papa Bue's Viking Jazz Band recorded "Praise of Nyboder" in 1959. And in the 2015 film The Danish Girl, Lili Elbe visits a friend at Suensonsgade 8 - a street named for the admiral on Store Kongensgade, in a district built for the men who served under him.

## Flight Context

Nyboder sits at 55.689 degrees north, 12.588 degrees east, in the Indre By district between Kongens Have and the harbour. From 1,500-3,000 ft the pattern gives it away before the colour does: a tight comb of parallel, identical roof ridges running perpendicular to two longer streets - unmistakably regular against the irregular medieval grain of the old city around it. Use the star-shaped Kastellet on the waterfront just to the northeast as your anchor, with Rosenborg Castle and the green rectangle of Kongens Have to the southwest. Copenhagen Kastrup (EKCH) is 9 km southeast, Roskilde (EKRK) roughly 30 km west, and the city centre lies within busy controlled airspace. Overhead midday sun flattens the ochre; morning or late-afternoon light along the row ends makes the yellow read strongly against the red pantile roofs.
