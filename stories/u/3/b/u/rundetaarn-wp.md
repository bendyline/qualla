# Rundetaarn

Copenhagen's Round Tower has no staircase - just a brick ramp spiralling seven and a half times to an observatory that has been in use since 1642.

**Location:** 55.6814, 12.5758

**Categories:** Towers, Copenhagen, Astronomy, Observatories, Christian IV

## Introduction

There are no stairs. To reach the platform 34.8 metres above Kobmagergade you walk a brick corridor that wraps seven and a half times around a hollow core, climbing 3.74 metres with every turn - a gradient closer to a country lane than a staircase. Follow the outer wall and you cover 257.5 metres at roughly one in ten. Hug the inner core and the distance drops to 85.5 metres, but the floor tilts up at one in three and your calves will tell you about it. The ramp is not a flourish. It was built this way because seventeenth-century astronomical instruments were heavy, fragile, and impossible to carry up a spiral stair.

## Longomontanus Makes His Case

Astronomy was a strategic science in the seventeenth century. Europe's states were pushing across oceans, navigation depended on accurate celestial tables, and national observatories went up one after another - the first at Leiden in 1632. Denmark had recently lost its advantage: Tycho Brahe had fallen out of favour and left the country, and his observatory Stjerneborg had been demolished. Brahe's former assistant Christian Longomontanus, now the king's astronomer and the first professor of astronomy at the University of Copenhagen, proposed a replacement in 1625. Meanwhile the university needed a students' church and a library. Someone had the sense to merge all three into one building, on ground Christian IV had bought in 1622 beside the Regensen dormitories. The result was the Trinitatis Complex: church, library hall and observatory tower in one block.

## Bricks from Emden, Stone from the Ramparts

Hans van Steenwinckel the Younger is generally credited with the design, though no document proves it, and he did not live to see it finished. From 24 November 1636 stone was hauled in for the foundations, first from Copenhagen's own ramparts and later from the country around Roskilde. Danish brickmakers could not meet the standard the project demanded, so in February 1637 a contract was signed with Henrik van Dingklage of Emden for shiploads of hard-burned Dutch brick of the slender type called muffer or mopper. The site was a knot of alleys and half-timbered houses; on 18 April 1637 two hundred men, soldiers and Bremerholm workers, went in to clear it. The foundation stone was laid that 7 July. Steenwinckel died in August 1639 and Leonhard Blasius was brought from the Netherlands to take over. Money ran out more than once; churches across Denmark and Norway were ordered to hand over a share of their income to keep the work going. The tower was finished in 1642, its masonry laid in alternating yellow and red brick, the colours of the House of Oldenburg.

## What the King Wrote on the Wall

High on the facade is a gilded rebus, a puzzle in symbols and letters that includes the four Hebrew consonants of the Tetragrammaton. Christian IV drafted it himself, and the draft in his own handwriting survives in the Danish National Archives. Read out, it asks Jehovah to lead right teaching and justice into the heart of the crowned King Christian IV, 1642. The king signed his building the way other men sign letters. Around the observation platform runs a wrought-iron lattice made in 1643 by Kaspar Fincke, the court metalwork artist, carrying the royal monogram and the letters RFP - Regna Firmat Pietas, piety strengthens the realms. Built into the hollow core there is also a toilet: a seat near the top and a shaft dropping the full height of the tower.

## Things People Have Ridden Up It

The ramp invites misuse, and Copenhagen has obliged for three centuries. In 1716 Tsar Peter the Great rode a horse to the top while visiting Frederik IV, with Catherine I following behind him in a carriage - a story that is repeated as fact in both Danish and English accounts. What is a myth is the more colourful version sometimes attached to it, that the tower originally had ordinary stairs and was rebuilt with the spiral for the tsar's benefit. It was not. The corridor was there from the start, for the instruments. In 1902 a Beaufort became the first motor car to make the climb. Every spring there is a unicycle race up and down, and the record stands at 1 minute 48.7 seconds, which is a long time to think about a one-in-three gradient.

## Still Open, Still Looking Up

The tower eventually lost the argument with progress. By the early nineteenth century instruments were outgrowing a structure that could not be widened, while light from the growing city and the vibration of street traffic below were spoiling the readings; the university built Ostervold Observatory on the decommissioned ramparts instead, opening it in 1861 to Christian Hansen's design. But Rundetaarn did not stay dark. In 1928 it was recommissioned as a public observatory, with a new domed observatory - seven metres high and six across, reached by a narrow stone stair from the platform - added the following year. It is open to the public from mid-October to mid-March, which makes this the oldest observatory building in Europe still doing the job it was built for. Since 1987 the Library Hall above the church has been an exhibition space and a concert venue, hosting around a hundred concerts a year. And the platform still delivers what Christian IV was really buying: the spires of old Copenhagen laid out below - the Marble Church, Christiansborg, the City Hall tower, the twisting spire of the Church of Our Saviour - and on a clear day the Oresund Bridge and the Swedish coast beyond it.

## Flight Context

Rundetaarn stands at 55.681 degrees north, 12.576 degrees east, in the heart of Copenhagen's Indre By, with its platform at 34.8 m above street level and the tower topping out around 42 m. From the air it is a fat cylinder rather than a spire, which makes it harder to pick out than the pointed towers around it - hunt instead for the Trinitatis Church roof it is attached to, midway between the Church of Our Lady to the southwest and Kongens Have to the northeast. From 1,500-2,500 ft the old city reads as a dense grey-brown medieval tangle ringed by the Lakes and the harbour, with Strogets pedestrian line running east-west just to the south. Copenhagen Kastrup (EKCH) is 8 km southeast, Roskilde (EKRK) about 30 km west, and the centre lies inside busy controlled airspace. Winter afternoons give the clearest air; the same visibility that shows Sweden from the platform will show you the Oresund Bridge from the cockpit.
