# Superkilen

A wedge of Nørrebro furnished with 108 objects imported from 62 countries — swings from Iraq, a Soviet bus stop, a Moroccan fountain — and a neighbourhood that has never entirely agreed on whether it worked.

**Location:** 55.7008, 12.5427

**Categories:** Parks in Copenhagen, Squares in Copenhagen, Bjarke Ingels buildings, Linear parks, Nørrebro

## Introduction

Set into the pavement beside a set of swings in outer Nørrebro is a small metal plate. It says, in Danish and in Arabic, that the swings came from Baghdad. A few dozen metres away another plate explains a bench from Brazil, and another a bus shelter of the sort that stood at Soviet roadsides, and another a fountain from Morocco, and another a rank of litter bins from England. There are 108 of these objects along the 750 metres of Superkilen, drawn from 62 countries, and every one of them carries its label in Danish and in the language of the place it came from. The park was not designed to look like anywhere. It was designed to look like everywhere its neighbours had come from — an argument, made in street furniture, about who a Copenhagen neighbourhood belongs to.

## Red, Black, Green

Superkilen runs as a wedge — kilen, in Danish — for 750 metres and about 30,000 square metres, from Nørrebroparken past the housing blocks of Mjølnerparken up to Tagensvej. It is divided into three stretches, each with its own colour and its own job. The Red Square is painted in reds, oranges and pinks, and handles sport, play and noise. The Black Market at the centre is the quieter room: a dark surface studded with barbecue grills and Chinese palm trees, meant as an outdoor living room rather than a plaza. The Green Park at the northern end gives up hard surface entirely for low hills, trees and grass, and belongs to picnics, dogs and football. A cycle track runs the length of all three, because this is Copenhagen and the park is also a commute.

## The Wedge Between Two Halves

The site was chosen for what it separated. Outer Nørrebro is among the most ethnically diverse districts in Denmark and among its most socially strained, and the strip that became Superkilen had been a barrier: two residential areas divided by a fence, the whole quarter cut off from the rest of the city by major roads. Nørrebro also carried a recent memory of unrest — the district saw rioting in 2006, in the period of the Danish cartoon controversy. The commission went out in June 2008 to the Danish artists' group Superflex, working with Bjarke Ingels Group and the German landscape practice Topotek 1. Design ran from January 2009 to February 2010, construction from August 2010 to June 2012, at a cost of roughly 8.9 million dollars. The park opened on 22 June 2012.

## Extreme Participation

The designers gave their method a deliberately immodest name: extreme public participation. Rather than the usual consultation evening in a community hall, they solicited proposals through the internet, email, newspapers and radio, asking residents to nominate objects from the countries they or their families came from. Roughly sixty nationalities live around the wedge; the brief was to represent all of them. A local governance board then chose which suggestions were built or bought. Superflex framed the result as an artwork in progress rather than a finished park, and treated diversity not as a problem the design had to solve but as the raw material it was made from. Humour was part of the strategy — the Soviet bus stop and the neon signs advertising Russian hotels and Chinese cosmetics are not solemn objects — and the designers were careful that the joke never fell on the communities themselves.

## What the Neighbourhood Said

The verdict at street level has been more mixed than the architectural press suggested. A user survey run by the local site 2200N.dk in January 2013, six months after opening, found roughly half of respondents judging Superkilen a success and about a third calling it a failure or a serious failure. The complaints were the unglamorous kind: noise, maintenance, and the awkwardness of running a cycle track through what is effectively an open-air museum. Others pointed out that for all the colour, the red and black squares had not made outer Nørrebro any greener. Set against that, the fear that most exercised the planners never materialised. This is a district with a long history of vandalism, and the park has largely been left alone.

## Prizes, and the Longer Test

The awards arrived quickly. Superkilen was shortlisted for the Design Museum's Design of the Year in London and for the European Union Prize for Contemporary Architecture, took an American Institute of Architects Honor Award for regional and urban design in 2013, and in 2016 was one of six winners of the Aga Khan Award for Architecture — cited for promoting integration between religious and ethnic groups in a place where tension between immigrant and host communities is real, and for doing it with a mixture of humour, history and hubris. That last word was the jury's, and it was fair. Whether a park can knit a divided quarter together is a question no ribbon-cutting answers. What is measurable is smaller and not nothing: two halves of a neighbourhood that used to be split by a fence now walk through to each other, past a fountain from Morocco.

## Flight Context

Superkilen lies at 55.7008°N, 12.5427°E in outer Nørrebro, roughly 3 km northwest of Copenhagen's medieval centre. From 1,500–2,500 feet it is unmistakable: a long red-and-pink stain running northeast between dense blocks of grey and red roofs, shading to black paving at its centre and green at its northern end near Tagensvej. Nørrebroparken anchors the southern tip. Copenhagen Airport (EKCH) is 11 km southeast; Roskilde (EKRK) is 28 km southwest. The colours read best in flat overcast light, which is common here; strong low sun washes out the red square.
