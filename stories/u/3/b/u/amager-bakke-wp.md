# Amager Bakke

A working incinerator that burns 400,000 tonnes of Copenhagen's rubbish a year, with three ski pistes, a hiking trail and the world's tallest climbing wall on its roof.

**Location:** 55.6847, 12.6205

**Categories:** Waste power stations in Denmark, Ski areas and resorts in Denmark, Artificial ski resorts, Sports venues in Copenhagen, Bjarke Ingels Group buildings

## Introduction

Point your skis downhill here and the mountain under your boots is on fire. Not metaphorically: eighty-five metres below the top of the piste, furnaces are consuming four hundred thousand tonnes of household waste a year and turning it into electricity and hot water for the city that threw it away. Amager Bakke — Amager Hill, better known abroad as CopenHill — is a waste-to-energy plant with a public mountain on its roof, and the two things are not a compromise between each other. Bjarke Ingels Group drew it as one object from the beginning. The roof pitch is the ski slope. The wall that holds up the boiler hall is the climbing wall. Construction started in 2013, the plant went into service on 30 March 2017, and the recreational half opened over the first weekend of October 2019.

## A power station that agreed to be a hill

Flat countries do not get mountains, and Denmark has spent a long time being philosophical about that. What Copenhagen had instead was an ageing incinerator on Amager, the low island that carries the airport and a good deal of the city's industry, and a decision to replace part of it. The argument BIG won was that a building this large — eighty-five metres, in a skyline that mostly stops at church spires — could not politely pretend to be invisible, so it should be useful twice. The plant cost in the region of $670 million and is run by Amager Resource Center, the municipal waste company. It can shift between operating modes depending on what the city needs and what power costs on the day, producing anywhere from zero to 63 MW of electricity while sending 157 to 247 MW of heat into Copenhagen's district heating network. On a cold February evening, most of what the building makes is warmth.

## Three pistes, no snow

The roof carries three runs of dry ski slope, open year round, along with hiking and running trails that switchback up the same incline, changing rooms and a café at the top. Bolted to the tallest face is a climbing wall eighty metres high, built by the Bulgarian manufacturer Walltopia — the tallest permanent climbing wall in the world. Somewhere between forty-two and fifty-seven thousand people come up here in a typical year, which is a strange number for a power station and a modest one for a mountain. The architecture world was more emphatic: in 2021 the building was named World Building of the Year at the World Architecture Festival, an award normally reserved for things that do not smell faintly of hot metal.

## The chimney that was drawn blowing rings

The most-repeated detail about Amager Bakke is also the hardest to see. The chimney was designed not to trail a continuous plume but to release its exhaust in discrete rings of water vapour — a visible, countable accounting of what the city burns, hanging over Amager like punctuation. Whether any visitor has watched one drift off the stack is a separate question, and one worth holding lightly. What the chimney demonstrably does is filter. Sulphur emissions are cut by about 99.5 percent and nitrogen oxides by roughly 95 percent, along with dioxins and hydrogen chloride, and the plant produces more clean water than it consumes. Its operators have called it the cleanest incineration plant in the world, which is a claim, not a measurement — but the flue-gas numbers behind it are real.

## The arithmetic nobody skis past

Copenhagen built this plant partly as a pillar of a promise: carbon neutrality by 2025. The trouble with burning rubbish for heat is that burning rubbish releases carbon, and the promise therefore depended on capturing it again. In 2022 Amager Resource Center was found ineligible for Denmark's national carbon capture and storage funding, and the 2025 target slipped out of reach — a failure that made international news precisely because Copenhagen had been the city everyone else was citing. A carbon capture unit has since been running at the plant; the liquefied CO₂ it produces is trucked to commercial greenhouses, where growers use it to push plant growth up by around a tenth. It is a genuine result and a small one. That gap, between the elegance of the building and the difficulty of the problem it was built to solve, is the honest reason to come here. You ride up past the boilers, you look out over the Øresund, and the thing you are standing on is still an incinerator.

## Flight Context

Amager Bakke stands at 55.6847°N, 12.6206°E on the northeast shoulder of Amager island, on the industrial waterfront between Copenhagen's inner harbour and the Sound. At 85 m it is one of the tallest structures on the island and reads clearly from 1,500–2,500 ft: a long asymmetric wedge with a pale sloping roof and a single square chimney, sitting alone among low port sheds. EKCH (Copenhagen Kastrup) lies about 8 km south-southeast, so the plant sits inside the terminal area and under regular arrival and departure traffic — check the Kastrup CTR before descending for a look. EKRK (Roskilde) is 35 km west. Across the water, ESMS (Malmö Sturup) is 35 km east-northeast beyond the Øresund Bridge, whose cable-stayed span makes an easy pointer back toward Amager. Visibility over the Sound is often excellent in spring and autumn; winter brings low stratus that will hide the roof entirely.
