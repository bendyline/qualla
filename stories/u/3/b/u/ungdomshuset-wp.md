# Ungdomshuset

The house where Clara Zetkin proposed International Women's Day was sold by Copenhagen for 2.6 million kroner, occupied by autonomists for twenty-five years, taken by helicopter in 2007, and demolished within a week.

**Location:** 55.6938, 12.5480

**Categories:** Anarchism in Denmark, Autonomism, Squatting in Denmark, Self-managed social centers, History of Copenhagen, Buildings and structures demolished in 2007

## Introduction

In 1999 the City of Copenhagen put a battered nineteenth-century meeting hall at Jagtvej 69 on the market, and the young people living in it hung a banner across the facade advertising the terms of sale: "For sale along with 500 autonome, stone throwing, violent psychopaths from hell." It was a joke, a threat and an accurate forecast all at once. A buyer came anyway. Eight years later, at seven in the morning on 1 March 2007, police special forces came through the roof and windows while a military helicopter worked overhead and the walls were sprayed with foam to blunt petrol bombs. Four days after that the house was gone.

## The People's House

The building went up on 12 November 1897 and was called Folkets Hus, the People's House. Copenhagen's labour movement built it because it had to: union organisation was unwelcome in the eyes of the authorities, reprisals against organisers were routine, and the movement needed halls that belonged to nobody else. This was the fourth such house in the city, and an outsized amount of history passed through it. In 1910 the Second International held an international women's conference there, and it was in that hall that Clara Zetkin put forward the idea of an International Women's Day. Lenin visited; so did Rosa Luxemburg. The building was bound up with the 1918 unemployment demonstration in which workers stormed the Stock Exchange. By the 1950s it had settled into something homelier — union meetings, boxing matches, end-of-season dances.

## Jagtvej 69

Then the ground beneath it changed hands, repeatedly and awkwardly. The supermarket chain Brugsen bought the site meaning to flatten it; the building's historic status blocked that, and in 1978 Brugsen sold the ground to the folk-music ensemble Tingluti. A water main burst that Tingluti could not afford to fix, and the property went to the municipality for 700,000 kroner. In 1982 the city assigned the house to a group of young people who renamed it Ungdomshuset — the Youth House. Nick Cave and Björk both played there in its early years, and by the 2000s it drew more than five hundred visitors a week. When fire, rot and fungus led the city to close the building as too expensive to repair in January 1996, the users repaired it themselves, and the fire-prevention department signed off on their work on 1 March 1997 — ten years to the day before the police came.

## Sold to Faderhuset

The buyer was a company called Human A/S, which paid around 2.6 million kroner in late 2000, took possession in 2001, and then sold its shares to Faderhuset — a small independent Christian congregation whose theology and whose new tenants were about as far apart as two groups in one city can be. The occupants carried on as though nothing had happened, and never once let the owners inside. Positions hardened on both sides. A foundation formed around the address offered Faderhuset 13 million kroner in December 2006, five times what the building had cost; Faderhuset refused. Four days later, around two thousand people marched in support of the house, some masked in breach of Danish law. Police ordered the crowd to disperse, stones and fireworks answered, barricades burned, and officers used tear gas — a rarity in Denmark. By dawn 273 people had been arrested.

## Seven in the Morning

The clearance was staged like a siege. Police sealed fifty metres around the building, brought in an airport crash tender, two boom cranes serving as modern siege towers and a military helicopter, foamed the walls, and sent special forces in from the roof, the windows and the ground. The response ran for days: Nørrebrogade blockaded, containers overturned, cars and bins dragged into barricades, a fire that spread to a kindergarten, and a secondary school's library and media room gutted, its books and computers burned in the street at a cost of about a million kroner. Somebody painted the Little Mermaid pink and tagged the rock beneath her with a 69 and a circled A. Police raided addresses across Nørrebro for six days and nights, arresting close to seven hundred people in three days, more than 140 of them foreigners held on a presumption of danger. Solidarity protests ran in Germany, Norway, Sweden, Poland, Austria, Finland, the Netherlands and Italy — and eight people stood outside Denmark's UN consulate in New York in eight degrees of frost. Demolition began on 5 March and finished on the 6th.

## Thursdays, and Dortheavej

What followed was stubborn rather than spectacular. Supporters marched every Thursday evening, from Blågårds Plads and later from Gammeltorv. On 8 March 2007 more than three thousand women demonstrated and were subjected to systematic identity checks. In October a campaign calling itself G13 announced it would openly occupy a disused waterworks at Grøndalsvænge Allé 13; several thousand came, the action stayed non-violent, and police arrested 436 people — the largest mass arrest in Denmark to that date. It was that restraint, publicly noticed, that shifted things. On 11 October the Lord Mayor, Ritt Bjerregaard, invited the house's spokesmen to negotiate. Keys to a building at Dortheavej 61 in Bispebjerg were handed over on 1 July 2008, sixteen months of Thursdays after the eviction, and the new Ungdomshuset opened officially that October. In December 2008 fifteen people who had been inside the house on 1 March were sentenced for preparing violence against police, eleven of them to a year and three months. Three years later a British undercover officer, Mark Kennedy, told Channel 4 that his intelligence had been instrumental in bringing the eviction about.

## Flight Context

The original Ungdomshuset stood at Jagtvej 69 in Nørrebro, 55.6938°N, 12.548°E, about 3 km northwest of Copenhagen's centre; the current social centre is at Dortheavej 61 in Bispebjerg, roughly 2 km further northwest. From 1,500–2,500 feet the Nørrebro site sits on the long straight line of Jagtvej, close to the wooded rectangle of Assistens Cemetery to the southeast — the most useful landmark in a district of near-identical block courtyards. Copenhagen Airport (EKCH) is 10 km southeast; Roskilde (EKRK) is 27 km southwest. Overcast Baltic light is the norm; the dense five-storey blockwork makes individual addresses hard to pick out without the cemetery as a reference.
