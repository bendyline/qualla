# Copenhagen Suborbitals

A volunteer, crowdfunded rocket program on a Copenhagen industrial island that has flown six vehicles since 2011 and still intends, someday, to put one of its own members above 100 kilometres.

**Location:** 55.6914, 12.6170

**Categories:** spaceflight, amateur rocketry, engineering, crowdfunding, Copenhagen, Denmark

## Introduction

The first launch attempt was defeated by a hair dryer. On 5 September 2010, off the Danish island of Bornholm, a homemade rocket sat on a homemade floating platform in the Baltic, and the liquid oxygen valve froze. The crew had planned for this: a consumer hair dryer was rigged to thaw it. In the event the hair dryer worked fine — its power supply failed. The countdown stopped. Copenhagen Suborbitals sailed home, promised to come back the next year, and did. Fifteen years on, the group is still at it, still amateur, still funded by strangers, and still working toward the same goal it started with: putting one of its own volunteers above 100 kilometres and bringing them back.

## A Workshop on an Industrial Island

Copenhagen Suborbitals was founded in 2008 by Kristian von Bengtson and Peter Madsen as a non-profit that ran most of its engineering arguments in public, on blogs and at lectures. Its home is Refshaleøen, the old shipyard island in Copenhagen's harbour, which is also where the engine test stand lives; by 2011 the group had run more than thirty test firings of various engine types there.

As of September 2024 there were sixty-three volunteers, who elect a chairman and board at an annual general assembly. The current chairman is Jørgen Skyt. Nobody is paid. The money comes from a supporters' association founded in 2010 that hit a hundred members within two days of forming, five hundred by November 2011, and around six hundred by early 2024; by 2015 that stream was worth roughly £12,500 a month. It is a rocket programme funded the way a football club is funded.

## Six Vehicles, and What Each One Taught

HEAT-1X flew on 3 June 2011 from the floating launcher Sputnik, carrying the one-person capsule Tycho Brahe with a full-size crash test dummy named Rescue Randy in the seat. Within seconds the stack pitched over to roughly thirty degrees off horizontal, and mission control cut the motor sixteen seconds into flight at 1.4 kilometres. It coasted to about 2.8 kilometres against a 30-kilometre target and came down 8.5 kilometres downrange. The capsule separated cleanly; neither parachute deployed. Rescue Randy took a 26 g impact with the sea and was recovered afterwards.

SMARAGD — emerald in Danish — went up on 27 July 2012, a two-stage 5.7-metre vehicle weighing 160 kilograms, and reached 8.2 kilometres, still the group's altitude record. Its electronics nosecone tore off under an estimated 20 g of acceleration. Two weeks later the Tycho Deep Space capsule was fired on a launch escape system test, and again the parachute failed. SAPPHIRE-1 flew successfully on 23 June 2013 to prove out an in-house guidance system. Nexø I in July 2016 was a partial success, starved of liquid oxygen by premature boil-off. Nexø II, on 4 August 2018, simply worked: apogee around 6.5 kilometres, clean parachute recovery. It is the last thing the group has flown.

## The Founder Who Left, and What He Did Next

The two founders fell out. Von Bengtson's departure was announced by the board on 23 February 2014, after a falling-out with Madsen; Madsen himself left in June of the same year.

Three years later, in August 2017, Madsen killed the Swedish freelance journalist Kim Wall aboard UC3 Nautilus, the homemade submarine he had built and which had once been used to push the group's launch platform out to sea. She had gone aboard to interview him. He was convicted in 2018 of murder, sexual assault, and indecent handling of a corpse, and sentenced to life imprisonment. Copenhagen Suborbitals issued a public statement about its former member and has kept its distance since. Kim Wall was thirty when she died, a reporter who had built a career out of going to difficult places and asking people to explain themselves.

## Spica, Target Date 20XX

The crewed vehicle has a name and a design and no launch date. Spica, sketched out in 2014, is planned to stand twelve to fourteen metres tall on a 950-millimetre diameter. Two pressure-hardened steel tanks at that diameter have been built. The original plan called for a single 100-kilonewton engine, but manufacturing problems killed the BPM-100 in favour of clustering the smaller BPM-25 — 25 kilonewtons each, burning liquid oxygen and ethanol, steering by thrust vectoring. Clustering costs thrust but buys roll authority and flow control. The guidance electronics and software are built in-house, and most of the systems were flight-proven on the little Nexø rockets between 2016 and 2018.

On the group's own mission timeline, the target year for Spica X — the first speculative crewed flight — is written as "20XX." That is an unusually honest thing for a space programme to publish.

## Teaching the Next Batch

In 2021 the group launched a Recruits Initiative to bring in and train young engineers, partly to feed Spica and partly on the argument that Denmark's space and defence industries need people who have actually built hardware. The recruits designed their own vehicle: a single-stage rocket five metres from nose to engine, about 165 kilograms fuelled, using a dynamic pressure-regulating system instead of a turbopump and a refined BPM-5 pushed to 10 kilonewtons. As of 2024 it was queued for static testing.

The honours the group already holds are real but modest against its ambition: the most powerful amateur rocket ever flown, the first to carry a full-size crash test dummy, the first to receive and execute a main engine cut-off command, and the organisation of a sea launch on a shoestring. On 3 October 2013 the Fédération Aéronautique Internationale gave them the Breitling Milestone Trophy in Kuala Lumpur. The highest any of their vehicles has ever reached is 8.2 kilometres. The Kármán line is at 100.

## Flight Context

The Copenhagen Suborbitals workshop sits at 55.6914 degrees north, 12.6170 degrees east on Refshaleøen, a former shipyard island on the east side of Copenhagen harbour, roughly 1 nm northeast of Christianshavn. From 1,500 to 3,000 feet it reads as a flat industrial peninsula of sheds, cranes and hardstanding, with the Copenhill/Amager Bakke waste-to-energy plant immediately south. Copenhagen Airport (EKCH) is 5 nm south-southeast; Roskilde (EKRK) is 19 nm west. The launches themselves happen far from here, from the ESD138/ESD139 military range in the Baltic Sea just off Nexø on the island of Bornholm, some 90 nm east-southeast — Bornholm Airport (EKRN) is the nearest field to that water. Launch campaigns are notified to mariners and aviators in advance.
