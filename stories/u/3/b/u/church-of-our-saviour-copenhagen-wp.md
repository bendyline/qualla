# Church of Our Saviour, Copenhagen

A Christianshavn baroque church whose golden spire is wrapped in an external staircase that spirals the wrong way, with 150 of its 400 steps in open air.

**Location:** 55.6728, 12.5939

**Categories:** church, baroque architecture, landmark, carillon, Copenhagen, Denmark

## Introduction

In Jules Verne's A Journey to the Center of the Earth, Professor Lidenbrock decides his nephew Axel is too afraid of heights to be useful on an expedition into a volcano. His remedy is the spire of Vor Frelsers Kirke in Christianshavn. He marches the young man up it for five consecutive days until the fear breaks. Verne chose well. The top 150 steps of this church's 400 are outside, on a staircase that winds around the spire in the open air, narrowing as it climbs, with the roofs of Copenhagen dropping away beneath the treads. The spire reaches 90 metres. There is a handrail. There is not much else.

## Fifty-Seven Years to Finish a Tower

When Christian IV laid out Christianshavn in 1617 he meant it as an independent merchants' town on the island of Amager, which meant it needed a church of its own. A temporary one was inaugurated in 1639. Construction of the permanent building, to Lambert van Haven's design, did not begin until 1682, and when it was consecrated fourteen years later in 1695 the altar was frankly provisional and the tower had no spire at all.

The altar problem was solved in 1732. The spire took longer. Not until 1747, under Frederik V, was the project revived, and the architect brought in — Lauritz de Thurah — promptly abandoned van Haven's scheme in favour of his own. The King approved it in 1749. Three years later it was done, and on 28 August 1752 Frederik V climbed his new tower at a ceremony. Fifty-seven years had passed since the church opened.

## The Architect Who Did Not Jump

Copenhagen has told itself for centuries that the architect threw himself off the top when he realised the staircase turned the wrong way — anticlockwise, counter to what any sensible spiral ought to do. It is a good story and it is false twice over. Van Haven had been dead for nearly fifty years when the spire went up, so it cannot be him. And de Thurah died in his own bed seven years after the work was finished, with nothing in the record suggesting he was ever unhappy with it.

The direction was a choice, not a blunder. De Thurah took his inspiration from the spiral lantern of Borromini's Sant'Ivo alla Sapienza in Rome, which turns the same way. The French illustrator Édouard Riou, drawing the church for the original illustrated edition of Verne's novel, got it wrong — his spiral goes clockwise.

## Black, Gold, and Deliberately Ugly

The spire is a timber-framed structure, octagonal at its base, with round-arched openings and circular windows in gilded frames flanked by composite-order pilasters. Four statues of the evangelists stand at the corners of the square tower where the octagon begins. Above them the staircase makes four turns to a small platform with a gilded railing, and from there it goes outside.

At the summit sits a vase-like form carrying a gilded globe, and on the globe a four-metre figure of Christ Triumphant holding a banner. Up close the proportions are grotesque, and the sculpture has a long-standing reputation as the ugliest thing in Copenhagen. That is the point. It was never meant to be seen from below the tower — only from across the harbour, from Amager, from the sea, where distance corrects it.

## Elephants, Pipes, and Forty-Eight Bells

The church itself is Dutch baroque on a Greek-cross plan, its walls of red and yellow tile set in a deliberately random pattern that breaks with the orderly banding of Christian IV's buildings. Nicodemus Tessin's altarpiece shows the Garden of Gethsemane: Jesus comforted by one angel while another hangs in the air beside him with a golden chalice, the whole framed by columns and a broken gable behind which a panel bearing the name of God in Hebrew is lit from behind.

The organ, built by the Botzen brothers between 1698 and 1700 and carrying Christian V's gilded monogram, is mounted on the wall and held up by two carved elephants. It has more than 4,000 pipes. In the tower hangs a concert carillon installed in 1928 and rebuilt in 1980 — forty-eight bronze bells spanning four octaves, the largest carillon in northern Europe. The heaviest bell weighs over two tonnes, the smallest ten kilograms, and the whole instrument comes to nearly twelve tonnes. It plays on the hour, every hour, from eight in the morning until midnight. In 1983 the church created Denmark's first full-time carillonneur post; Ulla Laage held it until 2006.

## What the Climb Buys

From the platform the city arranges itself into something you cannot get from the street. North lies Christiansborg's tower and the National Bank. East is Kløvermarken and the flat sprawl of Amager. Directly below, the Christianshavn Canal runs between rows of old merchant houses toward Wilders Plads, and beyond it the harbour mouth opens to the Øresund.

Down in the churchyard, inside its fence, lie the remains of the old graveyard — the merchant and shipbuilder Andreas Bjørn, the mayor Frederik Holmsted, the veterinarian Carl Viborg, and Otto Fabricius, who served as a missionary on the southwest coast of Greenland from 1768 to 1773 and came home to write Fauna Groenlandica, describing 473 animal species, 130 of them new to science. The congregation ran out of room here long ago. Our Saviour's Cemetery, inaugurated in 1790, sits out on Amager at the corner of Amagerbrogade and Prags Boulevard.

## Flight Context

Vor Frelsers Kirke stands at 55.6728 degrees north, 12.5939 degrees east, in Christianshavn on the Amager side of Copenhagen's harbour. The black-and-gold spire tops out at 90 metres and is one of the most distinctive vertical features in the city skyline — from 1,500 to 3,000 feet, look for the gilded corkscrew a short distance southeast of Christiansborg's copper tower, with the bastioned ramparts of Christianshavns Vold and the Christianshavn Canal immediately around it and the harbour channel running north–south to the west. Copenhagen Airport (EKCH) lies 4 nm to the south-southeast; Roskilde (EKRK) is 18 nm west. The gilding catches low sun spectacularly in the hour after sunrise and before sunset. Note that this is dense controlled airspace over central Copenhagen with drone restrictions in force.
