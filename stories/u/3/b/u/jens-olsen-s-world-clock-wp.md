# Jens Olsen's World Clock

A Copenhagen locksmith taught himself astronomy and spent the rest of his life designing a clock with 15,448 parts and a gear that turns once every 25,753 years - then died ten years before it started.

**Location:** 55.6756, 12.5694

**Categories:** Astronomical clocks, Clocks in Denmark, Tourist attractions in Copenhagen, Buildings and structures completed in 1955, Time in astronomy

## Introduction

One wheel in this clock turns once every 25,753 years. It tracks the slow wobble of Earth's axis, the precession that swings the pole star out of position over geological time, and it is doing so right now inside a glass case in Copenhagen City Hall, at a rate no living observer will ever perceive. Somewhere else in the same mechanism another gear completes a full revolution every ten seconds. Between those two extremes sit twelve movements and 15,448 individual parts, all of them driven by falling weights, all of them wound by hand once a week.

## The Locksmith Who Learned the Sky

Jens Olsen (1872-1945) trained as a klejnsmed - a locksmith - and only later took up the clockmaker's trade. The astronomy he taught himself. He worked on the calculations for the World Clock until 1928, after which the astronomer Elis Stromgren supervised them, and the drawings were produced between 1934 and 1936. Actual manufacture did not begin until 1943, when Olsen was seventy-one. He died in 1945, with the work barely started, and his closest collaborator Otto Mortensen carried it through the remaining decade. The clock was set going on 15 December 1955 by King Frederik IX and by Olsen's youngest grandchild, Birgit - which means that of the two people who started it, one was a head of state and the other was a child standing in for a man who had spent most of his adult life on a machine he never saw run.

## Twelve Movements

The clock is built in modules, and this was a deliberate choice rather than an engineering convenience. Each movement is separated and legible so that a visitor can see which mechanism does what, and most can be lifted out for maintenance without stopping the rest. The escapement is a Dennison-style double three-legged gravity escapement, the type used in tower clocks where accuracy matters more than efficiency; it decouples the pendulum almost entirely from variations in driving force, at the cost of needing a great deal of weight to run. The pendulum beats seconds, its rod is Invar to resist thermal drift, its impulse rods are sapphire, and the escape wheel carries an unusual five teeth. The gearing is largely gold-plated brass and the dials are rhodium-plated. The whole assembly stands on granite in a temperature- and humidity-controlled case, with the ventilation plant hidden in the basement.

## What the Dials Say

Mean time occupies the largest dial at the top of the centre section; sidereal time sits directly below it on a 24-hour face. At the bottom of the centre column is the main calendar, with dials for the dominical letter, epact, solar cycle, cycle of indiction and lunar cycle, and beneath them a display giving the day of the week for any date and the dates of moveable feasts and moon phases. The left section carries the equation of time - two hands, one for the universal value and one for this particular building, the gap between them being the constant that separates Copenhagen's longitude from the meridian its time zone is set by. Beside it the Synchronoscope gives the hour anywhere on Earth, using a fixed map drawn as a polar projection with a 24-hour ring rotating around it. Shutters on the sunrise and sunset dial creep open and closed across the year. On the right, the Stellar Heavens shows the sky overhead in the projection an astrolabe uses, with threads marking the meridian, zenith, tropics and equator.

## The Movement With No Face

Behind the main calendar, invisible from the front, sits the Equation Works - the part that makes the rest possible. It has its own driving weight and is regulated by a 36-tooth ratchet advanced by an impulse from the mean-time module, and its only job is to manufacture rates. Half a tropical year, 182 days 14 hours 54 minutes 23 seconds. The anomalistic year, 365 days 6 hours 13 minutes 56 seconds. Half a draconic month, half a synodic month, the oscillation of the lunar nodes at 173.31001 days, the oscillation of the lunar apsides at 205.89744 days, evection, the anomalistic lunar month. Tusi couples convert those rotations into clean sinusoidal push and pull; cranks produce rougher approximations; a pulley adds the effects of obliquity and eccentricity together to make the equation of time. The results travel out to the other movements three ways - shafts and bevel gears for continuous rotation, thin steel ribbons over bell cranks in the roof of the case for bounded analogue values, and rods for discrete impulses such as the one that flips the date at midnight.

## Pluto's Long Absence

On the right-hand side an orrery shows the planets circling a fixed sun against a zodiac ring. There are eight of them. Pluto was found in 1930, not long after Olsen finished his calculations, and for the next seventy-six years the World Clock's solar system was quietly out of date. Then in 2006 the International Astronomical Union redefined what a planet is, and a machine designed in the 1930s was correct again without anyone touching a gear. It is a good joke, but it points at something real about the thing: this clock is not a display of astronomical data so much as an argument that the heavens can be made out of brass, and that a locksmith with enough patience can hold 25,753 years in one room.

## Flight Context

The clock lives inside Copenhagen City Hall at 55.6756 N, 12.5694 E, on Radhuspladsen at the western end of Stroget in the heart of the medieval city. From the air the host building is the target: a long red-brick National Romantic block with a tall square clock tower, immediately northeast of the Tivoli Gardens and just north of H.C. Andersens Boulevard, which the city hall square opens onto. At 1,500-2,500 ft the square reads as the largest open paved space in central Copenhagen. Copenhagen Airport (EKCH) is 8 km southeast, Roskilde (EKRK) 31 km west, and the whole area is inside busy controlled airspace. Terrain is flat. The clock itself is in a dedicated room off the main hall, visible only from the ground - but the tower above it has a public viewing platform, which is the closest thing to an aerial view of the city that Copenhagen offers without an aircraft.
