# Assault on Copenhagen (1659)

On the freezing night of 11 February 1659, Swedish soldiers in white winter camouflage stormed the iced-over moats of Copenhagen and were repulsed by a city defended by craftsmen, students and Dutch marines.

**Location:** 55.6761, 12.5683

**Categories:** Northern War of 1655–1660, Sieges of Copenhagen, 1659 in Denmark, 17th century in Copenhagen

## Introduction

The Swedes lost their assault bridge on 9 February, and that was their mistake. The Danes measured it carefully: thirty-six feet long. Dutch marines, six hundred by one account, spent the afternoon and evening of 10 February breaking the ice on the moats of Copenhagen back forty-four feet, working in heavy snowfall under cover of darkness, so that when the Swedish stormtroopers arrived in their white winter clothing the bridges they carried fell short of the far bank by eight feet. King Charles X Gustav of Sweden had broken his own peace treaty to try to take the Danish capital. By morning the Swedes had lost as many as two thousand men, and the bridges were still floating in the icy moats, useless. The siege would drag on for another fifteen months, but the assault on Copenhagen on 11 February 1659 had answered the question of whether the city would fall.

## A king who could not stop

Charles X had everything a Swedish king of the seventeenth century could plausibly want. After the March Across the Belts a year earlier, in February 1658, his army had crossed frozen straits no commander had thought passable; the stunned Danes had signed the Treaty of Roskilde, ceding a third of their territory, including everything east of the Øresund. But at his Privy Council at Gottorp on 7 July 1658, Charles X resolved that this was not enough: he would conquer all of Denmark. Without warning, in defiance of his own treaty, he ordered his troops, who had never actually left Danish soil, to march on Copenhagen. The moral cost of breaking a fresh peace would be paid by the men who carried his bridges through snow.

## The city that decided to stay

When Swedish forces landed at Korsør on 7 August 1658, calls to flee swept Copenhagen. King Frederick III of Denmark refused, telling his court he would 'die in his nest', dø i sin rede in Danish. On 10 August he made an offer that bound the city to him: he granted commoners privileges that had been reserved for the nobility, in exchange for their labour and credit on the fortifications. The next day he ordered every building outside the city walls burned, even though those suburbs had housed roughly a third of the population, then closed the gates. They would not open for twenty-two months. Inside, three hundred cannon and mortars were dragged onto the ramparts; civilians were divided into nine companies and assigned sections of wall; muskets, arquebuses, morningstars, scythes, boiling water and tar were stockpiled. Two thousand Dutch soldiers, sent by the Republic, joined the eight thousand defenders. The professional Swedish army outside numbered about the same.

## The night of 11 February

The diversionary attack at Christianshavn and Slotsholmen on the evening of 9 February gave the defenders their gift: an abandoned Swedish assault bridge. Danish engineers measured it. Through the snowfall of 10 February, the Dutch marines and Danish workers widened the open water in the moats to forty-four feet, eight more than the thirty-six-foot bridges. Around midnight on the 11th, Swedish soldiers in white camouflage advanced across the frozen Sound from behind Valby Hill. They reached the moats. The bridges were too short. What followed has been painted many times, most famously by Vilhelm Rosenstand in 1889 with his canvas of Copenhagen students fighting on the ramparts. Cannons cut down soldiers caught between ice and water; defenders on the walls poured musket fire into men with nowhere to go. By dawn the Swedes had lost as many as two thousand men. The defenders lost about twenty.

## Aftermath and a feast

On 24 March 1659, Frederick III formally enacted the privileges he had promised the citizens, raising commoners to ranks previously closed to them. The change strained relations with the nobility and helped lead to the 1660 state of emergency in Denmark and the absolutist constitutional revolution that followed. The siege itself dragged on. It was only after Charles X died in early 1660 that his son Charles XI's regency council moved to end the war on all fronts; the Treaty of Copenhagen, signed on 27 May 1660, returned Bornholm and Trondheim to Denmark and ended the Second Northern War. Coins were minted at Copenhagen in 1659 thanking God for the city's preservation. February 11 was made a permanent holiday, the Taksigelsesfesten or Feast of Thanksgiving, marked by mandatory bell ringing, sermons and church attendance. It was celebrated annually until 1766, when it was awkwardly abolished after the Danish king's daughter Sophia Magdalena married the future Gustav III of Sweden, and remembering the deaths of Swedish soldiers became undiplomatic.

## Flight Context

Copenhagen sits at 55.676°N, 12.568°E on the eastern coast of Zealand, opposite the Swedish coast across the Øresund. The historic city centre is enclosed by the old defensive ring; Kastellet, where Danish professional soldiers were stationed in 1659, still stands as a star-shaped earthwork on the harbour's northern edge. EKCH (Copenhagen Kastrup) lies on Amager just south of the city; ESMS (Malmö Sturup) sits across the bridge in Sweden. From cruising altitude the Øresund Bridge between the two is the obvious modern landmark.
