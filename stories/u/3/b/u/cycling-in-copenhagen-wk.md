# Cycling in Copenhagen

In the city that gave its name to Copenhagenization, cycling is not a hobby or a statement but ordinary transport - with its own rush hour, its own etiquette, and its own fines.

**Location:** 55.6800, 12.5725

**Categories:** Cycling, Copenhagen, Transport in Denmark, Urban planning, Denmark

## Introduction

You cannot simply turn left in Copenhagen. Not on a bicycle, and not the way you would at home. The manoeuvre here is a two-stage box turn that locals call the Copenhagen left: you cross the intersection straight ahead, stop at the far kerb, pivot ninety degrees, wait for the light, and cross again. Do it any other way and you will be shouted at, and possibly fined 700 kroner. That single rule tells you most of what you need to know about this city. Cycling is not a lifestyle here. It is the transport system, and transport systems have rules.

## Boom, Bust, Boom

The first bicycles arrived from France in the 1890s, and within about seventeen years Copenhagen had become the cycling capital of Europe. The bicycle girl on the tourist posters was not marketing invention; she was the street. Then came Henry Ford's Model T, cheap enough and flattering enough to change what a Danish family wanted parked outside, and for roughly three decades Copenhagen followed the same road as everywhere else. By the 1970s the bicycle's share of traffic had collapsed to an all-time low of ten per cent. What turned it around was not nostalgia but arithmetic. Denmark had no significant oil reserves, two oil crises hit in quick succession, and fuel had to come from somewhere. Copenhageners demonstrated in large numbers for road space, and the town hall - slowly, over years, not in one heroic gesture - began giving it to them.

## Which Number You Believe

Copenhagen's cycling statistics are quoted constantly and rarely with their footnotes, so it is worth being careful. In 2004 the city measured a 41 per cent bicycle share and set itself a target of 50 per cent by 2015. Counting only working or studying residents of the municipality, the figure in 2012 was about 36 per cent. Counting all inhabitants who commute to work, school or university by bicycle, the widely cited number is 62 per cent. Across the eighteen municipalities of the wider metropolitan region, bicycles carried around 21 per cent of all trips by 2011. Every one of those is a real measurement of a different thing. What is not in dispute: roughly 560,000 bicycles in a city of fewer people than that, about 1.2 million kilometres pedalled every single day, and a network that had reached 397 kilometres by 2010 - by 2013 comprising some 350 kilometres of kerb-segregated track, 23 kilometres of on-street lane and 43 kilometres of green off-street route.

## The Snake

Infrastructure this heavily used eventually becomes architecture. Cykelslangen - the Bicycle Snake - opened on 28 June 2014, a 220-metre orange ribbon of elevated cycleway 4.6 metres wide, curling out over the harbour at Dybbolsbro with seven metres of clearance beneath it. Dissing+Weitling designed it, and it cost 32 million kroner to solve a problem that would elsewhere have been solved with a painted line. It is not alone. Bryggebroen, Lille Langebro, the Cirkelbroen and the Inner Harbour Bridge all cross water for people who are not in cars. Ride the chain of them and you cover most of the harbour without meeting a single lorry, which is the point: the bridges were built to make the bicycle the fastest way across the city rather than the most virtuous.

## Two Wheels, Three Wheels, Children

The most Copenhagen sight is not the commuter in office clothes. It is the cargo bike - a long box on two front wheels with a toddler or two inside, a week of groceries, sometimes a dog. Around twenty companies in the city design and sell bicycles, and the signature product is exactly this: Christiania Bikes, Nihola, and the Bullitt from Larry vs Harry. Between them the trade supports roughly 650 full-time jobs and turns over an estimated 1.3 billion kroner a year. It is a small industry by Danish standards and an enormous one by the standards of bicycle manufacturing, and it exists because the city built streets on which hauling a child by pedal is the obvious choice rather than the brave one.

## The Choreography

Bicycle nirvana it may be, but a first-time visitor should understand that this is fast, dense, unforgiving traffic. Copenhageners ride at a brisk pace, close together, on their way to work rather than out for a pleasant spin, and it takes very little to break the rhythm. Skip the rush hours - roughly 07:30 to 09:00 and 15:00 to 17:00 - until you have the hang of it. Keep right so faster riders can pass. Stay off the pavement, which is never permitted, and stay on the cycle track rather than the road wherever one exists. Signal: hand straight up means stopping, hand out to the side means turning. No right turn on red, whatever you see locals doing. Lights after dark are mandatory and the police enforce it. Most of these carry the same 700-kroner penalty, which is a strong argument for paying attention. If you need to cover more ground, bicycles ride free on the S-train, in the first, middle and last carriages; the Metro takes them too, outside its own rush-hour curfew and with a separate bicycle ticket.

## Flight Context

The cycling city is centred on 55.68 N, 12.5725 E, and from the air its infrastructure is genuinely visible. At 1,500-2,500 ft over the harbour you can pick out Cykelslangen as an orange curve near Dybbolsbro, the pale deck of Bryggebroen crossing to Islands Brygge, and the broad separated tracks running the length of H.C. Andersens Boulevard and Norrebrogade. The chain of Copenhagen's lakes - Sortedams, Peblinge and Sankt Jorgens - traces the line of the old ramparts and the cycle track that has run beside them since the 1910s. Copenhagen Airport (EKCH) is 8 km southeast on Amager; Roskilde (EKRK) 30 km west. Terrain is flat, which is half the explanation for everything below. Wind, not gradient, is the local cyclist's enemy, and it comes off the Oresund from the west most of the year.
