# Tivoli Gardens

Georg Carstensen talked a king into letting him build a pleasure garden by arguing that amused people do not think about politics. The argument worked, and the garden outlived the monarchy that granted it.

**Location:** 55.6736, 12.5683

**Categories:** 1843 establishments in Denmark, Amusement parks in Denmark, Culture in Copenhagen, History of Copenhagen, Pleasure gardens, Parks in Copenhagen

## Introduction

Georg Carstensen wanted fifteen acres of Copenhagen's defensive earthworks, and he knew exactly how to ask. When people are amusing themselves, he told King Christian VIII, they do not think about politics. It was 1843, revolution was in the European air, and the argument was good enough to earn a five-year charter over the glacis outside the West Gate — the sloping ground kept clear so that cannon could sweep it. On 15 August, Tivoli & Vauxhall opened there: a theatre, bandstands, restaurants, flower beds, a merry-go-round and a primitive scenic railway, arranged among buildings in the exotic style of an imaginary Orient. After dark, coloured lamps came on and fireworks burst over a lake that was not a lake at all, but a surviving stretch of the city moat. That five-year charter has now run for a hundred and eighty-three years.

## Borrowed Names, Northern Waltzes

Even the name was an import twice over. Tivoli came from the Jardin de Tivoli in Paris, which had taken it from the town of Tivoli outside Rome; Vauxhall came from the pleasure gardens in London. What Copenhagen supplied was the music. Hans Christian Lumbye was the garden's musical director from 1843 until 1872, and he wrote for the place the way a composer writes for a room he knows — "Salute to the Ticket Holders of Tivoli", "Carnival Joys", "A Festive Night at Tivoli". He had listened hard to the Strauss family in Vienna and was soon being called the Strauss of the North, a nickname that flatters Vienna as much as it flatters him. The Tivoli Symphony Orchestra still plays his work in the gardens he wrote it for.

## The Peacock's Tail

In 1874 a Chinese-style pantomime theatre replaced an older, smaller one on the same spot. Vilhelm Dahlerup, who also designed the Royal Danish Theatre, built it as a piece of historicist confectionery, and gave it a curtain that is not a curtain: a mechanical peacock's tail that unfolds across the proscenium and takes five men to operate. The audience stands outside in the open air; the stage is indoors. What plays there is Italian commedia dell'arte, brought to Denmark by Giuseppe Casorti and kept alive continuously since — Cassander the old father, his daughter Columbine, her lover Harlequin, and Pierrot, the foolish servant who has always been the children's favourite. Nobody speaks. In a garden that now draws visitors from everywhere, silence has turned out to be the most durable language available.

## The Exhibitions Nobody Advertises Now

There is a line in Tivoli's history that the garden does not put on its posters. In the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, Tivoli — like zoos and exhibition grounds across Europe — staged human exhibitions: groups of people from colonised parts of the world displayed to paying Danish crowds as objects of curiosity. The record of who they were is thin, which is itself part of the injury; the people put on show were catalogued by origin rather than by name, and most of their names are simply gone. They were not attractions. They were men, women and children who travelled, worked and in some cases died a long way from home so that a European public could look at them. Tivoli's charm is real. So is this, and the garden's own encyclopaedia entry keeps it on the page.

## The Centenary and the Fire

On 15 August 1943, Tivoli turned one hundred, and 112,802 people came through the gates in a single day — a record set in the fourth year of German occupation, which says something about what Copenhageners wanted from the place. Ten months later the occupation took it back. In late June 1944 the gardens were attacked by the Peter Group, a unit of the Danish Nazi Schalburg Corps carrying out reprisals for resistance sabotage of German war production, and the concert hall burned. German-controlled newspapers blamed the resistance. It was not until 1956 that a new concert hall stood in its place. Sections of the wooden roller coaster were blown up in the same campaign; officials decided that reopening mattered, and Rutschebanen was running again twenty-five days later.

## Never, So to Speak, Finished

Rutschebanen has been climbing its wooden hills since 1914, one of the oldest operating wooden coasters anywhere, and it is still braked by an operator riding the train to keep it from getting away down the drops. Around it the garden turns over constantly: Dæmonen throws an Immelmann loop and a zero-G roll into one minute and forty-six seconds, the Star Flyer swings riders eighty metres above the rooftops, and on summer Fridays the Fredagsrock series has filled the gardens for everyone from the Beach Boys to Kanye West. Four and a quarter million people came in 2024, making Tivoli the most visited amusement park in Scandinavia and the second oldest still operating anywhere, after Dyrehavsbakken up the coast at Klampenborg. "Tivoli will never, so to speak, be finished," Carstensen said in 1844. A century later Walt Disney said almost exactly the same thing about Disneyland — the connection between the two parks is a sentence, repeated, rather than anything better documented. In Danish, Norwegian, Swedish and Icelandic, meanwhile, tivoli stopped being a name at all. It just means amusement park.

## Flight Context

Tivoli occupies a walled rectangle at 55.6736°N, 12.5683°E, immediately southwest of Copenhagen Central Station and south of City Hall Square. From 2,000–3,000 feet the garden reads as a dark green block with a bright lake at its centre, wedged between the station's long glass roof and the wide open apron of Rådhuspladsen; the 80-metre Star Flyer tower is the tallest thing inside the wall. Copenhagen Airport (EKCH) is 7 km southeast, with the standard Øresund approaches passing east of the city; Roskilde (EKRK) is 29 km west. Between mid-November and early January the gardens are lit for Christmas and are visible after dark from a considerable distance.
