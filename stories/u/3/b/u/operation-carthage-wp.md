# Operation Carthage

On 21 March 1945 the RAF destroyed the Gestapo's Copenhagen headquarters at rooftop height - and, a mile and a half short of the target, a Catholic girls' school.

**Location:** 55.6778, 12.5617

**Categories:** World War II, Military history, Copenhagen, Aviation history, Danish resistance

## Introduction

The Gestapo put its prisoners in the attic on purpose. The Shellhus, a six-storey office block near the lakes in central Copenhagen, held the card indexes and dossiers that could unravel the entire Danish resistance, and the Germans understood perfectly well that the RAF might one day come for them. So they moved captured resistance members into cells directly beneath the roof, where the first bombs would fall. The building's best defence was not its flak. It was the Danes inside it. On the morning of 21 March 1945, eighteen Mosquitos came anyway.

## The Request the RAF Kept Refusing

The resistance had been asking for months. Destroy the Shellhus, they argued, and you destroy the files; hit it fast enough and the men held upstairs might get out in the confusion. The RAF said no. The target sat in a crowded city centre and could only be attacked from very low level, which meant that any error would land on Copenhagen rather than on the Germans. What changed the answer was partly persistence and partly precedent: on 31 October 1944 the same wing had wrecked the Gestapo headquarters in Aarhus and got away with it. Approval came in early 1945. Planning then took weeks. Scale models of the Shellhus and the streets around it were built so that pilots and navigators could memorise an approach they would fly at the height of the rooftops, at speed, with no second chance to look.

## Rooftop Level

The force lifted off from RAF Fersfield in Norfolk that morning and reached Copenhagen after eleven o'clock: eighteen de Havilland Mosquito FB.VI fighter-bombers of No. 140 Wing, drawn from No. 21 Squadron RAF, No. 464 Squadron RAAF and No. 487 Squadron RNZAF, flying in three waves of six. Two more Mosquitos from the RAF Film Production Unit came to photograph the result. Thirty Mustangs flew cover and went after the anti-aircraft guns. Then, in the first wave, one Mosquito clipped a lamp post. The impact damaged a wing, and the aircraft went down into a row of garages roughly a kilometre and a half short of the Shellhus, where it burned. The crews behind were looking for smoke and flame in a city they knew only from a model. They found smoke and flame in the wrong place.

## Frederiksberg Alle 74

The garages stood beside the Institut Jeanne d'Arc, a Catholic girls' school on Frederiksberg Alle run by the Sisters of St Joseph since 1924. That morning it held 482 children, 34 sisters and eight lay teachers. Two aircraft of the second wave, and all but one of the third, bombed the burning garages with high explosive and napalm in the belief that they were finishing off the target. The napalm carried fire into the surrounding streets. The conventional bombs brought down part of the school. Rescuers reached the wreckage and got children out ahead of the flames, and they saved many, and they did not save enough. Eighty-six children died. So did ten of the sisters who taught them, several teachers, two firemen, and two fathers who went into the building after their own children - something close to twenty adults, though Danish and British accounts differ by a name or two. Sixty-seven children and thirty-five adults were injured. Across the city, roughly 123 civilians were killed that day - Danish tallies run a little higher. They died because a wing touched a lamp post, and because the crews who followed bombed a fire instead of a building.

## What the Bombs Did Reach

The raid also worked. A reconnaissance aircraft photographed the Shellhus the following day and found the west wing of the six-storey building reduced almost to ground level; the Danish underground supplied a picture of it burning from end to end. The dossiers were gone, and Gestapo operations in Denmark were severely disrupted in the weeks that turned out to be the last of the occupation. Eighteen prisoners got out of the attic alive. Eight did not. Fifty-five German soldiers and forty-seven Danish employees of the Gestapo died in the building. On the Allied side, four Mosquitos and two Mustangs were lost and nine airmen were killed. The ruins gave up more later: on 14 July 1945 the remains of an unidentified man were recovered from the rubble and taken to the University of Copenhagen's Department of Forensic Medicine, and again four days later. Both were buried in Bispebjerg Cemetery that September, still nameless.

## The Long Silence

The aircrews were not told what had happened at the school until after victory in Europe. The Institut Jeanne d'Arc never reopened; most of its surviving pupils were transferred to Institut Sankt Joseph, and a French school for Copenhagen eventually opened elsewhere on Frederiksberg in 1954. A monument by the sculptor Max Andersen was raised on the school's site and inaugurated on 23 March 1953, eight years and two days after the raid. Housing now stands around it. In 2021 the Danish film The Shadow in My Eye put the children back at the centre of the story for an audience that had mostly inherited the Shellhus half of it. Both halves are true at once, and neither cancels the other. The raid freed men who would otherwise have been shot and burned the records of an occupying secret police. It also killed eighty-six children who had come to school that Wednesday morning. Copenhagen has never had to choose between those sentences, and it does not.

## Flight Context

The Shellhus site sits at 55.678 degrees north, 12.562 degrees east, in central Copenhagen between the Lakes and the main railway station; the Jeanne d'Arc memorial stands about 1.5 km to the southwest on Frederiksberg Alle. The 1945 attack was flown at rooftop height across the low, tightly built Frederiksberg and Vesterbro street grid - terrain that offers almost no vertical reference, which is precisely what made the raid so hard. Modern flight over the city centre is constrained: Copenhagen Kastrup (EKCH) lies 8 km southeast on Amager, Roskilde (EKRK) about 30 km west, and the whole area sits inside busy controlled airspace. From 2,000-3,000 ft the useful landmarks are the Lakes, the Radhuspladsen tower, the green wedge of Frederiksberg Gardens, and Frederiksberg Alle's straight tree-lined run northeast toward the city. Visibility in March is often 10 km or better under high overcast, with a low sun angle from the south.
