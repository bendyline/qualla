# Ny Carlsberg Glyptotek

A brewer's private collection that outgrew his house and became one of Europe's great sculpture museums - Egyptian mummies, Rodin bronzes, forty Gauguins, and palm trees under a copper dome.

**Location:** 55.6725, 12.5719

**Categories:** Ny Carlsberg Glyptotek, Art museums and galleries in Copenhagen, Collections of classical sculpture, Egyptological collections, Hack Kampmann buildings, Historicist architecture in Copenhagen

## Introduction

Walk in from H.C. Andersens Boulevard on a February afternoon and the first room you reach is a jungle. Tall palms stand under a dome of copper and wrought iron, a fountain runs beneath them, mosaic spreads across the floor, and the Danish winter presses against the glass overhead. Carl Jacobsen built this Winter Garden as bait. He was a brewer's son who suspected Danes would not walk into a museum of antiquities for the antiquities, so he gave them a reason - greenery, in a country that spends half the year without any - and trusted that once inside, they would keep walking. More than a century on, the trick still works.

## A Villa Overrun by Marble

Carl Jacobsen (1842-1914) inherited a brewing fortune and spent an extraordinary amount of it on sculpture. Antique work drew him first, though he accumulated French and Danish pieces steadily as well, and by 1882 the collection had colonised his home. When he extended his private villa with a winter garden that year, statues quickly outnumbered the plants in it. He opened the whole thing to the public in the same year, then kept building. By 1885 his house museum had swollen to nineteen galleries - the first fourteen designed by Vilhelm Dahlerup, the last four by Hack Kampmann, who also redesigned the original winter garden. The word Glyptotek came from Ludwig I's Glyptothek in Munich, and behind it lie two Greek roots: glyphein, to carve, and theke, a storing place. A place where carved things are kept.

## A Gift with Conditions

On 8 March 1888 Jacobsen gave the collection away, donating it to the Danish State and the City of Copenhagen on the single condition that they house it properly. Copenhagen had just abandoned its old fortifications, so the authorities offered a site on a ravelin outside Holcks Bastion in the Western Rampart, immediately south of Tivoli Gardens. Jacobsen disliked it. He thought it sat too far from the centre, and he found Tivoli common. What he wanted was a building on the emerging city hall square. He lost that argument and accepted the ravelin, the moat was filled in, and Dahlerup's museum opened on 1 May 1897 holding only the modern collection - French and Danish work of the preceding century.

## Then the Antiquities Arrived

In January 1899 Jacobsen handed over his antique collection too, which immediately made the year-old building inadequate. Kampmann designed the extension, a plainer neo-classical range of galleries wrapped around a central auditorium, and it was inaugurated in 1906; Dahlerup's Winter Garden was built to join the two wings, and the join is the best thing in the building. The Dahlerup wing is unapologetically lavish - red brick, polished granite columns, Venetian Renaissance swagger. Kampmann's is quiet by comparison. In 1996 Henning Larsen filled one of the courtyards with a minimalist infill that opens onto the roof, and a major restoration followed in 2006 under Dissing + Weitling with Bonde Ljungar Arkitekter.

## Ten Thousand Things

The collections now run past 10,000 works. The Egyptian holdings alone exceed 1,900 pieces spanning 3000 BCE to the first century CE, begun in 1882 when Jacobsen bought a sarcophagus from the Egyptian Museum in Cairo and expanded considerably when the Ny Carlsberg Foundation funded excavations in Egypt led by the English archaeologist W. M. F. Petrie; the mummies are displayed in a crypt-like gallery beneath the main floors. The Greek, Roman and Etruscan collection includes some 950 sculptures and Etruscan antiquities bought through Wolfgang Helbig, the German archaeologist who acted as Jacobsen's agent in Rome for twenty-five years - a reminder that this material was assembled through the nineteenth-century Roman dealer trade, decades before the export controls and provenance standards that govern antiquities today. The Near Eastern collection reaches from 6500 BCE to 650 CE across the Levant, Mesopotamia, Anatolia and Persia.

## Bronze, Paint and Acoustics

The French rooms are the reason many visitors come. Nineteenth-century painting runs from David and Manet through Monet, Cezanne, Pissarro, Renoir, Degas and Bonnard to van Gogh, Gauguin and Toulouse-Lautrec, with Gauguin the most heavily represented of all at more than forty works. The sculpture is stronger still: a Rodin collection reckoned the most important outside France, work by Carpeaux, and the complete set of Degas bronzes, dancers included. The Danish rooms hold Golden Age painting by Eckersberg, Kobke and Lundbye alongside the largest gathering of Danish Golden Age sculpture anywhere, and Stephan Sinding's figures turn up throughout the building. The European collection carries the story from Canova and Sergel to Picasso and Giacometti. Kampmann's auditorium, meanwhile, has proved to have unusually good acoustics and hosts the Helge Jacobsen concert series; the halls around it are used for early music, occasionally during opening hours, so the sculpture sometimes comes with singing. In 2019 the museum received 514,608 visitors.

## Flight Context

The Glyptotek stands at 55.6725 N, 12.5719 E, on the block between Tivoli Gardens and Christiansborg, one street back from the harbour. From 1,500-3,000 ft AGL locate Tivoli's compact green rectangle immediately northwest and the open expanse of Radhuspladsen beyond it; the museum is the roof cluster on Tivoli's southeastern edge, its glass Winter Garden dome catching sun in the middle of the complex. The line of the vanished Western Rampart still shows in the curve of the surrounding streets and lakes. Copenhagen Kastrup (EKCH) lies about 7 km southeast; Roskilde (EKRK) is roughly 28 km west. Central Copenhagen sits under Kastrup's approach corridors - a landmark to spot from an airliner, not a place to loiter over.
