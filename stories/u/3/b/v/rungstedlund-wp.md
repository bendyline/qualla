# Rungstedlund

Karen Blixen was born in this house on the Øresund shore, spent seventeen years running a failing coffee farm in Kenya, came back, wrote everything she is remembered for, and is buried in the garden.

**Location:** 55.8833, 12.5433

**Categories:** Historic house museums in Denmark, Literary museums in Denmark, Museums in the Capital Region of Denmark, Houses in Hørsholm Municipality, Karen Blixen

## Introduction

Karen Blixen was born at Rungstedlund on 17 April 1885 and buried in its garden on 7 September 1962, at the foot of a mound named for a poet who died a century before she was born. Between those two dates she spent seventeen years on the other side of the world trying to grow coffee at an altitude where coffee does not grow. Everything she is read for now was written in this house after that attempt collapsed - in one particular room, at a desk, in Danish and in English, under a man's name. The building is a low white country house on the Øresund coast road north of Copenhagen, unremarkable to look at, and it has been absorbing writers for two hundred and fifty years.

## The Inn on the Shore Road

The property's paper trail starts in 1520, when the Crown owned it. The oldest part of the house standing today dates from about 1680, when Rungstedlund was an inn attached to a working farm - a place where travellers on the coast road stopped, ate and slept. Ludvig Holberg stayed here. So did Johannes Ewald, who lived at the inn from 1773 to 1775 recovering from illness and wrote some of the best Danish verse of the century while he did: the ode *Rungsteds Lyksaligheder*, in praise of this exact stretch of shoreline. His 1779 singspiel *Fiskerne* contains the song "Kong Christian stod ved højen mast," which Denmark still uses as its royal anthem. The inn closed in 1803. Aron David, who owned the place from 1821 to 1868, folded the neighbouring properties of Rungstedgaard, Sømandshvile and Folehavegård into it.

## Wilhelm Dinesen's House

In 1879 the estate was bought by Wilhelm Dinesen, soldier and writer, who moved in with Ingeborg Westenholz after their marriage in 1881. Karen, born on the estate in 1885, was the second of their five children. The house then had four wings; two of them burned in 1898 and were never rebuilt, which is why the building today has the slightly incomplete, L-shaped look it does. On 28 March 1895 Wilhelm Dinesen hanged himself. Karen was nine. She would write about him with an admiration that never dimmed, and something of his restlessness carried straight into her - he had fought in the Second Schleswig War, joined the French army against Prussia, and spent sixteen months living among the Chippewa in Wisconsin before returning to Denmark to write hunting letters under the name Boganis. His daughter left for British East Africa at twenty-eight.

## Six Thousand Acres in the Ngong Hills

She arrived in December 1913 and married Bror von Blixen-Finecke in Mombasa the following January. The Karen Coffee Company's Mbogani farm ran to six thousand acres below the Ngong Hills, six hundred of them planted with coffee, and it was the wrong crop for the elevation. Drought, collapsing prices in the depression and poor management finished what altitude started. The farm existed at all because British colonial rule had reserved the Kenyan highlands for European settlers, and it functioned because Kikuyu, Wakamba, Kavirondo, Swahili and Maasai people worked it - a fact her book renders with warmth and, unavoidably, from the top of the arrangement. Modern readers of *Out of Africa* are divided about her, and the division is real: the same pages contain genuine attentiveness to individual people and the settled assumptions of a woman who owned the land they lived on. She contracted syphilis from her husband around 1915 and was divorced in 1925. Denys Finch Hatton, her lover through the later years, died in a light-aircraft crash in March 1931. She left Kenya that August, bankrupt.

## The Ewald Room

She came home at forty-six with no money, no marriage and no farm, and started writing. *Seven Gothic Tales* appeared in America in 1934 under the name Isak Dinesen, a pseudonym that was both a shield and, given her father, a claim. *Out of Africa* followed in 1937, then *Winter's Tales* in 1942, *Last Tales* in 1957, *Anecdotes of Destiny* in 1958 with "Babette's Feast" inside it, and *Shadows on the Grass* in 1960. Most of it was written in the Ewald Room, the corner of the house associated with the poet who had convalesced there in the 1770s. The Nobel committee came close three times: she was proposed in 1959, finished third in 1961, and was on the 1962 shortlist when she died that September, at seventy-seven, apparently of malnutrition. Members of the Swedish Academy later called passing her over a mistake.

## Ewald's Mound

In 1958 Blixen and her siblings set up the Rungstedlund Foundation to hold the estate after her death, and the Karen Blixen Museum opened in 1991. The house has been kept close to how she left it - her furniture, her decoration, her books, and a complete collection of her oil paintings, which almost nobody knows she made. The Danish Academy, which she helped found in 1960, is based here, and the Rungstedlund Award is presented on her birthday each year. Most of the grounds are managed as a bird sanctuary; there are flower beds cut every day through the summer to keep fresh flowers in the rooms. Her grave is at the foot of Ewalds Høj, a short walk from the room where she wrote and the room where she was born.

## Flight Context

Rungstedlund lies at 55.883 N, 12.543 E, on the Øresund coast at Rungsted about 24 km north of central Copenhagen. From the air the reference points are the shoreline and Rungsted Havn's marina basins; the house and its wooded park form a distinct green block just inland of the coast road, between the water and the railway. Recommended viewing altitude is 1,500 to 3,000 feet, low and slow along the coast - the Swedish shore is clearly visible across the sound on a good day. Copenhagen Kastrup (EKCH) is about 30 km south and its airspace covers the whole approach, so coordinate before running the coast; Roskilde (EKRK) to the south-west is the usual light-aircraft base, and Malmö Sturup (ESMS) lies across the Øresund. Summer afternoons bring sea haze; the crispest light on this coast is a cold clear morning with the sun coming up over Sweden.
