# Jægersborg Dyrehave

A UNESCO-listed forest north of Copenhagen whose roads were cut in a star pattern so that a seventeenth-century king could watch dogs run down deer from a single point.

**Location:** 55.7844, 12.5681

**Categories:** Parks and open spaces in Lyngby-Taarbæk Municipality, Forests of Greater Copenhagen, Tourist attractions in the Capital Region of Denmark, Danish Culture Canon, World Heritage Sites in Denmark

## Introduction

Look at Dyrehaven from above and the forest gives away its purpose. Straight rides cut through eleven square kilometres of oak and beech, converging on open ground in perfect spokes. This is not landscape design for beauty. It is a machine for watching, laid out so that a king standing at the hub could see his hounds drive a stag down any one of those avenues without moving. Christian V learned the technique at the court of Louis XIV and brought it home to Zealand, and three and a half centuries later the geometry is still legible from a light aircraft at two thousand feet. In July 2015 UNESCO inscribed it, along with two neighbouring forests, as the Par force hunting landscape in North Zealand - a World Heritage Site for a form of hunting nobody has practised here in generations.

## Boveskov, Fenced

The wood was old before it was royal. Recorded as Boveskov in Valdemar the Victorious's census of 1231, the Liber Census Daniæ, it lay in what is now the western and southern part of the park. In 1669 Frederik III decided to fence it and drive the surrounding wild deer inside. The method was earthwork: dig a ditch, pile the spoil into a bank on the outer side, top the bank with posts and woven branch fencing, and let the drop from bank to ditch do the rest. Deer could get in and not out. The scheme covered around three square kilometres and was never finished - Frederik III died in 1670 with the work incomplete. You can still walk the ditch and bank for a long stretch in the south-east of the park, a soft green ripple in the leaf litter that most visitors step over without noticing.

## The Village That Was Told to Leave

Christian V wanted something far larger than his father's enclosure. Par force hunting needs room, so he pushed the boundary north to take in the fields of Stokkerup and the woods that are now Jægersborg Hegn, tripling the park to sixteen square kilometres. The fields belonged to farmers, and the farmers were ordered out. They were told to pull down their own houses, carry the timber away, and use it to rebuild farms elsewhere that had been left empty by the Northern Wars. Their compensation was three years without taxes. The village pond still lies out on the open ground now called Eremitagesletten, a small flat gleam of water in the grass with no village around it - the only thing left of Stokkerup, and the reason the plain that tourists photograph for its emptiness is empty at all.

## Dinner Without Witnesses

At the centre of the star, on the crown of the plain, stands the Hermitage. Lauritz de Thurah built it for Christian VI between 1734 and 1736 at a cost of eighteen thousand rigsdaler, a Baroque banqueting lodge for the hunt. Its famous feature was mechanical. A hoist raised the laid table from the basement into the dining room, so that the king and his guests could eat with no servants in the room at all - dining *en ermitage*, in seclusion, which is where the building's name comes from. The apparatus was temperamental and was removed in the late eighteenth century. The lodge remains, small and pale and startlingly exposed on its rise, visible for kilometres across the grass and still the single point on which every hunting road in the forest converges.

## Nineteen Red Gates

You enter Dyrehaven through a red gate carrying the reigning monarch's insignia in paint on top; there are nineteen of them, each with an identical gatehouse alongside that now serves as a forest warden's home. The busiest is Klampenborg gate, straight across from the S-train station. Inside, the forest is managed as forest rather than as timber: old trees come down only when they threaten someone, which is why Dyrehaven holds gnarled, hollowing oaks that a commercial plantation would have cleared a century ago. Around 2,100 deer live among them - roughly 1,700 fallow, 300 red and 100 sika - and they are entirely unbothered by people, which unnerves first-time visitors more than it should. The park's northern edge shifted over time: Jægersborg Hegn was separated off in 1832, and the boundary was pushed past the Mølleåen stream in 1913.

## Theatre in the Wolf Valleys

Ulvedalene, the hilliest ground in the park, was shoved into its ridges and hollows by the last ice age; Danish children still sledge down Djævlebakken. In the summer of 1910 the actor Adam Poulsen and the newspaperman Henrik Cavling had the idea of staging plays in the natural bowl, opening with Oehlenschläger's *Hagbarth og Signe*. The architect Jens Ferdinand Willumsen laid out seating for four thousand with standing room for two thousand more, deliberately keeping tickets cheap. The open-air theatre ran until 1949, went quiet for almost fifty years, and was revived in 1996 by the actress Birgitte Price with Heiberg's *Elverhøj*. Productions come every two or three years now - *Røde Orm* in 2018, *Ragnarok* in 2023. The forest keeps its other rituals too: the Eremitageløbet road race on the first Sunday of October, and on the first Sunday of November the Hubertus Hunt, run since 1900, thirteen kilometres and thirty-five obstacles from Peter Lieps Hus with a pause at the Hermitage, about 160 riders and as many as 40,000 people standing in the cold to watch.

## Flight Context

Jægersborg Dyrehave lies at 55.784 N, 12.568 E, immediately north of Copenhagen in Lyngby-Taarbæk Municipality and running down to the Øresund shore at Klampenborg. It is unmistakable from the air: an eleven-square-kilometre block of dense canopy in otherwise suburban terrain, with the pale open sweep of Eremitagesletten in its northern half and the Hermitage lodge as a bright dot at the convergence of the star-cut rides. Recommended viewing altitude is 2,000 to 5,000 feet, high enough to take in the whole radial road pattern in one frame. The bright cluster of Dyrehavsbakken marks the southern corner. Copenhagen Kastrup (EKCH) is about 20 km south with busy controlled airspace overhead; Roskilde (EKRK) to the west is the usual general-aviation base, and Malmö Sturup (ESMS) sits across the sound in Sweden. Best light is low sun in autumn, when the beech canopy turns and the hunting avenues throw long shadows that make the geometry obvious.
