# Dyrehavsbakken

Nobody founded Bakken - it grew, unplanned, around a spring a woman found in the royal forest in 1583, and it has been entertaining Copenhagen ever since.

**Location:** 55.7761, 12.5739

**Categories:** Amusement parks in Denmark, Tourist attractions in the Capital Region of Denmark, Culture of Denmark, Buildings and structures in Lyngby-Taarbæk Municipality, 16th-century establishments in Denmark

## Introduction

Nobody founded Bakken. In 1583 a woman named Kirsten Piil found a natural spring in the royal forest north of Copenhagen, and that was the whole of it - no charter, no investor, no plan. Copenhagen's own water was foul enough that clean water counted as a small mercy, and word went around that this water healed. People walked out to drink it. Where crowds gather, sellers follow, and behind the sellers came the jugglers, musicians and hawkers who understood that a thirsty pilgrim with a coin in her pocket is a customer. Four and a half centuries later the spring is still there, in the southern corner of Jægersborg Dyrehave, and so are the crowds - between 2.5 and 2.9 million of them a year, riding a wooden roller coaster built in 1932 within earshot of the water that started it all.

## Kirsten Piil's Spring

Almost nothing is known about Kirsten Piil herself. Tradition holds that she was a pious woman whose devotion gave the water its curative power, which is the kind of thing tradition says when the records are silent. What is documented is the effect. Copenhagen in the late sixteenth century was a city where drinking the local water was a genuine gamble, and a clean spring an hour's walk into the woods was worth the walk. The sick came for the cure. The healthy came because everyone else was going. By the time anyone thought to write it down, the clearing around Kirsten Piils Kilde had become a seasonal market of tents and stalls, and the market had become the point of the trip. The spring is still marked in the forest today, a modest thing entirely out of proportion to what grew around it.

## Locked Out, Then Let Back In

The party was interrupted by a king. In 1669 Frederik III began fencing the woods to hold wild deer, and his son Christian V expanded the enclosure after 1670 into a vast hunting ground that took the name Jægersborg Dyrehave in 1671. Royal hunting meant closed gates, and for most of a century the crowds were shut out of their own spring. Frederik V reopened the forest to the public in 1756, and the entertainers came straight back. Pjerrot arrived around 1800 with the English Price family - the white-faced, red-mouthed clown who is still the park's mascot and still performs for children every day it is open. The permanent buildings followed: Sanssouci in 1866, the Bakkens Hvile music hall in 1877, and in 1885 a Tent Owners' Association that finally organised what had been three hundred years of improvisation.

## The Oldest Park, and What That Actually Means

Guinness World Records lists Bakken as the oldest amusement park in operation, and the claim rests on 1583. It is worth being precise about what is being claimed. No ride at Bakken is from the sixteenth century; almost nothing standing there is even from the nineteenth. What has been continuous is the place and its purpose - the same clearing, used to amuse the same city, without a break long enough to count as a stop. Tivoli Gardens, twelve kilometres south in central Copenhagen, opened in 1843 and remains Denmark's most visited attraction and the one the world has heard of. Bakken is the second. The two operate on opposite principles: Tivoli charges at the gate, while walking into Bakken costs nothing at all. You pay per ride, in coupons, which means the park is also just a place people go to eat, drink and watch the clown for free.

## Rutschebanen

The wooden coaster at the centre of the park has been running since 1932. Its numbers are modest by modern standards - an 852-metre course, a 22-metre high point, a top speed of about 75 km/h - and entirely beside the point. What made Rutschebanen famous was the brakeman. Until the end of the 2009 season a human being rode with each train, standing at the brake lever and controlling the descent by hand and judgement, the way coasters were run before computers took the job. American Coaster Enthusiasts recognised it as a Coaster Classic for exactly that reason. When the trains were updated after 2009 and the brakemen retired, ACE rescinded the designation. The ride is still there, still wooden, still loud in the trees. Around it the park has kept adding: Mariehønen in 1981, Mine Train Ulven in 1997, Tornado in 2009, De Vilde Mus in 2012. The Racing coaster ran from 1972 until 2020.

## A Day in the Woods

Bakken opens daily from the end of March to the end of August, which is very close to the span of a Danish summer. The season has a particular texture. Deer wander the forest right up to the edge of the lights - Dyrehaven holds around 2,100 of them - and you can walk out of a laser shoot-out and be among ancient oaks in under a minute. The food is stubbornly Danish underneath the hamburgers: pølser, æbleskiver, smørrebrød, Carlsberg and Tuborg in the lounges. The Circus Revue plays through the summer, cabaret runs at Bakkens Hvile, and Pjerrot does his rounds. It is not polished the way Tivoli is polished. That is more or less the reason Copenhageners keep coming, as they have since a woman found water in the woods.

## Flight Context

Dyrehavsbakken sits at 55.776 N, 12.574 E, about 10 km north of central Copenhagen at the southern edge of Jægersborg Dyrehave and roughly 600 m from Klampenborg S-train station. From the air the park reads as a bright cluster of structures punched into an otherwise unbroken canopy on the Øresund side of the forest, with the Hermitage plain opening pale and treeless to the north. Recommended viewing altitude is 2,000 to 4,000 feet - low enough to separate Rutschebanen's wooden structure from the surrounding trees. Copenhagen Kastrup (EKCH) lies about 20 km south and its northern approach and departure corridors run nearby, so expect controlled airspace; Roskilde (EKRK) is the practical general-aviation field to the west, and Malmö Sturup (ESMS) sits across the Øresund in Sweden. Summer visibility over the sound is generally good, with sea haze common on warm afternoons and low stratus off the water in spring and autumn.
