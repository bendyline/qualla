# Inge Lehmann

From a small office in Copenhagen, a Danish seismologist read a New Zealand earthquake wrong-side-out and found the solid iron heart of the planet - then spent the rest of a 104-year life being slowly given her due.

**Location:** 55.8706, 12.5044

**Categories:** Danish geophysicists, Seismologists, Danish women geologists, Women in science, History of science

## Introduction

In 1929 an earthquake shook the South Island of New Zealand, and seismographs in Sverdlovsk and Irkutsk recorded something they were not supposed to. Those Russian stations sat in the shadow zone - the band of the globe where the Earth's molten core was thought to bend and swallow seismic waves before they could arrive. Waves arrived anyway, at significant amplitude. Most people looking at those traces saw noise. Inge Lehmann, running Denmark's seismological department from an office in Copenhagen, saw a reflection, and reasoned that something hard and spherical was sitting at the centre of the planet, bouncing the signal back out. She was right. She is buried at Hørsholm Cemetery, north of the city, about five thousand kilometres above the thing she found.

## A School That Taught Girls the Same Things

Lehmann was born in Copenhagen on 13 May 1888 and grew up in Østerbro. Her father, Alfred Georg Ludvik Lehmann, was a pioneer of experimental psychology in Denmark; her mother, Ida Sophie Tørsleff, kept the house. The decisive institution in her childhood was a school. Hanna Adler - Niels Bohr's aunt - ran the Fællesskolen on a principle that was genuinely unusual in Denmark at the time: boys and girls in the same rooms, taking the same curriculum. The prevailing view held that intellectual strain during puberty damaged girls, and until 1903 they were not permitted to sit the entrance examination that opened upper-secondary school to boys at fifteen. Adler simply taught them the same material anyway, and staffed her school with women graduates whom the universities and gymnasiums would not hire. Lehmann named her father and Hanna Adler as the two greatest influences on her mind.

## The Cambridge Year

At eighteen she took a first-rank mark in the University of Copenhagen entrance examination, and in 1907 began mathematics, chemistry and physics there. From 1910 to 1911 she read mathematics at Newnham College, Cambridge, where she ran into the machinery of a university that admitted women to lectures but not to the degree, the standing, or the advancement. She was not permitted to participate fully in her own studies. During that first year she had a breakdown, and in 1912 she went home to Denmark. What followed was seven years as an actuarial assistant, from 1911 to 1918, calculating other people's risk. In March 1917 she broke off an engagement and decided against marrying at all, on the grounds that an academic career and a husband were not, for a woman of her generation, available at the same time.

## Learning Seismology on Her Own Time

The turn came in 1925, when she was assigned as assistant to the seismologist Niels Erik Nørlund. Nobody trained her in the field; she took it up and taught it to herself. By 1928 she had a magister scientiarum in seismology and had been made head of the seismological department of the Geodætisk Institut, responsible for three observatories, two of them in Greenland. She ran the Copenhagen instrument personally and wrote up its readings. Research was not in the job description. She did it anyway, in the margins of an administrative post, and it was from that position - not a professorship, not a laboratory, not a team - that she published the 1936 paper titled simply *P'*, arguing that the Earth has an inner core: a solid sphere inside the liquid one, a third shell beneath the mantle and the outer core.

## Two or Three Years, and Then Thirty-Five

The senior figures of the field came round quickly. Beno Gutenberg, Charles Richter and Harold Jeffreys had all adopted her interpretation within two or three years, which is fast for a claim that rewrote the interior of the planet. Confirmation by direct computation took considerably longer; the calculations that demonstrated she was right were not run until 1971. In the meantime she kept working. Maurice Ewing visited her station in 1951 and invited her to the Lamont Geological Observatory at Columbia, where she spent several months in 1952; she stepped down from the Geodætisk Institut in 1953 and, freed of administration, did some of her best work afterwards. With Ewing and Frank Press she identified a second boundary, a step increase in seismic velocity somewhere between 190 and 250 kilometres down, now called the Lehmann discontinuity. During the 1960s the seismic networks built to detect nuclear tests handed her better data than she had ever had, and she helped establish the International Seismological Centre between 1961 and 1967.

## In Vain

Writing to her nephew Niels Groes, Lehmann put her career in one line: "You should know how many incompetent men I had to compete with - in vain." The honours came, mostly late. The Harry Oscar Wood Award in 1960, the Emil Wiechert Medal in 1964, the Danish Royal Society's Gold Medal in 1965, election to the Royal Society in 1969 at eighty-one, the William Bowie Medal in 1971 as its first woman recipient at eighty-three, the Seismological Society of America's medal in 1977. She died in Copenhagen on 21 February 1993, aged 104. The American Geophysical Union created an Inge Lehmann Medal in 1996; an asteroid and a beetle carry her name; an 8.8-metre column of black diabase and bronze by Elisabeth Toubro was raised for her on Frue Plads in 2017, and her face is due on a Danish banknote in 2028. The inner core, meanwhile, has been where she said it was since long before any of them noticed.

## Flight Context

This memorial location sits at 55.871 N, 12.504 E, at Hørsholm Cemetery about 25 km north of central Copenhagen, roughly 4 km inland from the Øresund and a short distance from the site of the demolished Hirschholm Palace. There is nothing dramatic to see from the air - a walled burial ground in leafy suburb - so treat the coast and the Hørsholm lake and church as your visual references. Recommended altitude is 1,500 to 3,000 feet. Copenhagen Kastrup (EKCH) lies about 30 km south with heavy controlled airspace across the whole region; Roskilde (EKRK) to the south-west is the practical general-aviation field, and Malmö Sturup (ESMS) sits across the sound in Sweden. Conditions are typical of the North Zealand coast: good summer visibility with afternoon haze, low cloud and reduced visibility common in spring and autumn.
