# Roskilde Festival

Europe's largest non-profit music festival, run by volunteers since 1971 and permanently marked by the 30 June 2000 crowd crush in which nine young men died.

**Location:** 55.6217, 12.0767

**Categories:** denmark, music-festival, modern-culture, non-profit, disasters

## Introduction

Nine trees stand in a circle on the festival grounds south of Roskilde, around a stone cut with four words: How fragile we are. They were planted before the 2001 festival opened, one for each of the nine young men who died in a crowd crush during Pearl Jam's set on the Orange Stage on 30 June 2000. Everything else about Roskilde Festival — the 130,000 visitors, the volunteers, the surplus given away since 1972 — sits alongside that fact rather than balancing it. The festival has run since 1971. It is not the festival it was on 29 June 2000.

## Two Schoolboys and a Promoter

The first one was called the Sound Festival, held on 28 and 29 August 1971 by two high school students, Mogens Sandfær and Jesper Switzer Møller, with the promoter Carl Fischer. They had watched Newport, the Isle of Wight and Woodstock from a distance and wanted a Danish version. Roughly twenty bands played folk, jazz, rock and pop across two days on one stage, before 10,000 to 13,000 people. Accounts describe poor management and enormous enthusiasm in about equal measure. Out of time and money, the students withdrew. The 1972 edition, renamed the Fantasy Festival, was organised by the non-profit Roskilde Foundation, which had run the city's festival since 1932, with the American folk singer Tony Busch. That year produced a surplus, and the foundation gave away 50,000 kroner to local children's and youth projects. Volunteer labour and giving away the money have been the model ever since, and from 1973 the foundation has run it alone.

## The Shape of the Place

Until 2003 the stages were named after colours, a system abandoned once the names stopped matching the tents. Only the Orange Stage kept its name, and it remains the centre of the site: an open field in front of a canopy, no walls holding the crowd. Everything else is enclosed, the largest being Arena, formerly the Green Stage, an officially rated 17,000-capacity tent and the biggest in Europe. The campsite runs to nearly 80 hectares, is included in the ticket, and opens the Saturday before the music starts. Since 2018 the arrival has been choreographed: seven rows of a thousand fill the gate system, the rest wait in a field behind, and at 16:00 the rows release one at a time at ninety-second intervals. A Danish act opens the Orange Stage on the first day. Since 1999 the festival radio station has run a naked race around a fenced campsite track, the winning man and woman each taking home next year's ticket.

## 30 June 2000

Pearl Jam went on around 22:30 before roughly 50,000 people, an ordinary size for that stage. Rain had fallen heavily that day and the ground was slick. About half an hour into the set, wave-like surges ran through the crowd as people pushed toward the front, and at one spot a group of them went down. They did not get up. Others were pushed over the top of them, crowd surfers dropped into the gap that had opened, and the people underneath were suffocating within a few metres of thousands who could not see them. Security guards could not reach the spot through the density of the crowd; it was concertgoers lifted over the front barrier who told them how bad it was. The band was informed, stopped playing and asked the crowd to move back, which helped, but it still took several more minutes to reach everyone. Nine men died, most of them within about five minutes of falling. Twenty-six people were injured, three seriously. The dead were a 26-year-old German, a 23-year-old Dutchman, a 24-year-old Australian who died in hospital days later, three Swedes aged 22, 22 and 20, and three Danes, one 22 and two of them seventeen years old. Many in the audience understood what had happened only after they left the field.

## What Changed After

Pet Shop Boys and Oasis cancelled their sets. D.A.D., the Danish band unofficially treated as the festival's house band, was already booked to close the weekend and turned that set into a memorial for whoever was still on site. The structural changes have not stopped since, and the festival now runs a full safety review every year. Larger safety zones and more emergency exits went into the field layout, security staff were placed inside the audience rather than only at the barriers, video surveillance was extended, the chain of command tightened, and an emergency stop created so a concert can be halted at once. Roskilde helped found the YES Group, a safety network linking European festivals and specialists, and measures adopted here spread outward. Crowd surfing, identified as one of the precipitating factors, was subsequently banned at many European festivals. On 2 July 2011 a 35-year-old German woman died after climbing over a safety barrier and falling from the Tuborg tower; the tower was removed from the site entirely.

## Where the Money Goes

The festival has never been a business. Every surplus goes to humanitarian and cultural work, and since 2014 festivalgoers have been able to nominate and vote on which organisations receive it. The workforce is overwhelmingly volunteer. Recent editions have drawn more than 130,000 visitors, making it the largest festival in the Nordic countries and one of the largest in Europe. Both the 2020 and 2021 editions were cancelled for the COVID-19 pandemic; the festival returned in 2022. Every year the site is built from nothing on farmland, and every year the nine trees are already standing.

## Flight Context

The festival site lies at 55.6217 N, 12.0767 E, on open farmland immediately south of Roskilde and about 30 km west of Copenhagen. For most of the year it reads as empty fields; in late June and early July it becomes a temporary city, with the Orange Stage canopy and nearly 80 hectares of campsite visible from 2,000-4,000 feet. Roskilde Airport (EKRK) sits about 5 km southeast of the festival grounds — expect heavy general-aviation and helicopter movements during festival week and check NOTAMs for temporary restrictions. Copenhagen Kastrup (EKCH) is about 35 km east. Roskilde Cathedral's twin spires, 2 km north, are the best visual reference for lining up the approach.
