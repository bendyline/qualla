# Roskilde Cathedral

The brick Gothic cathedral that has served as the burial church of the Danish monarchy for six centuries, holding forty royal graves and eight hundred years of European architecture in one building.

**Location:** 55.6427, 12.0803

**Categories:** denmark, cathedral, unesco-world-heritage, brick-gothic, royal-history, medieval

## Introduction

At Christmas in 1026 one of Cnut the Great's housecarls murdered Ulf the Earl — inside the church at Roskilde, or at the royal farm beside it, depending on which medieval chronicler you believe. Ulf's widow was Estrid Svendsdatter, Cnut's own sister, and she demanded weregild, the compensation Norse law required for a killing. Then she spent it on a building. The old stave church came down and a stone cathedral rose in its place around 1026, funded by blood money, where Roskilde Cathedral stands today. Almost nothing about this church has ever stayed still.

## Capital, Farm, Stave Church

Harald Bluetooth made Roskilde his capital around the year 960. He had ruled from Jelling, where he raised the Jelling stones, but having united the Danes and Norwegians he needed to sit nearer the centre of power. He built a royal farm here and, beside it, a small stave church dedicated to the Holy Trinity. Almost nothing is known of that building. What is known is that Harald's son Sweyn Forkbeard raised a rebellion that drove his father into exile at Jomsborg, and that when Harald died in 985 or 986 the army that had risen against him carried his body back and buried it in the church he had built. Bishop Wilhelm began a third church between 1060 and 1073, completed by Svend Nordmand around 1080: a Romanesque basilica of travertine, a stone abundant along Roskilde Fjord, twin towers over the west front.

## Brick, Newly Arrived

In 1160 monks brought the art of firing bricks to Denmark from Italy, and within a generation the new material was reshaping the country's architecture. Absalon became Bishop of Roskilde in 1157 and began enlarging the travertine cathedral, raising the new church around the old one so services could continue inside. When he surrendered the see in 1191, only the lower apse, the choir towers and part of the transept stood. His successor Peder Sunesen had seen French Gothic and wanted it, pulling down the choir towers and narrowing the transept; recent research credits him as the real initiator of the building we see. The choir was consecrated in 1225 and the travertine cathedral came down. The nave took another fifty-five years, slowed by money, by a shortage of kilns and by Danish winters. Everything but the west towers was finished by 1280; a fire in 1282 set the interior back; the towers were completed in 1405. That layering is why UNESCO inscribed the cathedral in 1995 — one of Scandinavia's earliest brick Gothic churches, and the model that carried Brick Gothic across northern Europe.

## Forty Graves

Margrethe I died in 1412 and was buried at her family's church in Sorø. A year later Bishop Peder Jensen Lodehat, her former chancellor and confessor, moved her body to Roskilde. The monks at Sorø were furious: losing a queen meant losing the perpetual fees paid for requiem masses. The transfer may in fact have been arranged by her adoptive son, Eric of Pomerania, whose name is on the sarcophagus he gave in 1423. From that point the cathedral became the burial church of the Danish crown, and it now holds forty royal graves — thirty-seven documented, three only attributed. Harald Bluetooth is supposedly in the northwestern pier of the apse, though no grave or remains have ever been found. Estrid was believed to lie in the northeastern pier until a DNA test in 2003 showed the bones belonged to a woman far too young. The current theory names Margareta Hasbjörnsdatter, also called Estrid, who married Sweyn Estridsen's son Harald Hen.

## Fire, Reformation, Loot

On 14 May 1443 fire swept Roskilde, destroying most of the city and all but three of the clergy's houses. The heat cracked the cathedral's glass and melted the lead off the roof, and it was not rededicated until 1463. The Reformation of 1536 hit harder. Bishop Joachim Rønnow went to prison in Copenhagen Castle, the diocese moved to the capital, and the crown seized the church's property, which had included one farm in every four on Zealand and thirty large estates. When the chapter asked for a receipt, the king's men replied that none was needed for what already belonged to him. Christian IV gave much of it back in kind: the pulpit in 1610, his own burial chapel in 1614, the twin spires in 1633, a Renaissance sandstone portal in 1635. In 1658 the Treaty of Roskilde was signed inside these walls, and the departing Swedes took Margrethe I's golden dress from the closet beside her tomb. It hangs at Uppsala Cathedral still.

## A Church Still Waiting

Fire returned on 27 August 1968, when the Margrethe spire burned as its restoration neared completion. Staff and civil defence workers threw damp blankets over the canons' stalls, the altar and the sarcophagi as the spire threatened to fall into the choir. The craftsmen, it emerged, had been smoking and using blowtorches in the loft despite a total ban on heat sources. Empress Maria Feodorovna, the Danish princess who married Alexander III, rested here from 1928 until her reinterment in Saint Petersburg in September 2006. The building keeps gaining, too: Marcussen & Søn rebuilt the 1554 Raphaelis organ into a recreated 17th-century Danish instrument, finished in 1991. In 2018 the monument Bjørn Nørgaard designed for Margrethe II, who reigned from 1972 until her abdication in 2024, was installed in the chapel of St Brigitte, finished and covered. Some 165,000 visitors a year walk past a tomb that is waiting.

## Flight Context

Roskilde Cathedral stands at 55.6427 N, 12.0803 E, at the head of Roskilde Fjord about 30 km west of Copenhagen. The two green copper spires Christian IV added in 1633 are the tallest thing for miles and the cathedral reaches 75.7 metres (248 ft), making it the most reliable visual fix on Zealand's central plain. Best viewed from 2,500-4,000 feet on a downwind leg over the fjord, with the water leading northwest toward Skuldelev and Hundested. Roskilde Airport (EKRK) sits about 7 km southeast of the cathedral; Copenhagen Kastrup (EKCH) is roughly 36 km east. The Viking Ship Museum lies a kilometre north of the cathedral on the fjord shore — the two are easily seen in a single pass. Low cloud and drizzle are common year-round; clear, dry easterlies give the sharpest light on the brick.
