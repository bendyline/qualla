# Liselund

A thatched neoclassical palace and a garden built to manufacture feelings — and, in 1943, a route down the cliff to Sweden.

**Location:** 54.9995, 12.5238

**Categories:** Gardens, Historic houses, Neoclassical architecture, Møn, Denmark, Danish resistance

## Introduction

In a guest room of the Norwegian House at Liselund, the window shutters carry a small opening of yellow glass. Close them on the brightest July afternoon and the room fills with something that looks convincingly like moonlight. That single detail explains the whole place: six hectares in the north-eastern corner of the Danish island of Møn, engineered at considerable expense to produce particular feelings on demand. Antoine Bosc de la Calmette laid it out in the 1790s for his wife, Anna Catharina Elisabeth Iselin, whom everyone called Lise. Liselund means Lise's grove.

## A Huguenot Washes Up on Møn

The Calmettes were Huguenots, driven out of France to Holland generations earlier. Antoine's father worked as a diplomat, serving terms in Switzerland and Portugal before landing in Denmark, where the family was naturalised and recognised as Danish nobility in 1776. In January 1777 Antoine married Catharina Elisabeth Iselin, daughter of Reinhard Iselin, a Swiss baron who had also emigrated. In 1783 he was appointed prefect of Møn, and in the same year he bought six hectares on the island's eastern coast, in the parish of Magleby. He was not buying a residence. The family's working life ran out of Marienborg, on the other side of the island, and this was to be somewhere they could disappear for a few days or a few weeks with invited guests. Antoine lived from 1752 to 1803; Lise from 1759 to 1805.

## Building a Mood

The ground had to be drained, cleared and levelled before anything could be planted on it. Then it was landscaped to the standards of the day and stocked with trees, some of them rare in Denmark: oak, sweet chestnut, walnut, mulberry, spruce, cypress. Water is the essential ingredient of a Romantic garden, and Liselund has ponds threaded through gently rolling terrain, arranged so that the view keeps changing as you walk. The house went up in 1792 in French neoclassical style under a thatched roof — small, T-shaped, with the reception rooms below and nine bedrooms above, the interior probably fitted out by Joseph Christian Lillie, the leading decorator of the moment. Andreas Kirkerup, one of the foremost landscape architects of the age, designed both the house and the grounds. The park was finished in 1795. It is widely regarded as one of the finest examples of Romantic English gardening in Scandinavia.

## The Scatter of Foreign Houses

Scattered through the park are buildings that belong somewhere else entirely, each one placed to suit its setting and to shift the visitor's mood on arrival. The Swiss House, Schweizerhytten, stands at the foot of a Bronze Age burial mound near the entrance drive; it housed the gardener, but under its thatch were beautifully decorated rooms where guests could sleep. The Norwegian House sits in the roughest, most broken ground, near the cliffs, where the terrain does a passable impression of Norway. On the western hill, looking over the largest lake to the sea beyond, a Chinese summerhouse offered tea and a prospect. There are monuments too, most of them about love and friendship rather than conquest or the dead. Skrivermonumentet, a gift from Lise to her husband in the 1780s, is inscribed A l'amitié pure — to pure friendship. A marker beside the stream running down to the cliffs reads Endroit chéri de Lise: Lise's favourite spot.

## Andersen at Twenty-Four

Antoine and Lise had barely a decade in the finished park; he died in 1803, she in 1805. The long chapter belonged to their daughter-in-law Martha de la Calmette, who lived here as a widow for fifty-seven years until her death in 1877 and made the place a fixture of the Danish Golden Age. The poet Adam Gottlob Oehlenschläger came. So did the sculptor Bertel Thorvaldsen. The visitor who mattered most arrived in the summer of 1829, aged twenty-four and not yet famous: Hans Christian Andersen, who found Liselund delightful. It stayed with him. His poem Klintekorset paa Møen — Møn's Cliff Cross — took its subject from the large wooden cross standing at the edge of the chalk. Liselund also credits the enormous hollow willow beside the Swiss House with seeding The Tinderbox, an attribution that rests on tradition rather than on anything Andersen wrote down.

## Down the Cliff Path in the Dark

In 1887 Baron Fritz Rosenkrantz built a larger, Italianate house on the western slope, Liselund Ny Slot, which is a hotel today and was sold in June 2025 to Frederik Nørgaard, a Dane returning after thirty years in San Francisco. But the estate's hardest years came under Baron Niels Rosenkranz, who led the West Møn resistance group — organised a week after the German occupation of 9 April 1940, possibly the first such group in Denmark. From late 1942 it began moving Jewish families out of Denmark to neutral Sweden, and the work intensified in October 1943 when the Germans set out to round up Denmark's Jews systematically. People were led at night down the treacherous paths from Liselund park to the beach below, where small boats waited. The Gestapo suspected the route. When they came on the night of 10 October 1943, the group had been warned, and they found no one. Rosenkranz was also central to the rescue of 351 Stutthof prisoners landed at Klintholm Havn on 5 May 1945. Rising maintenance costs eventually put the old thatched manor and its park into the hands of the Danish State, administered by the National Museum of Denmark.

## Flight Context

Liselund lies at 54.9995°N, 12.5238°E, in the wooded north-eastern corner of Møn, immediately inland of the northern end of the Møns Klint chalk cliffs. From 1,500–2,500 ft the park reads as a break in the beech canopy — a cluster of small ponds and clearings set back a few hundred metres from the cliff edge, with the white face of the cliffs as the unmistakable reference to the south and east. Nearest airports: Copenhagen Kastrup (EKCH) roughly 100 km north-west, Roskilde (EKRK) roughly 95 km north-west, Lolland Falster/Maribo (EKMB) roughly 60 km west-south-west. The park is small and easy to overfly; use the cliff line and the wooded plateau to find it. Best light is mid-morning, before the eastern slope falls into shadow.
