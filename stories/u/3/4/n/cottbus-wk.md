# Cottbus

Brandenburg's second city is also Chóśebuz, a capital of the Sorbian minority - a cloth town that became a coal town and is now watching the pit that fed it fill with water to become Germany's largest artificial lake.

**Location:** 51.7608, 14.3319

**Categories:** cities, germany, brandenburg, lusatia, sorbian-culture, industrial-heritage

## Introduction

The hole is filling up. For decades the Cottbus-Nord open-cast mine chewed lignite out of the flat ground north-east of the city, feeding the power stations that kept East Germany lit. Then the diggers stopped, and the water started coming in. When the flooding is complete, Cottbus will sit on the shore of the largest artificial lake in Germany by surface area - a beach where a coal pit used to be. Not many places get to watch their own industrial past turn into a holiday destination in real time. Then again, Cottbus has had practice at reinvention: cloth town, garrison town, coal town, and - in a language most Germans have never heard spoken aloud - Chóśebuz.

## Chóśebuz on the Signs

Cottbus is one of the cultural centres of the Sorbs, the Slavic minority whose ancestors settled Lusatia long before Brandenburg existed as a margraviate, and whose language has clung on here through a thousand years of German rule. The city name itself is Sorbian, usually traced to a form recorded as Chotibuz - by one traditional reading, something close to "pretty little houses." That heritage is not a museum piece. Lower Sorbian appears alongside German on signage in the city, and the surrounding villages of the Spreewald remain the core of the language's shrinking territory. Twenty kilometres west, at Slawenburg Raddusch, a full-scale reconstruction of a ring fortress shows how the Lusizi - the Sorbs' ancestors - fortified this marshy country a thousand years ago. It is a strange thing to stand in a German city of a hundred thousand people and realise you are in a Slavic capital.

## Cloth, Grain and Tree Cake

Ask what Cottbus was historically famous for and you get three answers: cloth, grain and beer, and Baumkuchen - "tree cake," a cylindrical layer cake baked on a rotating spit until its cross-section shows rings like a felled trunk. The cloth mattered most. From around 1860 the industrial revolution arrived in force; the town's first clothmaking factory and first military barracks went up in the same era. The transformation was sealed in 1865, when the Berlin-Görlitz railway was routed through Cottbus, wiring the town into the empire's freight network and linking it to the mill towns of Guben and Forst and onward to Wrocław. Prosperity brought institutions with it. In 1885 Dr. Carl Thiem, considered the father of trauma surgery in Germany, opened a private clinic here that still operates today; the first telephone system followed in 1886, and a public textile college was founded after the three largest German carpet manufacturers merged.

## A Theatre Built for a Boom Town

Money that arrives suddenly wants somewhere to be seen spending itself, and in 1908 Cottbus got the Staatstheater - a jewel-box Art Nouveau house designed by Bernhard Sehring, the architect behind Berlin's Theater des Westens. The exterior is handsome; the interior is the point, all sinuous plasterwork and flowing ornament from the brief moment when Jugendstil ran through German design like a fever. Around it, the well-preserved medieval core and the 19th-century districts that grew up during the textile boom give the centre an unexpected density of good buildings, threaded with parks that have earned Cottbus a reputation as a green city. On the eastern edge lies Branitz, the landscape park that Prince Hermann von Pückler-Muskau shaped in his old age and where an earth pyramid rising from a lake serves as his grave - a story in its own right.

## Großstadt, Twice Over

The population arc tells the region's history without needing a single adjective. Cottbus first passed 100,000 residents in the 1970s - the threshold at which German bureaucracy grants a town the title Großstadt - and peaked above 130,000 in the East German years, when the mines and the plants and the barracks all needed hands. After reunification the hands were no longer needed. Unemployment rose, the young left for Munich and Hamburg and Berlin, and by 2019 the city was back down to around 100,000, hovering at the edge of losing its designation. It has not lost it. The population is rising again, part of a Germany-wide drift back into cities, helped by the ninety minutes of track between here and Berlin. The Soviet-era prefab suburbs are being steadily refurbished, and on weekend nights the town centre belongs to whoever has just watched FC Energie Cottbus play.

## Where the Spree Slows Down

North of the city the Spree loses its nerve and splits into hundreds of channels, and the result is the Spreewald - a waterlogged forest of alder and meadow navigated by flat-bottomed punts, where villages are reached by bridge or by boat. Burg lies about 15 km north-west and serves as the southern gateway. Closer in, the Spreeaue reserve protects 160 hectares along the river's course, laced with the cycle paths that fan out from Cottbus in every direction across country flat enough to make a beginner feel strong. The best introduction to all of it is the Saturday market on Oberkirchplatz, which opens at seven and packs up by midday: Spreewald growers selling their own produce, gherkins above all, the crop this marsh has been famous for since long before anyone dug for coal.

## Flight Context

Cottbus lies at 51.76 N, 14.33 E on the Spree in southern Brandenburg, roughly 100 km south-east of Berlin and 25 km from the Polish border. From 4,000-6,000 ft the city reads as a compact grey core in a broad green plain, with the braided channels of the Spreewald spreading north-west and the raw geometry of the Lusatian lignite country to the north-east - flooded pits, spoil ridges and the cooling towers at Jänschwalde make unmistakable navigation marks. The filling basin of the Cottbuser Ostsee sits immediately north-east of the built-up area. Cottbus-Drewitz (EDCD) is about 25 km east; Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) lies roughly 100 km north-west and Dresden (EDDC) about 110 km south-west. The terrain is nearly flat, so summer haze rather than cloud is the usual visibility limit; autumn and winter bring low stratus over the wet Spreewald ground.
