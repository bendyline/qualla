# Görlitz Synagogue

One of the few German synagogues to survive Kristallnacht standing, because the fire brigade ignored the order to let it burn - and it took until 2022 to put the Star of David back on its dome.

**Location:** 51.1528, 14.9911

**Categories:** Jewish heritage, Synagogues, Art Nouveau architecture, Görlitz, Saxony, Germany, Holocaust memory

## Introduction

The instruction given to fire brigades across Germany on the night of 9 November 1938 was to let the synagogues burn and protect only the buildings on either side. In Görlitz the arson went ahead as ordered. The fire brigade then turned up and put the fire out, and the damage never reached the structure. No one left a full account of why they did it. What survives is the consequence: a Jugendstil synagogue with a great domed hall, one of very few in this corner of Germany to come through that night intact. The following day someone climbed onto the roof and broke off the Star of David. Putting a new one back took eighty-four years.

## Not the Style They Expected

The Jewish community of Görlitz had grown from about 150 people in 1852 to 643 by 1880, and the older synagogue on Langenstrasse could no longer hold them. For the new building they hired William Lossow and Max Hans Kühne, the partnership that would go on to design Leipzig's monumental main station. Lossow and Kühne made a choice that stands out even now: instead of the Moorish and oriental idiom that architects reached for when designing synagogues in that period, they built in Art Nouveau - the confident, forward-looking style of the German present, applied to a building for a German congregation. Construction ran from 1909 to 1911 and the synagogue opened on 7 March 1911. The domed hall seated around 550. A smaller weekday synagogue alongside it held about fifty.

## The Building Outlived the Congregation

From 1933 the people who had filled those seats left Görlitz or were deported from it. The synagogue survived Kristallnacht; the community did not survive what followed. Services continued in a shrinking form until September 1940, when the last one was held, and then the doors closed on an empty building. That is the awkward, necessary fact about this place. Its rescue is genuinely worth telling - the firefighters who ignored an order were doing something dangerous and decent - but a saved building is not a saved congregation, and the survival of the walls is a measure of how completely everything inside them was destroyed.

## Eighty Years Behind a Locked Door

After the war, the Soviet military administration handed the building to the Jewish community in Dresden, the nearest one left. Dresden could not afford to maintain a synagogue in a city with no congregation to use it, and in November 1963 sold it back to the Görlitz city administration. It was never reconsecrated. For decades it stood shut, unheated and slowly failing, a ruin in slow motion behind a locked door. Restoration began in 1991, once reunification made money and attention available, and finished in December 2020 at a cost of roughly ten million euros - twenty-nine years of work. The pandemic pushed the opening ceremony to 12 July 2021. On 20 August that year, for the first time in eighty years, a Jewish service was held inside.

## The Argument About the Star

The restoration plans of the early 1990s did not include the Magen David, and the roof work of that decade left no structure capable of carrying one. When the Förderkreis Görlitzer Synagoge raised the question in 2008 - the year the city council marked the seventieth anniversary of the pogrom - the city administration blocked it. The estimate then was 13,000 euros. The Jewish community in Dresden withheld support for years. Agreement finally came in 2017, and in 2020 engineers were commissioned to solve the problem the 1990s had created. The new figure was 70,000 euros. Alex Jacobowitz, a musician and the chairman of the Jewish community in Görlitz, made a substantial donation; in May 2021 an anonymous donor gave the city the whole 70,000. On 12 September 2022 the star went back onto the dome, where it can be seen from the street again.

## What It Is Now

In December 2021 the city received something nobody expected to see again: fragments of a Torah scroll believed destroyed on the night of the pogrom, kept for more than fifty years by a priest in Kunnerwitz on the edge of town. The domed hall has been restored to seat 310 rather than the original 550 and now works as a cultural forum - guided tours of Jewish Görlitz, concerts, conferences, events that can be booked. The city council decided that the small weekday synagogue, twenty seats now, stays what it was built to be, and Jewish worship happens there. It is a modest arrangement for an enormous building. It is also the honest one, and it means the place is open rather than preserved.

## Flight Context

The Görlitz Synagogue stands at 51.153 N, 14.991 E, on Otto-Müller-Straße just southwest of the historic core of Görlitz, Germany's easternmost city. Best viewed between 1,000 and 2,500 feet AGL. From the air the synagogue reads as a domed block set apart from the dense medieval street grid; the reliable fix is the Lusatian Neisse, which runs north-south through the middle of the built-up area and forms the German-Polish border - everything on the east bank is Zgorzelec, Poland. Look for the tower of St Peter and Paul on the bluff above the river and work southwest from there. The Königshain Hills rise to the northwest; the terrain is otherwise low and open, so the city stands out clearly on any clear day. Dresden (EDDC) lies about 51 nm west, Wrocław (EPWR) roughly 86 nm east, Prague (LKPR) about 86 nm southwest, and Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) around 100 nm northwest. Morning haze along the Neisse valley is common in autumn.
