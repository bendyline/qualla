# Görlitz

Germany's easternmost town came through the Second World War with its medieval, Renaissance and Baroque streets intact, and since 1995 an anonymous benefactor has sent it more than half a million a year to keep them standing.

**Location:** 51.1528, 14.9872

**Categories:** Historic towns, Saxony, Germany, Poland, Border towns, Architecture, Film locations, Upper Lusatia

## Introduction

Every year since 1995, money has arrived in Görlitz from someone who will not give a name. More than half a million a year - marks at first, then euros - earmarked for a single purpose: repairing the old town. The total has passed ten million, and the donor's identity is still unknown. What the money bought is not in doubt. Görlitz came through the Second World War with its medieval, Renaissance and Baroque streets essentially untouched, then spent the next four and a half decades watching them crumble quietly for want of mortar and paint. Since reunification more than 700 buildings have been renovated. The Altstadtmillion, as the annual gift is known here, paid for a good share of that work, and the result is one of the best-preserved towns in Central Europe.

## The War That Went Around It

Görlitz is Germany's easternmost town, about 55,000 people on the west bank of the Neiße, and what makes it extraordinary is an absence. Dresden, ninety-five kilometres west, burned to its foundations in 1945 and was rebuilt from photographs. Görlitz was not. The medieval core survived, and so did the Renaissance and Baroque houses layered on top of it, and so did the belt of nineteenth-century tenements, villas and industrial buildings wrapped around the whole. Only the far outskirts look like anywhere else in Germany. The town did not escape 1945 entirely, though. When the border moved west that year, the Neiße became an international frontier, and everything on the east bank - roughly a third of the town - became Polish and was renamed Zgorzelec. The two halves have been looking at each other across the water ever since.

## Görliwood

Film crews worked out what all that preservation was worth before the tourists did. Since 1990 Görlitz has stood in for cities that no longer exist, letting directors shoot pre-1945 Germany on location instead of building it on a soundstage. Inglourious Basterds was filmed here. So were The Reader, The Book Thief and Valkyrie. Wes Anderson turned the town's Art Nouveau department store into the lobby of The Grand Budapest Hotel. The press nickname stuck: Görliwood. Walk across the Untermarkt on an ordinary weekday and you may well find cables taped down over the cobbles and someone asking you to hold still for thirty seconds while a take finishes. There is a neat irony in the arrangement. A town whose chief distinction is that nothing happened to it now earns a living playing places where everything did.

## Everyone's Clock Is Set to Görlitz

The 15th meridian east runs through the town, and a marker in the streets notes where. That line is the one that defines Central European Time, which means the clocks in Madrid, Paris, Rome, Oslo and Warsaw are all, in a strictly technical sense, set to Görlitz time. The rest of the geography is just as tidy. A pedestrian footbridge crosses the Neiße into Poland, and the walk takes about a minute. From the far bank the old town assembles itself into the view that turns up on every postcard, the twin towers of the Peterskirche riding high above the water with the river running below. Since Poland joined the Schengen Area in 2007 there have been no border checks in either direction. People cross to buy cigarettes, to eat, or simply because the light is better from over there.

## Jerusalem, Rebuilt in Silesia

Görlitz sits in historic Lower Silesia and Upper Lusatia at once, and the shop windows still know it. Work on the Holy Sepulchre complex began in 1465, a set of buildings laid out to reproduce the sacred sites of Jerusalem for townspeople who would never make the journey themselves - a pilgrimage in miniature, five weeks' travel compressed into a short walk. The Silesian Museum occupies a Renaissance house on the Untermarkt. Souvenir shops sell Bunzlauer Keramik, the dotted blue-and-cream stoneware named for Bolesławiec, forty kilometres east in Poland. Antiquarian booksellers still trade in the old town, mostly in German. In the restaurants, eating out costs noticeably less than in Dresden or Berlin, and the local dish to order is Schlesisches Himmelreich - Silesian Kingdom of Heaven - which turns out to be pork cooked slowly in plum gravy.

## The Volcano and the Flooded Mine

Southwest of town the Landeskrone rises 419 metres out of otherwise gentle country, an isolated volcano on the edge of the Lusatian volcanic field and impossible to mistake for anything else. There has been a fortification of some kind on top since around 900, and the oldest surviving structure is an observation tower from 1796. The walk up takes about an hour from the tram terminus at Biesnitz, and on a clear day the view reaches the Jizera and Giant Mountains. The town's brewery took the hill's name, Landskron, and by virtue of the town's position it is the easternmost brewery in Germany. South of the centre lies Lake Berzdorf, which is not natural either: an open-pit lignite mine closed in the early 1990s, and in 2002 the crews began letting water in. Görlitz swims in it now.

## Flight Context

Görlitz lies at 51.15 N, 14.99 E, on the Lusatian plain where the Neiße runs roughly north-south and forms the German-Polish border. Best viewed between 2,000 and 4,000 feet AGL. The town is unmistakable from the air: a compact dense old town on the west bank, the river cutting through, the Polish grid of Zgorzelec on the east bank, and the A4 / European Route E40 running east-west just north of the centre toward Wrocław. The single best navigation mark is the Landeskrone, an isolated 419-metre volcanic cone about 3 nm southwest of the town with a tower on the summit, standing alone above flat farmland. Lake Berzdorf, a flooded open-pit mine, sits immediately south of town and is the largest body of water in the area. Dresden (EDDC) is roughly 47 nm west; Wrocław-Copernicus (EPWR) about 72 nm east. A small general-aviation airfield serves the town itself. Winter brings frequent low stratus across the Lusatian plain; late spring and early autumn give the cleanest visibility.
