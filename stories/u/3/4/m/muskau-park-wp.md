# Muskau Park

Central Europe's largest English landscape garden, built by a prince who divorced his wife to fund it, now cut in two by the German-Polish border and maintained by both countries as a single composition.

**Location:** 51.5476, 14.7232

**Categories:** World Heritage Sites, Gardens, Upper Lusatia, Saxony, Poland, Germany, Landscape architecture, Germany–Poland border

## Introduction

In February 1826 Hermann von Pückler-Muskau and his wife Lucie were divorced, and the reason entered in the record was childlessness. The real reason was a garden. Pückler had been building an enormous landscape park around his castle on the Neisse since 1815 and was running out of money for it, and the divorce had been Lucie's own suggestion - it freed him to go abroad and marry a fortune. That September he sailed for England and spent the better part of two years hunting for an heiress. He found none. The English press treated the search as a joke and printed as much. He came home without a bride but with a manuscript, and the park he had been trying to pay for is now a UNESCO World Heritage Site with an international border running down the middle of it.

## Painting With Plants

Pückler described what he was doing as painting with plants, and he meant it literally - native species, water, bridges and paths arranged into views that were composed rather than merely planted. The park now covers 2.1 square kilometres in Germany and 3.5 in Poland, with a 17.9-square-kilometre buffer zone taking in Bad Muskau and Łęknica. The heart of it is the partly wooded rise east of the river, known as the Park on Terraces. He remodelled the Baroque Old Castle, which was really a former castle gate, and added a Gothic Revival chapel, an English cottage, several bridges and an orangery designed by Friedrich Ludwig Persius. The medieval fortress became the New Castle, the compositional centre, with paths radiating outward. Humphry Repton's ideas shaped the pleasure ground; Repton's son John Adey worked at Muskau from 1822. Building went on until 1845, when the debts finally caught him.

## Letters of a Dead Man

The manuscript he brought back from England turned out to be worth more than any marriage would have been. Briefe eines Verstorbenen - Letters of a Dead Man - appeared in four volumes between 1830 and 1831 and was an enormous success in Germany, then caused a genuine stir in English as Tour of a German Prince, translated by Sarah Austin in 1831 and 1832. A prince who had failed publicly at fortune-hunting became a celebrated travel writer instead, which is a better outcome than he had any right to expect. His name attached itself to other things too. Fürst-Pückler-Eis, the layered strawberry, vanilla and chocolate ice still sold across Germany, was named for him in 1839 by the royal Prussian court cook Louis Ferdinand Jungius. After selling Muskau in 1845 he bought Branitz near Cottbus and laid out another park. He died there in 1871, aged 85, and is buried under an earth pyramid of his own design.

## Bilillee

Her name was Bilillee. She was Oromo, born around 1825 in the kingdom of Gumma, in what is now southwestern Ethiopia, and she was taken from there and sold. In 1837 Pückler bought her at the slave market in Cairo. She was about twelve years old. He renamed her Machbuba - the beloved - and carried her with him through Asia Minor, Greece and Vienna, where European society treated her as a spectacle and showed no interest in the life she had been taken from. She reached Germany in the spring of 1840. She died at Muskau on 27 October that year, probably of tuberculosis, at about fifteen. She had been in the country a matter of months. She is buried in the St Jacobi cemetery at Bad Muskau, a short walk from the park gates. Pückler later wrote that she was the being he loved most in all the world. What she thought of him is not recorded; she was never in a position to write it down.

## A Border Through a Garden

The park was designed to be read from both banks, and in 1945 the arrangement collapsed. During the Battle of Berlin both castles were levelled and all four bridges over the Neisse were razed. Count von Arnim-Muskau was dispossessed by the Soviet Military Administration. Then the Oder-Neisse line was drawn along the river, and two-thirds of the composition ended up in Poland. For twenty years afterward the East German authorities had little appetite for restoring the legacy of a Junker prince, and it was only in the 1960s that the attitude began to shift. The Old Castle was rebuilt between 1965 and 1972. The New Castle and the bridges are still being worked on. The Englische Brücke, demolished with explosives in 1945, was repaired and rededicated on 17 October 2011 - a gap of sixty-six years.

## One Composition, Two Countries

After 1989 the German and Polish administrations began treating the park as the single object it was designed to be. A pedestrian bridge over the Neisse was rebuilt in 2003. Poland listed its share as a pomnik historii, a national Historic Monument, on 1 May 2004. That July, UNESCO inscribed the whole park as a joint German-Polish World Heritage Site, citing a design ambitious enough to be called utopian - one that pulled the surrounding town into the picture and went on to change the direction of landscape architecture across Europe. Since Poland entered the Schengen Area in 2007 there have been no checks at the water. You walk out of Bad Muskau, cross the bridge, and are in Łęknica without anything in the landscape telling you so. Which is precisely the effect Pückler was after, for entirely different reasons.

## Flight Context

Muskau Park straddles 51.55 N, 14.72 E, on the Lusatian Neisse where the river forms the German-Polish border, roughly 30 km north of the point where Saxony, Brandenburg and Poland meet. Best viewed between 1,500 and 3,000 feet AGL. From the air it reads as a large irregular block of mature parkland - open glades cut through dense old-growth canopy - straddling the river on both banks, sharply distinct from the geometric commercial forestry and former lignite workings that surround it on all sides. The Neisse itself runs north through the middle; Bad Muskau sits on the German bank, Łęknica on the Polish. The New Castle is the most prominent structure west of the river. Terrain is flat, generally between 100 and 150 metres, with no significant obstacles. Dresden (EDDC) lies about 44 nm southwest and Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) roughly 67 nm northwest. Autumn gives the clearest reading of the design, when the deciduous plantings turn and the composed sightlines separate visibly from the surrounding conifer plantations.
