# Saxon Switzerland climbing region

Germany's oldest non-Alpine climbing region, where chalk, nuts and cams are banned, protection means knots tied into slings, and a rulebook printed in 1913 still governs 17,000 routes.

**Location:** 50.9160, 14.1538

**Categories:** Rock climbing, Rock formations, Saxony, National parks, Outdoor recreation, Sport history

## Introduction

No chalk. No nuts, no cams, no pitons - no metal in the rock at all, beyond the occasional ring bolt placed by the person who climbed the route first. No bolting from above, ever. No climbing on wet or damp stone. Almost everything modern climbers reach for is forbidden in Saxon Switzerland, and it has been since a guidebook printed in 1913 wrote the customs down. The result is not nostalgia. It is the largest climbing area in Germany, with over 1,100 summits and more than 17,000 routes, and it is where free climbing - the idea that you get up using your body and use the rope only to catch you - was worked out before most of the world had a word for it.

## Bad Schandau Gymnasts, 1864

It began with gymnasts. In 1864 a group of Turner from the little Elbe town of Bad Schandau made the first modern ascent of the Falkenstein, and that date is the one everything else is measured from - the start of rock climbing as a sport in Germany outside the Alps. What followed over the next fifty years was less an evolution of equipment than an argument about ethics, and the argument was largely settled before 1900 by Oscar Schuster, who insisted on the principle that would define the place: no artificial aid to upward movement. Rudolf Fehrmann put the resulting rules into print in his 1913 climbing guide. They have been amended only slightly since. A climber arriving today is bound by conventions older than the Republic, older than two world wars, older than the fall of the wall that for forty years put this rock in a different country from most of the people who wanted to climb it.

## Knots Instead of Metal

The reason for the prohibitions is underfoot. Elbe sandstone is soft, and it is softest when wet - a cam or a nut torqued into a crack crushes the rock rather than gripping it, and a wire brush of a foothold disappears under a season of traffic. So protection here is textile. Climbers thread slings through natural holes and around natural features, and where there is no hole they tie a knot into the sling and jam the knot in a crack, where it seats and holds much as a camming device would. Ring bolts exist only on routes of grade V and upward, and only where nothing else is possible. They are placed by the first ascensionist, from the ground up, on lead. Later additions require a ruling from a committee of the Saxon Climbers' Federation. The practical consequence is enormous distances between points of protection. Whole hard routes carry one ring, or two, and the leader is often looking at a fall onto a knot in a piece of cord.

## Roman Numerals, and a Grade for Jumping

Saxony grades its own way. The system dates from the beginning of the twentieth century and runs in Roman numerals, with everything from VII upward split into a, b and c. It travelled with the ethic, and is still used across eastern Germany and in the Czech sandstone areas, where similar rules apply. Then there is the peculiarity that startles visitors: a separate scale, in Arabic numerals from 1 to 7, for horizontal jumps. Some summits are gained by leaping a gap from a neighbouring tower, and the difficulty of the leap is graded as carefully as the difficulty of the climb. Routes considered particularly good earn one or two asterisks in the guidebooks, a modest little honours system that carries real weight in a region with seventeen thousand of them to choose from.

## What Counts as a Summit

You may not climb whatever you like. Climbing is permitted only on free-standing rock towers with at least ten metres of prominence - three massifs are grandfathered in as historic exceptions - which rules out the cliff faces, the walls of the gorges, and everything that would be the obvious target anywhere else. Nearly every permitted summit carries a register and an abseil ring, both maintained by the Saxon Climbers' Federation, and the ritual is fixed: the leader places all the protection from the ground, belays the second from the top, the party signs the book, and everyone rappels. Toproping is frowned upon and permitted only where the rope cannot damage the stone. The rules are not merely a club tradition either. They form part of the mountain sports concept required under Saxony's National Park ordinance, which means the etiquette of 1913 now has the force of conservation law.

## The Teufelsturm and the Modern Hard End

The names carry weight out of proportion to the height of the rock. The Teufelsturm in the Schmilka area is the region's proving ground, its Alter Weg graded VIIb, the Talseite VIIIa and the Pferdefuß Xa. The American Oliver Perry-Smith left his name literally in the stone early in the twentieth century with the Perryriß, VIIb, on the Daxenstein in the Bielatal. Later the standard was pushed by Bernd Arnold, whose Wand der Abendröte on the Nonnengärtner went at IXb and whose 1000-Mark-Wand on the Südliche Pfaffenschluchtspitze went at IXc - numbers achieved with no chalk, no cams and rings a long way apart. The hardest line in the area is reckoned to be Circus Maximus on the Müllerstein, at XIb. Thirteen districts hold the rest, from the Affensteine and Schrammsteine above the Elbe to the Bielatal in the west, and beneath them all runs the same soft Cretaceous sandstone that made the rules necessary.

## Flight Context

The Saxon Switzerland climbing region centres near 50.92 N, 14.15 E, straddling the Elbe upstream of Pirna and running to the Czech border, about 20 nm southeast of Dresden. Best viewed between 2,000 and 4,000 feet AGL. From the air the region reads as a forested plateau shattered into pale towers and gorges, with the Elbe looping through the middle of it; the flat-topped table mountains - Lilienstein, Königstein with its fortress, Pfaffenstein, Gohrisch and Papststein - are the surest navigation marks, standing well clear of the tree canopy. The Schrammsteine form a long serrated ridge on the right bank near Bad Schandau. Nearest airport is Dresden (EDDC), about 20 nm northwest; Liberec (LKLB) lies roughly 35 nm east across the Czech border. Morning inversion fog fills the Elbe valley and the side gorges frequently, leaving the summits standing out of a white layer - spectacular from above, and the reason climbers here check the rock for damp before starting.
