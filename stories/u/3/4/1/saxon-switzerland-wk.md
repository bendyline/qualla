# Saxon Switzerland

A Cretaceous seabed cut into towers and table mountains by the Elbe, named after a country six hundred kilometres away by two homesick painters, and laced with four hundred kilometres of marked trail.

**Location:** 50.9204, 14.1435

**Categories:** National parks, Rock formations, Saxony, Germany, Hiking, Elbe Sandstone Mountains, Protected areas of Saxony

## Introduction

There is no Switzerland here, and there never was. The name came from two homesick Swiss artists working in Dresden in the eighteenth century - the engraver Adrian Zingg and the portraitist Anton Graff - who looked at this country of sandstone towers and gorges and were reminded of home. The comparison stuck so hard that it colonised the map. The German side is Sächsische Schweiz, Saxon Switzerland; across the border the Czech side became Bohemian Switzerland. Two painters named a landscape after a country six hundred kilometres away, and nobody has managed to undo it since. What they were actually looking at was a seabed. In the Cretaceous, when this corner of Europe lay under ocean, sand settled and hardened into rock. The sea withdrew. The Elbe stayed, and spent the intervening millions of years cutting that rock into fins, pinnacles and flat-topped table mountains.

## Small Mountains, Enormous Drama

The numbers do not sound impressive, and that is the point. Saxon Switzerland became a national park in 1990 and covers 93.5 square kilometres in two separate blocks, and the highest ground in it, the Großer Winterberg, reaches only 556 metres. What makes the place work is the drop. The Elbe runs through at 110 to 120 metres, so the rock stands four hundred-odd metres above the water in a series of near-vertical walls, and the eye reads that as far bigger country than it is. About forty percent of the park is dense woodland, some of it gorge forest that stays cold and damp all summer. Above it stand the landmarks: the Lilienstein, a table mountain rising to 415 metres in the middle of an Elbe meander, the serrated ridge of the Schrammsteine, and the Bastei, whose seven-arch stone bridge has drawn visitors and photographers since it was built in 1851.

## Four Hundred Kilometres of Marked Trail

Walking is the whole proposition here. The park maintains around 400 kilometres of signposted hiking trails and roughly 50 kilometres of cycle path, and the range of difficulty is wide: some routes are flat riverside strolls suitable for anyone, others climb hard through clefts in the rock and want a decent pair of legs. The classic is the Malerweg, the Painters' Path, a 116-kilometre loop that runs from Pirna out through Schmilka and back, following the routes the Romantic artists took when they came out from Dresden to sketch. Caspar David Friedrich walked this ground. So did Ludwig Richter. The trail was laid out to put modern walkers in the same places, looking at the same rocks, which is a rare thing in landscape tourism - the view has not needed updating in two hundred years.

## Arriving by Steamer

The river is still the best way in. Paddle steamers run upstream from Dresden through the Elbe valley, and they are genuine steamers: most of the fleet has been converted to oil, though one veteran paddle steamer still burns coal, with the engines open to view for anyone who cares to watch the linkages move. If you would rather not spend the afternoon on it, the S1 suburban train leaves Dresden every half hour and reaches the villages of the park in twenty to forty-five minutes, with alternate services continuing to Schöna on the Czech border in fifty-five. Eurocity trains call at Bad Schandau from Prague in an hour forty-five and from Berlin in two and a half hours. Cyclists take the Elbe Radweg, 54 kilometres from Dresden to Bad Schandau, riding upstream so the mountains stay in front of them. Road bridges cross only at Pirna and Bad Schandau; between them, small ferries carry pedestrians and bicycles.

## Boofen

There is a regional tradition here with no real equivalent elsewhere in Germany. Boofen means sleeping out under a rock overhang, without a tent, on the bare sandstone - and rather than banning it, the national park administration publishes a list of 58 authorized shelters and a map of where it is permitted. This is a considerable concession in a country that regulates most things thoroughly, and it comes with conditions: it is reserved for climbers on an active rock tour, allowed only from mid-June through January, and fires are prohibited across the park except at nine designated sites. Everything you carry in comes out again with you. Bring a proper bedroll, because sandstone leaches heat and the nights get cold even in July. The reward is waking up inside a national park at first light, with the rock overhead still holding yesterday's warmth and nobody else awake for a mile in any direction.

## Fortresses, Robbers and a Stage in the Rocks

For centuries this was awkward country to govern. Robbers worked the trade routes through the gorges, and the strongholds built to control them were carved into the rock rather than raised on top of it, which made them close to impossible to take. Many survive and can be walked into. The largest by far is Königstein, one of the biggest hilltop fortresses in Europe, which spent much of its working life as a prison for inconveniently well-connected people. Below it at Rathen sits the Felsenbühne, an open-air arena seating nearly two thousand with sandstone walls rising a hundred metres behind the stage. Come on a hot summer weekend and the riverside villages are packed. Come between late autumn and early spring, when snow dusts the tops, and outside the villages you will have the place almost entirely to yourself.

## Flight Context

Saxon Switzerland centres on 50.92 N, 14.14 E, upstream of Dresden along the Elbe, straddling the river as far as the Czech border. Best viewed between 2,000 and 4,000 feet AGL following the Elbe southeast out of Dresden. The river is the navigation line: past Pirna it enters a steep-sided sandstone gorge and begins throwing tight meanders. The flat-topped table mountains are the unmistakable marks - the Lilienstein standing alone inside a river loop at 415 metres, Königstein Fortress crowning the mesa opposite it, and the Pfaffenstein just south. North of the river the Bastei pinnacles and the seven-arch bridge stand clear of the forest above Rathen; east of Bad Schandau the Schrammsteine form a long serrated ridge. Terrain is modest in absolute height, topping out at 556 metres on the Großer Winterberg, but the walls are near-vertical. Dresden (EDDC) is about 19 nm northwest, Ústí nad Labem (LKUL) roughly 14 nm south across the Czech border, and Prague (LKPR) around 55 nm southwest. Morning valley fog along the Elbe is common and can fill the gorge completely while the tops stay in clear sun.
