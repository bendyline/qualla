# Attempts to escape Oflag IV-C

Hermann Göring called Colditz Castle escape-proof; its own guards ended up curating a museum of the forged papers, duplicate keys and bed-sheet ropes that proved him wrong.

**Location:** 51.1308, 12.8075

**Categories:** Military history of Germany during World War II, World War II prisoner-of-war camps in Germany, Colditz Castle, POW escapes and rescues during World War II, Historical sites

## Introduction

The guards opened a museum. Colditz Castle's security staff had confiscated so many duplicate keys, forged papers, home-made tools and ropes knotted from bed sheets that they put the haul on display, and one of the clay heads the Dutch prisoners had sculpted to stand in for absent men at roll call took pride of place. Hermann Göring had called this castle escape-proof. Between thirty and thirty-six men proved him wrong — the figure is still argued over, because German and Allied bookkeepers never agreed on what counted as getting away.

## The Only One of Its Kind

Oflag IV-C was a Sonderlager, a high-security camp and the only one of its type inside Germany, installed in a Renaissance castle on a rock spur above the Saxon town of Colditz. The German Army's logic was simple: gather the officers who had already escaped from somewhere else and keep them in one place. What it produced was a courtyard of British, Canadian, French, Polish, Dutch and Belgian officers, all experienced, all planning, all liable to wreck each other's work by accident. The answer was bureaucracy. Each national group appointed an escape officer to coordinate schedules, so a Frenchman coming out of a drain would not collide with a Pole coming down a rope. Pat Reid took the British post, and like most escape officers was forbidden to escape himself.

## Chocolate as Currency

MI9, the War Office department devoted to escape equipment, wrote to prisoners in code and hid contraband in parcels from families and invented charities — never in Red Cross parcels, since one discovery would end deliveries to every camp in Germany. What the Red Cross reliably supplied was money: chocolate and coffee outperformed Reichsmarks in wartime Saxony. Douglas Bader, who had lost both legs in a pre-war flying accident and was permitted down into the town, carried luxuries to trade; Flight Lieutenant Čeněk Chaloupka bought information and acquired a girlfriend there. Bribery cut both ways. In 1941 the British pooled 500 Reichsmark for a sentry, then spent three months extending a drain out of the canteen floor. The sentry reported them. Pushing his head into the open air on 29 May, Reid saw his own shadow on the Kommandantur wall and, beside it, a second shadow holding a gun. Coming out, the British burst into laughter rather than give their captors the satisfaction. "And the guard?" Hauptmann Priem noted afterwards. "He kept his 100 Marks; he got extra leave, promotion and the War Service Cross."

## Eight Months Under the Chapel

Nine French officers dug the longest tunnel ever attempted at Colditz, and their masterstroke was an entrance the Germans had sealed themselves. After two earlier attempts through the chapel's clock tower, the guards bricked up its doors on every floor, converting the shaft into a private, unwatched workshop. The French came in from the attics instead, descended to the cellars, and began digging horizontally in June 1941. Four metres in they hit rock, so they turned upward, sawing through seven oak floor timbers with blades filed from German table knives, then ran thirteen metres beneath the chapel before dropping again. The finished tunnel stretched forty-four metres, lit its whole length by electricity tapped from the chapel above. The wine cellar was swept and re-dusted with attic dust so the reddish clay would not betray them. On 15 January 1942 the Germans searched the sealed tower and lowered a small boy down the shaft. The tunnel was two metres short of daylight.

## The Watch She Did Not Pick Up

On 5 June 1941 a British party walking back from the park noticed a well-dressed woman drop her watch. One of them called after her. She kept walking, which was exactly the wrong thing to do, and the guards went after her: "she" was French Lieutenant Bouley. Disguise was the castle's favourite genre and its most reliable failure. On 28 December 1942 a French officer blew the courtyard lights, and while the real camp electrician Willi Pönert was inside repairing them, Lieutenant Perodeau — dressed almost identically, carrying a toolbox — walked out through the gate. At the next gate a guard asked for his token, the proof of identity issued precisely to stop this, and Perodeau surrendered without attempting to bluff. Flight Lieutenant Dominic Bruce chose cargo over costume, folding himself into a three-foot Red Cross packing case with a file and forty feet of bed-sheet rope. Carried up to a storeroom, he went down the wall that night and left the empty box inscribed: *Die Luft in Colditz gefällt mir nicht mehr. Auf Wiedersehen!*

## The Road Past Spiesshof

Getting out of the castle was only the first problem. The route that solved the second belonged to Dutch naval lieutenant Hans Larive, caught near the Swiss frontier in 1940 and interrogated by a Gestapo officer so certain Germany had already won that he explained, conversationally, where the border could be walked across. Larive memorised it: the Singen–Gottmadingen road, past the farm at Spiesshof on the tip of the Ramsen salient, then left onto a track and into Switzerland. He used it himself in August 1941, and after him Steinmetz, Luteyn, Neave, Reid and Wardle. Only one man died in all of this. In September 1944 Lieutenant Michael Sinclair went for the wire a final time; security officer Reinhold Eggers shouted a warning, the sentries fired, and a bullet struck Sinclair's elbow and ricocheted into his heart. The German garrison buried him with full military honours, under a Union Jack the guards had made themselves. He was the only man awarded the Distinguished Service Order for escaping in the Second World War.

## Flight Context

Colditz Castle stands at 51.131°N, 12.807°E, roughly 192 m above sea level on a spur over the Zwickauer Mulde in Saxony, about 45 km south-east of Leipzig and 40 km north-east of Chemnitz. From 3,000–5,000 ft AGL the pale Renaissance blocks and steep courtyard walls read clearly against the wooded river bend and the red-tiled town below. Leipzig/Halle (EDDP) lies about 50 km north-west, Leipzig–Altenburg (EDAC) to the south-west, and Dresden (EDDC) about 65 km east. Central German winters bring low stratus and valley haze along the Mulde; the courtyard and the drop to the river show best in mid-morning light.
