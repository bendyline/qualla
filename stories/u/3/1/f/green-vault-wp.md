# Green Vault

Augustus the Strong's mirrored treasure chambers in Dresden Castle, the 2019 axe raid that emptied three jewel sets, and what came back three years later.

**Location:** 51.0528, 13.7364

**Categories:** Museums in Germany, Buildings and structures in Dresden, Decorative arts museums in Germany, Art crime, Baroque

## Introduction

On the night of 25 November 2019, the most famous object in the Green Vault was on the other side of the Atlantic. The Dresden Green Diamond — forty-one carats, its colour the residue of natural radiation, the only large naturally green diamond ever found — was in New York, one of seventeen pieces on loan to the Metropolitan Museum of Art. Back in Dresden, before dawn, a fire in a nearby electrical box killed the streetlights outside the castle. Men cut through a window grille, went in with an axe, and came out with three of Augustus the Strong's jewel garnitures: twenty-one pieces set with more than four thousand three hundred diamonds. It took a few minutes to undo three centuries of accumulation.

## The Privy Repository

The rooms are older than the museum by nearly two hundred years. In 1547 the elector Moritz of Saxony ordered a new wing onto Dresden Castle, and four of its first-floor rooms were given elaborate moulded plaster ceilings with the column bases and capitals painted a bluish malachite green. Nobody planned the name; the colour supplied it. Officially these were the Geheime Verwahrung, the Privy Repository — thick walls, iron shutters, iron doors, built against fire and theft rather than for display. Through the seventeenth century the electors of Saxony used them exactly as intended, storing state documents alongside jewels in rooms almost nobody entered. The security arrangements of 1547 held for the better part of five hundred years, which is worth remembering when considering what happened in 2019.

## Augustus Draws a Floor Plan

Augustus the Strong founded the museum in 1723 and extended it in 1727, and a ground plan survives from that year with his own ideas sketched onto it. What he built was a sequence of eight interconnecting rooms staged by material: amber, then ivory, then white silver, then gilt silver, then the Pretiosensaal, the largest room, lined floor to ceiling with mirrors silvered with mercury, so that a visitor walking through it multiplied endlessly alongside the treasure. There are no showcases in the historic rooms. Gem vessels and bronzes stand exposed in front of mirrored walls, as he intended. He admitted a select public, which is why the Green Vault has a genuine claim to being among Europe's oldest museums — older than the British Museum of 1759, later than St Petersburg's Kunstkamera of 1714 and the Vatican's public Laocoön of 1506.

## Objects That Argue

Nothing here is only decorative. The Royal Household at Delhi, made by Johann Melchior Dinglinger, his brothers and their workshop between 1701 and 1708, holds 4,909 diamonds, 164 emeralds, 160 rubies, a sapphire, sixteen pearls and two cameos; Augustus paid almost 60,000 talers for it, more than Moritzburg Castle cost him — and he commissioned this fantasy of limitless imperial power while his own was collapsing in the Great Northern War. Elsewhere the register drops to the miniature: a cherry stone carved before 1589 with a reputed 185 faces, of which perhaps 113 can actually be made out; Jacob Zeller's ivory frigate crewed by fifty tiny sailors, finished in the summer of 1620 a few months before he died. The collection's celebrated figure with the emerald cluster still carries its eighteenth-century title, "Moor with Emerald Cluster," though the man Balthasar Permoser carved is an Indigenous South American, richly dressed, holding out on a tortoiseshell tray sixteen Colombian emeralds that Rudolf II had given to an earlier Elector Augustus in 1581 — a piece that says as much about how Dresden saw the world as about how it worked jewels.

## Two Vaults, One Castle

The bombing of Dresden in 1945 gutted these rooms; photographs from 1904 of the Jewel Chamber and the Pretiosensaal show an interior that no longer existed. Everything since has been reconstruction, and the museum now splits its holdings between two arguments about how treasure should be seen. The Historic Green Vault, on the first floor and covering about 2,000 square metres, restores Augustus's staging as it stood in 1733: roughly 3,000 objects, massed, mirrored, overwhelming, entered only by timed ticket in small groups. Directly above it the New Green Vault, of similar size across twelve rooms, does the opposite — around 1,100 pieces in neutral cases, each given room to be looked at on its own terms. One room is the whole argument in miniature: the Watzdorf Cabinet, holding a single hat clasp, with the Dresden Green Diamond at its centre.

## What Came Back

The thieves drove to Berlin, and the insured value of what they carried came to at least 113.8 million euros. The investigation, code-named Soko Epaulette after one of the missing pieces, ran for three years. On the night of 16 to 17 December 2022, following a plea agreement, Dresden prosecutors and Saxon police recovered thirty-one items in Berlin. On 16 May 2023 a Dresden court convicted five men, then aged between twenty-four and twenty-nine and connected to the Berlin-based Remmo family, of aggravated arson, theft with weapons and criminal damage; sentences ran from four years and four months to six years and three months, and a sixth defendant was acquitted. Two significant pieces are still gone: the epaulette that carried the Dresden White Diamond, and Queen Amalie Auguste's large bustle bow. The recovered jewels returned to public display in 2024, still bearing the damage they came back with, because they remain court property and cannot yet be restored.

## Flight Context

The Green Vault occupies the first and second floors of the western section of Dresden Castle at 51.053°N, 13.736°E, in the Altstadt on the south bank of the Elbe. From 2,000 to 4,000 feet the Baroque core reads as a single dense block between two river bends: the Zwinger to the west, the castle and Hofkirche in the middle, Brühl's Terrace along the water, and the dark dome of the Frauenkirche to the east — essentially the view Canaletto painted, from a higher seat. Dresden Airport (EDDC) lies about 9 km north of the centre with a single runway; Leipzig/Halle (EDDP) is roughly 100 km west-northwest; Prague (LKPR) about 130 km south across the Ore Mountains. The Elbe valley holds fog on winter mornings and channels wind along its axis; late afternoon light from the west sets the sandstone facades glowing amber, which is when the old town photographs best.
