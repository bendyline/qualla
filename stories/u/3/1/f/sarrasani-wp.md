# Sarrasani

A clown from Posen invented an Italian-sounding name, built a permanent stone circus in Dresden that he advertised as the Theatre of the 5000, and left a company that outlived the building, the regime and the country it started in.

**Location:** 51.0592, 13.7453

**Categories:** Circuses, Culture in Dresden, History of Dresden, Entertainment venues, German diaspora in Argentina

## Introduction

A circus is defined by leaving. It arrives, it astonishes, it packs into wagons overnight and the field is empty by morning — which is exactly what made Hans Stosch's decision so strange. In 1912 he opened a permanent stone circus building on the Carolaplatz in Dresden, designed by Max Littmann, one of the most sought-after theatre architects in Germany. Stosch had been born in Posen, in what is now Poznań, in 1873, and had spent his working life as a clown under the invented Italian name Giovanni Sarrasani. Now he had a house. He advertised it as the *Theater der 5000*, and the building police, less romantically, counted 3,860 seats. Both numbers tell you something true about him.

## The Theatre of the 5000

The seat count breaks down in the records with almost comic precision: 840 in the stalls and boxes, 920 in the first tier, 512 in the second, and 1,588 up in the gallery. Historians have generally concluded that five thousand was never achievable, and that Stosch — who by all accounts took safety seriously — knew it. The gap between the two figures is not fraud so much as trade practice. Sarrasani sold scale, and scale had to be announced. He had reached Radebeul near Dresden in 1901 and given his circus its official premiere in Meißen the following year, and within a decade he had persuaded a city known for opera houses that a circus deserved a permanent one too. The gallery seats, which outnumbered everything else, are the detail worth holding onto: this was mass entertainment, priced for people who could not afford the Semperoper.

## Two Hundred and Fifty Horses

The scale was real even if the seat count was not. In its peak years around 1926 and 1927 the company employed eight hundred people and kept 250 horses, 100 big cats, 22 elephants and 175 vehicles, along with two travelling tents that each held ten thousand spectators. Between 1923 and 1925 the whole apparatus — performers, staff, animals, equipment — crossed to South America on three ships and toured the continent. Stosch also understood publicity in a way that was ahead of his industry. He wrote adventure booklets, *Fahrten und Abenteuer* and *Mit Sarrasani in Südamerika*, in which the circus starred in its own fictionalised exploits, and roughly ten million copies went into circulation. People who never saw the tent still knew the name, which was the entire idea.

## Edward Two-Two

Among the acts was a Wild West show performed by Lakota Sioux from the Pine Ridge Reservation in South Dakota — men hired to enact, for German audiences, a version of themselves that those audiences had largely learned from adventure novels. The Karl May Museum opened in nearby Radebeul in the same era, which is the cultural weather this took place in. The best-remembered of the performers was Edward Two-Two, born around 1851 and billed by the circus as its Sioux chief. He worked at Sarrasani in 1913 and 1914 and died on 27 July 1914 in Essen, while the company was on tour. What happened next is harder to file away neatly. He had asked to be buried in Dresden, citing the treatment and recognition his family had received there, and his body was brought back and interred in the city's New Catholic Cemetery, where his grave still stands with an inscription in Lakota. He had travelled a very long way from Pine Ridge, and he chose where to stop.

## Three Directors and a Regime

Stosch-Sarrasani senior ran the circus until his death in São Paulo in 1934, having fallen out repeatedly with officials at home and particularly with the rising Nazi party, which pushed him toward emigration. His son inherited the name along with the debts and the political problem. Junior held the company together from 1934 to 1941 through pragmatic accommodation, including a negotiation with Joseph Goebbels to bring the circus back to Germany for the 1936 Olympics in Berlin. When he died the company passed to his widow, Trude Stosch-Sarrasani, who took charge in 1941 at the age of twenty-eight and ran it by suggestion rather than command. In 1944 she was imprisoned on a charge of anti-German behaviour and released after two weeks to keep performing, while her partner, the Hungarian acrobat Gabor Némedy, was held as leverage against her.

## Carolaplatz to Buenos Aires

The building was destroyed on 13 February 1945 in the bombing of Dresden. Trude survived the raid, and after the war she started again as an equestrian performer working for other people's circuses. In 1948 an Argentine producer invited her to rebuild Sarrasani in Buenos Aires; Juan Perón and Eva Perón attended, and a close relationship with them followed. Under the second Peronist government the company performed as the Circo Nacional Argentino, and for close to half a century the German name was effectively out of use. Trude ran it into the mid-1970s, adapting to cinema, television and shifting attitudes toward performing animals, and died on 4 June 2009 at San Clemente del Tuyú on the Atlantic coast, aged ninety-six. The Carolaplatz site carries housing now. What marks it is a fountain.

## Flight Context

The Sarrasani building stood at 51.0592°N, 13.7453°E, on the Carolaplatz in Dresden's Innere Neustadt, on the right bank of the Elbe immediately north of the Carolabrücke. Nothing of it survives: from 1,500–3,000 ft AGL the site reads as ordinary postwar and modern housing between the Albertplatz axis and the river, with the Japanese Palace a short distance west and the baroque cluster of the old town directly across the water. Use the Carolabrücke and the Neustadt riverbank as the reference. Dresden Airport (EDDC) lies about 8 km north; Leipzig/Halle (EDDP) roughly 100 km west-north-west. Autumn and winter mornings hold fog in the valley; the Neustadt bank shows best in morning light, with the sun behind you looking south across the Elbe.
