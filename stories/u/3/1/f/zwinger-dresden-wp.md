# Zwinger (Dresden)

Augustus the Strong's late-baroque pleasure palace in Dresden, built for orange trees and royal weddings, bombed nearly to dust in 1945, and patiently put back together stone by stone.

**Location:** 51.0531, 13.7339

**Categories:** palace, baroque, architecture, museum, germany, dresden

## Introduction

Augustus the Strong came home from Versailles in 1689 with an idea that would have struck most rulers as impractical and struck Augustus as obviously necessary. He would build a forecourt — just a forecourt, mind, not yet the palace itself — large enough to display every orange tree, every fountain, every sculpted pavilion that an Elector of Saxony and king-elect of Poland could possibly require for a really good party. The palace behind it was never built. The forecourt is the Zwinger, and three centuries later it is still throwing the party.

## A Word From the Walls

The name is older than the building and originally meant something quite different. In medieval German, a Zwinger was the killing ground between the inner and outer walls of a fortress, a narrow strip of land where attackers who breached the outer defenses found themselves trapped and exposed. Dresden's first city walls went up in the late 12th century, and by 1427, during the Hussite Wars, a second outer wall enclosed the Zwingergarten, a kitchen garden tucked between the fortifications. By the time Augustus the Strong started building in 1709, the walls were obsolete and the garden was about to become something nobody had ever attempted before: a palace whose entire purpose was joy. The name stuck anyway. A killing ground turned into a place for orange trees.

## Pöppelmann and Permoser

Matthäus Daniel Pöppelmann was the court architect, and Balthasar Permoser was the sculptor, and between them they produced one of the most exuberant statements of the late baroque anywhere in Germany. The Zwinger is not a building so much as a hollow square of buildings — pavilions linked by arcaded galleries, each pavilion topped with curving rooflines and crowded with sculpted figures, each balustrade lined with stone vases and carved nymphs. The Kronentor, the crown gate, sits where the southwest wall once ran, and you can still see traces of the medieval fortification in its lower courses. Construction stretched from 1710 to 1728. The complex was formally inaugurated in 1719 for the wedding of the electoral prince to the Habsburg archduchess Maria Josepha — though the interiors weren't finished, the outer shells provided exactly the kind of imperial backdrop the occasion required.

## Oranges, Then Paintings

The original use was practical in a way that sounds frivolous now: the Zwinger was an orangery, a place to overwinter the citrus trees Augustus had become obsessed with. It was also a festival ground, an open-air stage for the kind of court spectacles a king-elector who collected porcelain and mistresses with equal enthusiasm felt his subjects should be able to admire from a respectful distance. After Augustus died in 1733 the new-castle plans were quietly shelved, and the Zwinger lost some of its purpose. A century and a quarter later, the architect Gottfried Semper closed the open Elbe-facing side with the Sempergalerie, opened in 1855, and the building's second life began. The Old Masters Picture Gallery moved in. So did the Porcelain Collection — Augustus's own, the one for which he had once traded a regiment of dragoons to the Prussian king. So did the Royal Cabinet of Mathematical and Physical Instruments. The pleasure palace had become a museum of pleasures.

## February 1945

On the nights of February 13 and 14, 1945, RAF Bomber Command and the US Eighth Air Force destroyed the center of Dresden. The Zwinger burned with the rest of it. The art had been evacuated, prudently, before the raids — the Sistine Madonna by Raphael, the Vermeers, the Rembrandts, the porcelain — but the buildings themselves were almost entirely gutted. Photographs from the months after show roofless pavilions, collapsed galleries, the Wallpavillon reduced to a charred shell. Reconstruction began that same year, supported by the Soviet military administration. By 1951 parts of the restored complex were open to the public again. By 1963 the Zwinger had been substantially returned to its pre-war state, stone by stone, sculpture by sculpture, much of the work done from prewar photographs and the surviving fragments. The building you walk through now is partly 18th century, partly 1950s, and entirely Pöppelmann.

## The Party Continues

Augustus the Strong's accounts were notoriously expensive. His Polish-Saxon court ran on borrowed money, and the people of Saxony paid for it in taxes for generations after his death. It would be wrong to look at the Zwinger and not notice that — the curling rocailles and the gold-tipped finials were not free, and they were not paid for by the man whose name is on them. They were paid for by everyone else. But the building survived its patron and his debts and the bombs and the long Soviet aftermath, and it sits today on a square in the heart of Dresden where music still plays and the Sistine Madonna still looks down from her wall in the Sempergalerie, and the orange trees, in summer, still come out into the courtyard. Joy, as a building program, has its critics. It also has its defenders.

## Flight Context

The Zwinger sits at 51.053°N, 13.734°E in the heart of Dresden's Innere Altstadt, a few hundred metres from the Elbe. From the air, it is unmistakable: a hollow rectangle of pavilions and galleries surrounding a vast formal courtyard, pressed against the Semperoper to the north and Dresden Castle to the east. Dresden International Airport (EDDC) lies about 9 km northwest. Best viewed from low altitude in clear weather to make out the Kronentor and the Wallpavillon; the rebuilt complex glows pale ochre against the dark roofs of the Altstadt.
