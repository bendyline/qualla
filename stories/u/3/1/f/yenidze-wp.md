# Yenidze

Dresden forbade anything that looked like a factory near its baroque centre, so a cigarette manufacturer built his chimney as a minaret and put a stained-glass dome on the roof.

**Location:** 51.0590, 13.7269

**Categories:** Tourist attractions in Dresden, Buildings and structures in Dresden, Moorish Revival architecture in Germany, Tobacco buildings, Industrial heritage

## Introduction

The rule was simple: nothing in this part of Dresden could look like a factory. The baroque skyline was the city's asset, and a smokestack rising beside the Elbe among the domes and spires was not going to be permitted. Hugo Zietz needed a cigarette works and needed it here, so in 1907 he hired an architect named Martin Hammitzsch and set him the strangest brief in German industrial architecture: build a factory that reads as anything except a factory. Hammitzsch's answer went up between 1907 and 1909 and still stops traffic. The chimney is there — it is simply dressed as a minaret. The roof carries a dome of coloured glass twenty metres high. Six hundred windows, in a bewildering variety of shapes, are set into walls tiled in intricate colour patterns and unusual three-dimensional forms.

## The Rule That Built a Minaret

Read the building as a piece of problem-solving and it becomes very funny. Every element that would have given a factory away has been converted into something else. The stacks that had to vent the works became tall, banded, brightly capped towers. The mass of the production floors is broken up by window shapes that change from bay to bay, so the eye never settles into the rhythm that says warehouse. Where a factory would have shown brick, Yenidze shows glazed tile: pattern upon pattern, some of it modelled in relief so the surface catches the low light off the river. The glass dome, which would be a skylight over a shop floor anywhere else, becomes the building's crown. It is an industrial building wearing a very elaborate costume, and after more than a century the costume has become the point.

## Where the Leaf Came From

The costume was also advertising, and it was specific. Zietz — a Jewish entrepreneur whose firm was the Yenidze Tobacco and Cigarette Factory — imported his tobacco from Ottoman Yenidze in Thrace, a town that is now Genisea in Greece. His building announced the origin of the leaf to anyone standing across the Elbe. Hammitzsch borrowed his vocabulary from mosque architecture and from the Alhambra in Spain. It is worth being clear about what this is and is not. Yenidze is a European trader's picture of the Ottoman and Moorish world, assembled for commercial reasons in a Saxon city, and it has never been a place of worship. Dresden nicknamed it the "tobacco mosque." The name stuck locally, but it is no longer used officially, for the straightforward reason that the building is not a mosque and never was.

## The Architect Nobody Forgave

Dresden's architectural establishment did not find any of it charming. The city's chamber of architects expelled Hammitzsch over the design, judging it an affront to the dignity of a place that took its own skyline extremely seriously. He had, in fairness, done exactly what the regulations demanded and exactly what his client wanted, which is often the fastest route to professional disgrace. Years later Hammitzsch married Angela Hitler, the half-sister of Adolf Hitler — a connection that hangs oddly over a building commissioned by a Jewish businessman, and one worth knowing when you stand under the dome.

## The Night the Neighbourhood Went

On the nights of 13 to 15 February 1945, Allied bombers destroyed central Dresden. As many as twenty-five thousand people died, according to the commission of historians the city later appointed to establish the number — a figure argued about for decades because so little was left to count. The blocks around Yenidze were obliterated. The building came through it standing, having lost some of the coloured glass out of its dome and little else. For the next several decades that survival looked less like luck than like a curse. Yenidze stood in the middle of an emptiness, a fantastical tiled tower with almost nothing around it, too strange to demolish and too isolated to justify repairing.

## Offices Under the Dome

It took reunification and the rebuilding of the surrounding district in the 1990s to make restoration make sense, and the work was completed in 1996. Today around thirty companies and organisations rent offices behind the tilework. Under the glass dome, where a hundred years ago cigarette machinery ran, there is a restaurant across two floors with a summer terrace, and above it a company called 1001 Märchen stages storytelling evenings — fairy tales told beneath the coloured glass, most nights of the week. The performance is orientalist theatre in a building that is orientalist theatre, which is either perfectly appropriate or slightly too neat, depending on your mood. Either way, the dome is lit at night, and from the Elbe bridges Dresden's skyline of baroque domes now includes one that was designed to sell cigarettes.

## Flight Context

Yenidze stands at 51.059°N, 13.727°E, on the left bank of the Elbe just west of Dresden's historic centre, close to the Ostragehege and the Marienbrücke. From 1,500–3,000 ft AGL it is unmistakable: a single square tiled block with a luminous coloured-glass dome and minaret-like capped corner towers, sitting about a kilometre west of the Zwinger and Frauenkirche cluster. Dresden Airport (EDDC) lies about 9 km north at Klotzsche; Leipzig/Halle (EDDP) is roughly 100 km north-west. The Elbe valley traps morning mist in autumn and winter; the dome reads best in late-afternoon light from the north bank, and it is illuminated after dark.
