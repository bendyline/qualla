# Transparent Factory

Volkswagen built a car plant with glass walls and a Canadian maple floor in the corner of a baroque park, ran it for 23 years, and switched it off on 16 December 2025.

**Location:** 51.0444, 13.7556

**Categories:** Industrial architecture, Buildings and structures in Dresden, Motor vehicle assembly plants in Germany, Tourist attractions in Dresden, Glass architecture

## Introduction

Car plants are usually sealed things — long sheds on ring roads, lit from within, showing nothing. In a corner of Dresden's Großer Garten, Volkswagen built the opposite. The walls were glass. The floor was Canadian maple, laid end to end like a concert hall, and the assembly line running across it used 60,000 magnets. Up to 250 visitors a day could walk in and watch a luxury sedan being screwed together on parquet. The German name, Gläserne Manufaktur, puns on two kinds of transparency at once, the optical and the procedural, and the building meant both. There were no smokestacks and no toxic byproducts. The only noise nuisance was one the architects created on purpose: an outdoor speaker system that broadcasts recorded birdsong, territorial calls warning real birds that the glass is already someone else's patch.

## Glass in a Baroque Park

Gunter Henn of Henn GmbH designed it, and the site he was given would have made most industrial architects flinch. The Großer Garten is Dresden's great baroque park, and the plot on its northwestern corner had held a convention centre before the Second World War. Putting a Volkswagen plant there in a city better known for porcelain, opera and rebuilt sandstone required an argument, and the argument was the building itself: no chimneys, no noise, no fumes, 350 trees planted on the grounds, and a wall you could see straight through. It opened in 2002. From the park it read less like a factory than a very large vitrine, which was exactly the point. Craftsmanship was Dresden's brand long before anyone assembled a car here, and Volkswagen borrowed it deliberately, calling the place a Manufaktur rather than a Werk.

## The Tram That Brought the Doors

Almost nothing was actually made in the glass building. Stamping, welding and paint all happened at Zwickau, about 95 km to the west-southwest, and the painted steel bodies arrived in Dresden by truck. The remaining 1,200 parts and 34 preassembled components came a stranger way. Volkswagen kept a logistics centre a few kilometres off and ran the components in on the CarGoTram, a freight tram that shared the city's ordinary public transport tracks — the same rails carrying commuters to work carried gearboxes and door panels between them. It ran for the better part of two decades, through the whole Phaeton era, before the switch to electric production made it uneconomic and it was retired. For a while Dresden was the only city in the world where a tram stopping at Pirnaischer Platz might have a car factory's morning delivery inside it.

## Phaetons, and a Few Bentleys

The car the building was designed around was the Phaeton, Volkswagen's attempt at a full-size luxury sedan, and for years the two were inseparable — the visitor attractions, built by BRC Imagination Arts, included a Phaeton configurator, a motion-base virtual test drive in a real Phaeton, and a delivery ceremony staged for customers collecting their new car. Spare capacity went to Bentley. The Continental Flying Spur bound for European buyers was assembled here until 2006, when all of that work moved to Bentley's plant at Crewe in England, and Flying Spur production returned to Dresden in late 2013. Phaeton assembly stopped in March 2016 and the line went quiet. Whatever else the building had proved about glass and maple and the theatre of manufacturing, it had been built around a single model, and the model had reached its end.

## The Second Life

Production restarted in 2017 with the electric Golf, and from January 2021 the plant built the ID.3, sharing the model with the far larger Zwickau-Mosel works. The visitor offer changed with the cars: instead of configuring a Phaeton you could take a Volkswagen electric vehicle out for a thirty-minute drive, walk a virtual tour of Dresden, and look at displays on hybrid and battery technology. The building had always been half exhibition anyway. ZDF used to film the philosophy panel show Das Philosophische Quartett inside it, four people arguing about ethics on a factory floor, which is not a sentence that applies to many production sites. Through all of it the birdsong speakers kept playing outside, holding the territory against collisions.

## 16 December 2025

The last vehicle came off the line on 16 December 2025. Around 320 people had been working there that spring, and the closure was the first time the Volkswagen Group had shut a manufacturing plant inside Germany. The glass is not going dark, though. Volkswagen is keeping sales, test drives and events on the site, along with a programme showing visitors how cars are put together, and part of the former production floor is being handed to a joint innovation campus with the Technische Universität Dresden, with projects starting from 2026. A building designed to make manufacturing legible has outlived the manufacturing. That is an odd fate for a factory, but it is a survivable one — the thing everybody came to look at was never really the car.

## Flight Context

The Transparent Factory sits at 51.0444°N, 13.7556°E, on the northwestern corner of the Großer Garten and roughly 1.5 km southeast of Dresden's Altstadt. From 1,500–2,500 ft AGL it is one of the easiest buildings in the city to pick out: a low, wide rectangle of glass and pale steel pressed against the dark green block of the park, catching sun where every roof around it is red tile. Use the Großer Garten itself as the marker — a clean rectangle of woodland inside the built-up area — then look to its northwest corner. Dresden Airport (EDDC) lies about 10 km north, so this is busy controlled airspace; Leipzig/Halle (EDDP) is roughly 100 km west-northwest. The glass reads best in low, oblique light, early or late; at midday in summer it can flare badly enough to wash out from directly overhead.
