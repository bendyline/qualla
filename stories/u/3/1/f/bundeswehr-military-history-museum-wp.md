# Bundeswehr Military History Museum

Daniel Libeskind drove a steel-and-glass wedge through a 19th-century arsenal in Dresden — a museum building that physically refuses to let German military history be celebrated.

**Location:** 51.0784, 13.7600

**Categories:** Museums, Dresden, Daniel Libeskind, German military history, Saxony

## Introduction

Daniel Libeskind's addition to the Saxon arsenal in Dresden does not sit politely beside the older building. It splits it. A wedge of steel and glass stabs from one face through to the other, breaking the symmetry of the 1873 sandstone arsenal at a sharp angle, the way a shard would split a loaf of bread. The wedge is aimed, deliberately, toward the part of Dresden where the firebombing began on the night of 13 February 1945. This is the Bundeswehr Military History Museum, and the building itself is the first exhibit: a museum that argues with its own subject.

## An Arsenal That Kept Changing Sides

The original building went up between 1873 and 1876 as a Royal Saxon armory. By 1897 it had become a museum, and over the next century it kept being renamed by whoever happened to be running Saxony at the time. Royal Saxon Army Museum. Saxon Army Museum. Dresden Army Museum under the Wehrmacht. Army Museum of the GDR from 1972. In each iteration the institution did roughly the same work — celebrating the armed forces of a particular regime — and in each iteration the regime changed. The museum closed in 1989 as East Germany collapsed, and in 1990 it was renamed and taken over by the Bundeswehr, the army of a reunifying Germany. It took more than a decade for Germany to settle what kind of military museum a democracy could honestly run.

## Libeskind's Wedge

By 2001 the answer had emerged: a museum that refused the entire celebratory tradition. An architectural competition was held for an extension that would force visitors to reconsider the way they think about war. Daniel Libeskind won. His addition is a 30-metre wedge of steel and glass that pierces the older arsenal, splitting its façade and rising above its roofline. Libeskind's studio described the contrast in plain terms: the openness and transparency of the new façade represents democratic society, while the rigidity of the existing building represents the severity of the authoritarian past. The wedge points toward the part of the city where the firebombing began on the night of 13 February 1945, when British Lancasters opened the raids that American B-17s continued by day. The architecture itself becomes an accusation.

## Looking at War from the Inside Out

The exhibitions inside the museum follow the same logic. Traditional military museums display weapons and uniforms in chronological order, glorifying technology and strategy. The Bundeswehr museum chose a different approach. Visitors can take eleven thematic tours — on subjects like animals in war, war and play, war and suffering — alongside the chronological galleries. Witness accounts run alongside the tanks and rifles. The collections are immense: 4,250 firearms, 70,000 field uniforms, 1,100 paintings, nearly a million photographs. The original Brandtaucher, Germany's first submarine, sits in one gallery; a Soviet-East German Soyuz 29 capsule from 1978 sits in another. But the museum's stated focus is not the hardware. It is the individual who exercises violence, or suffers from it. The same wedge that splits the building from outside slices through the chronological exhibitions inside, breaking the illusion that military history can be told as a steady march of progress.

## A Museum About How to Run a Museum

The collection has a Spencer jacket from 1805 that belonged to Queen Louise of Mecklenburg-Strelitz, mother of the first German Emperor Wilhelm I. It has a copy of Hobbes's Leviathan and one of Clausewitz's On War. It has the rod-ring gun called Faule Magd from around 1450, and a destroyer war badge from 1957, and some 1,700 sabres and swords, alongside a firearms collection whose rare Saxon pieces make it one of the most important in Germany. None of these objects, on their own, justify Libeskind's wedge. What justifies the wedge is the question the building forces every visitor to confront before they see a single artifact: how do you exhibit the history of an army that destroyed Europe, in a country that has spent eighty years asking what its responsibility to that history should be? The Bundeswehr Military History Museum's answer is to refuse the celebratory frame entirely. The arsenal is still there. The wedge is what cuts it open.

## Flight Context

The museum sits at 51.078°N, 13.760°E, in the Albertstadt military district about 3 km north of central Dresden, on the right bank of the Elbe. From altitude, look for the dense urban core of Dresden along the river bend, with the Albertstadt district to the north. The nearest major airport is Dresden (EDDC), about 6 km north of the museum. Leipzig/Halle (EDDP) is about 110 km west-northwest. The Libeskind wedge is too small to identify from cruise altitude, but the regular grid of the Albertstadt military quarter is visible against the surrounding city.
