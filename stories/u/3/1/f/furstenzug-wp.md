# Fürstenzug

Dresden's mural of eight hundred years of Saxon rulers failed within a generation as plaster, so the city rebuilt it out of 23,000 porcelain tiles — and that is why it survived 1945.

**Location:** 51.0527, 13.7392

**Categories:** Culture in Dresden, Tourist attractions in Dresden, Public art in Germany, Meissen porcelain, Dresden Castle

## Introduction

The mural did not last thirty years. Wilhelm Walther finished his sgraffito procession of Saxon rulers along the Stallhof wall in 1876, and Dresden's damp winters and coal smoke set about ruining it almost immediately: the plaster softened, the figures blurred, and a monument meant for the ages began to look its worst within a generation. The fix the city settled on between 1904 and 1907 sounds like a joke until you are standing in front of it. They rebuilt the picture out of dishware. Roughly 23,000 Meissen porcelain tiles, each about 20.5 centimetres square, were fired with the design and mounted along Augustusstraße, and the finished wall runs 101.9 metres — the largest porcelain artwork in the world. It has outlasted the plaster it replaced by more than a century. It also outlasted the city around it.

## Eight Centuries in the Saddle

Thirty-five rulers ride this wall, and they ride in strict order. The line opens with Conrad, Margrave of Meissen, who held the mark from 1127 to 1156, and closes with George of Saxony, king for barely two years before his death in 1904. Between them come margraves, electors, dukes and kings of the House of Wettin: a run of Fredericks through the medieval centuries, four John Georges in unbroken succession from 1611 to 1694, and Augustus the Strong at the height of Saxon extravagance. Each man's name is inscribed beneath him and each wears the dress of his own century, so mail gives way to plate, plate to ruffs, ruffs to full-bottomed wigs, wigs to nineteenth-century military tailoring — the whole wardrobe of European power passing by at walking pace. Walther designed it for the eight hundredth anniversary of Wettin rule, which fell in 1889, in a country that had itself only just been assembled into an empire in 1871.

## The Crowd Behind the Kings

Follow the procession slowly and it stops being about the riders. Fifty-nine other figures walk among the horses — scholars, craftsmen, farmers, soldiers, children — the people on whose labour those eight ceremonial centuries actually rested. One girl stands in the children's group, and she is the only female figure along the entire 102 metres, which tells you something about what dynastic history chose to record. Last in the whole line, on foot and easy to miss, walks Wilhelm Walther himself. Two rulers are missing. Heinrich I von Eilenburg, the ancestor of around 1089 from whom the dynasty counted its eight hundred years, never made the wall at all. Neither did Frederick Augustus III, who became the last king of Saxony in 1904 and reigned until the monarchy ended in 1918 — by then the tiles were already fired and hung, and the procession had closed behind him.

## Thirteen February

On the night of 13 February 1945, Allied bombers began the raids that burned out the centre of Dresden. Thousands of people died in the firestorm. The Frauenkirche came down, Brühl's Terrace was gutted, and a Baroque city that had drawn visitors for two hundred years was reduced to a field of standing façades and open sky. The Fürstenzug came through with only minimal damage to its tiles. There is no mystery in it: the picture had already survived a kiln. Porcelain chosen to withstand a century of Dresden's weather turned out to be proof against something far worse, and when the smoke cleared, thirty-five Saxon princes were still riding along a wall in a quarter where almost nothing else was left standing. The city rebuilt around them for the next seventy years.

## How to Read a Wall

The Fürstenzug has never been in a museum. It is the outer face of the Stallhof, the stables courtyard of Dresden Castle, where the electors once staged tournaments, and by 1589 that wall was already carrying a painted fresco — which means Wettins have been depicted on this same stretch of masonry for well over four centuries. Come at it from Schlossplatz and the sequence unrolls in the right direction: riders above head height, names below, eighteen windows punched through the upper zone that the composition simply rides around. The wall stands 10.5 metres tall. Seeing all of it at once from the street is nearly impossible, and that turns out to be the point — the only honest way to take in the Fürstenzug is to walk its full length, at roughly the pace of the horses.

## Flight Context

The Fürstenzug runs along Augustusstraße at 51.0527°N, 13.7392°E, on the north-east edge of Dresden's Altstadt between Schlossplatz and the Neumarkt. From the air the landmark is not the wall itself but the block it belongs to: Dresden Castle and the Stallhof sit immediately south of the Elbe bend, with the Augustus Bridge running north from Schlossplatz and the rebuilt dome of the Frauenkirche a few hundred metres east. Dresden (EDDC) lies about 9 km north; Leipzig/Halle (EDDP) roughly 100 km north-west; Prague (LKPR) about 130 km south. The Elbe valley here traps morning fog in autumn and winter, and 1,500–2,500 ft AGL gives the clearest read on the Altstadt's roofline. Late afternoon light falls along Augustusstraße's north side, which is the side the porcelain is on.
