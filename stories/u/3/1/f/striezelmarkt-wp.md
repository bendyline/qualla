# Striezelmarkt

The oldest Christmas market of its kind began in 1434 as a single day of meat sales to break the Advent fast — and grew into 240 stands, three million visitors, and a fourteen-metre candle pyramid built by people whose ancestors carved because the mines closed.

**Location:** 51.0496, 13.7380

**Categories:** Christmas markets in Germany, Tourist attractions in Dresden, Recurring events established in the 15th century, Festivals in Saxony, Ore Mountains

## Introduction

It started as a butcher's day. In 1434 the Elector Friedrich II granted the citizens of Dresden a one-day market on the Altmarkt, held on the Monday before Christmas, and the point of it was meat. Advent was a fast, the fast ended at Christmas, and people needed to buy the roast. That is the whole origin of what is now generally counted among the first true Christmas markets in the world — no carols, no lights, no gingerbread, just a licensed opportunity to lay in provisions before a feast. Six centuries later the same square carries 240 stands for the entire Advent season and draws around three million people a year. In 2024 it marked its 590th anniversary.

## The Cake That Named the Market

The market is named after a cake, which is a decent indication of Dresden's priorities. *Striezel* comes from *Strüzel* or *Stroczel*, the local name for a loaf sold on the square, and the world now knows it as Stollen. It is a light, airy fruit bread, surprisingly low in sugar, and the genuine Dresden version is baked in the city and carries a seal depicting Augustus II, Elector of Saxony and King of Poland — a certification mark that predates most of the food labelling in Europe. The shape has a meaning too, and it is not the swaddled infant that tourists are sometimes told. *Stollen* is the German word for a mine adit, and the cake is shaped like a tunnel mouth. Everything on this square keeps pointing back at the mountains.

## Miners Who Became Woodcarvers

Silver and tin were found in the Ore Mountains around 1168, and the discovery pulled workers into the range south of Dresden for centuries. Then the work failed — undercut by the disruption of the Thirty Years' War and by the exhaustion of the easily reached ore — and the miners needed something else to sell. They took up woodcarving, and they carved what they knew. The *Schwibbogen*, the arched candle holder whose name comes from an arch that seems to hang between two walls, represents the mouth of a mine hung with guiding lights. During Advent, nearly every window in Dresden holds one. The candle pyramid works on rising heat: two to five turned tiers on a central rod, figures on each, a rotor at the top driven by the candle flames beneath. A domestic one stands about fifty centimetres tall. The one on the Altmarkt is fourteen metres, and the Christmas arch beside it is 13.5 metres wide and over five metres high.

## The Prune Chimney Sweeps

The strangest thing sold here is a small figure made of prunes: a chimney sweep in black with a top hat and a brush, called a *Pflaumentoffel*. The name translates roughly as plum devil, and it also goes by *Feuerrüpel*, but it was never meant to be a devil or a Santa — it is a sweep, and sweeps were luck. For a long time these were not sold from stands at all. Children carried them through the market on trays, working the crowd to earn a little money at Christmas, and Ludwig Richter drew them in a woodcut of 1853, *Ausverkauft wegen Geschäftsaufgabe* — sold out, going out of business. The *Striezelkinder* have a certain charm at this distance. It is worth remembering that they were children working a winter market because their families needed the coins, and that the practice was not sentimental to them. Selling by children was banned at the Striezelmarkt in 1910.

## Pfefferkuchen and Glühwein

Nuremberg is more famous for gingerbread, but Pulsnitz has been at it nearly as long. The town, about fifty kilometres from Dresden, was licensed to bake *Pfefferkuchen* by a decree of 1558, and in 1780 eight master bakers travelled down in person to sell their work on the Altmarkt. Their recipe defies the name twice over: *Pfefferkuchen* means pepper cake, though pepper here just meant any expensive imported spice, and the dough contains no ginger at all. What goes in is nutmeg, cinnamon, ground cloves and allspice, added after the dough has been left to mature for as long as six months, with no fat in it. The result is usually filled with jam and coated in chocolate. Alongside it, in decorated mugs that people take home and keep, comes the mulled wine — hot red wine with cloves and cinnamon, which on a December night in Saxony is less a drink than a heating system.

## The Square in December

At the centre stands a spruce roughly twenty metres tall, cut from the Tharandt woods south-west of the city and strung with lights. At the back of the square is a painted wooden fairy-tale castle that works as an enormous Advent calendar: a door opens each day and a puppet show comes out of it, and on Fridays Father Christmas turns up in person. There is a children's world with a prune-sweep cottage and a bakery where you can knead dough, a puppet theatre, a carousel, a small railway. A stage programme runs daily, and Advent weekends bring the Stollen festival and a festival for the pyramid and the Christmas arch. The market runs to Christmas Eve, then the stands come down and the Altmarkt goes back to being a large empty square in a cold city — which is what it was on that first Monday in 1434, when someone came to buy meat.

## Flight Context

The Striezelmarkt occupies the Altmarkt at 51.0496°N, 13.7380°E, in the centre of Dresden's old town about 400 m south-west of the Frauenkirche and just inland from the Elbe. From 1,500–3,000 ft AGL the Altmarkt is the large open rectangle immediately south of the Kreuzkirche, whose single dark tower is the nearest reference point; the Frauenkirche dome, the Zwinger and Brühl's Terrace lie a short distance north-east toward the river. During Advent the square is unmistakable after dark — a dense lit grid of stalls with the pyramid at its centre. Dresden Airport (EDDC) is about 9 km north; Leipzig/Halle (EDDP) roughly 100 km west-north-west. December brings frequent low cloud and valley fog, and daylight is short; the market is at its most photogenic from the air in the blue hour, around 16:00 local.
