# Brandtaucher

Wilhelm Bauer's 1850 submarine sank on its acceptance trial in Kiel harbour; the three men trapped inside escaped by deliberately flooding the hull, and the boat now sits in a Dresden museum.

**Location:** 51.0785, 13.7598

**Categories:** 19th-century submarines, Submarines of Germany, Ships preserved in museums, German inventions, Museum ships in Germany

## Introduction

Sixteen metres of water stood over the hatch, and the hatch would not open. Three men sat in the dark inside a hand-cranked iron submarine on the floor of Kiel harbour on 1 February 1851, and their commander told them the plan: let the sea in. Wilhelm Bauer had worked out that the hatch was held shut by the pressure difference across it, and that the only way to equalise the two sides was to let the harbour flood the boat until the trapped air was squeezed hard enough to match. It meant sitting still while cold water climbed past their boots, their knees, their chests, for six hours, on the reasoning of a man whose invention had just failed. Then the hatch gave. All three swam up. It was the first submarine escape ever witnessed and recorded, and the boat they left on the bottom is still with us.

## A Cavalryman's Sketch

Bauer was Bavarian, an engineer by inclination and a cavalryman by circumstance, serving in the First Schleswig War while the Danish navy blockaded German ports. Blockades are a problem of geometry: the ships sit where you cannot reach them. In January 1850 Bauer sketched an answer. A boat that could travel underwater might slip beneath a hull, fix a mine to it, back away, and detonate the charge from a safe distance — and the Danish fleet would never see what had done it. The drawing reached the Minister of Marine, who authorised a small working model. It was demonstrated in Kiel harbour in front of naval dignitaries and performed well enough that a full-size boat was approved. Nobody, however, was prepared to pay for it out of the naval budget. The money came instead from a public subscription: army personnel and Kiel civilians putting in what they could.

## What the Budget Cut Away

Subscriptions do not stretch, and everything expensive came out of the design. The intended diving depth dropped from thirty metres to nine and a half. The separate control compartment disappeared, replaced by a half-tonne adjustable weight for trimming and a twenty-tonne iron ballast keel. Most consequential of all, the enclosed ballast tanks were deleted. Instead of being confined, the water taken aboard simply pooled in the bottom of the hull below the deck plates, free to slosh from end to end whenever the boat changed attitude. Every naval architect since has recognised what that means: the boat's own ballast becomes a weight that runs downhill, so any pitch tends to steepen itself. The vessel that Schweffel & Howaldt built in Kiel in 1850 was clever, cheap and unstable, and the instability is almost certainly what killed the trial.

## Three Men and a Treadwheel

Brandtaucher — Fire-diver — was 8.07 metres long, just over two metres at the beam, and drew 2.63 metres. There was no engine of any kind. Propulsion came from the crew: three men walking inside large tread wheels geared to a single propeller, which is precisely as strenuous as it sounds. The boat could make three knots that way, but not for long, and the men who made it go were also the men breathing the fixed volume of air they had brought down with them. Riveted iron plate, a few small ports, and the smell of oil and wet metal — this was the working environment of an early military submersible, more than sixty years before submarines became a routine instrument of war.

## 1 February 1851

The acceptance trial went wrong quickly. Equipment failed, the loose water in the bilge ran forward, the bow went down, and Brandtaucher slid into a sixteen-metre hole in the harbour floor and stopped there. The pressure outside now held the hatch shut like a hand on a lid. Bauer's solution was the exact opposite of every instinct a trapped person has, and it depended on his crew trusting arithmetic over panic: open the boat deliberately, let it fill, and wait for the compressed air inside to reach the pressure of the water outside. There was no way to hurry it. Six hours went by. When the pressures finally matched, the hatch swung and the three of them rode the escaping air to the surface. Naval observers on the boats above saw them come up, and their account is the first record of anyone escaping from a sunken submarine.

## Raised, and Kept

Bauer went on to design submarines elsewhere, including one built for the Russian navy. His first boat, meanwhile, stayed on the harbour bottom for thirty-six years until it was found and lifted out on 5 July 1887, still intact. It went first to the Naval Academy at Kiel, then in 1906 to the Museum für Meereskunde in Berlin. East Germany restored it at Rostock between 1963 and 1965 and displayed it at the National People's Army museum in Potsdam. Today the oldest surviving submarine known to exist sits well inland, in the Militärhistorisches Museum der Bundeswehr in Dresden — a riveted iron hull small enough to walk around in a few seconds, and much easier to imagine three grown men trapped inside once you have stood beside it.

## Flight Context

Brandtaucher is displayed at 51.079°N, 13.760°E, in the Militärhistorisches Museum der Bundeswehr in Dresden's Albertstadt, about 3 km north-east of the Altstadt. The museum's 1870s arsenal building is easy to identify from 1,500–3,000 ft AGL by the sharp glass-and-steel wedge Daniel Libeskind drove through its façade, cutting across the symmetry of the original block. Dresden Airport (EDDC) lies roughly 6 km north at Klotzsche, so the site sits close under the approach path; Leipzig/Halle (EDDP) is about 100 km north-west. The boat's own story belongs some 440 km to the north, in Kiel harbour on the Baltic — worth a leg if you are tracing it. Elbe valley fog is common on autumn mornings.
