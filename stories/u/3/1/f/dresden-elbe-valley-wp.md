# Dresden Elbe Valley

Twenty kilometres of river, meadow and baroque skyline that became a World Heritage Site in 2004 and lost the title in 2009 — the first place in Europe ever struck off the list, over a bridge its own citizens had voted for.

**Location:** 51.0498, 13.8132

**Categories:** Former World Heritage Sites, Cultural landscapes of Germany, Geography of Dresden, Valleys of Saxony, Landmarks in Germany

## Introduction

Only three places on Earth have ever been removed from the UNESCO World Heritage list against their will, and one of them is a stretch of river running through the middle of Dresden. The Dresden Elbe Valley was inscribed in 2004, flagged as endangered in 2006, and deleted in 2009. What did it in was not neglect, or war, or a developer nobody could stop. It was a four-lane road bridge that the people of Dresden had approved in a referendum, defended in court, and built while the World Heritage Committee watched. The city was told, in effect, to choose between a crossing and a title. It chose the crossing.

## Twenty Kilometres of Meander

The protected landscape ran roughly twenty kilometres through the Dresden Basin, from the Loschwitz district in the south-east down to the Ostragehege plains in the north-west, following the Elbe as it swings through the city in long curves. Forested slopes on both sides — the edge of the Lusatian uplands and the Dresden Heath to the north-east — shelter the basin from the worst winds, and the result is a pocket of unusual warmth for this latitude: an annual average between about 9.3 and 10°C, mild enough that Saxony grows wine here. Along the banks lie the *Elbwiesen*, wide open meadows that have stayed free of buildings for the simplest of reasons. The river floods them regularly, so nobody has ever managed to build on them. Much of the area is protected under the EU Habitats Directive, with smaller nature reserves inside it.

## The Balcony of Europe

What made the valley a cultural landscape rather than merely a pretty one is the density of what sits on it. Above the left bank runs Brühl's Terrace, the raised promenade that has been called the Balcony of Europe since long before UNESCO existed, and packed in behind it are the Frauenkirche, the Semperoper, the Zwinger, Dresden Castle and the Catholic court church of the Saxon electors. Directly opposite, on the right bank, stands the Innere Neustadt with the Japanese Palace and the government quarter. Further out the register keeps going: Albrechtsberg Palace on the northern slopes, Pillnitz with its castle and old village upstream, the Yenidze tobacco factory disguised as a mosque, and at Loschwitz the improbable trio of the Blue Wonder bridge, the funicular and the suspension railway. The New Synagogue and the Saxon parliament are recent additions to the same skyline.

## A Vote and a Bridge

The Waldschlösschen Bridge had been argued over for decades before it was argued over internationally. In 2005 Dresden held a referendum and a majority voted to build it. The following year UNESCO put the valley on the List of World Heritage in Danger, with a warning stated about as bluntly as that body ever states anything: plans for the bridge "would have such a serious impact on the integrity of property's landscape that it may no longer deserve to be on the World Heritage List." On 20 July 2006 the city council halted the tender. Then the courts took over, and in a sequence that satisfied nobody, they compelled the city to start building at the end of 2007. In July 2008 the committee gave Dresden one more year, hoping construction would stop and the landscape be restored. It did not stop.

## Seville, 25 June 2009

The World Heritage Committee met in Seville and voted on 25 June 2009 to revoke the inscription: fourteen in favour of deletion, five against, two abstentions. It was the first European site ever struck from the list and only the second anywhere in the world. The reaction outside Germany tended toward bafflement — how does a city that spent sixty years rebuilding the Frauenkirche stone by numbered stone lose its heritage status on purpose? Inside Dresden the answer was more prosaic. The Blue Wonder and the Albert Bridge were congested, the eastern districts wanted a crossing, and a great many residents took the view that a living city is not a museum exhibit and does not owe a committee in Spain its traffic planning. Both positions were arguable. Only one of them got built.

## What Is Actually There Now

The bridge opened to traffic on 26 August 2013: a steel arch, 635 metres long, four lanes wide, carrying cars across the meadows below the Waldschlösschen. Whether it ruined the view is a question every visitor now gets to answer personally, standing on Brühl's Terrace and looking upstream. Meanwhile the thing UNESCO was protecting has gone on behaving exactly as before. The Elbe still floods, so the *Elbwiesen* are still empty, and in summer they fill instead with the *Filmnächte am Elbufer*, an open-air cinema that puts a screen on the grass with the whole baroque skyline standing behind it — arguably the best seat in the city, and one that only exists because the river makes building there impossible. The valley lost a designation. It did not lose the valley.

## Flight Context

The centre of the former World Heritage area sits at 51.0498°N, 13.8132°E, with the protected corridor running roughly 20 km along the Elbe from Loschwitz in the south-east to the Ostragehege in the north-west. From 2,000–4,000 ft AGL the whole thing reads at once: two enormous river meanders, wide green flood meadows on both banks with no buildings on them, and the domes and towers of the old town crowded onto the high left bank at Brühl's Terrace. The Waldschlösschen Bridge is the modern steel arch upstream of the historic core; the Blue Wonder is the pale blue truss further south-east at Loschwitz. Dresden Airport (EDDC) lies about 9 km north; Leipzig/Halle (EDDP) roughly 100 km west-north-west. Morning valley fog is common September through March, and clears to give the sharpest light on the skyline in the hour before sunset.
