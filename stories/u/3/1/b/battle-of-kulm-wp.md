# Battle of Kulm

An exhausted Russian rearguard refused to break in the mountain passes of northern Bohemia, buying the time the Coalition needed to encircle Napoleon at Leipzig two months later.

**Location:** 50.7128, 13.9570

**Categories:** battles, napoleonic wars, czech republic, bohemia, military history, 1813

## Introduction

Three days earlier, the Coalition had been beaten badly at Dresden. Napoleon had handled them roughly in the rain, and now the army of the tsar, the kaiser, and the king of Prussia was retreating south through the Erzgebirge passes, hoping to reach the safety of Bohemia before the French closed in behind them. General Dominique Vandamme, an aggressive and impatient corps commander, had been turned loose to chase them. If he could seize the mountain road that ran past the village of Kulm, the entire allied army might be trapped against the hills with nowhere to run. Standing between him and that road was a single Russian corps under General Alexander Ostermann-Tolstoy, outnumbered more than two to one, exhausted, and with no obvious reason to expect anyone would arrive in time to help.

## The Russian Stand

On the morning of 29 August 1813, Vandamme attacked with 34,000 men and 84 guns. Ostermann-Tolstoy had perhaps 14,700 soldiers under his command at first, fighting on broken ground around the village of Priesten and the small Bohemian town of Kulm. The Russians knew exactly what was at stake. If they gave way, the passes were lost and so was the army behind them. Ostermann-Tolstoy was badly wounded and lost his left arm; he turned the command over to his subordinate, Aleksei Yermolov. Through the day the Russians counter-attacked again and again, never quite holding for long, never quite breaking. By evening Coalition reinforcements had brought their numbers up to roughly 20,000. Vandamme's attacks had all been repulsed, but he still believed he held the advantage and meant to renew the attack the next day.

## The Trap Closes

He woke to find a Prussian corps under Friedrich von Kleist marching across his rear. Kleist had pushed his men across the mountains and emerged on the road behind the French. From the front and the flank, Russian and Austrian columns under Barclay de Tolly and Colloredo-Mansfeld pressed in. Vandamme tried to form his men into infantry squares to fight in two directions at once, but his troops were tired and many were unseasoned. The squares broke. Estimates of the French loss run from about 13,000 to 25,000 of his 34,000 men, killed, wounded or taken prisoner; Vandamme himself was among the captured. Most of his guns were lost too, 82 of 84 by one count. It was the kind of disaster Napoleon's army was supposed to inflict on others, not suffer.

## Vandamme Speaks Back

Vandamme was brought before the tsar himself, who, according to the story passed down through the French army, called him a brigand and a plunderer to his face. The captured general was not in a conciliating mood. "I am neither a plunderer nor a brigand," he is supposed to have replied, "but in any case my contemporaries and history will not reproach me for having murdered my own father." The reference, sharp as a stiletto, was to the widely believed rumor that Tsar Alexander I had been complicit in the strangling of his father, Emperor Paul I, in 1801. Whether the words were really spoken or improved by retelling, they preserved a grim French dignity in defeat.

## Why It Mattered

Kulm was a smaller battle than the great clashes that bracketed it, but it was one of those engagements whose consequences reached far beyond their numbers. The Coalition victory cancelled out Napoleon's success at Dresden. The retreating allied army, now safe in Bohemia, had time to regroup, refit, and rejoin the larger Coalition strategy laid out at Trachenberg: avoid Napoleon himself, beat his marshals when possible, wear him down. Within seven weeks the same allies would close on Napoleon at Leipzig with overwhelming numbers, and the German campaign would be over. None of that would have been possible if Ostermann-Tolstoy's tired men had given up the road through the passes. King Frederick William of Prussia created a special decoration for the Russian Guards who had fought at Kulm. The Kulm Cross, shaped like the Prussian Iron Cross, is now a rarity among collectors of Napoleonic-era awards.

## The Battlefield Today

Kulm is now Chlumec, a Czech town in the foothills of the Krušné hory, the mountains the Germans called the Erzgebirge. Most of the battlefield has long since been built over. There is a cast-iron monument topped with a lion, and in town a hotel that did not miss the marketing opportunity: it is called the Hotel Bonaparte. From the slopes above the town, looking south on a clear day, you can still see why the road mattered. The mountains close in, the passes narrow, and an army caught in the wrong direction would have nowhere to go. On 30 August 1813, that mattered to tens of thousands of men, and to the future of Europe.

## Flight Context

Located at 50.71N, 13.96E in northern Bohemia, eight kilometers northwest of Usti nad Labem in the foothills of the Krušné hory (Erzgebirge) mountains on the German-Czech border. The battlefield sits in rolling country at the southern end of the mountain passes that lead down from Saxony into Bohemia. Visible from cruising altitude as forested ridges descending to the Elbe valley. Nearest major airports are Dresden (EDDC) about 50km north-northwest and Prague-Vaclav Havel (LKPR) about 70km south-southeast.
