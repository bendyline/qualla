# Chemnitz

A Saxon factory city that was bombed flat, renamed after a philosopher who never visited, renamed back by a vote, and then spent the 2020s persuading Europe to come and look at it.

**Location:** 50.8333, 12.9167

**Categories:** Cities, Saxony, Industrial heritage, East Germany, Ore Mountains

## Introduction

In the courtyard of a Chemnitz department store, tree trunks stand upright that stopped growing about 291 million years ago. The Zeisigwald volcano buried a Lower Permian forest here under ash, and the wood turned to stone where it stood. Georgius Agricola wrote about the fossils in 1546. In 1751 a trunk 3.7 metres long was hauled off to Dresden behind twenty-eight horses, only to be destroyed there in 1849. The best of what has come out of the ground since is displayed in the Museum für Naturkunde inside the Tietz — a grand old department store that Chemnitz converted into a cultural centre rather than knock down. A buried forest, dug up and put back on its feet indoors, is an unreasonably apt emblem for this city.

## Saxon Manchester

The settlement grew up around a Benedictine monastery and was granted the rights of a free imperial city around 1170. Sitting at the foot of the Erzgebirge — the ore mountains — it became a trading town in the sixteenth century on the back of what came out of those mountains, then something much larger. The expansion of Saxon coal mining through the eighteenth century turned Chemnitz into one of the central places of German textile and machine-building industry, and Johann von Zimmermann built the European continent's first machine-tool factory here. By the early nineteenth century the nickname had stuck: Sächsisches Manchester, Saxon Manchester. The money that industry generated built whole districts at once. Kaßberg and Sonnenberg went up in the last third of the nineteenth century, and Kaßberg survives largely intact — street after street of stucco Gründerzeit façades, built in a hurry for a city that was filling up with people who worked in mills.

## 5 March 1945

The same industry made the city a target. Allied bombing destroyed 41 per cent of the built-up area over the course of the war, but the raid of 5 March 1945 was aimed squarely at the centre, and roughly 80 per cent of the historic inner city was gone by the time it ended. About 3,700 people were killed in the air war here. They were mill workers and toolmakers and their families, in a city whose skill at making machinery had put them on a target list, and the ones who came out of the cellars afterwards found the streets they had grown up in reduced to a plan drawing. Almost nothing medieval survived. What Chemnitz has of its own deep past is largely what was rebuilt or dug up later — which is why a stone forest in a courtyard carries more weight here than it would elsewhere.

## The Name That Wasn't Ours

On 10 May 1953 the city and its district were renamed Karl-Marx-Stadt, in honour of a man who had never visited Chemnitz and had no connection to it whatever. The name lasted thirty-seven years. A great deal of building went on during them, and much of it is still standing — wide boulevards, prefabricated housing blocks, the socialist-modern civic architecture of the centre. On 1 June 1990 the residents voted to take the old name back. What they did not do was erase the intervening decades from the streets, and the result is one of the most legible East German cityscapes left in Germany. Unlike Berlin or Leipzig, Chemnitz saw comparatively little demolition after reunification, so a GDR apartment slab, a nineteenth-century tenement and a glass retail box can sit within a few minutes' walk of each other without anyone finding it strange.

## Germany's Newest City Centre

The first wave of post-reunification money went to out-of-town shopping malls, and the middle of Chemnitz stayed a gap. Serious building in the centre did not begin until 1999, which is why the city has been described, not unkindly, as having Germany's most recent city centre — a whole quarter reconstructed in one go, on a scale comparable in German terms only to Potsdamer Platz. Helmut Jahn designed the Kaufhof department store. Hans Kollhoff supplied the façade for the Galerie Roter Turm. Ingenhoven and Partner did the Peek & Cloppenburg building. All of it clusters around the Roter Turm itself, the Red Tower, built in the late twelfth or early thirteenth century as part of the city wall and still the most conspicuous landmark in town: the oldest structure in Chemnitz, standing in the newest part of it.

## The Year of Looking

Chemnitz won the title of European Capital of Culture for 2025 on 28 October 2020, and opened its year on 18 January 2025 — a city of about 247,000 people, more used to being written about for its industry or its politics, spending twelve months asking visitors to look at it directly. The material was always there. Villa Esche, built in 1902 to designs by Henry van de Velde, is one of the finest Art Nouveau houses in Germany. The petrified forest sits in the Tietz. The Erzgebirge begins on the southern edge of town and runs up to 1,215 metres at the Fichtelberg, with centuries of mining tunnels, wood-carving villages and Christmas traditions along the way. Freiberg is 35 km northeast, Zwickau 40 km southwest, Colditz 50 km north, Dresden and Leipzig each about an hour. Chemnitz is not a place most people arrive at by accident, which is roughly the point.

## Flight Context

Chemnitz lies at 50.833°N, 12.917°E in southwestern Saxony, at the junction of the A4 and A72 motorways, on the northern approach to the Erzgebirge. The terrain is gently rolling around the city and rises steadily south toward the Czech border, reaching 1,215 m at the Fichtelberg above Oberwiesenthal roughly 50 km to the south — plan altitudes accordingly on southbound legs. Dresden (EDDC) is about 70 km northeast and Leipzig/Halle (EDDP) about 80 km northwest; Prague (LKPR) lies roughly 135 km south across the mountains. From the air the city reads as a compact modern core ringed by dense nineteenth-century blocks, with the Karl Marx Monument's plaza and the tower of the Roter Turm marking the centre. Winter brings low cloud and icing over the Erzgebirge ridge; the clearest views of the range come with post-frontal northwesterly flow.
