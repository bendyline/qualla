# Altzella Abbey

A Cistercian monastery founded in 1175 near Nossen, Altzella served two centuries as the burial place of the Wettin margraves of Meissen before the Reformation reduced it to picturesque ruin.

**Location:** 51.0603, 13.2765

**Categories:** Cistercian monasteries in Germany, Monasteries in Saxony, Burial sites of the House of Wettin

## Introduction

In 1175 a small group of twelve Cistercian monks walked into a clearing near the Freiberger Mulde river in central Saxony, sent from Pforta Abbey near Naumburg, and began building a house their order called Cella. Within a generation that house held the bones of the Wettin margraves of Meissen, the dynasty whose descendants would still be ruling Saxony seven hundred years later. By 1268 the community had grown wealthy enough to plant a daughter house near Guben, called Cella Nova, and the older foundation began to be known as Cella Vetus, the older Cella, or in German Altzelle and Altzella. By 1540 it was gone as a monastery, secularised by ducal decree. By 1800 it was something a Saxon Romantic could love: a quiet park of meadows and broken arches, with a memorial chapel sheltering the bones of margraves who had outlived their own monks.

## Twelve men and a forest

In 1162 Emperor Frederick I granted 800 Hufen of cleared land to a monastery that Otto II, Margrave of Meissen, was founding, a foundation his wife, Hedwig of Brandenburg, had wished for. Hufen of cleared land changed hands; some had been re-traded after silver was found nearby in 1168, the same silver strike that would build the wealth of the Wettin court for centuries. The first abbot brought twelve monks, the symbolic number, and they raised a Romanesque brick church with three aisles and a transept, dedicated in 1198. The west front showed Northern Italian influences. Construction continued in earnest until about 1230, by which time the abbey had its conventual buildings and the surviving Romanesque entrance gate.

## Burial place of the Wettins

Otto of Meissen died in 1190 and was entombed at the abbey, and that single decision turned a working monastery into a dynastic mausoleum. To house the growing number of Wettin tombs, the monks built a separate chapel, the Andreaskapelle, between 1339 and 1349. Frederick II, Margrave of Meissen, was buried there. So was his son Frederick III. The dynastic connection brought money and protection, and in 1436 the abbey could afford to spend 4,200 gulden buying the nearby Schloss Nossen with all its contents, restructuring the run-down castle as a residence for the abbot. Under abbots Vinzenz von Gruner and the humanist Martin von Lochau, who held office from 1501 until 1522, the abbey enjoyed its richest period. In 1506 the upper floor of the lay brothers' building was converted into a library, the kind of project that announces an institution at the height of its confidence.

## Secularisation

Confidence rarely lasts. The Reformation reached Saxony hard, and in 1540 Henry IV, Duke of Saxony, ordered the secularisation of Altzella. Under Augustus, Elector of Saxony, who came to power in 1553, the abbey buildings were judged to be in poor condition; large parts of them were demolished from 1557 at the latest and the stone reused elsewhere, partly to rebuild Schloss Nossen. Only the lay brothers' building survived, repurposed as a granary. The dynastic tombs presented a problem of their own. Between 1676 and 1787 the Saxon electors disinterred their Wettin ancestors and reburied them, with proper ceremony, in a new memorial chapel built for the purpose, the present-day Mausoleum at Altzella. The dynasty remembered itself.

## A romantic ruin

Around 1800, when Romantic taste had begun to find broken arches and overgrown courtyards more affecting than working churches, Altzella was landscaped into a Klosterpark. Ruins were left to look ruined; the surviving fragments of the Romanesque gate were framed by trees; the medieval grain store and the Wettin Mausoleum were preserved as objects of contemplation. The result is a park you walk through rather than a building you tour. In summer the precinct wall encloses meadows where Corpus Christi processions still pass and where local conferences and weddings happen in the restored buildings. Margraves who had wanted to be remembered by Cistercian prayer are remembered now by visitors who wander the grass. It is a strange afterlife for a foundation that began with twelve monks looking for solitude in the woods.

## Flight Context

Altzella Abbey lies near Nossen in central Saxony at 51.06°N, 13.28°E, in rolling country between the Erzgebirge foothills and the Elbe valley. From cruising altitude the abbey itself is a small green clearing, but Dresden lies about 30 km to the east and is the obvious visual anchor. EDDC (Dresden Klotzsche) is the closest major airport. EDDP (Leipzig/Halle) is roughly 80 km northwest. The Freiberger Mulde winds northwest past the site toward Döbeln.
