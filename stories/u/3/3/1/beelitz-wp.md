# Beelitz

A Brandenburg asparagus town whose sanatorium in the pines became a Great War hospital, a Soviet military clinic, and finally one of Europe's most photographed ruins - with a forest growing inside it.

**Location:** 52.2333, 12.9667

**Categories:** Localities in Potsdam-Mittelmark, Sanatoriums, Abandoned buildings and structures, Military hospitals, Brandenburg, Film locations

## Introduction

There is a forest growing inside one of the buildings. Pavilion B IV, the women's lung sanatorium at Beelitz-Heilstätten, burned in 1945, and nobody cleared the wreckage. Birches and pines took root in the rubble on what had been the upper floors, and for six decades they kept growing, roots working down through the masonry until the ruin was less a building than an enormous planter. Since 2015 a walkway has run out over it - nearly 800 metres of timber deck on steel, hanging between seventeen and twenty-three metres above the ground - so that visitors move through the canopy and look down into a roofless ward. All of this sits eighteen kilometres south of Potsdam, in a town otherwise famous for asparagus.

## Belizi, and an Accusation

A deed issued by Emperor Otto III in 997 mentions a settlement called Belizi, though historians note the name may belong to nearby Belzig instead. The parish church of Saint Mary and Saint Nicholas enters the record in 1247, and it does so through an ugly door: a report that Jews of the town had desecrated a consecrated host, which then bled. That accusation was made against Jewish communities across medieval Europe, and it was false wherever it was made. What it produced in Beelitz was a pilgrimage. From 1370 the host was kept in a chapel beside the church, and the faithful came to see it. A rise outside the Mühlentor carried the name Judenberg until 1945, when the town renamed it Friedensberg - peace hill. Local tradition holds that it was where Jews were burned. Nothing confirms it. But a town does not usually rename a hill for nothing.

## Sand, Hussars, and a Radio Mast

Beelitz sits on the rim of the Zauche, a glacial outwash plain of sand dumped by retreating ice, and sand is exactly what white asparagus wants - light, warm, easily heaped into the ridges that keep the spears from ever seeing daylight. Cultivation was first documented here in 1861, and Beelitzer Spargel now carries the town's name across Germany every spring. The place had other lives too. In 1731 Frederick William I of Prussia billeted a hussar regiment here, making Beelitz a garrison town; a Bundeswehr command is based here still. The village of Kanin, absorbed in 2001, spent centuries as an exclave of Electoral Saxony, and until 1815 that made it a smuggler's corridor and a bolthole for Prussian deserters, who needed only to step across a line on a map. Its fieldstone church, raised around 1138, is the oldest surviving church in Brandenburg.

## Air Was the Medicine

In the 1890s the Berlin workers' health insurance corporation went looking for pine woods south of the capital, and found them here. Against tuberculosis there was then no cure at all - only rest, food, and clean cold air - so the treatment was the architecture. Some sixty buildings went up, laid out to the plans of the Berlin architect Heino Schmieden, whose cogeneration plant was raised in 1898 to heat and power the whole estate. The pavilions were scattered rather than stacked, each set among the trees, with balconies and long glazed galleries where patients were wheeled out to lie in the open in every season. Berlin's factory workers came out by train and were fed, rested and aired. It was a machine for breathing, and one of the most ambitious pieces of public health architecture in Europe.

## Every Army That Came Through

The sanatorium had barely two decades of peace. From the opening of the First World War the Imperial German Army took it as a military hospital, and among the wounded sent there in October and November 1916 was a young infantry runner named Adolf Hitler, recovering from a leg wound taken at the Somme. The Second World War brought a new hospital complex on the site, designed by Egon Eiermann. In 1945 the Red Army arrived, and stayed: Beelitz-Heilstätten served as a Soviet military hospital for forty-nine years, outlasting the Berlin Wall and German reunification both, and closing only in 1994. Its last famous patient was Erich Honecker, admitted in December 1990 after being forced from power in East Germany. Four German states in a single century used the same buildings, and each one left its paperwork behind.

## Abandoned, Then Filmed

When the Soviets withdrew, attempts to privatise the estate mostly failed. Parts of it never stopped working - a neurological rehabilitation clinic and a centre for Parkinson's research and care still operate here - but the surgery, the psychiatric ward and the rifle range were simply shut in 1994 and left. Photographers found them almost immediately: peeling green tiles, staircases spiralling into daylight, an operating theatre going slowly back to moss. Film crews followed. Roman Polanski shot part of The Pianist here in 2002, Rammstein filmed "Mein Herz brennt" in the wards, and Valkyrie, Men & Chicken, A Cure for Wellness and the Netflix series 1899 all borrowed the ruins. Restoration in the mid-2010s brought much of the site back and quadrupled the local population. The decay that made Beelitz famous is also genuinely dangerous, and most of it remains private ground - which is why the way in is a ticket: guided tours through the shells, and the walkway through the trees above them.

## Flight Context

Beelitz lies at 52.233°N, 12.967°E, on flat glacial outwash country about 18 km south of Potsdam and 40 km southwest of central Berlin. From 2,000-4,000 ft the town itself is modest, but the Heilstätten complex north of it reads clearly: a rectangular grid of pale pavilion roofs set inside dark pine forest, cut through by the Berlin-Blankenheim railway and flanked by the A9 autobahn running north-south. In spring the surrounding asparagus fields show as long parallel ridges under white plastic sheeting - an unmistakable seasonal marker. Nearest airfield is Schönhagen (EDAZ), roughly 15 km east; Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) sits about 40 km northeast, and its controlled airspace should be checked before any low transit. Terrain is flat and forgiving; summer haze over the Brandenburg pine belt is the usual visibility limiter.
