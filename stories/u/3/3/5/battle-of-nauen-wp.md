# Battle of Nauen

On 17 June 1675 a Swedish rearguard threw its own guns into a Havelland lake rather than face oncoming Brandenburg cavalry — the running fight that set up Fehrbellin the next morning and made a small German state into a military power.

**Location:** 52.6036, 12.8583

**Categories:** Military-History, Brandenburg, Prussia, Sweden, 17th-Century

## Introduction

The Swedish gunners saw the horsemen coming across the causeway and did something soldiers almost never do: they tipped their own cannon into the lake. They had a good position — the marshy ground west of Nauen could only be crossed on one narrow bank, and they had thrown up fieldworks and sited guns to cover it. They abandoned all of it without firing. Behind them, a thousand-odd Brandenburg troopers had ridden through the night at a gallop to get there. It was 17 June 1675, the day before the Battle of Fehrbellin, and what happened around this small Havelland market town is the part of the story that usually gets one sentence.

## The Undefended Country

In December 1674 a Swedish army came south out of Swedish Pomerania into the March of Brandenburg and found almost nothing in its way. The Elector's soldiers were hundreds of kilometres off in Franconia, fighting the French as part of the Imperial army in the Franco-Dutch War, and the country they had left behind could not defend itself. The Swedes spent the winter and spring living off it. Not until June 1675 did the Brandenburg army turn around and march home, and what it managed then was a forced march hard enough to leave the troops spent when they arrived. The speed mattered more than the numbers. The Elector's whole plan depended on getting there before the Swedes understood he had got there at all.

## The Coup at Rathenow

Swedish Field Marshal Carl Gustav Wrangel's intention was to move from Havelberg across the Elbe, gain the left bank, link up with Hanoverian troops and push on Magdeburg. To secure the river line he sent a detachment forward under Colonel Wangelin to Rathenow. On 15 June Field Marshal Georg von Derfflinger talked his way in. Speaking fluent Swedish, he presented himself at the Havel Gate as an officer fleeing the Brandenburgers, the drawbridge came down, and his dragoons were inside the town before the garrison understood what had happened. Wangelin was taken prisoner. The coup undid the entire Swedish scheme at a stroke — with Rathenow gone, the Elbe crossing at Havelberg was out of reach. Lieutenant General Wolmar Wrangel issued fresh orders: the army would fall back through Nauen and secure the crossing of the Rhin at Fehrbellin instead. The Elector set off after them immediately. From that moment the campaign was no longer a manoeuvre. It was a chase.

## The Causeway and the Town

Lieutenant Colonel Sydow's twelve hundred horsemen reached the ground in front of Nauen first, which is why the abandoned guns went into the water. The Brandenburg cavalry chased the retreating Swedes to the edge of the town and cut them down as they ran — most of the men who died at Nauen died in flight, not in a stand-up fight, which is how these things usually went and is worth saying plainly. Inside the town a battalion of Swedish musketeers made a real stand, meeting the advance with heavy musket and cannon fire. Even before the Brandenburg guns and dragoons came up, two hundred troopers pushed back a rearguard of a thousand cuirassiers. The Swedes gave up Nauen and withdrew, taking further casualties as they went.

## The Bridge That Held

Beyond the town ran a stream, and at the far end of its bridge the Swedes had built a battery. That was where the Brandenburg pursuit stopped. The guns drove the cavalry back with losses, and behind the works the main Swedish army had drawn up in order of battle. Field Marshal Georg von Derfflinger got the damaged bridge repaired and three cannon into position, and his gunners knocked out the Swedish pieces — but the enemy line was still solid, and a frontal assault across a single bridge against a deployed army was more than the Elector was willing to buy. His own men had been in the saddle or on the road for days. The order went out to pull back into Nauen and camp. Roughly two hundred Swedish soldiers had been killed in and around the town; about sixty Brandenburgers.

## Five-Thirty the Next Morning

By the evening of 17 June the two armies were close enough to hear one another, and the Brandenburg camp expected to fight at first light in front of the gates of Nauen. The Swedes had other plans. They used the darkness to break contact and slip away northwest toward Fehrbellin. The Elector's army broke camp at half past five the next morning and went after them, and on 18 June 1675 caught them at the Rhin crossing. Fehrbellin is the battle the history books remember; it is the day Frederick William became the Great Elector, and the day a middling north German state began to be taken seriously as a military power. But Fehrbellin only happened because a Swedish army was already retreating, already short of guns, and already a day behind. That was decided at Nauen.

## Flight Context

The action took place around 52.604°N, 12.858°E, at and immediately west of Nauen in the Havelland, about 35 km west-northwest of central Berlin. Best viewed between 1,500 and 3,000 ft. The terrain is the story: this is the edge of the Havelländisches Luch, a broad drained lowland of black peat soil, and the wetland that forced the Swedes onto a single causeway is now a geometric grid of drainage ditches and field boundaries. Nauen is compact and easy to identify — the town sits at a rail and road junction with the Berlin–Hamburg line running through it, and the antenna masts of the Nauen transmitter station stand about 5 km north-northeast. Fehrbellin, where the decisive battle was fought the following day, lies roughly 25 km north-northwest. Nearest airfield is Bienenfarm (EDOI), a Sonderlandeplatz about 10 km west-northwest; Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is about 51 km southeast. Watch for the tall masts and their guy wires; low flight near the transmitter site is a hazard.
