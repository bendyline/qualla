# Margot Friedländer

She survived sixteen months hidden in Berlin and then Theresienstadt, spent sixty-four years as a New Yorker, and came home at eighty-eight to spend the rest of her life talking to German schoolchildren.

**Location:** 52.4990, 13.4249

**Categories:** berlin, germany, holocaust, jewish-history, world-war-ii, biography, kreuzberg, memorials

## Introduction

For most of her life Margot Friedländer was a New Yorker. She sewed for a living, then ran a travel agency, sending other people off to places; she and her husband Adolf took American citizenship and stayed away from Germany for decades. Then, in 2010, at eighty-eight, she moved back to the city she had been born in, hidden in and deported from, and started standing in front of German teenagers to tell them what had been done there. She kept at it for fifteen years. At 102 she was on the cover of German Vogue.

## The Button Factory

She was born Anni Margot Bendheim in Berlin on 5 November 1921, into a family that made buttons. The factory supplied the fashion studios of the city's Jewish textile quarter, so she grew up around fabric and pattern books, and in 1936 enrolled at a Berlin arts and crafts school to learn dress design. She was eleven when the Nazis came to power.

At seventeen she watched the November 1938 pogrom tear through the shops and synagogues of her own city. Her mother wanted the family out of Germany after that. Her father, a First World War veteran, would not go; accounts differ, and some have him leaving for Belgium after her parents divorced. It made no difference in the end. Her mother, her father and her younger brother were all murdered at Auschwitz.

## Try to Make Your Life

On 20 January 1943 she came home and saw a man outside the family's second-floor apartment. Turning around would have given her away, so she covered her yellow star, walked past him and carried on up to a neighbour on the third floor, who told her what had happened: the Gestapo had taken her brother Ralph, who was seventeen.

Her mother had not been in the flat. She had run to Jewish friends in Kreuzberg, and from there made her decision: she went to the Gestapo and turned herself in so her son would not face it alone. She left her handbag with those friends — an address book and an amber necklace inside — and with it a spoken message for her daughter: Versuche, dein Leben zu machen. Try to make your life. Margot never saw her again, and spent the next eighty-two years treating that sentence as an instruction.

## Sixteen Months, Sixteen People

She went underground and stayed hidden for roughly sixteen months in a city under bombardment. Sixteen Germans sheltered her in turn. Almost none of their names are recorded. They took a risk that could have killed them, for a young woman most of them did not know.

It ended in April 1944, when police stopped her as she left an air-raid shelter and asked for her papers. On the way to the station she told them she was Jewish. She was deported to Theresienstadt and survived. She called the camp cruel and never dressed it up, and said flatly that she lived because she arrived near the end of the war. People who arrived earlier did not get that luck.

## Deggendorf, Then New York

Adolf Friedländer proposed almost as soon as the camp was liberated, and she was clear-eyed about it for the rest of her life. 'I wasn't in love with Adolf,' she said. 'I needed time to become human again. It was the same for Adolf. The pain brought us closer together than being in love.' A rabbi married them at Theresienstadt on 26 June 1945, six weeks after liberation. They spent a year in a displaced persons camp at Deggendorf in Lower Bavaria, then sailed for New York in 1946.

Then came the ordinary decades obituaries tend to skip: work, a marriage, a neighbourhood, an American passport. She was a seamstress and later a travel agent, and for more than half a century she did not tell the story. It took Adolf's death in 1997 and a memoir class to open it; the book came out in 2008 under her mother's last sentence to her, with an English edition in 2014.

## The Work of the Last Fifteen Years

She came back in 2010 and had her German citizenship restored, and then did something few survivors chose to do: she spent her nineties and her hundreds in classrooms, talking to the grandchildren of the people who hunted her. Her reason was short. 'My brother did not have a chance. But the young people today do.'

Germany kept giving her things. President Christian Wulff pinned the Cross of the Order of Merit on her at Bellevue Palace on 9 November 2011 — the anniversary, to the day, of the pogrom she had watched at seventeen. An honorary doctorate, the Walther Rathenau Prize and the Officer's Cross followed, and since 2014 a prize in her name has gone to young people working against antisemitism and racism.

In the summer of 2024, aged 102, she appeared on the cover of German Vogue for an issue about love, and used the interview to talk about the antisemitism and nationalism she saw rising again. 'Look toward what brings us together,' she said. 'Be people. Be sensible.' She died in Berlin on 9 May 2025, at 103, on the day she was due to be given the Commander's Cross of the Order of Merit. Outside her old building in Kreuzberg there is a small brass square in the pavement. It says that Margot Bendheim lived here, born 1921, deported to Theresienstadt in 1944 — and then, unusually for such a stone, survived.

## Flight Context

The Kreuzberg address associated with her, at 52.4990 N, 13.4249 E, sits on Skalitzer Straße in Berlin-Kreuzberg, about 4 km southeast of the Brandenburg Gate. From the air the marker is the elevated U1 viaduct, a steel line running dead straight east-west above Skalitzer Straße, with the green block of Görlitzer Park immediately southeast and the Spree curving past 900 m east at the twin-towered Oberbaum Bridge. Tempelhof's disused circular apron and grass field lie about 3 km southwest and are the single most obvious feature in this part of the city. There is nothing to see at the site itself beyond a tenement block and a brass stone in the pavement; treat this as a neighbourhood overflight at 1,500-2,500 ft AGL rather than a landmark pass, and expect controlled airspace throughout inner Berlin. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is about 16 km southeast; Schönhagen (EDAZ) about 35 km southwest.
