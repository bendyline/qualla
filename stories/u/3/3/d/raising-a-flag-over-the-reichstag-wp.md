# Raising a Flag over the Reichstag

Yevgeny Khaldei climbed the smoking Reichstag on May 2, 1945, with a flag his uncle had sewn from three tablecloths, and made the photograph that defined Soviet victory.

**Location:** 52.5186, 13.3761

**Categories:** berlin, world-war-ii, photography, history, soviet-union

## Introduction

The flag in the photograph was not the first flag raised over the Reichstag. It was not the second, third, or fourth either. By the time Yevgeny Khaldei climbed the bombed-out building on May 2, 1945, with a Soviet banner his uncle had stitched from three red tablecloths, the actual flag-raisings were already days behind him. The image he made that morning, however, became the only one that mattered. It would be reproduced in millions of newspapers and textbooks and burned into the collective memory of the Second World War. Like the Iwo Jima photograph taken ten weeks earlier and an ocean away, it did not show the first flag; unlike that picture, it was partly staged and partly retouched, and still true to the thing it represented.

## The building Hitler did not use

The Reichstag had stood unused as a parliament for twelve years before the Red Army got to it. The 1933 fire gutted the chamber, and once Hitler had used the panic to push the Reichstag Fire Decree through and finish off the Weimar Republic, he had no use for the actual building. Nazi-era sessions of the rump parliament were held across the square at the Kroll Opera House. By 1945 the Reichstag was a hollowed-out symbol the Nazis themselves had abandoned. To the Soviets it remained the heart of the fascist enemy, and Stalin wanted a flag flying from its dome before May Day. The fighting inside the building was room-to-room and bitter; the defenders held out until May 2. When the Reichstag finally fell on May 2, the war in Europe had six days left to run.

## Many flags, many soldiers

The first attempts to plant a banner came on April 30, while Hitler was still alive in his bunker a kilometer away. Two Soviet planes dropped large red banners that snagged on the bombed dome. Reports of soldiers planting flags reached Marshal Zhukov, who broadcast that the Reichstag had been taken, but when correspondents arrived they found the building still in German hands. Throughout April 30 and May 1, small parties of Red Army soldiers scaled the building under fire to mount banners. Rakhimzhan Qoshqarbaev and Grigory Bulatov claimed the second-floor window flag at 14:25 on April 30. A team of Mikhail Minin, Gazi Zagitov, Aleksei Lisimenko, and Aleksei Bobrov reached the Goddess of Victory sculpture on the western pediment around 22:30. The flag that became the official Banner of Victory went up around 3 a.m. on May 1, raised in part by Meliton Kantaria of Georgia and Mikhail Yegorov of Smolensk, with Aleksei Berest, the political officer who led the team but whose role was long sidelined in the official version. It was the only one of the early flags to survive the German shelling that followed.

## Khaldei climbs the roof

Yevgeny Khaldei was a TASS war photographer from Donetsk, Jewish, and 28 years old. His mother had been murdered in a 1918 pogrom; his father and three of his four sisters were murdered by the Nazis during the war. He had been photographing the war since the German invasion in 1941. Before flying to Berlin, he asked his uncle to sew a Soviet flag large enough to be unmistakable in a photograph. His uncle stitched it from three red tablecloths and gave it to him. On the morning of May 2, with the building finally pacified, Khaldei found three soldiers willing to climb up with him: Aleksei Kovalev from Kazakhstan, who held the flag, and Abdulhakim Ismailov from Dagestan and Leonid Gorychev from Minsk, who supported him. He shot a full roll of film as Kovalev leaned out over the railing with the city of Berlin smoldering behind.

## What was edited out

Back in Moscow, the editor of Ogonyok magazine noticed something in one of the negatives. Ismailov, the soldier supporting Kovalev, appeared to be wearing a watch on each wrist. In the spring of 1945, two watches on a Red Army soldier in Berlin could be read as a sign of looting. Khaldei took a needle to the negative and removed the second watch. Some later defenders argued the second object was actually a compass, but the cleanup was real. Khaldei also added smoke to the background using another frame from his roll, increased the contrast, and slightly extended the sky to make the composition more square. The image ran in Ogonyok on May 13, 1945, days after Germany's surrender. The retouching does not make the photograph false. The flag was real. The soldiers were real. The Red Army had taken Berlin at a cost of some 80,000 Soviet and Polish dead and missing. What Khaldei did was tighten the symbol so the meaning could land.

## Afterlives

Kovalev and Ismailov both survived the war. Kovalev, the flag-bearer, lived out his life in Kyiv and died there in 1997. Ismailov went back to Dagestan and lived until 2010. Khaldei kept photographing for TASS until he was let go in 1948, a dismissal he blamed on the antisemitism of Stalin's late campaigns. He scraped by as a freelancer until Pravda hired him in 1959; the West only rediscovered him in the 1990s, when his work began appearing in major exhibitions. He died in 1997, and his daughter and heir, Anna, later went to court over the use of the photograph. The photograph itself, because it was created for TASS, belonged to the agency until its Russian copyright expired on January 1, 2016. Whatever the legal status, the image now belongs to everyone, in the way that all images that mark the end of something terrible eventually do.

## Flight Context

The Reichstag stands at 52.52 N, 13.38 E on the eastern edge of the Tiergarten in central Berlin, near the bend of the Spree River. Berlin Brandenburg Airport (EDDB) is about 19 km south-southeast. The building is highly visible from low altitude, marked by Norman Foster's glass dome added during the 1990s reconstruction. The Brandenburg Gate sits about 300 meters south.
