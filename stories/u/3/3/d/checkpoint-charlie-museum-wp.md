# Checkpoint Charlie Museum

A two-and-a-half-room apartment on Bernauer Straße opened in October 1962 with a single suggestion: be thankful to those border guards who do not shoot to kill.

**Location:** 52.5078, 13.3906

**Categories:** Berlin, Cold War, Berlin Wall, Museums, Human rights

## Introduction

The first version of the Checkpoint Charlie Museum opened on 19 October 1962, in an apartment of two and a half rooms on Bernauer Straße. The street had just been split lengthwise by the Berlin Wall, the buildings on the eastern side emptied and their windows bricked up. The exhibition's founding director, the human rights activist Rainer Hildebrandt, had a quiet line for visiting tourists. They should, he suggested, be thankful to those border guards who do not shoot to kill. It was an unusually generous framing. At least 140 people would die at the Wall, most of them trying to cross from East Berlin to West; eight of the dead were East German border guards killed on duty. The museum that grew out of that two-and-a-half-room apartment is built around the people on both sides of the wire.

## From Bernauer Straße to Friedrichstraße

The Berlin Wall went up on 13 August 1961. By 14 June 1963, the museum had moved to its permanent home on Friedrichstraße, in a building that took the name Haus am Checkpoint Charlie after the famous Allied crossing point a few metres away. The exhibition was deliberately not a conventional museum. Photographs and writings and physical objects were piled together in a way that struck visitors as chaotic. That was partly the point. The institution was as much a political centre as a display: a library, a publishing operation, a venue for lectures and films, and behind the scenes, an active hub of escape planning. Hildebrandt and his colleagues helped people get out. They named the umbrella organisation behind it all Arbeitsgemeinschaft 13. August — the working group of 13 August — after the date the wall began.

## The Apparatus of Escape

What survives in the collection is an inventory of human ingenuity under pressure. Hot-air balloons. Cars with hidden compartments behind dashboards and inside fuel tanks. A homemade chairlift strung between two buildings. A miniature submarine, a U-boat scaled down to one rider. Each object represents months of secret work by people whose neighbours might inform on them, whose escape might be detected at the final moment, and who would face years in Stasi custody if caught. East German general Heinz Hoffmann once boasted that the wall was the best border security system in the world. The museum was built to document that claim by displaying the lengths to which people would go to defeat it. The museum researches and maintains its own list of deaths at the Berlin Wall; the Berlin Wall Foundation's research counts at least 140. Each name had a life behind it: Peter Fechter, who bled to death in the death strip in 1962 while West Berlin watched and could not intervene; Chris Gueffroy, the last person shot trying to cross, in February 1989, nine months before the wall fell.

## Survival as a Private Museum

After 1989, the wall was gone and so was much of the museum's public funding. Under the leadership of Alexandra Hildebrandt — Rainer's widow, who continued his work — the museum reinvented itself as a private undertaking, what Hildebrandt described as a place of experience rather than a political headquarters. By the 2000s it had become, by press accounts, one of the most commercially successful museums in Europe, reportedly earning seven-figure profits and drawing more than 850,000 visitors a year. In 2002, Arbeitsgemeinschaft 13. August gave up its charitable status and the tax breaks that came with it. The museum's exhibitions began reaching outward to other struggles. Mikhail Khodorkovsky held his first press conference here after his release from Russian prison in December 2013, thanking the museum for its moral support. In 2016 it mounted an exhibition of work by Nadiya Savchenko, a Ukrainian pilot held by Russia, and joined the campaign for her release.

## The Memorial That Was Removed

In 2004, Alexandra Hildebrandt installed the Freedom Memorial in an empty lot near the museum. The memorial took the form of crosses representing the people killed trying to cross the wall. Within a year, the lease on the land was terminated by the owner and the memorial was taken down. Both the installation and its removal generated sharp public debate, which is in some ways the museum's natural condition. The Checkpoint Charlie crossing point itself has become a tourist set piece — actors in vintage uniforms posing with visitors for tips at the famous guard hut, fast food outlets crowding the corner where Friedrichstraße meets Zimmerstraße. The museum sits a few steps away, asking the harder questions. Who built the wall, who paid for it, who tried to cross it, and who is doing equivalent things, somewhere in the world, today.

## Flight Context

The Checkpoint Charlie Museum sits at 52.508°N, 13.391°E, on Friedrichstraße in the Mitte/Kreuzberg border area of central Berlin, about 1.3 km southeast of the Brandenburg Gate. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is about 18 km south-southeast. From altitude the famous crossing point is invisible, but the long, straight line of Friedrichstraße cuts north–south through central Berlin, crossing what was once the boundary between the American and Soviet sectors of the divided city.
