# Potsdamer Platz

Once the busiest intersection in Europe, then a weed-covered no man's land, then the largest building site on the continent — all on the same few hectares of Berlin.

**Location:** 52.5089, 13.3763

**Categories:** germany, berlin, architecture, cold-war, urban-history

## Introduction

In Wim Wenders's Der Himmel über Berlin, shot in 1987, an old man called Homer wanders out across a field of scrub grass and broken brick looking for Potsdamer Platz. He remembers the cafés, the cigarette sellers, the noise. He cannot find any of it, because he is standing in the middle of it. What the camera shows behind him is weeds, rubble and the graffitied concrete of the Berlin Wall. Sixty years earlier this ground had been the busiest traffic intersection in Europe, a place people compared to Piccadilly Circus and Times Square.

## Coffee at the Customs Queue

It began as a bottleneck. When Berlin threw a customs wall around itself in the 1730s, the gate at the west end of Leipziger Strasse carried the road to Potsdam, and everything arriving from the southwest stopped there to be counted. Just inside stood an octagonal parade ground, renamed Leipziger Platz in 1814 for Prussia's decisive defeat of Napoleon at Leipzig.

Huguenot families who had fled France and settled outside the gate noticed something about the queue: people stuck in it got hungry. They began selling coffee, bread and cakes from their doorsteps and roadside stalls, and that trade became the square's character for the next two centuries. The ground outside the gate was not named Potsdamer Platz until 1831. By then it had already stopped being a suburb and started being a destination.

## The Policeman in the Five-Sided Box

The railway arrived in 1838: the Potsdamer Bahnhof, terminus of a 26-kilometre line to Potsdam. The city would not let it breach the customs wall, so it stopped just short, and the traffic it generated did the rest. Germany's first electric street lights went up here in 1882, installed by Siemens.

By 1900 more than 100,000 people and 20,000 vehicles crossed the square daily; by the 1920s, 60,000 cars and 600 trams an hour, day and night, converging from five directions. Up to eleven policemen worked the intersection at once, with mixed results. The solution, borrowed from Fifth Avenue in New York, went into service on 15 December 1924: Germany's first traffic light tower, five-sided and 8.5 metres high, designed by Jean Kramer, with lamps by Siemens & Halske and one officer in a cabin at the top switching them by hand until they were automated in 1926.

## The World in One House

The Weimar years built here on a scale that now sounds invented. The Wertheim department store on Leipziger Platz ran a 330-metre granite and glass facade and contained 83 lifts, 1,000 telephones and five kilometres of pneumatic tubing. The Haus Vaterland reopened in 1928 advertising The World in One House: 8,000 guests at a time, a Bavarian beer hall, a Viennese wine bar, a Spanish bodega, a Japanese tearoom, a Wild West bar that quietly shut when America entered the war, and eight orchestras playing at once.

The Hotel Esplanade kept a room named for Kaiser Wilhelm II, who had held his gentlemen's evenings there, and later put up Chaplin and Garbo. At Café Josty, Karl Liebknecht read for hours and made political speeches from the pavement terrace; Erich Kästner wrote part of Emil and the Detectives at one of its tables. Germany's first radio broadcast went out from a building down Potsdamer Strasse on 29 October 1923.

## Erased Twice

Hitler's Reich Chancellery stood one block away, which put Potsdamer Platz inside the target area. Berlin absorbed 363 air raids; three, in November 1943 and February 1945, flattened almost everything here.

Trade restarted in the ruins within weeks, and a black market thrived because three occupation sectors met here: a dealer could cross a jurisdiction in a few paces. On 17 June 1953, when East German workers rose against their government and Soviet tanks came in to end it, some of the worst violence was here; people were shot dead by the Volkspolizei. Fifty-five deaths are documented across the uprising, and the true number was almost certainly higher.

When the Wall went up on 13 August 1961 the square was cut in half, then cleared: the East wanted an open field of fire. Demolition ran until 1976. Two things survived — the steel-framed Weinhaus Huth, standing alone in the waste with squatters inside, and a broken fragment of the Hotel Esplanade. Below ground the S-Bahn ran through without stopping: Potsdamer Platz became Berlin's most famous Geisterbahnhof, a ghost station sealed off and patrolled in the dark.

## Europe's Largest Building Site

The Wall was breached here on 11 November 1989, two days after it opened. What emerged was roughly 60 hectares of empty ground in the middle of a European capital. Daimler-Benz had gambled on a large piece of it in 1987, Sony and ABB bought in during 1990, and the masterplan competition went to Munich's Hilmer & Sattler in October 1991.

For most of the decade this was the biggest construction site in Europe, with a red viewing pavilion called the Info Box drawing thousands of spectators a week. Renzo Piano's 19 buildings for Daimler, in brick, terracotta and sandstone, opened on 2 October 1998. Helmut Jahn's Sony Center, its tented roof said to echo Mount Fuji, followed on 14 June 2000, with Sony's chairman Norio Ohga conducting the Berlin Philharmonic. One survivor was kept by main force: in March 1996 the Esplanade's Kaisersaal, all 1,300 tonnes, was lifted whole and walked 75 metres to its present spot, making two right-angled turns on the way. The square works again. Whether it belongs to Berliners or to visitors is still argued about, which may be the most Berlin outcome available.

## Flight Context

Potsdamer Platz sits at 52.5089° N, 13.3763° E, about 1 km south of the Brandenburg Gate and the Reichstag and just southeast of the Tiergarten. From the air it is one of the easiest features to identify in central Berlin: a tight cluster of glass towers rising abruptly out of low-rise blocks, with the translucent conical roof of the Sony Center catching light beside them and the green rectangle of the Tiergarten immediately northwest. The Kollhoff Tower and Bahn Tower reach roughly 100 m (about 330 ft), so terrain plus structure tops out near 450 ft MSL; ground elevation is about 110 ft. Recommended viewing altitude is 1,500–2,500 ft AGL, which frames the whole redevelopment against the Tiergarten and the line of the former Wall running north to the Brandenburg Gate. Checkpoint Charlie lies 700 m east, the Fernsehturm 3 km northeast at 368 m — the tallest obstacle in the city and the best long-range anchor. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB/BER) is 20 km southeast; former Tempelhof, now parkland, is 3.5 km south as a distinct oval. Controlled airspace covers the government quarter; low winter stratus is common.
