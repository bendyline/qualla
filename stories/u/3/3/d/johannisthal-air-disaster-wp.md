# Johannisthal air disaster

Two hours of engine repair let an October sun warm the hydrogen inside the German Navy's new airship — and when L 2 finally climbed away from Johannisthal on 17 October 1913, everyone aboard had less than a minute to live.

**Location:** 52.4367, 13.5178

**Categories:** Aviation history, Airships, Berlin, Disasters, German history

## Introduction

The engine trouble cost about two hours, and the two hours cost everyone on board. While mechanics worked on the forward car that morning, the October sun warmed twenty-seven thousand cubic metres of hydrogen inside the envelope of L 2 — the Imperial German Navy's newest airship, 158 metres of aluminium framework and rubberised fabric, out at Johannisthal for the trials that would decide whether the Navy accepted her. Warm hydrogen expands. When the mooring party finally let go at around half past ten on 17 October 1913, the ship went up far faster than anyone wanted, and the relief valves opened to bleed off the pressure. Twenty-eight men were aboard. None of them came home.

## Six Hundred Metres

L 2 climbed to roughly 610 metres — about two thousand feet — in a rush, venting hydrogen the whole way. The gas had to go somewhere, and what it found was the forward engine car slung beneath the hull, where a hot motor was running. A flame leapt out of that car. The gasbags directly above it went first, and the ship broke into fire in the middle of the sky. A second explosion followed as the wreck came down. When the fuel tanks hit the ground, everything left burned. It is the accident that hydrogen airships were always one mistake away from, and here the mistake was simply the sun.

## What the Field Saw

Johannisthal was Berlin's airfield, and airfields in 1913 drew crowds. Thousands of people had come out to watch the trials of a ship that, if accepted, was to be the flagship of Germany's new naval air fleet. They heard the detonation before they understood it; they watched the burning hull fall; and then they walked out along the road to where it had come down, and found — in the flat words of one American account published the following year — "nothing but a mass of crumpled aluminum and twisted wreckage." One man was pulled out alive: Lieutenant Baron von Bieul, a guest aboard for the trial, mortally injured. He did not survive the day. Berliners had bought a day out and been handed something none of them would forget.

## The Twenty-Eight

The men in the gondolas were not passengers in any ordinary sense. Most of the Navy's airship expertise was aboard that morning, because an acceptance trial is exactly the flight the specialists insist on attending. Felix Pietzker, the naval architect who had advised on the design, died in it. So did Korvettenkapitän Behnisch, who had only just been appointed to run the Admiralty's aviation department. The rest were officers, engineers and crew; the reports at the time noted that two of them were personal friends of the Kaiser. It was a small, tight professional world in which everybody had trained alongside everybody else. Twenty-eight funerals in a service that could count its trained airshipmen in dozens is not a statistic. It is a generation.

## What the Newspapers Got Wrong

The first accounts were confident and mistaken. The Chicago Daily News Almanac for 1914 told American readers that L 2 had been "destroyed by the explosion of a gasoline tank," put twenty-seven military men on board, and reported the ship falling from nine hundred feet. Petrol was a reasonable guess in 1913 and a wrong one; the investigation traced the fire to vented hydrogen drawn into a running engine, which is a subtler and more frightening failure, because no amount of care with the fuel system would have prevented it. Some of the names printed at the time were garbled in transmission and have been repeated ever since. It is worth reading those columns as what they were — the world's first draft, filed at speed from a field still smelling of burnt fabric.

## The Navy That Was Left

Five weeks earlier, on 9 September 1913, the Navy's first airship, L 1, had been forced down into the North Sea north of Heligoland in a gale; fourteen of the twenty men aboard drowned. Johannisthal followed. Between the two accidents the service lost most of the people who actually knew how to operate airships, and the planned expansion programme was suspended. Behnisch's job went to a young officer named Peter Strasser, who would spend the coming war building the naval airship arm into the force that bombed England — and who would die in one of his own ships in 1918. The crash site southeast of Berlin has been swallowed by the Adlershof science quarter, where the old airfield is a park and the wind tunnels are listed monuments. Nothing on the ground marks the spot where the twenty-eight came down.

## Flight Context

The crash site lies just outside the former Johannisthal airfield at roughly 52.4367°N, 13.5178°E, in the Treptow-Köpenick borough about 15 km southeast of central Berlin. From the air the old field is unmistakable: a broad open rectangle of grassland — the Landschaftspark Johannisthal — surrounded on all sides by the dense modern blocks of the Adlershof science and technology park, with the Aerodynamic Park's 1930s wind-tunnel buildings on its northern edge. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) sits only about 9 km south-southwest, so this ground lies under the terminal area and traffic here is heavily controlled; check the Berlin TMA structure before planning any low pass. Schönhagen (EDAZ) is about 35 km west-southwest and Strausberg (EDAY) about 30 km north-northeast. Best appreciated from 2,000 to 4,000 feet, where the green rectangle reads clearly against the built-up ground. The flat Brandenburg plain gives long sightlines but also frequent autumn fog — the same still, warming conditions that lifted L 2 too fast.
