# Bendlerblock

A Berlin office block where German officers ran the 20 July 1944 plot against Hitler, were shot in its courtyard the same night, and are now remembered in the building where they died.

**Location:** 52.5069, 13.3614

**Categories:** berlin, germany, world-war-ii, german-resistance, memorials, military-history, museums, tiergarten

## Introduction

Someone drove a truck into the courtyard and left the headlights burning, because there was no other light. Just before one in the morning on 21 July 1944, four officers were shot one after another against a wall of that yard off Bendlerstraße: Colonel Claus von Stauffenberg, General Friedrich Olbricht, Colonel Albrecht Mertz von Quirnheim, and Lieutenant Werner von Haeften, who had been Stauffenberg's adjutant. Stauffenberg called something out as the rifles came up. Witnesses could never agree whether it was 'Long live sacred Germany' or 'Long live secret Germany.' Eleven years later, to the day, the city took the street away from the man it had been named for and gave it to him.

## Tirpitz Had a Desk Here

The main block on the Landwehr Canal went up between 1911 and 1914, Neoclassical and heavy, built for the Imperial Naval Office that Grand Admiral Alfred von Tirpitz ran until 1916. The Imperial Admiralty Staff and the Imperial Navy Cabinet worked here too, both answering directly to Wilhelm II.

The street was older than the building. It had been Bendlerstraße since 1837, named for a chief mason and member of the Berlin city council who bought up land south of the Großer Tiergarten and turned it into the mansion district along Tiergartenstraße. After the First World War the Ministry of the Reichswehr moved into his street. The Nazis enlarged the complex considerably, and from 1938 several departments of the Oberkommando der Wehrmacht worked inside it, above all the Army High Command and the Abwehr, the military intelligence service.

## The Conspiracy Had an Office

Opposition to Hitler inside the German army was not a late conversion by men who saw the war being lost. As early as 1938, during the German occupation of Czechoslovakia, the Abwehr chief Admiral Wilhelm Canaris and Lieutenant Colonel Hans Oster were drafting plans for a coup from rooms in this building. Their intelligence service, whose job was to serve the regime, became a place where people who wanted to destroy it could be usefully employed.

By the early 1940s the centre of gravity had shifted down the corridor to the Army Office under General Friedrich Olbricht, who controlled the reserve army — and therefore controlled troops inside Germany. In October 1943 a badly wounded staff officer arrived as his chief of staff. Claus von Stauffenberg had lost an eye, his right hand and two fingers of his left in North Africa. He was thirty-six, and he could get into rooms where Hitler was.

## Between the Blast and the Volley

The bomb went off. Hitler survived. Everything after was a race the conspirators lost by hours: as news spread from East Prussia they could not take control of Germany, and the units they had set in motion began to stop.

By evening the Bendlerblock had turned on itself. General Friedrich Fromm, who had known enough to be ruined either way, ordered the conspirators arrested in their own headquarters and convened a summary court that lasted minutes. Generaloberst Ludwig Beck, the former army chief of staff who would have led the new government, was allowed to shoot himself. The other four were taken down to the courtyard. Fromm's speed bought him nothing. He was arrested the next day for connivance, condemned to death, and executed on 12 March 1945.

The building had one more use in that war. During the Battle of Berlin, General Helmuth Weidling commanded the city's defence from these rooms, and at six in the morning on 2 May 1945 he surrendered Berlin to General Vasily Chuikov of the Red Army.

## A Young Man with His Hands Bound

West Germany did not know for years what to do with the men who had tried to kill Hitler; a good many Germans regarded them as traitors who had broken an oath. The courtyard settled the argument slowly. On 20 July 1953, Berlin's mayor Ernst Reuter unveiled a bronze by Richard Scheibe there — a naked young man, standing, hands bound behind him — on a pedestal inscribed by the art historian Edwin Redslob. Carved into the wall at the entrance is a plain sentence: here, in the former Army High Command, Germans organised the attempt to overthrow the lawless National Socialist regime on 20 July 1944, and for this they sacrificed their lives.

A permanent exhibition, developed under the historian Friedrich Zipfel, opened in the same wing on 20 July 1968. It is now the German Resistance Memorial Center, and it deliberately does not stop at the officers. Communists, trade unionists, students, Jehovah's Witnesses and clergy are all in those rooms, because they were all in the same prisons.

## Still a Working Address

The Bendlerblock never became only a memorial. Since 1993 it has served as the Federal Ministry of Defence's second seat, and new recruits of the Bundeswehr's guard battalion swear their oath in the courtyard where the four men were shot — which is exactly the point. On 27 August 2025 the cabinet of Chancellor Friedrich Merz held its first meeting in the building.

That mixture of function and memory makes the ministry cautious about who gets in. Filming permission was first granted in 2003, to a television production of Stauffenberg starring Sebastian Koch, which went on to win the Deutscher Fernsehpreis. The request to re-stage the execution on the actual spot for Valkyrie, with Tom Cruise, took longer to approve. It was granted in the end, and before the cameras rolled the director Bryan Singer stopped the crew for a minute's silence for the men who had died there.

## Flight Context

Located at 52.5069 N, 13.3614 E on Stauffenbergstraße in Berlin-Tiergarten, on the north bank of the Landwehr Canal at the southern edge of the Großer Tiergarten. From the air the park is the giveaway: a dark green rectangle roughly 3 km long in the middle of the city, with the Siegessäule at its centre roundabout and the Brandenburg Gate at its eastern tip. The Bendlerblock is a large pale U-shaped complex on the canal about 700 m south of the Tiergarten's southern edge, with the Potsdamer Platz towers 1 km east and Tempelhof's unmistakable curved apron about 5 km southeast. Central Berlin is dense controlled airspace; plan any low pass at 1,500-2,500 ft AGL along the canal line and expect clearance limits. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is about 18 km southeast, Schönhagen (EDAZ) about 36 km south-southwest. Winter haze over the Spree basin often flattens the Tiergarten into grey, so the canal and the Siegessäule are the more reliable references.
