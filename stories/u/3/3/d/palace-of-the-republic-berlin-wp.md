# Palace of the Republic, Berlin

East Germany's parliament sat above a bowling alley, thirteen restaurants and a rooftop ice rink — and when the building came down, so did a great deal of what East Berliners remembered about their own lives.

**Location:** 52.5175, 13.4025

**Categories:** Cold War, East Germany, History of Berlin, Demolished buildings, Modernist architecture, Government buildings

## Introduction

East Berliners had a nickname for the building where their parliament met: Erichs Lampenladen, Erich's lamp shop. Hundreds of glass globes hung in tiers across the foyer of the Palace of the Republic, and the joke about Erich Honecker's taste in lighting wrote itself. It stuck because it was funny. It lasted because it was fond. That is the difficult thing about the Palast der Republik — the building housed a legislature that never voted against anything the ruling party wanted, and it was also where you took the children bowling on a Saturday afternoon.

## Thirteen Restaurants and a Cellblock

The inventory of what fit inside reads like the contents of a small city. Two large auditoria, art galleries, a theatre, a cinema, thirteen restaurants, five beer halls, a bowling alley fitted with Brunswick lanes, billiards rooms, a rooftop ice rink, a swimming pool, an indoor basketball court, a casino, a post office, a medical station, barbershops, salons and a discothèque. In the early 1980s one restaurant was converted into a video arcade for the children of Volkskammer deputies and staff. Less advertised were the police station with its underground cellblock, the helipad reserved for the Politburo, the secure garage for Central Committee cars, and the cameras trained on the public areas so that Stasi officers could watch who came and went. Heinz Graffunder designed the thing as an anti-elitist palace for the people: 180 metres long, 85 wide, 32 high, wrapped in brown mirror glass. It opened on 23 April 1976, and the public was admitted two days later.

## The Palace Before the Palace

The ground it stood on already had a history of erasure. The Berlin Palace, seat of Hohenzollern kings and German emperors, had been gutted by Allied bombing and then demolished in 1950 on the argument that there was no money to restore it and no reason to preserve a monument to Prussian imperialism. For most of two decades the cleared site did nothing much: a parade ground, then a parking lot, a hole in the middle of a capital. When the Volkskammer finally got a permanent home, it was built on that hole, and the eastern half of the plot was all the new building needed. Heavy vehicles rehearsing military parades on the western half turned out to shake the marshy Museum Island ground enough to endanger the glass façade, so the parades moved to Karl-Marx-Allee and the space became, once again, a car park.

## The Night the West Got In

The Great Hall was a hexagon roughly 67 metres across and 18 high, with lifting gear beneath the floor that let the stage change size and the seating swing between a thousand and forty-five hundred. Party congresses were held there. So was Ein Kessel Buntes, the variety show that half the country watched. On 31 January 1980, Tangerine Dream became the first Western group permitted to play East Berlin, with Honecker and Willi Stoph in the audience; the recording came out on the state label Amiga as Quichotte and reached the rest of the world six years later, renamed Pergamon. Harry Belafonte sang there in October 1983. So did Udo Lindenberg, the West German rock star, who had been ordered not to perform 'Sonderzug nach Pankow' — his mocking song about Honecker — on pain of arrest. He did not perform it. Santana played two nights in April 1987.

## The Last Vote

In October 1989 the palace hosted a state gala on the eve of the fortieth anniversary of the German Democratic Republic, with Mikhail Gorbachev among the guests. It was the fortieth and the last. Ten months later, during the night of 22–23 August 1990, the freely elected Volkskammer sat in that same hall and voted for the accession of the GDR to the Federal Republic, effective 3 October. The building's own end came faster than the country's. On 19 September 1990, two weeks before reunification, the Volkskammer closed the palace to the public: it was riddled with more than five thousand tonnes of asbestos, sprayed in for fire protection by a state that had outlawed the material in construction back in 1968. On 2 October the parliament dissolved itself, and the palace stood empty.

## What Gets Erased

The Bundestag voted in November 2003 to take it down and put the Berlin Palace back. Most East Germans opposed the demolition, and they said so in protest after protest, arguing that the building belonged to Berlin's history and to the reunification they had voted for inside it. For a while it lived on as a shell: the Terracotta Army was exhibited in the stripped interior, and Einstürzende Neubauten played a concert in the ruins. Demolition began on 6 February 2006 and dragged on to 2008 as more asbestos kept surfacing. About 35,000 tonnes of its steel were shipped to the United Arab Emirates and went into the Burj Khalifa. The Humboldt Forum that now occupies the site opened in 2021 with three replica baroque façades and one modern one; a few of the globe chandeliers and an original sign are on display inside. In Dresden, the palace's sister building, the Kulturpalast, still stands.

## Flight Context

The site sits at 52.5175°N, 13.4025°E, on the eastern end of Unter den Linden between the Lustgarten and Schlossplatz, with Museum Island and the Spree wrapping around it. From the air the reconstructed Berlin Palace now reads as a large rectangular block with a domed chapel corner, immediately southwest of the Berlin Cathedral's green copper dome; the Fernsehturm's needle stands about 800 m northeast and is the reliable landmark in any weather. Best appreciated from 2,000–4,000 ft on a Spree-following approach. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) lies roughly 22 km southeast; Tegel (EDDT) closed in November 2020. Winter haze over Mitte is common; late afternoon light picks out the Unter den Linden axis running west to the Brandenburg Gate.
