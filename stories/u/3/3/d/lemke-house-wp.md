# Lemke House

The last house Ludwig Mies van der Rohe designed in Germany cost 16,000 Reichsmarks, housed its owners for twelve years, and then spent nearly three decades as the Stasi's canteen and laundry.

**Location:** 52.5492, 13.4914

**Categories:** Berlin, Architecture, Modernism, Museums and galleries, German history

## Introduction

It is a single-storey L of dark red brick at the end of a quiet street in Hohenschönhausen, and if you did not know, you would walk past it. Karl and Martha Lemke ran a printing business in Berlin and wanted a house on the Obersee; in 1932 they bought a double plot running down to the water and, on a friend's recommendation, wrote to an architect. The architect was Ludwig Mies van der Rohe, then running the Bauhaus, and destined to be the last man who ever did. He drew them several schemes, mostly two storeys, and built them the cheapest and lowest one: roughly 160 square metres, flat-roofed, open on one side to the lake, for 16,000 Reichsmarks. It was the final house he designed in Germany before he left for the United States in 1938 — and the Lemkes got to live in it for twelve years.

## Brick, Water, and a Right Angle

The house does nothing showy, which is the point. Two wings meet at a right angle around a terrace that opens toward the Obersee, so the composition is finished not by a facade but by a lake. The brick is charcoal-fired and red, laid plain, and the flat roof sits on it without an eave — a combination that reads as odd from the street and entirely resolved from the garden. Inside, part of the furniture came from Mies's own studio or was designed by Lilly Reich, his collaborator and the person responsible for much of what people think of as Mies's interiors. The planting was laid out by the garden designer Karl Foerster. For a modest commission from a printing family, the Lemkes assembled a remarkable set of hands.

## Ordered Out, May 1945

In May 1945, after the Red Army took Alt-Hohenschönhausen, the Lemkes were told to leave the house as soon as possible. They did. The surrounding streets were declared a restricted area and their house was put to use as a garage and a store. This was the pattern for the whole district: a leafy suburb of villas on two small lakes, sealed off, its residents displaced, and its buildings assigned to whatever the occupying authority and then the new state required. The occupants who eventually arrived in the neighbouring villas worked for the Ministry for State Security, whose remand prison stood little more than a kilometre away.

## The Ministry's Laundry

The Stasi bought the house outright in 1962 and altered it heavily, house and garden both. What it wanted from a masterpiece of European modernism was floor space: for the next twenty-seven years the building served as a laundry and canteen for ministry staff, and part of the garden that Karl Foerster had planted was concreted over for a car park. The Berlin magistrate put it on the district monument list in 1977, which changed the paperwork rather than the plumbing. Repairs attempted in the 1980s achieved nothing much, because the money released for them was never enough. When the German Democratic Republic ended, the house was a wreck with a listed status and an internationally famous author.

## Surveillance Around the Edge

In 1990 the district took the property over from the Ministry and renamed it the Mies van der Rohe Haus. Four years later the Berlin sculptor Ruth Baumann installed a work in the garden called Aufsicht um die Kante — Surveillance around the edge — a title that does a great deal of work on a plot of ground where the neighbours had been listening for decades. The serious restoration ran from 2000 to 2002 and cost something over a million euros, stripping out the Stasi's alterations and putting the house back to what Mies had drawn. It is now a public exhibition space for contemporary art, run by the Lichtenberg district, and it draws on the order of 18,000 visitors a year.

## The Smallest Mies

Set beside the Barcelona Pavilion or the Farnsworth House, the Lemke House looks almost like a rehearsal — no travertine, no chrome columns, no glass wall running the length of a room. But the Lemkes did not want any of that, and Mies did not give it to them. What they got was a small, cheap, careful building that put its owners at the edge of a lake and got out of the way, and the fact that it survived a war, an eviction, a secret police force and a car park suggests the modesty was structural as well as stylistic. Go and stand on the terrace at the end of an afternoon. The water does the rest, which was always the plan.

## Flight Context

The Mies van der Rohe Haus stands at Oberseestraße 60 in Alt-Hohenschönhausen, Lichtenberg, at 52.5492°N, 13.4914°E — about 8 km northeast of Alexanderplatz. The navigational giveaway is the pair of small lakes it sits between: the Obersee and, immediately to its southwest, the Orankesee, two dark ovals set in a low-rise green suburb that contrasts sharply with the prefabricated GDR housing slabs further east. The Stasi remand prison — now the Berlin-Hohenschönhausen Memorial — lies roughly 1.5 km northwest, and the Berlin television tower stands on the horizon to the southwest as a bearing check. The house itself is a low single-storey roof on the north shore of the Obersee and will not be identifiable much above 1,500 feet; the lakes are the target. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is about 25 km south-southwest, Strausberg (EDAY) about 22 km east, Schönhagen (EDAZ) about 55 km southwest. Fly it low and slow in clear air; the surrounding tree cover closes the view of both lakes in full leaf.
