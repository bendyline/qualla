# Pergamon Altar

A Hellenistic altar that ancient writers almost entirely ignored became Berlin's most famous possession - and has now been out of public view for over a decade.

**Location:** 52.5211, 13.3969

**Categories:** Archaeology, Museums, Ancient history, Berlin, Sculpture, Cultural heritage

## Introduction

Berlin's most famous object has been invisible since 2014. The hall built to hold the Pergamon Altar - a room designed so that visitors could climb a real marble staircase into a Greek building - closed first, for structural work. Then, in October 2023, the rest of the Pergamon Museum closed behind it. The Staatliche Museen have fixed 4 June 2027 for the reopening of the north wing and the altar with it; the south wing is not expected back until around 2037. The greatest surviving work of Hellenistic sculpture currently sits behind hoarding, which is close to the condition it has spent most of its existence in: buried, broken up, crated, or in transit.

## One Roman Wrote It Down

Eumenes II ruled Pergamon in the first half of the second century BC, over a kingdom his father Attalus I had won by beating the Galatians and declaring himself king in 228 BC. The Attalids built to advertise. On a terrace of the acropolis, above the plain of what is now Bergama in western Turkey, they raised an altar roughly 36 metres wide and 33 deep, its western stairway nearly 20 metres across.

Then, near silence. Sometime in the second century AD a Roman named Lucius Ampelius jotted it into his liber memorialis under the heading Miracula Mundi: "At Pergamum there is a great marble altar, 40 feet high, with colossal sculptures. It also shows a Gigantomachy." Apart from a passing comparison by Pausanias, that sentence is the entire ancient literary record of a building three classical lists counted among the wonders of the world.

## Gods and Giants at Eye Level

The frieze runs 113 metres, interrupted only by the stairway - the longest surviving frieze from Greek antiquity after the Parthenon's. What makes it extraordinary is the height. The panels stand 2.3 metres tall at the base of the building, so the fighting happens at the viewer's own eye level, and the figures refuse to stay inside their frame: knees and elbows spill onto the steps, and Giants climb the stairs beside you.

The subject is the Gigantomachy, the war of the Olympians against the Giants, children of the earth goddess Gaia. Zeus fights three opponents at once with lightning and massed cloud. Beside him, Athena hauls the Giant Alkyoneus up by the hair to break his contact with the ground - he was immortal only while touching the earth - and Gaia rises out of the soil at his feet to watch her son die. One sculptor signed the work, on a cornice at the south projection: THEORRETOS. The rest of that workshop is anonymous.

## Burned for Lime

Christianity ended the altar's working life, and defence finished the building. In the seventh century, garrisons fortifying the acropolis against Arab raids pulled it apart for stone; Pergamon fell anyway in 716. By the nineteenth century the people of Bergama were quarrying what remained, and some of the marble was going into lime kilns.

Carl Humann, a German engineer surveying roads, first reached the site in 1864 or 1865 and spent years arguing that the ruins had to be protected. Nobody acted until 1877, when Alexander Conze took charge of Berlin's sculpture collection, matched two fragments already sitting in the city against the Ampelius text, and grasped what they were. Excavation began in September 1878 and ran to 1886; from 1879, under an agreement with the Ottoman government, the panels went to Berlin. The men doing it understood the transaction. "We are not insensitive to what it means to remove the remnants of a great monument from their original location," the German account reads.

## Trophy

Berlin then took fifty years to build a room worthy of it. A first Pergamon Museum opened in 1901 and had to be demolished over foundation problems; Alfred Messel's replacement, held up by war, revolution and the hyperinflation of 1922-23, opened in 1930. Nazi architects found the shape congenial, and in 1936 the interior minister Wilhelm Frick entertained the International Olympic Committee at a banquet laid out in front of the frieze.

The museum shut in 1939. The reliefs came down in 1941 and went into an air-raid shelter near the Berlin zoo, where the Red Army found them in 1945 and shipped them east as war trophies. They sat in the Hermitage's depot in Leningrad until 1958, returned to East Germany in 1959, and went back on view that October. The old repairs had done damage too: iron clamps pinning the fragments together were rusting, and rust expands, so the fixings were quietly splitting the marble from inside. Restoration ran from 1994 to 2004 and cost over three million euros.

## Whose Altar

Turkey's culture minister Istemihan Talay called for the altar's return in 1998 and again in 2001. The demand was never lodged formally, and Berlin's state museums, like most large European and American collections, decline as a matter of policy to hand antiquities back. The foundation is still in Bergama, along with stretches of wall and fragments of frieze found after the Germans left, and you can still walk the ground plan on the acropolis.

Two panels are missing from the Berlin reconstruction for a different reason entirely. In 1625 an English chaplain named William Petty carried them home for the Earl of Arundel, the collection was later dispersed, and nobody worked out what the slabs were until the 1960s. When the doors open again in June 2027, the argument about where the marble belongs will still be running.

## Flight Context

52.5211 degrees N, 13.3969 degrees E, on Museum Island in the middle of the Spree in central Berlin. From the air the island is unmistakable: a teardrop of masonry cut off from the mainland by two channels of the river, with the green copper dome of the Berlin Cathedral at its southern end and the smaller dome of the Bode Museum at the northern tip. The Pergamon Museum is the large courtyard building immediately northwest of the cathedral, currently wrapped in construction hoarding. The Fernsehturm stands about 800 metres east and makes the easiest first fix from any altitude. This is dense controlled airspace over the German capital, with overflight restrictions across the government quarter just to the west, so plan a cleared transit rather than a loiter. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) lies roughly 10 nm south-southeast; Strausberg (EDAY) is about 19 nm east. At 2,000-2,500 ft the whole Museumsinsel complex reads in a single pass, and you can see how much of the island is simply roof.
