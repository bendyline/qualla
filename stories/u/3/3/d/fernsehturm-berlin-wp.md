# Fernsehturm Berlin

East Germany built a 365-metre tower to prove what socialism could do, and the sun turned its steel sphere into a shining cross that Berliners named the Pope's Revenge.

**Location:** 52.5208, 13.4094

**Categories:** germany, berlin, architecture, cold-war, landmark

## Introduction

The government that took the crosses down from East Berlin's churches put a steel ball on a concrete spike in the middle of its capital, clad the sphere in tiled stainless steel, and then watched what the sun did with it. Hit at the right angle, the dome reflects light back in the shape of a Greek cross. It appears in the mornings and again in the evenings, and no one has ever managed to talk it out of the sky. Berliners named it Rache des Papstes — the Pope's Revenge. Ronald Reagan was still enjoying the joke in 1987, mentioning it in the Brandenburg Gate speech where he asked Gorbachev to tear down the Wall.

## Sputnik on a Spike

The tower took thirteen years to start. East Germany's postal service began planning a television mast for Berlin as early as 1952, chose a site in the southeast, broke ground — and then abandoned it on discovering the structure would sit 8 km from Schönefeld airport, at the edge of an approach corridor. Other locations were floated through the 1950s and killed by the cost of building the Berlin Wall.

By 1964 the government wanted something else entirely. The tower was to be central and visible, less a broadcast facility than a statement; the final choice of site was frankly political. Walter Ulbricht approved a design borrowing from the Fernsehturm in Stuttgart and from Sputnik, the first artificial satellite — a silver ball riding a shaft, socialism's own moon over Alexanderplatz. Building it meant flattening a large piece of the historic city centre. The Marienkirche, medieval and stubborn, was left standing at its foot, and stands there still.

## Fifty-Three Months

Work on the foundation began on 4 August 1965. Concreting of the shaft started the following March and moved fast: past 100 metres by October 1966, at its full 248.78 metres by 16 June 1967 — 8,000 cubic metres of concrete weighing 26,000 tonnes.

The sphere was the harder problem. Engineers assembled it from 120 separate segments, and to practise they built a 35-metre replica of the shaft on the ground between the Marienkirche and the Red Town Hall, fitting the pieces together there from April until November 1967. Assembly on the real shaft began in February 1968; the final segment went on in October. Then, at the start of 1969, water trickled into the ball and did considerable damage, and the whole sphere had to be sealed again. Estimated at 33 million marks, the project ended up costing more than 132 million. It was still finished in 53 months, which East Germany called record-breaking and, in fairness, was.

## The Pope's Revenge

Ulbricht inaugurated the tower on 3 October 1969, flanked by the party leadership, and used the occasion to launch the GDR's second television channel and colour broadcasting. Four days later, on Republic Day, the public was let in. In its first three years, four million people went up.

The cross showed up on its own. Sunlight on the tiled stainless steel of the dome reflects as a luminous Greek cross, and East Berliners — living in a state that had been stripping religious symbols from public view — found this extremely funny. Alongside the Pope's Revenge, the tower picked up a second nickname: St. Walter, after Ulbricht himself. The joke travelled. Whatever the leadership thought of it, they gave the building official monument protection in 1979, a status the Federal Republic kept after reunification.

## Two Hundred and Three Metres Up

Two lifts carry twelve people each to the observation deck in about forty seconds. There is also a staircase of 986 steps, which is not the recommended route. The deck sits at 203 metres, ringed by sixty windows and Berlin's highest bar; twenty-one steps above it, at 207 metres, a restaurant turns a slow full circle while you eat, its main kitchen more than two hundred metres below on the ground floor, for fire safety, the food riding up by lift.

Under the GDR, a seat in the Tele-Café was rationed to sixty minutes and the observation deck to thirty. Nobody times you now. On a clear day the view runs almost 60 km, far enough to pick out the Tropical Islands resort dome on the southern horizon. Roughly 1.2 million visitors come each year, six in ten from abroad, with Spaniards the largest single group. On 14 June 2011, Berlin's mayor greeted the fiftieth-millionth.

## The Symbol That Changed Sides

When the GDR dissolved, there were voices arguing that the tower should come down with it — the same argument that eventually took down the Palast der Republik, a short walk away. The Federal Republic kept it. Deutsche Telekom put more than 50 million marks into overhauling the broadcasting equipment, and in the summer of 1997 a new, more powerful antenna tip went on from 327 metres up, raising the total from 365 to 368 metres and making it the tallest structure in Germany. When it opened in 1969 it had been the second-tallest television tower on Earth, behind only Ostankino in Moscow.

The transformation was complete by then anyway. The tower that was built to say one thing now says another: it is simply what Berlin looks like, the shape that opens the establishing shot of any film set in the city, and it was cheerfully wrapped to resemble a magenta football for the 2006 World Cup. Not many Cold War monuments get to change sides that gracefully.

## Flight Context

The Fernsehturm stands at 52.5208° N, 13.4094° E, immediately southwest of Alexanderplatz in Mitte, and it is the single most useful visual reference in Berlin: at 368 m (roughly 1,207 ft) above a city that is otherwise flat and low-rise, the sphere is visible from every approach in clear air. Terrain elevation is about 110 ft. Treat the tower as a genuine obstacle — its top reaches roughly 1,320 ft MSL, and it is lit and marked accordingly; keep at least 1,000 ft clearance if orbiting, and prefer a viewing altitude of 2,000–3,000 ft AGL, which puts the sphere below you with the Spree, Museum Island, the Berlin Cathedral dome and the Rotes Rathaus arranged around its base. The Brandenburg Gate lies 2.5 km west along Unter den Linden, Potsdamer Platz 3 km southwest. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB/BER) is 21 km southeast; Berlin's inner city sits beneath controlled airspace, so expect clearance requirements. The cross reflection on the dome is a morning and evening phenomenon and needs direct sun; winter overcast over the North German Plain frequently removes it — and the view — for days at a time.
