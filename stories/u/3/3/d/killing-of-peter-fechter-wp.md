# Killing of Peter Fechter

An eighteen-year-old bricklayer, shot on Zimmerstraße in August 1962 and left where he fell, died over the better part of an hour with two of the world's armies watching and neither one moving.

**Location:** 52.5078, 13.3935

**Categories:** berlin, berlin-wall, cold-war, modern-history, germany, memorial

## Introduction

Peter Fechter's eldest sister had married a man in West Berlin. Before August 1961 that was simply a fact about the family, the sort of thing that arranged a Sunday: his parents and his brothers and sisters would ride across the city to see her, and ride home again in the evening. Peter was seventeen, a bricklayer, third of four children, raised in Weißensee where his father worked as a mechanical engineer and his mother sold goods behind a counter. Then on 13 August 1961 the sector border closed overnight, and a woman who lived a short tram ride away became unreachable. A year and four days later, Peter tried to get to that side of the city, and the world watched him die.

## A Bricklayer from Weißensee

He was born on 14 January 1944, in a Berlin that still had a year of bombing ahead of it. Weißensee ended up in the Soviet occupation zone, which meant that the Fechters' address decided the rest of their lives without anybody consulting them. Peter left school at fourteen and qualified as a bricklayer, a trade that put him on building sites in a city rebuilding itself out of rubble. His workmate Helmut Kulbeik later said the two of them had talked about going west for some time, and had even walked the border installations to see how they were put together, but that nothing had been decided. Then Peter applied through his firm for a legally sanctioned trip to West Germany. His work record was good. The application was refused anyway. It is a small bureaucratic fact, and it is probably the reason he stopped waiting.

## The Window on Zimmerstraße

On 17 August 1962 the two young men slipped into a carpenter's workshop on Zimmerstraße, close to the crossing the Allies called Checkpoint Charlie. From inside they watched the patrols until the timing looked right. The plan was to go out through a window into the strip of cleared ground between the wall and the parallel fence the East Germans had recently begun to build, sprint across it, and climb a barrier a little over two metres high, topped with barbed wire, into the Kreuzberg district. It very nearly worked. Both of them crossed the open ground and reached the last wall. Both of them started to climb. Then the border guards opened fire, and while Kulbeik got over the top and dropped into the West, Peter Fechter was hit in the pelvis and fell backwards into the strip.

## Fifty-Nine Minutes

He came down on the eastern side of the wire, in territory nobody in the West was permitted to enter, and he could be seen from the Kreuzberg pavement by hundreds of people. He called out for help for a long time. The first West Berlin police reports went in at 2:12 that afternoon; five minutes later an American lieutenant at Checkpoint Charlie telephoned Major General Albert Watson, the US commandant in Berlin, and was told to stand fast. West Berlin police were under orders not to cross under any circumstances. Somebody threw bandages over the wire; they landed where he could not reach them. The guards who had shot him did not come out either. He lay bleeding for roughly an hour while a crowd built on the far side of the wire, calling out for help that never came. When East German soldiers finally carried him away, the crowd began shouting the word murderers at them; he died of the wound that afternoon. A Berlin court, ruling on the case thirty-five years later, concluded that the shot through his hip had caused injuries he could not have survived. It is worth being exact about what that means. Fechter could not have been saved by the time he landed. He could have been reached, and held, and not left alone.

## Wall of Shame

He was the twenty-seventh person known to have died at the Berlin Wall, and the first whose death the world saw as it happened. The photographs and film taken from the Kreuzberg side travelled everywhere within days. Time ran him on its cover at the end of that August and used a phrase in the piece — Mauer der Schande, Wall of Shame — that stuck to the concrete for the next twenty-seven years. In the week after the shooting, at the invitation of West Berlin's mayor Willy Brandt, the Yale Russian Chorus sang a German setting of Mozart's Ave verum corpus near the spot. On the first anniversary Brandt returned with an American major general, James Polk, and laid a wreath. A cross went up on the western side, close as anyone could get to where he had actually lain.

## He Just Wanted Freedom

The case came to court in March 1997, thirty-five years afterwards. Two former border guards, Rolf Friedrich and Erich Schreiber, admitted the shooting and were convicted of manslaughter, receiving suspended sentences of twenty and twenty-one months. Three men had fired that day and one was already dead, so the court could not establish whose round had hit him. At sentencing both defendants apologised and said that if they could undo it they would. Artists have kept returning to the hour itself: the Finnish composer Aulis Sallinen wrote Mauermusik for him, and in 2012 the Canadian playwright Jordan Tannahill staged Peter Fechter: 59 Minutes, an imagining of what went through his mind. The memorial that matters most is the plainest. After reunification a stele went up on Zimmerstraße at the exact place on the eastern side where he died, and it carries six words: er wollte nur die Freiheit. He just wanted freedom.

## Flight Context

The Peter-Fechter-Stelle stands at 52.508°N, 13.394°E on Zimmerstraße in Berlin-Mitte, roughly 120 m east of the Checkpoint Charlie site and 1.5 km south of Unter den Linden. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is 18 km southeast; the closed Tempelhof field, 3 km south, is the obvious visual anchor from the air — a green oval with its runways still painted on. Below about 3,000 ft the old border is legible as a double line of cobblestones threading between office blocks, running east–west along Zimmerstraße before turning north toward the Brandenburg Gate. Berlin's frequent low stratus in autumn and winter can hide the ground entirely; the clearest light on the Mitte street grid is early morning.
