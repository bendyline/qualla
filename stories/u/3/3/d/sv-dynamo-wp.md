# SV Dynamo

The sports association of East Germany's police and secret police, chaired by Stasi chief Erich Mielke, won more than 200 Olympic medals and doped its athletes — some of them children.

**Location:** 52.5170, 13.4151

**Categories:** Berlin, East Germany, Sport, Stasi, Cold War, Football

## Introduction

Erich Mielke ran the Ministry for State Security from 1957 to 1989, and for even longer than that he ran a sports club. Sportvereinigung Dynamo was founded on 27 March 1953 and headquartered in Hohenschönhausen, in East Berlin. It was the athletic arm of the state's security apparatus — the Volkspolizei, the customs service, the fire brigade, and above all the Stasi, which paid for very nearly all of it. Mielke took the chairmanship at the founding conference and kept it until the state itself ended. What he built produced more than two hundred Olympic medals across four decades. It also produced a doping programme that reached down to children.

## A Republic Mapped in Club Badges

Dynamo was organised as a shadow of the country: fifteen regional units matching East Germany's fifteen districts, and within them some 290 sections, each a local sports community or club with its own speciality. Dresden did football. Hoppegarten did judo, shooting and parachuting. Klingenthal did Nordic skiing, Luckenwalde wrestling, Potsdam rowing and canoe sprint, Weißwasser ice hockey, and Zinnwald in Altenberg took biathlon, bobsleigh, luge and skeleton — the whole cold-weather portfolio in one Erzgebirge village. Membership went from 23,162 in its first year to 66,856 by 1955, nearly 11,000 of them children. By 1989 the central management office alone employed 1,400 people, a thousand of them from the Ministry of the Interior and 180 from the Stasi, under the Stasi major general Heinz Pommer. Most of it ran on Ministry for State Security money.

## Founded by Survivors

The association's politics were not decorative. Among its founders were communists who had fought the Nazis in the street battles of the Weimar years, and men who had come out of concentration camps. Constituent clubs were given honorary names drawn from the dead: SG Dynamo Dresden carried the name of Felix Dzerzhinsky, SG Dynamo Erfurt that of Richard Sorge. The association's highest distinction was the title of Dzerzhinsky Athlete. Young Dynamo athletes were taken on organised visits to the Soviet War Memorial in Treptower Park and to Sachsenhausen. Whatever the association became, this is where it started, and the people who started it had earned the anti-fascism they taught.

## Ten Titles in a Row

The showcase was football, and the football was BFC Dynamo. Under the coach Jürgen Bogs, the Berlin club won the DDR-Oberliga ten seasons running, from 1979 to 1988 — a sequence no East German side came near. Mielke was honorary chairman and gave the club, in the association's own description, personal, organisational and financial support. Whether he also gave it referees is the argument that has never closed. The East German football federation's own internal review of the 1984–85 season found that BFC had collected only a third as many yellow cards as its nearest rival, SG Dynamo Dresden. A separate review of the 1985 cup final concluded that 30 percent of the referee's decisions had been wrong, and that 80 percent of those had gone against Dresden. Bogs and his players have always insisted the titles came from youth development and coaching. Both things can be true, and neither settled the crowds: after 1990, average attendance at BFC fell to a few hundred.

## State Plan 14.25

In 1974 East Germany centralised what had been scattered practice into a secret programme, State Research Plan 14.25, run by a working group whose bland cover name translated as 'supportive substances'. The substance was mostly Oral-Turinabol, an anabolic steroid made by the state pharmaceutical firm Jenapharm. Across the country roughly ten thousand athletes were affected; as many as a thousand are estimated to have suffered serious and lasting physical or psychological harm. Some were as young as ten. Many were never told what they were swallowing. German media revealed after reunification that Dynamo athletes had been systematically doped from 1971 until reunification in 1990 under Stasi supervision and with the government's full backing. In 1998 two Dynamo Berlin doctors were convicted of administering hormones to underage girls between 1975 and 1984. Manfred Ewald, who had run East Germany's sports federation, received a suspended sentence of twenty-two months. In 2016 Germany established a compensation fund of €13.65 million for the athletes.

## Dissolved

SV Dynamo was wound up in early 1990, a few months before the state that owned it. Its clubs scattered — some folded, some renamed themselves, some carried on with the same badge and a very different relationship to their neighbours. Dynamo Dresden survived as a professional club. BFC Dynamo, the club with the ten titles, went through insolvency proceedings from 2001 and clawed its way back to the Regionalliga Nordost in 2014, still playing in front of a fraction of the crowds that its rivals draw. The medals are still in the record books. The people who were handed pills at fourteen and told nothing about what they were are still living with the consequences, which is the part of the record no league table holds.

## Flight Context

The association's actual home ground was the Sportforum in Hohenschönhausen, several kilometres north-east: from 2,000–3,000 ft it reads as a large cluster of halls, tracks and pitches on open ground beyond the S-Bahn ring, distinct from the dense inner city.
