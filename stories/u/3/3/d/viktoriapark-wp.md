# Viktoriapark

Berlin's most famous borough is named after a cast-iron cross on a 66-metre hill, and the waterfall running down that hill has to be switched on.

**Location:** 52.4889, 13.3806

**Categories:** germany, berlin, parks, monuments, history

## Introduction

Kreuzberg — the borough, the nightlife, the shorthand for an entire idea of Berlin — is named after a cross. It sits on top of a cast-iron spire on a hill 66 metres above sea level, which in a city built on a glacial floodplain counts as a summit. Everything else on the hill is engineered too. The waterfall tumbling 24 metres down the northern slope is fed by pumps and switched on for the season. The rocks it falls over were brought from the mountains of Silesia. Even the hill has been altered: in the 1870s the monument on top was jacked up eight metres and rotated twenty-one degrees so that it would line up properly with the street below.

## Schinkel's Spire and Twelve Battles

The monument came first, by seventy years. Frederick William III of Prussia commissioned it to mark the wars that drove Napoleon out of Germany, and Karl Friedrich Schinkel — the architect who gave Berlin its neoclassical face — designed something deliberately unlike his usual work: a neo-Gothic spire of cast iron, 18.83 metres tall and weighing 200 tons, dedicated on 30 March 1821. Twelve cast-iron genii stand in its niches, one for each of twelve battles, from Großgörschen and the Katzbach to Leipzig, Großbeeren, Dennewitz and Paris. An Iron Cross crowns the top. On the day it was dedicated the hill, until then the Tempelhofer Berg, acquired the name it still has, and eventually gave that name to the whole district beneath it.

## Lifted Eight Metres, Turned Twenty-One Degrees

By the 1870s Berlin was building up around the hill and swallowing the view. Wilhelm I ordered the entire 200-ton monument raised onto a new eight-metre base, a job done with twelve hydraulic presses under the supervision of the engineer Johann Wilhelm Schwedler, who also took the opportunity to swing the whole structure twenty-one degrees so that it faced squarely down Großbeerenstraße. That bought height. It did not solve the problem, so in 1879 the president of the royal police, Guido von Madai, simply decreed a maximum building height for the surrounding streets to keep the monument visible. Property owners went to court, and in 1882 the Prussian administrative court ruled against the police: their authority extended to public safety, not to the protection of views. The Kreuzberg judgement is still taught in German law faculties. A monument to liberation produced, incidentally, a landmark limit on state power.

## A Waterfall That Has to Be Switched On

Berlin decided to build a park around the monument in 1886 and bought 8.5 hectares of open ground the following year. The design belonged to Hermann Mächtig, the city's garden director, who used the hill's awkward slopes rather than flattening them: forested banks, tiny terraces, ramps, switchbacks and stairs, so that a modest rise reads as mountainous. His centrepiece was pure theatre. Water pumped to the summit is released beside the monument and drops 24 metres over stone brought from the Giant Mountains of Silesia, landing in a pool at the foot of Großbeerenstraße. It first ran on trial on 14 October 1893, and between 1898 and the First World War it was lit after dark in a shifting glare meant to resemble Bengal fire. War destroyed the pumphouse. For one day in August 1955, Berlin firefighters pumped the water back uphill with their own equipment so that the falls could run again.

## The Rare Haul

Mächtig's park filled steadily with bronze and stone. At the lower pool, opposite the end of Großbeerenstraße, Ernst Herter's 1896 sculpture Der seltene Fang — the rare haul — shows a fisherman grappling with a mermaid tangled in his net, an encounter the sculptor rendered with only the thinnest pretence of restraint. Six herms of German patriotic poets went up in the late 1890s; three metres each, and only three of them, Kleist, Rückert and Uhland, survived the Second World War. A stele by Otto Lessing carrying a bust of Robert Zeller, the city's lord mayor in the 1890s, lost its bust in the same war. In 1910 the army sold its parade ground on Katzbachstraße to the city, and the garden director Albert Brodersen used it to enlarge the park by half again. For a few years around 1930 the grounds also held enclosures of birds, badgers, foxes, reptiles and a pair of monkeys.

## Tunnels Under the Hill

In the summer of 1944 the Organisation Todt began driving five tunnels into the northern slope from Kreuzbergstraße, to be used as air-raid shelters. The work was done in part by Soviet prisoners held at Blücherplatz and forced to dig; construction continued into February 1945, and the tunnels were never finished. The hill was hit hard from above as well. On 30 January 1944, British bombs tore a line of destruction up Großbeerenstraße, across the waterfall — taking out the pumphouse — and into the monument itself, blowing away part of the octagonal base, which the city had been using as a safe store for plaster casts of major sculptures, among them the Quadriga from the Brandenburg Gate. Today the slopes carry vines instead. A small vineyard planted in 1968 with cuttings donated by Wiesbaden and Ingelheim still produces a Berlin wine called Kreuz-Neroberger, made a short walk from the spot where the casts were buried in rubble.

## Flight Context

Viktoriapark occupies the Kreuzberg hill at 52.4889° N, 13.3806° E, about 3 km south of Potsdamer Platz. The hill is the highest natural ground in inner Berlin at 66 m above sea level, and from the air it reads as a compact dark-green wedge of mature trees standing proud of the surrounding grid of nineteenth-century apartment blocks, with the cast-iron monument catching light on the summit. The best anchor is Tempelhofer Feld, the huge open former airfield 1.5 km to the south-east — impossible to miss and impossible to confuse with anything else. Potsdamer Platz's cluster of towers lies north, the Fernsehturm north-east at about 5 km. Best viewed from 1,500–2,500 ft AGL; the waterfall runs only in the warmer months and the tree cover hides the paths entirely in high summer, so the terrain reads better from late autumn to early spring. Central Berlin is controlled airspace. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) lies about 18 km south-east; Schönhagen (EDAZ) is roughly 32 km south-west and Strausberg (EDAY) about 40 km north-east for general aviation.
