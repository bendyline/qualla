# French Cathedral, Berlin

It is not a cathedral, the tower is not part of the church, and the name is a French pun about a dome — a Huguenot refugee chapel that Frederick the Great dressed up to balance a square.

**Location:** 52.5144, 13.3922

**Categories:** germany, berlin, architecture, religion

## Introduction

Neither building on Gendarmenmarkt is a cathedral. Neither has ever held a bishop's throne, which is the only thing that makes a cathedral a cathedral. The German word Dom is doing something else entirely here: it comes from the French dôme, meaning cupola, a piece of linguistic borrowing left behind by Frederick the Great, who spoke French by preference and rebuilt this square to look like an idea he had about civilisation. So the Französischer Dom is not the French Cathedral. It is the French dome — and strictly speaking, the dome is not even part of the church underneath it.

## A Quarter of the City Spoke French

Louis XIV revoked the Edict of Nantes in 1685 and French Protestants left in their tens of thousands. Brandenburg, underpopulated after the Thirty Years' War, opened its doors and got one of the great demographic windfalls in European history — skilled artisans, merchants, printers, gardeners, a whole professional class arriving at once. At the peak, Huguenots made up roughly a quarter of Berlin's population. The community needed a church of its own, and between 1701 and 1705 Louis Cayart and Abraham Quesnay built one in Friedrichstadt, modelled on the Huguenot temple at Charenton-Saint-Maurice outside Paris. That original no longer existed; it had been pulled down after the revocation. Its replica went up on Brandenburg soil, built by the people who had been driven away from it.

## Frederick's Symmetry

For eighty years the French Church was a plain Calvinist meeting house. Then in 1785, at Frederick the Great's instigation, Carl von Gontard built a domed tower alongside it — not attached to it, not owned by the same body, and not for any liturgical reason whatsoever. The point was the square. Frederick wanted Gendarmenmarkt to read as an ensemble, and an ensemble needed matching verticals, so he ordered towers for both churches flanking the theatre in the middle. The German church across the way was rebuilt as tower and sanctuary together. The French one got its dome bolted on beside it, a separate structure with a separate owner, which is why the building most people photograph as the French Cathedral is technically not the church at all.

## The Organ Argument

Calvinists sang psalms unaccompanied. That was the tradition the refugees brought with them, and it did not survive contact with Lutheran Berlin. In 1753 an organ went into the French Church, competing directly with the practice of unaccompanied congregational singing. In 1791 hymns were added to the psalms. When most Prussian Reformed and Lutheran congregations came under a single umbrella in 1817, the French Church of Friedrichstadt joined but kept its Calvinist identity, having already drifted a fair distance toward its neighbours. Otto March refurbished the interior in 1905 — more decorative than before, but still without images, holding to the Reformed suspicion of anything that might be venerated. The congregation today belongs to the Evangelical Church of Berlin-Brandenburg-Silesian Upper Lusatia.

## The Photograph from 1951

A Bundesarchiv photograph taken on 4 October 1951 shows what the war left: the tower gutted and open, the prayer hall damaged, the Schauspielhaus a shell across the square. Gendarmenmarkt had been renamed Platz der Akademie by then, and for a quarter-century after that photograph it stayed roughly in that condition — one of central East Berlin's great unresolved wounds, too ruined to use and too important to clear. Reconstruction of the Französischer Dom finally ran from 1977 to 1981. The façades were restored again between 2004 and 2006 at a cost of six million euros. It holds services, it hosts concerts, and the Evangelical Church in Germany uses it for its conventions.

## Sixty Bells

The tower carries a carillon of sixty bronze bells, installed in 1987 and spanning five octaves. Together they weigh twenty-nine tons; the largest is 5,700 kilograms and the smallest fourteen, a difference of more than four hundred to one. Eighteen of them were repaired or recast during the restoration work of the 2000s. Below the bells, at forty metres, an observation deck gives one of the best mid-height views in Mitte — not the panorama of the Fernsehturm, but close enough to the rooftops to read the city as a place people live in rather than a map. The Huguenot Museum occupies the tower as well, telling the story of the refugees whose descendants still worship in the church below. Three centuries on, a French dome that is not a dome sits on a square named for a regiment of gendarmes, in a city that took the exiles in and made a landmark of them.

## Flight Context

The Französischer Dom stands at 52.5144° N, 13.3922° E on the north side of Gendarmenmarkt in Berlin-Mitte, about 600 m south of Unter den Linden. From the air the square is one of the easiest targets in central Berlin: three classical buildings in a tight rectangle of open paving, with two near-identical domed towers standing at opposite ends and the columned portico of the Konzerthaus centred between them. The French dome is the northern of the pair. Best viewed from 2,000-3,000 ft AGL on a north-south track, which brings Museum Island, Unter den Linden, Gendarmenmarkt and Checkpoint Charlie into line within about two kilometres. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) lies roughly 22 km southeast; Schönhagen (EDAZ) is the nearest general-aviation field to the southwest and Strausberg (EDAY) to the east. Central Berlin is controlled airspace requiring prior coordination for low-level VFR flight. The copper domes read green in clear light and flatten out badly under the winter overcast common from November through February.
