# Berlin State Opera

Frederick the Great commissioned it in 1741. Furtwängler conducted in it under the Nazis. Daniel Barenboim led it for thirty-one years. The Linden Opera has weathered every German regime since Bach was alive.

**Location:** 52.5167, 13.3947

**Categories:** opera, music, Berlin, architecture, history, Frederick the Great

## Introduction

Frederick II had been King of Prussia for barely a year when building began. He was twenty-nine years old, in the middle of the First Silesian War with Austria, and he wanted Berlin to have an opera house grander than anything else north of the Alps — freestanding in its own square, faced like a Roman temple, the first part of a Forum Fredericianum that would announce the Hohenzollerns as patrons of the Enlightenment. Construction began in July 1741. The Hofoper opened on 7 December 1742 with Carl Heinrich Graun's Cesare e Cleopatra, even though the building was not entirely finished. The same Staatskapelle orchestra that played that night still plays there today. Its institutional roots go back to the sixteenth century. The house is often called the world's oldest state opera.

## A Palladian Idea in Berlin

Georg Wenzeslaus von Knobelsdorff designed the building, but the north and west façades are direct copies of Colen Campbell's elevations for the English country houses Stourhead and Wanstead. This made the Linden Opera not just architecturally significant in Germany but a deliberate import: Frederick was telling his subjects that Prussia would be sophisticated, cosmopolitan, plugged into the high architectural conversation of Europe. The building also had a quietly radical idea inside it. Earlier opera houses had been part of palaces, attached to courts. This one stood by itself in the open square. It was, as architectural historians often note, the first theater anywhere designed to be its own freestanding monumental building in a city. The opera was no longer a private royal entertainment. It was civic infrastructure.

## Burned, Bombed, Burned Again

Fire took the building on 18 August 1843. Carl Ferdinand Langhans rebuilt it. The Royal Opera House reopened the following year with Meyerbeer's Ein Feldlager in Schlesien. Then on the night of 9–10 April 1941, an RAF raid destroyed the interior. Hitler ordered immediate reconstruction despite wartime shortages — a propaganda gesture about the cultural permanence of the regime — and it reopened in December 1942 with Furtwängler conducting Die Meistersinger for the building's 200th anniversary. On 3 February 1945, three more bombs hit. This time most of the structure was gone, leaving only the Unter den Linden façade. The second postwar reconstruction would take a decade.

## The Conductors and the Regimes

The roll of musical leadership reads like a survey of German cultural history. Felix Mendelssohn conducted symphonic concerts here in the 1840s. Richard Strauss directed from 1899 to 1913. In the 1920s, Erich Kleiber gave the world premiere of Alban Berg's Wozzeck in 1925, with the composer present. Then in 1934, when Kleiber tried to perform symphonic excerpts from Berg's Lulu, the Nazi cultural apparatus erupted; Kleiber resigned and went into exile. Jewish musicians were dismissed from the ensemble. Otto Klemperer, Kurt Adler, and Fritz Busch all left Germany. Herbert von Karajan led the house from 1941 to 1945 — his ambitions and his Nazi Party membership both intact. The building reopened after the war as East Germany's state opera in 1955, again with Die Meistersinger.

## The Barenboim Era

Daniel Barenboim, born in Buenos Aires to Russian-Jewish parents who emigrated to Israel when he was nine, was appointed Generalmusikdirektor in 1992 — three years after the Berlin Wall fell, when the Linden Opera was rejoining the operatic world after four decades of Cold War isolation. In 2000 the orchestra elected him conductor for life. His tenure stretched thirty-one years, longer than any predecessor in the modern era. He led complete Wagner Ring cycles, while the house also staged baroque rediscoveries with René Jacobs and the Akademie für Alte Musik. He oversaw the 2009–2017 renovation by HG Merz that raised the roof, lengthened the proscenium, and brought capacity to 1,350 seats. In January 2023, citing health reasons, he stepped down. Christian Thielemann, who had stepped in as an emergency replacement during a Ring production, succeeded him as GMD on 1 September 2024.

## What Plays Tonight

Walk past on any evening Unter den Linden, in front of the Forum Fredericianum that Frederick the Great planned but never finished, and the building still does what it was built to do. The Staatskapelle still plays in the pit. The boxes still curve around an auditorium that has been rebuilt twice. A school class from a Berlin gymnasium might be in the cheap seats hearing Mozart for the first time. A retired couple from Hamburg might be in the parquet for Wagner. The repertoire spans baroque revivals to contemporary commissions. More than two hundred eighty years after Carl Heinrich Graun's opera opened the house, the Linden Opera is still the same orchestra in continuous service. Few institutions in Berlin can claim that.

## Flight Context

The opera house sits at 52.517°N, 13.395°E on Unter den Linden in central Berlin's Mitte district, on the south side of the boulevard at Bebelplatz (the former Forum Fredericianum). Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is about 18 km south-southeast. From cruising altitude look for the broad east-west axis of Unter den Linden running from the Brandenburg Gate to the Spree; the opera is the prominent classical building south of the boulevard, across from the Humboldt University main building.
