# Berlin Tempelhof Airport

The Nazi-era terminal designed to look like an eagle in flight became, instead, the place that saved West Berlin during the airlift. Today it is a park, a refugee shelter, and a stage for Formula E.

**Location:** 52.4731, 13.4039

**Categories:** airports, aviation, Berlin, history, Berlin Airlift, architecture

## Introduction

Norman Foster called Tempelhof 'the mother of all airports.' The British architect was right about the building's influence and a little wrong about whose mother it was. The 1.2-kilometer arc of shell limestone that Ernst Sagebiel designed for the Reich in the 1930s was meant to be the gateway to Hitler's Germania — an airport whose semicircular hangars, seen from above, would form the wings of an eagle in flight. The eagle was never finished. Construction started in 1936 and was still incomplete when the Soviets took the building during the Battle of Berlin in late April 1945. Three years later, the Allies were flying coal in through the same hangars, and Tempelhof became something its designers never imagined: the place that kept West Berlin alive.

## The Knights Templar to the Wright Brothers

The name is medieval. The land belonged in the thirteenth century to the Knights Templar — the Tempelhof, Temple Court — and after the order's suppression it passed through various owners until the Prussian army took it as a parade ground in 1720. In 1909 the French aviator Armand Zipfel made the first flight demonstration here, and Orville Wright followed later the same year. The airport was officially designated in 1923. Deutsche Luft Hansa, the predecessor of today's Lufthansa, was founded at Tempelhof on 6 January 1926. By the late 1930s it was one of Europe's three iconic prewar airports, alongside London's Croydon and the old Paris–Le Bourget.

## Sagebiel's Eagle

Hitler ordered Sagebiel to replace the 1929 terminal with something monumental. The new building, constructed between 1936 and 1941 but never quite finished, was intended to be among the largest buildings on Earth — and was, for a time. The canopy roof over the apron was a stunning piece of engineering: passengers walked from the terminal to their plane without ever stepping into rain. A one-mile hangar roof was supposed to form a tiered stadium for spectators at air shows for the master race. Inside, the building had its own railway station for direct U-Bahn access via the U6 line. It was less an airport than a propaganda stage. Most of it never operated as designed. The 1927 terminal kept handling traffic until 24 April 1945.

## Operation Vittles

On 20 June 1948, the Soviets cut every road and rail line into West Berlin. The Allied response began six days later, when American C-47 Skytrains delivered 80 tons of food into Tempelhof — far short of the 4,500 tons per day the city needed. Within months the operation scaled up dramatically. C-54 Skymasters landed every few minutes around the clock; the Combined Airlift Task Force was set up at Tempelhof under Major General William Tunner; American engineers laid two new 6,000-foot runways in the summer and fall of 1948. By the time the blockade ended in May 1949, the Western Allies had flown more than 2.3 million tons of supplies into the city. Seventy pilots — 39 British and 31 American — died in the operation. A monument outside the terminal lists all of their names.

## Pan Am, Karajan, and Closure

After the war, Tempelhof was the Western world's window on Berlin. American Overseas Airlines launched the first postwar commercial flight from New York via Shannon, Amsterdam, and Frankfurt on 18 May 1946. Pan Am acquired AOA in 1950 and made Tempelhof its German hub. By 1971 the airport handled more than five million passengers a year, the second-busiest in Germany. The runways, however, were too short for jets without thrust reversers — Air France moved to Tegel in 1960 because its Caravelles couldn't safely land. Pan Am introduced 727s in 1966 and managed. By the late 1970s, with the new Tegel terminal open and reunification still a decade away, Tempelhof's commercial role was fading. A non-binding 2008 referendum to save it failed to reach the required quorum. The last commercial flight, a Cirrus Airlines Dornier 328 to Mannheim, departed at 22:17 on 30 October 2008. Just before midnight, a Junkers Ju 52 and a 'raisin bomber' DC-3 took off in parallel, dipped their wings, and flew off to Schönefeld.

## Park, Shelter, Racetrack

The airfield reopened in May 2010 as Tempelhofer Feld, Berlin's largest public park. More than 200,000 Berliners showed up that opening weekend to bike, kite, skate, and stretch out on a runway 2.4 kilometers long. In 2014 a citizens' referendum permanently blocked any construction on the field — Berliners voted to keep their open space open. About 80% of the former airfield is now a protected habitat for skylarks and other red-listed species; large sections close seasonally for breeding. Since 2015, two former hangars have housed thousands of refugees, first from Syria, more recently from Ukraine. Formula E races run on a circuit laid out across the runways. The Sagebiel terminal — once meant to be the gateway to a thousand-year Reich — now hosts trade fairs, music festivals, and a long-running argument about what to do with one of the largest single buildings in the world.

## Flight Context

Tempelhof is at 52.473°N, 13.404°E in the Tempelhof-Schöneberg district of south-central Berlin, 5 km south of the city center. The airport closed to flights in 2008 (former ICAO EDDI). Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is 12 km southeast. From cruising altitude the site is unmistakable: a vast oval of grass with the long curved limestone terminal building forming the northwest perimeter and two parallel former runways crossing the green. The U6 U-Bahn line still runs to Platz der Luftbrücke station outside the main entrance.
