# Berlin Tempelhof Airport

The Nazi-era terminal designed to look like an eagle in flight was built on a cleared concentration camp and worked by forced labour — then became the place that kept West Berlin alive during the airlift.

**Location:** 52.4731, 13.4039

**Categories:** airports, aviation, Berlin, history, Berlin Airlift, architecture

## Introduction

Norman Foster called Tempelhof 'the mother of all airports.' The 1.2-kilometre arc of shell limestone that Ernst Sagebiel designed for the Reich in the 1930s was meant to be the gateway to Hitler's Germania — an airport whose semicircular hangars, seen from above, would form the wings of an eagle in flight. The eagle was never finished. Construction began in 1936 and was still incomplete when Soviet troops took the building during the Battle of Berlin at the end of April 1945. Three years after that, the Allies were flying coal in through the same hangars, and Tempelhof became the one thing its designers had never imagined: the building that kept West Berlin alive.

## Temple Court to Sagebiel's Eagle

The name is medieval: the land belonged in the thirteenth century to the Knights Templar — Tempelhof, Temple Court — and after the order's suppression the Prussian army eventually claimed it as a parade ground, in 1720. Armand Zipfel flew a demonstration here in 1909, and Orville Wright followed later that year. The field was designated an airport in 1923, and Deutsche Luft Hansa was founded on it on 6 January 1926. Then, in 1934, Ernst Sagebiel was ordered to replace the modest terminal with something monumental. What he raised between 1936 and 1941, and never quite finished, was intended to be among the largest structures on Earth, and was. Its canopy roof carried passengers from terminal to aircraft without a step into the rain. A mile of hangar roof was to have been tiered into a stadium for spectators at air and ground demonstrations, which tells you what the place was actually for. It was less an airport than a parade ground with runways attached.

## Columbia-Haus

The expansion had to clear the ground first, and one of the things standing on that ground was a camp. A former military prison on Tempelhof Field took its first prisoners in July 1933; from December 1934 it came under the Inspectorate of Concentration Camps as the Columbia concentration camp. It was closed in autumn 1936 to make room for the airfield's enlargement, and the men held there were moved to the newly built camp at Sachsenhausen, north of the city. The site drew forced labour back to it during the war. Prisoners of war and civilians conscripted from Poland, Czechoslovakia, and the Soviet Union lived in barracks at the northern end of the field, in two camps — one run by Lufthansa, one by the aircraft manufacturer Weser Flugzeugbau — whose sanitary conditions later excavation has documented in unsparing detail. They assembled aircraft in the hangars: parts arrived from across Berlin, and the finished machines were flown out over their heads.

## Operation Vittles

On 20 June 1948, the Soviets cut every road and rail line into West Berlin. The Allied response began six days later, when American C-47 Skytrains delivered 80 tons of food into Tempelhof — far short of the 4,500 tons per day the city needed. Within months the operation scaled up dramatically. C-54 Skymasters landed every few minutes around the clock; the Combined Airlift Task Force was set up at Tempelhof under Major General William Tunner; American engineers laid two new 6,000-foot runways in the summer and fall of 1948. By the time the blockade ended in May 1949, the Western Allies had flown more than 2.3 million tons of supplies into the city. Seventy pilots — 39 British and 31 American — died in the operation. A monument outside the terminal lists all of their names.

## Dinner or Jet?

After the war, Tempelhof was the Western world's window on Berlin. American Overseas Airlines flew the first postwar commercial service in from New York on 18 May 1946; Pan Am bought AOA in 1950 and made the airport its German hub. The short runways were the constant problem: Air France gave up and moved to Tegel in 1960 because its Caravelles could not land with a full payload, while Pan Am's 727s, introduced in 1966, could. BEA fought back with fewer seats and better food, and the Berlin press named the contest Dinner oder Düsen? — dinner or jets? The jets won. Traffic peaked in 1971 at more than five million passengers, over ninety percent of West Berlin's total, and then declined for thirty-seven years. A 2008 referendum to save the airport drew a clear majority of those who voted and nowhere near the required quorum. Four and a half minutes before midnight on 30 October, a Junkers Ju 52 and an airlift DC-3 took off in parallel, waved their wings, and flew away to Schönefeld. The field lights went out at midnight.

## Park, Shelter, Racetrack

The airfield reopened in May 2010 as Tempelhofer Feld, Berlin's largest public park. More than 200,000 people turned out that opening weekend to bike, kite, skate, and lie down on a runway two kilometres long, and in 2014 a citizens' referendum permanently blocked construction on it. Around eighty percent is now protected habitat for skylarks and other red-listed species, closed off seasonally for breeding. Two hangars served from 2015 as an emergency shelter, housing some 2,500 refugees at its peak. Formula E races across the taxiways. And the Sagebiel terminal — designed as the gateway to a thousand-year Reich, expanded over a closed concentration camp, redeemed by an airlift it was never meant to serve — hosts trade fairs, music festivals, and a permanent argument about what a city does with one of the largest buildings on Earth.

## Flight Context

Tempelhof is at 52.473°N, 13.404°E in the Tempelhof-Schöneberg district of south-central Berlin, 5 km south of the city center. The airport closed to flights in 2008 (former ICAO EDDI). Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is 12 km southeast. From cruising altitude the site is unmistakable: a vast oval of grass with the long curved limestone terminal building forming the northwest perimeter and two parallel former runways crossing the green. The U6 U-Bahn line still runs to Platz der Luftbrücke station outside the main entrance.
