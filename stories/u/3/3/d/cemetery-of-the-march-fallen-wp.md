# Cemetery of the March Fallen

Berlin's workshops emptied onto the barricades in March 1848 and buried their dead on a hill in Friedrichshain. Seventy years later, the city opened the same ground again.

**Location:** 52.5244, 13.4364

**Categories:** Berlin, Memorials, Revolution, 19th century, Cemeteries, Germany

## Introduction

The register of Berlin's dead of March 1848 reads like a directory of the city's trades. Of roughly 270 names, 157 belonged to craftspeople — 115 journeymen, 29 masters, 13 apprentices. Fifty-two were labourers, thirty-four servants, fifteen from the educated classes, four from the nobility. Eleven were women and four were children; the youngest was twelve, the oldest a labourer of seventy-four. These are the people who built the barricades across Berlin on 18 March 1848 and were shot off them by the king's soldiers. They lie on the highest ground in what is now the Volkspark Friedrichshain.

## The King on the Balcony

The city council voted to create the cemetery on 21 March, the day before the funeral. The plan was to bury the fallen soldiers here too, beside the civilians they had been ordered to fire on; the army refused to hand its dead over.

On 22 March Berlin buried the rest. The coffins lay on the Gendarmenmarkt dressed with flowers cut from the royal garden, and the city was hung in black, red and gold. Around a hundred thousand people came. Outside the Neue Kirche three men spoke in succession: the Protestant preacher Adolf Sydow, the Catholic chaplain Johann Nepomuk Ruhland of St Hedwig's, and the rabbi Michael Sachs — a sequence the Vossische Zeitung called unprecedented. The procession ran seven and a half kilometres, took four hours, and carried the banners of Berlin's trades. There were almost no medals and no uniforms. Crossing the Schlossplatz, King Friedrich Wilhelm IV stepped onto his balcony and removed his helmet, as agreed beforehand.

## Ernst Zinna, Locksmith

One hundred and eighty-three civilians went into the ground that day on the Lindenberg, a hill locals knew as the Kanonenberg, in a park then still unfinished. Over the following weeks the wounded kept dying and the graves rose to 254.

Among the first was Ernst Zinna, a journeyman locksmith born in 1830. Two years earlier the Prussian state had given him a lifesaving medal for pulling people out of the water. On 18 March he fought at the corner of Friedrichstrasse and Jägerstrasse, where troops shot him; he died at the Charité next day, still a teenager. He became a fixture of German democratic memory, with GDR schools named for him. On the day he was buried he was a boy who had made locks for a living.

## A Hedge of Thorns

The state understood at once what the graves were. From 1850 the Prussian State Ministry banned entry on 18 March, with police cordons on the approaches. Berliners came anyway, laid wreaths against the barriers, and commemorated in the beer gardens nearby. In 1851 the day ended in violence with casualties; in 1852, with the paths planted over to make them impassable, ten thousand still arrived. From 1853 the whole park was fenced.

Removal was the other tactic. A station was announced for the site twice, never built; a proposed orphanage was rejected in 1853 because children who saw the graves daily might be reminded of the revolution. In October 1856 Berlin's chief of police asked the magistrate to plant a thorn hedge around them, so the site might fall into oblivion. The magistrate refused. Coffins were quietly dug up in 1858, though never all, and on 15 May 1861 the cemetery reopened without restriction.

## The Second Set of Graves

In December 1918 the ground was opened for a second revolution. Fighting between the 6th and the 11th killed sixteen revolutionaries near Invalidenstrasse, among them members of the Red Soldiers' League, buried here on 21 December. On Christmas Eve government troops attacked the People's Naval Division quartered in the Berlin City Palace; eleven sailors died defending it and went into a third pit on 29 December. Erich Habersaath, shot on 9 November, had been the revolution's first Berlin casualty.

When the KPD and USPD asked that January to bury thirty-one dead of the Spartacus Uprising here, Karl Liebknecht among them, the magistrate refused; those graves are at Friedrichsfelde. In October 1925 Ludwig Hoffmann, Berlin's former city architect, gave the cemetery a new iron gate — his last completed work — with Thanatos kneeling on each pillar over a lowered torch. Under the Nazis the place was left to neglect: honouring these dead in public could cost you your freedom, and even the regime's opponents stayed away.

## One Stone for Both

On 18 March 1948, the centenary, a memorial stone went up in the middle of the ground. Its back carries 249 names and the words "an unknown." Its front carries Peter A. Steinhoff's text: you erected the memorial yourselves, and only a warning speaks from this stone — that our people will never renounce the cause you died for, to be united and free. A redesign in 1956–57 added three gravestones for the dead of 1918, and in 1960 Hans Kies's over-life-size bronze of an armed sailor went up on the wall's southwest corner.

From 1979 a West Berlin initiative, Aktion 18. März, laid a wreath here annually — disliked and tolerated by the GDR authorities. Since 1992 the ceremony has been shared, with the President of the Berlin House of Representatives laying the wreath. Two hundred and fifty-five March dead and thirty-three of the November dead lie inside a low stone wall, among eighteen stone slabs, three iron crosses, a stele and two cast-iron monuments — all that survived.

## Flight Context

The cemetery lies at 52.524°N 13.436°E, in the southern corner of the Volkspark Friedrichshain, about 1.6 km east-northeast of Alexanderplatz. From 2,000–3,000 ft AGL the park is the obvious feature: roughly half a square kilometre of woodland in an otherwise unbroken grid of apartment blocks, with two artificial hills raised from wartime rubble standing well above the surrounding roofline. The cemetery itself is small and hard to pick out — a walled rectangle at the park's southern edge, separated from the rest by Ernst-Zinna-Weg, the access road to the Friedrichshain hospital whose long ward blocks sit immediately south. Use the Fernsehturm at Alexanderplatz as the reference and track east-northeast; Karl-Marx-Allee runs parallel about 700 m to the south. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is roughly 19 km south-southeast; Strausberg (EDAY) lies about 30 km east and Schönhagen (EDAZ) southwest. The area sits under the Berlin CTR and TMA. In winter the bare canopy makes the walled plot far easier to see; in high summer it disappears entirely under the trees.
