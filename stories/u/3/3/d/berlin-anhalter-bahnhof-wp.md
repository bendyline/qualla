# Berlin Anhalter Bahnhof

Once Continental Europe's largest railway terminus and Berlin's gateway to Rome, Vienna and Athens, the Anhalter Bahnhof was demolished in three days in 1960, leaving a fragment of façade standing alone in an empty square.

**Location:** 52.5031, 13.3819

**Categories:** berlin, germany, kreuzberg, railway-history, world-war-ii, holocaust-memory, architecture, ruins

## Introduction

It took three days to destroy. Demolition of the Anhalter Bahnhof began on 25 August 1960 and was finished by the 27th, over considerable public protest, and what the crews left behind was the middle section of the front wall — a brick arch with a clock face and no clock, standing by itself on Askanischer Platz with nothing behind it. Eighty years earlier, on 15 June 1880, Kaiser Wilhelm I and Otto von Bismarck had come to open the building this fragment belonged to: the largest railway terminus in Germany and, at the time, in Continental Europe. Behind that arch there is now a sports pitch.

## Gateway to the South

Work began on 15 April 1839 and the first station opened on 1 July 1841, running as far as Jüterbog behind the very first locomotive that August Borsig ever built. The line ran through the historical state of Anhalt, which is where both it and the terminus got their name, and it kept extending — Dessau, Köthen, then Leipzig, Frankfurt am Main, Munich.

The original building was modest, its platform just fifteen metres wide, and by the 1870s it was hopeless. Franz Heinrich Schwechten's replacement, begun in 1876, was on another scale entirely. The façade ran 101 metres, with Ludwig Brunow's zinc figures of Day and Night flanking the clock. Inside were separate waiting rooms for four classes of ticket and a private entrance for visiting royalty, which saw regular use. Heinrich Seidel's iron and glass train shed measured 171 by 62 metres and rose 34 metres at the centre — 10,600 square metres of roof, under which forty thousand people could stand. In 1882 the terminus absorbed the Dresden services too, and with them Prague, Vienna, and destinations as distant as Rome, Naples and Athens. Berliners called it the Gateway to the South.

## Forty-Four Thousand a Day

By the 1930s trains left its six platforms every three to five minutes, carrying an average of 44,000 people a day — around sixteen million a year, against barely 49,000 passing through Berlin Tempelhof Airport. Across Askanischer Platz stood the Hotel Excelsior, the largest in Europe, joined to the station in 1927–28 by a pedestrian tunnel a hundred metres long, believed to be the longest of its kind anywhere.

None of that saved it on paper. Albert Speer's plan to rebuild Berlin as Germania put a triumphal north-south axis straight across the Anhalter's tracks. Long-distance traffic was to be pushed out to two enormous new stations on the Ringbahn, and the terminus itself converted into a public swimming pool.

## On the Regular Timetable

Between 1941 and 1945 the Anhalter Bahnhof was one of three Berlin stations used to deport the city's Jews. About 55,000 Berliners were taken from the three of them — roughly a third of the Jewish population the city had in 1933. From this station 9,600 people were deported, in groups of fifty to a hundred at a time, on 116 separate trains, all of them bound for Theresienstadt in occupied Czechoslovakia and from there to the camps further east.

The detail that matters here is how it was done. Elsewhere the deportations used freight wagons. From the Anhalter Bahnhof, people were put into ordinary passenger coaches, which were then coupled to scheduled trains running to the normal published timetable. Families with their luggage were loaded, fifty or a hundred at a time, into carriages attached to the express to Prague, in a station handling forty thousand travellers a day. Nothing was hidden. The neighbours who used this station every morning would have walked past it. A memorial board on the site today names what happened here.

## Water in the Tunnels

A massive raid on the night of 23 November 1943 wrecked the station and the rail infrastructure beyond it so thoroughly that only a few local services could run. Two more, on 3 and 26 February 1945, left the roof half gone, the rest unsafe, and no trains moving at all.

The worst thing to happen here was not aimed at the station. A new S-Bahn tunnel had opened beneath it in 1939, part of the North-South Link, and by late April 1945 those tunnels were full of civilians sheltering and wounded soldiers in hospital trains parked in underground sidings. On 2 May, with Hitler dead and the Soviets in the city, the remaining Nazi leadership ordered SS troops to blow the bulkheads where the tunnel passed under the Landwehrkanal, on the theory that flooding it would slow the Red Army. Up to 26 kilometres of tunnel and many stations went under. How many drowned has never been established: Soviet propaganda claimed as many as 15,000, more conservative estimates 200 to 300. The gap between those figures is itself part of what was lost.

## What Is Standing There Now

The end came from politics rather than bombs. The Anhalter Bahnhof stood in West Berlin but its trains came from the Soviet zone, and on 17 May 1952 the East German authorities simply switched them all to the Ostbahnhof. With no services, the terminus closed. It sat derelict for eight years, tracks and signals still in place, until the demolition crews arrived.

Day and Night on top of the surviving fragment are copies now, the weathered originals having gone to the German Museum of Technology across the Landwehr Canal. Where the train shed stood there is a playing field, and beyond it the Tempodrom concert venue, opened on 8 December 2001 under a peaked roof rising 37.5 metres. Walk south through the woodland toward the canal and crumbling sections of platform are still visible in the undergrowth. Behind the portico an Exilmuseum is planned, a privately funded initiative begun by the Nobel laureate Herta Müller and the Berlin art dealer Bernd Schultz, for the stories of those who fled Nazi Germany — many of them from this station. The S-Bahn platform below still carries the name: sealed off during the Wall years, refurbished and reopened on 1 March 1992, it remains the only thing here that is still, in any working sense, a railway station.

## Flight Context

The site sits at 52.5031 N, 13.3819 E in Berlin-Kreuzberg, roughly 700 m southeast of Potsdamer Platz. From the air the giveaway is the Tempodrom: a white peaked tent-like roof, 37.5 m at its apex, unlike anything around it, with the green rectangle of the former train shed site immediately north of it and the lone façade fragment facing Askanischer Platz beyond that. The Landwehr Canal runs east-west about 500 m south, with the German Museum of Technology's rooftop C-47 — an aircraft mounted on the building — on its far bank, one of the most distinctive small landmarks in Berlin. Potsdamer Platz's cluster of towers is 1 km northwest, the Tiergarten beyond that, and Tempelhof's unmistakable curved apron and 1.2 km terminal arc about 4 km southeast. This is dense controlled airspace over central Berlin; plan any low pass at 1,500-2,500 ft AGL, ideally tracking the canal, and expect clearance limits. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) lies about 16 km southeast; Schönhagen (EDAZ) about 35 km south-southwest. Low winter sun from the south throws the façade fragment's shadow across the open ground, which is the easiest way to pick it out from altitude.
