# Käthe Kollwitz

She lost a son to one war and a grandson to the next, and spent fifty years making the most unsparing images of grief in German art.

**Location:** 52.5364, 13.4174

**Categories:** Art, Artists, Berlin, World War I, World War II, Memorials

## Introduction

In an empty stone room on Unter den Linden, a bronze woman sits on the floor with her dead son collapsed into her lap. There is a round opening in the roof directly above her, so rain falls on the two of them, and snow, and whatever else Berlin does in February. Käthe Kollwitz had been dead almost fifty years when Helmut Kohl personally suggested enlarging her small original and installing it here in 1993, at the centre of the national memorial to the victims of war and tyranny. The choice was argued over then and is argued over now. What nobody disputed was that the sculptor had earned the commission the hard way.

## A Grandfather Who Was Thrown Out

She was born Käthe Schmidt in Königsberg in 1867, the fifth child of a mason and house builder who was also a Social Democrat. Her mother's father, Julius Rupp, had been expelled from the Prussian state church and had gone off to found his own congregation. Religion and socialism reached her in the same lessons, from the same grandfather, and never entirely separated afterwards.

Her father arranged drawing lessons in August 1879, when she was twelve. By sixteen she was sketching the labourers and peasants who came through his offices. She studied under Karl Stauffer-Bern at the School for Women Artists in Berlin, then painting in Munich, and in 1890 rented her first studio back in Königsberg. In 1891 she married Karl Kollwitz, a doctor who treated the poor, and moved into the Berlin apartment where she would live for fifty-two years.

## Weavers and Peasants

Between the births of her sons - Hans in 1892, Peter in 1896 - she saw Gerhart Hauptmann's play The Weavers, about the Silesian weavers' failed revolt of 1844, and abandoned the etchings she had been making for Zola's Germinal. The resulting cycle of six prints - three lithographs and three etchings, from Poverty to The End - made her reputation. It illustrates nothing. Her weavers are neither noble nor pitiable, simply people whose courage is not going to be enough.

The Peasant War came next, six years of drawing and discarding between 1902 and 1908, drawn from the German uprising of 1524-25. Kollwitz identified with Black Anna, a woman named among the rebels, and the etching Outbreak won her the Villa Romana Prize and a year in Florence.

## October 1914

Peter Kollwitz was eighteen. He volunteered when the war began and was killed in Flanders in October 1914, near Diksmuide, within weeks of reaching the front. By the end of that year his mother had drawings for a monument to him and his dead friends.

Then she could not do it. She destroyed the first version in 1919 and started over in 1925, and the two figures she finally arrived at - a father and a mother, kneeling, wearing Karl's face and her own - were installed in the Belgian cemetery at Roggevelde in 1932, eighteen years after the death they were made for. When Peter's grave was moved, The Grieving Parents went with him; they kneel now at Vladslo German war cemetery. There is no allegory in them. Two people are on their knees in a field of graves, and one of the graves is their son's.

## Professor, Then Not

In 1919 she became the first woman appointed professor at the Prussian Academy of Arts - an income, a large studio and a title. She spent it on posters. Germany's Children Starving, Bread and Never Again War all date from 1924, and each borrows the visual grammar of recruiting material to argue the opposite case. She had found woodcut by then, after seeing Ernst Barlach's prints - a medium blunt enough for what she needed to say. Years earlier, when a public appeal went out for old men and boys to fill the lines, she had answered in print: "There has been enough of dying! Let not another man fall!"

In 1933 she signed the Dringender Appell, the urgent call for the German left to unite against the Nazis. The new government forced her out of the Academy, pulled her work from museums, and banned her from exhibiting - then lifted one of her mother-and-child images for its own propaganda, caring nothing for what she had meant by it. She was too well known abroad to arrest. On her seventieth birthday more than 150 telegrams arrived from the art world, along with offers of refuge in the United States. She declined, afraid of what would be done to her family.

## Seed Corn

Karl died in 1940. Her eldest grandson - also called Peter - was killed in action in Russia in 1942. That same year she made a lithograph titled Seed Corn Must Not Be Ground, reusing the phrase she had thrown at the poet Richard Dehmel in 1918, when he called for more soldiers. The image is a mother with her arms flung over three children, holding them in.

She was evacuated from Berlin in 1943. Months later the apartment was bombed and much of her life's paper went with it. She spent her final months as a guest of Prince Ernst Heinrich of Saxony at Moritzburg, near Dresden, and died on 22 April 1945, sixteen days before Germany surrendered. Four museums are devoted to her now, and more than forty German schools carry her name. Gustav Seitz's statue of her has stood since 1960 in Kollwitzplatz, a short walk from the flat where she made nearly all of it.

## Flight Context

52.5364 degrees N, 13.4174 degrees E, at Kollwitzplatz in Prenzlauer Berg, about 2 km northeast of Museum Island. From the air the district reads as a dense grid of five-storey courtyard blocks with green squares punched out of it; Kollwitzplatz is the small triangular park where Knaackstrasse, Wörther Strasse and Kollwitzstrasse converge, and the round brick water tower a block to the south is the surest landmark. The Fernsehturm stands roughly 2 nm southwest and orients everything. Berlin's central airspace is tightly controlled, with restrictions over the government quarter to the west, so file for a transit rather than a hold; Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) lies about 12 nm south-southeast and Strausberg (EDAY) about 16 nm east. At 2,000 ft in clear air you can see both ends of her life's geography at once - the Prenzlauer Berg streets she drew, and Unter den Linden to the southwest, where the enlarged pietà sits under its open roof.
