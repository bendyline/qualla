# Trams in Berlin

The world's first electric tramway ran in a Berlin suburb in 1881, and the network's modern map still traces the line of the Berlin Wall almost exactly.

**Location:** 52.5370, 13.4837

**Categories:** Berlin, Transport, Railways, History of Berlin, Engineering

## Introduction

There is a turning loop by the Kupfergraben, across the water from Museum Island, where trams have been reversing since 28 August 1865. Horse trams reached it that summer; electric trams reach it now; in between the city was bombed flat, cut in two, walled, and stitched back together. Berlin's tram network is one of the oldest on earth and, at 193 kilometres and 226 million passengers a year, the third largest after Melbourne and St Petersburg. It is also a map of the twentieth century that nobody meant to draw. Look at where the lines stop in the west and you are looking at a border that was dismantled in 1989.

## 22 June 1865

Berlin had the traffic problem before it had the solution. Simon Kremser started a scheduled horse-bus between the Brandenburg Gate and Charlottenburg in 1825; Israel Moses Henoch, who had been running the city's cab service since 1815, opened the first bus line inside Berlin in 1840, from Alexanderplatz to the Potsdamer Bahnhof. By 1864 there were thirty-six bus companies competing on the same streets. Then on 22 June 1865 the first horse tramway in Germany opened, running from the Brandenburg Gate out along what is now the Straße des 17. Juni to Charlottenburg. Rails changed the arithmetic: a horse on smooth iron hauls far more than the same horse on cobbles. Within a decade companies were laying track in every direction. Two months after the opening, the line was extended along Dorotheenstraße to the Kupfergraben. That terminus never closed.

## An Elevated Railway, Laid Flat

On 16 May 1881, in the village of Groß-Lichterfelde south of the city, Werner von Siemens opened the world's first electric tramway. It ran from the suburban station at Lichterfelde Ost to the cadet school, and Siemens did not really want it there: he described it as an elevated line taken down off its pillars and girders, because what he actually wanted to build was an elevated electric network across Berlin. The town council said no. He got his elevated line in 1902, twenty-one years late. In the meantime the flat version conquered the city — overhead wires from the 1890s, near-complete electrification by 1902, and the last horse tram gone by 1910. There were tunnels too. From 28 December 1899 trams ran under the Spree between Stralau and Treptow until structural problems closed the crossing in 1932, and from 1916 to 1951 a second tunnel carried them beneath Unter den Linden.

## Peak, and Amputation

The private companies consolidated into one municipal operator, the BVG, in 1929. On its first day the BVG ran 89 tram lines over 634 kilometres of network, with more than 4,000 tramcars and over 14,400 employees; the average car covered 42,500 kilometres a year, and the system carried 929 million passengers that year alone. By 1930 the route length passed 630 kilometres across more than 90 lines, and that was the summit. The decline began almost immediately, and it began with politics as much as economics: on 31 October 1934 the oldest tram line in Germany was closed because Nazi planners were rebuilding the Charlottenburger Chaussee as a monumental east–west axis, and a tramway did not suit the vision. By 1938 the system was down to 71 lines and 2,800 cars. Bombing from March 1943 did the rest.

## Two Cities, Two Answers

On 1 August 1949 the BVG split with the city: 36 tram lines in the western sectors, 13 in the Soviet sector. Cross-border services were cut on 15 January 1953. Then the two halves made opposite decisions. West Berlin resolved from 1954 to abolish trams entirely as old-fashioned, replacing them with buses and U-Bahn extensions; by 1962 only 18 of the original 36 lines survived, and on 2 October 1967 the last tramcar in West Berlin ran the number 55 from Zoo Station out through Charlottenburg and Siemensdamm to Hakenfelde in Spandau. East Berlin closed its city-centre lines that same year, for the redevelopment of Alexanderplatz, but never seriously considered scrapping the network. Instead it extended it — new track running out through the 1970s and 1980s to the vast new housing estates at Marzahn, Hohenschönhausen and Hellersdorf, which is why those districts have trams and Charlottenburg does not.

## The Map Still Shows the Wall

The BVG absorbed its eastern counterpart in 1992 and inherited a network almost entirely on one side of a border that no longer existed. Undoing that has been slow. In 1995 track pushed west along Bornholmer Straße in two stages, reconnecting the Virchow hospital and the U-Bahn stations at Seestraße and Osloer Straße. Trams returned to Friedrichstraße station in 1997 and to Alexanderplatz in 1998. The MetroTram concept arrived on 12 December 2004, putting an M in front of the nine busiest lines and running them around the clock. The M10 crept along Bernauer Straße — the street the Wall ran down — to Nordbahnhof in 2006 and on to the Hauptbahnhof in 2015. In 2023 the whole system's supply voltage was raised from 600 to 750 volts. Twenty-two lines now serve more than 800 stops, and on a network map the western half of the city is still very nearly blank.

## Flight Context

The reference coordinates for the network sit at 52.537°N, 13.484°E, in the north-eastern inner city near the Landsberger Allee corridor — one of the busiest tram axes in Berlin. From 2,000–4,000 ft the tram network is genuinely legible from the air on a clear day: look for the broad radial boulevards running north-east and east out of the centre, each with a reserved central strip, and for the seven depots, which show as fans of parallel track beside large sheds. Marzahn's depot sits on the south side of Landsberger Allee east of Blumberger Damm; Lichtenberg's is north of the Lichtenberg station complex. The Fernsehturm, about 5 km west-south-west, anchors any run-in over the eastern districts. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) lies roughly 20 km almost due south, Strausberg (EDAY) about 30 km east-north-east, Schönhagen (EDAZ) some 43 km south-south-west. All of this is inside EDDB terminal airspace. November to February brings persistent haze and low stratus.
