# Deutsches Currywurst Museum

A Berlin museum devoted entirely to a sliced sausage in curried tomato sauce drew 350,000 visitors a year for nine years — and owed its existence to an exhibition about yams in Jamaica.

**Location:** 52.5088, 13.3912

**Categories:** germany, berlin, museums, food

## Introduction

The idea came from a yam. Martin Löwer was on holiday in Jamaica when he wandered into an exhibition about the yam root, and instead of finding it absurd, he found it instructive. If a country could build a display around a tuber, what would Berlin build one around? He came home with an answer that no other city could have given him, and in 2009, a short walk from Checkpoint Charlie, he opened a museum devoted to a sliced sausage in curried tomato sauce.

## Herta Heuwer's Corner

The founding story belongs to a woman with a snack stand in Charlottenburg. By her own account, Herta Heuwer put together the first currywurst in Berlin in September 1949. Consider the moment. The blockade had lifted only that May, after nearly a year in which everything the western sectors ate arrived by air. The city was still largely rubble, still rationed, still improvising, and a woman running a snack stand was working with whatever the occupation economy put in front of her. Whatever proportions of ketchup and curry powder she settled on, they worked. Her business grew into a shop on Kaiser-Friedrich-Straße that in its heyday stayed open around the clock and employed nineteen women behind the counter. She registered the sauce as a trademark under the name Chillup. Heuwer died in 1999, by which point her invention had become the closest thing Berlin has to a civic dish — sold at snack stands the length of the country, and almost entirely detached from the person who thought of it.

## Follow the Sauce Trail

The museum opened on 15 August 2009, sixty years almost to the month after Heuwer's improvisation, and it committed fully to the joke without ever quite winking. Visitors followed a "sauce trail" through the history of the dish. A spice chamber offered sniffing stations — cumin, paprika, the specific dusty sweetness of curry powder. Audio posts played currywurst songs, of which there are more than anyone would guess. There was a virtual sausage-making game called Curry Up!, a sofa built in the shape of a sausage, and a set of installations mounted deliberately low, at the eye level of a six-year-old. It engaged sight, smell and hearing, which covers three-fifths of the experience of eating one.

## Three Hundred and Fifty Thousand a Year

Berlin-Mitte, a few streets from Checkpoint Charlie, is not a neutral place to put a museum about lunch. The blocks around it hold the Topography of Terror, the Wall's surviving stretches, and a dense concentration of exhibitions about the twentieth century at its most punishing. Visitors arrive in Mitte prepared to be sobered. Into that landscape came a building where the subject was a sausage, and the response was emphatic: roughly 350,000 visitors a year, numbers that plenty of serious institutions would envy. Part of it was the location and the tour-bus traffic that flows past Checkpoint Charlie all day. Part of it, surely, was relief — the permission to spend forty minutes on something that costs nothing emotionally. Not every hour in this city has to be a reckoning, and the museum understood that about its own address better than most of its neighbours did.

## Closed Since December

It lasted nine years. On 21 December 2018 the Deutsches Currywurst Museum shut permanently, and it has not reopened. What remains is the thing itself, which was never in any danger. Berlin's Imbiss stands still slice the sausage, still ladle the sauce, still ask whether you want it with the skin on or off, which in Berlin is a real question and not a tourist one. The museum is gone, and the dish it honoured is served on almost every busy corner in the city. That is probably the outcome Herta Heuwer would have chosen.

## Flight Context

The former museum stood at 52.5088° N, 13.3912° E in Berlin-Mitte, roughly 300 m northeast of the Checkpoint Charlie crossing point on Friedrichstraße. From the air the district reads as a tight grid of five-to-seven-storey blocks between two clear anchors: the twin domes and colonnades of Gendarmenmarkt about 600 m north, and the open ground of the former death strip running east-west just to the south, still visible as a seam of newer buildings and parking. Best viewed from 2,000-3,000 ft AGL on an east-west track along the old Wall line, which strings together Potsdamer Platz, the Topography of Terror, and Checkpoint Charlie in under two kilometres. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) lies about 22 km southeast; Schönhagen (EDAZ) is the nearest general-aviation field to the southwest. Central Berlin airspace is controlled and busy — coordinate any low-level VFR transit in advance. Visibility is best from late spring through early autumn.
