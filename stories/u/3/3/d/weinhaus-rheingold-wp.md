# Weinhaus Rheingold

Fourteen halls, four thousand seats and its own power station beside Potsdamer Platz — the most lavish restaurant imperial Berlin ever built, and one of the worst investments anybody made there.

**Location:** 52.5095, 13.3751

**Categories:** germany, berlin, architecture, history, mitte

## Introduction

The building was designed around a concert hall. Then, before a stone was laid, the Berlin building police told the owners they could not hold concerts in it. Traffic at Potsdamer Platz was already impossible, the argument ran, and a grand auditorium would only make it worse; permission would be granted for a restaurant and nothing else. The plans were too far advanced to redraw, so Bruno Schmitz's auditorium went up anyway and was filled with dining tables. That was the first thing to go wrong with the Weinhaus Rheingold, which opened on 6 February 1907, could serve four thousand guests at once, and never in thirty-six years turned a decent profit.

## Beer Halls to Wagner

Carl and August Aschinger had built their fortune at the cheap end of eating out. Their Bierquellen — stand-up beer halls where a Berliner could get a meal and a glass without sitting down — had spread across the city since 1892, and by 1900 the firm was a joint-stock company with three million marks of capital. Then the brothers decided they wanted respectability. They bought and replaced the old Hotel Fürstenhof on Potsdamer Platz, and in 1905 paid four million marks for three awkwardly shaped parcels on Bellevuestraße and Potsdamer Straße. For the architect they went to Bruno Schmitz, a builder of national monuments — the Kyffhäuser, the Deutsches Eck — and for a name they went to Wagner: Das Rheingold, the first opera of the Ring. The critic Hans Schliepmann described what the clients wanted as a hall for the most distinguished concerts, a kind of private box for the very highest of Berlin society.

## Seven Months of Pumping

The site sat in groundwater, and getting a building into it took engineering that the daily papers followed like a serial. Sixty-five suction wells were drilled at five-metre intervals, each reaching 7.5 metres below the floor of the excavation and feeding a collector pipe and an electric pump that lifted the water into the sewer. The table was dropped in two stages, with a safety margin so that a pump failure would not flood the pit instantly. Pumps failed anyway, costing a week of work. Neighbouring houses had to be braced and underpinned, and cracks appeared regardless — the press ran headlines about houses collapsing at Potsdamer Platz. On one parcel the tenants held long leases and would not leave, so the new building rose beneath the upper floors of the old one, which hung above the site on tie rods and scaffolding until the last of them moved out.

## Fourteen Rooms, No Two Alike

Behind Franz Metzner's facade lay an interior designed to disorient: fourteen halls, each a different world. The Kaisersaal ran 17.5 metres by 35 and seated over a thousand under a barrel vault, with gold mosaic in the orchestra niches and four cast emperors watching the room. A mahogany hall of Makassar ebony and padauk where a gilded hoard could be lit from within while a jet of water played over it, producing, according to one visitor, the effect of liquid gold catching fire. Metzner's eight facade reliefs — Vanity, Art, Music, Beauty — treated the human body as structure rather than portrait, and traditional taste hated them. Theodor Heuss, then a young architecture critic and one day the first president of the Federal Republic, wrote that headless men in the panelling did not bother him in the slightest.

## Three Thousand Guests and Cold Soup

The euphoria lasted months. By July 1907 Carl Aschinger was asking his brother to come back out of the boardroom and take over operations, because the house was not working. Past about three thousand guests the kitchens hit their limit; food went cold in the electrically heated lifts on its way up; people waited. August Aschinger wrote that the financial side of the Rheingold was simply impossible and would have devoured the rest of the business, and called making it work the greatest task of his life. He fixed the kitchens. He could not fix the arithmetic. In January 1911 the company's thirty beer halls returned 14.6 percent on sales; the Hotel Fürstenhof returned 22.2; the Weinhaus Rheingold returned five. Berlin's middle classes never adopted it and the upper classes never needed it; it drifted into being a place tourists were told to see. Baedeker's 1910 guide filed it, coolly, among the less pretentious wine restaurants.

## Under the BahnTower

The rest is decline. A sale in 1919 fell through; the Art Nouveau splendour dated the moment the empire ended; cabaret and dancing from 1922 did not help. In 1928 the management admitted the house did not come close to covering its costs, and the Depression closed it altogether through 1931 and 1932. From 1935 it filled up again with Strength Through Joy coach parties. Wartime service as troop accommodation ended in the winter of 1940 for lack of coal. In 1943 several Reich ministries took an interest — the site lay near the north–south axis of Albert Speer's planned Germania — before it was sold to the Deutsche Reichspost; the stored fittings burned in a raid the following spring. The 1945 damage survey classed the ruin as repairable. It was cleared anyway by the early 1950s, and the ground has since been crossed by two different alignments of Potsdamer Straße. Today the BahnTower stands roughly where the great hall was.

## Flight Context

The site of the Weinhaus Rheingold is at 52.5095° N, 13.3752° E, on the Bellevuestraße side of Potsdamer Platz in Berlin-Mitte. Nothing of the building survives, so navigate to the successors: the BahnTower, the curved glass slab on the north side of the square, stands approximately over the old hall building, and the brick-red Kollhoff-Tower next to it covers the former Potsdamer Straße frontage. From the air the giveaway is the Sony Center's tented roof immediately north-west and the vast green of the Tiergarten beyond it, with the Brandenburg Gate about 1 km north. Potsdamer Platz reads clearly from 2,000–4,000 ft AGL as a tight knot of towers isolated between parkland and the low blocks of southern Mitte — a shape that only exists because everything here was flattened and then rebuilt twice. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) lies about 20 km south-east; Schönhagen (EDAZ) is roughly 37 km south-south-west and Strausberg (EDAY) about 38 km east-north-east. This is controlled airspace over the city centre.
