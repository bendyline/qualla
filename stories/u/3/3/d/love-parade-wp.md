# Love Parade

It began in West Berlin in 1989 as a birthday party for 150 people, legally registered as a political demonstration. It ended in 2010 on a ramp in Duisburg where 21 people were crushed to death.

**Location:** 52.5200, 13.3800

**Categories:** Music, Festivals, Berlin, Popular culture, Disasters

## Introduction

In July 1989 about 150 people walked down the Kurfürstendamm in West Berlin behind a van with a sound system on it. The occasion was more or less a birthday party for the DJ Matthias Roeingh, who worked as Dr. Motte. The motto was Friede, Freude, Eierkuchen - peace, joy, pancakes - a German phrase meaning roughly everything is lovely, which the organisers chose to take literally and assign meanings to: peace for disarmament, joy for the music, pancakes for a fair way of producing and distributing food. It was registered with the authorities as a political demonstration, which under German law means the state pays for the policing and the street sweeping afterwards. Four months later the Wall opened, and the party had a whole second city to invite.

## The Angel on the Column

Through 1996 the parade ran along the Kurfürstendamm, until the crowds outgrew the street. It moved to the Strasse des 17. Juni, the great axis driven through the Tiergarten, and organised itself around the Siegessäule at the centre of the park; the gilded figure on top became the parade's emblem. From 1997 through 2000, more than a million people came each year.

About forty trucks made up the procession, each fronted by a club, a label or a record shop, open on top, dancers on the deck, water-cooled speaker stacks aimed at a crowd nobody could accurately count. The day ended with the Abschlusskundgebung, the closing rally, when every truck was wired to the turntables at the victory column and a single DJ played to something close to a million people at once. Tiësto, Carl Cox, Paul van Dyk, WestBam, Marusha and Armin van Buuren all took that slot. Across the park at the Berlin Zoo the enthusiasm was not shared: after the 2001 parade, vets reported that more than half the animals had diarrhoea and blamed the bass.

## Free Party, Paid Bills

The demonstration status was always arguable, and eventually it broke. Critics pointed at churned lawns in the Tiergarten and a shortage of toilets, and opponents booked events of their own in Berlin to block the parade's registration with the police. Funding failed in 2004 and 2005 and the parade was cancelled outright, replaced in 2004 by a scaled-down Love Weekend with clubs across the city staying open three days straight. A fitness chain, McFit, brought it back in 2006. Roeingh walked away that same year, saying the thing had been commercialised past recognition.

In February 2007 the Berlin Senate declined to issue permits, and Berlin's parade was finished. The event moved to the Ruhr - to Essen in August 2007, where 1.2 million came, and to Dortmund in 2008, where it took over a stretch of the Bundesstrasse 1 under the motto Highway of Love and drew an official estimate of 1.6 million, the largest it ever was. Those figures deserve an asterisk. Entry was free and uncounted, and German media later reported that organisers had been overstating attendance for years.

## Duisburg

The 2009 parade in Bochum was cancelled. The 2010 event went to Duisburg, onto the grounds of a disused freight station reached through a single tunnel and a ramp - one way in, the same way out. Safety experts and a fire service investigator had said in advance that the site could not handle the numbers expected.

On 24 July, the crowd on that ramp compressed until people could not breathe. Twenty-one of them died: thirteen women and eight men, aged between eighteen and thirty-eight. Fourteen were German. The others had travelled from Spain, Italy, the Netherlands, Bosnia, China and Australia - two Spanish women, an Italian woman, a Dutch man, a Bosnian woman, a Chinese woman and an Australian woman, all of whom had come to a German industrial city for an afternoon of music. Another 652 people were injured.

The organisers claimed a crowd of 1.4 million against an expected 800,000; the police believed the real figure was nearer 400,000. Whichever number is closer, it was far too many for one ramp.

## No Verdict

Rainer Schaller, the promoter, ended the festival within days. "The Love Parade has always been a peaceful party," he said, "but it will forever be overshadowed by the accident, so out of respect for the victims the Love Parade will never take place again." There has been no Love Parade since.

Blame moved in circles from the start: an interior ministry review pointed at the organisers, and Schaller pointed at the police handling of the crowd flow. Charges against ten city officials and event planners were rejected in 2016, reinstated on appeal in 2017, and finally brought to trial that December, in a special courtroom built for the case in Düsseldorf. After 184 sittings, in May 2020, the court ended proceedings without a verdict - the judges found the remaining defendants' culpability minor and feared the ten-year limitation period would expire before any conclusion, with the pandemic slowing everything further. Nobody was convicted of anything. A memorial now stands at the site.

What the parade had been between 1989 and 2010 deserves accuracy too. For a gathering of that scale it was remarkably peaceful: the whole 2008 Dortmund event, with its official count of 1.6 million, produced charges for six robberies, three sexual offences and forty thefts. The disaster that ended it was not a failure of the crowd. It was a failure of the ground they were funnelled into.

## Flight Context

52.52 degrees N, 13.38 degrees E, over the eastern end of the Grosser Tiergarten in central Berlin - the parade's home ground from 1996 to 2006. The geography is unmistakable from the air: a rectangle of forest roughly 3 km by 1 km with the arrow-straight Strasse des 17. Juni running west to east through the middle of it, the Brandenburg Gate closing the eastern end, and the Siegessäule standing at the Grosser Stern roundabout where the axis crosses. That column, with its gilded figure, was the parade's emblem and is the easiest single fix in the city after the Fernsehturm, which stands about 2.5 nm east-northeast. The Reichstag sits just north of the gate, inside a restricted zone, so this is airspace to transit under clearance rather than circle. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) lies roughly 11 nm southeast and Schönhagen (EDAZ) about 19 nm southwest. At 2,000 ft the full length of the parade route reads end to end in one pass.
