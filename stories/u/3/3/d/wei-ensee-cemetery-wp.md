# Weißensee Cemetery

Forty-two hectares and nearly 116,000 graves in north-east Berlin: the largest preserved Jewish cemetery in Europe by area, which came through the Nazi years essentially untouched and is still burying its dead.

**Location:** 52.5447, 13.4583

**Categories:** germany, berlin, jewish-history, history, memorials

## Introduction

Almost nothing of Jewish Berlin came through the twelve years of Nazi rule intact. Synagogues burned or were bombed or were cleared away afterwards. Businesses were seized, homes emptied, and a community that had helped build the modern city was murdered, driven into exile, or reduced to those few who managed to hide. Then there is Weißensee. Forty-two hectares of ground in the north-east of Berlin, consecrated in September 1880, holding close to 116,000 graves — by area the largest preserved Jewish cemetery in Europe — and it survived almost completely. The damage it carries came from Allied bombs, not from the regime. Nobody has ever produced a fully satisfying explanation.

## Room for a Century

The cemetery exists because Berlin's Jewish community had run out of room twice over. The old burial ground on Große Hamburger Straße, opened in 1672, was full by 1827. Its replacement on Schönhauser Allee opened that same year and by the 1880s could offer little beyond spaces held for widows and widowers beside spouses already buried. So the community bought farmland out at Weißensee and commissioned the architect Hugo Licht, who laid out an Italian neo-Renaissance complex behind walls and buildings of a distinctive yellow brick, with the archive and administration at the gate. It opened on 9 September 1880. Licht divided the ground into 120 numbered fields, labelled from A1 to P5, each with its own geometry, and the plan he drew was generous enough that the community was still burying into it more than a century later.

## Money at the Edges

Walk the outer paths and you are among the mausoleums: Art Nouveau at its most confident, marble and bronze and ironwork commissioned by families who had made fortunes in publishing, department stores, hotels and manufacturing, and who wanted that visible in stone. The Berlin bourgeoisie of the imperial era is buried here at full volume. Walk inward and the ground changes. The central fields belong to people who could not afford display, and their rows are plainer, narrower and harder to reach — in places the paths have gone under ivy and self-seeded trees, and the stones stand at angles in green half-light. It is not a subtle arrangement, and it was not meant to be. The cemetery maps a society's class structure as faithfully as any tax record, and the index of who lies where has survived largely intact.

## The Twelve Years

Jewish cemeteries across Europe were desecrated, bulldozed, quarried for their stone. Weißensee was not, and the reasons remain unsettled. The historian Hermann Simon has suggested the simplest answer: the place was enormous, and destroying it thoroughly would have taken an effort nobody prioritised. A witness named Harry Kindermann recalled something stranger — a belief among the Nazis that a Golem guarded the ground, and a reluctance to come inside. Whatever kept them out, the cemetery did more than survive: during the war it gave temporary shelter to Jews living underground in Berlin, people hiding among the graves of their own families. About 4,000 graves were damaged, by Allied bombing. At the main entrance a memorial stone stands ringed by further stones, each cut with the name of a concentration camp. Beside it is a memorial from 1927 to the Jewish soldiers who died fighting for Germany in the First World War, and a plaque to those who fought Nazism.

## Forty Years of Weeds

After 1945 Jews from every part of Berlin were buried here until 1955. After that the city's division meant that only the small Jewish community of East Berlin used the cemetery, and it was a community with almost nobody left to tend the graves. Weeds took the fields. In the 1970s East Berlin planners proposed running an expressway across part of the ground to link Michelangelostraße with the new Hansastraße; the remaining community objected, and the road was abandoned, though the strip cleared for it stayed empty for years. Since 1990 that strip has been used for burials again. The cost of undoing decades of neglect has been put at 40 million euros. On the cemetery's 125th anniversary the community appealed for funding and proposed the site for the UNESCO World Heritage list, a bid backed by Berlin's then governing mayor Klaus Wowereit.

## Who Is Here

Louis Lewandowski, whose settings of the liturgy are still sung in synagogues on three continents, is buried at Weißensee. So is the painter Lesser Ury; the philosopher Hermann Cohen; the publisher Samuel Fischer, whose house issued Thomas Mann; the newspaper proprietor Rudolf Mosse; the department store founder Hermann Tietz; the hotelier Berthold Kempinski. The street that ends at the main gate is named for Herbert Baum, who led a Jewish resistance group in Berlin and who lies inside. The writer Stefan Heym is here, and so is Joseph Weizenbaum, the MIT computer scientist who built ELIZA and then spent decades warning about what people would believe a machine was telling them — born in Berlin, taken out as a boy in the 1930s, returned at the end of his life, buried in the city his family had to flee. Two kilometres north-east, on Wittlicher Straße, the Orthodox congregation Adass Jisroel has kept its own cemetery since 1873. Both are open. Both are still in use.

## Flight Context

The cemetery lies at 52.5447° N, 13.4583° E in Weißensee, in the borough of Pankow, roughly 6 km north-east of Alexanderplatz. It is one of the easiest targets in Berlin to identify from the air: a solid dark-green rectangle roughly 1 km long and 0.5 km wide, densely wooded and sharply walled, sitting inside an otherwise low-rise residential grid with no comparable green mass nearby. The Fernsehturm bears south-west at about 6 km and gives the bearing; the smaller Adass Jisroel cemetery sits 2 km north-east on Wittlicher Straße. Best viewed from 1,500–3,000 ft AGL. The tree canopy closes over the fields entirely between May and September, so the layout of the 120 grave fields and the yellow-brick gate buildings read far better from November to March. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is about 28 km south-south-east; Strausberg (EDAY) lies roughly 32 km east and Schönhagen (EDAZ) about 47 km south-west. Central Berlin is controlled airspace and low transits require coordination.
