# Germania (city)

Hitler and Albert Speer planned to rebuild Berlin as the capital of a world empire; almost nothing was built, but the demolitions, evictions and forced-labour camps the plan set in motion were entirely real.

**Location:** 52.5167, 13.3889

**Categories:** Nazi Germany, History of Berlin, Unbuilt architecture, World War II, Urban planning, Holocaust history

## Introduction

The largest surviving structure of Hitler's world capital is a grey concrete cylinder standing in a residential street in Tempelhof-Schöneberg, and it was built to fail. The Schwerbelastungskörper — 'heavy load-bearing body' — weighs 12,650 tonnes and was poured for one purpose: to find out whether Berlin's marshy ground could carry a triumphal arch bigger than anything in Paris. Instruments measured how far it sank. Anything under six centimetres would have meant the soil was sound. It went down nineteen. The arch was never built, and the block is still there, too solid to remove.

## A Name Before a City

Hitler chose the name himself. In his recorded table talk of 8 June 1942 he explained that Berlin, rebuilt, would be renamed Germania so that a Greater Germanic empire of the New Order would have an unmistakable centre. The official version was drier: the Comprehensive Construction Plan for the Reich Capital, run by Albert Speer, who had been made General Building Inspector for the Reich Capital in 1937 while still in his early thirties. Speer produced the drawings — the axes, the domes, the colonnades — and by his own later account regarded the whole thing as an architectural commission. It was in fact an administrative office with police powers over property, and it used them.

## The Hall That Could Not Stand

The centrepiece was to be the Volkshalle, the People's Hall, planned at roughly 290 metres high with a dome intended to hold something in the region of 180,000 people. Down the north–south axis from it, the Arch of Triumph was to be large enough to swallow the Arc de Triomphe several times over, and its interior surfaces were to carry the names of the 1,800,000 German dead of the First World War. Engineers were sceptical about the ground from the beginning, which is why the test cylinder exists at all. Berlin sits on sand and glacial till, and neither behaves well under concentrated load. The measurements taken from the Schwerbelastungskörper indicate that the soil would not have supported these buildings without enormous additional preparation — the arch would have required foundations approaching the scale of the monument.

## What Actually Got Built

A city axis, essentially. Charlottenburger Chaussee was widened into a monumental east–west boulevard — today it is the Straße des 17. Juni — and the Victory Column was hauled from its original position in front of the Reichstag and reinstalled in the middle of the Tiergarten, where it still stands. Speer presented the finished axis to Hitler for his fiftieth birthday in 1939 with a line he had clearly rehearsed: 'My Führer. I would like to report the completion of the east-west axis. May the work speak for itself.' War stopped nearly everything else. By 1943 the plan was dead in all but name, and by 1945 Berlin looked less like Germania than like the photographs of the cities the Luftwaffe had flattened first.

## Seven Thousand Apartments

The demolitions began in 1938 — the Alsen district, where the Great Hall was to rise, and parts of the Tiergarten quarter where the new axes would cross. Around seven thousand apartments came down in that year alone, and Speer's office faced an obvious problem: there was neither money nor material to rehouse the displaced Germans. The solution his inspectorate proposed was to take Jewish Berliners' homes instead. Under the Law on Tenancy with Jews, Jewish families lost the protection of ordinary tenancy and were evicted; the Jewish community itself was made to find them somewhere to go, under Gestapo supervision, which in practice meant crowding several families into flats designated for Jews. From 1939 nearly half of Berlin's Jewish population was forcibly rehoused this way. For a great many of them, those addresses were the last ones they had before deportation. This was not a side effect of the building plan. It was the housing policy the building plan required.

## The Camps and the Axis

Building on that scale also needed labour, and by the war years the General Building Inspectorate had become the third-largest operator of forced-labour camps in Berlin, behind only Siemens and the state railway; it was centrally involved in constructing or approving an estimated three thousand camps across the city. One of them, GBI Camp 75/76, went up in the middle of a residential neighbourhood in Niederschöneweide in 1943 — thirteen barracks meant for over two thousand people, occupied by Italian military internees and forced labourers from across Europe. The barracks survived the war and now house the Nazi Forced Labour Documentation Centre, one of the few places in Germany where the accommodation itself is the exhibit. As for the Arch of Triumph, its planned position was eventually occupied by something else entirely: after 1945 the Soviets built a war memorial on the great axis and buried around two thousand of their dead beneath it. The monument to German victory never rose. A monument to the soldiers who ended the regime stands roughly where it would have been.

## Flight Context

The Germania plan centred on 52.5167°N, 13.3889°E in the Tiergarten quarter. From the air the one fully realised piece is unmistakable: the arrow-straight Straße des 17. Juni running east–west through the green rectangle of the Tiergarten, with the Victory Column's gold figure marking the roundabout at its midpoint and the Brandenburg Gate closing the eastern end. The Soviet War Memorial sits on the north side of the boulevard just west of the gate. The Schwerbelastungskörper is 4 km south near the S-Bahn at General-Pape-Straße, a small grey cylinder easily missed. Fly 2,000–4,000 ft; low sun along the axis shows the alignment best. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) lies about 23 km southeast.
