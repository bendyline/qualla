# German Colonial Museum

For sixteen years a museum in Moabit existed to sell Germans on their empire, complete with a river running through the hall, replica villages emptied of the people who built them, and objects its own labels called booty.

**Location:** 52.5225, 13.3681

**Categories:** Moabit, German colonial empire, Museums established in the 1890s, Ethnographic museums in Germany, 1899 establishments in Germany, 1915 disestablishments in Germany

## Introduction

A river ran through the middle of the building. Not a fountain, not a basin — an actual watercourse laid across the floor of the great domed hall in Moabit, standing in for the Rufiji valley in East Africa, with boulders set into it so that visitors could step from stone to stone and feel they had crossed something. That was the German Colonial Museum's headline attraction, and it explains almost everything about the place. Kaiser Wilhelm II opened it on 13 October 1899, in the shell of a former naval panorama beside the Lehrter Bahnhof, and it closed for lack of money in 1915. It was never really a museum. It was an argument staged in three dimensions: that Germany's overseas empire was worth having, worth paying for, and worth being proud of.

## The Import Hall and the Export Hall

The tour began in a grand entrance hall dominated by a bust of Wilhelm II under the caption *The patron of our colonies*. Then came two rooms that made the case in the bluntest possible terms. In the Import Hall: rubber, cocoa, tropical hardwoods, precious stones — what flowed into the Reich. In the Export Hall: fertiliser, tropical medicine, wire, machinery — what German firms sold outward. The plan was to grow these rooms into a permanent export sample warehouse by 1920. Elsewhere, Protestant and Catholic missions presented their work, and a reading room stocked colonial literature and newspapers shipped home from the territories. The organisers were not coy about any of it. From the beginning the approach was propagandistic rather than scientific, and it used the most modern display technology available — photography, panorama painting, and meticulously constructed "lifelike" scenes.

## Villages With the People Taken Out

Past the Rufiji, the halls were divided into departments named for what the period called Schutzgebiete, "protected areas" — a word chosen so that conquest would sound like custody. In the Cameroon department you could step onto the replica verandah of a non-commissioned officers' mess and look out at a painted Atlantic as seen from Douala. The Togo department had huts built true to the original. Beside the German South West Africa display stood a reconstructed Herero camp. Kiautschou appeared as Chinese street life and a bay of naval ships; German New Guinea as a coastline of stilt houses, nets and boats. The dwellings were faithfully made. The people who built and lived in such dwellings were nowhere in the room. And the Herero camp stood on display through the years from 1904 to 1908, while German forces in South West Africa carried out the campaign against the Herero and the Nama now recognised as genocide — roughly ten thousand Nama were killed, about half the pre-war population.

## The Hundred Brought to Treptow

The collection had a source. From 1 May to 15 October 1896, Berlin's great industrial exposition filled Treptow Park, and attached to it was the German Colonial Exhibition. To make the constructed villages convincing, the organisers brought more than a hundred people from East Africa, Togo, Cameroon and New Guinea to Berlin to live in them for the season. They were not exhibits. They were men and women with families, languages and homes elsewhere, placed in a public park and expected to perform their own daily lives for seven million paying visitors across 168 days on which it rained 120 times. When the exposition closed that October, the question of what to do with the accumulated objects produced the museum in Moabit. What the arrangement had cost the people was not a question the organisers asked.

## Things That Belonged to Someone

Among the individual pieces were objects the museum itself listed as booty. One was a chair belonging to Hendrik Witbooi, the Nama kaptein who led the resistance to German rule in South West Africa and was killed in action on 29 October 1905, and who now appears on Namibian banknotes as a national hero. Another was the flag Adolf Lüderitz had raised at Angra Pequena to claim the coast. Alongside them sat stuffed animals, photographs and relief models, catalogued with the same evenness — a leader's furniture and a taxidermied antelope filed as comparable curiosities. "Booty" carries a great deal of freight. It means these things were taken from people who did not hand them over, and it was printed on the label without embarrassment, because embarrassment was not yet part of the vocabulary.

## What Became of It

The takings never covered the costs. By 1906 the museum was drawing subsidy from the Ethnological Museum, whose director Felix von Luschan wrote with undisguised irritation that the colonial panorama might be tucked "into a corner of our new building in such a way that it doesn't disrupt our other operations." In 1911 the German Colonial Newspaper reported 481,259 visitors and 2,931 lectures since opening, though how much the public genuinely cared remains unclear. Closure came in 1915. Two years later 3,342 objects were sold to the Linden-Museum in Stuttgart; nearly 70,000 more were probably shelved in the Ethnological Museum's archive and then carried off to the Soviet Union as war loot. The building became a machine factory and was demolished in 1928. The most notorious object of the German colonial era surfaced elsewhere: the skull of Mkwawa, the Hehe leader who destroyed a German column at Lugalo in 1891 and shot himself in 1898 rather than be taken, which the Treaty of Versailles had required Germany to return in 1919. It was found in 1953 in a Bremen museum that had itself once been called the Deutsches Kolonialmuseum, and handed back on 9 July 1954. It rests at Kalenga today.

## Flight Context

The site sits at 52.5225°N, 13.3681°E in Moabit, on the north bank of the Spree just west of Berlin Hauptbahnhof — which stands where the Lehrter Bahnhof once did. Nothing of the museum survives; the former naval panorama building came down in 1928, and the ground is now ordinary inner-city Berlin. From 3,000–4,000 ft the reference points are the Hauptbahnhof's glass cross-shaped roof, the sweep of the Spree, and the Reichstag dome a kilometre south-east. EDDB (Berlin Brandenburg) lies about 26 km to the south-east; EDAZ (Schönhagen) is roughly 45 km south-west for general aviation. Note that Tegel and Tempelhof are both permanently closed. Berlin's flat terrain gives good all-round visibility, but low stratus is common from November to February.
