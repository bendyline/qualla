# Marx-Engels Forum

A bronze Marx and Engels in the heart of East Berlin, sculpted by Ludwig Engelhardt in 1986, moved aside for the U-Bahn extension that runs beneath them, and since set back in place.

**Location:** 52.5183, 13.4042

**Categories:** berlin, germany, monuments, gdr-history, communism, public-art, cold-war

## Introduction

Marx is sitting. Engels is standing. They are slightly larger than life and cast in dark bronze, and Marx's lap has drawn decades of tourists climbing onto it for photographs. The Berlin sculptor Ludwig Engelhardt deliberately put them at eye level, accessible, touchable, with only a low base between them and the people who would come to look. The GDR's Culture Minister, Hans-Joachim Hoffmann, said that the point was that visitors would be able to talk with Marx and Engels rather than look up their nostrils. It was a remarkable thing for a state minister to say about state monuments. Within five years, the state that commissioned the statues was gone.

## The Quarter That Used to Be Here

Before the Second World War, the strip of land between the Spree and Alexanderplatz was the Heilige-Geist-Viertel, the Holy Ghost Quarter, a dense huddle of medieval streets and the city's main post office. Allied bombing in 1944 and 1945 leveled most of it. The East German government cleared what was left and let the ground sit empty for thirty years. When planning began in 1977, the GDR decided to fill the gap with a green park between the Palast der Republik and the Fernsehturm, and to put Marx and Engels at its center. The Nikolaiviertel next door was being rebuilt as a sanitized medieval quarter. The Forum was the new ideological one.

## Engelhardt's Reading

Engelhardt had been working on the figures since the late 1970s. What he produced is more melancholy than triumphalist. Marx looks tired. Engels looks patient. Behind them stand a marble relief by Werner Stötzer, stainless-steel steles with documentary photographs of the workers' movement by Arno Fischer and Peter Voigt, and bronze reliefs by Margret Middell. Some viewers have read the seated, weary Marx as an unintentional epitaph for the system that commissioned him. Whatever Engelhardt intended, the inauguration on 4 April 1986 fell within a window of about three and a half years before the Berlin Wall came down. The statues survived 1989 because Berliners argued for keeping them. They had become part of the cityscape that people walked through every day, and removing them felt too much like erasing a chapter rather than turning the page.

## Lives in the Frame

It is easy now to think of the GDR as a museum exhibit, but the people who attended the unveiling in 1986 were living their lives. They had jobs, families, weekend dachas in Brandenburg, opinions about the local football team. The Forum was where they took their children on Sunday afternoons. After 1990, some of those families lost everything they had built; others discovered Stasi files about themselves; many felt a quieter loss, the disappearance of a country that had also been home. The German word for that feeling, Ostalgie, gets mocked in headlines, but it is real and it is honest. Standing at the Forum today, you are looking at one of the few public reminders of forty years of lived experience for around sixteen million people.

## Moved for the U-Bahn

In 2010, work began on the extension of the U5 line under central Berlin. The construction site swallowed the Forum, and Marx and Engels were lifted from their pedestal and moved to the northwest corner of the square near Karl-Liebknecht-Straße, turned to face west rather than toward the TV tower. The U5 extension opened in December 2020. Some Berliners wanted the medieval quarter rebuilt, as Mayor Klaus Wowereit once floated; others wanted the GDR park preserved as a deliberate act of remembering. A 2021 design competition settled on a new green square; in 2022 the monument group was set back in its original place, facing the TV tower again, and in 2026 the Forum around it is a fenced construction site, with the wider area due to be finished around 2030. The statues themselves are mute on the matter, their backs to the reconstructed Berlin Palace across the Spree, where the Palace of the Republic used to stand.

## What You See Now

Visit once the rebuilding is done and you should find a green space again, with the bronze pair at its centre. There will probably be a tourist sitting on Marx's knee, arms slung casually across his shoulder, while a friend takes the photograph. There will be Berliners cutting through on their way to the Rotes Rathaus or the Nikolaiviertel, paying the statues no particular attention. The Fernsehturm rises behind the trees. The reconstructed dome of the Berlin Cathedral catches the light across the river. The Forum is a place caught between three eras, none of which has fully decided what to do with it.

## Flight Context

Located at 52.518 N, 13.404 E in Berlin's Mitte district, on the eastern bank of the Spree directly opposite the reconstructed Berlin Palace. The Fernsehturm at 368 meters dominates the immediate skyline a few blocks east. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is the nearest airport at about 18 km southeast. Best viewed at low altitude alongside the Spree island; the Forum itself is small and reads as a green clearing among the surrounding masonry.
