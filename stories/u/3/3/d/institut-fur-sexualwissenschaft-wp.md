# Institut für Sexualwissenschaft

Magnus Hirschfeld's Berlin institute was the world's first sexology research centre and the world's first trans clinic — until students hauled its library into the street and burned it in May 1933.

**Location:** 52.5189, 13.3652

**Categories:** lgbtq-history, berlin, weimar-republic, science, nazi-germany, book-burning, tiergarten

## Introduction

Four Latin words were carved on the front of the building: per scientiam ad justitiam. Through science to justice. It was the motto of Magnus Hirschfeld, a Jewish physician who had spent his career arguing that human sexuality belonged to careful research rather than criminal law, and from 1919 he had a house in the Tiergarten in which to prove it. For fourteen years the Institut für Sexualwissenschaft was the first place in the world where a person could walk in off a Berlin street, ask a doctor a question about their own body, and expect an honest answer. On 6 May 1933 a student organisation broke in and looted the collections; four days later, on 10 May, what remained of the library was hauled into the Opernplatz and burned.

## The House on the Tiergarten

Hirschfeld had been running the Scientific-Humanitarian Committee since 1897 — the world's first organisation campaigning for homosexual rights — and the institute was the committee's argument made physical. He bought the building from the government of the Free State of Prussia after the First World War and inaugurated it on 1 July 1919 alongside his collaborators Arthur Kronfeld, a well-known psychotherapist, and the dermatologist Friedrich Wertheim. Inside were a research library and archive, medical, psychological and ethnological divisions, examination rooms, a lecture hall, a marriage and sex counselling office, and a museum. Hirschfeld reckoned some 1,250 lectures were given in the first year alone. Visitors who could not pay were treated for free.

## Questions People Could Not Ask Anywhere Else

Much of the daily work had nothing to do with the topics that made the institute notorious. It treated venereal disease and alcoholism, ran gynecological examinations, counselled married couples, and made contraception available — with a particular commitment to reaching the poor, at a time when the Weimar constitution prohibited advertising birth control at all. When the rules loosened, the institute published an educational pamphlet on contraception in 1928 that had reached roughly 100,000 copies by 1932. A neighbouring building, bought in 1921, opened to the public in March 1922 for lectures and question-and-answer evenings, and the question audiences asked most often was about contraception. Not politics, not theory. How not to have another child you cannot feed.

## The First Trans Clinic

Hirschfeld coined the term transsexual in a 1923 essay, and trans people were both patients at the institute and members of its staff, working as receptionists and housekeepers. The clinic offered endocrinological and surgical care, including an early modern gender-affirming surgery in 1931. Testosterone was not synthesised until 1935, so masculinising hormone therapy was never available there. Ludwig Levy-Lenz, the institute's principal surgeon for these patients, developed early forms of facial feminisation and masculinisation surgery, and X-ray hair removal that caused burns. The historian Robert M. Beachy has written that although the techniques were experimental and ultimately dangerous, they "were developed largely in response to the ardent requests of patients." Levy-Lenz put it more simply: "never have I operated upon more grateful patients." Hirschfeld also negotiated with Berlin police for what were then called transvestite passes — documents issued through the institute that spared the bearer arrest for wearing clothes associated with a gender other than the one assigned at birth.

## The Sixth of May

Hirschfeld was in Ascona, Switzerland, when it happened. The Deutsche Studentenschaft attacked the institute in an organised operation and destroyed the collections; one estimate puts the loss at around 25,000 books. Artworks went into the fire, along with rare medical and anthropological documents and charts on intersex cases prepared for the International Medical Congress. Nothing comparable was assembled anywhere in the world again until the Kinsey Institute was founded in 1947, and a great deal of what burned had existed in no other copy. According to the Munich Documentation Centre for the History of National Socialism, a hundred book burnings were recorded in seventy German cities between March and October 1933. Commentators abroad reached for the same line, written a century earlier by Heinrich Heine: where one burns books, one will soon burn people.

## The Lists

The books were the visible loss. The address lists seized from the institute were the lethal one. They are believed to have assisted the round-ups that followed 28 June 1934, when Hitler had gay men in the SA murdered during the Night of the Long Knives, and the far harsher persecution that came after. Tens of thousands of those arrested ended in forced-labour or death camps, among them the institute's own staff, including August Bessunger. Kurt Hiller, a lawyer connected to the institute, had already been sent to a concentration camp in March 1933 and tortured there; he escaped Germany and survived the war. Karl Giese, Hirschfeld's partner, took his own life in 1938; his heir, the lawyer Karl Fein, was murdered in 1942 during deportation. Arthur Kronfeld and Felix Abraham also died by suicide. Hirschfeld tried to rebuild in Paris, gave up in 1934, and died at Nice in 1935.

## What Stands There Now

The buildings were a bombed-out ruin by 1944 and were cleared in the mid-1950s. The Haus der Kulturen der Welt now stands on the ground, its swooping concrete roof a landmark of a very different Berlin; a plaque marks what was there before, and a nearby garden carries the name of Lili Elbe. New institutes with the same name opened at Frankfurt in 1973 and at the Humboldt University in 1996, and in the 1950s W. Dorr Legg and ONE, Inc. in the United States gathered up surviving fragments of the library. The research lost in 1933 took the better part of a century to reconstruct, and much of it never was.

## Flight Context

The site lies at 52.519°N, 13.365°E on the northern edge of the Tiergarten in central Berlin, at the bend of the Spree — the Haus der Kulturen der Welt, with its distinctive curved white roof, now occupies the ground and is easy to identify from the air. The Reichstag and the Brandenburg Gate are 800 m southeast, the Siegessäule 1.5 km southwest, the Hauptbahnhof just across the river to the north. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is roughly 19 km southeast; Strausberg (EDAY) lies about 35 km east. Recommended viewing altitude 2,000-3,000 ft; this is the heart of Berlin's controlled airspace and clearance is required. Best light is late afternoon, when the Tiergarten canopy separates cleanly from the government quarter.
