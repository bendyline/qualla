# Nikolaiviertel

East Germany rebuilt Berlin's medieval heart for the city's 750th birthday — part salvaged fact, part concrete panel, and entirely honest about neither until you look closely.

**Location:** 52.5167, 13.4072

**Categories:** neighborhoods, Berlin, history, architecture, reconstruction, East Germany

## Introduction

The inn Berliners call their oldest was built in the 1980s, and it stands on the wrong side of the river. The original Zum Nußbaum was a gabled tavern on the Fischerinsel in old Cölln, its cellar carrying a sixteenth-century date, and it was destroyed with the rest of the old town in the war. The copy that draws drinkers today sits a few hundred metres east, in the Nikolaiviertel, wearing the old building's face on new walls. That single fact explains the whole quarter. This is Berlin's medieval core rebuilt from photographs, memory, salvaged stonework, and prefabricated concrete — a reconstruction that never quite pretends to be the thing it replaced, and never quite admits it isn't.

## The Ford at the Mills Dam

Two settlements founded Berlin, facing each other across the Spree at a crossing called the Mühlendamm, the mills dam, where the river could be forded. Cölln took the western bank; Alt-Berlin took the eastern one, and the Nikolaiviertel is what remains of it. At the centre stood the Nikolaikirche, begun as a late Romanesque basilica around 1230 and still the oldest church in the city. The alleys that grew around it — Eiergasse, Poststraße, Propststraße — kept their medieval shape through five centuries of Hohenzollern building booms, plague, occupation, and industrialisation. Then came the air raids and the Battle of Berlin. By May 1945 the quarter was rubble and the church was a shell with its twin spires gone.

## A Deadline in 1987

The ground sat largely empty for a generation. What eventually filled it was a birthday. Berlin's 750th anniversary fell in 1987, and both German states planned celebrations; East Berlin decided to give itself back its old town. Under the architect Günter Stahn, work ran through the first half of the 1980s to produce a quarter of narrow streets and stepped gables around the rebuilt Nikolaikirche, with close to 800 apartments above ground-floor shops and restaurants. Stahn was candid about what he was making. The project, he wrote, was not a recreation with every detail left unchanged but an attempt at constructed memory. It was also a political statement: a socialist state that had dynamited the Hohenzollern palace three decades earlier now rebuilding the burghers' town beside it.

## Gables Made of Panels

Look closely at the houses and the seams give them away. Most of the gabled fronts are standard Plattenbau construction — precast concrete panels hung on a frame, the same industrial system that produced East German housing estates from Rostock to Dresden, dressed here in historicising façades with arched windows and painted render. At a distance the effect is convincing. Up close the proportions are a little too regular, the window reveals a little too shallow. A few buildings are the real thing. The Knoblauchhaus of 1760, its neoclassical front added in the nineteenth century, survived and belonged to the Knoblauch family for generations, among them the architect Eduard and the physicist Karl-Hermann. It now holds a Biedermeier museum, the oldest civic museum in Berlin, and stands as a control sample: this is what the street actually looked like.

## The Palace That Moved Twelve Metres

The finest building in the quarter has the strangest history. The Ephraim-Palais was built in 1766 for Veitel Heine Ephraim, a Jewish court financier and mint contractor to Frederick II, and its curving Rococo corner at Poststraße and Mühlendamm became known as the most beautiful corner in Berlin. In 1936 the city demolished it to widen the road. The carved façade elements were not destroyed; they were crated and stored in what became West Berlin, where they sat through the division of the city. In 1982 the West Berlin authorities handed the fragments to the East Berlin magistrate to support the reconstruction — Cold War cooperation conducted in sandstone. The palace was rebuilt between 1983 and 1987 about twelve metres from where it had originally stood, because the widened street was still there. It is a museum now, and the corner is beautiful again, just slightly out of place.

## Ghosts, Beer, and Arguments

On the riverbank stands the red sandstone Kurfürstenhaus of 1897, built where an older house had stood. In that earlier building, on 23 December 1619, Elector John Sigismund died in his valet's home — he had fled the City Palace convinced that the White Lady, the apparition said to warn of Hohenzollern deaths, was walking its corridors. Berliners have never entirely agreed on what to make of the quarter that now surrounds his death site. Some call it kitsch, a socialist theme park with a Gothic hat. Others point out that it is the only place in Berlin where you can walk a medieval street plan at medieval scale, drink under a walnut tree, and stand inside a church begun eight hundred years ago. Both are right. The Nikolaiviertel is a copy that tells the truth about what was lost.

## Flight Context

The Nikolaiviertel occupies a compact block at 52.517°N, 13.407°E on the eastern bank of the Spree in Mitte, bounded by Rathausstraße, Spandauer Straße and the Mühlendamm. From the air it reads as a dense patch of small red-tiled roofs and narrow streets — unmistakably finer-grained than everything around it — with the twin spires of the Nikolaikirche at its centre. The Fernsehturm rises 368 m about 500 m to the northeast; the brick Rotes Rathaus sits immediately north, and the Humboldt Forum and Berlin Cathedral are across the river to the west. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is roughly 20 km southeast. Central Berlin airspace is restricted and requires coordination.
