# Unter den Linden

Prussia's grandest boulevard began as a shortcut to a hunting ground, took its name from trees that have twice been cut down, and still points ten kilometres straight across Berlin.

**Location:** 52.5166, 13.3810

**Categories:** streets, Berlin, Prussian history, architecture, urban design, history

## Introduction

Prussia's grandest avenue started as a shortcut to a hunting ground. In the sixteenth century, Elector John George of Brandenburg had a bridle path cut west from his palace on the Spree so he could reach the game in the Tiergarten without threading through town. A century later the Electress Dorothea, laying out a new suburb along its northern side, replaced the path with a boulevard of lime trees — the first of which, by tradition, she planted herself in 1680. Berliners have walked under them ever since, and lost them more than once. The trees standing there today are none of hers. They went into the ground in the 1950s, on a street that had been stripped bare twice within a single decade.

## The Electress and Her Lime Trees

The name confuses English speakers immediately. The linden of Unter den Linden is Tilia — the lime of Britain and Ireland, the basswood of North America, no relation whatsoever to the citrus fruit. Dorothea's new district took her own name, Dorotheenstadt, and was founded in 1674; the avenue was its spine. One stretch of the boulevard has never had trees at all. When Berlin threw up a baroque fortress ring in the aftermath of the Thirty Years' War, parts of the earthworks ran across what is now Bebelplatz, and the ground there was later absorbed into the street rather than planted. Walk east from the opera house today and the double row of limes simply stops. A seventeenth-century military engineering decision is still legible as a gap in the canopy.

## Ten Kilometres, Dead Straight

Frederick the Great meant to build a forum here. What he actually finished was smaller than the plan but formidable enough: his State Opera, St. Hedwig's Cathedral, the old library, and the palace of his brother Prince Henry, which is now the main building of Humboldt University. The result is a square ringed almost entirely by eighteenth-century architecture. Nearby stands the Zeughaus, the arsenal built between 1695 and 1706 and the oldest building on the street. Since 1937 the house numbers have counted up from the Schlossbrücke, so the arsenal is No. 2 and Schinkel's severe little Neue Wache of 1817 is No. 4. Look the other way and the geometry keeps going. Behind the Brandenburg Gate the line continues as the Straße des 17. Juni, then Bismarckstraße, then the Kaiserdamm, widened in 1904 into an axis running more than ten kilometres from the palace to the western outskirts. The planners had Paris in mind.

## The Address That Meant the Government

Before 1914 the German Foreign Ministry sat on this street, and diplomats used the address the way the British use Whitehall: Unter den Linden said the government, not the pavement. The boulevard's commercial side was just as loud. The Kaisergalerie, opened in 1873, packed more than fifty shops and cafés into a glass-roofed arcade; Alfred Grenander restyled it in cool New Objectivity lines in the early 1930s, and it was demolished in the 1950s. Johann Strauss III wrote a waltz named for the street in 1900. Today the western end runs to embassies — Russia occupies Nos. 63–65, on the site of the old Soviet mission — and finishes at the Hotel Adlon, rebuilt from scratch on the ground where the pre-war original stood. Statues of Alexander and Wilhelm von Humboldt keep watch outside the university their family founded.

## The Trees Come Down Twice

In 1934 and 1935, engineers cut the Nord-Süd-Tunnel for the S-Bahn beneath the boulevard, and most of the lime trees came down with the excavation. The survivors lasted another decade. In the final days of the war, as the city was fought through street by street, Berliners felled what remained for firewood — the trees were worth more as fuel than as scenery to people cooking in cellars. Replanting waited until the 1950s. Not everything came back. A street sign from before the 1930s was carried off by British forces and now sits in the Imperial War Museum in London, the name of Berlin's grandest street displayed in a case nearly a thousand kilometres from the kerb it was bolted to.

## Frederick Rides Back

Christian Daniel Rauch designed the equestrian Frederick the Great in 1839, and the bronze went up on the central strip in 1851 — king, cocked hat, and a plinth crowded with generals and philosophers. In May 1950 the East German authorities decided he had to go. The statue was dismantled that July, hauled to Potsdam, stored in pieces, and came close to being melted down before it was re-erected in the hippodrome at Charlottenhof Palace by the early 1960s. Then the politics turned again. In 1980 Erich Honecker called Frederick "the Great" in an interview, East German historians began publishing warmer assessments of Prussia, and the statue was brought back to Unter den Linden, where it still faces east toward the palace. He left the boulevard by political decision and returned by one. Beneath the Friedrichstraße crossing, an U-Bahn station now carries the street's name, and the limes above it are pushing seventy.

## Flight Context

Unter den Linden runs east–west at 52.517°N, 13.381°E through Berlin's Mitte district, roughly 1.5 km from the Schlossbrücke and Museum Island to Pariser Platz and the Brandenburg Gate. From the air it is one of the easiest features in Berlin to identify: a dead-straight green corridor that continues west as the Straße des 17. Juni through the Tiergarten, past the Victory Column, and on to the Kaiserdamm — a single line more than ten kilometres long. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is about 20 km southeast. The green copper dome of the Berlin Cathedral marks the eastern end; the Fernsehturm rises just beyond it. Central Berlin airspace is tightly controlled and the historic core is restricted.
