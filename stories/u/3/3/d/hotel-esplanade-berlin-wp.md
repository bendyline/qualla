# Hotel Esplanade Berlin

Berlin's most expensive hotel was bombed to a shell, marooned beside the Wall, filmed by Bob Fosse and Wim Wenders, and in 1996 had its surviving ballroom lifted whole and walked seventy-five metres across Potsdamer Platz.

**Location:** 52.5100, 13.3744

**Categories:** Hotels in Berlin, Potsdamer Platz, Buildings and structures in Mitte, Buildings and structures in Berlin destroyed during World War II, 1908 establishments in Germany

## Introduction

In March 1996 a room went for a walk. The Kaisersaal — the ballroom where Wilhelm II had held his gentlemen's evenings, one of the few fragments of the Hotel Esplanade to survive the winter of 1944–45 — was jacked up, put on rails and moved seventy-five metres across the building site that Potsdamer Platz had become, because the new Sony Center wanted the ground it stood on and the room was a protected monument that could not simply be knocked down. The operation cost around 75 million marks, which is a remarkable sum to spend on relocating a ballroom, and roughly three times what the entire hotel had cost to build. Berlin had lost so much of this square twice over that the price seemed reasonable.

## Twenty-Three Million Marks

The Esplanade was put up between 1907 and 1908 by the Fürstengruppe — the princely consortium of Hohenlohe, Fürstenberg and Henckel von Donnersmarck — to designs by Otto Rehnig, who had already built the nearby Hotel Excelsior. It opened with four hundred rooms and a construction bill of about 23 million marks, an unprecedented figure at the time. The façade was heavily ornamented sandstone in the Belle Époque manner, the interiors neo-baroque and neo-rococo, and the halls were designed to be seen in: a Palm Court, a Silver Hall, a breakfast room, and the Kaisersaal itself. In the inner courtyard the garden architect Willi Wendt laid out 1,600 square metres of planting, so that a guest could step out of the noisiest intersection in Europe into something resembling quiet.

## No Women at the Kaiser's Table

Wilhelm II used the hotel as a private club. He is said to have spent many evenings entertaining there, and the rule at those dinners was that no women were admitted — a detail that says as much about the last German Emperor's idea of an evening out as anything in his biography. The war ended that particular custom, and the hotel got a livelier one. In the Golden Twenties the Esplanade became the address for tea dances, held in the afternoon and broadcast live on the radio, so that a Berlin housewife could hear the orchestra playing in a room she would never enter. The guest book ran to Charlie Chaplin and Greta Garbo. For about fifteen years the place did exactly what it had been built to do.

## Carnival Balls in a Rubble Field

Allied bombing in the winter of 1944–45 destroyed about ninety per cent of it, along with most of Potsdamer Platz. What came through more or less intact was oddly specific: the Kaisersaal, the breakfast hall, a stairwell and the washrooms. These were restored after the war into a single surviving fragment known as the Esplanade Hall Building. The Palm Court ceiling was rebuilt; the Silver Hall was redone with silver walls and ceiling and pillars covered in abstract figures. And then, in the middle of a wilderness of brick and weeds where Europe's busiest crossroads had been, this remnant went back to work — a restaurant through the 1950s, then fancy-dress dances, carnival balls and fashion shows. People put on costumes and danced in a rubble field, which is either absurd or the most Berlin thing imaginable.

## A Set on the Wrong Side of Nothing

The Wall went up a short distance away in 1961 and the programme of festivities stopped almost immediately; there was no longer a city around the building to draw an audience from. It remained accessible with police permission, adrift in the death strip's margins, and found a third career as a film set. Bob Fosse shot interiors here for *Cabaret* in 1972, using a real Weimar ballroom to stand in for the Weimar Republic. Wim Wenders came back for *Wings of Desire*, filming in 1986, in which the ruins of Potsdamer Platz are less a location than the subject. After 1989 the fragment was listed as a protected monument — and then the plans for the rebuilt square were drawn, and the Kaisersaal turned out to be standing precisely where the Sony Center wanted to be.

## Seventy-Five Metres and Five Hundred Pieces

The first response was to schedule the ballroom for demolition. In 1993 a better idea arrived: move it. Three years later the move was executed, seventy-five metres across the site, and the Kaisersaal now sits inside the Sony Center's glass-and-steel atrium, an early-twentieth-century room preserved in the middle of a late-twentieth-century one. The breakfast hall was handled differently — taken apart into five hundred numbered pieces and reassembled indoors, where it survives as part of a restaurant. What was once a 400-room hotel is now two rooms and a stairwell, kept because a city that had erased this square twice decided it could not afford to do it a third time.

## Flight Context

The site is at 52.5100°N, 13.3744°E on the northern edge of Potsdamer Platz, in Mitte. The surviving Esplanade fragments are inside the Sony Center, whose tent-like fabric-and-glass roof over the central forum is one of the easiest structures to identify from the air in central Berlin — a pale conical canopy immediately north-west of the Potsdamer Platz towers. Fly it at 2,000–3,000 ft with the Tiergarten's green mass to the north-west and the Brandenburg Gate about 800 m north. EDDB (Berlin Brandenburg) lies roughly 22 km south-east; EDAZ (Schönhagen) about 42 km south-west for general aviation. Tegel and Tempelhof are permanently closed. The high-rise cluster here is the tallest thing between Alexanderplatz and Charlottenburg and reads well even in haze.
