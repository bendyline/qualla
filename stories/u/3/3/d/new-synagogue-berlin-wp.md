# New Synagogue (Berlin)

A Moorish-revival synagogue with three thousand seats, saved from the Kristallnacht mob by a police lieutenant with a legal argument and a service pistol — then bombed, demolished, and rebuilt only as far as its own front door.

**Location:** 52.5247, 13.3944

**Categories:** jewish-history, architecture, berlin, mitte, holocaust, memorial

## Introduction

Before dawn on 10 November 1938, a police lieutenant named Otto Bellgardt walked into a burning synagogue and ordered the men who had set the fire to leave. He had a legal argument and a service pistol, and he used both. The New Synagogue on Oranienburger Straße was a protected historical monument, Bellgardt told them, and he intended to uphold the law that protected it. The arsonists dispersed. The fire brigade got in and put out the blaze before it reached the fabric of the building. Across Germany and Austria that night, synagogues burned by the hundreds. This one — the largest in Germany — did not.

## Knoblauch's Alhambra

Eduard Knoblauch began the building in 1859 for a Berlin Jewish community that had outgrown its old synagogue and was still growing, swelled by immigrants arriving from the east. He did not live to see it finished. After his death in 1865, Friedrich August Stüler took over the construction, the interior, and most of the decisions that give the place its character. What they produced was Moorish revival at its most confident: polychrome brickwork ornamented with sculpted brick and terracotta, accented with coloured glazed tile, and a central dome with gilded ribs flanked by two smaller pavilion domes on the side wings. Berliners compared it to the Alhambra. Less visibly, it was also a monument of early iron construction — iron in the exterior columns, iron in the dome, iron carrying the floor of the main hall. When it was inaugurated in 1866, Otto von Bismarck, then Minister President of Prussia, was in the room.

## Three Thousand Seats

Behind the façade lay a front hall and then the great room itself, seating three thousand — the largest synagogue in the country. The plot's awkward alignment forced the architects to turn the building's axis slightly past the entrance, so the approach and the main hall were never quite on the same line. Services here had an organ and a choir, reflecting the liberal current running through Berlin Jewry at the time, and the hall doubled as a concert venue. In 1930 Albert Einstein played violin in it. Within a few years the character of those concerts had changed. Under the Kulturbund, the Nazi-supervised body administered by Kurt Singer, Jewish musicians were barred from ordinary German concert halls and permitted to perform only for Jewish audiences in Jewish spaces. Synagogues became the venues of last resort for a musical culture being squeezed out of public life.

## The Lieutenant and the Credit

The mob that broke into the Neue Synagoge on the night of 9 November 1938 knew what it was doing. Torah scrolls were desecrated, furniture was smashed, and everything that would burn was piled up inside and lit. Bellgardt, on duty at the local precinct, arrived in the early hours and stopped it. What happened afterwards is nearly as revealing as what happened during. His superior, Senior Lieutenant Wilhelm Krützfeld, who ran the precinct at Hackescher Markt, covered for him, and Berlin's police commissioner, Graf Helldorf, issued Krützfeld nothing worse than a verbal reprimand. Partly because of that cover, Krützfeld spent decades being credited with the rescue — a misattribution that outlived him, until neighbours who had been there that night set the record straight. Elsewhere in the city the outcome was the usual one. The equally grand synagogue on Prinzregentenstraße was plundered, torched, and destroyed. A bronze plaque is all that marks where it stood.

## The Bomb and the Wrecking Ball

The reprieve was temporary. The congregation used the main hall for the last time on 31 March 1940, a Sunday, for a benefit concert; within two years the deportations from Berlin were under way. On the night of 22–23 November 1943, Allied bombers hit Oranienburger Straße, and the hall that had held three thousand people burned out. What the war left, peace finished. In 1958 the East Berlin authorities pressed the surviving Jewish community into demolishing the ruined rear sections — the front hall, the great room, all of it — and the dome came down in the same decade. For thirty years the address was a gap in the streetscape: a wrecked frontage on a socialist boulevard, and nothing behind it.

## Where the Building Stops

Reconstruction began in 1988, in the last years of East Germany, and finished in 1993 with the site reborn as the Centrum Judaicum — archive, museum, and memorial. The gilded ribs are back on the skyline, visible from most of Mitte, and the ornament on the Oranienburger Straße frontage has been rebuilt piece by piece. What has not been rebuilt is the synagogue. The reconstruction stops at the line where the main hall once began: walk in through the entrance, past the restored rooms, and the building simply ends. That truncation is not an oversight. It is the exhibit. In May 1995 a small congregation re-established itself here, meeting at first in a converted cloakroom, and it remains the Berlin community's only Masorti synagogue — three thousand seats reduced to a handful of rooms, a dome, and a deliberate absence.

## Flight Context

The New Synagogue stands at 52.5247°N, 13.3944°E on Oranienburger Straße in Berlin-Mitte, in the district historically known as the Scheunenviertel. The gilded ribbed dome is the surest visual fix in this part of the city — it sits about 1 km northwest of Museum Island and the Berlin Cathedral, and roughly 1.2 km west-northwest of the Fernsehturm at Alexanderplatz, which is the dominant landmark for orientation from any altitude. The Brandenburg Gate is 1.5 km southwest, the Spree curls past 400 m to the south. Berlin Brandenburg Airport (EDDB) lies about 20 km southeast; Schönhagen (EDAZ) is roughly 45 km south. Recommended viewing altitude 1,500–2,500 ft for the dense Mitte street grid. Berlin's controlled airspace requires clearance, and summer haze frequently softens detail over the city centre.
