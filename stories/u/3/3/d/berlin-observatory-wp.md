# Berlin Observatory

The Prussian observatory that found Neptune on the first night anyone bothered to look, funded for a century by a monopoly on printing calendars, and eventually swallowed by the city it was built outside of.

**Location:** 52.5039, 13.3942

**Categories:** Berlin, Astronomy, Science, History, Architecture

## Introduction

The letter reached Berlin on 23 September 1846, which happened to be the director's fifty-fifth birthday. Urbain Le Verrier had worked backwards from the wobbles in the orbit of Uranus to a patch of sky where, he calculated, an undiscovered planet had to be. Other observatories had been offered the calculation and had ignored it. Johann Gottfried Galle asked Johann Franz Encke for permission to spend his birthday evening looking, and Encke agreed. Galle and the student assistant Heinrich Louis d'Arrest went to the telescope, and d'Arrest suggested pulling out a star chart so new it had not yet been posted to other observatories. Within the hour Galle called out an eighth-magnitude star, and d'Arrest, bent over the chart, shouted back that it was not on the map. Neptune was less than a degree from where Le Verrier had said it would be.

## Paid For by the Calendar

The institution that made that night possible had been financed for over a century by a print monopoly. In 1700 the Reichstag decided that the Protestant German states needed a corrected calendar without adopting the Pope's, and the patent for producing it went to a planned observatory in Berlin. Gottfried Kirch was appointed director eight days later; on 11 July, his forty-third birthday, the Elector Frederick III signed the document founding both an Academy and an Observatory, on plans drawn up by Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz. Calendar fees were very nearly its only income for generations. Kirch observed from private rooftops meanwhile, assisted by his wife Maria Margarethe, who discovered the comet of 1702, and his son Christfried. After Kirch died the family kept the work going. His daughter Christine computed the calendar for years and kept the accounts, which is to say she kept the observatory solvent.

## Two Nights a Week for the Public

A proper tower had been built onto the royal stables on Unter den Linden between 1700 and 1711 — twenty-seven metres, one of the first tower observatories of its century — but by the 1820s Berlin's astronomy was outclassed. Both Gauss and Bessel declined the directorship. On Bessel's recommendation the king called Johann Franz Encke in 1825, and Alexander von Humboldt went to work on the treasury. Humboldt got 8,500 taler out of Frederick William III for a nine-inch Fraunhofer refractor — the last great telescope left in the Munich workshop — approved six days after he asked. He also got the king to fund an entire new observatory, on a condition worth remembering: it had to open to the public two evenings a week. Karl Friedrich Schinkel drew the plans. The foundation stone went down in Kreuzberg on 22 October 1832, and Encke moved in on 24 April 1835 with a newly hired assistant named Galle.

## What Came Out of the Cross-Shaped House

Schinkel's building was laid out as a cross in a plain Hellenic style, with a temple front showing Apollo and his chariot in relief on the gable. Where the arms met sat a rotating iron dome 7.5 metres across, the first hemispherical dome with a rotation mechanism in Prussia; its foundations were kept separate from the rest of the structure so footsteps would not shake the instruments. The work that came out of it was constant and mostly unglamorous. Encke picked out the gap in Saturn's A ring in 1837 that now carries his name; Galle found the faint C ring in 1838 and three comets by 1840. Viktor Knorre turned up four asteroids by 1887, and Karl Friedrich Küstner detected the wobble of the Earth's own axis, the polar motion, from measurements made here. Arthur Auwers spent 1866 to 1900 in Berlin compiling his fundamental star catalogue. On the north wing, unveiled on the Kaiser's eighty-second birthday in 1879, was the reference mark from which Prussia measured every height in the kingdom.

## The City Catches Up

The observatory had been deliberately sited on the edge of town. Berlin did not stay that size. By the end of the nineteenth century the building stood boxed in by tenements and streetlights, and serious observing had become close to impossible — the same problem that had driven the institution out of Unter den Linden, repeating itself faster. Wilhelm Foerster, director until his death in 1903, argued from the mid-1890s for a move well outside the metropolitan area. Under Hermann von Struve the plan became concrete, and after test observations from June 1906, Paul Guthnick's recommendation carried the decision: the Schlosspark at Babelsberg in Potsdam. The Kreuzberg observatory was emptied out and torn down in August 1913, and selling the land paid for the new building and its instruments outright. Part of the ground became a street, named Enckestraße in 1927. The old site is still listed under IAU observatory code 548.

## Babelsberg, and What Was Taken

Babelsberg received the first large astronomical instrument Carl Zeiss ever built in Jena, a 65 cm refractor completed in 1915. In 1924 came a 120 cm reflector, then the second largest in the world and the largest in Europe. It did not stay. After the Second World War the reflector was dismantled and shipped to the Soviet Union as reparations, and it is still working there. The remaining building was left a torso until 2002, when it was restored and given a new dome; the library of the Leibniz Institute for Astrophysics Potsdam sits inside it now. Elsewhere in Berlin the scattered family is still visible — the Wilhelm Foerster Observatory, the Urania, and out in Treptow the Archenhold, founded by a man Foerster had once sent off to photograph nebulae.

## Flight Context

The Schinkel observatory stood at 52.504°N, 13.394°E, on the block now bounded by Enckestraße in Berlin-Kreuzberg; nothing of the building survives. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is about 17 km south-southeast; Schönhagen (EDAZ) roughly 35 km southwest. From 1,500–3,000 ft, work south from Potsdamer Platz along the line of Stresemannstraße: the site lies about 1 km southeast, between Friedrichstraße and Lindenstraße, immediately west of the zigzag zinc form of the Jewish Museum, which is the most reliable visual anchor in this part of Kreuzberg. The successor observatory at Babelsberg is 22 km southwest, on a wooded hill in the Schlosspark on the eastern edge of Potsdam. Winter haze and low stratus are common from November through February; on clear autumn nights the light dome over central Berlin makes the original problem here obvious from the air.
