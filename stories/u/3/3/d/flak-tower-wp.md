# Flak Tower

Berlin, Hamburg and Vienna each got concrete gun fortresses with walls three and a half metres thick — and most of them turned out to be easier to bury, climb or convert into hotels than to remove.

**Location:** 52.5265, 13.4319

**Categories:** germany, berlin, history, architecture

## Introduction

There is a hill in Volkspark Friedrichshain that Berliners call Mont Klamott — Rubble Mountain. It has paths, grass, trees, joggers. It is not a hill. Under the topsoil is a block of reinforced concrete roughly seventy metres on a side, built in six months in 1940-41 to shoot down bombers, and after the war the authorities concluded it would be easier to bury the thing than to remove it. Two songwriters have since written about the mound, and the joggers running over the top of it are, most of them, running over a gun platform.

## Six Months, and the Trains Rerouted

The Royal Air Force reached Berlin in 1940 and the shock was political as much as physical. The capital was supposed to be untouchable. Hitler ordered three enormous anti-aircraft towers built to defend it, took a personal interest in the design, and produced some sketches of his own. What followed was an extraordinary allocation of a wartime economy's resources: the national rail timetable was rewritten so that concrete, steel and timber could reach the building sites on schedule. The towers went up in six months. Eight complexes were built in all, three in Berlin, two in Hamburg and three in Vienna, with smaller single-purpose towers at outlying strongpoints from Angers in occupied France to Heligoland in the North Sea.

## Gun Tower and Lead Tower

Each complex came in a pair. The G-tower — Gefechtsturm, the combat or battery tower — carried the guns: eight 12.8 cm FlaK 40 in four twin mounts on the corner platforms, backed by 37 mm weapons and dozens of 20 mm Flakvierling quadruple guns for anything that came in low. A short distance away stood the L-tower, the fire-control tower, carrying the radar and the crews who told the guns where to shoot. Its radar dish retracted behind a concrete and steel dome when the shooting started. The first-generation G-tower was 70.5 metres square and 39 metres tall, with walls up to 3.5 metres thick and roof slabs thicker still. Hitler commissioned studies for towers three times that size and three times that firepower. The war ended first.

## Thirty Thousand People Inside

The guns were only half the purpose. Each tower was built as a shelter, designed for around ten thousand civilians with a hospital ward inside, and by the spring of 1945 those design figures had stopped meaning anything. During the Battle of Berlin as many as thirty thousand people crowded into a single tower, packed into stairwells and corridors while the 12.8 cm guns fired from the platforms above their heads and the concrete carried every round down through the floors. Outside, the Soviets brought up heavy artillery — 203 mm howitzers among the largest they had — and found they could not do meaningful damage. The Zoo tower was never taken. Its garrison surrendered on 30 April 1945, by which point the city around it had already ceased to exist in any recognisable form.

## What Was Stored in the Concrete

If a building cannot be destroyed, it becomes a vault. The Zoo tower in the Tiergarten took in artefacts from Berlin's museums, among them sections of the Pergamon Altar, moved into the safest space anyone could offer them. The Friedrichshain tower held part of the collection of the Kaiser Friedrich Museum. That decision ended badly. In May 1945, with the fighting over and the city ungoverned, fires broke out in the Friedrichshain tower. More than four hundred paintings and around three hundred sculptures were lost — burned, or taken during the chaos, and never recovered. Among them were works by Old Masters that exist now only as catalogue entries and photographs. The concrete held perfectly. Everything inside it did not.

## What Cannot Be Removed

Demolition proved as hard as the Soviets had found bombardment. The British needed two attempts and several tons of explosives to bring down the Zoo tower's G-tower in 1947, and returned in 1948 to finish the work. The L-tower had gone first, in July. Friedrichshain's towers were partly blown, then simply covered over with rubble and soil, which is how Mont Klamott acquired its name and its songs — Wolf Biermann wrote one, the rock band Silly another. At Humboldthain, one face of the G-tower still stands above the park; the interior can be visited, and the exposed wall has become a climbing route. Elsewhere the towers found stranger second lives. Vienna's Esterhazypark L-tower has housed a public aquarium, the Haus des Meeres, since 1957, with a climbing wall on the outside. Hamburg's Heiligengeistfeld G-tower spent years as a nightclub and music school before opening in 2024 as a hotel, five new floors added on top and a rooftop garden of 4,700 trees and shrubs planted over the gun platforms. In Vienna's Augarten a tower sits empty and cabled together with steel bands, home to thousands of pigeons. The Reich planned for a thousand years and accidentally got it: these are the most permanent structures it left behind.

## Flight Context

The Friedrichshain flak tower mound sits at 52.5265° N, 13.4319° E in Volkspark Friedrichshain, about 2.5 km east-northeast of Alexanderplatz. From the air the park reads as a large irregular green space with two distinct wooded knolls rising abruptly from otherwise flat ground — the giveaway, since central Berlin has no natural relief of that shape. The Humboldthain tower, whose exposed concrete face is genuinely visible from above, lies about 4 km northwest at roughly 52.546° N, 13.383° E, next to the Gesundbrunnen rail yards. The Fernsehturm is the dominant landmark 2.5 km southwest. Best viewed from 1,500-2,500 ft AGL on a northwest-southeast track linking Humboldthain, Hauptbahnhof and Volkspark Friedrichshain. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is about 22 km south-southeast; Strausberg (EDAY) lies roughly 30 km east and Schönhagen (EDAZ) southwest for general aviation. Central Berlin is controlled airspace requiring prior coordination; the mounds read best under low winter sun or after leaf-fall.
