# Bebelplatz

An eighteenth-century Berlin square ringed by opera house, cathedral and library, whose most important feature is an empty white room buried under the cobblestones.

**Location:** 52.5164, 13.3939

**Categories:** berlin, germany, mitte, memorials, nazi-germany, squares, architecture, literature

## Introduction

The most important thing on Bebelplatz is a hole, and you have to be standing almost on top of it to know it is there. Set flush into the cobbles near the middle of the square is a pane of glass about the size of a card table. Look down through it and you are looking into a white room, five metres deep, lined floor to ceiling with empty bookshelves. It is lit around the clock. At night it throws a pale glow up out of the pavement, so that the square appears to be leaking light. The room holds nothing, and it is sized precisely: it would take twenty thousand books, which is roughly what was burned here on the evening of 10 May 1933.

## A Square Built Around a Refusal

Frederick II of Prussia wanted a Forum Fridericianum here, on a grand scale. He did not get it, because his distant cousin Frederick William, Margrave of Brandenburg-Schwedt, owned a palace in the middle of the plan and declined to sell it for demolition. The king scaled his ambitions down and worked around the house — while extracting his cousin's garden and outbuildings in compensation, on which he promptly put the Royal Library.

The result, laid out between 1741 and 1743 as the Platz am Opernhaus, is cramped and almost perfectly preserved in outline: the State Opera on one side, St. Hedwig's Cathedral to the southeast — the first Catholic church built in Prussia after the Reformation — the Alte Bibliothek and the Old Palace to the west, and the palace of the king's brother Prince Henry, now the main building of Humboldt University. A detail on the boulevard outside gives away the older city: there are no linden trees on the stretch of Unter den Linden running east toward the Berlin Palace, because the baroque fortifications once stood there. The avenue starts where the walls ended.

## The Evening of 10 May

The books had been arriving for four days. On 6 May students dragged the library of the Institut für Sexualwissenschaft across the city and dumped it in the square. The burning itself was organised not by the Nazi party's own student body but by the nationalist German Student Association, which staged it first and stole the march. Parallel burnings took place that night in university cities across Germany.

Joseph Goebbels, the propaganda minister, was invited to speak and gave an inflammatory address before the pyre was lit. Members of the Nazi students' league, the SA, the SS and Hitler Youth groups filled the square along with ordinary spectators — by one count some seventy thousand people. About twenty thousand books went into the fire: Heinrich Mann, Erich Maria Remarque, Heinrich Heine, Karl Marx, Albert Einstein, Sigmund Freud, Kurt Tucholsky. Erich Kästner stood in the crowd and watched his own work burn. "I heard the wheedling tirades of the small, cunning liar," he wrote afterwards. "Funeral weather hung over the city."

## Heine Was Not Predicting Anything

A bronze plaque set into the paving carries one sentence: Das war ein Vorspiel nur, dort wo man Bücher verbrennt, verbrennt man am Ende auch Menschen. That was only a prelude; where they burn books, they will in the end also burn people.

It comes from Heinrich Heine's tragedy Almansor, and it is worth knowing what he was actually writing about. In the play the character Hassan says it on hearing that the Quran is being burned in the marketplace of Granada after the Christian conquest — a line about religious persecution in fifteenth-century Spain, written by a German Jewish poet more than a century before the National Socialists existed. Heine's own books were on the fire in 1933. The sentence became a prophecy by being quoted at the thing it described.

## The Empty Library

Micha Ullman's memorial went in during 1995, after a design competition two years earlier. It is called The Empty Library, and it refuses almost every convention of monument-making: nothing rises, nothing is inscribed on it, there is no figure and no dedication. A subterranean room measuring roughly 5.3 by 7 by 7 metres sits under the square, its walls fitted with plain white shelves and its volume calculated against the number of books destroyed.

You reach it only with your eyes, through the glass, and you have to lean over and look down — which is the point. The chamber is air-conditioned to stop condensation from fogging the view and lit continuously. The pane takes a beating from feet and weather and has to be replaced roughly every three months. A memorial to an absence, kept transparent at considerable ongoing expense.

## What a Square Is For

The square has changed names with the century. On 12 August 1910 it became Kaiser-Franz-Josef-Platz, for the Austrian emperor's eightieth birthday. Air raids and the Battle of Berlin then destroyed most of the buildings around it; the ensemble was rebuilt in the 1950s, and on 31 August 1947 the square was renamed for August Bebel, a founder of the Social Democratic Party.

What it should now be used for is a live argument. Humboldt students hold a book sale here every year on the anniversary. In 2006 it filled with more than 180 United Buddy Bears, each two metres tall and painted by a different artist. It has also hosted a winter skating rink and a fashion week party tent, both of which drew objections, and in 2012 a proposal to sink a car park for opera-goers beneath the square — and around the memorial — brought protests. A square with a glass panel in it is not a neutral surface, and Berlin has never quite agreed on what may be done above the room.

## Flight Context

Bebelplatz lies at 52.5164 N, 13.3939 E, on the south side of Unter den Linden in Berlin-Mitte. It is small — about 100 by 100 metres — so identify it by its neighbours rather than by itself. The dead-straight east-west line of Unter den Linden is the primary reference, running from the Brandenburg Gate about 1.2 km west to the Spree island about 400 m east, where the Berlin Cathedral's green copper dome and the reconstructed Berlin Palace (Humboldt Forum) are unmistakable, with the Fernsehturm's silver ball just beyond. Bebelplatz itself reads as a pale open rectangle immediately south of the boulevard, wrapped around the columned portico of the State Opera, with the domed drum of St. Hedwig's Cathedral on its southeast corner — that dome is the surest single marker. This is dense controlled airspace over the government and museum quarter; plan any low pass at 1,500-2,500 ft AGL along the boulevard and expect clearance limits. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is about 18 km southeast, Schönhagen (EDAZ) about 36 km south-southwest. After dark the memorial's lit shaft is visible from directly overhead as a small bright square in an otherwise dim plaza.
