# M-Bahn

Berlin's magnetic levitation railway spent seven years in testing, ran a proper ticketed service for thirteen days, and was then dismantled.

**Location:** 52.5110, 13.3718

**Categories:** Berlin, Rail transport, Cold War, Engineering, Transport history

## Introduction

Berlin's magnetic levitation railway carried fare-paying passengers on a published timetable for exactly thirteen days. Final approval as a public transport system came on 18 July 1991. The last train ran on 31 July. Behind those two weeks lay seven years of testing, a spectacular crash, and a piece of infrastructure built to solve a problem that had stopped existing twenty months before the tickets went on sale. The M-Bahn was not a failure of engineering. It was a perfectly good answer to a question the city had, until 9 November 1989, when the Berlin Wall opened and the question evaporated overnight.

## The Hole the Wall Made

The very first stretch of the Berlin U-Bahn included an elevated run between Gleisdreieck and Potsdamer Platz, threading above the streets on iron viaduct. Partition wrecked it. Gleisdreieck ended up in West Berlin while Potsdamer Platz station sat directly beneath the boundary with East Berlin, and once the Wall went up in 1961 trains from both sides simply stopped short — from the eastern side, at Mohrenstraße. Around 1972 the two remaining western stations before Potsdamer Platz closed as well, since another U-Bahn line already covered the same ground. That left the West Berlin district beside Potsdamer Platz with a viaduct running through it and no service on it. Something had to fill the gap, and the something the city chose was a maglev.

## Eighty-Five Percent Floating

The M-Bahn was propelled by a long stator linear motor, the same broad principle behind the Transrapid, but its designers made a pragmatic compromise their rivals did not. Only about 85 percent of the vehicle's weight rode on magnetic levitation; the remainder sat on conventional wheels. That decision simplified everything downstream — including the awkward business of crossing points, where a stretch of ordinary rail beneath the guideway carried the train over the gap at the crossover just south of Kemperplatz. The trains ran driverless under full automation, though the system had been designed so that a human could take control if needed. The route was 1.6 kilometres long with three stations: the disused U-Bahn platforms at Gleisdreieck, plus two new stops at Bernburger Straße and Kemperplatz. It was the second maglev in the world to open to public traffic, after Birmingham's airport shuttle and well before Shanghai's.

## Seven Years of Almost

Trial running without passengers started on 28 June 1984, and the years that followed were slow going. On 19 December 1988, during manual testing, a train entered Kemperplatz far too fast and punched through the station's glazed outer wall. One vehicle fell and was written off. The other hung six metres in the air for several days before it could be brought down. Public service finally began on 28 August 1989, but only as an extended trial with passengers aboard: intermittent, not guaranteed, and free of charge, because the system was still not certified as a common carrier. Ten weeks later the Wall opened, and the entire logic of the line quietly collapsed.

## Thirteen Days

Reunification made restoring the severed U2 both possible and obviously desirable, and the M-Bahn stood squarely on the alignment that line needed. Even so, the certification process ground on to its conclusion. On 18 July 1991 the M-Bahn was formally licensed as a passenger transport system, joined the BVG fare structure, and started running to a proper schedule like any other Berlin service. On 31 July the last train ran. The guideway came down, the U2 tunnel was rebuilt, the border fortifications were cleared out of it, and on 13 November 1993 through service resumed between the Zoo and Alexanderplatz, the line whole again after more than thirty years.

## A Ghost in Wings of Desire

There is one place the M-Bahn's Berlin still exists. Wim Wenders shot Wings of Desire in 1987, and the disused elevated track and the wasteland around Potsdamer Platz appear at length in the background — an emptied, weed-grown quarter that had been one of the busiest intersections in Europe before the war and would become a district of glass towers within a decade of the film's release. When Wenders was filming, the maglev on that viaduct was still the future of Berlin transit. Nobody in the frame knew that the future would arrive, be approved, sell tickets for less than a fortnight, and be taken apart for scrap.

## Flight Context

The M-Bahn ran between Gleisdreieck and Kemperplatz, just west of Potsdamer Platz, centred near 52.511°N, 13.372°E in the heart of Berlin. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) lies about 18 km southeast; the vast lozenge of the former Tempelhof airfield (the closed EDDI) sits 4 km south and is the single most useful visual anchor in the district. From 2,000–4,000 ft the Potsdamer Platz towers stand out against the green rectangle of the Tiergarten to the north, with the Gleisdreieck rail junction and its park immediately south. Central Berlin is dense controlled airspace under EDDB's terminal area, so check clearances before overflight. Nothing of the guideway remains.
