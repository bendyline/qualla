# Gethsemane Church

In the autumn of 1989 a red-brick church in Prenzlauer Berg stopped locking its doors, and became the room where East Germany's opposition could finally be counted.

**Location:** 52.5477, 13.4161

**Categories:** Lutheran churches in Berlin, Churches completed in 1893, Buildings and structures in Pankow, Peaceful Revolution, Heritage sites in Berlin

## Introduction

From 2 October 1989 the doors were simply left open — day and night, for weeks, in a country where every other room had an owner and a list. The Gethsemane Church took its motto from the passage its name refers to, the garden at the foot of the Mount of Olives where Jesus asked his disciples to stay awake with him: watch and pray. Thousands came. They stood on the plaza at the corner of Stargarder and Greifenhagener Straße, lit candles, read the names of people who had been arrested, and argued out loud about the future of the German Democratic Republic. The church had been standing there, unremarkable and well attended, for ninety-six years. It took five weeks in one autumn to make it the most closely watched building in East Berlin.

## A Parish Before There Was a Neighbourhood

The land was a gift. Caroline Griebenow, who owned a good deal of property in the district, donated the site — and it was refused at first, on the grounds that nothing had been built around it yet. Prenzlauer Berg was filling so fast at the end of the nineteenth century that the argument did not last. The Zionskirche congregation had outgrown its own building, so it commissioned the architect who had built that one, August Orth, city building councillor, to carve a new parish out of its northern half. The cornerstone went down on 20 March 1891 and the church was inaugurated on 26 February 1893, with Wilhelm II present in his capacity as supreme governor of the Prussian state church; he chose the name. Orth gave it red brick, buttresses and pinnacles, a copper spire over a vaulted entrance hall, and a plan that hedges between traditions — a long nave read from the outside, but inside a broad octagon under the crossing, galleries running round all sides but the east, so that the congregation could see and hear a preacher standing among them. The street bends slightly at the crossroads, which means the tower is visible from both ends of Stargarder Straße. It was designed to be the thing you look at.

## The Only Rooms the State Did Not Run

By the 1980s the building had acquired a second function nobody had designed for it. East German churches were infiltrated by informers and everyone knew it, but they were also the only spaces in the country not organised from above — which made them the only places where opponents of the regime could meet and count each other. Gethsemane became one of the most important. People turned up for peace prayers, for public debates, for rogation prayers said for those under arrest, and many of them were not parishioners or even believers. The congregation hosted part of the Protestant church assembly in 1987 and drew visitors from across the republic. Then on 17 January 1988, demonstrators were arrested at the official annual march for Rosa Luxemburg and Karl Liebknecht for carrying a banner quoting Luxemburg herself: freedom is always the freedom of the one who thinks differently. When the local elections of 7 May 1988 were shown to have been rigged, the numbers grew again.

## Watch and Pray

The fortieth anniversary of the GDR fell on 7 October 1989. The state had planned parades. On Schönhauser Allee, a few steps from the church, police and Stasi units moved on the crowds with force; some demonstrators made it through the doors, and hundreds were arrested, a number of them held for weeks. Inside, the vigil went on. Two days later Gottfried Forck, bishop of the eastern half of the Berlin-Brandenburg church, stood in the building and called for credible steps toward a democratic, law-governed future for East Germany — a sentence that would have been unthinkable in public a month earlier. Within five weeks the border was open. In March 1990, members of the first freely elected Volkskammer came here for a service before their first session, which is a strange and moving thing for a parliament to do, and entirely logical given where the previous eighteen months had happened.

## What the Building Kept

The church survived the Second World War intact, and it has collected the things other places lost. In the south transept stands Wilhelm Groß's expressionist wooden *Praying Christ* of 1923, showing the moment in the garden when Jesus asks for the cup to pass from him; it was given in honour of parishioners killed in the First World War. Outside the west portal, since 1993, stands a statue of the blessing Christ rescued from the Church of Reconciliation before the East German government blew that building up in 1985 to clear sightlines along the Wall. A copy of Ernst Barlach's *Geistkämpfer* was installed by the south façade on 8 November 1994, for the people who fought for democracy here. And in the churchyard is a plaque by Karl Biedermann commemorating German resistance to the Nazis, which the GDR refused to let stand on the site it had been made for, because it lacked the required heroic symbolism. It was put up on church land instead, on 3 October 1990 — the day the country that had rejected it ceased to exist.

## Flight Context

Gethsemane Church stands at 52.5477°N, 13.4161°E in Prenzlauer Berg, about 100 m east of Schönhauser Allee and adjacent to the combined S-Bahn and U-Bahn station of the same name. The copper spire rises 62 m and is the tallest thing for several blocks, making it easy to pick out against the uniform five-storey tenement grid of the northern inner city. From 2,500–4,000 ft, line up on the dead-straight run of Schönhauser Allee heading north-north-east from Alexanderplatz; the church sits just east of it, roughly 3.5 km north of the Fernsehturm, which is the obvious primary landmark. EDDB (Berlin Brandenburg) is about 28 km to the south-south-east; EDAY (Strausberg) lies roughly 30 km east for general aviation. Berlin's terrain is flat and the approach unobstructed, though winter low cloud frequently sits below 1,500 ft.
