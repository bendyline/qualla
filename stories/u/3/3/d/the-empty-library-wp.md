# The Empty Library

Micha Ullman's memorial on Bebelplatz is a white room of empty shelves sunk beneath the cobblestones, sized for the twenty thousand books the Nazis burned above it on 10 May 1933.

**Location:** 52.5165, 13.3939

**Categories:** berlin, germany, memorials, public-art, world-war-ii, jewish-history, censorship, mitte

## Introduction

Micha Ullman's parents left Germany in 1933. He was born in Tel Aviv six years later, and sixty-two years after they got out he was the artist chosen to mark the square in Berlin where the fires had been lit. What he proposed was to take something away. Nothing stands on Bebelplatz. There is a pane of glass set flush into the cobbles, easy to walk over without noticing, and if you stop and look down you see a small white room lined with empty shelves. It is lit day and night. The shelves would hold about twenty thousand books, which is roughly the number that burned here.

## A Dreary, Rainy Evening

It happened on 10 May 1933, and the weather was bad. Students of the National Socialist German Students' League carried armfuls of books out of the Old Library of the Friedrich-Wilhelms-Universität — Humboldt University today — and into the middle of what was then Kaiser-Franz-Josef-Platz. A good many of their professors came with them. SA and SS bands played. A large crowd stood in the rain to watch.

Joseph Goebbels, the Reich Minister of Propaganda, spoke at the fire. The era of exaggerated Jewish intellectualism was at an end, he said; the future German man would not merely be a man of books; and at this late hour he entrusted to the flames the intellectual garbage of the past. Over twenty thousand volumes went in — Jewish authors above all, and communist, liberal and socially critical writers with them. Thirty-four more burnings were staged across Germany in the same month.

## The Lists Came First

This was not a mob that got out of hand. On 6 April 1933 the German Student Association's Main Office for Press and Propaganda announced a nationwide campaign against what it called the un-German spirit, to culminate in a Säuberung — a cleansing — by fire. Local chapters were sent blacklists to circulate: Jewish, Marxist, socialist, anti-family and anti-German literature, itemised so that nobody would have to think about it.

Then the students went into their own libraries and their neighbours' shelves and pulled the named books out. Universities were among the first German institutions to Nazify themselves, and they did it enthusiastically, from below. That is the detail that unsettles people who stand on this square: the arson was committed by the reading class, with reading lists, in front of their teachers.

## Heine Saw It Coming

Set into the pavement beside the glass is a bronze plaque carrying a sentence Heinrich Heine wrote in 1821, in his play Almansor: that was only a prelude; where they burn books, they will in the end also burn people.

Heine was writing about Spain, about the burning of the Quran in Granada after the Reconquista, and he meant it as a warning about how one kind of destruction leads to another. A hundred and twelve years later, on this square, his own books went into the fire. The line has become the most quoted sentence in German memorial culture, and it is quoted so often partly because it is unbearably exact — the same city, the same regime, twelve years apart.

## The Shape of an Absence

The room below the glass is a cube: 706 centimetres square, 5.3 metres deep, lined floor to ceiling in white shelving. It is air-conditioned so that condensation does not fog the pane, and lit continuously, which means it glows faintly at night in the middle of a dark plaza.

Ullman's sculpture has always circled absence and memory, and here he built almost nothing. There is no flame, no torn page, no heroic figure, no explanatory tableau. There is a volume of missing books, given exact dimensions, placed under your feet. Visitors lean over the glass and mostly see their own reflection first, then the shelves, then how deep the room goes. The memorial was unveiled in 1995, and it does its work in a few seconds without saying a word.

## Life on Top of It

Years after the memorial was finished, a parking garage was excavated beneath Bebelplatz. Ullman objected in public, and his argument was not about noise or engineering. The point of the piece was that the ground under this square held a void; filling the rest of that ground with cars changed what the void meant. The garage was built anyway. It does contain an access route, which is how maintenance workers reach the room to clean it twice a year.

Above, ordinary life continues, which the design anticipated. The plaza sits off Unter den Linden between the Berlin State Opera and the green dome of St Hedwig's Cathedral, and it is a working city square, crossed all day by students, tourists and people on their way somewhere else. Most of them never look down. A memorial that can be missed is a strange thing to build on purpose, and it is the reason this one keeps working.

## Flight Context

Located at 52.5165 N, 13.3939 E on Bebelplatz in Berlin-Mitte, on the south side of Unter den Linden. The memorial itself is a single pane of glass in a cobbled square and cannot be seen from the air at all — navigate to the plaza instead. Unter den Linden is the obvious line: a straight tree-lined boulevard running east-west, with the Brandenburg Gate about 1.1 km west of Bebelplatz and Museum Island and the Berlin Cathedral's dome about 500 m northeast. Bebelplatz is the open rectangle immediately south of the boulevard, framed by the classical portico of the Berlin State Opera and the green copper dome of St Hedwig's Cathedral; the Fernsehturm stands about 1.2 km northeast as a coarse fix. Best at 1,500-2,500 ft AGL, and note that this is the most tightly controlled airspace in Berlin, directly over the government quarter. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) lies about 18 km southeast; Schönhagen (EDAZ) about 38 km south-southwest. Low winter sun on the glass occasionally throws a bright point in the plaza, visible from a few hundred feet.
