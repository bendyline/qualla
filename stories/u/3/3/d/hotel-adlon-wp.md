# Hotel Adlon

Berlin's most famous hotel survived the bombing of the city and then burned down over its own wine cellar, on the day the garrison surrendered.

**Location:** 52.5160, 13.3799

**Categories:** Berlin, Hotels, Architecture, World War II, Cold War

## Introduction

The bombs missed it. Through the raids that flattened Pariser Platz and hollowed out Unter den Linden, the Hotel Adlon stayed up — scorched, pressed into use as a military field hospital in the last days of the Battle of Berlin, but standing. What finished it was the wine cellar. On 2 May 1945, with the city's garrison surrendering above ground, Red Army soldiers found the stock below it. A fire started there and went up through the building. Berlin's most famous hotel, the one that had outlasted an empire, a republic and a dictatorship, burned down on the day the fighting ended.

## A Wine Merchant Asks the Kaiser for a Favour

Lorenz Adlon came from Mainz and made his money in wine and restaurants, running coffeehouses around Berlin including one in the Zoological Garden. In 1905 he bought two properties on Unter den Linden, having convinced himself — and then Kaiser Wilhelm II — that the capital of the German Empire was being embarrassed by Vienna, Paris and London, each of which had built a grand hotel while Berlin had not. One obstacle stood on the corner he wanted: the Palais Redern, a Neo-Renaissance landmark Karl Friedrich Schinkel had designed in 1830. The Kaiser interceded with its owners. Adlon bought the palace and demolished it. Carl Gause and Robert Leibnitz drew the replacement, which cost 20 million gold marks; two million of that was the bulk of Adlon's personal fortune.

## Number One Unter den Linden

Behind a deliberately sober façade sat the most modern hotel in Germany: hot and cold running water throughout, an on-site laundry, and its own power plant. The lobby carried enormous square marble columns. There was a palm court, a library, a music room, a ladies' lounge, a barber, a cigar shop and an interior garden built around a Japanese-themed elephant fountain. Absorbing the neighbouring Hotel Reichshof gave the Adlon 305 rooms, 391 beds and 140 bathrooms. The furniture came from Bembé of Mainz, the firm where Lorenz Adlon had once served as an apprentice carpenter. It opened on 23 October 1907 with the Kaiser in attendance, and because the rooms in the Stadtschloss were cold and drafty, Wilhelm took to paying an annual retainer so that suites stood ready for his guests. Tsar Nicholas II stayed. So did Thomas Edison, Henry Ford, John D. Rockefeller, Walther Rathenau, Gustav Stresemann and Thomas Mann. The address was the advertising: Number One Unter den Linden, because the avenue was numbered from the Brandenburg Gate end. In late 1936 the street was renumbered from the other direction and the Adlon became No. 77.

## The Man Who Would Not Look

Lorenz Adlon remained a monarchist after the Kaiser abdicated, with peculiar practical consequences. The central archway of the Brandenburg Gate had been reserved for the Kaiser alone, so Adlon could not accept that ordinary traffic would now pass through it. He crossed in front of it without looking. A car struck him there in 1918. Three years later, at exactly the same spot, another car struck him, and he died of his injuries on 7 April 1921. His son Louis took over with his wife Tilli and their five children; within months, at a New Year's Eve party, Louis met a German-born, American-raised guest named Hedwig Leythen — Hedda — and divorced Tilli to marry her.

## The Lobby in the Government Quarter

The Adlon sat among the embassies, a few blocks from the Reich Chancellery, which made it useful to whoever held power. The Nazis themselves preferred the Kaiserhof, closer to the Propaganda Ministry, but the Adlon kept its role as the city's social centre — the Foreign Press Ball, state visits, Himmler and Vidkun Quisling photographed in its rooms in 1942. It hosted quieter business too. In March 1933 the managing directors of the Jewish-owned Hermann Tietz department store group were called to a meeting here with their bank creditors and officials from the Reich Economics Ministry; within weeks the firm, the KaDeWe included, had been taken from them. The Adlons were not spared the decade either. Money trouble forced Louis to sell the hotel's Wilhelmstraße annex to the government in 1938, and it became offices for the Reich Ministry of Food and Agriculture. On 25 April 1945, Soviet troops arrested Louis at his home near Potsdam, reading his title of Generaldirektor as a general's rank. He died on a street in Falkensee on 7 May, heart failure according to the death certificate.

## Sixteen Rooms, and Then a New Building

The damaged wings at 70a and 70b Wilhelmstraße survived the fire and reopened in August 1945 with sixteen guest rooms and a restaurant set up in a former function room. It still called itself the Hotel Adlon, but it now stood in the Soviet sector and had nothing to do with the family; a man named Georg Behlert ran it. East Germany expropriated the property in December 1950 and pulled down the ruined main building. The stub on Wilhelmstraße limped on as a lodging house and was finally demolished in March 1984. Hedda Adlon had already played the only card left: in 1957 she signed contracts with Kempinski granting the company the right to buy the name and the site, should rebuilding ever become possible. She died in 1967, twenty-two years before anyone could seriously imagine it. The new Hotel Adlon Kempinski opened on Pariser Platz on 23 August 1997, a new building drawn in the spirit of the old one, facing the Brandenburg Gate across a square that had spent three decades as a death strip. It keeps the 1936 address.

## Flight Context

The Adlon stands at 52.516°N, 13.380°E, on the south side of Pariser Platz where Unter den Linden meets the Brandenburg Gate. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is about 20 km southeast; Schönhagen (EDAZ) roughly 40 km southwest. From 1,500–3,000 ft the site is unmistakable: follow the green rectangle of the Tiergarten east until it ends at the Straße des 17. Juni, and the Gate sits at that hinge with Pariser Platz immediately behind it and the dead-straight axis of Unter den Linden running east toward the Berlin Cathedral dome and the TV tower. The hotel is the block on the square's southern edge. Central Berlin is controlled airspace — check EDDB approach sectors before descending, and expect summer haze along the Spree in the mornings.
