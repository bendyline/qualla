# Soviet War Memorial (Treptower Park)

Ten hectares of Berlin park where about seven thousand Red Army soldiers are buried, beneath a twelve-metre bronze soldier that Germany is treaty-bound to maintain.

**Location:** 52.4861, 13.4717

**Categories:** Berlin, World War II, Monuments and memorials, Cemeteries, Cold War, East Germany

## Introduction

Most people walk the whole length of it before they realise they are in a cemetery. The axis opens with a woman kneeling in stone, passes through a gate made of two red granite flags with a soldier kneeling beneath each one, and then runs down a long sunken lawn flanked by sixteen limestone sarcophagi to a bronze soldier twelve metres tall on a mound at the far end. It reads as choreography, and it is. But the lawn is not decoration. Under it, in ten hectares of Treptower Park, lie about seven thousand soldiers of the Red Army — a small share of the roughly eighty thousand Soviet troops killed taking Berlin in the last days of April and the first days of May 1945. The monument is a grave. Everything else about it is argument.

## Sixteen Republics, One Sentence

The sarcophagi come in pairs down the length of the burial field, eight to a side, one for each of the sixteen Soviet republics as they stood in 1949. Each carries a carved relief of the war and a passage from Stalin, cut in Russian on one face and German on the other: eternal glory to the soldiers of the Soviet Army who gave their lives in the battle to free humanity from fascist enslavement. At the head of it all stands Yevgeny Vuchetich's soldier — seventy tonnes of bronze, a sword lowered in one hand, a German child carried in the other arm, a swastika splitting under his boots. Behind him the mound is built as a kurgan, the burial hill of the Eurasian steppe, so that the whole composition ends in a form older than any of the politics it carries. Three metres of grieving mother open the sequence; seventy tonnes of victorious son close it.

## The Sergeant and the Child

Marshal Vasily Chuikov gave the statue a source. During the final assault on the city centre, he said, a guards sergeant had gone out under German machine-gun fire to carry a three-year-old German girl to safety after her mother was killed. The soldier is usually identified as Nikolai Masalov, who lived from 1922 to 2001 and described picking up a crying child near the Potsdamer Brücke on 30 April 1945. Vuchetich himself declined the story. The figure, he insisted, was purely symbolic — not a portrait of any one man on any one afternoon. Both things ended up being true in the way monuments make them true. Berliners point at it and tell Masalov's story, and the sculptor's disclaimer sits in the footnotes where almost nobody reads it.

## Thirty-Three Designs and a Sports Field

The competition drew thirty-three entries, and the built result is a hybrid: the architect Yakov Belopolsky, the sculptor Vuchetich, the painter Alexander Gorpenko and the engineer Sarra Walerius each contributed to the scheme that won. Work began in June 1946 on a Treptower Park sports ground and ran for three years, employing some 1,200 workers, 200 stonemasons and 90 sculptors; the bronzes and the flame bowls were cast at the Lauchhammer art foundry in 1948. It has been repeated for decades that the granite came from the ruins of Hitler's Reich Chancellery, which makes a satisfying story and is not the case. The memorial opened on 8 May 1949, four years to the day after the war in Europe ended, and served for the next four decades as East Germany's central war memorial — the site to which schoolchildren, soldiers and delegations were taken.

## January 1990

As the Wall came down, someone sprayed anti-Soviet graffiti across the stonework. The ruling party's successor organisation called the vandals right-wing extremists and summoned a demonstration for 3 January 1990; a quarter of a million East Germans came. It was one of the last mass rallies the old apparatus was able to conjure, and the historian Stefan Wolle has argued that Stasi officers, fearing for their jobs, may have done the spraying themselves to manufacture the threat. What settled the memorial's future was not that rally but the Two Plus Four Treaty: Germany took on the upkeep of Soviet war graves and monuments and agreed to consult Moscow before altering them. Helmut Kohl and Boris Yeltsin stood here together on 31 August 1994, as the last Russian troops left. Since 1995 a vigil has been held every 9 May.

## A Difficult Inheritance

None of this makes the place comfortable. Berliners have long attached bitter nicknames to the statue that refer to the mass rapes committed by Soviet troops during the occupation — the memorial and the crimes belong to the same army and the same months, and the city has never found a way to hold both facts in one sentence. The €1.5 million restoration of 2003–04, when the soldier was barged to Rügen to be cleaned, re-patinated and fitted with 2,200 new screws before returning on 4 May 2004, drew objections from people who had been deported to the USSR and never compensated. Since Russia's invasion of Ukraine, calls to remove the monument have grown louder again, and the treaty obligation makes that difficult. Meanwhile the seven thousand men under the grass stay where they were put, which is the one thing everyone involved agrees on.

## Flight Context

The memorial lies at 52.486°N, 13.472°E in Treptower Park, on the south bank of the Spree in Berlin-Treptow. From 1,500–3,000 ft the site is one of the easiest targets in the city: a long pale axis cut through a big wooded rectangle, with the mound and the bronze soldier at its south-eastern end and the twin granite flags forming a distinct notch partway along. The Spree makes a wide south-easterly bend immediately north of the park, and the Archenhold Observatory's domes sit at the park's south-eastern edge. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is about 14 km almost due south, Schönhagen (EDAZ) roughly 40 km south-west, Strausberg (EDAY) about 30 km north-east. The Fernsehturm at Alexanderplatz, 5 km north-west, makes the simplest run-in. Expect controlled airspace throughout, and winter haze from November to February.
