# Oberbaum Bridge

Named after a spiked tree trunk, built as a fake medieval gate, blown apart in 1945 and cut in half by the border — the Oberbaumbrücke is Berlin's most photographed reconciliation.

**Location:** 52.5019, 13.4456

**Categories:** bridges, Berlin, Berlin Wall, architecture, history, Cold War

## Introduction

Berlin's prettiest bridge is named after a piece of anti-smuggling equipment. Every night at this bend in the Spree, a heavy tree trunk studded with iron spikes was lowered across the water to close the river to boats — the Oberbaum, the upstream boom, where the customs wall thrown around the city in 1732 met the river. A wooden drawbridge crossed beside it and served as the water gate into town. What stands there now is something else entirely: twin brick towers, pointed arches and cross vaults, an U-Bahn train sliding across the upper deck every few minutes. It is a Prussian architect's romantic idea of what a river gate ought to look like, built in 1896, long after the boom itself had stopped mattering.

## A Gate for a City That Had Outgrown Its Gates

By 1879 the wooden bridge had been patched and widened into Berlin's longest crossing at 154 metres, and it still could not carry the traffic. Plans for a stone replacement ran straight into Siemens & Halske, then designing the city's first elevated railway, who insisted the new bridge take road vehicles, pedestrians and trains together. Otto Stahn, an architect and government official, gave them all three and dressed the result in North German Brick Gothic: arcades, coats of arms, and two towers modelled on the Mitteltorturm in the Brandenburg town of Prenzlau. The towers hold nothing up. They are pure theatre, a reminder that this had once been the river gateway to Berlin. The bridge opened in 1896, timed for the Berlin Trades Exhibition. Six years later the first stretch of U-Bahn ran across it, nineteen passengers aboard, from Stralauer Tor at the eastern end to Potsdamer Platz.

## Cut, and Cut Again

In April 1945 the Wehrmacht dynamited the middle span to slow the Red Army's advance into the city. It failed to stop the Red Army and succeeded in leaving a hole in the bridge. When the victors divided Berlin into sectors, the line between the American and Soviet zones ran along the Spree here, so the crossing linked Kreuzberg in the West with Friedrichshain in the East. For a decade this hardly mattered; pedestrians, cars and trams came and went. Then the Wall went up in August 1961 and the bridge became border. Because the whole width of the river lay inside Friedrichshain, the East German fortifications ran right up to the Kreuzberg waterline. The West Berlin U-Bahn was cut short at Schlesisches Tor. From 21 December 1963 the Oberbaumbrücke served as a pedestrian crossing open only to West Berlin residents, made permanent by the Four Power Agreement of 1972. The towers were pulled down in the 1970s.

## The Water Belonged to the East

That detail — the border running along the Kreuzberg bank rather than down the middle of the channel — killed four children. Anyone who fell from the western shore was in East Berlin the instant they hit the water, and West Berlin firefighters who went in after them risked being shot. Cengaver Katrancı, aged eight, drowned near the bridge on 30 October 1972. Siegfried Kroboth, five, went into the river along the same stretch of bank in May 1973. In June 1974 it was Giuseppe Savoca, six, fishing with a stick until he lost his balance. Then, on 11 May 1975, five-year-old Çetin Mert chased a ball down the embankment at the Gröbenufer. It was his birthday; he was wearing new sneakers. West Berlin crews arrived within the hour and were refused permission to enter the water. East German guards reached the scene at ten past one. Divers recovered him five metres from the western shore. On 29 October 1975 the two sides finally signed an agreement on rescue operations in Berlin's border waters, and an emergency call column went up at the southern bridgehead the following year.

## Calatrava's Gap

After November 1989 the bridge had to be made whole. Reconstruction began in January 1992: the brickwork was restored to Stahn's design, towers and all, but the span the Wehrmacht had blown out was replaced with something deliberately of its own time — a steel middle section by the Spanish architect Santiago Calatrava, light and skeletal between the heavy Gothic ends. The bridge reopened to pedestrians and traffic on 9 November 1994, five years to the day after the Wall opened, with the U-Bahn to Warschauer Straße following a year later. Since 1997 a neon installation by Thorsten Goldberg has played out above the roadway: two hands locked in an endless game of rock, paper, scissors, a comment on how arbitrarily permission to cross gets granted — in the Cold War, and now for asylum seekers arriving in Germany.

## What Crosses It Now

Since 2001, Friedrichshain and Kreuzberg have been a single borough, and the bridge no longer crosses any boundary at all; it simply carries the U1 over the water past the East Side Gallery. Its silhouette is on the borough's coat of arms. Filmmakers keep coming back — Lola sprints across it in Run Lola Run, and John le Carré chose it as the place where Karla walks over to George Smiley in Smiley's People, which is the correct instinct: no other bridge in Europe looks so much like the boundary between two worlds. In April 2019 climate activists blockaded it, which is what people do now with a structure that used to be sealed by soldiers. From the Kreuzberg side at dusk, the towers light up and the trains rattle over, and the water below looks like nothing much at all.

## Flight Context

The Oberbaum Bridge crosses the Spree at 52.502°N, 13.446°E, linking Kreuzberg on the south bank with Friedrichshain on the north, about 4 km southeast of Berlin's historic centre. From the air the twin brick towers and the double-deck structure make it the most identifiable crossing on this reach of the river; the East Side Gallery runs 1.3 km northeast along Mühlenstraße from its northern end, and the 1990s Molecule Man sculpture stands in the river a short way upstream. Best appreciated at low level in clear conditions. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) lies about 20 km southeast; the Fernsehturm at Alexanderplatz, 368 m tall, sits 3 km northwest and is the primary visual reference. Central Berlin airspace is controlled and the inner city is restricted.
