# Windmills in Berlin

Around 1860 some 150 windmills turned on the hills around Berlin. Eight survive inside the city, including one that a film crew burned down in 1921 and volunteers replaced 87 years later.

**Location:** 52.4319, 13.4336

**Categories:** germany, berlin, history, engineering, architecture

## Introduction

In 1921 a film company needed a windmill to burn. A baker in Gatow, on Berlin's western edge, owned one that had stopped working and was falling apart, so he sold it to the production, and the crew set it alight for the final scene of a silent picture called Die Liebesabenteuer der schönen Evelyne. The film was banned in the United States, judged unfit for minors in Germany, and is now presumed lost. So is the mill. It burned on a 52-metre hill called the Windmühlenberg that had carried a mill since at least 1845, and after that the hill carried nothing at all for eighty-seven years.

## Monks with a Monopoly

Grinding grain was one of the great medieval rackets. Mill rights were tied to water rights, granted from above, and whoever held them held a licence to tax every loaf in the district. Two water mills were working the Berlin area by the thirteenth century, one on the Panke and one at the Mühlendamm between Alt-Berlin and Cölln. Windmills arrive in the record around 1375, in the villages of Buckow and Rudow and on a Lehnin monastery estate called Celendorpe, now the suburb of Zehlendorf. The Cistercians of Lehnin were the technologists of the Teltow, and they built accordingly: nineteen mills, each returning at least a hundred guilders a year to the abbey, with the millers themselves probably clearing three or four times that. Prussia broke the system open in 1810, when the Stein-Hardenberg reforms abolished guild restrictions and anyone with capital could build.

## One Hundred and Fifty

What followed was a construction boom on the sand hills. By about 1860 roughly 150 mills stood in Berlin and the villages that were then still independent of it, most of them post mills — the older, cheaper type that pivots bodily on a timber trestle — because few millers could afford the more efficient Dutch mill, where only the cap turns. They clustered where the wind was. Around thirty stood on Prenzlauer Berg, eight of them in a row along a ridge called the Windmühlenberg that gave the district its name and still supplies its coat of arms: four black windmill sails on a gold shield. Eleven worked near Müllerstraße in Wedding, eleven more along a lane south of Schöneberg now called Badensche Straße. Rixdorf specialised in tanning and fulling. The hills themselves are glacial: ground moraine of the Barnim and Teltow plateaus, capped with marl and sand dropped by meltwater some 15,000 years ago, just high enough to catch the air.

## Steam

Steam arrived around 1870 and made wind uneconomic almost immediately. Electrification and industrial-scale milling finished the job, so that by 1945 a landscape of 150 mills had been reduced to a handful, and most of those were no longer grinding anything. The Zehlendorfer Mühle, a rare round Dutch mill of 1881, went over to a petroleum engine by 1898 and an electric motor in 1921; its sails, cap and gallery came off in 1943 and 1944 so that they could not serve as a navigation mark for enemy aircraft, and the three brick storeys left over were quietly converted into a private house in 1997. The Jungfernmühle in Gropiusstadt held out longest of all, running on electricity until 1980 — the last windmill in Berlin to earn its keep, by then with no wind involved.

## The Miller's Daughter

The Jungfernmühle is also the city's oldest surviving corn mill, an octagonal Dutch mill built in 1757 by a Dutch carpenter, Adrian den Ouden, on the Amtsacker outside Potsdam's Nauen Gate and hauled to Rixdorf in 1860. Its name comes from a death. In the year it was built, the miller's daughter was caught by the turning sails and killed, falling onto the gallery below. Someone carved her likeness in oak and set it beneath the mill's shaft, and the carving is reported to be there still. Of the eight mills now inside the city limits, only one original is genuinely working: the Britzer Mühle on Buckower Damm, a twelve-sided gallery Dutch mill of 1866 with sails 25 metres across, whose cap rides on cast-iron rollers and is turned into the wind automatically by a fantail. Karl Albert August Stechan bought it in 1874 for 19,000 thalers. Since 1987 it has run an eighteen-month course that certifies hobby millers, and it sells its own bread.

## Eighty-Seven Years Later

The Gatow story got an ending. In 2004 the local history association bought a dismantled post mill for about 4,000 euros out of the bankruptcy estate of a firm that had meant to give it to Wriezen; the timbers had been sitting in a freight yard in the Oderbruch. The mill was reckoned to be around 225 years old and close in form to the one the film crew had burned. Getting it standing took until 2008 — a neighbour's appeal against the permit cost a year, and the site had to be squeezed onto the edge of the Windmühlenberg nature reserve, protected since 2002 for its rare dry-grassland flora. The finished mill was christened on 6 September 2008 in the presence of the governing mayor, Klaus Wowereit, in the year Gatow marked its 750th anniversary and put a post mill on the logo. The whole thing cost about 180,000 euros, most of it lottery money, and after eighty-seven years sails turned again on the hill.

## Flight Context

Berlin's surviving windmills are scattered rather than grouped, so this entry has no single target. The reference coordinate, 52.4319° N, 13.4336° E, sits in Britz in the south of the city, close to the working Britzer Mühle on Buckower Damm; the neighbouring Britzer Garten, a large formal park laid out for the 1985 garden show, is the obvious landmark from the air, and the mill stands on its edge beside an orchard. Tempelhofer Feld, the huge open former airfield, lies about 4 km north and orients everything. The others are spread wide: the Adlermühle in Mariendorf, the Jungfernmühle among the tower blocks of Gropiusstadt, the truncated Zehlendorfer Mühle out in the south-west, two mills in the tree-filled park of the German Museum of Technology beside the Gleisdreieck in Kreuzberg, and the rebuilt post mill on the 52-metre Windmühlenberg at Gatow, on the western edge of the city near the Havel. A mill is a small target: plan on 800–1,500 ft AGL, and note that sails read best in low sun when they cast shadow across the surrounding ground. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is about 12 km south-east of Britz; Schönhagen (EDAZ) lies roughly 27 km south-west and Strausberg (EDAY) about 40 km north-east.
