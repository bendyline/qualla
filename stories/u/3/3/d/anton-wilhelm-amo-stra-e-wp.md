# Anton-Wilhelm-Amo-Straße

A central Berlin street that carried a contested name for 318 years now honours the man who, in 1729, wrote a dissertation on the legal rights of Africans in Europe — a text that has since been lost.

**Location:** 52.5122, 13.3907

**Categories:** berlin, germany, mitte, colonial-history, african-diaspora, streets, philosophy, memory-politics

## Introduction

In 1729 a student at the University of Halle defended a dissertation with the title De Jure Maurorum in Europa: on the legal rights of Africans in Europe. Nobody has read it in nearly three centuries. The manuscript is lost, and all that survives is a summary printed in the university's annals the following year. Its author was Anton Wilhelm Amo, and in July 2025 a street in central Berlin — nine hundred metres of it, running east from Wilhelmstraße along the southern edge of Gendarmenmarkt — was renamed for him. The name it replaced had been on the maps since the end of May 1707, and getting rid of it took thirty years of argument and three trips through the German courts.

## The Man in the Lost Book

Amo was born around 1703 at Axim, on the coast of what is now Ghana, into the Nzema people. At about the age of four he was taken to Amsterdam by the Dutch West India Company; the sources disagree on whether he was enslaved or sent by a preacher, and that ambiguity has never been resolved. In Europe he was presented as a gift to the dukes of Brunswick-Wolfenbüttel — a human being given to a court as an object of curiosity and status — and baptised on 29 July 1708 in the palace chapel at Salzdahlum.

What the family did next was rarer than what they had done first: they educated him. He entered the law faculty at Halle in 1727, moved to Wittenberg, and took a doctorate in philosophy there in 1734. He lectured at Halle under the name Antonius Guilielmus Amo Afer, published a treatise on the art of philosophising soberly and accurately in 1738, and took a post at Jena in 1740.

## Going Home

The Germany Amo had built a career in narrowed around him. He was mocked publicly, including in a theatrical lampoon staged in Halle, and around 1747 he boarded a Dutch West India Company ship and went back to the Gold Coast. He found his father and his sister still living.

After that the record thins to almost nothing. Reports place him at Fort San Sebastian at Shama in the 1750s, possibly moved there to keep him from stirring up dissent among the people. He probably died around 1759. The exact date, place and manner of his death are not known — a philosopher who spent two decades in European universities, and the end of his life survives only as rumour. Stuttgart named a square for him in 2022, and two German museums mounted exhibitions on him in 2024.

## Ninth on the List

The street itself is older than Amo's doctorate. It was laid out around 1700 as part of Friedrichstadt, the planned quarter that pushed Berlin west, and originally stopped at Mauerstraße. At the end of May 1707 the new streets were officially named, an event recorded by Joachim Ernst Berger, Friedrichstadt's Lutheran pastor from 1697 to 1732. Ninth on his list is Mohren-Straße.

Why that name is the whole problem. The German word Mohr was used from the sixteenth century onwards for North African Berbers, then for Muslims generally, then for Black Africans — a term that flattened three different sets of people into one. Duden has described it as often perceived as discriminatory since 2012, and as obsolete and today discriminatory since 2019. What no document from 1707 supplies is a reason for the name.

## Four Explanations, No Proof

Four theories compete. In 1834 Leopold von Zedlitz recorded that the street was named for a Black man in the service of the Margrave of Schwedt, whose lord's generosity let him build a house there. In 1885 Hermann Vogt attributed it to Africans whom Frederick William I received from the Dutch and quartered on the street — an impossible chronology, since he took the throne in 1713 and the street had been named in 1707. A fourth theory, argued by the historian Ulrich van der Heyden, credits a delegation of twenty-six Africans from Pokesu, in the Brandenburg colony on the Gold Coast, who spent four months in Berlin; the activist Christian Kopp responds that the visit was in 1684, that the chief travelled with only his valet, and that Johann Bernhardt Schultz's 1688 map of Berlin shows no such road.

The third theory needs no theory. Brandenburg-Prussia held a slaving colony, and children were shipped from it to Berlin to serve as court musicians and valets. In 1680 the Elector Frederick William instructed his captain Bartelsen to bring back six enslaved boys of fourteen, fifteen and sixteen. In 1682 he ordered Captain Voss to return with twenty enslaved men aged twenty-five to thirty and twenty enslaved boys aged eight to sixteen.

## The Eighteenth of July

Berlin argued about the name from the 1990s, often refusing to write it out and calling it M-Straße instead. The Afro-German poet May Ayim was filmed protesting outside the U-Bahn station that carried it, in the 1997 documentary May Ayim: Hoffnung im Herz. In August 2020 the Mitte borough assembly recommended the change to Anton-Wilhelm-Amo-Straße.

Then came the courts. The Berlin administrative court rejected challenges from residents and the historian Götz Aly in July 2023; an appeal held the whole thing up through 2024; in July 2025 the Higher Administrative Court of Berlin-Brandenburg confirmed that residents had no standing to sue. The renaming took effect on 18 July 2025, with a ceremonial unveiling on 23 August, the International Day for the Remembrance of the Slave Trade and its Abolition. The Federal Ministry of Justice stands at numbers 36 and 37. The U-Bahn station beneath the western end still carries the old name; the transport authority stopped its own renaming plans, so the street above and the platform below currently disagree.

## Flight Context

Anton-Wilhelm-Amo-Straße runs roughly 900 m west to east at 52.5122 N, 13.3907 E, in Berlin-Mitte. The unmistakable fix from the air is Gendarmenmarkt, immediately to the north: a rectangular square with two nearly identical domed towers at either end — the Französischer Dom and the Deutscher Dom — flanking the columned Konzerthaus, one of the most legible building groups in central Berlin. The street forms part of the square's southern edge. Unter den Linden and the Museum Island lie about 500 m north, the Brandenburg Gate about 1 km northwest, and the Checkpoint Charlie crossing about 600 m south on Friedrichstraße. This is dense controlled airspace over the government quarter; expect clearance limits and plan any low pass at 1,500-2,500 ft AGL, ideally running the east-west line of Leipziger Straße. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is about 18 km southeast; Schönhagen (EDAZ) about 36 km south-southwest. Midday sun leaves the narrow streets of Friedrichstadt in shadow, so late afternoon gives better separation between the blocks and the square.
