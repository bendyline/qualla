# St. Mary's Church, Berlin

Berlin's oldest working church hides a 22-metre medieval Dance of Death, wears a tower by the architect of the Brandenburg Gate, and now stands alone in a field of grass beneath the Fernsehturm.

**Location:** 52.5206, 13.4067

**Categories:** Berlin, Churches, Medieval architecture, Mitte, History of Berlin

## Introduction

In 1861 the architect Friedrich August Stüler had the whitewash taken off a wall inside the tower hall of the Marienkirche, and a procession came back into the light after three hundred years. It runs twenty-two and a half metres along the wall and stands two metres high: bishops, priests, burghers, tradespeople, each one taken by the hand or the sleeve by a capering corpse. It was painted around 1484, a plague year, when the argument it makes — that death takes the mitre and the apron with equal indifference — needed no explaining. The Reformation painted over it. Berlin's oldest church had been keeping it in the dark ever since.

## Older Than Its Own Records

The first document naming the church is dated 3 January 1292, which calls it a parish church of St Mary the Virgin. Architectural historians put the building work earlier, around 1270. Its oldest parts are granite; everything above is brick, which gives the Marienkirche the deep red that the city later copied on purpose when it built the Rotes Rathaus a few hundred metres away. Along with the Nikolaikirche it is the oldest church in Berlin, and unlike the Nikolaikirche — now a museum — it is still a working one, the seat of the bishop of the Evangelical Church of Berlin-Brandenburg-Silesian Upper Lusatia. It was Catholic until the Reformation reached Berlin in 1539 and has been Lutheran since.

## Two Reckonings

The medieval record around this site is not gentle. In 1243 Jews in Berlin were accused of stealing consecrated wafers from the church — a fabricated charge of a kind used repeatedly across medieval Europe to justify killing Jewish communities — and a number of Jewish men and women were burned at the stake on a rise afterwards called the Judenberg. No one knows their names. Eighty-odd years later the violence ran the other way: in 1325 citizens of Berlin and Cölln killed the provost Nikolaus von Bernau, whose loyalty to the pope had put him against the towns, and the towns were made to raise an atonement cross for it. The Sühnekreuz still stands outside the church. It is easy to walk past a stone cross without reading it. This one is the receipt for a lynching.

## Schlüter, Wagner, Langhans

The interior accumulated its treasures the way parish churches do, one commission at a time. Andreas Schlüter carved the alabaster pulpit in 1702 and 1703, hanging the basket between angels under a canopy full of putti. Joachim Wagner built the organ between 1720 and 1722; it was inaugurated in 1723, and a reconstruction by Alfred Kern & Söhne completed in 2002 brought all forty of its registers back. The monumental tomb of Otto Christoph von Sparr, field marshal of Brandenburg-Prussia, was cut by the Flemish sculptor Artus Quellinus the Elder. Carl Hildebrand von Canstein, who founded the world's oldest Bible society, was buried here in 1719. Then in 1789 and 1790 Carl Gotthard Langhans — at that moment also building the Brandenburg Gate — capped the medieval tower with a neo-Gothic upper stage, bringing the whole thing to about ninety metres.

## The Neighbourhood That Vanished

For most of its life the Marienkirche was hemmed in. Photographs from the 1880s show it wedged into the Marienviertel, a dense quarter of houses and lanes with the Neuer Markt at its door; in the 1906 pictures the Luther monument stands outside it, ringed by tenements. Then came the war. The church was damaged and the district around it was destroyed, and afterwards the East German authorities restored the building through the 1950s while clearing away everything that had stood next to it. When the Fernsehturm opened in 1969 the transformation was complete: a thirteenth-century brick church standing by itself on open grass, dwarfed by a 368-metre television tower, with Karl-Liebknecht-Straße running past where the Marienviertel used to be. Inside, the dance goes on along the tower-hall wall, unbothered, as it has for five and a half centuries.

## Flight Context

The Marienkirche stands at 52.521°N, 13.407°E in Berlin-Mitte, on the north side of Karl-Liebknecht-Straße between Alexanderplatz and Museum Island. From the air it is easiest found by triangulation rather than by itself: the Fernsehturm is about 200 m south-west, the Rotes Rathaus roughly 400 m south, and the domed drum of the Berlin Cathedral 700 m west across the Spree. At 1,500–2,500 ft the church reads as a small dark-red rectangle with a green-grey spire, marooned in a wide field of lawn and paving — the emptiness around it is the surest identifier, since nothing else in central Berlin has that much unbuilt ground. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) lies about 19 km south-south-east and Schönhagen (EDAZ) roughly 39 km south-west. The whole of Mitte sits within EDDB's control zone and terminal airspace. Low stratus and haze are frequent from November through February; the best light on the brick is late afternoon.
