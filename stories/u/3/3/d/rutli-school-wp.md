# Rütli School

A Neukölln school that produced resistance fighters in the 1940s became, in 2006, Germany's national shorthand for educational collapse — and then rebuilt itself.

**Location:** 52.4875, 13.4336

**Categories:** Berlin, Neukölln, Education, German resistance, Nazi Germany, Germany

## Introduction

There is a plaque on a wall in Neukölln listing former pupils of this school who were killed by the Nazi state. One of the names is Hanno Günther, who started here at Easter 1928, aged seven, in a school where his mother taught. He was executed at Plötzensee prison in December 1942, twenty-one years old, for printing leaflets. Sixty-four years after that, the Rütli School became famous again, this time as the address every German newspaper used when it wanted a shorthand for educational failure. Both things are true about the same building on the same street, and the second is much better known than the first.

## Fourteen Hundred Children

The school opened in 1909 and was requisitioned as a barracks during the First World War, going back to teaching in 1920. By 1921 it held 1,400 pupils — a working-class Berlin school on a working-class Berlin street, at the scale those numbers imply.

In the 1920s it acquired a reputation for something more interesting than size. Most German schools of the period separated boys and girls; the Rütli School did not. Its pupils built things in workshops and were taken out of the building on field trips. It is worth holding onto that, because it complicates everything that came later. This was never a school that had merely declined. It had been a place where educational experiment was normal.

## The Baker's Apprentice

Hanno Günther was born in Berlin on 12 January 1921. He came to the Rütli School in 1928, spent a year at the Schulfarm Insel Scharfenberg in 1934 before being expelled, and trained as a baker under a master named Ernst Grüger. With a group of former Rütli classmates he read Marxist texts, and through the resistance organiser Elisabeth Pungs he found his way to a communist circle.

From July 1940 to January 1941 they produced a leaflet called "Das freie Wort" — The Free Word — signed with the name of an organisation that did not exist, the German Peace Front. It is a small thing to set against a state, and it was enough. Günther was arrested on 28 July 1941 along with Dagmar Petersen, Emmerich Schaper, Wolfgang Pander and Bernhard Sikorski. The People's Court tried him on 9 October 1942 and sentenced him to death. He was executed at Plötzensee on 3 December.

He was not alone among the school's alumni. Hildegard Jadamowitz also came out of these classrooms, as did Werner Steinbrink of the Herbert Baum group. The Nazis closed the school and turned it into a military hospital. It reopened in 1945.

## The Letter

In 2006 the teachers of the Rütli School wrote to the Berlin Senate's education office and asked for their own school to be shut down. The letter leaked, and the country read it.

"We must realize that the mood in some classes currently is marked by aggressiveness, disrespect, and ignorance towards adults," they wrote. "The tendency toward violence against property is growing... In most of the families of our students, they are the only ones getting up in the morning. For them, school is a stage and battleground for attention. The worst culprits become role models." Teachers described pupils arriving without pens or books, fights, fireworks, kicked-in doors, posters torn down as fast as they went up. Some staff would not enter a classroom without a mobile phone to call for help. Read carefully, the letter is less an accusation against children than a distress signal from adults who had run out of resources and said so in public — a braver and rarer thing than the coverage allowed.

## What the Word Hauptschule Meant

The press turned the story toward immigration almost immediately — the international headlines of that spring ran to phrases like "migrant ghettos" — and the debate that followed was about integration as much as education. Around ninety percent of the school's pupils come from non-German backgrounds.

What mostly went unsaid was structural. The Rütli School was a Hauptschule: the lowest of the three tracks in the German school system, the one children were sorted into young and from which the routes onward were narrowest. Berlin abolished the Hauptschule entirely in 2010–11, folding the type into a new comprehensive Sekundarschule, four years after the Rütli letter. A system that concentrated its most disadvantaged children in its least resourced schools was judged to have failed, and was dismantled. The children who were the story's villains in 2006 had been placed there by adults.

## Campus Rütli

The response, when it came, was not closure. In 2009 the Rütli School was merged with a neighbouring Realschule and a primary school into a single institution — the 1. Gemeinschaftsschule — and the whole site was rebuilt as Campus Rütli, with a nursery, a youth club, sports and leisure facilities, workshops and an advice centre added.

The school now has three gymnasiums, a kitchen, four computer rooms, an art studio, chemistry and physics labs, a wood and metal workshop and a music room. Pupils can learn to box, design and sell T-shirts under the label Rütli Wear, and take part in a programme called Zurück in die Zukunft — Back to the Future — which works to bring dropouts back into school. A partnership with the Maxim Gorki Theater has run since 2007. And somewhere in the building there is a museum about the school's own past, which now has two very different chapters to explain to the children walking past it.

## Flight Context

Campus Rütli lies at 52.4875°N 13.4336°E in north Neukölln, roughly 4 km southeast of the Brandenburg Gate. The clearest fix from the air is the Landwehrkanal, which runs east–west a few hundred metres north of the school and terminates just to the east where it meets the Spree — the block of dense Wilhelmine tenement courtyards between the canal and Sonnenallee is the Reuterkiez, and the school sits inside it. Two large landmarks bracket the area: the vast open green rectangle of the former Tempelhof airfield 2 km west, with its curved terminal and disused runways, and Görlitzer Park immediately north across the canal. The Fernsehturm stands 4 km north-northwest and gives a reliable bearing at any altitude. From 1,500–2,500 ft AGL the courtyard-block pattern of Neukölln is very legible — a solid grid of five-storey perimeter blocks with green interiors. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is about 15 km southeast, Schönhagen (EDAZ) 25 km southwest, Strausberg (EDAY) northeast. Berlin CTR applies throughout. Note that Tempelhof and Tegel are both permanently closed and neither is available as a field.
