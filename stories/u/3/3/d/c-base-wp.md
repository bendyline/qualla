# c-base

Berlin's television tower is not a television tower. It is the antenna of a space station that crashed here 4.5 billion years ago, and the people digging it out have been at it since 1995.

**Location:** 52.5129, 13.4206

**Categories:** Berlin, Technology, Hacker culture, Subculture, Modern history, Germany

## Introduction

The official story of the Fernsehturm at Alexanderplatz is that East Germany built it between 1965 and 1969 to broadcast television and to be seen from West Berlin. The c-base has a different account. In their telling, the tower is not a transmitter but an antenna — the only visible part of an enormous space station that crashed into this stretch of the north German plain roughly 4.5 billion years ago and has been buried under it ever since. East German and Soviet scientists found the tip. What they built around it was cover. And a few hundred metres downriver, in about 700 square metres of Berlin-Mitte, a non-profit association has spent three decades excavating and reconstructing the rest of the ship.

## An Extended Living Room

The prosaic version is that seventeen people signed the founding papers of c-base e.V. on 12 August 1995, six years after the Wall came down and at a moment when central Berlin was full of buildings nobody had a use for. Three of them — Hardy Engwer, who goes by cynk, Marten Suhr as mars, and Carsten Ussat as nomax — had been looking for what they described as an extended living room.

On paper the association exists to increase knowledge and skills relating to computer software, hardware and data networks, which is the kind of sentence you write for a German registry office. In practice it became the hub of Berlin's hacker and nerd scene, and it did so by committing completely to the fiction. The interior is built as a space station: rings, bulkheads, blue light, a core. At the centre sits the c-beam, the station's main computer, in the innermost ring where a Möbius-band generator is said to have once supplied the power.

## Before There Was a Word for It

In 1995 there was no widely used term for a room where people share tools, servers and soldering irons and let anyone in. c-base is recognised as one of the first hackerspaces in the world, and it belongs to the small group that gave the model to everyone else. Along with The Loft in San Diego and Metalab, it directly influenced the wave of hackerspaces that opened across the United States.

That influence travelled by visit rather than by manual. People came through Berlin, saw a room where the infrastructure was collectively owned and the door policy was an argument rather than a rule, and went home to try it. What is now a standard piece of urban furniture in a few hundred cities was, for several years, mostly this.

## Everybody Meets at the Base

The station functions as a venue for anyone who shares its purposes. The Chaos Computer Club uses it. So does freifunk.net, the community wireless network — in 2002 and 2003 c-base ran the BerlinBackBone project, putting free public internet access across the city by radio at a time when that was a political act as much as a technical one. Berlin's Wikipedia editors have held their regular meet-ups here since 2004, and the media art festival transmediale has worked out of the premises since the same year.

In September 2006 the German Pirate Party held its founding assembly at c-base. Later that month, from 14 to 16 September, the fourth Wizards of OS conference ran here. Since 2003 the Cosmic Open Stage has convened musicians weekly for jam sessions and concerts, known and unknown alike, in a room that officially belongs to a crashed spacecraft.

## Rules Recovered from the c-beam

Members do not confine themselves to computing. They play Go. They play Jugger, the team sport fought with padded weapons — and the c-base account is that the rules were not invented but recovered, extracted from files found on the c-beam. It is a joke that has been maintained with a completely straight face for so long that it has become a kind of institutional grammar. Once a year, an event called @c-terra lays out everything the association is currently doing.

Some of it points outward. The society has taken stands to large public festivals, including Children's Day, where members introduce young visitors to robotics and computer-aided design. A twelve-year-old who learns to drive a CAD program at a folding table in a Berlin park is not told about the Möbius-band generator. That comes later, if they keep coming back.

## Flight Context

c-base sits at 52.513°N 13.421°E in Berlin-Mitte, on the Spree just southeast of the Jannowitzbrücke, roughly 1.2 km southeast of the Fernsehturm. From 2,000–3,000 ft AGL, use the tower itself: it is the tallest structure in Germany and visible from most of Brandenburg, and c-base lies a short hop down the river from its base — follow the Spree southeast past Museum Island and the Nikolaiviertel until the elevated S-Bahn viaduct crosses the water at Jannowitzbrücke. The Oberbaumbrücke's twin brick towers stand about 2 km further upstream to the southeast, with Tresor's power-station home on Köpenicker Strasse between the two. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is roughly 20 km south-southeast; Strausberg (EDAY) is the closest general-aviation field to the east and Schönhagen (EDAZ) to the southwest. This is dense controlled airspace under the Berlin CTR and TMA — transit under clearance, and note that the Fernsehturm reaches 368 m, well above any low-level routing. Winter brings persistent low stratus; summer evenings light the Spree corridor beautifully from the west.
