# Clärchen's Ballroom

Berlin once had nine hundred ballrooms. One is still trading, in a back courtyard on Auguststrasse, and it has never stopped.

**Location:** 52.5269, 13.3967

**Categories:** Berlin, Nightlife, Music, Architecture, Modern history, Germany

## Introduction

Berlin at the turn of the twentieth century counted something like nine hundred ballrooms — rooms with a sprung floor and a band where working people went to dance on a Saturday. War, bombing, partition, redevelopment and changing taste took all of them but one. Clärchens Ballhaus opened on 13 September 1913 in the rear courtyard building of Auguststrasse 24/25, and it has been open for business, with interruptions you could count on one hand, ever since. It opened under the German Empire and has traded on through the Weimar Republic, the Third Reich, the German Democratic Republic and reunified Germany: two world wars and five systems of government, and the same floor throughout.

## Two Halls, One Above the Other

Fritz Bühler was born in 1862, his wife Clara in 1886. In 1913 they opened Bühler's Ballhaus in a building that had gone up around 1895 in the yard behind the street front, and its layout has dictated everything since. The ground floor is the dance hall: plain, high, built to take a crowd. Directly above it is the Spiegelsaal, the hall of mirrors, a smaller and considerably more ornate room lined with tall glass.

Setting a ballroom in a Hinterhaus was ordinary Berlin practice. The street building held the shops and the better flats; the courtyard behind held the workshops, the cheap rooms and the entertainment. Auguststrasse runs through Spandauer Vorstadt, a dense quarter of exactly those courtyards immediately north of the Spree, and one of the historic centres of Jewish Berlin. You still reach the ballroom by walking off the pavement, through an arch, and into a yard.

## Clara's Century

Fritz died in 1929. Clara kept the business, and in 1932 married Arthur Habermann, born in 1885, who helped her run it. The name the place is known by is hers.

The front building on Auguststrasse was destroyed during the Second World War — which is why the ballroom now has an open forecourt where a facade used to be — but the hall in the yard came through, and trading resumed once the war ended. What happened next is the strangest chapter. The address ended up in East Berlin, and Clärchens stayed a privately owned company through the entire GDR period, a small piece of pre-war commerce running on under a state that had abolished the category. From 1967 to 1989 it was managed by Clara's stepdaughter Elfriede Wolff and her husband, then by their son Stefan. Clara herself died in 1971. East Berliners and West Berliners both knew the address, and for decades it was one of the places they could plausibly arrange to meet.

## Ninety-One Years, Then the Sale

Clara's daughter inherited the property after reunification. Her son sold it in 2003 to Hans-Joachim Sander, and at the end of 2004 — on New Year's Eve, after ninety-one years — the ballroom closed.

It did not stay closed. Christian Schulz and David Regehr took the place on and made a decision that has defined the modern Clärchens: they changed almost nothing. The scuffs stayed, the mismatched furniture stayed, the plaster stayed as it was. They opened up the forecourt where the bombed front building had stood, and they brought the Spiegelsaal back into use — for years it had been nothing but a storeroom, its mirrors going quietly cloudy on the first floor above a room full of dancers. Yoram Roth bought the business in 2018, replaced the operators in late 2019, and reopened after renovation in July 2020, in the middle of a year when almost nothing else in Berlin was dancing.

## A Camera Points at It Roughly Once a Decade

The building is protected as a Berliner Kulturdenkmal, and film crews keep arriving. Wilma Pradetto made a documentary about Günter Schmidtke, the ballroom's cloakroom attendant. Andreas Kleinert shot Edith bei Clärchen in 1985, in the GDR. Maria Wischnewski's 2017 film brought the actress Katharina Thalbach back to remember her childhood in the place, and got Wim Wenders to describe it — accurately — as a time capsule.

It has also stood in for other eras. Clärchens appears in Stauffenberg (2004), in Inglourious Basterds (2009) and in We Do It For Money (2014), and in 2019 Max Raabe recorded his MTV Unplugged session in the hall. That is the peculiar position it now occupies: a room used as a set for a vanished Berlin, which is at the same time the last actual specimen of it, still selling beer on a Thursday to people who came to dance.

## Flight Context

Clärchens Ballhaus is at 52.527°N 13.397°E, in the courtyard behind Auguststrasse 24/25 in Spandauer Vorstadt, Berlin-Mitte. Nothing about it reads from the air — that is the nature of a Hinterhaus — so navigate by the neighbours. From 2,000–3,000 ft AGL, find the Fernsehturm at Alexanderplatz and track about 1.2 km west-northwest to the gilded ribbed dome of the New Synagogue on Oranienburger Strasse, the brightest single object in this quarter. Auguststrasse is the next parallel street north, and the ballroom sits roughly 300 m northeast of the dome. The Spree and the northern tip of Museum Island lie just under 500 m south; Friedrichstrasse station and the Berliner Ensemble are about a kilometre southwest. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is roughly 20 km south-southeast; Schönhagen (EDAZ) lies southwest and Strausberg (EDAY) east. Central Berlin sits under the Berlin CTR and TMA, and the government quarter's restricted airspace begins about 1.5 km to the west — check the structure before descending. The block is uniformly five and six storeys, so low sun angles throw the courtyards into deep shadow; the best light on this quarter is high summer, late afternoon.
