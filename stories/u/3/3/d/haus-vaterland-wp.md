# Haus Vaterland

A Weimar pleasure palace on Potsdamer Platz that sold the whole world in one building, taken from its Jewish operators by the Nazis, bombed out, burned out, and finally scrapped for 600 tonnes of steel.

**Location:** 52.5081, 13.3772

**Categories:** Berlin, Weimar Republic, Entertainment, Architecture, Nazi Germany, Lost Buildings

## Introduction

Every hour, on the hour, it rained on the Rhine. On the third floor of a building at the edge of Potsdamer Platz, diners looked out across a painted river winding between Sankt Goar and the Lorelei rock, and every sixty minutes the sky over that river darkened, thunder cracked out of concealed machinery, and pyrotechnic lightning ripped across the diorama while twenty women costumed as Rhine maidens danced between the tables. The house motto was not shy about it: Haus Vaterland machts gründlich – im Haus Vaterland gewitterts stündlich. Haus Vaterland does it thoroughly; in Haus Vaterland it storms hourly. Nothing here was subtle. Subtlety was not what anyone had paid for.

## Theodoric's Dome, Rebuilt in Berlin

The building went up between 1911 and 1912 as Haus Potsdam, designed by Franz Heinrich Schwechten, who had already given the city the Anhalter Bahnhof and the Kaiser Wilhelm Memorial Church. Six storeys of sandstone facing hid a steel frame. At the northern end, facing the square, sat a circular pavilion under a copper dome that rose 35 metres above the pavement with a row of statues beneath it — essentially a recreation of the mausoleum of the Ostrogothic king Theodoric the Great at Ravenna. Behind it a long, narrow wing ran some 100 metres alongside the Potsdamer Bahnhof. UFA, the film studio, kept its headquarters here until 1927. The lower floors held a 1,196-seat cinema and the Café Piccadilly, which seated around 2,500 under painted ceilings and Sienese marble; contemporaries compared it to the Moulin Rouge. When war came in 1914 the management swapped the English name for a patriotic one: Café Vaterland.

## The World in One House

Haus Potsdam faltered through the 1920s and was sold in 1927 to the Bank für Handel und Grundbesitz, which leased it for ten years to the Kempinski family of restaurateurs. The idea for what came next belonged to Leo Kronau, who had seen Coney Island and wanted better than Berlin's own pale imitation, Lunapark. He talked the Kempinskis into converting the place into a Haus der Nationen and became its first artistic director. Carl Stahl-Urach — the architect who had worked on Fritz Lang's Doctor Mabuse films — stuccoed the exterior and, more importantly, wired the dome. Roughly 4,000 bulbs arranged in intersecting arcs switched on and off to fake the illusion of spin, and a reporter for Germania saluted the "Babylonian dome" as proof that "here, world-capital life is pulsing." The historian David Clay Large was less charmed: "a beacon of commercial kitsch." He also called it a kind of proto-Disney World, which is closer to a compliment than it sounds.

## Six Bands and a Pneumatic Tube

The promise printed everywhere was die Welt in einem Haus — the world in one house. Behind the Rhine terrace lay a Bavarian beer hall, a Viennese wine garden where the steeple of St Stephen's stood against a painted starry sky and a lit tram crossed the Danube, a Wild West bar, a Spanish bodega, a Hungarian csarda, a Japanese tearoom, and a Turkish café of gilded arches and marble floors. Bands played to match. Under the dome, the Palmensaal was staged as a re-creation of the Garden of Eden, decorated with silver palm fronds and sculptures by Josef Thorak, its dance floor mounted on springs so that dancers would tire less. Feeding all of it was solved on the roof. A central kitchen filled the entire top floor: orders arrived by pneumatic tube, food descended by dumbwaiter, dirty plates rode back up to conveyor belts and machine washing. The one millionth guest came through in October 1929, barely a year after opening.

## Aryanised

The Kempinskis were Jewish, and the family had been feeding Berlin for sixty-five years. Under the Nazi policy of Aryanisation they were forced to sell out in 1937 for a fraction of what the business was worth, and they left Germany. The new management reworked the mix of restaurants and added two small rooms honouring friends of the Third Reich. Everything else carried on. Siegfried Kracauer, who had written that "Haus Vaterland includes the entire globe," had already noticed the seam running through it — the severe modern lobby one step from the luxuriant sentimentality of the dining rooms, the New Objectivity revealed as a façade. Franz Hessel read in the place the nascent totalitarianism of a monster Germany. The crowds kept coming, even after the bombers did.

## Walled-Up Windows

The British night raid of 22 and 23 November 1943 tore into the central section. On 3 February 1945 an American daylight raid left only the walls standing. What survived sat on the sector boundary of a divided city, which made the ruin useful to spies, to people fleeing east to west, and to anyone trading currency or goods off the books. On 17 June 1953, during the East German uprising, the shell burned out completely, along with Erich Mendelsohn's Columbushaus nearby. After that the windows were bricked up and the building left alone. When the Wall went up in 1961 it ran past the ruin's doorstep. The West Berlin Senate bought the site in 1972 as part of 8.5 hectares acquired for a road, and demolition followed in 1976; the 600 tonnes of iron and steel went out as scrap. When Potsdamer Platz was rebuilt after reunification, this was the only parcel on the square given no entertainment at all — judged too small, filled with offices. The new building there carries a semicircular façade, a nod to the dome that once spun without moving.

## Flight Context

The site sits at 52.508°N, 13.377°E on the southeast edge of Potsdamer Platz in Berlin-Mitte. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is about 18 km south-southeast; Schönhagen (EDAZ) lies roughly 37 km south-southwest. From 1,500–3,000 ft the square is easy to fix: follow the green rectangle of the Tiergarten east until it ends, and Potsdamer Platz is the cluster of tall towers immediately southeast of the park's corner — the tallest are the only high-rises in central Berlin. The Haus Vaterland parcel is on the square's southeastern side, now part of the Park Kolonnaden block with its curved façade. The old sector line, and later the Wall, ran within metres of the site; the paving line of the former Wall is still traceable across the square on clear days. Winter haze and low stratus are common from November through February.
