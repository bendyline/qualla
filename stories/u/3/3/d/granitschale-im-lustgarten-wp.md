# Granitschale im Lustgarten

Berliners call it the soup bowl: seventy-five tonnes of Swedish granite, nearly seven metres across, cut from an erratic boulder an ice sheet left in the Brandenburg woods and polished for two and a half years to a mirror finish.

**Location:** 52.5191, 13.3992

**Categories:** Museum Island, Granite sculptures in Germany, Biedermeier, Buildings and structures in Mitte, Karl Friedrich Schinkel

## Introduction

The stone did not come from Berlin, or from Prussia, or from anywhere a Prussian king could claim. It came from Karlshamn in southern Sweden, roughly 1.42 billion years old, and an ice sheet carried it south and dropped it in the Rauen hills east of the city, where it sat for ten thousand years as the Großer Markgrafenstein — a red granite lump some seven and a half metres in each direction, weighing perhaps 750 tonnes. In 1827 a Berlin stonemason named Christian Gottlieb Cantian went out there with twenty men and a plan to turn a piece of it into the largest bowl ever cut from a single stone. It took four years, three failed attempts, a specially built road through the forest, and more than double the approved budget. The result stands in front of the Altes Museum: 6.91 metres across, about 75 tonnes, and known to generations of Berliners simply as the Suppenschüssel, the soup bowl.

## A Matter of National Pride

The commission began as competitive shopping. Frederick William III heard about a large granite bowl in England and wanted a bigger one, declaring that the largest specimen of the species ought to stay in the country. Cantian promised something to outdo even the porphyry basin from Nero's Golden House in the Vatican. He first set his sights on a 600-tonne block near Oderberg in 1825 and started splitting it before abandoning the stone as too brittle. Granite mattered in the Biedermeier era in a way that is easy to miss now. It was so hard to work with the tools of the day that the material itself came to stand for endurance and unshakeable firmness; the same king had insisted on a granite pedestal for the Luther monument in Wittenberg for exactly that reason. A boulder in a Brandenburg wood was not just a rock. It was a patriotic argument waiting to be carved.

## Ninety-Five Wedges

Work started in May 1827, twenty stonemasons on site every day, with one or two blacksmiths doing nothing but shaping and hardening their tools. By mid-June the whole boulder had been rotated ninety degrees using ten winches, so the best slab could be split away. On 24 August 1827 ninety-five iron wedges were driven home. The split went wrong, leaving protrusions that had to be chipped off by hand; a second attempt in early November went wrong too. Only on 23 December was the resulting slab — five feet thick and 225 tonnes — turned over, a job requiring a hundred workers and twenty-three hoists. The underside was finished on 26 April 1828 and the hollowing ran until 4 August. The dimensions were absurd enough that workmen took their breakfast sitting on the rim.

## Six Weeks to the River

Getting it out was its own campaign. A boardwalk and a forest road were built to the Spree — the route is still traceable — and the bowl, by then down to seventy-odd tonnes, was rolled along on wooden rollers at about 188 metres a day. The journey took six weeks. Fifty-four men were needed to load it onto a braced wooden barge, and the Grünstraße bridge in Berlin had to be lowered substantially to let it pass. It arrived on 6 November 1828 and went into a purpose-built shed at the Packhof, where a ten-horsepower steam engine ground and polished it for two and a half years. Nobody in Germany had machine-polished a stone this hard before. The surface that emerged was a mirror. Johann Erdmann Hummel painted it repeatedly during the work, and his canvases are prized less for the bowl than for the reflections in it — including Cantian himself, in a top hat, at the edge of the frame.

## The Room It Never Entered

Karl Friedrich Schinkel had a place for it all along: the rotunda at the heart of the Altes Museum, then still under construction. Then Cantian reported that a 22-foot bowl was achievable, the king chose 22 feet, and Schinkel had a problem — at that size the object would wreck the proportions of the room it was meant to crown, and would not fit through the door besides. After several presentations, the king approved an outdoor site on 21 February 1829. Cantian wanted his bowl raised on tall columns; Schinkel wanted it low, on three plain granite plinths, so that you could look down into it. The king sided with Schinkel. It was set provisionally on 14 November 1831 and formally handed to the Royal Museum on 10 November 1834. The estimate had been 12,000 thalers; the final bill was 33,386.

## Parade Ground, Shell Fragments, Spray Paint

Nearly two centuries outdoors have not been kind. During the Weimar years the Lustgarten hosted rallies and the bowl served as a viewing platform, which scratched it. In the 1930s it was shifted north of the cathedral so the square could be paved for parades. Shell fragments hit it during the Battle of Berlin, and the damaged rim was eventually repaired with a patch of red granite that is still visible. Under the GDR it spent time parked among the cathedral works' huts before returning to its original position in 1981, for the two hundredth anniversary of Schinkel's birth. Cracks found during the grinding — blamed on frost, or on the violence of the splitting — have widened over time, and the mirror polish Hummel painted is long gone. In October 2020 someone sprayed it with graffiti, and the national outcry was, in its way, a compliment: after 190 years the soup bowl still counts as something Berlin owns.

## Flight Context

The Granite Bowl sits at 52.5191°N, 13.3992°E on the Lustgarten, directly in front of the Altes Museum on the north end of Museum Island. From the air the island itself is the landmark — a long narrow spit in the Spree threaded with bridges — with the green dome of the Berlin Cathedral immediately east of the bowl and the rebuilt Berlin Palace, now the Humboldt Forum, just south. At 2,000–3,000 ft the open rectangle of the Lustgarten reads clearly between the cathedral and the museum's colonnade. The Fernsehturm at Alexanderplatz, a few hundred metres east, is the best coarse reference. EDDB (Berlin Brandenburg) lies roughly 24 km south-east; EDAZ (Schönhagen) is about 45 km south-west. Tegel and Tempelhof are both closed. Best light for the island is early morning, when the low sun comes in along the axis of Unter den Linden.
