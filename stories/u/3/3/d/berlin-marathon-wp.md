# Berlin Marathon

A baker started it in 1974 with 244 finishers. Fifty years later it is the flattest, fastest marathon course in the world and has broken the world record thirteen times.

**Location:** 52.5164, 13.3778

**Categories:** Berlin, Sport, Athletics, Modern history, Germany

## Introduction

On 30 September 1990, three days before East and West Germany became one country again, the Berlin Marathon ran through the Brandenburg Gate. It was the first time the race had been allowed to. For sixteen years the course had been confined to West Berlin, looping back on itself at a wall, and now the field simply ran under the columns and kept going into the other half of the city. Observers reported that a great many of the runners were crying as they went through. The gate has anchored the course ever since: the race starts and finishes beside it, and every September something like fifty thousand people run a distance that used to be politically impossible.

## The Baker's Race

Horst Milde ran a bakery in Berlin and liked to run. In 1974 he organised a marathon, and 244 people finished it — 234 men and 10 women. Günter Hallas won in 2:44:53, Jutta von Haase in 3:22:01, times that thousands of amateurs now beat every year on the same streets.

It grew fast. Christa Vahlensieck set a women's world record here in 1977 at 2:34:47, the first of many. By 1981 the start had moved to the Reichstag and the field had reached 2,583 finishers, making it the largest road race in Germany. The following year, runners from East Berlin began entering under false names — crossing over, registering as somebody else, and running a race their own government had no intention of letting them enter. Discovery meant the attention of the Stasi, so they ran anonymously and went home.

## Why the Records Fall Here

Berlin is flat. There is no hill on the course worth the name, the turns are wide, the surface is good, and the last weekend of September in northern Germany is reliably cool. The marathon world record has been broken in Berlin on thirteen occasions — more than anywhere else on earth.

Ronaldo da Costa took the men's record here in 1998, the only South American ever to hold it. In 2001 Naoko Takahashi became the first woman to run a marathon under 2:20, finishing in 2:19:46. Seven men's world records fell in Berlin between 2003 and 2018. Eliud Kipchoge ran 2:01:39 here in 2018 and came back in 2022 to run 2:01:09; Tigist Assefa took the women's record in 2023 with 2:11:53. Both marks have since been beaten in Chicago, but both remain the Berlin course records. The depth is the real tell: in 2023, eight women broke 2:20 in the same race, nine men went under 2:05, and fifteen finished inside 2:06.

## Saturday Belongs to the Skaters

The marathon is a weekend, not a race. Inline skaters take the course on Saturday — a section added in 1997 that has become the largest inline skating marathon in the world and the closing round of the World Inline Cup. In 2019, 5,081 people skated it. The records belong to Bart Swings, 56:49 in 2015, and Maira Yaqueline Arias, 1:06:35 in 2017.

The wheelchair race has been part of the programme officially since 1981 and goes off early on Sunday, ahead of the runners. Heinz Frei has won the men's race twenty times. In 2023 Catherine Debrunner of Switzerland set a world record of 1:34:16, with two women finishing a single second behind her. Handcyclists were added in 2004, power walkers share the road, and since 1989 there has been a children's marathon over 4.2195 kilometres — exactly a tenth of the real thing.

## Fifty Thousand Finishers

The elite races are part of the World Marathon Majors, the annual series of top-level city races that carries a million-dollar prize purse. But the money and the world records are a thin layer on top of something much larger. Berlin is one of only a handful of marathons anywhere that has put more than 50,000 people across the finish line: 54,062 in 2024, briefly the most-finished marathon ever run until New York overtook it later that autumn, and 48,359 in 2025.

The sponsors have come and gone — Yanase, Canon, Alberto, real,-, and BMW since 2011 — and the organisation now sits with SCC Events. Milde's original idea has not changed much. A flat city, a cool morning, and a route that goes through the middle of everything.

## Flight Context

Start and finish sit at 52.516°N 13.378°E, on Strasse des 17. Juni immediately west of the Brandenburg Gate, with the finish line drawn through the gate itself. From 2,000–3,000 ft AGL the whole geography is legible in a single pass: the Tiergarten as a 3 km by 1 km rectangle of forest with the arrow-straight Strasse des 17. Juni bisecting it, the Siegessäule at the Grosser Stern roundabout mid-park, the Reichstag just north of the gate, and the Potsdamer Platz tower cluster a kilometre south. The race course then swings east and south through Mitte, Kreuzberg and Schöneberg and back — on race Sunday it reads from the air as a closed loop of empty streets inside a city that is still moving. The Reichstag lies inside a restricted zone, so transit under clearance rather than orbit; central Berlin sits beneath the Berlin CTR and TMA. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is roughly 20 km south-southeast; Schönhagen (EDAZ) is the nearest general-aviation field to the southwest and Strausberg (EDAY) to the east. Late September usually delivers the cool, stable, high-pressure air that makes the course fast and the visibility excellent.
