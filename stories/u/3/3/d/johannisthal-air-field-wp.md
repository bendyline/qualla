# Johannisthal Air Field

Germany's first commercial airfield opened three weeks after the world's first, taught the country to fly, spent forty years behind a fence, and is now a nature reserve surrounded by laboratories.

**Location:** 52.4333, 13.5167

**Categories:** Aviation history, Berlin, Airports, Science and technology, German history

## Introduction

On 13 September 1911 — her twenty-fifth birthday — Melli Beese took a Rumpler-Taube up from a field of trampled grass southeast of Berlin and came down holding German pilot's licence number 115, the first ever issued to a woman in Germany. Getting there had taken the better part of a year. When Amelie Hedwig Beese arrived at Johannisthal in November 1910 she could not find anyone willing to teach her; when she finally did, and went up for her qualifying flights, rival aviators tampered with her aircraft, apparently on the reasoning that a woman's licence would cheapen theirs. She flew anyway. Within a year she had opened her own flying school on the same field. That was Johannisthal: a strip of Brandenburg sand where German aviation learned almost everything it knew, including its bad habits.

## Three Weeks After Reims

The field opened on 26 September 1909, only weeks after the world's first airfield at Reims, and it was Germany's first commercial one — the second aerodrome in the country altogether, after Griesheim near Darmstadt. It sat fifteen kilometres southeast of the city centre, between the villages of Johannisthal and Adlershof, close enough for Berliners to come out by train on a Sunday and watch. Germany's first commercial flights left from here. Anthony Fokker, the Dutchman whose name would be painted on half the German fighters of the coming war, kept a workshop on the field. Until Tempelhof was developed in the 1920s, Johannisthal was simply Berlin's airport, and it stayed in use until 1952.

## Two Crashes, Eighty-Two Years Apart

On 17 October 1913 the Navy's airship L 2 rose too fast from Johannisthal, drew its own vented hydrogen into a running engine and burned in the sky in front of thousands of spectators. All twenty-eight men aboard died. Eighty-two years later, on the evening of 9 September 1995, a historic flight show at the old field ended with a Bf 108 rolling at low altitude and going in shortly after six o'clock. The pilot, Gerd Kahdemann, and his passenger were killed instantly. The passenger was Reinhard Furrer, the physicist who had flown as a payload specialist aboard Space Shuttle mission STS-61-A in October 1985, Germany's first Spacelab flight. A man who had been to orbit and back died at 1913's aerodrome, at an airshow, in a pre-war aeroplane.

## Behind the Fence

After the war the field lay in East Berlin, and for four decades the public simply could not go there. The National People's Army used the ground as a training area. Alongside it, the Academy of Sciences of the GDR assembled a research complex of fourteen scientific and technical institutions plus six service centres, employing something like 5,500 scientists and technicians. Adlershof also housed Deutscher Fernsehfunk, East German television, which meant that a good share of what the GDR knew about both physics and itself was produced within a kilometre or two of the same patch of grass. When the state that paid for all this dissolved, West German institutions absorbed some of the institutes and about 1,400 of the staff. The rest were let go.

## Wind Tunnels in the Grass

What survives from the flying years is oddly specific. In the 1920s and 1930s the German Experimental Institute for Aviation — the DVL, ancestor of today's DLR — put up laboratories, engine test beds, wind tunnels and hangars along the field's northern edge. Those buildings are listed monuments now, standing in what is called the Aerodynamic Park: heavy concrete cylinders and drums that look less like architecture than like instruments, which is what they were. Around them, on 4.2 square kilometres of former airfield, Berlin has built the Adlershof science and technology city, planned since 2003 for something on the order of 30,000 jobs and housing for 15,000 people.

## The Field Kept as a Field

The centre was left alone. Roughly 65 hectares of the old runway ground — the open middle of the aerodrome — was turned into protected green space rather than built over, and the result is a genuinely strange piece of Berlin: a dry sandy grassland, ringed by glass laboratories, in the middle of a capital city. Walk out into it and the ground is flat in every direction for several hundred metres, which is exactly the quality that made it an airfield in 1909. Melli Beese did not live to see any of this. Barred from German airfields during the First World War because marrying the French pilot Charles Boutard had made her a French national, interned with him at Wittstock, and unable afterwards to rebuild what she had lost, she took her own life in her Berlin flat on 21 December 1925. She was thirty-nine. Her licence number is still 115.

## Flight Context

The former Johannisthal Air Field lies at roughly 52.4333°N, 13.5167°E, in Treptow-Köpenick about 15 km southeast of the Brandenburg Gate. From the air it is one of the easier historical sites in Berlin to identify: an open pale-green rectangle roughly a kilometre across, with the dense modern grid of Adlershof wrapped around it and the drum-shaped 1930s wind-tunnel buildings of the Aerodynamic Park on its northern rim. The Spree runs a few kilometres to the north and a main southeastern rail line clips the site. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is only about 9 km south-southwest, so the site sits inside busy controlled airspace — study the Berlin TMA before descending anywhere near it. Schönhagen (EDAZ) lies about 35 km west-southwest, Strausberg (EDAY) about 30 km north-northeast. Best viewed from 2,000 to 4,000 feet. The surrounding plain is featureless enough that the green rectangle is the landmark, not the terrain.
