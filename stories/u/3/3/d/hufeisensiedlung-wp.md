# Hufeisensiedlung

Bruno Taut bent a 350-metre apartment block into a horseshoe around an ice-age pond in Neukölln, painted the working-class terraces deep blue and ochre, and put a bathroom in every flat — a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 2008.

**Location:** 52.4475, 13.4486

**Categories:** World Heritage Sites in Germany, Modernist architecture in Germany, Neukölln, Housing estates in Berlin, Bruno Taut buildings

## Introduction

There was a pond in the way. A Pfuhl, one of the shallow depressions the last ice age scattered across the Brandenburg plain, sitting in a field in Britz where Berlin needed housing more urgently than it had ever needed anything. The obvious move in 1925 was to fill it and build over it. Bruno Taut curved a three-storey apartment block 350 metres around it instead, twenty-five housing units bent into a horseshoe with the water and a green hollow at the centre, and named the estate after the shape. A century later the Hufeisensiedlung is one of six Berlin housing estates on the UNESCO World Heritage list, and the pond is still there.

## Third-Largest City in the World

The scale of the problem is hard to overstate. From 1850 to the late 1920s Berlin's population roughly doubled every twenty-five years, driven by industrialisation and then by the end of the First World War, and the Greater Berlin Act of 1920 had just swept the surrounding districts into a single municipality of 3.8 million people — more than the city holds today, and enough to make it the third-largest metropolis in the world after New York and London. Housing did not keep pace, least of all in working-class districts like Neukölln, Kreuzberg and Wedding, where families lived in the back courtyards of tenement blocks. The municipal answer was to build at industrial scale: 140,000 apartments went up between 1924 and 1931, most through non-profit cooperatives founded for the purpose. One of these, GEHAG, was established in 1924 and hired Bruno Taut as its chief architect.

## Around the Pond

The horseshoe is the signature, but it is a small part of the estate. The heritage-protected sections — six of them, built between 1925 and 1930 — cover about 29 hectares and contain 1,285 flats in three-storey blocks aligned to the streets, plus 679 terraced houses, each with a garden and a small terrace. A seventh section, south-east of the junction of Fritz-Reuter-Allee and Parchimer Allee, was built without Taut and lies outside the World Heritage designation. Taut worked with Martin Wagner, who had founded GEHAG and became city planning director of Greater Berlin in 1926, and with the landscape architect Leberecht Migge, whose designs for the green and open spaces were only partly realised by Ottokar Wagler, the parks director for Neukölln. The result reads as suburb and city at once: terraced rows and private gardens wrapped around a piece of communal ground that predates the city by ten thousand years.

## Deep Blue at the End of the Row

What startles visitors is not the plan but the paint. Taut used colour structurally rather than decoratively, and he used it on housing for people who had never been given anything designed. The terraced façades are dark red, yellow ochre, and — especially where a row ends and needs to announce itself — deep blue or brilliant white. Doors, windows, loggias, stairwells and the low attic storeys are picked out to contrast sharply with the walls behind them. Fronts and backs of the same house often carry entirely different combinations. Bright red and yellow clinker brick runs at the chimneys, the entrances and the base of the walls, so the colour is in the material as well as on it. And the single most characteristic element is the front door: each one built and painted as an event, on a street of houses that in any other city of the period would have been identical grey.

## A Bathroom in Every Flat

The interiors were left unfurnished but not unequipped, and by 1925 standards the specification was startling. Every unit had a kitchen, a bathroom and a separate bedroom. In the kitchen: a sink, a two-burner gas cooker, a larder set beneath the window, and a built-in cupboard in the corner or beside the loggia. In the bathroom: a toilet, a bathtub and a coal-fired water heater. Central heating existed only in a few houses in the sixth section; everywhere else a coal oven stood in each living room and bedroom, faced in glazed tiles in attractive colours, because Taut had opinions about those too. He did not want his carefully chosen rooms buried under pictures and knick-knacks, and rather than push residents toward the fashionable tubular steel of the Bauhaus he suggested they simplify what they already owned. Berliners noticed. To *taut* a flat entered the language as a verb, meaning, loosely, to strip it back.

## Six Hundred and Seventy-Nine Owners

The estate came through the Second World War relatively undamaged, which is more than most of Berlin managed. The threats since have been quieter. Post-war repairs lost plasterwork and original colours and the first restorations got them wrong, until the heritage authorities commissioned expert reports to establish what the right answers were — work that continues, backed by the federal Nationale Welterbestätten programme. Protection came in 1986, and on 7 July 2008 the estate was inscribed as one of the six Berlin Modernism Housing Estates on the World Heritage list; four of the six were built under Taut's leadership. The awkward turn came in 1998, when the Berlin Senate privatised GEHAG and the 679 terraced houses — until then all rented from a single landlord — were sold off to individual buyers. A monument that was designed and maintained as one thing is now several hundred separate front doors, each of them, as Taut intended, the most important part of the house.

## Flight Context

The Hufeisensiedlung lies at 52.4475°N, 13.4486°E in Britz, in the southern Neukölln district, roughly 9 km south-east of central Berlin. From the air it is unmistakable and needs no help: a single 350-metre horseshoe of three-storey roofs curled around a green hollow, surrounded by long ranks of terraced rows in red, ochre, blue and white — the colour is visible from a few thousand feet on a clear day. Best viewed from 1,500–2,500 ft; look for it just west of the Britzer Damm and north of the Britzer Garten's parkland. EDDB (Berlin Brandenburg) is only about 11 km to the south-east, so expect controlled airspace and arrival traffic here — coordinate before loitering. EDAZ (Schönhagen) lies roughly 33 km south-west. Tegel and Tempelhof are both closed; the open expanse of Tempelhofer Feld, 4 km north-west, is a useful landmark. Low winter sun flattens the colour, so late morning gives the best contrast.
