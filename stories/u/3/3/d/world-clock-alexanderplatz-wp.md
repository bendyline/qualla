# World Clock (Alexanderplatz)

A state that would not let its citizens leave built a ten-metre monument to every time zone on Earth — and left twenty cities off it for political reasons.

**Location:** 52.5212, 13.4133

**Categories:** germany, berlin, cold-war, east-germany, mitte

## Introduction

There is a particular kind of irony in a country that walled its people in and then erected, at the centre of its capital, a machine for telling the time in every city on the planet. The Urania World Clock went up on Alexanderplatz in 1969 and immediately became the thing East Berliners meant when they said they would meet you at the Alex. You could stand under it and read off the hour in Havana, Cairo, Vladivostok, San Francisco — places most of the people reading were not permitted to go. Twenty cities were missing entirely, left off because the state disapproved of them. Tel Aviv was one. Seoul and Cape Town were two more.

## A Square Four Times Its Old Size

The clock was part of a rebuild. Alexanderplatz had been bombed flat and East Germany spent the 1960s reorganising it into something four times the area it had covered at the end of the war, a paved expanse fronted by slab blocks and crowned by the Fernsehturm. The idea for a clock surfaced almost by accident: in 1966, during work on the square, workers turned up the wreckage of the Uraniasäule, a pre-war public clock and weather column that had stood nearby. The design went to Erich John, then a member of the planning group under Walter Womacka and a teacher of product design at the art school in Weißensee. Around 120 specialists across a dozen trades built what he drew, including the sculptor Hans-Joachim Kunsch. It was handed over to the public on 30 September 1969, a week before the republic's twentieth anniversary, and it weighs sixteen tons.

## How to Read It

The core of it is a rotunda with twenty-four faces — a regular icositetragon, if you want the word — one for each of the world's main time zones, with the names of that zone's principal cities engraved on the face. Numbers from one to twenty-four travel slowly around the outside of the drum through the day, each one set in a coloured rectangle exactly as long as one face. Find your city, look at the number beneath it, and that is the hour there; if the number has slid partway past the edge of the face, that fraction is your minutes. Places on half-hour offsets carry the correction in the engraving, so New Delhi reads NEW DELHI +30. Below, a compass rose is set into the paving, and four ordinary clock faces on the supporting column give Berlin time to anyone who cannot be bothered. Above, once every minute, a steel armature of rings and spheres representing the solar system turns on its axis. All ten metres of it is mechanical and never stops, and the motion is far too slow for the eye — you only see it in time-lapse.

## Twenty Missing Cities

The omissions were not oversights. A world clock in a socialist capital was a statement about which world was worth telling the time in, and the states East Germany did not recognise or did not like simply were not engraved. That went uncorrected for twenty-eight years. When the mechanism needed repair in 1997 — a job costing 350,000 marks — the city took the opportunity to bring the geography up to date. Leningrad became Saint Petersburg. Alma Ata became Almaty. Kyiv, which had been sitting on Moscow time, was moved to Eastern European Time, an adjustment of one hour that carried a good deal more than an hour's worth of meaning. And twenty cities that had been left off for political reasons were finally cut into the metal. Aurotec replaced the failing original parts, and the drum went back to turning.

## 7 October 1989

The republic turned forty on 7 October 1989, and the official celebrations were held with Gorbachev in attendance and a great deal of nervousness in the background. That evening opposition groups gathered at the World Clock — the obvious place, because it was where everybody in East Berlin already gathered — and set off from it towards the Palace of the Republic, chanting that they were the people. The state's answer was to arrest more than twelve hundred of them. It was not enough, and it was very late. Thirty-three days after the demonstration formed under the clock, the Berlin Wall opened.

## Meet Me at the Clock

Almost everything else around it has been rebuilt, re-clad or argued over since, and the clock has simply stayed, doing the one job that no committee could improve on: being a place where two people who have never met can agree to stand. East Germany put it on a commemorative coin in 1987. The federal government listed it as a protected monument in July 2015. Climate activists from the Last Generation threw orange paint over it in October 2023, which is its own backhanded compliment — you do not paint something nobody looks at. Erich John never received a design award for it, because Germany had no significant design prize at the time; he won one in 1982 for something else. He designed, instead, the most reliable meeting point in a city that has spent a century being unreliable.

## Flight Context

The World Clock stands at 52.5212° N, 13.4133° E on Alexanderplatz in Berlin-Mitte. At ten metres it is far too small to pick out from the air, but it is impossible to lose: it sits at the foot of the Fernsehturm, whose 368-metre shaft and mirrored sphere dominate every approach to central Berlin from any direction and in any weather that permits flight at all. Aim for the tower and the clock is in the paved square immediately north-east of it, between the tower's base and the two long slab buildings on the far side. Museum Island and the Spree lie 1 km west, the Rotes Rathaus with its brick tower just south-west. From 2,000–4,000 ft AGL the shadow of the Fernsehturm sweeping across Alexanderplatz through the day is itself a striking sight. This is controlled airspace directly over the city centre and low-level transit requires coordination. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is about 20 km south-east; Strausberg (EDAY) lies roughly 35 km east and Schönhagen (EDAZ) about 40 km south-west.
