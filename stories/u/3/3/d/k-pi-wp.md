# Køpi

A bombed-out tenement in Mitte that nobody would buy at auction, that its own residents offered to purchase for one mark, and that has outlasted three decades of developers by being worth more as a fight than as a building.

**Location:** 52.5078, 13.4261

**Categories:** Berlin, Counterculture, Squats, Urban history, German history

## Introduction

In 1999 the district of Mitte put 137 Köpenicker Straße up for forced auction. Almost thirty people registered to bid on a property valued at 5.4 million marks. Not one of them made an offer. The people already living in the building offered a single mark for it, and asked the city to designate the ground a special-use site for experimental living; both requests were refused. That exchange — a derelict tenement nobody wanted, and residents nobody would sell to — is the shortest available summary of Køpi, which has been squatted, legalised, foreclosed, auctioned, sold, threatened with eviction and still standing since February 1990.

## A Building That Is Its Own Back

What faces the street is not the front. The building went up in 1905, built by a Jewish businessman, with a symmetrical Gründerzeit facade flanked by two short wings; the front half was destroyed by bombing at the end of the Second World War and never rebuilt, so what pedestrians see now is the rear elevation, promoted by demolition. Most of the stucco is gone and the surfaces are pocked from the bombs and eighty years of not being repaired. The residents left it that way on purpose: they fixed the plumbing and ignored the front, on the reasoning that a building with a chequered history should be allowed to look like one — a decision that also, not coincidentally, kept investors uninterested.

## AEG's Lodgers

The war did more here than break the masonry. During the Second World War the Allgemeine Elektricitäts-Gesellschaft used the building to house French forced labourers — civilians brought to Berlin against their will to work for a company that needed hands and had the state's permission to take them. They lived in these rooms, and nothing on the facade says so. Afterwards the building fell inside East Berlin, and the German Democratic Republic put it to gentler use: sports activities upstairs, a bowling alley in the basement. By 1990 it stood empty and was scheduled for demolition.

## 23 February 1990

Instead, on 23 February 1990, Autonomen from West Berlin walked across the collapsing border and occupied it. The state that owned the building was in the process of ceasing to exist, and the police made no attempt to clear them out; it was the first time West Berliners had squatted a building in the East. The condition of the place was terrible and the rooms were enormous, and the rooms won. In 1991 the occupiers legalised their arrangement with the district council of Mitte. The name came from the street, Köpenicker Straße, but the squatters spelled it with a Danish ø, and Køpi has been written that way — and also Köpi, Koepi and, in foreign texts, Kopi — ever since. Painted across the front is a line that translates as: there are no borders between people, only between the top and the bottom.

## Plutonium 114

The ownership history reads like a farce with real stakes. The post-GDR state sold the building in 1995 to Volquard Petersen, who planned to turn the site into the Sonnenhöf, ran out of money and lost it to foreclosure — hence the empty auction of 1999. A second auction in 2007 succeeded, watched from outside the courtroom by 300 supporters and 300 police officers: the building went for €835,000, roughly half its estimated value, to Besnik Fichtner of a company called Plutonium 114. Within a week the residents had traced the paper trail to a Berlin property developer, Siegfried Nehls, and turned up unannounced at his parents' house, where his father invited them in for tea before his brother called the police. A month later the police raided Nehls's own headquarters and twenty other properties over financial irregularities. By 2008 Fichtner had fallen out with Nehls and signed a thirty-year rental contract with the people living in the house. Then he too fell into debt, and in 2013 Commerzbank called for another forced auction.

## 15 October 2021

The wagenplatz — the yard beside the house where people lived in trucks and caravans, about fifty of them in 2016 — was never covered by that rental contract, and it was the wagenplatz that finally went. An eviction order issued in September 2021 gave the residents until 15 October. A thousand supporters demonstrated on 3 October under the eyes of six hundred emergency personnel; on the day itself, police cleared the yard under court order at the owner's request. That evening as many as eight thousand people marched, and Köpenicker Straße saw serious rioting. The house itself was not cleared and has not been. In 2023 the owner went to court to evict residents from an adjoining building; in December 2024 the Berlin Regional Court threw the claim out. Inside, the project still runs on a Sunday plenary that only residents and member groups may attend, with mobile phones left at the door, and the building has held a bar, a vegan café, a concert hall, a cinema, an infoshop, a gym and a climbing wall. A 2017 report by the Senate Department of Internal Affairs and the police called the property "both a retreat for left-motivated criminals and as a starting point for crimes." A Køpi spokesperson replied that police, tour buses and building work on the block were felt as gentrification, and that "displeasure about these things occasionally results in criminally relevant actions." Both statements stand.

## Flight Context

Køpi stands at 137 Köpenicker Straße in Mitte, at roughly 52.5078°N, 13.4261°E — on the south side of the Spree, about 2 km east-southeast of Alexanderplatz. From the air the reference points are the river's long east-southeast reach through Mitte, the Jannowitzbrücke rail and U-Bahn crossing just to the northwest, and the television tower standing above everything about 2.5 km away on the same bearing; the East Side Gallery stretch of the Wall lies a further 2 km down the river on the opposite bank. The building itself is a single dark, patchy roof in a block of otherwise renovated frontages, and is unlikely to be picked out above 2,000 feet. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is about 17 km south-southeast; Strausberg (EDAY) about 27 km east-northeast, Schönhagen (EDAZ) about 45 km southwest. Central Berlin controlled airspace with a 368 m tower nearby: plan for 2,000 to 3,000 feet and expect summer haze over the inner districts.
