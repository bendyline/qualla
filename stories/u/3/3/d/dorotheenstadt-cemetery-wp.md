# Dorotheenstadt Cemetery

Four acres behind a wall on Chausseestraße hold Hegel, Fichte, Brecht, Schinkel and a stone cross for the men hanged in April 1945 — Germany's argument with itself, buried in one place.

**Location:** 52.5284, 13.3837

**Categories:** germany, berlin, history, literature

## Introduction

Bertolt Brecht spent his last years at 125 Chausseestraße, in an apartment overlooking a cemetery wall. He is now on the other side of it, a few dozen paces from where his desk stood, under a plain stone. Helene Weigel lies beside him. Within a short walk of that stone are the philosopher who defined German idealism, the philosopher who preceded him, the architect who gave Berlin its neoclassical face, and a cross bearing the names of men the SS shot in a park three streets away, in the last week of April 1945.

## A King's Land, Outside the Gate

Berlin in the second half of the eighteenth century was running out of room to bury people. The city was growing, vacant land was being built on, and the fear of epidemics made burials inside the walls increasingly untenable. Frederick II donated ground outside the Oranienburg Gate in the customs wall, and four cemeteries were laid out there; two survive, this one and the French cemetery of the Huguenots next door, with which it is constantly confused. The Dorotheenstadt and Friedrichswerder parishes established their plot in the early 1760s and the first burials took place in 1770. At the outset it was mostly a poor people's cemetery. Then Berlin University opened nearby in 1810, along with academies of science, art, architecture and singing, and the character of the ground changed entirely.

## Hegel's Wish

Cholera reached Berlin in the summer of 1831. Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel left for Kreuzberg, judged the danger past, came back in October, and was dead by 14 November. The official cause was cholera, though the illness may well have been something else. He was buried two days later, and where he was buried was not accidental: he had asked to lie beside Johann Gottlieb Fichte, who had taught philosophy at the same young university before him, and beside Karl Wilhelm Ferdinand Solger. The wish was honoured. Hegel and Fichte stand side by side, and generations of philosophy students have made the trip to Chausseestraße specifically to stand between them — a pilgrimage that Hegel, who thought a great deal about how history arranges itself, might have found appropriate.

## The Sculptors Buried Their Own

As the social standing of the dead rose, the quality of the monuments rose with it, and Berlin's artists ended up designing markers for one another. Johann Gottfried Schadow made monuments for his second wife and for himself; in 1851 his pupil Heinrich Kaehler's statuette of him, modelled back in 1822, was set on his grave. Christian Daniel Rauch sculpted the bust of the locomotive builder August Borsig. Karl Friedrich Schinkel is here, and Friedrich August Stüler, and Johann Heinrich Strack. At the end of the main axis stands a marble Martin Luther, a 1909 copy of Schadow's Wittenberg statue, moved here in 1975 after the Dorotheenstadt church that housed it was destroyed in the war. Keeping all of this standing is expensive: restoring Strack's grave alone, with imported Italian marble, cost a quarter of a million euros, and the mausoleum of Schinkel's pupil Friedrich Hitzig — its frescoes now almost without parallel in Berlin — was rescued from critical condition in 2007.

## The Cross and the Unnamed

A tall cross rises above a stone block near the wall. The block carries eight names: Klaus Bonhoeffer, Hans John, Richard Kuenzer, Carl Adolf Marks, Wilhelm zur Nieden, Friedrich Justus Perels, Rüdiger Schleicher, Hans Ludwig Sierks. All were connected to the July 1944 plot against Hitler, and all were killed by the SS in a nearby park on the night of 22-23 April 1945, with the Red Army already in the outer districts and the war days from ending. The monument also names Klaus's brother Dietrich Bonhoeffer, the theologian, and Hans von Dohnanyi, both murdered in concentration camps, and Justus Delbrück, who survived the war only to die in Soviet captivity. A short distance away a marker points to a mass grave holding sixty-four people killed near the cemetery in those same final days. Many of them were never identified, and the marker is the only record that they were ever here at all.

## Carry On

The twentieth century filled the remaining ground with the artists of a divided city. Behind a low hedge is a plot for members of the Academy of Arts, where Anna Seghers lies near Lin Jaldati, who survived three concentration camps and built a career afterwards as a dancer and singer of Jewish songs. Heiner Müller's grave carries a birdbath shaped like an ashtray, which anyone who watched the playwright work will recognise as accurate. Herbert Marcuse, born in Berlin, exiled to America in 1933, came back in 2003 as ashes under a stone that reads simply Weitermachen — carry on. Hanns Eisler, Christa Wolf, Heinrich Mann, John Heartfield, George Tabori, Wolfgang Herrndorf, Ernst Litfaß, inventor of the cylindrical advertising column that still bears his name, and Johannes Rau, the eighth president of the Federal Republic, who expressly asked to be buried here. In 1969 the dissident songwriter Wolf Biermann, living under surveillance at Chausseestraße 131, wrote a song about the Huguenot cemetery next door and reeled off the names on the other side of his wall. He was cataloguing his neighbours.

## Flight Context

The cemetery lies at 52.5284° N, 13.3837° E on Chausseestraße in Berlin-Mitte, about 1.5 km north of Unter den Linden. From the air it appears as a small dark green rectangle of mature trees, roughly 1.7 hectares, pressed between apartment blocks and immediately adjacent to a second, near-identical green rectangle — the French Huguenot cemetery. The pair is the giveaway. The Berlin Hauptbahnhof's glass shed lies about 1 km west-southwest and the Fernsehturm 2 km southeast, giving two easy anchors. Best viewed from 1,500-2,500 ft AGL; the tree canopy hides the graves entirely in summer, so the layout reads far better between November and March. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is about 25 km southeast; Schönhagen (EDAZ) lies southwest and Strausberg (EDAY) east for general aviation. Central Berlin is controlled airspace and requires coordination for low-level transit.
