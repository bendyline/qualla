# Scheunenviertel

Berlin's barn quarter became the address the state assigned to Jews in 1737, the landing place for Eastern European Jewish families after 1918, and the scene of a pogrom on 5 November 1923.

**Location:** 52.5261, 13.4100

**Categories:** Berlin, History of Berlin, Jewish history, Mitte, Neighbourhoods, Weimar Republic

## Introduction

Hay burns without being lit. Packed damp and stacked high, it heats from the inside until it smoulders, and in 1672 Elector Friedrich Wilhelm decided he had watched that happen inside Berlin's walls often enough. He ordered the barns out beyond the fortifications, onto open ground north of the old town. Barns went on multiplying there for generations — by the middle of the nineteenth century almost four thousand barns and stables stood in the excise ring around the inner city — but the name fastened onto this one corner and would not come off. Scheunenviertel. Barn quarter. By the late 1800s it had stopped describing agriculture altogether. It had become a Berlin sneer for a knot of narrow streets near what is now Rosa-Luxemburg-Platz, and for the people who lived in them.

## An Address by Royal Order

The original barn field covered about five hectares, bounded by Grenadierstraße, Prenzlauer Straße, Hirtenstraße and Linienstraße — six lanes running north to south with a pair of short cross-streets. In 1737 King Frederick William I ordered Berlin's Jewish residents out of their lodgings in the city proper and into quarters behind the fortifications; soldiers took over the rooms the Jewish families had vacated. It was a swap arranged by decree, and nobody asked the families. That order is where the quarter's long Jewish history begins — not with a community choosing a neighbourhood, but with a king assigning one. The Scheunenviertel is still often called Berlin's Jewish quarter, which is not quite right. The New Synagogue and the established institutions of Jewish Berlin stood next door, in the Spandauer Vorstadt. The Scheunenviertel was where the arrivals with the least money lived.

## Grenadierstraße

After the First World War, tens of thousands of Eastern European Jews came to Berlin, most of them fleeing violence at home. A great many landed here, in flats that were already full. By 1925 the northern end of Grenadierstraße had the highest population density in the city. By 1929 roughly a third of the quarter's residents were Jewish, and most of those were poor families from Galicia and Romania. The street worked as a street should: kosher restaurants, prayer rooms tucked into back courtyards, Hebrew lettering above the shopfronts, and since 1916 a Jewish community house where young people met. Comfortable Berliners came up from the west to look at it and wrote about what they had seen as though they had crossed a border. The people being looked at were mostly trying to make the rent.

## 5 November 1923

Money came apart that autumn. Prices doubled within a day; wages were carried home in sacks and spent before evening. On 5 November 1923 the unemployment office on Gormannstraße could not pay out — the notes were simply not there. A crowd of jobless men gathered outside, and men who had come for the purpose steered it toward Grenadierstraße. What followed was a pogrom. Shops and homes belonging to Jewish families were broken open and stripped; shopkeepers were pulled into the street and beaten, several of them badly hurt. It ran on into the evening. The police, stationed close by, stayed out of it for hours before they moved. The poorest people in Berlin were robbed by people barely less poor, while the state stood at the corner and watched. Ten days later the Rentenmark was issued and the inflation stopped. Nothing taken on 5 November came back.

## The Triangle

The old barn lanes were already going. Demolition began in 1902, and the cutting of Kaiser-Wilhelm-Straße between 1906 and 1908 finished them off. On the leftover southern apex the city laid out a small green square in 1910 and named it Bülowplatz. Around it gathered an unlikely set of neighbours: the Volksbühne theatre of 1914, the Karl-Liebknecht-Haus of 1910 that became the headquarters of the German Communist Party, and from 1929 Hans Poelzig's Babylon cinema, which still shows films and still hosts Berlinale screenings. On the evening of 9 August 1931, outside that cinema, two Berlin police captains — Paul Anlauf and Franz Lenck — were shot dead at point-blank range. One of the gunmen was a 23-year-old communist named Erich Mielke, who fled to Moscow and came back to run East Germany's Ministry for State Security from 1957 to 1989. He was not tried for the killings until the 1990s. A Berlin court convicted him of murder in 1993 and gave him six years.

## What Is Left

On 4 April 1933 the Nazis staged a raid on Grenadierstraße and brought photographers along to record it. From 1934 they began demolishing buildings they had designated Jewish; fifteen came down on Linienstraße alone by 1936. The synagogues and prayer rooms were wrecked in November 1938. Between 1941 and 1945, at least 196 Jewish residents of Grenadierstraße were deported and murdered — one street, in one small quarter. The buildings followed the people. A munitions store blew up in the barracks on Kleine Alexanderstraße on 16 March 1946, flattening much of what the bombing had spared, and between 1967 and 1970 the GDR cleared the eastern remnant for Karl-Liebknecht-Straße and raised prefabricated blocks in its place. Grass still covers a good deal of it. Since reunification the surviving streets have filled up with bars and galleries. A short walk west, a museum keeps the workshop where Otto Weidt employed blind and deaf Jewish workers, forged papers for them, and hid families in his studio.

## Flight Context

Rosa-Luxemburg-Platz and the surviving Scheunenviertel streets sit at 52.526°N, 13.410°E in Berlin-Mitte, about half a kilometre north of Alexanderplatz. The unmistakable fix is the Fernsehturm, roughly 600 m almost due south of the square; the drum-shaped roof of the Volksbühne marks the triangle itself, with Karl-Liebknecht-Straße running past on the south-east. From 1,500–2,500 ft the quarter reads as an irregular patchwork of nineteenth-century blocks, GDR slab housing and open grass — the grass is where the demolitions were. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) lies about 19 km south-south-east; Schönhagen (EDAZ) is roughly 39 km south-west. Central Berlin sits inside the EDDB control zone and terminal airspace, so clearances are mandatory. Winter haze and low stratus are common from November through February.
