# Topography of Terror

The Gestapo and SS ran the machinery of Nazi persecution from this short Berlin street; the ground where their offices stood is now deliberately, permanently open.

**Location:** 52.5064, 13.3837

**Categories:** berlin, germany, world-war-ii, holocaust, museums, memorials, berlin-wall, kreuzberg

## Introduction

For years after the war, Berliners drove bumper cars here. The plot on Prinz-Albrecht-Strasse had been bombed flat in early 1945 and the ruins pushed aside, so the city used the space the way cities use vacant land: a dumping ground for rubble hauled out of Kreuzberg, and a fairground ride. Nobody put up a sign. Beneath the gravel lay the cellars of the building where the Gestapo had interrogated its prisoners, and along the same street stood the offices from which the Reich Security Main Office had organised deportation and mass murder across a continent. It took until 1978 for anyone to say in print that this particular emptiness mattered.

## One Short Street

Three neighbouring properties on Prinz-Albrecht-Strasse and Wilhelmstrasse held the secret police headquarters, the offices of the SS security service, and from 1939 the Reich Security Main Office that fused them into a single apparatus. The Sicherheitspolizei worked here. So did the desk officers who directed the Einsatzgruppen, the mobile units that shot Jewish communities across Poland and the occupied Soviet Union. The address itself became a threat that Germans understood without elaboration.

In the cellar of the Gestapo building was a prison the police ran themselves, outside the ordinary courts. Roughly fifteen thousand opponents of the regime passed through it. People were beaten and tortured there, some were killed there, and the conspirators arrested after the failed attempt on Hitler's life in July 1944 were among those brought in for interrogation. Nothing about the work upstairs looked violent. It was filing, correspondence, transport scheduling, the ordinary output of an office building, and it ended in pits and gas chambers a thousand miles east.

## One Border on Top of Another

When the war ended, the line between the American and Soviet sectors of Berlin happened to run down Prinz-Albrecht-Strasse. The street was renamed Niederkirchnerstrasse, and from 1961 it carried the Berlin Wall along its southern edge. Here, unusually, the Wall was never taken down. About two hundred metres of it survive at the site, the longest remaining stretch of the outer wall, the face West Berliners actually saw. The East Side Gallery in Friedrichshain is longer but belonged to the inner barrier, invisible from the West. So the site carries two dictatorships stacked on the same few hundred metres of pavement, one under the ground and one running across it.

## Digging

The Berlin architecture critic Dieter Hoffmann-Axthelm was among the first, in essays from 1978, to argue that the vacant land had a history worth naming. The first exhibition went up in 1987 for Berlin's 750th anniversary, and in preparing it researchers located and excavated the Gestapo cellar. Those foundations, protected under a canopy but open to the weather, became the core of the memorial: a long trench of exposed brickwork that you walk beside rather than through. The excavation was carried out with East German researchers, and in 1989, in the last months before the border opened, a joint exhibition was shown on both sides of the Wall.

## The Building That Wasn't Built

A foundation took charge of the site in 1992, and its 1993 competition was won by the Swiss architect Peter Zumthor, whose design was likened to the skeleton of a barracks, daylight entering through glazed gaps between concrete beams. Critics loved it. Builders did not: the original contractor went insolvent partway through, no replacement would take on the fixed fee, Berlin refused to find another three to five million euros, and federal money was withheld pending progress that could not now be made. Three bare concrete stairwells stood on the site for the better part of a decade, a ruin of a memorial to a ruin. In 2004, with 13.9 million euros already spent, they were demolished over the protests of Zumthor and other architects. The replacement, by the architect Ursula Wilms with the landscape architect Heinz W. Hallmann, was finished in 2010 and opened on 6 May by Federal President Horst Köhler, sixty-five years after the end of the war in Europe. The public was let in the following day.

## Deliberately Empty

The documentation centre is a low glass box, and the grounds around it are gravel and scrub kept bare on purpose. Inside are the photographs: political prisoners recorded in the Gestapo's own files, and Berlin Jews on a station platform being loaded for the journey that killed them. Some of those pictures were taken by the men who worked here, which is the quiet argument the whole site makes. Persecution on this scale required not just fanatics but clerks, lawyers, budget officers and railway timetables, and the people who supplied them mostly went home in the evening to ordinary lives.

The foundation now coordinates memorial sites across Germany and works with others abroad, and it looks after the documentation centre in Niederschöneweide built on the last well-preserved forced-labour camp in the city, one of more than three thousand such compounds in wartime Berlin. Entry to both is free. Elsewhere in the city, memorials give you something to look at. Here the ground is left open, and what you are asked to look at is the absence of any excuse.

## Flight Context

Located at 52.5064 N, 13.3837 E on Niederkirchnerstrasse in Berlin, on the boundary of Mitte and Kreuzberg. From the air the site reads as a rectangle of bare pale gravel in an otherwise dense block, with the low glass documentation centre at its northern edge and the grey line of the surviving Berlin Wall segment running along the street. The bulk of the former Reich Air Ministry (now the federal finance ministry) sits immediately north; Potsdamer Platz is 600 m west and Checkpoint Charlie 700 m east. Best viewed at 1,500-2,500 ft AGL. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is about 18 km southeast. Central Berlin airspace requires clearance; the bare gravel gives high contrast in low winter sun.
