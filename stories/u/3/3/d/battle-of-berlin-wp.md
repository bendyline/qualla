# Battle of Berlin

From 16 April to 2 May 1945, well over two million Soviet and Polish soldiers closed on the capital of the Reich, and a city of more than two million civilians paid the price for the war the Nazi state had begun.

**Location:** 52.5186, 13.3761

**Categories:** Battles and operations of World War II, Battles of World War II involving the Soviet Union, 1945 in Germany, History of Berlin

## Introduction

By the time it ended, Berlin was no longer the city anyone had grown up in. The Soviet Berlin Strategic Offensive Operation, launched on 16 April 1945 and ended with the surrender of the city's garrison on 2 May, killed roughly eighty thousand Soviet and Polish soldiers, at least ninety-two thousand German troops, and an estimated one hundred and twenty-five thousand civilians over the whole operation. By a frequently cited estimate, as many as one hundred thousand women and girls in Berlin were raped by Soviet troops in the weeks of and after the battle, a wave of sexual violence whose survivors carried it for the rest of their lives. Hitler killed himself in his bunker on 30 April. The buildings he had ordered defended to the last were rubble. The war in Europe formally ended six days after the city fell. Almost nobody who lived through what happened in Berlin in those weeks remembered it as victory or defeat. They remembered it as something closer to the end of the world.

## Three fronts and the Seelow Heights

Stalin gave the assignment to two of his marshals and told them to race for it. Georgy Zhukov's 1st Belorussian Front would attack from the east; Ivan Konev's 1st Ukrainian Front from the south; Konstantin Rokossovsky's 2nd Belorussian Front would pin German forces north of the city. Together they fielded some 2.3 to 2.5 million men, including the 1st Polish Army, with 6,250 tanks and over 41,000 artillery pieces. Facing them, General Gotthard Heinrici, one of the German army's best defensive tacticians, fortified the Seelow Heights west of the Oder River. The Battle of the Seelow Heights, fought from 16 to 19 April, became one of the last pitched battles of the war: roughly a million Soviet soldiers ground forward against a hundred thousand German defenders. By the fourth day Zhukov had broken through. Konev had taken Forst the day before. The German 9th Army was encircled in a pocket west of Frankfurt an der Oder and would be destroyed trying to break out at the Battle of Halbe. The road to Berlin was open.

## The garrison

Helmuth Weidling assumed command inside the city on 23 April. What he had to defend with would have been laughable if it had not been tragic. The remnants of regular Wehrmacht divisions and Waffen-SS units were stiffened by Volkssturm, the desperate civilian militia drafted in the war's final months, and by Hitler Youth, some of the boys as young as fourteen, sent to fight Soviet armour with Panzerfausts in the streets where they had played as children. They died in numbers difficult to bear. Berliners sheltered in U-Bahn stations and cellars, water and electricity gone, the air hot from burning timbers and smelling of phosphorus and the dead. Western Allied bombers had already pounded the city for years; on the night of 20–21 April, just before the Soviets entered, RAF Mosquitos completed a thirty-six-night bombing campaign on Berlin. The civilian population, with no role in any of the decisions that had brought the front to their door, simply had to live through what was coming.

## Two flags over the Reichstag

Hitler turned fifty-six on 20 April, the day Soviet artillery began shelling Berlin's centre. He spent his last week underground in the Führerbunker beneath the Reich Chancellery, learning that no relief armies were coming, stripping Göring and Himmler of their offices, and on 30 April killing himself alongside Eva Braun. Outside, Soviet troops fought block by block toward the Reichstag, the symbol they had been told to capture. The Yevgeny Khaldei photograph of a Red Army soldier raising the Soviet flag over the Reichstag's roof, a re-enactment staged on 2 May and famously retouched, with smoke added and a second wristwatch, a possible sign of looting, scratched off the wrist of the soldier steadying the flag-bearer, came to stand for the whole campaign. The reality on the ground was less photogenic: house-to-house fighting through the Tiergarten, fires uncontrolled across whole districts, and the German garrison surrendering in pieces until Weidling formally surrendered to Vasily Chuikov of the 8th Guards Army on 2 May.

## What survived

Some German units fought westward in the days after the formal surrender, hoping to surrender to American or British forces rather than to the Soviets they feared. Hundreds of thousands of German soldiers became Soviet prisoners; many would not return home for years. The mass rape of German women by Soviet troops, documented by the anonymous diarist of A Woman in Berlin, later identified as Marta Hillers, and many others, was for decades a subject German society could barely speak about and Soviet historiography refused to discuss. The Western Allies, by Eisenhower's deliberate choice, had not contested the city; their main contribution had been the air war. By the time the official end of the war in Europe came on 8 May, Berlin was a wreckage of broken stone and unburied bodies. The city would be divided into four occupation sectors within weeks; eventually walled in two; eventually reunited. The cost of taking the capital of Nazi Germany had been borne by Soviet conscripts, German conscripts, and civilians who had no say in any of it.

## Flight Context

Berlin lies at 52.519°N, 13.376°E in the North German Plain, with the Spree River winding through the centre and the Havel chain of lakes to the west. The Reichstag and the Brandenburg Gate, both standing today, mark the centre. EDDB (Berlin Brandenburg) lies about 19 km south-southeast of the Reichstag; EDDT (Tegel) closed in November 2020. From cruising altitude the Tiergarten makes a green rectangle west of the city centre and the TV tower at Alexanderplatz is the tallest visual landmark.
