# Bode Museum

Wilhelm von Bode believed art collections should mix periods and regions. The neo-Baroque museum at the prow of Museum Island still does, even after a 100-kilogram gold coin walked out the window.

**Location:** 52.5219, 13.3947

**Categories:** museums, art, Berlin, Museum Island, architecture, UNESCO

## Introduction

At 3:20 in the morning on 27 March 2017, four men leaned a wooden ladder against the back wall of the Bode Museum, broke a window facing the S-Bahn tracks, and made off with a single object weighing exactly 100 kilograms. The Big Maple Leaf — a Canadian commemorative coin 50 centimeters across, 2.8 centimeters thick, struck in 24-karat gold and worth around €3.7 million — had been on loan from a private collector. German police believe the thieves wheeled it out the window, lowered it on the ladder to the railroad tracks below, and drove off in a wheelbarrow. The coin has never been recovered. The forensic dust at the scene suggests it was melted down within hours.

## The Prow of the Island

The building itself sits at the northern tip of Museum Island, where the Spree splits into two channels. Built between 1898 and 1904 by order of Kaiser Wilhelm II to plans by Ernst von Ihne, it is shaped to its triangular site — a small Baroque Revival palace whose curved façade rises directly from the water. The original name was the Kaiser-Friedrich-Museum, after Frederick III, the liberal-minded emperor who reigned for ninety-nine days in 1888 before dying of throat cancer. A bronze equestrian statue of Frederick once stood in the square in front; the East German authorities removed it after the war. The museum was renamed in 1956 to honor Wilhelm von Bode, its first director and the curator who shaped its founding philosophy.

## Bode's Idea: Mix Everything

Wilhelm von Bode (1845–1929) was one of the great museum directors of the late nineteenth century. His radical conviction, which now feels conventional, was that art should not be hung by genre alone. A Renaissance bronze deserved to sit near a Renaissance painting, near Renaissance furniture, near the Persian tapestry that might once have hung behind it. Period rooms, mixed media, the lived context of the object — all of these came from Bode. The Kaiser-Friedrich was his laboratory. After his refurbishment-era reopening in 2006 (a €156 million project that closed the building from 1997), the museum is now home to three major collections: the Skulpturensammlung of European sculpture from the early Middle Ages to the eighteenth century; the Museum für Byzantinische Kunst, with one of Europe's finest Byzantine collections outside Greece and Italy; and the Münzkabinett, the coin and medal cabinet whose stolen Maple Leaf was on loan.

## Sculpture and the Spree

Walk in through the south entrance and the rotunda greets you with a copy of the Equestrian Statue of Friedrich Wilhelm I — the original is at Schloss Charlottenburg — beneath a high cupola. From there the galleries radiate. Donatello's marble Madonna and Child (Pazzi Madonna). Late medieval altarpieces from Germany and the Low Countries. Bernini terra-cottas. Tilman Riemenschneider's astonishing limewood saints from Würzburg, carved with such precision you can count the stitches in their robes. The Trecento room holds Italian gilt-ground paintings rescued from the same flak tower that destroyed so much of the rest. The Byzantine collection runs from third-century sarcophagi to ivory carvings of the eleventh and twelfth centuries — objects gathered when imperial Germany was buying art on a scale matched only by American railroad money.

## What Was Lost in the Tower

During the Second World War, much of the museum's collection was moved to the Flakturm Friedrichshain — one of Berlin's massive antiaircraft towers — for safekeeping. The towers were nearly indestructible from the air. They were not safe from what came next. In May 1945, after the city fell, fires broke out in the Friedrichshain tower. The cause is still disputed — Soviet looters, accidental ignition, deliberate sabotage — but the consequences are clear. More than 400 paintings and around 300 sculptures from the museum's collection were lost, either burned in the fires or looted in the chaos. Some pieces have surfaced in the decades since, in private collections in Russia and the Caucasus. Most have not. The blank wall labels in certain galleries, where photographs stand in for vanished masterpieces, are part of the museum's argument with its own twentieth century.

## UNESCO and the Heist

Museum Island as a whole was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1999, in recognition of its development as a unified cultural ensemble between 1830 and 1930. The Bode is the youngest building in that ensemble and arguably the most photogenic, the small palace on the water that closes the northern end of the island. The 2017 coin theft made it briefly the most photographed for less flattering reasons. Three of the four thieves — members of a Berlin clan family — were eventually convicted in 2020 and sentenced to several years in prison; Ahmed Remmo's brother Wayci was acquitted. The Big Maple Leaf itself is presumed gone forever, sitting in someone's safe as a few dozen ingots. The Bode reopens its doors every Tuesday through Sunday. The Donatellos and the Riemenschneiders are still there. The window has been replaced.

## Flight Context

The Bode Museum sits at 52.522°N, 13.395°E at the northwest tip of Museum Island in central Berlin. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is 19 km southeast. From cruising altitude the building stands out as a prow shape where the Spree splits, with the Pergamon Museum, Neues Museum, and Berlin Cathedral arranged behind it down the length of the long, narrow island. The S-Bahn tracks (route by which the 2017 thieves escaped) skirt the island's eastern edge.
