# Bode Museum

Wilhelm von Bode believed art collections should mix periods and regions. The neo-Baroque museum at the prow of Museum Island still does, even after a 100-kilogram gold coin left through a window.

**Location:** 52.5219, 13.3947

**Categories:** museums, art, Berlin, Museum Island, architecture, UNESCO

## Introduction

In the early hours of 27 March 2017, thieves climbed up from the S-Bahn tracks with a ladder, got in through a window at the back of the Bode Museum, and made off with a single object weighing exactly 100 kilograms. The Big Maple Leaf — a Canadian commemorative coin 50 centimeters across, 2.8 centimeters thick, struck in 24-karat gold and worth around €3.7 million — had been on loan from a private collector. Police believe the thieves pushed it along the railway tracks in a wheelbarrow and lowered it by rope to a getaway car waiting at Monbijou Park. The coin has never been recovered; investigators believe it was cut into small pieces and sold.

## The Prow of the Island

The building itself sits at the northern tip of Museum Island, where the Spree splits into two channels. Built between 1898 and 1904 by order of Kaiser Wilhelm II to plans by Ernst von Ihne, it is shaped to its triangular site — a small Baroque Revival palace whose curved façade rises directly from the water. The original name was the Kaiser-Friedrich-Museum, after Frederick III, the liberal-minded emperor who reigned for ninety-nine days in 1888 before dying of throat cancer. A bronze equestrian statue of Frederick once stood in the square in front; the East German authorities destroyed it after the war. The museum was renamed in 1956 to honor Wilhelm von Bode, its first director and the curator who shaped its founding philosophy.

## Bode's Idea: Mix Everything

Wilhelm von Bode (1845–1929) was one of the great museum directors of the late nineteenth century. His radical conviction, which now feels conventional, was that art should not be hung by genre alone. A Renaissance bronze deserved to sit near a Renaissance painting, near Renaissance furniture, near the Persian tapestry that might once have hung behind it. Period rooms, mixed media, the lived context of the object — all of these came from Bode. The Kaiser-Friedrich was his laboratory. Since its reopening in 2006, after a €156 million refurbishment that closed the building from 1997, the museum has been home to three major collections: the Skulpturensammlung of European sculpture from the early Middle Ages to the eighteenth century; the Museum für Byzantinische Kunst, one of Europe's major Byzantine collections; and the Münzkabinett, the coin and medal cabinet whose stolen Maple Leaf was on loan.

## Sculpture and the Spree

Walk in through the main entrance at the prow and the great domed hall greets you with a copy of Andreas Schlüter's equestrian statue of the Great Elector, Frederick William of Brandenburg, on its original pedestal — the original statue stands at Schloss Charlottenburg — beneath a high cupola. From there the galleries radiate. Donatello's marble Madonna and Child (Pazzi Madonna). Late medieval altarpieces from Germany and the Low Countries. Bernini terra-cottas. Tilman Riemenschneider's astonishing limewood saints from Würzburg. The Trecento room holds Italian gold-ground paintings. The Byzantine collection runs from third-century sarcophagi to ivory carvings of the eleventh and twelfth centuries — objects gathered when imperial Germany was buying art on a scale matched only by American railroad money.

## What Was Lost in the Tower

During the Second World War, much of the museum's collection was moved to the Flakturm Friedrichshain — one of Berlin's massive antiaircraft towers — for safekeeping. The towers were nearly indestructible from the air. They were not safe from what came next. In May 1945, after the city fell, fires broke out in the Friedrichshain tower. The cause is still disputed — Soviet looters, accidental ignition, deliberate sabotage — but the consequences are clear. More than 400 paintings and around 300 sculptures from the museum's collection were lost, either burned in the fires or looted in the chaos. Soviet forces carried off much of what survived, and most of it did not return to Berlin until the 1950s. Much of the rest has never resurfaced. In 2015 the museum's exhibition The Lost Museum set photographic reproductions and plaster casts of vanished masterpieces beside damaged survivors, part of the museum's argument with its own twentieth century.

## UNESCO and the Heist

Museum Island as a whole was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1999, in recognition of its development as a unified cultural ensemble between 1830 and 1930. The Bode is arguably the most photogenic building in that ensemble, the small palace on the water that closes the northern end of the island. The 2017 coin theft made it briefly the most photographed for less flattering reasons. In 2020 a Berlin court convicted two cousins from a Berlin clan family and a museum security guard who had helped them, with sentences of between three years and four months and four and a half years; a fourth defendant was acquitted. The Big Maple Leaf itself is presumed gone forever, cut up and sold off in pieces. The Bode still opens its doors to visitors. The Donatellos and the Riemenschneiders are still there. The window has been replaced.

## Flight Context

The Bode Museum sits at 52.522°N, 13.395°E at the northwest tip of Museum Island in central Berlin. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is about 19 km south-southeast. From cruising altitude the building stands out as a prow shape where the Spree splits, with the Pergamon Museum, Neues Museum, and Berlin Cathedral arranged behind it down the length of the long, narrow island. The S-Bahn viaduct (the route by which the 2017 thieves came and went) crosses the island right beside the museum.
