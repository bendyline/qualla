# Natural History Museum, Berlin

Thirty million specimens sit behind the doors on Invalidenstrasse, and fewer than one in five thousand of them is ever put on show.

**Location:** 52.5302, 13.3792

**Categories:** Berlin, Museums, Natural history, Dinosaurs, Science, Germany

## Introduction

Do the arithmetic on the Museum für Naturkunde and it stops sounding like a museum. The collection runs to more than thirty million zoological, palaeontological and mineralogical specimens, including over ten thousand type specimens — the individual animals and plants against which every later identification of a species is checked. Fewer than one in five thousand of those thirty million is on display. The rest sit in drawers, jars and cabinets on Invalidenstrasse, worked on by researchers who fly in from everywhere, in a building far less a gallery than a very large filing system with a public entrance.

## Thirteen Metres of Neck and Ribcage

The thing everybody comes for stands in the central hall and is the largest mounted dinosaur skeleton in the world. Giraffatitan brancai — long known as Brachiosaurus — was assembled from bones almost all belonging to a single animal, with a few tail vertebrae borrowed from a second individual of the same size and species.

The historic mount stood 12.72 metres tall and 22.25 metres long until it was taken apart and rebuilt in 2007 to match newer science, which raised it to 13.27 metres. Alive, the long-necked herbivore probably weighed around fifty tonnes. The Diplodocus carnegiei mounted alongside it, a cast of the original in Pittsburgh, is actually longer at 27 metres, but next to the Giraffatitan it looks like a different order of animal — lower, narrower, lighter. Standing under the ribcage, your eye keeps failing to accept that the neck is real.

## Who Carried It Out

The bones came out of Tendaguru, in what was then German East Africa and is now Tanzania, excavated between 1909 and 1913 under the German palaeontologist Werner Janensch with Edwin Hennig as his assistant. Roughly 250 tonnes of fossil material was shipped to Berlin — one of the largest hauls in the history of the science.

It did not walk to the coast. The expedition employed local African workers — about 160 porters at the outset — and the fossils travelled overland to the port at Lindi on their shoulders, load by load. Their names are largely absent from the literature that made the expedition's European leaders famous. That absence is now part of the museum's own subject matter: German and Tanzanian commentators alike have questioned Berlin's claim to rightful ownership of the specimen. Tanzania has made no formal repatriation demand, and the museum's response has been to open up provenance research and build working partnerships with Tanzanian palaeontologists. The skeleton is a triumph of excavation and a colonial acquisition at the same time, and it cannot honestly be looked at as only one of those.

## A Feather, Then the Bird

In 1860 a single fossil feather turned up in the Solnhofen limestone of Bavaria, 150 million years old. It is in this museum. Eleven years later, from the same beds, came the animal it belonged to.

The Berlin specimen of Archaeopteryx lithographica is the most complete of the twelve known, and arguably the most famous fossil on Earth — not for its beauty, though it is beautiful, but for its timing. Darwin had published On the Origin of Species in 1859. Into that argument dropped a creature with a toothed, reptilian skull, clawed wings, a long bony tail, and the unmistakable impressions of feathers fanned into the stone around it. Here was the transition, lying flat in a slab, exactly where the theory said something like it ought to be. Visitors still crowd the case, and the object at the centre of it is smaller than a magpie.

## 3 February 1945

Around three-quarters of the collection had already been evacuated when the war reached the building. On 3 February 1945 a daylight raid by the United States Army Air Forces struck the east wing. Large parts of it collapsed, and several people sheltering in the air raid shelter were killed. The exhibition rooms for insects and mammals were destroyed and the great whale skeletons were buried in the rubble.

The institution had been on Invalidenstrasse since 1889, in a multi-wing complex designed by August Tiede on the site of the former Royal Iron Foundry. Its founding principle was odd for its time and standard now: only a portion of the holdings would ever be shown to the public, the rest reserved for research. The museum itself dates to 1810, when the collections of the new Berlin university were combined and opened to visitors for the first time; it left the university in 2009 to join the Leibniz Association.

## Twelve Kilometres of Shelving

Behind glass in the Wet Collection wing stand 276,000 jars on 12.6 kilometres of shelving, holding a million animals suspended in ethanol. It is the least theatrical room in Berlin and among the most memorable — a whole zoology reduced to pale shapes in ordered glass, lit like a library.

The rest keeps that scale. The mineral holdings run to roughly 250,000 specimens, of which about 4,500 are shown, alongside a large meteorite collection and the largest known piece of amber. There is a quagga, a huia and a thylacine, three animals that no longer exist, and Bobby the gorilla, a Berlin Zoo celebrity of the 1920s and 1930s. There is a Tyrannosaurus called Tristan with 170 of its roughly 300 bones preserved. In November 2018 the federal government and the city of Berlin committed more than 600 million euros to rebuilding and expanding the place — an unusually large sum for a natural history museum, and a reasonable one for thirty million objects.

## Flight Context

The museum sits at 52.5302°N 13.3792°E, on Invalidenstrasse in Mitte, about 1.5 km north-northwest of the Brandenburg Gate and 700 m northeast of Berlin Hauptbahnhof. From the air it reads as a long multi-wing block with interior courtyards on the north side of Invalidenstrasse, immediately east of the Hamburger Bahnhof and its distinctive twin towers, and just north of the Spree. Best navigational fix: pick up Hauptbahnhof — the vast glass east–west train shed with its perpendicular office bridge — and track northeast one long block. The Fernsehturm is 2.5 km southeast and the Reichstag dome 1.3 km south, so the whole cluster of landmarks sits inside one field of view from 2,000–3,000 ft AGL. This is the middle of the Berlin CTR with restricted airspace over the government quarter directly south, so transit under clearance only. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) lies about 22 km southeast; Schönhagen (EDAZ) is southwest and Strausberg (EDAY) east. Berlin's winter stratus can sit low over Mitte for days; spring and early autumn give the cleanest air over the city centre.
