# Zum schwarzen Ferkel

A Berlin wine shop that Strindberg renamed after a sagging wine sack over the door, and where he, Munch, Przybyszewski and Dagny Juel spent two years making each other into art.

**Location:** 52.5164, 13.3808

**Categories:** berlin, germany, literary-history, art-history, 1890s, unter-den-linden, scandinavian-culture, vanished-places

## Introduction

A wine sack hung on iron chains above the door, black and stuffed and shapeless, and August Strindberg decided it looked like a piglet. The shop beneath it had a perfectly respectable name — Gustav Türkes Weinhandlung und Probierstube, a wine merchant with a tasting room, at the corner of Unter den Linden and Neue Wilhelmstraße. Strindberg called it Zum schwarzen Ferkel instead, The Black Piglet, and the owner liked the joke enough to adopt it. Heinrich Heine, Robert Schumann and E. T. A. Hoffmann were all said to have drunk in the place before him. It is the crowd who came after that nobody has managed to forget.

## Escape from Friedrichshölle

Strindberg arrived in Berlin from Sweden in 1892, a year after his divorce from his first wife, the actress Siri von Essen. The Swedish writer Ola Hansson and his wife, the critic Laura Marholm, had invited him, and at first he lived with the couple out in Friedrichshagen on the eastern lakes. It did not last. In letters he began calling the place Friedrichshölle — Friedrichs-Hell — and eventually he decamped for the centre of the city, falling out with his hosts on the way.

He took a room in a pension on Neue Wilhelmstrasse, a few doors from Türke's wine shop. The men who followed him there had mostly been the Friedrichshagen circle first: the Finnish writer Adolf Paul, the German poet Richard Dehmel, the physician Carl Ludwig Schleich, the Polish journalist Stanisław Przybyszewski. It is Paul we have to thank for the story about the wine sack.

## Whoever Was in Berlin Sat at the Table

Edvard Munch reached Berlin in October 1892 for an exhibition that closed in scandal after seven days, and he became a fixture at the Ferkel almost immediately. The Norwegian writer Gunnar Heiberg joined in November. So did the painters Christian and Oda Krohg, the writer Axel Maurer and the poet Gabriel Finne.

Then the Danish poet Holger Drachmann arrived, quarrelled with Munch, and drove Strindberg out of the group for a while — long enough for him to begin courting the Austrian journalist Frida Uhl, who would become his second wife. The moment she left Berlin for Munich after their secret engagement, he was back at the wine shop.

Nobody organised any of this. There was no manifesto, no membership, no host. There was a table, and whoever happened to be in Berlin sat at it.

## Aspasia

Munch brought Dagny Juel to the Ferkel for the first time on 8 March 1893. She was twenty-five, Norwegian, in Berlin to study music, and she went on to write a drama called Den sterkere and short fiction of her own. The men at the table nicknamed her Aspasia and then spent an extraordinary amount of energy competing for her — Strindberg included, three weeks of it that March, while he was engaged to Frida Uhl.

Almost everything written about Juel for the next century was written by those men, and it reads accordingly: she appears as a muse, a temptation, a reason for quarrels, and only rarely as a writer. On 18 August 1893 she married Przybyszewski. On 5 June 1901, three days short of her thirty-fourth birthday, a young admirer named Władysław Emeryk shot her in a room at the Grand Hotel in Tbilisi while her five-year-old son Zenon was there. Emeryk turned the gun on himself the following day.

## What They Made of Each Other

Munch had loved her and felt betrayed by the marriage. He painted her again and again, and she is almost certainly the woman in Jealousy. Strindberg turned the whole Berlin period into a novel he called Klostret — The Cloister — which happened to be one of the wine shop's other nicknames, the one nobody remembers. He never published it. The manuscript waited until 1966, fifty-four years after his death, to reach print.

Adolf Paul got there first, with Strindberg-Erinnerungen und -Briefe in 1914. Between the memoir, the withheld novel and the canvases, a few dozen people drinking in a back room off Unter den Linden produced one of the most heavily documented two-year stretches in European art — and most of that documentation is score-settling.

## Nothing Left to Visit

There is no plaque, no bar, no reconstructed interior. The building at the corner was destroyed in the Second World War, and Neue Wilhelmstraße no longer runs where it ran; the street plan of this quarter was redrawn twice over, once by bombing and once by the wall that stood a few hundred metres west.

What is there now is the grandest stretch of Unter den Linden — embassies, hotels, lime trees, tour groups moving between the Brandenburg Gate and Museum Island, walking over the spot several thousand times a day. Most literary pilgrimages need a building to stand in front of. This one gets by without one. The novel exists, the memoir exists, the paintings exist, and between them they preserve a room that has been gone for eighty years.

## Flight Context

Located at 52.5164 N, 13.3808 E, on the grand eastern approach of Unter den Linden roughly 200 metres east of the Brandenburg Gate, in Berlin-Mitte. From the air the boulevard itself is the landmark: a dead-straight double avenue of lime trees running about 1.5 km east from the Gate and the Tiergarten to the domed bulk of the Berlin Cathedral and Museum Island on the Spree. The Ferkel's corner sits at the western end of that line, between the Gate and the Hotel Adlon block. The Fernsehturm's needle at Alexanderplatz, 2 km further east, is the coarse pointer; the Tiergarten's dark green rectangle to the west is the other. Central Berlin is dense controlled airspace with tight clearance limits — plan any low pass at 1,500–2,500 ft AGL and expect to be held to the line of the boulevard. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is about 19 km southeast, Schönhagen (EDAZ) about 38 km south-southwest. Summer haze over the Spree basin flattens the whole quarter into grey; the Gate's colonnade and the cathedral dome remain readable when the street grid does not.
