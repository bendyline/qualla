# Zentralfriedhof Friedrichsfelde

A municipal cemetery in Lichtenberg that became the burial ground of the German left, where a Politburo decided who was important enough to lie in which row.

**Location:** 52.5156, 13.5106

**Categories:** germany, berlin, history, memorials, east-germany

## Introduction

On the second Sunday of every January a crowd walks east through Lichtenberg to a cemetery gate, files into a sunken circle of grass and passes a porphyry obelisk cut with four words: Die Toten mahnen uns. The dead admonish us. They have come for two people arrested in Berlin on 15 January 1919 and murdered the same night, Karl Liebknecht and Rosa Luxemburg, whose names are on two of the ten stones ringing that obelisk. Neither stone marks a body. Liebknecht is buried elsewhere in the same cemetery; Luxemburg's remains were never conclusively identified. The Memorial to the Socialists is, among other things, a lesson in what a state will build over an absence.

## The Funeral That Changed the Cemetery

It began as municipal infrastructure. Berlin founded the Friedrichsfelde cemetery in 1881 on a rectangle of open ground a kilometre long and a quarter wide, laid out along the lines of Hamburg's great Ohlsdorf cemetery — a park with graves in it rather than a walled yard. What it became was decided by a single funeral. In 1900 Wilhelm Liebknecht, co-founder of the Social Democratic Party, was buried here, and the crowds that came turned the place into something the city had not planned. Over the following decades it filled with the leaders and the rank and file of the German left: trade unionists, editors, deputies, organisers. Carl Legien and Paul Singer and Franz Mehring and Hugo Haase lie in the same ground, and their politics did not always agree with one another in life.

## Mies van der Rohe in Brick

In January 1919 the coffins of the dead of the Spartacist rising were brought here, Liebknecht's among them, and Luxemburg's later that year. Seven years afterwards the art collector and Communist Party functionary Eduard Fuchs commissioned a monument for the mass grave from an architect who had not yet become the man history remembers: Ludwig Mies van der Rohe, four years from directing the Bauhaus and a quarter-century from Chicago. What he built resembled nothing else in his career — a broad, blunt mass of Oldenburg clinker brick, whole courses stepping forward and back so that the wall caught its own shadow, with a five-pointed star on the front. No supplier could be found who would make the star in stainless steel, so five rhombuses were ordered separately and assembled into one. It was unveiled on 13 June 1926. The star came off in February 1933, and in early 1935 the Nazi authorities had the whole structure taken down to its foundations. It survives only in photographs.

## Ten Graves and a Politburo

The replacement went up in 1951, well away from the original site, and was inaugurated by Wilhelm Pieck, the first president of East Germany. It is a circle of lawn with the obelisk at its centre, ten graves around it and a curving brick wall behind holding gravestones and niches of urns. Who got in was not a family matter. Until 1989 the decision rested with the Politburo of the ruling party, which also decided who was merely distinguished enough for the Pergolenweg section immediately behind the wall — near the leaders, but not among them, with room for up to three relatives' urns. Several of the ten central graves are cenotaphs. Ernst Thälmann, the Communist leader, was murdered at Buchenwald in 1944 and burned there. Rudolf Breitscheid lies at Stahnsdorf. The hierarchy was maintained anyway, in stone, for people whose bodies the state did not have.

## Three Hundred and Twenty-Seven Names

Set into the curved wall is a slab of red marble carrying 327 names — men and women who were killed between 1933 and 1945 for resisting the Nazi regime. Among them are Arvid Harnack, Harro Schulze-Boysen, John Sieg, Ilse Stöbe, Heinrich Koenen, and Hans and Hilde Coppi, most of them associated with the intelligence and resistance network the Gestapo labelled the Rote Kapelle. They were economists, journalists, an air force officer, a married couple. They passed information, printed leaflets, argued about what could be done, and were arrested, tried and executed. The tablet gives them no ranks and no biographies, only names in rows, which is both the limitation of such a monument and, standing in front of it, the point of one.

## Neighbours

The rest of the cemetery makes stranger company than the memorial intends. Käthe Kollwitz is here, whose entire life's work was about grief and the people who are left behind. So is Friedrich Simon Archenhold, who built the great refracting telescope at Treptow. So is Walter Womacka, the artist who ran the redesign of Alexanderplatz. Klaus Fuchs, the physicist who gave the Soviet Union the American bomb, lies in the Pergolenweg, as does Markus Wolf, who ran East German foreign intelligence for three decades. Erich Mielke, who ran the Stasi, was buried in 2000 in an anonymous communal urn plot, unmarked, a short walk from the monument the cemetery erected six years later to the victims of Stalinism. Nobody planned that adjacency. It is simply what happens when a country buries its history in one place and then goes on having a history.

## Flight Context

The Zentralfriedhof Friedrichsfelde lies at 52.5156° N, 13.5106° E in the Berlin borough of Lichtenberg, about 8 km east of Alexanderplatz. From the air it reads as a large, densely wooded rectangle roughly 1 km by 0.25 km, running east–west and standing out clearly against the surrounding pattern of prefabricated housing blocks and rail yards. The strongest nearby landmark is the Tierpark Berlin, the very large wooded zoo grounds about 2 km south-east — once you have the Tierpark, the cemetery is the next big block of trees to the north-west. The Fernsehturm bears west at roughly 8 km. Best viewed from 1,500–2,500 ft AGL; the memorial's circular lawn and curved wall sit near the western gate and are visible as a distinct clearing when the canopy is thin, so November to March gives the better read. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is about 19 km south-south-west; Strausberg (EDAY) lies roughly 27 km east-north-east and Schönhagen (EDAZ) about 47 km south-west. Airspace over eastern Berlin is controlled and requires coordination for low transit.
