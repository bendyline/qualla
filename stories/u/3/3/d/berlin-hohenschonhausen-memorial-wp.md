# Berlin-Hohenschönhausen Memorial

The Stasi's main political prison erased itself from East Berlin's maps for forty years. Today its survivors lead the tours.

**Location:** 52.5417, 13.5011

**Categories:** history, Berlin, Stasi, Cold War, memorial, human rights

## Introduction

Erika Riemann was sixteen when she drew a small bow in lipstick on a portrait of Stalin in her East German classroom in 1948. She spent the next eight years in Soviet and East German prisons, including a stretch at Hohenschönhausen. Decades later, after the Wall came down, she walked into the same prison again, this time as a guide. The Berlin-Hohenschönhausen Memorial does this on principle. Tours through the corridors, the interrogation rooms, and the windowless 'U-boat' cells in the cellar are led, whenever possible, by the people who once lived there as inmates. They tell their own stories.

## The Place That Did Not Exist

For forty years, Hohenschönhausen sat inside a restricted military zone in the Lichtenberg district of East Berlin and did not appear on city maps. The streets were sealed. The neighbors were Stasi families. Even the streetcar drivers who passed nearby could not see in. When citizens were brought here for questioning, they were driven in unmarked vans with caged compartments, deliberately routed in circles so they would lose any sense of where in the city they were. Most prisoners never knew the building's name until decades later, when reunification finally let the maps tell the truth. The exclusion zone is the reason the prison's documents survived: in November 1989, when crowds stormed the central Stasi headquarters at Normannenstrasse, no one came here. There was no public 'here' to come to.

## The U-Boat

The original cellar — built by the Soviet NKVD in 1945 and used by the Stasi from 1951 — was nicknamed das U-Boot, the submarine. Cells lit only by exposed bulbs ran round-the-clock. Prisoners were forbidden to know the time of day. Walls were lined with sheet metal that drummed against the slightest movement. In the early years torture was physical: water cells, sleep deprivation, beatings. From the 1960s on the methods turned psychological. The Stasi developed Zersetzung — 'decomposition' — designed to break a person's mind without leaving marks. Inmates were isolated for months. Confessions were extracted by patience and despair. The doctors in the prison hospital, twenty-eight beds in cells, included staff trained to recognize when a prisoner was near collapse and when he could be pushed further.

## Twenty Thousand Through the Camp

The site has two histories. From June 1945 the Soviet NKVD ran it as Special Camp No. 3, a transit point for Germans on their way to camps deeper in the Soviet system. Over twenty thousand people passed through. Official numbers list 886 deaths from malnutrition, disease, and cold between July 1945 and October 1946; independent estimates run as high as 3,000. Bodies were dumped in nearby bomb craters. After the East German Ministry of State Security took over in 1951, the function shifted from transit to interrogation. Tens of thousands of citizens — pastors, dissidents, would-be escapees, suspected Western sympathizers, people who told the wrong joke — were processed through the cells until 1989.

## The Survivors Who Built the Memorial

The site became a memorial in the early 1990s on the initiative of former inmates themselves. Many had spent decades unable to speak about what had happened to them. The first director, Hubertus Knabe, was a historian whose father had been imprisoned by the Stasi. The Foundation is funded jointly by the German federal government and the Berlin state government, and tours run year-round in German, English, and several other languages. The 2006 film The Lives of Others, set partly in this kind of facility, drew international attention. But the most important voices here are not in the films. They are the men and women in their seventies and eighties who walk visitors past the door of the cell where they spent eighteen months in 1972 or 1983 or 1985, and who explain, in steady voices, what was done and why it must not be forgotten.

## Memory as Policy

Hohenschönhausen is one of the founding members of the Platform of European Memory and Conscience, which links memorials of twentieth-century totalitarianism across the continent. The German federal government considers state-funded sites like this one part of national civic education; thousands of German schoolchildren visit each year. The memorial faces ongoing controversy — some Germans, especially older eastern citizens, resent what they see as one-sided reckoning with the GDR. But the principle holds. A regime that built itself on secrecy is being countered, here, with documentation. The streets that hid the prison for forty years now carry visitors to it on a public bus that runs every twenty minutes.

## Flight Context

The memorial is at 52.542°N, 13.501°E in the Alt-Hohenschönhausen district of northeast Berlin, about 8 km northeast of the city center. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is 22 km south. From cruising altitude, the site appears as a walled industrial complex set back from the Genslerstrasse, surrounded by Plattenbau apartment blocks built during the GDR era. The S-Bahn ring is visible to the south.
