# Stadion An der Alten Försterei

Union Berlin's supporters dug up and rebuilt their own terraces in 2008 rather than leave Köpenick, and every December they fill the ground with candles to sing carols.

**Location:** 52.4572, 13.5681

**Categories:** Berlin, Football, Stadiums, Köpenick, Sport

## Introduction

In the summer of 2008 the terraces at An der Alten Försterei were torn out by the people who stood on them. More than 2,300 supporters of 1. FC Union Berlin signed up in shifts — clearing rubble, hauling cinder, laying the ground for the concrete steps that professionals would pour and the cantilever roof that specialists would hang. They were not doing it for the romance of it. The German league had stopped renewing the ground's temporary safety licence in 2006, and the club had exactly two options: rebuild the stadium or leave Köpenick. Union had been playing on this patch by the old forester's house since 1920. They chose to rebuild, and then they picked up shovels.

## 1920: A New Pitch on the Edge of Town

The club arrived here because developers took its old home. SC Union Oberschöneweide lost its pitch to housing and moved south-east to the north-western corner of the borough of Köpenick, which in 1920 was still effectively outside the city. The first match on the new ground was a friendly against Viktoria 89 Berlin on 17 March 1920. The official opening came that August, and the club found an opponent worth the occasion: 1. FC Nürnberg, then the champions of Germany. The name has never changed since — An der Alten Försterei, at the old forester's house — and neither, in any fundamental way, has the shape of the place. It has always been a ground where most people stand.

## Slow Decay

Promotion to the DDR-Oberliga in 1966 forced the first expansion; the Gegengerade terrace was raised in 1970, and further extensions at both ends through the late 1970s and early 1980s took capacity to 22,500. Then the money and the crowds went, as they did at most East German clubs in the 1980s, and the terracing was left to crumble. Reunification put Union in the third tier with a ground the German FA regarded as one of the reasons they could not climb out of it. Capacity fell to 18,100. Through the late 1990s the club played on under special temporary licences, renewed year by year, until the DFL stopped issuing them in 2006 and the choice became unavoidable.

## Built by the People Who Stand In It

Union spent the 2008–09 season away at the Friedrich-Ludwig-Jahn-Sportpark in Prenzlauer Berg while the crumbling stone and cinder was replaced with concrete, the open terraces were roofed, and undersoil heating went in. The rebuilt ground opened on 8 July 2009 with a friendly against Hertha BSC, the club from the other side of the city. Phase two waited until funding arrived in May 2012: the old main stand came down and a new one went up, 100.5 metres wide and 23.5 metres high, holding 3,617 seats, for €15 million. Sponsors put in €10 million and the club €2 million; the last €3 million came from a holding company set up to own the stadium, whose shares were sold to the public and bought by more than four thousand members and sponsors. Celtic came for the reopening friendly in the summer of 2013. The first sell-out followed on 31 August 2013, when 21,717 people watched Union beat St. Pauli 3–2.

## Candles and Sofas

In 2003, eighty-nine people turned up at the ground a few days before Christmas to sing carols. Nobody organised it in any official sense; it simply happened, and then it happened again the next year, and larger. By 2015 the Weihnachtssingen drew 28,500 — Union supporters, players and fans of rival clubs from across Germany and Europe, standing in the dark with mulled wine and candles and the occasional flare, working through a set list that alternates carols with football chants. The other invention is stranger. For the 2014 World Cup the club invited supporters to bring their own sofas onto the pitch and watch the tournament there; more than 800 armchairs and settees were arranged in rows in front of a big screen for the duration. The following September, Linkin Park played the first concert ever held at the ground, to 35,000 people.

## What Comes Next

The stadium currently holds 22,012, of which only 3,617 are seats — the rest is terracing, which is unusual for a club that has spent recent seasons in the Bundesliga and in Europe. UEFA's requirements have already forced awkward compromises: in 2023 the first team played its Champions League matches across town at Hertha's Olympiastadion while the under-19s used the Försterei for the Youth League. At the general assembly in October 2024 the club set out the answer: an expansion to 40,500, with the three standing terraces replaced by two-tier seated stands and the main stand extended. Construction is to follow the completion of the new training centre, with the club estimating a start in the summer of 2026 and a temporary move to the Olympiastadion while the work is done. The terraces that 2,300 people dug out by hand will not survive it.

## Flight Context

The ground sits at 52.457°N, 13.568°E in Köpenick, in Berlin's south-east. It is easy to spot from 1,500–3,000 ft: a compact rectangular bowl with a continuous roof over three sides, set among trees at the edge of the Wuhlheide woodland, with the Spree looping past roughly 1 km to the north. Köpenick's old town and the confluence of the Spree and Dahme lie 3 km south-east, and the long straight of the Wuhlheide parkland runs north-west from the ground. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is about 12 km south-west, Strausberg (EDAY) roughly 27 km north-east, Schönhagen (EDAZ) about 47 km west-south-west. Expect EDDB terminal airspace throughout the approach. On winter evenings the floodlights make the ground visible long before the outline is; November to February brings frequent low stratus.
