# Berlin Wall

Eight weeks after East Germany's leader promised that nobody intended to build a wall, barbed wire went across Berlin in the dark — and stayed for 28 years.

**Location:** 52.5044, 13.4411

**Categories:** germany, berlin, cold-war, historic, memorial

## Introduction

On 15 June 1961, asked at a press conference about the sector border, Walter Ulbricht answered with a sentence Germans have been quoting ever since: Niemand hat die Absicht, eine Mauer zu errichten. Nobody has the intention of erecting a wall. It was the first time anyone in the leadership had used the word Mauer in that context, and it was a lie with a fuse on it. Eight weeks later, in the small hours of Sunday 13 August, work crews began unrolling barbed wire across streets, tram tracks and pavements. Berliners who went to bed in one city woke up in two. Germans still call that morning Barbed Wire Sunday.

## The Order Signed at a Garden Party

The decision was taken in the woods. On Saturday 12 August the GDR leadership gathered at a government guesthouse at Döllnsee, north of East Berlin, and it was there, somewhere between the food and the conversation, that Ulbricht signed the order. Wire went up first; the first concrete blocks were set on 17 August, while soldiers of the National People's Army stood in front of the rising barrier under orders to shoot anyone who ran.

The arithmetic behind it was simple and brutal. Since 1949 roughly 3.5 million East Germans had gone west — about a fifth of the country — and they skewed young and highly trained: engineers, physicians, teachers, skilled workers the state could not replace. East Berlin named the barrier the Antifaschistischer Schutzwall, the Anti-Fascist Protection Rampart. Willy Brandt, West Berlin's mayor, called it the Wall of Shame. Between those two names, commuters lost their jobs and families lost each other.

## The Ground Between the Walls

What made the Wall lethal was not the wall. In June 1962 a second, parallel barrier went up deeper inside East Berlin; the houses caught between the two were emptied, their residents relocated, the buildings razed. That cleared ground became the death strip, raked into smooth sand so footprints showed — useful for catching anyone crossing, and just as useful for catching which guards had skipped a patrol.

The barrier was rebuilt four times in 28 years. The final version, begun in 1975, carried an unglamorous official name: Stützwandelement UL 12.11. It took 45,000 reinforced concrete sections standing 3.6 metres high. Maintenance hatches needed two keys turned in two separate locks, so no one person could open them. At chosen points the concrete was deliberately built weaker, so Soviet armour could smash through if war came. It ran more than 140 kilometres, and it enclosed West Berlin completely.

## The First Names

Two days after the border closed, a border guard named Conrad Schumann jumped the wire into the West, and the photograph of him mid-leap travelled the world. On 22 August 1961, Ida Siekmann went out of her third-floor window at 48 Bernauer Strasse and did not survive the fall. She was the first person to die at the Wall. Two days later Günter Litfin, a 24-year-old tailor, tried to swim the Spree and was shot; guards had received shoot-to-kill orders that morning.

The death the world watched belonged to Peter Fechter. Eighteen years old, shot in the pelvis on 17 August 1962 near Zimmerstrasse, he lay tangled in wire inside the strip and bled to death over the better part of an hour while cameras rolled on the western side. Nobody came for him — not the guards who had fired, not the Western soldiers a few metres away, who would have been shot at for trying.

## Tunnels, Balloons and a Stolen Armoured Car

The escapes read like a catalogue of improvisation. At least 70 tunnels were driven under the Wall; 19 of them worked, carrying roughly 400 people out, and one delivered 29 of them in September 1962. Others went over by hot-air balloon, slid along aerial wires, or flew ultralights. One driver took a sports car through the early fortifications at speed; when a beam was fitted at checkpoints to stop that, the next attempt came in a convertible rigged so its roof and windscreen tore away on impact, the occupants lying flat and driving on underneath.

In April 1963 Wolfgang Engels — nineteen, a civilian employee of the army — stole a Soviet armoured personnel carrier and drove it into the Wall. Guards shot him badly, a West Berlin policeman returned fire and hauled him out of the wire, and Engels lived. More than 100,000 people attempted the crossing over the years; over 5,000 made it. At least 136 did not.

## As Far As I Know, Immediately

By autumn 1989 the Eastern Bloc was leaking everywhere. Hungary had begun dismantling its fence to Austria, thousands of East German holidaymakers had walked out through the gap, and on 4 November half a million people filled Alexanderplatz demanding change. Five days later the Politburo agreed to let citizens cross directly westward. Günter Schabowski, who had not been in the meeting, was handed the note shortly before a press conference and read it aloud. A reporter asked when it took effect. He hesitated, then said: as far as I know, immediately, without delay. People walked to the crossings, the overwhelmed guards opened them, and strangers ended the night on top of the concrete with champagne and hammers.

Little survives in place — eighty metres at the Topography of Terror, a painted stretch along the Spree called the East Side Gallery, a reconstructed section at Bernauer Strasse that alone shows the full apparatus. Elsewhere a double line of cobblestones traces the route through the streets. For what is harder to remove, Germans have a phrase: Mauer im Kopf, the wall in the head.

## Flight Context

The coordinates used here (52.5044° N, 13.4411° E) sit on Mühlenstrasse in Friedrichshain, at the East Side Gallery, where the longest surviving stretch of the Wall runs beside the River Spree next to the twin-towered Oberbaumbrücke. From the air the Wall's route is still legible as a seam in the city: a ribbon of newer buildings, wide vacant plots and parkland curving through an older street grid, clearest between Bernauer Strasse in the north and Potsdamer Platz in the centre. Recommended viewing altitude is 1,500–3,000 ft AGL over the inner city, which keeps the Spree, the Fernsehturm at Alexanderplatz and the Brandenburg Gate in one frame; the terrain is flat, roughly 100 ft elevation. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB/BER) lies about 18 km southeast and handles all commercial traffic. Disused Tempelhof, closed in 2008 and now a public park, reads as a distinctive oval about 4 km south of the Wall's central course, and former Tegel (EDDT) as a hexagon northwest of the centre. Autumn and winter bring low stratus and fog to the North German Plain; summer afternoons give the most reliable vertical visibility.
