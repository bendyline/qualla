# Tränenpalast

A glass pavilion at Friedrichstrasse station processed westbound crossings out of East Berlin for twenty-seven years, and Berliners named it for what happened on the pavement outside.

**Location:** 52.5208, 13.3869

**Categories:** berlin, germany, cold-war, berlin-wall, museums, east-germany, mitte, modern-architecture

## Introduction

Horst Lüderitz designed a building full of light. The architect for the East German state railway set a glass façade on narrow steel pillars, raised the whole structure on a pedestal two and a half metres high, and produced a pavilion that looked less like a border post than like the departure lounge of an optimistic decade. Berliners named it something else. They called it the Tränenpalast, the Palace of Tears, for what happened on the pavement outside. A West Berliner could spend an afternoon with an aunt in the East and then walk through those glass doors to go home. The aunt could not follow. The goodbyes happened in the open, in front of strangers, and every stranger knew exactly what they meant.

## A Station Cut in Half

Friedrichstrasse station stood entirely inside East Berlin, and after 13 August 1961 it became one of the strangest places in Europe. Several S-Bahn and U-Bahn lines running beneath the city could be reached only from the West, so West Berliners rode into the East simply to change trains, moving through a sealed transit zone in the middle of a country they were not allowed to enter. Anyone who did want to enter, or far more rarely to leave, passed through passport and customs control on the lower level of the main building. Within a year the traffic had outgrown it. In 1962 the GDR put up the glass pavilion beside the station to take the overflow, and the checkpoint stayed in service for twenty-seven years, closing in the same winter the Wall did.

## Six Queues, One Direction

Inside, travellers were sorted before they were inspected. There were separate checkpoints for West Berliners, West Germans, foreigners, diplomats, transit passengers and East Germans, and the line you joined told you how the state had already classified you. The building handled westbound crossings only; nobody was processed here on the way in. That single fact shaped everything about the place. Every person in the queue was leaving, and almost everyone they had come to see was staying. Documents went up into a control booth, and then you waited. The waiting was the instrument. One of those original booths still stands in the museum and takes about four seconds to walk through, which explains the checkpoint better than any wall text.

## The Pavement Outside

The name did not come from anything in the hall. It came from the concrete apron in front of the doors, where families said goodbye because that was as far as half the family could go. East German residents were not permitted to travel to West Berlin. A visit from the other side was a gift and an ending at once: a few hours in a flat, a coffee, a photograph, then the walk to Friedrichstrasse with everybody pretending the walk was ordinary. Some of those partings turned out to be the last. Berliners watching it happen day after day gave the building the name it still carries, and the name stuck because it was accurate.

## The Nightclub Years

On 9 November 1989 the crossing lost its reason to exist, and what followed was an oddly Berlin sort of afterlife. The hall became a venue, a nightclub and a stage, and stayed one until 2006. Before any of that, on 2 October 1990, the East German government placed the building under monument protection. It was among the last official acts of a state with a single day left to live, since reunification came on 3 October. The GDR's closing gesture toward the Tränenpalast was to make sure nobody could tear it down.

## Still Full of Light

Angela Merkel, who grew up on the eastern side of the border this building enforced, opened the permanent exhibition on 14 September 2011. More than thirty thousand people came in the first fortnight. Admission is free, and the Stiftung Haus der Geschichte has resisted the urge to crowd the hall. There are suitcases and parcels, a passport control booth, and screens carrying the voices of people who fled, who smuggled documents out, who were refused permission to attend a parent's funeral. Lüderitz's glass still does what he built it to do. Light comes in from every side, falls across the fittings of a checkpoint, and shows them for what they always were: office furniture, arranged by a state, to keep people apart.

## Flight Context

Located at 52.5208 N, 13.3869 E on the Reichstagufer, on the north bank of the Spree immediately west of Friedrichstrasse station in Berlin-Mitte. From the air the site reads as the low glass box beside the long shed roof of the station, with the river curving past it; the Reichstag and the government quarter lie about 800 m west, Museum Island about 1 km east. Best viewed from 1,500-2,500 ft AGL on a downstream run along the Spree. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is roughly 20 km southeast; Berlin's controlled airspace requires clearance over the city centre. Winter haze and low stratus are common from November through February.
