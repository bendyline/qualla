# Computerspielemuseum

On a socialist showcase boulevard in Friedrichshain, the world's first permanent museum of video game culture keeps 25,000 games playable — including one that shocks you when you lose.

**Location:** 52.5175, 13.4419

**Categories:** germany, berlin, museums, technology

## Introduction

Karl-Marx-Allee was built to look like the future. East Germany dressed its great boulevard in ceramic tile and wedding-cake towers, laid it out wide enough to march an army down, and called it the first socialist street in Berlin. Somewhere along that grand, slightly overbearing stretch of monumental optimism, behind an unremarkable ground-floor frontage, sits a museum devoted to a different future entirely — the one that arrived as a bouncing white dot on a black screen and never asked anyone's permission.

## First in the World, Twice

When the Computerspielemuseum opened in 1997, nothing else like it existed anywhere: the first permanent exhibition devoted to digital interactive entertainment culture. It ran for three years, closed its doors in 2000, and spent the next decade as an online-only institution while mounting some thirty exhibitions at home and abroad. The best known of them, pong.mythos, backed by the German Federal Cultural Foundation, became the most successful touring exhibition in the history of German computer games and was seen by more than 470,000 people. In 2011 the museum finally got its permanent home back, on Karl-Marx-Allee in Friedrichshain. Twelve thousand visitors came through in the first month alone.

## What a Museum of Play Collects

The numbers read like an inventory of a lost civilisation, which in a sense they are. Around 25,000 data storage devices carrying games. Roughly 12,000 technical magazines, the kind that once told a teenager which joystick to save for. Arcade cabinets, home computers, console systems, videos, posters and handbooks — one of the largest collections of entertainment hardware and software in Europe. Over three hundred exhibits are interactive, which is the point. A Commodore 64 runs Q*bert. A cabinet houses Computer Space, the coin-operated machine that predates the industry it started. You do not read about these things behind glass; you put your hands on them and discover how unforgiving they were.

## The Machine That Hits Back

Among the exhibits is the PainStation, and it is exactly what the name promises. Two players face each other across a Pong-style table. Lose a point and the machine delivers a consequence to the hand resting on its pad — heat, a lash, a mild electric shock. It is a joke about arcade masochism that stopped being entirely a joke the moment somebody built it, and it draws a straight line back to the whole appeal of the form: games have always been about the willingness to be punished for a while in exchange for getting better. Most museums ask visitors not to touch. This one asks whether they dare.

## Games Behind the Wall

East Germany produced exactly one arcade machine. The Poly-Play, built by VEB Polytechnik around 1985, offered eight games — deer hunting, downhill skiing, a burst-water-pipe puzzle, and a Pac-Man variant renamed Hase und Wolf — and about two thousand of them were made. They turned up in trade-union holiday homes, youth hostels and non-profit clubs, and many were run with the coin slot switched off, because charging children to play would have been the wrong sort of economics. The museum has made this history its own: the 2025 exhibition ColdWarGames — It's All a Game, mounted with the Allied Museum, put East German gaming alongside the propaganda and paranoia that surrounded it. A city that spent forty years divided turns out to have a divided arcade history too.

## Keeping the Past Playable

Preserving a video game is harder than preserving a painting. The medium dies with its machine, and the machines are dying — capacitors leak, floppy disks demagnetise, cathode-ray tubes go dark and cannot be replaced. The Computerspielemuseum works on this problem as a member of the International Council of Museums and a partner in the nestor digital-preservation network, and it has contributed to two European research projects with names that betray the size of the task: PLANETS, for long-term access through networked services, and Keeping Emulation Environments Portable. The collection itself belongs to a youth and social work association; the museum is run separately, and its permanent exhibition was paid for out of Berlin's lottery fund and the city's cultural investment programme. The work has been noticed — a German Children's Cultural Prize in 2002, and a special award at the Deutscher Computerspielpreis in 2017.

## Flight Context

The museum sits at 52.5175° N, 13.4419° E on Karl-Marx-Allee in Friedrichshain, roughly 3 km east of Alexanderplatz. From the air the boulevard is unmistakable: an exceptionally wide, dead-straight avenue running east-southeast from Alexanderplatz, flanked by the twin towers of Frankfurter Tor and lined with pale tiled blocks that catch the light differently from everything around them. The Fernsehturm's sphere, 368 m tall, is the primary landmark 3 km west and dominates the approach. Best viewed from 2,000-3,000 ft AGL tracking east along the boulevard; Volkspark Friedrichshain, with its two grassed-over flak tower mounds, lies just to the north. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is about 20 km south-southeast; Strausberg (EDAY) is the nearest general-aviation field, roughly 30 km east. Expect busy controlled airspace over central Berlin and low ceilings through the winter months.
