# Berlin International Film Festival

An American occupation officer proposed a film festival for a half-city that had been under blockade three years earlier — and it has been arguing with itself in public ever since.

**Location:** 52.5200, 13.3800

**Categories:** Berlin, Film, Culture, Cold War, Festivals, Germany

## Introduction

On 6 June 1951 a cinema in the Berlin suburb of Steglitz screened Alfred Hitchcock's Rebecca, a film already eleven years old, and called it the opening of an international film festival. The Titania-Palast got the honour because it was one of the few large halls in West Berlin still standing and still wired. Three years earlier the city around it had been under Soviet blockade, fed by aircraft landing every few minutes. The idea had come from Oscar Martay, a film officer with the American High Commission, who put it to a committee of West Berlin senators and West German film people on 9 October 1950. A festival was something a besieged half-city could do for itself. The American military administration was persuaded to help, and to lend money.

## Bears by Show of Hands

The first festival ran from 6 to 17 June 1951. Its director was a film historian named Alfred Bauer, who would hold the job until 1976. Among the winners was Disney's Cinderella, which took a Golden Bear in the music-film category and the audience award as well.

The audience award was not a sideshow. FIAPF, the international federation that accredited festivals, had decided after 1951 that only Cannes and Venice were entitled to appoint official juries. So from 1952 to 1955 Berlin's Golden and Silver Bears — and a Bronze Berlin Bear alongside them — were handed out by public vote. Whatever the trade press thought of a given film, the people in the seats decided. FIAPF relented in time for the 1956 edition, an international jury took over the main prizes, and the Berlinale has kept the shape ever since: a jury chaired by a recognisable cinema figure, and a great many other prizes awarded by everybody else.

## Screenings on the Other Side

For its first decade the festival did not entirely respect the line running through the city. Before the Wall went up in 1961, a selection of Berlinale films was also screened in East Berlin. After the Wall, the traffic reversed and thinned into something stranger. In 1963 East German television carried a daily Berlinale programme, broadcasting five films from the competition into living rooms on the other side of a border that people were being shot for crossing.

The festival's politics were never only external. In 1964, under pressure from the church, Bauer threw Vilgot Sjöman's 491 out of the competition. In 1968 Werner Herzog's first feature, Lebenszeichen, had its premiere here. Berlin was where European cinema came to have its arguments in front of an audience.

## The Year Nobody Won

The twentieth edition, in 1970, ended without prizes. In competition was o.k., an anti-war film by Michael Verhoeven that restages the rape and murder of a Vietnamese woman by American soldiers, transplanted to a Bavarian forest. The jury, chaired by the American director George Stevens, voted 7–2 to pull it, citing a FIAPF guideline that "all film festivals should contribute to better understanding between nations." Stevens called the film anti-American. Verhoeven answered that most Americans were themselves against the war.

One juror, the Yugoslav director Dušan Makavejev, refused to go along and defended the film publicly. Other directors withdrew their entries. The jury was accused of censorship, then dissolved; the competition was suspended and no awards were given. For a while it was unclear whether there would be a 1971 festival at all. There was — reorganised, and with a new International Forum for New Cinema attached to it, a section explicitly for the kind of work that had just blown the old one apart.

## The Founder's File

From 1987 the Berlinale gave an Alfred Bauer Prize, named for the man who had run it for twenty-five years, to films that opened new perspectives on the art form. In 2020 the festival suspended it, and then dropped it altogether.

Research had established that Bauer's role in the Reich Film Office during the Nazi period was considerably more substantial than the postwar record suggested — and that Bauer himself had worked to obscure it. The founding director of West Berlin's showcase festival, the man hired in part to demonstrate a clean break, had written his own account of the years before 1945 and been believed for seventy years. The prize was replaced by a Silver Bear Jury Prize. The festival did not rename its history, but it stopped putting his name on a trophy.

## Four Hundred Films in February

The Berlinale left June for February in 1978 and moved its centre of gravity to Potsdamer Platz in 2000. Today it screens around 400 films across nine sections, with roughly twenty competing for the Golden and Silver Bears, and it is the largest of the world's major festivals by attendance — about 325,000 tickets in 2020, with nearly 16,000 industry professionals from 130 countries. The European Film Market runs alongside it, the first big trade fair of the film year. Berlinale Talents, running since 2003, brings about 250 emerging filmmakers a year to sit in front of people like Frances McDormand, Walter Murch and Tilda Swinton.

Tricia Tuttle took over as sole director in April 2024. Her second edition, the 76th, ran 12–22 February 2026 with Wim Wenders chairing the jury; the Golden Bear went to İlker Çatak's Gelbe Briefe.

## Flight Context

The festival's coordinates, 52.52°N 13.38°E, put you over the Tiergarten's eastern edge with Potsdamer Platz just south — the Berlinale's home ground since 2000. The Berlinale Palast is the Theater am Potsdamer Platz at Marlene-Dietrich-Platz 1, a 1,639-seat musical house for eleven and a half months of the year. From 2,000–3,000 ft AGL the geography reads cleanly: the Potsdamer Platz tower cluster, the green rectangle of the Tiergarten running west with Strasse des 17. The Zoo Palast is about 3.5 km west-southwest near Bahnhof Zoo; the Titania-Palast, where the whole thing started, still stands about 8 km southwest in Steglitz.
