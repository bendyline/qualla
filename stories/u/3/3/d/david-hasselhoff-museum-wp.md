# David Hasselhoff Museum

In a Berlin hostel basement, a small museum honours an American actor's part in the fall of the Berlin Wall - a part he did not have, and has never worked very hard to deny.

**Location:** 52.5301, 13.4017

**Categories:** Museums, Berlin, Popular culture, Music, Cold War

## Introduction

Go down the stairs at the Circus Hostel in Mitte, past the bar, and there is a museum. It is not large. There is a toy KITT from Knight Rider, bought on eBay. There is Baywatch memorabilia. There is a genuine piece of the Berlin Wall, and beside it a replica of a scarf printed with piano keys, of the kind David Hasselhoff wore on the last night of 1989. And there is a copy of a book called Did David Hasselhoff End the Cold War?: Facts You Need to Know - YES he did, which is a parody of a real book by Emma Hartley with nearly the same title and a rather different answer. The whole room runs on the tension between those two titles, and it is funnier than it has any right to be.

## Small Beginnings, Formally Opened

The museum started in 2008 as a modest arrangement of objects in a hostel basement - the kind of joke somebody on staff makes and then cannot stop making. In 2014 it was rebuilt on a more elaborate scale, and it opened officially on 6 March 2015.

The billing describes its subject as "arguably Germany's most famous non-German", which is a claim you could genuinely argue rather than simply laugh at. Hasselhoff's German career in 1989 was real and enormous, in a way that Americans who know him only as a lifeguard still find baffling. Berlin has built museums to darker things in grander buildings. This one is a basement, and it knows it.

## Eight Weeks at Number One

The song was not originally his. Marc Seaberg recorded "Looking for Freedom" first, and Tony Marshall covered it as well. But the Hasselhoff version entered the West German chart in March 1989 at number 12 and reached the top by its third week, where it stayed for eight consecutive weeks.

Any account of that year that files this away as a novelty hit is missing something. A song about a rich man's son walking away from his inheritance to go looking for freedom was playing constantly, all through the summer of 1989, in a country with a wall down the middle of it. The audience supplied the meaning. Hasselhoff happened to be holding the microphone.

## The Night, Correctly Dated

Here is the part the internet gets wrong and the museum, in its way, gets right. The Berlin Wall opened on 9 November 1989. Hasselhoff performed at the Brandenburg Gate on New Year's Eve - 31 December 1989 - nearly two months afterwards, standing in a bucket crane above the crowd in a leather jacket wired with flashing lights and that piano-key scarf, singing to Berliners who had been walking freely through the gap for weeks. ZDF broadcast it. The footage resurfaced online years later, stripped of its date, and has been misunderstood ever since.

He did not bring down the Wall. Nothing about the evening claimed that he had. What he did was sing the year's biggest German hit at the exact spot where the year's biggest German story had happened, wearing something nobody has been able to forget.

## The Myth and Its Most Enthusiastic Curator

The legend grew afterwards, and its subject has not always tried hard to deflate it. On a German tour in 2004, Hasselhoff publicly lamented that the Checkpoint Charlie museum had no photograph of him on its walls - a remark that landed somewhere between a joke and a grievance, and that did more for the myth than any documentary could have. Emma Hartley borrowed the question for a book title. The hostel basement borrowed it back and answered it in capital letters.

What is actually on display down there is not an actor. It is a piece of folklore that Germany finds funny and has decided to keep. A fragment of the Wall sits in a case a short walk from where the Wall stood, next to a scarf that a television lifeguard wore to a party. Both objects date from 1989. Only one of them changed anything. Berlin carries more monuments to catastrophe per square kilometre than almost any city on earth, and seems glad to have somewhere it is allowed to be silly.

## Flight Context

52.5301 degrees N, 13.4017 degrees E, at Weinbergsweg on Rosenthaler Platz in Berlin-Mitte, roughly 1.5 km north of Unter den Linden. From the air, look for the wedge of green at Weinbergspark just north of the junction where six streets converge on Rosenthaler Platz - the hostel sits at the southern tip of that wedge, on Weinbergsweg, and the museum is under it. Torstrasse runs east-west through the junction and gives you the line. The Fernsehturm stands about 1.2 km south-southeast and fixes the whole neighbourhood at a glance; the former course of the Berlin Wall lies about 1 km northwest, along Bernauer Strasse, where a preserved section still stands. This is controlled airspace over central Berlin with restricted zones around the government quarter, so plan a cleared transit. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) lies roughly 11 nm south-southeast; Strausberg (EDAY) is about 18 nm east. At 2,000 ft you can see the Brandenburg Gate and this street corner in the same field of view, which is more or less the joke.
