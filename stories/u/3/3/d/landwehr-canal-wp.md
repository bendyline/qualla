# Landwehr Canal

Prussia's greatest garden designer cut a ten-kilometre shortcut across Berlin, and the century that followed filled it with everything the city could not keep: a murdered revolutionary, a flooded tunnel, a hijacked pleasure steamer.

**Location:** 52.4978, 13.3925

**Categories:** Berlin, Canals, German history, Engineering, Landscape architecture

## Introduction

The man who designed this ditch spent the rest of his career making Prussia beautiful. Peter Joseph Lenné laid out the park at Sanssouci and the walks of the Großer Tiergarten, and in 1845 — the year he was made Prussian Garden Director-General — he was handed a shipping problem instead. The Spree loops lazily through Berlin and the barge traffic was choking on it. Lenné's answer was a chord across the arc: ten and a half kilometres of new water from the eastern harbour to Charlottenburg, cut between 1845 and 1850 and opened on 2 September 1850. He gave it sloping banks rather than the vertical stone walls of a working harbour, twenty metres wide at the surface, with a lock at either end to hold the level. He would cut a second one, the Luisenstadt Canal, two years later. This one still looks like something drawn by a man who thought about trees.

## Ten Kilometres, Thirty-Six Bridges

The canal leaves the Spree at the Osthafen in Friedrichshain, drops immediately through the upper lock and runs dead straight southwest to meet the Neukölln Ship Canal, which links on to the Teltow. There it swings northwest into Kreuzberg and becomes, for a stretch along the Paul-Lincke-Ufer, one of the pleasantest addresses in the city. Further west the U1 comes down beside it on iron viaducts and shadows the water for about a kilometre, past the elevated platforms at Möckernbrücke and Hallesches Tor, before crossing on a high bridge that also vaults the old railway approach to the vanished Anhalter Bahnhof. In Tiergarten it threads between the Großer Tiergarten and the zoo, passes the lower lock and goes under the Stadtbahn viaduct, then rejoins the Spree in Charlottenburg at the waterways crossroads called Spreekreuz. Thirty-six bridges cross it. Two enlargements, in 1883–1890 and again in 1936–1941, widened it to twenty-two metres and deepened it to two — which is to say it was made for cargo and now carries almost nothing but tour boats and houseboats.

## January 1919

On 15 January 1919, with the Spartacist rising broken, Freikorps soldiers seized Rosa Luxemburg and Karl Liebknecht in a Wilmersdorf apartment and took them to the Eden Hotel, then the headquarters of the Guards Cavalry Rifle Division. Luxemburg was forty-seven, a Polish-born Marxist theorist who had spent much of the war in prison for opposing it and who had argued, against her own comrades, that a revolution without democratic freedom was not worth having. A private named Otto Runge clubbed Liebknecht with a rifle butt as they were put into the cars. Liebknecht was driven into the Tiergarten and shot in the back at close range. Luxemburg was shot in the car, about forty metres from the hotel door, and a lieutenant named Kurt Vogel threw her body over the parapet into the Landwehr Canal. It stayed in the water until the end of May. A memorial stands at the spot on the canal bank, on the edge of the Großer Tiergarten, marking the place where she went into the water rather than the place where she was killed.

## The Water That Came In

In the last days of the battle for Berlin in 1945, German engineers blew the retaining walls where a railway tunnel ran beneath the canal, and the canal went into the tunnels. Soviet troops had been using the underground for their advance; the intention was to stop them. What the water actually reached were the people sheltering below — civilians, and wounded soldiers evacuated into hospital trains parked in the dark. For eighty years the story has been told with a figure of thousands attached to it, and the figure has never been supported. Between eighty and a hundred and thirty bodies were recovered from the flooded tunnels, and researchers who have gone through the accounts conclude that most had already been killed in the fighting before the water arrived. Even the date is disputed — 27 April in some accounts, the first or second of May in others. The event is grim enough without the arithmetic.

## Fourteen Aboard the Friedrich Wolff

On 8 June 1962 a party of fourteen East Germans took over the river steamer Friedrich Wolff, bolted steel plates around the wheelhouse to stop bullets reaching the helmsman, and drove her out of the Spree into the Landwehr Canal. An East German patrol boat caught up and opened fire. West Berlin police on the bank fired back over the water, and all fourteen got ashore in the West. It is one of the very few Berlin escape stories that ends with everybody alive, and it happened on a waterway built for coal barges by a landscape gardener.

## The Trees

The most recent fight over the canal was about trees. In May 2007 the embankment wall subsided at a mooring, undermined by decades of propeller wash, and the federal waterways authority proposed works that would have taken out a great many of the trees lining the banks. Berlin refused to have it. A citizens' initiative collected more than 25,000 signatures, the argument went to formal mediation, and the process ran until December 2013 — producing, along with a compromise, a claimed €113 million in avoided costs. The canal Lenné drew for barges is now defended by its neighbours as scenery. Shell-Haus, the wave-fronted 1932 office block on the Tiergarten bank, still looks down at it, and the water goes on doing almost nothing, which is what it is now for.

## Flight Context

The Landwehr Canal runs about 10.7 km across inner Berlin, with the story's reference point around 52.4978°N, 13.3925°E near the Tiergarten reach. It is one of the most legible features in the city from the air: a straight, dark, tree-lined thread running roughly east-southeast to west-northwest, always about a kilometre south of the Spree and never converging with it until the ends. Follow it and you get a cross-section of Berlin — the Osthafen basins at the eastern end, the elevated U1 viaduct running beside it through Kreuzberg, the green mass of the Großer Tiergarten and the zoo enclosures on the northern side of its western reach, and the Spreekreuz junction in Charlottenburg where it rejoins the river. Thirty-six bridges make a regular ladder of cross-marks along its length. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is about 22 km southeast; Schönhagen (EDAZ) about 40 km southwest and Strausberg (EDAY) about 35 km east-northeast. Best traced from 1,500 to 3,000 feet; the tree canopy hides the water in high summer, so late autumn through early spring gives the cleanest line.
