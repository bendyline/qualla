# Rosenstrasse Protest

In February and March 1943, hundreds of women stood outside a building in central Berlin for a week demanding the return of their Jewish husbands, and the men walked out alive.

**Location:** 52.5218, 13.4044

**Categories:** berlin, germany, world-war-ii, holocaust, resistance, jewish-history, memorials, mitte

## Introduction

"Give us our husbands back." Hundreds of women stood on a narrow street in central Berlin at the end of February 1943 and shouted it at armed SS men, then went home in the dark and came back the next morning and shouted it again. Nobody had organised them. They had gone looking for men seized from factory floors at dawn, found them held in a Jewish community welfare office at Rosenstrasse 2-4, and refused to leave. "We acted from the heart," Elsa Holzer said long afterwards. "If you had to calculate whether you would do any good by protesting, you wouldn't have gone." It remains the only mass public demonstration by Germans against the deportation of Jews in the twelve years of the Third Reich.

## The Factory Action

On 27 February 1943, weeks after the surrender at Stalingrad and nine days after Goebbels demanded 'total war' from a roaring crowd at the Sportpalast, the Gestapo swept up the last Jews of Berlin at their workplaces. Around eleven thousand people were arrested in a day. Some eighteen hundred men were separated out and taken to Rosenstrasse: almost all married to non-Jewish women, along with others the regime classified as Geltungsjuden.

Those marriages were why the men were still alive. The regime had left Jews with German spouses alone, not out of scruple but out of calculation, wary of what in-laws and neighbours might say about a man they had known for twenty years. Most of these couples were a non-Jewish wife and a Jewish husband, and the wives had already spent a decade refusing to divorce under steady pressure. When the arrests came, the state was dealing with women who had been saying no for years.

## Seven Days on a Narrow Street

They came in shifts, in the cold, for a week. Goebbels ordered a news blackout, but no state can arrest eleven thousand people in its own capital in one day without the city noticing, and passers-by joined the crowd on the pavement. On the night of 1 March the RAF bombed Berlin, choosing a public holiday held in honour of the Luftwaffe for an especially heavy raid, and the street emptied. Ursula Braun, engaged to one of the men inside, remembered the split in what she felt: fury at the Nazis, who deserved it, and horror at the screaming people and the fires. Charlotte Israel, a Jewish woman whose husband was locked up, said she had always been terrified of air raids, but that night she was so enraged she could have laughed in scorn, until she remembered he could not leave the building. In the morning the women were back.

## The Argument Inside the Regime

The Reich Security Main Office wanted the women shot. Goebbels overruled it, and his reasoning had nothing to do with mercy. His deputy Leopold Gutterer recalled the calculation: force would spread the unrest from one neighbourhood to the next, arresting the protesters would only produce more relatives with a grievance, and the regime could not machine-gun thousands of unarmed women in the middle of Berlin and keep it quiet, not with foreign correspondents in town. Worse, a public argument about where deported people went threatened to expose the killing itself. On 6 March, as Gauleiter of Berlin, Goebbels ordered everyone in the building released, noting in his diary that the deportations would resume later and be carried out 'that much more thoroughly'. Then he had the German press report that the women had been protesting the British bombing, and that charitable giving was up seventy per cent, proof the national community had never been stronger.

## What the Women Did Not Know

The men went home. On 18 April 1943 they were ordered to stop wearing the yellow star, and Goebbels never tried to send them to Auschwitz again. The effects ran further than anyone on that pavement could have guessed. On 21 May, asked by the security police in Paris about French Jews married to Gentiles, Eichmann's deputy Rolf Günther ruled that they could not be deported until the German cases were settled; the same day Ernst Kaltenbrunner ordered German Jews in mixed marriages released from the camps, his memo noting that a repeat of Rosenstrasse was not desirable. The men survived the war.

Historians still argue about causation. Wolf Gruner contends the Gestapo never meant to deport intermarried Jews and had arrested them for registration, so the protest cannot be credited with a rescue. Nathan Stoltzfus answers that a dictatorship shrewd enough to retreat where retreat is cheap is more dangerous, not less. The dispute is about what the regime meant to do. It is not about what the women did.

## Block of Women

The building at Rosenstrasse 2-4 did not survive the war, and the street today gives nothing away. In a small park near the site stands Ingeborg Hunzinger's memorial, unveiled in 1995: three rough blocks of reddish sandstone drawn into a half circle around a couple holding one another. The inscription on the back reads that the strength of civil disobedience and the vigour of love overcome the violence of dictatorship, and that women stood here, defeating death. Margarethe von Trotta filmed the week in 2003; Petra Pau marked the seventy-fifth anniversary in 2018 with a speech in the Bundestag. Elsa Holzer put the thing more plainly than any monument manages. There was always a flood of people there, she said. It wasn't organised. Everyone was simply there, exactly like me, and that is what is so wonderful about it.

## Flight Context

Located at 52.5218 N, 13.4044 E on Rosenstrasse in Berlin-Mitte, just north of the Spree between the Berlin Cathedral and Alexanderplatz. From the air the reference points are unmistakable even though the memorial itself is not: the copper dome of the cathedral and the Museum Island complex sit 400 m southwest, the Fernsehturm rises 500 m east, and the small green square holding the sculpture lies between them beside the Karl-Liebknecht-Strasse. Best viewed at 1,000-2,000 ft AGL following the Spree eastbound. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is about 20 km southeast. The television tower is a significant obstruction at 368 m; central Berlin airspace requires clearance.
