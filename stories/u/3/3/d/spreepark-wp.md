# Spreepark

East Germany's only permanent amusement park went bankrupt, shipped six of its rides to Peru, and ended in a cocaine conviction — then spent twenty years rusting in a Berlin forest before the city started bringing it back.

**Location:** 52.4858, 13.4878

**Categories:** Berlin, Modern ruins, Amusement parks, East Germany, Urban renewal

## Introduction

In January 2002, the man running what had been East Berlin's only amusement park loaded six of its rides into twenty shipping containers and sailed them to Peru, telling the authorities they were going out for repairs. Two years later Norbert Witte was sentenced to seven years in a German prison for trying to smuggle 180 kilograms of cocaine back the other way, packed into the masts of a ride called the Fliegender Teppich — the Flying Carpet. Behind him in the Plänterwald he left 29.5 hectares of roller coaster, swan boats and plaster dinosaurs, €11 million of debt, and a Ferris wheel that stood over the treetops for the next two decades with nobody in it.

## The Only One in the Republic

It opened in 1969 as Kulturpark Plänterwald, on 29.5 hectares of ground where the forest meets the river Spree in the southeast of the city. For twenty years it held a strange distinction: it was the only permanent amusement park in East Germany, and the only one in either half of Berlin. West Berliners had no equivalent; East Germans had nowhere else to go. Everybody paid per ride, coin by coin, which meant a family could spend an afternoon there choosing carefully. Generations of children in the German Democratic Republic knew exactly one Ferris wheel, and this was it.

## The Western Turn

After reunification the state enterprise that ran the park was denationalised, in 1991, by Berlin's municipal authorities. Seven companies applied for the concession. It went to Spreepark Berlin GmbH — and, crucially, nobody properly checked the references of the man behind it, Norbert Witte. For a while it worked. New attractions went in, visitor numbers climbed to 1.5 million a year, and the park was rebuilt in a Western idiom: roller coasters, two water rides, a stage, a Western town, an English village. The asphalt apron around the Ferris wheel was torn up and replaced with a water landscape. The pay-per-ride system went too, replaced by a single admission of 29 Deutsche Mark for adults and 27 for children.

## The Numbers Stopped Working

Debts began accumulating from 1999. Management raised the entrance fee to 30 marks, which did not help, and the park had never solved a more basic problem: there was almost nowhere to park a car. Attendance slid. By 2001 only 400,000 visitors came through the gates, roughly a quarter of the peak, and Spreepark GmbH declared itself insolvent. The formal insolvency followed in August 2002, leaving €11 million in liabilities and a fully equipped amusement park in the middle of a public forest with no operator and no plan.

## Lima

On 18 January 2002, Witte left for Lima with his family and closest coworkers. The authorities let him take six attractions with him — the Fliegender Teppich, Butterfly, Spider, Baby-Flug, Wild River and Jet Star — loaded into twenty containers, ostensibly for repair. The Peruvian venture, a Lunapark, failed. What came next was the reason the story is still told: on 19 May 2004 a German court sentenced Witte to seven years for attempting to bring 180 kilograms of cocaine, valued at £14 million, from Peru to Germany inside the masts of the Flying Carpet. In October 2006 a Peruvian court sentenced his son Marcel Witte to twenty years for drug smuggling. A family business that had begun with a fairground ended with a father and a son in prisons on two continents.

## Rust, Fire, and a Slow Return

The park never reopened. Left to weather, it became one of Europe's great urban-exploration pilgrimages — dinosaurs tipping into the undergrowth, coaster track disappearing under birch saplings — and a location scouts loved, appearing in the 2011 film Hanna and in a music video for the German band Sizarr. Guided tours ran from 2011 until March 2014, when the City of Berlin bought the site back. That August, on the evening of the 10th, fire destroyed major parts of the park; firefighters found two separate blazes 200 metres apart that merged, which suggested they had been set. The Ferris wheel came down in 2021, its parts kept for possible re-use. Grün Berlin now holds the site, working to a plan presented in 2018 that layers culture and ecology over the ruins. The Eierhäuschen, a restored riverside inn, reopened with a beer garden in 2023 and became the permanent home of the Spreepark Art Space in March 2024, with free exhibitions and residencies for visiting artists. The full park is due to open again in spring 2027.

## Flight Context

Spreepark sits at 52.4858°N, 13.4878°E, in the Plänterwald forest on the south bank of the Spree in Treptow-Köpenick, about 6 km southeast of Mitte. From the air it reads as a distinct clearing punched into an otherwise solid block of woodland, immediately upstream of Treptower Park and its Soviet War Memorial, with the river curving past the northern edge. The Ferris wheel that made the site instantly recognisable was dismantled in 2021, so look instead for the pale scar of the old midway and the roofline of the Eierhäuschen at the water's edge. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) lies about 13 km almost due south. Best seen from 1,500 to 3,000 feet, and clearest in winter when the surrounding canopy is bare.
