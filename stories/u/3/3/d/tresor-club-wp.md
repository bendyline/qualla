# Tresor

A bank vault under a derelict East Berlin department store became the club where Detroit techno and a newly reunified city found each other.

**Location:** 52.5100, 13.3828

**Categories:** music, nightlife, berlin, modern-history, architecture, germany

## Introduction

In January 1991, two men driving down Leipziger Strasse stopped at a derelict department store, tracked down the caretaker, and talked their way in. Beneath the abandoned Wertheim building they found a bank vault — a low concrete room lined wall to wall with steel safe deposit boxes, doors hanging open, everything under decades of dust. The lock on the vault door was two concentric circles above a horizontal bar. Three months later that shape was a logo, the dust was still there, and the room had a sound system in it. Tresor — German for safe, or vault — opened in March 1991, five months after the country whose capital sat above it had ceased to exist.

## The Sound Came From Detroit

Tresor did not begin in the vault. It began in 1988, when a Berlin electronic label called Interfisch opened a club named Ufo, the original centre of house and techno in the city. Ufo ran out of money and closed in 1990. Interfisch's head, Dimitri Hegemann, went looking for another room, and the one he found happened to be under Leipziger Strasse.

What made Tresor more than a good room was a transatlantic wire. Hegemann brought Detroit producers to Berlin and put them on record. Tresor Records was founded in October 1991, seven months after the club opened, and its catalogue reads like a roll call of the music's founders: Juan Atkins, Jeff Mills, Blake Baxter, Eddie 'Flashin' Fowlkes, Robert Hood, Drexciya. Release number six was 3 Phase featuring Dr. Motte — 'Der Klang der Familie,' the sound of the family. That was roughly the arrangement. Detroit had invented the music. Berlin, suddenly full of empty buildings and short on rules, gave it somewhere to live.

## Two Floors, Two Tempers

The club grew by accretion, expanding and rebuilding itself several times over — an outdoor garden, then a second floor called Globus. The division of labour was clear enough. Downstairs in the vault, hemmed in by the deposit boxes, the programming was hard techno, industrial and acid: fast, metallic, unrelenting. Globus upstairs ran to house, warmer and looser. You picked your temperature by choosing a staircase.

The vault itself did most of the work. Concrete and steel are unforgiving surfaces, and a low ceiling pushes sound straight back down onto the floor. Nobody had written a design brief; no acoustician had been consulted. A bank had built a room to be impenetrable, and techno moved in.

## The Last Weekend

By the early 2000s Tresor was surviving on rolling short-term leases while Potsdamer Platz rebuilt itself into a glass district on the club's doorstep. The land underneath was worth a great deal, and the city sold it to an investor group planning offices. A documentary crew got there first: 'Tresor Berlin: The Vault & the Electronic Frontier,' directed by Mike Andrawis and released in 2004, traced the story from Hegemann's early Kreuzberg venues to the final months.

Tresor closed on 16 April 2005. It opened every single night of that April. The last event began on the Saturday, with queues stretching down Leipziger Strasse, and it was still going on Monday morning.

## Second Life in a Power Plant

Two years later the club came back, and it chose its new building with the same instincts. On 24 May 2007 Tresor reopened in the decommissioned southern tract of the Heizkraftwerk Berlin-Mitte, a combined heat and power plant on Köpenicker Strasse about three kilometres east of the old site. Reaching the main floor means walking a long, dim tunnel first — a deliberate echo of the descent into the old vault. Above it, the vast turbine hall now operates as Kraftwerk Berlin, hosting orchestras and installations that would have baffled anyone queuing in 1991.

The institution keeps expanding sideways. A second club, Tresor.West, opened in Dortmund in November 2019. In September 2022 the label published 'Tresor: True Stories,' a photobook covering thirty years. The original steel vault door survived the demolition too, and in 2019 it went on loan to the Humboldt Forum — a piece of banking hardware that ended up documenting a decade of German history it was never built for.
