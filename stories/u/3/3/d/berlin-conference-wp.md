# Berlin Conference

From November 1884 to February 1885, fourteen governments met at a building on Wilhelmstrasse to set the rules for the partition of Africa. No African was invited, and the one African ruler who asked to attend was refused.

**Location:** 52.5125, 13.3825

**Categories:** history, colonialism, Berlin, Africa, diplomacy, imperialism

## Introduction

No African was in the room. That single fact is the most important thing to know about the Berlin Conference of 1884–1885, and it is the fact most easily lost when the meeting is described in the language of nineteenth-century diplomacy. Otto von Bismarck convened the gathering at the request of Leopold II of Belgium. Fourteen governments — the European powers, the Ottoman Empire, and the United States — sent plenipotentiaries to a building on Wilhelmstrasse, number 77, today number 92, in what is now central Berlin's government quarter. Sultan Barghash bin Said of Zanzibar asked to be represented and was refused. The delegates met for fifteen weeks. They drew lines across a continent. The people who lived on the other side of those lines were spoken of, never to.

## What the General Act Decided

The signed text of 26 February 1885 is, on its face, a document about trade. Free navigation on the Congo and Niger rivers. Free trade across the Congo Basin. A protocol for notifying other signatories when one power claimed new African coast. A 'principle of effective occupation' that supposedly required a colonial power to actually administer territory before claiming it — a principle promptly ignored as soon as the conference closed. Buried in the diplomatic prose was the recognition of King Leopold's personal claim to a vast tract of central Africa as the property of his International Congo Society. Within months it would be renamed the Congo Free State. It belonged not to Belgium, but to one man.

## The Cost in Human Lives

What the conference enabled cannot be measured in treaties. In the Congo Free State that emerged from Leopold's claim, the most-cited estimate holds that the population fell by around ten million over the following two decades — deaths from forced rubber collection, mutilation, starvation, and disease. In the German colony of South West Africa, the Imperial Army carried out the destruction of the Herero and Nama peoples between 1904 and 1908, an act now widely described as the twentieth century's first genocide. By 1902, ninety percent of the African continent was under European control. The borders that followed paid no attention to language, kinship, watershed, or migration route. Maasai grazing lands ended up split between two colonial administrations. Hutu and Tutsi were thrown into a single Belgian colony whose imposed hierarchies would detonate, in 1994, in Rwanda.

## The Civilizing Mission as Cover Story

The diplomats said this was about ending slavery. David Livingstone, the Scottish missionary who had died in what is now Zambia in 1873, had called for a global crusade against the Arab slave trade in East Africa. His phrase was 'Christianity, commerce, and civilisation.' European powers borrowed his moral language and built an empire on it. Some of the slavery was real and was ended. Most of what replaced it was forced labor under European flags — rubber quotas in the Congo, hut taxes in Kenya, contract labor on South African mines. The new bondage was not called slavery. It killed more people.

## The Diplomats and Their Maps

Bismarck chaired. Henry Morton Stanley, the Welsh-American journalist who had become Leopold's agent in the Congo, attended as a technical adviser; the treaties he had extracted from Congo chiefs supplied the legal foundation for Leopold's claim. The American delegation — John A. Kasson and Henry Sanford — pushed for free trade and for recognition of Leopold's society. Seven of the fourteen governments present — Austria-Hungary, Russia, Denmark, the Netherlands, Sweden-Norway, the Ottoman Empire, and the United States — went home with no African territory at all. The maps on the table showed a continent largely blank, the blankness labelled 'unexplored,' which meant unmapped by Europeans. It was home to perhaps a hundred million people speaking more than a thousand languages.

## What Berlin Means in Africa

More than a century later, the conference is taught across Africa as a founding wound. The borders set in motion that winter still shape the political geography of fifty-four countries: the Sudan–South Sudan division, the partition of Somali pastoralists across five states, the mineral economy the Belgian Congo bequeathed to its successor. Historians argue, reasonably, that Berlin did not by itself carve up the continent — most of the actual boundaries came from bilateral deals struck before and after. But it was in Berlin that the powers agreed on the rules by which a continent could be claimed, and agreed that the people living there had no standing to object. The building at Wilhelmstrasse 77 did not survive the war. The street it stood on, the spine of imperial and then Nazi government, was bombed flat and rebuilt, and the address is now number 92. Fifteen weeks in a room that no longer exists still set the shape of a continent.

## Flight Context

The conference site at Wilhelmstrasse 92 (formerly No. 77) is at 52.512°N, 13.382°E in central Berlin's government district, about 600 m south of the Brandenburg Gate. The original building was destroyed in WWII. Berlin Brandenburg Airport (EDDB) is 20 km southeast. From cruising altitude the area appears as a dense block of government buildings between the Tiergarten green space to the west and the Spree to the east — Holocaust Memorial just to the north.
