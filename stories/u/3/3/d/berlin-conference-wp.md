# Berlin Conference

From November 1884 to February 1885, fourteen European powers met at a building on Wilhelmstrasse to draw lines across a continent whose people were never asked.

**Location:** 52.5125, 13.3825

**Categories:** history, colonialism, Berlin, Africa, diplomacy, imperialism

## Introduction

No African was in the room. That single fact is the most important thing to know about the Berlin Conference of 1884–1885, and it is the fact most easily lost when the meeting is described in the language of nineteenth-century diplomacy. Otto von Bismarck convened the gathering at the request of Leopold II of Belgium. Fourteen European powers and the United States sent plenipotentiaries to a townhouse on Wilhelmstrasse — number 77, today number 92, just steps from where the Reichstag now stands. They met for fifteen weeks. They drew lines across a continent. The people who lived on the other side of those lines were spoken of, never to.

## What the General Act Decided

The signed text of 26 February 1885 is, on its face, a document about trade. Free navigation on the Congo and Niger rivers. Free trade across the Congo Basin. A protocol for notifying other signatories when one power claimed new African coast. A 'principle of effective occupation' that supposedly required a colonial power to actually administer territory before claiming it — a principle promptly ignored as soon as the conference closed. Buried in the diplomatic prose was the recognition of King Leopold's personal claim to a vast tract of central Africa as the property of his International Congo Society. Within months it would be renamed the Congo Free State. It belonged not to Belgium, but to one man.

## The Cost in Human Lives

What the conference enabled cannot be measured in treaties. In the Congo Free State that emerged from Leopold's claim, an estimated ten million Africans died over the next two decades from forced rubber collection, mutilation, starvation, and disease. In the German colony of South West Africa, the Imperial Army would carry out the genocide of the Herero and Nama peoples between 1904 and 1908 — the twentieth century's first. By 1902, ninety percent of African land was under European control. The borders drawn in Berlin paid no attention to language, kinship, watershed, or migration. They cut the Maasai in two. They threw Hutu and Tutsi into a single Belgian colony whose internal hierarchies would detonate, ninety years later, in Rwanda.

## The Civilizing Mission as Cover Story

The diplomats said this was about ending slavery. David Livingstone, the Scottish missionary who had died in what is now Zambia in 1873, had called for a global crusade against the Arab slave trade in East Africa. His phrase was 'Christianity, commerce, and civilisation.' European powers borrowed his moral language and built an empire on it. Some of the slavery was real and was ended. Most of what replaced it was forced labor under European flags — rubber quotas in the Congo, hut taxes in Kenya, contract labor on South African mines. The new bondage was not called slavery. It killed more people.

## The Diplomats and Their Maps

Bismarck chaired. Henry Morton Stanley, the Welsh-American journalist turned colonial agent, attended as Leopold's technical adviser, his treaties with Congo chiefs serving as the legal foundation for Belgian claims. The American delegation — John A. Kasson and Henry Sanford — pushed for free trade and recognition of Leopold's society. Seven of the fourteen countries present, including Russia, Sweden-Norway, the Ottoman Empire, and the United States, would gain no territory at all. The maps they drew on showed a continent largely blank: 'unexplored,' meaning unmapped by Europeans. The blank spaces were home to perhaps 100 million people speaking more than 1,000 languages.

## What Berlin Means in Africa

More than a century later, the conference is taught in African schools as a founding wound. The arbitrary borders set in motion that February still shape the political geography of fifty-four countries. The Mali border, the Sudan-South Sudan division, the partition of Somali pastoralists across five states, the legacy of the Belgian Congo's mineral economy — every one of these traces back to a room in Berlin where no African voice was raised. The building at Wilhelmstrasse 77 is gone now, destroyed in the same Allied bombing that flattened Hitler's Reich Chancellery a few hundred meters away. There is no plaque. The site is mostly used for parking.

## Flight Context

The conference site at Wilhelmstrasse 92 (formerly No. 77) is at 52.512°N, 13.382°E in central Berlin's government district, about 600 m south of the Brandenburg Gate. The original building was destroyed in WWII. Berlin Brandenburg Airport (EDDB) is 20 km southeast. From cruising altitude the area appears as a dense block of government buildings between the Tiergarten green space to the west and the Spree to the east — Holocaust Memorial just to the north.
