# Alexis von Roenne

Hitler's most trusted analyst of Allied intentions believed every deception the British sent him — and historians still cannot agree whether he was the most useful fool of the war or its quietest saboteur.

**Location:** 52.5079, 13.3622

**Categories:** berlin, germany, world-war-ii, intelligence, german-resistance, military-history, tiergarten

## Introduction

Sometime in the spring of 1944, the British army stationed in Britain doubled in size. Not on any parade ground: in the German files. Forty-four divisions became eighty-nine, complete with unit designations, commanders and locations, and the officer who signed off on that arithmetic was the man Hitler trusted more than any other on the question of what the Allies intended to do. Around half of that order of battle did not exist. It had been invented in London and fed to Berlin, and Colonel Alexis Freiherr von Rönne passed it up the chain without visible hesitation. Eighty years on, the argument about him has never been settled: was he the most spectacularly deceived intelligence officer of the war, or was he running the deception from the receiving end?

## A Baltic Baron at a Desk

He was born on 22 February 1903 at Tukums, in the Courland Governorate of the Russian Empire — a town in what is now Latvia, in the Baltic German world that the twentieth century was about to dismantle. He married Ursula von Bülow, of the well-known Prussian family. He was, by every account including the hostile ones, extremely good at his job.

By 1943 he headed Fremde Heere West, the German army's intelligence branch for the western front, whose task was to work out what the Allies would do and where. His reputation had been built early in the war on getting exactly that right. Modern historians have taken to calling him "Hitler's favourite intelligence analyst," and the phrase is not ironic: it describes an officer whose assessments went to the top and were believed there. He also held anti-Nazi convictions, which he kept to himself.

## The Man Who Never Was

In 1943 a body washed ashore in Spain carrying documents that indicated the coming Allied blow would fall on Greece and Sardinia. The body was a British plant, the documents were forgeries, and the real target was Sicily. The operation was called Mincemeat, and it worked because someone in Berlin senior enough to matter certified the papers as genuine.

Von Rönne certified them. He persuaded Hitler to accept the intelligence, and German strength was shifted accordingly — away from the beaches where the landings actually came. The same pattern would repeat on a far larger scale the following year with Operation Bodyguard, the deception that hid the target of the Normandy landings. Whatever the reason for it, the effect is not in dispute: the divisions that were not on those beaches were not there partly because of the assessments that came off his desk.

## Eighty-Nine Divisions

The Bodyguard deception asked the Germans to believe several extravagant things at once, and von Rönne obliged. He was convinced that a fictitious British Fourth Army existed in Scotland and was about to be redeployed to Kent. He believed in the phantom invasion aimed at the Pas-de-Calais. A retrospective in The Independent put it flatly: the head of Fremde Heere West "was wrong on almost every important count other than to discount an invasion of Norway."

Ben Macintyre's summary is sharper still. Von Rönne, he wrote, "faithfully passed on every deception ruse fed to him, accepted the existence of every bogus unit regardless of evidence, and inflated forty-four divisions in Britain to an astonishing eighty-nine." Read one way, that sentence is an obituary for a career. Read another, it is a job description.

## Duped, or Deciding?

This is where the record stops being able to help. "It is quite possible," Macintyre has written, "that Lieutenant Colonel Alexis Baron von Roenne did not believe the Mincemeat deception for an instant." The case for deliberate sabotage rests on inference: an officer of demonstrated skill who suddenly stopped applying it, who loathed the regime he served, and whose errors ran in one consistent direction. The case against rests on the fact that intelligence services are fooled all the time, and that a man who genuinely wanted to change the war's outcome had other options.

Von Rönne never explained. He left no confession, no memorandum, nothing that would settle it — which may itself be the strongest argument either way. He was not tried for deceiving Hitler. Nobody in the Reich ever accused him of it.

## Plötzensee

What killed him was association. Arrested immediately after the 20 July 1944 attempt on Hitler's life because he had known several of the conspirators, he was released within days, then rearrested on 9 August. He was not part of the plot.

He came before Roland Freisler's People's Court on 5 October 1944 and used the proceedings to say something he had spent years not saying: that Nazi racial policy was irreconcilable with his Christian faith. The verdict of that court was never in doubt. On 12 October 1944 he was hanged at Plötzensee prison, on one of the meat hooks fixed to the beam in the execution shed there, a method chosen for the humiliation as much as the killing. He was forty-one. In his last letter to Ursula he wrote that in a moment he would be going home to our Lord in complete calm and in the certainty of salvation — the words of a man who had made his peace with something, though not necessarily with anything he had ever put in writing.

## Flight Context

The coordinates fixed to this story, 52.5079 N, 13.3622 E, mark the Bendlerblock on Stauffenbergstraße in Berlin-Tiergarten, the army headquarters complex at the heart of the 20 July plot and the arrests that swept von Rönne up. From the air the Tiergarten is the anchor: a dark green rectangle roughly 3 km long in the middle of the city, with the Siegessäule on its central roundabout and the Brandenburg Gate at its eastern tip. The Bendlerblock is a large pale U-shaped complex on the north bank of the Landwehr Canal about 700 m south of the park. The second point in this story lies about 4 km northwest at 52.5386 N, 13.3256 E — Plötzensee, hard by the Berlin-Spandau Ship Canal and the Westhafen basin, where the memorial occupies the surviving execution shed. Trace the canal system northwest from the Tiergarten and it takes you there. Central Berlin is dense controlled airspace; plan any low pass at 1,500-2,500 ft AGL and expect clearance limits. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is about 18 km southeast, Schönhagen (EDAZ) about 36 km south-southwest. Winter haze over the Spree basin flattens the Tiergarten to grey, so the canals and the Siegessäule are the more reliable references.
