# Märchenbrunnen

A city architect built Berlin a fountain of fairy tales because he watched children playing on the site — then spent twelve years arguing about it with the Kaiser.

**Location:** 52.5281, 13.4269

**Categories:** Berlin, Fountains, Sculpture, Fairy tales, Parks, Germany

## Introduction

In 1950 someone looked behind a high wall in a Friedrichshain vegetable garden and found Cinderella. Sleeping Beauty was there too, and Puss in Boots, and the seven dwarfs, and a good many of the hundred and six carved stone figures that had stood in the Volkspark Friedrichshain until the fighting of 1945 scattered them. Nobody has explained how they got there. Many were badly broken. They had been missing for five years, and their reappearance began a restoration campaign that has now run, on and off, for three quarters of a century.

## A Councillor Watches Children Play

Berlin had wanted something impressive at the park's entrance since 1893. When Ludwig Hoffmann took office as the city's building councillor in 1896 he inherited the municipal art commission's proposal — a decorative piece of architecture, dignified and forgettable — and threw it out.

What changed his mind, he wrote later in his memoirs, was a walk across the site. There were children everywhere. So instead of a monument he proposed a fountain of fairy tales, which is an unusual thing for a senior municipal official to talk his city into, and an unusual thing for that city to fund. The first version went public in 1901 at the Great Berlin Art Exhibition. Hoffmann was an architect who took sculpture seriously, and thought northern Germany — Berlin above all — badly neglected it. For this he went south, to three sculptors from Bavaria and Franconia.

## Twelve Years of the Kaiser's Corrections

Wilhelm II liked the plan. He also wanted changes, and that was the problem. Through his chief of police the Kaiser could approve or reject anything built on Berlin's streets and squares, while the city's own bodies answered for what went up on park ground. The fountain sat in the seam between the two authorities, and the argument over it ran for years.

Hoffmann chose not to confront the Kaiser directly. He revised, and revised again, and was attacked in the liberal press and by parts of the city council for giving way. Contracts for the concrete work could not be let until 1907. The finished cost, including gardening and roadworks, came to around 960,000 marks. On 15 June 1913 the grounds were handed over to the public — a date chosen because it was the twenty-fifth anniversary of Wilhelm II's accession to the throne. Twelve years of argument, resolved into a compliment.

## One Hundred and Six Stones

The ensemble runs 90 metres wide and 172 deep; the main basin alone measures 34 by 54. Water falls through four shallow cascades fed by one large and nine small jets, with seven stone frogs spouting into the pool, one of them cast as the Frog Prince. A semicircular arcade of nine arches closes the eastern end, a stone bowl ringed with dogs' heads at its centre, a gallery of animals above. Behind it sits the Dolphin Fountain, eight metres across, its rim crowded with children and beasts.

Ignatius Taschner carved the ten central figures, interpreting nine Grimm tales: Hansel and Gretel, Puss in Boots, Hans in Luck, The Seven Ravens, Cinderella, Little Red Riding Hood, Brother and Sister, Snow White, Sleeping Beauty. His Hansel and Gretel follows the less familiar 1819 version in which a duck ferries the rescued children home across the water. Georg Wrba supplied four hermae — an ogre, a giant's daughter, Rübezahl, Old Mother Frost — set along hedged side paths, plus six groups of children around the Dolphin Fountain. Josef Rauch cut fourteen marble game animals for the arcade.

## The Dwarf With Menzel's Face

One of Snow White's dwarfs is said to be a portrait. The face bears a clear resemblance to Adolph Menzel, the painter of Prussian history and of Berlin's iron rolling mills, and the story goes that carving him into the fountain was a quiet rebuke to Wilhelm II, who had declined to give Menzel a monument after his death.

It is a good story with a serious problem. Wilhelm II admired Menzel's Prussian pictures, ordered him a state funeral, and attended it himself. The alleged snub is hard to square with the record. The resemblance, though, is not in doubt — and standing in front of the dwarf, knowing both halves of the argument, is a better experience than being handed a tidy answer.

## Fenced After Dark

The park took heavy damage in the last weeks of the Second World War and the fountain with it. When the sculptures turned up again in 1950, the East Berlin city council set about repairs, but the originals were too far gone to reinstate; rough copies went in instead and the gardens were rebuilt to a plainer design. Further rounds of restoration followed in 1972–73 and again in 1982–83.

After 1990 the problem changed shape. Figures were broken off or carried away, graffiti spread across the arcade, and by the early 2000s the complex was in poor condition. A landmark restoration began in 2005, costing around 1.3 million euros — nine-tenths of it from Berlin, the federal government and the European Union, the rest from private donors. The first phase finished on 24 May 2007 and the whole site reopened that July, now enclosed by a fence that is locked at night. The water runs from spring through autumn and is shut off before the frosts.

## Flight Context

The Märchenbrunnen sits at 52.5281°N 13.4269°E, at the western entrance of the Volkspark Friedrichshain, roughly 2.5 km northeast of Alexanderplatz. From the air the park itself is the landmark: a broad irregular block of dark green pushed into dense grey Friedrichshain and Prenzlauer Berg housing, with two conspicuous rounded hills of postwar rubble standing well above the surrounding roofline. The fountain lies at the park's southwestern corner, its semicircular arcade and long axis just resolvable from 1,500–2,000 ft AGL in good light. The Fernsehturm, 2.5 km southwest, is the reliable pointer: hold the tower off the left wing on a northeasterly track and the park comes up ahead. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) lies about 22 km south-southeast; Strausberg (EDAY) is 25 km east and Schönhagen (EDAZ) southwest. Berlin CTR and TMA apply throughout. The fountain is worth seeing in leaf — the ensemble is framed by hedged walks that vanish under canopy in high summer and open up completely from November to April.
