# Rykestrasse Synagogue

Germany's largest synagogue survived the pogrom of 1938 for an unheroic reason — burning it would have burned down the apartments built around it.

**Location:** 52.5354, 13.4186

**Categories:** Berlin, Synagogues, Jewish history, Prenzlauer Berg, Nazi Germany, Germany

## Introduction

The largest synagogue in Germany came through the night of 9 November 1938 because of where it stands. It sits back from Rykestrasse, inside a block of apartment buildings, reached through a passage in the tenement at the front. Setting fire to it would have set fire to the neighbours. So the order that came down was for vandalism rather than arson: men went in, smashed the furnishings, set the Torah scrolls alight, and the flames were put out before they reached the fabric of the building. The synagogue stood. The congregation that filled it did not survive in anything like the same proportion, and it is that gap, not the masonry, that the building is really about.

## Built Because the Neighbourhood Could Not Afford Its Own

Berlin's Jewish population grew steeply through the second half of the nineteenth century, and as the city spread into new districts the need for synagogues within walking distance became pressing. In wealthier quarters, congregants solved this themselves, founding registered associations that built and ran their own premises. In Prenzlauer Berg most could not raise that kind of money.

So in 1902 the Jüdische Gemeinde zu Berlin bought the site and handed the job to its own building master, Johann Hoeniger. Construction began in 1903, and at noon on Sunday 4 September 1904 the synagogue was inaugurated, timed for Rosh Hashanah. It is a Romanesque Revival hall in brick, 45.78 metres long, 26.68 wide and 17.3 high, and it originally seated two thousand people. The setback from the street line was a matter of the site, not a defence. It became a defence thirty-four years later, entirely by accident.

## What a Congregation Argues About

The opening service used Handel's prelude in D major and the Ma Tovu, led by the cantor David Stabinski with Rabbi Adolf Rosenzweig preaching. During the First World War the Berlin community engaged Rabbi Martin Joseph as chaplain to Jewish Russian prisoners of war held in detention centres around the city; on the high holidays the German High Command permitted them to attend services here, which produced the strange spectacle of enemy soldiers praying in a Berlin synagogue under escort.

Congregants founded an association for the synagogue that organised lectures and festivities, collected money for members in need, and insisted on a say in who was hired as rabbi and cantor. Hugo Alexander was presiding over it in 1931. Then in January 1933 — the month the Nazis came to power — Sally Heilbrunn and Rabbi Moritz Freier gathered three hundred people to protest against replacing the old Rödelheim prayer books with Berlin's new unified edition. The protesters won. Those prayer books are still in use in this building. That is what the congregation was doing that January: arguing in public, at length, about a siddur.

## The Last Room Left

Once the new government had driven Jewish performers, artists and scientists off public stages and out of lecture halls, the synagogue opened its own. The Kulturbund Deutscher Juden staged concerts and talks here. The Jewish winter-aid fund put on benefit performances for poor Jews who had been excluded from state welfare.

On 16 February 1934 the synagogue choir gave the first performance of a new Friday-night liturgy composed by Jakob Dymont, the choirmaster at Adass Jisroel. The Rykestrasse choir was conducted by Kurt Burchard, born in 1877. He was murdered at Auschwitz in 1942. Rabbi Max Weyl had served the congregation since 1917 and was deported to Theresienstadt in 1942; among the students he had tutored was Regina Jonas, the first woman in the world to be ordained as a rabbi. These are not footnotes to the building's history. They are the people who made the room work, listed here because the alternative is to describe a synagogue and leave out everyone in it.

## Five Years of Silence

A story has attached itself to the building: that the Wehrmacht used it as a stable. It is unproven and unlikely. What is documented is duller and somehow worse — the prayer hall was used to store furniture.

There were no services here at all between April 1940 and July 1945. The building was intact, standing in its courtyard behind the tenement, while the community it had been built for was deported from the city in stages. The synagogue's own list of rabbis records those five years as one blank line.

## 13 July 1945

On Friday 13 July 1945, ten weeks after the fighting in Berlin stopped, a Shabbat service was held here again. A rabbi took the pulpit that month and held it until he died in office in 1965. Ödön Singer followed until 1969, and then the position stood empty for eighteen years, East Berlin's Jewish community having become very small. Isaac Newman served until May 1988; the post was vacant again after that until reunification. Since 1 January 1991 the synagogue has been served by the rabbis of the reunited Jüdische Gemeinde zu Berlin.

More than a year of restoration work brought back the prewar interior, and the synagogue was rededicated on 31 August 2007. Berlin today has the largest Jewish community in Germany — around 12,000 registered members and eight synagogues — and this is the biggest room among them, seating 1,074 where it once seated 2,000. Nobody reduced the capacity for architectural reasons.

## Flight Context

The synagogue stands at 52.5354°N 13.4186°E in Prenzlauer Berg, about 3 km northeast of the Brandenburg Gate — though it is genuinely difficult to pick out from the air, because it sits inside a courtyard and is screened from the street by the tenement in front of it. Look instead for its neighbours. Kollwitzplatz, a small triangular green square, lies 300 m southwest, and the enormous circular brick drum of the Zeiss-Grossplanetarium and the green strip of the Ernst-Thälmann-Park stand about a kilometre northeast. The most reliable anchor for the whole district is the Fernsehturm, 2 km southwest, with the elevated S-Bahn ring curving north of Prenzlauer Berg through Schönhauser Allee. From 1,500–2,500 ft AGL the area reads as an unusually complete grid of nineteenth-century perimeter blocks, with green courtyards enclosed on all four sides — the synagogue is inside one of them. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) lies about 22 km south-southeast; Strausberg (EDAY) is 28 km east and Schönhagen (EDAZ) southwest. Berlin CTR and TMA apply, with the government-quarter restriction to the southwest.
