# Großes Schauspielhaus

A failed market hall became a circus, then Max Reinhardt's Theatre of the Five Thousand under a ceiling of hanging plaster stalactites — and finally died because a hospital next door lowered the water table and rotted its oak piles.

**Location:** 52.5228, 13.3861

**Categories:** Theatres in Berlin, Expressionist architecture, Hans Poelzig buildings, Buildings and structures in Mitte, Demolished buildings and structures in Berlin

## Introduction

The building was killed by thirst. In 1980, construction work at the Charité hospital a few streets away drew down the groundwater under the Schiffbauerdamm, and the oak piles driven into that marshy riverbank in the 1860s — which had held for over a century on the simple condition of staying wet — began to fail. The theatre was evacuated at once, and East German authorities concealed the reason, because buildings on the West Berlin side of the Wall were sinking too and that was not a conversation anyone wanted. It was an unglamorous end for a hall that had been, in its best years, the most theatrically ambitious room in Europe: Max Reinhardt's Großes Schauspielhaus, seating five thousand under a dome hung with stucco stalactites, which Berliners called the Tropfsteinhöhle — the dripstone cave.

## A Market Hall Nobody Wanted

It started as municipal optimism. Between 1865 and 1867 Friedrich Hitzig built Berlin's first modern purpose-built market hall on the right bank of the Spree, loosely neoclassical, iron-framed, resting on an extensive raft of oak piling because the ground would not otherwise hold it. The public ignored it. Within a few months the hall had closed, and it spent the next stretch of its life as a food depot, including for the Prussian Army during the Franco-Prussian War. What saved it was the discovery that a large clear-span room with good sightlines is exactly what a circus needs. Albert Salamonsky opened the 5,000-seat Markthallen-Zirkus there in 1873, and the address became Am Zirkus 1 — a street name that outlived every building that justified it.

## Forty-Five Years of Circus

Circus Renz moved in from 1879, having lost its old home to the construction of Friedrichstraße station, and Ernst Renz pushed the capacity to 5,600 in 1888. Circus Schumann followed from 1899 to 1918. In between, the hall lent itself to whatever needed a lot of seats: Robert Koch convened his international congress on tuberculosis there in 1890, which meant the world's leading bacteriologists sat where the horses had been. In 1917 the property was sold to Deutsches Nationaltheater AG, the corporate vehicle of Max Reinhardt, who had run the Deutsches Theater since 1905 and was steadily acquiring stages across Berlin and Vienna. He wanted a theatre for crowds, and he had just bought a building shaped by half a century of crowds.

## The Dripstone Cave

Hans Poelzig began the conversion in 1918 and produced one of the strangest interiors expressionism ever managed. He added storeys to Hitzig's hall, threw an enormous dome over the auditorium, and covered the wrought-iron ceiling with stucco pendants that hung down like limestone formations in a cave, lit so that the whole ceiling seemed to drip. Poelzig's wife and collaborator Marlene Moeschke-Poelzig designed much of the foyer, where cantilevered columns of light stood free of the walls. Behind the effects was serious machinery: a revolving stage, a cyclorama, an adjustable forestage. It opened on 28 November 1919 with Aeschylus's *Oresteia* in Karl Gustav Vollmoeller's version, directed by Reinhardt, and *Danton's Death* and *Julius Caesar* followed. Critics reached for the phrase Theatre of the Five Thousand. Nobody had built a room quite like it, and — apart from Poelzig — nobody has since.

## Taken at a Tenth of Its Value

The Depression buried the theatre in debt, millions of Reichsmarks of it, much of it owed to the Bank der Arbeiter, Angestellten und Beamten. When the Nazis took power in 1933 they revoked the Deutsches Nationaltheater AG's tax exemption, and the resulting tax burden finished the company off. The bank, by then nazified as the Bank der Deutschen Arbeit and answering to the German Labour Front, absorbed the debt, and the shareholders — Reinhardt among them — were pressured into selling their stakes for roughly a tenth of what they were worth. Reinhardt, born Maximilian Goldmann to Jewish parents outside Vienna in 1873, had run eleven Berlin stages by 1930. He lost them, fled Austria after the Anschluss of 1938, became an American citizen in 1940, and died in New York on 30 October 1943. In 1934 the theatre reopened as the Theater des Volkes under the Strength through Joy programme, with Joseph Goebbels overseeing. Labour Front workers stripped out Poelzig's stalactite wall and installed a Führer's box in its place.

## Am Zirkus 1, Vacant

After the war it reopened under Soviet occupation as a variety house and was eventually renamed the Friedrichstadt-Palast, playing revues and hosting Free German Youth events with equal facility. Then came the groundwater, and the evacuation of 1980. A new Friedrichstadt-Palast opened a few blocks away in 1984, and the old building was pulled down; the Japanese photographer Ryuji Miyamoto shot it mid-demolition, Moeschke-Poelzig's light columns still standing in the wreckage. Nothing replaced it. The ground was divided between a plaza on the Spree, an empty lot, and Bertolt-Brecht-Platz, named for the playwright who worked next door at the Theater am Schiffbauerdamm. The neighbour survives. The Theatre of the Five Thousand is a name on a square and a gap in the street.

## Flight Context

The site lies at 52.5228°N, 13.3861°E on the Schiffbauerdamm in Mitte, on the north bank of the Spree immediately east of Friedrichstraße station. Nothing of the theatre remains — look instead for Bertolt-Brecht-Platz and the Berliner Ensemble's neo-baroque house right beside it, on the bend of the river. From 2,000–3,000 ft the Spree's tight double curve through Mitte and the elevated S-Bahn viaduct running east–west into Friedrichstraße station make the block easy to fix; the Reichstag and the government quarter are about 1 km west, the Fernsehturm 1.5 km east. EDDB (Berlin Brandenburg) is roughly 25 km south-east; EDAZ (Schönhagen) about 45 km south-west for light aircraft. Tegel and Tempelhof are permanently closed. Central Berlin's airspace is busy and the terrain is flat; haze rather than terrain is the usual visibility constraint.
