# Lindentunnel

Kaiser Wilhelm II would not have trams crossing Unter den Linden on the surface, so Berlin dug underneath it — and the tunnel has now spent far longer as a cellar than it ever spent as a railway.

**Location:** 52.5175, 13.3947

**Categories:** Berlin, Tunnels, Trams, Engineering, Unter den Linden, Germany

## Introduction

In April 1901 Wilhelm II forbade any further tram crossing of Unter den Linden at street level. He is supposed to have scrawled "No, it will be made underground!" across the plans, though the authenticity of the line has never been established. The instruction stuck regardless. Berlin's ceremonial boulevard would not be strung with wire or cut by tracks, and if the city wanted its north–south tram connection it would have to go beneath the lime trees. What got built is still down there: two parallel tunnels under the pavement between the Neue Wache and the State Opera, shut to the public for most of a century, holding stage scenery in the dark.

## The Crossing That Offended a Kaiser

Trams had crossed the boulevard since 22 September 1894, at a point agreed only after years of haggling between the city, the tram company and the palace. Unter den Linden was a legal oddity: most Berlin streets passed into municipal ownership in 1876, but not this one, and the Kaiser kept the final word on it.

The real irritant was the wire. Overhead lines were banned at prestigious locations on aesthetic grounds, so the Great Berlin Tramway ran battery railcars, then in October 1901 laid a slotted-tube conduit at the crossing. It failed: the ducts clogged with leaves and slush and the collectors snapped off at the slightest obstruction. Emergency overhead lines went up in the winter of 1906/1907 and became permanent the following year, stretched across sixty metres of roadway without a single intermediate support — exactly what the Kaiser had forbidden.

## Empress Augusta Sets the Ceiling

The design that emerged after the city and the tramway settled their concession dispute in 1911 was strange: not one tunnel but two, running together beneath the boulevard and splitting at its southern edge. The Opera House and St Hedwig's Church had objected to tracks between them, so the east tunnel — the Opera Tunnel — surfaced on one side of the Opera and the west tunnel, the Behrenstrassentunnel, on the other.

The east tunnel ran 354 metres including its ramps, the west 389. Ramps fell at one in twenty, the sharpest curve had a radius of 35 metres, and headroom differed: 4.65 metres east, 4.30 west. The reason is the most Berlin detail in the project. Double-deck trams were meant to use the east tunnel. The west could not be built to match because the contract had been awarded on condition that the monument to Empress Augusta, standing directly above it, was not to be moved.

## Dug by the Women of 1915

Excavation began on 7 September 1914, five weeks into the First World War, under Siemens & Halske. Work went quickly at first for an unhappy reason: firms across Berlin had lost their ordinary contracts to the war and had crews idle.

That did not last. Through 1915 the labour shortage bit as the remaining men were called up, and wherever it was possible women took over the heavy digging. Spoil went out in tipper wagons behind a benzene-burning locomotive, down to a barge-loading point on the Kupfergraben. Wall lamps were shaded to throw light only up and down, so trams could run through by day without switching on their own. The west tunnel opened on 17 December 1916 and the east two days later.

## Thirty-Five Years of Service

The west tunnel barely earned its keep. No line used it at first, and when the north–south U-Bahn opened its first stretch in January 1923 passengers simply moved down to the subway. After the "tram-free day" of 1923 the west bore stood empty, and in 1926 it was sealed behind a retaining wall, its ramps filled and the ground above levelled for the Opera's extension. A decade later that dead tunnel got one last use: lighting trials for a road tunnel proposed near the Brandenburg Gate as part of the plans for Germania.

The east tunnel worked hard, carrying as many as ten lines at its peak. Lines 12 and 13 were the last through it, halted in the spring of 1945 when the southern ramp was damaged. Trams returned after the war and ran until 2 September 1951, the service withdrawn as soon as the World Youth Festival was over. The final line through the Lindentunnel was the 46.

## A Cellar Under the Boulevard

What followed was seventy years of improvisation. The State Opera stored scenery down there, then grandstands for the parades on Marx-Engels-Platz. The East German People's Police parked personnel carriers in the east tunnel and kept a sealed room in the west one for the switching gear of their surveillance television system. In the 1960s a district-heating duct took some of the remaining headroom.

From 1994 the artist Ben Wagin took over most of the space for installations, setting a restored tram car on the north ramp ahead of the UN climate conference; he moved out in December 1998. Since September 2002 the Maxim Gorki Theater has used about eighty metres from the north ramp as a scenery store, its props lowered through a freight lift set into the pavement. Part of the west tunnel came down in 2003 for an underground car park, and movement cracks and spalling concrete have since required emergency propping. In December 2021 Berlin newspapers reported the structure could no longer take the weight above it; an eighteen-tonne axle limit imposed that spring pushed the city's double-decker buses off Unter den Linden. Demolition is planned once the U5 extension running beneath it is complete. No date has been set.

## Flight Context

The tunnel lies at 52.5175°N 13.3947°E, under the eastern end of Unter den Linden between the Neue Wache and the Berlin State Opera. Nothing of it is visible from the air apart from the ground it sits under, but that ground is one of the easiest features in Berlin to find: the dead-straight green corridor of Unter den Linden running east from the Brandenburg Gate to the Spree, with the Berlin Cathedral dome and the reconstructed palace of the Humboldt Forum closing the far end. From 2,000–2,500 ft AGL the axis reads as a single line — gate, boulevard, Bebelplatz opening to the south, cathedral. The Fernsehturm needle stands a kilometre beyond it and makes a reliable anchor at any altitude. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is about 20 km south-southeast; Schönhagen (EDAZ) lies southwest, Strausberg (EDAY) east. The whole area sits inside the Berlin CTR with restricted airspace over the government quarter, so transit only under clearance. Best light is a summer evening, when the sun runs straight down the boulevard's east–west axis; winter brings low stratus and short afternoons.
