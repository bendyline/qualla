# Franziskaner-Klosterkirche

A roofless Gothic brick shell on Klosterstraße is one of the oldest structures in Berlin — a Franciscan church that outlived the Reformation, seven centuries of renovation, and one air raid too many.

**Location:** 52.5183, 13.4128

**Categories:** germany, berlin, architecture, history

## Introduction

Rain falls straight into the nave. The roof has been gone since a morning in April 1945 and nobody has put one back, which turns out to suit the building: standing inside the Franziskaner-Klosterkirche you look up through empty Gothic window tracery at open sky, with tower blocks and traffic on three sides. Berlin is a city that mostly dates from the day before yesterday. This brick shell on Klosterstraße was already old when Frederick the Great was born.

## Grey Robes at the Edge of Town

Franciscans arrived in the young twin towns of Berlin and Cölln around the middle of the thirteenth century and built themselves a church, conventionally dated to 1250 though the sources argue over a range of a decade or two. That first building was fieldstone — glacial boulders split and laid in courses, the standard material of the north German plain — and it measured 52 metres by 16. Its remains are still in the north wall of what stands today, if you know to look for the change in texture. Before the century was out the friars began replacing it with something far more ambitious: a three-aisled brick basilica, finished in the first half of the fourteenth century. That is the shell you see. In 1365 Louis II, Elector of Brandenburg, was buried inside it, and around 1500 the whole church was given a going-over.

## The Monks Leave, the Schoolboys Arrive

The Reformation reached Berlin in 1539 and the monastery closed. What happened next is the reason the site mattered for the following four centuries: the empty conventual buildings were put to work. Berlin's first printing press was installed in them in 1571, run by Leonhard Thurneysser, an alchemist and physician who also restored the church itself between 1583 and 1584. Three years after the press arrived, in 1574, the Evangelisches Gymnasium zum Grauen Kloster opened in the same complex — the Grey Monastery School, which taught Berlin's schoolboys for the better part of four centuries. Karl Friedrich Schinkel sat in those classrooms, and so did Friedrich Ludwig Jahn, the gymnastics evangelist, and so did Otto von Bismarck. Not one of the monastic buildings survives.

## Four Centuries of Second Thoughts

Old churches are never finished, only interrupted, and this one was fussed over continuously. The seventeenth century took down the old staircase tower and put a timber stair on the west side. In 1712 the rood screen dividing nave from chancel was demolished — and later that same year a fire went through the roof. The 1719 repairs raised the floor a metre and bricked up two windows in the north choir. The nineteenth century was more drastic still: the gabled tower came down in 1826, two new towers went up on the west front in 1842, a sacristy was added and the floor lowered back to where it had been. Schinkel, an old boy of the school next door, drew up plans; so did Christian Gottlieb Cantian and a former track inspector named Berger, whose second design was the one built. Work ran to 1845. Then damp in the masonry closed the church in 1902, and in 1926 almost everything the nineteenth century had done was undone again. It was reconsecrated on 24 May 1936.

## 3 April 1945

Nine years. That is how long the restored church stood before an Allied air raid on 3 April 1945 destroyed it, five weeks from the end of the war in Europe and after nearly seven hundred years of continuous alteration and repair. The debris was cleared in 1950. Between 1959 and 1963 East German authorities secured the standing walls, which was a real decision rather than an obvious one — a ruined church was not an asset the state had any use for. The surviving monastic buildings were treated differently and demolished outright to make room for a park. What was left was a brick outline, roofless, held up by consolidation work, in a district that was being rebuilt around it from nothing.

## A Ruin with a Programme

The shell was restored again in 2003-04, and since 2016 the Mitte district authority has run it as a venue. Exhibitions, plays and concerts happen inside the walls; sculpture has been installed among them since the 1980s. It is an unusually good room for the purpose, because the acoustics are the acoustics of a basilica with no ceiling and the light changes hour by hour rather than being fixed by a stained-glass scheme. From the observation deck of the Fernsehturm, seven hundred metres northwest, the ruin reads as a small rectangle of exposed brick and green ground hemmed in by post-war blocks — medieval Berlin, still legible, in a determinedly modern view.

## Flight Context

The ruin sits at 52.5183° N, 13.4128° E on Klosterstraße in Berlin-Mitte, roughly 700 m southeast of Alexanderplatz. From the air it is a small roofless rectangle of red-brown brick, open at the top so the interior floor is directly visible — unmistakable once found, because nothing else nearby has that signature. The Fernsehturm's sphere is the primary anchor 700 m northwest; the twin brick spires of the Nikolaikirche lie about 500 m southwest and the Rotes Rathaus's tower sits between the two. Best viewed from 1,500-2,500 ft AGL on a north-south track, ideally near midday when sun reaches into the open nave. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) lies about 20 km south-southeast; Strausberg (EDAY) is the nearest general-aviation field to the east, Schönhagen (EDAZ) to the southwest. Central Berlin airspace is controlled and requires prior coordination for low-level VFR. Winter overcast is frequent from November to February.
