# Tunnel 57

Students spent five months digging 145 metres under the death strip from a disused bakery, got 57 people out over two nights in October 1964, and left behind a dead border guard the GDR turned into a national hero on a lie.

**Location:** 52.5363, 13.3927

**Categories:** berlin, berlin-wall, cold-war, modern-history, germany, engineering

## Introduction

One of the men who spent the summer of 1964 twelve metres beneath Berlin, hauling mud out in flour sacks, would be in orbit twenty-one years later. Reinhard Furrer was a physics student at the Free University then, one of thirty-five West Berliners who took shifts in the cellar of an abandoned bakery at Bernauer Straße 97 and dug east. They worked from April to October. What they produced ran 145 metres and lay 12 metres down — the longest, deepest and most expensive escape tunnel ever built under the Berlin Wall — and it surfaced inside a disused outhouse in a courtyard at Strelitzer Straße 55, in East Berlin.

## Five Months in a Bakery Cellar

The bakery was chosen for what stood opposite it. Along this stretch of Bernauer Straße the Wall was made partly of apartment buildings whose doors and windows had been bricked shut, which meant no guards patrolled directly overhead. Digging still took five months of miserable work. The diggers lived in the bakery for stretches at a time, taking turns at the face, packing spoil into flour sacks to carry back up, and rinsing the caked mud off themselves with buckets of water. The money came from an arrangement that would raise eyebrows today: the organisers sold film and photograph rights to news agencies. Stern covered the operation while publicly keeping its distance from it, having quietly helped pay for it. The largest single contribution, around 30,000 Deutschmarks, came from the West German Federal Ministry of Intra-German Relations.

## The Courtyard at Strelitzer Straße 55

Getting people to the hole was harder than digging it. Couriers crossed into East Berlin and made contact with some 120 people over the preceding months, arranging times and a route. On the nights of 3 and 4 October those who came were brought to the apartment building at Strelitzer Straße 55 and into the rear courtyard, where Reinhard Furrer met them and showed them where to climb down. They went through in the dark, one after another, on their hands and knees for the length of a city block. Fifty-seven made it out — families, older people, children — which is where the tunnel got the name it has carried ever since. It remains the single most successful escape under the Berlin Wall. But somewhere among the 120 people the couriers had approached was an informant, and the Stasi had been reading the plan for some time.

## The Sergeant Who Had Been a Schoolteacher

Egon Schultz was twenty-one. He had been born in January 1943 in Groß Jestin in Pomerania, the second son of a truck driver and a waitress; his older brother was a painter, and by the 1960s the family lived in Rostock. He trained as a schoolteacher at Putbus on Rügen and started teaching at nineteen, in September 1962, at a school in Dierkow just outside Rostock. Conscription interrupted that after a year. By October 1964 he had served two years of a three-year term with the border troops and held the rank of sergeant. On the night of 4 October he was on reserve duty at the Arkonaplatz command post when a request came for backup at Strelitzer Straße. The guards who went were not told they were walking into an escape operation. Two Stasi officers in plain clothes had already appeared at the outhouse, posing as fugitives and saying they wanted to fetch a friend before going through. They came back with soldiers.

## Shots in a Dark Courtyard

One of the escape helpers, Christian Zobel, fired at the approaching men and hit Egon Schultz in the shoulder. Schultz went down. As he tried to get back up, a burst from a fellow border soldier's Kalashnikov — fired blind into an unlit courtyard — struck him. He died of internal bleeding on the way to hospital. The East German leadership had the truth within days, from the autopsy and its own reports, and buried it. The following morning the East Berlin press announced that West Berlin terrorists had murdered a border guard, and the SED built a martyr out of him: a state funeral with full military honours, an official demand for Zobel's extradition, and, on the day he was buried, the renaming of the Rostock school where he had taught as the Egon Schultz Oberschule. In time more than a hundred schools and institutions carried his name. Strelitzer Straße itself was renamed after him — the street the tunnel had come up in.

## What the Files Said

The truth stayed classified until October 1990, when the Stasi records opened and the ballistic findings came out with them. A later investigation held that the soldier who fired the fatal rounds had acted under orders and in the belief he was under attack, and the case was closed. Christian Zobel never knew. He died in the 1980s convinced that he had killed a man, carrying a guilt that the records show was misplaced. In December 1991 the street went back to being Strelitzer Straße, and the school in Rostock was renamed for Käthe Kollwitz. Most of the diggers never went underground again, though Wolfgang Fuchs and Hasso Herschel kept running escapes for years. Furrer finished his physics degree, joined the German astronaut corps, and flew as a payload specialist on the Spacelab D1 mission in October 1985; he was killed in 1995 when a restored Messerschmitt Bf 108 he was riding in came down at an air display at Johannisthal. Above ground today, at the Berlin Wall Memorial, a row of small metal plates runs across the grass at right angles to the surviving wall, tracing where the tunnel passed underneath. Most visitors walk over them without looking down. There is nothing to see, and there never was. That was the point.

## Flight Context

The tunnel ran roughly northeast from 52.536°N, 13.393°E, under what is now the Berlin Wall Memorial grounds on Bernauer Straße in Mitte, about 2 km north of the Reichstag. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) lies 22 km southeast. From 2,000–4,000 ft the memorial reads as a long green corridor cutting diagonally through dense Gründerzeit blocks — the only substantial gap in the roofline for a kilometre in any direction, because the buildings along the old death strip were demolished and never replaced. The Fernsehturm stands 2 km southeast as the obvious fix. Winter haze over Berlin frequently reduces slant visibility below 5 km; late spring gives the cleanest look at the street pattern.
