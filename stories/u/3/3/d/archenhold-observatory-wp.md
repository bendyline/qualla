# Archenhold Observatory

A 21-metre telescope built for a trade fair, stranded in a Berlin park because nobody could afford to move it, and still the longest movable refractor in the world.

**Location:** 52.4858, 13.4764

**Categories:** Berlin, Astronomy, Science, Museums, Nazi Germany

## Introduction

It was supposed to be temporary. The Great Refractor went up in Treptower Park for the Great Industrial Exposition of Berlin in 1896, inside a wooden shed with a lecture hall attached, and the admission money was meant to pay for building it and then hauling it somewhere permanent. The exposition opened on 1 May with the telescope still unfinished; it only began working that September. By the time the fair closed there was no money left to move anything. So the city gave permission for the instrument to stay where it was, and Friedrich Simon Archenhold — who had no astronomer's salary and now had a 130-tonne telescope he could not relocate — decided the sensible thing to do was open it to the public. Berliners called it die Himmelskanone. The celestial cannon.

## The Celestial Cannon

The numbers explain the nickname. The lens, ground by C. A. Steinheil & Söhne, is 68 centimetres across; the tube in front of it runs 21 metres, and the whole moving assembly weighs 130 metric tons. Archenhold had drawn it up as a research instrument, and even at the time its design drew criticism — a refractor that long is an awkward, ungainly way to gather light, and the optics of the coming century belonged to mirrors. What it was, unarguably, was enormous. It is the longest movable refracting telescope in the world, a title it has held since the year it was assembled and looks unlikely ever to lose, because nobody will build another. It was decommissioned in 1958 and kept as a monument of technology, listed from 1967, and rebuilt over six years from 1977. When the work finished in 1983 the cannon could point and track again. It still can.

## Einstein, 2 June 1915

The wooden shed came down in 1908 and a permanent building was inaugurated on 4 April 1909, with a proper lecture hall. Six years later, on 2 June 1915, Albert Einstein stood in that hall and gave his first public talk on general relativity. The theory would not be finished for another five months, and the eclipse observations that made it famous were four years away; the audience in Treptow was hearing an argument about gravity and geometry that most physicists had not yet absorbed. It is a strange and fitting pairing — the century's most radical idea about light and space, first explained aloud in a building whose main asset was a telescope that critics had already called obsolete. Foerster's original point in backing the project had been that the public deserved access to science. That evening it got rather more than it had been promised.

## Forced Out of the Family's Own Observatory

Friedrich Archenhold ran the place for thirty-five years. His own research had begun with photographic technique — testing a short-focus astro-camera for Wilhelm Foerster, first at the Baltic, then at a station at Berlin-Halensee. He captured further photographic evidence of the nebula at Xi Persei, and images of Halley's comet, a fireball and noctilucent clouds in 1910. He joined the observing campaign on Nova Persei in 1901, when nobody yet understood what made a star erupt. In 1912 he published observations of Jupiter's Great Red Spot made through the giant telescope. His son Günter took over as director in 1931 and was forced to resign in 1936 because of the family's Jewish ancestry. The Archenholds were expelled from the observatory that carried their name. Some of them managed to emigrate. Others were sent to Nazi concentration camps. The observatory itself was absorbed into the city school system, which is how a family was erased from an institution without a brick being moved.

## Reopened for an Eclipse

A bomb struck the southwest wing during the war, but the Great Refractor came through without serious damage. Observing resumed in July 1945 — for a solar eclipse, with Edgar Mädlow as interim director. In 1946 the city renamed the place the Archenhold-Sternwarte, and on 1 June 1948 Diedrich Wattenberg, who had worked alongside Archenhold, took over. Visitor numbers climbed from 8,000 in 1946 to 25,000 by 1949. From 1959 the observatory turned increasingly toward teaching school physics and astronomy under the GDR, and the equipment grew to match: the first Zeiss small planetarium in East Germany that same year, two more domes in the grounds in 1962, a lecture theatre, and in the mid-1960s a solar physics cabinet designed with the optical engineer Edwin Rolf, which could throw an 80-centimetre image of the Sun onto a wall or spread its spectrum out to a metre and a half.

## Still Open Two Nights a Week

Dieter B. Herrmann became director in November 1976 and pushed the modernisation further; a research department for the history of astronomy had been running since 1972, and annual attendance through the 1980s held near 70,000. Archenhold had wanted a large planetarium for Berlin, and in 1987 he finally got one — the Zeiss Major Planetarium in the Ernst-Thälmann-Park, built under the same directorship. After reunification the observatory passed to the school administration, was refurbished in 1995 and 1996, and since 2002 has belonged to the German Museum of Technology; today it operates alongside the major planetarium and the Wilhelm Foerster Observatory as a single institution. It is the oldest and largest public observatory in Germany. On clear nights, people still queue in Treptower Park to look through a telescope that was built for a trade fair, condemned as out of date before it was finished, and never once packed up and taken away.

## Flight Context

The observatory sits at 52.486°N, 13.476°E in Treptower Park on the south bank of the Spree, in Berlin-Treptow. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) lies about 14 km almost due south; Schönhagen (EDAZ) is roughly 43 km southwest. From 1,500–3,000 ft the park is unmistakable — a large wooded rectangle where the Spree makes a wide southeasterly bend, with the Soviet War Memorial's tall central obelisk and long axial lawn cutting across it. The observatory buildings stand at the park's southeastern edge, and the Great Refractor's shelter and the two garden domes are visible as pale circular roofs against the trees. The Fernsehturm at Alexanderplatz is 5 km northwest and makes the easiest run-in. Winter haze and low stratus are common from November through February.
