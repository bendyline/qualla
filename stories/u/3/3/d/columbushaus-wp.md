# Columbushaus

Erich Mendelsohn's nine-storey glass-and-steel landmark at Potsdamer Platz survived the war, burned in the 1953 uprising, and vanished into the death strip four years later.

**Location:** 52.5100, 13.3761

**Categories:** germany, berlin, architecture, history

## Introduction

On 17 June 1953, the police station inside the Columbushaus went quiet. The uprising that had begun that week with East Berlin construction workers had spread across the city, and the crowd outside was in no mood to negotiate. The officers took off their uniforms and hung a white flag from a window. It did not help. The crowd set the building alight anyway, and by evening the most advanced office block Europe had produced in the 1930s was a blackened steel cage. Its architect, Erich Mendelsohn, was three months from death in San Francisco, an ocean and two decades removed from the square he had once tried to rebuild.

## A Little Skyscraper

Critics called it a "little skyscraper," which undersold it. Nine storeys of steel frame curved along the line of Friedrich-Ebert-Straße in horizontal bands, windows alternating with spandrels in a rhythm that made the building look as though it were moving. Mendelsohn shifted much of the upper-floor load onto the window frames of the outer walls, which meant almost no internal columns and floor plans that could be repartitioned at will — the client had insisted on that flexibility, in case the offices ever became a department store. On the shop floors below, cross girders and cantilevers carried the weight inward so the glazing could run unbroken. It was the first building in Germany with mechanical ventilation and, by common assessment, the most advanced office building on the continent. Mendelsohn later grumbled that the masonry courses were not his idea at all; the client wanted somewhere to hang neon signs, and he would have preferred nothing but metal and glass.

## The Store That Never Opened

The site had held the Grand Hotel Bellevue since the late 1880s. A consortium of German investors wanted a Berlin branch of Galeries Lafayette there and hired Mendelsohn for his modernist reputation: a rooftop restaurant over two floors, the store's name in giant letters around the roofline, a foyer doubling as a subway entrance. Confident of approval, they knocked the hotel down late in 1928, and Mendelsohn threw up a twenty-metre advertising hoarding along the old building's footprint, with shops at its base and paid advertising covering some of the landowners' costs. In February 1929 the city refused the design on traffic grounds. Then the investors decided to build elsewhere, and then the Depression stopped them building anything at all. Only in August 1931 did they announce a different tenant, Wertheim, and a different building. Mendelsohn's larger vision for the square — a ring of towers around a reordered Potsdamer Platz, worked up in sketches in 1928 and again in 1931 — got exactly one building constructed.

## Three Rooms on an Upper Floor

On 1 December 1939, a man named Richard von Hegener rented three or four offices in the building. The organisation he established there had an innocuous name and a bureaucratic function: it existed to administer the killing of disabled people and psychiatric patients across Germany, the programme later known as Action T4 after the address its headquarters moved to in the spring of 1940, Tiergartenstraße 4. Tens of thousands of people were murdered under it, each one somebody's child or parent, most of them taken from institutions where their families believed them safe. For a few months, the paperwork that condemned them moved through a building conceived as an argument for reason and light. Mendelsohn, who was Jewish, had fled Germany in the spring of 1933; his assets were seized and his name struck from the professional registers. He never came back.

## The Border Runs Through It

The steel frame did what steel frames do. Shelled during the battle for Berlin in 1945, the Columbushaus was damaged but not destroyed, and it stood in the Soviet sector. Wertheim kept selling from the ground floor until 1948, when the East Berlin Magistrat seized the property and handed the retail space to the state trading organisation HO. The People's Police moved in upstairs. After the fire of June 1953, the shell simply stood there, roofless, on what was becoming the most heavily policed ground in Europe. In 1957 it was demolished and the site cleared. The steel was salvaged and used again somewhere else — the only part of the building that outlived it.

## Norbert Kubat's Corner

The plot became a wedge of no-man's-land called the Lenné triangle: legally East Berlin, physically on the western side of the Wall. In March 1988 the two halves of the city agreed to swap sixteen small parcels of land so an autobahn extension could go through, with West Berlin paying 76 million Deutschmarks into the bargain. Before the transfer took effect on 1 July, environmentalists and squatters occupied the triangle, built an encampment, ran a radio station, and named the place Norbert Kubat Corner after a young man who had taken his own life in jail. Roughly six hundred people ended up there, drawn from across West Germany and beyond, with foreign television crews filming the whole business. When West Berlin police moved in with tear gas on the morning of 1 July, the last two hundred occupiers went up ladders and over the Wall — a mass flight from West to East, the only one of its kind. East German border guards helped them down with their dogs and bicycles, fed them breakfast, and walked them back through the diplomatic entrance at Friedrichstraße station. Thirty years of scrub woodland later, the site was cleared again for the Beisheim Center, whose hotels now stand roughly where the white flag hung.

## Flight Context

The site sits at 52.510° N, 13.376° E, at the northwest corner of Potsdamer Platz in Berlin-Mitte. Nothing of the Columbushaus survives; the Beisheim Center towers occupy the Lenné triangle today, with the Tiergarten's green mass immediately north and the Sony Center's tented roof a short hop southeast — the easiest visual anchor from the air. Best viewed from 2,000-3,000 ft AGL on a north-south track, which lines up the Brandenburg Gate, the grid of stelae at the Memorial to the Murdered Jews of Europe, and Potsdamer Platz in sequence along the line of the former death strip. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) lies about 25 km southeast; Schönhagen (EDAZ) is the nearest general-aviation field, southwest of the city, and Strausberg (EDAY) sits to the east. Berlin's controlled airspace is busy and low-level VFR routes need prior coordination. Winter haze and low stratus are common from November through February; late-spring afternoons give the cleanest light on the glass towers.
