# Hödel assassination attempt

On 11 May 1878 a shopkeeper's elbow deflected the first shot fired at Kaiser Wilhelm I on Unter den Linden — and within six months Bismarck had used the attempt, and a second one, to outlaw the German labour movement.

**Location:** 52.5173, 13.3947

**Categories:** 1878 in Germany, History of anarchism, Anarchism in Germany, 1870s in Berlin, German Empire

## Introduction

Mrs Julius Hauch had come to see the Emperor. She was at the front of the crowd on Unter den Linden on the Saturday afternoon of 11 May 1878, near number 7, opposite the Russian embassy, when a young man behind her began shoving to get past. She assumed he wanted a better view and refused to move. He raised his arm over her shoulder and fired, and at that exact moment she jostled him back, annoyed — and the bullet went into the air. That elbow is the reason Wilhelm I, King of Prussia since 1861 and German Emperor since 1871, rode home from the Tiergarten that day at the age of eighty-one. The young man was Max Hödel, twenty-one years old, and although he fired twice more and missed both times, the consequences of his three wasted shots reshaped German politics for a decade.

## Twenty-One Years Old

Hödel was born in Leipzig in 1857, the illegitimate son of a very young mother, and grew up poor and handed around — grandmother, then an uncle, then his mother again. At twelve he was jailed for a day for running away from home. At thirteen he ran again, was sent to the Zeitz reformatory, and was publicly whipped as a thief; his old school then issued him a certificate describing him as a confirmed thief, a document that followed him into working life. He trained as a tinsmith at fifteen, lost the apprenticeship after a fight, went back to the reformatory, and eventually qualified. None of this excuses what he did on Unter den Linden. It does explain something about a young man who had been told in writing, by an institution, what he was.

## From the Party to the Deed

He joined the Social Democratic Party, founded in 1875, and rose through it — travelling on party business, attending internal meetings, no longer needing to work. Somewhere along the way he met the anarchists organising in Leipzig around August Reinsdorf and Emil Werner, and he changed. On a propaganda tour in March 1878 he said out loud that Germany needed no Kaiser, no king and no laws, that the state should be abolished and everyone should do the same amount of work. The party expelled him, partly for that and partly over accusations of embezzlement. He reached Berlin on 26 April 1878, took a room from a widow on Stallschreiberstraße, and joined the conservative Christian Social Party — attending meetings, handing out leaflets, playing the devout activist. On 4 May he bought a revolver for eight marks. Two days later he sat for a photographer and asked for as many copies as possible, because within a week his name would be known all over the world.

## Three Shots and a Beating

After the first shot went wide he struck Mrs Hauch unconscious with the butt of the revolver, pushed past her, fired again and missed, jumped into the roadway, fired once at a pursuer, crouched to steady a third shot at the Emperor and missed that too. Wilhelm ordered his chasseur to give chase. Hödel ran, fought, and was caught by the crowd, which very nearly beat him to death; a worker named Carl Reinhold Gustav Kroger pulled him out, an act of ordinary decency for which Kroger was arrested as an accomplice and only later cleared. In custody Hödel announced that he was a friend of the Social Democrats but that the anarchists were his men, and that he had read Bakunin. The police searched him and found Social Democratic pamphlets and Christian Social brochures — and nothing anarchist at all.

## The Trial and the Blade

His chosen lawyer asked for eighty days to read the file and was refused, so the court appointed one who opened by apologising to the bench for having been given the case. Hödel spent the proceedings with a mocking expression, denying anarchist ties and presenting himself as a Christian Social or a Social Democrat — a performance later historians read as an attempt to keep the repression off the movement. The most damaging evidence was a letter to his parents saying he had sacrificed his life for the public good and regretted only that his shots had gone astray. The jury deliberated for twenty minutes; he laughed at the sentence. Chained in his cell to prevent escape and suicide, he refused to appeal — that wouldn't do any good, he said, off with my head. He was allowed to smoke and refused a visit from his parents. He smoked through the night of 15 August 1878, drank a glass of milk at six, walked three minutes to the block, and was beheaded in a single stroke.

## What Was Done With It

The attempt gave Bismarck an opening, but not yet a big enough one — his first anti-socialist bill failed. Three weeks after Hödel's arrest, on 2 June 1878, Karl Nobiling fired a double-barrelled shotgun from a window at 18 Unter den Linden and put more than thirty pellets into the eighty-one-year-old Emperor's head, neck, arms and shoulder; his helmet probably saved him. Nobiling shot himself and died of the wound in September. That was enough. Bismarck dissolved the Reichstag, campaigned on the panic, and pushed the Anti-Socialist Laws through in October 1878, banning the organisations, meetings and newspapers of a mass party that had expelled Hödel and had nothing to do with either shooting. Historians still argue about him. Rudolf Rocker read him as a confused boy adrift between ideologies; Andrew Carlson, writing in 1972, argued that Hödel knew exactly what he believed and that his Christian Social membership was camouflage. Either way, the people who paid for the three missed shots were mostly not in the street that afternoon.

## Flight Context

The attempt took place at 52.5173°N, 13.3947°E, on Unter den Linden near number 7 — the stretch between the Brandenburg Gate and the Bebelplatz, opposite the site of the Russian embassy, which still occupies the block today. From the air the boulevard is one of Berlin's most legible features: a dead-straight tree-lined axis running roughly east–west from the Brandenburg Gate and Tiergarten to Museum Island and the Spree, about 1.5 km end to end. Fly it at 2,000–3,000 ft with the Gate off the nose and the Fernsehturm beyond. EDDB (Berlin Brandenburg) lies about 25 km to the south-east; EDAZ (Schönhagen) roughly 45 km south-west. Tegel and Tempelhof are both closed. The linden trees mean the axis reads green in summer and as a bare double row in winter, when it is actually easier to trace.
