# Berlin Nord-Süd Tunnel

Berlin argued about this tunnel for fifty years, dug it under the Nazis, drowned it in the last week of the war, and has been repeating an inflated death toll about it ever since.

**Location:** 52.5171, 13.3850

**Categories:** Berlin, Railways, Engineering, Nazi Germany, Second World War, Cold War, Germany

## Introduction

Ask about the tunnel under Friedrichstrasse and someone will eventually tell you that the SS flooded it in the last days of the war and drowned thousands of people sheltering inside. The story has circulated for eighty years, and it is worth being precise about, because the documented facts are grim enough without the arithmetic. Something did detonate under the Landwehrkanal on 2 May 1945, the water did fill the entire North–South tunnel and part of the U-Bahn with it, and between 80 and 130 bodies were later recovered. Most of those people had almost certainly died in the fighting, in the tunnel, before the water arrived. Who set the charges, and on whose order, has never been established.

## Fifty Years of Argument

The idea was old. Berlin finished its east–west Stadtbahn viaduct in 1882 and immediately began wondering about a north–south equivalent, but neither answer worked. An elevated line would have had to be driven through solid blocks of city. A tunnel in the age of steam would have filled with smoke.

By the end of the nineteenth century Siemens was proposing something closer to what became the U-Bahn, linking the Potsdamer Bahnhof suburban station to the Stettiner Bahnhof. In 1911 a broad competition for the future shape of Berlin produced several more schemes. The 1920s revived the argument, and electrification finally settled it: an electric railway can run in a tube. The decision came in 1933, from a new National Socialist government looking for public works to absorb the unemployed. The first earth was moved in 1934.

## Nineteen Men at the Brandenburg Gate

The route made the engineering hard. It had to cross existing underground lines, pass beneath rivers and streams, and follow the streets above it, which meant curves as tight as 150 metres and a cramped bore only 3.83 metres high and 3.43 metres wide — restrictions that still dictate which trains can use it today.

On 20 August 1935 a section of the workings collapsed near the Brandenburg Gate. Nineteen workers were killed. They were labourers taken off the unemployment rolls for a prestige project, and they are the tunnel's first documented dead. The line opened in pieces regardless: the northern stretch down to Unter den Linden in the summer of 1936, in time for the Olympic Games; Brandenburger Tor to Potsdamer Platz on 15 April 1939; Potsdamer Platz through to Schöneberg on 9 October 1939.

## The Wrong Architecture for the Regime

Richard Brademann designed most of the stations — Bornholmer Strasse, Humboldthain, Oranienburger Strasse, Unter den Linden, Potsdamer Platz, Anhalter Bahnhof — and what he produced is one of the odder survivals of the period. These are New Objectivity rooms: flat white opaque glass panelling, clean sans-serif signage, no columns pretending to be Roman, none of the granite bombast the regime was building above ground at exactly the same moment. The style had been officially disowned. It went in anyway.

The exceptions are small and pointed. At the northern end of Potsdamer Platz, in the distribution hall whose exit served the New Reich Chancellery, there is a mosaic emblem that leaves no doubt about the decade. Otherwise the tunnel reads as if the modernists never left.

## 2 May 1945

By the last week of April 1945 the tunnel was a shelter and, then, part of the battlefield. The fighting for central Berlin ran through it, and people died in it before anything was blown up.

Then the roof gave way under the Landwehrkanal and the canal came in. Water ran the length of the tunnel and, through the connecting passage at Friedrichstrasse, into part of the U-Bahn as well. Accounts of what followed are, in the words of the historians who have tried to reconcile them, extraordinarily divergent. One theory holds that SS men set the charges, which would have required detailed knowledge of the structure; there are very few eyewitnesses who can confirm it. Others recall that the tunnel was already partly flooded with fresh water and sewage leaking from broken mains. What is documented is the recovery of between 80 and 130 bodies, and the judgement of researchers that these were most likely people killed in the final days of combat rather than drowned. The figure of thousands has never been supported by evidence. It has simply been repeated.

## Ghost Stations and Fraktur

Repairs began at once, but the tunnel was not continuously passable again until 15 November 1947. Then the city was cut in half. From 1961 every station in East Berlin was closed except Friedrichstrasse, which became a border crossing; West Berlin trains ran through the darkened platforms of the others without stopping, past armed guards, for the better part of three decades. No work was permitted on the tunnel during the division.

The stations came back one at a time: Oranienburger Strasse on 2 July 1990, Nordbahnhof and Unter den Linden on 1 September, Bornholmer Strasse — which straddled the old sector boundary — on 22 December. A full renovation from 1991 finally stripped out the last of the 1945 flood damage, and Potsdamer Platz reopened on 1 March 1992. Restorers have argued about the details ever since: the reproduction lettering is not the original Tannenberg Fraktur, and the letters stand proud where Brademann had them etched flush. Since 2009 the Unter den Linden station has been called Brandenburger Tor. The old name tiles are still on the wall.

## Flight Context

The tunnel's midpoint sits at 52.517°N 13.385°E, roughly beneath the Brandenburg Gate. There is nothing to see from the air — that is the point of it — but the alignment is easy to trace: from Gesundbrunnen and Bornholmer Strasse in the north, south through Nordbahnhof and Oranienburger Strasse, under the Spree, past Friedrichstrasse station, then beneath the Brandenburg Gate and Potsdamer Platz before running out to Yorckstrasse and Südkreuz in the south. From 2,000–3,000 ft AGL the surface markers line up as a chain: the roof of Hauptbahnhof, the Reichstag and its glass dome, the Brandenburg Gate at the head of Unter den Linden, and the Potsdamer Platz tower cluster a kilometre south of the gate. The Landwehrkanal — where the charge went off in 1945 — is the narrow waterway running east–west about 1.5 km south of the gate, parallel to the Spree. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is roughly 20 km south-southeast; Schönhagen (EDAZ) lies southwest and Strausberg (EDAY) east. The Reichstag sits inside a restricted zone and the whole area lies under the Berlin CTR and TMA, so transit under clearance. Winter here means low stratus and short daylight; summer evenings give long raking light down the Unter den Linden axis.
