# Berlin Crisis of 1961

In a single August night, East German troops sealed off West Berlin with barbed wire, splitting families and beginning twenty-eight years of the Berlin Wall.

**Location:** 52.5200, 13.3800

**Categories:** history, Cold War, Berlin, Berlin Wall, 1960s, East Germany

## Introduction

By the summer of 1961, more than 2.5 million East Germans had walked west through Berlin. They came on the S-Bahn carrying single suitcases. They came in working clothes pretending to be commuters. Doctors, engineers, machinists — the people East Germany could least afford to lose. Walter Ulbricht's republic was bleeding talent into West Berlin, and Ulbricht had finally convinced Nikita Khrushchev that the bleeding had to stop. On Saturday, August 12, the East German leadership held a garden party at a guesthouse in Döllnsee, north of the city. At the party, Ulbricht signed an order. By dawn the next morning, Berlin was cut in two.

## The Vienna Summit

Two months earlier, John F. Kennedy and Khrushchev had met in Vienna. Kennedy was 44, four months into his presidency, and still bruised from the Bay of Pigs disaster. Khrushchev had come to test him. The Soviet leader renewed the demand he had first made of Eisenhower in 1958: a separate peace treaty with East Germany, the withdrawal of Western forces from Berlin, the reduction of the divided city to a 'free' status under effective Soviet control. Kennedy did not concede. He flew home shaken and told the New York Times's James Reston that Khrushchev had 'beat the hell out of me.' On July 25, in a televised speech, he asked Congress for an extra $3.25 billion in defense spending and tripled the draft. 'We seek peace,' he said, 'but we shall not surrender.'

## August 13, Sunday Morning

At midnight East German border police, Volksarmee soldiers, and Soviet troops began closing the 156 kilometers of border surrounding the three Western sectors. By dawn the streets were torn up to block vehicles. Barbed wire ran where pavement had been the night before. Approximately 32,000 troops worked through the night. A Berliner who left for work that Saturday on the wrong side of the soon-to-be-Wall could not return home. Children were separated from grandparents. Spouses were separated from spouses. A nurse named Ida Siekmann would jump from her fourth-floor window on Bernauer Strasse eight days later, trying to escape; she became the first Wall fatality. She would not be the last.

## Tanks at Checkpoint Charlie

By late October the wire had become concrete, and a dispute over identity checks for Allied diplomats nearly turned a stalemate into a war. On October 27, General Lucius Clay — Kennedy's personal envoy in Berlin and the architect of the 1948 airlift — sent ten M48 tanks, some fitted with bulldozer blades, up to the Friedrichstrasse crossing. Within minutes 33 Soviet T-54s arrived at the Brandenburg Gate. Ten of those rolled forward to face the Americans across about 80 meters of asphalt. Both sides loaded live ammunition. Both sides raised alert levels — first the Berlin garrison, then NATO, then Strategic Air Command. For sixteen hours, the Cold War's hottest moment in Europe sat idling on a single street.

## Backchannel to Resolution

Khrushchev and Kennedy talked through Georgi Bolshakov, a GRU officer in Washington who had become Robert Kennedy's quiet line to the Kremlin. Kennedy offered a deal: if the Soviets pulled their tanks back first, he would moderate American posture in Berlin going forward. Khrushchev agreed. On the morning of October 28, a Soviet tank crept five meters backward. An American tank matched it. One pair at a time, the armor withdrew. 'It's not a very nice solution,' Kennedy reportedly said of the Wall, 'but a wall is a hell of a lot better than a war.' He was right about the war. He was speaking from a balcony 6,000 kilometers from the families now divided indefinitely.

## Twenty-Eight Years

The Wall would stand for 10,316 days. Approximately 140 people died trying to cross it — shot, drowned in the Spree, killed in tunnel collapses or while being pulled back from the death strip. The number of attempted escapes ran into the thousands. The number of separated families is uncountable. When the Wall opened on November 9, 1989, after a confused press conference by an East German official named Günter Schabowski, Berliners who had been children in August 1961 walked through the same checkpoints as middle-aged adults to embrace siblings they had last seen as teenagers. The crisis of 1961 was, in retrospect, the moment the Cold War in Europe froze into shape. The thaw would take a generation.

## Flight Context

The flashpoint at Checkpoint Charlie is at 52.508°N, 13.390°E on Friedrichstrasse in central Berlin. The line of the former Wall runs roughly north-south through the city; from cruising altitude it can still be traced as a band of newer construction and the surviving 'Death Strip' parks. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is 18 km southeast. Tegel (EDDT, closed 2020) handled most West Berlin air traffic during the crisis. Look for the Brandenburg Gate just west of the Tiergarten and follow Friedrichstrasse south to find the checkpoint replica.
