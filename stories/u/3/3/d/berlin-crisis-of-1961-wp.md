# Berlin Crisis of 1961

Khrushchev spent three years demanding the West leave Berlin; the year he finally forced the issue ended with American and Soviet tanks aimed at each other across eighty metres of Friedrichstraße.

**Location:** 52.5200, 13.3800

**Categories:** history, Cold War, Berlin, Berlin Wall, 1960s, East Germany

## Introduction

Behind drawn blinds at the American ambassador's residence in Vienna, in June 1961, John F. Kennedy told a New York Times reporter that he had just endured the roughest two days of his life. "He just beat the hell out of me," the president said of Nikita Khrushchev. James Reston printed the substance and kept the source anonymous. Kennedy was four months into the job and still carrying the Bay of Pigs; Khrushchev, he thought, had come to Vienna to find out what a man who made that kind of mistake was worth in a confrontation. The subject was Berlin, and the answer arrived ten weeks later in barbed wire.

## Six Months

The crisis had been running since November 1958, when Khrushchev gave the Western allies six months to settle the German question on his terms. Sign a peace treaty with both German states, he said, or the Soviet Union would sign a separate one with East Germany and hand it control of every road, rail line and air corridor into the city. West Berlin would become a demilitarised "free city" with the Western garrisons gone. The ultimatum failed because the allies did the unglamorous things that make ultimatums fail: they kept their troops in place, refused to recognise East Germany, and kept talking. Eisenhower and Khrushchev met at Camp David in 1959 and settled nothing while lowering the temperature. A Paris summit was set for May 1960 to finish the job; days before it opened, Soviet air defences shot down a U-2 flown by Francis Gary Powers, Eisenhower declined to apologise, and Khrushchev walked out before it began.

## Vienna, and the Speech That Followed

Both men arrived in Vienna wanting something the other could not give. Kennedy needed to look like a man of decision after Cuba; Khrushchev needed a win to show hardliners in Moscow and, increasingly, Mao Zedong in Beijing. The summit produced what one assessment called an almost total lack of substantive agreement. Afterwards Kennedy narrowed American policy to three essentials, all confined to the western half of the city: Western troops in West Berlin, the viability of the western sectors, and Western access to them. Whatever happened in the east was, by omission, not worth a war. On 25 July he took that position to television, asking Congress for $3.25 billion more in military spending, six new Army divisions, a tripled draft and a call-up of reserves. "We seek peace," he said, "but we shall not surrender."

## Everyone Knew

What is striking about August 1961 is how little of it was secret. East Germany had been stockpiling building materials in plain sight. Between 4 and 7 August the foreign ministers of the United States, Britain, France and West Germany met privately in Paris and discovered a shared unwillingness to fight over the border — a conclusion the KGB described to Khrushchev within weeks. On 6 August a functionary inside the ruling Socialist Unity Party handed American military intelligence the correct start date, and at the Berlin Watch Committee meeting on 9 August the chief of the US Military Liaison Mission predicted a wall outright. On Saturday 12 August, at a garden party in the woods at Döllnsee, Walter Ulbricht signed the order; roughly 32,000 troops closed 156 kilometres of border overnight. Kennedy did not move against it. On 30 August he called 148,000 Guardsmen and reservists to active duty instead.

## Sixteen Hours on Friedrichstraße

The wire became concrete, and the war nearly started over paperwork. East Berlin police had begun demanding identity documents from Allied personnel crossing at Friedrichstraße, which the Americans read as a unilateral rewrite of four-power rights. Soviet commandant Colonel Solovyev put the counter-argument in writing on 25 October and closed it with a warning that reads better now than it can have felt then: "We have tanks too." Two days later an American diplomat drove for the boundary again behind an escort of ten jeeps, and General Lucius Clay sent ten M48 tanks, some fitted with bulldozer blades, up to the checkpoint. Thirty-three Soviet T-54s reached the Brandenburg Gate almost immediately. Lieutenant Vern Pike, ordered to confirm they were Soviet rather than East German, walked over during a gap in the guard, climbed inside one and came out holding a Red Army newspaper. Ten of those tanks rolled down to Friedrichstraße and stopped within a hundred metres of the crossing. The Americans matched them, both sides carrying live ammunition under standing orders, and alert levels rose from the Berlin garrison to NATO and finally to Strategic Air Command.

## Five Metres

The way out ran through Georgi Bolshakov, a GRU officer in Washington who had become Robert Kennedy's quiet line to the Kremlin. Kennedy's offer was simple: pull back first, and the United States would go easier over Berlin in future. On the morning of 28 October a Soviet tank reversed about five metres, an American tank did the same, and pair by pair the armour unwound. Dean Rusk had already told Clay what Washington actually believed — "We had long since decided that Berlin is not a vital interest which would warrant determined recourse to force" — and Clay, who thought bulldozing sections of the Wall would have ended the crisis in the West's favour, was back in the United States by May 1962. Kennedy's own verdict was blunter. "It's not a very nice solution," he said of the Wall, "but a wall is a hell of a lot better than a war." The line drawn that August held for twenty-eight years.

## Flight Context

The flashpoint at Checkpoint Charlie is at 52.508°N, 13.390°E on Friedrichstraße in central Berlin. The line of the former Wall runs roughly north–south through the city; from 2,000–4,000 ft it can still be traced as a band of newer construction and surviving strips of open parkland. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) lies about 18 km southeast. Tegel (EDDT, closed 2020) handled most West Berlin air traffic during the crisis, and Tempelhof (EDDI, closed 2008) sits 4 km south of the checkpoint as an unmistakable bare oval. Find the Brandenburg Gate at the eastern end of the Tiergarten, then follow Friedrichstraße south to the crossing. Central Berlin is controlled airspace — coordinate with EDDB approach.
