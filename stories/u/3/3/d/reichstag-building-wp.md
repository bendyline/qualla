# Reichstag building

Burned in 1933, shelled in 1945, then stranded in West Berlin as a ruin no parliament was legally allowed to use — and rebuilt in the 1990s with the Red Army's graffiti deliberately left on the walls.

**Location:** 52.5186, 13.3761

**Categories:** Berlin, Government buildings, German history, Cold War, Architecture

## Introduction

On the interior walls of Germany's parliament, in corridors that members of the Bundestag use every working day, Soviet soldiers' names are still legible in Cyrillic. They were written in May 1945, in the smoke of a building the Red Army had just taken room by room. When Norman Foster rebuilt the Reichstag half a century later, one condition was written into the brief: the traces stay. Bullet holes stay. The graffiti stays, apart from the passages that German officials and Russian diplomats agreed were too obscene for a working legislature. A reunified democracy chose to conduct its business inside the physical evidence of how its last dictatorship ended.

## Two Contests and a Palace in the Way

Germany unified in 1871 without a home for its parliament, which then spent 23 years borrowing one. An 1872 design contest drew 103 architects and produced a winner, Ludwig Bohnstedt, whose plan required a site occupied by a Polish-Prussian aristocrat's palace that nobody could pry loose. Ten years later they tried again. Two hundred architects entered, the Frankfurt architect Paul Wallot won, and this time the ground was clear. Wilhelm I laid the foundation stone on 9 June 1884 at the east side of the Königsplatz, then died in 1888, the Year of Three Emperors, without seeing what he had started. His grandson Wilhelm II, who took a considerably dimmer view of parliaments, inherited it. Wallot covered the façade in crowns and eagles and raised four corner towers for the four kingdoms that had joined at unification — Prussia, Bavaria, Saxony, Württemberg. The steel-and-glass cupola was hailed as an engineering feat. The building's mash of styles was widely mocked.

## Four Words the Kaiser Did Not Want

Dem deutschen Volke — To the German People — went up over the main façade in 1916, in the third year of a war the empire was losing, and only after Wilhelm II's efforts to block the inscription failed. He understood exactly what it claimed. Whose building was this, and whose country? Two years later the question answered itself. On 9 November 1918, hours after Wilhelm's abdication was announced and two days before the fighting stopped, Philipp Scheidemann stepped onto a Reichstag balcony and proclaimed a republic. The republic kept the name and the building. It did not keep the peace. On 13 January 1920, workers gathered outside to protest a law that would restrict their rights, and as many as 42 of them were killed — the bloodiest demonstration in German history, on the doorstep of the institution meant to speak for them.

## The Fire and the Twelve Years After

On the night of 27 February 1933, precisely four weeks after Adolf Hitler was sworn in as chancellor, the building burned. Firefighters saved the shell and little else. Marinus van der Lubbe, a Dutch council communist, was the apparent culprit, but Hitler had no use for a lone arsonist. He announced a Communist plot against the government and induced President Paul von Hindenburg to issue the Reichstag Fire Decree, suspending civil liberties and licensing what the regime called a ruthless confrontation with the Communists. Whatever lit the fire, the fire lit the path. Parliament moved down the road to the Kroll Opera House and never acted as a parliament again. The gutted Reichstag became an exhibition hall, then a fortress with its windows bricked up in 1939, then a military hospital, then a radio tube plant run by AEG.

## A Ruin With No Country

The Red Army made the Reichstag a priority in the Battle of Berlin precisely because of what it symbolised, and Yevgeny Khaldei's photograph of a Soviet flag raised over the wreck on 2 May 1945 became one of the defining images of the war's end. Afterwards the building simply sat in West Berlin, roofless and useless. On 9 September 1948, during the Soviet blockade, an enormous crowd assembled in front of it to hear Mayor Ernst Reuter: "Ihr Völker der Welt… schaut auf diese Stadt" — you peoples of the world, look upon this city. In 1956 the West German government decided to restore rather than demolish, and Paul Baumgarten's reconstruction, running from 1961 to 1971, stripped the ornament and abandoned the cupola. Then nothing. Under the Four Power Agreement the Bundestag could not formally convene in West Berlin, so Germany's grandest parliament building hosted a history exhibition and the occasional rock concert.

## Wrapped, Gutted, Returned

On 26 May 1989, the Bethke brothers landed the ultralight aircraft they had used to escape East Germany on the ground in front of the building. Six months later the Wall opened. The official reunification ceremony was held here on 3 October 1990, fireworks and all, with Helmut Kohl, Richard von Weizsäcker and Willy Brandt among those present; the next day the parliament of a single Germany assembled in the Reichstag as a symbolic act. Whether it would stay took a fierce debate and a narrow vote on 20 June 1991. Before Foster's rebuild began, Christo and Jeanne-Claude wrapped the entire building in silvery fabric in 1995 — financed by selling their own drawings and early works — and millions came to see a parliament made briefly, beautifully anonymous. Then the fabric came off, the interior came out, and on 19 April 1999 the keys were handed to Bundestag president Wolfgang Thierse.

## Flight Context

The Reichstag stands at 52.5186°N, 13.3761°E, on Platz der Republik in Tiergarten, on the left bank of the Spree. From the air it is easiest to find by working outward from the Tiergarten: the park reads as a long dark-green rectangle west of central Berlin, and the Reichstag sits at its northeastern corner where the Spree makes a sharp northward bend. The Brandenburg Gate is one short block south. Foster's glass dome catches sunlight and is lit after dark, making the roofline unusually bright for a government building. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) lies about 19 km southeast; Schönhagen (EDAZ) is roughly 35 km southwest for general aviation. Berlin's city core sits at low elevation with frequent summer haze — the crispest views come after a cold front, when the dome and the river bend read clearly from 2,000 to 4,000 feet.
