# Trudelturm

A twenty-metre tower at Adlershof where the wind blows straight up, built in the 1930s so that model aircraft could be made to spin indoors and filmed doing it.

**Location:** 52.4317, 13.5295

**Categories:** germany, berlin, aviation, engineering, architecture

## Introduction

A spinning aeroplane does not fall like a stone. It rotates around its own vertical axis with one wing stalled and the other still lifting, dropping fast in a corkscrew while the controls do very little of what the pilot expects. For the first decades of powered flight, the only reliable way to learn anything about that condition was to climb to a safe altitude, provoke it deliberately, and find out on the way down whether you could stop it. Between 1934 and 1936 the German Aviation Research Institute built a way around the problem in the Berlin district of Adlershof: a plain round tower, roughly twenty metres high, inside which the wind blew straight up.

## The Wing That Stops Flying

A spin begins with a stall. The wing stops producing lift, and if one wing stalls harder than the other the aircraft rolls into an autorotation about its own vertical axis and descends fast, nose low, turning. Getting out again is possible, but recovery depends on knowing precisely how a particular airframe behaves once it is in there, and every airframe behaves differently. Tail shape matters. Mass distribution matters. What the engineers wanted was the ability to put an aeroplane into a spin, watch it closely, change one variable, and do it again — many times over, in an afternoon, with nobody aboard. That is a laboratory problem, not a flying problem, and it needed a laboratory built around it.

## A Model That Never Lands

The solution was to stop the aircraft falling and let the air do the moving instead. A precisely built scale model was released into a column of air rising up through the tower, and the speed of that column was regulated to match the model's rate of descent exactly. The result was a machine spinning furiously and going nowhere, suspended at the height of the observation gallery for as long as the operators wanted it there, while high-speed cameras ran. Everything that had previously happened once, high above the ground and at the far end of a test pilot's nerve, now happened at eye level and could be replayed frame by frame. The findings included how to catch an aircraft lurching earthward and bring it back under control. Contemporaries at the institute called the tower, without much modesty, an absolute technical innovation.

## 1934

The Trudelturm — spin tower — was built by the Deutsche Versuchsanstalt für Luftfahrt, the German Aviation Research Institute, on the edge of the Johannisthal airfield, one of the oldest in Germany. The construction dates are not incidental. Between 1934 and 1936 the German state was rebuilding a military air force in the open, and money for aeronautical research arrived on a scale the discipline had never seen. The spin tower was one item in a much larger building programme. Immediately beside it stands its enormous sibling, a wind tunnel roughly 130 metres long from the same period, and a sound-damped engine test stand completes the group now known as the Aerodynamic Park: about 9,200 square metres of laboratory in the shape of architecture, all of it on the Berlin State Monuments List.

## Zum Trudelturm

The internals are gone. Fans, ducting, the observation rig — all stripped out, so that what stands today is essentially a very well-made empty chimney with a staircase wound up the outside. That has not diminished it. The ground beneath belongs to Humboldt University's Adlershof campus, which sits inside WISTA, the science and technology park that has been spreading across roughly 420 hectares of the old airfield since 1992. Photonics laboratories and start-up leases now occupy the fields where Germany's first aviators taxied out. Since 2005 the footpath connecting Max-Born-Straße to Brook-Taylor-Straße has carried the name Zum Trudelturm — to the spin tower — which is a very Berlin way of honouring a monument: name the path that goes past it.

## Six Men Coming Down the Stairs

The tower has had a long second career as a backdrop, and the reason is legible the moment you see it. Round, grey, windowless, ribbed, with an exposed staircase spiralling up one flank, it reads on camera as a structure from no identifiable period and no obvious purpose. In 2005 the science-fiction film Æon Flux, set four hundred years in the future, sent Charlize Theron running up those outside stairs. In 2022 Rammstein put the building on the cover of Zeit, the six band members descending the same staircase in a single file, photographed by the Canadian musician Bryan Adams. Neither production had the slightest interest in aerodynamics. Both wanted a shape that looked left behind by a civilisation nobody could quite place, and Adlershof had one, standing quietly empty since the wind stopped.

## Flight Context

The Trudelturm stands at 52.4317° N, 13.5295° E in Adlershof, in Berlin's south-east, about 13 km from the city centre. From the air the tower itself is a poor target — twenty metres is nothing — so navigate by the ground around it: the wide open green of the former Johannisthal airfield lies immediately north-west, an unmistakable clear oval in an otherwise dense suburban grid, with the Aerodynamic Park on its south-eastern edge beside the long low bulk of the 130-metre wind tunnel. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) sits about 7 km south-south-west, so this is busy terminal airspace requiring coordination; the Fernsehturm bears north-west at roughly 13 km and anchors the view. Best seen from 1,000–2,000 ft AGL in slant morning or evening light, when the external staircase throws a hard diagonal shadow across the tower's flank. For general aviation, Schönhagen (EDAZ) lies about 36 km south-west and Strausberg (EDAY) about 31 km north-east.
