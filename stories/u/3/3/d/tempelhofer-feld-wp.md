# Tempelhofer Feld

A Prussian drill ground that became an airport, an arms factory worked by forced labourers, the lifeline of a blockaded city, and finally 355 hectares of grass that Berliners went to the ballot box to keep empty.

**Location:** 52.4733, 13.4014

**Categories:** Berlin, Urban parks, Aviation history, Cold War, Civic activism

## Introduction

On 3 November 1897, a short, fat, pencil-shaped craft made entirely of metal lifted off a drill field south of Berlin. It was the world's first rigid airship, designed by the Hungarian inventor David Schwarz and pushed along by a 12-horsepower Daimler engine. It flew, briefly, then came down hard and was destroyed. The pilot, Ernst Jagels, walked away with minor injuries. The field went on doing this — hosting whatever the century needed next, then discarding it. Parade ground, aerodrome, arms plant, lifeline of a besieged city. What sits there now is 355 hectares of open grass in the middle of a housing-starved capital, and the people of Berlin have voted to keep it that way.

## Where the King Watched His Army

Schöneberg farmers worked this ground until the 18th century, when the Prussian state found a better use for a large flat space beside a garrison town. From 1722, under Frederick William I, the Großes Feld — the Great Field — became a parade and drill ground and a manoeuvring area for the Prussian army. For the better part of two centuries, Berlin's answer to a state occasion was to march several thousand men across it. On 2 August 1881 the Hawaiian king Kalākaua stood as a guest at one of these parades, a monarch from the middle of the Pacific watching Prussia perform itself on a plain outside Berlin. The drilling carried on until the spring of 1914, which is a tidy way of saying it stopped when it stopped being rehearsal.

## The Terminal That Swallowed the Grass

Work to convert the field into an airport began in 1922, and the first buildings were finished by 1928. In 1934 Ernst Sagebiel drew up an expansion on a scale that had little to do with air traffic, and the new terminal went up between 1936 and 1941, its frontage arcing around the apron like a drawn bow. From 1940 the building was given over entirely to the arms industry, including the assembly and maintenance of Ju 87 dive bombers. That work needed hands, and the hands were taken. Thousands of forced labourers were brought from across occupied Europe, and their camps and accommodation stood on the field itself — people seized from their own countries, sleeping on the same ground where Berliners now fly kites and learn to skate.

## Every Ninety Seconds

Flight operations resumed after the war, and in June 1948 the Soviet Union cut the land routes into West Berlin. What followed made Tempelhof one of the most consequential airfields on earth. Through the blockade of 1948 and 1949, supply aircraft came in low over the tenements of Neukölln sometimes as often as every ninety seconds, near enough for residents to make out the crews. A city of two million was fed by aeroplane for the better part of a year. Afterwards Tempelhof slowly became surplus. Civilian traffic moved to Tegel and the field closed to it in 1970, reopened in 1985, and shut for good in 2008 to clear the way for Berlin Brandenburg — an airport that then took another twelve years to open.

## One Hundred and Two Arrests

The CDU and FDP tried to stop the closure with a referendum, which failed for want of public support. Then the opposite fight began. The Senate had promised that once the aircraft were gone the space would be opened to the public, and by June 2009 it had not been. Several thousand activists organised under the name Squat Tempelhof and announced they would walk in and take it. Residents, tenants' alliances and the Green party joined them. Roughly 1,500 police officers were deployed to prevent the occupation, and 102 demonstrators were arrested. The field opened as a public park on 8 May 2010 regardless. Around 235,000 people came that first weekend, onto what is now the largest inner-city open space in the world and, by a distance, Berlin's biggest park.

## The Argument That Won't Stay Settled

The proposals arrived almost at once: an international garden exhibition, a state library, housing along the edges. In September 2011 a citizens' action group called 100% Tempelhofer Feld formed to stop all of it. They announced a petition in autumn 2012, began gathering signatures that December, and on 25 May 2014 carried a referendum with 64.3 percent of the vote — a law forbidding state, residential and commercial building on the field. A decade later the question came back. In June 2024 the Berlin Senate voted to prepare the ground for housing and social infrastructure at the edges, followed by an ideas competition and a series of citizens' dialogues; a randomly selected dialogue workshop in September 2024 came down clearly against building. For now the runways stay empty, and nothing about that is accidental.

## Flight Context

Tempelhofer Feld lies at 52.4733°N, 13.4014°E, roughly 5 km south of Mitte between the Neukölln and Tempelhof districts. From the air it is unmistakable: a pale oval of grass punched out of dense city grid, with two parallel former runways still marked on it and the long curved bar of the old terminal closing off the western side along Tempelhofer Damm. Nothing else in inner Berlin reads like it. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is about 14 km southeast, and Schönhagen (EDAZ) roughly 30 km southwest. The field sits on the Teltow plateau, slightly higher than the Spree valley to the north, and the contrast between its emptiness and the surrounding rooftops is sharpest in low morning or evening light.
