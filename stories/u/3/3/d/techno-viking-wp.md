# Techno Viking

Four minutes filmed on a Berlin street in 2000 became one of the first great internet memes - and then its subject went to court and won back the rights to his own face.

**Location:** 52.5261, 13.4037

**Categories:** Internet culture, Popular culture, Berlin, Music, Law

## Introduction

A man is dancing in the middle of Rosenthaler Strasse when someone barges into the crowd and grabs a woman with blue hair. What happens next takes about four seconds. A bare-chested man with a blond braid, a beard and a Thor's-hammer pendant steps in, takes the intruder by both arms, walks him back the way he came, and levels a finger at him - stern, unhurried, entirely without violence. Then he turns, accepts a bottle of water that a stranger holds out to him upside down, drinks, and dances off down the street. The video artist Matthias Fritsch was filming at knee height and called the piece Kneecam No. 1. It was 8 July 2000, and the man in the frame had about one year of anonymity left.

## The Parade That Was Against Parades

The event was the Fuckparade, which began in 1997 under the name Hateparade - a counter-demonstration mounted by Berlin's hardcore and gabber crowd against the Love Parade's slide into sponsorship, and against what its organisers saw as commercial promoters abusing Germany's right of assembly. It changed its name a year later to avoid misunderstanding. Where the Love Parade filled the Tiergarten with a million people and branded trucks, the Fuckparade moved a few thousand through Mitte behind faster, harder music and no sponsors at all.

Fritsch shot four minutes of it. He put the clip online in 2001, genuinely curious whether viewers would read the confrontation as real or staged. That was the whole intent: an experimental artist, an ambiguous street scene, an audience invited to decide.

## Ten Million Views Before YouTube

The video went out into an internet that had no vocabulary for what happened to it. YouTube would not exist for another four years. The clip travelled by email, by forum link, and then through the video site Break.com, where at its peak it was drawing more than a million views a day and was watched over ten million times in six months.

Viewers gave the man a name he never chose. More than 700 responses and re-edits appeared. Somebody painted him in oils; he topped a British clip-show countdown; Weezer and the director Mathew Cullen wanted him for the "Pork and Beans" video, a wall-to-wall procession of meme figures, and could not get him. By mid-2010 the original had cleared 20 million views on YouTube alone. He had become one of the founding characters of internet culture without ever agreeing to appear in it.

## The Man Who Declined

Meanwhile people set about identifying him, in public and wrongly. One YouTube upload named the wrong man outright. In 2008 fans decided he was the mixed martial artist Keith Jardine. He was not. Through a lawyer, the man stated his position plainly: he had never been a public figure, and he had no wish to become one.

It is worth sitting with how unusual that is. An entire generation online knew his build, his braid and his walk, and he has never once traded on any of it - no interview, no appearance, no book, no name.

In January 2013 he took Fritsch to court in Berlin over his personality rights. German law recognises a right to one's own image, and the fact that footage was shot in a public street does not by itself license its commercial exploitation. That June the court found for him. Fritsch was ordered to pay 13,000 euros in damages - close to everything he had made from advertising and merchandise - plus 10,000 euros in costs, and to stop publishing the man's image. Blurring the face would not do, the court held, because he remained recognisable by his physique.

## What the Court Left Alone

The ruling drew a line that has held up rather well. Fritsch, who had filmed the man and profited from him, could no longer show him. The vast secondary sprawl - the remixes, cartoons, animated GIFs, statuettes and fan art made by strangers who had never met either party - was left standing as free expression. One person's commercial use of another person's face is a matter for a judge. A culture making jokes out of an image it already shares is not.

Fritsch crowdfunded a documentary about the whole affair, The Story of Technoviking, released in 2015, and cannot show the footage that made it necessary. The original clip survives in a thousand copies nobody controls, which is the trick the internet plays on everyone caught in it. Somewhere in Germany is a man who walked down Rosenthaler Strasse on a summer afternoon, stepped between a stranger and the person who had grabbed her, drank some water, and then spent thirteen years getting his face back. He won.

## Flight Context

52.5261 degrees N, 13.4037 degrees E, on Rosenthaler Strasse in Berlin-Mitte, a few hundred metres northwest of Alexanderplatz. From the air the street runs as a slight diagonal through an otherwise rectilinear block pattern, linking Hackescher Markt and the elevated S-Bahn viaduct in the southeast with Rosenthaler Platz to the northwest; the viaduct arcs are the clearest visual anchor, with the Spree and Museum Island a short distance south. The Fernsehturm at Alexanderplatz stands about 900 metres east-southeast and is visible from any altitude in clear weather. Central Berlin is dense controlled airspace with restricted zones over the government quarter to the west, so treat this as a cleared transit rather than a sightseeing orbit.
