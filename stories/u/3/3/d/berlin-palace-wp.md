# Berlin Palace

Built in 1443, blown up by the GDR in 1950, and reconstructed seventy years later, Berlin's royal residence has had two demolitions and one resurrection.

**Location:** 52.5175, 13.4028

**Categories:** palaces, architecture, Berlin, history, Hohenzollern, reconstruction

## Introduction

It took four months and nineteen tons of dynamite to bring down the Berlin Palace in 1950. The damage from Allied bombs five years earlier had been bad — roof gone, much of the interior burned out — but the building was structurally sound and could have been restored, as much of central Berlin's historic fabric eventually was. Walter Ulbricht did not want it restored. The new Socialist Unity Party leader announced the demolition in July 1950, calling the palace a symbol of Prussian militarism. So solid was the construction that the dome and its mount remained intact even after the rest of the building had collapsed. They had to bring it down separately.

## Five Hundred Years of Hohenzollern

The palace's foundations go back to 1443, when Frederick II 'Irontooth' of Brandenburg laid the first stone of a fortified residence on Cölln island, partly to remind the citizens of Berlin who their margraves were. It grew through five centuries of additions: the Renaissance reworking of 1538 under Joachim II; the great Baroque expansion begun in 1699 by Andreas Schlüter for the new King Frederick I of Prussia; the western extension by Eosander von Göthe; the dome added in 1845 by Friedrich August Stüler from Karl Friedrich Schinkel's design. By the time Wilhelm I was crowned the first German Emperor in 1871, the palace was the symbolic heart of an empire — even if the new Reichstag building down the street had become the actual center of power.

## Two Republics from One Balcony

On 9 November 1918, with the Kaiser's abdication a few hours old and the German Empire collapsing, Karl Liebknecht stepped onto a balcony of the palace and proclaimed a German socialist republic. Hours earlier, Philipp Scheidemann of the Social Democrats had proclaimed a parliamentary republic from a balcony of the Reichstag. The competing announcements, separated by a few hundred meters, were the first signal of the civil-war politics that would convulse Germany for the next fifteen years. Liebknecht would be murdered ten weeks later by Freikorps soldiers. The balcony from which he spoke was the only fragment of the original palace the GDR preserved. In 1964 it was bolted onto the new State Council Building a few blocks away, where it still serves as the entrance.

## The Palace of the Republic

In place of the demolished palace, the East German government left a parade ground called Marx-Engels-Platz. Then, between 1973 and 1976, Erich Honecker's government built the Palast der Republik on the site — a long bronze-mirrored modernist box that housed the East German parliament, a bowling alley, two large concert halls, thirteen restaurants, and a chandelier of 1,000 lamps that Berliners called Erichs Lampenladen, Erich's lamp shop. Many East Berliners loved it. They got married there. They saw their first concerts there. After reunification, asbestos in the structure forced its closure, and after years of debate the German parliament voted in 2003 to demolish it too. The second demolition took longer than the first, partly because of the asbestos and partly because removing it threatened the foundations of the Berliner Dom across the street.

## Reconstruction, Argument, Reopening

From 2013 to 2020, on the empty footprint, the Italian architect Franco Stella oversaw a €590 million reconstruction of the palace's three Baroque facades and original courtyard, wrapped around a wholly modern interior. The east face, toward the Spree, is contemporary glass and stone. The argument that drove the rebuilding was bitter. Critics called it a Disney pastiche; advocates argued that the historic ensemble of Museum Island made no sense without the palace's mass to balance it. The interior houses the Humboldt Forum — a vast complex of museums, restaurants, theatres, and lecture halls. Its opening collections of African and other non-European art arrived under their own controversy: many had been taken during the colonial era, and several have since been repatriated. The dome was crowned with a cross in May 2020, prompting another round of public debate over what the rebuilt building should mean.

## What Stands There Now

The last of the 19 sandstone statues on the balustrade was set in place in June 2025, completing the reconstruction begun more than a decade earlier. The reproduced bronze reliefs of the Eosander Portal — destroyed with the rest in 1950 — were back on the building in 2022. The palace is now once again among the largest in the world, although it serves none of its original functions. No king lives in it. No emperor rules from it. No socialist parliament debates inside it. It is a museum about how Germany sees itself, built where the building that asked that question for five centuries was twice taken down. Visitors enter through a courtyard called Schlüterhof and emerge in galleries of objects gathered from every part of the world, in a city that has spent a century arguing about which parts of its past to keep.

## Flight Context

The reconstructed palace stands at 52.518°N, 13.403°E on the eastern end of Museum Island in central Berlin, directly across from the Berlin Cathedral. From cruising altitude the building appears as a roughly square Baroque structure with a green dome at its center and the Spree wrapping around two sides. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is 19 km southeast. The Lustgarten green space is to the north, the Friedrichsbrücke crossings to the east. Schlossplatz, the open square in front, marks the western approach.
