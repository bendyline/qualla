# Chris Gueffroy

A twenty-year-old waiter from Treptow was shot on the last fence in February 1989, nine months before the Wall came down — the final person killed by gunfire at the Berlin Wall.

**Location:** 52.4589, 13.4690

**Categories:** berlin, berlin-wall, cold-war, modern-history, germany, memorial

## Introduction

Karin Gueffroy heard the shots. It was the night of 5 February 1989, bitterly cold, and the sound carried across Treptow from the direction of the canal, not far from her flat in East Berlin. She thought little of it; gunfire near the border was not extraordinary in that city. Two days later she called on one of her son's friends and learned that Chris had been planning to leave. That same evening the Stasi collected her for what they described as a clarification of circumstances. Somewhere in the hours of questioning that followed, she understood that her twenty-year-old son was dead.

## The Waiter Who Could Not Leave

Chris Gueffroy was born on 21 June 1968 in Pasewalk, a small town in what was then Bezirk Neubrandenburg, in the northern flatlands of East Germany. He trained in gastronomy and worked as a waiter — a job that paid better than most and left him more latitude than the average twenty-year-old had. What he could not stomach was the corruption that ran through the restaurant trade, the small daily transactions everybody accepted and nobody named. His friend Christian Gaudian, whom he had met at gastronomy school, felt the same. Neither had a grievance a court would have recognised. What weighed on Chris was simpler and heavier: the certainty that the shape of his life had already been decided for him, and that no year would ever come in which he got to choose where he lived. In mid-January 1989 he learned he was to be conscripted into the National People's Army that May. Within days the two of them had settled on leaving.

## Two Things They Believed That Were Not True

The plan rested on two assumptions, and both were wrong. Word had reached them that the Schießbefehl — the standing order to shoot anyone crossing the border — had been quietly rescinded. It had not. They had also heard that the Swedish prime minister Ingvar Carlsson was in East Berlin on a state visit, and reasoned that the guards would not fire while a Western head of government was in the city. Carlsson had already flown home. On the night of 5 February they made for the Britzer Zweigkanal, roughly two kilometres from the flat on Südostallee where Chris was living. They got over the inner wall and past the signal fence without being seen. Only the last metal lattice fence now stood between the two of them and the Neukölln side.

## The Fence Closest to the Border

They were spotted on that fence. Two patrols converged from either side; there were shouted warnings, then warning shots, then aimed fire. Chris Gueffroy was hit twice in the chest and died in the border strip, metres short of the West. Christian Gaudian, badly wounded, was arrested and on 24 May 1989 sentenced by the Pankow district court to three years' imprisonment for attempted illegal border-crossing in an aggravated case. He was released on bail that September and transferred to West Berlin on 17 October, three weeks before the crossings opened. Chris was the last person shot dead at the Berlin Wall. One more would die attempting it — Winfried Freudenberg, who fell from a homemade gas balloon over West Berlin on 8 March, a month later.

## One Hundred and Fifty Marks

The four border guards involved were decorated for it. Erich Wöllner, who commanded the Grenzkommando Mitte, awarded each of them the border troops' service medal and a prize of 150 East German Marks. Three years later the same four men stood in a Berlin courtroom. The presiding judge, Theodor Seidel, acquitted two of them — Mike Schmidt and Peter Schmett, by then a millwright and an electrician, both fathers of young children — on the finding that they "did not kill and did not intend to kill." Andreas Kuehnpast received a suspended sentence of two years. Ingo Heinrich, whose bullet struck Chris in the heart, was sentenced to three and a half years in prison, and in 1994 the Federal Court of Justice reduced that on appeal to two years, suspended. No one served a day for the last shooting at the Berlin Wall.

## His Mother's Case

On 12 January 1990, with the border open and the East German state coming apart around her, Karin Gueffroy filed a criminal complaint against persons unknown. She pressed for the case to be heard, sat through the trials, and has talked about her son publicly ever since. "All of those who died removed a brick," she has said, "and that finally resulted in the fall of the Wall." His name is inscribed in three places across the city: among the white crosses beside the Reichstag, in the Window of Remembrance at the Berlin Wall Memorial on Bernauer Straße, and on a stele raised in 2003 beside the Britzer Zweigkanal, a few hundred metres from where he was shot. A road near the water now carries his name. Stand at the stele and look the way he was running and there is nothing dramatic to see — canal, trees, low buildings, the ordinary outskirts of a city. That was what he was climbing towards.

## Flight Context

The memorial stele stands at 52.459°N, 13.469°E on the Britzer Zweigkanal in the Treptow-Köpenick borough of southeast Berlin, about 9 km southeast of Mitte. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) lies 10 km southeast, so approaches to BER's westerly runways pass almost overhead at circuit altitude. From 2,000–3,000 ft the canal reads as a straight dark line running southwest to northeast, with the wooded Königsheide to the north and the disused Johannisthal airfield — now a nature reserve — just beyond it. The old border ran along the canal here; the Berliner Mauerweg cycle path traces it, visible as a pale thread beside the water.
