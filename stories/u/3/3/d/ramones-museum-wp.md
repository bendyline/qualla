# Ramones Museum

The band that invented the sound of Queens has its museum four thousand miles away in Berlin, because one of the four grew up here.

**Location:** 52.5108, 13.4670

**Categories:** Berlin, Museums, Music, Punk rock, Ramones, Germany

## Introduction

There is no obvious reason for a museum of the Ramones to be in Germany. The band is inseparable from New York — Forest Hills, Queens, the Bowery, four men in the same jacket and the same surname, none of them related. Yet a collection of their jackets, set lists, gear and promotional junk sits in Friedrichshain, in the eastern half of a city they mostly played as visitors. The reason is Dee Dee. Douglas Glenn Colvin was born in Fort Lee, Virginia in 1951, to an American soldier and a German mother, and the army sent the family to West Berlin while he was still an infant. He lived here until he was fifteen.

## The Ramone Who Was Actually From Here

Fifteen years is not a detail; it is a childhood. Dee Dee Ramone learned the city before he learned New York, and when he finally left for Forest Hills with his mother and sister — getting away from his father's drinking — he arrived in Queens as a boy who had grown up somewhere else entirely.

Germany and Berlin kept surfacing in the band's songs afterwards, and never more plainly than at the end. "Born to Die in Berlin," on the last Ramones studio album, has Dee Dee singing the bridge in German. The line between a punk song and a homesick one is thinner than the genre likes to admit, and that track sits right on it — a man who spent a career being the loudest American in the room, singing in the language of the place he came from.

## A Thousand Things in One Flat

The collection began the way most collections do: one person, no plan, gradually running out of room. Flo Hayler accumulated Ramones material for years and kept it in his apartment. There is a threshold past which a private hoard becomes a public problem, and Hayler crossed it.

The museum opened on 15 September 2005 with more than a thousand objects — clothing, set lists, gear and promotional material spanning the band's working life from 1975 to 1996, much of it obtained from the band members themselves rather than from dealers. That provenance is what makes the place worth the trip. These are not replicas or licensed reproductions but the actual jackets and the actual paper, gathered by somebody who cared before there was any commercial reason to.

## Four Addresses in Twenty Years

The museum has moved almost as often as the band toured. It started in Kreuzberg in 2005, went to Mitte in 2008, returned to Kreuzberg in 2017, and settled in Friedrichshain in 2024 — four addresses in twenty years, none of them for long.

The Kreuzberg premises came with a café and, more importantly, a stage. From 2009 the museum began hosting live sets, starting with an acoustic performance by Alkaline Trio. Later that year C.J. Ramone became the first actual Ramone to play there. The list of acts that followed reads like a punk-rock guest book — Against Me!, Anti-Flag, Youth Brigade, The Flatliners, Riverboat Gamblers, Dave Hause, Brian Fallon, Hop Along, Smoke or Fire, Franz Nicolay, Mikey Erg, Slingshot Dakota, Dead To Me, The Static Age, and the Australian band Jet. In 2014 the museum started putting out records of its own, beginning with a split release between the Zatopeks and Dee Cracks. A museum that presses vinyl is not really behaving like a museum, which is presumably the point.

## Gabba Gabba, Ostkreuz

What the place gets right is scale. It is small, cluttered, low-lit and run by people who are visibly not curators, and that is a far more honest setting for the Ramones than a vitrine in an institution would be. The band played fast, cheap and short on purpose. A museum built in their honour ought to feel like a room, not a wing.

And it makes an argument that a New York museum could not. Berlin was divided and garrisoned for the whole of Dee Dee's childhood here, and walled for the last five years of it — an odd place to be an American kid, and a formative one. The Ramones came out of Queens. One quarter of them came out of this.

## Flight Context

The museum sits at 52.5108°N 13.4670°E in Friedrichshain, roughly 5 km east of the Brandenburg Gate and just north of the Spree. The dominant landmark from the air is the river itself curving through the eastern inner city, with the Oberbaumbrücke — a red-brick double-deck bridge carrying the U-Bahn across the water on arches, with two pointed towers mid-span — about 1.5 km southwest. Immediately upstream of it on the north bank runs the long surviving stretch of the Berlin Wall painted as the East Side Gallery, and just beyond that stands the Mercedes-Benz Arena. The great sprawling rail junction of Ostkreuz lies a kilometre east. From 1,500–2,500 ft AGL the whole cluster — river bend, red bridge, painted wall, arena, rail junction — is unmistakable. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is about 18 km southeast, Strausberg (EDAY) 25 km east, Schönhagen (EDAZ) southwest. Berlin CTR and TMA apply; the government quarter restriction lies west of here. Note that Tempelhof and Tegel are permanently closed as airfields.
