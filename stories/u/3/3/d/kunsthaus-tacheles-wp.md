# Kunsthaus Tacheles

A condemned Wilhelmine shopping arcade on Oranienburger Straße that artists occupied two months before the wrecking crews returned, held for twenty-two years, and finally lost to a bank.

**Location:** 52.5256, 13.3886

**Categories:** Berlin, Art, Architecture, Squatting, Cold War, Nazi Germany

## Introduction

The wrecking crews had already been here once. In 1980 they took the great ribbed dome off the top of the old Friedrichstraßenpassage, closed the cinema underneath it, and left the rest of the carcass standing on Oranienburger Straße with a date pencilled in for the remainder: April 1990. On 13 February 1990, a little under two months before that date, a group calling itself the Künstlerinitiative Tacheles walked in and refused to leave. They took their name from Yiddish — tacheles reden, to talk straight, to cut to the point. It was a good name for what they were doing, which was pointing at a building East Germany had given up on and saying, out loud, that it was worth something.

## A Shopping Arcade That Failed in Six Months

It was built in 1907 and 1908 as the Friedrichstraßenpassage, directly across from the New Synagogue, at a cost of roughly seven million marks. Five storeys of reinforced concrete carried a colossal ribbed dome, the façade hung off the frame rather than holding it up — early modern engineering in Classical and Gothic dress. The arcade ran clean through the block from Friedrichstraße to Oranienburger Straße, the second largest of its kind in Berlin. Its shareholders had a clever idea: the shops inside would not be walled off from one another but allowed to bleed together, with every customer paying at one central point of sale. The idea did not survive contact with customers. Six months after opening, in August 1908, the passage filed for bankruptcy. Wolf Wertheim then rented the complex and ran a department store here from 1909 until 1914, and the building was auctioned off shortly before the First World War.

## Showroom, Then Prison

After 1928 the Allgemeine Elektricitäts-Gesellschaft moved in and the owners renamed it the Haus der Technik. AEG used more than 10,000 square metres and twenty large display cases to show off what electricity could now do for a household, and some of the earliest German television transmissions went out from these rooms in the 1930s. Then the tenants changed. Nazi party members used the building increasingly through the early 1930s; the German Labour Front set up offices for Gau Kurmark here, took ownership in 1941, and the complex became a central office for the SS. In 1943 the skylights were sealed and the ridge turrets removed so that French prisoners of war could be held in the attic. During the Battle of Berlin the second cellar was flooded on Nazi orders. It has never been drained. Whatever is down there is still under water.

## Forty Years of Not Quite Demolishing It

The Free German Trade Union Federation took the building over in 1948 and then simply let it decay. Bits of it stayed useful — a travel agency in the repaired passage, an artists' school, a technical college for foreign trade, army storage in the cellar. The cinema in the Friedrichstraße gateway was forced out in 1958 because the fabric had grown too dangerous, reopened under another name, and was renamed Camera again in 1972. Engineering assessments in 1969 and 1977 both recommended demolition; the building had never once been renovated despite decades of hard use, and the site was wanted for a new street connecting Oranienburger Straße to Friedrichstraße. Demolition began in 1980 and stopped halfway. For ten years the half-flattened hulk stood there, a ruin with a shortcut planned through its middle, waiting for a state that would not outlast it.

## Straight Talking

The occupiers moved fast. They tried to have the building registered as a historic place; when that failed to stop the schedule, they got the Berlin Round Table to issue a last-minute injunction. Then they commissioned a fresh engineering survey, which reported that the condemned building was in surprisingly good shape — landmark status followed, confirmed by a second survey in February 1992. What grew inside was messier than the arguments that had saved it. Under the curator Jochen Sandig the centre expanded to hold studios, a cinema, a café, workshops and performance space, with a courtyard behind it filling up with sculpture welded from rubble, scrap and dead vehicles. The Mutoid Waste Company built there; Spiral Tribe played; the choreographer Sasha Waltz worked there early. East German and West German artists argued constantly over what the place was for. In 1991 the associated housing burned down, and many suspected the fire had been set.

## One Mark, Then One Million Euros

The Fundus Group had bought the site from the Berlin government in the mid-1990s and, in no hurry, granted the artists a ten-year lease in 1998 at a nominal rent of one Deutschmark. When the extended contract expired at the end of 2009, they were squatters again — and Fundus was insolvent, so HSH Nordbank, owed money, moved to sell. An eviction scheduled for 4 April 2011 was not carried out; the next day the roughly twenty businesses of the downstairs group left peacefully in return for a million euros from an anonymous source, and a wall nearly three metres high went up between the courtyard and the street within the week. The last forty to sixty artists walked out on 4 September 2012. The metal workshop and sculpture park held on until March 2013. The property sold to a New York firm in September 2014, Herzog & de Meuron took the lead on the redevelopment, and since September 2023 the building has housed Fotografiska Berlin — a photography museum, in the shell that outlived everyone who wanted it gone.

## Flight Context

Tacheles stands at 52.526°N, 13.389°E on Oranienburger Straße in Spandauer Vorstadt, Berlin-Mitte. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is about 20 km south-southeast; Schönhagen (EDAZ) roughly 40 km southwest. From 1,500–3,000 ft, find the Fernsehturm at Alexanderplatz, then track about 2 km west-northwest: the gilded ribbed dome of the New Synagogue on Oranienburger Straße is the landmark, and the Tacheles block sits directly opposite and just behind it, running through to Friedrichstraße. The site is now a completed perimeter-block development with courtyards, so from the air it reads as new build rather than ruin. Central Berlin is low and uniformly dense here; the Spree and Museum Island lie 700 m to the south. Winter haze and low stratus are common from November through February.
