# 1760 Raid on Berlin

For three days in October 1760 an Austrian and Russian army held Frederick the Great's capital, and Berlin's council had to decide not whether to surrender but to whom.

**Location:** 52.5186, 13.4081

**Categories:** berlin, germany, seven-years-war, military-history, prussia, 18th-century, mitte

## Introduction

On 9 October 1760, with two enemy armies at the gates and no Prussian field force within reach, the Berlin city council met to decide not whether to surrender but to whom. Russians and Austrians were both outside, allied against Prussia in the Seven Years' War, and the councillors judged that the Russians — foreigners from a great distance, keen to be thought well of in Europe — would be less dangerous to the city than the Austrians, who had scores of their own to settle. So the Prussian capital formally handed itself to the Russian Empire. The Austrians came in anyway, forcing their way into large parts of the city, and Berlin spent the next three days discovering exactly how much that first decision had been worth.

## Why the Capital Was Open

1759 had gone well for the allies and 1760 had not. Their invasion of Silesia had stalled despite overwhelming numbers, and in August they were beaten at the Battle of Liegnitz. But Frederick the Great had bought his Silesian campaign by stripping the rest of his kingdom, and the French pointed out what that left exposed: a capital with almost no army in it.

There was a template. A small Austrian force had briefly occupied Berlin in October 1757 and got away with it. The 1760 plan was more ambitious. The main allied army would feint toward Guben; under cover of that, a force under Gottlob Heinrich von Tottleben would break away and drive north for Berlin, with an Austrian corps under Count von Lacy following behind. Large numbers of Cossacks and light cavalry were assigned to the operation for one reason — speed. The whole thing depended on arriving before anyone could react.

## Seydlitz Leaves His Sickbed

It nearly failed at the first attempt. Tottleben crossed the Oder with a vanguard of 5,600 Russians and tried to take the city by coup de main on 5 October, and ran into resistance he had not budgeted for. The city's governor, General Hans Friedrich von Rochow, wanted to pull out. He was overruled by circumstance in the form of Friedrich Wilhelm von Seydlitz — Prussia's finest cavalry commander, who happened to be in Berlin recovering from wounds. Seydlitz rallied roughly 2,000 defenders and drove the Russians back from the gates.

It bought days, not victory. Prince Eugene of Württemberg brought troops back from fighting the Swedes in Pomerania, a Saxon contingent arrived, and the garrison swelled to about 18,000. Then Lacy's Austrians came up and the arithmetic reversed. The Austrians took Potsdam and Charlottenburg; the Prussian defenders abandoned Berlin and fell back on Spandau, leaving the council to its choice.

## What the Occupiers Did

The Russians opened with a demand for 4 million thalers in exchange for guaranteeing private property. Johann Ernst Gotzkowsky, a prominent Berlin merchant, took over the negotiation on the city's behalf and talked Tottleben down to 1.5 million.

What followed was neither a sack nor a courtesy visit. Several districts were ransacked and several royal palaces burned; the palaces at Charlottenburg, Schönhausen and Friedrichsfelde were wrecked, chiefly by Cossacks and by Saxon and Hungarian cavalry. The military infrastructure was the deliberate target — the arsenal, the foundries and the powder mills. Some 18,000 muskets and 143 cannon were seized, parts of the foundry destroyed, captured Austrian and Russian colours recovered, and around 1,200 prisoners of war released. Frederick, characteristically, worried about his art collection; the agent who reported to him said the Russians had taken some paintings and books, but the murals and gilding were intact and only a few marble statues had been knocked over.

## And What They Did Not Do

Berlin was not put to the sword. There was no massacre of civilians, no mass killing, no general sack of the kind eighteenth-century Europe knew perfectly well how to inflict — and the restraint was policy, not luck. The Russians, represented in negotiations by First Major John O'Rourke, were consciously working on their reputation abroad and made a point of correct treatment of the inhabitants. Tottleben posted safeguards through the city and had looting soldiers shot to enforce the order. The Austrians had less appetite for restraint, holding a grievance over Prussian conduct in occupied Saxony and on Austrian soil, and it was they who did most of the plundering. Potsdam's royal palaces were spared only because Austrian officers intervened at the Long Bridge.

One group did not get the benefit of any of this. The Austrians took roughly 130 boys, eleven and twelve years old, out of the Berlin military school and carried them off to Königsberg, where they were held until the war ended three years later.

## Three Days, and the Bill

On 12 October a rumour reached the allied commanders that Frederick was marching to relieve his capital. They had what they came for, and they left — the Austrians toward Saxony, the Russians back to the main army near Frankfurt an der Oder. Frederick, learning that Berlin was clear, turned around and went back to Silesia and Saxony. Weeks later he narrowly won the Battle of Torgau.

His reaction to the occupation was to be furious with his own people for not resisting harder. Tottleben's reward was worse: accused of being a Prussian spy, tried and sentenced to death, he was pardoned by Catherine the Great. Among the Russian officers who rode in that October was a young man named Alexander Suvorov, serving with Chernyshev's forces, who would end his career as generalissimo of the Russian Empire. Berlin got off relatively lightly in 1760. In 1806 the French took the city and stayed for seven years.

## Flight Context

The events centre on 52.5186 N, 13.4081 E — the old walled city on and around the Spree island in what is now Berlin-Mitte, the ground the occupiers actually held for three days. Almost nothing of the 1760 city survives above ground, so read the geography instead: the Spree loops around Museum Island, Unter den Linden runs west from it to the Brandenburg Gate, and the Fernsehturm's ball is the unmissable modern fix over the old town. The places that suffered are spread around the compass and still identifiable from the air — Charlottenburg Palace with its dome and formal gardens about 8 km west, Schönhausen Palace in Pankow about 6 km north, Friedrichsfelde in Lichtenberg about 8 km east, Spandau's star-shaped citadel at the Havel-Spree confluence about 13 km west, and Potsdam's palace landscape about 25 km southwest. Central Berlin is dense controlled airspace; expect clearance limits and plan any low pass at 1,500-2,500 ft AGL along the Spree. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) lies about 18 km southeast, Schönhagen (EDAZ) about 39 km south-southwest. Autumn morning fog in the Spree valley is common in October and can hide the river line entirely until mid-morning.
