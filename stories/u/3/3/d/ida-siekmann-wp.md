# Ida Siekmann

A West Prussian village girl who became a Berlin nurse, a widow whose front door opened onto another country, and — nine days after the barbed wire went up — the first person the Berlin Wall killed.

**Location:** 52.5402, 13.4029

**Categories:** Berlin, Berlin Wall, Cold War, Memorials, German history

## Introduction

Every time Ida Siekmann left her flat she crossed an international border, and for years it meant nothing at all. Her building at Bernauer Straße 48 stood in the Soviet sector; the pavement outside her front door belonged to the French sector of West Berlin. She was a nurse. She was a widow, though nobody wrote down when her husband died. Her sister Martha lived a few streets away on Lortzingstraße, on the western side, and the walk between them was so unremarkable that no record survives of how often she made it. That is most of what remains of her: a village in West Prussia, a profession spent looking after other people, an address, and a sister. Everything else the world knows about Ida Siekmann it learned in a single morning.

## Gorken, 1902

She was born on 23 August 1902 in Gorken, a village near Marienwerder in West Prussia, in a German Empire that had another sixteen years to run. The village is called Górki now, in Kwidzyn County in northern Poland; the border it sits behind is one that Ida Siekmann's generation watched being redrawn twice. At some point she moved to Berlin and became a nurse — the kind of biography that leaves almost no paper because nothing in it was newsworthy. By the summer of 1961 she was fifty-eight years old, living alone in a nineteenth-century tenement on a street in Mitte, and working in a profession that the city, after two wars, had needed rather badly.

## The Line Through the Doorway

The geography that killed her was an accident of bookkeeping. When Berlin was carved into four Allied sectors after 1945, the boundary along Bernauer Straße followed an older administrative line: the buildings on the southern side fell to Mitte, which became Soviet, while the roadway and both pavements fell to Wedding, which became French. So the border did not run down the middle of the street. It ran along the housefronts — through the doorsteps, through the window sills, through the thresholds of ordinary flats. For sixteen years this produced nothing more dramatic than a curiosity. Then, in the small hours of 13 August 1961, work crews began unrolling barbed wire across Berlin, and every front door on the south side of Bernauer Straße became a hole in the new frontier.

## Bricked Up

The regime moved quickly to close them. On 18 August, Walter Ulbricht ordered the border troops to brick up the ground-floor entrances and windows of the buildings on the southern side. Members of the Combat Groups of the Working Class and the Volkspolizei took up positions inside; residents were checked going in and out, and checked again in their own hallways. It did not stop people leaving. Those on the upper floors simply went out of the windows, and West Berlin firefighters took to standing on the pavement below with jumping sheets stretched between them, waiting to catch whoever came. For several days in August 1961 the street ran on that arrangement — a border guard behind the door, a fireman under the window.

## The Morning of 22 August

On the morning of 22 August, Ida Siekmann pushed her featherbeds and some of her belongings out of a window on the third floor and let them fall to the West Berlin pavement. She meant them to break her fall. Then, before the fire brigade could reach the building with its equipment, she went out after them. The bedding was not enough. She was badly injured on landing and died on the way to the Lazarus Hospital, a few streets away in Wedding — the hospital, in all likelihood, of a woman who had spent her working life in wards like it. She was one day short of her fifty-ninth birthday, and nine days had passed since the first roll of wire. She was the first person known to have died at the Berlin Wall, and for the next twenty-eight years she would not be the last.

## What Was Set Down Afterwards

She was cremated at the Wedding crematorium and her urn interred at the Urnenfriedhof Seestraße on 29 August 1961. The district of Wedding put up a memorial where she had landed, and it became one of the addresses that visiting statesmen were taken to: Archbishop Makarios III of Cyprus was photographed there in 1962, and on 23 August 1962 — the birthday she never reached, a year on — Robert F. Kennedy and Willy Brandt laid wreaths at the stone. Bernauer Straße 48 itself is gone; the buildings on that side were emptied by the autumn of 1961 and demolished in 1963, and the ground they stood on is now part of the Berlin Wall Memorial, where her name is kept with the others. In 2010, the city of Münster named a footpath Ida-Siekmann-Weg. It is a modest kind of commemoration, and probably the right one for a woman whose life was ordinary until the exact moment that history required it not to be.

## Flight Context

The Siekmann memorial and the Berlin Wall Memorial grounds lie on Bernauer Straße in Mitte, roughly 52.54°N, 13.40°E, about 2.5 km north-northwest of the Reichstag and the Spree bend. From the air the old Wall corridor here reads as a narrow green scar running southwest to northeast between Nordbahnhof and Mauerpark, cutting straight across an otherwise unbroken grid of nineteenth-century blocks — the clearest surviving trace of the border anywhere in the city and a reliable anchor for following its course. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is about 20 km south-southeast; Strausberg (EDAY) lies roughly 30 km east and Schönhagen (EDAZ) about 45 km southwest for general aviation. Best read between 1,500 and 3,000 feet; above that the corridor merges into the rail lands north of Mitte. Berlin's flat relief and frequent low stratus mean the memorial strip is easiest to pick out in raking winter light.
