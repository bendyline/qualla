# Forest Swastika

A hundred larches planted among Brandenburg pines held a Nazi emblem legible for two weeks a year, from the air only — and nobody noticed for fifty-four years.

**Location:** 53.4027, 13.7208

**Categories:** Brandenburg, Forests, German history, Nazi Germany, German reunification

## Introduction

For fifty weeks of the year the woodland near Zernikow looked like every other stretch of Brandenburg pine: dense, dark, unremarkable, the kind of managed forest that covers much of the Uckermark. But roughly a hundred of the trees in it were larches, and larches are conifers that behave like broadleaves. For a couple of weeks each autumn they turned gold against the surrounding green, and for a couple of weeks each spring they flushed pale before the pines caught up. In those two narrow windows, and only from directly overhead, the shape they had been planted in resolved into a swastika about 0.36 hectares in size. It had been there, by most accounts, since 1938. Nobody noticed until 1992.

## A Design With a Season

The trick was botanical rather than clever. Larch is one of the few deciduous conifers, dropping its needles every autumn and putting out soft new ones each spring, so a block of larch set inside a pine plantation will spend most of the year almost invisible and then, twice annually, blaze a different colour entirely. Whoever laid out the planting understood this. They also understood that the pattern would be meaningless at ground level: walking through the stand, you would register a change in tree species and nothing more. The design existed only in plan view, at a moment in German history when almost nobody had any reason or ability to look at rural Brandenburg from above.

## Who Planted It Is Not Known

Reports place the planting in 1938, and there the certainty ends. One account holds that it was made to mark Adolf Hitler's fiftieth birthday, though that fell on 20 April 1939 — a mismatch that has never been resolved. The work has variously been attributed to local Hitler Youth members and to a forest warden acting on his own. No planting record, no order, and no confession has surfaced. Then came the war, the division of Germany, and forty years in which this corner of the countryside sat inside the GDR, where aerial photography of one's own territory was not a casual civilian pastime. The pattern simply grew, unremarked, through decades of a state that defined itself by its opposition to the regime that made it.

## An Accidental Discovery

In 1992 an employee of a landscaping company was studying aerial photographs of the area, looking for irrigation infrastructure, when the shape resolved out of the pattern of trees. It is a small, strange detail of German reunification's aftermath: not archives or monuments, but a routine survey turning up something that had been quietly waiting in a forest for more than half a century, visible the whole time to anyone who happened to fly over in the right fortnight.

## Cut Down, Twice

Brandenburg's state authorities had two worries. One was straightforward damage to the region's image. The other was that the site might draw National Socialist sympathisers and become a place of pilgrimage. In 1995 they had 43 of the 100 larches felled, which should have settled it. It did not. The remaining 57 trees, helped along by regrowth, kept the figure legible, and in 2000 German tabloids published fresh aerial photographs showing it as plainly as before. By then roughly half the land beneath the trees had passed into private ownership, complicating any further clearance. Permission was obtained for the government-owned portion, and on 4 December 2000 a further 25 trees came down. That largely obscured the shape. The point was never landscaping. Twice, five years apart, the state chose to cut down healthy trees rather than let a Nazi symbol stand as a landmark or a pilgrimage site — a decision about what a place is allowed to mean, not just what it should look like.

## Other Shapes in Other Forests

Zernikow's was not unique. In the late 1970s American troops in Hesse came across a swastika planted in the same manner alongside the numerals 1933; who put it there has never been established. In September 2006 The New York Times reported a mirror-image fir-tree swastika roughly 200 metres across at Eki Naryn in Kyrgyzstan, on the edge of the Tian Shan, its origins buried under competing local stories. Not all such plantings carry that weight. On Monte Giano near Antrodoco in central Italy, pines were set out in 1939 to spell DVX — the Latin form of Duce — as part of a scheme to stabilise the slope against landslides, and they remain. And in Oregon a stand of larch planted in 2011 among pines makes a smiley face that drivers can pick out on Highway 18 between Grand Ronde and Willamina every autumn. Foresters have always known that a plantation is a message written slowly. Not every message carries the same weight, and not every message deserves to stand once it's read — which is exactly why Brandenburg spent five years and two fellings making sure this one didn't.

## Flight Context

The site lies near 53.403°N, 13.721°E, in pine forest close to Zernikow in the Nordwestuckermark, Uckermark district of northeastern Brandenburg. Neubrandenburg (EDBN) is about 35 km northwest; Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is roughly 115 km south. The design is largely obscured today and there is nothing to look for — the two clearance operations in 1995 and 2000 broke the shape, and the surrounding stand reads as ordinary managed woodland from any altitude. What the area does offer is a clear view of how the Uckermark is put together: a mosaic of glacial lakes, arable strips and rectangular forestry blocks, with the whole pattern most legible in late October when the deciduous species separate from the conifers.
