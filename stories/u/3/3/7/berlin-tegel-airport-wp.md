# Berlin Tegel Airport

Built in 90 days by French troops during the Berlin Airlift, Tegel grew into West Berlin's lifeline airport and closed in 2020 after a postwar life shaped by the Cold War.

**Location:** 52.5542, 13.2922

**Categories:** airports, aviation, Berlin, Cold War, history, Berlin Airlift

## Introduction

On 5 August 1948, French engineers broke ground in a forest northwest of Berlin. Ninety days later, on 5 November, a U.S. Air Force C-54 Skymaster touched down on what was then the longest runway in Europe. Tegel was built for an emergency. Stalin had blockaded West Berlin in June. The Allies were flying in everything the city ate, burned, or wore through three narrow air corridors, and Tempelhof's runways could not handle the volume. Tegel's first life lasted only as long as the airlift. Its second life — as West Berlin's main international airport for sixty years — was a Cold War accident that the city eventually came to love and that finally closed at midnight on 8 November 2020.

## Before the Runway

The site was a Hohenzollern hunting ground in the Jungfernheide forest, then a Prussian artillery range in the nineteenth century, then a base for the Royal Prussian Airship Battalion in 1906 — Zeppelins were tested overhead. After Versailles banned German military aviation, the field served other purposes. On 27 September 1930, Rudolf Nebel set up an experimental rocket research station here called the Raketenschießplatz Tegel, and a young engineer named Wernher von Braun spent his earliest professional years working on the test stands. In 1937 the rocket team moved to a new secret army facility on the Baltic coast called Peenemünde. The runways that von Braun's V-2s were aimed at would, twelve years later, include London, Antwerp, and the Allied bases that flew the Berlin Airlift home from.

## The Hexagon

When West Berlin's traffic outgrew the prefabricated airlift terminal, architects Meinhard von Gerkan and Volkwin Marg designed a radical replacement: a hexagonal terminal with passengers driving directly to their gate. Curbside check-in. Walk a few meters down a corridor. Onto the plane. The new terminal opened on 1 November 1974, and it was a small revolution in airport design — the absolute shortest path from car door to aircraft door. The hexagon was not built for the era of mass tourism or hub-and-spoke connecting traffic that would soon dominate aviation. It was built for the Cold War assumption that West Berliners would mostly fly point-to-point to other German cities and a handful of Western European destinations. When that assumption stopped applying, the hexagon's elegance became its limitation.

## Allied Airlines Only

Until 1990, only airlines from the United States, Britain, and France could fly through the Berlin air corridors. Pan American World Airways became the dominant carrier, eventually basing Boeing 727s, Airbus A300s, and A310s at Tegel. Air France ran daily service to Paris with Caravelles, then Boeing 727s, then the first commercial Airbus A320 service in history — Paris to Berlin via Düsseldorf on 8 April 1988. British Airways flew the routes inherited from BEA. Lufthansa, the West German national carrier, was banned from West Berlin until reunification opened the airport on 28 October 1990. The first Lufthansa service flew that day. The airline immediately bought Pan Am's entire Internal German Services division for $150 million, taking over its slots, gates, and seven of its 727s with their American crews.

## The Long Goodbye

Tegel was supposed to close in 2012, when the new Berlin Brandenburg Airport opened. BER did not open in 2012. It did not open in 2014, 2017, or 2019 either. The new airport became a national joke about German construction, and Tegel kept handling more than 24 million passengers a year — fourth-busiest airport in Germany — well past the point of being designed for half that. By the time BER finally opened on 31 October 2020, Tegel had been working overtime for nearly a decade. A 2017 referendum to keep Tegel open passed with 56% of the vote, but federal and state authorities overrode the result. The final scheduled flight, an Air France service to Paris-Charles de Gaulle, departed on 8 November 2020. Lufthansa's last departure the previous day was a special Airbus A350-900 to Munich. Crowds came in old uniforms to wave the airport goodbye.

## Urban Tech Republic

Tegel was decommissioned as an airfield on 4 May 2021. The hexagonal terminal will be preserved and reused — Berlin is converting the entire 495-hectare site into Urban Tech Republic, a research and industry campus focused on sustainable technology, with an attached residential quarter for around 10,000 people. The first new buildings are rising. The control tower will stay as a landmark. Beuth University of Applied Sciences is moving in. The site has also served less optimistic functions: a COVID-19 vaccination center in the pandemic, then from March 2022 a shelter for refugees from Ukraine. By June 2024 it housed roughly 5,000 people, mostly Ukrainian, at a state cost of around 30 million euros a month. The runways are still there, the hexagon is still there, and the airport that started as a Cold War emergency is now finishing its life as something else entirely.

## Flight Context

Tegel sits at 52.554°N, 13.292°E in the Reinickendorf district of northwest Berlin, 8 km northwest of the city center. The site is decommissioned (ICAO formerly EDDT) and Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is now the city's main airport, 24 km southeast of Tegel. From cruising altitude the former Tegel runways and hexagonal terminal are still clearly visible on the southern edge of the Tegeler Forst, with the parallel runway pair running roughly east-west. The Tegeler See lies just north of the site.
