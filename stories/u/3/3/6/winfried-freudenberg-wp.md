# Winfried Freudenberg

An East German engineer built a balloon out of taped polyethylene, crossed the Wall in twenty minutes, and then spent five hours in the dark above a city he could see and could not reach.

**Location:** 52.4297, 13.2239

**Categories:** Berlin, Cold War, Berlin Wall, Memorials, Human rights, Aviation, Historic sites

## Introduction

He got across. That is the part people forget about Winfried Freudenberg: the balloon worked, the border was behind him inside twenty minutes, and he then spent five hours drifting over West Berlin in the dark with nothing beneath him but a narrow wooden beam. There was no basket. There was no controlled way to release gas from an envelope that was climbing far faster and far higher than he had calculated. At about half past seven on the morning of 8 March 1989 he came down in the garden of a villa on Limastrasse, in Zehlendorf. He was thirty-two years old, and he was the last person to die attempting to cross the Berlin Wall.

## An Electrician from Lüttgenrode

He was born in Osterwieck on 29 August 1956 and grew up in Lüttgenrode, a village in Saxony-Anhalt close enough to the frontier that West Germany was something you could stand and look at. He trained as an electrician, took his secondary diploma at night school, went on to study information technology and qualified as an electronics engineer. At Ilmenau University he met Sabine, a chemist, and in the autumn of 1988 they married. What the Wall took from them was specific rather than abstract: the professional life they had both trained for existed on the other side of it, and the frustration of that had been accumulating for years. They decided to leave. Every method available to a couple in their position was a bad one. They chose the sky.

## The Job at the Gas Works

Freudenberg took a job at a public utility that supplied natural gas — not for the wages but for the access. The couple moved into an apartment in Prenzlauer Berg and began building. Through January and February 1989 they cut polyethylene into strips, taped the strips together and worked the seams until they had an envelope thirteen metres tall and eleven metres across, then bound the whole thing in a net of string. Under it hung a narrow wooden beam to sit on. That was the entire aircraft. It is worth stopping on what this required: two people in a rented flat, assembling a balloon large enough to lift a man, in a country where one curious neighbour would have ended everything, working from what an engineer could reason out on his own.

## The Waiter Who Looked Up

On the evening of 7 March they travelled out to a gas pressure regulating station in Blankenburg, in Berlin's north-east, to which Freudenberg had the keys. At midnight they began filling. Some time after one in the morning the pale bulk of it rose above the fence line, and at around half past one a waiter who had seen it telephoned the Volkspolizei. The patrol car arrived shortly after two. The balloon was still not full. With two people aboard it would not have lifted, and there was no time left to weigh the options, so Freudenberg took the beam alone and cut the tethers. Half-filled and carrying one man, the envelope was enormously buoyant and went up fast. The ballast bags trailing on their ropes caught a power line and tore it down in a burst of sparks. The border guards did not shoot — not out of mercy, but because the gas venting from the envelope might have exploded, and because they assumed the current had already killed him. Sabine, twenty-three, made it home. The Stasi were waiting.

## Five Hours Over a City He Could See

He was aloft for more than five hours. At wind speeds of roughly twenty kilometres an hour he must have reached West Berlin after about twenty minutes — the escape itself, the part that was supposed to be impossible, took less time than a bus ride. Then the problem turned inside out. The balloon kept climbing, well past anything he had planned for, and by the time it crossed over Tegel his altitude must have passed two thousand metres. It was March. It was night. He was sitting on a plank in the cold, above a city whose lights he could see and could not get down to. West Berlin police later concluded that he climbed the net in order to cut the envelope open, that he managed it, and that in whatever followed he lost his hold.

## What Was Left

He fell at about half past seven, shortly before the drift would have carried him back over East German territory. The remains of the balloon came down roughly a kilometre away, on the central reservation of a main road. His body lay in the garden for hours before anyone found it; he had died on impact. In the East, the police worked methodically through his friends, his family, his colleagues and his wife, looking for accomplices. Sabine was sentenced — and then, because the shooting of Chris Gueffroy, the last refugee killed by East German border guards, had drawn hard international attention to what the state was doing to people who tried to leave, the sentence was three years' probation, followed by amnesty in October 1989. The next month the border opened. Freudenberg's photograph now hangs at the Window of Remembrance at the Berlin Wall Memorial on Bernauer Straße, among all the others. His is the last.

## Flight Context

52.4297°N, 13.2239°E — Limastrasse, in Zehlendorf, southwestern Berlin. This is quiet villa suburb: large gardens, mature trees, no structure that reads as a landmark, and nothing on the ground to mark what happened. Orient instead on the water and the woods. The Grunewald forest begins immediately north, the Strandbad Wannsee beach lies about 3 km northwest along the Havel, and the Wannsee lakes fill the horizon to the west. Freudenberg's flight began at Blankenburg in Berlin's far north-east, some 25 km away in a straight line; he covered that distance in over five hours of drift, passing over the now-closed Tegel airport at more than 2,000 m.
