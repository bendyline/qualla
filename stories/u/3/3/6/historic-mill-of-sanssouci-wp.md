# Historic Mill of Sanssouci

The most quoted exchange in Prussian legal folklore almost certainly never happened - but the windmill it supposedly happened at still stands above Frederick the Great's summer palace.

**Location:** 52.4041, 13.0356

**Categories:** Windmills, Historic sites, Prussia, Potsdam, Folklore, Museums, UNESCO World Heritage

## Introduction

The mill came first. Before Frederick the Great cut a single terrace of Sanssouci into the hillside above Potsdam, before the vine-glazed steps and the gilded rooms, a windmill stood on the rise behind it grinding grain for local farmers. Frederick William I - the soldier king, a man with little patience for ornament - approved it in 1736. Construction began in 1737 and the mill was finished in 1738. The palace that would make the hill famous was not built until 1745. Almost everything you have heard about what happened between the two of them was invented later, by other people, in other countries.

## The Palace Arrived Second

That first mill was a post mill: the whole superstructure balanced on a wooden post and swung by hand to face whatever direction the wind came from. It worked that way for half a century before falling into disrepair. What replaced it, between 1787 and 1791, was not a repair but an upgrade. Frederick William II paid for a larger and grander smock mill on the Dutch pattern, built by the master builder Cornelius Wilhelm van der Bosch, and he paid for it because the mill had by then become famous far beyond Potsdam. It was famous for a conversation. Fifty years later Frederick William IV had the landscape architect Peter Joseph Lenné tidy the surrounding ground and sketched a triumphal avenue running from the Gate of Triumph past the new Orangery Palace to the Belvedere on the Klausberg, with the mill as a station along the route. The revolution of 1848 and empty coffers ended that plan.

## The Conversation That Never Happened

The version everyone knows was set down by the historian Franz Theodor Kugler in 1856. Frederick, irritated by the clatter of the sails, offers to buy the mill. The miller, Johann William Grävenitz, refuses. The king snaps: "Does he not know that I can take the mill away from him by virtue of my royal power without paying one groschen for it?" And the miller answers with the deference that makes the insult land: "Of course, your majesty, your majesty could easily do that, if - begging your pardon - it were not for the Supreme Court in Berlin." It is a perfect story. A commoner faces down an absolute monarch and wins, not with a weapon but with a citation. It is also, so far as the record shows, fiction.

## The Miller in the Actual Files

Frederick liked the mill. He called it "an ornament for the palace" and thought the turning sails underscored the rural character he wanted at Sanssouci. Grävenitz, by the accounts that survive, was a difficult neighbour who shorted local farmers on their flour and buried the king in petitions. One of those petitions worked. The new palace, the miller argued, was blocking his wind, so the crown should let him rebuild elsewhere and pay for the privilege. Frederick agreed. Shortly afterwards Grävenitz owned two mills at royal expense, and in time he sold the old one off. The stubborn peasant of the legend was, in life, a shrewd operator who knew precisely what a king's goodwill was worth.

## There Are Judges in Berlin

The legend borrowed its moral from a different case entirely. In 1768 Christian Arnold, who leased a mill at Pommerzig in the Neumark, went to law against his landlord, the Count of Schmettau, over water rights and the balance of his lease. Arnold lost, appealed to the king, and Frederick intervened on his behalf - wrongly, as it emerged later. When the Berlin Court of Appeal ruled against the miller a second time, Frederick demanded the judges be condemned and imprisoned in Spandau Citadel. His nephew and successor Frederick William II reopened the case after his death and blamed the whole affair on "incomplete, inadequate reporting" by "badly informed and preoccupied" advisers. Meanwhile the invented exchange was travelling. It reached print in France in 1787, was rewritten in verse by François Andrieux in 1797, staged as a comedy in 1798, and eventually boiled down to a single phrase that outlived every telling: Il y a des juges à Berlin. There are judges in Berlin.

## Burned, Measured, Rebuilt

The last tenant miller gave up his lease in 1858. The king refused every applicant who came after him, so in 1861 the building opened to visitors instead - a working mill retired into a monument. On 27 April 1945 a Soviet tank was hit by a panzerfaust on the ground between the mill and the drive up to Sanssouci Palace, and in the fighting that followed both the mill and the Swiss house at its foot burned. The Swiss house was never replaced. Restoration of the stone base began in 1983, stalled in 1990 for want of money, and resumed at the end of 1991 on donations from Brandenburg, the North Rhine-Westphalia Foundation and the Potsdam-Sanssouci palace foundation. Van der Bosch's drawings were long gone, so the reconstruction had to be worked out from old photographs and measurements taken off the surviving foundations. What stands there now rises 25.78 metres to the cap and 35.45 metres to the tip of a raised sail, on a stone pedestal 13.41 metres high, with four rods of 12 metres each. The Berlin-Brandenburg Mill Association has run it since 1995.

## Flight Context

52.4041°N, 13.0356°E, on the rise immediately north of Sanssouci Palace at the western end of Potsdam. From the air the terraced vineyard steps of Sanssouci and the long green rectangle of its park read clearly; the mill is the single tall vertical on the ridge behind the palace, with the long facade of the Orangery Palace to the west and the Belvedere on the Klausberg beyond it. Best appreciated on a slow westbound pass at 1,500-2,500 ft AGL. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) lies roughly 20 nm east-southeast; Schönhagen (EDAZ), the region's principal general aviation field, is about 12 nm south. Potsdam sits beneath Berlin's terminal airspace, so check clearances before descending. The Havel lakes to the north and east give an unmistakable navigational frame even in marginal visibility, and low winter sun throws the mill's shadow across the palace terraces.
