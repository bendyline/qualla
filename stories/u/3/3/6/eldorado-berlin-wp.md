# Eldorado (Berlin)

A Schöneberg nightclub that issued coins entitling the holder to dance with someone of the same sex — the emblematic queer room of Weimar Berlin, closed by the police weeks before Hitler took power and then handed to the SA.

**Location:** 52.4981, 13.3489

**Categories:** lgbtq-history, weimar-berlin, nightlife, berlin, schoneberg, nazi-germany

## Introduction

The club issued coins. Through a membership system, they entitled the holder to a dance with a partner of the same sex — an arrangement designed entirely around a law. Paragraph 175 had criminalised sexual acts between men in Germany since 1871, and would remain on the statute book in one form or another until 1994. The Eldorado could not repeal it. What the club could do was rent a room where, for a few hours at a time, it did not quite reach. Between 1928 and December 1932, the corner of Motzstraße and Kalckreuthstraße in Schöneberg held the best known of them.

## Five Addresses and One Name

There was never a single Eldorado. The name attached to a string of Berlin venues over the better part of a century: a place at Thorstraße 12 operating as early as 1848, and a bar at Alte Jakobstraße 60 in Mitte that advertised itself as a cosy home for older men. Three more belonged to Ludwig Konjetschni — Kantstraße 24 in Charlottenburg from about 1920 to 1928, which billed itself as the meeting point of the international sophisticated world; Lutherstraße 31/32 in Schöneberg from 1926 to 1930; and the Motzstraße corner from 1928. The Lutherstraße and Motzstraße houses were the openly gay ones. Motzstraße is the one that got painted, photographed, written up in guidebooks, and remembered.

## What Happened in the Room

The bill was drag performance. Men danced in gowns. One performer sang Parisian-sounding songs in a high soprano. Marlene Dietrich performed here. Cross-dressing in public was prohibited and tolerated inside, which is the shape of Weimar Berlin's tolerance in a single sentence — permitted on sufferance, in one building, until somebody in authority decided otherwise. The crowd was mixed by design: gay and lesbian Berliners, and heterosexual visitors who came to watch them. That second group included tourists working from Curt Moreck's 1931 Guide Through Licentious Berlin, which is how a subculture surviving on discretion came to be listed in a guidebook. It also meant that the people the club existed for were, on any given night, part of the entertainment for people it did not.

## Painted, Written, Remembered

The Eldorado is unusually well documented for a nightclub, because so many of its customers wrote and painted for a living. Otto Dix made a watercolour of it in 1927. Ernst Fritsch painted a triptych, Erinnerung an Eldorado, in 1929. Christian Schad's portrait Count St. Genois d'Anneaucourt, painted in 1927 and now in the Centre Pompidou, includes on its right-hand side a well-known Eldorado regular — the sources of the period use the word transsexual; today we would say a trans woman. Christopher Isherwood and W. H. Auden drank here, and Isherwood's Berlin books eventually became Cabaret. Klaus and Erika Mann were regulars, as were the journalist Egon Erwin Kisch and the sexologist Magnus Hirschfeld, whose Institute for Sexual Science stood on the far side of the Tiergarten. So, notoriously, was Ernst Röhm — head of the SA, a gay man near the top of the party that would destroy all of this, and who would be murdered by that party in 1934.

## December 1932

The end did not quite wait for the Nazis. In December 1932, Berlin's police president Kurt Melcher ordered the closure of what the order called homosexual dance amusements, and more than a dozen clubs shut, the Eldorado among them. Weeks later Hitler was chancellor. The SA took the empty space at Motzstraße 15 for a local headquarters — storm troopers occupying the room where the drag shows had been — and by May 1933 Hirschfeld's institute had been raided and its library destroyed. What followed for the people is the part no photograph shows. Paragraph 175 was enforced with a severity it had never had before; men were imprisoned in their thousands and sent to concentration camps, where those held under the statute were marked with a pink triangle. West Germany kept the Nazi wording of the law for more than two decades after the war ended. In 2017 the Bundestag finally quashed some fifty thousand convictions and offered compensation to the roughly five thousand men still alive to receive it.

## Motzstraße 24

The address outlived everyone who tried to erase what happened in it. Renumbered from Motzstraße 15 to Motzstraße 24, the same building is where, on 15 August 1971, the founders of Homosexuelle Aktion Westberlin met and formally constituted themselves — the first lesbian and gay organisation in postwar Germany, and the origin of the movement in both German states. The choice of address appears to have been coincidental, which is somehow better than if it had not been. From 1985 to 1991, Berlin's first radio programming with gay content took the name Eldorado. In 2023 Netflix released a documentary called Eldorado: Everything the Nazis Hate. And since 2015 the corner has held an organic grocery store. The room is gone; the neighbourhood is not. Schöneberg around Nollendorfplatz, a few streets away, is still the centre of gay Berlin.

## Flight Context

The Eldorado's Motzstraße site is at 52.498°N, 13.3489°E in Schöneberg, on the corner with Kalckreuthstraße. Nollendorfplatz — the hub of the surrounding district and the easiest street-level fix — lies about 350 m northeast; the KaDeWe department store block and Wittenbergplatz are roughly 450 m north.
