# Babelsberg Studio

The world's oldest large-scale film studio has been shooting outside Berlin since 1912, through Metropolis, Goebbels, the DEFA years and Wes Anderson.

**Location:** 52.3869, 13.1194

**Categories:** potsdam, germany, film, berlin, history, world-war-ii, east-germany, industry

## Introduction

The countdown was invented as a special effect. In 1929 Fritz Lang was shooting Woman in the Moon on a stage in Potsdam and wanted the launch to be unbearable, so he had his crew call the seconds backwards to zero — and the film is generally credited with the first countdown ever staged. Real launch crews took the idea from a silent movie. That is the peculiar thing about Babelsberg: the oldest large-scale film studio in the world, in production since 1912, where a startling share of what the twentieth century thinks it remembers was manufactured on 460,000 square metres of Brandenburg sand outside Berlin.

## A Glasshouse in the Pines

In 1911 the production company Deutsche Bioscop bought land at Neubabelsberg and put up a glasshouse studio — walls and roof of glass, because electric lighting could not yet do the work of the sun. The firm had a convoluted parentage: Jules Greenbaum founded it in 1899 and incorporated it in 1902, and in February 1908 the Frankfurt photochemicals manufacturer Carl Moritz Schleussner, who had made negative film for X-ray photography since 1896, took a two-thirds majority of its 140,000-mark valuation and by 1909 had bought the Greenbaum brothers out altogether.

Shooting started in February 1912 with The Dance of Death, directed by the Dane Urban Gad. Nothing in it suggested the site would still be turning out films a century later, through four German states and two occupations.

## The Hall Built for a City

Deutsche Bioscop merged with Erich Pommer's Decla-Film in 1920 to become Decla-Bioscop, which in 1928 merged into Ufa, the giant founded in 1917. Two years before that merger, in 1926, Ufa had raised an enormous stage here for a single production: Fritz Lang's Metropolis. The hall still stands, and it is now called the Marlene Dietrich Halle.

German Expressionist cinema is inseparable from this lot. On F. W. Murnau's The Last Laugh in 1924, the cameraman Karl Freund worked out the unchained camera — taking it off the tripod and moving it through the scene, now so normal that it takes an effort to see it as an invention. Marlene Dietrich, Alfred Hitchcock and Billy Wilder all served part of their apprenticeship here. In 1929 Ufa built the Tonkreuz, the first sound stage in Europe, to use the Tri-Ergon sound-on-film process; Melodie des Herzens with Willy Fritsch came out of it that year, and in April 1930 The Blue Angel premiered — Josef von Sternberg's film, with Dietrich and Emil Jannings.

## Under Goebbels

Between 1933 and 1945 roughly a thousand feature films were made in these halls and on this backlot, and the person directing that output was Joseph Goebbels. Most of it was entertainment: Congress Dances in 1931, La Habanera in 1937, The Woman of My Dreams in 1944, glossy revues designed to keep a country at war in a tolerable mood, which was policy rather than accident.

The same lot produced Jud Süß in 1940, a film engineered to make audiences hate Jewish people, and the regime made certain it was widely seen. That is not a footnote to the studio's history. The craft that built the Metropolis sets and invented the moving camera was still there in 1940; it was pointed at a different target. Babelsberg's technical brilliance never made it innocent.

## DEFA and the Rubble

On 17 May 1946 the Soviet occupation authorities licensed DEFA, and the next year Babelsberg became its headquarters. DEFA's first feature reached cinemas that October: Die Mörder sind unter uns, about a returning veteran who finds his old commanding officer doing very well in peacetime. It was Germany's first postwar film, shot in a landscape that needed no set dressing.

As East Germany's state film company, DEFA made more than 800 features before 1990, including 150 for children, and over 600 television films between 1959 and 1990, everything from fairy tales to drama the censors sometimes shelved. New York's Museum of Modern Art gave the DEFA years a retrospective in 2005.

In 2019 the studio made a film about its own worst day: Dream Factory, a love story between a German extra and a French dance double, set when the Wall went up on 13 August 1961 and the international co-productions stopped coming.

## Sold, and Sold Again

After 1989 the Treuhand handled privatisation, selling the former DEFA studios in August 1992 to the French group Compagnie Générale des Eaux, later folded into Vivendi Universal, which spent around 500 million euros over twelve years on the infrastructure. Vivendi sold on in July 2004 to Filmbetriebe Berlin Brandenburg, run by Carl Woebcken and Christoph Fisser, and the restructured company floated in spring 2005. Its best year since privatisation came in 2007, when twelve features shot here, among them Valkyrie — the studio that made Jud Süß hosting a film about the officers who tried to kill Hitler.

What followed reads like a decade of film listings: Inglourious Basterds, Cloud Atlas, The Grand Budapest Hotel, The Monuments Men, The Hunger Games: Mockingjay, Captain America: Civil War, Isle of Dogs, The Matrix Resurrections, and the series Babylon Berlin, Dark and 1899. In 2021 Europe's largest permanent virtual production stage was built here for that last one, operated by Dark Bay GmbH under Baran bo Odar and Jantje Friese, with roughly two million euros from Brandenburg's economics ministry. Since January 2022 it has belonged to TPG Real Estate Partners and FBB, trading as part of Cinespace Film Studios. The glasshouse is long gone. The address has not moved since 1911.

## Flight Context

Located at 52.3869 N, 13.1194 E in Potsdam-Babelsberg, on the southwestern edge of the Berlin conurbation, about 25 km southwest of Mitte. Water does the navigating here: the Griebnitzsee lies immediately north of the studio, the Havel and Templiner See spread west and south, and the Glienicke Bridge crosses the Havel about 4 km northwest with Wannsee beyond it. The studio itself reads from the air as a dense cluster of very large windowless halls with open backlot ground between them, unmistakably industrial in a district that is otherwise villas and pine. Sanssouci's formal park is roughly 5 km west, and the A115 autobahn runs northeast toward Berlin. Best viewed at 1,500-2,500 ft AGL; the site sits under the approach and departure structure for Berlin Brandenburg, so expect controlled airspace. EDDB is about 26 km east, Schönhagen (EDAZ) about 20 km south. Morning haze off the Havel lakes is common in autumn and will hide the halls before it hides the water.
