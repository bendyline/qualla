# Glienicke Bridge

A green iron bridge over the Havel where, four times in twenty-four years, the Cold War handed people back across a painted white line.

**Location:** 52.4133, 13.0903

**Categories:** Cold War, Berlin, Potsdam, Bridges, Espionage

## Introduction

At 8:52 on the morning of 10 February 1962, two men were waved forward from opposite ends of an iron bridge over the Havel and walked past each other near the middle. One was Francis Gary Powers, the U-2 pilot shot down over the Soviet Union twenty-one months earlier. The other was Rudolf Abel — real name William Fisher — a KGB colonel who had run Soviet networks out of a Brooklyn studio until his arrest in 1957. The deal had been brokered by Abel's own American defence lawyer, James B. Donovan, and it delivered a third man as well: the American graduate student Frederic Pryor, released at Checkpoint Charlie at the same hour. The bridge they used was a functional road crossing between Berlin and Potsdam that happened to sit on the seam between two worlds. After that morning it acquired a nickname that stuck for good.

## Schinkel's Bridge and the One That Replaced It

The first crossing here was timber, thrown across the Havel around 1660 so that hunting parties could reach the grounds near Stolpe. By the early 1800s the traffic between the Prussian capital and the Hohenzollern residence at Potsdam had outgrown wood, and Karl Friedrich Schinkel — the architect who gave Berlin much of its classical face — designed a brick and timber bascule bridge, finished in 1834. That one lasted about seventy years before it, too, became a bottleneck; its moving sections held up steamer traffic on the river every time they opened. A Prussian design competition in 1904 went to the Johann Caspar Harkort Company of Duisburg, and the iron bridge standing today was inaugurated on 16 November 1907. The UFA studio filmed Unter den Brücken here in 1944 and 1945. At the end of April 1945 an unexploded shell tore into the structure, and repairs were not finished until 1949 — by which point there were two Germanys, and the border between them ran down the middle of the deck.

## The Bridge of Unity

East Germany named it the Bridge of Unity, which was either optimism or a joke, depending on the decade. In the early Cold War years the Allies used it as the link between their Berlin sectors and the military liaison missions in Potsdam, while ordinary Berliners and Potsdamers mostly took the S-Bahn. On 27 May 1952 East German authorities closed the bridge to West Berliners and West Germans. After the Wall went up in August 1961 it closed to East Germans as well, leaving only Allied military personnel and foreign diplomats with the right to cross. That restriction made it singular. Of every checkpoint around West Berlin, the Glienicke Bridge was the only one under full Soviet control rather than East German administration — a distinction that mattered enormously when Moscow and Washington needed a place to do business without involving anyone else.

## Four Handovers

The exchanges were rare, slow to arrange, and stage-managed to the metre. After Powers and Abel in 1962 came a grimmer errand: in March 1985 the body of US military attaché Arthur D. Nicholson, shot by a Soviet sentry, was returned to the US Army at the bridge. That June, on the twelfth, three years of negotiation produced the largest swap yet — twenty-three people who had worked for American intelligence in Eastern Europe, traded for the Polish agent Marian Zacharski and three Soviet agents arrested in the West. The last exchange, on 11 February 1986, was also the most watched. Anatoly Shcharansky, a refusenik and human rights campaigner who had spent nine years in Soviet prisons and labour camps, walked west along with three Western agents; Karl Koecher and four others went the other way. His KGB escorts ordered him to walk straight across. Shcharansky, now known as Natan Sharansky, zigzagged.

## The Price of a Name

By the 1970s the bridge had outlived its design life and needed serious money spent on it, which produced one of the more revealing arguments of the divided city. West Berlin repaired its half in 1980. The eastern half kept deteriorating, and East Germany could not or would not pay. In 1985 the two governments reached a bargain that says almost everything about the era: West Berlin would fund the repairs on the East German half, and in exchange East Germany would formally drop the name Bridge of Unity and call the structure the Glienicke Bridge again. One side bought steelwork. The other side sold a word.

## 10 November 1989

The Wall opened on the evening of 9 November 1989. The Glienicke Bridge reopened to pedestrians the following evening, and the border fortifications and barricades came down through 1990 as Germany reunified. Today the Bundesstraße 1 runs across it, Potsdam tram route 93 meets Berlin bus 316 at the western end, and a plaque marks the line on the deck where the two states met. Glienicke Palace stands near the Berlin end, Jagdschloss Glienicke across the highway from it, and the water opens north into the Jungfernsee. Steven Spielberg brought a film crew here in 2015 to restage the Powers–Abel handover for Bridge of Spies, which meant that for a few days the bridge was crowded with people pretending to be the people who once really stood there, waiting for a signal to walk.

## Flight Context

The Glienicke Bridge crosses the Havel narrows at 52.413°N, 13.090°E, between the Jungfernsee to the north and the Glienicker Lake to the south. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) lies about 28 km east; Schönhagen (EDAZ) is roughly 24 km south. From 1,500–3,000 ft the bridge reads clearly as the single pinch point in a broad chain of lakes, with the green copper roofs of Potsdam on the west bank and the parkland of Glienicke Palace on the east. That waterway is also the old border: the Berlin–Brandenburg state line still follows it, so the geography that made the bridge useful to spies is unchanged. Haze off the lakes is common on summer mornings.
