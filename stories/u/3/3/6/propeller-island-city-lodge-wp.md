# Propeller Island City Lodge

A Berlin musician spent years turning an apartment block into thirty rooms you could sleep inside — coffins, cages, an upside-down ceiling — and then quietly shut the door on it.

**Location:** 52.4969, 13.3037

**Categories:** Hotels, Berlin, Installation art, Contemporary art, Unusual places

## Introduction

In one room the furniture is on the ceiling. Table, chairs and lamp are fixed upside down above your head; the beds are hidden under the floor, and you get into them by lifting a hatch. In another the ceiling is 1.4 metres high, so that adults move through it on their knees among plastic garden gnomes. In a third there are two elevated cages, and the cages are where you sleep. The Propeller Island City Lodge occupied an ordinary Wilmersdorf apartment building at Albrecht-Achilles-Straße 58, and for years it offered the least ordinary night's sleep in the city — not a design hotel, its maker insisted, but a habitable artwork that happened to take bookings.

## Four Rooms and a Pseudonym

It started as a way to cover the rent. In December 1997 the German musician and artist Lars Stroschen let out rooms in his own Berlin flat, and found the idea of a normal guest room unbearably dull. So he built four that were not: the Symbol room, the Orange room, the Castle room and the Mirrors room. He called the operation after the name he already published his audio-visual work under — Propeller Island, borrowed from Jules Verne's novel about an artificial floating island that sails the Pacific carrying the very rich. Then it grew. In 1998 Stroschen bought vacant space in the same building and started designing twenty-seven more rooms. Floors one and two opened in 2001, the third floor in August 2002, and by 2013 the place ran to thirty rooms plus a gallery, a reception and a breakfast room.

## The Manual

Guests were handed a set of written instructions on how to look after their room, because most of what surrounded them was fragile and none of it could be replaced from a catalogue. Nothing had been bought. Every construction was a one-off, built by hand, and Stroschen was insistent on the distinction between his work and stage dressing: no plastic, no papier-mâché, nothing that was merely decoration. Anything that moved, moved because it mechanically worked. There were no televisions and no commercial radio in any room. What there was instead was a sound system playing soundscapes Stroschen had recorded himself — he published them as CDs too — so the rooms came with their own audio, composed to match what you were looking at.

## A Catalogue of Impossible Bedrooms

The room list reads like an index to somebody's dreams. Freedom is a prison cell with an escape hole smashed through the wall. Gruft is Gothic, with coffins for beds and a labyrinth underneath them. Flying Bed tilts the whole floor so that the bed appears to be airborne. Forest builds the bed on a stack of logs; Wrapped is a tube like a mine shaft with beds terraced up its side; Glas House is assembled from stained glass and salvaged antique windows. Gallery has a circular bed that rotates on a foot pedal, so you can change what you are looking at without getting up. Grandma's hides its bathroom inside the wardrobe. Nightlight has walls of bronze and gold and a bathtub made from a giant plastic bag. Two Lions is styled like a circus train. Not one of them repeats another, which was the entire governing rule.

## Not a Design Hotel

Stroschen's own description of the place is worth taking seriously, because it explains why the Lodge never became a chain or a template. This is not one of those trendy design hotels, he wrote — it is more of a private planet, arranged to the taste of its inventor. He described the City Lodge as a magnet for creative people, for those tired of consumption, for the differently-sighted, philosophers and people looking for a new angle. The rooms were rented out as photo and video locations often enough that visitors sometimes felt they had seen them before. The intended effect was not novelty for its own sake but a nudge: a room built by one person's hand, working entirely against repetition, in a city full of identical hotel interiors.

## Closed for an Indefinite Period

It is not open. The Propeller Island website still stands, unchanged in its late-1990s HTML, and across the top of the front page sits a line in two languages: hotel closed, for repairs and a new concept — für unbestimmte Zeit stillgelegt, shut down for an indefinite period. A mobile number is given for press enquiries only. Whether the doors reopen is Stroschen's decision and nobody else's, which is consistent with everything else about the project. In the meantime the building at Albrecht-Achilles-Straße 58 looks like every other block on the street: an apartment building, parked cars, a row of doorbells. There is no way to tell from the pavement that behind one set of windows the chairs are bolted to the ceiling.
