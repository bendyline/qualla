# Plötzensee Prison

In a brick outbuilding at a Berlin prison the Nazi state killed 2,891 people from across occupied Europe; two of its rooms are now a memorial, and the prison next door is still open.

**Location:** 52.5410, 13.3220

**Categories:** berlin, germany, world-war-ii, german-resistance, memorials, prisons, holocaust, charlottenburg

## Introduction

The state sent the family a bill. A charge for every day the prisoner had spent in a cell, plus a separate charge for the execution itself, posted by the government that had just killed their daughter or husband in a brick outbuilding behind a prison in northwest Berlin. When Elfriede Scholz, a Berlin dressmaker, was beheaded at Plötzensee in December 1943 for what she had said about the war, the invoice sent to her surviving sister came to 495.80 Reichsmarks. At her trial Roland Freisler had told her that her brother was beyond reach but that she would not escape. Her brother was Erich Maria Remarque, who wrote All Quiet on the Western Front and got out in time. By the regime's own record, 2,891 people were executed at Plötzensee between 1933 and 1945.

## Named for a Fish

None of this was in the plan. The Prussian government under King William I resolved to build it in 1868; construction ran until 1879 on the estates of the Plötzensee manor, beyond the Berlin city limits in what was then the Province of Brandenburg. It took its name from the lake next door, itself named for the common roach — Plötze — caught in it.

It was enormous for its day: twenty-five and a half hectares behind the walls, up to 1,400 inmates, a church and a Jewish prayer room on the grounds, the largest prison in the German Empire. In the sixty-five years between its opening and 1933, thirty-six people were executed there, all convicted of murder and all beheaded with an axe, as the old penal code required. That was its entire history with the death penalty when the Nazis took power.

## One of Eleven Addresses

In 1936, on the order of Adolf Hitler and Reich Justice Minister Franz Gürtner, eleven central execution sites were designated across Germany, each with a full-time executioner. Plötzensee was one of them. A guillotine was brought from Bruchsal Prison in 1937 and installed in a single-storey brick work shed near the perimeter wall; the condemned walked to it from a cell block nearby. In 1942 a steel beam was bolted across the same room, fitted to hang eight people at a time. A 1943 agreement with the armed forces high command added Wehrmacht personnel to the shed's remit.

The sentences came from the Berlin Kammergericht, from special courts created for the purpose, and above all from the People's Court under Roland Freisler.

## Who They Were

About half the dead were German, most condemned for resistance: the network the Gestapo called the Red Orchestra, the officers and civilians of the 20 July plot, the Kreisau Circle. The rest came from across occupied Europe — 677 from Czechoslovakia, 253 Poles, 245 French citizens, resistance members, and people deported to Germany as forced labour and then sentenced. About 300 were women.

They are not a list. Helmuth Hübener was a Hamburg apprentice who typed out what he heard on the BBC and passed it around; he was beheaded at seventeen, the youngest person killed here. Mildred Harnack was an American literature teacher from Wisconsin. Otto and Elise Hampel were a Berlin working-class couple who left handwritten postcards in stairwells. Musa Cälil was a Tatar poet, Véra Obolensky a French resistance organiser, Galina Romanova a Ukrainian doctor. Erich and Elisabeth Gloeden and Elisabeth Kuznitzky were killed as a family, for hiding one man.

The dead were not left alone afterwards. Their bodies went to Hermann Stieve, an anatomist at what is now Humboldt University, who dissected them with his students and built 230 papers on the material — much of it on how extreme stress disrupts the menstrual cycle, knowledge he could obtain only because the state told women in advance when they would die.

## Five Nights in September

An RAF raid on the night of 3 September 1943 destroyed much of the prison and damaged the guillotine beyond repair. Rather than pause, State Secretary Curt Rothenberger of the Reich Ministry of Justice telephoned an order that everyone under sentence be killed immediately. Between 7 and 12 September roughly 250 people were hanged from the beam, eight at a time. Six of them, the records note, were killed erroneously — six people whose paperwork had not caught up, hanged anyway.

The executions ran on for another nineteen months. The last was carried out on 20 April 1945; five days later the Red Army reached the prison and the surviving inmates walked out.

## Two Rooms and a Drain

The Senate of Berlin dedicated the memorial on 14 September 1952. It is small, and it is the actual place: two surviving rooms of the shed, the drain in the floor, the beam overhead. The guillotine was gone, dismantled after the war and lost somewhere in the Soviet occupation zone. A wall was raised across the front with one dedication: to the victims of Hitler's dictatorship of the years 1933 to 1945.

Other buildings gathered around the silence — Maria Regina Martyrum, consecrated by the Catholic diocese in 1963, and the Protestant Church of Plötzensee, opened in 1970 with Alfred Hrdlicka's danse macabre on its walls. Outside, the streets of the surrounding housing estates carry the names of people killed inside.

The prison is still a prison — 577 men, where roughly one inmate in three is serving time for repeatedly riding public transport without a ticket. In December 2021 it was the first stop for Arne Semsrott's Freiheitsfonds, which pays those fines to get people out.

## Flight Context

Located at 52.541 N, 13.322 E in Berlin-Charlottenburg-Nord, roughly 8 km northwest of the Brandenburg Gate. The clearest air reference is water: the Berlin-Spandau Ship Canal runs north-south immediately east of the site, with the small Plötzensee lake just across it and the large Westhafen harbour basin about 1.5 km southeast. The A100 Stadtring motorway curves along the southern edge of the grounds, and the flat rectangle of the closed Tegel airport (EDDT, shut to traffic in 2020) lies about 3 km northwest with the Tegel forest beyond it. The memorial itself is a low brick building and a wall in a small enclosure, effectively invisible from altitude — navigate by canal, lake and motorway. Best at 1,500-2,500 ft AGL; central Berlin airspace is busy and controlled. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is about 23 km southeast, Schönhagen (EDAZ) about 40 km south.
