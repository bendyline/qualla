# Berlin Victory Column

A monument to three Prussian wars that the Nazis made taller, the French wanted demolished, and Berliners eventually turned into a meeting point for the Love Parade and the Pride march.

**Location:** 52.5145, 13.3501

**Categories:** Monuments and memorials, Berlin, Historic sites, Prussia, Architecture, Landmarks, 19th century

## Introduction

Heinrich Strack designed a column 60.5 metres tall. It stands 67 metres today, because in 1938 the office of Albert Speer had it dismantled, carted a little over a kilometre west into the Tiergarten, and reassembled with an extra drum spliced into the shaft. The roundabout it was moved to, the Großer Stern, was widened from 80 metres across to 200 to give it room. Everything about that operation was meant to serve a city called Germania that never got built. What survived instead was the column, standing in the middle of a traffic circle, taller than its architect ever intended and pointed in a different direction than he chose.

## Three Wars in Nine Years

Wilhelm I ordered monuments after the Danish war of 1864 and handed the whole programme to his court architect. By the time the column was finished, Prussia had also beaten Austria in 1866 and France in 1870-71, and a monument to one war had become a monument to three. It was inaugurated on 2 September 1873, the third anniversary of Sedan. Sixty gilded cannon barrels captured in those campaigns are set into the flutings of the lower drums. Four bronze reliefs ring the base: the storming of the Düppel redoubts by Alexander Calandrelli, Königgrätz by Moritz Schulz, Sedan by Karl Keil, and the troops' entry into Berlin by Albert Wolff. Behind the colonnade runs a glass mosaic made in 1876 by the Venetian firm of Salviati after a cartoon by Anton von Werner. At the unveiling, only the paper cartoon was in place.

## Goldelse

On top stands Victoria, 8.32 metres of gilded bronze weighing 35 tonnes, cast by the Berlin founder Hermann Gladenbeck to a design by Friedrich Drake. She holds a laurel wreath aloft in her right hand and a standard bearing the Iron Cross in her left, and an eagle perches on her helmet — a Roman goddess dressed closely enough as Prussia herself that Berliners read her both ways. Drake modelled her features on Victoria, Princess Royal of Britain, who was Crown Princess of Prussia at the time. The nickname came from the gilding and from a serial novel, E. Marlitt's Goldelse, which the magazine Die Gartenlaube ran in 1866. Golden Lizzy she has been ever since. From the old site she looked south down the Siegesallee; since 1939 she has faced west, toward Ernst-Reuter-Platz.

## What Speer Changed

The 1938-39 move was done by the contractor Philipp Holzmann, and it was more than a relocation. The base was widened by 6.5 metres, a fourth drum added 6.5 metres of height, and raising the colonnade contributed roughly another metre, the join hidden by the new roofline. The captured gun barrels were each shifted down one drum, and the flutings left empty at the top were filled with gilded laurel festoons. The entrance to the base moved from the north side to the south. Bismarck, Roon and Moltke were moved out to the northern edge of the same roundabout, which was to serve as a forum of the Second Reich. Then the war came, and the column came through the bombing and the battle for Berlin very nearly untouched.

## The Column That Nearly Went

On 2 May 1945 Polish soldiers raised their flag on it before marching off toward Nauen — an event Poland still marks each year as National Flag Day — and later said they regretted not blowing the thing up, not having known what it was. French troops raised the tricolour on the statue during the victory parade that year. In 1945 the reliefs were removed at French insistence; one stayed in the Spandau Citadel, the other three were missing until German diplomats traced them to a courtyard at the Musée de l'Armée in Paris. France first proposed swapping them for a painting of Napoleon crossing the Alps, an offer Berlin declined. Meanwhile the Soviet-installed city government resolved to demolish the column by August 1946, and in November France formally asked the Allied Kommandatura to pull it down. The British and Americans refused; the Soviets abstained. President François Mitterrand handed the reliefs back in May 1987 for Berlin's 750th anniversary, and all four went up again that year, two of them only in fragments.

## Whose Column It Is Now

In January 1991 the far-left Revolutionary Cells detonated a charge at the viewing platform; it damaged one support of the statue and nobody was up there. The platform reopened ten months later. Everything since has been the city taking the monument over for its own purposes. Wim Wenders put angels on it in Wings of Desire, and U2 shot a video there in homage. The Love Parade danced around it. In 1984 Berlin's gay city magazine named itself Siegessäule after it, and the Pride march routes past it for exactly that reason. On 24 July 2008 Barack Obama spoke at its foot to a crowd of more than 200,000. The last full renovation ran from March 2010 to May 2011 and included regilding Victoria with 1.2 kilograms of gold leaf. There are 285 steps to the platform at 50.66 metres, and no lift.

## Flight Context

52.5145°N, 13.3501°E, dead centre of the Großer Stern in the Großer Tiergarten. From the air it is one of Berlin's easiest fixes: five avenues converge on a 200-metre circle in the middle of a large green rectangle, with the gilded statue catching light at almost any sun angle. The Straße des 17. Juni runs east from it to the Brandenburg Gate and the Reichstag, and west to Ernst-Reuter-Platz. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) lies about 10 nm southeast, Schönhagen (EDAZ) about 20 nm south. The Tiergarten is under Berlin's controlled airspace and directly over the government quarter, so treat this as a view from an arrival or departure track rather than a sightseeing altitude. Late-afternoon light from the west is the best on the gilding; winter inversions can put the whole Tiergarten under grey.
