# Potsdam

The Prussian kings built their summer paradise here, and three Allied leaders carved up the postwar world from a borrowed mansion at the edge of town.

**Location:** 52.4000, 13.0667

**Categories:** germany, history, palaces, world-war-ii, potsdam, unesco

## Introduction

The name comes from a Slavic phrase meaning 'beneath the oaks,' which is what the Hevelli tribe called this spot on the Havel River sometime before the year 993. Twelve centuries later, beneath the oaks now stands one of the most concentrated landscapes of European royal ambition anywhere on Earth. Frederick the Great called his summer palace Sanssouci, French for 'without worries,' and built it on a hilltop terraced with grapevines so he could escape Berlin and play flute, write philosophy in French, and entertain Voltaire. Two hundred years later, in the summer of 1945, Truman, Stalin, and Churchill (and then Attlee) walked the gravel paths of Cecilienhof Palace at Potsdam's edge and decided what would happen to a defeated Germany, and to half a continent.

## Sans Souci, with worries

Frederick II ascended the Prussian throne in 1740 and almost immediately started waging wars that doubled his kingdom. Between campaigns he retreated to Potsdam to design a small one-story palace tucked into a vine terrace above the park. He chose the spot himself, drew sketches the architect had to refine, and named it Sanssouci because the whole point was to be without worries. The palace is intimate by royal standards, only ten rooms, and Frederick refused to let his queen visit. He kept his Italian greyhounds buried in the terrace below. The wider Sanssouci Park grew around it across the next century into a four-square-kilometer composition of palaces, follies, and avenues. The New Palace at the western end is the opposite of intimate, with more than 200 rooms built specifically to demonstrate that the Seven Years' War had not bankrupted Prussia. Frederick called it his fanfaronnade, his showing-off.

## The conference that drew the lines

In July and August 1945, with Berlin smoldering thirty kilometers east, the leaders of the Soviet Union, United States, and Britain met at Cecilienhof, a Tudor-revival mansion the Hohenzollerns had built during the First World War. They sat at a round oak table specially shipped from Moscow. Truman was new to the job, just three months in, and learned of the successful Trinity nuclear test while at the conference. Churchill arrived as Prime Minister and left mid-conference as a private citizen after losing the British general election; Clement Attlee took his chair. Stalin, who had traveled by armored train, set the tone. Over seventeen days they redrew the German border with Poland along the Oder-Neisse line, organized the four-power occupation, and laid the groundwork for what would, within months, become the Cold War. The room is preserved much as they left it.

## The Dutch quarter and the Russian colony

Potsdam is a sampler of European architecture because successive Prussian kings kept inviting people in. Frederick William I wanted Dutch craftsmen, so between 1734 and 1742 the Dutch architect Jan Bouman built 150 brick houses in pure Amsterdam style around two streets near the city center. The Hollandisches Viertel is the largest such quarter outside the Netherlands. North of the old town, Frederick William III welcomed a small group of Russian singers stranded in Prussia after the Napoleonic wars and built them a colony of log houses with carved gables, an Orthodox chapel on a hill above. The colony of Alexandrowka still stands, a fragment of rural Russia in suburban Brandenburg, and the descendants of those original singers lived there into the twentieth century.

## Babelsberg and the dream factories

South of the city center sits Babelsberg, where the German film industry was born and never quite left. The studios opened in 1912 and made Fritz Lang's Metropolis, Marlene Dietrich's The Blue Angel, and after 1945 the East German DEFA features that now look like accidental documentaries of life behind the Iron Curtain. The studios still operate, hosting recent productions from Inglourious Basterds to The Grand Budapest Hotel. Nearby, the Einstein Tower rises out of the woods on Telegraphenberg, an Erich Mendelsohn expressionist sculpture in concrete and brick built in 1921 to test general relativity. Einstein himself approved the design. The whole Potsdam science quarter, from the Astrophysical Institute to the Potsdam Institute for Climate Impact Research, makes this small city one of the densest concentrations of scientific research in Germany.

## What the war left, and what came back

Allied bombing on April 14, 1945, gutted the historic center. The City Palace, where Prussian kings had wintered for three centuries, was heavily damaged and then deliberately demolished in 1961 by East German authorities who had no use for monarchical heritage. The Garrison Church, where Hitler had stage-managed the Day of Potsdam ceremony with the elderly President Hindenburg in 1933, was also pulled down. After reunification, both began to come back. The reconstructed City Palace opened in 2014 as the seat of the Brandenburg state parliament, faithful to the eighteenth-century facades but modern inside. Around it, the Humboldtstrasse block has been rebuilt as careful copies of the Italian-inspired palazzos that once stood there. The result is unsettling and beautiful: a city center that looks centuries old but is mostly newer than the smartphone in your pocket.

## Flight Context

Potsdam sits at 52.40 N, 13.07 E on the Havel River, about 25 km southwest of central Berlin. Berlin Brandenburg Airport (EDDB) lies 40 km east. The Sanssouci complex spans roughly 4 sq km on the city's western edge and is highly visible from low altitude in clear conditions, with the New Palace forming a distinctive H-shape and the long parterre running east to Sanssouci itself.
