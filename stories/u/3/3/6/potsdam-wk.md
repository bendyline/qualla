# Potsdam

A city of 190,000 that arranged its tram network around the leisure architecture of dead kings, and spent forty years as the far side of a closed border.

**Location:** 52.4000, 13.0667

**Categories:** germany, brandenburg, potsdam, cities, palaces, unesco, film

## Introduction

You can read Potsdam off its bus timetable. The 695 runs a route that is essentially a list of eighteenth-century follies: historic steam engine house, Sanssouci, the Orangery, the Dragon House, the Belvedere, the New Palace. Tram 92 calls at the Dutch Quarter, the Russian colony and the hill with the temple on top. There is a Palaces Line, a Crown Estate Line, a Tropics Line and a Film City Line, and every one of them is a real service that real commuters ride to work. A city of about 190,000 people has built its public transport around the private amusements of the Prussian monarchy, and somehow that is the least strange thing about it.

## Thirty Kilometres From the Rabble

Potsdam exists because Berlin was exhausting. The Prussian kings wanted a retreat close enough to govern from and far enough from the noise and the rabble-rousers, and picked a bend of the Havel 30 km southwest of the capital. The Great Elector set the ambition early: the whole island, he declared, must become a paradise. Five architects were then given the better part of two centuries to deliver on that sentence. Peter Joseph Lenne laid out the parks; Georg Wenzeslaus von Knobelsdorff, Carl Philipp Christian von Gontard, Karl Friedrich Schinkel and Friedrich Ludwig Persius filled them, working under Frederick the Great at one end of the story and Kaiser Wilhelm II at the other.

The old function has quietly reasserted itself. Since reunification in 1990, a steady stream of well-off Berliners has moved out here or bought a second place, for more or less the reason Frederick did. Potsdam is also the capital of Brandenburg, a job it holds because voters rejected a proposal to merge Berlin and Brandenburg into a single state in the 1990s. Most of what draws visitors - Sanssouci, the New Garden, Babelsberg Park, Sacrow - carries a UNESCO World Heritage listing.

## Brick From Amsterdam, Logs From Russia

Potsdam's kings had a habit of importing skilled workers and then building them a small piece of home to keep them from leaving. Frederick William I wanted Dutch craftsmen, so between 1733 and 1740 the Dutch master builder Jan Bouman put up 134 red brick houses with stepped gables around two intersecting streets near the centre. The Hollandisches Viertel is still there, largely restored, holding more than seventy antique shops and most of the pubs worth walking to; the ones around Mittelstrasse are where the city's nightlife actually lives, Berlin having absorbed the rest.

The same instinct produced a weavers' quarter in Babelsberg for artisans brought in from Bohemia, and, north of the old town, a colony of carved log houses for a small group of Russian singers stranded in Prussia. Walk the city for an afternoon and you pass through three or four countries' worth of vernacular architecture, all of it built by Prussians to make foreigners feel at home enough to stay.

## The Dream Factory Suburb

Southeast of the centre, Babelsberg has been making films since 1912, which makes it the oldest large-scale film studio in the world and still the largest complex in Europe. Fritz Lang built the towers of Metropolis here. Marlene Dietrich became Marlene Dietrich here, in The Blue Angel. After 1945 the Soviet-founded DEFA ran the lot as East Germany's state studio, turning out more than 800 features before reunification; the international productions came back afterwards, from Inglourious Basterds to The Grand Budapest Hotel.

The studio anchors a whole quarter now, the Medienstadt: the Konrad Wolf Film University, broadcasters, a film museum and archive, and the Filmpark, a theme park where the stunt crews put on shows in a mock frontier town. The RE7 towards Bad Belzig stops at Medienstadt Babelsberg, and the tourist line that serves it is, inevitably, called the Film City Line.

## The Last Station Before the Border

For forty years, none of this was easy to reach. Potsdam sat in the German Democratic Republic and West Berlin sat across the water, so the S-Bahn from the west simply stopped: Wannsee was the end of the line, and beyond it the Havel lakes were a frontier rather than a Sunday afternoon. The bridges that carried the border became famous for the wrong reasons. Meanwhile the palaces the GDR did not know what to do with sat quietly deteriorating, on top of whatever the wartime bombing had already taken.

Almost everything you photograph in the old centre today is therefore younger than it looks. Facades were rebuilt after the war and rebuilt again after 1990, some of them from photographs. It gives central Potsdam a peculiar doubled quality: eighteenth-century in outline, a few decades old in fact, and honest about it if you look at the mortar. The RE1 from Friedrichstrasse now takes twenty-eight minutes.

## Lakes, Cherries and Physics

Potsdam is more water than palace. The Havel spreads out into a chain of lakes around the city, and the Griebnitzsee has a university campus on its shore where you can walk right down to the water.

The surrounding countryside is orchard land. Werder an der Havel, a few minutes west, holds a festival every spring for the fruit trees coming into bloom, and the region turns its crop into fruit wines: sour cherry, strawberry, blackberry. A shop inside the main station sells them; the supermarket a few doors along sells the identical bottles for considerably less. The other Potsdam sits out at Golm, where the university's science campus shares ground with three Max Planck Institutes and a Fraunhofer institute. Palaces on one side of town, gravitational-wave physics on the other, and a tram that will take you between them.

## Flight Context

Potsdam sits at 52.40 N, 13.07 E on the Havel, roughly 25-30 km southwest of central Berlin. From the air it reads as a town wrapped in lakes: the Havel widens north and east into the Jungfernsee, Griebnitzsee and Wannsee chain, with dense pine forest between the water bodies. Sanssouci Park occupies about 4 sq km on the western edge and is the easiest fix, the H-shaped bulk of the New Palace at its far end and the long parterre running east. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) lies about 40 km east; Schonhagen (EDAZ) is roughly 25 km southeast. Best light is late afternoon, when the sun rakes across the terraces and the palace roofs separate from the tree canopy.
