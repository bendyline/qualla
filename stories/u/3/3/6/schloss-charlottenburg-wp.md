# Schloss Charlottenburg

A summer house built for a queen who argued philosophy in its gardens with Leibniz, renamed for her after she died, and rebuilt from ruins by a woman who refused to let West Berlin knock it down.

**Location:** 52.5211, 13.2958

**Categories:** Berlin, Palaces, Baroque, Prussia, Gardens, World War II

## Introduction

Sophie Charlotte never heard the name. She was thirty-six when she died in 1705, and the palace she had commissioned out in the village of Lietzow was still called Lietzenburg; her husband renamed it after her in mourning. She had used the place the way almost nobody uses a palace — as somewhere to argue. Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz walked its gardens with her for hours at a stretch, and the conversations they had there became the seed of the Theodicy, the only full-length book he ever published. Everything built at Charlottenburg over the next two centuries was raised on top of that: a scholarly, musical, faintly irreverent court, held by a woman who died before the good rooms were finished.

## Lietzenburg

Johann Arnold Nering drew the first version, and it was modest by the standards of what came later — a single wing in Baroque style, built in storeys under a central cupola, its façade articulated with Corinthian pilasters and topped by a statue-bearing cornice. Nering died while the work was still going up, and Martin Grünberg and Andreas Schlüter finished it. The inauguration was held on 11 July 1699, the forty-second birthday of Sophie Charlotte's husband, Frederick III, Elector of Brandenburg. Out beyond the terrace, Simeon Godeau had already laid a formal garden in geometric parterres and avenues, in the manner of André Le Nôtre at Versailles.

## A Crown Needs a Setting

In 1701, Frederick placed a crown on his own head and became Frederick I, King in Prussia. A new title demanded new architecture. He had already sent Johann Friedrich von Eosander to Italy and France to study what other courts were building, and Versailles above all; Eosander came back in 1702 and set about swallowing Nering's neat little house inside something far grander. Two side wings went out to enclose a courtyard. The centre gained a domed tower and a larger vestibule, with a gilded Fortuna as a weathervane on top, designed by Andreas Heidt. An Orangery rose to the west, wintering rare plants indoors and turning them out each summer, when more than five hundred orange and citrus trees stood in the Baroque garden. Inside, the Flemish court painter Jan Anthonie Coxie spent a dozen years covering ceilings with flattery. His Porcelain Room shows Aurora in a seven-horse chariot driving Night away to clear the road for Apollo. Nobody in the room was expected to miss the point.

## The Room That Left

One Charlottenburg interior became more famous than the palace itself, and it is not there. The Amber Room — panelled floor to ceiling in worked amber, and called the eighth wonder of the world by people who had seen it — was designed by Andreas Schlüter and begun in 1701 by the Danish amber craftsman Gottfried Wolfram. Frederick I's son had no patience for that kind of expense. In 1716, Frederick William I gave the entire room away to Tsar Peter the Great as a diplomatic present, and it went east. It was eventually installed at the Catherine Palace outside St Petersburg, where German troops dismantled it in 1941 and shipped twenty-seven crates to Königsberg Castle. The documentary trail ends there. Nothing certain has been seen of it since the war.

## Frederick's Wing, Napoleon's Headquarters

Building resumed in 1740, when Frederick the Great came to the throne and ordered an east wing under Georg Wenzeslaus von Knobelsdorff. Outside it was restrained; inside, Johann August Nahl filled the upper floor with the White Hall, the Banqueting Hall, the Throne Room and the Golden Gallery, and the ground floor was fitted out for Frederick's wife Elisabeth Christine, who preferred Schönhausen and rarely came. Once Sanssouci was finished at Potsdam, Frederick rarely came either. The palace found its most constant residents later, in Frederick William III and Queen Louise, who lived in the east wing through most of a forty-three-year reign — interrupted in 1806, when Prussia lost at Jena–Auerstedt and Napoleon marched into Berlin and made Charlottenburg his headquarters. Louise's mausoleum was built in the park between 1810 and 1812 to a neoclassical design by Heinrich Gentz, extended afterwards by Schinkel, and extended again in 1890–91 to take Wilhelm I and Augusta. The last royal resident of the palace was Frederick III, whose entire reign lasted ninety-nine days in 1888.

## What Came Back

On the night of 22–23 November 1943, the most destructive RAF raid of the war on Berlin tore through Charlottenburg. The palace took direct hits: the domed centre, the New Wing, the theatre and the Great Orangery were wrecked. When East Germany demolished the ruined Stadtschloss in 1951, the assumption in the west was that Charlottenburg, damaged at least as badly, would go the same way. It did not, largely because Margarete Kühn, director of the State Palaces and Gardens, refused to let it. The palace was rebuilt to its former condition, with one honest concession to the century that had wrecked it: where the old ceiling paintings had burned, Hann Trier painted enormous modern ones. The Great Elector's equestrian statue took even longer to come home. Cast by Schlüter between 1696 and 1700, moved out of Berlin for safekeeping during the war, it was lost when the barge carrying it back sank, salvaged in 1949, and finally set up in the palace courtyard in 1952, where it still faces the entrance. From 2004 until early 2006, while Schloss Bellevue was being renovated, the President of Germany worked here.

## Flight Context

Charlottenburg Palace sits at 52.521°N, 13.296°E, about 5 km west-northwest of the Tiergarten. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is roughly 28 km southeast; Schönhagen (EDAZ) about 40 km south. From 1,500–3,000 ft the palace is one of the easiest landmarks in western Berlin: a long orange-roofed range with a green copper dome, its courtyard opening south toward the Spandauer Damm and its formal garden and parkland running north to the Spree. The Spree itself makes the best approach line — follow it west from the Tiergarten and the palace grounds appear on the south bank. The domed tower catches low sun well in the late afternoon. Expect winter haze and low cloud from November through February.
