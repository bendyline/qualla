# AVUS

Widely regarded as the world's first road built for cars alone — a toll route through the Grunewald that doubled as a race track for seventy-seven years, complete with a 43-degree brick banking that had nothing at the top of it.

**Location:** 52.4806, 13.2514

**Categories:** Berlin, Motorsport, Transport, Engineering, Weimar Republic, Nazi Germany

## Introduction

The bricks went up to forty-three degrees and then simply stopped. No wall, no fence, no catch fencing — the North Curve of the AVUS ended in air, and drivers who carried too much speed through it did not slide off, they launched. Berliners called it the wall of death, which for once was not journalistic decoration. In 1959, during a sports car race supporting the German Grand Prix, the French driver Jean Behra's Porsche RSK went over the top of the banking; he was thrown from the car and killed. That was the AVUS: a public road you paid a toll to drive, and for most of the twentieth century one of the fastest and least forgiving race tracks anyone had built.

## Ten Marks to Drive It

The idea belonged to the Kaiserlicher Automobilclub, which in 1907 proposed a toll-financed circuit serving both as a motorsport venue and a proving ground for the young German car industry. A development company followed in 1909, but money and permissions dragged, and construction did not start until spring 1913. The war stopped the work; Russian prisoners of war were put to it for a time, and the road was still unfinished when the fighting ended. From 1920 the industrialist Hugo Stinnes paid for the remainder. The Automobil-Verkehrs- und Übungsstraße opened with a race on 24 September 1921, and afterwards anyone could use it for a toll of ten marks. It is regarded as the first road anywhere built for cars alone. As a circuit it was peculiar: two carriageways running nearly ten kilometres apiece through the Grunewald forest, joined at each end by a hairpin — 19.569 km of almost nothing but throttle.

## The Grand Prix That Went Wrong

On 11 July 1926 the AVUS hosted the first international German Grand Prix, and it went badly before it started: two days earlier, in practice, a crash involving the young Italian driver Enrico Platé killed his riding mechanic. Race day brought heavy rain. Adolf Rosenberger lost control and struck the indicator board and the timekeeper's box, killing two track marshals outright; a third died of his injuries in hospital a few hours later. Four men who had come to work at a motor race did not go home from it. The winner was an unknown Mercedes-Benz salesman from Remagen named Rudolf Caracciola, and the day made his career. It also made the organisers' decision for them. From 1927 the German Grand Prix moved to the new and far safer Nürburgring, and the AVUS was resurfaced in asphalt and handed over to experiments — including rocket cars. On 23 May 1928 Fritz von Opel, nicknamed Rocket Fritz, ran an Opel RAK2 to 238 km/h here.

## The World's Fastest Race Track

Racing resumed in 1931 after the Depression, with Caracciola winning again. The 1932 race killed the Czechoslovak driver Prince George Christian of Lobkowicz, whose Bugatti Type 54 crashed at the southern hairpin. When Italian and French drivers took the next two runnings, the new Nazi government treated it as a national humiliation and threw its weight behind the Silver Arrows of Mercedes-Benz and Auto Union. By 1935 the flat curves could no longer contain average speeds well over 200 km/h. The 1936 season was skipped — the track spent that summer hosting the Olympic road cycling race, the marathon and the 50 km walk — and the North Curve was rebuilt in brick at forty-three degrees, with no barrier at its lip. The Silver Arrows raced the banked version exactly once, in 1937. Luigi Fagioli took pole at 4:08.2, the fastest motor racing lap in the world until 1957, and Hermann Lang won at an average of roughly 276 km/h — the fastest road race in history for nearly five decades.

## After the War, and a Myth to Correct

The banking never got its counterpart. A matching south turn was planned and never built; in 1940 the original Nikolassee hairpin was demolished so the AVUS could be tied into the growing autobahn network. Major racing had already stopped in early 1938, after Bernd Rosemeyer was killed attempting a land speed record on the Frankfurt–Darmstadt autobahn and the AVUS was judged too fast for Grand Prix cars. Postwar, the East German driver Paul Greifzu won the first race back on 1 July 1951, on a circuit shortened by a new south turn cut in halfway along. The only World Championship race here, the German Grand Prix of 2 August 1959, went to Tony Brooks — the weekend that killed Jean Behra. One thing widely repeated about the place is simply untrue: the Berlin Wall never cut the AVUS in half. Checkpoint Bravo at Dreilinden came no closer than about 1.6 km to the old South Turn.

## What You Drive Through Now

The banking came down in 1967 to make room for an expanded interchange beneath the Funkturm. Racing carried on flat and shrinking — touring cars and Formula Three, chicanes added to slow the entry to the North Curve, the track halved in length twice more. There were still deaths — the British driver Kieth O'dor was killed in a Super Touring race in September 1995. The last races ran in 1998, with a veterans' farewell the following year. Today the AVUS is the northern stretch of the Bundesautobahn 115, carrying ordinary commuter traffic. The round race control tower still stands at the north end wearing its Mercedes-Benz and Bosch insignia, and the old wooden grandstand is a protected monument. From the top of the Funkturm you can see something the drivers always knew: the AVUS is not quite straight.

## Flight Context

The AVUS runs roughly north–south through the Grunewald at 52.481°N, 13.251°E, linking the Funkturm interchange in Charlottenburg with Nikolassee. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is about 22 km east-southeast; Schönhagen (EDAZ) roughly 32 km south-southeast. From 1,500–3,000 ft it is one of the easiest features in Berlin to identify: a pair of dead-straight carriageways slicing about 8 km through unbroken forest, with the Berlin–Blankenheim railway running parallel alongside. The steel lattice of the Funkturm radio tower marks the north end and the old race control tower sits at its foot; the Grunewald's lakes and the Havel lie immediately west, the Olympiastadion 3 km north. Nothing of the 43-degree banking survives — the interchange under the Funkturm covers where it stood. Winter haze and low stratus are common from November through February.
