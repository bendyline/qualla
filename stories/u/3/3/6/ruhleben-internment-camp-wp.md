# Ruhleben internment camp

Up to 5,500 British civilians spent the First World War in a racecourse west of Berlin, six men to a horse stall — and built a post office, a football league, a university and an opera company inside the wire.

**Location:** 52.5333, 13.2333

**Categories:** World War I, Berlin, Historic sites, Internment, Football history, Music history

## Introduction

In October 1916 the Royal Horticultural Society in London took on a new affiliate and waived its subscription fee. The new members were gardening on a harness-racing track ten kilometres west of Berlin, and none of them could leave. They had started with flowers beside the barracks, for the reason people always start with flowers — because the alternative was looking at nothing — and progressed to vegetables planted in the middle of the racecourse when fresh food became hard to get. Ruhleben held between four and five and a half thousand civilian men for four years, and what they built inside it is one of the odder achievements of the First World War.

## Six Men to a Horse Stall

They were not soldiers. They were citizens of the Allied countries who happened to be in Germany when the war began — living there, working, studying, or simply on holiday — plus the crews of civilian ships caught in German harbours or taken at sea. A large contingent were trawlermen fished out of the North Sea in the war's first days, from Hull, from Grimsby, from Boston in Lincolnshire: men whose boats had been sunk under them and who found themselves interned for years for the offence of having a fishing licence and a British passport. The site had been laid out in 1908 as a trotting track, north of the Berlin–Hamburg railway, and the accommodation was what a racecourse has. The stable blocks averaged twenty-seven stalls; six men were put in each stall. The lofts above held about two hundred each.

## A Post Office, a Police Force, a Casino

The German authorities followed the Geneva Convention and let the detainees run their own internal affairs, which turned out to mean that several thousand bored and capable men invented a small state. They elected a police force. They started a magazine, a library, a postal service. The Ruhleben Express Delivery, organised by Albert Kamps, began carrying mail around the camp in July 1915 and was soon handling over six thousand items a month; it issued sixteen different stamps, which collectors now hunt. In April 1916 the German postal authorities decided a private post office inside a prison camp was illegal and shut it down. Independent businesses appeared anyway, including a casino. Letters, books, sports equipment and a printing press were all allowed in, and the men used every one of them.

## The Mikado, From Memory

The Ruhleben Musical Society formed in 1915. Its driving figure was Ernest MacMillan, a young Canadian who would later conduct the Toronto Symphony Orchestra, and who wanted to stage The Mikado. There was no score. So MacMillan and four other musicians, Benjamin Dale among them, wrote the entire thing out from memory, and it was performed with orchestra and costumes on a racecourse outside Berlin. They followed it with a pantomime Cinderella, then Trial by Jury, The Pirates of Penzance, The Yeomen of the Guard and The Gondoliers. James W. Gerard, the American ambassador, came to watch. MacMillan also lectured on each of Beethoven's symphonies in turn, playing them afterwards as piano duets with Dale, and acted with the camp drama society in Othello, Twelfth Night, Lady Windermere's Fan and The Importance of Being Earnest. Among the other interned musicians were Edgar Bainton, Edward Clark, and the Australian-born Arthur Benjamin.

## The Open-Air University

The British government supplied books free, and the YMCA put up a wooden hall to hold them — about five thousand volumes shipped from Britain. From that grew a school offering elementary and secondary education to the younger internees, and above it the Arts and Science Union, organised by the chemist Henry Stafford Hatfield, which ran lectures at university standard in the open air. The lecturers included the historian and author John Cecil Masterman and the physicists Henry Brose, Charles D. Ellis, and James Chadwick — who would discover the neutron in 1932 and take the Nobel Prize in Physics three years after that. The artist Charles Freegrove Winzer illustrated the camp magazine; he had been working for the French Red Cross and had visited his sister in Germany with the permission of the military authorities, and his internment was contested throughout. His lithographs of camp life are now in the Australian War Memorial.

## The League Behind the Wire

The camp contained an implausible concentration of professional footballers: three England internationals in Fred Pentland, Sam Wolstenholme and Steve Bloomer, Scotland's John Cameron, Germany's own Edwin Dutton, and John Brearley of Everton and Tottenham. They founded the Ruhleben Football Association with Pentland as chairman and Cameron as secretary, ran cup and league competitions between the barracks, and drew several thousand spectators to the bigger fixtures — around five hundred men played. On 2 May 1915 an England XI met a World XI captained by Cameron. Later there was a triangular tournament, the Coupe des Alliés, between British, French and Belgian sides. There was cricket too, and rugby, tennis and golf. Among the athletes was Harry Edward, held from 1915 to 1918, who two years after his release won bronze in both the 100 and 200 metres at the Antwerp Olympics — the first Black Briton to compete at the Games, and the first to win a medal there. Four years is a long time to lose, and Geoffrey Pyke, who escaped in 1915 and published a book about it the following year, was one of very few who did not lose them.

## Flight Context

52.5312°N, 13.2328°E, in Ruhleben on the boundary between Spandau and Charlottenburg-Wilmersdorf, west Berlin, on the north side of the Berlin–Hamburg railway. The trotting track closed in 1955 and the camp is gone entirely; the ground is now occupied by the Ruhleben sewage treatment works and a large waste incinerator, which from the air read as a cluster of circular tanks and industrial buildings — an unmistakable target beside the river Havel. The Olympiastadion sits about 1 nm south-southeast, the clearest landmark in this part of the city, and the U-Bahn line ends here at Ruhleben station, opened in 1929. The 1936 Olympic modern pentathlon was held on this site. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) lies about 14 nm southeast; Schönhagen (EDAZ) about 21 nm south-southeast. Tegel (EDDT), 3 nm northeast, closed in 2020 and is now a development area, not an airfield. Berlin's controlled airspace covers everything here.
