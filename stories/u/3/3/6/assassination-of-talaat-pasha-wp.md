# Assassination of Talaat Pasha

A Berlin street corner where an Armenian survivor shot the chief architect of the Armenian genocide, and a courtroom where the trial that followed turned into the trial of the crime itself.

**Location:** 52.5081, 13.3282

**Categories:** berlin, germany, history, armenian-genocide, human-rights, charlottenburg, trials

## Introduction

Erzindjan, in the Ottoman east, had about twenty thousand Armenian residents before the First World War. Afterwards it had none. Soghomon Tehlirian was born there, and when he came back east with Armenian volunteer units of the Russian army, he found that his family was gone. His memoirs would list eighty-five relatives, one name at a time. On a wet Tuesday morning, 15 March 1921, on Hardenbergstrasse in Berlin-Charlottenburg, Tehlirian walked up behind a heavyset man who had stepped out to buy a pair of gloves and shot him once in the back of the neck. The man was Talaat Pasha. Eleven weeks later a German jury was asked whether the shooting had been murder, and answered in one word.

## The Man Who Went Out for Gloves

Talaat Pasha led the Committee of Union and Progress and was the last powerful grand vizier of the Ottoman Empire. In 1915 he ordered nearly the whole Armenian population of the empire deported into the Syrian Desert, and when more people survived than he intended, ordered a second wave of massacres in 1916. Of 40,000 Armenians deported from Erzurum, fewer than 200 are estimated to have reached Deir ez-Zor. Talaat put the number who disappeared at around 1,150,000.

An Ottoman court-martial sentenced him to death in absentia on 5 July 1919. By then he was living in Berlin on a false passport, in a nine-room apartment at Hardenbergstrasse 4, shielded by Germany's refusal to extradite him. Talaat had been loyal to them, the German foreign minister explained, and their country remained open to him.

## Nemesis

Nobody enforced the sentence. So the Dashnaktsutyun, the Armenian Revolutionary Federation, built a clandestine programme called Operation Nemesis, listed a hundred men who had organised the killings, and put Talaat at the top. Volunteers were not scarce; most were young men who had survived the genocide or lost everyone in it. The operatives held to one rule that mattered: confirm the identity, and kill nobody else.

Tehlirian had already shot a man in Constantinople who helped compile the list of Armenian intellectuals deported on 24 April 1915, which is why Nemesis sent him to Berlin. In late February the conspirators spotted Talaat leaving Zoologischer Garten station, traced him to Hardenbergstrasse 4, and Tehlirian took a room at number 37, across the street, where he could watch the door. His orders were explicit and impossible: shoot the man, then stand over the body with your foot on it and wait for the handcuffs.

## Rain on Hardenbergstrasse

It was raining at a quarter to eleven when Talaat stepped out. Tehlirian was walking the other way, recognised him, crossed the street, came up from behind and fired a single 9mm round that killed him instantly. He stood over the body exactly as instructed. Then the crowd began shouting, he forgot the rest of it and ran. A shop assistant named Nikolaus Jessen caught him on Fasanenstrasse, where bystanders beat him badly. To the police he said: I am not the murderer; he was.

Four days later Berlin buried Talaat with honours. Two former German foreign ministers came, and a representative of the exiled Kaiser. The Foreign Office sent a wreath whose ribbon read, 'To a great statesman and a faithful friend.' Neither Constantinople nor the nationalists in Ankara had wanted the body. He was too much of a liability to claim.

## The Defendant Who Was Not on Trial

The trial ran two days, 2 and 3 June 1921, at the Moabit criminal court, and the defence had no intention of arguing about Tehlirian. It meant to try Talaat. Witnesses were called who had nothing to do with the shooting and everything to do with 1915. The missionary Johannes Lepsius read out a document in Talaat's own hand describing the destination of the deportations as nothingness. Grigoris Balakian, an Armenian priest deported on 24 April himself, told the court the official word was deportation and the reality was annihilation. Christine Terzibashian, a tobacconist's wife marched out of Erzurum, said three of her twenty-one relatives survived, and described men tied together and thrown into the river and a child taken from her arms. The truth, she said, was worse than she could tell it.

Tehlirian's own account did not hold up, though nobody knew it then. He testified that he had watched his mother and brother murdered and woken beneath his brother's body; in fact he had been with the Russian army when his family was killed. The prosecution never challenged a word of it, and the lie went undiscovered for decades.

## A Word That Did Not Exist Yet

The jury deliberated for an hour and answered the question of deliberate killing with a single word: no. It was unanimous, which left no appeal, and the courtroom applauded. Tehlirian was deported and spent years being treated for the collapses a modern doctor would probably call post-traumatic stress; he died in 1960 and is buried in Fresno. Talaat was exhumed in 1943 and given a state funeral in Istanbul.

The verdict proved harder to file away than to cheer. The New York Times set out the problem and declined to solve it: all assassins should be punished; this assassin should not be punished; and there you are. Among the readers was a Polish-Jewish law student named Raphael Lemkin, who asked his professor why Talaat could not simply have been tried in Germany, and was told that what a sovereign state did to its own citizens was nobody else's business. Lemkin thought the killing just and the method intolerable, and spent the next twenty-three years building the alternative. In 1944 he gave the crime a name: genocide.

## Flight Context

The site is at 52.5081 N, 13.3282 E, on Hardenbergstrasse in Berlin-Charlottenburg, between Ernst-Reuter-Platz and Zoologischer Garten station. From the air the block sits at the western end of the City West core: the ruined spire of the Kaiser Wilhelm Memorial Church is about 1 km east, the green mass of the Tiergarten begins just beyond it, and the Technical University campus fills the north side of the street. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) lies roughly 30 km southeast; Schonhagen (EDAZ) is about 40 km south-southwest. Central Berlin is dense controlled airspace, so this is a map-and-window subject rather than a low-level one; from an airliner descending into EDDB on a clear day, the Tiergarten and the Funkturm bracket the neighbourhood neatly.
