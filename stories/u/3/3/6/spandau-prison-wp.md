# Spandau Prison

Four armies guarded one man here for twenty-one years; when he died in 1987 the prison was ground to powder and scattered in the North Sea so that nothing would be left to make a pilgrimage to.

**Location:** 52.5211, 13.1853

**Categories:** Historic sites, Spandau, Berlin, Cold War, World War II, Prisons, Demolished buildings

## Introduction

Everything was ground to powder. When the demolition crews finished with Spandau Prison in August 1987, the brick and stone were milled down and either scattered in the North Sea or buried at the old airfield at Gatow. A shopping centre for British forces went up on the emptied site, and Berliners nicknamed it Hessco's. One set of keys escaped the grinder and now sits in a regimental museum at Berwick Barracks. The erasure was deliberate and thorough, because the four powers who had run the prison for forty years knew exactly what a surviving building would become once Rudolf Hess died inside it. Not a monument. A destination.

## Before the Seven

The prison went up on Wilhelmstraße in 1876 as a military detention centre for the Prussian Army, and from 1919 it took civilian inmates as well, holding as many as six hundred. Then came 28 February 1933. In the days after the Reichstag fire, the new regime began filling Spandau with people whose offence was opposition — journalists above all. Egon Kisch was held here. So was Carl von Ossietzky, arrested that same day and put into what the state called protective custody. On paper the Prussian Ministry of Justice ran the place; in practice the Gestapo worked inside it, and Kisch later wrote about what that meant. Ossietzky was moved on to Esterwegen concentration camp, was awarded the 1935 Nobel Peace Prize while still in custody, was forbidden to collect it, and died in 1938 of tuberculosis and the after-effects of his treatment. By the end of that first year the camps were open, and Spandau's protective-custody prisoners had been transferred into them.

## Seven Men, Numbered

On 18 July 1947 seven men arrived from Nuremberg and were issued numbers instead of names. Baldur von Schirach was No. 1, twenty years. Karl Dönitz, No. 2, ten years. Konstantin von Neurath, No. 3, fifteen. Erich Raeder, No. 4, life. Albert Speer, No. 5, twenty years. Walther Funk, No. 6, life. Rudolf Hess was No. 7, life. Between them they had helped run a state that killed tens of millions, and a prison built for six hundred was now staffed for seven. They brought their old rivalries with them. Speer, who had admitted guilt at Nuremberg, was disliked by the others for it. The two grand admirals stayed close and spent a decade arguing over whether Raeder's battleships or Dönitz's U-boats had lost the war. Neurath went home in 1954, Raeder in 1955, Dönitz in 1956, Funk in 1957, Schirach and Speer in the autumn of 1966.

## Sixty Guards, Six Towers

The security was extravagant to the point of absurdity. Three perimeters ringed the old brick building: a wall, then a higher wall topped with electrified wire, then barbed wire. Sixty soldiers were on duty, manning six machine-gun towers around the clock. Cells ran roughly three metres by 2.7, four metres to the ceiling, and an empty cell was kept between each pair of occupied ones so that nobody could tap out Morse. One spare cell became the library, another the chapel. The four occupying powers rotated control month by month, three months each per year, and you could tell whose turn it was by reading the flags at the Allied Control Authority building. Spandau and the Berlin Air Safety Centre were the only Four-Power institutions to outlast the collapse of the Allied Control Council. Having lost somewhere between ten and nineteen million civilians in the war, the Soviets had pressed at Nuremberg for executions and would not now agree to leniency. There was a colder calculation too. While one Nazi remained in Spandau, Soviet personnel had a guaranteed reason to enter West Berlin — one of only three places where they were permitted.

## The Last Prisoner

Schirach walked out on 30 September 1966 and Speer the following day, and after that the arithmetic turned strange: a fortress designed for six hundred, held by four armies, containing one man. Hess stayed for twenty-one more years. His closest companion became the American warden Eugene K. Bird, who eventually wrote a book about him called The Loneliest Man in the World. For more than two decades Hess refused every visitor as a matter of pride, relenting only in 1969, after a perforated ulcer took him to a hospital beyond the walls and he agreed at last to see his wife and grown son. Fearing for his mind, the directors loosened nearly everything. They moved him into the roomier old chapel, gave him a water heater so he could make tea when he liked, and stopped locking his door. He died on 17 August 1987, aged 93, by hanging.

## Ground to Powder

Demolition began within days. The rubble went into the North Sea or under the ground at Gatow, the site became a car park and the Britannia Centre Spandau, and the only intact piece of the building left anywhere was that ring of keys. What Berlin understood in 1987, the Bavarian town of Wunsiedel had to learn slowly. Hess was buried there beneath a stone reading Ich habs gewagt — I dared it — and the grave became a fixed point on the neo-Nazi calendar, drawing marches year after year. In 2011 the authorities simply declined to renew the lease on the plot. The remains were exhumed and cremated, and the headstone was destroyed. Two towns, twenty-four years apart, reached the same conclusion about places people want to gather at.

## Flight Context

52.5211°N, 13.1853°E, on Wilhelmstraße in Spandau, at the western edge of Berlin. There is nothing to identify from the air, and that was the point: the site now holds a car park and a shopping centre indistinguishable from its neighbours. Navigate by the water and the big landmarks instead. The Havel and the star-shaped Spandau Citadel lie about 3 km northeast, the Olympiastadion bowl about 4 km east-southeast, and the old Gatow airfield — now the Luftwaffe's military history museum, no longer open to traffic — about 6 km southwest. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is roughly 15 nm southeast and Schönhagen (EDAZ) about 19 nm south. Spandau sits beneath Berlin's controlled airspace with EDDB arrivals overhead, so plan a lateral offset and coordinate before transiting. Best visibility over the Havel lakes is late morning, before the afternoon haze builds over the city.
