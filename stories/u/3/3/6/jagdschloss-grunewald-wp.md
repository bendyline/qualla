# Jagdschloss Grunewald

Berlin's oldest surviving palace is a moated hunting box on a forest lake — the building that named the Grunewald, and the road to it that became the Kurfürstendamm.

**Location:** 52.4672, 13.2614

**Categories:** Palaces, Museums, Berlin, Historic sites, Renaissance, Art, Prussia

## Introduction

The Kurfürstendamm began as a log road through a swamp. In 1542 Elector Joachim II Hector of Brandenburg wanted a hunting lodge fifteen kilometres from his residence at Cölln on the Spree, and he needed to ride there without losing a horse in the mud, so a causeway of felled timber was laid southwest across the wet ground. The route survives as some of Berlin's most expensive real estate: Unter den Linden, Budapester Straße, the Ku'damm. At the far end, on the southern shore of a forest lake, stands the thing the road was built for. Joachim called it Zum grünen Wald, to the green forest. In time the name spread to the whole woodland, and around 1800 the building took the forest's name back.

## A Moat That Was Only for Show

The lodge went up in 1542-43 on an eight-by-twenty-one-metre platform, ringed by a moat on three sides and the lake on the fourth, reached by one wooden bridge. Around it ran outbuildings in a U and a wall with a parapet walk and a round tower, with kennels, stables and coach houses in wings running down to the water. There are even gunports at the entrance, though none of it would have stopped anybody. Artillery had made fortified houses pointless, the imperial peace proclaimed at Worms in 1495 had given princes a legal way to settle disputes, and the moat and battlements here were architecture in costume. The master builder was probably Caspar Theiss, whose name appears on a stone slab above the cellar door beneath a carved relief of a welcome drink, showing Theiss and the building clerk Kunz Buntschuh. No document confirms it.

## Anna Sydow

For roughly two decades the lodge was the permanent home of Anna Sydow, the wife of the man who ran the electoral foundry at Grimnitz and, from 1551, Joachim's mistress. The circumstances were ugly even by the standards of the court. That year, at Grimnitz, a rotten floor gave way beneath the elector and his second wife, Hedwig, daughter of King Sigismund I of Poland. He caught himself between the beams. She fell into the room below, broke her thigh on antlers hanging there, and never walked again without crutches. Her husband treated her injuries as licence, took up openly with Anna Sydow, and spent long stretches at Grunewald with her and their child. When Joachim died in 1571 his son John George had Anna taken to the fortress at Spandau, where she died four years later. Berliners preferred a different ending: that she was walled up alive in the spiral staircase of the western wing and walks the house at midnight. She was not. She was imprisoned until she died.

## The Baroque Rebuild

After Joachim's death, John George added the two near-square tower wings on the lake side, attributed to Count Rocco Guerrini. Then the place slid into neglect. The Great Elector ordered it patched in 1669 but built himself a newer one at Klein-Glienicke instead. In 1705 Frederick I, by then the first King in Prussia, put his court architect Martin Grünberg to work on a serious modernisation. The steep gable roofs and the busy silhouette of chimneys and dormer houses gave way to a mansard hip roof, and the main house was raised to match the corner wings. What the builders did with the discarded Renaissance stonework turned out to matter: they threw it into the moat, filled in during 1709. Excavations in the 1970s pulled the pieces out again, and from them a drawing of the original lodge could be reconstructed.

## Kings Who Stopped Hunting

Frederick William I hunted the Grunewald often but never stayed long, preferring Königs Wusterhausen and the heath he laid out southeast of Potsdam. His son had no interest at all. In Anti-Machiavel, written as crown prince, Frederick the Great filed hunting among the sensual pleasures that exercise the body greatly and improve the mind not at all. In 1765 he ordered the Berlin hunting yard's entire stock of equipment moved out here for storage, which meant lengthening a stable from about 65 metres to 168. Frederick William II furnished three rooms upstairs and in 1788 had Johann Friedrich Nagel paint the lodge from the northeast — the oldest picture of it there is. Meanwhile the lake itself was dropping: repeated lowering in the nineteenth century for peat cutting on the Dahlem meadows left the water about 2.8 metres below where it stood when the lodge was new.

## Cranach by the Lake

Berliners had discovered the Grunewald as a place to walk by the mid-nineteenth century, and hunting ended here in 1907. After the monarchy fell, the lodge passed to the Prussian state and became a museum in 1932. It came through the bombing of Berlin untouched, though fighting in the last days damaged some works and seventeen paintings went missing in looting by members of the Soviet occupying force. It reopened on 16 May 1949 with American permission — the first art museum in Berlin to open its doors again after the war — and some of the missing pictures came back, among them Cranach the Elder's Lucretia. Since 2011 it has held Berlin's largest Cranach collection: nine panels from a Passion cycle of 1537-38, Judith with the Head of Holofernes, and Cranach the Younger's portrait of Joachim II at sixty-five, still watching over the house he built. The only Renaissance hall left anywhere in Berlin is in this building, and hunting gear has filled an outbuilding beside it since 1977.

## Flight Context

52.4672°N, 13.2614°E, on the south shore of the Grunewaldsee inside the Grunewald forest, in the Dahlem district of southwest Berlin. From the air the forest block itself is the landmark — a dense green wedge between the Havel to the west and the built-up city to the east — with a chain of small lakes running through it: Hundekehlesee, Grunewaldsee, Krumme Lanke and Schlachtensee. The lodge shows as a white three-storey block with a red mansard roof and a small walled courtyard right at the water's edge, with open riding meadows to the south. Teufelsberg's radomes stand about 3.5 km north-northwest as a cross-check. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is about 11 nm east-southeast, Schönhagen (EDAZ) about 16 nm south-southeast. Berlin's controlled airspace covers all of this, so plan a high transit; the building is easiest to spot in winter, when bare canopy opens the shoreline up.
