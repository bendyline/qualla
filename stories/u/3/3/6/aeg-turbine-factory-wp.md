# AEG Turbine Factory

In 1909 Peter Behrens built a factory that refused to pretend it was anything else — and more than a century later it is still a working turbine plant, with Gropius, Mies van der Rohe and Le Corbusier having all passed through the office that drew it.

**Location:** 52.5300, 13.3228

**Categories:** Architecture, Berlin, Industry, Design, Modernism, Engineering

## Introduction

Before 1909 a factory in a European city had two options. It could look like a shed, which is what most of them were, or it could put on a costume — battlements, turrets, a historicist coat of paint over the machinery, so that respectable people passing on Huttenstraße would not have to think about what went on inside. Peter Behrens took the third option, which had not existed. He built a hall in Moabit with exposed steel columns, visible bolts, and glass walls fifteen metres high, and he did not disguise a single thing about it. The AEG turbine factory announced that industry was worth looking at. Berlin has been arguing with the consequences ever since, and Siemens is still making gas turbines inside it.

## What Stood Here First

The site had been industrial since 1892, when the Union-Elektricitäts-Gesellschaft set up on Huttenstraße to build electric trams. UEG was a joint venture of the machine-tool maker Ludwig Loewe & Co., the steel magnate August Thyssen, and the American Thomson-Houston Electric Company, aimed squarely at the electrical boom, and it ran into money trouble almost immediately. Allgemeine Elektricitäts-Gesellschaft — AEG — absorbed it in 1904 and inherited a plant that was already too small for what the company intended to do. Turbines were the future: the machines that turned steam into electricity, that electrified cities, that AEG meant to sell to the world. The old buildings could not hold them. What AEG needed was a hall big enough to assemble a turbine indoors, and it decided the hall should also be an argument.

## The First Industrial Designer

Behrens had been working for AEG since 1907, not as an architect but as an artistic consultant, and the scope of that job is why historians reach for the phrase first industrial designer. He drew the company's logo. He designed its publicity, its typography, its catalogues, its products — the electric kettles, the clocks. Then he designed the buildings the products were made in, several of them, across two Berlin sites. Nobody had previously tried to make everything a corporation touched look like one coherent idea, and the modern discipline of corporate identity essentially begins in his office. The turbine hall was the commission where the idea became visible from the street.

## Steel That Admits It Is Steel

The engineer was Karl Bernhard, and the structure is twenty-two steel frames set nine metres apart, carrying a hall about twenty-six metres wide and twenty-five metres high with a lower aisle attached along one side. The columns stand on the outside where you can see them, bolts and joints and all. Between them the glazing tilts very slightly inward as it rises, which sounds trivial and is not — it gives the long flanks a subtle taper, so the building leans in over the pavement instead of standing flat against it. The gable ends are reinforced concrete, banded at the piers with metal strapping that reads, unmistakably, as classical rustication, and the AEG monogram is cast directly into the concrete of the gable. Behrens was not rejecting the temple. He was building one, in the materials of a machine shop, for machines.

## Three Draughtsmen

Behrens's office in these years employed, at one time or another, Walter Gropius, Ludwig Mies van der Rohe and Le Corbusier, along with Adolf Meyer and Jean Kramer. It is one of the strangest concentrations of talent in the history of any profession: three men who would each, separately, define what twentieth-century architecture looked like, all drafting under the same roof for the same client at more or less the same moment. Whatever they took from Behrens — the discipline of standard parts, the willingness to let structure be the ornament, the belief that industry deserved serious buildings — they took it out into a century that had not yet happened.

## A Hundred Years of Turbines

In 1939 Jacob Schallberger and Paul Schmidt extended the hall northward, taking it to 207 metres. It was listed as a protected historical monument in 1956 and restored in 1978, and a plaque on the south side records the construction, the architects and the heritage status for anyone who walks up to read it. But the more remarkable fact is the one that requires no plaque: the building still does the job it was drawn for. It belongs to Siemens now, and gas turbines are still assembled inside those glass walls. Very little architecture survives a century of use. Almost none of it survives a century of the same use.

## Flight Context

The turbine hall stands at 52.530°N, 13.323°E on Huttenstraße in Moabit, about 4 km west-northwest of the Brandenburg Gate and inside Berlin's densest inner-city airspace. Best viewed between 1,500 and 2,500 ft. It is identifiable from the air as a single very long, narrow hall with a distinctive polygonal gable — 207 m end to end — set among industrial blocks in western Moabit, with the Spree looping close by to the south and west. The canal basins and cranes of the Westhafen lie about 1.5 km east-northeast; Berlin Hauptbahnhof's glass roof is roughly 3 km east-southeast; the Berlin-Spandau Ship Canal runs through the district.
