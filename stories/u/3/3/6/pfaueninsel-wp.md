# Pfaueninsel

A 67-hectare island in the Havel where a 17th-century chemist made ruby glass, a Prussian king kept nine hundred animals, and the peacocks still have the run of the place.

**Location:** 52.4344, 13.1308

**Categories:** Islands, Nature reserves, Berlin, Prussia, UNESCO World Heritage, Historic sites, Gardens

## Introduction

In the 1680s, on a small wooded island in the Havel then known as Rabbit Island, a chemist worked out how to make glass the colour of blood. Johann Kunckel had the backing of Elector Frederick William I of Brandenburg, who financed a glass foundry on the eastern end of the island and eventually handed him the whole of it. Artificial ruby glass was the result, a deep red that Kunckel learned to fix reliably in the melt - the sort of achievement that in that century sat somewhere between chemistry and alchemy. Then his patron died in 1688, the heir lost interest, the foundry burned in 1689 in a fire that may not have been an accident, and by 1692 Kunckel had gone to Stockholm to work for the King of Sweden. The island went quiet for a hundred years.

## A View Worth Building

It was 1793 before anyone wanted it again. Frederick William II bought the island and had a small pleasure palace raised on its western tip between 1794 and 1797, designed by Johann Gottlieb Brendel, for himself and his mistress Wilhelmine Enke. The siting was the whole point: the little Lustschloss could be seen across the water from the king's residence at the Marmorpalais in Potsdam, which made the island as much a view as a retreat. An English garden grew around it, planted under Johann August Eyserbeck and, from 1804, the royal gardener Ferdinand Fintelmann. At the opposite end they built a dairy disguised as a Gothic revival church - a working farm building wearing the costume of a ruined abbey, which tells you fairly precisely what the late eighteenth century wanted from a landscape.

## Nine Hundred Animals on Sixty-Seven Hectares

Frederick William III turned the place into a model farm and, between 1821 and 1834, handed the park to Peter Joseph Lenné and Karl Friedrich Schinkel. He also built a menagerie modelled on the one at the Jardin des Plantes in Paris. Alligators, kangaroos, wolves, chameleons, lions, llamas, bears, beavers, monkeys, eagles, buffalo and peacocks lived together on an island of sixty-seven hectares; at its peak the collection held more than nine hundred animals from over a hundred species, in Italian-villa enclosures with grottos and an aviary that still survives. The king fed many of them himself. He also opened the collection to the people of Berlin, and Berlin came in such numbers that from 1821 the island had to be shut four days a week. The special trains ran overcrowded regardless. In 1842 Frederick William IV moved every animal to the new Berlin Zoo, which opened in 1844 as the first in Germany.

## The Engine Master's Assistant

Among the people working on the island in 1830 was Harry Maitey, the first Hawaiian to come to Prussia. He had joined the crew of the Prussian ship Mentor at Honolulu in 1823 and landed in Prussia the following year; by 1830 he was assistant to the engine master on Pfaueninsel. Wilhelm von Humboldt sought him out for long conversations about the Hawaiian language and presented the results to the Berlin Academy of Sciences in 1828, which makes Maitey the source of Prussia's first serious scholarly account of his own tongue. He married with royal permission, moved across the water to Klein-Glienicke, and died in 1872. He is one of the very few people attached to this island who was neither a Hohenzollern nor a gardener, and what survives of him is thinner than it ought to be.

## The Outline of a Vanished House

The Palmenhaus went up in 1831 to a design by Schinkel: a timber hothouse stocked with tobacco, bananas, canna lilies, mangold, artichokes and rhubarb, admired by Alexander von Humboldt, who knew a good collection when he saw one. On the night of 19-20 May 1880 it burned to the ground. Nobody established the cause, though the likeliest explanation is a spark from its own chimney landing on its own timber. It was never rebuilt. The stone columns were left standing where the walls had been, so what remains is a full-size floor plan open to the sky, which you can walk through and furnish in your head.

## The Island at the Border

On 15 August 1936 the German government closed the Berlin Olympics here, with fireworks and an "Italian Night" laid on for a thousand invited guests. Nine years later the geography turned hard. After the war Pfaueninsel sat inside West Berlin with the East German frontier running along the water, and the shoreline at Sacrow to the north and west carried the GDR's border fortifications - an ornamental island facing a militarised one. In the 1960s film crews shooting the German Edgar Wallace thrillers used it for exteriors, which is its own kind of afterlife. Today it is a nature reserve, a Special Protection Area for wild birds, and part of the UNESCO World Heritage listing granted in 1990, administered by the Prussian palaces foundation. There is no bridge and there are no cars. A small ferry is the only way over, and the peacocks that gave the island its name wander loose.

## Flight Context

52.4344°N, 13.1308°E. Pfaueninsel is a distinct wooded island in the Havel between Kladow to the west and Wannsee to the east, downstream of the Großer Wannsee and upstream of the Jungfernsee. From the air the white two-towered Lustschloss on the western tip is the giveaway, with open lawns breaking the woodland canopy toward the middle of the island. It makes an excellent visual waypoint on the Havel lake chain running southwest out of Berlin toward Potsdam. Recommended altitude 1,500-2,500 ft AGL; the island is a protected nature reserve and a designated bird area, so keep passes high and quiet. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) lies about 17 nm east-southeast, Schönhagen (EDAZ) about 15 nm south. Morning haze often sits on the Havel in summer, but the island's shape stays legible from well out.
