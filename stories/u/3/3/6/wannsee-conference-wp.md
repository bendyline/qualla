# Wannsee Conference

On 20 January 1942, fifteen officials met for ninety minutes in a lakeside villa southwest of Berlin to coordinate the murder of Europe's Jews; the villa is now a memorial.

**Location:** 52.4331, 13.1656

**Categories:** berlin, germany, holocaust, world-war-ii, jewish-history, memorials, wannsee, museums

## Introduction

Adolf Eichmann prepared a table. It set out the Jewish population of every country in Europe, split between the lands already under German control and everywhere else, and it came to roughly eleven million. Estonia carried a word instead of a figure: judenfrei, the regime's term for a place emptied of Jews. The 4,500 Jewish people still living there when the Germans arrived had been murdered by the end of 1941, so there was nothing left to count. On the morning of 20 January 1942 it lay on a table in a villa above the Grosser Wannsee, where fifteen officials spent about ninety minutes working out how to do to the remaining eleven million what had been done in Estonia. Cognac was served near the end.

## Fifteen Men, Ninety Minutes

Hitler was not there, nor Himmler, nor Goebbels. The room held state secretaries and undersecretaries from the Reich Chancellery, the Foreign Office, Interior, Justice, the Four Year Plan and the General Government of occupied Poland, together with SS officers from Reinhard Heydrich's Reich Security Main Office. They were administrators, and they were young: half under forty, only two over fifty. Ten had been to university, eight held doctorates, eight were lawyers.

Heydrich had not summoned them to decide anything. He wanted it settled that when the deportations began no ministry would object, no jurisdiction would be disputed, and the fate of the deported would belong to the SS alone. Everyone who sat through the meeting became, by sitting through it, an accomplice, which is why Heydrich had detailed minutes circulated afterwards.

## The Killing Had Already Begun

Nothing started at Wannsee that was not already in motion. Since June 1941 the Einsatzgruppen had followed the German army into the Soviet Union with orders to shoot. On 8 July Heydrich directed that every Jewish man between fifteen and forty-five be killed; by August the orders covered women, children and the elderly, which is to say everyone. Hundreds of thousands of Polish, Serbian and Soviet Jews had been murdered before the invitations went out, most shot at the edge of pits within walking distance of their own homes. A new extermination camp was rising at Belzec as the meeting was scheduled.

Göring had authorised Heydrich in writing on 31 July 1941 to prepare what the regime called a 'total solution'. That December, with the Red Army holding outside Moscow and America in the war, Hitler told his party leadership that Europe's Jews were to be killed at once, not after a victory that had stopped arriving. Wannsee was the paperwork that followed.

## Office Language

Heydrich spoke for about an hour. Europe would be 'combed through from west to east'; Jews would be 'evacuated' eastward; the word murder never appears. Everyone present understood. Eichmann, who drafted the minutes under Heydrich's editing, admitted at his trial in Jerusalem that 'certain over-plain talk and jargon expressions had to be rendered into office language by me'.

The half hour that followed is the hardest part to read, because it is so procedural. Otto Hofmann and Wilhelm Stuckart raised the legal awkwardness of mixed marriages and floated dissolving them by decree, or mass sterilisation as a tidier route. Josef Bühler, speaking for occupied Poland, said he hoped the killing could begin in his territory as soon as possible. Exemptions were sketched for people over sixty-five and for First World War veterans badly wounded or decorated with the Iron Cross; they would go to Theresienstadt instead. Heydrich gave the reason without embarrassment: it would prevent interventions. It would stop German families making trouble over grandfathers and war heroes they knew by name.

## Copy Number Sixteen

Thirty copies went out. As the war ended, participants and their colleagues destroyed nearly all of them. One survived, number sixteen, sent to Martin Luther at the Foreign Office and found in March 1947 by the American prosecutor Robert Kempner among files seized from that ministry. It went into evidence at the subsequent Nuremberg trials, and it is why nobody can argue in good faith about what was intended.

Few of the fifteen were ever held to account. Heydrich was attacked by Czechoslovak agents in Prague in May 1942 and died of his wounds that June. Bühler was tried in Kraków and executed in 1948. Eichmann was hanged in Israel in 1962, twenty years after taking the notes. Several of the rest were never prosecuted.

## The House on the Lake

The villa at Am Grossen Wannsee 56-58 was built in 1914 and 1915 by the architect Paul Baumgarten for the businessman Ernst Marlier. Its second owner, the industrialist Friedrich Minoux, sold it in 1940 from a prison cell, convicted of fraud, to the Nordhav Foundation, a body Heydrich controlled that bought properties as guest houses for the SD. That is how a house with a view over the water came to be free for a meeting in January.

Afterwards it served as a school. In 1965 the historian Joseph Wulf, an Auschwitz survivor, proposed turning it into a memorial and documentation centre; West Germany was not interested. Worn down by that refusal and by his country's reluctance to prosecute the men who had done the killing, Wulf took his own life in 1974. On 20 January 1992, fifty years to the day, the villa opened as a memorial, and the library upstairs carries his name. Facsimiles of the minutes and Eichmann's list lie under glass. The list is a column of figures. Every figure was a street, a congregation, a family at dinner.

## Flight Context

Located at 52.4331 N, 13.1656 E on the western shore of the Grosser Wannsee in Berlin-Zehlendorf, about 20 km southwest of Mitte. From the air the setting is unmistakable: a chain of lakes and wooded islands where the Havel widens, with the Wannsee bathing beach on the eastern shore and the Glienicke Bridge 3 km north. The villa itself is a large white house set in lawns running down to the water, hard to pick out from above without the lake bearings. Best viewed at 1,500-2,500 ft AGL on a north-south run along the Havel chain. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is roughly 20 km east-southeast; Schönhagen (EDAZ) lies about 25 km south. Sailing traffic covers the lake in summer, which makes the water easy to identify and the house easy to miss.
