# Ich bin ein Berliner

Kennedy threw away weeks of prepared text on a Schöneberg balcony in June 1963 and improvised the most famous ten minutes of the Cold War — and no, he did not call himself a doughnut.

**Location:** 52.4849, 13.3444

**Categories:** berlin, cold-war, modern-history, germany, politics, berlin-wall

## Introduction

The cue card is in the National Archives, and the handwriting on it is the president's own. Ish bin ein Bearleener, it reads, with the stress marked on the third syllable — English spelling bent into the shape of a German sentence by a man who did not speak German. Beside it, in the same hand: kiwis romanus sum, a phonetic crib for classical Latin. John F. Kennedy had been working the phrase over privately for a week. On the morning of 26 June 1963 he carried the card into West Berlin, and by early afternoon he had used it to say something his advisers had specifically told him not to.

## Red Cloth Across the Gate

He went to the Wall first. At half past eleven Kennedy climbed a platform at the Brandenburg Gate to look east, and found that the East German authorities had got there ahead of him: large panels of red cloth had been hung between the columns so that the president of the United States could see nothing but fabric. He looked at it a while, then moved on to Checkpoint Charlie and another platform and another closed border. Twenty-two months earlier the East German government had sealed the city overnight to stop a haemorrhage of its own citizens — the engineers and doctors and teachers it could not replace. Washington had protested and done nothing, and West Berliners had noticed.

## He Put the Speech Down

The crowd on Rudolph Wilde Platz, the square in front of the Schöneberg town hall that served as West Berlin's seat of government, numbered around 120,000. Konrad Adenauer introduced him. Heinz Weber, of the American mission in Berlin, stood by to translate. A speech had been in preparation for weeks, cleared and softened through the usual channels, and Kennedy set it aside almost immediately. What came out instead ran ten minutes, considerably sharper than his staff had approved, and turned on a figure he had used once before — at a civic reception in New Orleans in May 1962, where he had reached for the old Roman boast civis Romanus sum. Two thousand years ago, he said, the proudest boast was civis Romanus sum. Today, in the world of freedom, the proudest boast is Ich bin ein Berliner.

## Let Them Come to Berlin

The square went quiet while Weber translated the German line back into German, and then it broke. Kennedy, plainly relieved, thanked his interpreter for translating his German — that got the laugh — and pressed on into the passage that actually alarmed Moscow. There are many people in the world who really don't understand what is the great issue between the free world and the Communist world. Let them come to Berlin. Some say communism is the wave of the future. Let them come to Berlin. Some say we can work with the Communists — and that one he delivered in German. Lasst sie nach Berlin kommen. Later came the line that needed no rhetoric at all: freedom has many difficulties and democracy is not perfect, but we have never had to put a wall up to keep our people in. He closed by returning to the phrase — all free men, wherever they may live, are citizens of Berlin — and then, according to the historian Andreas Daum, stood fiddling with his suit jacket and grinning like a boy who had just pulled something off.

## Not a Doughnut

Everyone has heard that he got it wrong: that ein turned a citizen of Berlin into a jam-filled pastry, and that the crowd laughed. Both halves are false. German drops the article when you state a profession or a birthplace, and keeps it when you speak figuratively — and figurative was exactly the point, since Kennedy was from Massachusetts. The pastry is a problem only outside Berlin: in the city itself and the country around it, the thing is a Pfannkuchen. The laughter on the recording comes seconds after the phrase, when he thanks Weber for the translation. The story appears to start with Len Deighton's 1983 spy novel Berlin Game, where the line belongs to a narrator the reader is not meant to trust. A New York Times review repeated it as fact and added the crowd laughing, an op-ed picked it up four years later, and from there it went everywhere — the BBC, CNN, Time, a Kennedy biography, Infinite Jest, even a technical manual for speech synthesis. Ted Sorensen, Kennedy's speechwriter, went to his grave claiming he had inserted the offending ein, apologising for a mistake nobody made.

## Two Different Presidents

The Soviets were not amused. Two weeks earlier, at American University, Kennedy had given a conciliatory address about improving relations and a strategy of peace; Nikita Khrushchev, reading the Berlin transcript days later, remarked that one would think the speeches had been made by two different presidents. The speech mattered in another, quieter way too: it was the first time an American president publicly acknowledged that East Berlin belonged to the Soviet bloc along with the rest of East Germany. Kennedy had five months to live. Ronald Reagan came to the Brandenburg Gate twenty-four years afterwards and told Mikhail Gorbachev to tear down this wall; Bill Clinton managed Berlin ist frei in 1994, with the Wall gone. The square where Kennedy spoke is now John-F.-Kennedy-Platz, with a plaque by the town hall entrance and a room above it given over to that afternoon. The original typescript sits in the National Archives in Washington, with the improvised sentences nowhere on it.

## Flight Context

Rathaus Schöneberg and John-F.-Kennedy-Platz sit at 52.485°N, 13.344°E, about 5 km southwest of the Brandenburg Gate. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) lies 22 km southeast. The town hall's square clock tower is a reliable visual mark from 2,000–3,000 ft, standing above an otherwise uniform roofline of five- and six-storey blocks. The green expanse of the closed Tempelhof airfield is 3 km east and unmistakable; the Grunewald forest fills the horizon 5 km west. Best light for the Schöneberg street grid is late afternoon, when the tower throws a long shadow across the square.
