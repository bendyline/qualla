# Sanssouci

Frederick the Great built a ten-room palace above a terraced vineyard in Potsdam, meant to die with him — and two and a half centuries later it draws millions.

**Location:** 52.4033, 13.0386

**Categories:** palace, potsdam, prussia, rococo, world-heritage-site, gardens, frederick-the-great

## Introduction

Frederick the Great built a fountain that would not work. He commissioned engineers who did not understand hydraulics, put up pumping houses and reservoirs, spent enormously, and then watched the Great Fountain below his vineyard terraces sit silent for the rest of his life. It stayed silent for a century. Only around 1842, after the invention of the steam engine and more than fifty years after Frederick's death, did the jets finally rise thirty-eight metres above the parterre — and the pumping station that made it possible was itself disguised as a Turkish mosque, chimney doubling as minaret. Sanssouci is full of this: a king's private taste, applied without much regard for what anyone else advised.

## A Vineyard First, a Palace Second

On 10 August 1744, Frederick ordered a bare hillside at Bornstedt turned into terraced vines. The slope had been wooded until his father, the soldier-king Frederick William I, had the trees felled so Potsdam could expand. What replaced them was engineered for sunlight: three broad terraces with convex centres to catch as much of it as possible, their retaining walls pierced by 168 glazed niches where figs were grown. Vines came from Portugal, Italy, France and nearby Neuruppin. Yew trees stood on strips of lawn, low box hedging enclosed trellised fruit, and 120 steps — now 132 — ran down the middle of the wheel. Only afterwards did the palace arrive on the brow of it, and Frederick called the building mein Weinberghäuschen: my little vineyard house.

## Ten Rooms and an Argument

Georg Wenzeslaus von Knobelsdorff designed and built Sanssouci between 1745 and 1747, working largely from Frederick's own amateur sketch. The result is a single-storey villa of ten principal rooms — closer to the Château de Marly than to Versailles, and small enough that visitors sometimes mistake their first sight of it for an orangery. Knobelsdorff urged a semi-basement storey, which would have raised the principal rooms, given the building some presence and kept the damp out. Frederick refused, and Sanssouci has had damp problems ever since. The two men then fell out over where in the park the palace should stand, and in 1746 Knobelsdorff was fired; the Dutch architect Jan Bouman finished the job. Frederick's grip on every decorative decision was so complete that the style has its own name: Frederician Rococo.

## Marble, Cedar and a Room Full of Parrots

The white-and-gold oval Marmorsaal sits under the central dome, its paired columns of Carrara marble, its floor an Italian marble intarsia radiating from a trelliswork oval, stucco putti dangling their feet from the cornice. In niches flanking the doorway stand Venus Urania and Apollo, by the French sculptor François Gaspard Adam — free nature and the arts, the whole thesis of the place in two figures. East of the hall lie Frederick's own rooms, ending in a circular library reached through a narrow passage from his bedroom, panelled in cedar, holding some 2,100 volumes of Greek and Roman writing and French literature bound in brown and red goat leather, heavy on Voltaire. Voltaire himself stayed in Potsdam from 1750 to 1753, and the guest room he used was decorated with carved apes, parrots, cranes and storks on yellow lacquered panels, which earned it the nickname the Flower Room. A 1746 Silbermann fortepiano still stands in the music room.

## The Palace He Built to Show Off

Twenty years later Frederick built the New Palace at the western end of the park's 2.5-kilometre avenue, and it is everything Sanssouci is not: enormous, Baroque, unmistakably a statement. Prussia had come close to defeat in the Seven Years' War, and the New Palace was constructed to demonstrate that its resources were undiminished. Frederick was perfectly candid about this, calling the project his fanfaronnade — his showing off. Having built it, he almost never stayed in it, reserving it for the rare occasions when a diplomat needed impressing. The comparison to Versailles that follows Sanssouci around gets this backwards. Sanssouci was not a retreat from a great palace, because the great palace did not yet exist. The retreat came first, and everything else was arranged around it.

## The Place That Was Meant to Die With Him

Frederick conceived Sanssouci as "a place that would die with him," and as he aged his frugality hardened into refusal — he would not permit repairs to the façade and allowed them indoors only grudgingly. After his death in 1786 the palace sat mostly empty and neglected. Then in 1840, a century after Frederick's accession, his great-grandnephew Frederick William IV moved into the guest rooms with his wife, kept the surviving furniture, hunted down replacements from Frederick's own time, and had the armchair in which the king died returned in 1843. The service wings were enlarged between 1840 and 1842; the west wing became the Ladies' Wing, since Frederick had never entertained women there and the building had no accommodation for them. The palace survived the fighting of 1945 intact, though the old windmill in the park did not. Much of the evacuated art went east as Soviet booty and only a fraction came back. Sanssouci became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1990, the library returned in 1992, thirty-six oil paintings between 1993 and 1995 — and after reunification, Frederick's body was brought back and buried in a tomb overlooking the terraces he had ordered dug into a bare hill. The palace he expected to outlive now receives more than two million visitors a year.

## Flight Context

Sanssouci stands at 52.403°N, 13.039°E on the northern edge of Potsdam, about 32 km west-southwest of Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB). From the air the site is unmistakable: six terraces stepping down a south-facing slope to the Great Fountain and its circular parterre, with the 2.5 km main avenue running due west to the far larger New Palace. The Havel lakes wrap around Potsdam to the east and south; the Glienicke Bridge lies 5 km northeast. Strausberg (EDAY) is roughly 65 km east. Recommended viewing altitude 1,500-2,500 ft for the terrace geometry and the park's follies — the Chinese House, the Temple of Friendship, Charlottenhof — though EDDB approach paths and Potsdam's restricted areas require attention. Morning light rakes across the terraces and shows their convex curve best.
