# Unité d'Habitation of Berlin

Le Corbusier designed this seventeen-storey concrete block above the Grunewald, lost an argument with Berlin's building authorities over twenty-four centimetres, and struck the finished building from his own catalogue of works.

**Location:** 52.5103, 13.2439

**Categories:** Architecture, Berlin, Modernism, Brutalism, Historic sites, Housing, Charlottenburg-Wilmersdorf

## Introduction

Le Corbusier designed this building and then refused to admit it existed. The Berlin Unité was deleted from his catalogue of works — an omission he maintained until his death in 1965 and which was confirmed after him, in 1967, when his authorised oeuvre was published for the last time. Seventeen storeys of raw concrete stand on doubled pilotis above the Grunewald: five hundred and thirty apartments, a wide interior street serving them, and colours chosen by Corbusier himself with Fernand Léger. The man whose name is on it decided it did not count. The argument that produced that decision was fought out in the Berlin press over a difference of twenty-four centimetres.

## A Competition of Systems

West Berlin in the mid-1950s was isolated, desperately short of housing and still full of ruins, and across the sector boundary the East had just finished the Karl-Marx-Allee with Soviet backing — a broad modern boulevard dressed in socialist realism. The Western answer was Interbau 57, an international building exhibition that brought Walter Gropius, Oscar Niemeyer, Max Taut, Frei Otto and Alvar Aalto to the Hansaviertel to design social housing. A special law, the LEX IBA, was written so that foreign architects could work outside the local building code. Corbusier had completed his Marseille Unité in 1952, and it was arguably the most famous piece of social housing anywhere on earth; Gropius said of it that 'any architect who does not find this building beautiful, had better lay down his pencil.' Inviting Corbusier was obvious. What he said he intended to build was a dwelling that would 'provide with silence and solitude before the sun, space and greenery... the perfect receptacle for the family.'

## Between the Stadium and the Rubble

The block was too big for the Hansaviertel. Its mass would have swamped the centre, so Corbusier was offered a choice of empty lots further out and took one with a strange pair of neighbours: the Olympic Stadium built for the 1936 Games on one side, and on the other the vast artificial mountain Berlin was raising out of its own war rubble. The plot is a triangle called the Heilsberger Dreieck, wedged between what is now Flatowallee and the S-Bahn line, close to the Heerstraße that runs dead straight toward the Brandenburg Gate. The ground turned out to be sand, so the pilotis carrying the whole structure had to be doubled to steady it. The compensation was height and the Grunewald — light and long views from every apartment, which Corbusier held to be non-negotiable in modern architecture.

## Twenty-Four Centimetres

Then came the fight. The Modulor is Corbusier's proportional system, derived from the human body, and he had built the Unité from no more than fifteen Modulor measures; the ceiling height on his drawings was 226 centimetres. Berlin's authorities wanted 250. It sounds like a quibble, and it was not. Raising the ceiling broke the golden-section relationship between an apartment's height of 266 centimetres and its width of 399, so the apartments had to be widened again to restore it — which made the entire building wider and taller than drawn. The argument ran at length and in public, through the newspapers of the day. Corbusier lost, and the loss propagated outward into every dimension of the block. The one place his 226 centimetres survives is the wide internal street, the rue intérieure, that runs down the middle of each access floor.

## What Was Taken Out

The deletions went further. Marseille and Nantes had an accessible roof terrace, a kindergarten, shops and businesses on the fifth floor, social services threaded through the structure — the whole point of a Unité was that it worked as a vertical village. Berlin's authorities and the developer cancelled nearly all of it. Corbusier argued hard to keep them and lost again, undone by contracts that left him only the exterior while the interior went to the city. The Berlin Building Department ruled that a kitchen could not legally open into a living room, so the two-level gallery was closed off, every area walled in and doors fitted throughout the plan. Jean Prouvé's steel and timber staircases and Charlotte Perriand's kitchens, drawn in 1956, were dropped. Most of the split-level units were cut in half into one-bedroom flats, leaving full-size Corbusier apartments only on the top two floors. Marseille has 337 units. Berlin ended with 530.

## Flatowallee 16

So he disowned it, and the building carried on without him. It opened as social housing let only to applicants who could prove their income; in 1979 it was privatised and sold off, with sitting tenants given first refusal. The condominium board has been renovating ever since — a car park, an entrance lobby, façades taken one at a time, the south face finished and the west in progress. The exterior colours came from Corbusier working with Fernand Léger, drawn from the keyboard of polychromy he had assembled in 1931, and the only surviving record of the Berlin scheme is a painted wooden model held in the Berlinische Galerie. Berlin placed the building under monument protection in 1994, and in 1997 the street outside was renamed Flatowallee; the address is number 16. A small shop in the lobby is all that is left of the communal life he wanted. Everybody calls it the Corbusierhaus, whatever he thought of it.

## Flight Context

52.5103°N, 13.2439°E, in Berlin-Westend, borough of Charlottenburg-Wilmersdorf, on rising ground at the edge of the Grunewald. It is one of the easiest buildings in Berlin to identify from the air: a single seventeen-storey concrete slab standing alone above a green plateau, its long axis roughly north-south, with the bowl of the Olympiastadion about 600 m north-northwest and the domes of the Teufelsberg listening station about 1.5 km south on top of the rubble hill. The lattice of the Funkturm lies roughly 2.5 km east-southeast, and Heerstraße runs east from the site in a straight line toward the city centre. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is about 13 nm southeast and Schönhagen (EDAZ) about 19 nm south-southwest. The site sits under Berlin's controlled airspace with EDDB traffic overhead, so coordinate before transiting. Afternoon light from the west brings out the béton brut texture and the coloured balcony recesses; flat overcast washes the façade out completely.
