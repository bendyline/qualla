# 1959 German Grand Prix

The only Formula One World Championship race ever run in two heats, on the fastest circuit in the world, in a city two years away from being walled in half — and the day after it killed Jean Behra.

**Location:** 52.4806, 13.2514

**Categories:** Motorsport, Formula-One, Berlin, Cold-War, 1950s

## Introduction

Formula One has held more than a thousand World Championship races and exactly one of them was scored on aggregate. On 2 August 1959 the German Grand Prix ran at the AVUS in West Berlin as two separate thirty-lap heats with a service break in between, and the reason was tyres. The circuit turned only left. Two enormous straights joined by a hairpin at one end and a brick banking at the other meant the right-hand tyres carried loads the left ones never saw, for 498 kilometres, at speeds nothing else in motorsport could match. Splitting the race in half let the teams change rubber. It also produced a Grand Prix whose winner led neither heat from flag to flag in the ordinary sense and whose result had to be added up.

## The Day Before

Jean Behra should have been in the race. The Frenchman, a former Ferrari driver, was entered in his own Behra-Porsche Special for the Grand Prix, and on 1 August he took a Porsche RSK out in the sports car support event. He was killed. Porsche withdrew its two Formula 2 cars, and that withdrawal opened two grid slots, which is how the reserve entrants Cliff Allison and Ian Burgess came to start at all. There is a cruelty in the detail that follows. Allison, in the fourth Ferrari, had set the fastest time in practice — 2:05.8, a tenth quicker than anyone — but as a reserve he was permitted to start only from the back of the field. Tony Brooks, second fastest, took pole. A man died on Saturday and the paperwork of Sunday carried on around the gap he left.

## Ferrari, Ferrari, Ferrari

Brooks led away and then went faster than he had all weekend, setting the race's quickest lap at an average of 149 mph — 240 km/h, an extraordinary figure for a Formula One car in 1959. He did not have it to himself. Masten Gregory's Cooper-Climax got in among the Ferraris in the first heat and stayed there, taking first one and then the other, the lead changing hands repeatedly along those endless straights until a big-end bolt let go on lap 23. Stirling Moss was out after a single lap with transmission failure. Jack Brabham, leading the championship, lasted fifteen. By the end of heat one the order was Brooks, Dan Gurney, Phil Hill — all three in Ferrari Dino 246s, all three teammates. The second heat lined up in that finishing order and produced a rerun, with Bruce McLaren harrying the red cars until his transfer gears failed. On aggregate it was Brooks, Gurney, Hill, then Maurice Trintignant, Jo Bonnier and Burgess.

## Hans in Luck

Hans Herrmann is the reason anyone who was there remembered this race for something other than the podium. A few laps into the second heat the German driver arrived at the approach to the southern hairpin in his BRM P25 with something badly wrong, struck the hay bales that passed for a barrier, and rolled the car. He was thrown clear and went sliding down the track while the car came apart behind him. The BRM was destroyed. Herrmann stood up without a scratch, and the escape earned him the nickname he carried for the rest of his career: Hans im Glück, Hans in Luck, after the Grimm tale. Twenty-four hours after Behra's death, on the same stretch of road, the margin between the two outcomes was nothing anybody could name.

## The Fastest Race Anyone Had Run

The overall average worked out at 143.3 miles per hour, which made this the fastest Formula One race yet run. The AVUS was reckoned the fastest motor racing circuit in the world at the time, quicker even than the Indianapolis oval — because Indianapolis at least required drivers to brake hard, and the AVUS did not ask much of a car beyond the courage to leave the throttle open. That was the appeal and it was also the objection: a circuit of two straights and a forty-three-degree brick banking with nothing at the top of it was a place where mistakes were not survivable, and the sport had already decided as much once, in 1938. Formula One did not come back. This remains the only World Championship Grand Prix ever held at the AVUS, and the only official German Grand Prix in championship history staged at neither the Nürburgring nor the Hockenheimring.

## A Grand Prix in a Divided City

The setting is easy to miss at this distance. West Berlin in August 1959 was an island inside East Germany, reachable by air and by road and rail routes the Soviets tolerated rather than guaranteed, and the Wall that would make the arrangement concrete was still two years off. A Formula One World Championship round in that city was a statement whether or not anybody framed it as one — an international sporting event held in a place the Western allies were determined to treat as ordinary. Brooks's win closed him to within four points of Brabham, 23 to 27, and set up a title fight he would ultimately lose to the Australian. Today the AVUS is the northern end of the Bundesautobahn 115 and carries commuters. Nothing about driving it now suggests that Formula One once ran here at an average of 143 miles an hour, twice, in the same afternoon.

## Flight Context

The AVUS runs roughly north–south through the Grunewald at 52.481°N, 13.251°E, and the 1959 circuit used the full length between the Funkturm interchange in Charlottenburg and the southern turn near Nikolassee. Best viewed between 1,500 and 3,000 ft. From the air it is one of the most legible features in Berlin: a pair of dead-straight carriageways cutting about 8 km through solid forest, with the Berlin–Blankenheim railway alongside and the steel lattice of the Funkturm marking the north end. The old round race control tower still stands at the tower's foot; the wooden grandstand is a protected monument. The 43-degree North Curve is gone — demolished in 1967 and buried under the autobahn interchange. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) lies about 21 km east-southeast and its controlled airspace covers the area; Schönhagen (EDAZ) is roughly 32 km south-southeast. Berlin's inner-city airspace is tightly structured — check the TMA and current NOTAMs. Low stratus and haze are common November through February.
