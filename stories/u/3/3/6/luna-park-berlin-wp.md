# Luna Park, Berlin

Europe's largest amusement park drew fifty thousand Berliners a day to the shore of the Halensee — and a motorway link road for the 1936 Olympics now runs across the site.

**Location:** 52.4960, 13.2820

**Categories:** Amusement parks, Berlin, Lost places, Weimar Republic, Entertainment history, Boxing

## Introduction

Traffic on the Halenseestraße runs over the ground where fifty thousand Berliners a day once came to be soaked, frightened and delighted. Luna Park was the largest amusement park in Europe, and for twenty-four years it occupied one lakeshore in Halensee: a water chute that dumped its boat into the lake, restaurants seating sixteen thousand, fireworks every night, jazz, boxing, an early escalator that people queued to ride simply because it was an escalator. It received its millionth visitor in 1910, its first full year under the name. Nothing of it survives above ground.

## Families Can Make Coffee Here

Around 1900 the site was a modest thing: a guest house on the east shore of the Halensee run by a businessman named Saeger, next to a popular open-air swimming pool, with a fairground attached — carousel, dice stalls, and a water slide down which a pontoon-like barge rolled into the lake. The Kurfürstendamm was still a forest path then. On Sundays families took carriages from Grunewald station, now Halensee, out into the Grunewald woods, and they stopped here on the way, under a motto that tells you everything about the economics: families can make coffee here. Bring your own. The restaurateur August Aschinger and Bernd Hoffmann, formerly head chef at the Kempinski, opened pleasure terraces on the spot, and in 1909 the whole thing was renamed Luna Park, after the Luna Park that had opened on Coney Island in 1903.

## A Fairy-Tale Palace With Towers

What went up was deliberately theatrical: towers, a broad staircase sweeping down to the lake, illumination that turned the water into a second version of the park. The model was explicit. Coney Island had shown what an amusement park could be — sensation, adventure, manufactured danger, the illusion of the whole wide world and the sense that technology could do anything at all — and Berlin built its own. The water chute ended in the lake. There was a mountain railway and a hippodrome. There was a rotating house. There was a wave pool. Berliners in their early years came at fifty thousand a day and considerably more at weekends, and sixteen thousand restaurant seats existed because on a good evening they were needed.

## What the Crowd Was Allowed to Look At

Some of the park's attractions were built around watching people who had not agreed to be watched. The wave pool acquired a nickname from the men who sat on its rim looking at women in the newest bathing costumes; the Shimmy-Treppe was a wobbling staircase with a fan at the bottom that blew women's skirts up. Both were considered comedy. Luna Park also staged Völkerschauen — "people shows" — in which men, women and children brought from Africa, Asia and the Pacific were put on display for paying German crowds, a standard feature of European amusement parks and zoos in those decades and exactly what it sounds like. They were not exhibits. They were people who had been transported thousands of miles to be stared at, and the park made money from it alongside the fireworks.

## Thirty Seconds

On 24 August 1926 a twenty-year-old fought for the vacant German light heavyweight title in the Luna Park ring. Max Schmeling knocked Max Diekmann out thirty seconds into the first round. It was Schmeling's first title, and the beginning of a career that would take him to Yankee Stadium, to the world heavyweight championship, and into a political symbolism he had not chosen. The Berlin crowd that night had no idea what it had watched. They had come out for an evening at the fair, the way they came out for the dance tournaments, the revues, the cabaret and the nightly rockets over the lake.

## A Road Straight Through It

The First World War closed the park, and the hyperinflation of the Weimar years hollowed out what reopened; by the late twenties the plant was in poor condition. A major renovation brought it back on 9 May 1929, and it worked — but it never got its old scale back. The final closure came in October 1933, and the whole complex was demolished the following year. For a short time the empty site served one last purpose: the puppeteer Alfredo Bannenberg, being hunted by the Nazis, hid in it. The regime had already decided the park was an eyesore. It was also, conveniently, in the way. The Halenseestraße was driven across the ground to give the 1936 Olympics a fast link between the Olympic Stadium, the Deutschlandhalle, the radio tower and the venues to the south. The lake is still there. The rest is road.

## Flight Context

52.496°N, 13.282°E, on the eastern shore of the Halensee in western Berlin, at the boundary of Charlottenburg-Wilmersdorf. The site is easy to find from the air even though nothing of the park remains: the Halensee is a small elongated lake immediately beside the tangle where the Stadtring motorway meets the Kurfürstendamm's western end, and the ribbon of asphalt running along its eastern side is the Halenseestraße, built over the fairground. Use the Funkturm and the Messe Berlin exhibition halls, about 1.5 km northwest, as your anchor, with the Olympiastadion a further 3 km beyond and the Grunewald forest filling the view to the southwest. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is about 11 nm southeast; Schönhagen (EDAZ) about 18 nm south-southeast. Tegel (EDDT), 5 nm north, closed to traffic in 2020 and is now a development site — do not plan on it. All of this sits inside Berlin's controlled airspace.
