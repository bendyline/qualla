# Potsdam Conference

Seventeen days at Cecilienhof in which three governments arranged the future of Germany, learned the atomic bomb worked, and signed off on the movement of millions of people.

**Location:** 52.4192, 13.0708

**Categories:** germany, potsdam, world-war-ii, history, cold-war, diplomacy

## Introduction

Three leaders sat down at Cecilienhof on 17 July 1945. Only two of them got up again. On 25 July the conference broke for two days so the British delegation could go home for the result of a general election, and Winston Churchill, who had spent five years as prime minister and arrived at Potsdam still holding the job, lost it. He never came back to the table. Clement Attlee, who had been in the room as leader of the opposition, returned as prime minister with Ernest Bevin in the foreign secretary's chair, and the conference finished arranging the postwar world with one of its three faces swapped out mid-sentence.

## Whatever Is Left of Berlin

Churchill pushed Harry Truman in May 1945 for a three-power summit in June; Truman wanted the invitation to come from Joseph Stalin instead, so it would not look as though the Americans and British were ganging up. With a nudge from Truman's aide Harry Hopkins, Stalin proposed the Berlin area, and Churchill wrote back that he would be delighted to meet in what was left of Berlin.

What was left of Berlin was not much. Potsdam, thirty kilometres southwest, still had Cecilienhof, a Tudor-revival country house the Hohenzollerns had built during the First World War, undamaged and large enough to hold three delegations. It sat inside the Soviet occupation zone, making Stalin host as well as guest.

## Nothing Was the Same as Yalta

Five months had passed since the Big Three last met, and almost everything had shifted. Franklin Roosevelt was dead and Truman had been president for three months. The Red Army now held Poland, Czechoslovakia, Hungary, Bulgaria and Romania; the Baltic states had been forcibly folded back into the Soviet Union; refugees were moving west out of all of it. Stalin had installed a communist government in Warsaw and described his grip on Eastern Europe as a defensive measure and a legitimate sphere of influence.

The British came in with a settled view of their host: Churchill's government had regarded Stalin since the early 1940s as a tyrant at the head of a vile system. Then the electorate removed Churchill mid-negotiation, and the man across from Stalin changed while the file stayed open.

## A New Weapon of Unusual Destructive Force

The Trinity device was detonated in the New Mexico desert on 16 July, the day before the conference opened, and word reached Truman in Potsdam. Some accounts suggest he had let the date slip precisely so the test result would be in hand. He told Stalin something without telling him much: the United States, he said, had a new weapon of unusual destructive force. Stalin took the news calmly and replied that he hoped Truman would make good use of it against the Japanese. He could afford to be calm. Soviet intelligence had been inside the Manhattan Project for years, and he knew more about the bomb than the man breaking the news.

On 26 July, Truman, Churchill and Chiang Kai-shek issued the Potsdam Declaration, demanding Japan's unconditional surrender or 'prompt and utter destruction'. The Soviet Union was not a signatory; it was still neutral in the Pacific war. The declaration did not mention the bomb. Prime Minister Kantaro Suzuki made no reply, which Washington read as rejection. The conference closed on 2 August. Hiroshima was bombed on 6 August, Nagasaki on 9 August.

## Redrawing a Country

The agreements on Germany were sweeping and specific. Occupation policy was framed around a list of aims that all began with the same letter in English: demilitarisation, denazification, democratisation, decentralisation, dismantling, decartelisation. The SS, the SA, the SD, the Gestapo and the armed forces were to be abolished outright, and Nazi racial laws struck from the books. Germany and Austria were each divided into four occupation zones, and Berlin and Vienna were each divided the same way, which is the sentence that produced the next forty-five years.

War criminals were to be tried; negotiators in London were already at work, and the agreement establishing the Nuremberg tribunal was signed there on 8 August. Germany's eastern border was pushed west to the Oder-Neisse line, removing East Prussia, almost all of Silesia, West Prussia and two thirds of Pomerania - roughly a quarter of the country's 1937 territory. Truman later recorded that Stalin presented this as a fait accompli, and that the Western Allies, taken by surprise, had to abandon the principles of the Atlantic Charter to accept it. Somewhere in the same set of documents, almost as an afterthought, Vietnam was partitioned at the 16th parallel.

## Orderly and Humane

One clause is worth reading slowly. German populations remaining east of the new border were to be transferred out of Poland, Czechoslovakia and Hungary, and the leaders specified that this be done in an orderly and humane manner. The provisional governments of those countries had already begun, so what Potsdam did was ratify a movement that was under way and put three respectable words on it.

The people covered by that clause were farmers and shopkeepers and schoolteachers, many from families that had lived in Silesia or the Sudetenland for centuries, and there were millions of them. Modern estimates put the number who died during the flight and expulsions at between 600,000 and 2.2 million. The Allied Control Council was assigned the logistics, distributing arrivals evenly among the occupation zones and reporting how many had already crossed. That was the machinery. The reality was columns of people on winter roads with what they could carry.

The conference ended in mutual congratulation, with the three governments confident that their collaboration would secure a just and lasting peace. Within eighteen months it was the Cold War.

## Flight Context

Cecilienhof stands at roughly 52.42 N, 13.07 E in the Neuer Garten on Potsdam's northern edge, on the neck of land between the Heiliger See and the Jungfernsee. From the air the setting is easy: the Havel widens into open water on three sides, the Glienicke Bridge crosses the channel about 2 km northeast, and the palace itself is a half-timbered English-country-house shape with tall brick chimneys set in mown lawns among old trees. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is about 40 km east-southeast; Schonhagen (EDAZ) lies roughly 25 km southeast. Morning light off the lakes is the best for picking the Neuer Garten out from the surrounding woodland.
