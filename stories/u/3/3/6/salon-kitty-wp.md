# Salon Kitty

A Berlin brothel that the SS wired for sound and staffed with women it had first arrested — an espionage operation aimed as much at Germany's own elite as at foreign visitors.

**Location:** 52.5010, 13.3115

**Categories:** espionage, world-war-ii, nazi-germany, berlin, surveillance, charlottenburg

## Introduction

The password was "I come from Rothenburg." Speak it at the door of 11 Giesebrechtstrasse and the madame would fetch a second album — twenty women who did not appear in the ordinary book. The guests who knew the phrase believed it bought them discretion. It did the opposite. Every room in the house had been wired, and in the basement a technician was cutting the conversation onto a wax disc. Salon Kitty was a brothel in one of Berlin's wealthiest districts, and it was also a listening post: an entire building turned into an instrument for recording what powerful men said when they were certain no one official was listening.

## The Choice at the Dutch Border

Katharina Zammit — everyone knew her as Kitty Schmidt — had run the salon in Charlottenburg since the early 1930s, and her guest list read like a directory of the German establishment: foreign diplomats, industrialists, senior civil servants, Nazi Party men. She could see where the country was going. Quietly, she moved most of her money to British banks, and on 28 June 1939 she tried to follow it. Sicherheitsdienst agents arrested her at the Dutch border and brought her to Gestapo headquarters, where a young counter-intelligence officer named Walter Schellenberg laid out her options: cooperate with the SD, or be sent to a concentration camp. She went back to Giesebrechtstrasse as the visible proprietor of a business she no longer controlled in any meaningful sense.

## Nine Rooms and a Workshop

The scheme belonged to Reinhard Heydrich, the SS general who ran the security apparatus and who understood better than most what could be done with a well-placed microphone. The salon's nine rooms were expanded and rebuilt to the highest standard the decade could manage; the point of the luxury was to make men comfortable enough to talk. Schellenberg had covert listening devices installed throughout and converted the basement into what the service called a workshop, where every conversation was recorded onto wax discs. The operation had two targets. Foreign visitors might let something useful slip. But Heydrich was at least as interested in his own side — a regime built on suspicion wanted to know what its generals and ministers said about it when the door closed. Around 25,000 recordings were made.

## Intelligent, Multilingual, Reliable

Staffing an intelligence operation of this kind meant recruiting, and Schellenberg's method of recruiting is the part of the story that gets glossed over in the retellings. He circulated a request to Berlin's administrative offices, marked top secret: "Wanted are women and girls, who are intelligent, multilingual, nationalistically minded and furthermore man-crazy." What followed was not an advertisement. Berlin's Sittenpolizei — the vice squad — arrested dozens of women already working as prostitutes in the city and sorted them for those judged "emotionally reliable." The ones selected were then trained: foreign languages, codes, military insignia, current affairs, and techniques for drawing information out of a man without letting him notice. They were taken into custody first and offered the work afterwards, by an apparatus that could imprison anyone it chose. Whatever the paperwork called them, they were conscripts.

## What the Microphones Heard

The salon reopened in March 1940, and selected officers and diplomats were let in on the codeword. Galeazzo Ciano, Mussolini's son-in-law and the foreign minister of Fascist Italy, proved to hold opinions of Hitler that were not remotely flattering — exactly the sort of material the operation existed to collect. SS General Sepp Dietrich asked for the entire special list for a single night and gave up nothing at all. Heydrich himself made several inspection visits; on those evenings the microphones were switched off. And the listening ran in more than one direction. A British agent, Roger Wilson, operating under cover as a Romanian press secretary named Ljubo Kolchev, noticed the German undercover staff at work. British intelligence then added taps of its own, and for a while London was hearing some of the same conversations the SD was filing away.

## The Discs Nobody Has Found

In July 1942 a British air raid destroyed the building's upper floors and the salon was shuttered. When it reopened, reduced to the first floor, the clients it drew were of no interest to the Reich, and the SD handed the business back to Schmidt with a warning about what would follow if she talked. She never did. She died in 1954 at the age of 71 without naming a single client, and not one of the 25,000 recordings has ever surfaced. The story reached the public through Schellenberg's 1956 memoir The Labyrinth, was expanded in Peter Norden's 1973 book Madam Kitty, and was then filmed in 1976 and imitated for decades afterwards — layer upon layer of sensationalism laid over an operation that was, at bottom, surveillance conducted by coercion. The business continued after the war under Schmidt's son and daughter. In the 1990s the building was used to house asylum-seekers, until local protest against the residents closed that too.

## Flight Context

The site sits at 52.501°N, 13.312°E on Giesebrechtstrasse in Charlottenburg, western Berlin, one street north of the Kurfürstendamm and about 2 km west of the Kaiser Wilhelm Memorial Church — the broken spire is the easiest visual fix in this part of the city. The Tiergarten and the Siegessäule lie 3 km east; the Funkturm and the Messe halls are 2 km southwest. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is roughly 20 km southeast; Schönhagen (EDAZ) lies about 40 km south. Recommended viewing altitude 2,000-3,000 ft for the dense Charlottenburg street grid; Berlin's controlled airspace requires clearance, and haze is common in summer.
