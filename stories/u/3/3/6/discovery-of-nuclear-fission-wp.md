# Discovery of Nuclear Fission

In December 1938 a laboratory in Berlin-Dahlem produced barium out of uranium, and the one person who could explain why had been driven out of the country five months earlier.

**Location:** 52.4481, 13.2842

**Categories:** Science history, Physics, Chemistry, Berlin, Nuclear, Historic sites, Universities

## Introduction

Otto Hahn did not believe his own results. In December 1938, on the ground floor of the Kaiser Wilhelm Institute for Chemistry in Berlin-Dahlem, he and Fritz Strassmann bombarded uranium with slow neutrons and found barium in the residue. Barium is roughly forty percent lighter than uranium. Nothing in forty years of radioactivity research explained how a nucleus could shed that much of itself. They wrote it up anyway on 22 December, hedging as hard as a chemist can hedge: as chemists, Hahn admitted, the evidence forced them to write barium - but "as nuclear chemists fairly close to physics we cannot yet bring ourselves to take this step which contradicts all previous experience in physics." Three days earlier he had posted the problem to the one physicist who could solve it, at an address in Sweden.

## The Ground Floor at Dahlem

For years the Berlin work had been a three-person operation. Lise Meitner ran the physics, Hahn the radiochemistry, and from 1935 Fritz Strassmann did most of the analytical chemistry, hired at half pay out of the director's discretionary fund because Meitner argued for him. Strassmann had turned down a well-paid industrial post that required political training and Nazi Party membership, and had resigned from the Society of German Chemists when it was absorbed into the German Labour Front. The cost of those two decisions was his career: no industry work, and no habilitation, which in Germany meant no independent research position. Their shared politics isolated all three inside the institute and pushed them closer to one another. It also, Hahn observed later, left them more time at the bench, since administration had been handed down to their assistants.

## Ten Marks and Summer Clothes

Meitner was Jewish and never pretended otherwise. The 1933 law that purged Jewish people from German public employment initially passed over her on technicalities: hired before 1914, a veteran of the First World War, an Austrian rather than a German citizen, at an institute that was half private. She lost her adjunct professorship at the University of Berlin regardless. Then Germany annexed Austria on 12 March 1938 and her citizenship vanished, and with it every exemption. Denmark would not honour her Austrian passport. On 13 July she crossed into the Netherlands with the Dutch physicist Dirk Coster, carrying summer clothes and, by her own later account, ten marks. Hahn had given her a diamond ring inherited from his mother, to sell if it came to that. She reached Copenhagen, then took the train to Stockholm on 1 August. Five months later his letter about the barium found her there.

## Christmas at Kungälv

She spent Christmas 1938 in the Swedish coastal town of Kungälv with her friend Eva von Bahr, and asked her nephew Otto Frisch, a physicist at Bohr's institute in Copenhagen, to join her. Between them they turned Hahn's impossible chemistry into physics. The liquid-drop picture of the nucleus allowed a droplet to stretch until surface tension gave way, and a stretched uranium nucleus would split. Meitner calculated the energy each event would release: about 200 megaelectronvolts. Frisch returned to Copenhagen and measured the recoil of the fragments on 13 January 1939, and the pulses on his Geiger counter came out exactly the size she had predicted. Then he needed a word. He asked William A. Arnold, an American biologist working down the hall, what biologists called it when a cell divided in two. Arnold said fission.

## What Idiots We Have Been

When Frisch told Bohr, Bohr struck his own forehead and said, "What idiots we have been!" The idea had been lying around for years. Ida Noddack, an analytical chemist of considerable standing, had suggested in print in September 1934 that neutron bombardment might break a heavy nucleus into several large pieces. Her paper was read in Rome, in Paris and in Berlin, and dismissed in all three. Meitner and Frisch's note reached Nature on 16 January 1939 and appeared on 11 February; Frisch's recoil paper followed a week later. In February, Hahn and Strassmann coined Uranspaltung - uranium fission - and predicted that the reaction would throw off additional neutrons, the sentence that made a chain reaction thinkable. Bohr and John Wheeler's full theory of the mechanism was published in Physical Review on 1 September 1939, the morning Germany invaded Poland.

## Leading To, Culminating In

The 1944 Nobel Prize in Chemistry went to Otto Hahn alone. The chemistry committee admired his work and could not see why physicists thought the Meitner-Frisch explanation mattered; Strassmann was passed over under a standing preference for the most senior member of a collaboration. When the physics committee considered Meitner and Frisch in 1946, nominated by Bohr, Max von Laue, James Franck and others, it decided theory counted only when it inspired great experiments - and its chairman, Manne Siegbahn, personally disliked her. Hahn named both colleagues in his Nobel lecture, and he and Meitner remained close friends until they died, both in 1968. In 1966 the United States gave the Enrico Fermi Award jointly to Hahn, Strassmann and Meitner, the first time it went to non-Americans and the first time to a woman; she was too ill to travel to the ceremony in Vienna, so Frisch accepted it for her. Her diploma credited work "leading to the discovery of fission." Hahn's said "culminating in." The Dahlem building was renamed for Hahn in 1956. In 2010 it was renamed again, the Hahn-Meitner Building.

## Flight Context

52.4481°N, 13.2842°E, in Berlin-Dahlem, where the Hahn-Meitner Building stands on what is now the Freie Universität campus. This is not a dramatic aerial target - a low institutional block in a quiet villa quarter - so navigate to it by the surrounding landmarks instead: the dark mass of the Grunewald forest immediately west, the Dahlem botanical garden's glasshouses just south, and the Wannsee lakes beyond the treeline. Central Berlin's controlled airspace covers the area, so VFR transit generally needs clearance; 1,500-2,500 ft AGL is the useful band where permitted. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) lies roughly 13 nm southeast. Pfaueninsel and Potsdam are a short run west along the Havel, which makes a natural continuation of the flight - the laboratory, then the lakes the physicists walked beside.
