# Liebermann Villa

Max Liebermann painted his Wannsee garden about two hundred times — and those paintings are the reason the garden could be rebuilt after everything that happened to the family who made it.

**Location:** 52.4288, 13.1649

**Categories:** Museums, Berlin, Art, Historic house museums, Gardens, Jewish history, Painters

## Introduction

He painted the same garden roughly two hundred times. In 1909 Max Liebermann bought a narrow strip of about 7,260 square metres running down to the Großer Wannsee, wanting somewhere out of the noise of Berlin, and from the summer of 1910 he spent his warm months there painting whatever was in front of him. The birch path. The hedged gardens. The terrace, the lawn, the flat water at the end of it. He called the house his little castle by the lake. He was sixty-two when he built it and he worked at that garden for twenty-five years, and it is because of those canvases that the garden exists today at all.

## A Narrow Strip Down to the Water

The architect was Paul Otto Baumgarten and the house was finished in April 1910, in the lakeside villa colony of Alsen — the address is now Colomierstraße 3, formerly Am Großen Wannsee 42. The front took its cues from an eighteenth-century country house, restrained where its neighbours were not. The plan is simple and it is the whole point: the building sits across the middle of the plot and cuts the garden in two. Stand in the centre of the house and you look straight down the lawn to the water. In front there is a terrace facing the Wannsee. Along one side of the grass runs the birch path, where the birches were allowed to stand wherever they had seeded themselves. Along the other are three gardens enclosed by hedges, small green rooms. Behind the house sit a garden house and a vegetable plot. It is a painter's arrangement — a set of framed views, each one a picture waiting to be made.

## Two Hundred Versions of the Same View

Liebermann was not a marginal figure working away in a private corner. He had co-founded the Berlin Secession and led it, and from 1920 he was president of the Prussian Academy of Arts, which made him the most institutionally powerful painter in Germany. What he did with his summers was go to Wannsee and paint the same forty metres of ground over and over: the same birches in different light, the same hedges in different weather, the terrace with people on it and the terrace empty. About forty of those pictures now hang in his former studio on the upper floor of the house, which means you can stand in the room where a painting was made, look out of the window at what it shows, and compare.

## Ich Kann Gar Nicht So Viel Fressen

In 1933, watching Nazi columns march through the Brandenburg Gate below the windows of his winter house on Pariser Platz, Liebermann said the sentence Berliners still quote: "Ich kann gar nicht soviel fressen, wie ich kotzen möchte." I could not possibly eat as much as I would like to throw up. On 7 May 1933 he resigned the honorary presidency of the Academy, his seat in its senate, and his membership. The regime banned him — he was a Jew, and his work belonged to what it called degenerate art. He died on 8 February 1935, aged eighty-seven. At the funeral three days later the Gestapo had forbidden people to attend. About a hundred friends and relatives came anyway. The historian Saul Friedländer counted three non-Jewish artists among them.

## Martha

Martha Liebermann was born in Berlin on 8 October 1857, married Max in 1884, and outlived him by eight years. The Reichspost took the Wannsee house from her far below what it was worth, and even that money never reached her. Their daughter Käthe had left Germany after Kristallnacht in November 1938 with her husband and their child. Martha stayed. On 5 March 1943, the day she was to be deported to Theresienstadt, she took an overdose of Veronal. The police found her in a coma. She died five days later, on 10 March, in the Jewish Hospital in Berlin, aged eighty-five. Less than five hundred metres up the same shore, at Am Großen Wannsee 56–58, senior German officials had met on 20 January 1942 to coordinate the deportation and murder of Europe's Jews. The two houses look out at the same water.

## Rebuilt From the Paintings

The Reichspost used the villa for training. From 1944 it was a military hospital, and it went on being a hospital until 1969; from 1972 to 2002 a diving club had it. The Max-Liebermann-Gesellschaft was founded in 1995 to get it back, and the restoration that followed cost around three million euros. The museum opened on 30 April 2006. Because the garden had been documented obsessively by the man who owned it, the reconstruction had unusually good evidence to work from: the birch path, the three hedged gardens, the terrace with the lake beyond it were all replanted from his own canvases. Downstairs the house tells the family's story, including the forced sale. Upstairs, in the studio, the pictures hang facing the thing they depict.
