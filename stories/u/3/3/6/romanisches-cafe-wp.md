# Romanisches Café

Weimar Berlin's central artists' café sorted its customers into a swimmers' pool and a non-swimmers' pool — and was erased so completely that a shopping centre now stands where it was.

**Location:** 52.5053, 13.3345

**Categories:** Berlin, Weimar Republic, Literary history, Cafés, Lost places, Art

## Introduction

The most important artists' café in Berlin sorted its customers by whether they could swim. There were two rooms. The small one, about twenty tables, was the Bassin für Schwimmer — the swimmers' pool — and you sat there if you had arrived, if your books sold, if your name meant something. The big room, seventy tables, was the Bassin für Nichtschwimmer, for everybody still trying. Chess players took the gallery. Outsiders got the terrace. Nobody enforced any of it; the seating simply expressed what everyone in the room already knew about everyone else, and for the better part of two decades a very large share of German culture was produced by people moving, or failing to move, between those two rooms.

## The Building With Two Towers

The Romanisches Haus went up between 1897 and 1899 to a design by Franz Schwechten, who was at that moment also building the Kaiser Wilhelm Memorial Church directly across the square — the same architect, the same heavy neo-Romanesque manner, which is where the café got its name. The house had two towers and stood at the eastern corner of Auguste-Viktoria-Platz, today Breitscheidplatz, roughly where the Europa-Center is now. The ground floor started life as a patisserie belonging to the Hotel Kaiserhof. Its transformation came from an accident of the war: the Café des Westens, the artists' haunt that had held the crown, shut in 1915, and the crowd needed somewhere to go. They came here, and after 1918 the Romanisches was the centre of literary and artistic Berlin.

## Who Was in the Room

The roll of regulars is close to a syllabus of the Weimar Republic. Bertolt Brecht and Hanns Eisler. Otto Dix and George Grosz. Alfred Döblin, working toward Berlin Alexanderplatz. Erich Maria Remarque, Joseph Roth, Ernst Toller, Franz Werfel, Else Lasker-Schüler, Irmgard Keun, Mascha Kaléko, Joachim Ringelnatz. The critic Alfred Kerr and the satirist Kurt Tucholsky, whose verdicts could finish a career over a cup of coffee. The Yiddish writers David Bergelson and Abraham Nahum Stencl. The psychiatrist Arthur Kronfeld, the architect Konrad Wachsmann, the sculptor Hugo Lederer, the publisher Franz Pfemfert. Sylvia von Harden sat here, and so did Otto Dix, whose portrait of her — cropped hair, monocle, cigarette, marble table — became one of the defining images of the decade. Billy Wilder came as a young Berlin newspaperman, years before Hollywood.

## The Waiting Room of Genius

Berliners gave it names. In the twenties the joke was to call it the Rachmonisches Café, a pun on rachmones, the Yiddish word for pity — an acknowledgment of how many of its customers needed some. Günter Birkenfeld called it the Wartesaal des Genius, the waiting room of genius, which is exactly right about a place where the ambitious sat for hours nursing one coffee, waiting to be noticed by somebody at a better table. Erich Kästner described in 1928 how a wave of admiration went through the room whenever one of the fortunate walked in. Established artists retreated into séparées to put a wall between themselves and the ambient talent. In the gallery, meanwhile, the chess players ignored all of it and played chess.

## 1927

The end was not sudden, and the first warning came early. In 1927 the Nazis staged a riot along the Kurfürstendamm, and the Romanisches Café — a known gathering place for the left-wing intellectuals they hated, and for Jewish writers and artists — was among the targets. As the Weimar years turned violent the café began to lose its function; the conversation was no longer the point. When the Nazi Party took power in 1933, most of the regulars left the country. Brecht, Döblin, Grosz, Keun, Kaléko, Lasker-Schüler, Remarque, Roth, Toller, Werfel, Wilder — the emigration is the same list as the guest list. The café survived as a room. It did not survive as what it had been.

## Nothing to Mark It

An Allied air raid destroyed the Romanisches Haus completely in 1943, and the ruin was cleared in the 1950s. Schwechten's other building on the square met a different fate: the shattered tower of the Kaiser Wilhelm Memorial Church was deliberately left standing as a memorial, and it is still there, black and broken, one of the most photographed objects in Berlin. His café building got no such treatment. In its place stands the Europa-Center, glass and concrete from the 1960s with a rotating Mercedes star on the roof, and on Budapester Straße in front of it a clock made of coloured lamps. Order a coffee anywhere on that plaza today and you are sitting in the non-swimmers' pool.

## Flight Context

52.5053°N, 13.3345°E, on the eastern side of Breitscheidplatz at the junction of the Kurfürstendamm and Budapester Straße, in Charlottenburg. The café is long gone, but the site is one of the easiest fixes in Berlin: look for the broken octagonal tower of the Kaiser Wilhelm Memorial Church with its blue glass companion block, at the head of the Kurfürstendamm, with the Europa-Center's slab immediately east. From there the long green rectangle of the Tiergarten runs about 3 km east to the Brandenburg Gate, with the Victory Column at its centre and the Zoologischer Garten station and rail viaduct just north of the square. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is about 10 nm southeast; Schönhagen (EDAZ) about 18 nm south. Tegel (EDDT), 5 nm northwest, closed in 2020. The whole area is deep inside Berlin's controlled airspace, so plan a high transit and expect clearance requirements.
