# Sugar Museum (Berlin)

The world's oldest sugar museum spent 108 years above a Wedding laboratory telling an uncomfortable story: how a Prussian root vegetable undercut an economy built on enslaved labour.

**Location:** 52.5456, 13.3453

**Categories:** Museums, Berlin, Industrial history, Science, Colonialism, Food and drink, Mitte

## Introduction

In 1747 a Berlin chemist named Andreas Sigismund Marggraf found sugar inside a beetroot. It was a quiet, odd discovery — the same substance Europe was buying at enormous expense from the Caribbean, sitting in a root that grew perfectly well in Prussian soil. Then nothing happened for a generation. Marggraf's student Franz Carl Achard finally began producing the stuff at Kaulsdorf from 1783, presented it to King Frederick William III in 1799, and with royal backing opened the first beet sugar works in the world at Cunern in Silesia in 1801. That chain of events is the reason Berlin had a sugar museum at all, and the reason that for 108 years it was the oldest such museum anywhere on earth.

## Upstairs from the Laboratory

A sugar research laboratory had been working in Berlin under Carl Scheibler since 1867, and it grew into an Institute for Sugar Industry that opened on 8 May 1904. The museum opened the very same day, on the building's upper floor — the first institution of its kind anywhere, owed largely to Edmund Oskar von Lippmann. It stayed put in the sugar-industry quarter of Wedding, on Amrumer Straße, for the next century and more: 450 square metres, about twenty thousand visitors a year, labels in German only with an English pamphlet for anyone who asked. It had the particular charm of a specialist collection nobody had got round to modernising. Lonely Planet called it 'quirky... a surprisingly entertaining exhibit where you'll learn all about the origin of sugar and its chemistry.'

## What the Cane Cost

The most serious room was the one about cane. From the sixteenth century onward sugar was a central product of European colonialism — grown in the Caribbean because the climate suited it, refined in Europe because the colonies were legally discouraged or outright forbidden from doing it themselves. The museum showed models of the ships and traced the rise of the great refining and trading cities: Antwerp, Amsterdam, Bordeaux, Hamburg, London. It did not leave out what the whole arrangement rested on. The people who cut and crushed the cane on those plantations were enslaved, and the displays did not soften the conditions they were held in, nor those inside the European refineries, where the industry pioneered the use of migrant labour — in England mostly Germans, hired for a reputation for hard work and for tolerating the heat. The wealth this generated bought political protection. The 'West India interest' was strong enough to outlast a popular boycott: in 1791 British campaigners urged consumers to give up sugar altogether, and Gillray drew a caricature of George III and his family mocking them for it. The museum's argument was that moral pressure alone did not finish the trade. Only once sugar could be raised in a European field did the abolitionists prevail, as they did in Britain with the Slave Trade Act of 1807.

## The Root That Undercut It

The Prussian half of the story sat next door, and at heart it is a story about plant breeding. Achard spent more than twenty years at Kaulsdorf selecting beets for sugar content, and every sugar beet variety grown in the world today descends from those plants. The museum kept a model of that first works in Silesia, which showed both how far the technology still had to travel and what it was like to work inside one. A fourteen-part diorama walked visitors through the whole process as it was done at Nauen around 1920, step by step, from washing the beets onward. In a little over a century, beet turned sugar in Berlin from a luxury into a staple, and the displays followed that all the way through to the modern industry's byproducts — the molasses and bagasse that become biodegradable plastics, ethanol and yeast.

## Sugar as Everything Else

The rest of the museum belonged to the consumer. One section traced sugar from the eighteenth century onward as a status symbol displayed in silver filigree baskets, then as a medicinal cure, and finally as an everyday ingredient present in far more food than most people realise. Another, in the winter garden, dealt with alcohol: seven thousand years of fermenting sugary liquids, Sumerians brewing beer six thousand years ago, and the long line running from there to wine, whisky and brandy. And then there were the temporary shows, which had a genuine streak of eccentricity — sugar motifs on postage stamps, silver sugar vessels, and in 2002 an exhibition of Berlin's landmarks modelled in sugar, the Brandenburg Gate included.

## Moved to Kreuzberg

The museum changed hands more than once — to the city of Berlin in 1945, to the Technische Universität in 1978 — and after a year of renovation it reopened on 22 September 1989, seven weeks before the Wall came down. From 1 November 1995 it operated as a branch of the German Museum of Technology. In November 2012, aged 108 and still the oldest sugar museum in the world, it closed in Wedding. That was not the end of it. In November 2015 the collection reappeared in Kreuzberg as a modernised permanent exhibit inside the Technikmuseum, which means the sugar-cast Brandenburg Gate and the crystallisation funnel from a fifteenth-century Cypriot mill are still on public view — about six kilometres south of the building where they spent a century.

## Flight Context

52.5456°N, 13.3453°E, on Amrumer Straße in Wedding, in the borough of Mitte. The museum itself is no longer here; the exhibit moved in 2015 to the German Museum of Technology on Trebbiner Straße in Kreuzberg, roughly 6 km south-southeast, which is easy to spot from the air by the Douglas C-47 mounted on its roof. For the original site, orient on the Westhafen basin and the Berlin-Spandau Ship Canal immediately west, and the large Charité Virchow hospital campus just north. The Fernsehturm stands about 5 km southeast and gives you the city centre at a glance. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is about 12 nm southeast, Schönhagen (EDAZ) about 22 nm south-southwest. This is dense inner-city Berlin directly beneath controlled airspace and EDDB approach flows — coordinate well in advance and expect no low transit.
