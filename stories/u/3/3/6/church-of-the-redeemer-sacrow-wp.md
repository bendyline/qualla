# Church of the Redeemer, Sacrow

A king designed a church that looks like a ship moored in the Havel; a century later the Berlin Wall ran through its churchyard and its bell tower was built into the barrier.

**Location:** 52.4246, 13.0965

**Categories:** Churches, Historic sites, Potsdam, UNESCO World Heritage, Architecture, Cold War, Prussia

## Introduction

For twenty-eight years the bell tower of the Church of the Redeemer did duty as a section of the Berlin Wall. When the border went up in August 1961 it ran straight across the church's property at Sacrow, and the East German engineers who needed a barrier on that stretch found one already standing: a twenty-metre campanile of yellow-rose brick, finished in 1844. The nave was left outside it, in the strip of ground between the wall and the water. Services carried on there until Christmas Eve 1961. A few days later the interior was wrecked — almost certainly by the border troops themselves — which handed the authorities the reason they needed to seal the building off entirely and close that gap in the line.

## A King Who Drew Churches

Frederick William IV of Prussia was nicknamed the Romantic on the Throne, and he sketched buildings the way other men doodle. He had drawn a church for Sacrow long before buying the estate in October 1840 for 60,000 thalers. What he wanted was Italian and early Christian rather than German village Gothic, set in a cove where Havel fishermen could shelter in a storm, with a nave standing as a bulwark against the storms of life. The church seal spells it out in Latin — Church of the Most Holy Redeemer in the Sacred Port. His court architect Ludwig Persius turned the sketches into a building, with Ferdinand von Arnim running the work. Construction began in 1841 and the dedication was held on 21 July 1844. It cost 45,234 thalers and 27 silver groschen, a third of that spent on pushing the structure out into the lake.

## The Ship at Anchor

The nave is modest — a little over nine metres high, eighteen long, eight wide, with an apse at the east end — but a covered arcade wraps it, which from outside reads as a three-aisled basilica, and the arcade runs out onto a semicircular platform standing in the Havel. From the water, or from the Berlin shore opposite, the church looks like an old vessel tied up at the bank. At distance the detached bell tower resembles the funnel of a Mississippi steamboat, and the reflection makes the illusion worse. Inside, gold stars sit on a blue coffered ceiling and Christ is enthroned in a Byzantine apse fresco painted by Adolph Eybel in 1845. From 1842 Peter Joseph Lenné laid out the cove, the twenty-four-hectare park and long sightlines toward Glienicke, Babelsberg and Potsdam.

## The First Antenna

In the summer of 1897 the physicists Adolf Slaby and Georg Graf von Arco climbed the campanile with equipment, trying to push Marconi's wireless telegraphy further than Marconi had. What they rigged in the tower was the first German antenna for wireless communication. On 27 August their signal reached the imperial naval station across the Jungfernsee at Potsdam, 1.6 kilometres away. A plaque of green dolomite went up over the tower door in 1928, Atlas holding the globe amid lightning, to record it. The tower's oldest inhabitant is a bronze bell traditionally dated to 1406 and probably salvaged from the medieval stone church that once stood in the village. Its companions did not last: a second bell was requisitioned for armaments in 1917, and the bell cast to replace it went the same way in 1944. In December 1941, when the official German Evangelical Church called on congregations to exclude baptised Christians of Jewish descent from Protestant life, the parish that Sacrow belonged to sent back a list of signatures in protest.

## Out of Reach

After 1961 the church stood in a zone its own congregation could not enter. The free-standing cedar altar table of 1844 was destroyed, and so was the organ, along with the gallery and the carved corbels. Then came two decades of simple neglect. By the late 1970s people on the West Berlin side of the Havel could see the sheet metal peeling off the roof and plants rooting in the edges of the nave, and a campaign started there to stop the decay. Much of the credit for what followed belongs to Richard von Weizsäcker, then governing mayor of West Berlin, who negotiated at length with the church authorities on both sides and with the East German government, and offered to share the cost. Work on the exterior began in 1984. The twelve linden-wood apostle figures were taken down and stored; other wooden fittings, photographed as late as 1981, were lost. Eight hectares of Lenné's landscape were destroyed outright by the border works, and the manor park was cut up by garages, kennels and a training ground for customs dogs.

## Christmas Eve, 1989

The Iron Curtain fell in November 1989, and on Christmas Eve a service was held in the Church of the Redeemer for the first time in twenty-eight years. The interior was still a ruin. Restoration proper ran from 1993 to 1995, working from old drawings and black-and-white photographs; the apostle figures went back between the clerestory windows, though nobody now knows which one stood where. In 1992 UNESCO added the church, the park and Sacrow Manor to the World Heritage listing of the palaces and parks of Potsdam and Berlin. A new organ with fourteen registers arrived in June 2009; until the gallery front can be finished, a convincing paper mock-up stands in its place. Sacrow now belongs to a Potsdam parish, but there is regular worship again in the little ship on the water, and concerts through the year.

## Flight Context

52.4246°N, 13.0965°E, on the eastern shore of the Jungfernsee about 300 metres south of Sacrow Manor. From the air the church is small but unmistakable: a pale rectangular nave with a low pitched roof standing on a semicircular platform that juts into the water, with a separate square tower on the forecourt behind it and wooded parkland on three sides. Potsdam's centre lies only 1.2 km across the water, but more than 10 km by road, which explains a good deal about the place. Use the Glienicke Bridge, roughly 2 km southeast, and Pfaueninsel to the northeast as anchors on the Havel lake chain. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is about 15 nm east-southeast, Schönhagen (EDAZ) about 14 nm south-southeast. This is a World Heritage landscape and a quiet one — 1,500-2,500 ft AGL is enough, and morning mist on the Jungfernsee often lifts to leave the tower standing clear above it.
