# Steinstücken

An island off an island: three hundred West Berliners who needed a checkpoint to buy bread, thanks to a land deal signed in 1787.

**Location:** 52.3894, 13.1300

**Categories:** Cold War, Berlin, Berlin Wall, Borders and enclaves, Neighborhoods

## Introduction

Buying bread meant a border check. So did going to school, getting to work, or visiting a grandmother two kilometres away. For roughly twenty years, the three hundred or so residents of Steinstücken lived in the only permanently inhabited exclave of West Berlin — a handful of streets sitting about a kilometre outside the city they legally belonged to, surrounded on every side by East Germany. West Berlin was already an island, an Allied-held enclave adrift in the GDR. Steinstücken was an island off that island, one of twelve such fragments and the only one where anyone actually lived. The reason had nothing to do with the Cold War. It came down to a piece of paperwork from 1787.

## A Bargain Struck in 1787

The land had belonged to Wendisch Stahnsdorf, a village that had probably already been abandoned by the time it appeared in the register of settlements commissioned by Emperor Charles IV in 1375. In 1787 farmers from the nearby village of Stolpe bought 151 acres of it, including part of the Potsdam Forest — ground that lay outside their own municipality, which mattered to nobody at the time. A small settlement grew there from 1817 and took the name Steinstücken, after a patch of village land where stones left by the ice age had once been turned up. Stolpe joined the new rural community of Wannsee in 1898. When Wannsee was folded into the Zehlendorf borough under the Greater Berlin Act of 1920, Steinstücken came with it, even though no part of Berlin physically touched it. For twenty-five years this was a curiosity for surveyors and nobody else. Residents shopped, worked and socialised in Babelsberg, the neighbouring district of Potsdam, and thought nothing of it.

## When the Line Became a Wall

In 1945 Berlin's city boundary turned into the dividing line between the Soviet zone and the American, British and French sectors. For a couple of years it remained an open border, more administrative than physical. Then the Berlin Blockade came, two German states were proclaimed, and that outer boundary hardened into a piece of the Iron Curtain — which left Steinstücken stranded. In 1951 the GDR moved to solve the anomaly by simply taking it, sending police and military units into the exclave. The United States objected, and the forces withdrew after a few days. But the residents paid for it: from then on they were barred from entering East Germany at all. Their entire connection to the world outside their own streets ran through two East German checkpoints and about a kilometre of road. Every errand, every school day, every visit to a friend meant showing papers, twice.

## Twenty Guards and a Helicopter

After the Wall went up in 1961, Steinstücken became something the GDR had not planned for. The exclave was ringed only by barbed wire, and it was Western ground — which made it the shortest route out of East Germany that anyone could reach on foot. More than twenty East German border guards, men posted precisely to stop escapes, escaped through it themselves. The regime responded by extending proper wall fortifications around the neighbourhood to seal the gap. The Americans responded differently. On 21 September 1961, General Lucius D. Clay flew in by helicopter — a deliberate, visible assertion that this scrap of land was under Allied protection — and a US military post was established there. Soldiers were rotated in and out by air from then on, because flying over the GDR was easier than negotiating passage across it. A helicopter memorial in the neighbourhood commemorates the arrangement.

## Four Million Marks and a Sliver of Land

Fixing the problem required the agreement of all four occupying powers, which took the better part of a decade. The Four Power Agreement on Berlin, signed on 3 September 1971 by the Soviet Union, the United States, the United Kingdom and France, tidied up a whole series of irregularities along the boundary between East Germany and West Berlin. One clause transferred a narrow sliver of ground linking Steinstücken to Kohlhasenbrück, the nearest part of Wannsee. West Berlin gave up six uninhabited exclaves in return and paid four million Deutsche Marks. A road, Bernhard-Beyer-Straße, was built along the sliver in 1972, and since the new border now wrapped around it, extensions of the Berlin Wall went up on both sides of the carriageway. Residents could finally drive to the rest of their own city without stopping — down a corridor walled on the left and the right.

## The Bridge That Is Berlin, and the Ground Beneath It That Is Not

One knot refused to untie. Railway tracks cut through Steinstücken, and people crossed between the two halves of the neighbourhood on the Stahnsdorfer Straße bridge at its northern edge. East Germany would not hand over the bridge, because the tracks underneath belonged to the state-owned Deutsche Reichsbahn. The compromise negotiators reached is a small masterpiece of Cold War logic: the bridge and the airspace above it became West Berlin, while the land below it, the tracks, and the airspace between them stayed East German. Reunification dismantled the fortifications, and Steinstücken now runs seamlessly into Babelsberg with nothing to mark where the wire once stood. The old border survives as the state line between Berlin and Brandenburg — which means the bridge is still in Berlin and the ground beneath it is still in Brandenburg.

## Flight Context

Steinstücken sits at 52.389°N, 13.130°E, on the southwestern fringe of Berlin where the city meets Potsdam-Babelsberg. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is about 25 km east and Schönhagen (EDAZ) roughly 21 km south. From 1,500–2,500 ft the neighbourhood is a small pocket of housing set among woodland and rail lines, hard to distinguish from Babelsberg beside it — which is the point, because nothing on the ground now reveals the border. The nearby chain of Havel lakes and the Glienicke Bridge 3 km north make useful visual anchors. Look for the rail alignment running northeast: that is the corridor Bernhard-Beyer-Straße followed out to Kohlhasenbrück.
