# Berlin Mosque

Germany's oldest surviving mosque, built by a small Lahore Ahmadiyya mission in 1920s Wilmersdorf, shot to pieces in 1945 and rebuilt minaret by minaret by the congregation that never left.

**Location:** 52.4875, 13.3117

**Categories:** Mosques, Religious sites, Berlin, Historic sites, Architecture, Islam in Germany, 20th century

## Introduction

In August 1945 roughly two hundred Muslims gathered in the surviving rooms of the community house on Brienner Straße and held a service. Nothing about the site invited it. During the battle for Berlin, German machine-gunners had taken firing positions on the mosque's two minarets; Red Army artillery had answered, and the answer had been thorough. The minarets were snapped off to stumps and a shell had gone into the dome. But the congregation was older than the damage and had no intention of dispersing. On 24 June 1952 the Berlin imam Mohammed Aman Hobohm preached a sermon that returned the building to its purpose. The minarets took much longer. The northern one went back up in 1999, the southern in 2001.

## A Mission Sent from Lahore

In December 1921, at the annual conference in Lahore, Maulana Muhammad Ali asked his community to fund mission houses in America and Germany. The following year Maulvi Abdul Majid and Maulana Sadr ud-Din founded the Berlin Muslim Mission — the second European posting of the Ahmadiyya Anjuman Isha'at-i-Islam Lahore after Woking in England — and the congregation was established in a flat at Giesebrechtstraße 5 in Charlottenburg. Weimar Berlin had several Muslim societies and student groups, several rival mosque schemes, and a good deal of friction between them. Only one scheme was ever built. The Berlin architect K. A. Hermann designed it after Mughal models, the Taj Mahal above all: a 26-metre dome, two slender minarets set apart from the building, and a prayer hall for about four hundred. Construction ran from 1924 to 1927, services were being held there from 1925, and the formal opening came on 23 March 1928. Every mark of it was raised by donation. The mosque was declared open "equally to Muslims of all Muhammadan nations and all religious directions."

## The Manager Who Stayed in Two Congregations

From 1925 the mosque's business manager was the writer Hugo Marcus, who had converted from Judaism to Islam and went on remaining a member of Berlin's Jewish community as well. That combination was not thought strange here. The community ran a journal, the Moslemische Revue, from 1924 to 1940; its editor Sadr ud-Din published the first German translation of the Qur'an by a Muslim in 1939. Its lecture evenings drew guests from across the city's intellectual life, Albert Einstein and Martin Buber among them. Converts arrived steadily — the Austrian journalist Leopold Weiss, who became Muhammad Asad, and Elsa Schiemann-Specht, who became Aziza Asad. In January 1934 the imam married the first German couple who had both embraced Islam, Abdullah Dayer and Fatima Adaresh.

## A Double Game Under the Swastika

The Nazi state was quick to see the use of a mosque in the capital, and the building was opened more than once to the antisemitic speeches of Mohammed Amin al-Husseini, who had found a welcome in Hitler's Germany. The community played a second game underneath the first. It quietly helped Hugo Marcus, its manager of many years, escape into exile in Switzerland. Mohammed Helmy, an Egyptian physician who had come to Berlin as a student in 1922 and moved in this circle, hid a Jewish teenager, Anna Boros, through the war years; Kamal el-Din Galal, who chaired the Islamic Central Institute, helped him using papers secretly taken from the Grand Mufti's office. Helmy was barred from practising medicine in 1937, interned for a time in 1939, and forbidden to marry the woman he loved. In 2013 Yad Vashem recognised him as Righteous Among the Nations, the first Arab to be so honoured.

## Rebuilt by the People Who Pray There

In November 1959 Maulana Muhammad Yahya Butt took over the Berlin mission and stayed as imam for twenty-seven years; by the community's own account 157 people entered Islam under his leadership, and in 1962 he revived the German Muslim Society. A preservation society was founded in November 1990, the mosque was placed under monument protection in 1993, the main house was repaired in 1996, and the two minarets were finally rebuilt at the end of the decade. The bill of more than 800,000 Deutschmarks was met by the congregation itself, the Berlin monuments office, the German Foundation for Monument Protection, and one member living in the United States. On the night of 8 January 2011 someone set off homemade incendiary devices in the entrance. The damage was to property only; the man responsible was sentenced that July.

## Sixty Members, Every Friday

The Islamic Community of Berlin, the German headquarters of the Lahore movement, has around sixty members. That is a small number for a building this size, and the mosque has never had the continuous German congregation its founders imagined; with immigration to Germany drawn overwhelmingly from Turkey, a mosque rooted in the Indian subcontinent lost whatever central role it once had. Berliners tend to read it as an exotic curiosity with little to do with Islam as it is actually practised in the city. That reading misses the point that people keep turning up. Friday prayers with the khutba have been held regularly again since 2010, at one o'clock in winter and half past one in summer. The office is staffed through business hours. The doors open wide on Open Monument Day and on the Day of the Open Mosque each 3 October, and anyone who wants to see what a hundred-year-old congregation looks like can walk in.

## Flight Context

52.4875°N, 13.3117°E, on the corner of Brienner and Berliner Straße in Wilmersdorf, southwest of central Berlin. From above, the pale dome and two detached minarets sit in an otherwise unbroken grid of five- and six-storey apartment blocks — a distinctive shape but a small one, best picked out from the northeast with Fehrbelliner Platz as the anchor. The green-domed Russian Orthodox Cathedral of the Resurrection stands about 200 metres away on Konstanzer Straße, built 1936-1938; the two together make an unmistakable pair. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) lies about 10 nm southeast, Schönhagen (EDAZ) about 18 nm south. Central Berlin sits beneath busy controlled airspace, so this is a sight for an arrival or a simulator rather than a low pass; plan 2,500 ft or above and expect summer haze to flatten the dome's relief before midday.
