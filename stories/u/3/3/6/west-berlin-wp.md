# West Berlin

A subsidised, occupied, draft-exempt half-city marooned 160 kilometres inside East Germany, which spent forty-one years being a political argument and accidentally became one of Europe's great countercultures.

**Location:** 52.5000, 13.2800

**Categories:** Cold War, Berlin, Divided cities, Germany, Enclaves, Counterculture

## Introduction

The surest way for a young West German man to stay out of the army was to move to the most exposed city in Europe. West Berlin lay roughly 160 kilometres inside East Germany, reachable overland only through corridors its own government did not control. Men who lived there were exempt from the Federal Republic's compulsory military service — not by anyone's design, but because conscription law was among the things the three Allied commandants would not let apply in a city they still formally occupied. So young West Germans came for the exemption, and the counterculture they built on top of it became one of the defining features of the place.

## A City That Belonged to Nobody

The legal architecture came from the London Protocol of 1944 and the Potsdam Agreement, which put Germany under four-power administration until a government acceptable to all parties could be formed. Berlin sat deep inside the Soviet zone and was carved up the same way — American, British and French sectors in the west, Soviet in the east. The arrangement was meant to be temporary. Instead it hardened, and West Berlin ended up in a category of its own: not legally part of West Germany, possessing no sovereignty, claimed by Bonn and contested by the Eastern Bloc. Every act of its House of Representatives took effect only if the three Allied commanders-in-chief confirmed it, and its delegates to the Bundestag could not vote. Yet most West Berliners were West German citizens, and a West Berliner could stand for election in the Federal Republic through a party list — which is how Willy Brandt, once the city's mayor, went on to become Chancellor.

## Showcase of the West

Bonn paid for the anomaly, and paid heavily, treating the city as a showcase of the West and subsidising it accordingly. With about two million inhabitants, West Berlin had the largest population of any city in Germany during the Cold War era, and the money went into universities, research institutes, orchestras and rent. Some of the separateness was pure theatre. The city ran its own postal administration and issued its own postage stamps right up to 1990, even though the service was completely integrated with West Germany's and used the same postal codes. Telephones were odder still: calling East Berlin from West Berlin needed only the short code 0372, where the same call from West Germany required a full international prefix. To keep conversations with the Federal Republic off cables the East could tap, they were carried by microwave radio relay.

## Corridors

Nothing reached the city except through someone else's territory. Four transit routes crossed the inner German border on Nazi-era autobahns, all of them funnelling traffic through East German checkpoints where seals were applied to western lorries so that nobody could hide in the cargo. Three air corridors, guaranteed to the Western Allies in 1944, ran to Hamburg, Hanover and Frankfurt, and every aircraft in them was tracked from the Berlin Air Safety Center in the Kammergericht building in Schöneberg. Lufthansa was not permitted to fly to West Berlin at all; only airlines of the occupying powers could serve the city. The border itself was a cartographer's nightmare, drawn along 1920 municipal boundaries and complicated by a dozen exclaves — Steinstücken among them — pieces of West Berlin stranded in East German countryside. In 1987 the prologue and first two stages of the Tour de France were held entirely within the city: a bicycle race with nowhere to go.

## The Exempt and the Arrived

The draft exemption made West Berlin a magnet for exactly the sort of person a walled-in city might not have expected to attract. Squats, studios, all-night bars and a permanent argument with authority took root in the old working-class boroughs of the American sector, Kreuzberg above all — a district whose boundary ran flush against the Wall on two sides, which kept its rents cheap and its politics sharp. The other great migration was not German at all. Active immigration and asylum policy pulled waves of new residents through the 1960s and 1970s, and Berlin became home to at least 178,000 Turkish and Turkish German residents, the largest Turkish community anywhere outside Turkey. They arrived as guest workers on the assumption, widely shared and largely wrong, that they would eventually leave. Their children rebuilt the city's food, music and street life.

## The Offer Nobody Made

For all the rhetoric about an island of freedom, the city's own allies occasionally wondered whether it was worth the trouble. Konrad Adenauer suggested to John F. Kennedy that the United States propose trading West Berlin away — swapping it for Thuringia and parts of Saxony and Mecklenburg, with the city's population relocated to West Germany. Adenauer did not expect the Soviets to accept; he hoped the offer would lower the temperature and perhaps strain relations between Moscow and East Berlin. The Kennedy administration considered the idea seriously and never made it. Two years later, on 26 June 1963, Kennedy came instead, stopping at Checkpoint Charlie before delivering four words at the Schöneberg city hall that settled the question in public if not in the files. The end, when it came, was administrative: on 3 October 1990 West Berlin simply ceased to exist, folded into a reunified Germany as a city-state, and in January 1991 Eberhard Diepgen took office as the first elected mayor of a united Berlin.

## Flight Context

West Berlin covered the western half of the city, centred near 52.500°N, 13.280°E, and its boundary is still legible from the air. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) lies about 25 km southeast of the old western centre; Tempelhof (EDDI) and Tegel (EDDT), the two airports that made the enclave survivable, closed in 2008 and 2020 respectively and both survive as open ground. From 3,000–6,000 ft the shape of the enclave shows up as a distinct urban block bounded by lakes and forest: the Grunewald and the Havel chain on the west, Tempelhofer Feld as a bare oval in the south, and the north–south seam of newer construction through the centre marking the line of the Wall. Steinstücken, the exclave that spent decades reachable only by helicopter, sits southwest near Potsdam. Berlin's airspace is busy and controlled — coordinate with EDDB approach.
