# 1948 Gatow Air Disaster

A mid-air collision over Berlin in April 1948 killed fifteen people, hardened Cold War distrust, and helped set the stage for the Berlin Blockade that began eleven weeks later.

**Location:** 52.4740, 13.1380

**Categories:** aviation-disaster, Berlin, Cold-War, Berlin-Blockade, Soviet-Union, British-European-Airways

## Introduction

On 5 April 1948 a British European Airways Vickers Viking carrying ten passengers and four crew was descending toward RAF Gatow, on the western edge of Berlin, when a Soviet Yakovlev Yak-3 fighter collided with it. The fighter had no business being there; the testimony afterwards was that its pilot had been flying aerobatics in airspace where Soviet aircraft were not supposed to operate and where nobody at Gatow had been told to expect him. Both aircraft went down. Fifteen people were killed — ten passengers, four crew, and the Soviet pilot. Within days the crash had stopped being an accident and become an argument, and within eleven weeks the Soviet Union would seal off every surface route into West Berlin.

## The People Who Died

The Viking was registration G-AIVP, first flown the year before, on a routine BEA service. Its four crew were all former Royal Air Force personnel — men who had come through the war and stayed in aviation because flying was what they knew. Captain John Ralph and First Officer Norman Merrington, who held the Distinguished Flying Cross from his wartime service, were at the controls. Behind them sat ten passengers, most of them British, civilians moving between Berlin and the British zone on what should have been an unremarkable morning. The Soviet pilot's name is not preserved in the standard accounts. He was a fighter pilot in the third spring after a war his country had won at a cost nobody in Berlin needed reminding of, and whatever his orders or his judgement had been that morning, he was as suddenly gone as the people he killed.

## Berlin in April

Spring 1948 in Berlin was the moment when the postwar Allied alliance audibly fractured. The four wartime powers — Americans, British, French, Soviets — had been jointly governing Germany from a single occupied capital, but the relationship was unraveling. On 20 March the Soviet representative had walked out of the Allied Control Council in protest of Western plans for German economic recovery. On 31 March the U.S. Congress had approved Marshall Plan funding. Soviet troops were already starting to obstruct the supply corridors that connected West Berlin to the western zones of Germany. Soviet aircraft had started routinely buzzing British and American flights into the city, harassing them in the corridors where they were entitled to fly. The Gatow collision happened in this atmosphere, five days after the Marshall Plan vote. Whether it was an accident, as the British inquiry concluded, or a planned act of intimidation that went catastrophically wrong, it became immediately a Cold War flashpoint.

## The Standoff at the Wreckage

What happened in the minutes after the crash showed how quickly Allied cooperation had eroded. The Viking went down in the Soviet zone of occupation; the Yak crashed in the British zone. RAF fire engines and ambulances were dispatched to the Viking site and were initially admitted into Soviet territory, then asked to leave. Soviet soldiers crossed into the British zone and set up a cordon around the Yak wreckage. The British commandant of Berlin, Major-General Herbert, drove out personally and ordered the Soviets to withdraw; the officer in charge refused. Eventually a more senior Soviet officer arrived and they negotiated a face-saving compromise — one Soviet guard to remain at the Yak, one British guard at the Viking. It is the small kind of incident, repeated and repeated, that makes wars.

## Two Inquiries, Two Answers

A joint British-Soviet commission of inquiry was convened on 10 April. The Soviet representative, Major-General Alexandrov, refused to hear evidence from German or American witnesses on the grounds that only British and Soviet evidence was relevant and that German testimony was unreliable in any case. The British walked out on 13 April. They convened their own court of inquiry, heard their evidence, and concluded that the Soviet pilot was entirely at fault — he had violated quadripartite flying rules, had not announced himself to air traffic control, had likely been doing aerobatics. The Viking had been flying at 1,500 feet, well below a cloud base of 3,000 feet. The Soviets announced the opposite finding: that the airliner had emerged from low cloud and flown into the fighter, and that the fault was entirely British. Two accounts of the same ninety seconds, no shared truth, and no mechanism left for arriving at one. On 24 June 1948 the Soviet Union sealed off every surface route into West Berlin. The first transport aircraft of the airlift went in on 26 June, and kept going for fifteen months.

## Flight Context

The site lies at 52.474°N, 13.138°E, by the former RAF Gatow airfield on the southwestern edge of Berlin near the Havel. Best viewed between 1,500 and 3,000 ft — roughly the band the Viking and the Yak were operating in. British forces handed the station to the German Air Force in September 1994 and it closed to air traffic in 1995; the grounds are now the General Steinhoff Kaserne and the Bundeswehr military history museum's airfield site, whose outdoor aircraft collection is easy to spot from above, with housing on part of the remainder. The runway and taxiway pattern is still legible. The Havel and the Wannsee lakes lie immediately east, Spandau to the north, Potsdam about 10 km southwest. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is roughly 28 km east-southeast; Schönhagen (EDAZ) about 30 km almost due south. Tempelhof and Tegel are both closed. Controlled airspace covers the area — check the Berlin TMA structure and current NOTAMs.
