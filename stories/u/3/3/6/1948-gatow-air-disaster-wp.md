# 1948 Gatow Air Disaster

A mid-air collision over Berlin in April 1948 killed fifteen people, hardened Cold War distrust, and helped set the stage for the Berlin Blockade that began ten weeks later.

**Location:** 52.4740, 13.1380

**Categories:** aviation-disaster, Berlin, Cold-War, Berlin-Blockade, Soviet-Union, British-European-Airways

## Introduction

On the morning of 5 April 1948, a British European Airways Vickers Viking with ten passengers and four crew was descending toward RAF Gatow on the western edge of Berlin when a Soviet Yakovlev Yak-3 fighter — apparently doing aerobatics in the airspace, which it was not supposed to be doing — collided with the airliner. The Viking went down. The Yak went down. Fifteen people died: ten passengers, four crew, and the lone Soviet pilot. None of them were named on the front pages the next day, because their names were less important than the diplomatic crisis the crash detonated. Within ten weeks the Soviets would seal off the ground routes into West Berlin and the Berlin Airlift would begin.

## The People Who Died

The Viking was registration G-AIVP, first flown the year before, on a routine BEA service. The four crew members were all former Royal Air Force personnel, men who had survived the war and had stayed in aviation. Captain John Ralph and First Officer Norman Merrington, who held the Distinguished Flying Cross from his wartime service, were at the controls. There were ten passengers, most of them British, civilians traveling between Berlin and the British zone of Germany on what should have been an unremarkable trip. The Soviet pilot in the Yak-3 — name not preserved in the standard accounts — was, by the testimony recorded at the British inquiry, performing aerobatic maneuvers in airspace where Soviet fighters had no business being and where Allied air traffic control had no warning of his presence. He died in the collision too. Whatever his orders or his judgment had been that morning, he was as suddenly gone as the people he killed.

## Berlin in April

Spring 1948 in Berlin was the moment when the postwar Allied alliance audibly fractured. The four wartime powers — Americans, British, French, Soviets — had been jointly governing Germany from a single occupied capital, but the relationship was unraveling. On 20 March the Soviet representative had walked out of the Allied Control Council in protest of Western plans for German economic recovery. On 31 March the U.S. Congress had approved Marshall Plan funding. Soviet troops were already starting to obstruct the supply corridors that connected West Berlin to the western zones of Germany. Soviet aircraft had started routinely buzzing British and American flights into the city, harassing them in the corridors where they were entitled to fly. The Gatow collision happened in this atmosphere, six days after the Marshall Plan vote. Whether it was an accident, as the British inquiry concluded, or a planned act of intimidation that went catastrophically wrong, it became immediately a Cold War flashpoint.

## The Standoff at the Wreckage

What happened in the minutes after the crash showed how quickly Allied cooperation had eroded. The Viking went down in the Soviet zone of occupation; the Yak crashed in the British zone. RAF fire engines and ambulances were dispatched to the Viking site and were initially admitted into Soviet territory, then asked to leave. Soviet soldiers crossed into the British zone and set up a cordon around the Yak wreckage. The British commandant of Berlin, Major-General Edwin Otway Herbert, drove out personally and ordered the Soviets to withdraw; the officer in charge refused. Eventually a more senior Soviet officer arrived and they negotiated a face-saving compromise — one Soviet guard to remain at the Yak, one British guard at the Viking. It is the small kind of incident, repeated and repeated, that makes wars.

## Two Inquiries, Two Answers

A joint British-Soviet commission of inquiry was convened on 10 April. The Soviet representative, Major-General Alexandrov, refused to hear evidence from German or American witnesses on the grounds that only British and Soviet evidence was relevant and that German testimony was unreliable in any case. The British walked out on 13 April. They convened their own court of inquiry, heard their evidence, and concluded that the Soviet pilot was entirely at fault — he had violated quadripartite flying rules, had not announced himself to air traffic control, had likely been doing aerobatics. The Viking had been flying at 1,500 feet, well below the cloud base of 3,000 feet; visibility was good. The Soviets convened their own inquiry and concluded that the Viking had emerged from low cloud and was entirely to blame. Two parallel reports, two opposite conclusions, no shared truth. Ten weeks later, on 24 June 1948, the Soviet Union sealed off all surface routes into West Berlin. The Berlin Airlift began the next day.

## Flight Context

1948 Gatow Air Disaster (historical site): 52.474 N, 13.138 E, near the former RAF Gatow airfield on the southwestern edge of Berlin, Germany. Best viewed below 3000 feet. RAF Gatow ceased airfield operations in 1994 and the site is now a residential development with the Bundeswehr Military History Museum's aircraft collection on part of the grounds. The runway pattern is still partly visible from the air. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is about 30 km southeast and is now Berlin's primary commercial airport. Tempelhof (EDDI) is closed. Class C airspace around EDDB; check NOTAMs.
