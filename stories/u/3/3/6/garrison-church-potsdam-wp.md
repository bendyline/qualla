# Garrison Church, Potsdam

Potsdam's tallest building for two centuries, burned in 1945, dynamited by the GDR in 1968, and rebuilt as a tower that Germany is still arguing about.

**Location:** 52.3958, 13.0536

**Categories:** Churches, Potsdam, Historic sites, Prussia, Architecture, World War II, Reconstruction

## Introduction

For a century and a half, a tune from The Magic Flute rang out over Potsdam every half hour. The carillon in the Garrison Church tower played Üb' immer Treu' und Redlichkeit — always be true and faithful — to the melody Mozart wrote for Papageno's aria, and between the set pieces short melodies rang out roughly every seven and a half minutes, some requested by people in the street below. It was the sound of a garrison town, and it stopped on 14 April 1945, when the bells came loose one by one in a burning tower and fell eighty metres to the ground.

## A King Who Needed a Tower

The first garrison church went up between 1720 and 1722 on this spot: a square half-timbered building with a two-storey tower holding a thirty-five-bell carillon from Amsterdam. Its congregation was soldiers, many from Frederick William I's regiment of unusually tall grenadiers. Within a few years the walls cracked and the building began to tilt on the marshy ground; in 1730 it came down. Philipp Gerlach designed the replacement, consecrated on 17 August 1732. The king was preoccupied with towers — he had twice watched the nearly finished tower of Berlin's St Peter's Church collapse — so climbing the 365 steps of his own in August 1735, freshly recovered from serious illness, came as a relief. At 88.4 metres it was the tallest thing in Potsdam, one of the three spires in the view that made the city famous.

## Bach, Napoleon and the Crypt

Joachim Wagner built the organ in 1731-32, twenty-five stops across three manuals and pedal. Suitably for a military church, it had stops imitating trombones and kettledrums, mounted on carved angels beside a circling sun and a wing-beating eagle. Johann Sebastian Bach played it on his Potsdam visit and pronounced it a very fine instrument. Frederick William I built a crypt beneath the altar in 1735 and was buried in it the day after his death in 1740. His son Frederick the Great wanted to lie on the terrace at Sanssouci; against his wishes he was placed beside his father in 1786. Tsar Alexander I came down into that crypt in November 1805 with Frederick William III and Queen Louise to seal an alliance against Napoleon. Napoleon himself visited in October 1806 after Jena-Auerstedt, placing the church under his personal protection while quartering cavalry in two other Potsdam churches.

## 21 March 1933

The Nazis chose the date deliberately: the anniversary of the opening of the first Reichstag under Wilhelm I in 1871. The reopening of parliament was staged here as a ceremony of continuity, with Hindenburg standing in for the Kaiser and old Prussia handing something to the new regime. The handshake between Hitler and the president, photographed by Theo Eisenhard, became the image of the day, and commemorative Reichsmark coins showing the church were struck the year after. The congregation was less obliging than the propaganda suggested. Many of the officers who tried to kill Hitler on 20 July 1944 belonged to this parish. And Otto Becker, who served as organist here from 1910 to 1945 and gave more than two thousand concerts on the organ and carillon, was also organist of Potsdam's synagogue from 1915 — until 1933, when that ended.

## Fire, Then Dynamite

British bombers came over Potsdam on 14 and 15 April 1945. The church looked at first to have escaped, but the Lange Stall next door took a direct hit and the firestorm came in through windows broken the night before. The galleries and roof went first, then the tower, where the wooden shutters the carillon mechanism needed let the fire race upward. There was no water pressure; the firemen could only watch. What was left was a shell and a stump. In 1949 the surviving congregation renamed itself the Church of the Holy Cross, explicitly to be free of the burden of Prussian militarism, and built a chapel inside the tower ruin. Work on the tower landings was under way in 1966 when Potsdam's party leadership stopped it. A year later Walter Ulbricht asked what right the ruin had to exist in Potsdam. The city voted to remove it on 26 April 1968 — not unanimously; four delegates said no. The first blasts came on 14 May, half the tower survived a second attempt on 19 June, and the rest came down on Sunday 23 June.

## Rebuilt, and Argued Over

New carillon bells were cast from 1987 by a society founded to keep the tradition alive and handed to the city in April 1991. Since 2004 the church has belonged to the Cross of Nails community, the reconciliation network born out of the bombing of Coventry. A foundation was created on 23 June 2008 — the fortieth anniversary of the demolition — to rebuild the tower not as a Prussian monument but as a place of remembrance. Federal money followed, twelve million euros of it, and construction began in 2017. On Easter Monday 2024 a Cross of Nails chapel opened in the new tower, and on 22 August the tower itself was inaugurated with President Frank-Walter Steinmeier present, with an exhibition and a viewing platform 57 metres up. More than a hundred people demonstrated across the street against the whole project. Work on the cupola started in March 2025, due to finish in 2027. Whether a church so thoroughly used for propaganda should stand again is a question Potsdam has not settled, and the tower now exists partly to keep asking it.

## Flight Context

52.3958°N, 13.0536°E, in Potsdam's historic centre just off Breite Straße, a few hundred metres south of the Old Market. The rebuilt tower is again the tallest structure in the old town and is easy to find from the air: a slim baroque shaft rising out of low blocks, with the green dome of the St Nicholas Church and the city palace to the northeast and the Havel curving past to the south and west. The viewing platform sits at 57 metres and the tower is still growing as the cupola goes on. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) lies about 16 nm east, Schönhagen (EDAZ) about 12 nm south-southeast. Potsdam sits under Berlin's controlled airspace with EDDB approach traffic overhead, so keep well clear laterally; morning light from the east gives the tower its cleanest silhouette against the Havel.
