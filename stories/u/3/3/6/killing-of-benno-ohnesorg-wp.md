# Killing of Benno Ohnesorg

A twenty-six-year-old literature student at his first demonstration was shot in the back of the head by a West Berlin policeman in 1967 — and forty-two years later, the Stasi archives revealed who the policeman had been working for.

**Location:** 52.5106, 13.3096

**Categories:** cold-war, protest, berlin, charlottenburg, west-germany, 1960s

## Introduction

He had never been to a demonstration before. Benno Ohnesorg was twenty-six, a student of Romance and German literature in West Berlin, recently married, and his wife was pregnant with their first child. On the evening of 2 June 1967 he walked to the Deutsche Oper because the Shah of Iran was inside watching The Magic Flute and a crowd had gathered outside to object. Ohnesorg was not a militant. He was one of the people who turn up once, out of a general sense that something ought to be said. Before the evening was over he was lying in a courtyard off Krumme Straße with a bullet in the back of his head.

## The Shah at the Opera

Mohammad Reza Pahlavi's state visit to West Germany had been contentious from the moment it was announced, and by the time he reached Berlin the protests had grown large and loud. Outside the opera house on 2 June, the demonstration ran into an organised counter-demonstration — supporters of the Shah, among them agents of SAVAK, his intelligence service. The counter-demonstrators attacked. Police moved in to clear the forecourt, and a noisy evening turned into a rout: demonstrators scattered into the surrounding side streets, pursued, while the performance carried on inside. Whatever the intention had been, the effect was that a great many young Germans spent an hour learning what their own police force would do to them on behalf of a visiting monarch.

## The Courtyard on Krumme Straße

Ohnesorg ended up in the courtyard of Krumme Straße 66, one of the people who had ducked into a building to get off the street. Police were there too. They beat him. Then, with his hands raised, he was shot once in the back of the head by an officer named Karl-Heinz Kurras. A photographer was in the courtyard. The image he made was printed across the country within days and is now held by the Deutsches Historisches Museum; more than any speech or pamphlet that summer, it is what moved West Germany. A week after the killing, a funeral caravan carried Ohnesorg's coffin out of West Berlin, through the East German checkpoints, and down the transit corridor to his hometown of Hanover, where he was buried.

## Acquitted

Kurras claimed self-defence. He stood trial the same year and was acquitted on 27 November 1967, less than six months after the shooting. For a generation of West German students, that verdict did more damage than the bullet had. A young man had been killed by the state at his first political demonstration, and the state had examined itself, at length and in public, and found nothing to answer for. The country had spent twenty years insisting it had broken with its own past. To a great many people under thirty, the courtyard at Krumme Straße 66 and the courtroom that followed looked like evidence that the break had been partial at best.

## What Grew From It

The response was immediate, and it did not stop. Rudi Dutschke, the most visible figure in the student movement, led the protest actions that followed. Days after the burial in Hanover, speaking at a conference called "The University and Democracy," he argued for radical and possibly illegal action, and drew a famous rebuke from the philosopher Jürgen Habermas, who called the position "left fascism." The argument between them — how far a democracy's critics may go before they become the thing they oppose — ran through West German public life for the next fifteen years. Some of it went further than Habermas feared. An armed group founded around 1971 took the name Movement 2 June, after the date Ohnesorg died, and the Red Army Faction emerged from the same current of radicalisation. Most of that cohort, though, took the ordinary route, and the teenagers and twenty-somethings of 1967 grew up to staff the parties and ministries of the country they had marched against.

## The File Nobody Expected

In 2009, researchers working through the Stasi archives found Karl-Heinz Kurras's file. The West Berlin policeman who shot Benno Ohnesorg had been an informal collaborator of the East German secret police and a long-standing member of the SED, the ruling party on the other side of the Wall. No motive has ever been established, and the obvious theory — that the killing was ordered from East Berlin — has never had evidence behind it. Prosecutors opened a fresh investigation and closed it in November 2011: witnesses dead, memories no longer reliable, and the relevant files apparently destroyed by East German foreign intelligence in the months between the fall of the Wall and reunification. In January 2012 Der Spiegel published its own reconstruction, concluding that the shot had not been fired in self-defence, that it was probably deliberate, and that West Berlin police had suppressed what they knew. Ohnesorg had been dead forty-four years. There was no longer anyone left to charge. Outside the Deutsche Oper, on the forecourt he was cleared from, Alfred Hrdlicka's memorial to him still stands.
