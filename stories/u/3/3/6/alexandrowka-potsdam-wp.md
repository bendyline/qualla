# Alexandrowka (Potsdam)

Twelve Russian soldiers taken prisoner in 1812 never went home; a Prussian king built them a village of log houses north of Potsdam and gave each household a cow.

**Location:** 52.4106, 13.0569

**Categories:** Historic sites, Potsdam, Prussia, Russian diaspora, UNESCO World Heritage, Architecture, Museums

## Introduction

They were prisoners of war, and what changed their lives was that they could sing. Of the Russian soldiers captured during the campaign of 1812, sixty-two were still in Potsdam by October of that year, and someone had the idea of turning them into a choir. It was formally attached to the King's 1st Prussian Guard Regiment. Within weeks the ground shifted under them - Prussia signed the Convention of Tauroggen on 30 December 1812, and by the spring of 1813 was fighting alongside Russia against Napoleon - but the singers stayed where they were, performing in the king's camp. Fourteen years after the war ended, twelve of them were still living in Potsdam. Frederick William III built them a village.

## A Gift Passed Between Monarchs

Tsar Alexander I had allowed the choir to remain in Prussia and then topped it up, transferring seven grenadiers out of one of his own regiments into the Prussian king's guard, and sending more in 1815 to replace the men who had died. The friendship between the Hohenzollern king and the Romanov tsar was genuine and politically useful, and the choir was one of its decorations. Singers moved between courts the way a painting or a saddle horse might. When Alexander died in 1825, Frederick William III chose to remember him with houses. On 10 April 1826 the king issued the order, and construction ran through 1826 and 1827. He named the place Alexandrowka: a memorial to a dead friend, occupied by twelve living men who had never asked to stay in Germany at all.

## Drawn as a Cross

Peter Joseph Lenné laid the colony out as a hippodrome, an elongated oval, with two main avenues crossing inside it to form a St. Andrew's cross - the diagonal cross of Andrew the First-Called, Russia's patron saint. Read from above it is a piece of iconography. Walked through at ground level it is an orchard. Twelve homesteads sit along the curves, detached gabled houses of one and two storeys, each with a balcony and a loggia at the front and a small stable joined on by a covered passage. The log-cabin look is itself the Prussian compromise: the houses are timber-framed in the ordinary German manner, built by craftsmen from the Prussian guard regiments, with half-round logs fixed to the façades to evoke a Russian blockhouse that was never actually built that way. The roofs were a compromise too - the model called for thatch, but Potsdam chose wooden boarding, replaced at the end of the 19th century by slate. A two-storey warden's house stands slightly apart, without the generous garden the others were given.

## A Cow, and Conditions

The singers moved into fully furnished houses in 1827. Every household received a cow, and the gardens had been laid out with their needs in mind. But the gift came with a clause. A colonist could pass his holding to a male descendant and to nobody else. He could not sell it, rent it out, or borrow against it. It was a home and it was also a tether. In September 1829 a Russian Orthodox memorial church dedicated to Alexander Nevsky was consecrated on the wooded rise above the colony, and beside it stood a fourteenth house, home to a royal footman of Russian origin named Tarnowsky. Nearby, the warden of the king's tea room lived on the upper floor of another house. The royal statutes governing the colonists' obligations and privileges stayed in force until 1945.

## The Long Fade

The last of the original singers died in 1861. A century after the colony was founded only four families remained, and after land reform just two could trace a direct line back to the men who had arrived as prisoners. The last member of the Shishkoff family connected to Alexandrowka died in 2008. The place itself passed through every ownership the century could invent: private Hohenzollern property, maintained by the family once the 1st Guards Regiment on Foot was disbanded in 1919, expropriated in 1926, reordered under Soviet occupation and then the GDR, and largely returned to private hands after reunification. Since January 2005 House No. 2 has held a museum explaining how the blockhouses were built and who lived in them. The colony is part of the UNESCO World Heritage listing for Potsdam's palaces and parks - the corner of it that was never a palace, only a place where twelve men whose voices had bought them a village went on living.

## Flight Context

52.4106°N, 13.0569°E, north of Potsdam's old centre and about a mile and a half east of Sanssouci Park. From the air Alexandrowka is unmistakable once you know what to look for: an elongated oval of orchard with two avenues crossing inside it, a geometry that matches nothing else in the city. The Alexander Nevsky church sits on the wooded Kapellenberg rise immediately north; the New Garden and the Jungfernsee lie to the east. Recommended viewing altitude 1,500-2,500 ft AGL, with morning light picking out the pale log gables against the fruit trees. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is roughly 20 nm east-southeast, Schönhagen (EDAZ) about 13 nm south. The area is beneath Berlin's terminal airspace, so verify clearance before descending, and treat the Havel lake chain to the east as your primary visual reference.
