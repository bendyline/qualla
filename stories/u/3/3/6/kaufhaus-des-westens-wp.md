# Kaufhaus des Westens

A department store built for Wilhelmine Berlin's richest customers, taken from its Jewish owners, gutted by a crashing bomber, and rebuilt into the shop East Berliners walked to first.

**Location:** 52.5017, 13.3411

**Categories:** Berlin, Retail, Architecture, Nazi Germany, Cold War, Food

## Introduction

On the night of 28 January 1944, a Royal Air Force Halifax came down through the roof of Berlin's grandest department store. German defences had hit the bomber somewhere over the city, and it fell into the block on Tauentzienstraße, killing all seven men aboard. The impact took out the upper floors. The fire that followed burned out everything beneath them. By morning the Kaufhaus des Westens — thirty-seven years old, the building that had taught Berlin how to want things — was a shell with sky where its ceilings should have been. It reopened. That is the shape of the whole story: a store that keeps outliving the reasons it should not still be standing.

## The Top Five Hundred

Adolf Jandorf already had six Berlin shops by 1905, all of them selling everyday goods to ordinary buyers. The seventh was meant for a different customer entirely. A cultural weekly of the day described the plan as an attempt to satisfy "the spoiled demands of the top ten thousand, the top one thousand, the very top five hundred" — an ambition that only made sense in Wilhelmine Berlin, where new money was loud and moving westward. Jandorf followed it out to Tauentzienstraße, between Wittenbergplatz and Breitscheidplatz. He called the venture a Kaufhaus rather than the usual Warenhaus, a small distinction carrying a large claim, and he used the abbreviation KaDeWe from the first day; a company history published in 1932 traced that habit of clipping company names to American practice. Johann Emil Schaudt designed the building. It opened on 27 March 1907 with 24,000 square metres of floor.

## Aryanised

Ownership passed in June 1927 to Hermann Tietz OHG, which modernised the store and drew up plans for two more floors. Those plans died with the Nazi seizure of power, and so did far more than that. Hermann Tietz OHG was a Jewish-owned partnership. In March 1933 the firm's managing directors were summoned to a meeting with their bank creditors and officials from the Reich Economics Ministry, where the banks made clear that credit would continue only under an "Aryan" chief executive. By the end of that month the way was clear. Historians reckon the creditors took a group of more than thirty companies for under a tenth of what it was worth. The Tietz family left Germany. The new owner, Georg Karg, renamed the business Hertie — the first syllables of Hermann Tietz, the name kept for the goodwill attached to it and the family scrubbed out from behind it. The extra floors were never built.

## Seven Airmen and a Fire

By the winter of 1943–44 Berlin was a target every few nights, and the district around Wittenbergplatz had learned the rhythm of sirens and cellars. What hit the store on the night of 28/29 January was not a bomb but the aircraft carrying them, brought down over the city with its crew of seven still aboard. They died in the building. The fire finished what the impact started, and for six years one of the best corners in Berlin was rubble. The first two floors reopened in 1950; full reconstruction of all seven took until 1956. By then the store had acquired a second meaning it never asked for. It was proof of the Wirtschaftswunder, and it was an argument aimed east across the sector line about which system put goods on shelves.

## Six Floors Up

That argument is made most effectively on the sixth floor. The food hall occupies the entire level — more than thirty restaurants, bars and counters under one roof — and for a great many Berliners it is the reason to come at all. Beneath it, the rest of the building carries over 380,000 different articles across more than 60,000 square metres of retail space, which makes KaDeWe the second-largest department store in Europe after Harrods in London. On an ordinary day, 40,000 to 50,000 people come through the doors. The store is technically in the far northwestern corner of Schöneberg, though nobody says so; for eight decades it was simply the centre of West Berlin's idea of itself.

## Welcome Money

When the border opened on 9 November 1989, the East Germans who crossed were entitled to 100 Deutschmarks each — Begrüßungsgeld, welcome money, a West German payment that had existed for years and had never been claimed at anything close to this volume. Between November and December the state handed out an estimated three to four billion marks of it. Much of that money walked straight into the shops on Tauentzienstraße, and a great deal of it went up to the sixth floor, where visitors stood in front of counters of fruit and cheese and sausage without necessarily buying anything. What KaDeWe sold that month was mostly the sight of itself. The years since have been less steady than the legend suggests: the Bangkok-based Central Group bought in during 2015, the KaDeWe Group filed for bankruptcy on 29 January 2024 under the rent on its own building, and Central then bought the building outright that April for a billion euros. Through all of it, the doors stayed open.

## Flight Context

KaDeWe stands at 52.502°N, 13.341°E on Tauentzienstraße in the old western centre of Berlin. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) lies about 22 km southeast; Schönhagen (EDAZ) is roughly 35 km south-southwest. From 1,500–3,000 ft the block is easiest to find by working west from the Tiergarten: the Zoologischer Garten, then Breitscheidplatz with the broken tower of the Kaiser Wilhelm Memorial Church, then the long straight run of Tauentzienstraße southeast to Wittenbergplatz, with the store filling the block on its south side. Berlin's western districts are low and dense, so the church spire is the most reliable visual anchor. Winter haze and low stratus are common from November through February.
