# KGB Prison, Potsdam

A house built for a Protestant women's charity became the Soviet counter-intelligence remand prison for occupied Germany; the names its prisoners scratched into the cell walls are still there.

**Location:** 52.4165, 13.0644

**Categories:** Memorials, Museums, Potsdam, Cold War, Historic sites, Soviet occupation, Human rights

## Introduction

The walls talk. In the cells at Leistikowstraße 1, people scratched names, dates, tallies of days, prayers and messages for whoever would be pushed in next, and enough of those marks survived that a scholarly volume has been published on them alone — Sprechende Wände, speaking walls. That is a large share of what remains of the people held here. The Soviet counter-intelligence service that ran the house as a remand prison from 1945 kept its records elsewhere, and how many passed through it is still unknown. What is known is that about eighty of them were shot after death sentences, somewhere in Potsdam or in Moscow, at a place that has never been identified.

## A House Built for Charity

The building was put up between 1916 and 1918 by the Evangelical Church Benevolent Society, as the offices and residence of the Protestant Women's Aid. It stood on a quiet villa street in the Nauener Vorstadt, on the edge of the New Garden, and its address until 1945 was Mirbachstraße 1. After the Potsdam Conference in August 1945, Soviet forces sealed off roughly a hundred houses in the surrounding blocks and renamed the whole district Military Town No. 7. Inside it went the headquarters of Soviet military counter-intelligence, installed in what had been the Kaiserin Augusta Foundation's girls' boarding school. The house next door held the interrogation department. And the women's charity building, one door further along, became the central transit and remand prison for the Soviet zone of Germany — a job it kept for decades.

## Who Was Brought Here

Until 1955 the prison held people of several nationalities, Soviet citizens above all, and Germans as well. The German prisoners were typically arrested on suspicion of belonging to the Werwolf, the Nazi partisan movement that was supposed to fight on after the surrender, or of spying for the Western Allies from the western sectors of Berlin. Many were held for months without any legal representation at all: registered, photographed, fingerprinted, interrogated, and in a proportion of cases beaten. Soviet military tribunals then convicted them, and the evidence was usually a confession that had been extracted rather than given. Sentences ran to lengths that bore no relation to anything the accused had done. What happened afterwards varied only in direction: a Soviet special camp on German soil, or a train east to Vorkuta above the Arctic Circle and the other camps of the Gulag.

## The Service Imprisoned Its Own

It is worth being precise about who filled these cells, because the popular name for the building flattens it. The service that ran the prison from 1945 was wartime military counter-intelligence, SMERSH; only from 1954 did it become the KGB's Third Main Directorate. And the majority of those it held were not Germans but Soviet citizens. From 1955 onward, in fact, the prison held nobody else — only Soviet soldiers and the civilian staff attached to the garrisons in Germany. The charges tell you what the institution was afraid of: desertion, collaboration, or simply having grown too close to the German population living on the other side of the fence. Young conscripts a long way from home were locked up here for talking to their neighbours, and the practice continued into the mid-1980s.

## Handed Back

The prison stayed in operation until the KGB itself was dissolved in 1991, after which the building was used as a storeroom. When Russian troops withdrew from Germany in 1994 it was returned to the Evangelical Church Benevolent Society — the same organisation that had built it as a charity house seventy-eight years earlier, and which now found itself the owner of an intact Soviet prison. That inheritance was not simple. Fifteen years passed between the handover and the opening of a memorial, years spent deciding what the house was now for and who would carry the cost of keeping it.

## Speaking Walls

The building was restored in 2007 and 2008, and on 29 March 2009 the Leistikowstraße Memorial and Meeting Place opened, in a conversion designed by the Munich architect Wolfgang Brune. Brandenburg, the federal government and private donors put up 2.2 million euros. The permanent exhibition, open since April 2012, is built around the history of the prison and the individual fates of the people held in it, and its spine is the inscriptions themselves — left where they were cut, in the cells where they were cut. Since June 2023 the site has been run entirely by the Brandenburg Memorials Foundation. A history trail now leads through the surrounding streets of the former secret-service town, past the ordinary suburban villas that housed it, and admission to the memorial is free.

## Flight Context

52.4165°N, 13.0644°E, on Leistikowstraße in Potsdam's Nauener Vorstadt, on the northern edge of the city between the New Garden and the Jungfernsee. This is not a landmark from the air and never was meant to be: a plain three-storey house with a steep roof, standing among mature trees in a grid of villa streets, which is exactly why the site was chosen. Navigate to it instead by the water and the parks. Schloss Cecilienhof, where the Potsdam Conference met in 1945, lies about a kilometre north in the New Garden; the Glienicke Bridge is roughly 2.5 km northeast; the Heiliger See sits immediately east of the quarter. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is about 16 nm east, Schönhagen (EDAZ) about 13 nm south-southeast. Potsdam lies beneath Berlin's controlled airspace with EDDB approach traffic above, so plan a lateral offset and a high transit; the villa canopy hides the house completely in summer leaf.
