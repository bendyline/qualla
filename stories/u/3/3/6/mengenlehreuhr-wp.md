# Mengenlehreuhr

A seven-metre column of coloured lamps on Budapester Straße tells the time in blocks of five — and eleven letters of an unsolved CIA cipher point straight at it.

**Location:** 52.5052, 13.3389

**Categories:** Berlin, Clocks, Public art, Codes and ciphers, Engineering, West Berlin

## Introduction

Outside CIA headquarters at Langley, Virginia, a copper scroll carries four encrypted passages that the sculptor Jim Sanborn has been refusing to explain since 1990. Three have been broken. The fourth, K4, has not. In November 2010 and again in November 2014, Sanborn gave the world two words from it — characters 64 to 74 read BERLIN CLOCK. Asked whether he meant the Mengenlehreuhr, he answered: "You'd better delve into that particular clock." Then, unhelpfully, he added that there are several really interesting clocks in Berlin. The Mengenlehreuhr stands seven metres tall on Budapester Straße in front of the Europa-Center, and it does not have hands, numbers, or a face in any conventional sense. It has twenty-four glowing panels, and you read it by doing arithmetic.

## How to Read It

Start at the top. The single round lamp is the second-marker: on for odd seconds, off for even, telling you nothing except that the machine is alive. Below it are two rows of red panels, four and four. Each panel in the upper row is worth five hours, each in the lower row one hour, so two lit above and two lit below gives you ten plus two — twelve. Below those are the minutes: a row of eleven, each worth five minutes, and a row of four, each worth one. Six lit in the long row and two in the short row gives thirty plus two. Twelve thirty-two. Twenty-four fields in all — one, four, four, eleven, four — and every possible time from 00:00 to 23:59 has exactly one pattern. All eight hour lamps burning would mean twenty-four o'clock, which does not exist, so that state never appears.

## The Watchmaker's Idea

Dieter Binninger was born in 1938, trained as a watchmaker, took an engineering degree at the Fachhochschule in Bingen, and ran a firm called Videor. From 1973 he manufactured around 1,973 desk and wall versions of his set-theory clock before the big one existed. The Senate of Berlin commissioned the full-size version, and it went up on 17 June 1975 on the central reservation of the Kurfürstendamm at Uhlandstraße, in what was then West Berlin. Guinness recorded it as the first public clock in the world to tell the time by illuminated coloured fields. It is a strange kind of monument: a piece of applied mathematics standing on a traffic island, and the whole point of it is that a person walking past has to stop and add.

## North of Helmstedt

On 5 March 1991 a private aircraft, a TB10 Tobago, came down north of Helmstedt — the town on the old inner-German crossing that West Berliners had driven through for four decades to reach the rest of the country. Binninger was aboard. So were his son and the pilot. None of them survived; he was in his early fifties. Afterwards his widow could not carry the cost of maintaining the clock, and in 1995 the Senate took it out of service and removed it from the Kurfürstendamm. That should have been the end of it. A business initiative stepped in, and in 1996 the clock was re-erected about a kilometre east, outside the tourist information centre at the Europa-Center on Budapester Straße, where it has stood ever since.

## Positions 64 to 74

Kryptos is a curving copper screen of ciphertext in a courtyard at Langley, installed in 1990 and still not fully solved after more than three decades of work by professional cryptanalysts, the NSA, and an obsessive amateur community. K1 through K3 fell in the 1990s. K4 — ninety-seven characters — has held. Sanborn's released cribs are all anyone has: one section decrypts to BERLIN, and the section immediately after it to CLOCK. Whether that points to this device, to the Weltzeituhr on Alexanderplatz, or to something else entirely, Sanborn has declined to say. What is certain is that people fly to Berlin specifically to stand in front of a shopping-centre forecourt and stare at a column of lamps, hoping it will tell them something other than the time.

## Where It Stands Now

The setting is not dignified and that suits it. Budapester Straße is a busy road at the edge of Breitscheidplatz; the Europa-Center behind the clock is a slab of 1960s West Berlin optimism with a rotating Mercedes star on the roof, and the broken tower of the Kaiser Wilhelm Memorial Church stands a short walk away. The clock's colours read at a distance — the red hour blocks, the yellow minute blocks, the three red panels in the long minute row that mark quarter past, half past and quarter to so you can take a fast reading without counting. It changes every sixty seconds, and once a minute the pattern shifts in a way that looks, to anyone who has not learned the code, entirely arbitrary.

## Flight Context

52.5052°N, 13.3389°E, on Budapester Straße at the western edge of Breitscheidplatz in Charlottenburg, central Berlin. At seven metres the clock itself is far too small to see from the air, but its address is one of the most identifiable spots in the city: look for the broken octagonal tower of the Kaiser Wilhelm Memorial Church and the blue glass block beside it, with the long green rectangle of the Tiergarten running east from there to the Brandenburg Gate and the Victory Column standing at its centre about 1.5 km away. The Zoologischer Garten station and the Bahn viaduct run past the north side; the Kurfürstendamm strikes off southwest, and the clock's original site at Uhlandstraße is about 1 km along it. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is about 10 nm southeast; Schönhagen (EDAZ) about 18 nm south. Tegel (EDDT), 5 nm northwest, is closed. The area sits deep inside Berlin's controlled airspace. After dark the clock is a small point of red and yellow at street level — worth walking to rather than flying over.
