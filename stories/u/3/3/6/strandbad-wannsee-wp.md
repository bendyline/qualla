# Strandbad Wannsee

Berlin built itself a seaside on a lake, trucking Baltic sand onto 1,275 metres of Havel shoreline so that people who could never reach the coast would have one anyway.

**Location:** 52.4386, 13.1789

**Categories:** Berlin, Architecture, Historic sites, Lakes, Recreation, Modernism, Steglitz-Zehlendorf

## Introduction

The sand is not from here. Every grain of the 1,275-metre beach at the Strandbad Wannsee was brought in from the Baltic coast, because Berlin decided that people who could not afford the sea should be given one anyway. The water in front of it is the Großer Wannsee, a wide bay of the Havel, and the eastern shore is broad and shallow and warms quickly — which is why, well over a century ago, Berliners living four to a room in tenement flats began walking out here on their days off. What grew from that is now one of the largest inland lidos in Europe: eighty metres of beach depth, room for twelve thousand bathers, rows of wicker Strandkörbe, a nudist section, and a slide at the end of the shallows.

## Two Hundred Metres of Permission

Public bathing where men and women could see one another was illegal, and for a while that settled the matter. Berlin kept growing anyway — capital since 1871, its working population packed into small flats with little light and less air — and people went to the lakes whether or not anyone approved. In 1907 the Teltow district authority gave way to the pressure and permitted bathing along a 200-metre stretch of the eastern shore. The permission arrived wrapped in rules, including what you were allowed to wear, set down by police regulation. Construction began on 8 May 1907. Two years later a lease was signed with a Nikolassee merchant named Frankenthal, and the Freibad Wannsee took its first shape: a 65-metre beach for men, another for women, a 350-metre family section between them, all separated by wooden fences. Tents served as changing rooms, and a fence ran round the property to discourage anyone who had come only to watch.

## What Martin Wagner Wanted

By 1924 the city had taken the lido over, swapped the tents for thatched pavilions, installed the sanitation that had been missing all along and stretched the beach to 800 metres. It stayed open year-round now, for winter swimmers and skaters. Once the S-Bahn ran through from Potsdam to Erkner, one year counted 900,000 visitors and the old arrangements were plainly finished. Martin Wagner had proposed a permanent building as far back as 1910, and the First World War had shelved it; now, as municipal construction director, he got it back. Wagner handled the ensemble and its arrangement, his colleague Richard Ermisch the architecture. Work began in 1929 and the buildings opened in 1930. What the two had actually drawn was far larger — five more two-storey halls, a great circular restaurant, a pier with a café, a marina, an open-air theatre, a medicinal bath, a kindergarten, a guesthouse. The Depression took nearly all of it, and even the surviving four-building complex was only completed after the mayor came out to see how much already stood. About two million Reichsmark went into it.

## The Sign That Went Away for the Olympics

The long, low buildings look as though they have grown out of the sandhills behind them, and the Nazis disliked them for being 'un-German.' They disliked the lido's politics too. Fighting between rival groups and among the staff damaged its standing, and that of Hermann Clajus, director since 1926. In 1935 the municipal corporation that had run it was dissolved; management passed to Wilmersdorf, then to Zehlendorf after the administrative reform of 1937. Non-party staff were replaced by party members, and Wehrmacht and SA bands took over the entertainment. A sign barring Jews from the premises quietly disappeared during the 1936 Olympics, while the world was watching, and from 1938 the exclusion was written into law instead. Families who had spent every summer of their lives on this sand were told the water was no longer theirs.

## Pack Your Swimming Trunks

Through the war it stayed open to those still permitted to use it — 425,000 came in 1944 — and by luck the buildings came through the bombing intact. Afterwards the lido did exactly the job it had done in 1907, only for a city of rubble: 615,000 people paid the entry fee in 1947. There were closures. During the Berlin Blockade, power cuts stopped the waste pumps and the lake would have been fouled. In 1952 live fire from a nearby American range made the beach unsafe. And then, on 26 June 1951, a seven-year-old named Cornelia Froboess sang a song her father had composed, to words by Hans Bradtke. Pack die Badehose ein — pack your swimming trunks — sent a generation of Berlin children out to this beach, and it has never quite stopped.

## The Long Repair

Decline arrived slowly and from several directions at once. New public baths competed for attention, the buildings aged, rising wages let people travel further than the Wannsee, and eventually the bus service ended, leaving visitors to walk from Nikolassee station or fight for one of the few parking spaces. Restoration began in 2004 under the Stiftung Denkmalschutz Berlin, with the stated aim of putting the ensemble back as closely as possible to what Wagner and Ermisch left, forecourt included. The money has not reached everything — the old restaurant, aptly named the Lido, still stands derelict, and the showers that once sat on the roof of the colonnade are long gone. Because the listing covers the open ground and forest as well as the masonry, maintenance here is half building conservation and half forestry. The site today runs to roughly 355,000 square metres, of which 130,000 is water.

## Flight Context

52.4386°N, 13.1789°E, on the eastern shore of the Großer Wannsee, in the Nikolassee locality of Steglitz-Zehlendorf — not, despite the name, in Wannsee itself. From the air it is unmistakable: a long pale ribbon of imported sand set against the dark green of the Grunewald, with four low parallel buildings and a colonnade lying behind it and the wooded island of Schwanenwerder just off to the north. The Wannsee Conference villa is about 1.2 km southwest along the same shore, Pfaueninsel roughly 4 km west, and the Glienicke Bridge some 7 km southwest. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) lies about 13 nm southeast, Schönhagen (EDAZ) about 14 nm south. The lake sits beneath Berlin's controlled airspace with EDDB traffic above, so coordinate before transiting and keep a lateral offset. Morning light off the water is flat and bright; late afternoon sun across the Havel gives the best definition on the buildings.
