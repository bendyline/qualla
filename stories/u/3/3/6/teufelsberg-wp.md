# Teufelsberg

A hill made out of the wreckage of Berlin, piled over a Nazi college the Allies could not blow up, topped by an American listening post and now covered in graffiti.

**Location:** 52.4977, 13.2429

**Categories:** Berlin, Cold War, Espionage, Modern ruins, Street art

## Introduction

There is a hill in the Grunewald forest that did not exist in 1945. Teufelsberg stands 120.1 metres above sea level and about 80 metres above the plateau around it, which puts it above Großer Müggelberg, the natural high point on the far side of the city. Every metre of it is Berlin. Roughly 75 million cubic metres of pulverised brick, plaster and roof tile were carted out of the ruins and stacked here over twenty years. Underneath, buried rather than demolished, sits an unfinished Nazi military-technical college designed by Albert Speer. The Allies tried explosives on it. The building held. Covering it with the city's rubble turned out to be easier than blowing it up.

## A City Carted Uphill

The hill exists in this particular spot because of politics. After the attempted Communist takeover of Greater Berlin's city parliament in September 1948, the four-sector administration split, and separate governments formed for East and West. Cooperation with surrounding Brandenburg largely ended along with it. East Berlin could haul its rubble beyond the city boundary; West Berlin, ringed on three sides by territory it no longer had arrangements with, had to dump its own wreckage inside its own fence. In June 1950 the West Berlin Magistrate designated a new disposal site in the Grunewald, on top of Speer's buried college. Germany has many such Schuttberge, rubble mountains raised over its bombed cities. This one is unique for what lies beneath it. The name came from the Teufelssee, the Devil's Lake, just to the south.

## Ski Jumps and Antennas

Berliners, being Berliners, put a ski jump on it. The first, 24 metres and designed by the ski jumper and architect Heini Klopfer, opened in February 1955 while trucks were still adding to the summit. A larger one followed on 4 March 1962, with room for 5,000 spectators. By then somebody else had noticed the height. In July 1961, mobile Allied listening units began operating on Teufelsberg, having spent months testing vantage points across West Berlin for the best reception of Soviet, East German and other Warsaw Pact military traffic. Nothing else came close. The hill made of a destroyed city turned out to be the single best place in the western sectors from which to listen to the army that had destroyed it.

## The Hill

Construction of a permanent facility began in October 1963, and what grew on the summit was eventually run by the National Security Agency as Field Station Berlin: a cluster of towers under white geodesic radomes, staffed around the clock. The American soldiers who worked there called it, without ceremony, The Hill. Most of them lived in the American Sector and commuted to a site that was technically in the British Sector — one of the small absurdities of a city carved into four pieces. Their work was listening: intercepting and translating radio traffic from the other side of a border a few kilometres away. Enough of them wrote about it afterwards that Field Station Berlin has its own small literature of memoirs and novels, most of them by linguists who spent their twenties in headphones.

## Rumours Under the Domes

A site kept that secret inevitably grows stories. The most persistent held that the Americans had sunk a shaft down into Speer's buried ruins and were using them. It was never proven, and it probably began with something mundane: technicians servicing one of the first inflated radomes reached the upper levels through an airlock and a passage embedded in the structure's central column, and word of a "tunnel" got out. From there, speculation did what speculation does. One version made it an underground escape route. Another, in a landlocked city 150 kilometres from the sea, made it a submarine base.

## Whose Hill Now

When the Cold War ended, investors bought the field station from the City of Berlin, planning hotels and apartments with talk of a spy museum among the antennas. Then Berlin's building boom produced a glut, the numbers stopped working, and construction was abandoned mid-project. The site has been privately held since 1996 and carries a mortgage of nearly 50 million dollars, which is why the city has never simply bought it back. In 2006 the land-use plan reclassified the hilltop as forest, ending any prospect of building. Graffiti moved into the vacuum, and by the time the landlord opened the gates to visitors in the summer of 2016 the ruins had become one of Europe's largest street-art galleries — broken glass underfoot, torn radome skins snapping in the wind, a pitch-dark stairwell up the central tower. It received heritage protection in 2018. The veterans of Field Station Berlin, meanwhile, have campaigned since 2009 for a memorial named for Major Arthur D. Nicholson, shot and killed by a Soviet sentry near Ludwigslust on 24 March 1985 and counted as the last American military fatality of the Cold War. In September 2013 they returned to the hill and dedicated a plaque.

## Flight Context

Teufelsberg rises at 52.4977°N, 13.2429°E, in the northern part of the Grunewald forest west of central Berlin, about 8 km west-southwest of the Tiergarten. From the air it is one of the easiest landmarks in the region: a solitary rounded summit lifting out of an otherwise flat sheet of forest, with the white domes and towers of the old field station breaking above the treeline. The Havel river and the Grunewald lakes lie immediately west. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is roughly 23 km southeast; Schönhagen (EDAZ) about 35 km south. Best viewed from 1,500 to 3,000 feet — the height difference against the surrounding plateau is only about 80 metres, so from high cruise the hill flattens into the forest canopy and the domes become the only clue.
