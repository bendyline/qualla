# Battle of Rossbach

Frederick the Great defeated a French and Imperial army twice his size in ninety minutes, with most of his infantry never even firing their muskets.

**Location:** 51.2694, 11.8944

**Categories:** battles, seven years war, germany, saxony-anhalt, military history, 1757

## Introduction

Frederick II of Prussia liked to repeat, with the slightly smug pride that he could rarely entirely suppress, that he had won the Battle of Rossbach with most of his infantry having their muskets shouldered. The boast was almost true. On 5 November 1757, on a low rise in the Saxon countryside near a village whose name nobody outside Prussia could quite pronounce, Frederick used a force of about 22,000 men, of whom perhaps a quarter were ever directly engaged, to break a combined French and Imperial army of more than 41,000. The whole battle took roughly ninety minutes. The Prussians lost something like 550 men killed and wounded; the allies lost ten thousand, more than half of those captured or simply missing in the panicked retreat. It was the kind of result that does not happen often in war, and it gave the eighteenth century a vivid object lesson in what a well-trained, well-led, properly handled army could do to a poorly trained one with an inattentive commander.

## The Reichsarmee Was Not Ready

The Holy Roman Empire was a peculiar entity by 1757, a patchwork of more than three hundred German principalities, free cities, and ecclesiastical states held together loosely under the Habsburg emperor in Vienna. When Empress Maria Theresa wanted an army to march alongside her French allies against Frederick of Prussia, she had to ask the constituent states to send contingents. They did, reluctantly. The result was the Reichsarmee, an assemblage of small units from many small states, many Protestant and skeptical about fighting other Protestants on behalf of Catholic Vienna, most without proper training, most poorly equipped, none used to working together. Their commander, Joseph of Saxe-Hildburghausen, was a cautious man with a reputation for slowness. The French troops attached to him under the Prince of Soubise were better, but Soubise himself was not Napoleon. The whole expedition had advanced into Saxony with a vague plan to invade Prussia and a deep underestimation of the man waiting for them.

## The Wheeling Pivot

On the morning of 5 November, Soubise and Saxe-Hildburghausen decided to maneuver around the Prussian camp at Rossbach and attack from the south. They marched their columns in a long, slow wheeling movement across Frederick's front, not bothering to scout ahead very carefully because they were sure the Prussians would simply retreat in the face of their numerical superiority. They were doing what amounted to a parade march in the presence of a serpent. Frederick watched them from the roof of a peasant cottage and read the situation immediately. He let the allies finish most of their wheel, then sent the Prussian cavalry general Friedrich Wilhelm von Seydlitz, with thirty-eight squadrons of cavalry, in a screened movement around the low ridge of the Janus Hill, to come down on the head of the marching allied column from a direction nobody was watching. At the same time, Colonel Karl Friedrich von Moller's mobile artillery, eighteen guns trained to deploy and redeploy at speed, took up position on the heights. Three Prussian infantry battalions formed up in support.

## Ninety Minutes

Seydlitz, just turned thirty-six, was about to demonstrate why Frederick had passed over two senior generals to give him command of the cavalry. His thirty-eight squadrons came down on the head of the allied column at full gallop, riders knee-to-knee, horses shoulder-to-shoulder, and shattered it. The French and Imperial cavalry tried to form for a countercharge and were broken before they could organize. The infantry behind them, still strung out in marching column, never had a chance to deploy into line. Moller's guns came up and began enfilading the column from the heights. The Prussian infantry battalions, advancing with their famous four-volleys-a-minute discipline, finished what the cavalry had started. Within ninety minutes the allied army was running. Some units retreated in good order; many simply dissolved. The French general Custine and the Imperial Prince Saxe-Hildburghausen barely escaped capture. Roughly 5,000 allied troops were taken prisoner; 6,600 French and 3,552 Imperials were killed, wounded, or missing. The Prussian losses, depending on which careful count you trust, were somewhere between 169 and 500 men. Frederick was right that most of his infantry had not fired.

## What Rossbach Meant

The battle had two large consequences. The first was strategic: France lost its enthusiasm for the Prussian war almost immediately. The Treaty of Versailles in 1759 quietly reduced French commitments to the coalition against Frederick, and the French effort thereafter was directed mostly at Hanover in the west, where Frederick's brother-in-law Ferdinand of Brunswick kept them tied down for the rest of the war. This left Austria largely on her own against Prussia in central Europe. The second consequence was psychological. For ordinary Germans across many of the small states, Rossbach was strangely satisfying. The French had been raiding and burning across the Rhineland for sixty years under Louis XIV's wars; the memory was fresh. Watching a French army get thrashed by a German one was, for many, a kind of overdue retribution. Newspapers and pamphleteers across Germany celebrated. A nascent sense of German nationalism, which would matter very much in later centuries, found one of its earliest expressions in the satisfaction of seeing the French chased back across the border.

## The Lake That Drowned the Battlefield

What happened to the actual ground at Rossbach is one of those grim modern footnotes. The area was rich in lignite, the soft brown coal that powered East German industry, and from 1865 to 1990 it was strip-mined on a scale that obliterated most of the landscape Frederick fought over. Eighteen settlements and 12,500 people were resettled to make way for the open pits. The village of Rossbach itself was destroyed in 1963. After German reunification, the abandoned mine was flooded to create the Geiseltalsee, an artificial lake covering 18.4 square kilometers, now a popular swimming and recreation area in southern Saxony-Anhalt. A handful of nineteenth-century battlefield monuments survive in the surrounding villages of Reichardtswerben and Burgwerben. The county highway is named Von-Seydlitz-Strasse for the cavalry general who decided everything in those ninety minutes. Most of the rest is underwater now, fossils from a Triassic ocean rediscovered in the bottom of the pit during the flooding, and the silent water of the lake covering the field where two armies once met.

## Flight Context

Located at 51.27N, 11.89E in southern Saxony-Anhalt, near the modern town of Braunsbedra and the Geiseltalsee lake, about 30km west of Leipzig. The original battlefield has largely been destroyed by 20th-century lignite mining and replaced by an artificial lake. Visible from cruising altitude as the prominent oval lake surrounded by farmland and small villages. Nearest major airport is Leipzig/Halle (EDDP) about 30km east.
