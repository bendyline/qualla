# Protestant Reformation

A travel route through the schism that broke Western Christianity - Wittenberg's printing shops, a Wartburg attic, a Zurich sausage supper, and the pilgrim trails that outlived the pilgrimage.

**Location:** 51.8667, 12.6433

**Categories:** reformation, religious history, itinerary, germany, switzerland, europe

## Introduction

In March 1522, in a printer's house in Zurich, guests sat down during Lent and ate smoked sausage. The printer was Christoph Froschauer, the meat had hung over a year, and the portions were small - the point was never the sausage. Huldrych Zwingli, pastor of the Grossmünster, ate none of it, but when the diners were accused of heresy he preached in their defence: fasting rules found nowhere in scripture should be a matter of choice, not command. Within a few years the Swiss church had broken with Rome. The Reformation is usually narrated through theses and imperial diets. It travels better as a route - through printing shops, castle attics, market squares and kitchens.

## Wittenberg, Where the Argument Escaped

On 31 October 1517 a middle-ranking theology professor wrote to Albert of Brandenburg, archbishop of Mainz and Magdeburg, enclosing ninety-five propositions for academic debate. Whether Martin Luther also nailed them to the door of Wittenberg's All Saints' Church has never been proven, though it is hardly improbable. What happened next is not in doubt. Wittenberg was a printing town, and movable type carried the theses across Western Europe within months. The place is still built around that argument: the castle church, the Town Church where Luther preached, the Lutherhaus in the old Augustinian monastery, and the Melanchthonhaus next door. Together with the Luther sites in Eisleben they form a UNESCO World Heritage listing. Each June the town stages Luthers Hochzeit for Luther's wedding to Katharina von Bora on 27 June 1525 - a former friar marrying a nun who had fled her convent, which was exactly as scandalous as it sounds.

## From a Miner's Son to Junker Jörg

Luther was born in Eisleben in 1483, the son of a miner, and was meant for the law until a near miss with lightning in 1505 sent him into an Augustinian friary and, from there, to theology at Wittenberg. Summoned to Augsburg in 1518 and Worms in 1521, he refused to retract. Excommunicated, he burned the papal decree in public; outlawed, he was hidden by his own sovereign in the Wartburg above Eisenach. He lived there from May 1521 to March 1522 under the name Junker Jörg, grew a beard, and translated the New Testament from Greek into German in roughly ten weeks. He died in 1546 in the town where he was born.

Any honest itinerary carries the rest of it too. When Jewish communities declined to join the church he was building, Luther turned on them, and in On the Jews and Their Lies he called for synagogues to be burned and Jews expelled or put to forced labour. Hitler cited him approvingly. Since the late 20th century several Lutheran bodies have formally apologised to Jewish communities for their founder's words.

## Zurich, Marburg, Geneva

Zwingli's Zurich went its own way, attacking Lenten fasting, clerical celibacy and religious imagery, and his teaching spread through the cantons. In 1529 he and Luther met at Marburg to settle whether Christ is really present in the Eucharist. They could not agree, and that failure is why Lutheran and Reformed churches are still separate bodies. John Calvin, born in Picardy in 1509, spent most of his adult life in Geneva and gave the Reformed tradition its second name. The Reformation Wall in the university grounds was unveiled in 1909 for the four hundredth anniversary of his birth: five-metre figures of Farel, Calvin, Beza and the Scot John Knox, whose Presbyterianism has shaped the Church of Scotland since 1560. Knox's house still stands on Edinburgh's High Street. In France the Calvinists became the Huguenots, perhaps a tenth of the kingdom, thousands of them killed in the St Bartholomew's Day massacre of 1572.

## The North, Reformed From Above

In Scandinavia the Reformation arrived by royal decree, not public argument. Gustav Vasa founded the Church of Sweden at his coronation in 1527 and stripped the old church of land, silver and bells. Denmark followed in 1536 and Norway in 1537, and the Danish-Norwegian king seized the church wholesale, effectively making himself its archbishop. Catholicism was outlawed across the realm - Holstein, the Faroes, Iceland, Greenland - and Norway lost its National Council and Chancellor to become, in practice, a Danish province. Trondheim, medieval Nidaros, fell from archiepiscopal seat to provincial town; its cathedral and St Magnus in Kirkwall remain the great monuments of the Catholic north. Of roughly a thousand medieval stave churches in Norway, some thirty survived the years that followed. Finland got the better of the bargain: Mikael Agricola, sent to Wittenberg and taught by Luther and Melanchthon, went home and invented written Finnish.

## Walking It, Eating It

The Lutherwege - Luther Trails - thread through Saxony-Anhalt, Thuringia, Saxony and Hesse, linking the places where he was born, studied, hid, argued and died. There is a joke buried in them: Luther thought pilgrimage pointless, and pilgrimages play almost no part in Protestant devotion. Norway runs the older version, the pilgrimsleden to Trondheim, climbing from the Oslo area up Gudbrandsdalen and over Dovrefjell past medieval churches and lodgings that sheltered walkers before the Reformation shut the trade down. Food is not incidental here. Around Reformation Day in late October, bakeries across the central German states sell Reformationsbrötchen - yeast rolls under sugar glaze with a dab of jam. Beer is fair game too: monks brewed most of it, Luther's own Augustinians included, and he wrote warmly about it. Be sceptical, though, of taverns advertising food as it was in Luther's time - none has a documented connection to the man.

## Flight Context

Wittenberg, the anchor point of this route, sits at 51.867°N, 12.643°E on the Elbe in Saxony-Anhalt. From 3,000-5,000 ft the river's broad meanders and the castle church's distinctive round tower with its crown make the town easy to pick out on the north bank. Leipzig/Halle (EDDP) lies about 60 km south-southwest and is the practical gateway; Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is roughly 85 km northeast, and Erfurt-Weimar (EDDE) serves the Thuringian end of the trail, with the Wartburg above Eisenach at 50.966°N, 10.307°E on a forested ridge that stands out sharply in low sun. Central German weather gives its best flying visibility in spring and early autumn - the same window in which the Reformation festivals and the Reformation Day pastries appear.
