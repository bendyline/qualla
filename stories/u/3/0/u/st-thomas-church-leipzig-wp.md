# St. Thomas Church, Leipzig

The late-Gothic hall church where Bach worked for twenty-seven years, where a boys' choir founded in 1212 still sings, and where his bones were finally laid two centuries after he died.

**Location:** 51.3393, 12.3726

**Categories:** church, gothic architecture, music history, bach, leipzig, germany

## Introduction

Johann Sebastian Bach was buried in a Leipzig churchyard in 1750 and then, in the ordinary way of unremarkable men, mislaid. No monument marked the spot. When anyone finally went looking, in 1894, the search at the Johannisfriedhof had to work by inference; a coffin was opened, the skull inside was studied, and the remains were declared his. In 1900 they were moved into a vault beneath St John's Church. Bombs damaged that church in 1943 and the authorities pulled the ruin down in 1949. So in 1950, two centuries after his death, Bach was carried across the old town to the church where he had actually worked and laid in the chancel of the Thomaskirche. Later scholars have questioned whether the bones are his at all. The flowers on the slab arrive anyway.

## Silver From the Ore Mountains

There has been a church on this ground since at least the 12th century - the foundations of a Romanesque building still lie under the choir and the crossing. The choir was rebuilt in Gothic style in 1355, and then Leipzig came into money. Silver was found in the Ore Mountains to the southeast, the wealth flowed downhill into the trading city, and between 1482 and 1496 the old Romanesque nave was demolished and replaced with the late-Gothic hall church that stands today: 76 metres long and 25 wide, its walls rising 18 metres to carry a roof of startling steepness. The pitch is 63 degrees, which is less a roof than a wedge, and it lifts to a crown 45 metres up; the tower goes to 68. Martin Luther preached from this pulpit in 1539, the year the Reformation arrived formally in Leipzig, and the church has been Lutheran ever since.

## Third Choice

Leipzig did not particularly want Bach. The council offered the post of Thomaskantor to Georg Philipp Telemann first, and then to Christoph Graupner, and both preferred to stay where they were; only after two refusals did the job go to the Kapellmeister from Köthen. He took it in 1723 and held it for twenty-seven years, until his death. The work was relentless and largely administrative in the way that great art often is - he taught at the Thomasschule, trained the boys, and provided music for St Thomas and St Nicholas, with two lesser churches on the edges of his brief. Out of that grind came the cantata cycles, the Mass in B minor, and the St Matthew Passion, first heard on Good Friday, 11 April 1727. His grave went unmarked, and it took the better part of a century before anyone thought to insist in stone that the third choice had been the right one.

## Eight Hundred Years of Boys

The choir is older than the building around it. The Thomanerchor was founded in 1212, some 270 years before the present nave went up, and it is still singing, which makes it one of the oldest boys' choirs anywhere. Bach directed it. So did a long line of Thomaskantors before and after him, an office that has been held by working composers for eight centuries. The instruments have been rather less permanent. The great Sauer organ was restored by Christian Scheffler to its 1908 state over two decades of careful undoing. Alongside it stands the Woehl organ of 1999-2000, built specifically for Bach's music: its case copies the Scheibe organ he knew in Leipzig's university church, and its stops follow an instrument his relative Johann Christoph Bach designed in Eisenach, Bach's birthplace.

## The Gloriosa and the Missing Statue

Eight bells hang in the two chambers of the tower, together weighing nearly eleven tonnes. The oldest and finest is the Gloriosa, cast in 1477 by Theodericus Reinhard and incised with drawings by Nikolaus Eisenberg - five and a fifth tonnes of bronze, rung only on the highest feasts. In 2020 and 2021 the historic bells were refurbished and four new ones cast, inscribed with words drawn from Bach's motets, and on Reformation Day 2021 all eight rang together for the first time. The building underneath them has had a harder century. An Allied raid damaged the tower on 4 December 1943; sulphur from the surrounding coal country then ate at the stonework and even at the Gothic paintings inside, insects and damp went to work on the roof timbers, and in 2000 the World Monuments Fund listed the church as at risk. Outside stands a six-metre bronze of Felix Mendelssohn, who lived in Leipzig from 1835 until his death in 1847. It replaces one erected in 1892 and torn down by the Nazis on 9 November 1936 because the composer was of Jewish descent. The new statue was unveiled on 18 October 2008.

## What You Hear Now

The church is not a museum, which is the point of it. Services continue and the Thomanerchor still sings its regular motets, in a room whose shape has not changed since 1496 - broad and low-ceilinged, the sound spreading sideways rather than soaring, exactly the kind of space in which counterpoint stays legible. The coloured glass in the choir, added from 1889 onward, sets Bach and Luther among kings and kaisers. A crucifix by Caspar Friedrich Löbel is one of the few objects in the building that Bach would recognise. Everything else - the organs, the glass, the bells that ring above the door, the slab in the chancel floor - has been argued over, replaced, lost or brought back by people who could not let the music go.

## Flight Context

The Thomaskirche stands at 51.339°N, 12.373°E on the western edge of Leipzig's inner ring, the ring itself tracing the oval of the demolished city walls and making the old town obvious from the air. The church is easy to identify by its extraordinary roof: a 63-degree pitch rising to 45 metres, with a single 68-metre tower at the west end - from 2,000-3,000 ft it reads as a dark blade set beside the paler roofs around it, with the Neues Rathaus tower to the south and the vast train shed of the Hauptbahnhof to the northeast. Leipzig/Halle (EDDP) is roughly 15 km northwest, Leipzig-Altenburg (EDAC) about 40 km south. The terrain is flat lowland; morning haze is common in the Leipzig Bay in autumn, but visibility is usually generous once it burns off.
