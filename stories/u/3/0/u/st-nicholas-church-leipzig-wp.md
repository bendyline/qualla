# St. Nicholas Church, Leipzig

A Gothic church whose columns were rebuilt as palm trees in the 1780s, and whose Monday peace prayers, begun in 1982 for a congregation of almost nobody, ended up bringing down a state.

**Location:** 51.3403, 12.3786

**Categories:** Lutheran churches in Leipzig, Gothic hall churches in Germany, Churches dedicated to St. Nicholas, Peaceful Revolution, History of Leipzig

## Introduction

The columns inside the Nikolaikirche end in palm fronds. Not carved capitals suggesting foliage - actual pale green leaves, sprouting out of white palm capitals and unfolding across the ceiling, above shafts washed in a soft reddish pink. It is the single most surprising church interior in Saxony, and it is entirely deliberate: between 1784 and 1797 the city architect Johann Carl Friedrich Dauthe rebuilt the inside of a medieval Gothic church according to the theory of the French abbé Marc-Antoine Laugier, who argued that all architecture descends from a primitive shelter of tree trunks and branches, and that a building should say so. So Dauthe turned the Gothic piers into fluted classical shafts, replaced the ribbed vaults with coffered ceilings and rosettes, and put a grove of stone palms inside a church that had already been standing for six hundred years.

## The Merchants' Saint

Construction began around 1165, in a town that lived on the trade roads. The dedication is to Saint Nicholas, patron of travellers and merchants - a choice that tells you exactly what Leipzig understood itself to be. The first church was Romanesque with twin towers; it was enlarged in the Gothic style in the early sixteenth century into the broad hall church whose shape still governs the interior. The Baroque main tower went up in 1730 and the portal dates from 1759, so the exterior reads as three or four different centuries arguing politely with one another. On 3 July 1646 an infant was baptised here who would grow up to invent the calculus independently of Newton, design a mechanical calculator and coin the phrase "the best of all possible worlds": Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, the son of a Leipzig professor of moral philosophy. The church has been Protestant since 1539, and today Catholic congregations are permitted to use it as well.

## Bach's Other Building

From 1723 to 1750 Johann Sebastian Bach was Thomaskantor, and the title is misleading: he was music director for both the Thomaskirche and the Nikolaikirche, and it was the Nikolaikirche, not St Thomas, that got the premieres. The St John Passion was first heard here on Good Friday 1724, and four of the five performances Bach gave of it in his lifetime took place under these vaults - though not, for the first twenty years, under palm trees. Dauthe's rebuild came a generation after Bach's death. What Bach heard was Gothic: dim, high, stone-hard, with a very long reverberation. The performances of his cantatas by the Thomanerchor that happen here now take place in a room he would not immediately recognise.

## Open to All

On 20 September 1982 the deacon Günter Johannsen began a Monday-evening peace prayer at the Nikolaikirche. Almost nobody came. It kept going anyway, coordinated from 1986 by Christoph Wonneberger, and it kept to a rule that the parish stated plainly: offen für alle, open to all. In a country where every organisation was a state organisation, a room that admitted anyone was an extraordinary thing to maintain. Draft resisters came, then people applying to emigrate, then environmentalists, then the merely curious. State security filled the front pews with informers to crowd out the congregation, which changed nothing except that the informers had to sit through the prayers. Pastor Christian Führer, who led the church through those years, kept the door open. By September 1989 the prayers were ending in demonstrations outside.

## The Ninth of October

The church seats over 1,400. On Monday 9 October 1989 it was full hours before the five o'clock prayer. Everyone inside knew what the state had prepared: security forces on standby, hospital beds cleared, and behind all of it the crackdown in Beijing four months earlier. That evening the conductor Kurt Masur read out an appeal, co-signed by two other public figures and three local Party officials, calling for restraint and dialogue - a document that had no legal force whatsoever and turned out to matter enormously. The congregation came out carrying candles, which occupies both hands and makes violence awkward, and joined a crowd on the ring road that ran into the tens of thousands. No order to shoot was given. Local commanders pulled back rather than risk a massacre. "Wir sind das Volk" - we are the people. A month later the Berlin Wall was open.

## What Stands Outside

In 1999 a single column echoing those inside was raised on the Nikolaikirchhof, designed by the artist Andreas Stötzner: Dauthe's grove stepped out of the building and into the square where the crowds had gathered, a memorial to a revolution that killed nobody. The Monday prayers are still held. Inside, the organ Friedrich Ladegast built in 1862 remains the largest in Saxony, rebuilt with pneumatic action by Wilhelm Sauer in 1902-03 and restored to mechanical tracker action by Eule Orgelbau in 2004, with the stops Sauer had removed reconstructed. Restoration of the interior has been running since 1968 and the parish is still short of money for it, which is a fair description of most great churches. Stand under the palms on a weekday morning when it is empty and the two histories sit in the same light: an eighteenth-century argument about the origins of architecture, and an autumn when this room held the nerve of a city.

## Flight Context

The Nikolaikirche stands at 51.3403°N, 12.3786°E, inside Leipzig's inner ring in the east half of the old town. From the air the Baroque tower is the marker - a single tall spire about 400 metres north-east of the Thomaskirche's steeper Gothic tower, with the Augustusplatz and the City-Hochhaus a further 300 metres east. A pass at 2,500 to 4,000 feet over the Promenadenring frames the whole medieval core; below 2,000 feet the two church towers separate cleanly. EDDP (Leipzig/Halle Airport) is roughly 17 km north-west at Schkeuditz, elevation 470 ft, with parallel 3,600-metre runways 08L/26R and 08R/26L. EDAC (Leipzig-Altenburg, 640 ft, single runway 04/22) lies 42 km south. The old town is a controlled and busy piece of airspace under EDDP's approach paths - check the TMA before descending, and note that winter stratus over the Leipzig basin frequently hides the towers when the airport itself is clear.
