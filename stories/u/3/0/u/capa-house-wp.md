# Capa House

A Leipzig apartment building where Robert Capa photographed the death of Raymond J. Bowman, a 21-year-old machine gunner from Rochester, twenty days before Germany surrendered.

**Location:** 51.3382, 12.3421

**Categories:** Buildings and structures in Leipzig, Museums in Leipzig, World War II sites in Germany, Photography, Memorials

## Introduction

Robert Capa was standing a few paces behind the machine gun when the sniper's round came through, and he kept shooting. One frame shows Private First Class Raymond J. Bowman at the balcony rail of an upstairs flat, hunched over a Browning .30 calibre trained down the boulevard toward the bridge his division was fighting to cross. The next shows him on his back, blood running from his neck. Then Capa photographed the blood alone, spreading across the balcony floor toward the camera. The date was 18 April 1945. Bowman was twenty-one years old, from Rochester, New York, and Germany surrendered twenty days after he died.

## A Goldsmith's Grand House

Nothing about the building anticipated any of this. Jahnallee 61 went up in 1909 and 1910 as a tenement in Lindenau, a working district on Leipzig's western edge, designed by the architect F. Otto Gerstenberger in the sober, blocky German Reform style that replaced Wilhelmine ornament with mass and proportion. It was built as a grand house for the goldsmith Oskar Menzel junior, and it is a listed monument today for its architecture as much as for what happened on its balcony. That balcony was domestic infrastructure — somewhere to air the bedding, watch the street, take the evening sun. In April 1945 a machine-gun crew from the 2nd US Infantry Division found it and saw a firing position with a clear view of the bridge, and that is the only reason anyone outside Leipzig knows the address.

## Americans Still Died

Capa's sequence ran as a two-page spread in Life on 14 May 1945, under the headline "An Episode: Americans Still Died." By then the war in Europe was six days over, and the point of the pictures was exactly that timing: the killing had continued right up to the edge, and the men doing the dying were not making history, they were making it to the far side of a river. The photograph became known as The Picture of the Last Man to Die, which is the kind of title that turns a person into a category. For decades afterwards the soldier on the balcony was widely known simply as an unidentified American. German and American researchers worked at it for years; Der Spiegel was still calling it the mystery of the unknown soldier in February 2013.

## The Man Who Was Standing Next to Him

Lehman Riggs was on the gun with Bowman that morning and lived. He came back to Leipzig in 2012, and again on 17 April 2016, aged 96, for a ceremony most visitors would have understood as an event about a photograph. For Riggs it was about a man he had been kneeling beside. That day the corner of Jahnallee and Lützner Straße was renamed Bowmanstraße, a commemorative plaque was unveiled, and a small exhibition room opened inside the building. There is a Capastraße in Leipzig too. It is a rare thing for a city to give one of its street signs to a private soldier who was in town for a single day, and it is the difference between honouring an image and naming the person inside it.

## Jahnallee 61, Today

The building came through the twentieth century worn but intact, and a thorough preservation completed in 2016 by the investor Horst Langner brought it back — the surviving pieces restored, the balcony kept, part of the ground floor given over to a café with the exhibition room attached. The café changed hands and closed in September 2021. On 1 July 2023 the site reopened in the former café space as a permanent memorial and meeting place, run by CAPA Culture gGmbH with the Stadtgeschichtliches Museum Leipzig curating the permanent exhibition. It now sits among the city's principal sites of remembrance for the Nazi period, the war, and the liberation of Leipzig by American troops. What it commemorates, though, is narrower and harder than any of those headings: one young man, one morning, one shot from a window across the way, twenty days from the end.

## Flight Context

The Capa House stands at 51.338°N, 12.342°E on Jahnallee in Leipzig-Lindenau, roughly two kilometres west of the city centre. From 2,000 to 4,000 feet the geography of that April morning is legible in one glance: Jahnallee runs dead straight west out of the centre, crossing the braided channels of the White Elster and the dark ribbon of the Auwald floodplain forest — the water the 2nd Infantry Division was pushing across. Leipzig/Halle (EDDP) lies about 15 km north-northwest with parallel runways and heavy overnight cargo traffic; Leipzig-Altenburg (EDAC) is roughly 40 km south; Dresden (EDDC) is about 100 km east-southeast. Low stratus is common over the basin from November to February; the western districts photograph best in clear morning light, when the sun is behind the camera and the tenement facades are lit square-on.
