# Old Bach Monument

When Felix Mendelssohn raised the world's first monument to Johann Sebastian Bach in 1843, Bach had been dead 93 years and largely forgotten. Mendelssohn paid for it himself.

**Location:** 51.3388, 12.3714

**Categories:** monument, Leipzig, Bach, music, Mendelssohn

## Introduction

When Johann Sebastian Bach died in Leipzig in 1750, his music went out of fashion almost immediately. His sons inherited the manuscripts and treated them as professional inventory. Within a generation, the great cantatas were largely unperformed; the St. Matthew Passion sat unplayed for nearly eighty years. Bach was respected as a craftsman but considered old, intricate, contrapuntally exhausting. Then in 1829 a twenty-year-old Felix Mendelssohn conducted the St. Matthew Passion in Berlin, the first performance since Bach's death, and the city wept. Eleven years later Mendelssohn arrived in Leipzig as music director of the Gewandhaus Orchestra and decided that the world's first monument to Bach belonged in the city where Bach had spent his last 27 years — and that he would raise the money for it himself.

## Three Concerts and a Sandstone Pillar

Mendelssohn's fundraising method was elegant. On 6 August 1840 he gave a solo organ recital at the Thomaskirche — Bach's own church — playing six Bach works framed by his own improvisations. The hall was full. The following spring, on 4 April 1841, he conducted a complete St. Matthew Passion in the same church. The third concert went on the day of the unveiling itself, in 1843, with a programme of Bach orchestral and choral works at the Gewandhaus. The proceeds of the three events covered most of the cost. Mendelssohn quietly made up the shortfall from his own funds. The monument was designed by the Dresden painter Eduard Bendemann together with the sculptor Ernst Rietschel and the artist Julius Hubner, executed in Elbe sandstone, and placed directly behind the St. Thomas School where Bach had taught.

## What the Stone Says

The monument is a small thing, easy to miss in the crowded square beside the church. Eight columns rise from an octagonal base to support a square capital like a tiny stone temple. Four Romanesque arches face north, south, east, and west, each filled with a half-relief in sandstone. North: Bach's bust in profile, the only conventional portrait on the structure. West: an angel teaching boys how to sing, a quiet acknowledgment of Bach's twenty-seven years as cantor of the St. Thomas School, where his job was to teach Latin and music to children who slept in the same building they sang in. South: two angels beneath a cross, one holding the crown of thorns, the other a chalice and palm branch — the iconography of the Passions. East: an angel playing a small organ with a foot bellows, a reference to Bach the keyboardist, the man whose pedal technique was so famous in his lifetime that critics suspected witchcraft. The monument was unveiled on 23 April 1843. The Thomanerchor — the boys' choir Bach himself once led — sang Singet dem Herrn ein neues Lied, BWV 225, on the day of the unveiling.

## The Other Bach Monument

Sixty-five years later, in 1908, Leipzig built a second, larger monument to Bach a short walk away. The new one shows Bach standing, full-length, in cantor's robes, by the sculptor Carl Seffner. It became the famous Bach monument, the one in the postcards. Mendelssohn's modest 1843 pillar was demoted to the Old Bach Monument — Altes Bachdenkmal — to distinguish it. The original sandstone has been restored several times, most recently in 2005. The current finial is the third; the second has been kept since the early 1980s in the Bach Archive in Leipzig. Period engravings show that the original finial was significantly larger than the one in place today, but the proportions of the small temple have remained essentially as Bendemann drew them.

## Why It Matters That He Bothered

Mendelssohn died in 1847, four years after the unveiling, at the age of 38. The Bach revival he had helped to start was already running on its own momentum and would gather pace through the rest of the nineteenth century, eventually producing the Bach Gesellschaft edition that catalogued the surviving works and made the systematic performance of Bach possible. The Old Bach Monument is the physical artifact of a moment that almost did not happen. Mendelssohn was a busy composer and an exhausted music director; he did not need a side project. His own music would itself be banned in Germany within a hundred years of the monument's unveiling — the Nazis, who could not erase his Jewish ancestry, removed his name from concert programmes and ordered his statue in front of the Gewandhaus torn down in 1936. The Bach monument, paid for by a Jewish composer to honour a Lutheran cantor, survived because no one in 1936 quite remembered who had paid for it.

## Flight Context

The Old Bach Monument stands in the Thomaskirchhof, the small square beside the Thomaskirche in central Leipzig, at 51.339 degrees north, 12.371 degrees east. From low altitude over the city the green copper roof of the Thomaskirche is the most prominent landmark in the inner ring; the monument itself is a small octagonal pillar in the cobbled square at its south side. Best viewing altitude 1,500 to 3,000 feet AGL. Nearest airport is Leipzig-Halle (EDDP), about 18 km northwest of the city.
