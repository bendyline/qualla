# Bombing of Leipzig in World War II

Thirty-five minutes of bombs in the early hours of 4 December 1943 killed more than 1,800 people in Leipzig and burned much of the city Bach knew to ashes.

**Location:** 51.3400, 12.3750

**Categories:** World War II, Strategic bombing, Leipzig, Saxony, Germany

## Introduction

The all-clear sounded at 5:39 a.m. on 4 December 1943, two hours after the air raid warning that woke Leipzig in the dark. Between those two sirens, 442 Royal Air Force bombers had crossed the city in thirty-five minutes and dropped almost 1,400 tons of high explosive and incendiary bombs into a medieval city centre packed too tightly to burn safely. City records counted more than 1,800 dead, with some 4,000 injured. The Café Zimmermann, where Johann Sebastian Bach had once led his collegium musicum on Friday evenings, burned with the rest. So did the nave of St. John's Church, where Bach himself lay buried.

## A City the Bombers Could Reach

Leipzig had felt safe. Through the first years of the war the long flight from Britain seemed to put the city out of reach, and at 700,000 inhabitants it was the sixth-largest city of the Reich, important for trade fairs and railway junctions and, more quietly, for the Erla Maschinenwerk plants in Heiterblick, Abtnaundorf and Mockau where Messerschmitt Bf 109 fighters were assembled. Then came Kassel in October 1943, and the calculation changed. RAF Bomber Command had proven it could strike central Germany. At the turn of August and September a small raid had hit the northern districts of Eutritzsch and Schönefeld, killing four people, and on 20 October a planned raid was scattered by cloud. The major raid was simply waiting for the right night.

## A Firestorm in the Old Town

The route was designed to deceive. Bombers crossed the Dutch coast at the Zuiderzee, flew east toward Berlin as if attacking it again, then turned south. The plan was to keep Leipzig's defenders guessing about the real target for as long as possible. In the dense old centre, where buildings shared walls and timber roofs, the incendiaries built a firestorm so violent that Hans Rumpf, the Reich's general inspector of fire fighting, who happened to be in Leipzig that night, said it surpassed Hamburg. Half the local fire brigade had been sent to help in Berlin the previous night. The hoses arriving from the hinterland did not fit Leipzig's hydrants, only thirty per cent of which were standardised. The water supply collapsed. The toll would have been worse: many people ignored the order to stay in their cellars until the all-clear and fled in time or fought the fires.

## What the Fire Took

By dawn, the Old and New Theatres were gone. So was the New Trade Exchange, the Alte Waage, St. Matthew's Church, the nave of St. John's, and the Augusteum, the main building of the University. The Museum of Fine Art was destroyed. Of the University's 92 departments, 58 were hit, including the world's first psychology laboratory, founded by Wilhelm Wundt in 1879. The roof of the Old Town Hall burned out, but a concrete ceiling installed during a 20th-century renovation saved the floors below. The official tally listed 1,067 commercial buildings, 472 factory buildings, 56 schools, 29 fair buildings and nine churches destroyed, including the Anglican All Saints' Church. None of these numbers reach the people inside them.

## The Raids That Followed

The 4 December raid was not the last. During Big Week in February 1944, more than 700 British bombers struck the southern suburbs of Connewitz, Schleußig and Großzschocher in a night raid, dropping 2,300 tons. American bombers came back the same afternoon to hit the Erla plant at Heiterblick, destroying 65 per cent of the factory. About 970 more people died across that single day. The second Gewandhaus concert hall in the Musikviertel was almost completely lost. By May 1944 more than 15,000 buildings in Leipzig had been hit; about 140,000 residents were homeless. American bombers returned on 27 February 1945 in an area raid that killed 1,044 people, and raids continued into April 1945, days before American troops took the city. In all, an estimated 6,000 people in Leipzig were killed in the air war. Section XXVIII of the Südfriedhof, the Southern Cemetery, holds 3,474 of the bombing victims, in graves that mark the war's particular weight on a city most people remember for music.

## Flight Context

Leipzig sits in central Germany at 51.34°N, 12.38°E, on the broad plain between the Harz Mountains to the west and the Erzgebirge to the southeast. The city is anchored by the rebuilt Innenstadt, the tower of the New Town Hall and the City-Hochhaus. The nearest airport is Leipzig/Halle (EDDP), about 15 km northwest. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) lies about 140 km north-northeast. From cruise altitude in clear weather, Leipzig appears as a dense urban core surrounded by the agricultural patchwork of Saxony, with the Saale river system to the west.
