# Battle of Breitenfeld (1631)

On 17 September 1631, on a field of crossroads ten kilometres north of Leipzig, Gustavus Adolphus and the Swedish-Saxon army shattered Tilly's Imperial-Catholic League force and turned the Thirty Years' War.

**Location:** 51.4000, 12.3333

**Categories:** Battles of the Thirty Years' War, Battles involving Sweden, Battles involving the Holy Roman Empire, 1631 in Europe, History of Saxony

## Introduction

Imagine the wind in your face. The Swedish musketeers at Breitenfeld stood with the south-westerly blowing dust and gunpowder smoke straight into their eyes for two hours of artillery cannonade, watching their lighter, more dispersed lines absorb the iron hail of Tilly's tercios. They held; the tercios bled. Then, at two o'clock in the afternoon on 17 September 1631, Field Marshal Pappenheim's cuirassiers charged the Swedish right wing without orders and were repulsed seven times in quick succession. By six in the evening, the Imperial-Catholic League army that had been the master of central Germany for a decade lay broken on a Saxon field. The casualties on the Catholic side ran to between seven and eight thousand killed and another six thousand captured, two-thirds of Tilly's army gone in a single afternoon. The Thirty Years' War still had seventeen years left to run, but the Protestant cause now had a champion who could win pitched battles, and the war's terrible balance had tipped.

## A king from the north

Gustavus II Adolphus of Sweden landed in Pomerania in June 1630 with about eighteen thousand troops, paid for in part by French subsidies channelled by Cardinal Richelieu, who preferred a strong Protestant counterweight to the Habsburgs in Germany even though France was Catholic. The Sack of Magdeburg in May 1631 by Imperial troops, in which tens of thousands of civilians died, gave the Protestant German princes the warning they needed about the alternative. By the summer of 1631 the Treaty of Bärwalde had brought Saxony and Brandenburg, however nervously, into Sweden's orbit. The combined Swedish-Saxon army that marched on Leipzig in September numbered around forty thousand. Tilly's Imperial-League army, encamped near the city, numbered roughly thirty-one thousand veterans hardened by years of fighting in Bohemia and the Rhineland. Tilly was sixty-two and had not lost a battle.

## Tercios and brigades

The two armies represented two ways of fighting. Tilly's tercios were dense rectangles of pikemen ringed by musketeers, the Spanish-derived formation that had dominated European battlefields since the previous century: durable, terrifying in advance, slow to manoeuvre. Gustavus's Swedish brigades were thinner, more dispersed, with musketeers trained to fire concentrated platoon volleys at three to five times the rate of their opposite numbers. Their regimental artillery, the famous lighter Swedish guns directed by Lennart Torstensson, could move with the infantry and fire canister into close formations. When the cannonade began at noon, Torstensson's heavier guns took some time to come into position; once they did, the rate of Swedish fire was three to five volleys to one Imperial volley. The tercios were perfect targets, packed shoulder to shoulder; the Swedes were thin enough that an Imperial cannonball often passed through the gaps.

## The Saxon collapse and the Swedish pivot

The first crisis was Saxon. Half an hour after Pappenheim's first charge, Imperial cavalry under Fürstenberg and Isolani slammed into the Saxon line on the Swedish left. The Saxon Horse Guards routed almost at once. By four in the afternoon most of the Saxon army was streaming east toward Eilenburg, even as the Elector John George tried to rally them and was carried along in the flight. A third of the Protestant army had effectively disappeared. Tilly, seeing the gap, wheeled his tercios left for the killing blow against the exposed Swedish flank. It was here that Gustavus Adolphus's training paid off. Field Marshal Gustav Horn, commanding the Swedish left, re-aligned his thin line at a ninety-degree angle to face the new threat in fifteen minutes, using a sunken country road as a makeshift defensive obstacle. Reinforcements led by Colonel John Hepburn, with three Swedish brigades and reserves from the second line, arrived in time to stabilise the front. Hepburn's Scottish musketeers, kneeling and standing in alternating ranks, poured concentrated platoon fire into the advancing tercios.

## What it cost

By six in the evening Banér's cavalry had finally broken Pappenheim and Gustavus had personally led a charge that captured Tilly's artillery on the Galgenberg ridge. The captured guns were turned around and used to fire into the surviving tercios. Tilly was wounded three times by musket bullets, struck twice more in the head with a sword, and was saved only by Imperial Colonel Rudolf Maximilian von Sachsen-Lauenburg shooting down the German officer trying to capture him. Pappenheim formed a last rear-guard with the remnants of four regiments at the Linkelwald forest and bought time for the Imperial flight. Of the four thousand one hundred men in those regiments at dawn, six hundred were left at sunset. The Swedish army lost around three thousand five hundred and fifty; the Saxons two to three thousand more. Tilly's army lost two-thirds of its strength in a few hours. Imperial prisoners were absorbed into Swedish ranks. One hundred and twenty captured banners were sent home to be hung in the Riddarholmen Church in Stockholm, where some of them still hang. Gustavus would die fifteen months later at Lützen. The war he had reshaped would grind on until 1648.

## Flight Context

The battlefield lies at 51.40°N, 12.33°E about ten kilometres north of Leipzig in Saxony, today bisected by the A14 motorway and the B2 road. The villages of Breitenfeld, Seehausen, Podelwitz and Wiederitzsch all survive as Leipzig suburbs. EDDP (Leipzig/Halle) lies about 12 km west-northwest of the site. From cruising altitude the broad open country of the Leipzig basin makes the modern airport and motorway exits the easiest visual reference for finding the original battlefield.
