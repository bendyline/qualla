# Richard Wagner Memorial

Wagner stands short and unimposing on a marble pedestal in his birthplace, with the older composer behind him as a black bronze shadow.

**Location:** 51.3425, 12.3704

**Categories:** leipzig, germany, memorial, music, wagner, art

## Introduction

Stephan Balkenhol made the bronze figure 1.8 meters tall, only slightly taller than Wagner himself, who stood 1.66 meters. The composer is shown young, in everyday clothes, not posed, not pointing, not draped in operatic significance. Behind him rises a four-meter-tall black bronze cutout of his older silhouette like a shadow he could never quite outwalk. The whole memorial stands on a marble pedestal designed a century earlier by Max Klinger, with three Rhinemaidens carved naked across the front. It was unveiled in Leipzig on May 22, 2013, the composer's 200th birthday, and the public had opinions. Some applauded. Others shook their heads. Wagner without ambivalence is not really Wagner.

## The pedestal that waited a century

Max Klinger was Leipzig's most famous artist when a memorial committee led by the mayor won him over in 1903 to design a Wagner monument. By 1911 he had a plan: a two-meter marble cube carved with figures from Wagner's operas, topped by a five-meter standing statue of the composer. The cornerstone was laid on Wagner's hundredth birthday in 1913. Then the First World War broke out, money ran short, the marble Klinger had ordered from Laas in South Tyrol was still being worked there, and Klinger died in 1920 before he could complete the work. Only the pedestal eventually arrived in Leipzig, in 1924. After reworking by Klinger's heir, the sculptor Johannes Hartmann, it was set up in the Klingerhain in the southern Palmengarten, where it stood for more than eighty years. Some locals nicknamed it the porn cube because of the naked Rhinemaidens. The actual statue was never made.

## The Nazi monument that almost happened

In 1932, with Wagner's fiftieth death anniversary approaching, Leipzig started over. The sculptor Emil Hipp won a new design competition. On March 6, 1934, Adolf Hitler personally laid the cornerstone, with Mayor Carl Goerdeler at his side, of what was now branded the Richard Wagner National Monument near the Elster basin. Hitler was a Wagner obsessive; the music had been the soundtrack of his political imagination since his youth in Linz and Vienna. Hipp worked on the friezes and figures until 1944, but the war ended before they could be installed in Leipzig. After 1945 the city refused to take delivery, even though it had paid for the work, because the project's Nazi associations had become impossible to scrub off. The park laid out for it, the Richard-Wagner-Hain, kept its name. The monument never came.

## Balkenhol's reply

In 2005 Wagner admirers founded the Wagner Memorial Association to complete Klinger's unfinished monument at last. Nine artists submitted designs. In 2011 the jury chose Stephan Balkenhol, a German sculptor known for refusing the heroic gesture, and a heated public debate followed. Balkenhol funded most of the work himself by selling twenty-five small bronze replicas of the monument. The staircase Klinger had specified, demolished in the 1970s, had been rebuilt in 2010 with the marble pedestal at the top, and Balkenhol placed his short bronze Wagner on it, with the older composer behind in silhouette. Balkenhol called the design a paraphrase of Klinger. Scholars have since read it as a counter-monument. Wagner gets a human dimension here, no exaggeration, no pathos, just a short man on a pedestal, with the weight of his later self looming behind. Behind that, today, stands an office building once used by the Stasi.

## The shadow behind the figure

Wagner is one of the most consequential composers in Western music. He made the leitmotif the backbone of his music dramas. He pushed the orchestra to sizes that had not been imagined. The Ring cycle, Tristan und Isolde, Parsifal, his work redrew what opera could be and dragged late Romanticism into something darker and stranger. He was also, by his own writing, a virulent anti-Semite. His 1850 essay Das Judenthum in der Musik attacked Jewish composers on essentially racial grounds and was reissued under his own name in 1869. Hitler revered him and claimed him as a forerunner; the Bayreuth Festival under Winifred Wagner became a regular Nazi pilgrimage site. None of this disqualifies the music, which is what it is. None of it is erased by the music either. Balkenhol's monument leaves the question open. The young Wagner stands forward in color. The shadow he became stands behind in black.

## Flight Context

The memorial sits at 51.34 N, 12.37 E on the Goerdelerring on the northwest edge of Leipzig's old town center, part of the Promenadenring that loops the historic core. Leipzig/Halle Airport (EDDP) is about 15 km northwest. The memorial itself is too small to see from altitude, but it sits within the green ring road that is highly visible from the air, encircling the dense Leipzig city center.
