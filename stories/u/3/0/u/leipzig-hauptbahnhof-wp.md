# Leipzig Hauptbahnhof

Europe's largest railway station by floor area has two identical entrance halls, two of everything - because Saxony and Prussia would not share a waiting room.

**Location:** 51.3453, 12.3822

**Categories:** Railway stations in Leipzig, Railway stations in Germany opened in 1915, Buildings and structures in Leipzig, Transport architecture, History of Saxony

## Introduction

Walk in through the west entrance and you arrive in a vast domed hall of pale stone. Walk in through the east entrance, four hundred paces away, and you arrive in the same hall again - same dome, same proportions, same staircases, mirrored. This was not an aesthetic decision. When Leipzig finally built a single station in place of its scattered termini, two rival state railways had to be housed under one roof: the Royal Saxon State Railways and the Prussian state railways, who had spent decades in what German railway historians still call the Prussian-Saxon railway war. Neither would enter the other's building. So the architects gave them one station with two of everything, split down an invisible line that ran the whole 298-metre length of the facade. The rivalry is long dead. The doubled building is still standing, and at more than 83,000 square metres of floor area it remains the largest railway station in Europe.

## Two Kingdoms, One Roof

Leipzig had been the most important railway junction in the Kingdom of Saxony since the line to Dresden opened in 1839, followed by Magdeburg, Hof and Großkorbetha. Trains left from separate termini scattered around the edge of the old town, which worked badly and got worse as the population climbed after German unification in 1871. In 1898 the city council resolved on a joint terminus north of the centre. A contract with both railway administrations was signed in 1902, and an architectural competition drew 76 entries in 1906. The winning design by William Lossow and Max Hans Kühne gave each company its own domed entrance hall, facing the street as identical twins. The foundation stone went down on 16 November 1909; platforms opened one by one from 1912; the finished station was inaugurated on 4 December 1915, with 26 platforms and a cross-platform concourse 270 metres long. Even after the national Deutsche Reichsbahn was created in 1920, the two halves were administered separately. Not until 1934 was the station managed as one.

## The Roof Comes Down

On 7 July 1944 the United States Eighth Air Force bombed Leipzig, and the roof over the concourse collapsed. The western entrance hall was destroyed. Travellers waiting for trains and railway staff at work were killed in the raid - the station was a working building full of ordinary people in the middle of a working day. Rail traffic stopped altogether in April 1945. The station carries a darker record too. Leipzig had the largest Jewish community in Saxony, more than 13,000 people by 1925, and the deportation of the city's Jews began on 21 January 1942 and continued until 13 February 1945, when the last 220 were sent to Theresienstadt. A memorial in the station remembers them: the people who passed through this concourse in one direction only.

## Fifty Years to Finish

Trains ran again within months of the war's end, through a building with holes in it. The eastern hall, less damaged, was restored by 1949; East Germany's Deutsche Reichsbahn rebuilt the western hall to its original appearance in the early 1950s. But the concourse stayed open to the sky. Photographs from 1956 and 1957 show passengers crossing a wet stone floor under nothing at all, umbrellas up indoors. The glazed platform halls were reglazed by autumn 1957, and only after the East German Council of Ministers resolved on a full reconstruction in 1954 did the roof come back for good. The restoration was completed on 4 December 1965 - fifty years to the day after the station first opened. Most heavily bombed German stations were never rebuilt. This one was rebuilt twice over, by a state with very little money and a strong sense of what the building meant.

## The Mall Underneath

After reunification Deutsche Bahn did something few railway operators would risk: it lifted the concourse floor out and dug two basement levels beneath it, inserting some 20,000 square metres of shops under the historic halls while restoring everything above. The architects were HPP of Düsseldorf; the rebuilt station opened on 12 November 1997. In December 2013 the Leipzig City Tunnel opened, running under the centre via Markt station, and the terminus that had been a dead end for a century became a through station as well. Roughly 120,000 to 135,000 people pass through daily. On the site of closed platform 24 sits a small parked museum - a Class 52 steam locomotive, an SVT 137 diesel railcar, and three Reichsbahn electrics, E04, E44 and E94 - left where they last stopped. In 2021 a European ranking named Leipzig Hauptbahnhof the best railway station on the continent.

## Flight Context

Leipzig Hauptbahnhof sits at 51.345°N, 12.382°E on the northern edge of Leipzig's inner ring road. From 3,000 to 5,000 feet the station reads unmistakably from the air: a 298-metre wall of stone facing the ring, with long parallel glazed platform halls fanning north behind it and the rail throat spreading toward Schkeuditz. Use the twin domes at each end of the facade to confirm the identification, and the green ring of the Promenadenring to place the old town immediately south. EDDP (Leipzig/Halle Airport) lies about 15 km north-west at Schkeuditz, elevation 470 ft, with parallel 3,600-metre runways 08L/26R and 08R/26L - the DHL European hub, so expect heavy freight movements at night. EDAC (Leipzig-Altenburg, elevation 640 ft, single 2,435-metre runway 04/22) lies 42 km south and is general aviation only. Haze from the Leipzig basin can soften the city outline on summer afternoons; winter mornings give the sharpest view of the glazed roofs.
