# Wave-Gotik-Treffen

Every Pentecost, twenty thousand people in black take over Bach's city - a festival that began as an illegal gathering of 150 in a Potsdam park and survived its own bankruptcy without a single arrest.

**Location:** 51.3403, 12.3747

**Categories:** Goth festivals, Music festivals in Germany, Culture in Leipzig, Goth subculture, Tourist attractions in Leipzig

## Introduction

At Whitsun the trams in Leipzig fill up with Victorian widows, cyberpunks in respirator masks, medieval pilgrims and men in frock coats carrying picnic hampers, and none of them get a second look. For four days over Pentecost the Wave-Gotik-Treffen turns a city of more than 600,000 into one of the largest gatherings of the gothic, cybergoth, steampunk and rivethead subcultures in the world, with something like 200 bands playing across forty-odd venues from a Baroque concert hall to the crypt of a war memorial. Around 20,000 people come. What makes it worth a story is not the costume - it is that this began as a few dozen people in a park who were breaking the law by being there, and that the one year it fell apart completely, nothing bad happened.

## Belvedere, Walpurgis Night

The first attempt at a Treffen was made in 1988, at the Belvedere on Potsdam's Pfingstberg, on Walpurgis Night. About twenty people set out to meet; roughly 150 turned up. In East Germany that constituted an unauthorised assembly, and the state regarded the whole subculture - the clothes, the music, the deliberate refusal of the official aesthetic of optimism - as something to be watched and broken up, which is what happened. Four years and one collapsed republic later, in 1992, the first official Wave-Gotik-Treffen was held in the Eiskeller club in Leipzig, now Conne Island, and drew around two thousand people. The growth after that was steep and largely unplanned. Leipzig, a city that had just lost most of its industry, turned out to have exactly the right combination of cheap space, empty halls and no particular opinion about how anyone dressed.

## The Year It Collapsed

The biggest edition was in 2000: more than 300 acts and an estimated 25,000 visitors. On the third day it went bankrupt in the middle of itself. Accounts of why differ - mismanagement, mass ticket forgery, industry infighting - but the practical effect was immediate and total. The organisers were gone. The security firm walked off. Most of the bands and much of the technical crew left. Police prepared for 25,000 stranded, unrefunded festivalgoers to riot. Instead volunteers took over the site, several bands played for nothing, and the audience stewarded itself through a final concert. There was no disorder of any kind. It is the detail that people who go to the WGT will tell you first, and they are right to: a subculture that spends its time on mortality and ruin, handed a genuine disaster, simply cleaned up and finished the show.

## Forty Venues and a Tram Ticket

The centre of gravity is the agra exhibition grounds about seven kilometres south of the old town, where two halls of roughly 10,000 capacity handle the headline concerts and the main market, a third holds the bistro and dance floor, and the campsite spreads out behind. But the festival is dispersed across the whole city by design. Concerts run in the Schauspielhaus, in churches, in the Parkbühne under the trees of the Clara-Zetkin-Park and in the crypt of the Völkerschlachtdenkmal, the vast memorial to the Battle of Leipzig. A ticket includes public transport for the duration, which is why the trams look the way they do. Museums join in: the Egyptian Museum, the Museum of Fine Arts and the Grassi museums offer free entry or special tours to ticket-holders. Alongside the concerts there are readings, film screenings, absinthe brunches, live role-play, workshops and a medieval market.

## The Picnic in the Park

The events that best explain the festival cost nothing and involve no amplification. At the Torhaus Dölitz a Heidnisches Dorf - a pagan village - sets up for the weekend. In the Clara-Zetkin-Park, hundreds of people in full Victorian dress lay out blankets, parasols, china and cake for an afternoon picnic that is open to anyone walking past, and Leipzig families with pushchairs stop to look and are photographed as often as they photograph. The date moves with the church calendar: Pentecost, the Sunday seven weeks after Easter, so anywhere from 10 May to 13 June. Unofficial parties start on the Thursday. Monday is a public holiday in Germany, so the last club nights run until Tuesday morning, and then twenty thousand people fold up their coats and go home, and the trams go back to normal.

## Flight Context

The festival's main site, the agra grounds at Markkleeberg, lies at roughly 51.29°N, 12.38°E, about seven kilometres south of Leipzig's centre; the festival's registered coordinates in the city are 51.340°N, 12.375°E. From the air in late May or early June the agra is easy to find: large exhibition halls with a big field of tents beside them, immediately west of the lakes of the Neuseenland, the flooded former lignite pits south of the city. Use the Völkerschlachtdenkmal - a 91-metre stone block of a monument south-east of the centre, one of the most conspicuous structures in Saxony - as the turning point between the old town venues and the agra. Fly the corridor at 2,500 to 4,000 feet. EDDP (Leipzig/Halle) is about 22 km north-north-west, elevation 470 ft, parallel 3,600-metre runways 08L/26R and 08R/26L; EDAC (Leipzig-Altenburg, 640 ft, runway 04/22) is 42 km south. Whitsun weather in Saxony is unreliable - clear mornings, convective build-up by mid-afternoon.
