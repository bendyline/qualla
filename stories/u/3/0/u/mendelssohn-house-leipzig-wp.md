# Mendelssohn House, Leipzig

The Leipzig flat where Felix Mendelssohn wrote his last works and died at thirty-eight - a house that outlasted the regime which pulled his statue down in the night and melted it for scrap.

**Location:** 51.3361, 12.3833

**Categories:** Biographical museums in Germany, Music museums in Germany, Museums in Leipzig, Felix Mendelssohn, Historic house museums in Germany

## Introduction

Mendelssohn moved in with a signed lease and two years left to live. In 1845 he took the piano nobile of a new late-classical building on Königstraße - the street is Goldschmidtstraße now, the number is 12 - and filled it with a family, a grand piano and the paperwork of a man running the musical life of a city. He was thirty-six. His daughter Elisabeth was born in these rooms. He shared the direction of the 1845-46 Gewandhaus season with the young Dane Niels Gade, finished the oratorio Elijah here, and took it to Birmingham for its premiere on 26 August 1846, where it was commissioned and received with acclaim. Fourteen months later, on 4 November 1847, he died in this flat after a series of strokes. He was thirty-eight years old.

## The Two Years That Were Left

He had come to Leipzig in 1835 as director of the Gewandhaus Orchestra, and the decade that followed made the city a musical capital. In 1841 Friedrich Wilhelm IV pulled him to Berlin as court composer, a post he disliked; he kept conducting in Leipzig as a guest, and in 1843 he founded the Conservatorium there, Germany's first music conservatory, opening it on 2 April in the Gewandhaus buildings. He resigned the Berlin appointments in 1844 and came back for good. The flat on Königstraße was the home of that return. The rooms have been restored to the way they looked while he lived in them, furnished in late Biedermeier - upright, unshowy, comfortable. Among the objects on display are watercolours he painted himself. He was a genuinely good landscape painter, and it is a strange thing to stand in a composer's apartment and find that the best-lit corner holds his brushwork rather than his manuscripts.

## The Statue Comes Down

In 1892 Leipzig put up a monument to him outside the Gewandhaus: a bronze figure 2.8 metres tall by the sculptor Werner Stein, holding a baton and a score, on a granite base that brought the whole monument to almost seven metres. It stood for forty-four years. Mendelssohn had been baptised a Christian as a child, but under the Nazis his Jewish family origins made him a target; Jewish composers were condemned and driven out of German musical life, and the regime went so far as to commission replacement music for his incidental score to A Midsummer Night's Dream, one of the most-loved pieces of orchestral music in Europe. On the night of 9-10 November 1936 the monument was pulled down. The bronze was almost certainly melted for war material; the granite base was sold to a stonemason in 1942.

## The Mayor Who Resigned Over It

Leipzig's Oberbürgermeister at the time was Carl Friedrich Goerdeler, a conservative nationalist who had argued for months with his deputy Rudolf Haake about the statue. Goerdeler had reluctantly agreed it could be moved from its prominent spot at the Gewandhaus. Before leaving on a trade delegation to Finland in autumn 1936 he met Hitler and Goebbels and secured their promise that nothing would happen to it in his absence. Haake destroyed it while he was away, then told him the quarrel was really about Goerdeler's attitude on what the regime called the Jewish question. Goerdeler tried to have the monument rebuilt, failed, refused to stand for re-election, and left office on 31 March 1937. He spent the next seven years at the centre of the German resistance. Had the 20 July 1944 plot succeeded he would have become chancellor. He was hanged at Plötzensee on 2 February 1945.

## Masur's Rescue

The flat itself survived the war, the GDR and a century and a half of being nothing more than an address. What brought it back was Kurt Masur, the Gewandhaus conductor, who initiated the building's restoration and its opening as a museum. It opened on 4 November 1997, the hundred and fiftieth anniversary of Mendelssohn's death, and is listed in the federal government's Blaubuch as a cultural site of national importance. The Felix Mendelssohn Bartholdy Foundation, formed in 2003 with the backing of the Leipzig city council, funds complete editions of his music, letters and paintings and supports young musicians; Masur founded the International Mendelssohn Academy here in 2008. An extension opened on 3 February 2014 with an interactive conducting station and a children's area in the garden house, and in November 2017 the second floor was given over to his sister Fanny Hensel, a formidable composer in her own right, and to Masur himself. In 2018 the house received the European Heritage Label.

## Sunday at Eleven

The most important room is the music salon, and the most important thing that happens in it happens every Sunday at eleven in the morning, when there is a chamber-music matinee. It is the same room, the same scale of audience, the same repertoire tradition the Mendelssohn family kept in their own homes: a few dozen people, close enough to hear the players breathe. Outside, the monument is back. On 18 October 2008 a reconstruction by the Dresden sculptor Christian Schulze, worked up from photogrammetric measurements of old photographs and cast at Lauchhammer, was raised in Leipzig at a cost of 355,000 euros paid largely by donations. It took seventy-two years to put the statue back. The man it commemorates lived thirty-eight.

## Flight Context

The Mendelssohn House stands at 51.336°N, 12.383°E, just south-east of Leipzig's inner ring in the streets behind the Gewandhaus and the Neues Rathaus. It is a modest four-storey townhouse and will not be identifiable from the air on its own - navigate instead by the Augustusplatz, the glass slab of the Gewandhaus and the City-Hochhaus tower a few hundred metres north-west, then look south-east across the ring. A 2,500 to 4,000 foot pass over the inner city puts the whole cluster - Nikolaikirche, Thomaskirche, Augustusplatz, Hauptbahnhof - in a single frame. EDDP (Leipzig/Halle) lies about 17 km north-west, elevation 470 ft, parallel runways 08L/26R and 08R/26L. EDAC (Leipzig-Altenburg) is 42 km south. Central Leipzig sits in a shallow basin; expect summer haze and, in winter, low stratus that can close the city view entirely while the airport stays open.
