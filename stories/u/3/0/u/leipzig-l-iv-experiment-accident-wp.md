# Leipzig L-IV experiment accident

On 23 June 1942 a uranium pile in a Leipzig university laboratory caught fire and blew itself apart - the first nuclear accident in history, five months before the first working reactor existed.

**Location:** 51.3403, 12.3748

**Categories:** Nuclear history, Science and technology, World War II, Leipzig, Germany

## Introduction

The world had its first reactor accident before it had a working reactor. On 23 June 1942, in a laboratory at the Physical Institute of Leipzig University, an aluminium sphere packed with uranium powder and heavy water caught fire, boiled its own cooling jacket, and tore itself open. Burning uranium sprayed to a ceiling six metres overhead. Chicago Pile-1 would not go critical beneath the stands at Stagg Field until 2 December that year. The Germans got there first - to the accident.

## The Uranium Machine

Uranmaschine was the term the German project used: not a power plant, not a weapon, but a machine to see whether uranium could be persuaded to keep a neutron chain going. The Leipzig group worked through a numbered series - L-I, L-II, L-III - each a different arrangement of fuel and moderator, each asking the same question in a slightly better way.

L-IV was the one that answered. Roughly 750 kilograms of uranium powder and 140 kilograms of heavy water sat inside an aluminium sphere, and in the first half of 1942 the assembly gave back more neutrons than it received - a gain of about thirteen percent. The number is small and it is not ambiguous. It was the first neutron increase measured in a uranium and heavy-water pile within the German programme - Hans von Halban's team in Paris had reached the same milestone three years earlier - and it told the Germans that the physics worked.

## Whose Hands Were on It

Werner Heisenberg's name is attached to the Leipzig work, and he led the group until 1942. Over the winter of 1939-40 he had written the assessments of whether energy could be extracted from uranium - for a reactor, and for a bomb. After those reports he stepped back from the bench and left the execution of L-I through L-IV largely to his collaborators.

The experiments belonged in practice to Robert Döpel and to Klara Döpel, his wife, who had worked as a lawyer until 1933 and afterwards as his research partner. Both Döpels are named alongside Heisenberg on the paper that came out of the accident. It is a useful reminder that the famous name on a wartime physics result is often not the person who was standing next to the apparatus.

## Twenty Days, Then Blisters

L-IV had been running twenty days when blisters formed at a gasket, and the device was opened to check for a heavy-water leak. On 23 June, at Robert Döpel's request, Werner Paschen lifted the machine. Air went in with him.

Finely divided uranium is pyrophoric - it ignites on contact with air - and the charge caught at once. Burning uranium boiled the water jacket around the sphere until steam pressure had nowhere left to go and split the vessel apart. Glowing powder shot to the six-metre ceiling and scattered across the laboratory, and the apparatus climbed to something near a thousand degrees. Heisenberg was sent for. There was nothing he could do. The fire spread into the building around them.

## A Paper Almost Nobody Could Read

Robert Döpel, Klara Döpel and Heisenberg wrote up the results, and the paper went into the Kernphysikalische Forschungsberichte - the classified internal reporting channel of the Uranverein, the German uranium project. Publication, in the ordinary sense of the word, did not happen; the readership was whoever already held clearance.

The accident ended uranium work at Leipzig for good. It also changed the engineering: after 23 June the programme abandoned powdered uranium and used compact metallic fuel instead. The last German pile, B-VIII, was assembled from 664 uranium cubes in a rock cellar at Haigerloch in early 1945. It came close to a self-sustaining reaction. It never got there.

## What Happened to Leipzig

The city fared worse than the experiment. In a letter written in December 1943, Robert Döpel described air raids that had destroyed three-quarters of Leipzig, his institute among them; the heaviest of those attacks, on the night of 4 December, killed more than 1,800 people. Klara Döpel was killed in an air raid on 6 April 1945, at work in the physics building, a month before Germany surrendered.

There is no exclusion zone at Leipzig and no monument to what happened here. The first nuclear accident in history left a burnt laboratory, a classified report, and a date that sits oddly early in the story we usually tell about the atomic age - months before Chicago, years before Hanford, decades before the names everyone knows.

## Flight Context

The Physical Institute stood at 51.340 N, 12.375 E, just east of Leipzig's old city centre. From 2,500 to 4,000 feet the city reads clearly: the compact ring of the Altstadt, the 142-metre City-Hochhaus tower rising from it, and the vast stone drum of the Völkerschlachtdenkmal - the Monument to the Battle of the Nations - standing alone in parkland to the southeast, the single most reliable visual fix in the Leipzig basin. Leipzig/Halle (EDDP) lies roughly 13 km northwest and is a major freight hub, so expect busy controlled airspace and heavy night movements; Leipzig-Altenburg (EDAC) sits about 45 km south. Winter inversions over the surrounding lignite basin can hold haze at low level for days.
