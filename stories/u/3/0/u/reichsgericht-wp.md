# Reichsgericht

Germany's supreme court from 1879 to 1945 - founded to guarantee judicial independence, and remembered for the trials where it did not.

**Location:** 51.3331, 12.3697

**Categories:** Former supreme courts, Legal history of Germany, Buildings and structures in Leipzig, Musikviertel, Nazi Germany

## Introduction

On 4 November 1933, in a courtroom under this dome, a Bulgarian communist named Georgi Dimitrov spent an afternoon taking apart Hermann Göring in cross-examination. Dimitrov was one of five men on trial for burning down the Reichstag. He defended himself, and he pressed Göring until the second man in the Nazi state lost his temper and shouted from the witness box. Seven weeks later the Reichsgericht acquitted Dimitrov and three co-defendants for lack of evidence. It was the last significant thing this court did that a regime could not control, and it cost the court its jurisdiction: within months Hitler had built the People's Court to take treason cases away from judges who might still ask for proof.

## One Law for a New Country

The Reichsgericht opened on 1 October 1879, the day the Imperial Judiciary Acts came into force. Germany had been a single country for eight years and still ran a legal system stitched from Prussian general law, Rhenish law, Baden, Saxony and uncodified common law side by side. The acts standardised court types and procedure, established judicial independence, and guaranteed unrestricted access to the courts - a genuinely liberal settlement. The new supreme court made judgments consistent across the empire until a national civil code took effect on 1 January 1900. Its building came later: Ludwig Hoffmann and Peter Dybwad's Italian Renaissance block, completed in 1895 in Leipzig's new Musikviertel, with two great courtyards, a portico, a central cupola and gable sculpture by Otto Lessing. It is a building that means to be believed.

## Blind in the Right Eye

The judges were monarchist conservatives, and their rulings showed it. In its verdict of 12 October 1907 the court convicted Karl Liebknecht of preliminary acts of high treason and gave him eighteen months, holding that a soldier's unconditional obedience to the emperor was a central provision of the constitution; Liebknecht had argued that unconstitutional orders were void. After the right-wing Kapp Putsch of 1920, the court tried three participants and convicted exactly one - the putsch government's interior minister, given the statutory minimum of five years. None of the coup's leaders was ever tried at all. In the Leipzig war crimes trials of 1921 barely anyone was punished, and two naval officers convicted of sinking a British hospital ship had their verdicts quietly overturned later. On 23 November 1931 the court sent the journalist Carl von Ossietzky to prison for eighteen months for espionage, because his magazine had exposed the Reichswehr's secret and illegal rearmament. Contemporaries had a phrase for the pattern: the judiciary was blind in its right eye.

## The Reichstag Fire Trial

The trial ran from 21 September to 23 December 1933, mostly in Leipzig, with a month in Berlin to examine the burnt building. Presiding was Wilhelm Bünger. In the dock were Marinus van der Lubbe, a young Dutch council communist arrested at the scene; Ernst Torgler, the Communist parliamentary leader; and three Bulgarians, Dimitrov, Blagoi Popov and Vasil Tanev. The regime wanted a communist conspiracy proved. The court gave it one conviction. Van der Lubbe was sentenced to death under a law passed after the fire and applied backwards to reach him, and was beheaded in Leipzig on 10 January 1934, three days short of his twenty-fifth birthday. The other four walked out, and Hitler created the Volksgerichtshof in 1934 - a court that would not repeat the mistake of acquitting anybody.

## "Those Outside the National Community"

The Law on Admission to the Bar forced Jewish and Social Democratic judges off the bench, including a serving Senate president, and barred Jewish advocates from practising before the court. The Reichsgericht then went further than it was asked. In 1935, months before the Nuremberg Laws existed, it ruled that a spouse's Jewish descent was in itself grounds to annul a marriage - a formal legal basis for such annulments was not created until the Marriage Act of 1938. In contract law it held that "the National Socialist worldview requires that only those of German origin (and those legally equal to them) be treated as legally valid in the German Reich." The legal philosopher Karl Larenz had written a few months earlier: "A person is only a legal comrade if he is a national comrade; a national comrade is a person of German blood. Those outside the national community are not within the law." Behind each of those sentences are people who found that a marriage, a debt, a lease or a livelihood had stopped being enforceable because of who their grandparents were.

## What Became of the Court

The last president of the Reichsgericht, Erwin Bumke, killed himself two days after the US Army entered Leipzig in April 1945. From 25 August 1945 the Soviet NKVD arrested 39 of the court's judges, more than a third of its bench. Four came home, released between 1950 and 1955; the rest starved to death or died of disease in captivity. The Allies dissolved the court and never revived it. In 1950 the Federal Court of Justice took over its work in West Germany, with former Reichsgericht judges among its first appointments - the same continuity of personnel that shaped so much of the young Federal Republic. East Germany's Supreme Court took the role in the other Germany. After reunification the building was restored, and since then it has housed the Bundesverwaltungsgericht, the Federal Administrative Court: the court where citizens sue the state, working in the rooms where the state once wrote citizens out of the law.

## Flight Context

The Reichsgericht building stands at 51.333°N, 12.370°E in Leipzig's Musikviertel, immediately south-west of the inner ring and a few hundred metres from the Neues Rathaus tower. From the air it is one of the more legible buildings in the city: a broad rectangular block around two courtyards with a green-grey central cupola, facing the Simsonplatz, with the wooded Johannapark and the Elsterbecken waterway just to its west. Fly it at 2,000 to 3,500 feet on a run down the ring road and it appears just after the town hall tower. EDDP (Leipzig/Halle) lies about 18 km north-west at Schkeuditz, elevation 470 ft, parallel 3,600-metre runways 08L/26R and 08R/26L. EDAC (Leipzig-Altenburg, 640 ft, runway 04/22) is 42 km south. The cupola's dull metal reads poorly in flat overcast; low-angle winter sun picks it out best.
