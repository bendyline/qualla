# Halle Cathedral

Founded in 1271 as a Dominican monastery, Halle's so-called Dom became the relic-stuffed showpiece of Cardinal Albrecht of Brandenburg, whose indulgence sales pushed Luther to write the 95 Theses.

**Location:** 51.4842, 11.9644

**Categories:** Cathedrals, Reformation history, Saxon Renaissance, Halle landmarks, Religious history

## Introduction

The Dom of Halle is not really a cathedral. It has never been the seat of a bishop, and the Dominican order that built it in the late 13th century was forbidden by its rules from giving its churches a tower or a separate choir. The simple three-aisle hall at Halle was finished in 1330 and might have remained an unremarkable provincial church. Then, in 1520, Cardinal Albrecht of Brandenburg arrived with ambitions, money, and a relic collection so vast it was rumored to grant millions of years off purgatory to anyone who venerated it. To pay for his ambitions, Albrecht promoted indulgences across half of Germany. A monk named Martin Luther, watching from Wittenberg, sat down to write a list of objections.

## From Dominican Cloister to Stiftskirche

The original monastery was founded in 1271 and dedicated to Saint Paul at the Holy Cross, Sankt Paul zum heiligen Kreuz. Built by the Dominican preaching order, it followed the order's austere rules: no bell tower, no separated choir, just a simple three-nave hall optimized for hearing sermons. It served the monks for nearly 250 years. In 1520 the young Cardinal-Archbishop Albrecht of Brandenburg, who held the archbishoprics of Magdeburg and Mainz simultaneously and was Primate of the Holy Roman Empire, decided that Halle should become the relic and devotional center of his ecclesiastical empire. He commissioned the architect Bastian Binder to remodel the church's exterior with rounded gables in the new Renaissance style and reconsecrated it in 1523 as a collegiate church. From that date it was probably first called a Dom, although it had no bishop.

## Albrecht's Heiltum

Albrecht assembled what became known as the Halle Heiltum, a relic collection on a scale comparable to those of Wittenberg or Aachen. By the time the catalogue was published in 1520, it contained over 8,000 individual relics, with indulgences attached that promised, in total, 39 million years and 245 days off purgatory for anyone who venerated all of them on the prescribed feast days. Albrecht commissioned the leading artists of the German Renaissance to fit out the church accordingly. Matthias Grunewald, the visionary painter of the Isenheim Altarpiece, produced The Meeting of St Erasmus and St Maurice for Halle. Lucas Cranach the Elder and his workshop produced sixteen altarpieces with a total of 140 panels between 1519 and 1525; only two central panels and a few wings survive. Peter Schro carved stone sculptures. The result was an extraordinary fusion of late Gothic structure and early Renaissance imagery, one of the great works of what historians call the Saxon Renaissance.

## The 95 Theses

To finance the building program and the relic collection, and to pay off the substantial loans Albrecht had taken to acquire his multiple bishoprics, the Cardinal authorized the sale of indulgences across his territories. The Dominican preacher Johann Tetzel was the most aggressive of the salesmen, traveling from town to town with the famous slogan, in one form or another: 'When the coin in the coffer rings, the soul from purgatory springs.' Martin Luther, an Augustinian friar and theology professor at the University of Wittenberg, was outraged that ordinary people were being told that purchased pieces of paper could buy salvation. On October 31, 1517, he posted his Ninety-Five Theses at the Schlosskirche in Wittenberg, addressed in part to Albrecht himself. The Reformation that followed swept across Germany within a generation. Halle, ironically, became one of its strongholds. Albrecht was forced out of Halle in 1541; he took the church's portable fittings with him to Aschaffenburg, where they remain to this day.

## The Boy Handel at the Organ

After Albrecht's expulsion, the church passed to a series of secular rulers who used it as a court chapel. Augustus, Duke of Saxe-Weissenfels, added galleries and a larger altar in the mid-17th century, giving the interior an early Baroque feel. In 1680 Frederick William, Elector of Brandenburg, made it a parish church under the Reformed Protestant tradition. In 1702, a seventeen-year-old George Frideric Handel, who had been born in Halle and was studying law at the University of Halle, was hired as the cathedral organist for a year's probationary period. He played the church's Baroque instrument before leaving for Hamburg and eventually for England, where he would write the Messiah. The current main organ, built by the brothers Friedrich Wilhelm and August Ferdinand Waldner in 1851, replaced the Baroque instrument; Handel, by then dead nearly a century, of course never touched it, but the church proudly remembers him anyway.

## What the Walls Have Seen

The interior was 're-Gothicised' between 1883 and 1896 in the spirit of the German Romantic medieval revival, removing some of the Baroque additions to expose what 19th-century restorers thought the original Dominican structure should look like. The walls and interior were restored by the East German Denkmalpflege between 1957 and 1959; the most recent comprehensive renovation, by the Domstiftung Sachsen-Anhalt, ran from 1996 to about 2005. The cathedral today belongs to the small Evangelical Reformed Church in Germany. It sits in the historic center of Halle, near the Marktplatz with its statue of Handel. It is open daily for prayer and for visitors. Cranach's two surviving panels and Grunewald's Meeting of St Erasmus and St Maurice live elsewhere now, in museums in Munich and Berlin. What remains here is the building itself, a 750-year-old hall that briefly held the most ambitious Catholic relic collection north of the Alps and unwittingly helped trigger the religious revolution that emptied it.

## Flight Context

Located at 51.48 degrees N, 11.96 degrees E in the historic center of Halle (Saale), Saxony-Anhalt. Leipzig/Halle Airport (EDDP) lies about 22 km east-southeast. Halle sits on the Saale River about 35 km northwest of Leipzig and 145 km southwest of Berlin. From altitude, the Dom is one of several church spires in the compact Altstadt; the largest landmark is the Marktkirche with its four towers a short walk away.
