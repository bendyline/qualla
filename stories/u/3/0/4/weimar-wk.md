# Weimar

A Thuringian town of 65,000 that gave its name to German classicism, to Germany's first democracy, and - eight kilometres up the hill - to Buchenwald.

**Location:** 50.9811, 11.3294

**Categories:** city, germany, thuringia, literature, history, unesco, world-war-two

## Introduction

German school groups come to Weimar by the coachload, and the itinerary tends to run the same way: Goethe's house in the morning, Buchenwald in the afternoon. It is an exhausting day and an honest one, because those two stops are the town in miniature. Weimar is small - around 65,000 people, tucked into the Thuringian basin between Erfurt and Jena - and for a couple of centuries it has been carrying more German history than a place this size should reasonably have to. The country's greatest writers worked here. Its first democratic constitution was written and signed here. The Bauhaus started here. And on the wooded ridge just northwest of the centre, the SS built a camp through which 280,000 people passed. You can walk the whole old town in an afternoon. Making sense of it takes considerably longer.

## A Duchess Assembles a Court

None of it was inevitable. Weimar was a minor ducal seat until Anna Amalia, widowed in her teens, governed as regent from 1758 to 1775 and set about turning the place into somewhere writers would want to be. She succeeded to a degree nobody could have predicted. Wieland came as tutor to her son. Goethe arrived in 1775 and stayed until his death in 1832, holding ministerial office alongside the writing. Herder came as court preacher, Schiller followed, and the fifty years bracketed by those arrivals are what Germans mean by Weimar Classicism. The library Anna Amalia expanded, founded in 1691 and now named for her, holds the rococo hall that a fire gutted in 2004, destroying forty to fifty thousand volumes beyond repair before the building reopened in 2007. Later the town collected others: Liszt from 1842, and Nietzsche, who moved here in 1897 already too ill to work and died three years later.

## The Constitution Nobody Wanted to Write in Berlin

In January 1919, with the empire gone and Berlin still dangerous, the newly elected National Assembly needed somewhere calm to sit. Friedrich Ebert had a second motive for choosing Weimar. While the Allies were deliberating the terms of Versailles, he wanted them reminded of the Germany of Goethe and Schiller rather than the one that had just lost the war. The deputies met in the Deutsches Nationaltheater on Theaterplatz, a neo-classical building put up in 1906 and 1907, and there they argued out the most liberal constitution Germany had ever had. It was adopted on 31 July 1919 by 262 votes to 75, with one abstention, signed by Ebert on 11 August and in force from 14 August. The republic took the town's name. Fourteen years later that republic was dead, and the name it borrowed has meant fragility ever since.

## Modernism, Briefly Tolerated

The same year the constitution was drafted, Walter Gropius founded the Bauhaus in Weimar - 1 April 1919, in the buildings Henry van de Velde had designed for the ducal arts and crafts school. For a few years the town held the most radical design school in Europe and the birthplace of German democracy simultaneously. It did not hold both for long. Conservative politicians in the Thuringian state government turned on the school as the political temperature rose, cutting its budget and demanding it justify itself through public exhibition; the Bauhaus mounted the experimental Haus am Horn in 1923 and moved to Dessau in 1925. What stayed behind became a technical school and, in 1996, renamed itself the Bauhaus-University Weimar. The Bauhaus sites here were inscribed by UNESCO in 1996, and Classical Weimar - Goethe's house, the parks, the library - followed in 1998.

## The Hill Above the Town

The Ettersberg is a wooded ridge eight kilometres northwest of the market square, and Weimar's citizens had walked it for generations. When the SS built a camp there in July 1937, they intended to call it Ettersberg, and the name was rejected - the hill carried too many associations with Goethe. They chose Buchenwald instead, beech forest. Clearing the trees, they left a single oak standing, said to be one of Goethe's. Some 280,000 people were imprisoned there over the next eight years and 56,545 died: political prisoners, Jews, Roma, Soviet prisoners of war, resistance fighters from across occupied Europe, men and women who had families and trades and names. American troops of the 6th Armored Division reached the camp on 11 April 1945. The site's history did not end there. From August 1945 until 1 March 1950 the Soviet secret police ran Special Camp No. 2 on the same ground, holding 28,455 people, of whom 7,127 died.

## Walking It

The practical Weimar is easy and pleasant, which is part of what makes the rest so disorienting. From the station it is a ten-minute stroll downhill along Carl-August-Allee, round the Neues Museum and on into the centre, or a few minutes on bus lines 2, 3 or 6 to Goetheplatz. Nobody needs a car here. The Klassik Stiftung Weimar looks after more than twenty sites and sells a MuseumCard covering all of them for €49, or €29 concession, which pays for itself quickly. On the market square, sausage stands sell Thüringer Bratwurst that locals will tell you at length is the only real version, and the cake counters run to Zupfkuchen at around a euro. Weimar was European Capital of Culture in 1999. Jena and Erfurt are each a quarter of an hour away; Leipzig is under an hour. But the ridge is always there on the northwest horizon, and the town has stopped pretending otherwise.

## Flight Context

Weimar sits at 50.98 degrees N, 11.33 degrees E in the Thuringian basin of central Germany, about 80 km southwest of Leipzig. From the air the town is compact and low, with the tower of the Stadtkirche and the theatre square marking the centre and the Ilm river park spreading green to the southeast. The single most useful landmark is the Ettersberg, the long wooded ridge running east to west about 8 km northwest of the centre - the Buchenwald memorial's bell tower stands on its southern slope and is visible for some distance. Nearest airports: EDDE (Erfurt-Weimar) about 26 km west, EDDP (Leipzig/Halle) about 80 km northeast, and EDDB (Berlin Brandenburg) roughly 250 km northeast. Recommended viewing altitude 2,500 to 4,000 feet. Basin fog is common on still autumn and winter mornings and can leave the Ettersberg standing clear above a filled valley.
