# Leipziger Baumwollspinnerei

Once the largest cotton mill on the European continent, fed by plantations in a German colony in East Africa - now ten hectares of galleries and studios that made Leipzig an art capital again.

**Location:** 51.3266, 12.3186

**Categories:** industry, art, germany, saxony, leipzig, colonialism, adaptive-reuse

## Introduction

The freight lift still works, and it still sounds like industry: a shudder, a groan, and a slow ascent past floors where 240,000 spindles once turned. What comes out at the top is a painter's studio with north light and a concrete floor. This is the Baumwollspinnerei in the Leipzig district of Lindenau, ten hectares of red brick where cotton thread was made for 109 years and where, since the mid-1990s, roughly a hundred artists have worked instead. The site's own slogan is characteristically dry: from cotton to culture. Both halves of that sentence have longer stories behind them than the phrase admits.

## A Town That Made Thread

The mill was founded in 1884 and within a quarter of a century had become the largest cotton spinning works on the European continent. It peaked in 1907 with about 240,000 spindles across roughly 100,000 square metres of working floor. What grew around it was not a factory but a settlement: more than twenty production buildings, housing for the workers, kindergartens, a recreation ground, its own rail sidings connecting to the line between Lindenau and Plagwitz. Up to 4,000 people worked here, three shifts a day, right through the East German decades when the plant ran as VEB Leipziger Baumwollspinnerei. Company welfare of this kind was partly benevolence and partly arithmetic - a mill that housed its spinners near the gates lost fewer of them - but it produced a genuine quarter of the city, and much of the housing is still lived in.

## Where the Cotton Came From

Until the First World War, some of the raw cotton was not bought on the world market but grown on plantations the company itself controlled in German East Africa - Cherhami near Sadani on the coast, and Kissanke on the Wami River, in what is now mainland Tanzania. The people who cleared, planted and picked those fields were East Africans living under a colonial administration, and cotton was one of the crops that administration pressed hardest. The bales came north by ship and then by rail into Lindenau, which means the thread coming off the Leipzig spindles was the product of two workforces a continent apart - and only one of them could bargain over what it was paid. Germany lost its colonies at the end of the war, and the plantation arrangement ended with them.

## The Machines Stop

Reunification finished the mill. East German textiles could not survive contact with a global market, and thread production was halted in 1993 after 109 years. What was left was an enormous, structurally sound, almost entirely empty industrial estate in a city losing population fast. Halls that had housed spinning frames turned out to have exactly the qualities painters want and can never afford: high ceilings, enormous windows, floors that will take any weight, and rents that in the early nineties were close to nothing. Artists moved in one at a time, without a plan and without a developer, and the site filled itself from the bottom up. The old platform edges of the disused Lindenau-Plagwitz railway are still there among the buildings, gently going to grass.

## The New Leipzig School

What happened next was not supposed to happen to a post-industrial East German suburb. Painters working at the Spinnerei - among them Neo Rauch, Rosa Loy, Matthias Weischer and Hans Aichinger - became the centre of what critics christened the New Leipzig School, and by the mid-2000s international collectors were flying in to see a converted cotton mill in Lindenau. The Guardian ran a piece in 2007 headlined 'The hottest place on earth'. Around ten galleries operate on the site, alongside a communal arts centre in Halle 14, which was renovated between 2002 and 2012 and now runs exhibitions and an education programme in the old spinning halls. Fashion designers, architects, printers, a goldsmith, a pottery, a porcelain maker, a film club and an art-supply shop fill in the rest. Rather more than half the available floor space has been let again.

## Going

The Spinnerei is open to visitors and free to wander, which is unusual for a working artists' colony and takes some getting used to; studios are private, galleries are not, and the difference is signposted. The big days are the Rundgang openings, when most of the galleries launch shows on the same evening and the courtyards fill. Tram lines from central Leipzig reach Plagwitz in about fifteen minutes, and the site is a short walk from there. Nothing has been prettified. The brick is still soot-dark in places, the yards are cobbled and uneven, and the buildings are numbered rather than named, so the address of a well-known gallery may simply be Halle 12, second floor.

## Flight Context

The site sits at 51.327 N, 12.319 E in Lindenau, western Leipzig, Saxony, at roughly 110 m elevation on the flat North German plain. From 2,000-3,000 ft AGL it reads unmistakably as a dense block of long parallel red-brick sheds with sawtooth roofs, set apart from the surrounding grid of tenement streets, with the wooded green corridor of the Weisse Elster floodplain immediately east. Leipzig city centre and the twin towers of the Hauptbahnhof lie 4 km north-east; the Monument to the Battle of the Nations is 6 km south-east. Leipzig/Halle (EDDP) is about 12 km north-north-west - expect to be inside its control zone. Leipzig-Altenburg (EDAC) lies 40 km south. The plain is prone to winter fog and low stratus; visibility is generally best in late spring.
