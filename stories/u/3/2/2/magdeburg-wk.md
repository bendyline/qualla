# Magdeburg

The mayor who held two copper hemispheres together with nothing but a vacuum also rebuilt this city from a few hundred survivors — and Magdeburg has been starting over ever since.

**Location:** 52.1316, 11.6400

**Categories:** cities, germany, saxony-anhalt, ottonian, medieval, elbe, architecture, science-history

## Introduction

In 1656 the mayor of Magdeburg fitted two copper bowls together, pumped the air out from between them, and hitched eight horses to each side. The horses strained and the bowls held. Otto von Guericke had made emptiness visible, and he did it in a city that twenty-five years earlier had been burned down to a few hundred survivors. Guericke got out days before the imperial army came over the walls in 1631. He came back to rubble, sat on the reconstruction committee, took up brewing to rebuild his fortune, and in 1646 was elected mayor — a job he kept for more than thirty years. Magdeburg's talent for beginning again starts with him. It has been needed more than once since.

## The City of Ottos

Charlemagne's scribes wrote the name down in 805, as Magadoburg. A century and a half later Otto I established a convent here around 937 and made the place something like an eastern capital — the Ottonian dynasty's forward base on the Elbe, facing the Slavic lands beyond. Otto died in 973 and was buried in the cathedral, where his sarcophagus still stands beside that of his English wife Eadgyth. What travelled furthest from Magdeburg, though, was not an emperor but a legal document. The town law codified here became the basis of German municipal law from 1188, and over the following centuries it was adopted and adapted by towns across Central and Eastern Europe, carried east by settlers who wanted the rights a Magdeburg burgher already had. Put Guericke beside Otto I and you get the local nickname: the city of Ottos.

## Two Fires, Three Centuries Apart

Magdeburg has twice been destroyed almost completely. The first time was 20 May 1631, when the imperial army under Tilly and Pappenheim took the Protestant city and an estimated 20,000 people died; the permanent population dropped that year to barely a few hundred. The second came on the night of 16 January 1945, when an RAF raid burned out much of the centre and killed an estimated 2,000 to 2,500. What rose afterwards was a socialist city of wide avenues and prefabricated blocks — the Karstadt store in the middle of town still wears that architecture without apology. Breiter Weg, once among the grandest shopping streets in Europe, came back in a modern idiom rather than the one it lost. Then reunification closed most of the factories and the young left for the west, and the city has spent the decades since arguing itself back into shape.

## Nine Centuries in a Fifteen-Minute Walk

Start at the Monastery of Our Lady, an eleventh-century Romanesque complex of round arches and heavy walls that now houses a museum of modern art — one of the oldest structures in the city, filled with the newest work in it. Walk south to the cathedral, begun in 1209 and finished in 1520, the first Gothic cathedral built in Germany, with Otto in the choir and the twin towers that still fix the skyline. Then turn a corner into the Green Citadel, designed by Friedensreich Hundertwasser before his death in 2000 and completed in 2005: no straight lines anywhere, grass growing on the roof, trees rooted in the building itself. Nearby stands the Magdeburger Reiter of about 1240, the first free-standing equestrian sculpture made north of the Alps. Four eras, one short walk, no transitions.

## The Green Side of the River

Magdeburg ranks among Germany's greenest cities and the claim holds up on foot: parkland runs down both banks of the Elbe, and the Elbauenpark on the east side was laid out for the 1999 federal garden show. Its centrepiece is the Jahrtausendturm, a 60-metre timber structure among the tallest wooden towers in the world, with 243 steps climbing to a viewing deck at 43 metres. Below it the Elberadweg — the Elbe cycle path, signposted with a white 'e' on blue — runs the length of the river through the biosphere reserve, past beer gardens and moorings. Two small ferries cross the water from March to October. River cruises leave from the Petriförder, including a run north to the waterway cross, where one canal is carried over the Elbe on a bridge of its own.

## Zone 010

Getting around is easy because the whole city is a single fare zone. Trams, buses, ferries and the S-Bahn share the Marego system, and within Magdeburg you buy for Zone 010: €2.00 buys an hour, €4.60 buys a day, and the day ticket is usually the better bargain. Machines outside vehicles take coins only; machines on board take notes up to €10. A ticket bought outside has to be punched once you are aboard. Ten tram lines reach nearly everything a visitor wants, with waits under five minutes in the centre, though after 21:00 the network thins to three night trams and three night buses — worth checking before a late evening. Trains put Halberstadt about 45 minutes away, where a church is playing a piece of music scheduled to finish in the year 2640.

## Flight Context

Magdeburg sits at 52.132°N, 11.640°E on the Elbe in Saxony-Anhalt, roughly 130 km west of Berlin on flat North German Plain terrain. The cathedral's twin towers — 99 and 101 metres — are the primary visual anchor, standing above the west bank at the southern edge of the old town; the Jahrtausendturm rises from the Elbauenpark across the river, and the Magdeburg Water Bridge carries its canal over the Elbe about 10 km north, one of the most legible man-made features in the region from the air. Best appreciated at 2,000–3,000 ft AGL on a run up the river from the south. Nearest fields are Magdeburg City (EDBM) just north-east of the centre and Magdeburg-Cochstedt (EDBC) to the south-west, with Leipzig/Halle (EDDP) about 100 km south-east, Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) about 130 km east, and Hannover (EDDV) to the west. Expect river fog along the Elbe valley on calm autumn mornings and low ceilings through the winter.
