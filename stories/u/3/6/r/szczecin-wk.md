# Szczecin

A Baltic port with Parisian boulevards, Szczecin spent eight centuries as Stettin before 1945 changed its country, its language and almost every person living in it.

**Location:** 53.4247, 14.5553

**Categories:** cities, poland, pomerania, ports, world-war-ii, solidarity

## Introduction

Plac Grunwaldzki does not look Polish, and it does not look particularly German either. Avenues radiate from a circular plaza lined with tall bourgeois façades, and if you squint away the Cyrillic-free shop signs you could be standing in the 9th arrondissement. That is roughly the intention. When the old fortress walls came down and Stettin expanded in the 1880s, the new quarters were laid out in the grand radial manner that Baron Haussmann had just imposed on Paris - star-shaped squares, long sight lines, boulevards wide enough to be a statement. The Prussians built it. The Poles inherited it. Today the city calls the district its Parisian quarter, and the roundabout-and-avenue logic is still applied when new areas of Szczecin get built.

## A Stronghold on the Odra

The site has been worth holding for a very long time. Tacitus placed the East Germanic Rugians somewhere in this area in the first century; they left during the Great Migration, and by the eighth century the West Slavic Pomeranians had built a stronghold here. A tenth-century chronicle by Abraham ben Jacob calls it one of the major towns of Pomerania. Duke Mieszko I of Poland annexed the area in 967, but Polish control did not hold, and the town broke free within a few decades. Real, lasting incorporation waited until 1121-1122, when Bolesław III Wrymouth reconquered Pomerania and its duke, Warcisław I, accepted Polish overlordship and founded the Griffin dynasty. The name was first written down in 1133, as Stetin. When the rival port of Wolin declined in the twelfth century the town's fortunes rose with the Baltic trade; city rights came in 1243, Hanseatic League membership in 1278. Until the early seventeenth century this was the capital of the Pomeranian dukedom. Swedish troops occupied the duchy in 1630, and when the Griffin line died out seven years later the city passed into Swedish hands, then to the Kingdom of Prussia, then briefly - courtesy of Napoleon - to the French Empire, before settling into a long career as the port of Berlin.

## The Year Everything Changed Hands

Allied bombing gutted the city centre, the Old Town and the industrial districts. What the war did to the buildings, the peace did to the people. Under the Potsdam agreements Poland's border was pushed west to the Oder, and the line was drawn to put Stettin - a city on the river's western bank - on the Polish side. The German inhabitants, families whose roots here went back generations, were expelled westward. Into the emptied streets came Poles, many of them themselves displaced from the eastern territories Poland had just lost to the Soviet Union, arriving in a place whose street signs, churches and cemeteries were all in a language they did not read. Two populations were moved by decisions taken in conference rooms hundreds of miles away, and both lost homes they had not chosen to leave. Szczecin was rebuilt by people who had to learn their own city from scratch.

## The Terrace Above the River

The best place to understand the city is the long stone terrace called Wały Chrobrego, raised above the Oder on the site of former fortifications and lined with monumental civic buildings. Prussian Stettin built it and named it the Hakenterrasse after a mayor; postwar Poland renamed it for Bolesław Chrobry, the first crowned king of the Poles, which is about as complete a change of ownership as a promenade can undergo. Below it, excursion boats set off through the working port and its waterways. Above, the view runs across the river to Lake Dąbie and the Regalica channel. For the other angles, climb the tower of St Jacob's Cathedral, or take the free lift to the café on top of the Pazim building, or go up one of the towers of the Ducal Castle, which sits a few hundred metres inland and has its own long story to tell.

## Shipyard Town

Szczecin is Poland's third-largest port, and for most of the postwar era its identity was welded together in the shipyards. That gave the city a role in the events that eventually unmade Communist Poland. In December 1970, when the government announced sharp food price rises days before Christmas, shipyard workers here walked out alongside those in Gdańsk and Gdynia; the strikes were put down with live ammunition, and workers were killed. A decade later, in August 1980, Szczecin struck again in parallel with the Gdańsk yards, and the Szczecin Agreement signed at the Warski shipyard came a day before the more famous one on the coast. The city is remembered, with Gdańsk, as one of the birthplaces of Solidarity. A monument by the water marks the dead of 1970. The maritime industry has shrunk but not vanished: the port is busy, the repair yard still works, and the cranes remain part of the skyline.

## Following the Red Line

Szczecin makes its own case efficiently. A red line is painted on the pavement of the city centre, connecting some forty numbered sights with an explanatory sign at each; it starts and ends at the main railway station, and following it is the single best hour a visitor can spend here. Along the way, stop for paszteciki - deep-fried pastries stuffed with meat or with cheese and mushrooms, eaten hot with a cup of red barszcz, and found nowhere else in quite this form. In the evening the bars cluster around the Stary Rynek and ul. Bogusława, pouring the local Bosman lager. It is a city with a liberal reputation by Polish standards, closer to Berlin than to Warsaw both on the map and, some of its residents would say, in temperament.

## Flight Context

Szczecin sits at 53.42 N, 14.56 E on the Oder (Polish: Odra), about 20 km south of the Baltic and roughly 130 km north-east of Berlin. The approach is unmistakable: the river braids into a maze of channels and port basins between the left-bank city centre and the right-bank districts, with Lake Dąbie to the east and the broad Szczecin Lagoon opening north-west toward the sea. From 3,000-5,000 ft look for the pale twin towers of the Ducal Castle, the long civic frontage of Wały Chrobrego above the water, and the port cranes strung along the eastern quays. Solidarity Szczecin-Goleniów (EPSC) is about 35 km north-east; Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) lies roughly 130 km south-west across the German border, and Heringsdorf (EDAH) about 75 km north. Baltic weather governs: sea fog and low cloud are common in spring, with the clearest air usually behind a cold front in autumn.
