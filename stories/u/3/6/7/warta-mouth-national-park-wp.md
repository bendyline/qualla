# Warta Mouth National Park

Poland's youngest national park protects a river valley nobody got around to straightening — 80 square kilometres of floodplain that turns into a lake every spring and back into pasture every summer.

**Location:** 52.5772, 14.7258

**Categories:** National parks of Poland, Parks in Lubusz Voivodeship, Natura 2000 in Poland, Ramsar sites in Poland, Protected areas established in 2001, Wetlands of Poland

## Introduction

For a few weeks each spring the southern half of this park stops being a landscape and becomes a lake. Water levels in the Warta's floodplain swing by as much as four metres over the course of a year, peaking in March and April, and the ground absorbs the surplus exactly as it always has. Come in July and you are walking on grazed meadow threaded with ditches. Come in April and the same ground is water to the horizon, with the tops of willows standing out of it. Nothing is going wrong when that happens. Almost every other major river valley in Europe has been engineered out of the habit; here, at the point where the Warta empties into the Oder on the Polish-German border, it is the whole reason the place is protected.

## A Sponge and a Levee

The Warta cuts the park in two, and the halves behave differently. The southern section — the former Słońsk reserve — is the sponge, taking the annual flood and holding it. The northern half, known as the Northern Polder, sits behind a levee that separates it from the river and is laced with canals instead, drier and more managed. Together they cover 80.38 square kilometres, divided into strict protection over roughly 6.8 square kilometres, active protection over about 40, and landscape protection across the remaining 34, with a buffer zone reaching into four surrounding municipalities. The Postomia's floodplain shaped much of it. The result is a working hydrological machine that happens to be a nature reserve — or a nature reserve that happens to be flood infrastructure, depending on which agency you ask.

## Two Hundred and Forty-Five

That is the number of bird species recorded here, and it is the reason for everything else. Around 160 species breed in the park, including seven or eight kinds of duck. Twenty-six of them appear on BirdLife International's endangered list: the aquatic warbler, the corncrake, the black-tailed godwit, the common crane, the great bittern and the little bittern, the black tern. In winter the park holds Poland's largest concentration of whooper swans, and the sheer volume of wintering ducks pulls in something like thirty white-tailed eagles — a density of large raptors that would be remarkable anywhere in lowland Europe. The Ramsar Convention recognised the Słońsk reserve in 1984, well before the national park existed, on exactly this basis: the wetland was internationally important long before Poland formalised it.

## The Valley Nobody Straightened

The plant communities here are wildly heterogeneous, and the forested parts carry centuries of human fingerprints. But the swampy ground nearest the Warta has largely been left alone, and that makes it something close to a control specimen — biologists from Poland and across Europe come to study a floodplain that still works, because so few of them do. The contrast is right next door. A short distance west, across the Oder, lies the Oderbruch, where Frederick the Great's engineers cut the river a new channel in the 1750s and turned 32,500 hectares of marsh into farmland. The seventeen percent of the Oderbruch that ended up on the Polish side of the postwar border kept its wetland character. This park is what the whole valley used to look like. The Słońsk reserve dates from 1977; the national park was created in 2001 out of the reserve plus parts of the Ujście Warty Landscape Park, making it the youngest of Poland's twenty-three.

## Fighting the Grass

The greatest threat to the park is not drainage, pollution or development. It is plants. Left undisturbed, taller vegetation creeps back into the wet meadows and pastures and closes them over, and the ground-nesting birds that need open sightlines simply stop nesting. So the park's managers spend their effort on holding succession back — mowing and grazing to keep the meadows meadows. It is a useful corrective to the idea that conservation means leaving things alone. The mammals are less demanding: thirty-four species, otters working the channels and beavers rebuilding the hydrology in their own interest, which in a floodplain is broadly the same as everyone else's.

## Chyrzyno

The park is run out of the village of Chyrzyno, just east of Kostrzyn nad Odrą. A Centre of Natural Education opened there on 31 May 1996, five years before the park itself existed, and has been running school field trips, ornithology camps and ecology workshops since that autumn. Two nature walks called Ptasim szlakiem — Along the Birds' Trail — cut through the most valuable parts of the old Słońsk reserve, alongside a network of walking and cycling routes. The park board keeps a small lodge with five rooms sleeping fifteen and a guest house for about thirty, which is the entire visitor accommodation and tells you what kind of place this is. In 2009 it was named a European Destination of Excellence in the non-traditional tourism category. Around 52,000 people came in 2015 — a rounding error next to Poland's mountain parks, and a large part of the appeal.

## Flight Context

The park centres on roughly 52.577°N, 14.726°E in Lubusz Voivodeship, immediately east of Kostrzyn nad Odrą where the Warta meets the Oder. Stay at or above 2,000 ft AGL over the reserve: this is one of the densest bird concentrations in Poland, which makes low passes both a strike hazard and a genuine disturbance to nesting and roosting flocks. From altitude the structure reads clearly — the Warta braiding west through the middle, the straight levee line walling off the Northern Polder with its grid of canals, and the southern floodplain either meadow or open water depending entirely on the month. National road 22 runs along the northern edge with roadside observation points. The Oder a few kilometres west is the international border. Nearest airports: Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) about 90 km west, Zielona Góra-Babimost (EPZG) about 90 km southeast, Szczecin-Goleniów (EPSC) about 110 km north. Expect standing water and a mirror-flat surface in March and April, dry green pasture by midsummer, and dense valley fog on calm autumn mornings.
