# Kostrzyn nad Odrą

The fortress where an eighteen-year-old Frederick the Great was made to watch his friend beheaded was ground to rubble in 1945 and carted off to rebuild Warsaw, leaving an old town that is now only streets.

**Location:** 52.5883, 14.6667

**Categories:** Cities and towns in Lubusz Voivodeship, Fortresses in Poland, Poland–Germany border crossings, World War II sites in Poland, Divided cities

## Introduction

On 6 November 1730 a prisoner in the fortress at Küstrin was brought to a window and made to watch his closest friend beheaded in the courtyard below. The prisoner was eighteen years old and named Frederick; he had tried to desert his father's army and flee to England. The friend was Hans Hermann von Katte, a young officer who had helped him plan it. Frederick's father, Frederick William I, wanted the lesson delivered where his son could not look away. Frederick would become Frederick the Great, and the fortress at the junction of the Oder and the Warta became the place where his youth ended. Two hundred and fifteen years later the courtyard, the castle around it, and very nearly the entire town were gone.

## Where Two Rivers Meet

The headland where the Warta empties into the Oder was worth holding long before anyone thought of bastions. A settlement of Slavic Pomeranians stood here, on and off a tributary of Poland through the tenth century. Some three hundred years later the district passed to the Knights Templar, who were permitted to run a market under German law, and around 1261 Küstrin and the formerly Polish county of Lebus were absorbed into the Mark Brandenburg, which named its new eastern acquisition the Neumark. A fifteenth-century castle grew into something far more serious in the second half of the sixteenth, when the town served as the Neumark's capital and Italian-style bastion works were thrown around it. From 1535 to 1571 it was the residence of a junior branch of the Hohenzollerns. Seventeenth-century extensions made Küstrin one of the most heavily fortified towns in Germany, and in the 1880s a ring of four outlying forts — Czarnów, Gorgast, Sarbinowo and Żabice — pushed the defences further out.

## Fortress, Then Factory Town

A fortress on two rivers attracts sieges, and Küstrin collected them. In August 1758 a Russian army under Count Fermor was besieging the place when Frederick — the same Frederick, now king for eighteen years — came east to break the operation up, and lifted the siege by drawing the Russians off to Zorndorf in the marshes northeast of town. But the nineteenth century changed Küstrin more than any siege did. The railway arrived, industry followed quickly, and by the time Germany lost the First World War the place was manufacturing more than it was garrisoning. Most of the military installations came down after 1918. Then, under the Nazi government, Küstrin was rearmed and re-garrisoned, and the geography that had made it valuable to Brandenburg made it valuable again to a state that expected to be invaded from the east.

## Ninety-Five Percent

In January 1945 the Soviet advance reached the Oder and Küstrin was declared a fortress — a position to be held to the last, because the road to Berlin ran through it. The fighting lasted into March. When the town finally fell on 11 March 1945, close to ninety-five percent of its buildings had been destroyed, including all thirty-two of its factories. The fortifications that had survived Russians, French and two centuries of weather did not survive this. Neither did the streets of houses inside them, nor most of the ordinary lives that had been lived in those houses: the civilians who had not left were caught in a siege nobody was going to lift.

## The Town That Was Carried to Warsaw

The postwar border followed the Oder, so the east bank went to Poland and the small western district of Kietz stayed on the German side as Küstrin-Kietz — one town cut in two, like Frankfurt an der Oder and Görlitz downstream and upstream. The German population was expelled. Then the rubble itself was put to work: the debris of the old town was loaded up and shipped to Warsaw, where it went into rebuilding a capital that German forces had systematically demolished in 1944. The castle where the young Frederick had been held was razed and its bricks went with the rest. Modern Kostrzyn nad Odrą was rebuilt inland, around the railway station east of the Warta's mouth, which is why the town of roughly 17,800 people has so little visible connection to the fortress that gave it its name.

## A Street Grid With No Town

Walk into the fortress today and you walk on cobbled streets with nothing along them. Foundations, kerbstones, cellar vaults, the outlines of houses in the grass — the plan of the old town survives in full while the town itself does not, and the effect is stranger than any ruin, because ruins at least stand up. The Berlin Gate is intact enough to walk through. Bastion Filip and the Chyżańska Gate hold their shape. Proposals to reconstruct parts of the old town surface periodically and then stall. What visitors actually come for is more mixed: German day-trippers taking the RB26 regional train from Berlin-Lichtenberg or Ostkreuz, a little over an hour to the end of the line, for cheaper shopping; birdwatchers heading east to Warta Mouth National Park; and, for sixteen summers between 2004 and 2019, several hundred thousand people a year arriving for one of the largest music festivals in Europe. Trains also run from Gorzów Wielkopolski, Szczecin and Zielona Góra.

## Flight Context

Kostrzyn nad Odrą sits at 52.588°N, 14.667°E in Lubusz Voivodeship, at the confluence of the Warta and the Oder. The Y where the two rivers join is the navigational feature for the whole region — visible for tens of kilometres and impossible to confuse with anything else. Fly 1,500 to 3,000 ft AGL: at that height the star-shaped outline of the old fortress is still legible on the ground west of the modern town, an angular pattern of earthworks and empty street grid on the tongue of land between the rivers. Küstrin-Kietz lies directly across the Oder on the German side, and the river is the international border, so plan airspace accordingly. East and southeast of town the Warta Mouth wetlands spread out in braided channels and seasonal flood. Nearest airports: Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) about 85 km west, Zielona Góra-Babimost (EPZG) about 90 km southeast, Szczecin-Goleniów (EPSC) about 110 km north. Flat terrain, no obstacles of consequence, but river fog is common on still mornings and can hide the confluence entirely until mid-morning.
