# Kleist Museum

A museum in a former garrison school holds twelve letters in Heinrich von Kleist's hand - almost everything that survives of a writer his own century could not read.

**Location:** 52.3438, 14.5573

**Categories:** museums, literature, germany, brandenburg, history

## Introduction

Heinrich von Kleist died on 21 November 1811 beside the Kleiner Wannsee outside Berlin, in a suicide pact with Henriette Vogel, who was dying of cancer and had asked him to shoot her first. He was thirty-four. By the measures of his own day he had failed at nearly everything: plays unperformed or badly received, a journal that folded, a Prussian civil service career he could not hold, a family who thought him a disgrace. The museum devoted to him now stands in the town where he was born, five minutes from the Oder. Among the things it keeps are twelve letters in his handwriting.

## Twelve Letters and Ten Thousand Books

The holdings are small and extraordinarily concentrated. Every first edition of Kleist's work is here, along with manuscripts and those dozen surviving letters. Around them the museum has built a research library of roughly ten thousand volumes on Kleist and his period, the sort of collection that exists in perhaps two places on earth. The rest divides into three named strands. The art collection gathers works made in response to Kleist - painters and printmakers answering the texts. The Theatralia covers everything to do with staging him: designs, programmes, photographs, the physical residue of two centuries of productions. The Musealia are objects from his life. The museum also looks after the literary estates of his relatives Ewald Christian and Franz Alexander von Kleist, and of Caroline and Friedrich de la Motte Fouqué.

## The Museum That Burned

The idea of making Frankfurt a centre for Kleist research dates to 1919, and the Kleist-Gesellschaft was founded on 4 March 1920 in Berlin with its seat in Frankfurt (Oder). In 1922-23 the society opened the first museum in the house where the writer had been born. In 1937 the Kleist exhibition of the Oderland Museum, kept in the Lienau House, was folded into the same collection. Then came April 1945. Frankfurt was declared a fortress to slow the Red Army's road to Berlin, the population fled, and the nearly empty city burned. Both the Kleist House and the Lienau House were destroyed. Whatever had been assembled over two decades went with them, and the work of collecting a poet who left very little behind had to begin from nothing for the second time.

## A Garrison School Full of Poetry

After the war a new collection was pieced together in the municipal library, item by item. When the former Garnisonsschule - the garrison school - was converted, the collection moved in, and the building opened officially on 20 September 1969 as the Kleist-Gedenk- und Forschungsstätte, the Kleist Memorial and Research Site. In 1995 an association was constituted to run it, and on 17 October 2013 a new wing opened alongside: 5.4 million euros of it, joined to the now-listed school by a link of glass and steel. There is a certain justice in the address. A building put up to educate a garrison town now holds the papers of a man who joined the Prussian army as a boy and spent the rest of his life failing to be the officer it wanted.

## What He Actually Wrote

Kleist entered the Prussian army in 1792 and served in the Rhine campaign of 1796, retiring as a lieutenant in 1799. He then enrolled at the Viadrina in his home town to read law, philosophy, natural science and Latin, and took a finance ministry post in Berlin in 1800. The writing came in a rush. Die Familie Schroffenstein appeared in 1803, and 1808 alone produced the comedy Der zerbrochne Krug, Amphitryon, Penthesilea, the novella Die Marquise von O... and Michael Kohlhaas, whose horse-dealer hero pursues justice until it destroys him and everyone near him. Das Käthchen von Heilbronn followed in 1810. Prinz Friedrich von Homburg, now generally regarded as his finest play, was published in 1821, ten years after his death, to an audience that was finally ready for it.

## Two Hundred and Fifty

The reputation arrived late and then would not stop growing. Kleist is now read as one of the strangest and most modern writers in German - a stylist whose sentences pile clause on clause until they nearly break, and whose plots turn on the moment a person's certainty about the world comes apart in their hands. The Kleist Prize, named for him, remains among the significant awards in German literature. The museum keeps his name in circulation with a summer festival each June and the Kleist-Festtage each October, and a new permanent exhibition is planned for 2027, the two hundred and fiftieth anniversary of his birth on 18 October 1777 - in this town, a few streets from where the museum now stands.

## Flight Context

The Kleist Museum stands at 52.34 N, 14.56 E on Faberstraße in central Frankfurt (Oder), a few hundred metres west of the Oder and the Polish border. This is a walking-scale destination rather than an aerial one; from the air the relevant feature is the city itself, with the river carrying the international boundary and Słubice pressed against the far bank. From 2,500-4,000 ft the tower of St Mary's church, the Oder bridge and the twin urban footprints resolve clearly, and the flat farmland east of Słubice is the ground on which the Battle of Kunersdorf was fought in 1759. Berlin Brandenburg (EDDB) is roughly 80 km west; Eisenhüttenstadt (EDAE) about 25 km south with a 1,170 m asphalt runway; Zielona Góra-Babimost (EPZG) about 85 km south-east in Poland. Cross-border airspace applies - check both German and Polish notices. Expect summer continental haze and low winter stratus in the river valley.
