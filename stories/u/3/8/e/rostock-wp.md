# Rostock

A Baltic port that has been trading since 1218 and teaching since 1419, rebuilt after four nights of bombing in 1942 and still reckoning with a week in August 1992.

**Location:** 54.0833, 12.1333

**Categories:** germany, baltic, hanseatic, ports, cities, history

## Introduction

On 29 December 1566 a twenty-year-old Danish nobleman walked out into the dark near Rostock to settle an argument with a sword. He and his third cousin Manderup Parsberg had quarrelled at an engagement party three weeks earlier over which of them was the better mathematician. Tycho Brahe lost the bridge of his nose that night and wore a brass prosthesis for the rest of his life, as analysis of his exhumed remains established in 2012. He also stayed on at the University of Rostock, which by then had been teaching for nearly a century and a half. That is Rostock in one anecdote: a Baltic trading port where the sea paid for the books, and the students were not always the quiet ones.

## Fork of a River

The name is Slavic. Roztoc, from ras-tokŭ, means something like the fork or the spreading-out of a river, which is exactly what the Warnow does here on its way to the sea. In 1218 Heinrich Borwin, prince of Mecklenburg, granted the settlement Lübeck law, and in 1251 it joined the Hanseatic League - the merchant network that made red brick the architectural language of the southern Baltic, because the North German Plain offers clay and almost no building stone. The Neuer Markt holds a town hall that began in the thirteenth century as a Brick Gothic building and gained a Baroque facade in the eighteenth, plus six original gable houses from the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries. The Nikolaikirche, oldest of the churches, dates from the mid-thirteenth century and now serves as an exhibition hall and concert venue. Overhead, at nearly all times, herring gulls.

## The University That Kept Going

The university opened in 1419 with the confirmation of Pope Martin V, which makes it the oldest in continental northern Europe and the Baltic region, and the third-oldest in Germany still operating continuously. Political quarrels pushed the whole institution to Greifswald between 1437 and 1443 before it came back. Albrecht Kossel, born here in 1853, took the 1910 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for working out the chemical composition of nucleic acids - the chemistry underneath what would later be called DNA. At the five-hundredth anniversary in 1919 the university handed honorary doctorates to Albert Einstein and Max Planck. Today around 13,766 students are enrolled, more than 1,300 of them doctoral candidates. Outside the main buildings on Universitätsplatz stands a bronze fountain officially named the Brunnen der Lebensfreude. Nobody calls it that: its nude figures earned it the name Pornobrunnen and it has stuck.

## Four Nights in April

Rearmament brought aircraft factories - the Arado works at Warnemünde, the Heinkel plant - and payrolls that pushed the population from roughly 100,000 in 1935 to 121,192 by 1939. That industry made the city a target. On the nights of 23 to 27 April 1942 the Royal Air Force bombed Rostock repeatedly, and the fires reached deep into the medieval centre. By the end of the war nearly a quarter of the city's dwellings had been destroyed outright and around sixty percent were damaged. Soviet troops of the 2nd Belorussian Front took the city on 2 May 1945. Reconstruction had a purpose: East Germany, cut off from Hamburg and Bremen, needed a seaport of its own, and Rostock became its largest. Shipbuilding here was older than the republic that leaned on it - the Neptun yard traces back to a works founded in 1850 and renamed in 1890, when it became Mecklenburg's first big industrial enterprise. The population peaked near 254,000 in 1988 and stands around 204,900 today.

## Warnemünde

The city runs about sixteen kilometres downriver to the sea, and at the end of it Warnemünde is a different place entirely. First mentioned in 1195, it was bought outright by Rostock on 11 March 1323 and spent five centuries as the city's fishing village. The Alter Strom, the old river channel, is still lined with low gabled houses, boats and fish stalls. The lighthouse dates from 1897 and stands 37 metres; beside it squats the Teepott, a shell-concrete roof designed by Ulrich Müther in 1968 and one of the more elegant things East German engineering produced. The beach is the widest on the German Baltic coast, up to 150 metres of sand. A cruise terminal opened on 1 May 2005, ferries run to Gedser, Trelleborg and Helsinki, and every August the Hanse Sail brings tall ships into the harbour and draws roughly a million people.

## August 1992

Rostock's hardest recent history is three days in the Lichtenhagen district. A high-rise known as the Sonnenblumenhaus held the state reception centre for asylum seekers - a facility built for around 300 people a month that had processed some 11,500 by the summer of 1992, with people sleeping outside it. From 22 August, several hundred militant far-right rioters attacked the building with stones and petrol bombs while roughly 3,000 local onlookers stood and applauded. Police were overwhelmed; 204 officers were injured and 370 people detained. When the shelter was evacuated on 24 August, a neighbouring block containing 115 Vietnamese residents, a social worker and a ZDF television crew was mistakenly left occupied. The mob stormed it and set it alight; those inside barricaded their doors while it burned. Nobody died, by luck rather than design. Rostock has not buried the episode; it is marked and commemorated. Joachim Gauck, born here in 1940 and federal president from 2012 to 2017, built much of his public life around exactly that kind of unfinished reckoning.

## Flight Context

54.083°N, 12.133°E, on the Warnow roughly 16 km inland from the Baltic. Rostock-Laage (ETNL) lies about 20 km southeast; Hamburg and Berlin Brandenburg are the nearest large international fields. From 2,000 to 4,000 feet the old town reads clearly: three tall brick towers - St Mary's, St Peter's and the Nikolaikirche - above a tight grid of tiled roofs, with the Warnow's wide channel curving north past the container and ferry terminals to the twin jetties at Warnemünde. Follow the river to find the beach and the 37-metre lighthouse. Baltic haze and low sea fog are common in spring and early summer and can sit over Warnemünde while the inland approach stays clear.
