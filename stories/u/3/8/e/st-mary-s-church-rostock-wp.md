# St. Mary's Church, Rostock

Rostock's great brick basilica holds the only medieval astronomical clock still running on its original works - and a calendar disc that gives out in 2150.

**Location:** 54.0895, 12.1386

**Categories:** germany, baltic, churches, brick-gothic, clocks, architecture, history

## Introduction

There is a bronze disc inside this church that stops working in 2150. Everything above it has been turning since 1472, when Hans Düringer of Nuremberg installed the astronomical clock in St. Mary's, Rostock - the only medieval astronomical clock anywhere still running on its original works. Time it can handle indefinitely. Dates are the problem: a calendar has to be printed with actual years on it, and eventually you run out. The current disc went in for 2018, replacing one that had served since 1885, and it carries the church deep into the twenty-third century. Somebody, in about a hundred and twenty years, will have to make the next one. Standing in front of the thing is disorienting in a way that few objects manage - a machine built two decades before Columbus sailed, still keeping time, with an expiry date in the future.

## Clay, Because There Was Nothing Else

A church stood on this site by 1232; in 1265 St. Mary's was designated Rostock's main parish church. What went up in the thirteenth century was rebuilt and enlarged at the end of the fourteenth into what stands today: a triple-nave, cross-shaped basilica in Brick Gothic, the style the Hanseatic ports invented because the North German Plain gave them clay and almost no stone. The proportions are not modest. The building runs about 76 metres long, the central vault lifts 31.5 metres, and the tower reaches 86.32 metres - though the tower took the longest of anything here, its Baroque lantern not finished until the end of the eighteenth century. Five hundred years, foundation to finial. It sits on the Ziegenmarkt, the goat market, which tells you what the neighbourhood was doing while the masons worked.

## The Machine and the Apostles

Düringer's clock replaced an earlier astrolabe mechanism and runs on five separate works. Read it from the bottom up. The lowest register is the calendar, the disc that expires. The middle holds the clock proper - hour, zodiac, phase of the moon, month. The top is theatre: at twelve o'clock, noon and midnight, the twelve apostles process past a figure of Christ for a blessing before entering eternal bliss. Eleven of them get through. The last is Judas, and the door shuts in front of him. That small, hard piece of medieval stagecraft has been performing twice a day for more than five and a half centuries, and the gears driving it are the ones Düringer cut.

## What the Reformation Left Alone

Rostock turned Lutheran in 1531, and St. Mary's has held a Lutheran congregation since. Some of the older furnishings survived the change. The bronze baptismal font dates from around 1290, stands 2.95 metres, and is the largest and most significant medieval bronze font in the Baltic region; four kneeling men representing earth, water, air and fire carry the basin, and a bronze eagle crowns the lid. The late Gothic St. Roch altar of about 1530, donated by the barber-surgeons' guild, carries life-sized figures of Roch, Sebastian and Anthony - the saints people prayed to against plague - with an angel pointing to the plague spot on Roch's leg. It is one of thirty-nine side altars the church once contained. The Renaissance pulpit, traditionally attributed to Rudolf Stockmann and dated 1574 - a disputed pairing, since Stockmann isn't documented in Rostock until 1577 - was the first thing installed after the Reformation, and it was deliberately placed in the middle of the church because the acoustics are poor and the preacher needed to be near people. A Baroque sounding board was added in 1723.

## Glass, Pipes and Princes

The high altar of 1721, built largely of painted wood by a Berlin craftsman, stacks three scenes: the Last Supper, the Resurrection, the outpouring of the Holy Spirit. Above the nave the Marienorgel presents a colossal Baroque facade designed by the Rostock organ builder Paul Schmidt in 1770 - a case that promised more than the instrument behind it delivered, since faults in the wind supply forced Ernst Marx of Berlin to rebuild the whole thing in 1793. The Sauer firm rebuilt it again in 1938. Today it carries 83 stops and some 5,700 pipes across four manuals and pedal, roughly thirty stops inherited from earlier instruments. Beneath it sits a princely loge built between 1749 and 1751, one of the largest such seats in any church. And in the south transept hangs 26 metres of stained glass showing Christ as judge of the world, made by a Tyrolean workshop in Innsbruck between 1894 and 1904, among the largest single windows in Europe.

## Three Incendiaries and a Sexton

In April 1942 the Royal Air Force bombed Rostock across four nights, and much of the old town burned. Three incendiary bombs went through the roof of St. Mary's tower. The sexton, Friedrich Bombowski, put them out - working with his daughter and an auxiliary air force detachment, in a burning tower, while the district around them was destroyed. She took severe smoke poisoning and died in May 1945. The city's Lord Mayor at the time, Walter Volgmann, a former local Nazi party leader, remarked that the old hut should have burned down. It did not. The Jakobikirche, one of Rostock's four town churches, was less lucky: badly damaged in the war, it was later demolished. Inside St. Mary's hangs Egon Tschirch's painting from 1942, Die zerstörte Stadt - the destroyed town - showing the church standing in a field of ruins, which is more or less exactly what Bombowski left it as.

## Flight Context

54.0895°N, 12.1386°E, in Rostock's old town just north of the Neuer Markt. The 86-metre tower is the tallest of the three brick towers on the skyline and the easiest fix from the air, best seen from 1,500 to 3,000 feet with the Warnow channel running north on your right. Rostock-Laage (ETNL) is about 20 km southeast; Hamburg and Berlin Brandenburg are the nearest large international fields. Low sea fog off the Baltic can flatten the old-town skyline in spring and early summer even when the inland horizon is clear.
