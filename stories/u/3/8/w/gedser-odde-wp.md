# Gedser Odde

A boulder on a low clay cliff marks the last of Denmark, the last of Scandinavia and the last of the Nordic countries - and on a good March morning forty thousand eiders go past it in a single day.

**Location:** 54.5592, 11.9697

**Categories:** Geology of Falster, Birdwatching sites in Denmark, Coastal Landmarks, Extreme Points, Denmark, Falster

## Introduction

The country ends at a stone. Sydstenen - the south stone - sits on a low cliff at the tip of Falster, and everything Danish, everything Scandinavian, everything Nordic is behind you when you stand at it. What lies ahead is water, and then Germany. The point is not dramatic in the way a headland ought to be: the cliffs here are five to seven metres of clay and gravel, and the whole place would be unremarkable if it were not the end of something. It is also, on the right morning in spring, one of the great bird spectacles of northern Europe.

## The Edge of the Ice

The ridge that ends at Gedser Odde is a terminal moraine - the line where the last ice sheet stopped and dropped everything it had been carrying. It runs across Falster from Idestrup through Skelby to Gedser, part of the maximum glaciation line that crosses the island from Orehoved in the north to Gedser in the south. The moraine does not actually end at the stone. It continues underwater for another eighteen kilometres to the south-east as Gedser Rev, a shoal that has been a hazard to shipping for as long as there has been shipping in the western Baltic. Denmark's southernmost point, in other words, is the last place a glacier put down its load before the meltwater took over.

## Reading the Cliff

The coastal cliff at Gedser Odde is almost always exposed, which makes it unusually easy to read. The ice-age strata are plainly visible: moraine clay studded with boulders, dumped by the melting glacier. Look for traces of pink, which come from red sandstone the ice plucked from the floor of the Baltic and carried here. Lower down are lake sediments, laid bare as the terrain subsided. In the postglacial layers are the fossils of freshwater snails and mussels - hard evidence that the Baltic went through a freshwater phase, a lake before it was a sea. Few places let you see the argument laid out so plainly in a single face of clay.

## Forty Thousand Wings

Migration funnels here, and windy days are the best days. Common eiders pass the point in numbers that are difficult to credit - from late March into early April, and again in autumn, forty thousand in a day is not unusual. Common scoters are around all year in flocks of up to three thousand. Autumn brings geese in their thousands. Red-throated loons, pomarine skuas, long-tailed jaegers and great skuas cross the area, and Gedser Odde is probably the best place in the country for little gulls, with up to a thousand a day in both spring and autumn. Between the point and Gedser Marina the raptors come through: rough-legged buzzards, western marsh harriers, common buzzards, and red kites that often fly very low. Smaller birds cross too - western yellow wagtails, meadow pipits, chaffinches and bramblings. Then there is the rarity list, which is what really brings people with telescopes: little egret, black stork, Steller's eider, short-toed snake eagle, Blyth's reed warbler, great reed warbler, eastern olivaceous warbler, barred warbler, rock bunting.

## Light, Radar and Cable

Denmark's southernmost lighthouse, Gedser Fyr, stands twenty metres tall about 700 metres north-west of the point, carrying a light and a racon - a radar beacon that answers a ship's radar pulse with a coded return, useful when the reef offshore is invisible and the coastline is this low. Beside it is Marinestation Gedser, a radar station built during the Cold War to watch a stretch of Baltic that mattered a great deal when the far shore was East Germany. The 2023 storm surge damaged it. Submarine cables leave the point running north-east, south-east and south-south-east, and Falster's east coast is protected by a dyke for about sixteen kilometres north of here. Gedser itself is a small place - 610 residents at the start of 2026 - kept in business by the Scandlines ferries that have run to Rostock since 1995.

## The Mill That Set the Pattern

In 1957, a few kilometres from the south stone, the engineer Johannes Juul put up a wind turbine that changed the industry. It had three blades on a 24-metre rotor and produced 200 kilowatts of alternating current, and it combined electromechanical yawing, an asynchronous generator, and stall-regulated blades with aerodynamic tip brakes for emergencies. That combination was cheap, safe and unglamorous, and it became the template the world eventually settled on as the Danish design - three blades, upwind, turned to face the weather. The Gedser turbine ran from 1957 to 1967, was refurbished in 1975 for a few more years, and was taken down in 1992. Its nacelle and blades were reassembled in 2006 at the Energimuseet near Bjerringbro in Jutland. Denmark's southernmost farmland is where the modern wind turbine was first got right.

## Flight Context

Gedser Odde lies at 54.559°N, 11.970°E at the southern tip of Falster - the southernmost land in Denmark, Scandinavia and the Nordic countries. From 1,500-2,500 ft the point is easy to identify: a blunt low headland with a pale clay cliff edge, the 20-metre Gedser Fyr lighthouse and the radar station 0.7 km north-west, and the town and ferry harbour of Gedser a short distance up the west side. Watch for the shoal water of Gedser Rev streaking away to the south-east. Lolland Falster Airport at Maribo (EKMB) is about 45 km west-north-west; Copenhagen (EKCH) is roughly 130 km north-north-east; Rostock-Laage (ETNL) lies 75 km south across the Baltic. Ferry traffic to Rostock is constant on the direct southerly line. Low cloud and sea fog are common; the migration seasons that make the point famous are also the windiest.
