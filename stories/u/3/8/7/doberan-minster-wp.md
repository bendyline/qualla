# Doberan Minster

An order that forbade ornament built, in a Mecklenburg swamp, the most completely furnished medieval church interior in northern Germany - and almost all of it is still there.

**Location:** 54.1078, 11.9098

**Categories:** church, cistercian, brick-gothic, medieval, architecture, germany, mecklenburg-vorpommern, religious-site

## Introduction

The Cistercians made plainness a rule. No personal property, no decoration, no display: churches were to be smooth, sparse and quiet. Then, in a marsh near the Baltic coast, the monks of Doberan spent two centuries filling their church with an eleven-metre sacrament tower, a fifteen-metre triumphal cross, a painted screen across the nave, carved choir stalls, an astronomical clock and the oldest winged altar in the history of art. Almost none of it left. No other Cistercian church in Europe retains this much of its own original furniture, which makes Doberan less a building that once held medieval things than a medieval interior that happens to still have a roof.

## Three Years to Make a Brick

Mecklenburg has no building stone. It has clay, so the abbey became a brickworks. Sand, clay and water went into wooden forms, dried, and were fired in field ovens; the cycle took three years, and the resulting abbey-format brick weighed some eight kilograms. The lime mortar was kept free of gypsum so the joints would not expand and crumble. Building on the present church began around 1280, and the shell went up in something like fifteen years - fast, on ground so wet that columns, crossing arches and piers all work to keep the church out of the bog. The nave rises twenty-six metres over a width of eleven, the interior runs seventy-six metres end to end, and the whole thing was consecrated on 3 June 1368. The abbey itself was older: founded in 1171, destroyed in a Slavic uprising in 1179, refounded at Doberan in 1186.

## The Oldest Winged Altar There Is

The high altar was made around 1300 by artists whose names nobody recorded, and it is the oldest winged altar known - not merely the oldest in Germany, the oldest anywhere. Four metres tall, with three filigree spires above and the central one reaching six metres, it works on the medieval logic of typology: the upper row shows scenes from the New Testament, the middle row shows the Old Testament scenes that were understood to prefigure them. Moses striking the rock stands opposite the scourging of Christ. Abraham offering Isaac faces the crucifixion. Around 1350 a lower row was added with the apostles and the two patron saints, Sebastian and Pope Fabian. A wooden figure of Mary stood in the central niche until about 1400, when it was moved into a hanging candle holder as the apocalyptic woman clothed with the sun.

## Eleven Metres of Oak

To the north of the altar stands the sacrament tower, built around 1350 to 1360 in the shape of an enormous Gothic monstrance. It is 11.6 metres high, hexagonal, carved from single oak timbers, and the oldest of its kind in Germany. The compartment level with the Madonna held the consecrated host until the abbey was dissolved in 1552. Every figure on it relates to the Mass: David with his harp, Abel offering a lamb, Moses and the manna, Melchizedek the priest-king. Beside it stands the chalice cupboard of about 1310, with room for twenty sets of mass vessels and interior door paintings that have been cleaned but never retouched.

## The Screen Down the Middle

For a Cistercian house the church was two churches. Choir monks worshipped in the east, lay brothers in the west, and between them stood a screen taller than a man, carrying a double-sided cross altar and a triumphal cross fifteen metres high. Dated to about 1360 to 1370 and largely finished for the consecration in 1368, it is the most monumental object of its kind in Europe, and it is painted on both sides for its two audiences - the Passion of Christ facing the monks, the life of Mary facing the lay brothers, each scene paired with its Old Testament echo. The cross itself is shown as a living tree, branches and all, on the reading that the instrument of death became the tree of life. The lay brothers' pews below it date from around 1280 and carry a carved argument between a devil and a labouring brother. The devil says: Brother, what are you doing here? Come with me. The brother replies: You will find nothing bad on me, you repulsive beast, depart from me.

## What the Swedes Took

Above the western entrance hangs the face of an astronomical clock built around 1390 by Nicolaus Lilienfeld and laid out on the assumption that the earth stands at the centre of everything. In 1637 Swedish soldiers stripped off its silver apostle figures and melted them into coin, and the mechanism was destroyed. The dial survived in remarkable condition, which is the pattern for the whole building: it lost pieces and kept its substance. The abbey was dissolved in 1552 and the church carried on regardless, as Lutheran parish church and as the ducal burial place it had always been. The oak effigy of Queen Margarete of Denmark, who died in 1282, is reckoned the oldest tomb sculpture in Mecklenburg-Vorpommern, and Albrecht III - Duke of Mecklenburg and King of Sweden until Margaret I of Denmark defeated him in 1389 - lies in the octagonal burial chapel of 1420 behind the high altar. As for the site itself, the founding legend says the monks doubted this swamp until a swan rose from a thicket crying dobr, dobr - good, good - and they took it as instruction. Doberan is not a World Heritage site; a bid failed in 2014. It is still, by most reckonings, the most important medieval building in the state.

## Flight Context

Doberan Minster stands at 54.108 degrees N, 11.910 degrees E, at the southern edge of Bad Doberan, about six kilometres inland from the Baltic and roughly fifteen kilometres west of Rostock. From the air the church is easy to pick out: a large cruciform brick mass with a steep copper-green roof and a slender ridge turret, standing apart from the low town around it inside the surviving line of the medieval abbey wall, which runs about 1,400 metres and still encloses the precinct including the thirteenth-century charnel house north of the church. The coast runs east-west just to the north, with Heiligendamm's white buildings on the shoreline and the Molli railway threading between the two. Recommended viewing altitude 1,500 to 3,000 feet; the roof geometry and the walled precinct read best in low morning or late afternoon light. Nearest airports: ETNL (Rostock-Laage) about 30 km southeast, EDHL (Lubeck) about 80 km west-southwest, EDDH (Hamburg) about 125 km southwest. Coastal Mecklenburg is prone to low cloud and sea fog pushing inland on northerly winds.
