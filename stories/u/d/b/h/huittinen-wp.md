# Huittinen

A flat Satakunta farming town that produced Finland's oldest stone sculpture, its wartime president, and a working gold mine.

**Location:** 61.1764, 22.6986

**Categories:** Finland, Satakunta, Cities and towns in Finland, Archaeology, Mining towns in Finland

## Introduction

A farm worker turned it up in a potato field at Palojoki in 1903: a piece of soapstone nearly fifteen centimetres long, carved into the head of an elk. He can have had little sense of what he was holding. The carving is between eight and nine thousand years old — the oldest stone sculpture ever found in Finland, and by most reckonings the country's best-known archaeological object. It changed hands at a market in Turku the following year. Today it sits in the National Museum in Helsinki, and Huittinen, a low, unhurried farming town in Satakunta, keeps a replica.

## Ten Centimetres of Soapstone

Soapstone does not occur in western Finland. Whoever made the elk either carried the raw block across the country or traded for it, which means that eight thousand years ago the people living along the Kokemäenjoki were tied into networks reaching far to the east. The head is drilled with a hole for mounting on a rod — a detail that rules out idle whittling. This was almost certainly a sceptre: something raised, carried, meant to be seen.

The elk is not Huittinen's only deep history. There is a Comb Ceramic dwelling site at Korkeakoski, a later burial ground in the village of Sammu, and scattered cemeteries besides. Researchers reading the finds have concluded that the first people to settle here came mainly from the northwest, following the lower course of the Kokemäenjoki inland. The river brought them; the fields kept them.

## The President Who Signed in His Own Name

Risto Ryti was born in Huittinen on 3 February 1889. He governed the Bank of Finland from 1923 to 1939 and became prime minister on 1 December 1939, the day after Soviet bombs opened the Winter War. On 19 December 1940 he became president.

His presidency turned on a letter. In 1944, with the front giving way, Ryti wrote personally to Hitler pledging that Finland would seek no separate peace with the Soviet Union, in return for German weapons. He signed as himself rather than binding the state — and on 4 August 1944 he resigned, so that his successor Mannerheim could negotiate peace unencumbered by the promise. It bought Finland its exit and cost Ryti everything else. He was convicted of crimes against peace in 1946 and sentenced to ten years. President Paasikivi pardoned him in 1949 as his health collapsed in prison. When he died on 25 October 1956, the country buried him with full presidential honours.

## Kaija Above the Fields

The parish appears in written record in 1414, and the grey stone church of St Catherine of Alexandria — known locally by the affectionate shortenings Kaija and Kaisa — went up around 1500, a single-nave hall of about 14 by 28 metres under star vaulting. Fire took it on 23 May 1783, destroying the roof, the tower and part of the furnishings, though the stone shell held. Rebuilding began the same summer to designs by Christian Friedrich Schröder, and between 1791 and 1795 a new tower rose to 38 metres.

In country this flat, thirty-eight metres is a landmark for miles. Inside, medieval painted crosses survive along with a seven-pointed star, an altar from 1753 stands in the eastern chapel, and bells cast in 1796, 1816 and 1848 still hang above. The organ, with 34 stops, dates from 1935. Later extensions in 1794 and 1860 reshaped the plan into a cross church.

## Ferry Village

Huittinen's centre is called Lauttakylä — ferry village — and the name is a plain description of how it came to exist. The old Helsinki–Pori road crossed the Turku–Tampere road here, at the point where travellers had to be ferried over the Loimijoki. Junctions attract traffic; traffic attracts everything else.

Buses began running from Lauttakylä in every direction as early as the 1920s, and the habit stuck. Lauttakylän Auto grew into the largest bus operator in Finland based in a rural municipality, and before Huittinen became a town its bus station was the busiest rural station in the country. The present station opened in 1965. The railway, by contrast, all but skipped the place: about a hundred metres of the Tampere–Pori line clip the municipal boundary near the northern end of Kuukinmaantie, and that is the entire allotment. The nearest stations are in Kokemäki and Sastamala.

## Granodiorite and Gold

Huittinen is flat in a way that takes some getting used to. The lowest ground follows the rivers through the centre of town, and the bedrock — mostly granodiorite — is nowhere exposed within the city area, buried everywhere under clay and till. An esker runs through from the Kokemäki side in the northwest and continues by way of Huhtamo toward Punkalaidun. The highest ground lies south toward Vampula, where the hills clear 100 metres above sea level; Ripovuori, Kännönvuori and Korkeakallio are the viewpoints people actually climb.

Underneath all that quiet farmland there is gold. The Jokisivu mine, worked by Polar Mining, yields around 300 to 400 kilograms a year — not a rush, not a boomtown, just a steady annual withdrawal from a landscape that gives up nothing on the surface. Some 9,546 people live here as of the end of 2025, on roughly 540 square kilometres.

## Flight Context

Huittinen lies at 61.18°N, 22.70°E in Satakunta, 63 km southeast of Pori and 75 km southwest of Tampere. The terrain is notably flat — broad river-clay plains stitched with the Loimijoki and Kokemäenjoki and cut into large rectangular fields — so the 38-metre tower of St Catherine's church is the single most conspicuous vertical mark for many kilometres. Best viewed at 2,000–3,500 ft AGL, where the esker running northwest to southeast and the river confluences read clearly. Higher ground rises past 100 m to the south toward Vampula. Nearest airports: Pori (EFPO) about 63 km northwest, Tampere-Pirkkala (EFTP) about 75 km northeast, and Turku (EFTU) roughly 95 km southwest. Flat, open country means good visibility but few natural checkpoints; the road junction at Lauttakylä and the church tower are the reliable ones. Spring flooding on the river plains can make the low ground unmistakably wet from the air.
