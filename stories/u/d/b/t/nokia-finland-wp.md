# Nokia, Finland

The town that gave the world's most famous Finnish brand its name is itself named after a fur animal that probably never lived here — and has always refused to put up a monument to the company.

**Location:** 61.4767, 23.5053

**Categories:** Nokia, Finland, Pirkanmaa, Finland, Tampere metropolitan area, Industrial heritage, Cities and towns in Finland

## Introduction

The animal on Nokia's coat of arms is a sable, and sables almost certainly never lived in Finland. The name is the trouble. In modern Finnish noki means soot; the archaic word nois, plural nokia, meant the sable, the soot-marten, and once that creature was gone the word attached itself to whatever dark-furred animal people could still catch. Gustaf von Numers designed the arms on that understanding and they were confirmed on 25 October 1951. Later scholarship suggests the old word may have meant beaver all along. So the town that lent its name to the most recognisable brand Finland has ever produced is named, at best guess, after a rodent it has never been able to identify.

## Big and Little Nokia

The first written trace is a document of 1505 recording two farms, Stoora och Lilla Nokia — Big and Little Nokia in the Swedish of the administration. The pair were combined into a manor, and the district belonged to the parish of Pirkkala. The name outlived every arrangement built on top of it.

What followed is a small comedy of renaming. In 1922 the sprawling parish of Suur-Pirkkala was cut into a northern and a southern half, the northern one stretching east almost into the heart of present-day Tampere. In 1938 it renamed itself Nokia while the southern half simply kept Pirkkala. Suoniemi was absorbed in 1973 and Tottijärvi in 1976, and Nokia became a city in 1977. It now has 36,571 residents, making it the thirty-third largest municipality in Finland and, after Tampere, the second largest in Pirkanmaa.

## Kalmaanmäki, the Last Week of 1596

Jaakko Ilkka was a prosperous cavalry farmer, not a firebrand, and when the peasants of South Ostrobothnia chose him as their leader at the end of November 1596 he ordered every able-bodied man in the district to join him. Most did. On the march south he sent the lame and the sick back to their homes, which tells you something about what he expected. His column reached Hämeenkyrö on 22 December and Nokia on Christmas Day, where he took over the manor.

Klaus Fleming brought roughly three thousand three hundred men against him: twelve hundred professional cavalry, two thousand infantry, and artillery. The clubmen — farmers with clubs, flails and whatever iron they had — repulsed a first probe at Kalmaanmäki on 27 December. The main battle came on the 31st, and few actually died in the fighting itself. It was afterwards, when the peasant army broke and ran, that Fleming's horsemen rode them down and killed several hundred. Ilkka escaped, was caught within weeks and executed. Between two and a half and three thousand peasants died before the rising ended in February 1597, and Fleming's soldiers then plundered the farms of the districts that had risen. Finland never had another peasant revolt on that scale.

## Idestam's Second Mill

In 1868 a mining engineer named Fredrik Idestam put his second groundwood pulp mill on the bank of the Nokianvirta, and in 1871 gave his enterprises the name of the river's town: Nokia Aktiebolag. The Finnish Rubber Works arrived in 1904. In 1967 the two merged with the Finnish Cable Works to form Nokia Corporation, and between 1988 and 1996 the conglomerate was cut apart again, its branches sold or spun off one by one.

The pieces that stayed are still here. The rubber works operate as Nokian Tyres; the paper mill established in 1880 now belongs to Essity. What left was the part everyone remembers. Telecommunications began in the early 1960s as an electronics division of the cable works — in Helsinki, not here — and the headquarters have long since settled in Espoo. All the company retains in town is the Nokia mansion. The city has been asked repeatedly to commemorate the firm it named, and has always said no, on the grounds that no mobile phone was ever built here.

## Two Days in November

On 28 November 2007 a maintenance worker at the Kullaanvuori treatment plant opened a valve to flush a sewage line. The valve should not have existed. Somebody had installed it illegally, connecting the sewage pipes to the drinking-water pipes, and the pressure difference sent the flow backwards into the town supply.

It ran for two days before anyone believed the complaints, which were at first put down to unrelated repair work elsewhere. Around twelve thousand people were on that supply. Thousands fell ill with vomiting and diarrhoea, hundreds went to hospital, and laboratory work eventually identified norovirus, campylobacter, E. coli, salmonella, rotavirus and more besides in the water they had been drinking, cooking with and bathing children in. The town banned tap water and handed out bottles while Finland's Accident Investigation Board took the case. For a country that treats clean water as a settled fact of life, it was a genuine shock.

## The Town Beside the Water

Nokia today sits fifteen kilometres west of central Tampere on the river that runs out of Lake Pyhäjärvi. AGCO Power, Nokian Tyres, Purso, Patria Aviation and the Essity mill are the large employers; the spa, the factory shops and the waterways carry the visitors. There is a brewery, and the Nokia Revival, a charismatic Christian movement begun here in 1990 that drew national attention for years.

What there is not is a monument to the phones. It remains a working industrial town on a river, doing roughly what it has done since Idestam turned up with his mill, and refusing on principle to be a museum of somebody else's success.

## Flight Context

Nokia lies at 61.48°N, 23.51°E, 15 km west of Tampere in the Tampere metropolitan area. The town straddles the Nokianvirta, the short river draining the western end of Lake Pyhäjärvi into the Kokemäki watershed — from 2,500–4,000 ft AGL that river, its dam at Siuronkoski and the mill complexes along it are the clearest markers, with the Vihola marina on the lake shore and Finnish national road 11 crossing the water at Murhasaari. Tampere-Pirkkala (EFTP) is the nearest airport at 16 km by road, close enough that Nokia sits under its approach and departure paths; Teisko airfield (EFTS) is north of Tampere and Pori (EFPO) 95 km west. Expect winter industrial haze along the river valley and, in high summer, strong glare off Pyhäjärvi in low evening sun.
