# Urjala

A municipality of four thousand people that produced Finland's greatest novelist, one of the world's first women MPs, a governor of Russian America, and 221 unbroken years of glassmaking.

**Location:** 61.0833, 23.5500

**Categories:** Finland, Literature, Glassmaking, Political History, Rural Finland

## Introduction

All that remains of the cottage is the foundation stones. Väinö Linna was born there on 20 December 1920, the seventh child of a butcher whose own father had been a tenant farmer on the Honkola estate, and by the time he was seven his father was dead. He got six years of school. Then he cut timber and worked other men's fields until he left for Tampere in 1938 to become a fitter at the Finlayson mills. Nothing about that biography suggests a man who would end up on the twenty-markka note. Urjala is a municipality of about four and a half thousand people spread across five hundred square kilometres of Pirkanmaa farmland and forest, and it has an oddly heavy share of Finnish history stored inside it.

## Writing the Village Back

Linna went to the Continuation War as a squad leader and came home with material he did not know what to do with for a decade. The Unknown Soldier, published in 1954, sold 175,000 copies in six months and permanently changed how Finns talked about the war - not as heroic sacrifice but as what it looked like from inside a machine-gun crew. Then he turned around and went home. For the trilogy Under the North Star, which appeared between 1959 and 1962, he set out deliberately to write the world of his childhood, and he did the work: he went back to Urjala and interviewed elderly people who remembered it. The books follow the Koskela family from 1880 through the 1918 Civil War, and the second volume refuses to spare either side its atrocities - a choice that mattered enormously in a country that had spent forty years not discussing it. The third volume won the Nordic Council's literature prize in 1963.

## Two Hundred and Twenty-One Years of Glass

In 1793 Jacob Wilhelm de Pont built a glassworks at Nuutajärvi, in the north of the parish, sited to burn the enormous forests around it. It made window panes, bottles and pharmacy glass - practical things - and it became the oldest glass factory in Finland, a title it never lost because it simply kept going. The de Pont and Furuhjelm families held it until 1843, then Johan Agapetus Törngren, then Torsten Costiander from 1869. Wärtsilä bought it in 1950 after a fire, and by then the work had shifted decisively toward design. Gunnel Nyman, Saara Hopea, Kaj Franck and Oiva Toikka all worked here; Toikka's Kastehelmi glass, dimpled like dew on a leaf, came out of this village. Fiskars finally moved production to Iittala in 2014, ending 221 years of continuous glassmaking. The village did not empty out. Independent glass artists, a training centre, the Prykäri glass museum, galleries and a hotel occupy the Swiss-styled wooden buildings put up in the 1850s, and roughly 70,000 people came to see it in 2023.

## The Servant Who Went to Parliament

Mimmi Kanervo was born in Urjala on 26 May 1870 and went into service as a domestic worker. She became secretary of the Finnish Domestic and Restaurant Workers' Union, and in 1907 - in the first election held under Finland's new universal suffrage - she was one of nineteen women returned to parliament, the first female MPs anywhere in the world. She sat for the Social Democrats until April 1917, re-elected six times, serving on the banking, customs and finance committees. In 1918 she was imprisoned for her politics. She died in Helsinki in 1922, aged fifty-one. Her life and the events Linna would write about half a century later run through exactly the same years and the same country, from opposite ends of the same parish.

## The Governor of Russian America

Johan Hampus Furuhjelm belonged to the family that co-owned the Nuutajärvi glassworks in its first fifty years, and his career took him about as far from a Häme parish as it was possible to go. Born in Helsinki in 1821, he entered the Imperial Russian Navy and was appointed governor of Russian America in December 1858, running the Alaskan colony until March 1864 - years in which he worked to improve relations with the indigenous peoples of the territory and renegotiated the strange, lucrative contract under which Alaskan ice was shipped to California. Afterwards he governed Primorsky Krai, commanded Russia's Pacific ports, and served as governor of Taganrog, where he founded a naval school and a public library. He died near Urjala on 21 September 1909, aged eighty-eight.

## The Parish Itself

The church at Laukeela was raised in a single building season in 1806 to plans by Martti Tolpo, a church builder from Vihti - pine, seating well over a thousand, which tells you how many people a rural Finnish parish expected on a Sunday. In the 1860s the regional architect Carl Albert Edelfelt reworked it into an early Nordic Gothic, adding corner turrets and carved detail and raising the tower to 35 metres in 1869. It is a listed nationally significant built environment, and it is entirely made of wood. Urjala today has 4,440 residents across 505 square kilometres, a density of about nine people per square kilometre, with Tampere 60 kilometres north and Forssa just over the southern boundary. The upper secondary school is named after Väinö Linna.

## Flight Context

Urjala church village lies at 61.083 N, 23.550 E in southern Pirkanmaa, about 60 km south of Tampere and roughly 30 km north of Forssa. The municipality is a patchwork of open field, dark spruce forest and small lakes, drained by the Tarpianjoki river; from altitude the clearest markers are the lake chain and the tall wooden church tower at Laukeela, which stands well clear of the low village around it. The Nuutajärvi glass village sits in the northeast of the municipality by its namesake lake. The Turku-Toijala railway runs through the eastern side of the area. Recommended viewing altitude 2,500-4,500 ft. Nearest commercial airport is Tampere-Pirkkala (EFTP), about 50 km north; Turku (EFTU) and Helsinki-Vantaa (EFHK) are both within about 130 km. The light-aviation strip at Forssa is the nearest field. Winter brings frequent low stratus over this part of Häme; the best light for the church and lakes is late afternoon in early autumn.
