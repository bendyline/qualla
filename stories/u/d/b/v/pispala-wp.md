# Pispala

A working-class district built without a plan on Finland's highest gravel esker, saved from the bulldozers by its own residents, and still holding the country's oldest working public sauna.

**Location:** 61.5061, 23.7025

**Categories:** Pispala, Tampere, Pirkanmaa, Finland, Districts and neighbourhoods, Sauna culture, Literary landmarks

## Introduction

Pour molten lead through a sieve at the top of a fifty-five-metre tower and it arrives at the bottom as spheres. Surface tension does the work during the fall: each droplet pulls itself round on the way down and hardens before it strikes the water waiting underneath. That is precisely what the tower on the northern slope of Pispalanharju was built to do. Oy K. Hjorth Ab raised it in 1908 on a steel frame shipped in from Krupp, worked two per cent antimony into the lead to harden it and arsenic to make it fragment properly on impact, and turned out as much as three hundred thousand kilograms of shot a year until production stopped in 1972. It is the only shot tower left in Finland.

## The Highest Gravel Ridge in Finland

The tower needed height, and the ridge supplied it. Pispalanharju is Finland's highest gravel esker, a spine of sand and stone laid down by meltwater under the retreating ice sheet and now forming part of a continuous chain that runs through Tampere alongside Epilänharju and Pyynikinharju. It separates two lakes at two different altitudes: Näsijärvi on the north side, Pyhäjärvi on the south, eighteen metres below it. Pispala clings to the northern slope, two and a half kilometres west of the city centre.

The name is older than any of the building. It comes from the House of Pispa, which carried the obligation to lodge bishops passing through, and it turns up in writing for the first time in 1492. For centuries after that nothing much happened here. As late as 1869 the whole ridge held two farms — Pispala and Provasti — and a great deal of gravel.

## Built Without a Plan

Then Tampere industrialised, and the mills needed hands. Factory and construction workers came, most of them from Tampere itself or from Ostrobothnia, and they built on the slope without any unified development plan to tell them how. That absence is the reason the place looks the way it does. Wooden houses sit at every angle the ground allows, painted whatever colour was going; where a street would not fit, someone put in stairs instead. The Pispalan portaat still climb from Tahmela up Hirvikatu to the crest, and runners use them the way other cities use a track.

Administratively it belonged to Pirkkala until 1922, then to the northern half of the divided parish, and was transferred to Tampere in 1937. The following year that northern half renamed itself Nokia. For fifteen years, in other words, this hillside was part of the town that would give the world's best-known Finnish company its name.

## Moreeni

Lauri Viita was born on the ridge on 17 December 1916, a carpenter's son with no formal training as a writer and no intention of acquiring any. His poetry collection Betonimylläri appeared in 1947. His novel came three years later and he called it Moreeni — moraine, the glacial till the hill itself is made of.

It follows the Nieminen family, seven children, the carpenter Iisakki and his wife Joosefiina, the eldest Paavali and the youngest Erkki, who is Viita in thin disguise. The book runs from the Civil War of 1918 through the depression of the 1930s, and what made it new was the angle. It looked at 1918 from inside the working class rather than down at it, and it opened the way for a generation of Finnish working-class fiction. Leftist critics complained he was insufficiently class-conscious anyway. On 22 December 1965, five days after his forty-ninth birthday, a lorry driven by a drunk driver struck the taxi he was riding in and killed him. His monument stands near the highest point of the ridge.

## Rajaportti, 1906

The oldest public sauna still working anywhere in Finland is here, in a plain wooden building that opened in 1906. Rajaportti is now owned by the City of Tampere and run by the Pispala Sauna Association, wood-heated, mixed in reputation and unmixed in practice, and it has been letting strangers sit in the same steam for a hundred and twenty years.

It is not an isolated survival. Hirvitalo, the Pispala Centre of Contemporary Arts, occupies a house on Hirvikatu — moose street — and runs exhibitions indoors and events all over the district. Pispala Library was founded by residents in 2001 after the city library closed its branch here; it has computers, a small gallery and an anarchist shelf. Kurpitsatalo, the Pumpkin House, works allotments and marks the turning year with old pagan festivals and music. Pispalan Moreeni ry, founded in 1969, has kept the old shot factory alive as a cultural venue since 1990.

## The Plan That Failed

None of this was supposed to survive. In the 1970s the city drew up plans for the radical development of Pispala, which in the language of that decade meant clearing the wooden houses and putting up concrete. Residents fought it, researchers backed them, and the plans collapsed. The houses were renovated instead.

What that saved is now, with neighbouring Pyynikki, widely regarded as the most beautiful part of Tampere, and visitors are steered up here for the view and for the improvised architecture a proper master plan would have erased. Olavi Virta lived here, and Hannu Salama, and Seela Sella. The comparisons people reach for — Užupis, Montmartre, Greenwich Village, Christiania — all describe districts that turned bohemian because nobody in charge was paying attention. Pispala earned it the same way: by being built by people who were never asked.

## Flight Context

Pispala sits at 61.51°N, 23.70°E, on the western edge of Tampere. The defining feature from the air is the esker itself — a narrow ridge running roughly east–west between Lake Näsijärvi to the north and Lake Pyhäjärvi to the south, with eighteen metres of elevation difference between the two waters. Best appreciated from 1,500–3,000 ft AGL on a southerly or northerly pass, when the ridge line and the terraced wooden housing on its northern flank read clearly against the lakes. The 55-metre shot tower on the northern slope is the single most useful visual mark; the Tampere–Pori railway runs along the ridge's northern foot. Tampere-Pirkkala Airport (EFTP) lies 12 km south across Pyhäjärvi, and Tampere Central Station is 2.5 km east. Autumn colour on the ridge is exceptional; winter fog frequently fills both lake basins while the crest stays clear.
