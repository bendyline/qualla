# Moomin Museum

A five-storey doll's house built without a single blueprint anchors the world's first museum devoted to Tove Jansson's Moomins.

**Location:** 61.4958, 23.7817

**Categories:** Museums in Tampere, Art museums and galleries in Finland, Moomins, Cartooning museums, Children's museums, Tourist attractions in Tampere

## Introduction

The Moominhouse was supposed to be round. Tove Jansson had drawn it that way for three decades — a blue tower with a conical cap, narrow as a lighthouse, a house with no obvious front or back. Then the model was accepted for the 1979 biennial of illustration in Bratislava, and the organisers assigned it a corner. A round house set into a corner wastes the corner. So Jansson, her partner Tuulikki Pietilä and their friend Pentti Eistola squared it off: five storeys, two and a half metres tall, assembled floor by floor with no drawings of any kind. Decades later it stands under Tampere Hall, still square, and still the first thing most visitors go looking for.

## Built Without a Single Drawing

No blueprints were made. The three builders worked upward improvisationally, one floor at a time, and the result belongs to no architectural school at all — it borrows from several and commits to none. Only the ground floor was sketched in advance, by Tuulikki Pietilä's brother Reima, who was by then one of the most inventive architects in Finland. After Bratislava a world tour was planned and quietly abandoned; the house travelled the Nordic countries instead, from 1980 to 1983, and came home needing repairs. It went back to Jansson's studio, reappeared at the Tampere art museum in 1986, and has been on permanent display since 1987. Photographs of its rooms illustrate An Unwanted Guest, the last of the four Moomin picture books Jansson made.

## One Thousand and Nine Originals

In 1986 Jansson and Pietilä gave their Moomin work to Tampere — 1,009 drawings and paintings, plus dozens of three-dimensional tableaux built by hand. The core is the original artwork for the twelve Moomin books Jansson wrote and illustrated between 1945 and 1977: ink on paper and board, with close to fifty gouache and watercolour paintings among them. Seen at actual size, the drawings are startlingly small. Jansson worked in a scale that assumes a child holding the book close, and the ink lines carry the tiny hesitations and corrections that printing erases. The tableaux run the other direction, pulling flat scenes into three dimensions: forty miniature stage-sets of Moominvalley weather, floods and quiet domestic evenings, each lit from inside its own glass case.

## The Woman Who Became Too-ticky

Tuulikki Pietilä was a graphic artist and professor, a printmaker who worked in engraving, woodcut, lithography and serigraphy. She met Jansson in 1955 and they were partners for the rest of Jansson's life, until 2001. Readers have long recognised her in Too-ticky, the calm, practical figure who turns up in the Moomin books knowing how to survive a winter. Most of the tableaux in the collection are hers, made with the patience of someone used to plates and burins, and Jansson frequently worked as the assistant rather than the author — passing tools, gluing, taking direction. Pentti Eistola, a physician, helped on and off. It is a strange and rather lovely inversion: the most famous illustrator in Finland, holding things steady while someone else built her world.

## Four Addresses and a Statue

The collection has been unusually restless for something so small. Until the end of 2012 it lived in the basement of Metso, Tampere's central library — a building Reima Pietilä designed with Raili Pietilä and finished in 1986, the very year the Moomin works were donated. That December it moved to the basement of the Tampere art museum, with a handful of illustrations parked in the Vapriikki museum centre during the shuffle, and the renovated Moominvalley reopened on 2 January 2013. In 2016 came the last move, to Tampere Hall, along with a new name and the Moomin statue that had stood outside the library. The Moomin Museum opened in June 2017 — at the time, the first museum of its kind anywhere in the world.

## Flight Context

Tampere Hall sits at 61.4958°N, 23.7817°E, roughly a kilometre east of the Tammerkoski rapids in central Tampere. From the air the city reads by its water: Näsijärvi to the north, Pyhäjärvi to the south, and the short industrial channel between them lined with red-brick mill blocks. Näsinneula tower, 168 m tall on the northwest shore, is the reliable visual fix; Tampere Hall's low white roof lies about 2 km southeast of it. Nearest field is Tampere–Pirkkala (EFTP), 7 nm southwest at 390 ft elevation with a single 2,700 m runway 06/24. Helsinki–Vantaa (EFHK) is about 85 nm south-southeast. Best light in June and July, when Tampere gets close to nineteen hours of daylight; low overcast and snow showers are common from November through March.
