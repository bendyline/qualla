# Tampere Market Hall

Built in 1901 to bring an unruly open-air market indoors, Tampere's Kauppahalli is still the largest market hall in the Nordic countries — and still argues for its own survival.

**Location:** 61.4970, 23.7587

**Categories:** Buildings and structures in Tampere, Market halls, Food and markets, Architecture

## Introduction

The building exists because a market got out of hand. By the closing decades of the nineteenth century, trading in Tampere had swollen past anything the city could regulate — carts, trestles, produce and noise spilling across open ground in a town whose population the cotton mills were doubling and redoubling. The municipal answer was to put the whole business under a roof, behind doors, with rented stalls and posted rules. Planning began in 1894, construction in 1899, and the Kauppahalli opened in 1901 on the block between Hämeenkatu and Hallituskatu. A hundred and twenty-five years later the disorder has not so much been eliminated as domesticated. It now happens indoors, at 174 counters, and it still smells extraordinary.

## Åberg's Long Room

Hjalmar Åberg drew the hall, and drew the office building beside it on the Hämeenkatu frontage as part of the same complex. What he produced is 2,100 square metres of floor and about 18,500 cubic metres of volume — figures that mean little until you stand in the middle of it and register that the far end is genuinely far away. It remains the largest market hall in the Nordic countries, a title Tampere has held since the year it opened and which no Finnish, Swedish, Norwegian or Danish city has taken off it. Halls of the same generation survive in Kuopio, Oulu, Turku and Vaasa, all of them cousins of this one, all of them smaller. The scale here is a straightforward record of how fast an industrial boom town needed to feed itself.

## A Hundred and Seventy-Four Counters

The hall holds 174 sales outlets; by 2013 they were being worked by 39 businesses, and roughly forty per cent of the space had gone over to cafés and restaurants — a shift that has been going on for decades and that regulars watch with a mixture of pleasure and suspicion. Continuity survives at the counters that have simply refused to change hands. The Joenniemi flower shop has traded here since 1940. Lindgren Brothers have sold meat since 1949, which is to say that the same family name has been behind the same glass in the same room for more than seventy-five years. Around them, newer arrivals — the restaurant 4 Vuodenaikaa among the best known — have made the hall a destination rather than an errand. Tampere's own mustamakkara, the blood sausage the city eats with lingonberry jam and defends against all outside opinion, is never far away in this part of town.

## Eleven Years of Argument

The hall itself was placed under protection in 1973 by the regional administration of Häme. The office building on the Hämeenkatu side had a harder time of it. Between 1972 and 1983 its fate was fought over in a dispute that ran for eleven years, in a period when Finnish city centres were routinely losing their nineteenth-century fabric to redevelopment. The preservationists won. It is easy now to walk past the outcome without noticing there was ever a question, which is what winning looks like in conservation: nothing happens, visibly, forever. Åberg's two buildings still stand as one complex, which was how he meant them to be read.

## Where a City Buys Its Lunch

Hämeenkatu is Tampere's spine, running from the railway station across the Tammerkoski rapids, and the market hall sits a short walk from the middle of it. Step in from that street on a February morning and the temperature change alone is worth the door — cold, blue, sub-zero light behind you, and in front of you a long bright room full of rye bread, cured fish, cheese, coffee and people who have been coming here since childhood. Cities of a quarter of a million do not usually keep an institution like this alive; supermarkets and shopping centres are supposed to have finished them off. Tampere's has now outlasted the cotton industry that built the demand for it in the first place.

## Flight Context

Tampere Market Hall stands at 61.4970°N, 23.7587°E, on the block between Hämeenkatu and Hallituskatu in the central grid, roughly 400 metres south of the Tammerkoski rapids. From the air the long single-span roof reads clearly against the smaller-scale roofscape of the surrounding city blocks; use Hämeenkatu itself as the guide line, running dead straight west from the railway station across the rapids. Tampere Cathedral to the east and the Finlayson chimney to the northwest bracket the approach. Recommended viewing altitude is 1,500–2,000 feet AGL over the city centre; climb to 4,000 feet to frame the whole isthmus between Lake Näsijärvi and Lake Pyhäjärvi. Tampere-Pirkkala (EFTP) is about 15 km southwest; Helsinki-Vantaa (EFHK) roughly 160 km south-southeast. Note that the centre is dense and low-level flight over it is restricted — the sensible approach is a lakeside transit with an oblique view inland.
