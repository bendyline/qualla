# Tampere Cathedral

A grey granite church in Tampere whose scandalous Symbolist interior — skeletons, naked boys and a winged serpent — was approved by a single vote and is now regarded as a national treasure.

**Location:** 61.5025, 23.7698

**Categories:** Churches, Architecture, Art, Finland, Tampere, National Romanticism

## Introduction

The committee supervising the decoration of St John's Church in Tampere approved Hugo Simberg's paintings by one vote. The objections were specific and, for a Lutheran parish in 1906, not unreasonable: skeletons gardening beside the altar, twelve naked boys carrying a rose garland right around the gallery, and a winged serpent on a red ground at the highest point of the ceiling, directly above everyone's head. One hand raised the other way and Finland would have lost what is now considered the summit of its Symbolist painting. Simberg finished the work and died eleven years later, aged forty-four. He never saw the building become a cathedral.

## Sonck's Grey Rock

Lars Sonck designed it, and he designed it heavy. Built between 1902 and 1907, while Finland was still a grand duchy of the Russian Empire, the church is faced in grey granite quarried at Uusikaupunki and on the island of Lepäinen — rough-cut, deliberately unpolished stone that reads as geology rather than masonry. This was National Romanticism, the style through which Finnish architects argued for a Finnish identity in stone. The tower rises to 64 metres. Inside, the hall seats around two thousand. Approach it from the streets of Jussinkylä and the effect is of a large boulder that happens to have windows: the building looks less constructed than uncovered.

## Death Keeps a Garden

Flanking the altar are church versions of the two paintings Simberg is remembered for. The Garden of Death, first painted in 1896, shows black-robed figures who look exactly like the classical reaper — and who are watering pot plants, tending, patiently, with something close to affection. The Wounded Angel, from 1903, shows two boys carrying a stretcher with a blindfolded, bloodied angel on it across a bleak landscape. Neither is comforting and neither is quite grim. In 2006, a public vote organised by the Ateneum named The Wounded Angel Finland's national painting. It hangs in a museum in Helsinki, but a version of it is here, on a wall, where the congregation sat with it for a century before the rest of the country caught up.

## The Garland Boys

Around the gallery runs Köynnöksenkantajat — the garland bearers. Twelve boys, naked, carry a rose garland in procession above the heads of the worshippers. Simberg painted them plainly, without allegorical drapery or classical alibi, and the parishioners of Tampere noticed. Nudity in a church hall was one complaint. The mood was another: the boys process, and a procession bearing flowers has an obvious other meaning. Whatever Simberg intended, the frieze refuses to settle into decoration. It circles the room, and it keeps moving whether you look at it or not.

## The Serpent at the Apex

The most contested image is the one hardest to avoid. At the very top of the vault, on a red ground, a winged serpent holds the apple of knowledge. Contemporaries read it as sin and corruption installed at the highest point in the building — the position a church normally reserves for something considerably more reassuring. The argument did not end when the paint dried. After the Second World War, church inspections demanded that the offensive frescoes be removed. They were not. Taste turned instead, and the works that a committee nearly voted down are now catalogued among the masterpieces of Finnish Symbolism.

## Resurrection, and an Organ

Magnus Enckell painted the altarpiece, a Resurrection showing people of all races rising together, and designed the choir window. In a Finnish church of 1907 that was its own quiet argument, made at the front of the room where nobody could miss it. The main organ arrived in 1929, built by the Kangasala Organ Works around pipework from the original 1907 instrument; with close to seventy stops it ranks among the four largest church organs in Finland. St John's Church became Tampere Cathedral in 1923, seat of the newly formed diocese, and took the name it carries now. The paintings that scandalised the parish are the reason most visitors come through the door.

## Flight Context

61.5025°N, 23.7698°E, in the Jussinkylä district a few hundred metres east of Tampere's railway station. The 64-metre tower is recognisable from 1,500-3,000 ft AGL, the grey granite mass standing clear of the surrounding city blocks. Use the Tammerkoski rapids running north-south through the city centre as the primary reference, with Näsijärvi to the north and Pyhäjärvi to the south; the cathedral sits east of the rapids. Tampere-Pirkkala (EFTP) is about 13 km southwest; Helsinki-Vantaa (EFHK) about 160 km southeast. Long summer daylight gives clean oblique light on the granite; expect low cloud bases and short daylight windows from November to January.
