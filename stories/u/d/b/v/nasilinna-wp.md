# Näsilinna

A neo-baroque palace finished in 1898 for a cotton dynasty that died before it could move in, and later shot through four thousand times in Finland's civil war.

**Location:** 61.5042, 23.7533

**Categories:** Baroque Revival architecture in Finland, Buildings and structures in Tampere, Museums in Tampere, Palaces in Finland, Finnish Civil War

## Introduction

Olga von Nottbeck died in October 1898, giving birth to twins at Baden-Baden. Six months later her husband Peter died in a Paris hospital after surgery on his caecum. The palace they had commissioned on the rock above Tampere was finished that same year, all cream-coloured neo-baroque swagger with the lake spread out below it, and neither of them ever properly lived there. Their children inherited a house they were too young to run. By 1905 it belonged to the city. Näsilinna — Näsi Castle — has spent almost its entire existence being something other than a home.

## A Name Nobody Can Explain

Its first name was Milavida, and the honest answer to what that means is that no one knows. It is not Finnish, not obviously Swedish, not clearly anything. The name arrived with an earlier building: a wooden villa put up beside Näsikallio in 1860, where the von Nottbecks lived while the family fortune was still being made. When Peter von Nottbeck replaced it with something permanent, he kept the word. The stone palace that Karl August Wrede designed and completed in 1898 sits on the crown of the same rock, its baroque revival details — pediments, pilasters, a heavy cornice line — pitched deliberately against the red-brick mill town below. Tampere called it a castle almost immediately, and the nickname stuck harder than the name.

## Cotton, and the First Electric Light

The money came from thread. Wilhelm von Nottbeck (1816–1890) ran the Finlayson cotton mill on the Tammerkoski, the enterprise a Scottish engineer named James Finlayson had founded in 1820, and under his management it became the engine of the city. The Nottbecks lived between Tampere and St Petersburg and moved through Europe with the ease of people for whom borders were an administrative detail. Their mill was ahead of everyone: on 15 March 1882 the Plevna weaving hall was lit with Thomas Edison's bulbs, the first building anywhere in the Nordic countries or the Russian Empire to have electric light. When Edvard von Nottbeck, guardian to Peter's orphaned children, sold Milavida to the city of Tampere in 1905, the family's century in Finland was effectively over.

## Three Weeks in the Spring of 1918

The Finnish Civil War turned Näsikallio into high ground, and high ground in a city fight is worth dying for. The palace held a Red Cross hospital and a Red Guard base, and the Battle of Tampere — 15 March to 6 April 1918, the bloodiest of the war — broke over it. Around four thousand rounds struck the building. On 3 April a White company under the jäger Gunnar Melin punched through the Red lines and took the palace, then lost it again by evening; before withdrawing, Melin's men shot twenty prisoners who had already surrendered, in the palace yard. Photographs of the bodies against the eastern gable survive. They were among six hundred to a thousand Red fighters killed in those three weeks, and a further thousand executed after the surrender.

## What Happened to the Prisoners

More than ten thousand Reds were herded into Tampere's Central Square when the fighting stopped. Most were mill workers and their families — the people who had run the looms in the buildings the Nottbecks owned. Over the next five months, 1,228 of them died in the Tampere prison camp of execution, disease and hunger. The dead on the White side numbered somewhere between six hundred and eight hundred and twenty; at least twenty civilians were killed by artillery alone. Finland spent most of the twentieth century unable to agree on what to call any of this, and Tampere carried the argument in its street names and its silences. The palace stood through it and kept its shell, if not its plasterwork.

## Seventeen Years Behind Tarpaulins

From 1908 the building housed the Häme Museum, a general regional collection that stayed put through the Second World War — when anti-aircraft crews and the guards of the Pori railway bridges were billeted inside — and closed in 1997. Then nothing, for seventeen years. Renovation finally came in 2013–2015. The ground-floor halls were returned to something like the von Nottbecks' taste and now hold a restaurant, a lobby café and a reception area; the second floor kept its 1950s room divisions and became exhibition space. Museum Milavida opened there in 2015 with a permanent show about the family, alongside changing exhibitions of fashion and design: Ferragamo shoes, Valentino evening gowns, Timo Sarpaneva's printed fabrics, and in 2018 a crown made for a Finnish king who was never crowned.

## Flight Context

Näsilinna crowns Näsikallio at 61.5042°N, 23.7533°E, a rocky rise on the northern edge of central Tampere overlooking Lake Näsijärvi. From the air it reads as a pale rectangular block on a wooded knoll, conspicuous against the dark roofs around it and roughly 600 m east of Näsinneula observation tower — use the tower as the fix and look inland. Recommended viewing 1,000–2,000 ft AGL; the hill's elevation makes the palace visible from well out over the lake. Tampere–Pirkkala (EFTP) is 7 nm southwest, elevation 390 ft, single runway 06/24. Näsijärvi is typically ice-covered from December into April, and low stratus over the lake in autumn can obscure the hilltop while the shoreline stays clear.
