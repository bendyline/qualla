# Pyynikki Observation Tower

A 26-metre granite tower on what Finns call the highest gravel esker on Earth, better known to locals for its doughnuts than its view.

**Location:** 61.4963, 23.7320

**Categories:** Observation towers in Finland, Towers completed in 1929, Buildings and structures in Tampere, Tourist attractions in Tampere, Pyynikki, Glacial landforms

## Introduction

The queue at the foot of the tower is usually not for the view. It is for doughnuts — sugar-crusted munkki, fried to a recipe the café has refused to modernise, sold from a counter that has been feeding walkers on this ridge far longer than the granite tower above it has stood. Climb the twenty-six metres anyway. From the open top you can see two lakes at once, and the water between them is what built the city.

## A Ridge of Gravel

Pyynikki is not a hill in the ordinary sense. It is an esker — a long spine of sand and gravel dumped by meltwater rivers running under the retreating ice sheet, then left standing when the ice was gone. The ridge rises about 85 metres above Lake Pyhäjärvi, and Finns will tell you, with some justification and a good deal of local pride, that it is the highest gravel esker in the world.

The pines here grow in sand. Paths cut down the flanks toward the water, and the whole ridge has been protected as parkland since the 1830s, which makes it one of the oldest deliberately preserved landscapes in the country. A café opened among the trees in 1868, sixty-one years before anyone thought to put a stone tower on top of the ridge.

## Two Towers

The first observation tower went up in 1888 to a design by the architect Georg Schreck. It was wooden, and it did not survive the twentieth century's arrival in Tampere: the fighting of the 1918 Battle of Tampere left it damaged, and eventually it came down.

Planning for a replacement began in 1925. What the city built instead was permanent — twenty-six metres of red granite designed by the assistant city architect Vilho Kolho, squat and solid where the old tower had been spindly. Standing on the ridge crest, its top sits 152 metres above sea level and 75 metres above the surface of Pyhäjärvi. It was finished in 1929, timed for the 150th anniversary of Tampere's founding in 1779.

## The Autumn the Kuru Went Down

The anniversary celebrations never happened as planned. On 7 September 1929, the steamer Kuru capsized in a gale on Näsijärvi, the lake spread out to the north of the tower, and 136 people drowned. It remains the worst maritime disaster in Finland's inland waters.

The cause was structural and entirely avoidable. A third deck had been added to the vessel in 1927 without a proper stability assessment, raising her centre of gravity; when a hard wind caught her and water pooled on deck with nowhere to drain, she went over. The city cancelled the tower's opening ceremonies. Visitors climbing the stairs today look out over the water where it happened, usually without knowing it — the lake is simply a bright plate of grey-blue to the north, dotted with islands.

## Ears on the Ridge

A tall stone box on the highest point of a city has uses beyond sightseeing. From 1930 the Finnish broadcaster Yleisradio ran a medium-wave transmitter from the tower's west wing. During the Winter War of 1939–40 the structure served as an air defence observation post, watching the sky over a city that had already learned what shelling does to a neighbourhood. Radio 957 began broadcasting from here in 1985, and a well-known amateur radio station still operates at the base.

The tower is listed by the Finnish Heritage Agency among the built cultural environments of national significance — a category that covers the doughnut café as much as the granite.

## Happily Eclipsed

In 1971 Tampere opened Näsinneula, a 168-metre needle with a restaurant that turns once every forty-five minutes, and at a stroke the tourist coaches had somewhere else to go. Näsinneula is still the tallest observation tower in the Nordic countries. Pyynikki was left to the people who live here.

It turns out to have been the better outcome. Entry to the park and café costs nothing; climbing the tower costs three euros for adults and two for children up to fifteen. There is a lift, and taking it is a small mistake — the stairs pass observation windows that frame the ridge, the pines and the roofs in slices, so the full view at the top arrives as a release rather than a transaction. Näsijärvi to the north, Pyhäjärvi to the south, the city stitched between them, and a paper bag of warm munkki waiting at the bottom.

## Flight Context

61.4963°N, 23.7320°E, on the crest of the Pyynikki esker between Tampere city centre and Pispala, with the tower top 152 m above sea level. The best line is a low pass at 1,500–2,500 ft AGL along the isthmus: Näsijärvi fills the north, Pyhäjärvi the south, and the narrow neck of land between them carries the whole city. The tower itself is small and dark red against the pine ridge — find the wooded spine first, then look for the stone box on the high point. Näsinneula's 168 m needle at Särkänniemi, 2 km northeast, is the unmistakable anchor point. Tampere–Pirkkala (EFTP) is 13 km southwest, single 2,700 m runway, also home to the Finnish Air Force's Satakunta Air Command. Summer offers long northern daylight; November through February brings low cloud and short flying days.
