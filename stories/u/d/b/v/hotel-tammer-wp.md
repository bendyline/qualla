# Hotel Tammer

Tampere's grand hotel was finished for the city's 150th birthday in 1929 — then the lake steamer Kuru went down and the celebrations were cancelled.

**Location:** 61.5008, 23.7650

**Categories:** 1929 establishments in Finland, Hotels established in 1929, Hotels in Tampere, Kyttälä, Historic hotels

## Introduction

Tampere had planned an excellent autumn. The town had received its charter from Gustav III on 1 October 1779, which made 1929 its hundred and fiftieth year, and the Technical Society of Tampere had spent close to eight million marks on a hotel fit for the occasion — a Nordic classicist pile by the city architect, standing where the Tammerkoski rapids leave the centre. It was finished in time. Then, on 7 September 1929, the lake steamer Kuru rolled over in a gale on Näsijärvi with 150 passengers and twelve crew aboard, and around 138 of them drowned within sight of the city they had just left. Every anniversary event was cancelled. The Grand Hotel Tammer opened into a town in mourning, without ceremony, and has been open more or less ever since.

## Strömmer's Statement

Bertel Strömmer was Tampere's town architect, and the Tammer is his argument that an industrial city deserved architecture of international standing. He built in Nordic Classicism — the restrained, stripped, faintly severe classical manner that swept Finland and Sweden in the 1920s before functionalism arrived and swept it away again. The result is one of the oldest hotels still operating in Finland; a 2014 account in the local press ranked it third-oldest in the country. The Finnish National Board of Antiquities lists the building as part of the protected Tammerkoski heritage area, so the fabric stays as Strömmer left it: the original rooms downstairs, the restored dining hall, the proportions of a period when a hotel lobby was expected to intimidate slightly. Alexander Adlivankin, a Franco-Russian restaurateur, opened the first restaurant with a chef brought from Japan, Swedish waitstaff and imported French silverware and chandeliers, which tells you the ambition involved — reportedly only the potato peeler spoke Finnish.

## The Kuru

The Kuru had been given a third deck in 1927 without a proper stability review, and the extra weight sat high. On 7 September 1929 the wind on Näsijärvi reached eight on the Beaufort scale, gusting harder, and the ship went over. The dead included Ida Vihuri, a member of the Finnish parliament. Most were not public figures at all — people crossing a lake on an ordinary autumn day, in a country where lake steamers were simply how you travelled. The counted death toll was 136 and is usually given as 138; either figure remains the worst loss of life in Finland's inland waters. The wreck was raised within the year, the cabin structures cut away to lower her centre of gravity, and the Kuru sailed on until 1939. The hotel a few kilometres away opened its doors in the shadow of the disaster, and the two events have been joined in Tampere's memory ever since.

## Depression, then Marshals and Cosmonauts

The 1930s nearly finished the hotel off. The depression years drove the Tammer into serious financial trouble almost immediately after opening, and only the improving economy at the end of the decade saved it. What followed was a long run as the room in which Tampere met anyone worth meeting. Marshal Gustaf Mannerheim stayed here. So did Frans Emil Sillanpää, who won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1939 and was a frequent guest, and Kalle Päätalo, whose enormous autobiographical cycle made him one of Finland's most-read novelists. The Soviet cosmonaut Yuri Gagarin came through too. Ownership has changed hands more often than the building has: the Tammer belonged to the Finnish chain Sokos Hotels from 1995 to 2016 as Solo Sokos Hotel Tammer, and became a Radisson Blu on 31 March 2016. In summer the terrace restaurant Tammerin puisto puts tables outside.

## The Plaque About the Ban

Somewhere in the building is a plaque recording a prohibition: in 1946, the men of Lauri Törni's wartime detachment were banned from gathering here. Törni was born in Viipuri in 1919 and became one of the most decorated soldiers of Finland's wars, awarded the Mannerheim Cross in July 1944 for leading a unit that operated behind Soviet lines. After the 1944 armistice he took German training and served briefly with the Waffen-SS, and post-war Finland — under Allied Control Commission supervision and in no position to be sentimental — convicted him of treason in 1946 and sentenced him to six years. President Paasikivi pardoned him in 1948. He fled to the United States, enlisted in 1954 as Larry Thorne, made captain in the Special Forces, and died when his helicopter went down near Da Nang on 18 October 1965. His remains were identified in 2003 and buried at Arlington. The plaque commemorates none of that — only a hotel management, in a nervous year, deciding which veterans could not be seen in its bar.

## Flight Context

The Radisson Blu Grand Hotel Tammer stands at 61.5008°N, 23.7650°E, on the east side of the Tammerkoski rapids in central Tampere, in the Kyttälä district. From the air, pick up the rapids as a straight dark channel running north–south through the city between Lake Näsijärvi and Lake Pyhäjärvi; the hotel sits near their northern end, close to Tampere Cathedral's tower and across the water from the Finlayson mill complex. The site of the Kuru sinking lies out on Näsijärvi a short distance north, a broad open stretch of water that shows clearly why an autumn gale was dangerous there. Recommended viewing altitude is 1,500–2,500 feet AGL for the city, or 4,000–5,000 feet to hold both lakes and the northern lake basin in one frame. Tampere-Pirkkala (EFTP) is about 15 km southwest; Helsinki-Vantaa (EFHK) roughly 160 km south-southeast. Autumn brings the low cloud and strong westerlies that made Näsijärvi lethal in 1929 — brief the winds before any low-level lake transit.
