# Tammerkoski

A short, steep channel of rapids that dropped eighteen metres between two lakes, powered a city into existence, and is now one of Finland's official national landscapes with tram wires strung over it.

**Location:** 61.4981, 23.7639

**Categories:** Rivers of Finland, Industrial heritage, National landscapes of Finland, Tampere, Hydroelectric power

## Introduction

Every so often, Tampere switches its river off. The gates upstream close, the channel between the dams runs dry, and for a few days engineers walk on bare rock in the middle of the city, checking the footings of bridges that normally stand in white water. People lean over the rails to watch. What they are looking down at is the reason Tampere exists at all: a narrow notch in the bedrock where Lake Näsijärvi spills eighteen metres down into Lake Pyhäjärvi, and where, for six centuries, somebody has always been arguing about who gets to use it.

## Eighteen Metres, Six Centuries of Argument

Tammerkoski turns up in official documents as early as 1405, and the first dams were built on it during that same century. Water this useful was never going to be shared quietly. In 1466 the settlements of Takahuhti and Messukylä fell out with Tammerkoski over their respective shares of the milling rights — a quarrel important enough to be written down, which suggests it was neither the first nor the politest. A hundred years later the crown noticed what the peasants had built and tried to replace their dams with its own. The locals refused, and the crown gave up. It is a small episode, easy to skip, but it establishes something durable about the place. The rapids were common property long before they were industrial property, and the people living beside them had opinions.

## The King's Signature

By the 17th century a well-known market had grown on the banks, clustered near the Tammerkoski manor just west of the crossing. The main market shifted to Harju early in the following century, but the reputation stayed. Then, in 1775, King Gustav III of Sweden travelled through Finland and signed a charter founding a town on the rapids; it was called Tammerkoski before it was called Tampere, and full city rights followed on 1 October 1779. Fewer than two hundred people lived in it. The king's surveyors were not being visionary about textiles or turbines. They were being practical about falling water, which in 1775 was the only serious source of industrial power that anyone had.

## Four Turbines in a Row

Three dams and four power stations now occupy the channel, stacked down the drop like steps on a stair. The highest dam sits between the old Finlayson and Tampella works and feeds a station on either bank — Tampella's completed in 1916, Finlayson's in 1926. Below them is the city's own Keskiputous plant of 1932, and at the bottom the Alakoski dam beside the Tako paperboard mill, finished in 1937. Together they come to roughly 18 megawatts and around 74 gigawatt-hours a year, which is modest by modern standards and rather beside the point. Tako still runs, the last major industrial tenant on the water, turning out high-grade board for luxury packaging — including cartons for French perfume. Water that once drove the largest cotton mill in the Nordic countries now helps wrap scent bottles.

## Bronze on the Rail

Crossings came slowly. The first known wooden bridge spanned the rapids as early as the 16th century, an iron one replaced it in 1884, and the present Hämeensilta — sixty-eight metres of concrete arch faced in red granite — opened in 1929. What makes people stop halfway across are the four bronze figures standing at its corners, each four and a half metres tall, cast by Wäinö Aaltonen and paid for by the industrialist Rafael Haarla. Three of them are medieval birkarls: a tax collector, a merchant and a hunter, the men who once worked this waterway for tribute and fur. The fourth is the Finnish Maiden, and a plaster version of her travelled to the 1939 New York World's Fair. Most traffic now uses Satakunnansilta, Paasikivensilta or Ratinan silta. Hämeensilta is where the photographs get taken.

## A National Landscape with Trams Over It

In 1995 the Finnish Ministry of the Environment named twenty-seven national landscapes — the officially agreed views of the country. Most are lakes, ridges and archipelago. Tammerkoski is the one with tram wires above it. Red brick factory walls rise straight out of the canal, floodlit once the light goes, and anglers work the pools below the dams; the water rates class II for quality, which is respectable for a channel that spent a century as a power source. In May 2026 the rapids became the spine of Tampere's national urban park, Finland's twelfth, running out from here along the shores of both lakes. And every May Day the city's engineering freshmen are still lowered into the water by crane, which is one way of maintaining a working relationship with a river.

## Flight Context

Tammerkoski runs at 61.4981°N, 23.7639°E, a short north-south channel linking Lake Näsijärvi to Lake Pyhäjärvi through the middle of Tampere. From the air the rapids read as a bright seam between two large lakes, flanked by dense blocks of dark red brick — the clearest visual fix on central Finland's lake district. The 168-metre Näsinneula tower stands 1.5 km northwest on the Näsijärvi shore and is the best single landmark for locating the channel. Recommended viewing altitude 2,000-3,500 ft AGL for the rapids and factory quarter, 6,000-8,000 ft to hold both lakes in one frame. Tampere-Pirkkala (EFTP) lies roughly 13 km southwest; Helsinki-Vantaa (EFHK) is 150 km south-southeast. Summer visibility is generally excellent; winter brings low cloud, freezing precipitation and lake-effect haze, and the lakes ice over from roughly December to April, which flattens the contrast between water and land.
