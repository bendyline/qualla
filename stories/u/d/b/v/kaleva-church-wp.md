# Kaleva Church

Raili and Reima Pietilä won a competition with a line of near-poetry and built a 30-metre concrete church that Tampere immediately nicknamed the Silo of Souls.

**Location:** 61.4991, 23.7940

**Categories:** Lutheran churches in Finland, Buildings and structures in Tampere, Modern architecture, Reima and Raili Pietilä buildings, Tourist attractions in Tampere

## Introduction

The winning entry in the 1959 competition arrived under the motto "Hellitä mäkivyötä, meridiaani" — roughly, loosen the hill-belt, meridian. It beat 48 other submissions. Seven years later, when the finished building stood on its rocky knoll in Tampere with seventeen curving concrete walls rising thirty-five metres into the Finnish sky, the city looked at it and reached a verdict of its own. They called it Sielujen siilo. The Silo of Souls. The parish vicar was named Paavo Viljanen, and since vilja is the Finnish word for grain, the alternative nickname wrote itself: Viljanen's silo. Nobody, in the end, quotes the motto. Everybody remembers the grain elevator.

## A Parish Without a Building

Kaleva parish came into existence in 1953 and spent its first years borrowing space. In 1959 the board of Tampere's Evangelical Lutheran congregations opened a design competition, and forty-nine architects answered. The winner was Reima Pietilä, working with Raili Paatelainen — his architectural partner and, from 1963, his wife. Her name is frequently left off the credits for this building and belongs on them; the practice was collaborative and the church is a Pietilä double signature. The commission was confirmed in 1962, construction ran from 1964 to 1966, and the church was consecrated that August. The site handed the architects a problem worth having: a small rock outcrop in the Liisankallio district, sitting exactly where two of Tampere's major arteries, Teiskontie and Sammonkatu, converge. A building there would be seen from every direction and could not have a back.

## Twelve Days of Concrete

The Pietiläs' answer was a ring of seventeen tall, narrow, hollow concrete elements — each one a subtly different curve, convex or concave, none repeating — separated by full-height wooden-framed windows. There are eighteen windows and eighteen exterior doors, so that the building is essentially all wall and all entrance at once. The walls were built by slip forming, a technique in which the formwork climbs continuously as concrete is poured beneath it. Finnish grain elevators were going up this way at the time, which is precisely why the nickname stuck. The whole thirty-five-metre height was cast in twelve days: roughly 4,500 cubic metres of concrete and 150 tonnes of reinforcing steel, poured almost without pause. Fair-faced slip-cast concrete had not been used on a Finnish public building before. In 1966 it read as either the future or an industrial accident, depending on who was looking.

## Turned Inside Out

The competition drawings proposed raw concrete on the exterior and ceramic tile within. During construction the scheme was inverted. Pale sand-coloured tile now faces the street, pitched to sit alongside the postwar apartment blocks lining both roads, while the concrete is the surface the congregation actually lives with. The effect inside is not what the exterior threatens. Light enters through the tall slots between the wall elements, which are angled and offset so that the beams sweep across the concrete rather than pointing anywhere in particular, and the main hall opens up thirty metres overhead with seating for 1,120 people. Every furnishing is lacquered Finnish pine, which warms a space that could easily have been punishing. Reima Pietilä also made the altar sculpture, Särkynyt ruoko — Broken Reed — installed in 1969. The organ came from the Kangasala organ works: three manuals and pedal, forty-one stops, some 3,500 pipes.

## The Fish and the Rhombus

What shape is it, actually? The parish and the city both describe the floor plan as a fish, the ancient Christian symbol scratched on catacomb walls long before anyone built a cathedral. Architectural historians at Docomomo, less lyrically, call it an irregular rhombus. Both readings survive a look at the drawings, and the disagreement is the interesting part: this is a building that resists being reduced to one diagram. The flat roof carries a bell tower shaped as an abstracted cross, housing three bells cast in West Germany, weighing 1,300, 840 and 550 kilograms and tuned to E, G and A. They rang at noon every day until 2020, when the practice ended. A chapel, sacristy and parish rooms fill out the remainder of a structure whose total floor area runs to some 3,600 square metres.

## The Nickname Became Affection

Kaleva Church was placed under the protection of the Church Act in 2006 and is now routinely named among the key works of postwar Finnish modernism — Finland's most monumental example, in one official assessment, of newer church architecture. It appears in brutalism surveys and architecture pilgrimages, and visitors come to Tampere specifically for it. The congregation, meanwhile, has never stopped calling it the silo. That is not really an insult anymore. A grain elevator is a structure built to hold something essential through a long northern winter, standing taller than anything around it, visible from the edge of town. For a church at a crossroads on a hill, there are worse metaphors to be stuck with.

## Flight Context

Kaleva Church stands at 61.4991°N, 23.7940°E at Liisanpuisto 1, on a low rock outcrop in the Liisankallio district about 1.5 km east of central Tampere, at the junction of Teiskontie and Sammonkatu. From the air it is one of the easiest buildings in the city to identify: a pale, fluted oval mass roughly 35 metres tall with a flat roof and an abstract cross-form bell tower, standing clear of the surrounding four- and five-storey apartment blocks. Use the Tampere isthmus for orientation — Lake Näsijärvi north, Lake Pyhäjärvi south, the Tammerkoski rapids between them, and the 168-metre Näsinneula tower northwest of the church. Descend to 1,500–2,500 ft AGL for the wall geometry; the shadow lines between the seventeen concrete elements read best in low, raking light. Tampere–Pirkkala (EFTP) is roughly 15 km southwest; Helsinki–Vantaa (EFHK) about 150 km south. Winter snow cover reduces contrast against the pale tile cladding; spring and autumn low sun give the strongest modelling.
