# Plevna, Tampere

A windowless Tampere weaving hall named for a Bulgarian siege became the first building in the Nordic countries lit by electric bulbs — and is now a ten-screen cinema and a brewery.

**Location:** 61.5011, 23.7583

**Categories:** Buildings and structures in Tampere, Industrial buildings in Finland, Cinemas, Industrial heritage

## Introduction

On 15 March 1882, inside a windowless brick weaving hall on the bank of the Tammerkoski rapids, incandescent bulbs came on. Thomas Edison had made the things practical barely two years earlier. Now they were burning in a Finnish cotton mill — the first building in the Nordic countries, and in the Russian Empire to which Finland then belonged as an autonomous Grand Duchy, to be lit by electric light. The hall was called Plevna, after a siege fought five years earlier and two thousand kilometres away. Today the same walls hold ten cinema screens and a brewery, and on any given evening several hundred people sit in the dark where twelve hundred power looms once ran.

## Named for a Bulgarian Siege

From July to December 1877, Russian and Romanian armies threw themselves at the Ottoman garrison holding Pleven, in what is now Bulgaria, under Osman Nuri Pasha. The defence lasted nearly five months and cost the attackers something on the order of fifty thousand casualties. Europe followed the whole grinding business in the newspapers, and the place-name entered the language: towns in Montana, Ontario and Missouri still carry it. Finland's connection was more direct than most. The Finnish Guard's sharpshooters served in the campaign as troops of the Grand Duchy, and when Finlayson's vast new weaving hall was completed in 1877 it was named in recognition of their part. A cotton shed in Tampere thus commemorates a battle in the Balkans, which is the kind of thing that happens when a small country's army belongs to somebody else's empire.

## Twelve Hundred Looms

Georg Gunliffe and F. L. Calonius designed the hall for Finlayson and it went up between 1876 and 1877. On completion it held the largest weaving floor in the Nordic countries: twelve hundred power looms under one roof. The red brick façade has almost no windows, because the light came from above through ceiling glazing — a design that keeps daylight even and shadowless across the looms while denying the weavers anything to look at but their work. A bridge built in 1876 linked Plevna to the Katuvapriikki building and served as the factory's main entrance; it has since been taken down. The mill's prosperity rested on things the company's own historians no longer soften. Children worked here. The raw cotton arriving from across the Atlantic in the mid-nineteenth century had been picked by enslaved people, and Finlayson's ledgers were no more innocent of that than any other cotton spinner's in Europe.

## The Night the Bulbs Came On

Why would a cotton mill be among the first buildings anywhere to want electric light? Consider the conditions. Tampere sits at 61 degrees north, where December offers perhaps five usable hours of daylight, and a weaving hall lit from the ceiling goes dark early. Consider also what fills the air in a cotton mill: fine airborne fibre, for which open flame is a permanent and well-understood enemy. Edison's sealed glass bulb answered both problems at once. The installation of 15 March 1882 came only a couple of years after Edison's bulb became a commercial proposition at all, and no other building in the Nordic countries or the Russian Empire had beaten Plevna to it. Tampere has been quietly pleased about that ever since, and a city monument marks the event. For the weavers on the floor, the change was simpler and larger than any of that: for the first time, the winter shift could see.

## Ten Screens and a Brewery

Finlayson stopped spinning cotton in Tampere in the mid-1990s, and Plevna found second and third lives almost immediately. The brewery restaurant opened in 1994, serving its own beers alongside those of the Koskipanimo microbrewery next door; since February 2018 customers have been able to buy them to take away. In 1999 Finnkino opened a cinema in the building — ten auditoriums, 1,653 seats, the largest in Finland outside the Helsinki region. Each March it becomes the principal venue of the Tampere Film Festival, founded in 1969 and the oldest short-film festival in Northern Europe, which screens some five hundred films across five days. The symmetry is almost too neat. A hall built to be lit artificially, in an age when that was a marvel, now sells tickets to sit in it with the lights off.

## Flight Context

Plevna stands at 61.5011°N, 23.7583°E, in the Finlayson mill district on the west bank of the Tammerkoski rapids, central Tampere. From above, look for the long, low, wide-span roof running roughly east–west among the taller multi-storey blocks — the weaving hall's footprint is unmistakably different from the spinning mills around it. The Finlayson chimney is the best single landmark; the rapids themselves cut a straight dark line between Lake Näsijärvi to the north and Lake Pyhäjärvi to the south. Recommended viewing altitude is 1,500–2,500 feet AGL for detail on the mill roofs, or 4,000–5,000 feet to hold the whole isthmus and both lakes in frame. Tampere-Pirkkala (EFTP) lies about 15 km southwest and is the practical base; Helsinki-Vantaa (EFHK) is roughly 160 km south-southeast. Winter afternoons darken fast this far north — plan low-level passes for the middle of the day between November and February.
