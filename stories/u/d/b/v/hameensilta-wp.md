# Hämeensilta

Tampere's central bridge was demolished and rebuilt in 2018, then dressed again in its own 1929 granite and its four bronze giants, so that nothing appeared to change.

**Location:** 61.4979, 23.7636

**Categories:** Bridges in Finland, Bridges completed in 1929, Buildings and structures in Tampere, Public sculpture, Transport in Tampere

## Introduction

In February 2018, crews began taking Hämeensilta apart. When they finished, the bridge looked exactly as it had before. The reddish granite facing the arch is the same stone that was laid in 1929, lifted off and set back; the four bronze figures standing on the railings are the same ones Wäinö Aaltonen cast that year. Everything between them is new, and it now carries a tram.

## Eighteen Metres of Falling Water

Tampere exists because of the Tammerkoski. The rapids drop eighteen metres from Lake Näsijärvi down to Lake Pyhäjärvi through a narrow neck of land, and that fall of water is what put mills, and then a city, on this spot. Documented since 1405 and dammed from the fifteenth century onward, the channel is now stepped by three dams and four power stations, its banks lined with the red-brick mill buildings that have mostly become restaurants and museums.

A river with a city on both sides needs crossings, and this one has had them for a long time. The first known bridge over the Tammerkoski went up in the sixteenth century, when the ground belonged to the parish of Messukylä. Tampere itself was chartered in 1779. A wooden bridge followed in 1807, an iron one replaced it in 1884, and by the 1920s a fast-industrialising city had outgrown that too.

## No First Prize Awarded

The city ran a design competition in 1924 and received sixteen proposals. The jury awarded no first prize — not one entry was judged directly buildable. What eventually rose was worked up around a concept by the engineer E. R. Eriksson and constructed by the Helsinki firm Constructor Oy.

The result is a single reinforced-concrete vault with a main span of forty metres, sixty-eight metres long overall and twenty-eight and a half metres wide, faced in reddish granite quarried at Kalvola. It is not a delicate bridge. It reads as a slab of stone laid across the rapids, which is roughly the point: Hämeenkatu, the city's main street, runs straight across it, tying the Kyttälä district on the east bank to the older centre on the west. The name comes from Häme, the Finnish name for the historical province of Tavastia. Construction finished in February 1929.

## The Celebration That Was Called Off

The bridge was meant to be inaugurated as part of Tampere's 150th anniversary, the same civic birthday that produced the granite observation tower on the Pyynikki ridge across town. Then, on 7 September 1929, the steamer Kuru capsized in a gale on Näsijärvi and 136 people drowned — the worst disaster ever to occur on Finland's inland waters, and in a city of this size, a loss with a personal edge for a great many families.

The dedication ceremony was cancelled. Hämeensilta opened on 1 October 1929 without speeches.

## Four Figures on the Railing

The statues came from an industrialist's anniversary present to himself. Rafael Haarla paid for them to mark twenty years of his factory, the writer Jalmari Finne devised what they should represent, and Wäinö Aaltonen, one of Finland's foremost sculptors, made them. Unveiled on 26 September 1929, the four bronzes stand about 4.5 metres tall on the bridge parapets.

One is the Finnish Maiden, the national personification, and she has travelled: she has appeared on a postage stamp and was shown at the New York World's Fair in 1939. The other three — a Tax Collector, a Merchant and a Hunter — represent the birkarls, the medieval traders and tribute-takers whose name is entangled with Pirkkala, the parish just downstream. Placing a tax collector permanently on your city's front door is a choice with a certain dry humour to it, and the four of them are the reason the bridge is among Tampere's best-known landmarks.

## New Bridge, Old Skin

By the 2010s Tampere was building a tram network, and Hämeenkatu was on the route. The bridge could not take it, so between February 2018 and the end of 2019 it was rebuilt — the southern half first, the northern half from April 2019, with a lane of traffic and a shared foot-and-cycle path kept open throughout so that the city's main street never quite closed.

The rebuild was structural, not cosmetic. Underneath, everything is new and sized for tram loads; on top, the deck now carries combined tram and bus lanes, footways and a one-way cycle track on each side. The original granite cladding was taken off, kept, and reinstalled. Aaltonen's four bronzes were restored and returned to their posts. The first test tram rolled across on 15 November 2020, and passenger services on lines 1 and 3 began on 9 August 2021. The Maiden and the tax collector, who had watched trolleybuses and then cars pass beneath them for ninety years, went back to work with a new sound to get used to.

## Flight Context

61.4979°N, 23.7636°E, spanning the Tammerkoski rapids in the middle of Tampere on the line of Hämeenkatu. From the air the rapids are the giveaway: a straight bright channel of white water running north–south between Näsijärvi and Pyhäjärvi, crossed by a short sequence of bridges — Satakunnansilta (1900) upstream, Hämeensilta the wide one at the centre. The 168 m Näsinneula needle at Särkänniemi, about 1.5 km northwest, is the best fix in any visibility. Useful altitude is 1,500–2,500 ft AGL, tracking the rapids from north to south. Tampere–Pirkkala (EFTP) is 13 km southwest, single 2,700 m runway; Helsinki–Vantaa (EFHK) about 160 km south. Winter fog forms readily over open water below the dams, and December daylight in central Finland is short and slanting.
