# Viikinsaari

Fifteen hectares in Lake Pyhäjärvi that a confectioner turned into a restaurant, a city bought for its mill workers, and a sculptor turned from a lock-up into a chapel.

**Location:** 61.4833, 23.6833

**Categories:** Lake islands of Finland, Tampere, Tourist attractions in Tampere, Geography of Pirkanmaa, Nature reserves

## Introduction

The chapel on Viikinsaari used to be the lock-up. In 1900 the City of Tampere put up a building on this island in Lake Pyhäjärvi to hold its offices and a small holding cell, a structure local people took to calling the castle of Viikinsaari. In 1995 the artist Ilkka Väätti converted it into a chapel that deliberately draws on both Eastern and Western Christian traditions, and it is now a work of art in its own right. All of this sits about twenty minutes by boat from the middle of Finland's third-largest city, on fifteen hectares of woodland where most of the ground may never be built on.

## Kaidesaari, Before the Name Changed

The island first appears in writing in 1596, in the court records of Pirkkala. For most of its documented life it was Kaidesaari, the narrow island, and it kept that name into the nineteenth century, when it passed to the Viikki manor and took the manor's name instead. Kaidesaari has not disappeared entirely; the restaurant on the island still carries it, Wanha Kaidesaari, the old Kaidesaari. Fourteen and nine-tenths hectares is not a great deal of land. It is enough for a swimming beach, a stand of ferns, a dance floor and a chapel, and small enough that a walk round the footpath still leaves time to catch the boat home.

## The Confectioner's Ferry

Carl Gustav Tallqvist was a confectioner, and in 1866 he did the obvious thing with a wooded island a short row from a fast-growing industrial town: he opened a restaurant on it. Then he did the less obvious thing and had a boat built so that anyone could get there. The ferry made the restaurant, and in 1881 Tallqvist bought the island outright from the Viikki manor. After his death the place went quiet. For a stretch of years the most productive activity on Viikinsaari was cattle grazing, and the restaurant that had drawn Tampere across the water stood empty on an island nobody was quite using.

## A Park for the Mill Workers

The City of Tampere bought the island in 1893 with an explicit model in mind: Korkeasaari in Helsinki, a public island for people who owned no summer cottage to escape to. Tampere was a town of cotton mills, paper works and shift schedules, and the point was to give the people inside those buildings somewhere green to spend a Sunday. A new restaurant went up in the 1890s. The offices and the lock-up followed in 1900, designed by the architect Berndt Blom. A dance pavilion arrived, and with it the summer evenings that made the island's name. Through the early twentieth century Viikinsaari was working-class Tampere's recreation ground. It faded somewhat in the later decades of the century, and has been busy and carefully tended again for years now.

## Ten and a Half Hectares Left Alone

The western end of the island is a nature reserve covering 10.53 of its 14.9 hectares, a startling ratio for a place that also offers mini golf. Botanists have counted 253 vascular plant species there, roughly 150 of them native, among them rare orchids and a broad colony of ostrich ferns — the tall, shuttlecock-shaped fern that turns a Finnish forest floor into something prehistoric by July. About thirty bird species nest on the island, and the collared flycatcher, scarce in Finland, is thought to nest there too, though no one has confirmed it. A marked footpath runs through the protected ground. On one side of Viikinsaari you can stand alone among the ferns; on the other, somebody is grilling sausages over an open fire.

## Sausages, Waltzes and Metal

In summer a boat runs out from Laukontori harbour in the centre of Tampere, operated by Suomen Hopealinja. In winter, when Pyhäjärvi freezes hard, people simply walk or ski across. The city's culture services programme the island through the warm months with nature tours for children, dance lessons and music evenings, and the info office lends out whatever visitors forget to bring: rowing boats, fishing rods, mini golf clubs, petanque, croquet, badminton, darts, footballs, volleyballs, board games. There are beaches, playgrounds and a fire pit kept ready for grilling. And since 2015 the island has hosted Saarihelvetti, a metal festival whose name means island hell — which, on a June evening on a nature reserve twenty minutes from town, is either a contradiction or exactly the joke.

## Flight Context

Viikinsaari lies at 61.4833°N, 23.6833°E, in Lake Pyhäjärvi about 3 km southwest of central Tampere. From the air it reads as a compact, wooded, oval island with a jetty and a cluster of pale buildings on its eastern end and unbroken tree cover to the west — the developed and protected halves are visible as a clean split. Use the Pyynikki ridge on the northern shore and the Laukontori harbour front at the head of the lake as approach references. A viewing altitude of 1,500–2,500 feet AGL frames the island and its ferry track together. Tampere–Pirkkala Airport (EFTP) is roughly 9 km south-southwest, close enough that arrival and departure paths cross this water; Helsinki–Vantaa (EFHK) is about 145 km southeast. In midwinter the lake freezes and the island loses its outline against white ice.
