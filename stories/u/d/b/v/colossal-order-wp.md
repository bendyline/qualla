# Colossal Order

A Tampere studio that no investor wanted to fund made the best-selling Finnish game ever, then walked away from the franchise it built.

**Location:** 61.4977, 23.7670

**Categories:** Video game companies of Finland, Companies based in Tampere, Video game companies established in 2009, Technology and industry, Modern culture

## Introduction

Stockholm's city government has run transport scenarios inside a video game. Polish road authorities redesigned a motorway interchange after a YouTuber demonstrated its flaws in the same software. The game is Cities: Skylines, and the studio behind it had thirteen people on staff the day it shipped, working out of an office in Tampere, the Finnish city of mills and rapids where the studio was born and still sits.

## The Company Nobody Would Fund

Colossal Order was founded in the summer of 2009 by a group of developers who had been making mobile games at a Tampere company called Universomo. They did something slightly unusual for a studio started by programmers: they went outside their own circle to hire a chief executive, and Mariina Hallikainen, who has run the company ever since, came in from elsewhere.

Their first pitch was a public transport simulator. Investors were unimpressed — a game about running bus timetables did not look like a large market. So the money came from the Finnish state instead, in small, unglamorous pieces: the regional Centre for Economic Development, Transport and the Environment, the innovation agency Tekes, the DigiDemo fund of the Promotion Centre for Audiovisual Culture, plus a handful of private backers willing to take a flyer. In its earliest days the company was coached through the Yritystalli enterprise programme at Tampere University of Technology.

## Buses Before Cities

Cities in Motion arrived in 2011, a sequel in 2013. Both asked the player to do something few games had bothered with: keep a city's public transport network solvent, plotting tram routes and metro tunnels and watching commuters vote with their feet.

The publisher was Paradox Interactive, the Swedish house known for strategy games that expect patience from their audience. The working relationship became unusually loose for the industry — Colossal Order designed what it wanted, with the publisher setting schedules rather than creative direction. That freedom shows up in what the studio chose to prioritise. It kept a full-time programmer on modding tools alone, an investment that looks eccentric until you see what the community built with them.

## The Door SimCity Left Open

Paradox turned down the city-builder idea when it was first proposed, reasoning that nobody beats SimCity at its own game. Then in 2013 EA's SimCity reboot launched into a wall of server failures and design complaints, and the reasoning inverted overnight. The project was greenlit.

Cities: Skylines was released on 10 March 2015 and sold 250,000 copies in twenty-four hours, half a million within a week, a million within a month. By June 2022 it had passed twelve million across all platforms, making it both Paradox's best-selling title and the best-selling Finnish game ever made. Critics did not hedge — the PC version holds a Metacritic average of 85, and reviewers who had spent two years complaining about SimCity had somewhere to point. That October, Colossal Order was named Finnish Game Developer of the Year at the DigiExpo convention.

Support ran long and deep: fourteen expansion packs, twenty-two content packs and twenty-three music packs before the studio was done.

## When the Simulation Escapes

Something strange happens when a city-builder's traffic model is good enough. Players stop treating it as a toy. Stockholm's planners used Cities: Skylines in their own transport work; a Polish interchange was reworked after a player showed, inside the game, why it would jam. The modding scene turned it into a general-purpose sandbox, importing real terrain and rebuilding actual neighbourhoods block by block.

None of that was the studio's plan. It is what happens when a small team spends years refining how simulated people get to work, and then hands the keys to everyone else.

## A Sequel, and a Parting

Cities: Skylines II was announced on 6 March 2023 and released for Windows on 24 October that year, into serious trouble. Performance was poor on the hardware most players owned. The first paid add-on, Beach Properties, sat at four per cent positive Steam reviews three days after release; Paradox pulled it from sale, refunded buyers and promised to fix the game before selling more of it. Console versions were postponed, then postponed indefinitely. The game still passed a million copies by February 2024, and by that spring the performance had genuinely improved, but the launch had cost something that sales figures do not measure.

On 17 November 2025, after sixteen years together, Colossal Order and Paradox announced they were parting ways by mutual agreement. Development of the series passed at the start of 2026 to Iceflake Studios — which is, as it happens, also in Tampere, a few minutes across the same city.

## Flight Context

61.4977°N, 23.7670°E, in central Tampere just north of the Tammerkoski rapids and a short walk from Hämeensilta. This is a city-centre office address rather than a landmark, so navigate by the rapids: the 18-metre chain of falls between Näsijärvi and Pyhäjärvi, lined with red-brick mill buildings, runs straight through the middle of the frame. Näsinneula, the 168 m observation needle at Särkänniemi on the northwest shore, is the reliable visual fix. Useful viewing altitude is 2,000–3,000 ft AGL over the isthmus. Tampere–Pirkkala (EFTP) lies 13 km southwest with a single 2,700 m runway; Helsinki–Vantaa (EFHK) is about 160 km south. Clear high-pressure days in late spring give the sharpest look at the lake-and-ridge geometry that the studio's own games spent a decade abstracting.
