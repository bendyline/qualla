# Finlayson industrial area

The cotton mill that made Tampere was the largest industrial complex in the Nordic countries for seventy years, and in one record year a thousand of its workers were children.

**Location:** 61.5017, 23.7598

**Categories:** Industrial heritage, Buildings and structures in Tampere, Textile industry, Labour history, Tourist attractions in Tampere

## Introduction

On 15 March 1882, someone in the Plevna weaving hall threw a switch and Thomas Edison's bulbs came on — the first electric light in the Nordic countries, and in the whole Russian Empire. It is the fact Tampere tells about itself most often, and it is true. Less often told is who was standing underneath. The weavers, most of them women and a good many of them children, worked twelve-hour shifts in a hall of 1,200 power looms that had no windows in its walls at all; daylight, when there was any, came down through the roof. The same year, the mill installed the largest steam engine in Finland. The future arrived here early. It arrived on top of people.

## The Quaker Who Came to Hand Out Bibles

James Finlayson was born in Penicuik, Scotland, in 1772 — a Quaker and a self-taught engineer. He had already built a textile works in St Petersburg with the backing of Tsar Alexander I when, in 1819, he travelled through the Grand Duchy of Finland on a religious mission distributing bibles and noticed the Tammerkoski rapids. The Senate of Finland granted him the right to their waterpower. His factory, finished in 1823, did not make cloth at first; it made machines for other people's mills, and only turned to cotton in 1828. Finlayson never saw what his name became. On 1 March 1836 he handed the works to Georg Adolf Rauch, physician to the Russian emperor, and the St Petersburg merchant Carl Samuel Nottbeck, stipulating only that they keep the company's name. Then he went home to Edinburgh, where he died in 1852. Not one of the buildings he commissioned still stands.

## Six Floors of Cast Iron

What the new owners built instead is still there, and still the oldest thing in the complex. TR 1 is known as Kuusvooninkinen, from kuusi, Finnish for six, and våning, Swedish for floor; John Barker was among its designers and it was finished in 1837. Six storeys made it a skyscraper in a town of low wooden houses, and the trick that allowed it came out of the Fiskars ironworks — cast iron pillars carrying the floors in place of internal load-bearing walls. That one decision makes it Finland's first modern industrial building. Spinning machinery was in place by 1838 and running by 1839. Fire was the permanent terror of any cotton mill, and when an automatic firefighting system went in during 1892, the stair tower shared with the neighbouring TR 2 had to be raised so the water tank could sit high enough to feed it.

## One Thousand and One Children

In 1858 the company advertised for three hundred girls over the age of ten. In 1870, its record year, 1,001 children were at work in Finlayson's mills. The day was twelve hours long in air thick with cotton dust and noise loud enough to make speech pointless, and the work itself was monotonous in a way that is hard to convey — standing at a frame, catching broken threads, hour after hour, for roughly half an adult's wage. About two thirds of Finland's textile workers were women. The company liked to say it was the first employer in the country to offer a woman an independent living and a room of her own, and for many that was not untrue. Both things hold at once. The mill was a way out of rural destitution during the famine years of the 1860s, and it took a generation's childhood as the fare.

## A Company That Was Also a Town

Finlayson did not simply employ Tampere; for a while it more or less was Tampere. It ran a hospital, a pharmacy, a school, a library, a daycare, a children's home, dormitories for girls and for boys, a nursing home and its own fire brigade, and it operated a company welfare scheme decades before the state had one. In 1879 it built its own church in red brick and Gothic Revival, designed by the city architect Frans Ludvig Calonius. The Finlayson parish consisted entirely of cotton mill workers and their families, and in the 19th century that came to nearly half of everyone living in Tampere. Meanwhile the von Nottbeck family raised a palace above the works and a second mansion, Näsilinna, looking out over the lake. Both survive. So does the sixty-five-metre brick chimney put up beside the Siperia building in 1899.

## A Cinema in the Weaving Hall

Finlayson was the largest cotton mill in Finland by the 1840s and the largest industrial complex in the Nordic countries from the 1850s until the 1920s, with around three thousand people still on its books at the end of the 19th century. Textile production in the area finally stopped in the early 1990s. The city had seen it coming: a competition for the district's future ran in 1988, won by the 8 Studio architectural office, and a new zoning plan passed the council in 1995. Almost everything was kept. Plevna's windowless weaving hall is now a ten-screen cinema and a brewery restaurant; the Seelanti building holds a supermarket; the outlet store opened in 1923 still sells Finlayson linens on Kuninkaankatu. And the Finnish Labour Museum Werstas, the largest free-admission museum in the country, tells the workers' half of the story inside the walls where it happened.

## Flight Context

The Finlayson area occupies the west bank of the Tammerkoski rapids at 61.5017°N, 23.7598°E, immediately north of Satakunnankatu in central Tampere. From the air it is a solid block of dark red brick roofs on the left bank, with the former Tampella works facing it across the water; the 65-metre chimney beside the Siperia building is the sharpest vertical marker. The 168-metre Näsinneula observation tower stands about 1 km northwest and provides the easiest first fix. Recommended viewing altitude 1,500-3,000 ft AGL to resolve individual mill buildings and the dam between Finlayson and Tampella. Tampere-Pirkkala (EFTP) is roughly 13 km southwest, Helsinki-Vantaa (EFHK) 150 km south-southeast. Note the built-up city centre directly beneath and the tram corridor along Hämeenkatu 400 m south. Clear summer air gives excellent definition on the brickwork; winter overcast and freezing precipitation are common from November through March.
