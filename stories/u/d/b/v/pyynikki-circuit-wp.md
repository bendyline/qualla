# Pyynikki Circuit

For two seasons in the early 1960s the motorcycle world championship raced through a Tampere suburb on ordinary public roads - eleven corners, no run-off, pine trunks for barriers - until the circuit was judged too narrow and, a few years later, simply too dangerous.

## Introduction

One minute, forty and three-tenths of a second. That is the fastest lap ever officially recorded at Pyynikki, set by Mike Hailwood on an MV Agusta in 1963 - an average of about 129 km/h around 3.608 kilometres of ordinary Tampere road. Eleven corners. No run-off. Pine trunks and kerbstones and, on parts of the lap, the front gates of people's houses. On race day the district was a round of the Grand Prix motorcycle world championship. On Monday morning the same tarmac went back to being a street, with the same residents pulling out of the same driveways.

## Pyynikinajot

The races were called Pyynikinajot, the Pyynikki runs, and they began in 1932 - a clockwise loop laid out on public roads through forest and a residential quarter on the great gravel ridge that gives the district its name, west of central Tampere and above the shore of Lake Pyhäjärvi. Finland took to road racing the way it later took to rallying, with a national talent for going quickly on surfaces that do not encourage it. The Second World War stopped everything; the country fought the Soviet Union twice and then Germany, and the motorcycles were needed elsewhere. Racing resumed in 1946 and continued, summer after summer, for another twenty-five years.

## Two Seasons of the World Championship

In 1962 and 1963 the Finnish motorcycle Grand Prix at Pyynikki counted as a round of the world championship, which put the fastest riders alive on a suburban street. Luigi Taveri won the 50cc class in 1962 and Jim Redman the 125cc; Alan Shepherd set a 125cc lap record of 1:58.200 on an MZ that year. In 1963 Mike Hailwood - nine-time world champion, and later a George Medal for hauling Clay Regazzoni out of a burning Formula One car at Kyalami while his own suit caught fire - won both the 350cc and the 500cc races and left both lap records behind him: 1:47.300 and 1:40.300, on the MV Agusta fours. Then the championship looked at the place properly. The circuit was too narrow for the size of the fields, and from 1964 the Finnish Grand Prix moved east to Imatra.

## The Arithmetic Nobody Wanted to Do

Losing the world championship did not end the racing; local and national meetings ran at Pyynikki for another seven years. But road circuits were living on borrowed time everywhere in the 1960s. Bikes had grown faster than the roads they ran on, and the safety margin on a street course is whatever happens to be beside the road - a hedge, a wall, a spectator standing where he was told not to. Across Europe, one venue after another was struck off. In 1971 racing at Pyynikki was banned outright on safety grounds, which means Tampere's road circuit was closed by a decision rather than by a disaster - the rarer and better way for these things to end.

## Saarinen's Last Summer There

The final season belonged to a rider from Turku who was about to become the best in the world. Jarno Saarinen won two classes at Pyynikki in 1971. Born in December 1945, he had learned racing on frozen lakes - he took the Finnish 250cc ice racing championship in 1965 - and he rode in a way nobody quite had before, hanging his body off the inside of the machine with a knee out toward the road, the posture every rider now uses without thinking about where it came from. In 1972 he won the 250cc world championship. On 20 May 1973 he was killed at Monza, at twenty-seven, in a chain-reaction crash in the 250cc race that also took Renzo Pasolini's life. Finland's greatest motorcycle racer had one of his last home wins on these streets.

## What There Is to See

Nothing, and that is the point. There is no grandstand to look for, no gate, no crumbling paddock, no ruin, because the circuit was never anything but a public road that people occasionally closed. The 3.608 kilometres are still there under Pyynikki: pine woods, quiet houses, the ridge falling away toward Pyhäjärvi, cyclists and dog walkers and delivery vans. Stand at a corner and the geometry is still legible if you know what you are looking at - the camber, the approach, the way the trees crowd the exit. Hailwood averaged about 129 km/h through here in 1963. These days the loudest thing on the lap is a city bus in low gear.

## Flight Context

The former Pyynikki Circuit lies around 61.4944°N, 23.7297°E, in the Pyynikki district roughly two kilometres west of central Tampere, Finland. Nothing on the ground marks it, so navigate by terrain: Pyynikki is the wooded gravel ridge running east-west along the northern shore of Lake Pyhäjärvi, separating the lake from the city, crowned by a squat red granite observation tower. The 3.608 km loop ran on public roads through the pine woods and residential streets on and below that ridge. From 1,500-2,500 feet the street pattern of the district is clear; the Pispala ridge continues west toward Lake Näsijärvi and the narrows at Santalahti. Tampere-Pirkkala (EFTP) is about 11 km southwest; Helsinki-Vantaa (EFHK) is roughly 150 km south-southeast. The pine canopy hides the roads in summer; snow and bare deciduous cover in winter make the layout far easier to trace.
