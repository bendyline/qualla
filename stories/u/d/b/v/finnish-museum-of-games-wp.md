# Finnish Museum of Games

A national games museum paid for by 1,120 strangers, installed in the Tampere factory halls that once turned out locomotives and artillery.

**Location:** 61.5030, 23.7604

**Categories:** Video game museums, Museums in Tampere, Museums established in 2017, Tourist attractions in Tampere

## Introduction

In 2015 a museum that did not yet exist asked the public for money, and 1,120 people handed over 85,860 euros. Some were players in their forties who still owned a working Commodore; some were studios with more cash than most Finnish museums see in a decade. Supercell and Housemarque were among the backers, and so was Colossal Order — a Tampere outfit that released Cities: Skylines that same March and has been quietly running the world's traffic ever since. The Finnish Museum of Games opened in January 2017 in the halls of a nineteenth-century linen mill and foundry, and by the end of that June more than 100,000 people had walked through it.

## Under the Old Linen Mill

Vapriikki, the museum centre that houses it, takes its name from the Swedish word for factory, and the joke is entirely accurate. These are the old Tampella works on the Tammerkoski, founded in 1861 out of a linen mill and a foundry, and over the following century they produced an almost comic range of things: locomotives both steam and diesel, paper machines, steam boilers, turbines, mining drills, mortars and coastal artillery. The last machines stopped in 2000. The buildings reopened as culture, and Vapriikki now stacks seven museums into 14,000 square metres — natural history, media, the postal service, dolls and dress, the Finnish Ice Hockey Hall of Fame, the regional collection of Pirkanmaa, and the games.

## One Hundred Games and Six Rooms

The permanent exhibition is built around a hundred Finnish games and six thematic period rooms, plus a video-game arcade and space for shows that change. Around eighty-five of the digital titles are playable, which is the point and also the hardest part of the job — keeping decades of dying hardware alive so a fourteen-year-old can put their hands on a machine older than their parents' marriage. The period rooms do the emotional work. A game is not only its code; it is the carpet you sat on, the shape of the television, the specific quality of a Finnish afternoon in which there was nothing else to do. Reconstruct the room and the memory arrives without being asked for.

## Dice, Cardboard and People in Costume

Screens are only half of it. The museum collects and shows board games, card games, miniature wargames, tabletop roleplaying games and live-action roleplay — a category most institutions have no idea how to display, since the artefact of a larp is mostly other people. Ropecon, the long-running Finnish roleplaying convention, has had an exhibition of its own. So have pixel art, personal play histories gathered from ordinary players, and the development of the virtual-reality game P.O.L.L.E.N. The curators also sat more than forty Finnish game developers down in front of a camera for half an hour each, then put the edited versions on YouTube, building an oral history of an industry while the people who made it are still around to talk.

## The Man Who Simulated the Forest

One stand rewards a slow look. Suunnistussimulaattori is a three-dimensional cooperative sports game that drops you into Finnish terrain with a virtual orienteering map and compass and asks you to find your way — running through rendered pine forest, checking bearings, making the same route choices you would make on a real course. Finnish and foreign orienteers genuinely trained on it, treating it as an alternative to Catching Features, until development stopped in 2016. Behind glass beside the screen sit real orienteering gear and souvenirs, given to the museum by Antero Pulli, the athlete who built it. It is a small monument to the peculiar Finnish conviction that a forest is a thing you can be good at.

## Taken Seriously

Recognition came fast. In April 2017, three months after opening, the museum took the Sensation of the Year award at the Finnish Game Awards — the industry acknowledging that someone had finally started keeping its records. The following year brought something rarer: the Society for the History of Technology gave the Finnish Museum of Games its 2018 Dibner Award for Excellence in Museum Exhibits, an international prize normally aimed at institutions handling steam engines and telegraphs. For a country of five and a half million that has produced Angry Birds, Clash of Clans, Max Payne and Cities: Skylines, having a national archive of play is less a novelty than an overdue piece of bookkeeping. It just happens to be housed where the mortars used to be made.

## Flight Context

The Finnish Museum of Games sits inside the Vapriikki centre at 61.5030°N, 23.7604°E, on the northeast bank of the Tammerkoski just above the rapids in central Tampere. From the air, look for the long red-brick Tampella complex hard against the water where Näsijärvi drains south — a continuous terrace of long brick blocks and chimneys, unmistakable amid the modern city. Näsinneula tower, 1 km west-northwest, is the fastest way to orient yourself; track east-southeast to the first big brick mass on the channel. Useful viewing altitude 1,000–2,000 ft AGL. Tampere–Pirkkala (EFTP) is 7 nm southwest, elevation 390 ft, single runway 06/24. Long summer daylight gives good low-angle light on the brick; from November to March expect short days, frequent low cloud and a frozen Näsijärvi to the north.
