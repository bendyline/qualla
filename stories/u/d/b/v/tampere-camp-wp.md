# Tampere Camp

For five months in 1918, a pine heath east of Tampere held around ten thousand prisoners of Finland's civil war, and buried more than a thousand of them.

**Location:** 61.4952, 23.7980

**Categories:** Finnish Civil War prison camps, History of Pirkanmaa, Buildings and structures in Tampere, Memorials and cemeteries, 20th century history

## Introduction

Wilho Kuusijärvi was nine years old when he was shot in May 1918. He is the youngest recorded victim of the camp that stood on the Kalevankangas heath, a stretch of pine and sand two kilometres east of central Tampere. For five months that year the heath held thousands of people behind barbed wire. It is a city park now, ringed by apartment blocks, with nothing on the ground to say what happened there.

## Three Weeks and a Surrender

The camp began as a problem of arithmetic. Between 15 March and 6 April 1918, White and Red forces fought the largest and deadliest battle of the Finnish Civil War through the streets of Tampere, and artillery levelled the eastern working-class districts of Tammela and Kyttälä almost completely. When the Red Guards capitulated on 6 April, the victors found themselves holding somewhere in the region of eleven thousand prisoners — an entire industrial city's worth of mill hands, carpenters, seamstresses and teenagers who had picked up rifles.

The killing did not stop at the surrender. Up to a thousand Reds were shot in the days that followed, along with the Russian soldiers found in the city. The rest were gathered into the Central Square and held there for the better part of a day, then marched east past the last houses to a heath where the Russian Imperial Army had kept a garrison.

## Twenty-One Barracks

The camp opened on 6 April, the same day the city fell. Twenty-one military barracks and a scatter of outbuildings stood inside a perimeter of barbed wire, with machine guns covering the open ground. Some prisoners were held at first in schools and other public buildings around town; by the end of May all of them had been moved out to Kalevankangas.

The population peaked at the end of June at roughly 7,700, and around ten thousand people passed through in total. Several hundred were women. As many as eighty were children. Rations were thin from the beginning, and families were not allowed to send food parcels until August — four months into a summer in which prisoners were already visibly starving. Sanitation designed for a garrison collapsed under the weight of a small town, and disease moved through the barracks with nothing to slow it.

## Counting the Dead

No single figure is agreed on, and the disagreement says something about how these deaths were recorded. Military accounts give 1,228. The Finnish war casualties database lists 1,362. The range usually cited is 1,200 to 1,400, and by September 1918 alone, 878 had already died.

They died three ways: shot, starved, or infected. One guard, Sakeus Koivunen, a barge man from Murole, became notorious for the number of prisoners he executed. In December 1918 he was himself found dead near the spot where Kaleva Church now stands, and whether he was killed or took his own life has never been established. And there is Wilho Kuusijärvi, nine years old, shot in May. His name survives in the record because a nine-year-old fits none of the categories the war used to explain itself.

## Wound Down

On 15 September 1918 the army handed the site to the National Prison Agency and it became a forced labour institution. Most prisoners were released to await trial. The remainder were moved out over the following winter to camps elsewhere in Finland, the place closed in early 1919, and its paperwork was finally wound up that spring.

What had happened at Kalevankangas was not commemorated quickly. The monument that exists was raised by Tampere's own workers' associations rather than by the state, and it went up twenty-three years after the barbed wire came down.

## Karjalanpuisto

The heath is Karjalanpuisto today, a park in the Kaleva district — a neighbourhood of roughly ten thousand residents, students and pensioners mostly, whose landmark is the Kaleva Church, designed after a 1959 competition and built between 1964 and 1966. Nothing marks the camp itself. You can walk the whole site without learning a thing about it.

The dead are close by, in the mass grave at Kalevankangas cemetery, where at least 2,751 people from the battle and the executions that followed it are buried together. The memorial standing over them was unveiled in the spring of 1941, paid for by the city's workers' associations with municipal support, and carved by the sculptor Jussi Hietanen. Hietanen knew the ground. He had been a prisoner in the camp himself, at fifteen.

## Flight Context

61.4952°N, 23.7980°E, in the Kaleva district about 2 km east of Tampere's Central Square. From the air the site reads as a green rectangle among 1950s and 1960s apartment blocks; the nearest unmistakable marker is Kaleva Church, whose tall white concrete shell sits just to the north. The Tammerkoski rapids and the two lakes that bracket the city — Näsijärvi to the north, Pyhäjärvi to the south — give an easy orientation from 2,000–3,000 ft AGL. Tampere–Pirkkala (EFTP) lies 13 km southwest with a single 2,700 m runway; Helsinki–Vantaa (EFHK) is roughly 160 km south. Winter light here is short and low, and lake-effect cloud over Näsijärvi can shut the city in quickly.
