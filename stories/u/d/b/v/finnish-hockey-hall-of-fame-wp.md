# Finnish Hockey Hall of Fame

In a converted Tampere textile works, Finland keeps the real championship trophy and hands its honoured hockey figures a number instead of a plaque.

**Location:** 61.5031, 23.7603

**Categories:** Ice hockey museums and halls of fame, Ice hockey in Finland, Halls of fame in Finland, Sport in Tampere, Museums in Finland

## Introduction

The real Kanada-malja lives in a glass case in Tampere. Finland's ice hockey championship trophy — donated in 1950 by Finns living in Canada, which is where the name comes from — has been kept at the Finnish Ice Hockey Museum since 1991. Each spring the champions get their hands on the genuine article, and then it comes back to the case and they keep a silver copy. It sits in a former textile works alongside the Aurora Borealis Cup, a wall of Liiga awards, and the record of 286 people who have been given a number rather than a plaque.

## Lion Number 232

Since 1985 the museum has been naming Suomen Jääkiekkoleijona — Finnish Ice Hockey Lions — from among players, coaches, referees, administrators and journalists who have shaped the game in Finland. Each is assigned the next number in the sequence, and the number sticks like a jersey. Aarne Honkavaara is number 10. Jari Kurri is 113. Teemu Selänne is 232, Saku Koivu 228, Kimmo Timonen 251, Mikko Koivu 264. Through the induction class of 2025 there are 286 Lions in all, of whom thirteen are women — Kirsi Hänninen is 247, Anu Saarni 267 — a figure that moved slowly for decades and has moved fastest lately. The numbering has a side effect the founders may not have planned. It flattens the hall of fame into one continuous list, so a forward from the 1950s and a coach from the 2010s share the same sequence.

## Founded on a Summer Day in 1979

The hall of fame was established on 14 June 1979. Among its founders were Aarne Honkavaara, Kalervo Kummola, Kimmo Leinonen and Harry Lindblad — the same names that then took turns steering it. Lindblad chaired from 1979 to 1983, Honkavaara from 1983 to 1996, Unto Wiitala from 1996 to 2001, Leinonen again from 2011 to 2018. Six years passed between founding the institution and inducting anybody into it, which suggests the founders spent a while working out precisely what they were honouring. The mission they settled on is unglamorous and useful: to record, document and exhibit the objects, photographs and printed material of Finnish ice hockey, before the people who remember any of it stop being available to ask.

## A Weaving Mill Full of Museums

The hall of fame occupies part of the Vapriikki Museum Centre, housed in the old Tampella works on the eastern bank of the Tammerkoski rapids; the name comes from the Swedish word for factory. Vapriikki opened to the public in 1996 and was completed in 2000, covering 14,000 square metres with roughly half of that given to exhibitions. Seven museums share the roof. The Finnish Ice Hockey Museum sits alongside the Finnish Museum of Games, a postal museum, a media museum, a natural history museum, a doll and dress museum, and the regional museum of Pirkanmaa. In 2017, the year Finland marked a century of independence, Vapriikki was chosen as Museum of the Century. More than 170,000 people came through the doors in 2019.

## Why This City

A national hockey hall of fame could reasonably have gone to Helsinki. It went to Tampere, and the reason stands a couple of kilometres east. Hakametsä opened on 29 January 1965 as the first indoor ice arena in Finland, and for fifty-six years it housed both of the city's top-flight clubs, Ilves and Tappara — a rivalry conducted, improbably, inside a shared front door. The building holds 7,300. Both clubs moved out to the Nokia Arena for the 2021–22 season, and Hakametsä is being redeveloped as a sports campus with apartments and offices around it, its ice deliberately kept for the people who skate rather than the people who are watched. The museum a short ride away is, in effect, the memory that arena accumulated.

## What the Cases Hold

Past the trophies, the collection is mostly cloth and worn equipment: sweaters, sticks, pads and gloves from seasons gone by, photographs, printed ephemera, the paper trail of a sport that arrived in Finland from elsewhere and became one of the things the country is known for. The captions carry the numbers. Erik Hämäläinen, Lion 193, appears playing his 1,001st and final league regular-season game for Lukko in March 2008. Jukka Jalonen, 210, is photographed at the gold-medal celebrations in Helsinki after the 2011 world championship. Pentti Lund, 84, wears a New York Rangers sweater in 1951. Raimo Helminen, 192, is in Ilves colours. It is a small museum inside a large building, and its subject is a country's memory of its own winters.

## Flight Context

The Finnish Hockey Hall of Fame is at 61.5031°N, 23.7603°E, inside the Vapriikki Museum Centre in the former Tampella works on the eastern bank of the Tammerkoski rapids, just north of central Tampere. From the air, look for the long red-brick industrial range and tall chimney on the east bank, mirrored by the Finlayson complex on the west bank, with the rapids running between them from Näsijärvi down to Pyhäjärvi. The Nokia Arena, home of Ilves and Tappara since 2021, sits about a kilometre south over the railway yard and makes a second useful marker. A viewing altitude of 1,500–3,000 feet AGL takes in both riverbanks. Tampere–Pirkkala Airport (EFTP) lies roughly 13 km southwest; Helsinki–Vantaa (EFHK) about 145 km southeast. Expect low ceilings and brief daylight from November to February.
