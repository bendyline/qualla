# Pyynikki Esker

A river that ran inside a glacier left its bed standing up instead of cut down - and the gravel ridge above Tampere, which the city calls the highest longitudinal esker in the world, has been a lookout, a burial ground and a painter's subject ever since.

**Location:** 61.4962, 23.7266

**Categories:** Eskers, Landforms of Finland, Pyynikki, Scenic viewpoints, Tourist attractions in Finland, Glacial geology

## Introduction

The ridge was built by a river with no banks. As the last ice sheet withdrew across Finland, meltwater ran in tunnels through and beneath the ice, carrying gravel and sand along and dropping it in long sinuous heaps on the bedrock below. When the ice finally went, the riverbed was left standing up rather than cut down: a raised causeway of sorted gravel, curving across the land like a road built by nobody. Geologists call the result an esker. Tampere grew on either side of one, and the city calls Pyynikki the highest longitudinal esker in the world - 160 metres above sea level, 80 metres above the surface of Lake Pyhäjärvi at its foot.

## Sand Remembers Being a River

Gravel and sand, mostly, sorted and rounded by moving water, which is why the ridge behaves differently from the granite the rest of Finland is built on. The consequence shows on the slopes. Old European red pines stand here with parts of their root systems hanging in open air, because the ground beneath them keeps easing downhill toward Pyhäjärvi, decade by decade, uncovering what had been buried. Junipers grow here under legal protection, though you may still pick the berries provided you do not bend a branch or pull up a plant - a very Finnish compromise between conservation and the right to roam. Norway maples have taken hold on the slopes. In late summer the southern face gives up chanterelles and lingonberries to anyone with a basket.

## A Ridge Across a Country

Pyynikki is not a hill; it is a fragment of something much longer. The formation it belongs to runs from South Ostrobothnia in the west all the way to the Salpausselkä ridges in the southeast, the great terminal moraines that mark where the retreating ice front stalled and stood for centuries. Immediately west of Pyynikki the ridge carries on as the Pispala esker and Tahmela, and the steep painted wooden houses of Pispala are stacked up its flanks. To the east it becomes Kalevankangas. Beyond the city, in Kangasala, it surfaces again as four more named ridges - Kirkkoharju, Kuohunharju, Keisarinharju and Vehoniemenharju - a chain of viewpoints strung across the lakeland. All of it is the same buried river, still lying more or less where it fell.

## The Ground People Chose for Burying

Follow the ridge east out of Pyynikki and it becomes Kalevankangas, where Tampere has buried its dead for close to a century and a half. That is not a coincidence of real estate. Sand and gravel drain, and they dig - which matters a great deal in a country whose ground freezes solid for months, and Finnish towns learned early to put their cemeteries on eskers. The same qualities that make an esker easy to walk on and easy to plant pines in make it easy to open. So the ridge that carries the city's finest view at one end holds most of its dead at the other, including the thousands who died in the prison camp on that heath after the civil war of 1918. One landform, doing both jobs.

## The Highest Thing for Miles

Height on a landscape this flat is a military asset, and the ridge has been used as one. At half past eight on the morning of 6 April 1918, a white flag went up on the wooden observation tower that then stood on Pyynikki. It ended the Battle of Tampere, three weeks of house-to-house fighting through the mill district in which Finns killed roughly nineteen hundred of each other. The flag was raised there for the same reason the city built a lookout on the spot in the first place: from the crest of the esker, everyone below could see it, on both sides of the line. Walkers pass that ground constantly without knowing what was signalled from it.

## Helmi Biese's Winter

In 1900 the painter Helmi Biese carried her materials up here and produced Talvinen näköala Pyynikiltä - Winter View from Pyynikki - which became her best-known picture and now hangs in the Ateneum collection in Helsinki. Biese, born in 1867, had trained at the Finnish Art Association's drawing school and in Gunnar Berndtson's studio alongside the symbolists Magnus Enckell and Beda Stjernschantz, and she made winter landscapes and sea views her subject in a period when Finnish painters were busy inventing what their country looked like. She chose this ridge for it. More than a century later the view is still there, and so is the slow slide of sand that keeps lifting pine roots into the air. The river finished running ten thousand years ago. Its bed has not entirely stopped moving.

## Flight Context

The Pyynikki Esker runs roughly east-west at 61.4962°N, 23.7266°E, immediately west of central Tampere, Finland, with a crest 160 metres above sea level - about 80 metres above Lake Pyhäjärvi on its southern flank. Plan sightseeing runs at 1,500-3,000 feet AGL. From the air the ridge is the obvious dark wooded spine separating Lake Pyhäjärvi to the south from the built-up city and Lake Näsijärvi to the north; a squat red granite observation tower marks the high point, and the Tammerkoski rapids with their red-brick mill corridor lie about two kilometres east. Westward the formation continues as Pispalanharju, recognisable by the steep terraces of painted wooden houses; eastward it runs on through Kalevankangas and toward the Kangasala ridges, a well-known scenic line of viewpoints over the lakes. Tampere-Pirkkala (EFTP) is about 11 km southwest; Helsinki-Vantaa (EFHK) roughly 150 km south-southeast; Jyväskylä (EFJY) about 140 km northeast. Best light is low summer evening sun along the ridge axis; winter gives frozen lakes on both sides and excellent contrast, but very short days.
