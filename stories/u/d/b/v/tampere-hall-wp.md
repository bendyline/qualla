# Tampere Hall

The architects who won Tampere's 1990 concert hall called their entry "the factory whistle" - and the Nordic countries' largest concert and congress centre has been summoning the city ever since, for opera, for presidents, and for several thousand people in home-made armour.

**Location:** 61.4958, 23.7819

**Categories:** Buildings and structures in Tampere, Concert halls in Finland, Tourist attractions in Tampere, 1990 establishments in Finland, Event venues established in 1990

## Introduction

The winning entry in the 1982 architecture competition carried a blunt Finnish name: Tehtaan pilli, the factory whistle. It was an odd thing to call a concert hall, and exactly the right thing. Tampere had spent a century organised around the sirens of the cotton and paper mills on the Tammerkoski rapids, sounds that told thousands of people when to start work and when to stop. Sakari Aartelo and Esa Piironen proposed that the city's next great summoning building should call people to music instead. Eight years later Tampere Hall opened on the southern edge of Sorsapuisto, a park with a duck pond in the middle of it, half a kilometre from the railway station.

## Four Floors, Three Halls

The building covers 31,718 square metres across four storeys - a low, broad postmodern mass that reads more like an airport terminal than a temple, which was rather the point. Inside are three main auditoria: the largest seats 1,756, a small hall takes 500, and the Sorsapuisto hall 650. Across the street stands the main building of Tampere University, known until the 2019 merger that created it as the University of Tampere. The city of Tampere owns the whole thing, which is the ordinary Nordic arrangement. A municipality of roughly a quarter of a million people decided it wanted the largest concert and congress centre in the Nordic countries, and then went and built one.

## Juice Went First

The first concert in the new hall was not a symphony. On 19 February 1990, months before any official ceremony, the stage went to Juice Leskinen, the songwriter whose sardonic Finnish rock had been the sound of Tampere's bars and record shops for two decades. The formal inauguration followed on 29 September that year, with President Mauno Koivisto in the audience and the full apparatus of a state occasion in the foyer. The order of those two events tells you something about the building's intentions. It was designed to hold both - the president and the rock singer, the international medical congress and the school choir - and it has never much bothered to choose between them.

## The Opera, the Orchestra, and the Cosplayers

Tampere Opera and the Tampere Philharmonic Orchestra both work out of the hall, giving it a resident classical life that most convention centres lack. The congress side has done well too: by 2015 the venue had been voted Finland's best congress venue seven times. But the single event that fills the building fullest is neither opera nor conference. It is Tracon, the annual role-playing and anime cosplay convention, which each summer turns the marble foyers into a press of home-made armour, dyed wigs and carefully painted foam swords. The hall also hosted Tampere's first Finncon, the Finnish science-fiction gathering. There is a particular pleasure in a postmodern congress centre discovering that its largest audience arrives in costume.

## The Moomins Move In

Between 2015 and 2017 the hall was expanded and rebuilt at a cost of more than twenty million euros. Three new event spaces appeared - Maestro, Duetto and Riffi - and so did something a congress centre had no obvious reason to want: permanent, purpose-built galleries for the Moomin Museum, which opened there in the summer of 2017. It was a deliberate bet. Tampere already held the world's largest collection of Tove Jansson's original Moomin artwork and had been shuffling it between temporary addresses for years; giving it a home inside the city's flagship venue put children's book illustration on the same floor plan as the medical congresses and the philharmonic. The queue for the museum and the queue for the evening concert now share a lobby, which is not how most concert halls imagine their audience.

## The Country Watching

In December 2013 the president's Independence Day reception - Finland's grandest annual ritual, normally held at the Presidential Palace in Helsinki - was staged in Tampere Hall instead. Sauli Niinistö shook hands in Sorsapuisto, and more than two million people watched it on television. In a country of five and a half million, that is most of the adults who were not actually at the party. For one December evening the hall was the entire nation's front room. Then the trucks backed up to the loading dock, the carpets came up, and the next congress loaded in - which is, in the end, what a factory whistle is for.

## Flight Context

Tampere Hall stands at 61.4958°N, 23.7819°E, on the southern edge of Sorsapuisto in central Tampere, Finland. From 2,000-4,000 feet the city reads unmistakably: Tampere occupies the narrow isthmus between Lake Näsijärvi to the north and Lake Pyhäjärvi to the south, joined by the Tammerkoski rapids and their line of red-brick mill buildings. Tampere Hall sits east of that spine, a broad low roof beside a green park with a small pond, with the railway station and its fan of tracks half a kilometre away. Tampere-Pirkkala (EFTP) lies about 13 km southwest of the city centre and is the primary approach; Helsinki-Vantaa (EFHK) is roughly 150 km south-southeast. Summer brings long light and frequent haze off the lakes; winter days are short, and low overcast is common from November through February.
