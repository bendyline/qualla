# Kalevankangas Cemetery

Tampere's main burial ground holds sixty-six names carved in stone and at least 2,751 people with none - the arithmetic of a civil war, kept in one place.

**Location:** 61.4933, 23.7950

**Categories:** Cemeteries in Finland, Lutheran cemeteries in Finland, Buildings and structures in Tampere, Finnish Civil War, Cemeteries established in the 1880s

## Introduction

Two monuments stand in this cemetery, a few hundred metres apart, and the difference between them is the whole history of 1918. One remembers sixty-six people who died fighting for the White side in the Finnish Civil War; their names are cut into stone by the chapel, and the first of them were buried here on 6 April, the day Tampere fell. The other stands over mass graves holding at least 2,751 people from the losing side - killed in the fighting for the city, executed after it, or dead of hunger and disease in the prison camps that followed. Most of them were Tampere's own: mill hands, machinists, women from the weaving sheds. The stone above them went up in 1941, and almost none of them have a name on it.

## Seventeen Hectares of Pine and Granite

Kalevankangas was consecrated on 25 July 1880, on a sandy ridge east of the growing factory town. It covers about seventeen hectares and holds more than fifteen hundred trees of thirty-two species, so that the whole place reads as woodland with graves in it rather than lawn with trees. The chapel was dedicated in 1914, designed by W. G. Palmqvist and Einar Sjöström. The main gate came in 1937, drawn by the city architect Bertel Strömmer, whose buildings gave central Tampere much of its 1930s face, with relief sculpture by Gunnar Finne. Strömmer was buried here himself in 1962, a few minutes' walk from his own gate.

## The Fitter Who Rewrote the Country

Väinö Linna worked at the Finlayson textile mills as a fitter, from 1938 to 1940 and again from 1944 to 1954, reading Dostoyevsky on his own time. Then he wrote The Unknown Soldier, a novel about ordinary conscripts that Finland has never stopped arguing about, and the Under the North Star trilogy, which followed a tenant-farming family through the decades that produced the graves at the other end of this cemetery. He is buried here. So is Lauri Viita, the poet and carpenter's son from Pirkkala, and Mirkka Rekola, one of the sharpest Finnish poets of her generation. So is Juice Leskinen, whose songs have been sung by every Finnish generation since the 1970s, and Markku Peltola, the long-faced actor of The Man Without a Past, and the singer Kikka Sirén, whose grave still collects visitors.

## Throwers and Jumpers

Tampere produced field athletes the way it produced textiles, and a good number of them ended up on this ridge. Verner Järvinen, an Olympic medallist in the discus in the first decade of the century, lies here; so does his son Matti, who won javelin gold at the 1932 Los Angeles Games with 72.71 metres and broke the world record ten times in a row between 1930 and 1936, finishing at 77.23. The tower of the Helsinki Olympic Stadium was built to a height of 72.71 metres in honour of that throw - a monument to a man whose actual grave is a modest stone in a Tampere pine wood. Julius Saaristo, born in the city in 1891, took gold in the two-handed javelin and silver in the conventional event at Stockholm in 1912. Vilho Tuulos, another Tampere native, won the triple jump at Antwerp in 1920 and took bronze in the same event in 1924 and again in 1928.

## The Cinema on Hämeenkatu

On the evening of 23 October 1927, about 225 people - most of them factory workers - were watching a film in the Imatra cinema, a converted warehouse at Hämeenkatu 12. Nitrate film caught fire in the projection room at around twenty to eight. The projectionist tried to smother the burning reel and could not. The wooden interior went up fast; people on the balcony jumped down onto those below, and twenty-eight were carried to hospital injured. Twenty died that night and one more later, all of them from the balcony. In 1928 a memorial designed by the architect Veikko Kallio was raised over the common grave where eighteen of the twenty-one lie together.

## Soldiers, Aces and the Tatar Corner

The hero graves section holds 756 Finns killed in the Winter War, the Continuation War and the Lapland War, with a further memorial to 149 more who lie beyond borders that moved after 1944 and could not be brought home. Among the veterans buried here is Hans Wind, the Brewster and Messerschmitt pilot credited with seventy-five aerial victories and one of only four men to receive the Mannerheim Cross twice; he died in Tampere in 1995, having spent his post-war life in business. In another corner is the burial ground of Tampere's Tatar community - Mishar Tatars who came from the Nizhny Novgorod region in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, Finland's oldest Muslim population, still only six or seven hundred people nationwide. Their stones sit under the same pines as everyone else's, which is, in the end, what a municipal cemetery is for.

## Flight Context

Kalevankangas Cemetery lies at 61.4933°N, 23.7950°E on a sandy ridge in the Kalevanharju district, roughly 2 km east of central Tampere and about 2 km west of the Hakametsä ice halls. From the air it appears as a dense rectangle of mature conifers set against surrounding apartment blocks - a distinct dark green block with the pale chapel roof near its western edge. Tampere-Pirkkala Airport (EFTP) is approximately 15 km southwest; Helsinki-Vantaa (EFHK) about 160 km south. The 168-metre Näsinneula tower stands 3 km northwest as the region's primary visual reference. Best appreciated from 1,000-2,000 ft AGL, where the grave rows are still resolvable. Late autumn and winter bring low light and frequent overcast; the ridge is at its most legible after leaf-fall.
