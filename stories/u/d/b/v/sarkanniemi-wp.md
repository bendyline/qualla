# Särkänniemi

A concrete needle went up on a sandy cape in 1971, and Finland's second-biggest amusement park grew underneath it.

**Location:** 61.5053, 23.7439

**Categories:** Amusement parks in Finland, Buildings and structures in Tampere, Aquaria in Finland, Tourist attractions in Tampere, Observation towers, Amusement parks opened in 1975

## Introduction

The needle came first. On 28 April 1971, four years before there was an amusement park to look down on, a 168-metre concrete spike opened on a sandy cape at the edge of Lake Näsijärvi. Pekka Ilveskoski had designed it after seeing Seattle's Space Needle, and he borrowed the revolving restaurant from the Puijo Tower in Kuopio. Näsinneula is still the tallest free-standing structure in Finland and still the tallest observation tower in the Nordic countries. The rides, the aquarium, the roller coasters and the six hundred thousand people who come every year all arrived later, arranging themselves around a landmark that had been built for the view alone.

## A Slow Revolution

The restaurant sits 124 metres above the water and turns a full circle every forty-five minutes - slowly enough that you notice the change only when you look up from your plate and find the lake has moved. Näsijärvi fills the north. To the south the red-brick chimneys of Tampere's old cotton and linen mills stand in the narrow isthmus between two lakes, the reason the city exists at all. The Finnish word särkkä means a sandbank or a ridge, and niemi is a cape, so the name is simply a description: the sandbank cape. It described a quiet piece of shoreline for centuries before anyone put a needle on it.

## Assembled Piece by Piece

Särkänniemi was never built all at once. An aquarium and a planetarium opened on the cape in 1969. A small private amusement park, Neulan Huvipuisto, began running rides in 1973. The city of Tampere took the operation over and opened Särkänniemi proper on 1 May 1975, and it has owned the place ever since - a municipal amusement park, which is a more common arrangement in Finland than elsewhere. In 1979 the Sara Hildén Art Museum opened on the same headland, so that a modern art collection now shares a car park with a log flume. Doghill Fairytale Farm handles the smallest visitors. Only Linnanmäki in Helsinki draws more people, and the two parks have spent fifty years measuring themselves against each other.

## Machines That Come and Go

Rides here have a lifespan, and the roster reads like a record of changing tastes. Vauhtimato, the Speedy Worm, has been carrying nervous six-year-olds since 1984. Tukkijoki, the log flume, opened in 1982 with a four-hundred-metre course and a thirteen-metre plunge that soaks the front row. Koskiseikkailu followed in 1999, sending six-passenger boats through a tunnel and under a waterfall. The inverted coaster Tornado arrived in 2001, the motorcycle-launch MotoGee in 2010, and Hype - a launched steel coaster - in 2017. Others have gone: Half Pipe closed in 2019, and Trombi was dismantled in 2025 to clear space for a new coaster. Konect, that replacement, opens for summer 2026. The park keeps rebuilding itself under the same unchanging tower.

## What the Park Gave Up

For decades Särkänniemi kept dolphins, and for decades that was ordinary. It stopped being ordinary. In 2016 the park closed its dolphinarium and sent the animals to Attica Zoological Park in Greece - a decision made under real public pressure, and one that removed a headline attraction without replacing it. The aquarium remains, holding 550,000 litres and more than three thousand animals from around two hundred species. Its lower floor is a mangrove swamp, where archer fish spit at insects above the waterline and blowfish drift among the roots. Angry Birds Land opened here on 28 April 2012, the first anywhere in the world, built around a Finnish game that briefly conquered every phone on Earth. The license ended and the area closed at the end of 2022, which tells you something about how fast a phenomenon ages.

## The View Both Ways

There are ambitious plans for the cape - a hotel, a spa, restaurants, water attractions, apartments - and Tampere has grown toward them. The city's light rail opened in 2021 and reached the western districts in January 2025, running along the shore below the tower. Ride the lift up on a clear evening in June, when Finnish daylight refuses to end, and you can see the whole logic of the place at once: the mill city on its rapids, the two lakes at different heights, the ridge of Pyynikki, and directly beneath your feet a wooden coaster's worth of screaming. Näsinneula was built so people could look at Tampere. Tampere has been looking back at it ever since.

## Flight Context

Särkänniemi occupies a cape at 61.5053°N, 23.7439°E on the southern shore of Lake Näsijärvi, immediately northwest of central Tampere. Näsinneula tower rises 168 metres above ground and is the most conspicuous landmark in the region - visible for tens of kilometres across the lake in clear air and a reliable visual fix on approach to the city. Tampere-Pirkkala Airport (EFTP) lies roughly 15 km southwest; Helsinki-Vantaa (EFHK) is about 160 km south. A viewing altitude of 2,000-3,000 ft AGL frames the cape, the tower and the narrow isthmus of mill buildings between Näsijärvi and Pyhäjärvi in a single pass. Summer visibility is generally excellent; winter brings low cloud, short daylight and frequent icing conditions.
