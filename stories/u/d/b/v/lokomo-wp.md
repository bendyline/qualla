# Lokomo

A Tampere works that built 671 locomotives, road graders called steel bears, and the two Soviet submersibles that later lit up the wreck of the Titanic.

**Location:** 61.4845, 23.7644

**Categories:** Industrial heritage, Railways, Engineering, Finland, Tampere, Cold War

## Introduction

The submersibles that lit the wreck of the Titanic for James Cameron's cameras were not built in Leningrad or Vladivostok. MIR-1 and MIR-2 were assembled in Tampere, on the Hatanpää shore, in a works that had spent its first four decades turning out steam locomotives for the Finnish railways. They were delivered to the Soviet Academy of Sciences in 1987, rated to 6,000 metres, and Washington was not pleased about it. The Pentagon worried that the Soviet Union might use the technology to sweep American listening equipment off the ocean floor, and Rauma-Repola, Lokomo's parent company, was privately threatened with economic sanctions. A Finnish locomotive shop had walked into the middle of the Cold War.

## A Deliberate Rival

Lokomo was founded in 1915 to take work away from the company across town. Werner Ryselin had run the locomotive division at Tampella, Tampere's established engineering house; he left, and with the engineer Jalmar Castrén, Professor J. J. Karvonen and a group of local businessmen he set up a competitor a short distance away. Among the backers was Emil Aaltonen, born Emil Widell in 1869, who had begun as a shoemaker's apprentice and built the largest shoe factory in the Nordic countries — more than 1,300 workers by the 1930s, offering some 4,500 models. Aaltonen took a majority holding in Lokomo in 1926. Castrén went in a different direction: professor of structural statics at the Helsinki University of Technology, three times transport minister, and from December 1922 the head of Finland's State Railways for the next two decades. He helped found a locomotive factory, then spent twenty years running the railway it sold to.

## Six Hundred and Seventy-One

The first engine took five years to arrive. Hv1 number 575 was completed in 1920 — a 4-6-0 passenger locomotive on Finland's broad 1,524 millimetre gauge, one of forty-two of its class built between 1915 and 1921 by Tampella and Lokomo together. They were rated at 80 kilometres per hour until 1934, when the limit was raised to 95, and the last of them was not withdrawn until 1967. Nearly half a century of service from a design drawn before Finland was even an independent country. Lokomo built 671 locomotives in all, steam until 1957 and diesel until 1969, and 575 never left home: it stands plinthed on the ground of the works in Hatanpää, the first thing the company ever made and the last thing left of that trade.

## Church Bells, Anchors, Steel Bears

Locomotive orders alone were never enough to keep the shops busy, so Lokomo made whatever heavy iron Finland happened to need. Rock crushers from 1921, and alongside them church bells, ships' anchors, concrete mixers, road rollers. After the Second World War came the war reparations owed to the Soviet Union, narrow-gauge locomotives among them, hammered out to a schedule set by the peace terms. From the 1950s the catalogue widened again: roughly 900 excavators, truck-mounted cranes, forestry machines, and road graders sold under the brand Teräskarhu — steel bear. It was an odd, sprawling catalogue for a company with a locomotive in its name, and it carried the works for thirty years. Japanese competition closed those lines in 1985.

## Six Thousand Metres

The submersible contract came in the mid-1980s and was unlike anything the works had attempted. Each MIR carries its crew inside a sphere of maraging steel, an alloy roughly thirty per cent cobalt with nickel, chrome and titanium in smaller measure, giving about ten per cent better strength-to-weight performance than titanium alone. That sphere is what makes 6,000 metres survivable. The MIRs filmed the Titanic at 3,821 metres for Cameron's 1997 picture. In 2007 one of them made the first manned descent to the seabed directly beneath the geographic North Pole, 4,261 metres down under the ice. Later came Lake Baikal, and in 2011 Lake Geneva. Machines built beside a Finnish lake spent their careers at the bottom of other people's water.

## What Still Stands in Hatanpää

Rauma-Repola bought Lokomo in 1970. The business passed to Rauma Oy and then, after merger, into Metso — the name most people in Tampere now use for the site. Around 800 people worked there in 2005. Metso still builds crushers on the Hatanpää ground, sold worldwide under the Lokotrack name, which keeps four letters of the old company alive in every machine. The foundry went to Tevo Oy in 2015 and trades as Tevo Lokomo. The locomotive stays where it is, black and enormous and entirely stationary, in front of a factory that has outlived every product it was founded to make.

## Flight Context

61.4845°N, 23.7644°E, on the Hatanpää shore of Pyhäjärvi about two kilometres south of Tampere city centre. Best seen from 1,500-2,500 ft AGL on a northbound approach: the works sit between the lakeshore and the main southern rail approach into Tampere, with the Tammerkoski rapids and the 168-metre Näsinneula tower marking the city centre beyond. Tampere-Pirkkala (EFTP) lies roughly 10 km to the southwest; Helsinki-Vantaa (EFHK) is about 160 km southeast. Summer visibility over the lake basin is excellent; December through February brings low light, frequent low stratus and ice haze off the frozen water.
