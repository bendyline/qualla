# Junkarsborg

A medieval fort dated less by documents than by the silver in its soil, standing on half a hectare of wooded island in the Mustionjoki river.

**Location:** 60.1533, 23.7860

**Categories:** Castles in Finland, Ruins in Finland, Archaeological sites in Finland, Raseborg, Buildings and structures in Uusimaa, 14th-century establishments in Finland, History of Uusimaa

## Introduction

In 1994 a vegetation survey of a small wooded island in the Mustionjoki river turned up hops and meadow oat grass. Neither of those plants finds its way into a southern Finnish forest unaided. They are settlement indicators — the botanical residue of people who brewed and kept animals — and the people who left them there had been gone for roughly five centuries. Above the hops sit the low rectangular earth ramparts of Junkarsborg, a medieval castle dated less by any document than by the silver coins archaeologists have pulled out of its ground.

## Twenty Metres Square

It was never large. The main fortress measured something like twenty metres by twenty, a compact keep planted on a promontory that juts into the river where it meets the water of Päsarträsket. On the landward side a second, outer fortification covered the approach, and across the neck of the promontory the builders cut a ditch about sixty metres long. That ditch is the reason Junkarsborg is described today as sitting on an island: the moat severed the headland from the shore, and the half-hectare scrap of ground left behind — roughly a hundred metres on its southwest-to-northeast axis — has been an island ever since. It can still only be reached on foot.

## A Toll Gate of Timber and Stone

Position explains the whole building. The Mustionjoki, called Svartå in Swedish, was a road, and a fort planted in the middle of it controlled both the water traffic moving inland and the land routes crossing at that point, roughly sixty kilometres west of where Helsinki would eventually be founded. Archaeological work suggests the site was fortified in stages rather than raised in one campaign: an early phase in the twelfth or thirteenth century, probably timber, followed by a rebuilding in the 1300s that combined wood with stone. Whoever held it collected from whoever passed. That is a common enough medieval arrangement, but it is unusually legible here, because the river still runs where it ran and the choke point is still obvious.

## What Three Digs Turned Up

Junkarsborg has been excavated three times — in 1891 and 1892, again in 1937, and across four seasons from 1950 to 1954. The finds are the ordinary furniture of a working fort: bronze and iron objects, ceramics, tiles, animal bone. The dating comes from the silver. Coins recovered from the site span roughly 1320 to 1420 and cluster heavily in the late fourteenth century, which brackets the castle's active life more firmly than any surviving text does. Some of the pottery is more revealing than the metal. It is German stoneware of Rhineland type, resembling the wares fired at Siegburg, meaning that a small river fort in medieval Nyland was drinking from cups shipped the length of the Baltic.

## Superseded by Stone

A few kilometres away, in the 1370s, a far more serious castle went up. The first building phase at Raseborg was finished somewhere between 1373 and 1378, and the earliest written mention of it comes from 1378. Archaeologists generally read Junkarsborg as its predecessor — the older, smaller position whose functions Raseborg simply absorbed. Junkarsborg fell out of use in the fifteenth century and was never revived. Raseborg lasted longer but lost in the end to the same indifferent force that shapes this whole coast: postglacial rebound lifted the land, the sea retreated, boats could no longer reach the castle rock, and in 1553 it too was abandoned, three years after Helsinki was founded and became the more useful place to be.

## Earth and Trees

What survives is not romantic. There are no walls to walk beneath and no towers to climb — only rectangular banks of earth under forest cover, the outline of something that was once worth defending, and the ditch that made the island. Work carried out in 2005 and 2006 improved access and cleaned up the site's appearance, so the crossing and the ramparts are easier to read than they were. It rewards patience rather than spectacle. Stand on the bank, look down at the river running past on both sides, and the logic of the place assembles itself: a narrow water, a narrow neck, and a very small square of ground that once decided who got through.

## Flight Context

60.1533 N, 23.7860 E, on an island in the Mustionjoki (Svartå) river near Karis in the city of Raseborg, about 75 km west of Helsinki. From 1,500-2,500 ft look for the river's dark thread through farmland and forest and the small wooded island where it broadens toward Päsarträsket; the earthworks themselves are under tree canopy and effectively invisible from the air except in late autumn and early spring when the leaves are off. Nummela Airfield (EFNU) lies about 45 km northeast, Hanko (EFHN) roughly 40 km southwest, Helsinki-Vantaa (EFHK) about 75 km east-northeast.
