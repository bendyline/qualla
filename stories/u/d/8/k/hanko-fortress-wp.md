# Hanko Fortress

A red granite fortress that was never finished, changed owners without a shot, and was finally blown up by the garrison that held it.

**Location:** 59.8008, 22.9436

**Categories:** Military history of Sweden, Forts in Finland, Hanko, Finland, Historical sites, Lighthouses, Islands

## Introduction

Hanko's fortress was never finished, and the people who eventually destroyed it were the ones who owned it. Three granite islets southwest of the town — Gustavsvärn, Gustaf Adolf and Meijerfeldt — were fortified from the 1790s onward into a triangle that closed the shipping channel into Hanko harbour, backed by works on the mainland heights at Kuningattarenvuori. Sixty-odd years later, in the late summer of 1854, the Russian garrison packed the island batteries with powder and blew them apart rather than let a British fleet take them intact. What is left is a ruin of dark red granite quarried out of the island it stands on, three kilometres off the tip of Finland's southernmost peninsula, and it is a handsome enough wreck that Albert Edelfelt came out to paint it.

## Gustav's Line in the Water

The peninsula had been fortified in a temporary way for a long time before anyone tried to build in stone. A field redoubt called Brunerskärin Skanssi went up on Tulliniemi at the end of the 17th century — earth, timber and a few cannon, thrown together for a season's campaigning and left to rot.

What changed the calculation was Gustav III's war against Russia in 1788–1790. Sweden came out of it with the uncomfortable knowledge that its eastern approaches were open, and the decision was taken to replace the improvised works around Hanko harbour with permanent fortifications. Three islands were fortified and renamed for the occasion. The design fell to the commander of the fortress brigade, von Kierting, assisted by Captain Carl Nycander, and work on the islands was under way by the spring of 1793. Gustavsvärn was laid out as a tiered bastion fort, its walls cut from the island's own red granite so that fortress and bedrock became difficult to tell apart. Construction dragged into the early 19th century and then simply stopped. The artillery tower at the heart of the design was never built.

## Handed Over Without a Shot

The Finnish War settled the question of ownership in 1808–1809 and Hanko Fortress played almost no part in it. The garrison surrendered to the Russians without a fight, and Finland passed from Sweden to Russia as an autonomous Grand Duchy. Russian engineers briefly resumed the building work, then thought better of it: by 1812 the fortress had been judged unnecessary and abandoned.

For four decades the islands sat empty. Ice ground at the piers each spring, gulls nested in the casemates, and the unfinished walls weathered from raw quarry-red to something softer. Hanko itself was still only a fishing and pilot station — the railway, the winter harbour and the town charter were all half a century away. Nobody had much reason to think about the fortifications until a war a thousand miles to the south changed the arithmetic of the Baltic entirely.

## The Powder in Its Own Walls

When the Crimean War brought an Anglo-French fleet into the Baltic in 1854, Gustavsvärn was hurriedly brought back into service. At the start of the campaign the fort mounted 61 cannon, and it turned away a British approach — a rare success in a season of Russian reverses. But the fortress was expensive to hold, incomplete by design, and terribly exposed. In August 1854 an allied force reduced Bomarsund in Åland and took its garrison prisoner, and that example decided the matter. Rather than hand the Royal Navy another prize, the Russian high command ordered such isolated coastal works destroyed. In the late summer of 1854 the batteries on Gustavsvärn, Gustaf Adolf and Meijerfeldt were blown up by the men who had been defending them.

The war did not leave Hanko alone afterwards. On 5 June 1855 a cutter from HMS Cossack came in under a flag of truce to put seven captured Finnish sailors ashore. Russian troops on the beach fired on the landing party. Four men in the boat were killed, one was left badly wounded, and the survivors were taken prisoner — an episode that caused real anger in the British press and did nothing for the reputation of either side.

## Ruins, Beacons and Summer Boats

The ruins found a second career almost immediately. In 1865 a navigation light was raised inside the shattered walls, tended by two keepers, and a foghorn station and keeper's dwelling followed in 1900. Those buildings were themselves destroyed after the Second World War. In 1951 a squat reinforced-concrete beacon roughly eleven metres tall went up in their place — one of the smallest lighthouses in Finland, and still working.

Today Gustavsvärn is protected as an ancient monument under Finland's Antiquities Act, and it is the best-preserved of the four elements of the old fortress. Hanko runs summer boat transport out to it; private craft can moor at the pier. The same cruises usually take in Hauensuoli, the sheltered rock channel a few kilometres away that sailors called Pike's Gut, where more than 640 carvings survive on the shoreline cliffs. Crews waiting out contrary winds cut their names, their family arms and their crowns into the granite there, the oldest of them in the 15th century. Between the two sites you get the whole logic of the place in an afternoon: this was where ships had to slow down, so this was where everything happened.

## Flight Context

Gustavsvärn sits at 59.801°N, 22.944°E, about 3 km southwest of Hanko town and a short hop from the tip of the Hankoniemi peninsula. From the air the fortress islets read as three low granite knuckles in open water, with the small white 1951 beacon marking the largest of them and the channel into Hanko's harbour threading between. The whole triangle is best seen from 1,500–3,000 ft AGL, low enough to pick out the bastion outlines in the rock. Nearest airports: Hanko (EFHN) about 8 km northeast, Turku (EFTU) about 100 km north-northwest, Helsinki-Vantaa (EFHK) about 125 km northeast. Note that active Finnish Defence Forces areas and firing ranges lie immediately south around Russarö — check NOTAMs before routing over the outer islands. Late summer offers the clearest water and best contrast; autumn and winter bring sea fog and low ceilings off the Gulf of Finland.
