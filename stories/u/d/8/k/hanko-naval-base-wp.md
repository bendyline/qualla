# Hanko Naval Base

Finland went to war rather than lease Hanko to the Soviet Union, lost, and leased it anyway — for twenty months.

**Location:** 59.8330, 23.0830

**Categories:** Former naval bases in Finland, Installations of the Soviet Navy, Winter War, Continuation War, Finland–Soviet Union relations, Military history, Hanko, Finland

## Introduction

In October 1939 a young New Zealand journalist named Geoffrey Cox was told by the Finnish General Staff exactly which piece of their country was not for sale. Petsamo on the Arctic coast, yes, if it truly came to that. But not Hanko. In Russian hands, they said, the peninsula would be a pistol pressed into Finland's back, and it was the real key to the Gulf of Finland. What Moscow offered in exchange lay north of the Finnish waistline: stretches of forest of no military value whatsoever. Finland refused, the Winter War followed, and five months later — having lost it — Finland handed Hanko over anyway. The Soviet naval base that resulted lasted less than two years.

## Why This Particular Peninsula

Hankoniemi is a long sandy finger reaching southwest into the Baltic, ending at the southernmost point of mainland Finland roughly a hundred kilometres from Helsinki. Its value is entirely geometric. The Gulf of Finland narrows here, and whoever holds Hanko together with the Estonian shore opposite can meter every ship going in or out — including everything bound for Leningrad. Hanko's harbour was also the only Finnish port that stayed open in winter, which is why the railway from Hyvinkää and the town charter both arrived in the 1870s.

Russia had understood the geometry long before the Soviet Union did. Under the Grand Duchy, Hanko's outer islands were fortified in stone from the 1790s, abandoned, and then fortified again between 1912 and 1917 as part of Peter the Great's Naval Fortress — a chain of coastal batteries meant to bar the sea road to St Petersburg. When Finland became independent in 1918 it inherited those emplacements and the guns still standing in them, and it kept them.

## Midnight, 22 March 1940

The Moscow Peace Treaty of 12 March 1940 ended the Winter War and Finland's negotiators signed terms they had no power to soften. Among them was a thirty-year lease to the Soviet Union of 115 square kilometres taking in the outer Hanko peninsula, the town of Hanko itself, the surrounding waters and about four hundred islands.

The handover was set for midnight on 22 March, which gave ten days. Finnish coastal artillerymen and civilian work crews spent them dragging the heavy guns off the outer islands, and made the deadline with almost nothing to spare. The civilian population had to go too. Around eight thousand people — a whole town, its shops, its schools, its summer villas — packed what they could carry and left. It would be almost two years before any of them came home, and when they did they came back to a landscape they had to be warned not to walk across.

## A Naval Base That Was Mostly Infantry

Soviet engineers and troops began arriving in April 1940, and what they built was only nominally naval. A small fleet detachment operated from the harbour; the overwhelming majority of the garrison was ground forces, eventually numbering somewhere between 25,000 and 30,000 men with infantry, airborne and armoured units among them. The base watched maritime traffic across the mouth of the gulf, and it sat like a loaded weapon on Finland's southwestern flank.

Both sides dug. The Soviets fortified the boundary of the leased area; the Finns fortified their side of the same line, across cleared forest. Moscow then pressed for the right to move troops and supplies by rail across southern Finland to reach the base — a demand Finland resisted and granted only in narrow form. Nothing did more to poison relations in 1940. Sergey Kabanov took command in March 1941, three months before the base was cut off.

## The Siege Neither Side Wanted

When the Continuation War began in June 1941, Finnish troops sealed the neck of the peninsula and the Hanko garrison was isolated. Then, for five months, remarkably little happened — deliberately. The Finns had no intention of storming 25,000 entrenched men, and the Soviets had no intention of breaking out. The front settled into trench warfare: artillery exchanges, sniping, patrol clashes, and small amphibious raids among the surrounding skerries. The sharpest fighting was for the Bengtskär lighthouse, where a Soviet night landing in fog nearly succeeded against its tiny Finnish garrison before being driven off.

Finnish losses on the Hanko front came to 961 men — 297 of them killed. Soviet losses in the fighting itself were roughly 800 killed and 1,500 wounded. The truly terrible figures belong to the withdrawal.

## Leaving, and What Was Left

Between 16 October and 1 December 1941 the Soviet Baltic Fleet lifted around 23,000 troops out of Hanko to Leningrad in a series of convoys, and paid for it. Three destroyers, the large transports Andrei Zhdanov and Iosif Stalin, and a string of smaller vessels were sunk or crippled on Finnish minefields and under coastal artillery fire. Estimates of Soviet dead during the evacuation alone run from three to five thousand — men who had spent five months in a static siege and drowned in the dark on the way home. The last troops were gone on 2 December.

They left the leased area heavily mined, and clearing it took long enough that returning families were kept out of their own streets. The Soviet Union never came back: under the 1944 armistice it took a lease at Porkkala instead, closer to Helsinki, and Kabanov ended up commanding that too. The 1947 Paris Peace Treaties formally extinguished the Hanko lease, twenty-seven years short of its term. Today the peninsula is a summer resort with a working harbour, and the only uniforms belong to the Finnish Defence Forces on the closed islands offshore.

## Flight Context

The Soviet base centred on Hanko town and harbour at 59.833°N, 23.083°E, with the leased area covering the outer 115 km² of the Hankoniemi peninsula and roughly 400 surrounding islands. From the air the peninsula is unmistakable: a narrow sand-and-pine spit angling southwest into open Baltic, fringed by a dense skerry field, with Hanko's harbour and the town's water tower at the tip. The old lease boundary still shows in places as a line of clearings and overgrown earthworks across the neck of the peninsula. Best viewed at 3,000–6,000 ft AGL, high enough to take in the whole spit and the archipelago at once. Nearest airports: Hanko (EFHN) on the peninsula, Turku (EFTU) about 95 km north-northwest, Helsinki-Vantaa (EFHK) about 115 km northeast. Russarö and other Finnish military islands lie immediately south — restricted airspace and firing notices apply, so check NOTAMs. Clear northerly airflow in late spring and summer gives the best visibility; sea fog forms fast over the gulf in autumn.
