# Russarö

A low granite island six kilometres south of Hanko whose guns once drove off a Soviet cruiser, and which has been closed to the public ever since.

**Location:** 59.7719, 22.9472

**Categories:** Hanko, Finland, Finnish islands in the Baltic, Islands of Uusimaa, Lighthouses, Military history, Winter War

## Introduction

At five minutes to ten on the second morning of the Winter War, six guns on a low granite island south of Hanko began ranging on a target twenty-four kilometres out to sea. The target was the Soviet light cruiser Kirov, lead ship of the newest class in the Baltic Fleet, sent with two destroyers to shoot Russarö's coastal battery into silence. Kirov turned her starboard side to the island and returned fire two minutes later from twenty kilometres — by which point the gunners ashore had found their settings. Shell splashes began climbing out of the water around all three ships. Kirov broke off and ran for Liepāja with seventeen of her crew dead and thirty wounded, and Russarö, having won the argument, has stayed closed to the public ever since.

## Six Guns With a Chilean Past

The battery that fired on Kirov had reached Russarö by an unlikely route. In 1912 Chile ordered fourteen 234 mm naval guns from Bethlehem Steel in Pennsylvania. The deal collapsed, and in 1914 Russia bought the whole lot — a wartime bargain in heavy artillery. Six of them ended up in individual pits on Russarö, arranged as three two-gun sections, as part of Peter the Great's Naval Fortress: the chain of coastal batteries built between 1912 and 1917 to bar the sea approach to St Petersburg.

The Russians did not do this by halves. Between 1914 and 1917 the small island acquired more than forty buildings, a road network and even a railway to move ammunition to the emplacements. When Finland became independent in 1918 it took over the installation and found six of the American guns still in working order. They were unusual enough in European service to be worth keeping, and Finnish coastal artillerymen were still running exercises on them through the 1930s, dwarfed in the surviving photographs by barrels close to twelve metres long.

## The Eye of Hanko

Long before there were guns there were lights. Ships have been guided past Russarö since the early 1700s, first by daymarks, then by a beacon the Russian Emperor ordered in 1826 and which was burning by 1838. That wooden structure was destroyed in 1854, in the same Crimean War panic that saw the Russians blow up their own fortress at Gustavsvärn a few kilometres north.

The replacement, built in 1863, is still standing and still lit. It is an octagonal tower of brick on a natural stone base, five storeys with a vaulted ceiling on the top floor, twenty-one metres tall and lifting its light thirty-four metres above the sea. Four white flashes every forty-five seconds carry roughly seventeen nautical miles. Because Hanko was Finland's only harbour that stayed open through the winter, Russarö became the first Finnish lighthouse kept burning in the cold months — which is how it earned the name locals still use, Hangon silmä, the Eye of Hanko. It was automated in 1949. The tower has since fallen into a poor enough state that nobody is allowed to climb it.

## Ten Days in March

The Winter War ended on 12 March 1940 with a treaty that put Russarö inside a 115-square-kilometre area leased to the Soviet Union for thirty years. Handover was fixed for midnight on 22 March. That left ten days to strip the island of everything worth taking, and the Finns spent them hauling all six 234 mm guns out of their pits and off Russarö with civilian work crews — an operation that finished barely inside the deadline.

For the next twenty months the island belonged to the Soviet Hanko naval base. When that base was evacuated in the closing weeks of 1941, the withdrawing troops left the whole leased area heavily mined, which made the return of Finnish civilians a slow and dangerous business. Russarö went back to the Finnish Defence Forces, and it has never left them.

## A Closed Island That Still Reports the Weather

Russarö today is an active military area. The Finnish Army publishes firing notices for it, private boats may not land, and the island's role as a coastal training fort means the granite is periodically loud. Somewhere between the batteries and the lighthouse sits a Finnish Meteorological Institute station, one of the long-running observation posts feeding sea-area forecasts for the Gulf of Finland. If you have ever heard a Finnish marine forecast, you have heard numbers that came off this rock.

Access is not impossible, only escorted. In summer, day cruises run out to the island from Hanko's Eastern Harbour, six kilometres of open water each way, threading the same channel Kirov's gunners were trying to close. What you find at the end is a treeless slab of pink-grey granite, low enough that swells break clean across its southern edge, carrying a lighthouse, a weather station and the concrete geometry of a century of coastal defence. It is not a picturesque island. It is a functional one, and it has been functional continuously for longer than Finland has been a country.

## Flight Context

Russarö lies at 59.772°N, 22.947°E, about 6 km south of Hanko town and marking the outer edge of the Hanko archipelago at the mouth of the Gulf of Finland. It is a distinctive navigation fix: a bare, low, roughly oval island with the octagonal 1863 lighthouse as its only tall structure, sitting well clear of the skerry belt in open water. Excellent coastal waypoint at any altitude, but the surrounding area is an active Finnish Defence Forces range with published firing notices and airspace restrictions — check NOTAMs and do not plan low passes. View from 2,500–5,000 ft AGL and offset to the north to keep Hanko's harbour and Gustavsvärn in the same frame. Nearest airports: Hanko (EFHN) about 12 km north-northeast, Turku (EFTU) about 105 km north-northwest, Helsinki-Vantaa (EFHK) about 130 km northeast. The outer gulf generates sea fog quickly in spring and autumn; midsummer usually gives long, clear, low-angle light.
