# Osmussaar

A sixty-metre mountain of Ordovician limestone with only six metres showing, said to hold Odin's grave, emptied of its people in 1940 and now a nature reserve with two residents.

**Location:** 59.2917, 23.3917

**Categories:** Estonia, Islands, Baltic Sea, Gulf of Finland, Nature reserves, Geology, World War II

## Introduction

Osmussaar is sixty metres tall. Standing on it you would never believe that, because only about six of those metres are above water — a low pale shelf of limestone in the mouth of the Gulf of Finland, 4.8 square kilometres of it, seven and a half kilometres off the Estonian coast, with cliffs you could jump off without much conviction. The rest of the island is underwater, rising off the seabed like a mesa. It is a surviving fragment of the Baltic Klint, the limestone escarpment that runs the length of this coast, and the westernmost place before Öland where that escarpment breaks the surface of the sea at all. Two people live here.

## The Klint Comes Up for Air

The rock is Ordovician limestone, laid down as seafloor when this part of the world sat near the equator, and on Osmussaar it wears a covering of Quaternary marine sediment nowhere more than two metres thick. That is essentially the whole island: a slab and a dusting. The north and east coasts break into cliffs of up to six metres; the western shore is heaped instead with shingle fields made entirely of limestone pebbles, ground and rounded and stacked into ridges by the sea. Osmussaar itself is astonishingly young. It came up out of the water only 2,000 to 3,000 years ago, as the crust rebounded from the weight of the vanished ice sheet, and it is still rising at roughly three millimetres a year. Everything human that has ever happened here happened on brand-new ground.

## Where Odin Is Buried

The old name was Odensholm — Odinsholm in some spellings — and it means what it looks like: the holm of the Vikings' chief god, who according to a legend told here is buried on the island. Whether anyone was actually living on Osmussaar in the Viking Age is unresolved, though it is entirely possible. What is documented is a Swedish-speaking community present continuously from at least the 14th century, farming thin soil, fishing, and salvaging what the shoals broke. Almost nothing survives in writing from before the 1700s. The Estonian name, Osmussaar, has resisted explanation altogether — nobody has produced a derivation that convinces. An island can hold a god's grave in one language and an unsolved word in another.

## Estonia Is Not Supposed to Have Earthquakes

The Baltic shield is old, cold, and geologically about as quiet as continental crust gets. Then, on 25 October 1976, the ground under this stretch of water moved hard enough to register 4.7 — still the strongest earthquake ever recorded in Estonia. The focus lay five to seven kilometres northeast of Osmussaar, ten to thirteen kilometres down. Sections of the cliff along the island's northeast coast sheared off and fell. Buildings on the island were damaged, which by 1976 meant the property of a small Soviet naval detachment, since nobody else was permitted to be there. For an island that surfaced within the span of recorded history and has been rising ever since, a sudden lurch is not entirely out of character.

## The Sailing Fleet of 1944

In October 1944, with Finland newly out of its alliance with Germany, Osmussaar became a staging point for one of the war's more improbable operations. Finnish galeases — wooden coastal sailing traders, crewed by their civilian owners' men — ran from here to Rohuküla near Haapsalu and across to Hiiumaa and Saaremaa to put Soviet troops ashore for the Moonsund landings. Each galeas carried roughly an infantry company, plus horses and field guns. On 4 October they landed men at Heltermaa, and the following day two Soviet detachments and two brigades went in to Taaliku and Triigi. German submarines hunted them: on 16 October U-481 destroyed the galeases Endla, Dan and Maria with gunfire and by ramming, and on 25 October U-958 torpedoed the Linnea. Eight Finnish crewmen were killed. Sixteen of the fifty galeases and twenty-two of the fifty motorboats were lost overall. On 2 November 1944 the Finnish naval headquarters wound the unit up, released its crews, and handed the surviving boats back to the men who owned them.

## Emptied, Then Given to the Birds

About 130 people lived here before the war, most of them Estonian Swedes. On 12 June 1940 they were removed to Vormsi so the Soviet Union could make the island a military base, and the last of them crossed to Sweden in February 1944. The roofless chapel beside the Kappelkärre shoreline is what is left of their parish. The military that replaced them never quite materialised. Plans called for a garrison of up to 1,200 men; the large unit was probably disbanded by 1947, and thereafter the island held no more than forty. When Soviet forces finally withdrew, Osmussaar had nobody at all on it until 2001, when two people moved back. In 1996 the whole island was designated a landscape protection area for its geology, its plant communities, and its birds. Tourism arrived in 2002 with about 2,000 visitors; up to 3,000 now come each summer, walk out over the shingle, and go home again.

## Flight Context

Osmussaar sits at approximately 59.29°N, 23.39°E, 7.5 km off Estonia's northwestern coast at the mouth of the Gulf of Finland — one of the cleanest visual waypoints on this shoreline. From the air it reads as a flat pale limestone platform about five kilometres long and barely more than a kilometre wide, rimmed by white shingle, with a black-and-white banded lighthouse at the northern end and low scrub on the exposed western side. Põõsaspea cape on the mainland points straight at it from the southeast; the Hanko peninsula lies about 55 km north across open water. Best at 2,000–5,000 ft, when the shoal shelf around the island and the drowned rim of the Neugrund impact structure offshore show as bright turquoise against deeper blue. Nearby airports: EETN (Tallinn) about 85 km east, EEKA (Kärdla, Hiiumaa) 55 km southwest, EFHN (Hanko) 55 km north, EFHK (Helsinki-Vantaa) 115 km north-northeast. Sea fog is frequent in spring and early summer, and the surrounding water is a protected bird area with heavy low-level migration traffic in spring and autumn.
