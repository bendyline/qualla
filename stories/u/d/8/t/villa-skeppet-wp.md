# Villa Skeppet

Alvar Aalto's last and smallest house, built in Ekenäs to lure a Mediterranean sailor home to Finland — and to be lived in by the friend who would write Aalto's life.

**Location:** 59.9698, 23.4373

**Categories:** Alvar Aalto buildings, Modernist architecture in Finland, Houses in Finland, House museums in Finland, Raseborg, Buildings and structures in Uusimaa

## Introduction

"I will make you a house so beautiful that you will want to return to Finland." Alvar Aalto was in his seventies when he made that promise to Göran Schildt, and he kept it. By 1968 Schildt — author, art historian, Mediterranean sailor — had a house on the Greek island of Leros and no permanent address in the country he was born in. Aalto and his wife Elissa came to inspect a level, park-like plot in Ekenäs in the spring of 1969. What went up there over the following year turned out to be the last house Aalto ever designed, and the smallest.

## A Promise Made in a Hospital Bed

To understand why Aalto had to bargain for his friend's return, you have to go back thirty years. Schildt, born in Helsinki in 1917 and the son of the writer Runar Schildt, was severely wounded in the abdomen by an explosive bullet during Finland's Winter War of 1939–40. He spent roughly eighteen months in hospital. Lying there, he made himself a bargain of his own: if he survived this, he would get a boat and sail into every corner of the Mediterranean.

He survived, and in 1948 he did exactly that, taking a ketch named *Daphne* from northern Europe down through the French waterways to the Ligurian coast. The book he wrote about the voyage, *In the Wake of a Wish* (1949), made his name. *Daphne* eventually found a home berth on Leros, where Schildt set up house in 1965. Alvar Aalto was among the guests who sailed aboard her; the friendship dated from 1952, and it rested on a shared appetite for the Mediterranean — its light, its landscapes, its civilisations.

## The Prow Above the Garage

The Schildts were a childless couple who wanted something modest and comfortable. Aalto gave them modest, then quietly overruled them on comfortable, inserting a living room out of all proportion to the rest of the house.

The site dropped about a metre and a half from road to yard, a problem Aalto solved by lifting that living room up over the garage. Clad in vertical weatherboarding above white rendered brick, it fans outward as it rises, and it points toward the eastern harbour and the southern bay. The people of Ekenäs took one look and renamed the place: the house had been Villa Schildt, but from inside and out the effect is of a ship's prow lifting toward the water, so Skeppet — Swedish for the ship — is what stuck.

Aalto declined to fence the garden, screening it instead with panels of diagonally set boards, and dropped an amoeba-shaped lily pond into the middle of the inner yard. The open fireplace in the living room is less a hearth than an abstract sculpture.

## Rooms for the Two Who Lived There

Inside, the fan-shaped living room and the central hall share a ceiling and an open balustrade, so the two spaces read as one volume with a study, a bedroom, a combined kitchen and dining area and the sauna opening off it. The sauna sits in its own timber wing, joined to the house by lattice work rather than a corridor.

The plan makes room for the way this particular couple worked and entertained. There is a wing for writing. The separate guest building holds a sauna and a guest room, and that guest room was fitted out in themed sections devoted to Strindberg and Ibsen, furnished with pieces of the period. Throughout, the interiors carry the Mediterranean current that had drawn Aalto and Schildt together in the first place, furnished with Aalto's own designs. It is a small house that never feels cramped, largely because Aalto spent all his generosity in one room and was ruthless everywhere else.

## The Biographer's House

Aalto died in 1976. Schildt, who had been aboard *Daphne* with him and had watched him work for nearly a quarter of a century, then wrote the three-volume biography that remains the standard account of Aalto's life — and he was living in the house Aalto had built him while he did it. He kept writing until the end of a long career: some thirty books in Swedish, decades of art criticism, election to the Royal Swedish Academy of Fine Arts in 2000.

Schildt died in March 2009. The Christine and Göran Schildt Foundation now owns Villa Skeppet, and in December 2020 the house opened to the general public for guided tours. Visiting it is an oddly intimate experience for an architectural pilgrimage. Most Aalto landmarks are institutions — a sanatorium, a town hall, a concert hall. This one is a favour between two old men, one of whom did not want to come home.

## Flight Context

Villa Skeppet stands at 59.9698°N, 23.4373°E in Ekenäs (Tammisaari), part of the town of Raseborg on Finland's southern coast. It is a small, low, white-and-timber house on flat wooded ground close to the water, so it will not resolve as a landmark from altitude — navigate instead by the Ekenäs town centre, its harbour inlets and the intricate fringe of the Ekenäs archipelago immediately south. Sightseeing altitude 1,000–2,000 ft AGL. Hanko Airfield (EFHN), Finland's southernmost, lies roughly 35 km southwest; Helsinki-Vantaa (EFHK) is about 80 km northeast and Turku (EFTU) about 90 km northwest. The Gulf of Finland here generates advection fog in spring and early summer, and the low sun angle in winter makes the coastline read as silhouette rather than detail.
