# Raseborg Museum

A geneticist and his wife willed a small Finnish coastal town an art museum neither of them lived to see open — the newest room in a collection begun in 1906.

**Location:** 59.9741, 23.4378

**Categories:** Raseborg, History museums in Finland, Art museums, Museums in Uusimaa, 1906 establishments in Finland, Helene Schjerfbeck

## Introduction

Albert and Clara de la Chapelle both died before the museum they paid for opened its doors. She died in the spring of 2020; he died that December. Chappe — the house of contemporary art their foundation built on the Ekenäs waterfront, named for them — took its first visitors in April 2023, and a great deal of what hangs inside had been theirs. The building cost roughly seven million euros and was handed to the city of Raseborg when it was finished. It is now the newest part of Raseborg Museum, an institution that has been gathering up this stretch of coast since 1906, back when it was called Ekenäs Museum and Ekenäs was still a town in its own right.

## The Geneticist Who Collected Pictures

Albert de la Chapelle was born in Helsinki on 11 February 1933 and spent his working life inside the human genome. He founded Finland's first Department of Medical Genetics at the University of Helsinki. He identified the condition now known as de la Chapelle syndrome, showing that a person could be born male with two X chromosomes — a finding that later put him publicly at odds with the International Olympic Committee over its gender verification testing, because his own laboratory had demonstrated how untidy chromosomes actually are. He mapped MSH2, one of the genes behind Lynch syndrome and its hereditary colorectal cancers, and worked on inherited predisposition to thyroid cancer. Alongside all of that, he and Clara bought art. They set up a foundation with a single stated purpose: build a museum in Ekenäs, then give it away.

## A Town That Kept Its Wood

Gustav Vasa granted Ekenäs its town charter on 15 December 1546, making it Finland's seventh-oldest town and the first founded after the medieval period had closed. The old quarter is still standing and still wooden, most of it put up across the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, and protected tightly enough that residents cannot renovate their own houses freely. Swedish is the majority language here; Finnish speakers are the minority, which is the reverse of the national picture. On 1 January 2009 Ekenäs stopped being a municipality, merging with Karis and Pohja into the city of Raseborg — Snappertuna and Tenala had already been absorbed into Ekenäs itself, in 1977 and 1993 respectively. The museum followed the same logic three years earlier, dropping the name Ekenäs Museum in 2006. It now serves as the regional responsibility museum for Western Uusimaa, charged with a whole region's heritage rather than a single town's attic.

## The Painter Who Came to the Coast

The museum's second permanent exhibition belongs to Helene Schjerfbeck, born in Helsinki on 10 July 1862. After her mother died she moved to Ekenäs in the mid-1920s and stayed for most of the following fifteen years, which is why her pictures are here rather than somewhere louder. When the Winter War began in December 1939 she left for a farm at Tenala for about a year, then came back to Ekenäs in mid-1940. She died at Saltsjöbaden in Sweden on 23 January 1946. Her work travelled an enormous distance in one lifetime, from French-influenced realism toward late self-portraits so pared back that a New York Times critic described them as nearly abstract images in which pure paint and cryptic description are held in perfect balance. An early biography cast her as fragile and reclusive; scholars have spent decades arguing that this was a misreading of a woman fully engaged with the art of her century.

## The House of Art by the Sea

Chappe calls itself Konstens hus vid havet — the house of art by the sea. Asmo Jaaksi of JKMM Architects won the competition to design it, with a scheme the donors themselves favoured, and the finished building stacks its exhibition halls across three floors next to the older museum centre. Shortages of materials and labour pushed the opening back, so the doors did not swing until April 2023. Between the two institutions, a visitor can move in a few minutes from a nineteenth-century Ekenäs household through Schjerfbeck's shrinking, sharpening self-portraits and into a room of contemporary work bought by a cancer geneticist. One Finnish Museum Card covers the lot.

## Flight Context

59.9741 N, 23.4378 E, on the Gulf of Finland coast in Ekenäs, city of Raseborg. Best seen from 2,000-4,000 ft: the museum quarter sits at the seaward edge of the wooden Old Town, immediately behind the harbour and the tightly braided channels of the Ekenäs archipelago, which fan out south into open water. Hanko Airfield (EFHN) lies roughly 25 km southwest, Helsinki-Vantaa (EFHK) about 90 km east-northeast, and Turku (EFTU) about 90 km northwest. Summer haze over the Gulf can flatten contrast badly; the sharpest light is late spring and early autumn, when the low sun separates the red-ochre town roofs from the water.
