# Tammisaari Prison Camp

At Dragsvik, outside the small town of Ekenäs, nearly three thousand people starved to death in Finnish custody in the summer of 1918 — and the ground is still a working garrison.

**Location:** 59.9878, 23.4861

**Categories:** Finnish Civil War prison camps, Mass graves in Finland, Communism in Finland, Raseborg, Historical sites, Military history

## Introduction

In August 1918 the chief physician of the Tammisaari camp wrote a report he did not expect anyone outside Finland to read. Robert Tigerstedt was a physiologist of international standing rather than a polemicist, which made the sentence he set down all the more damning for its restraint: such a death rate had never been seen before, and nothing like it could have happened even during the times of Czarist Russia. Finnish Social Democrats leaked the report to the Swedish press, and from there it moved through Scandinavia and on to Britain. What Tigerstedt was describing was a row of former Russian barracks at Dragsvik, on a sheltered bay outside the small Swedish-speaking town of Ekenäs — Tammisaari in Finnish — near the landward end of the Hanko peninsula. By then roughly one prisoner in three who had passed through its gates was dead.

## The Losing Side of a Four-Month War

Finland's civil war ran from January to May 1918 and ended with the Whites — the government side, reinforced in April by a German expeditionary division that came ashore at Hanko — in complete control. What followed was not a mopping-up operation but a mass internment. Something like 80,000 Red Guards, Red officials, their sympathisers and their families were taken into custody, among them 4,700 women and 1,500 children. Few were revolutionaries in any professional sense. They were farm labourers, sawmill hands, factory workers, dockers and their wives, from a country where landless rural workers had almost no political voice and where the previous year's food shortages had already turned hunger into politics.

The first of them reached Dragsvik in early May, moving into barracks the Imperial Russian Army had vacated. In mid-May the camp held fewer than a hundred men. By midsummer it held just under 8,700 — roughly eleven percent of every Red taken prisoner in Finland.

## Thirty a Day

Nothing at Dragsvik had been built for eight thousand people. There was no food supply on that scale, no sanitation, no accommodation; the camp simply absorbed whatever was sent to it. Finland's harvest and distribution had been wrecked by the fighting and the whole country was short of bread. Inside the camps, short meant a half-plate of thin soup and a piece of bread, and prisoners dug for roots to fill out the difference. Dysentery moved through the barracks, then pneumonia, and in July the Spanish influenza reached Finland and found at Dragsvik a population already too weakened to resist it.

The monthly ledger reads 492 dead in June, roughly 1,100 in July, roughly 1,040 in August, 406 in September. Through that summer an average of thirty people died every day in a compound the size of a large village. The War Victims of Finland 1914–1922 database records 2,997 deaths at Tammisaari between May and December 1918, a mortality rate of 34 percent — the worst of any camp in the country. Very few of them were executed. They starved, in Finnish custody, through a mild Baltic summer, a few kilometres from a town going about its business.

## Parole, and the Prison That Stayed

On 15 September 1918 the camp as such closed. Most of the survivors were released on parole, part of a wave of amnesties that gradually emptied the Finnish camps over the following year. Dragsvik itself did not empty. It became a forced-labour prison for Reds under sentence, and in the early 1920s it was repurposed again, this time for political prisoners — members of the outlawed Communist Party of Finland and the Socialist Workers' Party of Finland. Across the twenties and thirties the count ran between roughly a hundred and six hundred, peaking after the Communist Laws of 1930.

In July 1933 more than 370 of those prisoners went on hunger strike. It lasted eleven days and ended with five men dead from force-feeding. Tammisaari remained in use as a penitentiary until 1940, and the list of who passed through reads like a roster of twentieth-century Finnish communism: Ville Pessi, Arvo Tuominen, Aimo Aaltonen. One of them, Yrjö Leino, held there from 1935 to 1938, was Finland's minister of the interior by 1945.

## The Largest Grave in Finland

During the Second World War the political prisoners were dispersed to newer camps and the Finnish Army took the site over as a garrison. It still is one. The Nyland Brigade trains coastal jaegers at Dragsvik — the only unit in the Finnish Defence Forces that instructs in Swedish — and conscripts drill on the same ground where the barracks stood.

Outside the fence lies a mass grave holding around three thousand people, the largest in the country. Former Red Guards raised the first memorial there in 1951. When the tradition of annual visits eventually lapsed, a citizens' committee formed in 1985 to take up the work; in 1988 it enlarged the memorial with plaques carrying the names then known, and more were unveiled in 1993 as researchers recovered them. A permanent exhibition opened beside the grave in 2008. In 1973 the Finnish state paid reparations to 11,600 surviving former camp prisoners, which was as close to an official reckoning as that generation received.

Aarne Arvonen spent a year at Tammisaari. He was twenty when they took him, he had fought for the Red Guard, and he lived until New Year's Day 2009 — dying at 111, the last surviving veteran of Finland's civil war and the last Finn alive who had been born in the 1800s.

## Flight Context

Dragsvik lies at 59.988°N, 23.486°E, on the north shore of Dragsviksfjärden about 3 km north of central Ekenäs (Tammisaari) and roughly 90 km west-southwest of Helsinki. The garrison reads from the air as a compact grid of barracks and parade ground set against forest and a narrow inlet of the Baltic archipelago; the memorial and mass grave sit just outside it. Best appreciated at 2,000–4,000 ft AGL, where the relationship between the garrison, the water and the town is legible in one view. Nearest airports: Helsinki-Vantaa (EFHK) about 95 km northeast, Hanko (EFHN) about 40 km southwest, Turku (EFTU) about 90 km northwest. Note that Finnish military airspace restrictions and firing-area notices apply around active garrisons — check NOTAMs. Summer visibility over the archipelago is generally excellent; winter brings low cloud, sea fog off open water and short daylight.
