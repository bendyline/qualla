# Raseborg

A Swedish-speaking municipality on Finland's south coast with more sea than land, named after a castle whose harbour drained away centuries ago.

**Location:** 59.9750, 23.4361

**Categories:** finland, uusimaa, archipelago, castle, swedish-speaking-finland, ironworks, coastal, geology

## Introduction

The castle lost its sea. When Raseborg was built in the 1370s it sat on a small island at the head of a bay, close enough to the water that ships came to its walls; today the ruin looks out over a meadow with cattle in it, and the Gulf of Finland is a walk away. Nothing dramatic happened. The land simply rose, as it has been rising across the northern Baltic ever since the ice sheet that pressed it down melted off, and the harbour turned into pasture at a pace no single generation could see. The municipality that borrowed the castle's name in 2009 lives with the same slow arithmetic: a coast that keeps quietly gaining ground, and a landscape that is still mostly water.

## The Fortress Against Tallinn

Raseborg Castle was finished somewhere between 1373 and 1378, and the first written mention of it comes from 1378. Its job was commercial as much as military. Across the gulf lay Tallinn, a Hanseatic city with a grip on Baltic trade, and the Swedish crown wanted a strongpoint on the northern shore to protect its own interests in southern Finland. For roughly two centuries the castle held the administrative centre of western Uusimaa. Then Gustav Vasa founded Helsinki in 1550, the strategic centre of gravity shifted east, and the retreating water made the castle harder to reach by boat every decade. It was abandoned in 1553. Restoration began in the 1890s, the ruins are open to anyone who walks up, and since 1966 a summer theatre has staged plays each July in the shadow of the walls — the loudest the site has been in five hundred years.

## Three Towns Became One

Modern Raseborg is a 2009 invention. On the first day of that year the towns of Ekenäs, Karis and Pojo (Pohja) dissolved themselves into a single municipality that now stretches from the archipelago west of the Hanko peninsula to the archipelago east of it. About 26,856 people live in it, making it Finland's thirty-ninth largest municipality by population and one of its emptiest by density — roughly twenty-three people per square kilometre. The language balance is the thing outsiders notice first: 63.3 percent of residents speak Swedish, 30.3 percent Finnish, and 6.4 percent something else. This is one of the parts of Finland where Swedish is not a minority courtesy but the working language of the shop, the school and the football terrace.

## More Sea Than Land

The municipality covers 2,354 square kilometres, and here is the detail that explains everything about the place: 1,206 of those are water and only 1,149 are land. Raseborg is, by area, more sea than ground. That water is not open ocean but a maze of skerries, sounds and fairways, the shipping route between Helsinki and the Archipelago Sea threading straight through it. Behind the coast the terrain has an older logic. The ridge rising north of Karis station is Lojoåsen, known in Finnish as Lohjanharju, part of the Salpausselkä terminal moraine — a wall of gravel and sand dumped where the retreating ice sheet stalled. It divides the lake country of interior Finland from the Gulf of Finland, and it runs on southwest to become the Hanko peninsula itself.

## Iron Villages, Second Lives

Inland from the coast are the bruk, the old ironworks villages that were southern Finland's first industry. Fiskars was founded in 1649 by the German-born merchant Petter Thorwöste, who smelted iron and copper here where the rapids could turn a wheel. The company that grew out of it became a global brand and eventually took its factories elsewhere, which should have killed the village. Instead artists and designers moved into the empty workshops. Around six hundred people now live in the works area, the old buildings house glass, ceramics, metalwork and furniture studios, and since 2019 the village has run its own art and design biennale. Nearby Billnäs, another ironworks on the same river system, has followed a similar arc from forge to festival venue.

## Getting Around a Scattered Place

Karis is the hinge. The town sits inland at a railway junction on the Kustbanan coastal line between Helsinki and Turku, with lines branching to Hanko and Hyvinkää, and the old station building has been repurposed as a pub. From there buses and a cycleway run down to Ekenäs on the coast, and a twenty-minute bus service reaches Fiskars year-round. The historic King's Road, the medieval post route that ran from Turku through Helsinki to Vyborg, crosses the municipality, which is why so much of the settlement pattern sits where it does. For anyone arriving on their own keel, the guest harbour at Ekenäs is the busiest berth on this stretch of coast. Ekenäs IF, promoted in 2023, carried a town of this size into Finland's top football division; their ground holds 1,900.

## Flight Context

Raseborg's administrative centre sits at 59.975 N, 23.436 E on Finland's south coast, roughly 80 km west of Helsinki and 115 km southeast of Turku. From the air the municipality is unmistakable: a shattered coastline of hundreds of islands and sounds, with the long finger of the Hanko peninsula running southwest and the Salpausselkä ridge visible as a wooded rise behind the coast. Raseborg Castle ruin stands inland near Snappertuna, a grey stone stump above open fields. Nearest airports: Hanko-Täktom (EFHN) 30 km southwest, Nummela (EFNU) 45 km northeast, Helsinki-Vantaa (EFHK) 80 km northeast, Turku (EFTU) 100 km northwest. Recommended viewing altitude 2,000-4,000 ft for the archipelago pattern. Sea fog is frequent in spring; the sounds freeze in most winters, and the ice roads and skerries read very differently under snow.
