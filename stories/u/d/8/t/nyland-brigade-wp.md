# Nyland Brigade

The only unit in the Finnish Defence Forces that teaches in Swedish, training coastal jaegers on ground that held one of the deadliest prison camps of the 1918 civil war.

**Location:** 59.9861, 23.4921

**Categories:** military, finland, raseborg, finnish-navy, swedish-speaking-finland, civil-war, archipelago, history

## Introduction

At Dragsvik everything is taught in Swedish and every order is given in Finnish. That is not an accident of paperwork; it is the whole reason the Nyland Brigade exists in its present form. Finland has two national languages, and a substantial minority of its citizens live their daily lives in Swedish. This garrison on the Raseborg coast is the single place in the Finnish Defence Forces where a conscript can learn to fight in his own first language — about 1,500 soldiers a year pass through it — while still drilling to the Finnish commands that will be shouted across any joint operation. The compromise is exact, deliberate, and a hundred years in the making.

## Jaegers of the Skerries

The brigade is a marine-type unit of the Finnish Navy, and what it produces is coastal jaegers: troops trained to fight in an environment of islands, sounds and shallow water where a wrong channel is as dangerous as a wrong hill. The Vasa Coastal Jaeger Battalion turns out jaegers, boatsmen, coastal missile crews and reconnaissance troops; the Ekenäs Coastal Battalion handles mortar and signal troops, military police, drivers, supply specialists and combat engineers. Above that sits the High Readiness Unit, whose soldiers are picked after basic training and serve 347 days regardless of what job they end up doing. Movement is everything here. The brigade runs XA-185 Pasi armoured personnel carriers ashore and Jehu, Jurmo, Uisko and G-class landing craft afloat, and its anti-ship punch has shifted from the old 130 K 54 coastal guns to Spike-ER missiles that a small team can carry into a skerry and hide.

## A Lineage Stitched From Four Wars

The unit as such was named in 1957, but its traditions run back to the Nyland and Tavastehus Cavalry Regiment that Gustavus Adolphus established in 1626, a formation that later distinguished itself at Siikajoki during the Finnish War against Russia. Everything between is a reshuffling of Finland's twentieth century. The brigade also traces itself to the 3rd Jaeger Regiment of the Civil War, renamed the Uusimaa Regiment between the wars. After the Winter War that regiment helped form the 13th Brigade, which became Infantry Regiment 13 in the Continuation War, then the 4th Infantry Regiment, then the 4th Brigade, and in 1957 the Nyland Brigade. It stayed an Army unit until 1998, when it transferred to the Navy — a change of service that finally matched the water it had always trained on.

## What Happened at Dragsvik in 1918

The same barracks ground holds a much darker history, and the brigade's own century-long lineage runs directly through it. In May 1918, after the Finnish Civil War ended in a victory for the Whites, the Dragsvik barracks became a prison camp for captured Red Guards and people merely suspected of the wrong politics. At its fullest, between 20 and 24 June 1918, about 8,597 people were held here — roughly one in nine of all Red prisoners in the country. There was no adequate sanitation, no functioning medical care and not enough food. Typhus and relapsing fever moved through the crowded barracks, and between June and December approximately 2,873 prisoners died of hunger and disease. Very roughly, one in three of the people brought here did not leave. The total dead for 1918 reached 3,023, including 31 who were executed. The Red Guards had been drawn overwhelmingly from Finland's working people, and most of those who died at Dragsvik had already laid down their arms before they were brought here. They died within sight of a coastline their families knew.

## The Camp After the Camp

The place did not close when the dying slowed. From 1923 Tammisaari operated as a forced-labour institution holding ordinary convicts alongside communist political prisoners, and those political prisoners did what prisoners with time and conviction do: they ran study circles, organized, and pushed back, staging hunger strikes in 1929 and again in 1933. During the Winter War the facility held people the state judged politically unreliable. It was finally decommissioned in 1940, when the military took the ground back for its original purpose. Finland spent much of the twentieth century unable to talk plainly about 1918, and the reckoning came late. At Dragsvik, the memory and the garrison now share an address.

## Allies on the Archipelago

The last few years have made this quiet corner of the coast busy again. U.S. Marines have trained at the Nyland Brigade garrison since 2022 — one contingent took part in a Swedish Heritage Parade at Dragsvik in November 2023, which is a fair summary of how the place works — and the tempo increased after Finland's Defence Cooperation Agreement with the United States entered into force in 2024. The reason is geography that has not changed since 1626. The Gulf of Finland narrows here, the archipelago offers cover and confusion in equal measure, and troops who can move a missile team by landing craft through a maze of skerries in the dark are a specific and hard-to-replace capability. The brigade has always been in the business of knowing this water. Now other navies come to learn it too.

## Flight Context

The Nyland Brigade garrison sits at Dragsvik, 59.986 N, 23.492 E, on the eastern shore of the bay just northeast of Ekenäs (Tammisaari) in Raseborg, about 80 km west of Helsinki. From the air it reads as an ordered block of barracks, hardstanding and jetties on a wooded point at the head of a long inlet, with the Ekenäs archipelago opening to the south. Note this is an active military installation: restricted and danger areas apply in the surrounding waters and airspace, and NOTAMs should be checked before routing close. Nearest airports: Hanko-Täktom (EFHN) 30 km southwest, Nummela (EFNU) 45 km northeast, Helsinki-Vantaa (EFHK) 80 km northeast, Turku (EFTU) 100 km northwest. Recommended viewing altitude 2,500-4,000 ft, standing well offshore. Winter ice on the inlet is part of the training environment, not an obstacle to it.
