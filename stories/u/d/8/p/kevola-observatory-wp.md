# Kevola Observatory

A working observatory on a Finnish farm, built for an astronomer who grew up on the land beneath it, housing a telescope that once found 807 asteroids.

**Location:** 60.4200, 22.7650

**Categories:** Astronomical observatories in Finland, Paimio, Buildings and structures in Southwest Finland, Astronomy, Women in science

## Introduction

Hilkka Rantaseppä-Helenius went to university intending to become a mathematics teacher. Yrjö Väisälä talked her out of it. Two decades later, in 1963, the Astronomy-Optical Society put up an observatory dome on a low hill on her family's farm in Paimio - which meant that a working astronomer at Tuorla Observatory could, unusually among her profession, do serious sky work in her own farmyard. She was fifty when she died in an accident in 1975. The asteroid 1530 Rantaseppä carries her name, and the observatory is still there, still open on clear nights.

## The Wizard of Tuorla and His Circle

Finnish asteroid hunting in the middle of the twentieth century was essentially one man and the people he pulled into his orbit. Yrjö Väisälä, born in 1891, worked as much in optics as in astronomy and had already flagged the problem in his lecture notes as early as 1924. It was only after Bernhard Schmidt published his wide-field camera design in 1930 that Väisälä moved quickly to solve its curved focal surface, flattening it by setting a doubly convex lens just in front of the plate holder. The design bears his name. The Minor Planet Center credits him personally with 128 asteroid discoveries between 1935 and 1944, plus three comets. He founded the societies, he trained the observers, and he was known as the Wizard of Tuorla. He died in 1971. His brothers were the meteorologist Vilho Väisälä and the mathematician Kalle Väisälä, and it is a fund in the three brothers' names that would eventually buy the ground under Kevola.

## Silent in Eleven Languages

The other central figure was Liisi Oterma, born in 1915, who came to the University of Turku wanting to study Sanskrit and, finding it was not offered, took astronomy instead. She became Väisälä's assistant, took her master's in 1938, worked as an observer from 1941, and in 1955 became the first Finnish woman to earn a doctorate in astronomy - on telescope optics. She was made professor in 1965 and succeeded Väisälä as director of Tuorla Observatory in 1971. The Minor Planet Center credits her with 54 minor planets and she has three periodic comets to her name: 38P/Stephan-Oterma, 39P/Oterma and 139P/Väisälä-Oterma. She was also fluent in German, French, Italian, Spanish, Hungarian, English, Esperanto and Arabic among others, and so retiring that a Copenhagen colleague, Anders Reiz, said she was silent in eleven languages. She avoided cameras; only a handful of photographs of her exist. On 16 November 1938, working at the Iso-Heikkilä Observatory in Turku, she found the object that would become 1540 Kevola - a dark carbonaceous body roughly 42 kilometres across, circling the outer asteroid belt every four years and ten months. It was named for this observatory, the citation published in February 1976.

## The Instrument That Found 807 Worlds

Under the dome sits the reason any of this matters: a 50-centimetre anastigmatic Schmidt-Väisälä camera, once world-famous for the width and cleanliness of its field. The corrector lens is 50 centimetres across, the primary mirror 60, the focal length 1,031 millimetres, and the optics deliver a 6.7-degree field onto 12 by 12 centimetre plates. That is an enormous bite of sky to photograph at once, and it is precisely what asteroid hunting demands - expose, wait, expose again, look for the dot that moved. Before it came to Paimio the telescope stood at Iso-Heikkilä in Turku, where it was used to find 807 minor planets and seven comets. Two finder scopes ride alongside it, of 18 and 8 centimetres; the larger has often been turned on Pluto.

## Keeping the Roof Open

The site has three buildings: the dome, a zenith tower, and a heated hut for writing up observations. The zenith tower once held a 250/5150-millimetre telescope aimed straight up at the point overhead, used to track the wandering of the Earth's rotational axis; when the Astronomy-Optical Society wound up in 1983 the instrument went to Tuorla Observatory in Kaarina. English-language accounts still describe that tower as unused, which is out of date. In 1986 the buildings passed to Turun Ursa, the Turku amateur astronomy association Väisälä also founded. The land stayed rented until 2002, when a grant from the Vilho, Yrjö and Kalle Väisälä Fund let Ursa buy it outright. Then, over five years ending in spring 2015, Turun Ursa and the Salo association UrSalo rebuilt the place with European Union rural development money: the zenith tower got a new aluminium dome and a PlaneWave telescope went in beneath it. The skies at Ilttula are far darker than Turku's, though the lights of the old main road and a nearby radio mast still bleed into the low sky to the south. It remains what it was built to be - a small hill, a big lens, and people willing to stay up.

## Flight Context

60.42°N, 22.765°E, on a low hill in the Ilttula area of Paimio between the old and new Turku-Helsinki highways, some 35 km east of Turku. The observatory itself is tiny from the air - a white dome and a squat tower among fields and forest - so navigate instead by the E18 motorway and road 110 running east-west just north and south of it, and by Paimio Sanatorium about 6 km northwest. Suggested viewing altitude 1,500-2,500 ft AGL. Nearest airport is Turku (EFTU), roughly 33 km west-northwest; Kiikala gliding field (EFIK) lies about 28 km east near Salo, and Helsinki-Vantaa (EFHK) about 110 km east. Note the radio mast in the vicinity. Clear, dry winter nights give the best seeing here; midsummer offers effectively no astronomical darkness at this latitude.
