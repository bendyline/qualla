# German submarine U-479

A Nobel laureate posed at her periscope for the cameras; a year later she lay on the Baltic floor with fifty-one men aboard and not one ship sunk to her name.

**Location:** 59.3333, 23.1667

**Categories:** World War II, Shipwrecks, Submarines, Baltic Sea, Gulf of Finland, Maritime history

## Introduction

In June 1944, in the harbour at Arendal on Norway's southern coast, an eighty-four-year-old novelist bent to a submarine periscope while a German photographer recorded the moment. The man at the eyepiece was Knut Hamsun, who had won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1920 and who, the year before, had posted his Nobel medal to Joseph Goebbels as a gift. The boat under his feet was U-479. Neither writer nor submarine would finish the war with much to show for it. Hamsun would be arrested, examined, and fined. U-479 would sink nothing at all, and would end on the floor of the Gulf of Finland with fifty-one men inside her.

## A Boat Built for a Bigger Ocean

The Type VIIC was the workhorse of the U-boat arm, and U-479 was an unremarkable example of the breed: laid down at Deutsche Werke in Kiel on 19 November 1942 as yard number 310, launched on 14 August 1943, commissioned on 27 October. She displaced 769 tonnes surfaced and 871 submerged, ran 67.10 metres bow to stern, and could dive to 230 metres. Surfaced, with her two Germaniawerft diesels turning, she had the legs for 8,500 nautical miles at 10 knots — enough to cross the Atlantic and loiter. None of that mattered where she was sent. The Gulf of Finland is a narrow, shallow, brackish corridor, in many places too thin to hide a boat that could theoretically go seven times deeper than the seabed allowed. It was also, by 1944, one of the most densely mined bodies of water on earth.

## The Photograph

Hamsun was the most celebrated writer Norway had produced, and he had spent the occupation defending the men who governed it. He backed Vidkun Quisling's regime, wrote in support of Hitler, and in 1943 sent his Nobel medal to the Reich's propaganda minister. The visit to Arendal was arranged for exactly the image it produced: a great man of Norwegian letters looking through German optics, apparently pleased. Whatever Hamsun saw in the eyepiece, the photograph did its work and outlived the boat. After the war he was detained on 13 June 1945 and charged with treason. A psychiatric examination found his mental faculties permanently impaired, the criminal case collapsed, and in 1948 he was instead fined 325,000 kroner in a civil action — reduced by the supreme court from 575,000. He died at Grimstad in 1952, aged ninety-two, still argued over.

## Four Patrols, No Victories

U-479 trained with the 5th U-boat Flotilla before joining the 8th for operations, and her working life amounted to a handful of short runs through the eastern Baltic. She went from Kiel to Arendal, back to Kiel, then on to Helsinki. Her first patrol proper began there on 13 July 1944. Five days later, in Vyborg Bay, she put a torpedo into the Soviet submarine-chaser MO-304 and damaged her — fifty-six tons, a boat smaller than a coastal ferry, and the only mark U-479 ever made on the war. Two more sorties out of Helsinki in August passed without incident. A fourth ended at Danzig, now Gdańsk. In the same weeks, Finland signed an armistice with the Soviet Union and turned on its former German allies, and the sea U-479 had been operating in quietly closed around her.

## 27 November 1944

There was no battle. A Soviet mine detonated against the hull somewhere northwest of the Estonian island of Osmussaar, and fifty-one men died — most of them in their twenties, none of them recovered. The boat simply stopped reporting. For families in Germany, that meant a telegram with no place name attached and no body to bury, an ending repeated across the U-boat service so often that the phrase for it became routine: lost with all hands. Of the 1,181 U-boats that entered German service before the surrender, 785 were lost. U-479 had sunk nothing, saved nothing, and changed nothing. She took her crew down anyway.

## Ninety-Five Metres Down

Divers found her in 2009. The wreck lies in Estonian waters at roughly 95 metres, deep enough that the cold, dark, low-oxygen Baltic has kept her remarkably intact and far enough down that only technical divers reach her. Estonian and Finnish teams returned through the 2010s: a sonar survey by the Estonian vessel VLT-089 on 24 July 2018, filming by the Finnish research boat Deep Explorer on 8 and 9 September, and a further VLT-089 visit that October. The damage they documented is consistent with mining. A photogrammetric 3D model of the hull is now published online, so anyone can rotate the wreck on a screen. Estonia treats the site as a war grave. Nobody goes inside.

## Flight Context

The wreck lies at approximately 59.33°N, 23.17°E, in the mouth of the Gulf of Finland in Estonian waters, about 95 metres down — nothing whatever is visible from the air. What you can see is the geography that killed her: the low limestone slab of Osmussaar roughly 15 km to the southeast, and the Hanko peninsula with Russarö lighthouse some 55 km to the north. This narrow gate between Finland and Estonia was the choke point both sides mined. Best appreciated at 2,000–5,000 ft in clear air, when the shoals and the paleness of shallow water over the Baltic Klint show through. Nearby airports: EETN (Tallinn) roughly 95 km east-southeast, EFHN (Hanko) 55 km north, EEKA (Kärdla, Hiiumaa) 70 km southwest, EFHK (Helsinki-Vantaa) 110 km northeast. Sea fog and low stratus are common here from spring through early summer.
