# Nousiainen

A farming parish north of Turku where Finland's first bishops are believed to have resided, and where, legend says, the saint venerated as the country's national saint was laid to rest.

**Location:** 60.5986, 22.0833

**Categories:** municipalities, finland, southwest-finland, medieval-history, pilgrimage, churches, farmland

## Introduction

Two figures share the coat of arms of Nousiainen. One is Bishop Henry, venerated as Finland's national saint though never formally canonised. The other is Lalli, the peasant who, according to legend, killed him. It is a strange pair to find on a municipal shield, but this parish has a particular claim on the story. Legend holds that Lalli caught up with the bishop on the ice of Lake Köyliönjärvi, traditionally on 20 January 1156, and that Henry was then buried here, in a quiet country of fields and forest some 20 kilometres north of Turku. Today Nousiainen, Nousis in Swedish, is a mostly rural municipality in the inland of Finland Proper. For a time, though, it was where the first bishops of Finland resided.

## Where the Bishops First Sat

Nousiainen was an "ancient parish", a unit of social organisation, before Swedish rule. Its name comes from a pre-Christian Finnish personal name, Nousia, which some 10 to 20 men still carried in 2022, and the written record catches the place in 1232 as de Nousia and in 1234 as Nosis. The bishop's seat of Finland is believed to have stood here until 1229 or 1249, when it was moved to Koroinen, now part of Turku. Losing the bishop did not cost Nousiainen its importance. Because Henry was thought to have been buried here, the parish remained a pilgrimage site through the Middle Ages, right up to the Reformation. Whether there was ever a Henry to bury is another matter. Some historians challenge the legend to the point of calling it pure imagination, noting that there is no direct evidence of either Henry or the crusade his story belongs to. The parish shield keeps both characters anyway.

## An Empty Tomb in Brass

The heart of the parish is St. Henry's Church, built of grey stone and brick as the bishop's burial church and originally dedicated to Saint Henry and the Virgin Mary. The bones did not stay. His remains, or at least what were thought to be his bones, were moved to Turku around the end of the thirteenth century and laid in the cathedral there. What Nousiainen kept was a memorial. The church holds a cenotaph from 1429, an empty tomb, donated by Bishop Tavast. Its body is a black stone from Belgium that resembles limestone, and it is covered with brass plates engraved with scenes from Henry's life. It is a grand piece of work, and a replica of it stands in the National Museum of Finland. A parish that lost its saint kept his story instead, cut into metal.

## Fields, Forest and a Border Stone

Agriculture has always been Nousiainen's most significant industry, and the land shows it. In the south-west, near the centre and Highway 8, open fields dominate, broken by small woods, and the same pattern follows the little Hirvijoki river inland. Valpperintie runs along the river to the village of Valpperi, halfway across the municipality. Beyond Valpperi, to the north-east, forest takes over. The municipality reaches about 20 kilometres from the parish village to Kuhankuono, an old border stone in Kurjenrahka National Park, mentioned in a document from 1381 and once the point where eight municipalities met. The park, established in 1998, covers roughly 30 square kilometres, mostly bog, with primeval forests that in places have gone unmanaged for more than 150 years. On the shore of Savojärvi in the village of Saksala, the municipal recreational area of Rantapiha serves as one of the park's main starting points. Nummi, today's administrative seat, appears in the record in 1380 as Nummusby. Court sessions for Nousiainen, Masku and Santamala were held there, and by 1556 it was the parish's largest village.

## Motorway, Rail Line and Bike Path

Nousiainen lies on the E8 between Masku and Mynämäki, and stretches of the run north from Turku are motorway. The parish village sits 4 kilometres east of the junction. From there, road 2014 forms the municipality's spine, and its changing names trace the journey inland: Valpperintie at first, Saksalantie in Valpperi, Mynäjärventie in Saksala. Smaller roads carry on north-east toward Yläne. The station on the Turku–Uusikaupunki railway is now a community centre. The electrified line carries freight, but there is no passenger service: in 2026 regional planners were still seeking funding for an overhaul, estimated at about 180 million euros, that the authorities say passenger trains would need. The parish is easy to reach by bicycle, too. Cycleways and smaller roads roughly follow the E8 from Turku as regional biking route 3, and the route continues north to Mynämäki, where it meets the northern branch of the Archipelago Trail and EuroVelo 10, the long route around the Baltic Sea. On the gravel back roads traffic can move fast, so riders keep to the right. The parish even has a dish of its own. In the 1980s, sweetened potato casserole, imelet perunloora in the local dialect, was named the parish's traditional dish.

## Flight Context

Nousiainen's parish village lies at about 60.60°N, 22.08°E in Southwest Finland, roughly 10 nautical miles north of Turku and about 2 nautical miles east of the E8 (Highway 8) junction. From 2,000-3,000 ft, the E8 corridor running north from Turku, partly motorway, is the best line to follow; look east of it for the parish village and the open farmland that follows the small Hirvijoki river north-east toward Valpperi, after which forest closes in. Kurjenrahka National Park, mostly open bog, lies at the far inland end of the municipality, about 11 nautical miles from the parish village. Turku Airport (EFTU) is about 7 nautical miles to the south-east. Open fields make the river valley easy to read in summer; in winter, snow-covered fields stand out against the darker forest.
