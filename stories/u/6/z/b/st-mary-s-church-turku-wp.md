# St. Mary's Church, Turku

A greystone church on Turku's northern edge whose vaults are painted with Hanseatic sailing ships, and whose floor turned out to be covering two hundred coffins.

**Location:** 60.4734, 22.2932

**Categories:** Lutheran churches in Turku, Medieval stone churches in Finland, Gothic architecture in Finland

## Introduction

In 2025 the floor came up, and the parish met its own dead. Renovation work inside the medieval greystone church at Maaria, on the northern edge of Turku, exposed roughly two hundred wooden coffins about a metre and a half down, stacked three and four layers deep and remarkably well preserved. Most of them date to the early 1800s. Nobody had forgotten they were there in any legal sense, but nobody alive had seen them either, and across Southwest Finland almost every comparable church had long since been cleared out. Maaria had simply been left alone, with the result that a fairly ordinary country parish now holds one of the region's richest records of how its people were buried.

## Two Hundred Coffins, Three Layers Deep

Burial beneath a church floor was a matter of status and money before it became a matter of public health. Finland banned the practice in 1822, which is why the Maaria coffins cluster in the decades just before that date: these are among the last people to be laid inside the building rather than outside it. What survives with them is unusually informative. Clothing, jewellery and coins went into the ground alongside the dead, and those objects say plainly enough who had means and who did not, what people wore and what they thought worth keeping close. It is worth remembering what the layers actually are. Each one is a family that carried somebody up this hill, in a parish where the church was the centre of the year, and then went home.

## Ships in the Vaults

Look up, and the painting scheme is unlike anything else in the country. The medieval lime paintings on the walls and vaults were made by the same people who built the church, and their choice of subjects is idiosyncratic to the point of being unexplained. Five Hanseatic cogs, the fat-bellied trading ships that carried northern Europe's commerce, sail across the vaulting. There are crosses in the forms associated with the Virgin, and figures of saints, and there are jatulintarhat, the concentric labyrinth patterns that turn up carved and laid out in stone along the Finnish coast and whose original purpose is still argued over. The scholar Markus Hiekkanen, who has catalogued Finland's medieval stone churches more thoroughly than anyone, considers the Maaria imagery to stand in a class of its own.

## A Building With No Birth Certificate

No document records when the present church was built, and so the date has to be extracted from the fabric. Hiekkanen reads the mouldings and the profiles and puts the main construction in the 1440s, with the decorative gables added roughly fifty years later, a common enough sequence in Finnish greystone churches where money arrived in instalments. Wooden churches had stood on the site since the early 1200s, so the stone building represents a parish upgrading, not founding, its place of worship. The result is now listed among Finland's nationally significant built cultural environments, along with the parsonage beside it.

## Our Lady's Parish

The name is a translation left half-finished. The church was dedicated to the Virgin Mary, and in medieval Swedish the parish was Vårfrukyrko socken, Our Lady's church parish. Finnish speakers kept the Mary and dropped the rest, and Maaria is what remained. It became a municipality in its own right in 1868 and then dissolved by degrees into the city it bordered: the church village, Raunistula and the island of Hirvensalo were annexed to Turku in 1944, and the remainder was absorbed at the start of 1967, when 3,076 people were still on its books. The church now stands on Vanha Tampereentie in the Räntämäki district, a medieval building on a city street, with the suburb having grown out to meet it rather than the other way round.

## The Sculptor in the Churchyard

In the family grave outside lies Wäinö Aaltonen, born in Marttila in 1894 and dead in Helsinki in 1966, the sculptor who did more than anyone to give independent Finland a face it recognised. His 1925 figure of the runner Paavo Nurmi, cast and recast, became the country's most exported image of itself. He made the Aleksis Kivi memorial that stands before the National Theatre in Helsinki and the bronzes in the plenary chamber of Parliament House. A trip to Italy in 1923 pushed his naturalism toward something harder-edged and more cubist, and he worked stone directly at a time when few did. Turku has a museum devoted to him, designed in part by his architect son. He is buried in the churchyard of a church painted with ships.

## Flight Context

60.4734 N, 22.2932 E, in the Räntämäki district on the northern edge of Turku, beside Vanha Tampereentie. This is a small greystone church with a low profile, so navigate to it by the road and the surrounding graveyard rather than by silhouette; the Aura River valley lies a short distance to the east. Turku Airport (EFTU) is roughly 4 km to the northwest, which puts the church almost directly beneath arriving and departing traffic, so treat this as a low-level sightseeing target only with proper coordination. A viewing altitude of 1,000 to 2,000 feet AGL frames the church, parsonage and cemetery together. Autumn and winter overcast is common here; the best contrast comes on clear spring mornings when the greystone catches low sun.
