# Old Rauma: Six Hundred Wooden Houses, Still Occupied

The largest coherently preserved wooden town centre in the Nordic countries is not a museum - roughly 800 people sleep in its 600-odd houses every night, which is precisely the thing UNESCO listed in 1991.

**Location:** 61.1283, 21.5132

**Categories:** world-heritage, finland, architecture, wooden-towns, historic-districts, preservation

## Introduction

Finnish towns were built of wood, and wooden towns burn. Rauma's burned twice in the space of two generations - in 1640, and again in 1682, when fire took most of it. What makes Old Rauma the largest coherently preserved wooden town area in the Nordic countries is not that it escaped the flames but what the survivors did afterwards: they put their new houses back on the medieval street lines, board by board, in the idiom their grandparents had used. Some three and a half centuries later, roughly six hundred buildings stand across twenty-nine hectares of it, and about eight hundred people go to sleep inside them every night. UNESCO inscribed the district on the World Heritage List in 1991, under criteria (iv) and (v).

## What the Fire Left

The 1682 fire is the hinge of the whole story. Almost nothing wooden predates it, and the oldest houses now standing date to the 18th century, raised in the decades after the burn. What did survive was the plan - a street network laid out in the Middle Ages, narrow and gently crooked, with plot boundaries settled before anyone had drawn an accurate map of Finland. Rebuilding followed those lines instead of rationalising them, which is why walking Old Rauma feels less like touring an architectural collection than like moving through a medieval town wearing 18th- and 19th-century clothes.

The clothes repay attention. Rauma grew wealthy on sail during the 1800s, and its shipowners spent the money where neighbours would see it: carved eaves, panelled doors, pilasters and cornices, all executed in painted timber. It is Neo-Renaissance detailing rendered in a material that has to be scraped and repainted every generation - grandeur on a maintenance schedule.

## Two Buildings in Stone

In a district defined by timber, the exceptions stand out. The Church of the Holy Cross was built as the church of a Franciscan monastery, probably between 1515 and 1520, and it served that community until 1538, when the friary was dissolved in the Swedish Reformation. For roughly the next hundred years the building sat empty. Then fire destroyed Rauma's other 15th-century church, the Church of the Holy Trinity, in 1640, and the abandoned monastery church was pressed into service as the Lutheran parish church - the job it has held ever since. Medieval wall paintings still cover its choir.

The only other old stone building in Old Rauma is the Old Town Hall, completed in 1776 and now a museum. Two buildings in stone and six hundred in wood: that ratio is the entire point of the place.

## Kirsti and Marela

Two houses are open as museums, and read together they map the social distance inside a single seafaring town. Kirsti is a seaman's house of the 18th and 19th centuries - modest rooms, working furniture, the home of a family whose living depended on somebody else's ship coming back. Marela belonged to a shipowner. It dates to the 18th century behind a 19th-century facade, and it is furnished as it stood when a prosperous captain's family lived there at the height of Rauma's sailing era.

Walk between the two in ten minutes and the arithmetic of the age becomes plain. The tar and timber that paid for Marela's panelling were loaded and sailed by men who came home to houses like Kirsti's.

## Six Hundred Houses, Eight Hundred Neighbours

Old Rauma is not a heritage park. Around eight hundred people live inside the World Heritage boundary, most of the buildings are in private hands, and the district's twenty-nine hectares hold the ordinary furniture of a small Finnish town centre: shops, restaurants, cafés and artists' studios clustered around the Market Square, which still works as an actual market where you can buy fish. In summer the courtyards behind the houses open up as terraces and galleries. In February, once the tour buses have gone, they are back gardens again.

That continuity is not a bonus feature - it is the listed value. UNESCO's citation calls Old Rauma an outstanding example of an old Nordic town constructed in wood and a witness to the history of traditional settlement in northern Europe, a claim that quietly depends on people still settling there.

## The Price of the Plaque

Living inside a World Heritage Site comes with paperwork. The City of Rauma steers the district through a detailed local plan, and a specialist body is consulted on projects that require a permit - which in practice covers much of what an owner might reasonably want to do to a two-hundred-year-old timber house. Windows, cladding, roofing, paint colour, insulation: each is simultaneously a maintenance question and a heritage question, and the two answers do not always agree. A wooden building that is never altered rots. One altered carelessly stops being the thing that was listed.

That tension is permanent, and probably healthy. Old Rauma exists because six hundred houses were continuously repaired by people whose main concern was keeping the rain out, not because anyone managed to freeze the town in place. The plaque arrived long afterwards, and it is the residents who keep earning it.

## Flight Context

Old Rauma occupies the centre of Rauma at 61.1283°N, 21.5132°E, roughly a kilometre inland from the Gulf of Bothnia shoreline on Finland's west coast. From the air it reads as a distinct patch of fine-grained texture: small irregular blocks and narrow lanes, dense red and dark roofs, set inside the wider and more regular street grid of the modern town. The tower of the Church of the Holy Cross is the best single fix, and the port and paper mill complex on the coast to the west orients everything else. Best viewed at 1,500-3,000 feet in clear conditions, where the medieval plot pattern separates cleanly from the 20th-century blocks around it. Nearest airport is Pori (EFPO), about 49 km north by road; Turku (EFTU) lies about 94 km south; Helsinki-Vantaa (EFHK) is the international gateway. Long summer twilight gives excellent low-angle light; winter brings snow cover that erases roof colour and low cloud that often closes the coast.
