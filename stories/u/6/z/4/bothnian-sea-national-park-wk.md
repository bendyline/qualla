# Bothnian Sea National Park

A Finnish national park that is about 98 percent sea: a 160 km strip of skerries, grey seals, lighthouses from tsarist times to the 1950s, and a fortress island whose roads were laid by prisoners.

**Location:** 60.9790, 21.0986

**Categories:** National parks of Finland, Marine parks, Lighthouses, Birdwatching, Bothnian Sea, Satakunta

## Introduction

About 98 percent of Bothnian Sea National Park is water. The rest is a scatter of bays, islands, islets and skerries off the coast of southwest Finland, strung along a strip some 160 km long from north to south but only 10 to 20 km wide, and most of it can be reached only by boat. The park, Selkämeren kansallispuisto in Finnish, was established in 2011 with an unusual main purpose: to preserve the underwater nature of the northern Baltic Sea, along with some important nesting sites for seabirds. Arrive by boat and you find lighthouse islands, old fortresses and grey seals. Put on a mask, though, and you are looking at what the park was really made to protect.

## A Park Below the Waterline

Because most of the nature here is underwater, scuba diving is permitted, and snorkelling, swimming and boating are all part of a visit. Above the surface, the grey seal, Halichoerus grypus, is abundant. The islands themselves are rough going, with stony footpaths and wet rocks that turn truly slippery under a coat of algae, and both ticks and snakes are common. Some islands are surprisingly rich all the same. Isokari, reached by summer boats from Uusikaupunki, holds around 380 plant species, with herb-rich forests above rocky and cobble shores. Iso-Enskari ranges from sandy beaches to boulder-covered shores, with old coniferous forest, ponds and an ancient shoreline along its northeastern edge. Marked nature trails cross Iso-Enskari, Säppi and Kuuskajaskari, and the islands are small enough to explore without a map.

## A Lighthouse for a New Empire

Some islands carry remarkable maritime and naval history, and Isokari, along with Katanpää, is perhaps the most important. When Finland became an autonomous part of the Russian Empire in 1809, a new lighthouse was needed in Kustavi. Seafarers from Ostrobothnia had to be steered onto a new sea lane, so that they no longer travelled the old route through the Swedish archipelago and paid their pilotage fees to the Swedes. Pilot Major Gustaf Brodd designed the tower, inspired by the Tolbuhin lighthouse off Kronstadt. Completed in 1833, during the reign of Tsar Nicholas I, it was only the third lighthouse built in Finland and the tallest at the time, though its lamp was not lit until 1838. At 49.4 metres it is still Finland's second-highest lighthouse. Pilots had worked from Isokari since the late 18th century, and their house of 1858 is one of the best-preserved pilot buildings in the archipelago.

## Lights Up the Coast

Farther north, two more lighthouses can be reached by summer boat trips. On Säppi, a 150-hectare island of herb-rich woodland, construction of the present lighthouse began in the spring of 1873, and the light was lit that September. Designed by the Finnish architect Axel Hampus Dalström, the white tower with its red lantern stands 28.21 metres tall, and the Finnish Heritage Agency has designated the island a nationally significant built cultural environment. Kylmäpihlaja belongs to a different age. Standing on a small island in Rauma's outer archipelago, some 10 km from the mainland, it was completed in 1953 to replace a lightvessel and was the last manned lighthouse built in Finland: a square tower of 104 steps that doubled as a pilot station. After the pilots moved out in the 1990s, the City of Rauma turned it into a seasonal hotel, and today it offers rooms, a restaurant and even a chapel.

## Cobblestones and Cannon

Katanpää carries the heaviest history. Russian activity on the island began in the early 1910s, construction of a fort followed in 1915, and through the First World War it served as a fortress island guarding the sea lanes. Prisoners were brought in as manual labour from the eastern part of Russia, from Manchuria and from the Amur region, people taken half a continent from home, and they built kilometres of paved cobblestone roads across the island. Between 1930 and 1940 Katanpää also held a prison, with around 100 inmates at its peak working in a quarry. The last military personnel left in the mid-1990s, and the island passed to Metsähallitus, the state agency that manages Finland's national parks, in 1999. Visitors now find well-preserved Russian-era decorative wooden buildings, fortifications and concreted cannon batteries. Kuuskajaskari, reached by summer water bus from Rauma, has a gentler past: a grazing ground and fishermen's base since the 17th century, it later hosted a Finnish Defence Forces coastal and training battery, and four decommissioned coastal guns remain.

## Thousands on the Wing

Only the areas around Pori and Merikarvia can be reached by car, and the most accessible corner is Preiviikinlahti, a bay in Pori near Yyteri with easy nature trails and bird watching towers. Two of the towers, Leveäkari and Huhtala, and a viewing platform at Langoura, which the guide calls the Lankoori tower, stand inside the park, and the guide says Lankoori can be reached by wheelchair. Waterfowl nest here in notable numbers, but spring and autumn bring the real spectacle, when literally thousands of migrating seabirds pass through. The bay also shelters some rare aquatic and semi-aquatic plants. Everywhere else, getting around means a boat. Entrance, boating, hiking and tent camping are free, and picking berries and edible mushrooms is allowed, but nothing else may be taken, damaged or moved, and visitors carry out all their waste. The weather deserves respect too: the wind on the mainland may be light even while a gale blows at sea.

## Flight Context

The park's reference point is 60.98°N, 21.10°E, over open water off southwest Finland, but the park itself is a narrow strip about 160 km long from north to south and only 10-20 km wide, almost all of it sea. From 3,000-5,000 feet the lighthouses make the best visual fixes: Isokari's 49.4 m tower, Finland's second-highest, among the islands reached from Uusikaupunki; the square Kylmäpihlaja tower about 5 nautical miles off the mainland in Rauma's outer archipelago; and Säppi's white tower with its red lantern, about 7.5 nautical miles southwest of the Port of Pori. On the mainland, Preiviikinlahti bay near Yyteri in Pori marks the park's most accessible corner. Pori Airport (EFPO) lies about 1.5 nautical miles south of Pori's centre and about 35 nautical miles northeast of the park's reference point, and Turku Airport (EFTU), about 4 nautical miles north of Turku, lies some 44 nautical miles to the southeast. Stay well clear of bird-nesting islets at low level. Check marine forecasts before flying the coast: the wind can be light on the mainland while a gale blows offshore.
