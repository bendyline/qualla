# Pori Brewery

The Finnish brewery that ran longest on a single site, home of Karhu beer and of Finland's first canned lager, closed in 2009 to a boycott campaign and a symbolic funeral procession.

**Location:** 61.4894, 21.7894

**Categories:** breweries, industrial heritage, Finnish history, food and drink, company towns

## Introduction

On 24 September 2009, Pori held a funeral for a brewery. The procession was symbolic and entirely serious. The next day the lights went out at Porin Oluttehdas, closing 156 years of brewing on a single block near the Kokemäenjoki — no Finnish brewery had run continuously longer on one site. Six months earlier, when Carlsberg announced the closure, a citizens' movement had formed and declared a boycott of every Koff product until the decision was reversed. One tabloid ran its headline in capitals: KALJASOTA. Beer war. The decision was not reversed.

## The Date on the Label

The 1853 stamped on Pori's bottles to the very end was, strictly speaking, borrowed. Brewing on that block began in 1854, when Anton Björnberg, K. J. Lönegren and Isaak Karström — the same three men who had founded Pori's engineering works five years earlier — started Bayerska Bryggeriet, the Bavarian Brewery. The 1853 belonged to a competitor across town, the brewery of the merchant Gustav Adolf Bäckman. The two combined in 1898 under the name Bäckmans Ölbryggeri Ab, and Bäckman's earlier founding year came with the deal. It stayed on the labels for the next 111 years, which is either careless bookkeeping or very good branding, depending on how much you care about a single digit.

## Salmelin, Pyynikki, and Forty Years of Aatos Leino

Ownership wandered. In the 1920s the brewery passed to Consul Sulo Salmelin of Tampere, who already owned the Pyynikki brewery there, and in 1928 he folded Bäckman's operation together with Satakunnan Mallasjuoma into a new company, Porin Oluttehdas Osakeyhtiö. From 1940 to 1959 Pori belonged to Pyynikki outright, and along the way, in 1941, it took over the Rauma brewery of 1868 and held it until Rauma closed in 1969. Independence returned in 1959 when Rosa Salmelin handed Pyynikki to her son M. Erik Salmelin, who cut Pori loose as its own company again. The man who then ran it, Aatos Leino, had come through the door in 1929 as a salesman and clerk, become manager in 1934, and retired only in 1969 after forty years. Careers like that are how a brewery stops being a town's employer and becomes its identity.

## The Bear

Karhu was born in Pori in the 1920s and very nearly died young: production stopped in the early 1930s and did not resume until 1958, and then only in the weakest legal class of beer. A medium-strength Karhu followed in 1961, the same year Porin Oluttehdas became the first Finnish brewery to put beer in cans. For decades the bear stayed local, a Satakunta drink you had to come here to have, and only in 1993 did Sinebrychoff take it nationwide. It worked spectacularly. By 2015, Karhu A was the best-selling beer brand by volume in Alko, Finland's state monopoly shops, made to a recipe developed by Ilkka Vanhatalo, the Pori brewery's own production manager and brewmaster. In its final years roughly 80 per cent of everything Pori made was Karhu.

## The Argument Nobody Won

Sinebrychoff bought the brewery in 1972 and Carlsberg bought Sinebrychoff in 1997, and for a while none of that changed much on the ground: about 40 million litres a year, roughly a tenth of Sinebrychoff's total output. Then Karhu's bottling shifted to Kerava, and in March 2009 the closure was announced. The city of Pori objected that its cultural history was being dismantled despite the substantial investments the city had made. The boycott campaign made national news, and the arguments about a company town losing the thing it was known for were rehearsed in every Finnish paper. None of it moved the decision. Brewing stopped that September, the funeral procession walked, and Karhu — a beer made in Pori since before the Winter War — has come out of Kerava, outside Helsinki, ever since.

## What Still Brews There

The tanks and process equipment were stripped out and sold on, and the plot was zoned for housing. The block did not go quiet, though. In July 2013 Pori Jazz erected a temporary stage called Otava Factory on the old warehouse ground and put concerts where the barrels had been — a neat return of a favour, since Porin Olut had been among the sponsors funding the very first Pori Jazz back in 1966. And beer never entirely stopped being made here. A microbrewery founded in 2008 as Karhupanimo set up shop in the brewery's former power plant building; it now belongs to Panimoravintola Beer Hunter's and trades as Ravintola Panimo. On a block that has been brewing since the 1850s, somebody is still mashing in.

## Flight Context

61.4894N, 21.7894E, in central Pori on the south bank of the Kokemäenjoki, one block from the old town and adjacent to the former Hotel Otava. From the air the site reads as a cluster of nineteenth- and twentieth-century industrial brick between the river and the city grid, with the Kirjurinluoto delta islands immediately northwest. Pori Airport (EFPO) lies about 3 kilometres south, so this ground passes directly beneath the approach to runway 30. Tampere-Pirkkala (EFTP) is roughly 100 kilometres east-southeast and Turku (EFTU) about 115 kilometres south-southeast. Best viewed from 1,000 to 2,000 feet with the river on your left running out to the Bothnian Sea. Watch for elevated traffic during the Pori Jazz week in July.
