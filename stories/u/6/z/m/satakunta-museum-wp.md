# Satakunta Museum

Sixty donated objects in a schoolteacher's house in 1888 grew into a regional museum holding more than 80,000 artefacts and 300,000 photographs of Satakunta.

**Location:** 61.4899, 21.7912

**Categories:** Buildings and structures in Pori, City museums, History museums in Finland, Museums in Satakunta, Museums established in 1888, 1888 establishments in Finland

## Introduction

It began with about sixty things in a teacher's house. In 1888 the Pori Finnish Society decided the region needed somewhere to keep its own past, put out a call for donations, and by the end of the first season had gathered roughly sixty antiquities. There was no building. The objects went to the home of a schoolmaster named Matti Kauppinen, who found room for them among his own furniture. Within a year the count had passed six hundred. The Satakunta Museum has been accumulating ever since, and the arithmetic of that first year - sixty, then six hundred - turns out to describe the whole institution. It holds over 80,000 artefacts today, and an archive of more than 300,000 photographs, maps and architectural drawings.

## Kauppinen's Thirty-Four Years

A collection kept in a private house needs an institution around it, and that took time. The Finnish Senate formally recognised the museum in 1893. The city of Pori assumed ownership in January 1899. Along the way the objects moved through three rented storage spaces before landing in 1903 in a building called Iltaman talo, which stood where the Pori city library stands now. Kauppinen himself became director in 1896 and stayed until 1930, and under him the collection grew from hundreds to somewhere near thirteen thousand objects, most of which he acquired personally. He did not do it alone. Aleksander Winter contributed roughly four thousand items gathered on fieldwork across the region - farm tools, textiles, household objects, the ordinary equipment of Satakunta life that nobody else thought worth saving because it was still in use.

## A Building Made for It

The museum finally got premises designed for the job in the 1970s. The foundation stone was laid in 1971 and the building, by the architect Olaf Kuttner, was completed in 1973 near the city centre on the bank of the Kokemäenjoki. It gives exhibitions three floors plus space for temporary shows, and it is unmistakably of its decade - concrete, horizontal, unromantic. Pori has a good deal of that period in its centre, enough that the city's 1960s and 1970s building has drawn comparisons to East German architecture, usually not as a compliment. The museum earns its box. The permanent exhibition Elon merkkejä, Traces of Life, opened in 2018 to replace a display that had run since 2002, and the following spring the museum was named Finland's Museum of the Year.

## A Museum with Satellites

The Satakunta Museum is not one building but a constellation. Luontotalo Arkki, a natural history house, opened in 1999. The Rosenlew Museum followed in 2006. Rakennuskulttuuritalo Toivo works on the region's building heritage. Korsmanin talo preserves a working-class home as it stood in the 1950s, which is a harder and more valuable thing to keep than a manor house - grand interiors get saved as a matter of course, while the rooms where most people actually lived get modernised out of existence. Out at Reposaari, the old harbour island west of the city, the museum keeps the tug Santtu afloat. In 2020 the institution was designated Satakunta's regional responsibility museum, and Johanna Jakomaa took over as director the same year.

## The Rosenlew Papers

The Rosenlew Museum is worth its own paragraph. In 2003 the museum received the historical collection of Rosenlew, the industrial group that grew out of a Pori merchant house into one of Finland's substantial manufacturing concerns, and in 2006 it opened the collection to the public in a former crown warehouse at Aittaluoto. There is a long shadow behind that name. When Pori burned on 22 May 1852 the fire started in a house on Kuninkaankatu belonging to the heirs of the merchant Carl Fredrik Rosenlew, who had died earlier that year. Three quarters of the city went with it. That the family name later became an industrial dynasty, and that its papers are now the property of the museum that keeps the city's memory, is the kind of loop a regional collection tends to close if it lasts long enough.

## Why It Matters

Regional museums are easy to walk past. They rarely hold anything a national gallery would fight over, and their subject is by definition local. But Satakunta is a province of forests, river valleys and a hard Bothnian coastline, with a farming and seafaring population whose material life left almost no written record. Somebody had to decide that a fish trap, a loom, a photograph of a harbour crew was worth a catalogue number. In 1888 a group of Pori men decided exactly that, and a schoolteacher gave up part of his house to prove it. Everything in the collection today descends from that judgement.

## Flight Context

Satakunta Museum stands at 61.490N, 21.791E on the Kokemäenjoki in central Pori, western Finland. The river is the navigation feature: it flows west from the city and fans into the largest river delta in the Nordic countries before meeting the Gulf of Bothnia about 10 km away, a mosaic of channels, reed beds and low islands that is also a major bird area. Pori Airport (EFPO) is roughly 3 km northwest of the museum. Tampere-Pirkkala (EFTP) lies about 100 km east-southeast and Turku (EFTU) about 130 km south. Fly 1,500-3,000 feet for the city grid with its wide crossing esplanade parks; the harbour island of Reposaari, where the museum's tug is kept, is about 25 km west at the seaward end of the delta. Expect coastal stratus and haze in spring and autumn and heavy snow showers off the sea in winter.
