# Kirjurinluoto

An island the Kokemäenjoki built after Pori was founded, which went from tar-barrel yard to English landscape park to a delta that holds eighty thousand people for a jazz festival.

**Location:** 61.5139, 21.7588

**Categories:** parks, islands, music festivals, urban parks, Finnish history

## Introduction

When Pori was founded in 1558 there was nothing here to stand on. Shallow open sea washed over the ground where eighty thousand people now face a stage. The islets of the Kokemäenjoki delta are recent arrivals, assembled out of land uplift and river silt within a few human lifetimes: Kirjurinluoto is absent from the city's map of 1696, had risen enough by the end of that century to pasture cattle, and sits there in full on the map of 1852. Nearly everything that has happened on this island has happened on ground the river made after the fact.

## A Clerk's Share of the River

The name is a salary. In the 1800s the islet formed part of the payment in kind owed to the city notary — the kirjuri, the clerk — and it has carried his job title ever since; the Swedish name, Skrivarholmen, says exactly the same thing. Neighbouring islets were parcelled out the same way, which is why Pori's delta reads like a municipal payroll: Pormestarinluoto for the mayor, Raatimiehenluoto for the alderman. Kirjurinluoto proper covers only 25 hectares, and it has silted onto the larger Raatimiehenluoto and Hanhiluoto, so most of what people now call Kirjurinluoto is, strictly speaking, somewhere else.

## From Tar Barrels to Bandstands

Through the middle of the 1800s the islet did unglamorous work, stacked with sawn timber and tar barrels waiting to leave from Pori's river harbour. Then the Kokemäenjoki shallowed, dredging turned expensive, the harbour lost its purpose, and the town's prosperous families found they had an empty green island in the middle of the water. In 1887 the pharmacist Robert Junnelius proposed making it a proper park. The gardener Anders Fredrik Rydberg drew up plans in 1891, paths and a restaurant site included, and the English-style park was finished in 1897. A Jugend restaurant by Arthur Fagerholm and Torkel Nordman followed in 1910, a song stage in 1921, and a summer theatre in the same decade. In 1924 came a swimming establishment with pools fenced off from the river in timber, complete with piers, stands and diving towers, which lasted until Pori's outdoor pool opened in 1957.

## Boats, Ferries, and Taavi's Bridge

Getting there was the problem for most of that history. For decades the only crossing was by boat. A motor ferry ran from 1933 to 1968, and army engineers then laid a pontoon bridge from the north bank. The first fixed link, Raumansilta, opened in 1973 — a park nearly a century old finally reached by road. Pormestarinsilta replaced the pontoon crossing with a permanent bridge in 2001. The most characteristic solution is still in service: the Taavi bridge, a summer pontoon walkway from Eteläranta since 1994, named for Taavi Koskinen, who used to drive the ferry. It is towed away before the ice arrives and floated back each April once the river clears.

## Eighty Thousand on New Ground

Pori Jazz brought its concerts to the island early, and in 1967 the city's music board declined the festival a grant but let it use Kirjurinluoto free of charge — arguably the best deal in Finnish festival history. Audiences then grew until the island had to be rebuilt around them. Lokki, the Seagull, roofed to resemble a bird's wings, was designed by the Pori architect Michael Kuttner and inaugurated on 12 July 1989 where a 1970s stage had stood; in 1998 more than 25,000 people packed in front of it for a bill that included Phil Collins. By the early 2000s even that was too small, and the Kirjurinluoto Arena rose on Raatimiehenluoto with room for around 80,000. Metallica, Iron Maiden and Kylie Minogue have all played the delta, and Sonisphere came in 2009 and 2010. Lokki, refurbished in 2010, now carries the Porispere rock festival and the Pori Jazz Kids Festival.

## The Halls That Never Rose

Twice the city has tried to put a concert hall on this island, and twice nothing came of it. In the early 1980s a competition for a music and congress building — which would have ranked among Finland's largest — was won by the Danish practice Kjær & Richter, and the ground stayed empty. In January 2013 a 2,500-seat concert hall was announced for completion the following year; the ground stayed empty again. What exists instead is stubbornly ordinary and thoroughly used: the free Pelle Hermanni playground, open all year, a minigolf course running since 1952 and among the oldest in the country, a disc golf course, a beach, ponds dug in the 1930s and 1960s, and summer enclosures of donkeys, goats, sheep and rabbits. All of it belongs to the Pori National Urban Park. The grandest thing here remains the river, which made the ground everything else stands on.

## Flight Context

61.5139N, 21.7588E, in the Kokemäenjoki delta immediately northwest of central Pori. From the air the island group is unmistakable: a braided river splitting around green islets, with the Kirjurinluoto Arena's open concert bowl and the wing-shaped roof of the Lokki stage both visible on the ground. Pori Airport (EFPO) lies about 6 kilometres south-southeast, Tampere-Pirkkala (EFTP) roughly 105 kilometres east-southeast and Turku (EFTU) about 120 kilometres south-southeast. Best viewed from 1,500 to 2,500 feet, which keeps the whole delta and the city centre in one frame. Expect heavy VFR and charter traffic into EFPO during the Pori Jazz week in July, and river fog on calm mornings in autumn.
