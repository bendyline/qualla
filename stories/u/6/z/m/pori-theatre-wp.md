# Pori Theatre

Seven painted muses look down on a Pori auditorium whose proscenium copies a Stockholm opera house, and whose company was born from a shotgun marriage between a workers' theatre and a bourgeois one.

**Location:** 61.4894, 21.7922

**Categories:** Theatres in Finland, Buildings and structures in Pori, Theatres completed in 1884, 1931 establishments in Finland

## Introduction

Look up before the lights go down. Across the ceiling of Pori Theatre, the Helsinki decorative painter Salomo Wuorio spread seven allegorical muses - the classical machinery of European art, delivered to a Finnish timber and tar port on the Gulf of Bothnia. Then look forward. The proscenium arch framing the stage was modelled on the Royal Opera House that Stockholm opened in 1773, with relief portraits of Shakespeare and Mozart set on either side of the opening. A provincial town of a few thousand people finished this building on 29 December 1884 and furnished it with the iconography of a capital. The ambition was not subtle, and it was not misplaced.

## A Borrowed Arch and Seven Muses

The Swedish architect Johan Erik Stenberg designed the house, and it remains among the most admired theatre interiors in Finland. The auditorium originally seated 450. It holds 313 now, 79 of them on the balcony that runs around the room, the reduction being what happens when nineteenth-century seat widths meet twentieth-century bodies and fire regulations. In 1974 an extension added a studio stage, the Pieni nayttamo, seating about a hundred - a room for work that would drown in the main hall. The building has been overhauled repeatedly: restorations in 1972 and 1979, facade work in the 2000s, and a thorough replacement of technical systems in 2017. What Stenberg drew has survived all of it. The gilt, the painted ceiling and the arch borrowed from Gustav III's Stockholm are still doing their job.

## Two Theatres, One Roof

Pori's theatrical life did not begin in Finnish. The first Swedish-language troupes formed here in the 1840s, when Swedish was still the language of commerce, administration and polite society across the country. Finnish-language theatre followed, and it followed from below. Porin Tyovaen Teatteri - the Pori Workers' Theatre - was founded in 1909 and became the town's first professional company. Porin Nayttamo, a separate and more respectable stage, began in 1925. The two spent years circling one another before Kaarlo Saarenheimo brokered a merger in 1930, and the combined Pori Theatre began operating in 1931 under its first director, Pekka Niskanen. Finland had been independent for barely a decade. Putting the workers' theatre and the bourgeois one into a single building was a small rehearsal for a larger national argument about who culture belonged to.

## Names on the Programme

The company has been a training ground for people who went on to matter elsewhere. Matti Kassila directed here before becoming one of the defining figures of Finnish cinema. Ilmari Unho worked here too, another director who moved between stage and screen. Among the actors were Tommi Rinne, Saara Ranin, Leo Lastumaki and Sylvi Salonen, and Helge Ranin directed. That flow of talent through a regional house is easy to underestimate. Finland is a country of five and a half million people with a dense network of professional theatres in towns most outsiders could not place on a map, and Pori's is one of the reasons that network holds. Christian Lindroos has led the theatre as artistic director since 2021.

## When the Horns Come In

Every July the building changes character. Pori Jazz started in 1966 on the river island of Kirjurinluoto as a two-day event drawing about 1,500 people, and it has run every year since, growing into one of Europe's oldest and largest festivals of its kind - nine days, more than a hundred concerts across eleven venues, somewhere between 120,000 and 160,000 visitors. Pori Theatre has been one of its concert venues since 1967, the second year. Miles Davis played the festival. So did James Brown, Betty Carter and Sting. For one week a year the muses on the ceiling preside over something Wuorio could not have imagined when he painted them, in a room built for unamplified nineteenth-century declamation.

## The Doctor in Bronze

Outside stands a statue of Hj. Nortamo - Frans Hjalmar Nortamo, born in Rauma in 1860, dead in Pori in 1931. He was a physician by trade and a writer by compulsion, and his achievement was linguistic. His Raumlaissi jaarituksi, or Yarns from Rauma, collected in book form in 1920, were the first Finnish-language texts genuinely written in a non-standard dialect. The Rauma dialect he used is thick with the vocabulary of a seafaring town, salted with Swedish, English and German, and Nortamo's stories are much of the reason anyone still knows how it sounded. He belongs by the theatre door. Both the man and the building were arguments that a language spoken by a small number of people on a cold coast deserved to be taken seriously.

## Flight Context

Pori Theatre stands at 61.489N, 21.792E in the old stone quarter of central Pori, western Finland, a few hundred metres from the Kokemaenjoki. The river is the primary visual reference from the air, running west to the Gulf of Bothnia where it fans into the largest delta in the Nordic countries. Pori Airport (EFPO) is about 3 km northwest of the centre; Tampere-Pirkkala (EFTP) lies roughly 100 km east-southeast and Turku (EFTU) about 130 km south. Fly 1,500-2,500 feet to pick out the theatre block, which sits inside the grid of wide esplanade parks laid out after the 1852 fire. In mid-July the Kirjurinluoto festival grounds on the river island north of the centre are conspicuous. Expect coastal low cloud in spring and autumn and very short daylight in December and January.
