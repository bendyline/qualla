# Pori Old Town Hall

Carl Ludvig Engel died before his Pori town hall was finished; eleven years later it stood almost alone as three quarters of the city burned around it.

**Location:** 61.4896, 21.7938

**Categories:** City and town halls in Finland, Buildings and structures in Pori, Carl Ludvig Engel buildings, Government buildings completed in 1841, Neoclassical architecture in Finland

## Introduction

Two Latin words sit on the pediment: CURIA ARCTOPOLIS. The first means court or council house. The second is a piece of learned showing-off - arktos is Greek for bear, polis for city, so Arctopolis is Bear City, a scholar's rendering of the Swedish name Bjorneborg. The bear had been on the town's seal since the seventeenth century, and when Pori built itself a proper town hall in 1841 it put the bear's name up in a dead language where everyone could see it. That inscription has now watched the city burn down and be rebuilt around it. The building beneath it is the oldest important thing in central Pori, and it is standing largely by luck.

## An Architect Who Never Saw It

The design came from Carl Ludvig Engel, the Berlin-born architect who did more than anyone to give Finland a public face. Born in Charlottenburg in 1778 and trained at Berlin's Bauakademie, Engel drifted through Tallinn and St Petersburg before meeting Johan Albrecht Ehrenstrom in Turku in 1815 and being handed the reconstruction of Helsinki as capital of the new Grand Duchy. Senate Square is his: the Senate House of 1818, the university's main building of 1828, the cathedral begun in 1818. From 1824 he ran the Intendant's Office and shaped public buildings across the whole country. Pori's town hall is a late work in that programme, neoclassical and severe, and Engel never laid eyes on the finished thing. He died in Helsinki on 14 May 1840. The building was completed the following year.

## Saturday Afternoon, 22 May 1852

The fire started around three in the afternoon in a house on Kuninkaankatu belonging to the heirs of a merchant, Carl Fredrik Rosenlew, who had died earlier that year. Nobody ever settled the cause. It may have been careless fire under a washing vat; it may have been a bread oven throwing sparks into a roof structure that smouldered unnoticed for hours. What is certain is that the wind was near gale force and the town was built of wood under tarred shingle. Twenty-four stone buildings and 991 wooden ones burned - about three quarters of Pori. Only two people died, both apparently of medical collapse while fighting the flames. But roughly 4,700 of the town's 6,300 residents lost their homes in an afternoon, and of the thousand-odd destroyed buildings only 169 carried insurance. The town hall survived. Its tower did not.

## The Pit City

What followed the fire is the part that rarely makes the guidebooks. Those who could rebuild, rebuilt. Those who could not afford either construction or rent walked southeast to the Paarnainen heath and dug. They cut dwellings into the sandy ground and lived in them - a settlement Pori called the kuoppakaupunki, the pit city. It was not a brief emergency measure. People lived there for something like forty years, into the 1890s, within sight of the handsome new grid rising behind them. Georg Theodor Chiewitz drew that grid in 1852 and Emperor Nicholas I approved it: four districts separated by esplanade parks far wider than any street needed to be, an elegant plan whose actual purpose was to stop the next fire jumping from one quarter to the next. The parks are still there, and they are the reason central Pori feels so open.

## The Tower That Came Back

The town hall was patched up almost immediately, close to Engel's original drawings, but it stood without its tower for four decades. In 1891 Johan Erik Stenberg - the Swedish architect who had built Pori's theatre a few streets away seven years earlier - designed a replacement fire watch tower. The bell inside it is older than the tower it hangs in: cast by Juho Yli-Konni in 1853, the year after the fire, and still in use. There is something fitting about a town that nearly burned to nothing keeping a watchtower and a bell on its council house long after the practical need had passed.

## Jail, Jazz and a Bronze Duke

The building served as Pori's city hall until 1961, when the administration outgrew it and moved on. It has kept working since. The basement that once held the city jail is now a restaurant, Raatihuoneen Kellari, and the offices of Pori Jazz operate from the building - a festival with Miles Davis and James Brown in its history, run out of a nineteenth-century courthouse. Outside stands Emil Cedercreutz's 1938 bronze of the Satakunta bear, which students traditionally cap on May Day. In 2008 a statue of Duke John was unveiled in front of the town hall, 450 years after he founded Pori in 1558. He went on to become John III of Sweden, was crowned in July 1569, and died in Stockholm in 1592. In bronze he watches a street plan he would not recognise, in front of a building carrying his city's name in Latin.

## Flight Context

Pori Old Town Hall stands at 61.490N, 21.794E in the centre of Pori, western Finland, just south of the Kokemaenjoki. From the air the 1852 Chiewitz plan is the giveaway: a regular grid cut by two unusually wide esplanade boulevards crossing at right angles, unlike anything in the surrounding countryside. The river runs west and spreads into the largest delta in the Nordic countries before reaching the Gulf of Bothnia roughly 10 km away. Pori Airport (EFPO) is about 3 km northwest of the town hall. Tampere-Pirkkala (EFTP) lies about 100 km east-southeast, Turku (EFTU) about 130 km south. Best viewing 1,200-2,500 feet; the clock and watch tower makes the building identifiable within the block. Sea fog and low stratus off the Bothnian Sea are frequent in spring and autumn.
