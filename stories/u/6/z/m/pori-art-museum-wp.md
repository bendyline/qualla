# Pori Art Museum

A harbour weighing house on the Kokemaenjoki now holds Yoko Ono and Gallen-Kallela, because an Ahlstrom heiress decided Pori should take modern art seriously.

**Location:** 61.4901, 21.7942

**Categories:** Buildings and structures in Pori, Modern art museums, Art museums and galleries in Finland, Art museums and galleries established in 1979, 1979 establishments in Finland, Museums in Satakunta

## Introduction

For its first hundred years the building had exactly one job: weighing things. Tar barrels, sawn timber, sacks of grain - cargo moving through Pori's harbour crossed the scales inside this long hall on the bank of the Kokemaenjoki, and a clerk wrote the number down. C. J. von Heideken drew it between 1858 and 1860 as a packing and weighing house, a piece of commercial plumbing for a Finnish port town that had just burned to the ground and was rebuilding in a hurry. It now holds Yoko Ono, Dennis Oppenheim and Leonhard Lapin. That gap between what the building was made for and what it contains is no accident. It is what happens when an heiress decides that Pori, of all places, is going to take modern art seriously.

## Built to Weigh Tar

Von Heideken was Swedish, born in Stockholm in 1832, and he arrived in Pori as city architect in 1853 - one year after the great fire that took roughly three quarters of the town. He stayed five years and left a handful of buildings behind him, the central church among them, and this one. The weighing house was pure function: heavy walls, a long plan, high windows for light, floors strong enough for freight. In 1897 Gustaf Nystrom enlarged it, adding two-storey towers at either end. Nystrom had recently become Finland's first professor of architecture, and that fact says something about how much the port mattered to Pori. A rising national figure was worth hiring to extend a customs shed. Eventually the scales fell silent and the cargo went elsewhere. The building stayed where it was.

## The Ahlstrom Daughter

Maire Gullichsen was born at Noormarkku in 1907, granddaughter of Antti Ahlstrom, one of the wealthiest men in nineteenth-century Finland, and daughter of the industrialist Walter Ahlstrom. She could have spent that inheritance quietly. She spent it on the avant-garde instead. In 1935 she co-founded Artek with Alvar and Aino Aalto and the art historian Nils-Gustav Hahl, the company that carried Finnish modernist furniture out into the world, and in the same year she helped establish the Free Art School alongside Ethel Thesleff, Irja Noponen and Saara Castren - an open-access institution that did more for the breakthrough of abstract painting in Finland than any academy managed. Between 1937 and 1939 she commissioned Villa Mairea from the Aaltos and worked on its interiors herself. The Maire Gullichsen Art Foundation followed in 1971. She died at Noormarkku in 1990, in the town where she had been born.

## Her Son Drew the Rooms

The museum was established in 1979, largely on Gullichsen's insistence, and the conversion of the old weighing house went to her son Kristian, by then one of Finland's most respected architects. He worked the interiors through 1979 to 1981, when the museum properly opened. It is a restrained piece of design. The freight hall's proportions survive intact, and the art sits inside them without picking a fight. Roughly 800 of the building's 2,800 square metres are exhibition space. Between 1997 and 2000 the museum gained an underground extension of some 350 square metres, tucked beneath the neighbouring courtyard and reached along a glass corridor - a way of growing without laying a finger on the facade. Kristian Gullichsen died in 2021, thirty-one years after his mother.

## What Hangs There

Two collections anchor the place. The Maire Gullichsen Art Foundation holding runs to around 545 works and concentrates on Finnish art from the late nineteenth century through to the 1980s. The Pori municipal collection is larger, at over two thousand works, and leans twentieth century, with older Finnish painters alongside: Victor Westerholm, and Akseli Gallen-Kallela, whose most famous Pori work is not in the museum at all but out at Kappara cemetery, where he frescoed the mausoleum Fritz Arthur Juselius built for his daughter Sigrid, dead of tuberculosis at eleven. For a city of 83,000 the international names come as a jolt - Jose Bedia Valdes, Ian McKeever, the Estonian Leonhard Lapin, Yan Pei-Ming, Georges Rousse, Yoko Ono, Dennis Oppenheim. Behind the galleries sit a library of some ten thousand titles and an archive holding more than 80,000 photographs.

## Smoke, and After

In the summer of 2021 fire broke out in the building. Nobody has established who or what started it. Repairs to a listed nineteenth-century structure holding a public collection are never quick to arrange, and it took until the beginning of 2023 for the state to award 675,000 euros toward putting the damage right. The museum came through it. It is open again on the riverbank, running changing exhibitions alongside the collections, with the Poriginal gallery operating at the same address. Pori has 83,000 people and a contemporary art museum with Yoko Ono in the storerooms, which is not the ratio anyone would predict. The scales are long gone. The building found better work.

## Flight Context

Pori Art Museum sits at 61.490N, 21.794E on the south bank of the Kokemaenjoki in central Pori, western Finland. The river and its broad delta - the largest in the Nordic countries - are the unmistakable navigation feature, splitting into channels as it reaches the Gulf of Bothnia about 10 km downstream. Pori Airport (EFPO) lies roughly 3 km northwest of the city centre. Tampere-Pirkkala (EFTP) is about 100 km east-southeast and Turku (EFTU) about 130 km south. Best viewing is 1,500-3,000 feet, where the 1852 grid plan with its wide esplanade parks reads clearly against the older riverfront. Coastal haze and low stratus are common in spring and autumn; winter brings short daylight and frequent snow showers off the Bothnian Sea.
