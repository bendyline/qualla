# Pori Jazz: From 600 People to a Third of a Million

A handful of local jazz fans staged a two-day festival on a Finnish park island in July 1966 and drew 600 people to the main concert. By 2019 the festival's own count was 349,518 visitors.

**Location:** 61.4961, 21.7807

**Categories:** music-festivals, finland, jazz, pori, culture, events

## Introduction

Six hundred people turned up to the main concert. That was 1966, on a park island in the delta of the Kokemäenjoki river, and the organisers were a group of local young jazz enthusiasts in a west-coast Finnish industrial city with no particular musical reputation. In 2019, by the festival's own count, Pori Jazz drew 349,518 visitors. Somewhere between those two numbers sits one of the more improbable success stories in European music: a festival in a town of a bit over eighty thousand that has hosted Miles Davis, Ray Charles, Stevie Wonder, Björk and Kanye West, and has run every summer since 1966 except for two it did not choose to miss.

## The Enthusiasts

Pori Jazz was not conceived by a promoter or a tourist board. It came out of a local circle of young jazz fans who put on a two-day event in July 1966 and organised themselves as a non-profit association, Pori Jazz 66 ry - the name still on the paperwork. Among the founders was Jyrki Kangas, born in Pori in 1944, who had learned double bass at the conservatoire and went on to serve as the festival's artistic director until 2009. Finnish jazz circles came to call him simply Mr Pori Jazz.

That origin matters to how the thing feels. A festival built by fans rather than investors tends to programme for the people already in the room, and the roll of names Pori Jazz has drawn to a river island in Satakunta reads like a list somebody made out of genuine appetite: Cab Calloway, Benny Goodman, Dizzy Gillespie, Oscar Peterson, Muddy Waters, Bo Diddley.

## How Two Days Became Nine

The arithmetic of the growth is unusually well documented. Between 1975 and 1984 the festival settled into a four-day format. From 1985 it stretched to nine days, pulling 50,000 to 60,000 people. Attendance crossed 100,000 in the early 1990s and has stayed in six figures since; Finnish festival listings now put a typical year around 200,000, with the 2019 peak of 349,518 standing as the high-water mark.

The programming widened as the crowds did. The first festival was built on acoustic jazz, and then electric jazz arrived, and blues, soul, funk, hip-hop, Afro-Cuban and Brazilian music, world music and, on occasion, straightforward pop. What never changed was the name. A festival called Pori Jazz that books Kylie Minogue is either a contradiction or a definition of what jazz festivals became, and Pori has never seemed troubled by which.

## The Island

Kirjurinluoto had been a stage long before anyone played bebop on it. Wealthy citizens of Pori used the island as a recreation ground in the latter half of the 19th century; its English landscape garden was completed in 1897 to a design by the Swedish garden designer Anders Fredrik Rydberg, its Art Nouveau restaurant went up in 1910 to drawings by the architect Torkel Nordman, and the first stage for music and theatre followed in 1921. There was no road bridge until 1973.

The permanent stage known as Lokki - the Seagull - was completed in 1974. The purpose-built Kirjurinluoto Arena came in 2001: five hectares of amphitheatre with another twenty-two hectares for camping and parking, an audience capacity reaching 80,000, and a claim to be the only venue in Finland designed specifically for open-air events. It sits inside the Pori National Urban Park, which is a genuinely strange thing to say about a field that has hosted Metallica and Iron Maiden.

## Jazz Street

Not all of it happens behind a ticket gate. Through the festival week a Jazz Street runs along the Kokemäenjoki riverbank in central Pori, lined with smaller stages, clubs, street food and bars. The outdoor music there is free; some of the club programmes charge. There is a separate Pori Jazz Kids Festival for children.

This is the part that turns a concert series into a town-wide event. A nine-day festival with over a hundred concerts across roughly a dozen venues cannot be contained on one island, and the overflow is what a visitor without a wristband actually experiences: a Finnish river town in July, light until midnight, with brass coming out of a doorway two streets over.

## Two Silent Summers

The pandemic hit Pori Jazz harder than a cancellation notice suggests. The 2020 festival was called off after production spending had already been committed, and since the association's revenue rests mainly on ticket sales, there was little to recover it with. When 2021 fell through as well, organisers asked ticket-holders to simply hold on to their tickets for the following summer. The 55th edition finally took place in 2022, and Charles Lloyd played it.

Because of those two lost years the edition count now trails the calendar. The 2026 festival, with main concert days from 10 to 18 July, was the fifty-ninth in sixty-one summers - still one of the oldest and best-known jazz festivals in Europe, and still run from an island that was a garden before it was a stage.

## Flight Context

Kirjurinluoto lies at 61.4961°N, 21.7807°E, an island in the delta of the Kokemäenjoki river at Pori on Finland's west coast, roughly 20 km inland from the Gulf of Bothnia. From the air the delta is the landmark: braided river channels splitting around wooded islands immediately northwest of the Pori city grid, with the arena's open amphitheatre bowl and camping fields showing as a large clearing on the island. Pori Airport (EFPO) sits just a few kilometres from the venue, which makes this an unusually easy target; Turku (EFTU) is about 130 km south and Helsinki-Vantaa (EFHK) is the international gateway. Best viewed at 1,500-3,000 feet. During festival week in mid-July the site is unmistakable - stage structures, tent fields and vehicle lines - and Finnish midsummer light means usable visibility until well past 22:00 local. Expect coastal sea breeze and low stratus off the Bothnian Sea in the mornings.
