# Pori

A Finnish port city founded in 1558 because the sea kept retreating, burned down nine times, rebuilt on boulevards, and now known for a jazz festival, a grieving father's mausoleum and the night Finnish-language theatre began in a hotel dining room.

**Location:** 61.4867, 21.7975

## Introduction

On the evening of 13 October 1872, in the dining room of the Hotel Otava in Pori, a touring company put on a play in Finnish. It sounds like nothing. It was not nothing: Finnish had only been granted legal standing equal to Swedish nine years earlier, the educated classes still conducted their cultural life in Swedish, and a theatre performing in the language of the countryside was a political act as much as an artistic one. The company was the Finnish Theatre, run by the siblings Kaarlo and Emilie Bergbom. It would eventually become the Finnish National Theatre in Helsinki. But it started here, in a hotel in a timber port on the Gulf of Bothnia, and Pori has never quite let anyone forget it.

## A Town Founded Because the Sea Left

Duke John of Finland - later King John III of Sweden - chartered Pori on 8 March 1558, and his reasoning was geological. Land uplift had been raising this coast out of the sea for centuries, and by the 1500s the Kokemäenjoki had grown too shallow for large ships to reach the medieval town of Ulvila upstream. So the duke moved the trade downriver, closer to the water, and wrote that it seemed best "to build a strong market town alongside the sea." The Swedish name, Björneborg, means bear fortress; the Latin was Arctopolis, bear city. Finns still call it Karhukaupunki. The bear on the coat of arms comes from a seventeenth-century seal, and the city motto - Deus protector noster, God our protector - came straight off Duke John's own arms. Today about 83,000 people live here, making Pori Finland's tenth largest municipality.

## Nine Fires and the Boulevards They Bought

Pori has burned nine times, a record surpassed in Finland only by Oulu's ten. The worst came on 22 May 1852 and took three quarters of the buildings in the city. What rose afterwards was not a reconstruction but a replacement: the architect Georg Theodor Chiewitz drew a new plan of unusually wide, park-like streets, approved by Emperor Nicholas I, and that generous grid is still the shape of Pori today. Wide streets were a fire break before they were an amenity. The Empire-style old town on the south bank of the river dates from this rebuilding, as do the stone blocks along Eteläranta, the south shore. The Old Town Hall of 1841 is one of the handful of buildings that survived the fire it was designed before.

## Sigrid

Sigrid Jusélius was eleven when tuberculosis killed her in 1898. Her father, the businessman Fritz Arthur Jusélius, responded by commissioning a mausoleum for her at Käppärä Cemetery - a Gothic Revival chapel by the architect Josef Stenbäck, built between 1901 and 1903, and the only mausoleum in Finland. He hired Akseli Gallen-Kallela, one of the country's greatest painters, to fresco the interior with a cycle running Spring, Construction, By the River of Tuonela, Destruction, Winter, Autumn; Pekka Halonen painted the vestibule. In 1931 a fire destroyed all of it. His son Jorma repainted the whole cycle from Akseli's surviving sketches between 1933 and 1939, and the building reopened in 1941. Halonen's vestibule was never restored. A father's grief, painted twice.

## The Gullichsen Effect

In 1937 a young industrialist couple, Harry and Maire Gullichsen, commissioned a house at Noormarkku north of the city from Alvar Aalto. Maire was the driving client, and what she got by 1939 was Villa Mairea - a U-shaped modernist house wrapped around a garden with a kidney-shaped pool and a sauna in deliberately rustic timber, now regarded as one of Aalto's finest works and studied worldwide. Noormarkku was annexed to Pori in 2010. Maire Gullichsen's other legacy sits in the city centre: the collection she assembled became the foundation of the Pori Art Museum, which opened in 1979 in a former weigh house dating to 1860. A single patron, in other words, produced both the most admired private house in Finland and the town's modern art institution.

## Jazz, Porirock, and Twenty Thousand in the Market Square

Pori Jazz began in 1966 as a two-day event on the delta island of Kirjurinluoto in front of about 1,500 people. It now runs nine days in July and draws somewhere between 120,000 and 160,000, having hosted Miles Davis, Ray Charles, B. B. King, Bob Dylan, Elton John and Kanye West along the way. Kirjurinluoto Arena is the only open-air concert park in Finland built for nothing but concerts - five hectares of amphitheatre, twenty-two more for camping - and it shares its week with SuomiAreena, a national public-debate forum. The city has a rougher musical lineage too: Dingo and Yö both came out of here, and Finns coined the word Porirock for the sound. When the hockey club Ässät took the championship in 2013, twenty thousand people filled the market square for the parade.

## Flight Context

Pori sits at 61.487°N, 21.798°E on the Kokemäenjoki about 10 km from the Gulf of Bothnia. Best viewed at 3,000-5,000 feet: the 1852 Chiewitz grid reads clearly as unusually wide, tree-lined streets, with the river splitting around the green wedge of Kirjurinluoto right beside the centre and the delta fanning west toward Reposaari and Mäntyluoto. Yyteri's six-kilometre beach marks the coast to the northwest. Nearest airport: Pori (EFPO), only 2.6 km south of the city centre at 45 ft elevation, with runway 12/30 at 2,351 m and 17/35 at 801 m; the Finnish Aviation Academy trains here, so expect light training traffic in the circuit. Tampere-Pirkkala (EFTP) is about 110 km east. Clear, long-lit summer evenings; low ceilings and sea haze off the Bothnian coast in spring and autumn.
