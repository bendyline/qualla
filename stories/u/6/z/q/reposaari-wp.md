# Reposaari

An island of wooden houses and stowaway Mediterranean plants at the outer edge of the Kokemäenjoki delta, where Finland's busiest export harbour once stood and fifty-three sailors are still remembered.

**Location:** 61.6132, 21.4457

**Categories:** Finnish islands in the Baltic, Fishing communities, Landforms of Satakunta, Pori, Villages in Satakunta

## Introduction

More than seventy plant species grow on Reposaari that have no business being this far north. Thistles and sweet clover from the southern Baltic, wildflowers from the Mediterranean, a handful whose ancestors travelled from South America - all of them arrived as stowaways in ships' ballast, the sand and gravel that empty vessels carried for stability and shovelled onto the shore before taking on Finnish timber. The crews sailed away. The sand stayed, and the seeds buried in it did what seeds do. A century and a half later, this island at the outer edge of the Kokemäenjoki delta still grows a rough botanical map of every port it used to trade with.

## The Harbour at the End of the River

Land rises here. Post-glacial rebound has been lifting the Satakunta coast out of the sea for thousands of years, and as it rose the Kokemäenjoki silted up and pushed its working harbour steadily seaward. Reposaari was where that retreat finally stopped, because the island had something the river could not silt away: deep, sheltered water. German Hanseatic merchants used it as a staging point on their way upstream in medieval times. From the eighteenth century into the early 1900s it served as the Port of Pori outright, and through the 1870s it handled more export traffic than any other harbour in Finland. A steam sawmill founded in 1873 on Swedish capital turned the island into a mill town with a foreign accent, and a shipyard, Reposaaren Konepaja, worked alongside it.

## A Grid Laid on Sand

The grid the village still uses was drawn in 1874, at the height of the timber boom, and the houses that fill it are mostly wooden and mostly from the decades on either side of 1900. One survivor predates the grid entirely - a hotel built in 1838, now a restaurant. The Finnish Heritage Agency lists the whole settlement as a Nationally Important Built Cultural Environment, which in practice means the streetscape has been protected from the tidy demolitions that flattened comparable mill villages elsewhere. The sawmill closed in 1974 and the engineering works went the same decade; the population has drifted down ever since, from around a thousand at the turn of the millennium to roughly 720 today. Until 1956, when a causeway and rail link were completed, you could only get here by water.

## A Church Ordered from Stockholm

Reposaari's church, finished in 1876, was designed in Stockholm by a Swedish decorative painter named Herman Müller, and it looks it. The plan is a regular hexagon in wood, the roof rises to a narrow lantern, and the whole silhouette owes more to Norwegian stave and mountain churches than to anything on the Finnish coast. The bell tower starts octagonal and finishes square, carrying two bells - one cast in Helsinki in 1879, one in Bochum in 1885. Josef Stenbäck reworked the interior in 1928, after which Lennart Segerstråle painted the ceiling in sea-greys and greens with Christ as ruler of the sea. A votive model of Sir Francis Drake's Golden Hind hangs inside. In 2009 Sauli Niinistö, then Speaker of Parliament and later President of Finland, married Jenni Haukio here.

## Fifty-Three Men, October 1925

On 3 October 1925 four Finnish Navy vessels left Uusikaupunki for Vaasa on a routine autumn cruise. The weather turned to hurricane force. The larger gunboats found shelter; the small torpedo boats fell behind, and aboard S2 worn shaft bearings set up a vibration that opened the hull to the sea faster than her pumps could answer. Her last radio message, at 13:23 on 4 October, was six words: "I cannot work now." All fifty-three men aboard were lost. Divers found the wreck upside down in fifteen metres of water the following June and raised her that August; twenty-three of the crew were recovered from the hull and buried together in the Reposaari churchyard on 15 August 1926. Wäinö Aaltonen's memorial to them was unveiled in 1927. Nearby lies a separate grave for foreign sailors who died on the island.

## Guns, Fish and a Village That Sends People Out

A coastal battery went up on the island in 1935 and spent the war firing at Soviet aircraft raiding the Port of Pori. The Navy held the ground until 1964; restoration in the 1990s opened it as a park of two Canet naval guns, dugouts and trenches, with an observation tower rebuilt in 1992. The commercial harbour, meanwhile, reinvented itself: a fishing port established in 1967 is now among the most important in the country, with the Merimesta fish restaurant added in 2001. For a village this size, Reposaari has exported an improbable number of people - the singer Eino Grön, Jussi Hakulinen of the rock band Yö, and the Greco-Roman wrestler Kelpo Gröndahl, who took Olympic gold in 1952, later sat in Parliament, and is buried in the churchyard he grew up beside.

## Flight Context

Reposaari lies at 61.613°N, 21.446°E, roughly 30 km northwest of central Pori at the seaward tip of the Kokemäenjoki delta. Best viewed at 1,500-2,500 feet, where the 1874 street grid reads clearly as a rectangle of wooden roofs against the pine and sand of the island. The causeway and rail embankment to the mainland make an unmistakable line from the southeast; Kallo Lighthouse and the Mäntyluoto port cranes sit a few kilometres south. Nearest airport: Pori (EFPO), about 25 km southeast, with a 2,351 m runway. Tampere-Pirkkala (EFTP) is roughly 130 km inland. Expect sea fog in spring and early summer and strong onshore winds from the Bothnian Sea in autumn - the same weather that took S2 down in October 1925.
