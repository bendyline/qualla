# Yyteri

Six kilometres of dune and open sand on the Bothnian coast, young enough that the ground the Rolling Stones played on in 1965 had only been dry land for two centuries.

**Location:** 61.5642, 21.5225

**Categories:** Pori, Beaches of Finland, Tourist attractions in Satakunta, Landforms of Satakunta, Sand dunes

## Introduction

At Midsummer 1965 the Rolling Stones played their first ever concert in Finland, and they played it on a beach. Thirteen thousand people came out to a stretch of sand west of Pori that did not yet even belong to the city - Yyteri was still part of the rural municipality then, a place with a dance pavilion, a lot of pine trees and nothing much else. The band went on. The dunes absorbed the noise the way dunes do. Sixty years later the pavilion crowds are gone, but the sand is still there, still moving, and still one of the strangest pieces of ground in northern Europe.

## Ground That Arrived Recently

Everything from Kyläsaari to Mäntyluoto - the whole Yyterinniemi peninsula - is sand and gravel, and almost none of it is old. Post-glacial rebound is still lifting this coastline out of the Gulf of Bothnia, and the beach you walk on emerged from the sea roughly two to three hundred years ago. That is recent enough that a Finnish family tree can outrun the geology. The open sand runs about six kilometres facing the Bothnian Sea, with more beach tucked around Preiviikinlahti bay, and behind it lies a dune field remarkable enough by European standards for its sheer size. Of the fifteen or so square kilometres of sand, about half has never been built on. The oldest and highest dune ridge, Keisarinpankki, rises straight off the waterline.

## How Sand Holds Itself Together

A dune is an argument between wind and roots. Coarse shore grasses pin the loose sand down, pine forest takes over further inland, and where the vegetation breaks the wind starts moving the ridge again. The whole shoreline is protected, which is less about keeping people out than about keeping the plant cover intact - boardwalks and marked paths exist so that ten thousand pairs of feet do not undo what the grasses spent decades doing. The pines have their own troubles. The great web-spinning sawfly, Acantholyda posticalis, stripped needles across Yyteri's forests badly enough that in 2011 researchers tried releasing parasitic nematodes into the soil to knock the larvae back - an unusually experimental approach to Finnish forest health, and one that worked.

## The Argument on the Sand

For decades a few hundred metres in the middle of the beach were set aside as a unisex naturist area, one of only two officially designated in the whole of Finland. In April 2021 the city closed it, citing complaints from families about behaviour around the edges of the zone rather than about nudity itself. Naturist organisations pushed back and Pori began looking for somewhere else to put it. The episode says something about how a beach that started as an outlying strip of the rural municipality became public property in the fullest sense: everybody has a claim on it now, and the claims do not all agree.

## Watching, Walking, Playing

Yyteri is a serious birding site - more than ten observation towers stand around the beach and the shallow water of Preiviikinlahti, which draws waders and migrating flocks in numbers that justify the hardware. More than thirty kilometres of marked trails run through the pines and behind the dunes. The spa hotel that anchors the resort side of the place went up in the 1970s and is still working, alongside an eighteen-hole golf course, riding stables, and enough surfable wind to keep boards in the water. In summer the sand hosts Yyteri Beach Futis, a beach-football tournament with a distinctly Finnish appetite for celebration. Sixteen kilometres away, the Yyteri speedway stadium has staged the final of the Finnish Individual Speedway Championship six times between 1983 and 2019.

## The Long Light

What Yyteri really trades in is latitude. At 61.5 degrees north the June sun barely bothers to set, and low light rakes across six kilometres of pale sand for hours at a stretch, throwing the dune ridges into relief and turning the shallow water the colour of weak tea. In a cold winter the same beach goes hard under packed snow and drift ice piles along the waterline, and the towers that hold birdwatchers in May hold nobody at all. The Stones got the good version, the Midsummer one. Most Finns who come here are chasing the same three weeks.

## Flight Context

Yyteri sits at 61.564°N, 21.523°E, about 17 km northwest of central Pori on the Yyterinniemi peninsula. Best viewed at 1,500-3,000 feet: the six-kilometre arc of pale sand and the darker dune-and-pine belt behind it are unmistakable against the Bothnian Sea, and Preiviikinlahti bay curves in to the south. Reposaari and the Mäntyluoto port lie a short hop north; Kallo Lighthouse marks the channel. Nearest airport: Pori (EFPO), roughly 20 km southeast, runway 12/30 at 2,351 m; Tampere-Pirkkala (EFTP) is about 130 km east-southeast. Visibility is generally excellent in high summer; expect sea haze in spring and low ceilings with strong westerlies in autumn.
