# Berdychiv Carmelite Monastery

A fortress-monastery in central Ukraine that has drawn pilgrims for centuries, survived war and suppression, and stands near the site of one of the Holocaust's most haunting photographs.

**Location:** 49.8974, 28.5747

**Categories:** Religious Sites, History, Ukraine, Pilgrimage

## Introduction

The monastery at Berdychiv began its life as a fortress. In 1630, the voivode of Kiev, Janusz Tyszkiewicz Łohojski, handed a stronghold in the town over to the Discalced Carmelites, and four years later, in 1634, they began building. What rose on that defensible ground was both sanctuary and citadel, a place of prayer wrapped in thick walls. Nearly four centuries later it is still standing, still drawing pilgrims by the thousands, and still carrying the memory of one of the darkest moments ever recorded on film. Few buildings hold so much light and so much shadow in the same stone.

## Fortress of the Barefoot Friars

Construction finished in 1642, but the peace did not last. During the Khmelnytsky Uprising the building was destroyed, then rebuilt, a cycle familiar to almost everything in this much-contested corner of Europe. What made Berdychiv famous was not its walls but a painting: the icon known as Our Lady of Berdyczow. On 16 July 1756, Pope Benedict XIV granted the icon golden crowns, a rare honor that turned the monastery into a major pilgrimage destination, especially for Polish Catholics. Through the eighteenth century the Carmelites here ran printing presses and schools, making Berdychiv a small center of learning as well as devotion. Then came the partitions of Poland. The town passed into the Russian Empire, and the monastery's presses and classrooms were forced to close.

## A Photograph Beside a Grave

There is an image from the Holocaust in Ukraine that many people have seen without knowing where it was taken. It is often titled The Last Jew in Vinnitsa. It shows a man kneeling at the edge of a mass grave already filled with the dead, a rifle held to his head by a member of an Einsatzgruppe, the mobile killing units of the SS. Behind him, uniformed men look on. Recent research suggests the photograph was most likely made not in Vinnytsia at all, but here, at the then-abandoned Berdychiv Carmelite Monastery, whose walls appear in an alternate print, probably on 28 July 1941. The man's name is not known. It is important that his humanity is. He was someone's son, perhaps someone's father, one of many thousands of Jewish residents of this region murdered in those terrible months. The monastery grounds ask to be remembered as the place where such a life was ended, not only as a place of pilgrimage.

## Restored and Renewed

In 1991, as Ukraine declared its independence, the monastery was returned to the Roman Catholic Church. The icon of Our Lady of Berdyczow was restored, and in 1998 Pope John Paul II crowned it once again, a gesture that reconnected the sanctuary to its long tradition. Pilgrims returned, and they still come by the thousands each year, drawn from among Catholics and Eastern Orthodox believers alike. The site's standing continued to rise. On 25 June 2024, Pope Francis decreed that the sanctuary be elevated to the rank of minor basilica, an honor announced that July in a Mass presided over by Cardinal Pietro Parolin.

## A Living Sanctuary

To visit Berdychiv today is to walk through a place that has been fortress, printing house, school, ruin, killing ground, and shrine, sometimes several of these within a single lifetime. The Carmelites remain, and their order has kept a presence here even through recent hardship; during the ongoing war the head of the Discalced Carmelites came to celebrate Mass in solidarity with the region. A place like this resists easy summary. It holds centuries of faith and one unbearable afternoon in equal measure, and it asks its visitors to carry both away with them, the devotion and the grief, the crowns of gold and the grave in the yard.

## Flight Context

The monastery stands at 49.90°N, 28.57°E, in the city of Berdychiv in Zhytomyr Oblast, central Ukraine. From about 3,500 feet, look for the fortified church complex rising above the town's rooftops near the Hnylopiat River. The nearest airport is Zhytomyr (UKKV) to the northwest; Vinnytsia/Havryshivka (UKWW) lies to the south.
