# Museum of Shipbuilding and the Fleet

In a mansion where admirals once ran a fleet, a museum traces Mykolaiv's love affair with ships, from Greek galleys to a warship built perfectly round.

**Location:** 46.9781, 31.9861

**Categories:** Museum, Maritime, History, Ukraine

## Introduction

The building itself is the first exhibit. Known simply as the Ancient House, this graceful mansion in the classical style once served as the headquarters and residence of the men who commanded the Black Sea Fleet, from 1794 to 1900. Admirals with names woven through Russian naval history, Mordvinov, de Traversay, Greig, Lazarev, worked and lived within these walls while the fleet took shape on the rivers below. Today the house holds the Museum of Shipbuilding and the Fleet, and it is hard to imagine a more fitting home for a collection devoted to a city that has always measured its life in ships.

## From Galleys to Cossack Fleets

The museum's twelve rooms hold nearly three thousand objects, and they begin at the very beginning. The first hall reaches back to antiquity and the era of Kievan Rus, tracing how people first learned to navigate down the Dnieper and the Southern Bug to the Black Sea and on to the Mediterranean. Here are pictures of ancient Greek ships, illustrations drawn for the Histories of Herodotus, archaeological finds recovered from nearby Olbia, and fragments of ancient stone anchors that once held vessels against the current. Among the most compelling displays are those devoted to the Zaporizhian Cossacks and their long, hard fight for access to the Black Sea, a struggle by river-borne fighters that runs like a thread through the region's whole history.

## The Round Warship and the Age of Steam

The museum's proudest possession is a diorama depicting shipbuilding at the Mykolaiv Admiralty in the first quarter of the nineteenth century, a window onto the yards in their imperial prime. A hall devoted to the Crimean War of 1853 to 1856 gathers authentic relics, naval flags, ship cannons, and the medals and papers of the sailors who served, alongside a bust honoring a seaman who performed a heroic deed in the defense of Sevastopol. That war marked the great transition from sail to steam, and the collection captures the moment vividly with a model of the Novgorod, an extraordinary circular ironclad warship launched by the Mykolaiv Admiralty in 1873 and designed by the engineer Andrei Popov, a vessel built genuinely round, like a floating disc.

## A City Told in Ships

Out in the courtyard, beneath the open sky, stand cannons from the Black Sea Fleet, weathered survivors of long-ago battles. Inside, the story continues into the modern age with ship's equipment, navigation instruments, fragments of shells, and even collections of ship's tableware, the everyday objects of life at sea. A final section carries the account through the late twentieth century and the work of Mykolaiv's great yards, including the Black Sea Shipyard, the historic works once known for the 61 Communards, and the younger Okean yard. Taken together, the collection tells a single continuous story, that of a city whose entire identity has been bound up, for centuries, with the building of ships.

## Flight Context

The museum stands in central Mykolaiv at about 46.98 degrees north, 31.99 degrees east, in the historic Ancient House near the city's old admiralty district by the Southern Bug. From the air the surrounding old town grid and the nearby riverfront are the best reference points. Nearest airport is Mykolaiv International (UKON) north of the city; Kherson (UKOH) and Odesa International (UKOO) lie further along the coast.
