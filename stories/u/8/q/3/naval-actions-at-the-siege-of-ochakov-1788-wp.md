# Naval Actions at the Siege of Ochakov (1788)

The American naval hero John Paul Jones, made a Russian rear admiral by Catherine the Great, fought a summer of brutal shallow-water battles at the mouth of the Dnieper that all but destroyed an Ottoman fleet.

**Location:** 46.6000, 31.5500

**Categories:** Naval History, Russian Empire, Ottoman Empire, Ukraine

## Introduction

The man who had defied the Royal Navy off the coast of Britain a decade earlier now flew a Russian flag at the mouth of the Dnieper. In June 1788, John Paul Jones, the Scottish-born hero of the American Revolution, took command of a Russian sailing squadron in the shallow Liman where the Dnieper and Bug rivers meet the Black Sea. Catherine the Great had made him a rear admiral and set him against the Ottoman fleet guarding the fortress of Ochakov. What followed was not one grand battle but a summer of vicious, close-quarters fighting in treacherous water, and it ended with an Ottoman fleet in ruins.

## A Struggle for a Strategic Corner

Everything turned on Ochakov, an Ottoman stronghold commanding the point where the combined Dnieper-Bug estuary, the Liman, opened into the Black Sea. Hold it, and you controlled access between the rivers and the sea. The Russians brought two forces to bear on the water: a small squadron of sailing warships, and a flotilla of oared gunboats under Prince Charles of Nassau-Siegen, both answering to Prince Potemkin. On 6 June, command of the sailing ships passed to John Paul Jones. Against them the Ottoman admiral, the Kapudan Pasha Hassan el Ghazi, commanded a large mixed fleet, hard for contemporaries to count precisely but reckoned at a dozen battleships, more than a dozen frigates and xebecs, and a swarm of galleys, bomb vessels, and gunboats. Jones's ships were the weaker on paper; several had their gun counts reduced because the water was too shallow to float them fully armed.

## Fire in the Shallows

The fighting came in sharp bursts through June. On the 18th, Ottoman galleys and small craft swarmed the inshore end of the Russian line at dawn; the defenders were outnumbered until Jones and Nassau-Siegen swung their forces into action and drove the attackers off. The decisive clash came on the 27th and 28th. The Ottoman flagship ran aground and the fleet anchored in confusion; when the wind finally let the Russians attack before dawn on the 28th, they closed in hard. It was a grinding fight in shifting winds and shoal water. A Russian vessel, the Malyi Aleksandr, was sunk by Ottoman bombs, but an Ottoman battleship was set ablaze, and then its flagship burned as well. As the Ottomans tried to withdraw entirely, the guns of a newly built Russian battery at Kinburn, across the water, forced them so far north that nine of their ships ran aground.

## The Reckoning on the Water

The grounded ships were doomed. The next morning the Russian flotilla surrounded them and the stranded small craft and destroyed them all but one, a 54-gun battleship they refloated and kept. The two days cost the Ottomans dearly, several battleships and other vessels burned or taken, and well over a thousand men captured, against Russian losses in the flotilla of eighteen killed and sixty-seven wounded. It was not finished. On 9 July, as the Russian army began its assault on Ochakov itself, the flotilla fell on the remaining Ottoman vessels in the roadstead, capturing a few and burning the rest in a fight that ran from before dawn until mid-morning. The larger Ottoman fleet, unwilling to run the deadly gauntlet of the batteries again, had already put to sea to meet a second Russian squadron in the Battle of Fidonisi to the south.

## A Hero in Another Empire's War

For John Paul Jones, the Liman was a strange and short chapter. He had crossed an ocean and a decade from the deck of the Bonhomme Richard to serve a Russian empress in a war between empires that were not his own, and Catherine, it was said, believed he would reach Constantinople. He never did. Almost as soon as the fighting ebbed, Potemkin and Nassau-Siegen moved to claim the victories for themselves, and their version reached the empress first. Within a year Jones had left Russian service, bound for Paris and out of the war entirely. But the campaign he helped fight had done its work. The Ottoman fleet was shattered in the Liman, Ochakov's seaward shield was gone, and the fortress that had guarded the mouth of the Dnieper would fall that December, opening the northern Black Sea coast to a new order.

## Flight Context

The naval actions ranged across the Liman at the mouth of the Dnieper-Bug estuary, centered near 46.60°N, 31.55°E, off Ochakov (Ochakiv). From 3,000-5,000 feet the theater is legible: the wide, shallow estuary opening westward to the Black Sea, Ochakiv on the north shore, and the Kinburn Spit reaching in from the south whose battery helped decide the fight. Nearest airports are Mykolaiv (UKON) up the estuary and Odesa (UKOO) to the west.
