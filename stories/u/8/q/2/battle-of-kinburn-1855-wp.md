# Battle of Kinburn (1855)

A minor engagement at the mouth of the Dnieper became a turning point in naval history, the day armored warships shrugged off cannon fire and made every wooden fleet in the world obsolete.

**Location:** 46.5611, 31.5242

**Categories:** Military History, Crimean War, Naval History, Ukraine

## Introduction

The Russian gunners could see their shots strike home and simply not matter. Round after round slammed into the three ungainly vessels anchored just six hundred yards off Kinburn's walls, and the iron plating threw the fire off or let it burst harmlessly against the armor. In three hours on 17 October 1855, near the end of the Crimean War, a small Anglo-French squadron reduced a Russian coastal fortress to rubble while barely bleeding, and in doing so proved something the world's admiralties could no longer ignore: the age of the wooden warship was ending.

## Something for the Fleets to Do

By the autumn of 1855 the war's decisive act was already over. The Anglo-French army had stormed ashore in Crimea the year before and finally taken the great naval base of Sevastopol, freeing the allied fleets for new work. The commanders debated bigger prizes, a drive on Kherson, campaigns in Bessarabia or the Caucasus, before settling on something modest at French urging: the capture of the fort at Kinburn, which guarded the mouth of the Dnieper. The British were skeptical it would achieve much. Fox Maule-Ramsay, then Secretary of State for War, remarked that without a plan to exploit the fort's capture, the operation's only real purpose was to give the fleets something to do. It was, by that measure, a sideshow. It would also change how navies were built for the next fifty years.

## The Untested Armored Batteries

Kinburn's defenses were sturdy but outdated: a stone main fort mounting fifty guns, backed by two smaller works down the spit, all armed with nothing heavier than 24-pounders. Major General Maxim Kokhanovitch held it with about fifteen hundred men. Against this the allies assembled a fleet of ships of the line, frigates, gunboats, and bomb vessels under British Rear Admiral Edmund Lyons and French Vice Admiral Armand Joseph Bruat, carrying eight thousand troops to besiege the fort. The truly novel weapons were three French floating batteries, the first ironclad warships ever built, each mounting eighteen 50-pounder guns behind four inches of wrought iron. Observers openly doubted them. They were slow, they handled poorly, and no one knew whether the armor would hold against real gunfire. Nobody would wonder for long.

## Three Hours to Rewrite the Rules

To mask their intent, the allied fleet feinted west toward Odessa before doubling back, and landed the army behind the forts under cover of night. When the bombardment opened around nine on 17 October, the deep-draft ships of the line struggled against the shoals, so the work fell to the shallower vessels, and above all to the three ironclads. Anchored barely six hundred yards from the walls, they proved immune to everything the Russians threw at them, while their guns methodically shattered the fortifications and their contingents of Royal Marines cut down the exposed Russian gun crews. Only one shell found a weak point, slipping through a gunport on the battery Devastation and killing two men, the only allied deaths in the entire action. Fires spread through the main fort, its guns fell silent, and a single white flag rose above the walls. Kokhanovitch walked out to surrender. Most of his garrison was allowed to march away without their weapons.

## The Arms Race That Followed

In pure strategy, Kinburn changed little. Without the forces to push on and take Mykolaiv, the allies could not exploit their prize, and the war ended months later with the Treaty of Paris after Austrian diplomatic pressure pushed Tsar Alexander II to make peace. But every naval officer who watched had learned the same lesson. In his report Vice Admiral Bruat wrote that "everything may be expected from these formidable engines of war." Coupled with the slaughter that shell-firing guns had inflicted on wooden ships earlier in the war, the ironclads' success at Kinburn convinced France and Britain to build their fleets anew in iron. Napoleon III's program produced Gloire, the first sea-going ironclad; Britain answered with armored frigates of its own; and a naval construction race was on that would run until the Franco-Prussian War in 1870. A backwater fort at the Dnieper's mouth had launched the modern battleship.

## Flight Context

Kinburn fortress stood at the western tip of the Kinburn Spit, 46.56°N, 31.52°E, on the south side of the Dnieper-Bug estuary. From 3,000-5,000 feet the long sand spit and its lagoons point west into the Black Sea, with Ochakiv across the strait to the north where the allied fleet massed. Nearest airports are Mykolaiv (UKON) and Kherson (UKOH).
