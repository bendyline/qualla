# Battle of Kinburn (1787)

On a sandy spit at the mouth of the Dnieper, a Russian general fought on through two wounds and had two horses killed under him to hurl an Ottoman landing back into the sea.

**Location:** 46.5611, 31.5242

**Categories:** Military History, Russian Empire, Ottoman Empire, Ukraine

## Introduction

The commander was at prayer when the invasion began. As Ottoman ships pounded the coast and thousands of soldiers waded ashore onto the Kinburn Spit, Alexander Suvorov stayed in church for the Feast of the Intercession, reading the dispatches brought to him at the altar, and waited. He would not strike until the enemy had committed fully and reinforcements were near. By nightfall on 12 October 1787, that patience had cost him a wound to the side, a wound to the hand, two horses shot dead beneath him, and very nearly his life, and it had won one of the opening victories of a new Russo-Turkish War.

## A Weak Fort on a Strip of Sand

Kinburn was never much to look at. The fortress sat on a low sandbank at the tip of a spit reaching into the Black Sea, part of the Dnieper delta, guarding the approaches to the Russian shipbuilding base upriver at Kherson. Its guns were many but feeble, weak in both power and range. For the Ottomans, that made it a tempting first target: seize the spit, and you deny Russia a foothold for besieging the great fortress of Ochakov across the water. From the anchorage off Ochakov, the Ottoman fleet massed an intimidating weight of firepower, ships of the line, frigates, floating gun batteries, and gunboats, some four hundred guns in all, carrying an army in more than twenty transports. Suvorov, holding the fort with a few thousand infantry and Cossack cavalry scattered within a day's ride, was outgunned from the first.

## The Landing and the Trenches

Through late September the Ottoman ships probed the coast, firing to locate the hidden Russian batteries. One bombardment was broken up by the sudden intervention of a Russian galley, the Desna, sent in by its Maltese captain on his own initiative to drive off the enemy gunboats. Then, on the morning of 12 October, under an intensifying barrage, some six thousand troops came ashore, most onto the spit's narrow tongue itself. The pasha of Ochakov made his intent brutally clear: he ordered the ships to pull back after landing the men, so that the soldiers, cut off from any retreat, would have no choice but to win or die. Digging in, the landing force scraped out fifteen trench lines and began working their way toward the fort. Suvorov let them come. He called up his reinforcements, kept his composure, and let the enemy dig themselves into a trap on ground with the sea at their backs.

## Twice Wounded, Twice Rallied

At midday, with the Ottomans within two hundred paces, Suvorov attacked. His grenadiers took ten of the fifteen trenches before flanking fire from the fleet and the ferocity of the defenders threw them back. Suvorov led the second charge himself and was wounded in the side and hand; word of it rippled through the ranks and they wavered again. He survived only because a grenadier named Stepan Novikov shielded him at the critical moment. By late afternoon the last reinforcements had arrived. Suvorov attacked a third time, loosing his Cossacks around the enemy's flank across the shallows and into their rear. This time the line held. The Ottomans were pinned against the shore, so close to their own fleet that the ships had to cease fire for fear of hitting their own men, while Russian regimental guns raked the packed ranks with canister at point-blank range.

## The Reeds at Dawn

When darkness fell, perhaps six hundred survivors rowed back to the ships; several hundred more hid in the coastal reeds and were hunted down by Cossacks the next morning. The cost was staggeringly one-sided. Russian losses came to fewer than a hundred and forty killed and roughly three hundred wounded. The landing force was all but destroyed, its dead numbered around four thousand, and among the captured were two French officers dressed as Turks, later exiled to Siberia. Suvorov singled out the Schlisselburg grenadiers for special praise. From Saint Petersburg, Catherine the Great sent him the Order of St. Andrew, the empire's highest honor, with a single line: "You deserved it by faith and by faithfulness." Kinburn was the first of the triumphs that would make Suvorov a legend, the general who, it was said, never lost a battle.

## Flight Context

The battle was fought at the western tip of the Kinburn Spit, 46.56°N, 31.52°E, on the south shore of the Dnieper-Bug estuary. From 3,000-5,000 feet the setting is dramatic: a long, thin finger of sand and lagoons pointing west into the Black Sea, with the town of Ochakiv visible across the strait to the north. Nearest airports are Mykolaiv (UKON) up the estuary and Kherson (UKOH) to the east.
