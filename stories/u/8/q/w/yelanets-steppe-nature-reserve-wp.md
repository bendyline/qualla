# Yelanets Steppe Nature Reserve

On the last virgin grassland of Ukraine's Black Sea steppe, a herd of American bison grazes a landscape that scientists are slowly stitching back to its wild original.

**Location:** 47.6347, 31.9083

**Categories:** Ukraine, Nature, Wildlife, Conservation

## Introduction

There are bison in Ukraine, and they came from the American plains. On the rolling grassland of the Yelanets Steppe Nature Reserve, a hundred kilometers north of the Black Sea, a herd of American bison grazes ground that once belonged to a wilder cast of creatures entirely. Their presence is startling and deliberate, an experiment in restoring what a vanishing steppe has lost. This is the only steppe reserve in Right-bank Ukraine, the land west of the Dnieper River, and it protects a fragment of the largest untouched grassland tract left along the northern Black Sea coast, a survivor in a region long ago given over almost entirely to the plow.

## The Last Virgin Grass

The steppe once rolled unbroken across this part of Eurasia, a sea of grass that farmers gradually converted into fields until only scraps of the original remained. Yelanets protects one of those scraps, set on a southern spur of the Dnieper Upland about 140 kilometers west of the great river. The reserve began modestly, a 300-hectare parcel set aside in 1978, and grew piece by piece to 1,675 hectares by 2019. Roughly a third of that land is not virgin at all but recovering farmland, slowly coaxed back toward native steppe. It is patient, unglamorous work, letting local grasses and wildflowers reclaim old plowlines season after season. On the untouched ground, botanists have counted 615 species of vascular plants, a richness that a passing glance across the grass would never suggest.

## A Semi-Wild Menagerie

Yelanets has long been a place for reintroducing animals to a landscape that lost them. Scientists here have recorded 28 species of mammals, among them red fox, roe deer, wild boar, the burrowing Bobak marmot, and the American bison, alongside 137 kinds of birds, six reptiles, and five amphibians. Back in the 1970s the ambition ran wider still, with cattle, Turkmenian kulan, spotted deer, and mouflon all kept in semi-feral conditions beside the bison, a strange assembly of species from across the world turned loose on the Ukrainian plain. The site even held a seventy-hectare enclosure that functioned as a kind of living zoo, since removed and its animals relocated. What remains is quieter and more genuinely wild, a working laboratory for the question of how a broken grassland might be made whole again.

## A Refuge, Not a Playground

Yelanets is a strict nature reserve, and that word strict is doing real work. Its purpose is protection and science, not recreation. Mass tourism, new construction, hunting, and fishing are all forbidden, and public access is deliberately limited so the grassland can recover on its own terms. The people who spend the most time here are researchers and the schoolchildren who come for the reserve's education programs, learning to read a landscape that most Ukrainians have only ever seen as farmland. It is a modest place by the measure of a national park, without vistas or crowds, but its value lies precisely in restraint. In a region where the steppe has nearly vanished, Yelanets keeps a living memory of what the whole country once looked like, bison and all.

## Flight Context

The reserve lies at 47.63°N, 31.91°E, on a spur of the Dnieper Upland in northern Mykolaiv Oblast, roughly 100 kilometers north of the Black Sea. From about 3,000 to 6,000 feet, look for a patch of open, uncultivated grassland set among the geometric field patterns of surrounding farmland. Ukrainian airspace is closed to civil aviation; the nearest operating airport is Chișinău (ICAO LUKK) in Moldova.
