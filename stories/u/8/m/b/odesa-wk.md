# Odesa

A cosmopolitan Black Sea port dreamed up by an empress and built by four foreigners, whose graceful grid of the old town is now a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

**Location:** 46.4775, 30.7326

**Categories:** cities, history, Black Sea, Odesa

## Introduction

Odesa was invented on purpose. Where most great cities grew slowly from a village or a fort, this one was decreed into being: in 1794, Empress Catherine the Great looked at an ice-free harbor with a vast productive hinterland behind it and decided a city should stand there. She named it Odesa. Then she left the actual building to a remarkable cast of foreigners, and what they made on the edge of the steppe became, within a generation, one of the most cosmopolitan cities in Europe.

## Four Foreigners and a Grid

Catherine had meant her favorite, Potemkin, to develop the region, but he died in 1791, and she followed in 1796. The work fell to others, most of them from far away. A Naples-born officer of Spanish descent, Jose de Ribas, had captured the old Ottoman fortress of Hacibey and persuaded Catherine to build a port there. A French aristocrat, the Duc de Richelieu, served as its first governor and shaped its early character. The Count of Langeron, another Frenchman, later made it a free port. And an engineer, Franz de Vollan, laid out the streets in a grid, a novelty in its day and still the graceful skeleton of the old town. Long before any of them, there had been settlement here: a Hellenistic seaport in the centuries before Christ, and the medieval Tatar town of Hacibey, founded in 1440. Odesa was new, but its ground was ancient.

## The Cosmopolitan Boom

As a free port from 1819 to 1858, Odesa boomed. Grain flowed out through its harbor to the Mediterranean and the world, and money and people flowed in. It grew into the fourth-largest city of the Russian Empire, behind only Moscow, Saint Petersburg, and Warsaw. Crucially, it became a magnet for labor, creativity, and outcasts from everywhere, a place where Greeks, Italians, Jews, Ukrainians, Russians, and others mixed and made something new. You can read that mixing in the architecture even now, an exuberant hodgepodge of Italianate, Renaissance, Neoclassical, and Art Nouveau facades lining the avenues. That cosmopolitan spirit gave Odesa its famous character: quick-witted, worldly, and a little roguish, a city that has always prized cleverness and knows it.

## Walking the Old Town

The heart of the city is the grid running from the Pryvoz market and railway station in the south up to the Potemkin Steps in the north, with Catherine Street as its two-kilometer spine. A fine walking circuit begins at the rebuilt Orthodox cathedral on Soborna Square, drifts north through the City Gardens, and comes out onto the seafront promenade of Prymorskyi Boulevard, where the land falls away and the docks and sea open below. At the head of the great staircase, the boulevard passes the city hall and, a block inland, the Opera House. In 2023, UNESCO added the Historic Centre of Odesa to its list of World Heritage Sites — and, citing threats from the Russian invasion, simultaneously placed it on the List of World Heritage in Danger — a recognition of exactly this ensemble. The city has known hard times, and much of its grandeur wears its age. But at dusk, when the low sun catches the old facades, you can still see plainly why the entrepreneurs of the nineteenth century built as boldly as they did.

## Flight Context

Odesa sits at 46.4775 N, 30.7326 E on the northwestern shore of the Black Sea. From about 3,000 feet, the early-nineteenth-century grid of the old town is unmistakable above the harbor, with the Potemkin Steps and Opera House marking the seafront and the sprawling port below. The nearest airport is Odesa International (ICAO: UKOO), roughly 8 km southwest of the center.
