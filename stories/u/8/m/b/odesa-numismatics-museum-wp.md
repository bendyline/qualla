# Odesa Numismatics Museum

A small museum in central Odesa holding coins struck by Greek colonists on the Black Sea two thousand years ago, including the ancient world's money shaped like dolphins.

**Location:** 46.4825, 30.7380

**Categories:** museums, history, archaeology, Odesa

## Introduction

Some of the oldest money in this museum was never round. In the Greek colony of Olbia, on the shore north of the Black Sea, coins were cast in the shape of dolphins, small bronze creatures that changed hands as currency more than two thousand years ago. The Odesa Numismatics Museum keeps such things: the coins of the ancient city-states that once ringed this coast, arranged in cases at 33 Hretska Street, in the center of a city that has always understood the movement of money.

## The Coins of Lost Cities

Between the fifth and fourth centuries BC, the Greek poleis scattered along the northern Black Sea began striking their own silver and copper: Olbia, Tyras, Chersonesos, Panticapaeum, Phanagoria, and others whose names now belong mostly to archaeologists. Olbia and Panticapaeum minted in gold as well, and the coinage continued into the third century AD. To hold one of these coins is to hold the everyday economics of a vanished world, the price of grain or wine or passage across the water, pressed into metal. The images on them shifted with the times; Olbian coins of the Hellenistic era show a growing taste for aesthetic refinement, the sacred imagery of earlier days giving way to something more worldly. Odesa sits near where several of these cities once stood, which is why the museum can gather so many of their coins in one place.

## From Dolphins to Tridents

The collection does not stop at antiquity. It runs forward through the medieval art of Kievan Rus', a separate holding that traces Ukrainian craft across centuries: pre-Christian pendants and brooches called lunnitsa, amulets and signet rings, and then the objects of the Christian age, icons and crosses and the small hinged reliquary crosses known as enkolpyons. Among the rarest pieces are metal breastplates bearing the heraldry of the Rurik dynasty, the two-pronged and three-pronged marks that Ukrainian historians study with particular care, for the trident would one day become the national emblem of Ukraine itself. Alongside the coins sits a modest collection of ancient pottery, the plain utensils of the region's earliest inhabitants, which never matched Greek workmanship but tells its own quieter story of daily life on this coast.

## A Collector's Long Memory

The museum's roots reach back to the early 1970s, when a small local-history museum called Old Odesa was set up in the watchtower of a ruined fortress in Shevchenko Park. Its founder was Rudolf Tsiporkis, a collector of encyclopedic knowledge about the city, a man whose hoard of old postcards alone ran past fifteen hundred pieces. He lived a long life and died in 1999 at the age of ninety. That same year, honoring his memory and continuing his work, the Odesa Numismatics Museum organized an exhibition of his postcards and documents under the old name, the Gallery Old Odesa. It is a fitting lineage for a place built on the idea that small objects carry large histories, that a coin or a postcard can hold a whole vanished city inside it.

## Flight Context

The Odesa Numismatics Museum stands at 46.4825 N, 30.738 E on Hretska Street, in the historic core of Odesa a short walk from the harbor. From about 2,000 feet, the dense old-town grid near the seafront marks its neighborhood, with Shevchenko Park and the coast to the east. The nearest airport is Odesa International (ICAO: UKOO), roughly 8 km southwest.
