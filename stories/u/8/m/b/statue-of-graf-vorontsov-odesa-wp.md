# Statue of Graf Vorontsov

The Odesa monument to the governor who built the city, mocked in verse by Pushkin and fought over by every regime since.

**Location:** 46.4841, 30.7316

**Categories:** Art and Monuments, History, Cities, Literature

## Introduction

The young poet worked in the governor's office and could not stand the man. So Alexander Pushkin, exiled to Odesa in the 1820s and chafing under his employer, did what poets do: he wrote a stinging little verse. Half milord, half merchant, it began, half wise man, half fool, half scoundrel, but there is hope he will at last be whole. The target of that acid quatrain was Mikhail Semyonovich Vorontsov, and decades later a grateful city raised a bronze statue to him anyway. The monument has stood in central Odesa ever since, admired, resented, and repeatedly caught in the crossfire of history.

## A Monument Paid for by an Empire

Whatever Pushkin thought of him, Vorontsov was the administrator who did more than almost anyone to shape early Odesa, and when he died in 1856 the city moved quickly to honor him. The Society of Agriculture of South Russia, which he had led, proposed a monument and won the tsar's permission to raise funds by subscription. Money flowed in from across the empire. The bronze figure that resulted, with the architectural work attributed to Francesco Boffo, one of the designers who gave Odesa its handsome early face, was set on a pedestal carved with scenes from Vorontsov's career, including the surrender of Varna during a Russo-Turkish war. It was a grand statement, an entire empire chipping in to remember one of its builders.

## The Chain That Snapped

The statue's most famous moment came when it refused to fall. In the second half of the 1930s, Soviet authorities set out to pull the figure down, wrapping a thick steel chain around the monument and hitching it to a powerful tractor. The engine strained, the chain went taut, and then it broke. Vorontsov stayed standing. In a city that treasures a good story, the failed demolition became legend, the bronze governor outlasting the machine sent to topple him. The statue would survive that decade, and the war that followed, still watching over its square while regimes rose and fell around it.

## Neighbors in Bronze

History kept rearranging the square. After the Second World War, a bronze statue of Stalin was raised nearby to mark an anniversary of the Red Army, so that for a time the imperial-era count and the Soviet leader stood almost side by side, an odd pairing of two very different pasts. Over the years the Vorontsov monument was formally recognized and protected, listed as a work of monumental art and architecture of importance, its status confirmed by official decree. For most of the twentieth century it seemed a permanent fixture, one of those landmarks a city stops noticing precisely because it has always been there.

## Removed from the Register

The permanence did not last. On 11 November 2023, the Cabinet of Ministers of Ukraine removed the statue from the register of protected monuments, part of a broader set of laws aimed at names and symbols tied to the Russian Empire and the Soviet Union. The decision placed the old bronze governor at the center of a much larger reckoning over which pieces of a complicated past a city chooses to keep. For now the figure remains a fixed point in the memory of Odesa, wrapped in nearly two centuries of argument, from a poet's biting verse to a snapped chain to a modern change of law. Few statues have been quarreled over so long, or endured so much.

## Flight Context

The Vorontsov monument stands on a central Odesa square at 46.484°N, 30.732°E, near the Transfiguration Cathedral and a short walk from Prymorskyi Boulevard. From about 2,500 feet the open square and the nearby cathedral help locate it within the dense old-city grid. The nearest airport is Odesa International (UKOO), roughly 4 nm to the southwest.
