# Potemkin Stairs

Odesa's great staircase to the sea is an optical trick in stone, so cleverly built that the steps vanish from above and the landings vanish from below.

**Location:** 46.4885, 30.7420

**Categories:** landmarks, architecture, history, Odesa

## Introduction

Stand at the bottom of Odesa's most famous staircase and you see nothing but steps, an unbroken cascade of stone rising toward the sky. Climb to the top, turn around, and the steps have disappeared entirely, leaving only a series of flat landings dropping toward the harbor. This is no accident. The stairs were built as a deliberate illusion, wider at the base than at the crown, so that they seem far longer and grander than they truly are. It is the best-known symbol of the city, and it fools every visitor exactly as intended.

## A Doorway from the Sea

Odesa sits on a high steppe plateau, and for its first decades the only way down to the harbor was a tangle of winding paths and crude wooden steps. The great staircase, completed in 1841, solved that with monumental style, becoming the formal entrance to the city for anyone arriving by ship. It cost 800,000 rubles, an enormous sum, and its greenish-grey sandstone was shipped in all the way from Trieste, then an Austrian port on the Adriatic. Officially it is the Prymorski Stairs, though it has worn many names: the Boulevard Steps, the Giant Staircase, the Richelieu Steps. Whatever it was called, it did the same work, carrying the city down to the water in a single sweeping gesture of stone, about 142 meters long, that still takes the breath away.

## The Duke at the Top

At the crown of the stairs stands the city's very first monument, unveiled in 1828: a bronze figure in a Roman toga depicting the Duc de Richelieu, the French aristocrat who governed Odesa for eleven years and presided over its early growth and prosperity. The sculptor Ivan Martos designed it, and the statue has watched over the seafront ever since, greeting arrivals as they reach the top of the climb. For those unwilling to make that climb on foot, a funicular railway was built alongside the stairs in 1906. It ran for seventy-three years, through revolution and war, before being replaced by an escalator in 1970, but the stairs themselves have never stopped being the city's grand ceremonial approach.

## Immortalized in a Single Sequence

The staircase carries a name borrowed from the movies as much as from history. In 1955, in the Soviet era, the Prymorski Stairs were officially renamed the Potemkin Stairs, marking the fiftieth anniversary of the 1905 mutiny aboard the battleship Potemkin. That uprising had been immortalized in Sergei Eisenstein's silent film, whose harrowing sequence on these very steps became one of the most studied and imitated scenes in all of cinema, so famous that it fixed the staircase in the world's imagination forever. After Ukraine regained independence in 1991, the historic name Prymorski Stairs was formally restored, as it was for many streets across the city. But the two names now live side by side, and the world still knows the great descent to the Black Sea, most of all, as the Potemkin Stairs.

## Flight Context

The Potemkin Stairs descend from 46.4885 N, 30.7420 E, linking Prymorskyi Boulevard at the top of the plateau to the harbor below. From about 2,000 feet, look for the broad staircase and the green boulevard at the edge of the old town, with the port and its breakwaters spread out beneath. The nearest airport is Odesa International (ICAO: UKOO), roughly 8 km southwest of the center.
