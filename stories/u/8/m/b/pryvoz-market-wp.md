# Pryvoz Market

Odesa's raucous two-century-old food market, where the produce is fresh, the haggling is theater, and the news travels faster than anywhere else in the city.

**Location:** 46.4697, 30.7325

**Categories:** Markets, History, Food and Culture, Cities

## Introduction

One day in the 1940s, a four-year-old elephant named Murza broke away from a convoy leaving the Odesa zoo, ambled into the fruit section of Pryvoz Market, and helped himself. He ate apples by the handful, fished pickled cucumbers out of a barrel, sampled the cabbage, and worked through the dried fruit before anyone managed to lead him home. The escapade was so beloved that it inspired a Soviet comedy film. It also says something true about Pryvoz: this is a place where anything can happen, and usually does.

## From Muddy Cart-Yard to Institution

Pryvoz began in 1827 as little more than a stopping place, an unpaved patch of ground where horse-drawn carts pulled up to unload whatever they had brought to sell. For decades it was gloriously informal. There were no permanent buildings, no official scales or weights, and until the 1860s the traders paid no market fee at all. The name itself comes from a word meaning brought in, a nod to those endless wagons arriving from the countryside. Pryvoz grew up beside the older Stary bazaar, Odesa's first, but it was Pryvoz that captured the city's heart. Even after fire and plague forced a rebuilding in 1902, and even as covered halls and a modern shopping center rose around it, the market never lost the improvised, elbow-to-elbow energy of its cart-yard beginnings.

## The Fruit Passage and Its Rogues

In 1913 the market gained its one true architectural jewel, the Fruit Passage, designed by the celebrated Odesa architect Fedir Nesturkh, its covered galleries opening through graceful arches. But Pryvoz has always been as famous for its characters as its buildings. In the 1920s the market fell under the control of a notorious swindler named Jacob Pireman, who was said to have bought his post with a bribe, then squeezed tribute from every merchant and siphoned public funds meant for repairs. When his schemes came to light he was tried and shot. Odesa legend insists that before his arrest he buried a fortune in gold and diamonds somewhere on the grounds, a treasure that, as far as anyone knows, has never been found.

## The City's Truest Voice

To walk through Pryvoz is to swim through the sensory life of Odesa: silver sprats gleaming in trays, boiled shrimp and fresh carp, glossy eggplants, ropes of dried fish, and mountains of spice. Yet the market has never been only about buying and selling. It is where Odesa exchanges its news, its gossip, and its jokes, delivered in the sharp, self-mocking wit the city is known for. The writer Isaac Babel immortalized this world in his Odessa Stories, whose gangsters and traders speak in the swagger of the local streets. To read Babel is to hear Pryvoz. To visit Pryvoz is to step into Babel's pages.

## Endurance in a Hard Season

For nearly two hundred years Pryvoz has been a fixed point in a changing city, outlasting empires, revolutions, and reinventions. That endurance was tested again on 24 July 2025, when a drone struck the market during the wider war, causing significant damage. Odesites returned, as they always have, to sweep up and reopen their stalls. A market like this is more than a place to shop. It is a daily ritual, a gathering of neighbors, a proof that ordinary life goes on. Long after the elephant and the swindler, the sprats still glitter and the haggling still rings out, and Pryvoz remains the beating, bargaining heart of Odesa.

## Flight Context

Pryvoz Market lies just inland from the Odesa waterfront at 46.470°N, 30.733°E, near the main railway station. From about 2,000 feet the dense block of market halls stands out against the surrounding city grid, roughly a mile northeast of the Potemkin Stairs and the port. The nearest airport is Odesa International (UKOO), around 4 nm to the southwest.
