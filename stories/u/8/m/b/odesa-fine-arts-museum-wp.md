# Odesa Fine Arts Museum

A magnate's palace built from Odesa's own pale limestone, now home to seascapes by Aivazovsky, early Kandinsky, and a grotto hidden beneath the floors.

**Location:** 46.4934, 30.7288

**Categories:** art, museums, architecture, Odesa

## Introduction

The limestone that built this palace came from a single quarry on a landowner's estate, the same pale stone that would face most of Odesa's grand public buildings. Seweryn Potocki ordered the house in 1805 and never saw it finished; the Napoleonic Wars stalled the interior work, and he died in 1829, a year after the rooms were finally done. Two centuries later, the palace he commissioned holds more than ten thousand works of art across twenty-six halls, and beneath its central floor, if you know to look, there is a grotto.

## A Palace Built of Odesa

The Italian architect Francesco Boffo supervised construction, a man whose palaces and public buildings shaped Odesa and the Crimea alike. What he raised for Potocki was a textbook magnate residence of the era: two floors, a portico crowned with a tympanum and carried on six classical columns, side wings joined to the main house by curving galleries that enclose a formal courtyard. Behind it lies an English-style landscape garden with a romantic grotto tucked into the grounds. The palace passed through hands for the better part of a century. Potocki's distant relative Olga Potocka inherited it; a later mayor of Odesa bought it in 1888; and in 1892 it was sold to the Odesa Society of Fine Arts, who in 1899 opened it as a museum. The house had become a home for looking at beauty rather than merely living in it.

## Seas, Storms, and Early Kandinsky

The collection reaches from the sixteenth century to the twentieth, but its heart is Russian and Ukrainian painting of the late nineteenth and early twentieth. Here hang canvases by Ivan Aivazovsky, the great marine painter whose storms and luminous seas seem especially at home in a Black Sea port, alongside some early works by Wassily Kandinsky, made before he dissolved the world into pure color and line. There is a substantial holding of the Peredvizhniki, the wandering realists who carried Russian art to the provinces, and the only collection of Soviet-era painting in the city, both the officially approved and the once-forbidden. But the museum treasures its own too: the local TURH school, painters like Kyriak Kostandi who worked in Odesa and looked at Odesa, giving the city a school of its own.

## The Grotto and the Anniversary

Beneath the museum lie cellars and galleries, and within one of them, under the very center of the building, someone built an underground grotto. In the 1960s, restorers working from historical documents brought it back, and today it forms part of the tour of the palace's history, a cool, strange chamber hidden below the paintings. The museum has known hard days as well as beautiful ones. On 5 November 2023, the day it was marking its 124th anniversary, the building was damaged, its windows blown out and works pulled from the walls. The museum reported that none of its collection was destroyed. That such a place has survived nearly a century and a quarter, weathering wars and losses, is itself part of what visitors come to feel here: art outlasts a great deal.

## Flight Context

The Odesa Fine Arts Museum sits at 46.4934 N, 30.7288 E on Sofiivska Street, in the historic center a few blocks inland from the harbor. From about 2,000 feet, look for the dense grid of the old town near the coastline, with the palace's courtyard opening toward the street. The nearest airport is Odesa International (ICAO: UKOO), roughly 8 km southwest.
