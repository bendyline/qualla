# Moldavanka

A working-class Odesa neighborhood older than the city itself, immortalized by Isaac Babel as the raffish heart of Jewish Odesa and its legendary underworld.

**Location:** 46.4742, 30.7144

**Categories:** Neighborhoods, History, Odesa, Culture

## Introduction

Isaac Babel gave this neighborhood something few districts ever receive: a literature of its own. In his Odessa Stories, the crooked lanes of Moldavanka became a stage for gangsters with poetry in their threats and grandmothers who feared no one, a world so vivid it half-invented the Odesa of the imagination. The real Moldavanka is a historical quarter in southern Ukraine's great port city, and by legend it is older than Odesa itself, a settlement that was already here when the empire came to build its fortress on the sea.

## Older Than the City It Joined

Odesa was officially founded in 1794 on the ruins of an Ottoman fortress called Khadjibey, and the new name appears in official correspondence by January 1795. But a Moldavian colony had taken root nearby even earlier. Legend holds that the settlement predates Odesa by about thirty years, founded by Romanians who had come in the late 1760s to build the Ottoman fortress of Yeni Dunia and stayed on. They owned small plots, raising village-style houses and tending vineyards and gardens on the edge of what would become a metropolis. As Odesa's population multiplied roughly fifteenfold between 1795 and 1814, colonists of every background crowded into this fringe beyond the official boundaries, and Moldavanka grew into the dominant settlement outside the planned city. Even after architects like Francesco Boffo and Giorgio Torricelli folded it into the general plan, its old grid of streets and squares stayed stubbornly its own.

## The Factory Quarter

This was never the Odesa of marble stairs and opera boxes. Moldavanka was the city's industrial backyard, the place where plants and factories rose and where the people who worked them lived, often in the modest workers' shacks for which the neighborhood became known. Into the twentieth century it carried a reputation as a poor and lawless district, and that reputation, fair or not, became part of its legend. Much of it stays industrial to this day, though the story keeps turning: as old factories close, some are demolished, and high-rise apartments and business centers rise in their footprints. The district holds its history unevenly, a layering of vineyard, tenement, and construction crane, but the grid Babel walked still runs beneath it all.

## The Soul of Jewish Odesa

Before the Bolshevik Revolution of 1917, Moldavanka was the heart of the city's Orthodox Jewish quarter, a densely woven community of tradespeople, laborers, and families that gave Odesa much of its wit, its music, and its distinctive voice. Babel drew that world with unsparing affection in his Odessa Stories and the play Sunset, and its streets echo in the jazz-tinged songs Mark Bernes made famous. Its most notorious son was Mishka Yaponchik, the gangster whose exploits helped inspire Babel's fictional crime lord Benya Krik. To reduce Moldavanka to its outlaws, though, would be to miss the point. This was a living neighborhood of real people who worked hard, worshipped, argued, and endured, and whose culture proved so vital that a writer turned their ordinary streets into legend.

## Flight Context

Located at 46.47 degrees N, 30.71 degrees E, inland and west of Odesa's harbor near Mykhailiv Square, where Dalnytsia Street meets Balkiv Street. The nearest airport is Odesa International (UKOO), about 6 km to the southwest. From a low circling altitude of 2,000 to 3,000 feet, the older tight street grid of Moldavanka contrasts with the broad central boulevards toward the sea to the east.
