# Monument to the Founders of Odesa

A bronze empress who was raised, toppled, rebuilt, and finally removed, tracing a full century of how a city argues with its own past.

**Location:** 46.4873, 30.7393

**Categories:** Monuments, History, Odesa, Culture

## Introduction

Few statues have been born, destroyed, and reborn as many times as the empress who once stood on European Square. Unveiled in 1900 amid fireworks and citywide celebration, the Monument to the Founders of Odesa honored Catherine II and the four companions credited with the city's creation. It stood for twenty years, vanished for eighty-seven, returned for fifteen, and then, on December 28, 2022, came down for good. The square where it rose is quiet now, but its bronze figures carry the whole restless story of a port that has never stopped debating who it belongs to.

## A City's Hundredth Birthday Present

The monument began as a centennial gift a city gave itself. Catherine II had ordered Odesa's founding by rescript in 1794, on the site of the Ottoman settlement of Khadjibey, and as the hundredth anniversary approached, the Odesa City Council resolved to mark it in bronze. A design contest opened in 1891, and by 1893 imperial permission had been granted for a monument estimated to cost 57,000 rubles. Models were prepared by the Imperial Academy of Arts academician M. P. Popov, with the bronze figures cast under the sculptor Boris Eduards and the marble master Leopold Mentsione. When it was unveiled on May 6, 1900, the empress presided over four companions: the city's builder Jose de Ribas, the engineer Francois de Wollant, and the statesmen Platon Zubov and Grigory Potemkin. It was the kind of grand civic gesture the confident young city loved.

## Toppled, Then Raised Again

Revolution has a habit of pulling down what empires put up. In 1920, three years after the October Revolution, the monument was dismantled as a symbol of the old regime, part of the Soviet campaign of monumental propaganda; the figures of Catherine's companions went into the local history museum, while the empress herself was partly destroyed. For decades the pedestal held a different meaning: in 1965 a monument to the sailors of the battleship Potemkin rose in its place. The idea of restoration surfaced in the 1990s but stalled, until the Odesa businessman and city councilman Ruslan Tarpan financed a painstaking reconstruction. Craftsmen recast the empress in bronze, restored the original companion figures, and quarried Ukrainian granite for a new pedestal, recreating some 6,000 stucco details on the surrounding buildings. The reborn monument was reconsecrated, in its way, with a symbolic stone laid in July 2007.

## The Last Removal

In its final years the monument became a lightning rod. During the Russian invasion of Ukraine, it was repeatedly doused with red paint, and petitions circulated proposing all manner of replacements. As part of the broader derussification underway across the country, the Odesa City Council voted on November 30, 2022, to dismantle and relocate the statue, a decision backed by 43 members; surveys indicated that most Odesans supported the removal, and even the mayor, initially opposed, came around to the polls. On December 28, 2022, the figures came down and were moved to the Odesa Fine Arts Museum. Whatever one makes of the empress and her companions, the square's long saga is a reminder that monuments are never quite finished. Each generation decides anew whose memory it will carry, and Odesa, a city built on argument and reinvention, decided once again.

## Flight Context

Located at 46.487 degrees N, 30.739 degrees E, on European Square (formerly Katerynska Square) in central Odesa, a short walk inland from the harbor. The nearest airport is Odesa International (UKOO), about 8 km southwest. From a low circling altitude of 2,000 to 3,000 feet, the square sits within the historic grid near Prymorskyi Boulevard and the Opera House, with the Black Sea port just to the east.
