# Prymorskyi Boulevard

The elegant seafront promenade at the heart of old Odesa, crowned by the cinema-famous Potemkin Stairs.

**Location:** 46.4879, 30.7412

**Categories:** History, Architecture, Cities, Cinema

## Introduction

At the top of a monumental granite staircase, a bronze duke in a Roman toga gazes out toward the Black Sea, one arm extended as if presenting the harbor below to his guests. Behind him runs a broad, tree-lined promenade where Odesa has strolled, courted, argued, and mourned for two centuries. This is Prymorskyi Boulevard, and the stairs at its center are among the most filmed steps on Earth. The scene of a runaway baby carriage bouncing down them, staged here in a 1925 silent film, became so famous that directors have been quoting it ever since.

## A Boulevard That Kept Changing Its Name

Few streets have worn as many identities. Laid out in the 1820s as simply New Boulevard, it became the first asphalt-paved road anywhere in the Russian Empire, a modern ribbon along the top of the seaside bluff. In 1877 it was renamed Nikolai, honoring a local figure named Malakhov, though the public soon attached the name instead to Tsar Nicholas II. After the Russian Revolution it briefly carried the name of Aleksandr Feldman, an assassinated local commissar, a rechristening that Odesa's famously irreverent comedians could not resist mocking. Through every name change, the boulevard itself stayed exactly what it had always been: the city's front parlor, the place where Odesa presented its best face to the sea and to the world.

## The Stairs the World Learned by Heart

The Potemkin Stairs descend the bluff in a shallow, theatrical sweep, an optical trick that makes them look longer from below and shorter from above. They were built as the grand ceremonial entrance from the harbor to the city, so that arriving travelers climbed toward Odesa rather than merely walking into it. Their global fame came not from architecture but from film. Sergei Eisenstein staged a massacre on these steps in his 1925 movie Battleship Potemkin, and the sequence, a pram tumbling downward through panicked crowds, entered the language of cinema itself. Directors from Brian De Palma onward have paid homage to it. Tourists climb the stairs today partly to stand where an imaginary tragedy was filmed a century ago.

## Palaces, Shells, and Survival

The boulevard runs from Odesa City Hall to the colonnaded Vorontsov Palace, built under Governor Mikhail Semyonovich Vorontsov, the reforming administrator who did more than anyone to give early Odesa its polished European look. This exposed seafront has known war before. During the Crimean War in the 1850s, British and French naval guns shelled the boulevard from the water. In our own century it has been struck again: a September 2023 missile attack damaged nine buildings inside the Historic Centre of Odesa, now a UNESCO World Heritage Site. That the graceful promenade still stands, its plane trees still shading the same benches, is itself a quiet act of endurance.

## Odesa's Living Room

To understand the boulevard, walk it in the evening. Families push strollers past the Duke's statue while musicians play near the top of the stairs and the sea breathes below. This is where locals bring visiting relatives, where couples take their first walks, where the city marks both its celebrations and its griefs. Odesa has always thought of itself as a place apart, cosmopolitan and sharp-tongued and proud, and nowhere is that self-portrait clearer than here. The boulevard does not just offer a view of the Black Sea. It offers a view of Odesa itself, gathered in one long, elegant stretch above the water.

## Flight Context

Prymorskyi Boulevard sits at 46.488°N, 30.741°E along the Odesa seafront. From roughly 2,500 feet the Potemkin Stairs and the semicircular buildings flanking them are unmistakable where the bluff meets the harbor, with the colonnaded Vorontsov Palace at the boulevard's northern end. The nearest airport is Odesa International (UKOO), about 4.5 nm to the southwest.
