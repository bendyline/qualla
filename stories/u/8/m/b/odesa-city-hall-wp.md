# Odesa City Hall

A former merchants' exchange watched over by the gods of trade and harvest, where the clock still chimes the city's own tune.

**Location:** 46.4856, 30.7440

**Categories:** architecture, history, civic landmarks, Odesa

## Introduction

Every half hour, a clock above a colonnaded entrance on Prymorskyi Boulevard chimes a melody called "Odesa My Town." It is the same tune that greets trains pulling into the city's station, lifted from a Soviet operetta, and it drifts out over a small square where a monument to Alexander Pushkin stands near a naval gun pulled from the seabed. The building beneath the clock was never meant to govern. It was built to trade.

## The Gods of the Exchange

Look up at the frontage and two figures preside over the boulevard: Ceres, goddess of agriculture, and Mercury, god of commerce. The sculptor Luigi Iorini gave them their forms, and their meaning is plain enough. This was Odesa's stock exchange, a place where grain and money changed hands, and the two deities announced its business to anyone who passed. Francesco Boffo and Gregorio Toricelli designed the original Neoclassical building between 1828 and 1834, in the early decades of a port city that grew rich on wheat shipped from the steppe. Franz Morandi rebuilt it in the early 1870s. Before the Greek gods arrived, a lion and a bear by Joseph and Peter Genari flanked the entrance niches. Trade, harvest, and a pair of guardian beasts. The building wore its purpose openly.

## A Poet's Thirteen Months

The little square in front is called Dumska, and it holds a monument to Pushkin, who spent thirteen months in Odesa. The city left a mark on him, as it has on so many exiles and visitors passing through a port that always faced outward toward the Black Sea and the wider world. Nearby sits a piece of naval artillery with a stranger provenance: a gun recovered from a British frigate that ran aground and sank near the town during the Crimean War. It is an odd trophy to keep on a civic square, a relic of a nineteenth-century siege fished from the water and set beside a poet's likeness. Together they capture something about Odesa, a place layered with the debris of empires that fought over its harbor and the writers who could not stop describing it.

## When Politics Came to the Doors

The exchange eventually became a city hall, and with governance came the friction of governance. On a December day in 2012, a dispute over renaming a city street spilled into a scuffle at the building's doors. Several members of law enforcement were treated for minor injuries. The particulars were argued over afterward, as such things always are, each side describing the same afternoon differently. What lingers is not the quarrel itself but what it revealed: that a building raised to move grain and money had become, over nearly two centuries, the place where a divided city worked out who it was and whose names its streets would carry. The clock kept its schedule through all of it, chiming the city's tune every thirty minutes over the boulevard below.

## Flight Context

Odesa City Hall sits at 46.4856 N, 30.7440 E on Prymorskyi Boulevard, near the top of the Black Sea coastline. From roughly 2,000 feet you can pick out the tree-lined boulevard running parallel to the shore, with the port and harbor breakwaters just beyond. The nearest airport is Odesa International (ICAO: UKOO), about 7 km southwest of the city center.
