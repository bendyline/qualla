# Odesa University

A Black Sea university founded in 1865 whose graduates scattered across the world, from the physicist who imagined the Big Bang to a future president of Zanzibar.

**Location:** 46.4876, 30.7309

**Categories:** education, history, science, Odesa

## Introduction

One student of this university theorized the afterglow of the Big Bang. Another became the first prime minister of the Russian Empire. A third chaired the Petrograd Soviet during the revolution, and a fourth, generations later, became president of Zanzibar. For an institution founded on a single stretch of Black Sea coast in 1865, Odesa University has thrown its alumni to an improbable number of corners of the world, and stamped its name on the history of physics, mathematics, medicine, and revolution alike.

## Named for a Nobel Immunologist

The university's full name is the Odesa I.I. Mechnykov National University, honoring Ilya Mechnikov, the pioneering immunologist who won the Nobel Prize for his work on how the body's cells defend against infection. It is a fitting patron for a place that has always leaned toward science. Today the university sprawls across about seventy hectares in two parts of the city, organized into ten faculties spanning biology and chemistry, mathematics and physics, history and philology. It maintains its own botanic garden, an astronomical observatory, and a research institute of physics, and its many laboratories carry forward a tradition of inquiry more than a century and a half old. Beyond the classrooms lie the ordinary supports of student life: dormitories, cafeterias, a stadium, even a rest base by the sea in the village of Chernomorka. For roughly a hundred and sixty years the university has reached beyond its own shore, and it now belongs to networks like the European University Association and the Black Sea Universities Network, still facing outward as Odesa always has.

## The Scattering of Minds

The roll of alumni reads like a map of the twentieth century's upheavals. George Gamow, born in Odesa in 1904, would become one of the great theoretical physicists and cosmologists, a central figure in the theory of the expanding universe. Moses Schoenfinkel laid foundations in mathematical logic still taught today. Sergei Witte rose to become the Russian Empire's first prime minister, and Leon Trotsky, the revolutionary who led the Petrograd Soviet, passed through as well. The physiologist Ivan Sechenov, the microbiology pioneer Danylo Zabolotny, and the theologian Georges Florovsky all belong to this lineage. So do figures from far beyond the region: the Bulgarian intellectual Aleko Konstantinov, who founded his country's tourist movement, and Israeli statesmen like Kadish Luz and Mordechai Namir.

## A World in One Classroom

What strikes you about that list is not only its brilliance but its breadth. A university on the edge of the Black Sea educated a future president of Zanzibar, Ali Mohamed Shein; a Romanian historian and politician, Ion Nistor; a Macedonian philologist, Krste Misirkov; a Polish brigadier-general, Mariusz Zaruski; and a Russian-American rabbi, Joshua Goldberg. Odesa was a port that drew people from across empires and seas, and its university mirrored that mixing, gathering students of many nations and sending them back out transformed. The place itself is quieter than its graduates' destinies suggest, a working university of lecture halls, laboratories, and dormitories. But walk its grounds and you are standing where an astonishing number of consequential lives first took shape, in a city that has always been a crossroads.

## Flight Context

The main buildings of Odesa University stand near 46.4876 N, 30.7310 E, in the historic center of the city a short distance inland from the harbor. From about 2,000 feet, the dense old-town grid and the green of the botanic garden mark the university's core, with the Black Sea coastline to the east. The nearest airport is Odesa International (ICAO: UKOO), roughly 8 km southwest.
