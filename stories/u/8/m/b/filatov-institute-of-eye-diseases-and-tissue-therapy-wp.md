# Filatov Institute of Eye Diseases and Tissue Therapy

The Odesa institute where Vladimir Filatov turned a bold idea into restored sight for hundreds of thousands, and where Ukraine still trains its eye surgeons today.

**Location:** 46.4543, 30.7618

**Categories:** Medicine, History, Odesa, Science

## Introduction

In 1931, Vladimir Filatov did something that had never worked before: he restored a blind person's sight using a cornea taken from someone who had died. That single operation, performed here in Odesa, opened the way to modern eye banking and gave the world its first successful corneal transplant from a cadaver donor. The institute Filatov founded on Frantsuzkyi Boulevard still carries his name and his mission. Behind its unassuming L-shaped building, generations of Ukrainians have walked in unable to see and walked out into a sharpened world.

## The Surgeon and His Idea

Filatov was a stubborn man with a generous idea. Corneas suitable for transplant were desperately scarce, so he turned to an unlikely source, discovering that a cornea recovered from the recently deceased and chilled to just a few degrees above freezing for several days actually grafted better than one used immediately. The finding sounded macabre; the result was miraculous. He is remembered as a father of keratoplasty, the transplantation of the cornea, and his second great contribution, tissue therapy, grew from a hypothesis that stressed tissues release substances capable of stimulating a body's own powers of healing. In April 1936, under a Soviet resolution, he founded the Institute of Experimental Ophthalmology with a staff of just twenty. Its purpose-built home on what was then Proletarian Boulevard was completed in 1939.

## A Name Written and Rewritten

The institute's letterhead reads like a chronicle of the twentieth century. Founded in 1936, it was renamed the Ukrainian Experimental Institute of Eye Diseases in 1942, then in 1945 given the name of its founder while Filatov was still alive and still operating. Titles lengthened and shifted through 1956, 1965, and beyond, absorbing the words tissue therapy and the changing names of the academies that oversaw it. In 1986, the Soviet supreme council pinned the Order of the Red Banner to its record. After Ukraine's independence, the honors continued, and in 2012 it settled into its present name. Filatov himself led the institute from 1936 until 1956, followed by Nadiia Puchkivska, Ivan Lohai, and, from 2004, Nataliia Pasiechnikova, a lineage of directors carrying one man's work across nearly a century.

## Where Ukraine Comes to See

Today the Filatov Institute is a working hospital on a grand scale, a national referral center where roughly 700 patients pass through the outpatient clinic on a single day. Its 700 staff, including 27 professors, run specialist services for some of ophthalmology's hardest cases: eye trauma, severe burns, tumors of the eye, and the delicate work of pediatric surgery. It teaches, too, tied to the Odesa National Medical University and training doctors from intern level upward, and it has published the country's Journal of Ophthalmology since 1946. The campus grew building by building around the 1939 original, adding a polyclinic, surgical wings, laboratories, and a library that began with volumes Filatov donated himself and now holds more than 78,000 works. A museum preserves his office and his house at 53 Frantsuzkyi Boulevard, keeping close the memory of the man who taught the world to see again.

## Flight Context

Located at 46.45 degrees N, 30.76 degrees E, along Frantsuzkyi Boulevard in southern Odesa, close to the Black Sea shoreline. The nearest airport is Odesa International (UKOO), about 6 km to the west. From a low circling altitude of 2,000 to 3,000 feet the tree-lined boulevard and the coastal bluffs above the sea help fix the institute's leafy campus; the water's edge lies just to the southeast.
