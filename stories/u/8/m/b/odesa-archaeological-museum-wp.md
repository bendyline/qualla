# Odesa Archaeological Museum

Ukraine's oldest archaeological museum, where Scythian gold, Egyptian sarcophagi, and a coin struck by a medieval prince tell six thousand years of Black Sea history.

**Location:** 46.4850, 30.7440

**Categories:** Museums, Archaeology, Odesa, History

## Introduction

In a museum barely older than the city around it, you can hold six thousand years in your gaze. Founded in 1825, the Odesa Archaeological Museum is among the oldest of its kind in Ukraine, and its galleries run from the first tools of the Stone Age to the coins of medieval princes. Behind a grand Neoclassical facade on Lanzheronivska Street, guarded at its entrance by a copy of the ancient Laocoon sculpture group, lies the largest collection of the Northern Black Sea coast's deep past, more than 170,000 objects gathered from the steppe, the sea, and the sands of Egypt.

## A Society of Diggers

The museum was the work of curious, well-connected men. It opened in 1825 as the Odessa City Museum of Antiquities, founded by Ivan Blaramberg, who became its first director. Its growth owed much to the Imperial Odessa Society of History and Antiquities, a body granted the right to conduct excavations across the south of the Russian Empire, feeding a steady stream of finds into the young collection. A distinguished line of directors followed Blaramberg through the decades, and the institution grew into something more than a display of curiosities. Since 1997 it has functioned as a research institute as well as a museum, studying the earliest human settlement of the Black Sea region and the archaeology of the Middle Ages, publishing its findings and restoring its treasures. It was, from the start, a place where scholarship and spectacle went hand in hand.

## The Golden Pantry

Some rooms here glitter. The museum's Golden Pantry displays objects worked in precious metals, the oldest dating to the start of the second millennium BC, alongside ornaments drawn from Scythian and Sarmatian burial grounds, the graves of medieval nomads, and the workshops of Slavic craftsmen. Its coin collection numbers in the tens of thousands, and the rarest examples in gold and silver come from ancient Greece, Rome, Byzantium, and Kyivan Rus. Among them is a genuine marvel: a gold coin struck for Prince Volodymyr, one of only eleven such coins known to exist, and one of just two in all of Ukraine. To stand before it is to look at money that changed hands more than a thousand years ago, minted by the ruler who brought Christianity to the Rus. Few small cases anywhere hold so much history in so little metal.

## From the Nile to Snake Island

The reach of this museum is startling. It holds the only collection of Ancient Egyptian monuments in Ukraine, and the third-largest in the former Soviet Union, wooden and stone sarcophagi, funeral goods, and fragments of papyrus inked with hieroglyphs, brought across a sea to a city Egypt's pharaohs never knew. Closer to home, the collection tells the story of southern Ukraine itself, with finds from the great prehistoric Trypillia and Usatovo cultures and Bronze Age hoards like the Antonivsky Treasure. The work continues in the field: museum expeditions excavate ancient sites along the Danube and the Dniester estuary, including the Greek colony of Nikonion, and have carried their trowels as far as Snake Island in the Black Sea. Working with colleagues from a dozen countries, the museum keeps doing what it has done since 1825, coaxing the past back into the light.

## Flight Context

Located at 46.485 degrees N, 30.744 degrees E, at 4 Lanzheronivska Street in central Odesa, near Prymorskyi Boulevard and the harbor. The nearest airport is Odesa International (UKOO), about 8 km southwest. From a low circling altitude of 2,000 to 3,000 feet, the museum sits within the historic grid a short distance from the Opera House, with the Black Sea port immediately to the east.
