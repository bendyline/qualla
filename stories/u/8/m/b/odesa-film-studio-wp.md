# Odesa Film Studio

One of the oldest film studios in the former Russian Empire, where Dovzhenko learned to make cinema and a Soviet detective drama became a national obsession.

**Location:** 46.4596, 30.7571

**Categories:** cinema, history, culture, Odesa

## Introduction

In 1907, a projectionist named Myron Grossman opened a small film studio in Odesa and called it Myrograph. It was the oldest recorded studio in all of Ukraine, and it was already twelve years old when, on a spring day in 1919, the local Red Army committee folded it and a handful of other private studios into something new. What emerged on a seven-hectare stretch of land beside the Black Sea would outlast the empire that founded it, the union that followed, and nearly every filmmaker who walked through its gates.

## Born in the Chaos of Revolution

The studio's official birthday is 23 May 1919, though its first incarnation was hardly glamorous: it was listed as the political film section of the Red Army's 41st Division, and its first feature was titled Spiders and Flies. The men who had built Odesa's private studios, Grossman among them, fled the country as the Russian Civil War and the Ukrainian War of Independence swept through, running from political prosecution. What they left behind was reorganized, renamed, and pressed into service. By 1922 it had become the main production facility of the All-Ukrainian Photo Cinema Administration, known as VUFKU, and it was here, in the 1920s, that Ukrainian cinema found its first great voice.

## The Dovzhenko Years

Oleksandr Dovzhenko arrived in the late 1920s and, in a few astonishing years, made the silent films that would place him among the masters of world cinema. Zvenyhora came in 1928. Arsenal followed in 1929. These were not entertainments so much as visual poems, and they were made here, on this shore, by a director learning his craft in real time. The writer Isaak Babel worked at the studio in the same era. Decades later, in 1955, the studio revived its own production after a wartime stretch spent evacuated to Tashkent, and a new generation followed: Kira Muratova, whose Brief Encounters and The Asthenic Syndrome would become touchstones of Soviet art film, made much of her work in these pavilions.

## The Film Everyone Watched

In 1979 the studio released The Meeting Place Cannot Be Changed, a detective drama that became its highest-grossing film and a genuine phenomenon across the Soviet Union. For a studio that had spent decades making silent poems and difficult art films, it was a reminder that Odesa could also make something the whole country wanted to see. The place has kept collecting itself ever since. Inside its building sits a cinema museum holding more than twelve thousand items, an archive of catalogs, photographs, and equipment gathered over a century. In 2019, on the studio's hundredth anniversary, the National Bank of Ukraine struck a commemorative coin in its honor, and the postal service issued a stamp. A studio born in a civil war had lived long enough to be minted into currency.

## Flight Context

The Odesa Film Studio stands at 46.4596 N, 30.7571 E on French Boulevard, right at the Black Sea shoreline in the southeastern part of the city. From about 2,000 feet, look for the green coastal strip where the boulevard meets the water, with the studio's pavilions set among trees near the bluffs. The nearest airport is Odesa International (ICAO: UKOO), roughly 7 km to the southwest.
