# Odesa Opera and Ballet Theatre

A Viennese-built opera house so acoustically perfect a whisper carries to the back row, once funded by a quarantine scam and cooled by wagonloads of ice.

**Location:** 46.4855, 30.7413

**Categories:** architecture, opera, history, Odesa

## Introduction

In the 1830s, when ticket sales at Odesa's theatre ran low, a certain medical inspector had a solution. He happened to own the theatre, and he also controlled the port's quarantine, so he would announce that an infection had been found among newly arrived passengers, order them held at their own expense, and funnel the quarantine fees into hiring a star performer for his stage. It is one of the stranger origin stories of any opera house in Europe, and it belongs to a building that has always mixed grandeur with a certain Odesan cunning.

## Fire and Rebirth

The first theatre opened on this spot in 1810, its colonnaded entrance facing the sea, designed by the Italian Francesco Frapolli and refined by the Frenchman Jean-Francois Thomas de Thomon, who had also built St. Petersburg's Old Stock Exchange. It had no foyer. On the night of 2 January 1873, fire gutted the building. A fundraising campaign began at once, and the city held an international competition for a new design; forty were submitted and none was chosen. In the end the plan followed the lines of Dresden's Semperoper. Two Viennese architects, Ferdinand Fellner and Hermann Helmer, laid the foundation stone in September 1884 and finished in October 1887, at a cost of 1.3 million rubles. There is a story that when the people of Odesa heard the price, they gasped, and when they saw the building, they gasped again, this time in admiration.

## Whispers and Gilded Stucco

The new theatre was a marvel of its age. Its horseshoe hall was shaped so precisely that a performer's faintest whisper reaches every seat, and the auditorium, modeled on the style of Louis XVI, glows with gilded stucco figures. Mindful of the fire that had destroyed its predecessor, the architects gave the foyer twenty-four exits. It was the first building in Odesa lit by Edison's electric system, a beacon of modernity on the seafront. Cooling it in the Black Sea summers required real ingenuity: workers lowered wagonloads of ice and straw down a thirty-five-foot shaft, hauled them through a tunnel to a basement beneath the hall, and let the chilled air rise through vents under the seats. In niches on the facade stand busts of Glinka, Gogol, Griboyedov, and Pushkin, a small assembly of the artists the theatre meant to honor.

## The House That Survived

Great voices have filled this hall. Feodor Chaliapin, the towering Russian bass, gave many concerts here, and generations of singers and dancers have followed. The building has proven remarkably durable, too. When Nikita Khrushchev visited Odesa during the Second World War, immediately after the German army was driven out, he was concerned about the state of the city and inspected the theatre; he reported that only a single corner had been damaged by an enemy shell. The house was remodeled in the 1960s and restored again in 2007, each generation tending to the beauty the last had built. Alongside the Potemkin Stairs, it remains the most famous structure in Odesa, the emblem the city puts forward when it wants to show the world its face.

## Flight Context

The Odesa Opera and Ballet Theatre stands at 46.4855 N, 30.7413 E, just inland from Prymorskyi Boulevard near the top of the seafront. From about 2,000 feet, its distinctive rounded neo-baroque form and the green square beside it stand out against the old-town grid, with the harbor and the Potemkin Stairs close by. The nearest airport is Odesa International (ICAO: UKOO), roughly 8 km southwest.
