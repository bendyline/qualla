# Derybasivska Street

Odesa's beloved pedestrian promenade, named for the Spanish-born admiral who built the city, where a fountain, a bronze chair, and a river of April jokers converge.

**Location:** 46.4844, 30.7344

**Categories:** Streets, Odesa, History, Culture

## Introduction

There is a bronze chair on this street that anyone who has read a certain Soviet comic novel will recognize instantly. It sits in Odesa's oldest park, a wink to "The Twelve Chairs," the tale of a hunt for jewels hidden inside furniture. That single sculpture tells you what kind of place Derybasivska is: a street that takes its pleasures seriously and its history with a grin. Running through the heart of Odesa, this pedestrian promenade carries the name of Jose de Ribas, the Spanish-born officer in Russian service who founded the city and once kept a house along its length.

## The Man Who Built a City

Jose de Ribas, also known by his Catalan birth name Josep de Ribas, was no ordinary namesake. He was the builder of Odesa, head of both its military and civil administration in the city's earliest days, and he lived on the very street that now bears his name. Beside the street lies Odesa's first park, laid out in 1803 by the de Ribas brothers, Joseph and Felix, just as the young port was finding its feet on the Black Sea coast. Wander in today and you meet a small bestiary of monuments: a fountain and a bandstand, a lion and a lioness with her cubs carved in stone, and that famous chair. It is a park that grew up alongside the city, and it still feels like Odesa's living room.

## A Street of Many Names

Few streets have worn so many identities. It began as Gimnazskaya Street, named for the Gymnasium that opened its doors on April 16, 1804. On July 6, 1811, it took de Ribas's name for the first time. Then the twentieth century arrived with its shifting politics: under early Bolshevik rule, from 1920 to 1938, it honored the German socialist Ferdinand Lassalle; from 1938 to 1941 it was Chkalov Street, after the celebrated Soviet aviator. Only on November 19, 1941, did it settle at last into Derybasivska, the name it keeps today. Each rechristening was a small act of politics written onto a signpost, yet Odesans went on calling it, affectionately, simply Deribasovskaya through it all.

## Where the City Laughs

The cars are long gone. In the spring of 1984, Derybasivska closed to traffic and became a pedestrian zone; the trolleybuses that once rumbled along routes 1 and 2 were rerouted to neighboring streets, and the pavement was handed back to people on foot. The street honors Odesa's playful spirit in bronze, with two monuments to the singer Leonid Utyosov, including a telephone that plays his music, and a tribute to Sergey Utochkin, the pioneering aviator. Each April 1, the Humorina festival sends a procession the full length of the street, where tens of thousands of onlookers and costumed jokers pack the promenade. On that day, more than any other, Derybasivska is exactly what it was built to be: the place where the whole city comes to be happy together.

## Flight Context

Located at 46.48 degrees N, 30.73 degrees E, cutting through the historic grid of central Odesa near the Black Sea. The nearest airport is Odesa International (UKOO), about 8 km to the southwest. The tree-lined pedestrian corridor and the adjacent City Garden read clearly from a low circling altitude of 2,000 to 3,000 feet in clear weather; look for the green rectangle of the old park set within the dense street grid.
