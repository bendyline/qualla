# Isthmus of Perekop

A narrow neck of land that is the only overland gateway to Crimea, so fiercely contested that its very name means the Trench.

**Location:** 46.1500, 33.6667

**Categories:** Geography, Military History, Crimea, History

## Introduction

Everyone who has ever wanted Crimea has had to come through here first. The Isthmus of Perekop is a strip of land only about five kilometers wide, the single thread by which the Crimean Peninsula hangs from the Ukrainian mainland, with the Black Sea on one side and the strange, half-solid salt lagoon called the Syvash on the other. For thousands of years, whoever held this narrow gate held the key to the peninsula beyond. The names it has carried tell you everything: in Greek, Slavic, and Crimean Tatar alike, they all mean roughly the same thing, the Trench, the dug-out, the ditch.

## The Gate With Many Names

The naming runs deep, because the digging did. The ancient Greeks called the place Taphros, meaning a dug-out trench, after a defensive ditch cut across the isthmus between the seas, and there seems to have been a town of the same name nearby. The Crimean Tatars borrowed the idea into their own tongue as Or Qapi, joining Or, trench, with Qapi, gate. The Slavic name Perekop means, once again, a dug-out. Three languages, three peoples, one relentless idea: that this place is a barrier, a threshold, a wall of earth defending the way south. From antiquity through the Byzantine era the Greeks fortified it; after them the Crimean Tatars did the same, and in the fifteenth century it briefly belonged to the far-off maritime Republic of Genoa.

## Blood in the Narrows

The strategic value of the isthmus made it a killing ground. In 1783 the region passed to the Russian Empire, which built up the town of Perekop as a county seat. The twentieth century brought its worst battles. In November 1920, during the Russian Civil War, the Red Army stormed the isthmus against the White forces of General Pyotr Wrangel, who held Crimea. The Reds won, but the human toll spilled far beyond the soldiers: as many as 140,000 civilians fled across the Black Sea to Istanbul, refugees driven from their homeland by the fighting. Then came the Second World War. In September 1941, German and Romanian troops under Erich von Manstein forced the isthmus in a battle that lasted five days, opening the road to the siege of Sevastopol. In May 1944, the Red Army fought its way back and reclaimed the peninsula.

## The Lifeline and the Line

The isthmus carries more than armies. Rich salt deposits in its southern reaches have long made it commercially valuable, and for decades the North Crimean Canal ran across it, piping fresh water from the Dnieper River to a peninsula that has little of its own, until that flow was cut in 2014. The neck of land is also a border. The line between Crimea and Kherson Oblast crosses the northern part of the isthmus, and after 2014 it became a frontier fortified with fresh trenches, a modern echo of the ancient ditch that gave the place its name. Whether as a canal, a salt works, or a militarized boundary, the Isthmus of Perekop remains what it has always been: the single narrow point on which the fate of Crimea turns.

## Flight Context

The Isthmus of Perekop lies at about 46.15°N, 33.67°E, a slim land bridge between the Black Sea to the west and the shimmering, shallow Syvash lagoon to the east. From altitude it reads as a narrow neck of land pinched between two very different bodies of water, the open sea on one hand and the pale, mottled salt flats of the Syvash on the other. Kherson (ICAO UKOH) lies to the north; Simferopol (UKFF) to the south within Crimea.
