# Siege of Perekop (1920)

In November 1920, the Russian Civil War reached its bitter end at the same narrow isthmus that had guarded Crimea for centuries, when Red Army soldiers waded through a freezing lagoon to flank the White defenders.

**Location:** 46.1494, 33.6722

**Categories:** Military History, Russian Civil War, Crimea, Ukraine

## Introduction

The men crossed at night, in eleven degrees below zero, chest-deep in the brine of a lagoon that everyone had assumed was uncrossable. On the far shore stood the last stronghold of the White cause in southern Russia, the Crimean Peninsula, sealed off behind the Turkish Wall at Perekop, the same ancient earthwork Tatars and tsars had fought over for centuries. The Reds outnumbered the defenders four to one. When the wind pushed the water back and bared the mudflats of the Syvash, they went across. What followed, in November 1920, was the closing chapter of the Russian Civil War.

## The Last Redoubt

By late 1920, the White movement had been driven back across all of Russia into a single peninsula, and the Isthmus of Perekop was the door they meant to hold. The White army had spent the autumn of 1919 turning the ancient defenses into a modern fortress belt, a project General Pyotr Wrangel inherited and continued after he took command in April 1920. The main line rested on the Turkish Wall itself, eleven kilometers long, fronted by an old Tatar ditch up to ten meters deep, three lines of trenches, and belts of barbed wire. A second line waited at Yushun, further south among a maze of salt lakes. Roughly ten thousand men held Perekop and Yushun, with thousands more strung along the Syvash and the Arabat Spit and fourteen thousand in reserve. Against them, Mikhail Frunze marshaled the Southern Front of the Red Army and the insurgent bands of Nestor Makhno's Ukrainians, an invasion force four times the defenders' strength.

## The Crossing of the Syvash

Frunze had first meant to strike at Chongar, but ice trapped his supporting flotilla far to the east, so the main blow shifted to Perekop. A frontal assault on the Turkish Wall on 8 November failed bloodily; the defenders, sheltered behind the crest of the rampart, cut down attackers who could reach no further than the wire-choked ditch, and one Red brigade reportedly lost half its men in red shirts against the machine guns. So the Reds went around. On the night of 8 November, a shock group of some twenty thousand crossed seven kilometers of the Syvash lagoon in a screaming wind at minus eleven degrees, wading through freezing shallows that the retreating water had left passable. They struck the lightly held Lithuanian Peninsula and turned into the rear of the Perekop line. When the wind shifted and the water threatened to rise and cut them off, local villagers were mobilized to restore the fords by hand.

## Break-in at Yushun

The fighting concentrated among the narrow defiles between the salt lakes at Yushun, where numbers alone could not decide things quickly. For three days the lines heaved back and forth, each side at moments threatening to encircle the other. Wrangel's cavalry, ordered into a battle already being lost so the infantry could pull back, drove the Reds nearly to the sea before charging into a trap: Makhno's horsemen feigned retreat, then unmasked a line of machine-gun carts, the famous tachanki, and mowed the White riders down. On 11 November the Yushun fortifications finally gave way along their whole length, and the Chongar line broke the same day. The entrance to Crimea stood open at last, though the regiments who forced it were nearly annihilated in the doing.

## The Ships That Waited at the Docks

What came after was not slaughter but exodus. Frunze granted a day's pause and wired Wrangel an offer to surrender; no answer came. Instead the White army fell back to the ports, Yevpatoria, Sevastopol, Yalta, Feodosia, and Kerch, where it carried out something the earlier retreats from Odessa and Novorossiysk had failed to manage: an orderly evacuation. Well over a hundred thousand soldiers and civilians boarded ships bound for exile they would mostly never return from. In one small, human moment, a destroyer already clear of the moorings suddenly wheeled back into the harbor: someone had remembered a battalion left guarding the docks. They were snatched aboard, and the ship rejoined the fleet. By 17 November the Red Army held Yalta, the last front of the Civil War was gone, and Crimea passed into a Soviet future its people had not chosen.

## Flight Context

From 6,000 feet the decisive terrain reads clearly: the thin land bridge, the shining maze of the Syvash salt lagoon to the east that the Red Army waded across, and the string of lakes at Yushun to the south.
