# Siege of Perekop (1736)

The day a Russian field marshal broke through the ancient wall that had sealed off Crimea for centuries, opening the peninsula to an empire for the first time.

**Location:** 46.1522, 33.7015

**Categories:** Military History, Crimea, Russian Empire, Fortifications

## Introduction

For centuries, everyone who wanted into Crimea faced the same narrow bottleneck: a five-mile neck of land, barely above sea level, sealed shut by a wall, a ditch, and a stone fortress the Tatars called Or Qapi, the Gate. On a spring morning in 1736, a German-born field marshal in Russian service decided the Gate would fall. Within half an hour of the first assault, his stormtroopers stood atop the rampart that had held back invaders for generations. What happened next changed the fate of a khanate.

## The Gate at the Narrow Neck

Geography made Perekop. The Crimean Peninsula hangs from the mainland by the Isthmus of Perekop, a strip of land so slender that a single fortified line could bar the way to an entire country. The Crimean Khanate understood this perfectly. Their fortress here, Or Qapi, meaning the Gate in the Turkic tongue, took its Russian name from the same idea: perekop, a cut or a ditch dug across the land. For generations the wall did its work, and Crimea remained beyond the reach of the tsars. Approaching on 30 May 1736, the Russian vanguard first had to beat back a charge of Crimean cavalry some 40 kilometers north of the wall. Only then did the isthmus itself come into view, low and flat and bristling with defenses, the last barrier between the Russian army and the peninsula it had never entered.

## Half an Hour to the Top

The field marshal was Burkhard Christoph von Münnich, known to his adopted country as Minikh. He wasted no time. Rather than settle in for a long siege, he ordered an immediate assault, feinting hard against one flank while his real blow fell on the other. The stormtroopers dropped into the deep ditch, then hauled themselves up the rampart with pikes, dragging spiked barricades called chevaux de frise behind them. Thirty minutes later, they had the crest. Resistance collapsed almost everywhere at once. Only a single detachment holding one watchtower fought on, refusing to yield for a full hour before it was overwhelmed to the last man. The rest laid down their arms on the promise they could go home. A young corporal named Vasily Dolgorukov distinguished himself so plainly in the storming that he was promoted on the spot, the first rung of a career that would one day bring him back to conquer Crimea outright.

## The Victory That Melted Away

With the Gate in ruins, the way south lay open, and the Russian Empire set foot inside Crimea for the first time. Minikh pressed on to the heart of the khanate, reaching the capital at Bakhchisaray and the town of Akmescit, today's Simferopol. It should have been a triumph. Instead, the campaign came apart not on the battlefield but in the ranks. Disease swept through the soldiers; a livestock epidemic, an epizooty, killed the animals that moved the army; and mutiny simmered as supplies ran short. Münnich had won the wall and lost the war of endurance. He withdrew from the peninsula, leaving Crimea in Tatar hands a while longer. Yet the illusion of an impregnable frontier was gone. Perekop had been breached, and everyone now knew it could be breached again.

## Flight Context

The Isthmus of Perekop lies at 46.15°N, 33.70°E, the slender land bridge joining Crimea to the mainland. From 6,000-8,000 feet the narrow neck is unmistakable, pinched between the salt lagoons of the Syvash to the east and Karkinit Bay to the west. The nearest airfields are at Kherson (UKOH) to the north and Simferopol (UKFF) to the south.
