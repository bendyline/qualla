# Russian yacht Livadia (1873)

The gilded imperial yacht of the Romanovs that traded its luxury for war, then vanished into the fog off Crimea, its wreck a mystery divers puzzled over for decades.

**Location:** 45.3478, 32.4961

**Categories:** maritime, history, shipwreck, Crimea

## Introduction

For decades, divers off Cape Tarkhan-Kut on Crimea's windswept western tip knew the wreck only as "that little steamer." It lay in shallow water near the Tarkhankut Lighthouse, a jumble of copper lining and rotted deck that no one could name. Not until October 2008 did divers from Tula finally confirm what they had been swimming over all along: the Livadia, an imperial yacht of the House of Romanov, wrecked here on a foggy October morning in 1878. It was a strange, quiet end for a ship that had been built to carry emperors and had, unusually for a pleasure vessel, sailed into a war.

## A Palace That Floated

The Livadia was born of imperial dissatisfaction. In 1860, Grand Duke Konstantin Nikolayevich, the general admiral of the Russian Imperial Navy, had converted an old paddle steamer called the Tigr into a yacht for the Romanovs, but the 62-meter vessel lacked the space and comforts a royal family expected. There was talk of ordering a replacement in England; instead, the commission went to Captain Leopold Schwede at the Nikolaev (now Mykolaiv) Admiralty. Work began in late 1869. Displacing 1,965 tons, the finished Livadia was smaller than her grander Baltic Sea sister, the Derzhava, yet she matched that ship in the opulence of her imperial suites, which were designed by the celebrated court architect Ippolit Monighetti. She was, in effect, a floating fragment of a palace, fitted out for a dynasty.

## The Only Yacht That Went to War

Most royal yachts spend their lives in calm ceremony, but the Livadia earned a distinction none of her Russian sisters could claim: she saw active combat. During the Russo-Turkish War of 1877 to 1878, fought between the Russian and Ottoman empires across the Black Sea and the Balkans, the Livadia served in the conflict, the only Russian imperial yacht ever to do so. It is an odd image, this gilded vessel with its emperor's staterooms pressed into the hard business of naval war. Whatever grandeur she had been built for, for a season she belonged to the fleet rather than the family, a pleasure craft called up to serve the empire that had made her.

## Lost in the Fog, Found in the Sand

The Livadia's end came suddenly. On the foggy morning of 22 October 1878, with none of the Romanovs aboard, she ran aground near the Tarkhankut Lighthouse at the western edge of the Crimean peninsula. The crew reached shore safely and managed to salvage most of the royal furnishings, carrying the emperor's finery back onto solid land. The ship herself could not be saved; forty-seven days after the grounding, the waves finished her, breaking the hull apart where she lay. The name did not die with her. In 1880 it passed to a new experimental yacht built for the Romanovs in Scotland. But the original Livadia stayed put in the shallows off Cape Tarkhan-Kut, her wooden body dissolving into the seabed until only the copper and the deck timbers remained.

## The Steamer Without a Name

For generations after she sank, the Livadia lay in plain sight and utter anonymity. Divers exploring the shallows off the cape found her often enough, but no one could say what she was; among themselves they called the wreck simply that little steamer, an unremarkable ruin among the rocks. The mystery held until October 2008, when divers from the Russian city of Tula finally examined the site closely and confirmed her identity. By then the wooden hull had disintegrated completely, but the remains of the deck and the copper lining were still recognizable, and their sensors detected still more metal buried in the sand below. A yacht built to carry an emperor across the Black Sea had spent 130 years as a nameless heap of copper and timber, until at last someone came along who could give the little steamer back its name.

## Flight Context

The wreck site lies near 45.3478 N, 32.4961 E, off Cape Tarkhan-Kut at the western tip of Crimea. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet the striking white Tarkhankut Lighthouse and the pale limestone cliffs of the cape mark the spot where the yacht went down in the shallows below. Saky (Saki) airfield lies to the east along the Crimean coast.
