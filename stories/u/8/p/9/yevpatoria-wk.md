# Yevpatoria

A 2,500-year-old Crimean port where Greek colonists, Crimean Tatars, Karaites, and Ottoman traders each left their mark on a single crowded quarter locals call the Small Jerusalem.

**Location:** 45.2000, 33.3583

**Categories:** coastal, history, multicultural, resort

## Introduction

Walk a few blocks through Yevpatoria's old town and you cross more faiths than most cities hold in their entire history. A mosque built for Ottoman worshippers stands near a Karaite prayer house, which stands near an Orthodox church, all within the tangle of narrow streets that locals now market as the "Small Jerusalem." It is not hyperbole so much as inventory. For twenty-five centuries, this shallow bay on Crimea's western coast has collected the people who came to trade, pray, and settle, and the old quarter is where their overlapping worlds still press against one another.

## The City With Many Names

Yevpatoria has been renamed almost as often as it has changed hands. Greek colonists founded the first settlement here, Kerkinitis, around 500 BC, on a coast that would later fall within the dominions of King Mithradates VI of Pontus. The modern name derives from his cognomen, Eupator, meaning "of good father." Between the seventh and tenth centuries it was a Khazar town; later it answered to the Mongols and the Crimean Khanate. Crimean Tatars called it Kezlev, Ottomans wrote it as Gozleve, and the Russian medieval form Kozlov was simply a reshaping of the Tatar word. When the Russian Empire took Crimea in 1783, the town was formally renamed Evpatoriya the following year, reaching back past a thousand years of local history to a Greek king for its official identity.

## A Small Jerusalem

The nickname is earned in the old town, where the walking tour threads between the houses of worship of communities that lived side by side rather than in sequence. Christians, Muslims, and Jews built here, and among the Jewish population were the Karaites, a distinct people with their own scripture-centered tradition and their own cuisine, still served in the quarter today. To wander these streets is to feel a multicultural inheritance that most of Crimea has lost to war and displacement. The stone is worn, the lanes are close, and the layered heritage is not staged for tourists but simply what remained after each empire receded and left its congregation behind.

## Beaches, Poets, and All-Night Sound

Yevpatoria has long drawn visitors for its gentle shore and warm summers; before Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine, the sea embankment filled with more than 100,000 travelers from across Eastern Europe each high season, though war and sanctions have since narrowed the crowds mostly to Russian visitors. The Polish poet Adam Mickiewicz passed through in 1825 and wrote one of his Crimean Sonnets here, later translated into Russian by Mikhail Lermontov. In a different register entirely, the region became famous for Kazantip, an electronic dance festival staged for years at nearby Popovka, whose entry pass was called a "viZa" and whose crowds swelled the tiny host town far beyond its resident population. Between the sonnets and the sound systems, the town keeps finding new reasons to be visited.

## Flight Context

Yevpatoria sits at 45.20 degrees north, 33.36 degrees east on Crimea's flat western coast, best viewed from around 4,000 to 6,000 feet where the long sandy embankment and the shallow bays stand out against the steppe. The dense old-town grid marks the historic core near the shoreline. The nearest major airfield is Simferopol (UKFF), roughly 60 kilometers to the east.
