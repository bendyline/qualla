# Yevpatoria RT-70 Radio Telescope

A 70-meter dish on the Crimean coast that did not just listen to the cosmos but shouted back, sending humanity's messages toward the stars.

**Location:** 45.1892, 33.1875

**Categories:** Space, Science, Astronomy, Crimea

## Introduction

Most radio telescopes only listen. This one could shout. The Yevpatoria RT-70, a dish seventy meters across on the Crimean coast, was one of only two instruments on Earth capable of aiming a beam of radio waves at a distant star and transmitting a deliberate message toward whoever, or whatever, might be out there to receive it. From this coast near Yevpatoria, teams composed calls to the cosmos and flung them into the dark. A machine built to hear the faintest whisper from space was also used, on a handful of remarkable occasions, to make the loudest human noise ever pointed at another world.

## A Dish That Could Talk Back

With a reflector seventy meters in diameter, the RT-70 ranks among the largest single-dish radio telescopes ever built. What set it apart was not just its size but its power. Coupled to enormous transmitters, the instrument could do more than passively detect signals; it could conduct active experiments, sending tightly focused electromagnetic beams into space and then analyzing whatever returned. This dual ability made it one of only two facilities in the world able to transmit messages to extraterrestrial civilizations. It was here that projects like the Cosmic Calls, the Teen Age Message, and A Message From Earth were beamed skyward, humanity's attempts, however hopeful, to introduce itself to the galaxy. Each of these transmissions was an act of faith as much as science, a bottle thrown into a cosmic ocean with no guarantee anyone would ever find it, carrying pictures, sounds, and words chosen to say something about who we are to a listener no one has ever met.

## Half a Century of Deep Space

The RT-70 joined the Soviet space program in 1978 and quickly became a workhorse of deep-space exploration. It supported the Venera probes to Venus and the ambitious Vega program, tracked the Phobos missions and later spacecraft like Spektr-R, and lent its power to radar studies that scanned passing worlds. Under observatory code 255, it bounced radar off asteroids including Toutatis and Bennu, and off a small body christened Golevka, whose very name stitched together the first letters of three observatories that studied it: Goldstone, Evpatoria, and Kashima. That naming captures something about how the instrument worked, as one node in a global web of dishes turning their attention to the same faint targets. The telescope even earned a place in national memory, appearing on Russia's commemorative 100-ruble banknote issued in 2015, its distinctive silhouette printed for millions to carry in their pockets.

## Silence on the Coast

A telescope like this is nearly irreplaceable; instruments of its scale and transmitting power are built only a handful of times in a century. In August 2025, that fragility became stark. The main dish of the RT-70 was struck by a Ukrainian drone in the course of the Russo-Ukrainian War, damaging an instrument that had spent decades reaching across the solar system and, on rare occasions, beyond it. There is a particular poignancy in the wounding of a machine whose defining purpose was communication, an antenna that once sent friendly greetings toward the stars, silenced amid a conflict on the ground below. Instruments like this are the product of decades of accumulated skill and enormous investment, the kind of thing a society builds only when it dreams big, and they cannot simply be replaced when they are lost. Its long watch over deep space, spanning nearly half a century of tracking probes and scanning worlds, was interrupted in an instant. Whatever else it had been used for, the RT-70 stood for a moment when human beings, reaching out from a small planet, tried to make themselves heard across the emptiness.

## Flight Context

The Yevpatoria RT-70 radio telescope stands near 45.19°N, 33.19°E on the coastal plain west of Yevpatoria. Its vast 70-meter dish is a striking landmark from 3,000–5,000 feet, visible against the flat steppe near the Black Sea. The nearest airport is Saky (UKFI) to the southeast; the coastal city of Yevpatoria lies just to the east.
