# Kropyvnytskyi

A central Ukrainian city that has changed its name three times and once cradled the first professional Ukrainian theater, still calling itself a little Paris.

**Location:** 48.5103, 32.2667

**Categories:** Ukraine, Cities, Culture, History

## Introduction

Few cities have been renamed as often as this one, and each name marks a different empire's grip on the same streets beside the Inhul River. It began in 1754 as Yelisavetgrad, spent much of the twentieth century as Kirovohrad, and finally became Kropyvnytskyi, named for a founder of Ukrainian theater. The locals reach for a fonder title. They call their home a little Paris, remembering the elegant look the downtown wore before the twentieth century reshaped it, and if you walk the center today you can still catch the ghost of it in ornamented facades, some freshly restored, others patiently waiting their turn.

## A City of Many Names

The successive names tell the region's history in shorthand. Founded around a fortress named for the Russian empress Elizabeth, the town carried her name for more than a century and a half. Under the Soviet Union it was rechristened Kirovohrad, and in 1939 it became the administrative center of the surrounding oblast. Only in recent decades did it take the name Kropyvnytskyi, honoring Marko Kropyvnytskyi, one of the fathers of Ukrainian professional theater. Set on the Dnieper Upland where the Inhul winds through, the city grew into an industrial and cultural hub for central Ukraine, a motorway junction stitched into the map by the M-12 and M-13 highways that run west toward Lviv and south toward the Moldovan border. Each name it has worn still lingers somewhere, in an old map, a grandparent's habit, a street that never quite got repainted.

## Where Ukrainian Theater Was Born

Kropyvnytskyi holds a distinction that outshines its modest skyline: the first professional Ukrainian theater opened here. In an era when the Ukrainian language and stage faced heavy restriction, that was no small act, and it planted a cultural root the city still draws pride from. The decades from the mid-nineteenth century into the early twentieth were its golden age, when industry climbed and culture flowered together, and the theater tradition begun in those years gives the modern name its meaning. To be renamed for a playwright, rather than an empress or a Soviet official, is a quiet declaration of what the city chooses to remember about itself. The stage came first, and the stage endures.

## Everyday Central Ukraine

Kropyvnytskyi does not trade on famous monuments, and it is honest about that. Its pleasures are the unhurried ones of a working central-Ukrainian city: cheap and hearty meals, walkable parks, a downtown slowly reclaiming its old grace. The local table leans, oddly, toward pizza, with pizzerias outnumbering nearly everything else, but the food to seek out is the national kind, the deep-red borshch, the plump vareniki dumplings, and the fried potato deruny that locals also call draniki. Language here is its own map, a blend of Russian, Ukrainian, and the mixed speech called surzhyk. From the city, roads fan out to Uman and its famous park to the west, to Mykolaiv and the Black Sea coast to the south, and to a Cold War missile museum on the oblast's edge, an easy hub for a wider Ukrainian journey.

## Flight Context

Kropyvnytskyi sits at 48.51°N, 32.27°E, on the Dnieper Upland along the Inhul River in central Ukraine. From about 6,000 to 9,000 feet, the river bend through the city center and the converging M-12 and M-13 highways are the clearest landmarks. Ukrainian airspace is closed to civil aviation; the nearest operating airport is Chișinău (ICAO LUKK) in Moldova to the west.
