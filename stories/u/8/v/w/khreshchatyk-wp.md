# Khreshchatyk

Kyiv's main street was blown apart by some of the longest-range radio-controlled bombs used in the war, rebuilt from rubble, and has since become the stage where the whole nation gathers.

**Location:** 50.4472, 30.5219

**Categories:** History, Urban, Culture, Kyiv

## Introduction

In the autumn of 1941, buildings along Kyiv's main street began exploding for no visible reason. There was no artillery, no aircraft overhead, just detonations rippling down Khreshchatyk one after another. The bombs had been planted by retreating Red Army engineers and triggered by radio signal from more than four hundred kilometers away, among the longest-range radio-controlled explosions attempted in warfare up to that point (an earlier Red Army radio mine, detonated near Strugi Krasnye that July, had been triggered from about 150 kilometers). The blasts tore the heart out of the street and killed occupiers and civilians alike. What makes Khreshchatyk extraordinary is not that it was destroyed, but that Kyiv rebuilt it into something grander, and then made it the place where the entire country comes to be heard.

## From Ravine to Grand Avenue

The street was not always a street. Its name comes from the Slavic word for cross, because the valley it follows is crossed by several ravines, and in ancient times this was a wooded hollow where the people of Kyiv strung nets to catch wild game. A small river once ran along the bottom. The first houses, built in 1797, were wooden, raised as the ravine was slowly filled in. Only in the 1830s and 40s did Khreshchatyk begin to become the city's principal thoroughfare, its design fixed in 1837. Banks arrived, then a stock exchange, department stores, and hotels, until it was called the only real street in Kyiv and ranked among the most famous in the whole Russian Empire. It saw the future early: gas lamps in 1872, then in 1892 the empire's first electric tram line, running down from Khreshchatyk to Podil.

## The Street Rebuilt

After the wartime destruction, Soviet planners chose not to restore the old Khreshchatyk but to remake it. Thirty damaged buildings that might have been saved were pulled down, and through the 1950s and 60s the street was widened to some seventy-five meters and lined with new buildings in the monumental Stalinist neoclassical style, the city council headquarters, the central post office, the trade unions building with its clock. To soften the grandeur, planners replanted the avenue with chestnut trees, which became emblems of the city itself. Khreshchatyk drew Kyiv's first metro service in 1960 and became the system's first transfer station in 1976. On May Days and Victory Days, parades filled it. It was, by design, a stage as much as a street, built to hold crowds and be seen.

## Where the Nation Gathers

Since Ukraine's independence, Khreshchatyk and the adjoining Maidan Nezalezhnosti have become the country's public square in the fullest sense. The national flag was first raised here in 1990. Independence Day parades have run down the avenue since 1994. And when Ukrainians have wanted to change their country, they have come here to do it: to the Ukraine without Kuchma protests at the turn of the millennium, to the Orange Revolution in the winter of 2004, when more than a million people at its peak crowded the street and its tent camp. In late 2013 and early 2014 came the Revolution of Dignity, and with it the street's most painful chapter. The deadliest clashes fell nearby on Instytutska Street, and in memory of those who died there, part of that street was renamed in 2015 for the Heavenly Hundred, the fallen honored in the fabric of the city itself.

## A Street on Weekends

For all its weight of history, Khreshchatyk is also simply where Kyiv comes to relax. On weekends the avenue closes to traffic and fills with pedestrians, street musicians, concerts, and festivals along its 1.2-kilometer run from European Square through the Maidan to the nineteenth-century Besarabsky Market. There are the department store TSUM, the underground shopping arcades, the narrow lane of Kyiv Passage, the art center in the Besarabsky quarter. Four metro stations serve the street below. Following the 2022 invasion, the annual military parade was set aside, and in its place the avenue displayed the burned remains of enemy vehicles. It was one more turn in the long life of a street that has been ravine, showcase, battleground, and gathering place, and that keeps, through all of it, being the center of the city.

## Flight Context

Khreshchatyk runs about 1.2 km through central Kyiv at roughly 50.447 N, 30.522 E, northeast to southwest from European Square through Maidan Nezalezhnosti to Bessarabska Square. From about 1,500 feet AGL, look for the broad, tree-lined avenue cutting through the dense city center, opening at the Maidan's plaza with its tall Independence Monument column. Nearest airport is Kyiv International (Zhuliany), ICAO UKKK, about 6 km southwest; Boryspil International, UKBB, lies east of the city.
