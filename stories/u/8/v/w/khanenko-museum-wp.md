# Khanenko Museum

A husband and wife spent forty years assembling Ukraine's greatest art collection, gave it to their country, and then watched history try to erase their names from it.

**Location:** 50.4411, 30.5144

**Categories:** Museums, Art, History, Kyiv

## Introduction

Bohdan and Varvara Khanenko spent forty years chasing beautiful things across Europe. They went to the auctions, sat with the great art historians, walked through private collections most people never saw, and slowly built what became the finest trove of art in Ukraine, Rubens and Bellini, early Byzantine icons, Chinese porcelain, Egyptian bronze. Their one ambition was to give it to Kyiv. Bohdan signed the will a month before he died in 1917; Varvara signed the deed of gift the next year, handing everything, collection, mansion, and library, to the nation for nothing. What followed is a harder story than the collecting: a story of a widow refusing to leave her home, of names struck from a wall, and of treasures carried off in wartime that have never come back.

## An Art History Theatre

The heart of the museum is the Khanenkos' own mansion at 15 Tereshchenkivska Street, and it was conceived as something more than a house. Varvara was the eldest daughter of Mykola Tereshchenko, the Ukrainian sugar magnate, and the estate came to her from her father in 1888. She and Bohdan then spent years designing its interiors as a kind of art history theatre, commissioning rooms in the spirit of different eras: a Gothic drawing room, a Renaissance one, a Rococo Golden Chamber, a Dutch Burgher dining room. Artists of real distinction contributed, among them Mikhail Vrubel. Upstairs, by contrast, the couple's private rooms were nearly ascetic, plain quarters above a palace built to display everything they loved. The house was itself a document of the late nineteenth century, an argument in plaster and paint about how beauty should be housed and seen.

## The Widow Who Would Not Leave

When the Bolshevik government nationalized the Khanenko property in June 1919, the collection became the State Museum of Art, and elderly Varvara Khanenko found herself a resident in what had been her own home. Ukrainian scholars intervened on her behalf, securing her the right to stay, and she chaired the museum committee until her death in 1922, working with the museum's first curator to build its earliest displays. It was a dignified arrangement, and it did not last. In 1924, two years after she died, the authorities removed the Khanenko name from the museum entirely, the official reason being their lack of revolutionary services to proletarian culture. The couple who had given the country its greatest collection were, by decree, unpersoned from it. Their names would not return to the title until 1999, more than seventy years later.

## What War and Ideology Took

The collection has survived being pillaged twice over. Through the 1920s and 30s the Soviet state sold museum treasures abroad to raise hard currency, and some of the Khanenkos' finest works vanished into that campaign: a diptych of Adam and Eve by Lucas Cranach the Elder, golden objects from the Kyivan Rus period, rare Iranian silver. Only once did Ukrainian cultural figures manage to stop a seizure, saving a sixteenth-century Persian carpet. Then came the war. In 1941 the most valuable holdings were evacuated east to Ufa, but the artworks left in Kyiv were looted by the Nazi occupiers and smuggled out during their 1943 retreat. The museum still works to find and repatriate them, and in 1998 it published a catalogue of the stolen objects, refusing to let the losses be quietly forgotten.

## Icons Older Than the City

For all it has lost, the museum still holds wonders. In a single room hang four early Byzantine icons painted in the sixth and seventh centuries, in encaustic, colored wax, not long before the iconoclasts began smashing such images across the empire. They are among the oldest surviving works of their kind anywhere, older than Kyiv itself, and they came to the museum in 1940. Beyond them stretch the Khanenkos' European masters and a deep collection of Asian art, housed since 2006 in the neighboring Sakhnovsky mansion, where the halls run through Buddhist and Islamic art and the arts of China and Japan. More than twenty-five thousand objects in all, roughly a thousand on permanent view. The couple wanted their city to have a museum of the world's art. Through everything the twentieth century threw at it, their gift endures, their names once again on the door.

## Flight Context

The Khanenko Museum stands at 50.4411 N, 30.5144 E on Tereshchenkivska Street in central Kyiv, near the university district and Taras Shevchenko Park. From about 1,500 feet AGL the two nineteenth-century mansions read as ornate blocks within the dense central grid, a short distance southwest of Khreshchatyk and the Golden Gate. Nearest airport is Kyiv International (Zhuliany), ICAO UKKK, about 5 km southwest; Boryspil International, UKBB, lies east of the city.
