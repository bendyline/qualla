# Mother Ukraine

A 62-meter titanium woman raises a sword over Kyiv, though her sculptor wanted her to hold a palm branch of peace instead.

**Location:** 50.4265, 30.5631

**Categories:** Monuments, Kyiv, World War II memorials, Ukrainian history

## Introduction

The sculptor wanted her to hold a palm branch. A symbol of peace, raised over the hills of Kyiv where so much blood had already been spilled. The Communist Party wanted a sword. The Party won, and so for more than four decades a woman of titanium, sixty-two meters tall, has stood above the Dnipro with a sixteen-meter blade lifted toward the sky. She weighs 560 tonnes. Her sword alone weighs nine. From miles off she catches the light, and up close she is less a statue than a landscape, a colossus you crane your neck at until it aches.

## The Woman Who Kept Changing Faces

No single person modeled Mother Ukraine, and that turns out to fit her strangely well. The Soviet sculptor Yevgeny Vuchetich, who had already built the towering Motherland Calls in Volgograd, began the design and based her features on a coworker, Mila Hazinsky. When Vuchetich died in 1974, only the eyes and eyebrows of his original face survived into the finished monument. The Ukrainian sculptor Vasyl Borodai took over and reshaped much of the rest, reportedly using the sculptor Halyna Kalchenko as a model, though engineers said the real reason for the changes was to fix aerodynamic flaws that Moscow specialists had found in wind tunnel testing. A statue this large behaves like a sail. Her face is a committee of the living and the dead, revised as much by physics as by art.

## A Fortune in Rubles

Building her was controversial before the first plate of steel went up. Final plans were drawn in 1978, construction began in 1979, and the costs climbed past what anyone would admit. The director of construction was asked to confirm a figure of nine million rubles and called even that a conservative estimate; the true cost is thought to have reached twelve million. To grasp the scale of that sum, an engineer in the Soviet Union of the time earned around a hundred rubles a month. Critics said the money could have fed people, housed them, healed them. The monument opened anyway on Victory Day, the ninth of May 1981, a year behind schedule, with Soviet leader Leonid Brezhnev in attendance and the sword catching the spring sun.

## Changing the Shield

For decades the woman's great shield bore the hammer and sickle of the Soviet Union. After Ukraine outlawed communist symbols in 2015, pressure grew to change it, and in 2023 the city acted. Surveys reported that among 800,000 consulted citizens, 85 percent favored replacing the Soviet emblem with Ukraine's trident, the tryzub. The old crest came off on the first of August. Crews then fitted the new one high on the shield through intense wind, heavy rain, and Russian air raids, racing to finish before Ukraine's Independence Day on the twenty-fourth. That a country at war would send workers up a sixty-meter statue under bombardment to swap one emblem for another says something about how much this figure means, and about how a monument built to serve one state can be quietly reclaimed by another.

## The Guardian of the Hills

She anchors the National Museum of the History of Ukraine in the Second World War, the memorial complex spread across the slopes near the Kyiv Pechersk Lavra, the ancient cave monastery whose golden domes she watches over. The eternal flame at her feet burns through so much gas that in leaner years it could only be lit on the most important holidays. She has drifted into stories far from home too: a scene in the novel World War Z imagines a Ukrainian tank commander fleeing a burning Kyiv beneath her gaze, and she appears in music videos and reality television. Whatever her origins as a Soviet victory monument, she has become something else now, watching over a city that has decided, palm branch or no, to keep her.

## Flight Context

Mother Ukraine stands at 50.4265 N, 30.5631 E on Kyiv's right-bank Pechersk hills, above the Dnipro. She is unmistakable from the air at 2,000 to 4,000 feet AGL, the raised sword and shield catching sun above the green slopes; the gold domes of the Kyiv Pechersk Lavra sit just to the northwest. Nearest airports are Kyiv Zhuliany (UKKK) about 7 km southwest and Boryspil International (UKBB) roughly 30 km east.
