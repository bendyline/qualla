# National Museum of Folk Architecture and Folkways of Ukraine

A Soviet official trucked a dismantled windmill past Communist Party headquarters and was scolded for hauling junk; today that junk is a 133-hectare open-air village of old Ukraine at Pyrohiv.

**Location:** 50.3545, 30.5122

**Categories:** Museums, Kyiv, Ukrainian culture, Open-air museums

## Introduction

A truck rolled slowly past the Central Committee building in Kyiv, carrying the dismantled bones of a peasant windmill. Someone at a high window noticed, and the phone rang. The first secretary of the Communist Party of Ukraine wanted to know why Petro Tronko was hauling junk through the capital. Tronko, a Soviet deputy minister who had made it his mission to save the vanishing wooden world of the Ukrainian village, answered him calmly: you will thank me for this junk one day. Half a century later, that windmill stands on a green hill south of Kyiv among more than three hundred rescued buildings, and Tronko has his answer.

## The Man Who Saved the Village

The land at Pyrohiv had been settled since the Bronze Age and was first named in records in 1627 as a holding of the Kyiv Pechersk Lavra, but the museum itself was a modern act of will. In 1969 the Soviet Ukrainian government approved a plan, pushed by writers and scholars alarmed that regional folk culture was slipping away, to build an open-air museum of traditional architecture and life. Its founder and, in the words of one colleague, its very soul was Petro Tronko, who chose the site himself and secured the land. Across 133.5 hectares he and his teams laid out the whole ethnographic map of Ukraine, arranging cottages, churches, and windmills by region so that a visitor could walk from the Carpathians to Polissia to the Middle Dnieper in an afternoon.

## Buildings That Traveled

Nothing here is a replica. Each structure is a genuine old building, found in some distant village, carefully taken apart, transported, conserved, and reassembled piece by piece on the hill. That work demanded specialized restoration workshops and endless patience, since a hand-hewn wooden church or a thatched khata does not survive being moved unless every timber is treated with care. The collection grew to more than three hundred pieces of folk architecture drawn from every major region, filled with some forty thousand objects of daily life: embroidered costumes, woven carpets, ceramics, ironwork, glassware, musical instruments. The oldest church on the grounds, the Naddniprianska, was built in 1742. Opened to visitors in 1976, the museum became a place where the ordinary genius of village builders was gathered and finally honored.

## A Living Village

Pyrohiv is not a place of glass cases and hushed rooms. Its centerpiece, Windmill Hill, catches the wind exactly as it was built to, the great sails standing over a landscape of cottages with painted walls and thatched roofs. On folk holidays the museum comes fully alive: blacksmiths hammer, potters throw, weavers work their looms, and craftspeople in traditional dress make things by hand in front of visitors, just as their great-grandparents did. Open-air celebrations mark the turning of the seasons. Walking the sectors, you pass a commoner's home from one region, a village church from another, a well, a beehive, a farmstead, each furnished with authentic objects, so that the everyday texture of old Ukrainian life is not described but simply inhabited.

## Fragile Treasures

A museum of wood and thatch lives always with fire. In the 2000s several of the museum's buildings burned, and the blaze of September 2006 destroyed one house and badly damaged two others. Officials concluded it was arson, set to cover the theft of a valuable collection of eighteenth-century painted chests displayed in the burned building. In Soviet times the museum had kept its own fire brigade and security team; after the Soviet collapse, underfunding disbanded them, leaving the treasures more exposed. Development has pressed at the edges too, with commercial projects proposed near the entrance. Yet Pyrohiv endures, a fragile ark of a rural world that industrialization and history might otherwise have swept away entirely, its windmills still turning above the roofs of a village that no longer exists anywhere but here.

## Flight Context

The museum lies at 50.3545 N, 30.5122 E in the Pyrohiv neighborhood of Kyiv's Holosiivskyi District, south of the city center. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL in clear light, look for the cluster of windmills on the open hilltop and the scattered dark rooflines of wooden churches across the green 133-hectare grounds. Nearest airports are Kyiv Zhuliany (UKKK) about 7 km north and Boryspil International (UKBB) roughly 35 km northeast.
