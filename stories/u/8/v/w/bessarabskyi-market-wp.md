# Bessarabskyi Market

At the head of Kyiv's grandest avenue stands a covered market a sugar tycoon paid for from beyond the grave, where light pours through a glass roof onto stalls of salo, honey, and fruit.

**Location:** 50.4424, 30.5216

**Categories:** Landmarks, Architecture, Ukraine, Food and Markets

## Introduction

The gift came from a dead man. In 1904, Lazar Brodsky, the sugar magnate whose refineries had helped make Kyiv rich, died and left half a million rubles to the city with a single purpose: to build a proper covered market. Six years later the money went to work, and by 1912 the Bessarabka rose at the head of the Khreshchatyk, Kyiv's great central avenue, a temple of commerce built for onions and honey and lard. More than a century on, it is still exactly that, one of the oldest and most beautiful covered markets in Europe, and still, every single day, a working bazaar.

## A Market Under Glass

The building went up between 1910 and 1912 to a design by the Polish architect Henryk Julian Gay, in the flowing lines of late Art Nouveau that were then sweeping across Europe. Its glory is the roof: a great arc of glass rising some fifteen meters, so that daylight floods down onto the trading floor below. The façade was never left plain. Sculptors carved it with bas-reliefs of livestock and peasant scenes, cattle and harvest imagery that told anyone passing exactly what business was done inside. Gay's plans laid out around 896 square meters of trading space, with dozens of individual stalls, a vast central hall where more than two hundred vendors could sell at once, and even a restaurant. The grand opening, on 16 July 1912, drew the city's and the region's leadership to admire what Brodsky's bequest had built.

## The Name from the South

The market takes its name from Bessarabia, the historical region to the southwest, a fertile land the Russian Empire had won during its long series of wars with the Ottoman Turks. Part of old Bessarabia lies today in southwestern Ukraine, in the Odesa Oblast, and part beyond the border. The square where the market stands, Bessarabska Square, carried that southern name, and the hall took it in turn. It is a fitting label for a place built to gather the produce of a rich agricultural country. The name hints at the reach of the trade that once flowed through here, drawing goods toward the capital from the black-earth farmland and the warm south alike.

## Anchor of the Khreshchatyk

Location made the Bessarabka more than a market; it made it a landmark. It sits at the southwestern end of the Khreshchatyk, the broad boulevard that is the beating heart of Kyiv, the street where the city gathers to celebrate, to protest, and simply to stroll. Anyone walking the length of that famous avenue arrives, at its far end, at the domed bulk of the market hall. Through revolution, two world wars, Soviet decades, and independence, the Bessarabka has held its corner. Buildings all around it have risen and fallen, but this one endured, a fixed point that generations of Kyivans have used to orient themselves in their own city.

## Still Alive with Trade

What makes the Bessarabka remarkable is not merely that it survives, but that it still does the job it was built for. Step inside and the glass roof lights row upon row of stalls piled with fruit and vegetables, jars of honey, cured meats, pickles, and mounds of Ukrainian salo, the cured pork fat that is a national delicacy and, for many, a point of pride. Vendors call out prices and press samples on passersby, their traditional stalls now joined by a food hall of dining corners and a bar added in a 2025 renovation. It is at once a tourist sight and a genuine grocery, a place where a visitor comes to photograph the architecture and a local comes to buy dinner. That double life, monument and marketplace, is precisely what Lazar Brodsky's gift was meant to create, and it has outlasted almost everyone who was there to see it open.

## Flight Context

The Bessarabskyi Market stands at 50.44°N, 30.52°E, in central Kyiv on Bessarabska Square at the southwestern end of the Khreshchatyk. From altitude it is a small landmark within the dense city center; the long line of the Khreshchatyk and the Dnieper River to the east are the easier orienting features. The nearest airport is Kyiv's Zhuliany/Ihor Sikorsky (UKKK); Boryspil International (UKBB) lies farther east.
