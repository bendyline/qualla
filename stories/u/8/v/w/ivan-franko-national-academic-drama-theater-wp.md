# Ivan Franko National Academic Drama Theater

A troupe of artists in their twenties founded Ukraine's leading theater in 1920, then kept it alive on foot, touring the mines of the Donbas when no other company could survive.

**Location:** 50.4456, 30.5283

**Categories:** Culture, Theater, History, Kyiv

## Introduction

They were absurdly young. When the Ivan Franko Theater staged its first performance in Vinnytsia on the 28th of January, 1920, its founder Hnat Yura was thirty-two, and around him stood a company barely out of adolescence: his wife Olha Rubchakivna was seventeen, others in their early twenties. What this handful of young people built would become the first stage of Ukraine, the country's leading drama theater, home over the decades to its greatest actors. But nothing about that future was guaranteed on opening night. In its first years the theater had no home at all, and its survival came down to something almost embarrassingly simple: an unwillingness to stop working.

## A Theater on Foot

The early Franko Theater was a company perpetually in motion. It spent its first years traveling the towns and villages of central Ukraine, then pushed east into the Donbas, carrying a repertoire that mixed Lesya Ukrainka's Forest Song with Beaumarchais and the folk classics. Yura himself said flatly that no theater could have survived the conditions his did, that only a craving for work and an incredible dedication to the stage kept it alive. The actors traveled for six months at a stretch, through mines and factories and workers' settlements, sometimes on foot. One performance of Forest Song in Horlivka took place in a huge park on a blazing sunny day, with no scenery at all, the actors dressed only in the fabulous costumes their designer had made. That brutal Donbas tour, improbably, was what convinced the authorities the company mattered, elevating it to a state theater.

## Coming Home to Kyiv

The theater's path to its permanent stage ran through a game of cities. In 1923 it moved to Kharkiv, then the capital of Soviet Ukraine, and in the summer of 1926 the government sent it to Kyiv, swapping places with the Berezil Theater that traveled the other way. Since then the Franko Theater has occupied the building of the former Solovtsov Theater, set in a small park beside Bankova Street in the heart of the government quarter. Here it built its core company through the 1920s and 30s and produced the masterworks of national and world drama, from Beaumarchais and Schiller to Hasek's Good Soldier Svejk. A monument in the little park still honors Yura in the role of Svejk, the character he made his mascot, the clown-survivor who somehow matched the theater's own knack for outlasting hard times.

## The Classics as Refuge

The 1930s pressed the theater hard toward propaganda. By the middle of the decade it had switched almost entirely to Soviet-themed plays; of thirteen productions staged between 1930 and 1934, only one was a classic. Sensing what was being lost, Yura returned in 1933 to The Marriage of Figaro, the beloved production that had carried the company through its earliest struggles, and played Figaro himself. Again and again, the classics became a refuge, a way to keep the craft alive when the times demanded slogans. During the Second World War the company worked on in evacuation, in Semipalatinsk and Tashkent, staging Shevchenko and Kotliarevsky far from home. In 1940 it earned the academic title; the great directors and actors who followed, above all Serhiy Danchenko, made it once more, in the phrase people used, the First Scene of Ukraine.

## Stupka's Stage

From 2001 to 2012 the theater was led by Bohdan Stupka, a Hero and People's Artist of Ukraine and one of the country's most revered performers. He widened the repertoire in ways that would have startled earlier generations, bringing ancient Indian drama, a twentieth-century Polish avant-gardist, and the great Russian novelists to the Kyiv stage, and inviting directors of wildly opposing methods to test the company. He left behind a charge as much as a legacy. We have no moral right, he said, to erase what the luminaries of this stage created; instead we must accomplish what they did not, because the theater's future in fifty or a hundred years depends on how the next generation remembers us. His words still hang over a company that has spent more than a century refusing to be forgotten, its roughly forty productions a season carrying Ukrainian theater into the world.

## Flight Context

The Ivan Franko Theater sits at 50.4456 N, 30.5283 E in a small park beside Bankova Street in Kyiv's central government quarter, Pecherskyi District, near the House with Chimaeras and the Presidential Office. From about 1,500 feet AGL, look for the ornate theater building amid dense city blocks just west of the Dnieper bluff and the green stripe of Khreshchatyk. Nearest airport is Kyiv International (Zhuliany), ICAO UKKK, about 7 km west; Boryspil International, UKBB, lies east of the city.
