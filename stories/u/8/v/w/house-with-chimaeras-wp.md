# House with Chimaeras

Mermaids, dolphins, and drowning ships crawl across an apartment building a Polish architect built to win a bet on unbuildable swampland.

**Location:** 50.4450, 30.5286

**Categories:** Architecture, Art Nouveau, History, Kyiv

## Introduction

Look up on Bankova Street and the walls begin to move. Frogs perch on the roofline. Mermaids coil beneath the windows. Ships sink slowly into the facade, and the antlers of hunted game jut out over the sidewalk, all of it rendered in gray cement gone soft with a century of weather. The House with Chimaeras was never meant to be quiet. The Polish architect Wladyslaw Horodecki built it between 1901 and 1902 as his own luxury apartment block, and he covered it in a bestiary so strange that Kyivans have been telling stories about it ever since. That he chose this particular hillside, a lot the city had forbidden anyone to build on, only deepened the legend.

## The Gaudi of Kyiv

Horodecki trained at the Imperial Academy of Arts in Saint Petersburg and arrived in Kyiv in 1890, where he would live for nearly thirty years. His house is pure Art Nouveau, then a daring new style built on flowing lines and motifs drawn from plants and living creatures. He pushed the idea to its limit, filling the exterior with mythical beasts and big-game animals until admirers began calling him the Gaudi of Kyiv, comparing his work to Barcelona's Casa Mila. The technical daring matched the visual audacity. Part of the foundation rested on concrete piles, part on a continuous base, two approaches that usually cannot be mixed. Horodecki somehow married them and made the impossible lot hold. Inside, each floor was a single grand apartment reached by elevator; he kept the fourth floor, some 380 square meters, for himself, complete with a cowshed on the premises so he could have fresh milk.

## Sala's Sculpted Menagerie

The creatures crawling across the house are the work of the Italian sculptor Emilio Sala, who handled both the exterior and the astonishing interior. On the roof he set mermaids, dolphins, and frogs; on the outer walls, sinking ships and hunting trophies; inside, grand staircases and chandeliers shaped like enormous catfish tangled in the stems of lotus flowers. Everything was cast in cement, and that choice was itself a kind of advertisement. Horodecki co-directed a cement company, and in an age when the material was still an unglamorous novelty, the entire fantastical house doubled as a showroom, proof of what the new stuff could do. The mermaids and the drowning galleons were art, certainly, but they were also a salesman's flourish, a promise carved in the very product he hoped the city would buy.

## Three Legends and a Foreclosure

Kyiv has spun the house into folklore, and three stories cling to it. The first holds that Horodecki's daughter drowned herself in the Dnieper over a doomed love or a family rift, and that a grieving, heartbroken father raised this gloomy house in her memory. The second, more likely, says Horodecki built it to win a bet, that rival architects had declared the swampy hillside unbuildable and he proved them wrong. The third is a curse: that when he lost the building to his creditors in 1913, he cursed every tenant to ruin, and the tale insists that the businesses who rented rooms there did go bankrupt or fall apart. What the record confirms is plainer and sadder. Financial trouble forced Horodecki to sell; the house was auctioned off, later crammed with nine or ten families per apartment, and eventually turned into a Communist Party polyclinic. The dreamer lost his masterpiece, whatever the reason.

## A Palace for Presidents

The house has risen far above its polyclinic years. Sitting directly across from the office of the President of Ukraine, it was restored beginning in 2003 and made an official residence for diplomatic ceremonies, with rooms for negotiations, private talks, document signings, and the press. During the works, laborers uncovered an entire lower floor that Soviet builders had filled in decades earlier to shore up the foundation, a buried story returned to daylight. The street outside is now closed to cars, a guarded pedestrian zone. Where Horodecki once schemed to sell cement and win an argument with skeptics, heads of state now pass beneath his mermaids. The animals still watch from the walls, indifferent to who walks below, exactly as their creator intended.

## Flight Context

The House with Chimaeras stands at 50.445 N, 30.529 E on Bankova Street in Kyiv's Lypky/Pecherskyi district, directly opposite the Presidential Office Building. From about 1,500 feet AGL it is hard to pick out its detailed sculpture, but it sits within the dense government quarter just west of the Dnieper bluff, near the Ivan Franko Theater and Mariinskyi Palace. Nearest airport is Kyiv International (Zhuliany), ICAO UKKK, roughly 7 km west; Boryspil International, UKBB, lies to the east.
