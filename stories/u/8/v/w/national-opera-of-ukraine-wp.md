# National Opera of Ukraine

During an intermission in 1911, with the Tsar watching from his box, a gunman crossed the aisle of Kyiv's opera house and shot the prime minister of the Russian Empire.

**Location:** 50.4467, 30.5125

**Categories:** Opera houses, Kyiv, Music, Ukrainian culture

## Introduction

The second act had ended, and Prime Minister Pyotr Stolypin stood near the front of the parterre, his back to the stage, chatting during the intermission. The Tsar was in the imperial box. Stolypin's bodyguard had stepped out for a cigarette. A young man in evening dress named Dmitry Bogrov walked toward him and fired twice, once into the arm and once into the chest. Wounded, Stolypin turned toward the imperial box, saw the Tsar, and made a gesture with both hands, waving him back to safety. The orchestra struck up God Save the Tsar. It was September 1911, inside the Kyiv opera house, and one of the empire's most powerful men was dying between the music.

## A Company Born in a Borrowed Hall

The Kyiv Opera was founded in the summer of 1867, making it the second oldest opera company in Ukraine after Odesa and before Lviv. Its founder, Ferdinand Berger, gathered singers, musicians, and conductors and persuaded the city council to lend the troupe the City Theatre. The first performance, on November 8, 1867, was declared a civic holiday, and the company opened with Askold's Tomb, an opera drawn from the ancient legends of Kyiv itself. Early seasons leaned on Russian works and translated European favorites, from Rossini's Barber of Seville to Mozart's Marriage of Figaro, though it was the operas of Verdi that Kyivans came to love best. A company that would one day sing in Ukrainian began life performing largely in Russian, in a theater it did not yet own.

## Fire and Rebirth

On the fourth of February 1896, after a morning performance of Tchaikovsky's Eugene Onegin, an unextinguished candle set the City Theatre alight. Within hours the building was gone, and with it one of the largest musical libraries in Europe, along with costumes and sets for countless productions. Homeless, the troupe scattered across the city, performing on other stages and once even in the ring of Krutikov's Circus. The city then held an international competition for a new opera house, won by the architect Victor Schröter, whose Neo-Renaissance building opened in the first years of the twentieth century. Its stage was among the largest in Europe, engineered to the newest standards, a fitting home for a company reborn from ashes and now built to last.

## The Sound of a Nation

The opera house was never only about music. In 1917, as the Russian Empire collapsed, it hosted congresses that helped proclaim Ukrainian self-rule, and under the short-lived Ukrainian State it began staging Faust, La Traviata, and Madame Butterfly in Ukrainian. Even after the Soviet takeover, a 1926 decree kept operas in the Ukrainian language, a policy that quietly affirmed the worth of Ukrainian culture and endured until the early 1990s. The Soviets nationalized the theater, decorated it with the Order of Lenin, and in 1939 named it for Taras Shevchenko, the poet who is Ukraine's national conscience. In 1981 the company premiered a ballet on the life of Olga of Kyiv, the medieval princess, to mark the city's 1,500th anniversary.

## The Curtain and the War

In the decades since, the Shevchenko company has carried its work across the world, touring Japan, Australia, Brazil, the United States, and dozens of other countries, its ballet company famous for a Romeo and Juliet, first staged in 1971, that ran for over forty years and won a UNESCO medal as a definitive reading of Prokofiev. In 2017 Ukraine marked the company's 150th anniversary. Then history intruded once more. The last performance scheduled before the 2022 Russian invasion was Natalka Poltavka, a beloved Ukrainian opera about ordinary village life and love. That such a tender, homegrown work should be the final note before the guns is its own kind of eloquence, a reminder that this house has always been where Ukraine sings itself into being, in the best of times and the worst.

## Flight Context

The National Opera stands at 50.4467 N, 30.5125 E on Volodymyrska Street in Kyiv's central Shevchenkivskyi District. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL, orient on the historic core near St. Volodymyr's Cathedral and the Golden Gate; the ornate Neo-Renaissance opera house sits among the dense streets just east of them. Nearest airports are Kyiv Zhuliany (UKKK) about 6 km southwest and Boryspil International (UKBB) roughly 32 km east.
