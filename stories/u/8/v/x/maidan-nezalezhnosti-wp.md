# Maidan Nezalezhnosti

Kyiv's Independence Square has worn seven names in a century and a half, and twice in a decade its pavement became the place where Ukraine remade itself.

**Location:** 50.4502, 30.5239

**Categories:** history, landmark, ukraine, kyiv

## Introduction

The name is a small history lesson in itself. Maidan is a word Ukrainian borrowed from Persian, by way of the Turkic languages of the steppe, meaning an open space where a town gathers, a place to meet and to march. Of all the squares in Kyiv, only this one is officially a maidan; every other is a plain ploshcha, the ordinary Ukrainian word for square. That single borrowed word now carries enormous weight, because Independence Square, Maidan Nezalezhnosti, is where a nation has repeatedly decided who it wants to be, on cold pavement, under snow, in front of the watching world. To say the Maidan, in Ukraine today, is to name not a place but a moment when the people gathered and would not go home.

## Seven Names for One Place

Few plots of ground have been renamed so often, and the list reads like a compressed history of the city itself. Before the tenth century this low, marshy spot outside the walls was called Perevisyshche, and later Kyivans knew it bluntly as Goat Swamp. Beside it once stood the Lyadski Gates of old Kyiv, torn down when Batu Khan's Mongols stormed the city in 1240. As Kyiv boomed into the third city of the Russian Empire, the square became a market, then Duma Square when the city council built here, then Soviet Square, then Kalinin Square, then, in 1977, October Revolution Square, crowned with a cubist monument to the Bolshevik uprising. On 26 August 1991, two days after Ukraine declared independence, it took the name it holds now. Each renaming buried the one before, until the square became a palimpsest of every regime that ruled the city.

## The Guardian on the Column

The square most people picture today was largely rebuilt in the early 2000s, and its landmarks tell a story older than any government. Near the fountains stands a monument to Kyi, Shchek, Khoryv, and their sister Lybid, the legendary founders of the city. Nearby rises the Cossack folk hero Mamay and the Archangel Michael, Kyiv's ancient protector. At the center soars the Independence Monument, a tall victory column raised in 2001 for the tenth anniversary of independence, and atop it stands Berehynia, a protective figure drawn from Ukrainian folklore, arms lifted, a guardian spirit watching over the square below. Beneath the pavement, a shopping mall replaced the grim old underpass Kyivans had nicknamed the Tube. Not everyone loved the redesign at first, and some suspected the reconstruction ordered in 2001 was partly meant to disrupt the protests then filling the square. But the new figures soon became inseparable from the image of the city center. It is a stage set built from myth and memory, waiting, as it turned out, for history to arrive.

## Where a Country Chose Itself

History arrived again and again. In 1990 students staged the hunger strikes of the Revolution on Granite here, forcing a Soviet official's resignation. In 2001 came the Ukraine without Kuchma protests. In late 2004, during the Orange Revolution, hundreds of thousands filled the square and pitched tents for weeks in the cold, and the world watched. Then, in the winter of 2013 and 2014, the Revolution of Dignity turned this ground into something graver. People died on and around the square, remembered now as the Heavenly Hundred, and the nearby Trade Unions House burned. After that, Kyiv quietly moved its Christmas fairs and celebrations to another square, deciding that festivity did not belong where people had been killed. Today a makeshift memorial marks those lost in the years since, and a nearby avenue is named for the Heroes of the Heavenly Hundred. The maidan remains what its name always promised: the open place where a people comes together, and refuses to be moved.

## Flight Context

Independence Square sits along Khreshchatyk Street in central Kyiv at 50.4503 N, 30.5239 E. From 2,000 to 4,000 feet the tall Independence Monument, the broad cut of Khreshchatyk, and the Dnipro to the east help orient the view, with the gold domes of Saint Sophia just to the northwest. Kyiv Zhuliany (UKKK) is the nearest airport; Boryspil International (UKBB) lies east across the river.
