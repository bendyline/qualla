# Dovzhenko Film Studios

Kyiv's great film studio shot its very first movie at night, because by day the crews were still building the walls around them.

**Location:** 50.4558, 30.4498

**Categories:** film, history, ukraine, kyiv

## Introduction

The first film ever made here was shot in darkness. On the night of 12 October 1927, cameras rolled on a comedy called Ivanko and the Butcher inside pavilions that did not yet exist by daylight. The builders worked through the daylight hours raising the walls; the filmmakers moved in after dusk to make movies among the scaffolding. That improvised beginning set the tone for what became the Dovzhenko Film Studios, one of the oldest and largest in Ukraine, a place that has been building itself and making art in the same breath for nearly a century on the western edge of Kyiv. From those scaffolded first nights would come films that traveled the world and others the state tried to bury.

## A Cinema Factory on the Steppe

It began as an act of ambition by a young Soviet Ukraine hungry for its own image. In 1925 the state film directorate invited proposals for a cinema factory, and out of twenty entries chose the design of Valerian Rykov, who led a team of architecture students from the Kyiv Art Institute to break ground in 1927. When it opened it was the largest studio in the Ukrainian republic. The talent came fast, and it came from everywhere: directors, cameramen, and writers, among them the poet Mykola Bazhan, who would together shape a national art. Within two years the studio was turning out ten films annually. Alexander Dovzhenko himself worked here, and in 1929 Dziga Vertov produced his dizzying documentary Man with a Movie Camera through the studio, a film still studied and celebrated around the world for reinventing what a camera could do.

## War, Exile, and a Return

History kept interrupting the work. From 1930 the state took firm control of cinema, bending it toward the spread of communist ideology, yet the 1930s were still formative years for the Kyiv film factory. Then came October 1941. As German forces closed on the city, the entire studio was uprooted and evacuated more than two thousand miles east to Tashkent, in Soviet Central Asia, where it kept making films until Kyiv was freed. During the occupation, the abandoned premises were turned to producing propaganda. When peace returned, so did the studio, and in 1957 it was renamed in honor of Oleksandr Dovzhenko, the poet-filmmaker whose lyrical Earth had made the place famous. Along the avenue beside the studio still grows an apple orchard, planted on Dovzhenko's own order, a quiet memorial that blossoms every spring. Few film studios anywhere carry their history quite so literally, with a founder's trees still rooted at the gate and his name on the sign above it.

## Where Ukraine Filmed Its Soul

For all the ideology imposed from above, extraordinary art escaped these gates. In 1964 Sergei Parajanov shot Shadows of Forgotten Ancestors here, a hallucinatory Carpathian folk tragedy that stunned audiences and drew the suspicion of Soviet censors precisely because it was too original, too Ukrainian. The studio's roster runs from wartime dramas and gentle comedies like Chasing Two Hares and Queen of the Gas Station to poetic experiments that pushed hard against the limits of the permitted. After independence in 1991, the films turned openly to subjects the Soviet era had buried, including Famine-33, a searing reckoning with the great famine of the 1930s that Soviet authorities had denied for decades. In 2009 the complex opened a studio for the digital restoration of Ukrainian films, giving its own history a way to survive into the future. Today the Dovzhenko studio still operates as a full production facility and houses a museum in the pavilion where Dovzhenko once filmed, its walls lined with plaques honoring the artists who worked in the dark to make a country visible to itself.

## Flight Context

The studio complex lies on Peremohy Avenue in western Kyiv at 50.4558 N, 30.4498 E. From 2,500 to 4,500 feet the dense city grid and the green ribbon of the Dnipro to the east help orient the view, with Kyiv Zhuliany (UKKK) airport just to the south of the studio. Boryspil International (UKBB) sits farther east across the river.
