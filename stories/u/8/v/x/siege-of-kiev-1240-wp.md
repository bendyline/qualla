# Siege of Kiev (1240)

In the winter of 1240, the Mongols crossed a frozen Dnieper and battered down the walls of the greatest city in the Rus' world, leaving only six of its forty great buildings standing.

**Location:** 50.4500, 30.5236

**Categories:** history, medieval, Mongol, Kyiv

## Introduction

They may have waited for the river to freeze. A Mongol army moves on wheels as much as on horses, and to bring their catapults, baggage carts, and rolling yurts across the wide Dnieper, Batu Khan's host likely paused until mid-November 1240, when the ice grew thick enough to bear the weight. Then they crossed. On the far bank stood Kyiv, the mother of Rus' cities, its golden domes catching the low winter sun. A Mongol prince who saw it, Batu's cousin Mongke, was reportedly struck by its splendor and sent envoys to offer terms of surrender. The Kievans killed them. What came next was one of the most catastrophic sieges of the medieval world.

## The Storm Gathers

The blow had been coming for years. Batu Khan launched his western campaign in late 1237, crushing the northeastern Rus' principality of Ryazan, then turning to destroy Vladimir and Kozelsk, then taking Pereyaslav and Chernihiv. City after city fell, and Kyiv was next in his sights. The city changed hands in the chaos; by 1240 it was held by the powerful prince Daniel of Galicia, Danylo Romanovych, but he was not there. Danylo had gone to Hungary, desperately seeking allies to stave off the invasion, and command in Kyiv fell to his voivode, Dmytro. Before the main assault even began, Batu's forces destroyed the Chorni Klobuky, a body of allied warriors marching to relieve the city. Then the entire Mongol army encamped outside the walls, and the encirclement was complete.

## Nine Days of Thunder

The siege was short and brutal. On 28 November the Mongols positioned their catapults near one of old Kyiv's three gates, at a spot where tree cover crept almost up to the walls and gave the engines shelter. For several days the bombardment pounded the fortifications, a relentless hammering of stone against stone. On 6 December the walls finally gave way, and the fighting spilled into the streets, house to house, hand to hand. The Kievans suffered terribly. In the surviving accounts one small human detail stands out: the defenders had earlier captured a Mongol soldier named Tovrul, who told them the names of every enemy commander, so they went into their last battle knowing exactly who was destroying them. Voivode Dmytro was struck by an arrow but fought on.

## What Was Left

When the smoke cleared, most of Kyiv had burned. By one chronicle's account, only six of the city's forty great buildings still stood. A place that had dazzled visitors, that a Mongol prince had paused to admire, was reduced to ruins in little more than a week. Yet the same records preserve one act of restraint amid the devastation: Dmytro, the wounded commander, was shown mercy and spared, reportedly out of respect for his courage. It is a thin thread of humanity in a dark story, and perhaps that is why the chroniclers held onto it. The people of Kyiv had no such mercy; the sources speak of heavy losses and of a population left, at best, in servitude to a new overlord.

## The World That Followed

The fall of Kyiv broke the back of resistance in the region. Galicia and Volhynia were forced to submit to Batu Khan, opening the road for the Mongols to sweep on into Hungary and Poland. Their westward advance halted only in 1242, when word arrived that the Great Khan Ogedei had died and Batu was summoned home to help choose a successor. In the aftermath, the Rus' princes made their pilgrimages of submission to Sarai, the new Golden Horde capital on the Volga, to be confirmed in their lands as vassals of the khan. For Kyiv, the change was profound. The city that had been the beating heart of the Rus' world entered a long twilight of foreign overlordship, its greatest age now behind it. But its memory, and its ruined stones, endured.

## Flight Context

The siege unfolded around medieval Kyiv at roughly 50.4500 N, 30.5236 E on the Dnieper's high right bank. From 2,500 to 3,500 feet the historic upper town and the broad river the Mongols crossed on ice are the defining features, with St. Sophia's domes marking the ancient core.
