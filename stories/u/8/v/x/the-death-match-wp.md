# The Death Match

In occupied Kyiv in 1942, a team of bakery workers, most of them former Dynamo stars, beat a German side twice, and a legend grew up around them that took fifty years to untangle from the truth.

**Location:** 50.4560, 30.4812

**Categories:** history, World War II, football, Kyiv

## Introduction

The men who played were bakers, at least on paper. In the summer of 1942, in a Kyiv under German occupation, a group of former professional footballers had found work at Bread Factory No. 1, baking loaves for the occupiers because it was the surest way to feed their families. When they took the field on 9 August as a scratch team called Start, most of them had once worn the colors of Dynamo Kyiv or Lokomotiv Kyiv. Before some 2,000 spectators they beat a German side, Flakelf, by 5 to 3. What happened to them afterward would become one of the most retold, and most distorted, stories of the war.

## A Team from a Bakery

When the Germans took Kyiv, they dissolved all Soviet clubs and later permitted new ones. The famous Dynamo goalkeeper Mykola Trusevich and several teammates chose the bakery over a club they distrusted, because working there guaranteed food for themselves and their families. The bakery's director, an engineer named Joseph Kordik, encouraged them to form a team, and Start was born. On 6 August they beat Flakelf 5 to 1. Three days later came the rematch, and again Start won, 5 to 3. There is a small, human detail the sources preserve: after the second victory the players shared a glass of homemade vodka and gathered for a party that evening, ordinary men savoring an extraordinary afternoon, with no idea what lay ahead.

## The Legend and the Facts

After the war, Soviet writers turned the match into myth. In the telling that reached millions, an SS officer had warned the players to lose, they had defiantly won anyway, and they were marched straight from the pitch to execution as punishment. The story inspired novels, a film seen by some 32 million Soviet viewers, and eventually echoes in Western movies. When Ukrainian historians finally examined the archives after 1991, a more complicated truth emerged. The Germans arrested several Start players, but not until more than a week after the match, and the eyewitnesses, including one of the ball boys and surviving players themselves, said there had been no SS ultimatum, no armed guards with dogs ringing the field. The victory was real; the fairy tale around it was not.

## What Really Happened to Them

The real fates of these men were no less tragic for being tangled. In mid-August the Gestapo arrested a group of the players at the bakery. Some, denounced as former members of the Soviet security services, were sent to a labor camp. One, Mykola Korotkykh, was tortured to death after an identity card was found on him. Several months later, amid camp reprisals that had nothing to do with football, others were killed, among them the goalkeeper Trusevich. Some players survived and, after the war, were treated with suspicion by Soviet authorities for having lived under occupation at all. Their deaths belonged not to a single defiant afternoon but to the vast, indiscriminate cruelty of the war swirling around them.

## Remembering Them Honestly

It fell to the survivors to set the record straight. On the 50th anniversary of the match, the former Dynamo player Makar Honcharenko spoke on Kyiv radio and refused the propaganda version. His teammates, he said, died as so many Soviet people died, caught between two totalitarian systems bent on destroying each other, destined to become victims of a massacre far larger than any game. That honesty does not diminish the men; it dignifies them. They were not comic-book heroes struck down for a scoreline. They were footballers and fathers who kept playing the game they loved in the middle of a catastrophe, and who deserve to be remembered as they actually were. Monuments in Kyiv still honor them, and the stadium where they played once bore the name of their team.
