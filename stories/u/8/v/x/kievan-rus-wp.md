# Kievan Rus'

A thousand years ago Kyiv was the seat of a medieval superpower whose princesses became queens across Europe and whose choice of faith still echoes today.

**Location:** 50.4500, 30.5250

**Categories:** history, medieval, ukraine, kyiv

## Introduction

When a prince named Vladimir set out to choose a religion for his realm, he sent envoys to sample the options. They visited Muslims, Jews, and the Latin Christians of Rome, and found reasons to pass on each. Then they entered the cathedral of Hagia Sophia in Constantinople, stood beneath its impossible dome, and later reported that they no longer knew whether they were on earth or in heaven. On the strength of that beauty, Vladimir accepted Byzantine Christianity around the year 988 and remade his people. That people were the Kievan Rus, and from their capital on the Dnipro they ruled the largest state in Europe, a civilization most Westerners have entirely forgotten. For two glittering centuries, Kyiv was not a provincial outpost but a center of the medieval world.

## A Capital That Rivaled Paris

By the year 1200, Kyiv held somewhere between 36,000 and 50,000 people, roughly the size of Paris at the same moment and larger than London. This was no rough frontier town. Literacy ran high in the great Rus cities. Novgorod laid wooden pavement and a sewage system that most of Europe lacked. The realm's law code, the Russkaya Pravda, promulgated under Yaroslav the Wise, favored fines over the executioner's axe and granted women rights of property and inheritance unusual for the age. Trade flowed down the Dnipro to Constantinople and out across the steppe, carried past the nomadic Pechenegs through alliances that shifted like weather; keeping that river road open mattered so much that peace with the raiders was a matter of survival. On the eve of its fall, one Soviet scholar counted some three hundred urban centers scattered across the land of the Rus, a network of towns binding an enormous territory together.

## The Family That Married Europe

Yaroslav the Wise understood that a dynasty could reach further than an army. He built Kyiv's Saint Sophia Cathedral, patronized the monasteries that grew into the great Kyiv Pechersk Lavra, promulgated the realm's first written law code, and married his own children into half the courts of the continent. He himself wed the only legitimate daughter of the Swedish king who Christianized Sweden. His own daughters became queens of France, Hungary, and Norway; his sister wed a Polish king; his sons married a Byzantine emperor's daughter and a pope's niece. A granddaughter became a German empress, and a grandson married the daughter of the last Anglo-Saxon king of England before the Norman Conquest swept that world away. The Rurikid rulers of Kyiv were, for a few glittering generations, among the best-connected royalty in Christendom, their bloodline threaded quietly through the pedigrees of kings from the Atlantic to the Byzantine frontier.

## The Siege That Ended a World

The end came from the east. In the thirteenth century the Mongol armies of Batu Khan swept across the Rus lands, storming city after city, and in December 1240 they laid siege to Kyiv itself. The fall of the capital is generally taken as the end of Kievan Rus. Batu pushed on to raid Poland and Hungary and founded the Golden Horde on the lower Volga, and the unified realm the Rurikids had built dissolved into the shadow of the steppe empire. What survived was memory and inheritance. Three modern nations, Ukraine, Russia, and Belarus, trace threads of their origin to this vanished state, and historians increasingly describe it on its own terms: a multi-ethnic medieval power that belonged wholly to none of its heirs. The word Rus itself, and the land it named, have been claimed and reclaimed ever since, but the state that bore the name was its own thing, larger and stranger than any later map. Beneath Kyiv's golden domes, the ground still remembers when this was the center of the eastern Christian world.

## Flight Context

The historic heart of Kievan Rus lies on the high right bank of the Dnipro at roughly 50.45 N, 30.525 E, around Saint Sophia Cathedral and the Kyiv Pechersk Lavra. From 3,000 to 5,000 feet the river's broad curve and the clustered gold domes make unmistakable landmarks. Kyiv Zhuliany (UKKK) is the nearest airport; Boryspil International (UKBB) lies east across the river.
