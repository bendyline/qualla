# National Art Museum of Ukraine

Behind two enormous concrete lions on a Kyiv hillside sits the most complete collection of Ukrainian art on Earth, a museum that changed its name almost as often as the country changed hands.

**Location:** 50.4494, 30.5311

**Categories:** museum, art, architecture, Kyiv

## Introduction

Two concrete lions crouch at the top of a broad flight of stairs on Hrushevsky Street, guarding a temple front that looks as though it was airlifted from ancient Greece and set down above the Dnieper. They have kept watch for more than a century, through empire and revolution, occupation and independence. Behind them lies the National Art Museum of Ukraine, home to the most representative collection of Ukrainian figurative art in the world, roughly forty thousand works reaching from the icons of Kievan Rus' to the restless experiments of the present day. The lions never moved. Almost everything around them did.

## A Temple for a New Idea

The building was born from an idea that was radical for Kyiv at the end of the 19th century: a museum freely open to the public, funded by the city's intellectuals and merchant patrons rather than reserved for a court. The design began with Moscow architect Petr Boitsov, but it was the Polish architect Wladyslaw Horodecki who carried it through to completion around the turn of the century. The result is unabashedly neoclassical, a precise six-column Doric porch crowned by a frieze depicting the Triumph of Arts. An Italian sculptor, Emilio Sala, gave the entrance its griffins and those two massive lions. Much of the cost was covered privately: the wealthy Tereshchenko family, sugar magnates who also founded another Kyiv museum, paid a substantial share. When the doors opened in 1904, just before Christmas, the institution carried the imperial name of Emperor Nicholas II.

## A Museum of Many Names

Few buildings in Kyiv have worn as many names. After the 1919 nationalization it became the First State Museum, then the Taras Shevchenko All-Ukrainian Historical Museum, then the State Ukrainian Museum, then the State Museum of Ukrainian Art. During the German occupation of Kyiv in 1942, its collection was folded into a Russian holding under the label the State Museum of East European Art. The old status was restored in 1944, and only in 1994, three years after Ukraine's independence, did it settle into the name it holds today. Each relabeling was more than bureaucracy. The titles tracked the fortunes of a country that kept being redefined by others, and they marked the moments when parts of the collection were split off to seed new museums across the city.

## Icons, Cossacks, and the Forbidden Avant-Garde

Inside, the story of Ukrainian art unfolds almost chronologically. It opens with a rarity: a twelfth-century polychrome wooden relief of Saint George, Byzantine in origin, followed by medieval icons carried in from Western Ukraine and Galicia. The Baroque rooms glow with gilded iconostases and the first secular portraits ever painted in Ukraine, alongside the beloved folk image of the Cossack bandura player. Then comes the twentieth century, and with it a wound. The avant-garde figures gathered here, including the sculptor Alexander Archipenko and the followers of Mykhailo Boychuk, were branded during the Soviet era as repressed and prohibited artists; the Boychukists in particular were largely erased. Only now do their canvases hang where they belong. The museum also holds work tied to Kazimir Malevich, the Kyiv-born pioneer of geometric abstraction whose black square helped rewrite modern art.

## History Delivered to the Door

The collection is still growing, and sometimes history arrives unannounced. In April 2014, after President Viktor Yanukovych fled the country, artworks recovered from his lavish estate went on public display in these very halls, an unplanned exhibition that drew crowds curious to see what power had hoarded behind closed gates. In the years since, the museum has also quietly gathered the underground art of the Soviet decades, the paintings that circulated privately from the late 1950s until 1991 because they could not circulate at all. It is fitting work for this place. A museum that survived so many renamings has become, in effect, a keeper of the things earlier regimes tried to hide.

## Flight Context

The museum sits at 50.4494 N, 30.5311 E on Kyiv's right-bank bluffs above the Dnieper, near government hill. From roughly 2,500 to 3,500 feet the white Doric temple front stands out against the surrounding parkland, with the broad Dnieper River and Kyiv's cluster of golden domes as the dominant landmarks to the east and north. The nearest major fields are Kyiv Zhuliany (UKKK) to the southwest and Boryspil International (UKBB) farther east.
