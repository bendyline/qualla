# Mikhail Bulgakov Museum

On Kyiv's winding artists' street stands a house that a great writer turned into a novel, and that his neighbors' daughter saved so the novel could become a museum.

**Location:** 50.4608, 30.5147

**Categories:** literature, museum, ukraine, kyiv

## Introduction

The writer put his own house into a book, and now you can walk into the book. Mikhail Bulgakov grew up at No. 13 on the Andriyivskyy Descent, the steep cobbled street that spills down a Kyiv hillside, and when he wrote his 1925 novel The White Guard, this house became the home of his fictional Turbin family, its rooms and its address rendered in vivid detail. Today those same rooms are a museum, filled with the furniture and objects of a vanished household. Few places anywhere let you step so completely off the page and into the world that inspired it. The address, the staircase, the light through the windows, all of it belongs equally to memory and to fiction.

## The House on the Descent

The building went up in 1888, designed by the architect N. Gardenin, a comfortable house on one of Kyiv's most atmospheric streets. Bulgakov, born in the city in 1891, lived here through the seasons that would shape everything he wrote: schoolboy, then medical student, then family doctor, then the writer who would produce The White Guard and, later, the immortal The Master and Margarita. He also drew on this period for his Theatre Love Story, so that much of his early imagination is rooted in these rooms and this street. The Andriyivskyy Descent runs directly through his fiction, and to climb its worn cobbles today, past artists selling paintings from the pavement, is to move through the physical geography of his mind. The house sits partway down, marked by a memorial plaque bearing his portrait, and the address on that plaque is spelled exactly as Bulgakov rendered it in his novel, number thirteen, so that fact and fiction meet at the front door.

## The Woman Who Kept It

This museum exists because of a quiet act of devotion that lasted decades. Inna Konchakovskaia, who lived from 1902 to 1985, was the daughter of the house's owner and a niece of the composer Witold Maliszewski. Her father was the very man Bulgakov had woven into The White Guard as one of its characters, so she grew up inside the fiction as well as the house. Through the hard Soviet years, when the past was so often erased and property so easily lost, she preserved this unique home intact. When the museum finally opened on 15 May 1991, timed to the hundredth anniversary of Bulgakov's birth, it could fill its rooms with nearly 2,500 objects, his belongings, books, postcards, and photographs, precisely because someone had refused to let them scatter. The atmosphere inside deliberately blurs the line between the writer's real life and the fictional household he built from it.

## A Writer Claimed by Two Nations

Bulgakov's legacy is a knot that the museum has not tried to hide. He was born in Kyiv but wrote in Russian, and both Russian and Ukrainian culture have long claimed him as their own. There is a sister Bulgakov museum in Moscow, dedicated to the apartment that inspired The Master and Margarita, so the writer's memory is quite literally split between two cities. After 2022, some in Ukraine called for the Kyiv house to close, arguing that Bulgakov had opposed Ukrainian statehood and could not be counted a Ukrainian writer. The museum's director and Ukraine's culture minister opposed shutting it down, and it remains open, continuing its research, publishing unreleased material, and hosting book-club evenings. The staff had already made their own position plain years earlier, posting a notice in 2014 that discouraged anyone supportive of the military occupation of Ukraine from visiting. In a house where fiction and history were always intertwined, the arguments over the man who lived here feel, somehow, entirely in character.

## Flight Context

The Bulgakov House stands at No. 13 Andriyivskyy Descent on Kyiv's right bank at 50.4608 N, 30.5147 E, just below Saint Andrew's Church. From 2,000 to 4,000 feet the green-domed Saint Andrew's Church atop the descent and the Dnipro to the east make useful landmarks. Kyiv Zhuliany (UKKK) is the nearest airport; Boryspil International (UKBB) lies east across the river.
