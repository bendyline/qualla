# Sulitjelma

An Arctic mining village where 1,300 workers gathered on lake ice in January 1907 because it was the only ground the company did not own.

**Location:** 67.1330, 16.0765

**Categories:** Fauske Municipality, Villages in Nordland, Valleys of Nordland, Populated places of Arctic Norway, Mining history, Labour history

## Introduction

On 13 January 1907, roughly 1,300 people walked out onto the frozen surface of Langvatnet. They had waded through snow a metre deep to get there, and they had picked the ice deliberately: it was the only ground in Sulitjelma that the mining company did not own. A miner named Ole Kristoffer Sundt climbed onto a margarine box to address them. When the vote came, supporters were told to move to one side, and the mass of dark figures sliding across white ice looked, to observers up on the shore, like a military manoeuvre. By nightfall the workers of Norway's second-largest industrial company had unions of their own.

## Before the Copper

Long before anyone thought of this valley as industrial, it was Sámi ground. Archaeological finds place people here more than a thousand years back, and Sámi families brought reindeer to these summer pastures through the end of the 16th century. Norwegian settlers arrived around 1848 to clear farms on the valley floor, 140 metres above sea level along the shore of Langvatnet, ringed by mountains and glaciers. Then in 1858 a Sámi man named Mons Andreas Petersen — Mons Petter, as everyone called him — picked up chalcopyrite in these hills and sent samples down to Venset. The rock was copper ore, and there was a great deal of it. Petersen died in 1886, poor, in the same year that the Swedish industrialist and consul Nils Persson finally took a serious interest in what he had found. A mine would eventually carry Petersen's name. The fortune went elsewhere.

## From Fifty People to Three Thousand

Persson bought the mineral rights in 1887 and opened a test mine that February. Sulitelma Aktiebolags Gruber was registered as a Swedish company in 1891, and the valley changed at a speed that is hard to picture now. Around 1890 perhaps fifty people lived here; by 1910 the figure was close to three thousand. The company built everything a remote mountain settlement needed and then a good deal more — a power station in 1893, a smelter at Fagerli in 1894 that burned down four years later, a copper smelting shed in 1899, concentration plants, aerial tramways, and the narrow-gauge Sulitjelmabanen railway, begun in May 1891 and running between Sjønstå and Fossen by September 1892. Engineers working here developed the Knudsen process and operated some of the world's first electric copper smelters. At its 1913 peak the company employed 1,750 people.

## Sixteen Men to a Room

None of that wealth reached the barracks. Miners slept as many as sixteen to a room, and families with quarters of their own were obliged to board unmarried workers and feed them from the same cramped kitchen. Because the company owned the housing, the roads, the land, the transport and the shops, wages had a way of returning to the company before they were ever really spent. The workforce was strikingly mixed for such an isolated place: Norwegians alongside Swedes, Finns, Kven, Sámi and Germans. The distance between labourers, foremen and officials showed in everything from where a man slept to what he wore. Martin Tranmæl described Sulitjelma as a small tsardom where capital ruled unchecked. Across the century of operation, more than a hundred workers were killed inside the mountain, and many more died later of what the dust and the shifts had done to them.

## The Ice, and What Followed

At least seven strikes broke out between 1894 and 1904, and 74 workers lost their jobs over them. Then at the end of 1906 management introduced numbered lead badges, to be worn underground and handed back at the end of each ten-hour shift. Around 120 men at the Charlotta mine refused, tipped over the collection box — we are not slaves, they said — and were dismissed on the spot. The meeting on the ice followed within weeks. A telegram went to the Norwegian Union of General Workers requesting 1,500 membership books; thirteen local unions formed, roughly one per mine; that spring brought Sulitjelma's first May Day procession through the settlement. Cooperative shops broke the company store's grip. A technical school and a public library opened, and children finally got better schooling. None of it came easily. Lockouts followed in 1911, 1916 and 1917, and the 1911 strike for a raise from 30 to 35 øre an hour ended in arbitration that granted a single øre over two years.

## Occupation, Collapse, and the Village That Stayed

In 1918 the conflict turned physical. Conscientious objectors had taken work in the mines, and when police arrested the antimilitarist organiser Johan Medby, workers disarmed the officers and ran the authorities out of the valley. The military occupied Sulitjelma, tens of thousands demonstrated elsewhere in solidarity, Medby was sentenced to five years, and foreign workers were expelled. After the German invasion of 1940 the mines fed the occupier's war industry, so critically that the Gestapo tolerated known resistance activity rather than risk interrupting production. Copper prices collapsed in 1975. The state took the operation over in 1983 as Sulitjelma Bergverk AS, the Sandnes smelter — the last copper smelter in Norway — closed on 3 February 1987, and mining ended in 1991. What is left underground is still the largest known copper deposit in the country. Some 472 people live here now. The mining museum at Fagerli and the visitor mine at Grunnstollen, where a locomotive carries you 1.6 kilometres into the rock, keep the century legible, and Nils Gaup's 2023 film Sulis 1907 put the ice meeting on cinema screens. The village also produced Geir Lundestad, born here in 1945, who spent a quarter-century running the Norwegian Nobel Institute.

## Flight Context

Sulitjelma sits at 67.133°N, 16.077°E, at 140 m elevation on the eastern shore of Langvatnet, roughly 44 km east of Fauske by county road 830 — a road laid on the bed of the old mining railway. Approach from the west down the Langvatnet trench for the clearest run; the lake's long, narrow shape is the unmistakable navigational feature, with Blåmannsisen and the Sulitjelma glacier to the east and the border massif rising to 1,908 m at Suliskongen. Recommended viewing altitude 4,000–6,000 ft AGL to keep the valley walls and the terraced mine benches in frame. Nearest airport is Bodø (ENBO), about 75 km west; Harstad/Narvik Evenes (ENEV) lies roughly 155 km north-northeast. There is no airfield at Fauske. Expect valley cloud and rapid icing in winter, when snow cover is reliable and temperatures run about 2 °C colder than at Fauske; long summer daylight above the Arctic Circle gives generous evening light.
