# Sulitjelma Church

The copper town consecrated its graveyard five years before it consecrated its church — and of the 556 people buried in that first ground, half were children under ten.

**Location:** 67.1355, 16.0577

**Categories:** Fauske Municipality, Churches in Nordland, Wooden churches in Norway, 19th-century Church of Norway church buildings, Churches completed in 1899, Long churches in Norway, Mining history

## Introduction

Sulitjelma buried its dead before it had anywhere to pray for them. The parish received royal permission for a cemetery in September 1893, the ground at Fagerli was consecrated on 21 October 1894, and the first burial took place that December. The white wooden church did not arrive until 1899. In most Norwegian settlements the sequence runs the other way around, and the reversal here is not an accident of paperwork. The mines had opened, families were arriving faster than anyone had planned for, and the thing the young community needed first was somewhere to put the children.

## A Royal Signature in May

Permission to build came by royal resolution on 27 May 1899, and construction began almost immediately — remarkably fast work for a mountain valley 44 kilometres from the nearest town, though by then the mining company had a railway, a smelter and a power station, so it also had timber, tools and men who could swing a hammer. Bishop Peter Wilhelm Bøckman consecrated the finished building on 12 November 1899, less than six months after the signature. The plans were drawn by Worm Hirsch Lund, described in Norwegian records as an engineer rather than an architect, which suits a place built entirely by industry. The result is a long church, the simplest and oldest floor plan in the Norwegian repertoire: nave and chancel in a single straight line, painted white, wooden throughout, with room for around 300 people. It serves the Sulitjelma parish in the Salten deanery, part of the Diocese of Sør-Hålogaland.

## Five Hundred and Fifty-Six

The old cemetery down at Fagerli stayed in use until 1921, and the arithmetic of what happened there is difficult to read without stopping. Of 556 people buried in those twenty-seven years, 277 were children under the age of ten. Another 61 were stillborn. Among the adults, 82 per cent died before turning forty, and just four of the 556 lived to be eighty. These were not statistics to anyone at the time. They were sons and daughters of miners who slept sixteen to a room in barracks the company owned, in a valley where disease travelled fast through crowded housing and where a widow whose husband died underground could not expect to stay. Whoever stood at the front of the new church in November 1899 was preaching to a congregation that had already been to that hillside far too many times.

## White Paint, Black Slag

Set against the industrial landscape it was built to serve, the church works by contrast. Sulitjelma in 1899 was smoke, sulphur, ore trains, tailings and the noise of a smelting shed that opened the same year; the church is a clean white rectangle with a slender spire, holding its ground on a rise above the lake. It absorbed everything a company town threw at it. Baptisms of children whose fathers were on shift. Confirmations of boys who would be underground within a year or two. Funerals, constantly. And it stood through the years when Sulitjelma became one of the most radical labour communities in Norway — the lead badges of 1906, the 1,300 workers who met on the ice of Langvatnet in January 1907, the first May Day procession that spring, the military occupation of 1918. The church and the union hall were both places where miners went to be told they mattered.

## Naming the Dead

In November 1999, for the church's centenary, Sulitjelma historielag and the Sulitjelma welfare fund put up a plaque listing everyone buried in the old Fagerli ground. It took real work: cemetery records, parish books and old burial maps were cross-checked until 485 of the 556 names could be established, many of them with a plot location attached. Seventy-one people remain unnamed. It is a modest monument by any measure — a board of names on an Arctic hillside — but a century after the fact, in a village where the company once controlled the housing, the shop and the road, somebody decided that the children at least deserved to be listed by name rather than counted.

## Kisthammeren and the Chapel

A second cemetery was consecrated at Kisthammeren on 14 November 1920, taking over as the Fagerli ground filled. Then in 1996 the parish opened a new graveyard on the southern side of the village and built Sulitjelma Chapel on the site, consecrated on 11 October that year. The project was driven largely by a local women's association that raised roughly 66,000 kroner from the community itself, and the altar was made from materials taken from the mines. Mining had stopped five years earlier, in 1991, after a century that had taken more than a hundred workers' lives inside the mountain. Building the chapel's altar out of the rock and metal that shaped the valley was not sentimentality. It was a small, exact piece of accounting.

## Flight Context

Sulitjelma Church sits at 67.1355°N, 16.058°E, at roughly 150 m elevation on a rise above the north shore of Langvatnet in Sulitjelma village, about 44 km east of Fauske via county road 830. From the air the church is the clearest man-made landmark in the settlement: a white long church with a slender spire, standing out sharply against dark slate roofs, exposed rock and the old smelter ground to the west. Langvatnet's narrow east-west trench is the navigational anchor, with the Sulitjelma glacier and Blåmannsisen to the east and the border massif rising to 1,908 m at Suliskongen. Recommended viewing altitude 2,500–4,000 ft AGL for a clear read of the church, the old industrial terraces and the cemetery ground on the southern side of the village. Nearest airport is Bodø (ENBO), about 75 km west; Harstad/Narvik Evenes (ENEV) lies roughly 155 km north-northeast. Expect low valley cloud and heavy snow in winter, with temperatures about 2 °C below Fauske; the long Arctic summer evenings light the white cladding beautifully well past ordinary sunset hours.
