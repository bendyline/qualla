# Pieljekaise National Park

One of the nine national parks Sweden created in 1909, set aside in the Lapland fells not for its peaks but for its mountain birch forest.

**Location:** 66.3543, 16.7683

**Categories:** national-parks, sweden, lapland, hiking, sami, birch-forest, mountains

## Introduction

Seen from the side, the mountain's two summits look like an ear. The Sami named it for exactly that: Bieljijgájse, which means "the ear peaks". In Swedish it is Pieljekaise, and the national park around it carries the name. At about 1,140 metres it is the highest point in the park, standing over a low mountain country of small lakes, open heath and birch forest in the Arjeplog mountains of Swedish Lapland, between the small communities of Jäkkvik and Adolfström. None of this is dramatic terrain, and that is rather the point. When Sweden drew a boundary here in 1909, it was protecting trees, not peaks.

## Nine Parks and a Birch Forest

In 1909 the Riksdag passed a law on national parks, and nine opened that year, making Sweden the first country in Europe to establish such parks. Pieljekaise was one of the nine, which places it among the oldest national parks in Sweden. Its purpose was specific. The park was created to keep mountain birch forest in its natural state, protecting what is described as one of the largest continuous mountain birch forests in the country. The park covers about 155 square kilometres, and two-thirds of it is mountain birch, climbing the slopes to around 800 metres above sea level. These untouched, wide-stretching forests link the valleys to the open, treeless plateau above, so the park works as a transition zone between forested lowland and alpine high country. Walk uphill far enough and the birches give way to open, treeless ground.

## Tall Herbs Under Low Trees

The terrain rolls rather than climbs. Valleys, low fells and open plateaus make up most of it, a landscape shaped by glaciers and scattered with small lakes and wetlands. Mountain birch dominates, with dwarf shrubs, mosses and lichens growing beneath. In places the ground is far richer than that. Pieljekaise is rich in herbaceous plants, and its meadow forests hold tall species such as northern wolfsbane, angelica, alpine sow-thistle and wood cranesbill, while purple saxifrage grows in the park as well. The area is a refuge for birds and predators. Moose and willow grouse live here, and sometimes wolverines or bears, while birdlife is at its richest in the summer months. Those months do not last. The climate is subarctic, with short, cool summers and long, cold winters, and snow typically lingers well into late spring.

## Reindeer Country

Long before 1909, this was a working landscape. The area has been a place for Sami reindeer herding since ancient times, and the park lies within the Semisjaur-Njarg sameby, the Sami reindeer-herding community whose lands include it. The park's name comes from the Sami language, by way of its highest mountain, and Bieljijgájse, the ear peaks, is a description as much as a name. The law of 1909 was written to protect a forest. The place it protects is shared, though, with the birds and predators that take refuge here and with the herders and reindeer who have used this country for far longer than the park has existed.

## A Rest Hut on the King's Trail

There are no roads inside Pieljekaise and no motorised access; travel here is on foot. The main way in is from Jäkkvik, on road 95, the Silver Road, where the Kungsleden passes through the village and leads directly into the park. The Kungsleden stage between Jäkkvik and Adolfström runs somewhere between 22 and 27 kilometres, depending on the source, and the walking is relatively easy. A second marked route, the Viejenäsleden, runs about 22 kilometres from Jäkkvik to Viejenäs, south-east of the Kungsleden. For wide views, walkers climb the summits: Pieljekaise itself, Vuomavarre at 874 metres, Báktek at 788. In clear weather the view reaches all the way to the Norwegian peaks in the west. There is no entrance fee, and the right of public access, allemansrätten, applies alongside the national park rules. There are no shops or other services either, only a simple rest hut beside the Kungsleden, about 8 kilometres from Jäkkvik, with a day room, stove and outhouse and a locked four-bed room whose key is rented through Jäkkvik's shop or the trading post at Adolfström, so supplies come from Jäkkvik, and stream water should be purified or boiled. Weather can change quickly, cold and wet even in summer. Beyond the park, the Kungsleden continues to Ammarnäs or Kvikkjokk, and the Vindelfjällen Nature Reserve lies nearby.

## Flight Context

Pieljekaise National Park lies at about 66.35°N, 16.77°E in Arjeplog Municipality, Norrbotten County, in the low Arjeplog mountains of Swedish Lapland. Jäkkvik, on road 95 (the Silver Road), is about 5 nautical miles to the east-north-east, and Adolfström about 5 nautical miles to the south-south-west; the park lies between them. The highest summit, Pieljekaise, reaches about 1,140 m (roughly 3,700 ft), so stay well above that; 5,000-6,000 ft MSL gives a good view of the pattern of birch forest on the lower slopes (up to about 800 m) giving way to open, treeless plateau, with small lakes scattered across it. The Norwegian mountains lie to the west. Arvidsjaur Airport (ESNX) is about 76 nautical miles to the south-east and Hemavan Airport (ESUT) about 52 nautical miles to the south-west. Weather can change quickly here even in summer, and snow typically lingers into late spring.
