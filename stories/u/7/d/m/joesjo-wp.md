# Joesjö

A village abandoned by its founders, restarted by one stubborn settler in 1844, and remembered mostly as the birthplace of the greatest male alpine skier who ever raced.

**Location:** 65.7286, 14.6242

**Categories:** Villages, Sweden, Lapland, Skiing, Border regions

## Introduction

Two brothers from Kittelfjäll cleared the first farm at Joesjö in 1837, and within a few years they were gone — driven out by a run of misfortunes the record only gestures at. The clearing sat empty. Then in 1844 a man named König Olofsson Tiger arrived from Degerfors, took over the abandoned ground, and stayed. Most of the people living in Joesjö today descend from him. The village sits about 30 kilometres west of Tärnaby at the end of road 1116, in fell country where Sweden thins toward its edge and the nearest Norwegian settlement lies barely 20 kilometres on. In South Sámi the place is Jovjaevrie. Its most famous son left at the age of four.

## Founded Twice

Very few villages get a second beginning, and fewer still remember the name of the person who gave it to them. Joesjö does. König Olofsson Tiger walked into a failed settlement and made it work, and the genealogy of the place still runs back through him — an unusual kind of foundation myth, one with a documented date and an ordinary man at the centre of it rather than a saint or a king.

What sustained the settlement was never soil. This far into the fells, farming was always a negotiation with a short season. The waters of Övre Jovattnet hold Arctic char and trout, and fishing has carried more weight here than any crop ever did. Farming has since given way almost entirely to tourism and the small enterprises that follow it. Tärna Vilt AB, the village's game-meat business, runs a shop and a winter campsite — a single company covering most of what a visitor might need.

## A Border That Worked Both Ways

For a long stretch of the twentieth century, Joesjö was not the end of a road but the middle of one. Up to the 1950s it served as a trading centre between Swedes and their neighbours from Midt-Helgeland across the Norwegian line, a place where two national economies met informally and did business without much reference to the capitals on either side.

Geography made that inevitable. The border here is a line drawn across a continuous landscape of fell, lake and birch scrub, and the people on both sides of it had more in common with each other than with anyone further away. Reaching Joesjö from Tärnaby still means driving 10 kilometres west on the E12 — the Blue Road, running all the way from the Norwegian coast into Russian Karelia — then turning onto Krutfjällsvägen and following it 25 kilometres more. The turnoff is signed for two destinations at once: Joesjö and Hattfjelldal. One of them is in another country.

## The Boy Who Left at Four

Ingemar Stenmark was born in Joesjö on 18 March 1956. His family moved to Tärnaby when he was four, so the village can claim the birth certificate and little else — but the birth certificate is worth something, because what followed has not been matched.

Stenmark won 86 World Cup races: 46 giant slaloms and 40 slaloms, the two disciplines that reward precision over nerve. He took two Olympic golds at Lake Placid in 1980, one in each of his events, and five world championship titles. When he retired in 1989 the record of 86 wins stood as the highest in the sport, and it stayed unbroken for more than three decades. Mikaela Shiffrin finally passed it in 2023. Among men it still stands. Whole nations with alpine traditions and enormous budgets have not produced a skier who came close, and this one started in a village at the end of road 1116.

## What the Fells Give

Joesjö is not a viewpoint or an attraction. It is a working handful of houses in a landscape that gives up its rewards slowly — fishing water, hunting ground, snow that arrives early and stays late, and the particular quiet of a place where the next settlement in any direction is a serious drive away.

That quiet is now the product. Visitors come for the fishing in summer and the snow in winter, and the village that once traded reindeer meat and manufactured goods across the border now trades solitude. It is a small, sturdy economy, and it has kept people here through the collapse of the farming that first justified the settlement. Two brothers gave up on this ground in the 1830s. Nearly two centuries later, their failure looks less like a verdict on the place than on the difficulty of being early.

## Flight Context

Joesjö lies at 65.73°N, 14.62°E, roughly 40 km west of Hemavan and 20 km east of the Norwegian border. Best viewed from 4,000–6,000 ft AGL, following road 1116 (Krutfjällsvägen) west from its junction with the E12. Key visual references: the lake system of Jovattnet running northeast–southwest, the Krutfjället massif to the south, and the deep-blue Överuman reservoir to the northeast where the E12 crosses into Norway. Nearest airports are Hemavan Tärnaby (ESUT, 458 m elevation) about 40 km east and Mo i Rana Røssvoll (ENRA) about 90 km northwest. This is high-latitude fell country: expect rapid cloud formation over the ridges, strong lee turbulence in westerly flow, and only a few hours of usable light in December and January.
