# Røyrvik Municipality

Norway's third-smallest municipality by population — 423 people spread across 1,585 square kilometres of lake, border mountain and South Sami reindeer country.

**Location:** 64.8838, 13.5631

**Categories:** Norway, Trøndelag, Namdalen, Municipalities, Sami culture, Mining, Lakes, Border regions

## Introduction

Kennet Tømmermo Reitan was twenty-two when the voters of Røyrvik made him their mayor. His list, Samarbeidslista, took sixty per cent of the ballots in September 2023, which is a decisive mandate anywhere and, in a municipality of 423 people, also a manageable number of conversations. That figure is the key to understanding the place. Røyrvik holds the third-smallest population of any municipality in Norway — only Modalen and Utsira have fewer — while occupying 1,585 square kilometres of lake, forest and border mountain, which makes it the 50th largest of the country's 357 municipalities by area. Divide the one number by the other and you get a population density of 0.3 people per square kilometre.

## Raarvihke, Written Alongside

The municipality has two official names, and neither outranks the other. On 13 June 2014 the national government approved Raarvihke as a co-equal name in Southern Sami; paired with the Sami word for municipality it becomes Raarvihken tjïelte. The recognition was late but the presence is not. South Sami families have herded reindeer through these mountains for a very long time — in neighbouring Børgefjell, herding predates the arrival of Norwegian settlers in the late 1700s by at least five centuries — and reindeer husbandry still earns a living here alongside farming. The coat of arms granted on 13 December 1985 makes the same point in heraldic shorthand. It is a white cross crosslet on blue, lifted from a motif that recurs on pottery, handwork and the traditional pewter embroidery made by the ancestors of the South Sami people who live in the municipality today. The crosses read as a sun, its four arms pointing to the compass points. Alfhild Vekterli designed it; Einar H. Skjervold reworked it.

## Char Inlet

The municipality takes its name from an old farm, Røirviken in the older spelling, and the name is disarmingly literal: røyr for Arctic char, vik for inlet. A place named after a fish tells you what mattered. Water dominates the map here. Limingen, one of Norway's ten largest lakes, stretches south from the village at 418 metres above sea level and drops 192 metres at its deepest; dams now swing its level by as much as ten metres through the year. The village of Røyrvik sits at the northern end of that lake, 425 metres up, roughly twenty kilometres west of the Swedish border — a border the municipal boundary follows on its eastern side. North lie Namsvatnet and Jengelvatnet; south, beyond Limingen, Tunnsjøen and the shallow lake-plain of Tunnsjøflyan. The highest ground is Jetnamsklumpen at 1,512 metres, standing inside Børgefjell National Park, a wilderness so lightly tracked that walkers can go days without meeting anyone.

## A Century, and Barely Any Growth

Røyrvik was separated from the sprawling Grong Municipality on 1 July 1923 with 392 inhabitants. A hundred years later the figure is 423. The boundaries have never been redrawn — only the county above them changed, when Nord-Trøndelag was folded into Trøndelag on 1 January 2018 — so the arithmetic is unusually clean, and the recent direction of travel is down: the population fell 15.1 per cent over the previous decade. The list of mayors carries the century's one sharp interruption. Petter Vekterli, elected in 1935, was gone by 1942, replaced by Harald Kleppestø of Nasjonal Samling, the party that governed under German occupation. Vekterli returned in 1945 and served until 1963, then served again from 1968 to 1979. Eleven representatives sit on the municipal council, which for 423 people works out at one councillor for roughly every thirty-eight residents.

## What the Mine Left Behind

Prospectors were poking at the ground in the Gjersvik area from 1911 until the First World War, but the real industrial chapter came later. Grong Gruber worked the Joma orebody from 1972 until 1998, and for a quarter of a century Røyrvik was a mining municipality with wages to match. The buildings survive and now shelter small industrial firms; the ore did not go quietly. In 2021, NRK reported that zinc and cadmium were still leaching into the lake Hudningsvatnet more than two decades after closure, with the eastern basin rated poor for chemical quality. The Norwegian Environment Agency notes that metal concentrations across several water bodies have fallen substantially since 2006, but the legacy has not washed out. Meanwhile the municipality has tried other things — electronics assembly, electronic document management, and a small alpine ski centre opened in 1986. Røyrvik also produced the physicist Jan Myrheim, born here in 1948.

## Flight Context

Røyrvik village lies at 64.884°N, 13.563°E, at the north end of Lake Limingen and 425 metres above sea level, about 20 km west of the Norway–Sweden border. Best viewed from 3,000–8,000 feet on a north–south track that follows the chain of lakes: Tunnsjøen and Tunnsjøflyan to the south, Limingen at the village, Namsvatnet to the north, with the Børgefjell massif filling the northeastern horizon. Jetnamsklumpen (1,512 m) is the highest point in the municipality. Nearest fields are Mosjøen Airport, Kjærstad (ENMS), roughly 100 km north-northwest, and Namsos Airport, Høknesøra (ENNM), roughly 105 km southwest; Trondheim Airport, Værnes (ENVA) is the nearest major field far to the south. This is high inland terrain near 65°N: expect rapid cloud build-up over the border mountains, strong winter icing, deep snow cover into May, and very long daylight at midsummer.
