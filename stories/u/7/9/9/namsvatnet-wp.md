# Namsvatnet

A 17-kilometre lake at the doorstep of Børgefjell, source of the river Namsen, dammed in 1959 and now rising and falling through fourteen metres a year.

**Location:** 64.9974, 13.5759

**Categories:** Norway, Trøndelag, Lakes, Hydroelectric, Børgefjell, Wilderness, Fishing

## Introduction

The year 1959 gave Namsvatnet two things at once. One was a dam: the lake was regulated for hydroelectric generation, its surface allowed to swing between 454 and 440 metres above sea level, and people living along the shoreline had to move. The other was a chapel — Kristi krybbe, Christ's manger, a small octagonal timber building with brown walls and a turf roof, seating about fifty, designed by Peter Helland-Hansen and standing at the far northern end of the water. The engineers were taking a valley and the carpenters were raising a room to sit in. Both projects are still there, and you reach the second one by boat across the first.

## Fourteen Metres of Give

Namsvatnet is a substantial body of water by any measure: roughly 39 square kilometres of surface, seventeen kilometres long and about four across, 455 metres up, 84 metres deep at its deepest and averaging 28. Its catchment covers some 700 square kilometres. Since 1959 it has also been a reservoir, holding 458 million cubic metres of usable storage, and a tunnel now carries part of its water south toward the Vektaren reservoir instead of letting it run its old course. The river Namsen used to be the lake's primary outlet, and the Namsen still begins here — but a river whose headwater is a regulated reservoir is a river on someone else's schedule. Fourteen metres of vertical drawdown leaves a visible mark: a bathtub ring of bare, scoured shoreline that widens through the winter and drowns again in spring.

## Three Rivers Out of the Wilderness

The water arrives from the northeast, out of Børgefjell National Park. Three main streams feed the lake: Storelva, draining the lake Jengelvatnet; Virmaelva; and Orelva, coming down from Ovrejaevrie. Børgefjell itself was protected in 1963 and now covers about 1,447 square kilometres of Trøndelag and Nordland along the Swedish border — a park deliberately left almost untouched, with very few marked trails and almost no visitor facilities, where a walker can go days without seeing another person. It is best known as a stronghold of the Arctic fox, though wolverines are the more numerous large carnivore, and golden eagles and rough-legged buzzards work the open ground. All of it is grazed by domesticated reindeer, as it has been for centuries.

## Char, Trout and Minnow

Three fish species share the lake: Arctic char, trout, and the small silver cyprinid Norwegians call ørekyt — the European minnow. The char is the one the district is named for, since the municipality of Røyrvik takes its name from an old farm whose name means, plainly, char inlet, and it is the species best suited to cold, deep, nutrient-poor northern water. The minnow is the complicated one. It is native to parts of Norway, including some watercourses in the old Nord-Trøndelag county, but across the twentieth century anglers using it as live bait carried it into every county in the country. It now sits in the very high risk category of Norway's alien species list, because in lean water it outcompetes trout fry along the shoreline. Anglers keep coming regardless, and tourism has become one of the steadier economic activities on a lake whose other industry is measured in megawatt-hours.

## Nine Kilometres by Water

There is no road along the length of Namsvatnet. From Namsvassgrenda at the southern end, a boat service runs about nine kilometres up the lake to the northern shore, and that crossing is the practical gateway to Børgefjell for anyone not prepared to walk in from a great distance. It also delivers people to Kristi krybbe, which is privately owned and used during the summer months — a mountain chapel on the edge of one of the emptiest landscapes in Europe. The combination is very Norwegian: an industrial reservoir, a wilderness park with no infrastructure, a fifty-seat chapel under a turf roof, and a small boat that connects all three. In Southern Sami the lake is Nååmesjenjaevrie — the name the country wore before the reservoir arrived.

## Flight Context

Namsvatnet lies at approximately 64.997°N, 13.576°E, at 455 metres above sea level in Røyrvik Municipality. It is a long, narrow, roughly southwest–northeast lake about 17 km by 4 km, unmistakable from altitude and an excellent visual reference for the Børgefjell approach. Best viewed from 4,000–8,000 feet along its axis, with the Børgefjell massif and the Swedish border to the northeast and Lake Limingen about 15 km south. In late winter the drawdown ring of exposed shoreline is clearly visible from the air. Nearest fields are Mosjøen Airport, Kjærstad (ENMS) roughly 90 km north-northwest and Namsos Airport, Høknesøra (ENNM) roughly 115 km southwest. Expect orographic cloud over Børgefjell, strong channelled winds down the lake, and ice cover well into spring at this elevation and latitude.
