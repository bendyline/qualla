# Namsskogan (village)

The administrative centre of Namsskogan sits on an island in the Namsen and got its railway station on 5 July 1940, three months into the German occupation of Norway.

**Location:** 64.9288, 13.1595

**Categories:** Villages in Trøndelag, Namsskogan Municipality

## Introduction

The station at Namsskogan opened on 5 July 1940. Hold that date against the calendar: German forces had invaded Norway on 9 April, resistance on the mainland had collapsed the previous month, and the country was under occupation. The rails reaching this island village in the middle of the Namsen were not a liberation or a gift — they were a Norwegian project, approved by parliament in 1923 and under construction north from Grong since 1932, that the occupying army suddenly found extremely useful. A line running north through Norway's long empty middle is a strategic object. The village got its station in the summer everything else went wrong.

## Two Hundred and Thirty-Three People, Densely Packed

The village has a population density of 803 people per square kilometre, a figure that sounds vaguely urban until you see the other number: the settlement covers 0.29 square kilometres. Two hundred and thirty-three people as of January 2025, packed onto a scrap of land at 215 metres above sea level, and the land is genuinely a scrap — Namsskogan sits on an island in the Namsen, though the village spills onto both banks as well. It is about 20 kilometres south of the Nordland county border, which makes it one of the last places in Trøndelag that anyone driving north passes through, and the administrative centre of a municipality nearly 4,900 times its own size.

## The Line Comes North

Building a railway up the spine of inland Norway was a decades-long national commitment before it was a wartime one. The Storting approved the plan in 1923; work north from Grong started in 1932; through the summers as many as 1,500 men were on the job at once, in a landscape with almost no towns to house them. Then came April 1940 and everything accelerated. The Wehrmacht assigned the Nordland Line high military priority, and the labour force across the project swelled from under 900 in the early thirties to nearly 5,000 by September 1940. Namsskogan Station stands 290.25 kilometres from Trondheim. Trains still call there — the platforms were rebuilt as recently as 2022 — and the same corridor carries the E6 highway past the front doors of the village.

## A Name Made in 1923

Both the village and the municipality got their name in 1923, assembled from the river and the trees: Namsen plus the plural of skog, so "the woodlands around Namsen." The river deserves the billing. The Namsen rises in springs in Børgefjell and runs 228 kilometres to the sea at Namsos, draining some 6,298 square kilometres on the way, and among Atlantic salmon anglers it carries the nickname Queen of Rivers. Fish over 23 kilograms are not remarkable here. The local method is harling — a boat crosses slowly bank to bank while drifting downstream, dragging a lure across the path of salmon working their way up. Namsskogan sits well upstream of all that, near where the river is still deciding how big it intends to become.

## Church, Neighbours, and Names That Stick

Three kilometres west, out in open country, stands Bjørhusdal Church: a brown wooden long church built in 1970 to plans by Arne Aursand, seating about 120, serving the northern half of the municipality. The older and grander church is Trones, 30 kilometres south, which has been standing since 1832. Between them, 12 kilometres down the valley, is the village of Brekkvasselv. Names hold their ground in a place this small — the mayor elected in 2023, Elisabeth Vollmo Bjørhusdal, carries the same valley name as the church west of town, and the very first mayor of the municipality, back in 1923, was a man called Kristian Trones.

## What It Is Like to Be Passed Through

There is a particular character to settlements built along a through-route in a thinly peopled country. Everything moves past: the freight trains, the summer caravans heading for Lofoten, the lorries running fish south. The village supplies what a district of eight hundred people needs — administration, school, a station, a place to stop — and watches a considerable share of Norway's north–south traffic go by the window. That is not a lament. Being on the line is why Namsskogan is here at all, and why a village of 233 has both a railway station and a highway that reaches the Arctic.

## Flight Context

Namsskogan village is at 64.9288°N, 13.1595°E, 215 m (705 ft) above sea level in the Namdalen valley, about 20 km south of the Nordland county border. Recommended viewing altitude 2,000–4,000 ft AGL. The navigation picture is unusually clean: the Namsen river, the E6 highway and the Nordlandsbanen railway run as a single braided corridor through otherwise unbroken forest, and the village occupies an island in the river with buildings on both banks. Bjørhusdal Church lies 3 km west in open ground; Brekkvasselv is 12 km south. Nearest airports: Namsos Airport, Høknesøra (ENNM) to the southwest, Mosjøen Airport, Kjærstad (ENMS) to the north, Trondheim Airport Værnes (ENVA) to the south. Expect valley fog on still autumn mornings, extended snow cover from November to April, and very limited daylight in December at this latitude.
