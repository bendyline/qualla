# Helgeland Museum

One museum spread across eighteen municipalities of coast, island and inland valley, run from an office on a street that the town once planned to bulldoze for parking.

**Location:** 65.8374, 13.1871

**Categories:** Museums in Nordland, Helgeland, Vefsn Municipality, Cultural heritage, Historic preservation, Norway

## Introduction

In 1970 the municipality of Mosjøen had a plan for Sjøgata. The old wooden houses and wharves along the waterfront were dark, damp, awkward to heat and inconvenient to reach, and the plan proposed clearing the lot to make room for cars. What stopped it was not a preservation order but an argument, carried on by residents, heritage campaigners and an organisation called Lydiabryggas Venner, with support that eventually included the then Crown Princess Sonja. Restoration began in the 1970s instead of demolition. Half a century later the administrative office of the Helgeland Museum sits on that street, which turns out to be an appropriate address for an institution whose whole business is arguing that old things are worth the trouble.

## The Street That Was Nearly a Car Park

Sjøgata is Northern Norway's longest continuous run of nineteenth-century wooden houses and wharves, a working waterfront that simply never got demolished or burned. The oldest building on it, Fru Haugans Hotel, goes back to 1794 and is still taking guests. Much of the streetscape is now protected. In 2004 the Sjøgata environment received the Olavsrosa, the mark awarded to Norwegian heritage attractions that meet a certain standard of authenticity, and the street has become the town's principal attraction - restaurants, galleries, workshops and museum rooms occupying buildings that were built for salt fish, timber and general trade. Walking it, you notice that the wharves still lean out over the water. They were not designed for tourists. They were designed for boats coming alongside.

## One Museum, Eighteen Municipalities

The Helgeland Museum was formally established on 19 March 2003, the end point of a consolidation that had begun two years earlier, and it is best understood as a solution to a geography problem. Helgeland covers roughly 18,832 square kilometres and holds about 85,000 people, scattered across four towns and a long tail of islands, fjord-heads and valleys. No single community out there can support a conservator, a textile specialist, a building archaeologist and an archivist. So the foundation pools them. Eighteen municipalities collaborate on the operation; the museum runs a group of eighteen museums and building collections across them, with about 49 permanent staff. The concept, in the organisation's own words, is to distribute expertise across Helgeland, so that a specialist in one place works for every place.

## What Is Actually in the Collection

The list of divisions reads like a survey of how people have made a living on this coast. There is the Petter Dass Museum at Alstahaug, built around the priest and poet who served that parish from 1689 to 1707 and wrote Nordlands Trompet. There is the eider duck house on Vega, in an archipelago inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage list in 2004 for a frugal way of life built on fishing and the harvesting of eider down - a tradition in which the work, and the expertise, was largely women's. There are old trading posts at Nordvika on Dønna and the Falch site on Rødøy, open-air museums at Velfjord, Herøy, Sømna, Vevelstad and Grane, the Rana Museum inland, the Hemnes Museum, a lensmann's farm at Hattfjelldal, and the wartime coastal battery at Grønsvik in Lurøy. Fishing, farming, trade, faith, war and down.

## The Home Division

The Vefsn Museum, the division on the museum's own doorstep, tells the story of how Mosjøen became the first town on Helgeland in 1875, which is a shorter story than most Norwegian town histories and a stranger one. The place went from a strand of traders and craftsmen to a chartered trading town, a town of officials, and eventually a garrison town in about a generation, largely on the back of a timber boom driven by English capital. Alongside the permanent material the museum runs rotating cultural-history exhibitions and gallery space for contemporary work through the year. It is a small operation, but it sits inside the buildings its subject matter is about, which not every museum can claim.

## Flight Context

The Helgeland Museum's administrative office is on Sjøgata in Mosjøen at approximately 65.837 N, 13.187 E, on the west bank at the head of the Vefsnfjorden where the Vefsna and Skjerva meet the sea. From the air, Sjøgata is the line of small wooden buildings and piers along the near shore of the fjord immediately south of the river mouth; Øyfjellet, 814 m, rises directly opposite on the east side. The museum's other divisions run the length of Helgeland, from Bindal in the south to Rødøy in the north, so a coastal transit at 4,000-6,000 ft between ENST and ENRA passes over most of them. Mosjøen Airport, Kjærstad (ENMS) lies about 5 km south of town, 1,019 m runway, elevation 73 m. Sandnessjøen Airport, Stokka (ENST) and Mo i Rana Airport, Røssvoll (ENRA) are the alternates. Coastal Helgeland is frequently marginal VFR; the inner fjord at Mosjøen holds low stratus and fog in autumn.
