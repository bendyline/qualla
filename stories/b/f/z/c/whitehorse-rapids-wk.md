# Whitehorse Rapids

The white-maned rapids that drowned stampeders, launched a young Jack London's writing career, and gave the Yukon capital its name now lie silent beneath a hydroelectric reservoir.

**Location:** 60.6889, -134.9633

**Categories:** yukon, klondike, gold-rush, river, history, jack-london, hydroelectric

## Introduction

The rapids are gone, but the city still bears their name. Drive into Whitehorse today and the Yukon River slips past placid and silvery, its dangerous teeth pulled by a concrete dam built in 1958. Yet for two frantic seasons during the Klondike Gold Rush, this stretch of water churned with whitecaps that nineteenth-century travelers thought resembled the manes of charging horses - and proved every bit as wild. In the spring of 1898, after winter ice broke up, a hundred and fifty boats were smashed against the basalt walls in a matter of days and five men drowned trying to reach the gold fields. A young California writer named Jack London talked his way into a piloting job and learned, somewhere between the canyon and the rapids, that there was a better fortune to be had in stories than in claims.

## A Choke Point With Teeth

Two obstacles in quick succession turned this section of the Yukon River into a graveyard for the unprepared. First came Miles Canyon, where the river squeezed through a narrow basalt gorge cut by ancient lava flows, walls rising sheer on either side as the current accelerated into a standing wave. Two miles downstream, the river burst out into the White Horse Rapids themselves - a longer, shallower stretch of broken water where the curling whitecaps gave the rapids their name. Stampeders coming down from the Chilkoot and White Pass trails had to run both, or portage. The North-West Mounted Police, alarmed by the carnage of early 1898, eventually forbade boats to attempt the rapids without an experienced pilot at the steering oar. Women and children were ordered to walk around. The order arrived too late for many.

## Norman Macaulay's Five Miles of Tramway

Where there is danger, there is opportunity. Norman Macaulay came up from Dyea in the fall of 1897 and set up a roadhouse at the head of the portage. Over the following winter, with eighteen men, a few horses, and a small forest of spruce logs, he cut a five-mile trail and laid a wooden tramway down the east bank of the river. The job took twenty-one days. By the summer of 1898, his horse-drawn cars were hauling up to eighty tonnes a day - three cents a pound for freight, twenty-five dollars for a boat. A small town called Canyon City sprang up at the upper end with a hotel, saloon, restaurant, stables, and a Mounted Police detachment. In August 1899 the White Pass and Yukon Corporation bought the tramway for a hundred and eighty-five thousand dollars. The next year, when the railroad reached Whitehorse from Skagway, Canyon City emptied within a season.

## Jack London at the Tiller

On September 25, 1897, a twenty-one-year-old Jack London steered a homemade boat called the *Yukon Belle* through Miles Canyon and the White Horse Rapids. He had built her at Lake Bennett with a partner, lashed together from green-cut spruce. The run took only a few minutes; London came through clean, with the unearned confidence of youth. Word spread quickly along the river bank, and the older stampeders who had watched him steer hired him to pilot their boats through too, paying him what may have been the first money he ever earned for his nerve. London never made his fortune in gold - he came home from the Klondike the following year with scurvy and twenty-six dollars worth of dust - but the river, the bone-deep cold, the men he met in the cabins around Dawson, all of it ended up in *The Call of the Wild*, *White Fang*, and the short stories that made him one of the best-paid writers in America.

## The Day the River Went Quiet

The Whitehorse Hydroelectric Dam, completed in 1958, raised the river upstream into a long narrow reservoir now called Schwatka Lake, after the U.S. Army lieutenant Frederick Schwatka who mapped the Yukon's full length in 1883. The dam drowned the rapids, the basalt teeth, the eddies, the standing waves - everything except Miles Canyon itself, which still survives upstream of the impoundment, its cliffs spanned today by a footbridge that any visitor can cross. Schwatka Lake powers a city of about twenty-eight thousand people and floats a fleet of pleasure craft. Salmon that once ran past these waters are now lifted around the dam by a wooden fish ladder, the longest of its kind in the world. The rapids have not flowed for almost seventy years, and yet every license plate and every road sign in the territory still announces their name.

## Ghost Geography

Some places persist because their absence shaped what came next. Without these rapids, there would have been no portage; without the portage, no tramway; without the tramway, no town at the head of it. The railroad terminated where the river became navigable again, and Whitehorse grew up where the freight had to be reloaded onto sternwheelers bound for Dawson. The danger built the city. Walk the riverfront promenade today, past the restored *S.S. Klondike* and the log cabin churches and the Yukon Beringia Centre, and the water moves slow and easy under a low northern sky. Stand on the footbridge over Miles Canyon, the only relic of the original gauntlet, and listen. The river still hisses through the narrows below your feet, faintly, the way an old man clears his throat. It is the smallest and the loudest reminder of what this water used to do.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 60.6889N, 134.9633W (approximate; submerged rapids lie within Schwatka Lake just south of downtown Whitehorse). The Yukon River corridor is the dominant linear feature - follow it south from the city and the reservoir narrows visibly into Miles Canyon, with the dam visible at the north end of Schwatka Lake. Recommended viewing altitude 2,500-4,500 ft AGL to keep the canyon walls in frame. The Erik Nielsen Whitehorse International Airport (CYXY) sits on the bench above town at 2,317 ft elevation, with VFR routes along the river valley well established. Surrounding terrain rises sharply to the west into the Yukon Plateau; expect strong valley winds in summer afternoons and severe icing risk in shoulder seasons. Whitehorse Daily reports often include fish-ladder activity June through August.
