# Matsushima

One of Japan's Three Great Views, where 260 pine-crowned islands once sheltered a bay from a devastating tsunami -- and inspired centuries of poetry, painting, and pilgrimage.

**Location:** 38.3667, 141.0833

**Categories:** scenic-landscape, coastal, islands, japanese-culture, natural-disaster

## Introduction

There is a cave on the island of Komonejima called Chomei-ana, about five meters tall, and local folklore held that anyone who passed through it in a pleasure boat would live three years longer. Then the 2011 earthquake brought it down. That kind of story -- beauty intertwined with fragility, legend meeting geology -- runs through everything about Matsushima. This archipelago of roughly 260 islands in Miyagi Prefecture has been celebrated as one of Japan's Three Great Views since the Edo period, painted by Yoshu Chikanobu in ukiyo-e woodblock prints, visited by the haiku master Matsuo Basho in 1689, and claimed as a favorite retreat by the powerful daimyo Date Masamune. The islands are volcanic tuff sculpted by wind and tide into arches, pillars, and shelves, each capped with the gnarled Japanese pines that give the bay its name.

## The View in Four Acts

Japanese tradition prescribes four specific mountaintop viewpoints for contemplating Matsushima, each with its own poetic designation. From Otakamori, the bay unfolds as the Magnificent View -- a sweeping, high-altitude panorama. Tomiyama presents the Beautiful View, intimate and layered. The promontory at Ogitani delivers the Enchanting View, its vantage jutting into the water so the islands seem to surround you. And from Tamonzan comes the Grand View, stately and composed. These are not casual labels. They represent a centuries-old aesthetic framework for experiencing landscape, where the angle of observation matters as much as the thing observed. Each viewpoint reframes the same 260 islands into a different emotional register.

## Basho's Silence

When Basho reached Matsushima during his 1689 journey recorded in Oku no Hosomichi, the bay's beauty supposedly struck him dumb. The famous verse attributed to him -- 'Matsushima, ah! Matsushima, Matsushima!' -- was actually composed by his traveling companion Kawai Sora. The distinction matters: the real poem about Matsushima is the one Basho did not write, his silence becoming a more eloquent tribute than any seventeen syllables could manage. Date Masamune, the one-eyed lord of Sendai Domain, was equally captivated. His favorite among the islands was Senganjima, and he poured resources into rebuilding the nearby temple of Zuigan-ji in lavish Momoyama style between 1604 and 1609, importing lumber from Mount Kumano and craftsmen from Kyoto.

## When the Wave Came

On March 11, 2011, the magnitude 9.0 Tohoku earthquake triggered a tsunami that annihilated coastal communities up and down northeastern Japan. Matsushima braced for the worst. The initial wave reached 3.2 meters; the second climbed to 3.8 meters. But the 260 islands that had drawn poets and painters for centuries performed one more service: they broke the tsunami's force. The bay absorbed the blow. Three people were confirmed killed in Matsushima -- a devastating loss, but a fraction of the nearly 20,000 who perished along the open coast. The red Togetsukyō Bridge on Ojima was swept away entirely, but the temples and shrines survived. Electricity returned within a week. The Senseki train line to Sendai resumed service by late May. An NPR headline captured the irony: 'Tsunami Spares Japan's Pine-Covered Islands.'

## Cruising the Archipelago

Sightseeing boats depart from the Matsushima waterfront regularly, threading through the island maze on routes that also connect to the port of Hon-Shiogama. From the deck, the islands reveal details invisible from the hilltop viewpoints -- sea caves carved into the tuff, pines growing at impossible angles from rock faces, the distinctive shapes that have earned individual islands names like Futagojima, Kaerujima, and Meganejima. Matsushima-Kaigan Station, 40 minutes from Sendai on the JR Senseki Line, puts visitors within walking distance of the ferry pier, the temples, and the waterfront promenade. The town itself is compact and manageable, its seaside attractions clustered near the station. Beyond the boats, the bay sustains working oyster beds, and the harvest season beginning each October transforms the main street into an open-air oyster market.

## Flight Context

Located at 38.37N, 141.08E on the Pacific coast of Miyagi Prefecture, approximately 14km northeast of Sendai. The bay and its 260 islands are unmistakable from altitude -- a dense cluster of green dots in sheltered water, contrasting with the open coastline to the north and south. Nearest major airport: Sendai Airport (RJSS), roughly 20nm south. Sendai city and its urban grid are visible to the southwest. Approach from the east over the Pacific for the best perspective on the island formations. The Senseki railway line traces the coast between Sendai and the town. Clear skies recommended; haze common in summer months.
