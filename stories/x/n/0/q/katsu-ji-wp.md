# Katsuō-ji: The Temple That Defeats All Odds

A mountain temple near Osaka whose very name means 'victory,' Katsuō-ji has drawn emperors and shoguns for over a thousand years -- and today its grounds are scattered with thousands of daruma dolls left by visitors who came seeking luck and found it.

## Introduction

Thousands of small, round, red-painted faces stare out from every surface -- ledges, steps, moss-covered stones, the branches of maple trees. They are daruma dolls, each one a wish fulfilled. Visitors to Katsuō-ji buy them hoping for victory in business, exams, love, or simply life, and when fortune arrives, they return the dolls to the temple grounds. The accumulation is staggering. The temple sits in the forested hills of Minoh, Osaka Prefecture, eight kilometers northeast of the city below, and its full name -- Ocho-san Katsuō-ji -- carries the character for "victory" right in its title. That name has a story of its own, one that begins with an emperor's illness, a priest's prayer, and a word that proved too powerful to keep.

## Brothers in the Mountain

According to the temple's own chronicle, the Katsuō-ji Engi, two brothers named Zenchū and Zensan arrived in the Aomatani valley in 727 and devoted themselves to Buddhist training, establishing a hermitage in the forested mountains. About forty years later, in 765, a man named Kaisei -- the son of Emperor Kōnin and half-brother of Emperor Kanmu -- studied under the pair and entered the priesthood. In 775, Kaisei completed a transcription of 600 volumes of the Great Perfection of Wisdom Sutra, dedicating the manuscript to a newly built hall he named Miroku-ji. This is considered the direct founding of the temple. Five years later, a sculptor named Myōkan carved the principal image: a standing statue of the Thousand-Armed, Eleven-Faced Kannon. Documentary evidence for these early centuries is thin, but the tradition is consistent with other mountain temples in the region claimed to have been founded or restored by Kaisei.

## A Name Too Strong for a King

By the Heian period, Katsuō-ji had become a center of mountain worship, drawing emperors and aristocrats into its forested precincts. In 880, the temple's head priest led a procession to pray for the recovery of the ailing Emperor Seiwa. The prayers were credited with the emperor's improvement, and in gratitude Seiwa bestowed the name "Shōō-ji" on the temple -- characters meaning "victorious king." But the court quickly realized those same characters could be read as "to defeat the king," a meaning no one wanted hanging over an imperial prayer site. The name was changed to Katsuō-ji, shifting the meaning to "victorious tail" or "surpassing," safely distancing the word from royal overthrow while preserving the sense of triumph. The Nihon Sandai Jitsuroku, a historical chronicle, records Emperor Seiwa's pilgrimage to Mount Katsuō in 880, marking the temple's first appearance in verified written records.

## Fire, War, and Rebuilding

The temple's history follows a pattern common to Japan's great religious sites: construction, destruction, resurrection. In 1184, the entire complex burned during the Battle of Ichinotani, one of the decisive conflicts of the Genpei War. Minamoto no Yoritomo, the warlord who would become Japan's first shogun, ordered the temple rebuilt in 1188, entrusting the project to his retainers Kumagai Naozane and Kajiwara Kagetoki. The Buddhist reformer Hōnen stayed at the temple from 1208 to 1210 after returning from exile. In 1269, Emperor Go-Saga appointed the temple an official place of prayer for the imperial family. The fourth Muromachi shogun, Ashikaga Yoshimochi, designated it a prayer temple for the shogun's family in 1423. Fire struck again on February 14, 1495, destroying the complex a second time. Over a century later, in 1603, Toyotomi Hideyori rebuilt the main gate and funded reconstruction of the main hall. In 1674, the temple changed its sectarian affiliation from Tendai to Shingon, becoming a branch of Shakamon-in Temple at Mount Kōya.

## Stone Sentinels Along the Path

The approach to Katsuō-ji is lined with 36 stone markers, each erected roughly 109 meters apart along the old pilgrimage road from the large torii gate on the Saigoku Kaidō. These machiishi take the form of five-ringed stupas, each ring representing one of the five elements -- earth, water, fire, wind, and space -- stacked as square, circular, triangular, crescent, and jewel-shaped stones. Eight of these markers, from the lower stupa in front of the gate to the seventh, date to 1247 based on their design and temple records, making them the oldest known examples of their kind in Japan. Underground, eight stone storehouses once guarded the temple's precinct boundaries. Each contained a bronze statue placed in a Shigaraki ware container, representing the Four Heavenly Kings and the Four Wisdom Kings. First mentioned in temple documents in 1230, the storehouses fell into disrepair before archaeological excavations in 1962-1963 recovered the statues and their containers, now designated National Important Cultural Properties.

## The Temple of Winners

Today, Katsuō-ji is the 23rd stop on the Saigoku Kannon Pilgrimage, a circuit of 33 temples across the Kansai region that has drawn pilgrims for over a thousand years. But it is the daruma dolls that define the modern experience. The word "katsu" in the temple's name means winning, and visitors buy the small round dolls as talismans for victory in every conceivable endeavor. When success comes, the daruma is returned and left somewhere on the grounds -- tucked into hedgerows, balanced on stone walls, crowded onto shelves. The temple's precincts, with their intact boundary markers, were designated a National Historic Site of Japan in 1966. The principal image hall shelters a triad of wooden statues carved from single pieces of hinoki cypress, their solemn faces and swirling spiral hair retaining the unmistakable aesthetic of the early Heian period. From the air, the temple complex appears as a cluster of traditional rooflines amid dense green forest, a pocket of stillness in the hills above the Osaka urban sprawl.
