# Omi Kokucho: The Provincial Capital Lost and Found

The ruins of Omi Province's eighth-century government capital -- a double-moated administrative complex of halls, granaries, and corridors -- lay hidden beneath the neighborhoods of Otsu for a thousand years before archaeologists uncovered the first provincial capital ever matched to ancient records.

**Location:** 34.9746, 135.9201

**Categories:** archaeological-site, historic-site, nara-period, government, shiga-prefecture

## Introduction

In 1935, a scholar studying the Wamyo Ruijusho -- a tenth-century Japanese encyclopedia -- matched a set of field boundaries in the Oe neighborhood of Otsu, Shiga Prefecture, to the description of a long-vanished government complex. It was the first time anyone had pinpointed the actual location of a kokufu, one of the provincial capitals that the Nara-period centralized government had established across Japan. The discovery was a landmark in Japanese archaeology, but the full story of what lay beneath the soil would take decades more to emerge. When excavators finally broke ground between 1963 and 1965, they found not just one administrative compound but a layered complex of moats, ramparts, wooden palisades, and foundation stones stretching across an area of eight to nine cho -- roughly twenty acres of bureaucratic architecture that once governed the entire province of Omi.

## An Empire in Miniature

Under the Ritsuryo system that organized Japan from the late seventh century onward, each province received its own kokufu -- a miniature capital where a centrally appointed governor, or kokushi, administered everything from tax collection to military defense. The Omi Provincial Capital was one of approximately sixty such complexes built across Japan. Its design followed a strict template: a double concentric enclosure, each ring surrounded by a moat and earthen rampart topped with a wooden palisade. The inner compound measured 72.8 meters east to west and 109 meters north to south. Inside, a raised earthen platform held the main hall -- a seven-by-five bay building approximately 23.1 meters by 15 meters -- flanked by side halls to the east and west, connected by covered corridors to form a U-shaped courtyard. A second, slightly smaller hall of six by five bays stood behind the main structure. Surrounding these government buildings were offices for farming administration, finance, policing, and military matters, along with a provincial school, garrison, and storehouses for taxes paid in rice.

## Two Lives, Then Silence

Excavation revealed that the complex had been built and rebuilt through two distinct phases. The first construction dates to the middle of the Nara period, roughly the mid-eighth century, when the Ritsuryo system was at its administrative peak. The second phase of building stretched from the end of the Nara period into the early Heian period. Then, sometime around the end of the tenth century, the complex was abandoned entirely. The reasons are not spelled out in surviving records, but the decline mirrors a broader pattern across Japan: as provincial governance weakened and local warrior clans accumulated power, the kokufu system gradually lost its grip. The buildings decayed, the moats silted in, the wooden palisades rotted, and the entire complex vanished beneath centuries of farming and settlement. When scholars finally came looking, only the foundation stones and the faint outlines of moats in the soil remained to testify that an entire provincial government had once operated here.

## The Satellite Sites

The Omi Provincial Capital was not a single compound but a constellation of related facilities spread across the landscape. Roughly 500 meters southeast of the main ruins, archaeologists uncovered the Soyama Site -- foundations of twelve granaries used for storing taxation rice, the grain tributes that funded the provincial government. This site was added to the National Historic Site designation in 1998. Three hundred meters south of the main complex's south gate lies the Aoe Site, a smaller moated and palisaded enclosure containing remains from three separate construction phases. The buildings' purpose remains uncertain, but the roof tiles are identical to those found at both the main capital and the Soyama Site, suggesting these structures housed additional government offices. Pottery inscribed with the era name 'Tempyo Shoko' and ink stones used for official correspondence support that interpretation. The Aoe Site was added to the designation in 2005. A third area northwest of Aoe, the Nakaji Site, contains two building traces with matching roof tiles. Its position suggests it may have served as the governor's official residence. It joined the protected area in 2009.

## Tiles That Tell the Story

One of the most revealing aspects of the Omi Provincial Capital is its roof tiles. In Nara and Heian Japan, roof tile production was a standardized, centrally controlled craft, and the patterns pressed into tile ends served as a kind of bureaucratic fingerprint. The identical tiles found at the main capital, the Soyama granaries, the Aoe offices, and the Nakaji residence prove these were not independent structures but components of a single administrative network -- a coordinated provincial government spread across the Oe neighborhood of what is now Otsu. Today, portions of the rampart and foundation platform have been reconstructed, and visitors can walk among the restored pillar bases of the main hall. The site sits in a quiet residential area, a ten-minute walk from the Jinryodanchi bus stop on the Ohmi Railway Bus from Ishiyama Station. The reconstructions are modest -- low earthen walls, stone markers, interpretive panels -- but they sketch the outline of a complex that once represented the full reach of imperial authority in Omi Province.

## Flight Context

Located at 34.97°N, 135.92°E in the Oe neighborhood of Otsu, Shiga Prefecture, on the southwestern shore of Lake Biwa. From altitude, the site sits within a residential area south of central Otsu, with the distinctive expanse of Lake Biwa immediately to the northeast. Best viewed at 3,000-5,000 feet AGL.
