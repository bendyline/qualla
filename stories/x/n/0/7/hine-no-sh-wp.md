# Hine-no-sho

A medieval Japanese feudal estate stretching from the coast of Osaka Bay to the Izumi Mountains, whose shrines, temples, irrigation ponds, and castle ruins survive as a remarkably intact landscape -- fourteen sites collectively designated a National Historic Site.

**Location:** 34.3582, 135.3676

**Categories:** historic-site, medieval-japan, osaka, cultural-landscape, feudal-estate

## Introduction

Sometime around the year 1501, Kujo Masamoto -- a former kampaku, the highest-ranking advisor to the emperor -- left Kyoto and moved to a rural estate in what is now Izumisano, Osaka Prefecture. He stayed for three and a half years. During that time, he wrote down everything he saw: the shrines, the temples, the irrigation ponds, the gentle hills that rolled from the coast of Osaka Bay toward the Izumi Mountains. That record, made by a retired statesman with nothing left to do but observe, became the foundation for one of Japan's most unusual National Historic Site designations. In 1998, fourteen separate locations connected with Hine-no-sho -- a vast feudal estate, or shoen, that operated from the thirteenth century through the sixteenth -- were collectively recognized as a single historical landscape. The designation was expanded in 2005 and 2013, and the estate has since been recognized as a Japan Heritage site.

## An Estate Built Where Monks Failed

The land that became Hine-no-sho was wild country before the Kujo family claimed it. The powerful monastery on Mount Koya applied twice -- in 1205 and again in 1222 -- for permission to develop the territory into a tax-free shoen, an autonomous estate that would fund the temple's operations. Both times, permission was granted. Both times, Mount Koya failed to develop the land. In 1234, the aristocrat Kujo Michiie stepped in, taking over the territory that stretched from Osaka Bay's coastal flats to the foothills of the Izumi Mountains. This was unusual. The Kujo family already controlled numerous shoen received through donation, but Hine-no-sho was a rare case of a noble family building an estate from scratch rather than inheriting one. Initially spanning four villages -- Iriyamada, Hineno, Ihara, and Tsuruhara -- it was a self-contained world of paddies, shrines, and fortified retreats.

## Centuries of Contested Harvest

For a feudal estate, Hine-no-sho had remarkably bad luck with powerful neighbors. During the Muromachi period, the shugo -- military governor -- of Izumi Province, the Hosokawa clan, seized two of the estate's four villages outright. The remaining two villages, corresponding to modern Hineno, Oki, and Tsuchimaru neighborhoods of Izumisano plus parts of neighboring Kumatori, carried on under Kujo control. But the Nanboku-cho period brought civil war to the region, and constant fighting between rival imperial courts eroded the family's grip. Samurai raided the estate's resources. By the middle of the sixteenth century, control had passed entirely to Negoro-ji temple, a powerful and militant Buddhist institution. The Kujo family's three-century tenure as lords of this land ended not with a dramatic battle but with a slow, grinding loss of authority to armed monks and local warlords.

## A Landscape of National Treasures

What makes Hine-no-sho extraordinary is not any single monument but the way its pieces fit together as a living medieval landscape. Hine Jinja, a shrine founded in the Asuka or Nara period and listed in the ancient Engishiki records, anchors the sacred core. Adjacent Jigen-in temple, traditionally founded in 673 as the jingu-ji of Hine Jinja, houses a wooden Tahoto pagoda built in 1271 -- designated a National Treasure -- and a Kamakura-period main hall classified as a National Important Cultural Property. Nearby Sofuku-ji contains a Tenman-gu shrine from the Tensho era (1573-1585) described in Kujo family documents, itself an Important Cultural Property. Scattered among these are Tsuchimaru and Ameyama Castles, yamashiro-style mountain fortifications used from the Nanboku-cho through Sengoku periods as refuges by the Kujo lords. Even the irrigation infrastructure -- canals fed from the Kashii River, natural ponds that predate the estate itself -- survives as part of the designated landscape.

## The Retired Statesman's Gift

Kujo Masamoto's detailed diary from 1501 to 1504 did more than describe a rural estate. It froze a medieval landscape in text, creating a baseline that archaeologists and historians could use five centuries later to verify what remained. His descriptions of shrines, ponds, and hills matched what surveyors found in the 1980s with striking accuracy. Excavations from 2002 to 2003 confirmed the ruins of Chofuku-ji, the temple that Masamoto's own villa had been converted into after his departure -- a site that disappeared from historical records after 1611 and now lies beneath paddy fields, backfilled after study. The landscape Masamoto recorded is still recognizably the same one visitors walk through today, a patchwork of sacred groves, irrigation channels, and terraced rice fields stretching between the mountains and the bay.

## Flight Context

Located at 34.36N, 135.37E on the coastal plain of southern Osaka Prefecture, in present-day Izumisano. The estate landscape stretches from the shore of Osaka Bay eastward into the foothills of the Izumi Mountains. From the air, look for the transition zone between dense coastal development and the terraced agricultural land climbing into the hills. Kansai International Airport (RJBB) is immediately adjacent, built on an artificial island in Osaka Bay just offshore -- making this one of the closest National Historic Sites to a major international airport. Osaka Itami (RJOO) lies approximately 30nm north. The Hanwa Expressway corridor runs through the area. Expect marine influence weather with good visibility along the coast, potential haze from Osaka Bay.
