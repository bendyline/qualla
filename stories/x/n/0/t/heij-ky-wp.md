# Heijō-kyō

Japan's first truly international capital, Heijō-kyō was modeled after China's Chang'an and served as the seat of imperial power from 710 to 784, leaving behind a UNESCO World Heritage landscape in modern Nara.

**Location:** 34.6911, 135.7947

**Categories:** Former capitals of Japan, Nara period, Planned capitals, Special Places of Scenic Beauty, Special Historic Sites

## Introduction

In 708, Empress Genmei issued an order that would reshape Japan: move the imperial capital to a new city modeled on Chang'an, the cosmopolitan heart of Tang-dynasty China. Two years later, Heijō-kyō was complete -- an irregular rectangle of more than 25 square kilometers, laid out on a rigid Chinese-inspired grid, with a grand imperial palace anchoring the northern edge and the Suzakumon gate marking the ceremonial southern entrance. For most of the next seven decades, this was the political nerve center of Japan and its first true international city, where merchants from China, Korea, and India arrived along Silk Road trade routes, bringing foreign goods, religions, and ideas to a population that peaked between 50,000 and 100,000. Today, the palace grounds survive as an open archaeological park in the western suburbs of Nara, a UNESCO World Heritage Site where reconstructed vermilion gates and audience halls stand against the backdrop of the Yamato hills.

## A Capital Built on a Chinese Blueprint

Heijō-kyō borrowed heavily from Chang'an but adapted the model to Japanese conditions. The city lacked the massive rammed-earth walls that enclosed its Chinese inspiration, reflecting a political reality in which no foreign army threatened the Japanese heartland. The grid plan divided the city into rectangular blocks, with the imperial Heijō Palace occupying a commanding position at the northern center -- a symbolic arrangement placing the emperor, like the North Star, at the apex of the earthly order. Within the palace compound stood the Daigokuden, the Great Audience Hall where the emperor conducted state ceremonies, and a sprawling complex of administrative buildings, gardens, and aristocratic residences. A broad ceremonial boulevard, Suzaku-ōji, ran south from the palace gate to the city limits, dividing Heijō-kyō into eastern and western halves. The rigid geometry broadcast a message: Japan was a civilized state, organized on continental principles, worthy of respect on the international stage.

## The Nara Period's Living Stage

The decades of Heijō-kyō's primacy -- roughly 710 to 784, with a brief interruption when the capital shifted to Kuni-kyō and then Shigaraki between 740 and 745 -- were among the most culturally productive in Japanese history. Emperor Shōmu commissioned the colossal bronze Buddha of Tōdai-ji, established provincial temples across the nation, and welcomed foreign scholars and monks who enriched Japanese Buddhism, art, and literature. The great temples that clustered around the capital -- Tōdai-ji, Kōfuku-ji, Yakushi-ji, Gangō-ji, Daian-ji, Saidai-ji -- became centers of learning and artistic patronage whose influence extended across East Asia. Silk Road goods flowed into the Shōsōin treasure repository at Tōdai-ji: Persian glassware, Central Asian textiles, and Chinese musical instruments that survive in extraordinary condition to this day. Heijō-kyō was not merely a seat of government; it was a crossroads where the cultures of the known world met on Japanese soil.

## Abandonment and Rediscovery

In 784, Emperor Kanmu moved the capital to Nagaoka-kyō, and a decade later to Heian-kyō -- modern Kyoto -- where it would remain for over a thousand years. Heijō-kyō was swiftly abandoned. Fields reclaimed the grid streets, and rice paddies covered the palace grounds. For centuries, the site slumbered beneath agricultural land on the western outskirts of Nara, its exact boundaries known only from historical texts. Modern archaeology began in earnest in the early 20th century, gradually revealing the scale of the buried capital. Excavations uncovered foundations, drainage systems, wooden tablets inscribed with administrative records, and the outlines of gardens and ceremonial spaces. In 1998, the Heijō Palace site was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site under the Historic Monuments of Ancient Nara, alongside the great temples that once surrounded it. The 1,300th anniversary in 2010 brought major commemorative events, including costume reenactments and a full-scale replica of a diplomatic ship once sent to Tang China.

## Walking the Ruins Today

The Heijō Palace archaeological park is a striking urban landscape -- a vast, largely empty expanse of grass and gravel punctuated by a handful of meticulously reconstructed buildings. The rebuilt Suzakumon gate, with its massive vermilion pillars and tiled double roof, marks the old southern entrance and frames the view north toward the reconstructed Daigokuden, the imposing Great Audience Hall where emperors once held court. Between these two landmarks, the scale of the original palace becomes palpable: this was a compound designed to overawe, with open courtyards large enough to assemble thousands. The Heijō Palace Site Museum and an excavation exhibition hall provide context, displaying artifacts and architectural models. The Eastern Palace Garden, reconstructed from archaeological evidence, offers a glimpse of aristocratic leisure during the Nara period. For visitors arriving from the bustle of Nara's temple district, the emptiness of the palace grounds is itself the point -- a reminder that entire civilizations can vanish beneath a farmer's plow, leaving only foundations and the memory of grandeur.
