# Shogo-in: The Mountain Monks' Imperial Temple

A Kyoto temple where 25 of 37 head priests came from the imperial family, Shogo-in served as the headquarters of Japan's mountain ascetic tradition and twice became a temporary imperial palace -- yet remains one of the city's least-visited treasures.

**Location:** 35.0192, 135.7803

**Categories:** temple, historic-site, buddhism, kyoto, japan

## Introduction

Of the 37 head priests who led Shogo-in through the centuries before the Meiji period, 25 came from the Japanese imperial family. The remaining twelve descended from the regent houses that governed alongside the emperor. No other temple in Kyoto can claim such concentrated royal lineage -- and yet Shogo-in sits just north of the famous Heian Shrine in the Shogoin-Nakamachi neighborhood of Sakyo-ku, largely overlooked by the tourists streaming past to more celebrated destinations. This is the central temple of the Honzan school of Shugendo, the ancient Japanese practice of mountain asceticism, and the headquarters that once governed every regional organization controlling Shugendo practitioners across the country. Twice during the late Edo period, when the imperial palace was unavailable, the emperor moved here. The grounds were designated a National Historic Site in 1937.

## A Monk, an Emperor, and a Mountain Pilgrimage

Shogo-in owes its existence to a pilgrimage. In 1090, the retired Emperor Shirakawa wished to make the arduous journey to the Kumano Sanzan -- the three sacred shrines deep in the mountainous Kii Peninsula south of Kyoto. He needed a guide who understood the mountain paths and the spiritual practices they demanded. That guide was Zoyo, a monk of Onjo-ji temple and a renowned practitioner of Shugendo, the syncretic tradition blending Buddhism, Shinto, and Taoism through rigorous mountain asceticism. Zoyo had trained on Mount Omine in Kumano, following in the footsteps of his master Enchin. For successfully leading the emperor's pilgrimage, Zoyo was appointed the first Kumano Sanzan Kengyo -- general manager of the three sacred sites -- and received a temple near the Kumano Shrine in Kyoto, said to have been founded by the legendary ascetic En no Gyoja. Zoyo built Shogo-in on these grounds, though the name itself does not appear in documentary records until 1132, after his death.

## Imperial Blood and Feudal Storms

What elevated Shogo-in beyond an ordinary temple was the decision of Prince Shizue, son of the retired Emperor Go-Shirakawa, to take the tonsure and join the monastery. From that moment, Shogo-in became a monzeki temple -- a designation reserved for institutions whose head was a member of the imperial family or a descendant of the five regent houses. Prince Kakusuke, son of the retired Emperor Go-Saga, held the position of monzeki for an extraordinary 70 years, simultaneously serving as abbot of Onjo-ji and inspector of all three Kumano Sanzan sites. But the temple could not escape the turbulence of medieval Japan. After Kakusuke's death, succession disputes entangled Shogo-in in the conflict between the Southern and Northern Courts, drawing the intervention of the Muromachi shogunate. During the devastating Onin War, the temple burned to the ground. Its head priest, Michioki, was accused of sympathizing with the Western Army and fled to Mino Province before eventually being pardoned.

## Destruction, Relocation, and Return

Throughout the Sengoku period of civil warfare, Shogo-in was governed by head priests from the powerful Konoe family, who served as intermediaries between the shogunate and regional warlords. The temple's physical fate continued to be turbulent. Toyotomi Hideyoshi ordered it moved from its original site to Karasuma Imadegawa. Then the great Kyoto fire of 1676 destroyed it again during the Edo period. When it was finally rebuilt, the temple returned to its original location -- the spot where Zoyo had established it nearly six centuries earlier. That continuity of place carries weight in a city where fires, wars, and earthquakes have erased so much. Twice in the late Edo period, the emperor himself took residence at Shogo-in when the palace was unavailable, a testament to the temple's stature and its intimate connection with the throne.

## Treasures Behind Quiet Walls

The temple's current structures include the Main Hall and the graceful Shinden Hall, rebuilt after centuries of destruction. Among Shogo-in's most significant possessions is a statue of Enchin -- the Buddhist monk whose lineage of Shugendo practice stretches back through the temple's entire history -- dated to 1143 and designated an Important Cultural Property. In 2000, a multi-year renovation of the temple was completed to mark the 1,300th anniversary of the death of En no Gyoja, the semi-legendary founder of Shugendo itself. The central image of worship, the honzon, is a figure of Fudo Myoo -- the fearsome, flame-wreathed Buddhist deity associated with discipline, willpower, and the conquest of inner obstacles. For a temple dedicated to the mountain ascetics who once climbed sacred peaks in prayer and physical endurance, the choice of guardian deity could not be more fitting.

## Flight Context

Coordinates: 35.019N, 135.780E in the Sakyo-ku district of Kyoto, Japan. The temple sits immediately north of Heian Shrine, whose distinctive vermillion torii is one of the most visible landmarks in eastern Kyoto from the air. The compound is approximately 1.3 km north of Higashiyama Station. From altitude, look for the cluster of traditional rooflines nestled among modern buildings in the triangle between Heian Shrine's grounds and the Kyoto University campus.
