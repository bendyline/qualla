# Ryozen Kannon: The Goddess Who Remembers Two Million

A 24-meter concrete statue of the Bodhisattva of Compassion rises above Kyoto's Higashiyama hills, built in 1955 as a Buddhist alternative to nationalist war shrines, honoring two million Japanese war dead alongside memorials to Allied prisoners of war.

**Location:** 34.9999, 135.7819

**Categories:** war-memorial, statue, buddhist-site, kyoto, japan

## Introduction

Four times a day, monks from different Buddhist sects gather beneath a 24-meter concrete goddess to chant for the dead. The names they invoke number two million. Ryozen Kannon rises above the eastern hills of Kyoto in the Higashiyama district, her serene white face gazing westward over a city that was deliberately spared from Allied bombing during World War II. She holds no weapon, carries no banner. In her left hand rests a jewel; her right is raised in a gesture of reassurance. Built a decade after the war's end, this towering figure of Kannon -- the Bodhisattva of Compassion known elsewhere as Avalokitesvara -- was designed not as a celebration of military sacrifice but as a prayer for peace, a deliberate contrast to the nationalist overtones of Tokyo's Yasukuni Shrine.

## A Bus Magnate's Act of Contrition

Hirosuke Ishikawa was not a monk or a politician but a transportation entrepreneur, the founder of Teisan Kanko Bus Company. In the years following Japan's surrender in 1945, as the country struggled through occupation and reconstruction, Ishikawa conceived a memorial that would honor the war dead through Buddhist compassion rather than Shinto martial tradition. He commissioned the statue, which was unveiled on June 8, 1955 -- ten years and one day after the formal end of hostilities. The design drew from a thirteenth-century painting of Kannon held at Kyoto's Daitoku-ji temple and was realized by the renowned sculptor Yamazaki Choun. The finished colossus, weighing approximately 500 tons, stands on a hill just minutes from the geisha district of Gion, visible above the tree line from parks and temple walks throughout eastern Kyoto.

## The Shrine Beneath Her Gaze

Visitors who climb the steps and pass through the front gate discover the memorial is far more than a single statue. Inside the base of the Kannon figure, an enshrined image of the Eleven-Headed Kannon awaits, flanked by depictions of Fujin, the god of wind, and Raijin, the god of thunder. The Reihaiden Hall nearby houses roughly 600,000 small Buddhist memorial tablets, or ihai, inscribed for those who died in the Asia-Pacific conflict -- including tablets for Korean victims, an acknowledgment of Japan's colonial-era conscription that few other Japanese memorials have matched. Soil brought from war cemeteries around the world is preserved in vessels on the grounds. The reflecting pool at the entrance, Kagami Ike, blooms with lotus flowers in summer, while visitors light incense sticks and offer prayers at stations dedicated to each of the twelve zodiac guardian figures.

## Across the Divide

What makes Ryozen Kannon unusual among Japanese war memorials is a small monument erected shortly after the statue's 1955 unveiling: a memorial to Allied prisoners of war who died in Japanese captivity between 1941 and 1945. A Christian-style chapel on the grounds contains documentation of Allied personnel who perished, and the monument itself professes world peace and reconciliation. This transnational gesture was rare in 1950s Japan, where public discussion of wartime atrocities remained largely taboo. The memorial to the Unknown Soldier, added in 1959, extends the site's inclusive vision further. Where Yasukuni Shrine has generated diplomatic controversy by enshrining convicted war criminals alongside ordinary soldiers, Ryozen Kannon sidesteps that debate entirely by framing remembrance through Buddhist theology: all who suffered deserve compassion, regardless of nationality or circumstance.

## A Quiet Landmark in a Crowded City

Despite its imposing height, Ryozen Kannon receives only a fraction of the visitors who flock to nearby Kiyomizu-dera or the vermilion torii of Fushimi Inari. That relative quiet suits its purpose. Open daily from 8:40 in the morning until 4:20 in the afternoon, the grounds offer a contemplative detour from the tourist density of Higashiyama. Visitors can walk through the Tainai Meguri, a passage beneath the statue symbolizing spiritual rebirth, and turn a wishing ball inscribed with prayers. The Pufferfish Tumulus, an oddity on the grounds, memorializes the souls of fugu consumed in Japanese cuisine -- a reminder that Buddhist compassion, in this place, extends even to fish. Nearby stand Maruyama Park, Yasaka Shrine, and the Kodai-ji temple, but Ryozen Kannon occupies its own quiet register: not a tourist spectacle, but a place where grief and hope coexist at monumental scale.
