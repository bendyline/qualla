# Chinzan-so Garden: The House of Camellias

A Tokyo garden where a haiku master once gazed from his hut, a Meiji-era prince hosted the Emperor among camellia blossoms, and a thousand-year-old pagoda assembled without nails stands among cultural treasures gathered from across Japan.

**Location:** 35.7124, 139.7254

**Categories:** garden, historic-site, tokyo, japan, cultural-heritage

## Introduction

Somewhere in the 1600s, Matsuo Basho -- the wandering poet whose sparse haiku distilled entire landscapes into seventeen syllables -- settled into a simple hut overlooking a wooded hillside in what is now Bunkyo, Tokyo. He stayed four years, watching the seasons shift across the terrain below. Three centuries later, the same undulating topography caught the eye of one of Japan's most powerful men, and the hillside became a garden whose name translates to a single, fragrant promise: the House of Camellias. Chinzan-so Garden has been shaped by poets, princes, and collectors, each layer adding depth to a landscape that compresses centuries of Japanese culture into a single walk through its grounds.

## The Prince and the Emperor's Table

In the Meiji period, Prince Aritomo Yamagata -- a field marshal, two-time prime minister, and one of the most influential statesmen in modern Japanese history -- chose this hillside for his private estate. He named it Chinzan-so, the House of Camellia, for the profusion of camellia flowers that bloomed across the property each winter. Yamagata was a man who understood landscapes as instruments of power; he used the natural rise and fall of the terrain to design a garden that felt both intimate and grand. The estate became a venue for political meetings of the highest order. Emperor Meiji himself held conferences here with his senior dignitaries, conducting the business of a rapidly modernizing nation amid the quiet rustling of camellia leaves. The garden was not merely beautiful -- it was where decisions shaping Japan's future were made.

## A Thousand Years Without a Nail

When the property passed to Baron Heitaro Fujita, the garden's role shifted from political stage to cultural treasury. Fujita scoured Japan for historical monuments, transporting artifacts from Kyoto, Toba, and beyond. His most remarkable acquisition was a three-story pagoda from the mountains of Hiroshima Prefecture, built by monks of the Chikurin-ji temple without the use of a single nail -- a structure already a thousand years old when Fujita arranged its careful relocation to Tokyo. The Shiratama Inari Shrine, removed from the grounds of Shimogamo in Kyoto in 1924, now stands at the center of the garden. Carved Taoist and Buddhist images appear along the pathways, and more than thirty stone lanterns -- each with its own provenance and character -- punctuate the greenery. The garden became a kind of open-air museum, gathering Japan's scattered cultural heritage into one living collection.

## Water, Wood, and Living Stone

Beyond the historical artifacts, Chinzan-so's landscape holds its own natural wonders. A large pond reflects the surrounding trees and structures, fed by a natural spring that has flowed from the hillside for centuries. A waterfall adds the constant sound of moving water to the garden's quieter spaces. Near the center of the grounds stands a sacred tree estimated at five hundred years old, its trunk measuring four and a half meters around the base -- a living monument that predates the garden's human history by several hundred years. The effect of walking the paths is cumulative: each turn reveals another lantern, another carved figure, another view of the pagoda framed by foliage. The garden's undulating topography, which first attracted Yamagata, means that sightlines constantly change with elevation, creating a sense of depth and discovery that belies the garden's relatively compact size.

## Surviving and Persisting

Founded in 1877, the garden has endured more than a century of Tokyo's relentless transformation. The property is now part of the Hotel Chinzanso Tokyo, accessible by walking from Edogawabashi Station on the Yurakucho Line. Despite this modern context, the garden retains its character as a place where deep time is visible. A visitor walking these paths moves from a poet's 17th-century view to a prince's Meiji-era ambitions to a baron's Taisho-era collecting impulse, all compressed into a few hectares. The camellia trees that gave Chinzan-so its name still bloom each winter, their flowers dropping whole to the ground in the Japanese tradition -- not petal by petal, but all at once. It is a garden that has always belonged to people who understood that beauty and history are inseparable, and that a landscape shaped by attentive hands across centuries becomes something more than the sum of its parts.

## Flight Context

Located at 35.712N, 139.725E in Tokyo's Bunkyo ward, on a hillside northeast of Waseda University.
