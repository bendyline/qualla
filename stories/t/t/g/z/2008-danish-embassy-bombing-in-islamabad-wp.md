# 2008 Danish Embassy Bombing in Islamabad

On June 2, 2008, a suicide car bomb detonated outside the Danish embassy in Islamabad, killing at least six people in an al-Qaeda attack linked to the Jyllands-Posten Muhammad cartoons controversy.

**Location:** 33.7322, 73.0676

**Categories:** disasters-and-events, military-and-conflict, historical

## Introduction

At 12:10 in the afternoon on June 2, 2008, a stolen Toyota Corolla bearing diplomatic plates accelerated past the entrance of the Danish embassy in Islamabad and stopped in the parking lot. Seconds later, the car exploded. The blast killed at least six people, wounded twenty-four others, left a crater in front of the building, and sent a column of black smoke visible across the Pakistani capital. It was the kind of attack that had been explicitly threatened, repeatedly warned about, and ultimately not prevented -- a suicide bombing claimed by al-Qaeda as retribution for a set of newspaper cartoons published two years earlier and five thousand kilometers away.

## Twelve Minutes Past Noon

The embassy's surveillance cameras captured the entire sequence: the car's arrival at high speed, the pause in the parking lot, the detonation. The stolen Corolla had evaded heightened security measures because it carried diplomatic registration plates -- a detail that speaks to the planning behind the attack. The explosion inflicted severe structural damage on the embassy and on a neighboring Pakistani Development Organisation building that absorbed much of the blast. Windowpanes shattered at the nearby India House, residence of India's ambassador to Pakistan. Among the six dead were two local employees of the embassy, a Danish citizen of Pakistani origin who did not work there, and a security guard at a United Nations Development Programme building nearby. Six wounded were UNDP employees. None of the Danish diplomatic staff were killed, though the embassy's charge d'affaires, Michael Hjortso, confirmed the casualties.

## Warnings Unheeded

The attack did not arrive without notice. The Danish embassy had received multiple threats in the months preceding the bombing, the most recent just weeks before the blast. In April 2008, al-Qaeda's then-deputy leader Ayman al-Zawahiri had publicly urged Muslims to strike Danish targets, citing both the Jyllands-Posten Muhammad cartoons controversy and Denmark's military presence in Afghanistan. The cartoons -- originally published in September 2005 and reprinted in February 2006 -- had already triggered protests, boycotts, and violence across the Muslim world. Denmark had adjusted the threat level at its embassies in the Islamic world, but the adjustments proved insufficient. The car bomb demonstrated that the cartoon controversy, which some Western observers had begun to consider fading, retained lethal momentum among extremist networks.

## Claiming the Violence

Three days after the bombing, on June 5, an online posting attributed to Mustafa Abu al-Yazid appeared on a jihadist website claiming al-Qaeda had planned and executed the attack. Denmark's national security intelligence agency, PET, had initially suspected the Taliban, but the Taliban denied involvement. PET ultimately concluded that al-Qaeda was responsible. The mastermind was identified as Fahid Mohammed Ally Msalam, al-Qaeda's chief of operations for Pakistan, who was killed alongside his lieutenant Sheikh Ahmed Salim Swedan in a drone strike on January 1, 2009 -- seven months after the embassy bombing. In Pakistan, three men were charged in connection with the attack, but a Pakistani court acquitted all three on September 25, 2010. The legal outcome left the families of those killed without judicial closure.

## Reverberations

The Danish government learned of the attack at 9:00 AM Copenhagen time. Foreign Minister Per Stig Moller condemned the bombing immediately, and Prime Minister Anders Fogh Rasmussen followed with his own statement that afternoon. The Norwegian and Swedish embassies in Islamabad were evacuated as a precaution, and Denmark advised all its citizens against travel to Pakistan. The bombing was the second terrorist attack in 2008 to target Scandinavians -- the first was the January assault on the Serena Hotel in Kabul, where a Norwegian delegation led by Foreign Minister Jonas Gahr Store was staying at the time. Together, the two attacks underscored how the cartoon controversy and Nordic military deployments in Afghanistan had made Scandinavian institutions targets far from home. For the people of Islamabad, the bombing was another scar on a city that had already endured multiple attacks, a reminder that the violence consuming Pakistan's frontier regions could reach its quiet, tree-lined capital at any moment.
