# 2020 Aden Airport Attack

Three guided missiles struck Aden International Airport on December 30, 2020, as Yemen's new unity government arrived -- killing 28 people, mostly civilians, in an attack the UN called a potential war crime.

**Location:** 12.8256, 45.0364

**Categories:** conflict, historical-event, aviation

## Introduction

The red carpet had already been rolled out. Hundreds of people -- local officials, airport workers, journalists, ordinary citizens -- had gathered at Aden International Airport on December 30, 2020, to witness something that felt, for a moment, like hope. Yemen's new unity government, painstakingly assembled over a year of Saudi-mediated negotiations between feuding factions, was flying in from Riyadh. Television cameras were ready to broadcast the arrival. Then, at 13:24, as cabinet members descended the aircraft stairs, the first missile hit the terminal. A second struck the parking bay thirty-five seconds later -- exactly where the plane had originally been scheduled to park before a last-minute change. A third impacted the garden wall outside. Twenty-eight people died in the attack, and 107 were wounded. The cabinet members survived. Most of those who did not were the civilians who had come to welcome them.

## A Government Born in Exile

Understanding the attack requires understanding what Yemen's unity government represented -- and who stood to lose from its success. Since Houthi rebels captured the capital Sanaa in 2014, Yemen had fractured into competing authorities: the internationally recognized government, the Houthis in the north, and the secessionist Southern Transitional Council that had seized Aden itself. The new 24-member cabinet, sworn in by President Abdrabbuh Mansur Hadi in Riyadh, included equal representation from northern and southern regions. It was the product of exhausting Saudi-led diplomacy aimed at unifying at least two of these factions against the Houthis. The televised return to Aden was meant to signal a functioning state reasserting itself. Instead, the missiles turned a moment of political theater into a scene of devastation that laid bare just how far Yemen remained from anything resembling peace.

## Sixty-Nine Seconds

The attack unfolded with a precision that investigations would later attribute to GPS-guided ballistic missiles. Interior Minister Ibrahim Haydan's official report documented the sequence: the first missile hit the airport lounge at 13:24:34, the second struck the aircraft parking area at 13:25:09, and the third hit the garden wall at 13:25:33. Sixty-nine seconds, three impacts. A joint investigation by Bellingcat and the Yemeni Archive traced the launches to Houthi-controlled territory -- two missiles from the Dhamar Governorate to the north, one from the vicinity of Taiz International Airport. The evidence that the plane itself was the intended target was chilling: the second missile landed precisely where the aircraft had been scheduled to park before a crowd on the apron forced a last-minute change in position. The dead included three International Committee of the Red Cross workers, a Belqees TV reporter who was broadcasting live when his connection went silent, and Yasmin al-Awadhi, a deputy minister who was one of the few women in Yemen's government.

## Ripples Beyond the Runway

The attack's consequences radiated outward in ways its planners may not have anticipated. U.S. Secretary of State Mike Pompeo cited it as justification for designating the Houthis a Foreign Terrorist Organization -- a move that alarmed humanitarian agencies working in a country where ninety percent of food is imported. The World Food Programme's executive director warned the designation would be "a death sentence to hundreds of thousands, if not millions of innocent people." The timing was politically charged: the designation took effect on January 19, 2021, one day before Joe Biden's inauguration. Biden's incoming secretary of state, Antony Blinken, called it impractical and proposed immediate review. By February 16, the designation was formally revoked. Meanwhile, the Houthis denied responsibility, blaming internal feuding between the government and Southern Transitional Council. Saudi-led coalition airstrikes hit Sanaa the morning after the attack. The cycle of violence that the unity government was supposed to interrupt continued unbroken.

## The Weight of Witness

The UN special envoy for Yemen, Martin Griffiths, called the airport attack "a serious violation of International Humanitarian Law" that "potentially amounts to a war crime." The Arab League condemned it as a "cowardly terrorist act." Condemnations came from Egypt, India, Jordan, Turkey, the United Kingdom, and the United States. But for the families of the twenty-eight people killed -- the airport staff, the aid workers, the journalists, the soldiers, the deputy minister -- international statements offered little solace. Tens of thousands of Yemenis marched in Sanaa to protest the subsequent terrorist designation, not the attack itself, illustrating how deeply the country's fractures run. The airport reopened within days. Reconstruction proceeded alongside the continuing war. Aden International Airport, a place meant to connect Yemen to the wider world, had instead become another monument to the country's inability to find its way out of a conflict that has killed hundreds of thousands and displaced millions since 2014.
