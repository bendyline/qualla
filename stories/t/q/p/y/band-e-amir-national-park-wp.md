# Band-e Amir National Park

Afghanistan's first national park protects six startlingly turquoise lakes formed by natural travertine dams in the Hindu Kush, a landscape of rare beauty entangled with the country's contested present.

**Location:** 34.8397, 67.2308

**Categories:** National parks of Afghanistan, Geography of Bamyan Province, Hazarajat, Lake groups of Afghanistan, Landforms of Bamyan Province, Protected areas established in 2009, Important Bird Areas of Afghanistan

## Introduction

When Afghanistan designated Band-e Amir as its first national park in 2009, it was trying to protect something that needed no human help to be extraordinary. Six lakes of deep, almost unnatural turquoise occupy basins at 2,900 meters in Bamyan Province, their waters held back by travertine dams that mineral-laden springs have been building for thousands of years. The park was a statement of normalcy, an assertion that a country torn by decades of war could still set land aside for beauty. The Wildlife Conservation Society helped establish it, rangers were hired, and ecotourism was proposed as a path toward sustainable livelihoods. Beneath the optimism, the landscape told a more complicated story.

## A Park Born in Wartime

The formal establishment of Band-e Amir National Park came with a park office, a warden, and a small group of rangers. The Wildlife Conservation Society became the only non-governmental organization with a permanent presence, supporting staff and working with local communities on conservation. The vision was straightforward: ecotourism would reduce economic pressure on natural resources and give people a reason to protect rather than exploit the land. Reality proved more stubborn. The local economy remained poor, outside investment scarce. Tourists came primarily in summer, when the weather cooperated and the roads were passable. Winter tourism never materialized. Residents continued to graze livestock within park boundaries, collect shrubs for fuel, and practice rain-fed farming on land that was now officially protected. The gap between the park on paper and the park on the ground remained wide.

## What the Land Sustains

The ecological case for protection was never in doubt. BirdLife International designated a tract overlapping the park as an Important Bird Area, citing populations of Himalayan snowcocks, Hume's larks, white-winged snowfinches, Afghan snowfinches, and Eurasian crimson-winged finches. More than 170 bird species have been recorded in the area. The lakes themselves, fed by carbon dioxide-rich springs that deposit calcium carbonate as the gas escapes, represent a geological process visible in few places on Earth. The travertine walls grow at their own pace, indifferent to the political calendar. Illegal hunting of birds and mammals is formally prohibited by the park office, but enforcement is limited and no current data exist to evaluate the actual status of wildlife populations. Conservation here is aspirational, constrained by resources, geography, and conflict.

## Closed Gates

In August 2023, the Taliban banned women from entering Band-e Amir National Park. The acting Minister of Virtue and Vice, Mohammad Khaled Hanafi, alleged that women had not been observing hijab within the park and called on religious clerics and security agencies to forbid their entry until a solution was found. The ban added Band-e Amir to a lengthening list of public spaces closed to Afghan women under Taliban governance. For a park whose establishment had symbolized the possibility of civic life continuing amid conflict, the restriction represented a different kind of loss. The lakes remained open to male visitors. The turquoise water continued its slow geological work. But the promise of the park as a place for all Afghans shrank considerably.

## The Weight of Tourism

Despite everything, people come. In 2013, approximately 6,000 local tourists visited Band-e Amir each year. By 2024, Bamyan Province as a whole was reporting about 130,000 visitors annually, though how many reached Band-e Amir specifically is unclear. They arrive by minivan over unpaved roads from Bamiyan, 75 kilometers to the east, the same town where the Taliban destroyed two massive Buddha statues carved into cliff faces in 2001. The journey takes roughly three hours. At the lakes, visitors find a parking area, a few vendor stalls, and swan-shaped pedal boats available for rent on Band-e Haibat. The pedal boats are tacky. They also represent commerce, which represents normalcy, which is not nothing. Behind the small mosque-like tomb of Amir, a women's bathing area was once screened from view by a hut built into the water. Whether it still functions under the current restrictions is an open question.

## The Turquoise Keeps Building

Band-e Amir does not need people to exist, but people need Band-e Amir to mean something. For conservationists, it is proof that Afghanistan contains landscapes worthy of international protection. For ordinary visitors, it is a weekend destination in a country where such things are rare and precious. For the Hazara community of Bamyan Province, it is part of their homeland, a cultural landmark as much as a natural one. The springs go on dissolving limestone, carrying it to the surface, and depositing it as travertine. The dams go on growing. The water goes on being that particular shade of turquoise that photographs never quite capture, a color that demands to be seen in person, in the thin mountain light, at an altitude where breathing takes effort and the sky is a shade of blue that lowland skies cannot match. The park's future depends on politics, security, and funding. The lakes' future depends on geology. One of these is more reliable than the other.

## Flight Context
