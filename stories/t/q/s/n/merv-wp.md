# Merv

Once possibly the largest city on Earth, this Silk Road oasis in modern Turkmenistan flourished for five millennia before the Mongols opened its gates and ended its story in a single week.

**Location:** 37.6628, 62.1925

## Introduction

In 1221, the city of Merv opened its gates. This was a mistake. Tolui Khan, son of Genghis Khan, entered with his army, and historical accounts report that 700,000 people were slaughtered. The number may be exaggerated, as medieval chroniclers often were, but the destruction was real and total. A city that Arab geographers had called "the mother of the world" and "the capital of the eastern Islamic world," a city whose structures were visible from a day's journey away, a city that may have held 500,000 residents and been the largest on Earth, was reduced to ruins it never recovered from. What makes Merv's story extraordinary is not just how it ended, but how long and how brilliantly it had burned before that.

## Five Thousand Years at an Oasis

Human settlement at Merv reaches back to the third millennium BC, making it one of the oldest continuously inhabited places in Central Asia. The site sits on the Murghab River in what is now the Mary Region of Turkmenistan, a green oasis in the Karakum Desert sustained by snowmelt from the mountains of Afghanistan. In the Zoroastrian scriptures, it appears as Mouru, one of sixteen perfect lands created by the god Ahura Mazda. The Achaemenid Persians knew it as Margu. Darius the Great recorded it in the Behistun inscriptions around 515 BC. The Seleucid ruler Antiochus Soter rebuilt it and renamed it Antiochia Margiana in the third century BC. After the Battle of Carrhae in 53 BC, some 10,000 Roman prisoners of war may have been deported here, adding yet another layer to its already cosmopolitan population.

## Where Every Religion Met

What distinguished Merv from other Silk Road cities was its religious diversity. Zoroastrians worshipped here for centuries. Buddhist monastery temples flourished at Gyaur Kala until Islamization. Manichaeans found a haven. Christians of the Church of the East established a metropolitan province that lasted from the fifth to the eleventh century. When the Arab armies arrived after 651, Merv became the capital of the Umayyad province of Khorasan. In 671, some 50,000 Arab troops were settled here as a colony. A Chinese traveler named Du Huan, captured at the Battle of Talas in 751 and brought through the caliphate, observed that in Merv, Arabs and Persians lived in mixed concentrations. The eighth Imam of Twelver Shia Islam, Ali ar-Ridha, lived here for several years. Al-Muqanna, the "Veiled Prophet," started a movement claiming to be an incarnation of God. Every faith found something worth claiming in this oasis.

## The Intellectual Capital

Omar Khayyam worked at the observatory in Merv. Yaqut al-Hamawi counted ten giant libraries, one containing 12,000 volumes. The Persian geographer al-Istakhri wrote that the people of Merv were noted above all Iranians for "their talents and education." The tenth-century historian al-Muqaddasi called the city "delightful, fine, elegant, brilliant, extensive, and pleasant." Ibn Hawqal declared that no other city could match its palaces, groves, gardens, and streams. This was not provincial boasting. From 813 to 818, Caliph al-Ma'mun governed the entire Islamic world from Merv, making it the de facto capital of a civilization stretching from Spain to Central Asia. The Barmakid family, based here, played a crucial role in transferring Greek knowledge into the Arab intellectual tradition, drawing on learning that had been present in Merv since the days of the Seleucids and Greco-Bactrians.

## The Seljuq Zenith

In 1037, the Seljuq Turkmens took Merv peacefully. The previous Ghaznavid ruler was so unpopular that the city simply changed hands. Under the Seljuqs, Merv reached its greatest extent. Sultan Ahmad Sanjar made it his capital from 1118 until his death in 1157, and his tomb still stands among the ruins. Arab and Persian geographers called Merv "the rendezvous of great and small" and "the chief city of Khurasan." The vizier Nizam al-Mulk founded a major library and madrasa. The market was described as the best in Iran and Khurasan. By 1150, according to historian Tertius Chandler, Merv was the world's largest city with 200,000 inhabitants. By 1210, that number may have reached 500,000, exceeding Constantinople and Baghdad. Merv's architecture even inspired the Abbasid replanning of Baghdad, the student surpassing the teacher.

## What the Desert Remembers

After the Mongol destruction of 1221, Merv was partly rebuilt but never regained its stature. It changed hands among Timurids, Uzbeks, and others, a diminished city trading on a diminished reputation. Between 1788 and 1789, Shah Murad of the Emirate of Bukhara razed it one final time and deported its remaining population. By the 1800s, the oasis was deserted. Today the ruins spread across the Karakum Desert near the modern city of Mary, a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1999. The Tomb of Ahmad Sanjar still stands, its dome visible across the flat terrain. The walls of successive cities, built one beside the other across millennia, trace the outlines of Gyaur Kala, Sultan Kala, and later enclosures. The desert is patient with ruins. It buries them slowly, preserving what the Mongols and the Bukharans did not finish. Five thousand years of civilization lie beneath this sand, waiting for the archaeologists whose work has only just begun.
