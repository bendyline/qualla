# Sigiriya

The ancient rock fortress where a patricidal king built a palace in the sky, decorated it with the largest mural gallery in the ancient world, and lost it all in eighteen years to the brother he had tried to escape.

**Location:** 7.9569, 80.7597

**Categories:** Anuradhapura period, Archaeological protected monuments in Matale District, Inselbergs of Asia, Landforms of Sri Lanka, Natural monoliths, Palaces in Sri Lanka, Terraced gardens

## Introduction

In 477 CE, a prince named Kashyapa killed his father, King Dhatusena of Anuradhapura, and seized the throne of Sri Lanka. His half-brother Moggallana, the legitimate heir, fled to South India. Kashyapa, fearing the inevitable return, abandoned the old capital and moved his entire court to an almost impossible location: the flat summit of a 180-meter granite column rising from the central plains. There, on a rock that could be approached only through a lion's open jaws, he built a palace, surrounded it with the most sophisticated gardens of the ancient world, and waited for his brother to come. Moggallana came in 495 CE. Kashyapa lost. The palace in the sky lasted just eighteen years as a royal seat, but what it left behind has endured for fifteen centuries.

## The Patricide and the Fortress

The coup was not a solo act. Migara, King Dhatusena's own nephew and army commander, assisted Kashyapa in overthrowing his father. Kashyapa was Dhatusena's son by a non-royal consort, which meant the throne should have passed to Moggallana, born of the queen. The ancient chronicle Culavamsa records Kashyapa's seizure of power, but the details of Dhatusena's fate are grim and disputed. What is clear is that Kashyapa understood his position was precarious, and he responded with extraordinary ambition. Rather than fortify the existing capital at Anuradhapura, he relocated to Sigiriya, transforming a rock that had served as a Buddhist monastery since the 3rd century BCE into an impregnable citadel. The site he chose was not empty: monks and ascetics had inhabited its caves and shelters for nearly a thousand years before Kashyapa arrived.

## A City Designed from the Center Out

Sigiriya was not merely a fortress with a palace on top. It was a planned city whose layout ranks among the most sophisticated examples of first-millennium urban design. The entire plan radiates outward from coordinates at the center of the summit palace, with eastern and western entrances aligned along the central east-west axis. To the west, a symmetrical royal park features water-retaining structures with surface and subsurface hydraulic systems, some of which still function today. Three ramparts and two moats enclose this western precinct. To the east, an inner city stretches 700 by 500 meters behind high earthen ramparts. Beyond that, an outer residential area extends 1,000 by 1,500 meters with its own gateways and a man-made lake to the south. The plan uses an echo principle, duplicating layouts on either side of both axes, a mathematical precision that modern archaeologists did not fully appreciate until the Cultural Triangle Project began systematic excavation in 1982.

## The Largest Picture in the World

Halfway up the western face of the rock, sheltered in natural depressions, survive fragments of what was once an immense painted gallery. John Still, visiting in 1907, called it perhaps the largest picture in the world: the painted band once stretched nearly 140 meters long and up to 40 meters high. The figures are women, rendered in a style unique to Sigiriya. Their painters applied color in sweeping strokes, pressing harder on one side to create tonal gradations that give the flat images a sense of volume. Art historians note the similarity to the Ajanta Caves in India, but the Sigiriya style has its own distinctive character, with sketchy lines rather than the sharp boundary lines of other Anuradhapura-period paintings. On October 14, 1967, vandals splashed paint across the frescoes. The restoration, led by Italian conservator Luciano Maranzi, took until April 1968 and was considered the most difficult task ever undertaken by Sri Lanka's Department of Archaeology. A 2010 report found that the 22 surviving frescoes have been gradually fading since 1930.

## Rediscovery and What Remains

After Kashyapa's defeat, Moggallana returned the capital to Anuradhapura. Sigiriya reverted to a Buddhist monastery, which it remained until the 13th or 14th century. Then silence. No records mention the site for two hundred years, until the Kingdom of Kandy briefly used it as an outpost in the 16th and 17th centuries. In 1831, Major Jonathan Forbes of the British Army's 78th Highlanders, riding back from Polonnaruwa, stumbled upon what he described as the brushwood-covered summit of the rock of Sigiri. Archaeological work began in the 1890s under H.C.P. Bell, but comprehensive excavation did not start until the Cultural Triangle Project of 1982. Today the Lion Gate survives only as a pair of massive brick paws flanking the staircase, the lion's head having collapsed long ago. On the summit, cisterns cut into the rock and palace foundations remain. Alternative histories suggest Dhatusena himself began the construction, with Kashyapa completing it in his father's honor. The truth may be more complicated than any single chronicle records.

## Flight Context

Sigiriya (Lion Rock) is at 7.957N, 80.760E, a massive granite monolith approximately 180 meters high, rising dramatically from flat central Sri Lankan terrain. The rock is unmistakable from the air: an isolated red-brown column surrounded by the geometric outlines of ancient gardens, moats, and ramparts. The rectangular water garden layout (900x800m) is visible from altitude. Pidurangala Rock is approximately 1 km to the north. Dambulla Cave Temple is 19 km southwest. Nearest airport: Sigiriya (VCCS/GIU), approximately 5 km away. Bandaranaike International (VCBI/CMB) is the main international airport, about 170 km west. Best viewing altitude: 3,000-6,000 feet AGL on clear days.
