# Aranthalawa: The Saffron Children

On June 2, 1987, thirty-three Buddhist monks - most of them boys between 7 and 18 - were massacred by LTTE fighters near the village of Aranthalawa in eastern Sri Lanka, one of the most devastating atrocities of the civil war.

**Location:** 7.5259, 81.4765

## Introduction

They were on their way to sit exams. Thirty-three Buddhist monks, most of them boys and teenagers, boarded a bus at their temple in Mahavapi on the morning of June 2, 1987, heading on a pilgrimage to the Kelaniya Raja Maha Vihara. They wore saffron robes and carried little else. In the Theravada tradition, monks are incapable of defending themselves - nonviolence is not a choice but a vow woven into every aspect of monastic life. The bus traveled through the Ampara District of eastern Sri Lanka, a region already scarred by the civil war between the Sinhalese-dominated government and the Liberation Tigers of Tamil Eelam. Near the village of Nuwaragalatenna, twenty armed LTTE cadres approached the vehicle.

## The Road to Kelaniya

The bus driver mistook the armed men for Sri Lankan security forces - an easy error in a conflict zone where uniforms and weapons were everyday sights. He let them board. The cadres, led by an LTTE commander known as Reagan, ordered the driver to continue. What happened next was swift and merciless. Among the thirty-three monks killed, thirty were young novices between the ages of seven and eighteen. These were boys who had entered monastic life as children, as is customary in Sri Lankan Buddhist tradition - families sending sons to the temple for education, discipline, and spiritual formation. Four civilians traveling on the bus also died. Three monks survived with critical injuries that would require medical care for the rest of their lives. A fourth survived but was permanently disabled.

## A Silence in the Jungle

Home guards stationed at Nuwaragalatenna heard the gunshots. The sound traveled across rice paddies and jungle, a distance that measured both geography and helplessness. After confirming the monks had been attacked, the guards alerted the Special Task Force and moved toward the gunfire. A van driver who had been shot at near the initial hijacking site also reached the STF with news. Together, they located the bus in the jungle and evacuated the survivors to hospital. In Theravada Buddhism, harming a monk is considered one of the gravest offenses - a violation not just of law but of a fundamental moral order shared across Buddhist and Hindu traditions. The attackers had killed the most defenseless people imaginable: children in robes, bound by vows of peace.

## The Weight of Remembrance

Every year, Sri Lanka commemorates the Aranthalawa massacre. The anniversary has become a fixed point in the national calendar of grief - a day when the country pauses to remember not a battle or a military setback but the killing of children. In 2007, on the twentieth anniversary, commemorations stretched over four days in Colombo and Ampara. President Mahinda Rajapaksa led the main ceremony. The Mahanayake Theras of all Buddhist chapters participated. An all-night Pirith ceremony was held, and Sanghika Dana was offered to two hundred monks the following morning. An exhibition displayed over three hundred photographs documenting LTTE attacks on Buddhist sites across the island.

## The Wider War

The Aranthalawa massacre did not occur in isolation. The Sri Lankan civil war, which lasted from 1983 to 2009, produced atrocities on all sides - targeting Sinhalese, Tamil, and Muslim civilians alike. The LTTE attacked the sacred Jaya Sri Maha Bodhi in Anuradhapura, killing approximately 146 pilgrims. Government forces carried out their own brutalities against Tamil civilians. Each atrocity fed the next in a cycle that consumed a generation. The massacre of monks at Aranthalawa became a symbol of one particular dimension of the war's cruelty: the deliberate targeting of those who had renounced the capacity for violence, whose entire existence was organized around peace. A memorial now stands near the site, in a landscape where the jungle has reclaimed much of what the war touched.
