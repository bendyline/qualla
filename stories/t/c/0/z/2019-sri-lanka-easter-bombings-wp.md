# 2019 Sri Lanka Easter Bombings

On Easter Sunday 2019, coordinated suicide bombings struck three churches and three luxury hotels across Sri Lanka, killing 269 people in one of the deadliest terrorist attacks in South Asian history.

**Location:** 6.9333, 79.8667

**Categories:** terrorism, history, tragedy, sri-lanka, religion

## Introduction

They were singing hymns. In Negombo, in Batticaloa, in Colombo, congregations had gathered on Easter Sunday morning, 21 April 2019, to celebrate the holiest day on the Christian calendar. Hotels along Colombo's waterfront were serving breakfast to tourists from dozens of countries. By mid-morning, 269 people would be dead, more than 500 wounded, and Sri Lanka would be reeling from the worst act of terrorism in the island nation's history since the end of its civil war a decade earlier.

## A Sunday Shattered

The attacks came in waves. Shortly after 8:45 a.m., near-simultaneous explosions tore through St. Anthony's Shrine in Colombo, St. Sebastian's Church in Negombo, and Zion Church in Batticaloa. Minutes later, suicide bombers detonated inside the Shangri-La, the Cinnamon Grand, and the Kingsbury hotels in central Colombo. Later that day, two smaller blasts struck a housing complex in Dematagoda and a guest house in Dehiwala. Eight suicide bombers carried out the attacks, along with three police officers who died in the line of duty. The scale was staggering: churches packed with families, hotel restaurants full of travelers, ordinary streets on what should have been a day of peace. At least 100 of the injured lost their eyesight from the blasts.

## Warnings Unheeded

What made the tragedy doubly agonizing was that it might have been prevented. Sri Lanka's Muslim community had itself warned authorities about the radical group National Thowheeth Jama'ath, whose members would carry out the bombings. Intelligence agencies had received specific warnings. Yet those warnings were not acted upon in time, lost in political rifts between President Maithripala Sirisena and Prime Minister Ranil Wickremesinghe. The attackers, linked to ISIS, had planned for months in plain sight. A state official later described the bombings as retaliation for the Christchurch mosque shootings in New Zealand the previous month, though the full motivations remain debated.

## The Human Cost

Numbers flatten grief. Behind the figure of 269 dead were families broken apart on a holiday morning. Among the victims were worshippers who had come to celebrate resurrection and renewal. Foreign nationals from more than a dozen countries died in the hotel blasts. Children lost parents. Parents lost children. In Negombo, a predominantly Catholic fishing town north of Colombo, the attack at St. Sebastian's was particularly devastating, killing over a hundred people in a single blast. The town, sometimes called 'Little Rome' for its deep Catholic roots, was plunged into mourning. In the weeks and months that followed, some survivors succumbed to their injuries; as late as 2024, victims were still dying from complications.

## Accountability and Its Absence

The quest for justice has been long and incomplete. Eleven members of the National Thowheeth Jama'ath were charged, but the question of who enabled the attacks has haunted Sri Lankan politics ever since. A Presidential Commission of Inquiry investigated the intelligence failures. In 2023, a Channel 4 investigation featured whistleblowers alleging that elements within Sri Lanka's State Intelligence Service were complicit, claims that were denied but that deepened public suspicion. A 2023 poll found that 53 percent of Sri Lankans believed local political forces were involved. Catholic Church leaders, led by Malcolm Cardinal Ranjith, have demanded international accountability, arguing that domestic justice has been insufficient. In January 2023, a court ordered former President Sirisena to compensate victims' families for the security failures on his watch.

## What Remains

Sri Lanka's Christians, roughly seven percent of the population, had experienced sporadic persecution before 2019, but nothing approaching this scale. The bombings reshaped the country's security landscape and its politics. They also revealed something about the communities they targeted: congregations rebuilt, returned, and continued to worship. At St. Anthony's Shrine in Colombo, where the first bomb detonated, services resumed. The shrine, shared by Catholics, Buddhists, Hindus, and Muslims alike as a site of common devotion, became a symbol of resilience against the very divisions the attackers sought to deepen. The dead are remembered there, in a city that refused to let a single terrible morning define it.
