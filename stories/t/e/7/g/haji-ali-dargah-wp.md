# Haji Ali Dargah

A 15th-century Sufi shrine perched on an islet in the Arabian Sea, reachable only at low tide via a narrow causeway that the ocean swallows twice a day.

**Location:** 18.9825, 72.8088

**Categories:** religious-site, sufi-shrine, architecture, landmark, mumbai

## Introduction

Twice a day, the Arabian Sea erases the path to Haji Ali Dargah. The narrow causeway that connects this whitewashed shrine to the Mumbai mainland disappears under the tide, and for a few hours the dargah becomes what it has always been at heart: an island, separated from the city of twenty million by a stretch of salt water barely a kilometer wide. Then the tide retreats, the concrete path reappears, and the pilgrims return -- Hindu, Muslim, Christian, tourist -- walking with the sea lapping at their feet on both sides.

## A Merchant Who Left Everything Behind

The shrine honors Pir Haji Ali Shah Bukhari, a wealthy Muslim merchant from Bukhara in present-day Uzbekistan who belonged to the Chishti Order of Sufism. He traveled the world in the early to mid-15th century before settling in what is now Mumbai. According to legend, he once encountered a woman weeping on the road because she had spilled a vessel of oil and feared her husband's anger. Bukhari asked her to show him where the oil had fallen, jabbed his finger into the soil, and the oil gushed back to the surface. Before his death, he instructed his followers not to bury him in any graveyard but to cast his shroud into the ocean, and let it wash ashore wherever it may. The dargah was constructed in 1431 on the islet where, tradition holds, his coffin came to rest.

## Glass, Marble, and the Names of God

The architecture is a striking example of Indo-Islamic style. A marble courtyard surrounds the central shrine, where the tomb lies beneath a brocaded red and green chaddar -- a ceremonial cover sheet -- supported by an exquisite silver frame resting on marble pillars. The main hall dazzles with mirror work: blue, green, and yellow chips of glass arranged in kaleidoscopic patterns that interweave with Arabic calligraphy spelling the ninety-nine names of Allah. Minarets rise above the whitewashed walls. The renovation that began in October 2008 brought fresh white marble from Makrana in Rajasthan -- the same quarry that supplied the Taj Mahal -- giving the dargah what its trustees described as the feel of a taj rising from Mumbai's brackish sea water.

## The Walk Between Two Waters

The causeway from the Mahalakshmi precinct to the islet stretches nearly one kilometer, with no railings to separate the walker from the sea. During high tide, waves crash across the path and the dargah appears to float in isolation. During low tide, the walk becomes a kind of pilgrimage in itself -- the sea visible on both sides, the city skyline receding behind, the shrine growing larger ahead. On Thursdays and Fridays, the dargah draws its largest crowds, and Sufi musicians sometimes perform Qawwali devotional music inside the shrine, their voices carrying across the water. People of all faiths visit to seek the saint's blessing, making it one of Mumbai's rare spaces where religious boundaries dissolve in the salt air.

## Opening the Inner Sanctum

For four years, women were barred from entering the shrine's sanctum sanctorum. The ban, imposed in June 2012, sparked the "Haji Ali for All" movement -- a feminist campaign led by the Bharatiya Muslim Mahila Andolan and the Bhumata Brigade demanding equal right to pray. On 26 August 2016, the Bombay High Court ruled that women could enter the inner sanctum, and on 29 November 2016 the ruling was implemented. The campaign drew national attention to questions of gender equality in religious spaces across India, and the dargah became, unexpectedly, a landmark in the country's ongoing struggle over who gets to stand in the most sacred places.
