# Niyamgiri

Where a tribal community told a multinational mining corporation no - and where India's Supreme Court said the hills belonged to the people who worship them.

**Location:** 19.5833, 83.4000

**Categories:** india, indigenous-rights, environmental, odisha, sacred-site

## Introduction

In the summer of 2013, twelve village councils in the hills of southwest Odisha were asked a question no Indian court had posed before: should a multinational corporation be allowed to mine your sacred mountain? One by one, all twelve said no. All twelve Palli Sabhas - village assemblies - voted unanimously to reject Vedanta Resources' plan to strip-mine bauxite from the Niyamgiri range. The Supreme Court of India had ordered the referendum, and the verdict of the Dongria Kondh people was final. Niyamgiri still stands, forested and unmined, a place where indigenous rights prevailed over industrial ambition.

## Sacred Slopes

The Niyamgiri range rises between the Kalahandi and Rayagada districts in Odisha's southwestern corner, its dense forests among the most pristine remaining in India. The Karlapat Wildlife Sanctuary borders it to the northwest, and the Kotgarh Wildlife Sanctuary anchors the northeast. For the Dongria Kondh and Kutia Kondh tribal communities - classified as Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Groups by the Indian government - these hills are not a landscape but a living deity. The highest points hold special spiritual significance, and the perennial springs flowing from the upper plateau feed the Vamsadhara River, sustaining both ecosystems and livelihoods downstream. The Kondh people depend on the forest for survival: food, medicine, shelter. Their relationship with Niyamgiri is not sentimental. It is material, spiritual, and existential.

## The Corporation Arrives

In June 2003, Vedanta Resources signed a memorandum of understanding with the Government of Odisha to build a one-million-tonne-per-year aluminium refinery and coal-fired power station in Kalahandi district. The bauxite to feed the refinery would come from Niyamgiri's mountain top. Clearances from the Ministry of Environment and Forests arrived quickly - by September 2004, the project had approval. But in September 2005, the Central Empowered Committee reported to the Supreme Court that these clearances violated both the Forest Conservation Act of 1980 and the Environment Protection Act of 1986. The legal battle had begun, and it would grind on for a decade. The Supreme Court denied mining permission in 2007 unless conditions were met, then reversed course in 2008 with a Stage II clearance that overruled its own committee's findings.

## The World Takes Notice

Between 2008 and 2009, tribal communities staged protests in Bhubaneswar and across Kalahandi. The story crossed borders. In 2010, the Church of England divested from Vedanta over human rights concerns. Norway's Government Pension Fund followed. An expert committee led by N. C. Saxena, a former Planning Commission secretary, delivered a damning verdict in August 2010: mining at Niyamgiri would violate the Forest Rights Act of 2006 and threaten the survival of the Dongria Kondh, affecting nearly twenty percent of their global population. The committee found that Vedanta had "consistently violated" multiple environmental and forest protection laws, in some cases with active help from state officials. By July 2011, the Ministry of Environment revoked the mining license for Niyam Dongar - the sacred peak at the heart of the dispute.

## The People Decide

The legal climax came on April 18, 2013, when the Supreme Court issued a landmark order: let the tribal people themselves decide. Through their gram sabhas - village councils empowered under the Panchayats Extension to Scheduled Areas Act of 1996 - the Dongria Kondh would vote on whether mining should proceed. The court invoked the principle of free, prior, and informed consent, a standard more commonly applied in international indigenous rights law than in Indian mining disputes. Between July and August 2013, the councils of all twelve affected villages voted unanimously against the project. In January 2014, the Ministry of Environment issued a complete ban on mining at Niyamgiri. The aluminium refinery at nearby Lanjigarh was permitted to continue operating, but the mountain itself was protected. The Dongria Kondh had won a battle that most observers assumed they would lose.

## What Remains at Stake

Niyamgiri's victory is celebrated as a precedent for indigenous environmental rights in India, but it is not a permanent settlement. The bauxite remains in the ground, and commercial pressures do not disappear because a court ruled against them. The Saxena committee's report captured the tension clearly: in law, forests now include forest dwellers and are not limited to trees and wildlife. But enforcing that principle requires political will that has wavered before. The hills endure for now - forested, sacred, home to communities whose way of life depends on their preservation. From the air, the Niyamgiri range appears as an unbroken canopy of green in a landscape increasingly marked by roads and settlements. What you are seeing is the shape of resistance.

## Flight Context

Niyamgiri is located at 19.58N, 83.40E in southwestern Odisha, between the Kalahandi and Rayagada districts. The hill range is visible as a densely forested massif surrounded by lower-lying agricultural and settlement areas. The Karlapat Wildlife Sanctuary lies to the northwest and Kotgarh Wildlife Sanctuary to the northeast. The nearest significant airfield is Jeypore (no ICAO code for commercial use) roughly 80 km southeast, while Visakhapatnam Airport (VOVZ) is approximately 250 km to the south. The terrain rises to over 1,500 meters at the highest points. Best viewed at 5,000-8,000 feet in clear conditions to appreciate the contrast between the forested hills and surrounding landscape.
