# Charar-e-Sharief Shrine

A 15th-century Sufi shrine in Kashmir's Budgam district, built to honor the mystic Nund Rishi, destroyed in a controversial 1995 military operation, and rebuilt as a symbol of the valley's contested spiritual heritage.

**Location:** 33.8664, 74.7667

**Categories:** 15th-century mosques in India, Budgam district, Dargahs in Jammu and Kashmir, Sufi shrines in India

## Introduction

The town of Charari Sharief sits in the Budgam district of Kashmir, unremarkable at first glance - a cluster of buildings in a mountain-ringed valley, prayer calls drifting over tin roofs. But for nearly six centuries, this town has been a destination. Pilgrims come for the shrine of Nund Rishi, the 15th-century Sufi saint also known as Sheikh Noor-ud-din Noorani, whose tomb has drawn both Muslims and Hindus in a tradition of communal devotion that long predates the region's modern conflicts. That the shrine had to be rebuilt at all tells a darker story, one that residents of Charari Sharief carry in living memory.

## The Saint of the Valley

Nund Rishi died around 1438, and even in his lifetime he had become something larger than an individual. A Kashmiri Sufi mystic, he taught in the Rishi tradition - an ascetic, contemplative school of Islam unique to the Kashmir Valley, one that borrowed freely from the region's Hindu and Buddhist heritage. His poetry, composed in Kashmiri, emphasized simplicity, inner devotion, and the unity of creation. When he was buried in Charari Sharief, his grave became a place of pilgrimage almost immediately. In 1446, Sultan Zain-ul-Abidin, the eighth sultan of Kashmir and a ruler remembered for his religious tolerance, laid the foundation stone of a formal shrine at the burial site. The gesture was significant: a Muslim sovereign consecrating the tomb of a mystic whose teachings transcended sectarian boundaries.

## Rebuilt, Repaired, Rebuilt Again

The shrine's physical history reads like a ledger of the valley's own turbulence. After Zain-ul-Abidin's original construction, the structure suffered partial damage over time. Yakub Shah Chak, one of the later Chak dynasty rulers, ordered repairs. During the 19th century, an Afghan governor named Atta Mohammad Khan undertook a more substantial reconstruction. The shrine compound was further developed during the tenure of Bakshi Ghulam Mohammad, who served as prime minister of Jammu and Kashmir. In 1979, the Jammu and Kashmir Academy of Art, Culture and Languages installed an epigraphic stone at Nund Rishi's mausoleum, formally recognizing the site's historical significance. Each restoration was an act of devotion layered onto the last, a structure perpetually shaped by the hands of whoever governed the valley.

## May 1995

On May 11, 1995, militants belonging to Hizbul Mujahideen took shelter inside the shrine during a confrontation with Indian security forces. What followed devastated the town. More than 25,000 residents fled to neighboring villages. By the time the fighting subsided, approximately 1,000 houses had been damaged and 200 shops destroyed. Around thirty militants and fifteen members of the security forces were killed, along with at least one civilian - a 65-year-old local resident caught in crossfire. The shrine itself burned. The circumstances of its destruction remain deeply contested: Indian state accounts attributed the fire to the militants, while local Kashmiri residents and witnesses have consistently maintained that Indian Army operations caused the blaze. Journalists were reportedly prevented from entering the area during the operation, leaving the competing narratives unresolved.

## What the Ashes Left Behind

The shrine was rebuilt. Pilgrims returned. But the events of 1995 became part of the site's meaning in ways that no reconstruction could erase. For many Kashmiris, the destruction of Charar-e-Sharief stands as a defining episode in the region's long conflict - a moment when a place of spiritual communion became collateral in a military operation. The shrine today functions as both a house of worship and an unspoken memorial. Visitors come to pray at the tomb of Nund Rishi, to participate in a devotional tradition that has survived sultans, Mughal governors, Afghan rulers, and modern warfare. The wooden architecture has been restored, the courtyard swept clean. But the town remembers what happened, and the pilgrims who come know they are visiting a place where faith and politics have collided with consequences that no amount of rebuilding can fully resolve.
