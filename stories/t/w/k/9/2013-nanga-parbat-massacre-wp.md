# 2013 Nanga Parbat Massacre

On a June night in 2013, militants disguised as paramilitary scouts stormed a high-altitude base camp on Nanga Parbat, killing ten foreign mountaineers and a local cook in the first terrorist attack ever to target climbers in Pakistan's Gilgit-Baltistan.

**Location:** 35.4550, 73.9497

**Categories:** terrorism, mountaineering, massacre, Pakistan, Gilgit-Baltistan, Nanga Parbat

## Introduction

They had come to climb the Killer Mountain. Nanga Parbat, at 8,126 meters the ninth-highest peak on Earth, earned that grim nickname in the early twentieth century when successive expeditions ended in death. But the mountain's dangers had always been natural ones -- avalanches, exposure, altitude. On the night of June 22, 2013, the danger that entered the base camp at 4,200 meters wore stolen uniforms and carried automatic weapons. By morning, ten mountaineers from five nations and a Pakistani camp cook lay dead. The attack shattered the assumption that Gilgit-Baltistan, a region long considered Pakistan's safest corner, was immune to the militancy tearing through the country's south and west.

## Ten O'Clock at Base Camp

The climbers had gathered at the Nanga Parbat base camp -- the staging ground for summit attempts on the mountain's Diamir Face. Among them were some of the most accomplished high-altitude mountaineers in the world. Yang Chunfeng from China had climbed eleven of the fourteen eight-thousanders. His compatriot Rao Jianfeng had climbed ten. Ernestas Marksaitis of Lithuania had solo-climbed Broad Peak and attempted K2. Two Slovak ski-alpinists, Anton Dobes and Peter Sperka, had come from the High Tatras. Three Ukrainian climbers were preparing their own attempt. At ten o'clock that night, roughly sixteen attackers entered the camp after abducting two Pakistani guides on the approach trail. They collected passports, took photographs of their captives, and demanded money and satellite phones. Then they ordered the climbers to turn away and opened fire.

## A Kidnapping Gone Wrong

The Tehrik-i-Taliban Pakistan claimed responsibility within hours, calling it retaliation for the U.S. drone strike that killed Taliban commander Wali-ur-Rehman on May 29, 2013. But later investigation revealed a more complex plan. The primary objective had been to kidnap Chen Honglu, a Chinese-American citizen, and trade him for a Taliban commander held in Afghanistan. When the attack began, Chen burst from his tent and tackled one of the militants using martial arts. The fighter, a man named Mujeeb, panicked and shot him dead -- destroying the mission's purpose and enraging the terrorist commanders. With their hostage plan in ruins, the attackers executed the remaining climbers. Zhang Jingchuan, a Chinese mountaineer, survived by sprinting thirty meters to a trench where he hid for forty minutes before crawling back to a cabin and calling police on a satellite phone. Sher Khan, a Pakistani climber, also survived and later described the methodical cruelty of the attack in harrowing detail.

## The Hunt Through Diamer

Within four days, all sixteen attackers were identified -- a breakthrough owed largely to the Diamer jirga, the local tribal council. Volunteers from the jirga were dropped by helicopter into valleys surrounding the attack site, gathered intelligence, and returned for debriefing in the nearby town of Chilas. Police learned that ten attackers came from Diamer district, three from Mansehra, and three from Kohistan, though all had been trained in Pakistan's Federally Administered Tribal Areas, 760 kilometers to the south along the Afghan border. Arrests began on June 30 when Shafi Muhammad was found hiding in a house in Chilas. But the investigation came at a lethal cost. On August 7, Taliban gunmen ambushed and killed three security officials who were working the case, including a police superintendent and two army officers. That attack prompted the Pakistan Army to enter Diamer in force for the first time, sealing Chilas and conducting operations over several days.

## A Mountain Community Shattered

The massacre rippled far beyond Gilgit-Baltistan. German expedition organizers canceled trips to Nanga Parbat. The UN Secretary-General condemned the attack. China demanded Pakistan 'severely punish' the perpetrators. Ukrainian families of the slain climbers sought compensation that Pakistan's government refused. The Alpine Club of Pakistan mourned the blow to a mountaineering culture that had been one of the region's few connections to the wider world. For local communities that depended on expeditions for income, the damage was existential. Porters, guides, cooks, and guesthouse owners watched their livelihoods evaporate as the international climbing community retreated. The attack also punctured the narrative that Gilgit-Baltistan was somehow exempt from Pakistan's broader security crisis -- a belief that had given the region its identity as a place where outsiders were welcome.

## The Killer Mountain Endures

Nanga Parbat still rises above the Indus Valley, its Diamir Face catching the last light each evening in shades of gold and violet. Expeditions have returned in the years since, though never in the numbers that preceded June 2013. The base camp where ten climbers spent their final night sits on a high glacial moraine, reachable only on foot or horseback, surrounded by some of the most spectacular mountain scenery on Earth. The victims were people who chose to face the mountain's natural dangers willingly -- the altitude, the cold, the unstable ice. What they could not have anticipated was violence carried up from the lowlands to a place that felt, at 4,200 meters, like it existed beyond the reach of human cruelty. Their names are remembered in memorial walls and climbing journals from Bratislava to Kyiv to Kunming, by families and communities who lost not just individuals but a shared faith that the mountains were a refuge.

## Flight Context

Located at 35.455N, 73.950E on the Diamir Face of Nanga Parbat (8,126m / 26,660 ft), the ninth-highest peak in the world. The base camp sits at approximately 4,200m elevation. Nanga Parbat is one of the most visually dramatic peaks in the Karakoram-Himalaya range, rising sharply above the Indus River valley. Gilgit Airport (OPGL) is the nearest airfield, approximately 100 km to the north. Islamabad International Airport (OPIS) lies roughly 300 km to the south. Best viewed from 20,000-25,000 ft AGL to appreciate the mountain's massive Rupal Face -- the largest rock wall in the world. Weather is highly variable with frequent cloud cover in monsoon season (June-September).
