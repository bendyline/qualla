# Kerala Massacre

In a remote Afghan village in 1979, government forces allegedly killed hundreds of unarmed men, an atrocity whose truth remained contested for decades and whose perpetrator was finally arrested 36 years later in the Netherlands.

**Location:** 34.8764, 71.1561

**Categories:** Massacres in Afghanistan, 1979 in Afghanistan, Cold War history, War crimes, Human rights

## Introduction

The village's real name is Kilara, or Kidari in the local dialect. Western journalists called it Kerala, a confusion that persists to this day, borrowing the name of an Indian state seven thousand kilometers to the south. But there is nothing confused about what Afghan refugees described happening there on April 20, 1979: government soldiers arriving in tanks, ordering men to shout pro-communist slogans, and opening fire when those men shouted Allahu akbar instead. Whether the death toll was the 640 reported by the Associated Press or the more than one thousand claimed by The Christian Science Monitor, the accounts painted a picture of systematic execution in eastern Afghanistan's Kunar Province, barely a year after the Marxist Saur Revolution had seized power in Kabul.

## A Province on the Edge

Kunar Province sits in a narrow river valley near the Pakistani border, its steep ridges and terraced fields forming a landscape that has resisted central authority for centuries. By early 1979, rebels from Kerala had been harassing the provincial capital of Assadabad for weeks, disrupting operations to the point that a United Nations hydroelectric project in the area had to be abandoned entirely. The previous month, the 1979 Herat uprising had shaken the government, and rebel leaders based across the border in Pakistan had declared a holy war. The Marxist government in Kabul viewed eastern Afghanistan as hostile territory, and Kunar as one of its most dangerous pressure points. Into this volatile landscape, roughly 200 armed troops and police rolled on that Friday morning, including elements of the 444th Commando Battalion and the 11th Division of the Afghan Army. The force arrived in tanks, a show of power in a village that had no means to resist what came next.

## What the Refugees Described

The accounts came from Afghans who had fled to refugee camps in Pakistan, and they were harrowing in their specificity. Officers armed with Kalashnikovs gathered the village men and ordered them to shout pro-communist slogans. When the men refused, chanting Allahu akbar instead, they were allegedly ordered to crouch down facing the tanks. Then the shooting began. According to the refugees, bulldozers arrived afterward to bury the dead in a mass grave. Some of those buried, they said, were not dead but wounded, meaning they were buried alive. Named among the perpetrators were Major Sadiq Alamyar, commander of the 444th Commando Battalion, along with Major Bahramuddin and Major Gul Rang of the 11th Division. Bahramuddin was later killed during a mutiny in Jalalabad. Gul Rang survived, living in Kabul and then Peshawar before dying in 2004. The suggestion from analysts was that this was a local initiative by government forces stationed in Kunar, not an order handed down from the national command.

## Denial and Counter-Narrative

Moscow's response was unequivocal denial. Soviet media official Repin dismissed the reports as pure invention, insisting that no Soviet officer had set foot in the village of Kerala or even in all of Kunar Province. He called the accounts a deliberate slander fabricated as part of an imperialist propaganda campaign against the USSR and Afghanistan. The language of the rebuttal was remarkable for its vehemence: the fabrications were called so vile they would not be worth answering, were it not for the fact that this deliberate slander had been spread all over the world. For decades, the massacre existed in a contested space, real enough for the families who lost fathers and sons, denied by the government that bore responsibility. The Afghan Independent Human Rights Commission would later investigate, noting even that Western reporters had gotten the village name wrong, recording it as Kerala rather than Kilara.

## Justice Deferred, Then Denied

Thirty-six years passed before anyone faced legal consequences. In October 2015, Dutch National Police arrested Sadeq Alamyar in the Netherlands on suspicion of war crimes, acting on a criminal complaint filed back in 2008. Alamyar, who had commanded the elite 444th Commando Battalion, was accused not merely of ordering the killings but of having shot victims himself. His journey from Afghanistan to Holland traced its own arc of Cold War upheaval. A member of the Khalq faction, Alamyar had been jailed during the 1980s under rival Parchamite leader Babrak Karmal. He eventually fled Afghanistan and was granted asylum in the Netherlands, building a quiet life in a country that was simultaneously developing the legal framework to prosecute exactly the crimes he was accused of committing. Families of the victims expressed gratitude for the arrest, calling it a long-overdue step toward accountability. But in December 2017, Dutch prosecutors dropped the case for lack of evidence. The massacre remained unresolved, its victims unacknowledged by any court.

## What Remains in Kunar

The valley below is quiet now in a way it was not in 1979, though Kunar Province has seen its share of conflict in the decades since, from the Soviet-Afghan War through the Taliban insurgency to the American presence after 2001. The village of Kilara still sits in this corridor where Afghanistan meets Pakistan, where mountain passes have funneled armies, refugees, and smugglers for centuries. No memorial marks the site. No tribunal has established a definitive account of what happened on that April morning. What endures are the testimonies of people who fled, passed through refugee camps, and told their stories to journalists who wrote them down with the wrong village name. For the families of the dead, the distinction between Kerala and Kilara matters less than the fact that their loss has never been officially recognized, their grief never formally addressed by any authority.
