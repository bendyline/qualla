# 2010 Zahedan Bombings

On July 15, 2010, two suicide bombers struck worshippers gathered at Zahedan's Grand Mosque, killing 27 people and exposing the sectarian fault lines of Iran's restive southeastern frontier.

**Categories:** disaster, terrorism, historical-event, middle-east

## Introduction

The worshippers had gathered to celebrate. July 15, 2010, was the birthday of Husayn ibn Ali, a revered figure in Shia Islam, and the Jamia mosque in Zahedan -- the capital of Iran's Sistan-Baluchestan province -- was full. Shia faithful and members of the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps mingled in the crowd outside the mosque entrance. Then, minutes apart, two suicide bombers detonated. The first attacker was disguised in women's clothing. The second struck as people rushed to help the wounded. Twenty-seven people died and roughly 270 were injured, eleven of them critically. In a city already defined by ethnic and sectarian tensions, the attack tore open wounds that Iran's government and its people had long struggled to close.

## A Province on the Edge

Zahedan sits at 1,352 meters elevation in Iran's far southeast, roughly 40 kilometers from the tripoint where Iran, Afghanistan, and Pakistan meet. It is the capital of Sistan-Baluchestan, Iran's second-largest province by area and one of its poorest. The population is predominantly ethnic Baluch and largely Sunni Muslim, placing the province at odds with the Shia-majority government in Tehran. For decades, this combination of ethnic marginalization, economic neglect, and sectarian difference has fueled resentment. Smuggling routes crisscross the porous borders with Pakistan and Afghanistan, and armed groups have found recruitment easy among young men with few prospects. By 2010, the region had already endured years of low-grade insurgency, bombings, and government crackdowns that made daily life precarious for ordinary residents caught between the state and those who claimed to fight it.

## Revenge as a Declaration

Responsibility for the bombings was claimed by Jundallah, a Sunni militant group that had operated in Sistan-Baluchestan since the early 2000s. The group cast the attack as revenge: in the months before July 15, Iran had captured and executed Jundallah's leader, Abdolmalek Rigi, and his brother Abdolhamid Rigi. Abdolmalek Rigi had been seized in February 2010 after his plane was intercepted by Iranian fighter jets, and he was hanged in June. Jundallah had vowed retaliation, and the Zahedan bombings -- targeting Shia worshippers and Revolutionary Guard members at a religious celebration -- were designed to inflict maximum sectarian pain. The two bombers were later identified by Jundallah as Mohammad Rigi and Abdul Baaset Rigi, members of the same Rigi clan as the group's executed leaders.

## Blame Spirals Outward

The Iranian government's response moved quickly from grief to accusation. President Mahmoud Ahmadinejad blamed "US-backed terrorist groups" and called on Pakistan's government to be held accountable. The Interior Minister pointed to "Zionists." The provincial governor invoked "the intelligence services of arrogant powers," a standard Iranian euphemism for Western nations. Iran's Foreign Ministry spokesman demanded that Western countries "stop supporting terrorists instead of giving them shelter." The accusations reflected a genuine Iranian suspicion -- shared across the political spectrum -- that foreign intelligence agencies had cultivated Jundallah as a tool against Tehran. But the rhetoric also served a domestic purpose, deflecting attention from the government's own failure to secure a province whose grievances it had long neglected. Meanwhile, a Sunni cleric in Zahedan, Molavi Nazir Ahmad Salami, condemned the attack and urged both communities to resist the sectarian rift the bombers intended to create.

## The Weight of Twenty-Seven

On July 17, two days after the attack, tens of thousands of mourners filled the streets of Zahedan for the burial of the dead. The scale of the gathering revealed something the political rhetoric obscured: the victims were not abstractions in a geopolitical argument. They were neighbors, family members, people who had come to a mosque on a holiday. Three members of Iran's parliament from Sistan-Baluchestan resigned in the aftermath, citing the government's inability to maintain security in their constituencies. The UN Security Council condemned the bombings in the "strongest terms." President Obama called them "outrageous terrorist attacks" and affirmed that "the murder of innocent civilians in their place of worship is an intolerable offense." The condemnations came from across the globe -- the EU, Canada, Jordan, Vietnam, Hezbollah -- each filtered through its own political lens, but all acknowledging the same basic fact: people had gone to pray, and they had been killed for it.

## Fault Lines That Endure

The 2010 Zahedan bombings did not begin the violence in Sistan-Baluchestan, and they did not end it. The province had suffered a similar attack in 2007, when a car bomb targeted a bus carrying Revolutionary Guard members in the same city. In the years after 2010, further attacks followed, even as Iranian security forces intensified their operations in the region. The underlying conditions -- a Sunni Baluch population governed by a Shia Persian state, chronic poverty, porous borders, and the ready availability of weapons and recruits -- remained largely unchanged. Zahedan itself continued as it had before: a dusty, sprawling city of over half a million people, its streets crowded with the traffic of three countries' border trade, its mosque rebuilt, its scars carried quietly by those who survived. The bombings are remembered not as an isolated event but as one chapter in a longer story that Sistan-Baluchestan's people did not choose and cannot easily escape.
