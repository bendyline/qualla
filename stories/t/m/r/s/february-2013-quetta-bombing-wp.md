# February 2013 Quetta Bombing

A bomb concealed in a water tank killed at least 91 people and injured 190 at a market in Hazara Town, Quetta, in the second major attack against the Shia Hazara community in a single month.

## Introduction

The bomb was hidden inside a water tank mounted on a tractor trailer -- between 70 and 80 kilograms of explosives disguised as something so mundane it would not merit a second glance in a busy market. On February 16, 2013, it detonated in Hazara Town on the outskirts of Quetta, killing at least 91 people and injuring 190 more. It was late in the market day. Shoppers were buying food for dinner. Children were leaving school. The blast destroyed buildings, collapsed a two-story house onto the people inside, and left a silence broken only by sporadic gunfire as terrified residents feared a second attack. Lashkar-e-Jhangvi claimed responsibility. It was the second time in a month that the al-Qaeda-affiliated Sunni extremist group had massacred members of Quetta's Shia Hazara community.

## A Month of Massacres

Just weeks earlier, in January 2013, twin bombings in Quetta had killed 130 people, most of them Hazara. That attack had been so devastating that it triggered the dismissal of the entire Balochistan provincial government -- an extraordinary political consequence that signaled how badly the state had failed to protect its citizens. Yet the February bombing proved that even dramatic political upheaval could not prevent the next attack. Lashkar-e-Jhangvi, an organization with links to al-Qaeda and variously described as either a breakaway faction of the banned Sipah-e-Sahaba party or its armed wing, had made the Hazara community its explicit target. The group operated with a kind of grim openness, claiming attacks publicly and daring the state to stop them. In two months, more than 220 people -- overwhelmingly Hazara Shia -- had been killed in Quetta alone.

## The Scene at Hazara Town

The market area that absorbed the blast contained grocery stores, language schools, and a large computer center -- the ordinary infrastructure of a community trying to educate its children and feed its families. Initial reports suggested the bomb had been attached to a parked motorbike, but investigation soon revealed the far more elaborate concealment in the water tank. The explosion shattered buildings across the area, and for hours afterward, rescue workers pulled bodies from beneath rubble while the death toll climbed. Photos from the scene showed desperate people rushing the injured into ambulances and private vehicles as emergency services became overwhelmed. Angry members of the Hazara community set up roadblocks with burning tires and fired into the air -- not in celebration, but as a perimeter. They feared another bomb. In a community that had been attacked twice in thirty days, the assumption of a follow-up strike was not paranoia. It was experience.

## The Dead Who Would Not Be Buried

What happened next was unprecedented. Hazara leaders refused to bury their dead. They placed the bodies in the open and issued a 48-hour ultimatum to the Pakistani government: launch a military operation against the attackers, or hand the city over to the army. It was an act of protest so visceral, so rooted in grief and fury, that it could not be ignored. The dead became witnesses to the state's failure. Protests erupted across Pakistan -- 1,500 marchers in Lahore, demonstrations in Muzaffarabad and Multan, a peaceful sit-in by the Shia Ulema Council in Karachi. Balochistan's Governor, Zulfikar Ali Magsi, publicly criticized the country's security forces: "Our security institutions, police, FC and others are either scared or cannot take action against them." President Asif Ali Zardari and Prime Minister Raja Pervez Ashraf condemned the attack and vowed to pursue the perpetrators. Three days after the bombing, security forces arrested one of the masterminds along with 170 suspects and killed four high-profile militants.

## The Weight of Repetition

The February 2013 Quetta bombing occupies a particular place in Pakistan's history of sectarian violence -- not because it was the worst single attack, but because of its proximity to the January bombings and the political crisis that followed. Together, the two attacks demonstrated a pattern that the Hazara community had long understood: security promises made after one massacre would not prevent the next. The weapons and bomb-making materials seized after the February attack, the arrests and the operations, came after the fact. For the families of the 91 people killed -- the shoppers, the students, the shopkeepers going about the most unremarkable of routines -- the timeline of government response mattered less than the fact that the bomb had been allowed to reach the market at all. Quetta's Hazara neighborhoods remain some of the most security-conscious places in Pakistan, their residents living within boundaries drawn not by choice but by threat.
