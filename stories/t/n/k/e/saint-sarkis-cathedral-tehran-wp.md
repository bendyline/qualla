# Saint Sarkis Cathedral, Tehran

The largest church in Tehran, Saint Sarkis Cathedral is a white marble Armenian Apostolic cathedral completed in 1970, whose courtyard holds a genocide memorial inscribed with the date 24 April 1915.

**Location:** 35.7150, 51.4150

**Categories:** religious-sites, architecture, armenian-heritage, cultural-heritage, memorials

## Introduction

On the corner of Karimkhan Zand Street and Nejatollahi Street in central Tehran, white marble rises in a form that looks neither entirely Iranian nor entirely European. Two belfries flank the entrance, their domes topped with octagonal rods. A hexagonal main dome floats above the nave. This is Saint Sarkis Cathedral, the largest church in a city of over eight million, and the seat of the Armenian Diocese of Tehran. Completed in 1970, the cathedral serves a community whose presence in Iran predates the building by centuries -- Armenians have lived on the Iranian plateau since antiquity, and many of the oldest Armenian churches and monasteries in the world stand on Iranian soil.

## White Marble and a Hexagonal Sky

Saint Sarkis stretches 36.5 meters long and 17.8 meters wide, a single-nave church built from concrete and clad entirely in white marble -- walls, floor, interior surfaces. The architects originally designed the hexagonal dome to rest on the walls alone, without interior columns. When structural concerns emerged about the dome's stability, four bearing columns were added to support it. The effect inside is still one of openness, the nave uninterrupted by the heavy piers typical of older Armenian churches. Paintings fill the walls above the half-circle altar on the eastern side: Saint Mary with Jesus, the Ascension, John the Baptist performing the baptism of Christ, and the Crucifixion. On each side of the entrance hall, the two belfries house bells operated by electric power, their ringing a familiar sound for the neighborhood.

## A Community That Stayed

The cathedral's congregation belongs to one of the oldest continuous Christian communities in the Middle East. Armenians have inhabited the Iranian plateau for millennia. Their numbers grew dramatically under the Safavid dynasty, when Shah Abbas I relocated thousands of Armenian families to Isfahan in the early 1600s, establishing the Julfa quarter that still stands today. Over the following centuries, Armenian communities spread to Tehran and other cities across Iran. During the Armenian Genocide beginning in 1915, roughly 50,000 Armenians fled the Ottoman Empire into Persia, adding to the existing population. By the late 1970s, Tehran alone was home to an estimated 110,000 Armenians. Construction of Saint Sarkis began in 1964, financed by the sons of Armenian benefactor Markar Sarkisian -- Gurgen and Vasgen -- who built it in memory of their parents after their father died before the project could begin.

## The Memorial in the Courtyard

Step into the cathedral's courtyard and you find something that transforms the space from a place of worship into a place of witness. A white marble monument stands 3.5 meters tall, its pedestal inscribed in both Persian and Armenian with a single date: 24 April 1915. That was the day Ottoman authorities arrested Armenian intellectuals and community leaders in Constantinople, the event now recognized as the beginning of the Armenian Genocide. The memorial was unveiled on 24 April 1973, the fifty-eighth anniversary. Its presence in Tehran carries particular weight. Iran was not a perpetrator but a neighbor -- a country that received refugees even as the Persian Campaign of World War I brought Ottoman armies onto its own soil. The monument stands as both remembrance and gratitude, acknowledging the Armenian dead while implicitly honoring the Persian land that sheltered survivors.

## Sacred Ground on a Busy Street

Saint Sarkis sits on one of Tehran's busiest thoroughfares, Karimkhan Zand Street, where traffic noise and commercial energy press against its marble walls. The contrast is deliberate in effect, if not in design. Step through the entrance and the city drops away. The white interior absorbs sound. Light filters through the dome. The choir gallery above the entrance fills the nave with hymns sung in a language that has been spoken on this plateau for more than two thousand years. In 2006, community member Hrair Hagopian renovated the baptism pool and the church interior in memory of his wife Vartoohi Davidian, a gesture that reflects the cathedral's ongoing role as a living institution rather than a relic. Babies are still baptized here. Marriages are celebrated. The Armenian liturgy continues in a city that has changed beyond recognition since the cathedral's doors first opened.

## Flight Context

Located at 35.715N, 51.415E in central Tehran, near the intersection of Karimkhan Zand Street and Nejatollahi Street. Mehrabad International Airport (OIII) lies approximately 8 km to the west. The cathedral is in the dense urban core of southern-central Tehran. From the air, the white marble structure and its twin belfries are distinctive against the surrounding urban fabric. The Alborz Mountains form the northern horizon.
