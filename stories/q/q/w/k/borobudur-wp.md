# Stone Mountain of Enlightenment

Borobudur is the world's largest Buddhist temple, a 9th-century monument of 2,672 relief panels and 504 Buddha statues that was abandoned, buried under volcanic ash for centuries, and painstakingly restored to become Indonesia's most visited landmark.

**Location:** -7.6070, 110.2040

**Categories:** temples, world-heritage, archaeology, buddhism, indonesia

## Introduction

The monument was never lost, exactly. Local Javanese always knew something was buried on the hill between the twin volcanoes Sundoro-Sumbing and Merbabu-Merapi, in the fertile plain they called the garden of Java. Folk stories warned of misfortune for anyone who visited -- a crown prince in 1757 reportedly died the day after peering at a Buddha statue trapped inside a perforated stupa, pitying what he called his "unfortunate friend." When British governor Stamford Raffles sent an engineer to investigate in 1814, it took 200 men two months of cutting, burning, and digging to reveal what centuries of volcanic ash and jungle growth had concealed: the largest Buddhist temple on Earth.

## A Pyramid Built for Pilgrimage

Borobudur is not a conventional temple with interior rooms. It is a solid mass of approximately 55,000 cubic meters of gray andesite stone, cut from nearby quarries and the bed of the Progo River, laid without mortar and joined by knobs, indentations, and dovetails. Nine stacked platforms -- six square, three circular -- rise to a central dome 35 meters above ground level. The design functions as a three-dimensional mandala: pilgrims enter from the east and walk clockwise through 3,000 meters of corridor, ascending through progressively simpler terraces that represent the Buddhist journey from the realm of desire through the world of forms to the formless world beyond. Along the way, 2,672 relief panels and originally 504 Buddha statues tell that story in stone. The architect, known only as Gunadharma from Javanese folk tales, used a measurement system called the tala -- the length of a human face from hairline to chin -- and embedded a ratio of 4:6:9 throughout the structure, a proportion shared with the nearby temples of Pawon and Mendut and thought to carry cosmological significance.

## Stories Carved in Stone

The reliefs are encyclopedic. Along the lower galleries, 1,460 narrative panels depict the life of the historical Buddha, his previous incarnations as told in the Jataka tales, and the wanderings of the youth Sudhana in search of ultimate wisdom. But the panels are also an unintentional documentary of 8th-century Javanese life: kings and queens in their courts, hermits in forests, commoners in marketplaces, farmers tending rice, and ships with double outriggers sailing open water. One such vessel, the Borobudur Ship, became the basis for a full-scale replica that sailed from Indonesia to Africa in 2004, now displayed in the Samudra Raksa Museum a few hundred meters north of the temple. Hidden beneath an encasement base, 160 additional panels depict the Karmawibhangga -- the workings of cause and effect -- with unflinching scenes of theft, murder, and hellish punishment alongside images of charity, pilgrimage, and reward. These hidden reliefs were photographed by pioneering Javanese photographer Kassian Cephas in 1890 before being covered again; only the southeast corner remains visible today.

## Abandoned to Ash and Jungle

The Sailendra dynasty began construction of Borobudur in the 8th century, during a remarkable period when Hindu and Buddhist monarchs coexisted peacefully enough for a Hindu king to patronize a Buddhist monument. The Karangtengah inscription of 824 AD records the inauguration of a sacred building called Jinalaya -- the realm of those who have conquered desire -- by Pramodhawardhani, daughter of King Samaratungga. By the 10th century, a series of volcanic eruptions prompted the Mataram kingdom to relocate its capital to East Java, and Borobudur slipped into disuse. The monument appears vaguely in a 14th-century Majapahit text as "the vihara in Budur," but by the time Java's population converted to Islam in the 15th century, active pilgrimage had ceased. Volcanic ash accumulated. Trees took root in the stonework. Superstition replaced reverence.

## The Long Work of Restoration

Borobudur's rediscovery set off a century-long effort to understand and preserve it. After Raffles's initial clearing in 1814, the Dutch resident Hartmann fully unearthed the complex by 1835. Dutch engineer Van Erp led the first major restoration from 1907 to 1911, dismantling and rebuilding the upper three circular platforms and their stupas, though his use of concrete introduced damaging alkali salts into the stone. By the 1950s, drainage failures had caused the earthen core to swell, pushing walls outward and threatening collapse. Indonesia's "Save Borobudur" campaign, launched in 1968, culminated in a massive UNESCO-funded restoration between 1975 and 1982. Over one million stones were dismantled, catalogued, cleaned, and reassembled. New drainage channels were embedded, impermeable layers added, and the foundation stabilized. The project employed 600 people and cost nearly $7 million. UNESCO listed Borobudur as a World Heritage Site in 1991.

## Sacred Ground, Contested Space

Borobudur is many things simultaneously: a World Heritage Site, Indonesia's most-visited monument, an active Buddhist pilgrimage destination, and a symbol of national identity whose image appears on banknotes, stamps, and the emblem of Central Java province. Each year during Vesak, thousands of Buddhists walk from the nearby temple of Mendut through Pawon to Borobudur, retracing the ancient ritual path. But the monument's fame brings pressure. Over 1.4 million domestic tourists visited in 2022, and the narrow stone stairways -- nearly half of their 2,033 surfaces severely worn -- now require wooden coverings to prevent further erosion. Vandalism and graffiti prompted access restrictions; since 2022, only 1,200 visitors per day are admitted, wearing bamboo slippers. In 1985, nine stupas were damaged by bombs planted by Islamic extremists. In 2014, security was tightened after ISIS-affiliated threats. Borobudur endures these pressures as it has endured volcanic eruptions, centuries of neglect, and colonial looting -- not easily, but stubbornly.

## Flight Context

Located at 7.61S, 110.20E on the Kedu Plain of Central Java, between the volcanic pairs Sundoro-Sumbing (to the northwest) and Merbabu-Merapi (to the east). The monument appears as a stepped gray structure on a hilltop surrounded by green agricultural land. Best viewed at 3,000-5,000 feet AGL. Mount Merapi (7.54S, 110.44E) is visible 28 km to the east. Nearby airports: WARQ (Adisucipto/Yogyakarta, 40 km SE), WAHS (Ahmad Yani/Semarang, 75 km NE). Check volcanic ash NOTAMs for Merapi activity.
