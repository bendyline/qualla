# Litchfield National Park

Named for a 19th-century explorer and home to ancient Aboriginal sacred landscapes, Litchfield National Park shelters permanent spring-fed waterfalls, rare bat colonies, magnetic termite mounds, and the haunting sandstone formations of the Lost City.

**Location:** -13.0547, 130.9050

**Categories:** National parks, Australia, Northern Territory, Indigenous Australia, Waterfalls, Wildlife

## Introduction

The Kungarakan and Marranunggu peoples believe their ancestral spirits are still actively present in this landscape — that those spirits shaped the waterfalls, the sandstone columns, the animals. Proclaimed a national park only in 1986, Litchfield carries a much older designation. The park was named for Frederick Henry Litchfield, who walked through this country in 1864 as part of the Finniss Expedition, but it had been named and known long before any European arrived.

## Deep Time on the Tabletop

The defining geography of Litchfield is the Tabletop Range — a sandstone plateau that lifts itself above the surrounding savanna and sends water cascading off its edges. Aboriginal people have lived across this landscape for thousands of years. The park covers approximately 1,500 square kilometres, and the knowledge embedded in it — the names, the sacred sites, the understanding of where the water runs in dry months — belongs to the Kungarakan and Marranunggu peoples.

The European pastoral layer is visible in the ruins of Blyth Homestead, built in 1929 and abandoned to the bush by the 1960s. The Sargent family ran cattle and worked a tin mine here under conditions that the park now interprets honestly: difficult, isolated, and finally unsustainable. The Bamboo Creek Tin Mine, preserved as a heritage site, shows the small-scale labour-intensive operations — many run by Chinese workers in the 19th and early 20th centuries — that extracted what the country offered before larger industry moved in.

## The Waterfalls

Florence Falls drops as a double plunge into a swimming hole enclosed by monsoon forest. One hundred and sixty steps lead down to it. Wangi Falls is the park's most popular attraction — spring-fed, accessible year-round, with a picnic area and a pool that draws swimmers throughout the dry season. Tolmer Falls is off-limits for swimming: the plunge pool at its base houses colonies of rare ghost bats and orange horseshoe bats that would not survive the disturbance. The falls cascade over two high escarpments and the view alone is worth the walk.

Buley Rockhole strings together a series of cascading pools along a creek, each tumbling into the next, cool and clear and easy to reach. These are the waterfalls that run through the dry season, fed by underground springs that the sandstone plateau stores across the wet months.

## Mounds and Formations

Two natural formations in Litchfield stop visitors in their tracks. The magnetic termite mounds are flat, blade-shaped structures oriented north-south, built by termites as passive solar regulators that keep the internal temperature of the nest stable through the day. They stand up to two metres tall and look, from a distance, like a field of grey tombstones — unsettling and impressive in equal measure.

The Lost City requires a four-wheel drive to reach and is the most challenging destination in the park. Sandstone blocks and pillars, sculpted by wind and rain over thousands of years, rise from the bush in configurations that genuinely suggest abandoned architecture. The name is fanciful, but the formations earn it. Some columns stand in isolation; others cluster as if the walls between them have dissolved.

## What the Seasons Demand

Litchfield's character changes completely between the dry season (May to October) and the wet (November to April). In the dry, the park is open, accessible, and busy — visitors swim at Florence and Wangi, walk the Tabletop Track, and camp at designated sites. In the wet, the 4WD tracks flood and close, the northern roads can become impassable, and the park asserts its own terms. The annual rainfall around Batchelor exceeds 1,487 millimetres, the vast majority of it falling in those five wet months. The waterfalls thunder in January in ways that bear no resemblance to their dry-season versions. Tjaetaba Falls, a drop along Greenant Creek, flows near an Aboriginal sacred site; visitors are asked to swim only above the falls.

## Flight Context

Litchfield National Park is centred on approximately 13.05°S, 130.91°E, about 100 km south-west of Darwin. The Tabletop Range sandstone plateau is the park's most identifiable feature from altitude — a flat-topped escarpment visible against the surrounding flat savanna. Darwin Airport (YPDN) is the nearest major facility. Batchelor Airfield (YBCR) serves as the closest airstrip, approximately 15 km from the park entrance via sealed road. Recommended viewing altitude of 3,000–5,000 ft gives a clear view of the escarpment topography. Best visibility in dry season, May–October.
