# Alice Springs

Australia's most famous outback town, a settlement of around 28,000 in the geographic center of the continent where Arrernte culture, frontier history, and modern desert life converge.

**Location:** -23.6975, 133.8836

**Categories:** cities, outback, indigenous-culture, history

## Introduction

The Todd River runs through the middle of Alice Springs, except when it does not, which is most of the time. The riverbed is usually dry sand, and the town has turned this into a point of pride: every year, competitors in the Henley-on-Todd Regatta race bottomless boats across the sand, making it the only dry river regatta in the world. This cheerful absurdity captures something essential about Alice Springs, a town that exists 1,500 kilometers from the nearest major city, in a desert that regularly hits 47 degrees Celsius, yet sustains a population of around 28,000 people, dozens of art galleries, and a social calendar that includes camel racing.

## Mparntwe Before Alice

The Arrernte people have lived in this part of central Australia for at least 30,000 years, calling the township area Mparntwe, meaning "watering place." Their country is defined by the MacDonnell Ranges, which run east and west of the modern town, creating a landscape of gorges, waterholes, and mountain habitats. According to Arrernte traditional histories, this landscape was shaped by the Yeperenye, Ntyarlke, and Utnerrengatye caterpillars and by Akngwelye, the wild dogs. Sites of deep significance surround the town: Anthwerrke (Emily Gap), Akeyulerre (Billy Goat Hill), Ntaripe (Heavitree Gap), and Alhekulyele (Mt Gillen). In May 2000, the Arrernte were recognized by the Federal Court as the Traditional Owners of Alice Springs, the first successful native title claim in an Australian urban area. Five dialects of the Arrernte language are still spoken across central Australia.

## A Town Named for a Wife

The European settlement began with a telegraph repeater station, built in 1872 as part of the Overland Telegraph Line linking Adelaide to Darwin. Surveyor William Whitfield Mills named the waterhole near the station after Alice Todd, wife of the telegraph's driving force, Superintendent Charles Todd. The nearby rivers bear Charles's name. For decades the settlement was called Stuart, after the explorer John McDouall Stuart, and was not officially renamed Alice Springs until 1933. Gold discovered at Arltunga, 100 kilometers east, in 1887 brought the first significant European settlement, though the town's population remained tiny. The first aircraft landed in 1921. Adelaide House, central Australia's first hospital, was built by the Rev. John Flynn in 1926, when the European population numbered about 40. It was the arrival of the railway in 1929 that finally sparked growth, and Aboriginal Centralians still outnumbered European residents until the mid-1930s.

## War and the American Connection

World War II transformed Alice Springs from an isolated settlement of fewer than 500 into a military staging base handling close to 200,000 personnel. When Japanese forces threatened Darwin and its sea routes were cut, Alice Springs became the wartime civilian capital of the Northern Territory. The town's military population peaked at around 8,000, and General Douglas MacArthur passed through, giving Alice considerable fame. The war left behind sealed roads, expanded water supplies, and surplus military equipment that jumpstarted the postwar economy. A quieter but more enduring American presence arrived in the 1950s with the establishment of US Air Force Detachment 421, and expanded in the 1970s when Pine Gap, the joint Australian-US satellite tracking station, was built 19 kilometers southwest. Today roughly 2,000 Alice Springs residents hold American citizenship, and the community celebrates the 4th of July and Thanksgiving alongside Australian holidays.

## Desert Culture, Desert Light

Alice Springs has become one of Australia's most important centers for Aboriginal art. The Todd Mall hosts numerous galleries, and the Araluen Centre for Arts and Entertainment presents everything from ballets to local Aboriginal performances. The Parrtjima festival, held each April, uses the MacDonnell Ranges as a canvas for light installations celebrating Aboriginal culture, with the name meaning "shedding both light and understanding" in Arrernte. The town's museums are excellent for their size: the Old Timer's Traeger Museum holds soapstone carvings by Arrernte artist Erlikilyika alongside artifacts from the town's early Afghan and German residents. CAAMA, the Central Australian Aboriginal Media Association, operates one of the largest radio transmission footprints in the world from studios in Alice Springs, along with a music label, a television production company, and a technical division that together form Australia's largest Indigenous media company.

## The Red Centre's Capital

Everything in Alice Springs is defined by distance. Adelaide is 1,530 kilometers south. Darwin is 1,498 kilometers north. The Ghan train, named for the Afghan cameleers who once supplied the outback by camel, connects all three on a weekly journey that is part transportation, part pilgrimage. The 223-kilometer Larapinta Trail follows the West MacDonnell Ranges and is considered among the world's great long-distance walks. Uluru sits 440 kilometers to the southwest. The town serves as the economic and logistical hub for a region of over 546,000 square kilometers, home to mining communities, cattle stations, and Pine Gap. It is a place where the Royal Flying Doctor Service maintains a dispatch center, where the School of the Air delivers lessons to children in remote homesteads, and where the Finke Desert Race sends 500 competitors tearing across 500 kilometers of dirt. The desert demands resourcefulness, and Alice Springs has made resourcefulness its defining trait.

## Flight Context

Located at 23.70S, 133.87E in the geographic center of Australia, straddling the usually dry Todd River on the northern side of the MacDonnell Ranges. Alice Springs Airport (YBAS) handles flights to all major Australian cities. The town is clearly visible from the air, a green-and-brown grid nestled in the gap of the MacDonnell Ranges with red desert stretching in every direction. Pine Gap is visible 19 km to the southwest. Best viewed from 5,000-8,000 ft AGL for the dramatic contrast between the town and its desert surroundings.
