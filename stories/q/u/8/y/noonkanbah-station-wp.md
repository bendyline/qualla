# Noonkanbah Station

A Kimberley cattle station where a 1980 convoy of drilling rigs escorted by hundreds of police forced its way onto Aboriginal sacred land, making Noonkanbah a turning point in Australian land rights history.

**Location:** -18.4990, 124.8310

**Categories:** history, indigenous-culture, pastoral, land-rights

## Introduction

On 7 August 1980, a convoy of 45 drilling rigs and trucks left Perth heading for the remote Kimberley, escorted by hundreds of police. Their destination was Noonkanbah Station, a pastoral lease on the Fitzroy River where the Yungngora people had lived for millennia and where the Western Australian government had authorised oil exploration on sacred ground. The confrontation that followed -- picket lines broken, protesters dragged aside, drill bits turning on land of deep spiritual significance -- became one of the defining moments in Australia's long reckoning with Indigenous land rights.

## Empire on the Fitzroy

The Emanuel brothers, Isadore and Sydney, pegged out Noonkanbah in the early 1880s after their father sent them north to claim Kimberley country. At its peak the station sprawled across roughly 4,000 square kilometres of Fitzroy River floodplain -- a vast operation that, by 1920, was advertised with neighbouring Gogo and Liveringa stations as a single block of over one million acres. The 1920 auction listing reads like an inventory of frontier ambition: 65,000 sheep, 70 horses, 250 donkeys, 50 mules, 50 cattle, a 20-stand shearing shed, and 400 miles of sheep fencing, all abundantly watered by the Fitzroy River running through the centre of the run. During the drought of 1936, when surrounding stations lost everything, Noonkanbah's plains still held good feed, and a record 100,000 sheep were shorn that season.

## Wool, War, and Flood

For decades Noonkanbah's superfine wool commanded premium prices at London sales -- 720 bales of greasy wool sold at 64 pence per pound in 1953. The station participated in the innovative Air Beef Scheme, flying sheep 115 miles to distant abattoirs. During World War II, the Royal Australian Air Force established a base at the station's civil airfield on 1 March 1943, with petrol and bomb dumps large enough to support B-24 Liberators. The Netherlands East Indies Air Force used it as a staging base, and multiple RAAF squadrons operated from its strips. But the Kimberley always extracted its price. The drought of 1952-53, the first in 70 years, halved cattle numbers across the region. When the Fitzroy flooded in 1954, immediately after that drought, the swollen river drowned 300 to 400 sheep in boggy ground at Noonkanbah. Even the deep past surfaced here: bore drilling on the station yielded fossils of Erythrobatrachus noonkanbahensis, a Triassic amphibian species named for the place where its remains were found.

## Walking Off

The Yungngora people -- the traditional owners whose country the station occupied -- had worked for successive station owners for generations. In 1971, they walked off over a dispute about pay and conditions. Five years later, the Aboriginal Land Fund purchased the station so it could be developed by its traditional owners. The Yungngora Community began running it themselves, transforming the old woolshed into a community centre. But the mining boom of the 1970s had already planted hundreds of resource tenements across the pastoral lease. An anthropological report confirmed what the Yungngora had always known: the land held deep spiritual significance. Premier Charles Court dismissed those findings.

## The Convoy

Court was adamant that exploration company AMAX would drill for oil regardless of the sacred sites. When the Yungngora refused to grant access, the state government authorised the operation under police protection. The convoy that left Perth on 7 August 1980 was extraordinary in its scale -- 45 non-union drilling rigs and trucks, flanked by hundreds of officers. Violent confrontations erupted as the convoy forced its way through community picket lines onto sacred land. The images broadcast across Australia shocked many who had given little thought to the tensions between resource extraction and Indigenous rights. Noonkanbah became a national symbol, galvanising the land rights movement and reshaping the way Australians understood what was at stake when governments and mining companies overrode Indigenous objections. The drill, as it happened, found no commercially viable oil.

## Recognition at Last

In April 2007, after decades of legal and political struggle, the Yungngora people had their native title recognised over the Noonkanbah land. The station remains in community hands, operated by the Yungngora Community on roughly 1,800 square kilometres of country that holds both pastoral and sacred significance. The journey from the Emanuel brothers' sheep empire to Indigenous self-determination spans more than a century of Australian history -- the booms and busts of wool, the disruptions of war, the injustices of dispossession, and the long, unfinished work of recognition. Today the ruins of the old station buildings still stand near the Fitzroy, and the woolshed serves not as a place of colonial industry but as the heart of a community that outlasted it.

## Flight Context
