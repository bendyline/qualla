# Purnululu National Park

The Bungle Bungle Range's striped sandstone domes — orange from oxidised iron, grey from living cyanobacteria — have been shaped by 350 million years of erosion into a landscape unlike anything else on Earth.

**Location:** -17.4488, 128.5461

**Categories:** National parks of Western Australia, World Heritage Sites in Western Australia, Geology of Western Australia, Kimberley (Western Australia), Indigenous Australia, Natural wonders

## Introduction

Geologists who first studied them in the 1980s were baffled. The domes looked painted — alternating bands of warm orange and cool grey wrapping each tower in horizontal stripes, as precise as something drawn with a compass. The orange bands turned out to be iron oxide, baked dry. The grey bands? Living cyanobacteria, ancient single-celled organisms growing on the moist layers below, staining the rock dark. The Bungle Bungles are not painted. They are alive.

## Beehives of Devonian Stone

The Bungle Bungle Range sits within Purnululu National Park in Western Australia's East Kimberley, roughly 300 kilometres south of Kununurra. The range is a plateau of Devonian sandstone — laid down over 350 million years ago — carved by wind, rain, and time into thousands of beehive-shaped towers. Elevations reach 578 metres above sea level, and the plateau is cut through by gorges dropping a hundred metres, sheer-sided and slot-narrow. How the towers formed into their distinctive shapes is still not fully understood. What is known is that the surface crust of iron oxide and bacteria is fragile; even light foot traffic can shatter it. The towers endure because they are left largely alone. The park covers 239,723 hectares and was listed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2003, recognised as one of the most impressive occurrences of sandstone tower karst terrain in the world.

## Country of the Jaru, Gija, and Malngin

The Jaru, Gija (sometimes spelled Kija), and Malngin peoples have lived in this country for thousands of years. The name Purnululu comes from the Kija language — it simply means sandstone. For the traditional owners, these towers are not a spectacle but a home landscape, woven through with stories, ceremony, and law. In 2022, the Federal Court of Australia recognised both the Purnululu and Gajangana Jaru native title claimants as traditional owners, making Purnululu only the second conservation park to achieve this recognition after amendments to the Native Title Act. The Bungle Bungles Aboriginal Corporation now holds native title in trust for all three groups, and the park is managed jointly with the Western Australian Department of Biodiversity, Conservation and Attractions. In 2024, the Gija community and the Western Australian Museum published an interactive display of the Gija creation story — available both online and installed in the park itself.

## Cathedral Gorge and the Domes Walk

Two walks define the Purnululu experience. The Domes Walk winds through a maze of the beehive towers at ground level, where the scale of the rock becomes physical — each dome taller than a four-storey building, the passages between them narrow and shadowed. Cathedral Gorge ends in a vast circular chamber, its walls curving overhead like a stone amphitheatre, the silence so complete that a whispered word carries. These gorges were carved not by rivers but by the slow patient chemistry of dissolutional weathering: water working its way through the sandstone over millennia, removing grains one by one. The plateau's apparent permanence is an illusion. The Bungle Bungles are dissolving, slowly and continuously, into the Kimberley plain below.

## The Road In

Getting to Purnululu requires effort. The Spring Creek Track branches off the Great Northern Highway about 250 kilometres south of Kununurra and runs 53 kilometres to the visitor centre — a three-hour drive on a track passable only by four-wheel-drive vehicles, and only in the dry season from April to December. In the wet season, the gorges flood, the track closes, and the park returns to the exclusive company of its traditional owners and the wildlife that depends on it. For those unwilling or unable to manage the road, helicopter flights operate out of Bellburn Airstrip within the park, or from Warmun roadhouse nearby. Scenic aircraft flights are also available from Kununurra and Lake Argyle. From the air, the full geometry of the range becomes visible: thousands of domes spreading across the plateau like a field of enormous eggs, each one striped, each one unique.

## Flight Context

Purnululu National Park is located at approximately 17.45°S, 128.55°E in the East Kimberley region of Western Australia. Flying at 5,000–8,000 feet, the Bungle Bungle Range is unmistakable — a plateau of closely packed orange-and-grey striped domes unlike any other terrain feature in the region. The park lies roughly 300 km south of Kununurra (YPKU), which has the nearest major airstrip. Bellburn Airstrip (within the park) serves light aircraft and helicopters during the dry season. Best viewed in the morning when low sun angle emphasises the striped banding of the domes.
