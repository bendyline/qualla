# Ningaloo Coast

A 705,015-hectare World Heritage Site where Australia's largest fringing coral reef meets one of the continent's most remote and pristine coastlines.

**Location:** -22.7390, 113.6280

**Categories:** nature, marine, world-heritage, conservation, reef

## Introduction

Most coral reefs keep their distance. They sit kilometers offshore, accessible only by boat, their colors hidden beneath open water. Ningaloo breaks the pattern. Along 260 kilometers of Western Australia's Gascoyne coast, the reef presses so close to shore that snorkelers can wade in from the sand at Coral Bay and find themselves surrounded by coral within minutes. It is the largest fringing reef in Australia and the only major reef system in the world positioned this close to a landmass. The name itself, from the Wajarri language, means promontory or high land jutting into the sea.

## The Heritage Boundary

The World Heritage inscription in 2011 drew a boundary around more than just the reef. The 705,015-hectare site encompasses the Ningaloo Marine Park in both Commonwealth and state waters, the Muiron Islands Marine Management Area, Cape Range National Park, Jurabi and Bundegi coastal parks, and even the Learmonth Air Weapons Range. The site had already been placed on the Australian National Heritage List on 6 January 2010 under the Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation Act. Within this boundary, a mosaic of sanctuary zones, conservation areas, and general-use zones governs what can and cannot happen. Sixteen sanctuary zones alone dot the marine park, from Bundegi in the north to Turtles Sanctuary Zone in the south. The complexity of overlapping jurisdictions reflects how many different ecosystems crowd into one stretch of coast.

## Life in the Shallows

The numbers tell part of the story: 500 species of fish, 300 species of coral, 600 species of molluscs, and an uncounted abundance of marine invertebrates. But statistics flatten what the reef actually looks like. Loggerhead, green, and hawksbill turtles haul themselves onto Ningaloo's beaches to nest, returning every couple of years to the same stretches of sand. Whale sharks cruise the coast between April and July, the largest fish in the ocean filtering plankton through gaping mouths wide enough to swallow a beach ball. Humpback whales pass through from June to November, breaching and slapping their flukes while their calves learn to swim. In 2006, scientists from the Australian Institute of Marine Science discovered gardens of sponges in the park's deeper waters, organisms thought to be species completely new to science. The reef keeps revealing what it has been quietly growing all along.

## The Battle for Mauds Landing

In the early 2000s, a proposed resort at Mauds Landing threatened to transform one of the coast's most sensitive stretches. The site was a crucial nesting ground for loggerhead turtles, and conservationists argued the development would degrade the entire marine park. The novelist Tim Winton, who lives in the area, became a prominent voice against the project. When he won the WA Premier's Book Award in 2002, he donated the prize money to the community campaign. The resort was ultimately abandoned, but developers have never fully lost interest in the area. The fight mattered beyond the immediate outcome: it demonstrated that public pressure could protect remote coastline, and it built the political will that eventually led to World Heritage inscription. The Yamatji peoples of the Baiyungu and Yinigudura, the traditional owners of this country, remain central to the ongoing stewardship of a coast they have known for thousands of years.

## The Science of Persistence

In 2007, the Ningaloo Collaboration Cluster brought together CSIRO, the Sustainable Tourism Cooperative Research Centre, and researchers from Curtin University, Murdoch University, the University of Western Australia, the Australian National University, and the University of Queensland. Their aim was ambitious: to build a dynamic model of Ningaloo that integrates socioeconomic pressures with ecological realities. The reef has so far weathered threats that have devastated other systems. The bleaching event of 2021-2022, which badly damaged sections of the Great Barrier Reef, had little effect on Ningaloo. But a marine heatwave in 2025 struck harder, a reminder that even remote reefs are not immune to warming oceans. The collaboration between scientists, traditional owners, local communities, and government agencies represents an ongoing effort to understand what keeps Ningaloo resilient, and how long that resilience might last.

## Flight Context

Centered at approximately 22.74°S, 113.63°E, roughly 1,200 km north of Perth along the Gascoyne coast. The reef is visible from altitude as a vivid turquoise line against the darker Indian Ocean. Nearest airport is Learmonth (YPLM) near Exmouth. Cape Range National Park's limestone ridges run parallel to the coast and are visible from 10,000 ft. The Muiron Islands lie offshore at the northern extent of the World Heritage area.
