# Banda Islands

Ten volcanic islands in the Banda Sea where the world's entire nutmeg supply once grew, sparking colonial wars that reshaped global trade.

**Location:** -4.5833, 129.9167

**Categories:** islands, colonial-history, massacre, spice-trade, volcanic

## Introduction

The earliest mention of the Banda Islands appears in Chinese records dating to 200 BCE, but for millennia no European knew where nutmeg came from. Arab traders kept the secret jealously, selling the spice to Venetian merchants at prices that financed fleets. When the Portuguese apothecary Tome Pires finally visited in the early 1500s, he found a thriving archipelago of perhaps 3,000 people governed by orang kaya -- 'rich men' -- who traded nutmeg, mace, cloves, and bird-of-paradise feathers across a network stretching from Java to New Guinea. Within a century, European powers would destroy that world entirely.

## A Caldera in Deep Water

The Banda Islands are a volcanic group of ten small islands rising from ocean four kilometers deep, about 140 kilometers south of Seram and 2,000 kilometers east of Java. Their total land area is roughly 172 square kilometers, but the maritime territory reaches over 736 square kilometers. The main group sits in a drowned caldera: Banda Neira and the still-smoking Gunung Api occupy the crater's center, while the crescent of Banda Besar and the smaller Pulau Pisang trace the ancient volcanic rim. Gunung Api remains active, its peak reaching about 650 meters. To the west, Pulau Ai and Pulau Run stand apart. To the southeast lies Pulau Hatta, 19 kilometers from the administrative center. Human presence on these islands stretches back at least 8,000 years, evidenced by a rock shelter site on Pulau Ai.

## Blood Price of a Dried Seed

The Portuguese arrived in 1512 under Antonio de Abreu, spending a month filling their ships with nutmeg and mace. But it was the Dutch East India Company that would claim the Bandas through force. In 1609, Admiral Verhoeff arrived to build a fort; the Bandanese orang kaya lured him to a meeting and beheaded him along with 46 Dutchmen. The young merchant Jan Pieterszoon Coen escaped that ambush. Twelve years later, as VOC governor-general, he returned with soldiers and a settling of scores. In 1621, Coen's forces landed on Bandaneira and Banda Besar, forcing the orang kaya to sign an impossible treaty, then using their inevitable failure to comply as justification for mass killing. Forty leaders were beheaded, their heads impaled on bamboo spears. The executions were carried out by Japanese mercenaries in Dutch employ. Villages were destroyed. Of a population estimated at 13,000 to 15,000, perhaps a thousand survived on the islands -- spread through nutmeg groves as forced laborers.

## The Deal That Made Manhattan

Before the massacres, the English had established trading posts on Ai and Run, paying higher prices than the Dutch and winning Bandanese loyalty. The rivalry turned violent: in 1615 the Dutch invaded Ai with 900 soldiers and Japanese mercenaries. The English retreated to Run, launched a midnight counter-attack, retook Ai, and killed 200 Dutchmen. A year later a larger Dutch force besieged Ai until its defenders ran out of ammunition. The fort was renamed Fort Revenge. On Run, Captain Nathaniel Courthope held out for 1,540 days with 39 Europeans and local allies before dying in a Dutch attack in 1620. The English claimed Run for decades afterward, but the 1667 Treaty of Breda settled it: the Dutch kept their nutmeg monopoly across the Bandas, and the English kept Manhattan -- a swap that seemed fair at the time. The Dutch then chopped down every nutmeg tree on Run to ensure the English could never profit from it.

## Sunrise Assault on Fort Belgica

The Dutch monopoly lasted almost two centuries until the Napoleonic Wars reshuffled alliances. In May 1810, a British squadron of three ships -- the frigate HMS Piedmontaise, sloop Barracouta, and transport Mandarin -- departed Madras carrying a hundred soldiers. Captain Christopher Cole took a circuitous route to avoid detection. On August 9, the British appeared at Banda Neira and stormed Fort Belgica at sunrise. The battle ended within hours. Fort Nassau surrendered after some subterfuge, and within days the entire archipelago was in British hands. Captain Charles Foote became Lieutenant-Governor. More consequentially, the British transplanted nutmeg trees to Ceylon, Grenada, Singapore, and other colonies in 1817, ending the Bandas' unique position as the world's sole source of the spice.

## Echoes in the Language

Today roughly 21,000 people live across the Banda Islands, most descended from the laborers, enslaved people, convicts, and immigrants the Dutch brought to replace the Bandanese they killed or expelled. They speak Banda Malay, a dialect distinct from Ambonese Malay, famous for its lilting accent and laced with Dutch loanwords -- the words for fork, spoon, floor, and porch all come from Dutch. Portuguese borrowings survive too, shared with but pronounced differently from Ambonese Malay. On the Kei Islands to the east, in villages called Banda Eli and Banda Elat, descendants of refugees who fled the 1621 massacres still speak a version of the original Banda language. Four centuries later, they remember where their ancestors came from. Pre-colonial ritual practices persist on the islands themselves, performed by communities who inherited them across generations of displacement and survival.

## Flight Context

Located at 4.58S, 129.92E in the Banda Sea, approximately 140 km south of Seram Island and 2,000 km east of Java. The active volcanic cone of Gunung Api (650 m) is the primary visual landmark, smoking perpetually. The caldera formation is visible from altitude -- Banda Neira and Gunung Api at center, the crescent of Banda Besar tracing the rim. Banda Neira has a small airstrip (ICAO: WPAT). Nearest major airport is Pattimura Airport (WAPP) in Ambon. Expect tropical maritime conditions with afternoon convection.
