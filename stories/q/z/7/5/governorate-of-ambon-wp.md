# The Governorate of Ambon: Fort Victoria and the Clove Monopoly

For fourteen years, a tiny island in the Maluku archipelago served as the capital of Dutch commercial power in Asia -- all because of a dried flower bud worth its weight in gold.

**Location:** -3.6667, 128.1667

**Categories:** colonial-history, trade, Dutch-East-India-Company, fortifications

## Introduction

On February 22, 1605, the Dutch admiral Steven van der Hagen sailed into the harbor at Ambon and captured Fort Victoria from the Portuguese without firing a shot. It was one of the most consequential bloodless takeovers in colonial history. Within five years, this small tropical island -- barely forty kilometers long -- became the administrative headquarters of the Dutch East India Company's entire Asian operation, the nerve center from which the VOC orchestrated its domination of the global spice trade. The prize was cloves. Ambon was the world's foremost producer of them, and the Dutch intended to keep it that way by any means necessary, including the destruction of every clove tree growing on any island they did not control.

## The Portuguese Foothold

The Portuguese landed on Ambon Island in 1513, the first Europeans to reach these remote eastern waters. They had been expelled from the sultanate of Ternate to the north and needed a new base for their Maluku operations. Establishing a factory in 1521, they spent nearly sixty years struggling to secure peaceful control of it. The problem was Hitu, a Muslim settlement on the island's northern coast with deep trading and religious ties to Java's port cities. The Portuguese never managed to control the local spice trade, and their attempts to extend authority over the nearby Banda Islands -- the world's sole source of nutmeg -- ended in failure. What the Portuguese did leave behind proved more durable than their forts: a creole trade language called Portugis that was spoken on the island well into the nineteenth century, and family names that Ambonese still carry today.

## The VOC's Eastern Capital

Under Dutch rule, Ambon became the center of something unprecedented: a corporate empire administered not by a government but by a joint-stock company. From 1610 to 1619, the Governorate of Ambon oversaw the VOC's possessions across East Asia, managing a commercial network that stretched from Japan to the Cape of Good Hope. The Dutch enforced their clove monopoly with ruthless efficiency, prohibiting the rearing of clove trees on all other islands under their control. Governors arrived from the Netherlands and ruled from Fort Victoria for terms of a few years, managing a garrison, a trading post, and a population that had never asked for any of it. The governor list reads like a roll call of Dutch colonial ambition -- Frederick de Houtman, Herman van Speult, Arnold de Vlaming van Outshoorn -- names that meant nothing to the Ambonese who lived under them. When the VOC founded Batavia in 1619, the capital shifted to Java, but Ambon remained the clove monopoly's beating heart for another two centuries.

## The Amboyna Massacre and Its Long Shadow

In 1623, an incident on Ambon altered the trajectory of European colonial rivalry. Dutch authorities uncovered what they claimed was a conspiracy between VOC-employed Japanese mercenaries and English East India Company merchants to seize Fort Victoria and assassinate the governor. Under interrogation that included waterboarding, most suspects confessed. Ten Japanese soldiers and ten English merchants were found guilty of treason and executed. England demanded justice; the judges were recalled to the Netherlands and tried, but in 1632 they were acquitted. The story might have ended there, except that it proved too useful as propaganda. Decades later, Oliver Cromwell cited embellished accounts of the so-called Amboyna massacre as a pretext for the First Anglo-Dutch War in 1652 and the Second in 1665. The playwright John Dryden turned it into a full tragedy for the London stage. The seventeenth-century narrative of innocent English merchants slaughtered by Dutch cruelty persisted in British popular history for centuries -- a reminder of how a single event on a small island can echo across empires.

## Between Empires

Ambon changed hands with the tides of European war. When France overran the Dutch Republic in 1795 and established the Batavian Republic, the exiled Prince of Orange sent the Kew Letters asking Britain to occupy Dutch colonies for safekeeping. British Admiral Rainier arrived at Ambon in 1796, and the last Dutch governor, Alexander Cornabe, accepted the transfer. The Peace of Amiens returned the island to Dutch control in 1802, but by then the VOC had been nationalized, so Ambon became a state colony rather than a corporate possession. The British took it again in 1810, returned it again in 1814, and the island settled into the long twilight of Dutch East Indies rule. That era ended not with another European war but with Indonesian independence. In 1949, Maluku was transferred to Indonesia under agreements that theoretically allowed Moluccans to choose whether to join the new nation. When the Republic of South Maluku declared independence in April 1950, the Indonesian army invaded Ambon that September, ending nearly 350 years of the island's singular place in European colonial history.
