# Panama Canal Zone

For 76 years, a 553-square-mile strip of American territory bisected the nation of Panama, complete with its own towns, courts, commissaries, and a racial caste system built into the payroll.

**Location:** 9.1177, -79.7202

**Categories:** History of Panama, Panama Canal, United States territories, Colonialism, Cold War

## Introduction

If you crossed the wrong street in Panama City during the mid-twentieth century, you left the Republic of Panama and entered the United States. No passport required, no border checkpoint -- just a shift in jurisdiction so seamless it was almost absurd. The Panama Canal Zone, a 553-square-mile concession stretching five miles on each side of the canal, existed from 1903 to 1979 as one of the strangest political entities in the Americas: an American enclave that cut a sovereign nation in half, governed by Army engineers, supplied by government commissaries, and stratified by a payroll system that sorted human beings by the color of their skin.

## Carved from a Revolution

The Canal Zone was born from impatience and gunboat diplomacy. When Colombia's Senate rejected a canal treaty in August 1903, President Theodore Roosevelt backed Panamanian rebels seeking independence. American warships blocked Colombian troop movements, and within days Panama was a new nation. Philippe Bunau-Varilla, a Frenchman representing the Panamanians, rushed to sign the Hay-Bunau-Varilla Treaty before actual Panamanian diplomats could arrive to negotiate. The treaty granted the United States a 20-mile-wide zone with full authority to deploy troops, pass laws, and govern as it saw fit. When Panama hesitated to ratify, Bunau-Varilla warned that American protection would be withdrawn. The Panamanians agreed, even adding a constitutional provision allowing the United States to intervene to preserve public order. The Canal Zone was formally turned over on May 4, 1904, when American officials raised the flag over the old French canal company offices in Panama City.

## Gold Roll, Silver Roll

The Zone's labor force operated under a two-tier system that turned race into bureaucracy. Employees were classified as either "gold roll" or "silver roll" -- terms inherited from the nineteenth-century Panama Railroad, which paid Americans in gold coin and local workers in silver. In practice, the distinction was racial. White Americans and Northern Europeans went on the gold roll; Black Caribbean workers and Southern Europeans went on the silver. Black Americans were generally not hired at all. In November 1906, Chief Engineer John Stevens ordered most Black workers removed from the gold roll, and in 1908 President Roosevelt barred non-Americans from it entirely. The system governed everything: gold roll workers received superior housing, better food, and higher pay. Silver roll workers -- who numbered 13,000 compared to 3,700 on the gold roll -- were classified as "mostly aliens." The designations persisted for decades, a Jim Crow system exported to the tropics.

## A Company Town Between Two Oceans

Life in the Canal Zone was unlike anywhere else. Nobody owned a home; everyone rented from the government, with housing assigned by seniority. The Panama Canal Company ran the utility companies, the stores, and the commissary, which boasted that its meat had been refrigerated from Chicago slaughterhouse to consumer counter. There were no independent shops. Two high schools served the Zone's children -- Balboa on the Pacific side and Cristobal on the Atlantic. A junior college offered night classes. The commissary grew into an empire of 22 general stores, 7 cigar stores, 22 hostels, and 2 hotels, generating constant friction with Panamanian merchants who could not compete with its prices or quality. Panama passed laws restricting imports from the Zone, but commissary goods regularly surfaced in Panamanian vendor displays, prized for their quality. Everyone worked for the company or the government in one form or another. The Zone was divided into Pacific and Atlantic sides, separated by Gatun Lake, each with its own townships and military installations.

## Martyrs and Treaties

From the beginning, the Zone's existence was a wound in Panamanian sovereignty. Major rioting erupted on May 21, 1958, and again on November 3, 1959. When the Thatcher Ferry Bridge -- now the Bridge of the Americas -- opened in 1962, demonstrations followed. The crisis peaked on January 9, 1964, when student protests over flag-flying rights escalated into three days of violence. About 20 Panamanians and several American soldiers died. Panama broke diplomatic relations with the United States. The carnage forced Washington to ease its grip, allowing Panamanian flags to fly alongside American ones. After years of negotiation, President Jimmy Carter and Panamanian leader Omar Torrijos signed the Torrijos-Carter Treaties on September 7, 1977. On October 1, 1979, the Canal Zone officially ceased to exist, though the United States retained certain defense sites as enclaves -- 44 in total -- within Panamanian territory.

## The Last Flag Lowered

The transfer unfolded over two decades. Military areas, defense sites, and canal operating areas were handed over in stages, with some parcels becoming American enclaves surrounded entirely by Panamanian jurisdiction. The final handover came at noon on December 31, 1999, when every last parcel of former Canal Zone territory came under Panamanian control. Among the Zone's more unlikely legacies: John McCain, the 2008 Republican presidential nominee, was born at Coco Solo Naval Air Station in 1936, sparking minor debate over whether he qualified as a "natural-born citizen." Musician Stephen Stills graduated from Lincoln High School in the Zone. The filmmaker Frederick Wiseman captured its twilight years in his 1977 documentary Canal Zone. What remains today are the towns, the cemeteries, and the memory of an American colony that existed not across an ocean but inside another country.

## Flight Context

The former Panama Canal Zone extended approximately 5 miles on each side of the Panama Canal at 9.118N, 79.720W, spanning 553 square miles across the Isthmus of Panama. From altitude, the former Zone's boundaries are invisible, but its legacy is visible in the American-built townships of Balboa, Gamboa, and the military installations flanking the canal. Nearest airports: Tocumen International (MPTO) and Marcos A. Gelabert/Albrook (MPMG). The former Albrook Air Force Base is now a mall and domestic airport. Recommended viewing altitude: 5,000-8,000 feet to see the canal corridor and former Zone townships.
