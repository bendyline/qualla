# New Haven: Yale's City and America's Pizza Capital

The city that's been synonymous with Yale since 1718, where the New Haven pizza style rivals New York and Chicago, and where town and gown have coexisted uneasily for three centuries.

**Location:** 41.3083, -72.9279

**Categories:** connecticut, new-haven, city, yale, pizza

## Introduction

Yale University has dominated New Haven since 1718, when the Collegiate School moved from Saybrook and renamed itself for benefactor Elihu Yale. The relationship has always been complicated - Yale owns vast amounts of tax-exempt real estate, Yale students and faculty often view the city as backdrop rather than community, and New Haven struggles with problems that seem incongruous next to Ivy League privilege. But the town-gown tension is productive too: Yale brings intellectual ferment and economic activity; New Haven keeps Yale grounded in reality. And somehow, in this unlikely place, Italian immigrants in the early 20th century created a pizza style that partisans argue surpasses New York - thin, charred, coal-oven, with clams instead of pepperoni on the signature pie.

## The Oldest Pizzeria in America

Frank Pepe opened his coal-fired pizzeria on Wooster Street in 1925, establishing what would become New Haven's signature contribution to American food. The style - thin crust charred in a coal oven, irregularly shaped, served in a no-frills atmosphere - diverged from New York. The signature pie is the white clam pizza: freshly shucked clams, olive oil, garlic, oregano, no tomato sauce. Sally's Apizza opened in 1938 a few blocks away; the rivalry between Pepe's and Sally's has continued for 85 years. Modern Apizza, a third contender, opened in 1934. Pizza pilgrims wait hours for tables; the New Haven pizza community is fanatical. Whether New Haven pizza exceeds New York is debated endlessly and may be undecidable, but that the argument exists at all reflects New Haven's unlikely culinary significance.

## Old Campus

Yale's Old Campus is where it all began - 300-year-old elms shading Georgian buildings, Gothic towers rising beyond. Nathan Hale studied here; so did George H.W. Bush and George W. Bush, Bill and Hillary Clinton, Cole Porter, Samuel F.B. Morse, Noah Webster. The residential colleges - modeled on Oxford and Cambridge, their courtyards closed to the public - create a city within the city. The Beinecke Rare Book Library houses a Gutenberg Bible in a windowless marble cube designed to protect its contents from light. The Yale Center for British Art, designed by Louis Kahn, contains the largest collection of British art outside Britain. Yale is not just New Haven's biggest employer; it's the reason New Haven matters beyond Connecticut.

## Town and Gown

The tension between New Haven and Yale is old and ongoing. Yale's tax exemption costs the city millions; New Haven's crime rates alarmed Yale parents and alumni. Decades of urban renewal displaced residents while Yale expanded. The relationship has improved - Yale now makes voluntary payments to the city, invests in New Haven schools, partners on economic development - but suspicions remain. New Haven's Black population, concentrated in neighborhoods far from Yale's gates, has borne much of the city's struggle. The contrast between Yale's wealth and New Haven's poverty is visible walking a few blocks in any direction. The city needs Yale; Yale needs the city; neither is entirely comfortable with the arrangement.

## The Green

New Haven's Green has been public space since 1638, one of the first planned public squares in America. Three churches share the Green: United Church on the Green (Congregational), Center Church (where the crypt contains graves of New Haven's founders), and Trinity Church (Episcopal). The Green is where protests gathered, where festivals happen, where Yale meets the city. The Amistad Memorial, dedicated in 1992, honors the African captives who, in 1839, overthrew the slave ship Amistad and were defended in New Haven courts. New Haven's history connects to both Yale's privilege and the struggle for Black freedom; the Green holds both memories.

## Elm City

Tweed-New Haven Airport (HVN) offers limited service; most visitors use Hartford (BDL) or New York area airports and drive. Union Station, designed by Cass Gilbert, serves Amtrak and Metro-North to New York (90 minutes). The campus is walkable; Chapel Street and Broadway offer shops and restaurants beyond pizza. The Shubert Theatre has been a Broadway tryout venue since 1914; shows that premiered here include 'Oklahoma!,' 'A Streetcar Named Desire,' and 'The Sound of Music.' Long Wharf Theatre presents regional premieres. From altitude, New Haven appears as urban development along the harbor where the Quinnipiac and West rivers meet - the Elm City (named for American elms that once lined its streets), where Yale has been since 1718, and where the best pizza in America may be served.
