# Freddy's Fashion Mart Attack

A 1995 arson and shooting at a Harlem clothing store that killed eight people, born from a rent dispute that exposed deep racial and economic fault lines on 125th Street.

**Location:** 40.8097, -73.9508

**Categories:** disasters-and-attacks, african-american-history, new-york-city-history, harlem

## Introduction

Across from the Apollo Theater, at 272 West 125th Street, a clothing store called Freddy's Fashion Mart once occupied a ground-floor storefront. By December 1995, it had become the focal point of weeks of heated protest over a sublease dispute involving a Black-owned record shop, a Jewish merchant, and a Black church that owned the building. On the morning of December 8, a man walked into the store with a revolver and a container of flammable liquid. By afternoon, eight people were dead. The tragedy exposed fractures within Harlem that no one wanted to acknowledge -- tensions between communities that shared the same streets but not the same power.

## A Dispute on Harlem's Main Street

The chain of events began with a landlord's decision. The United House of Prayer, a Black Pentecostal church that owned the retail property, asked its tenant Fred Harari to expand his clothing store. To do so, Harari needed to evict his long-time subtenant Sikhulu Shange, a Black South African who ran The Record Shack from a small adjoining space. For Shange and his supporters, the eviction felt like erasure -- a Black-owned business pushed out to benefit someone they saw as an outsider. The activist Al Sharpton led weeks of protests on the sidewalk outside Freddy's, calling Harari a "white interloper." The rhetoric hardened. Picketers shouted at customers entering the store. What had started as a lease disagreement became something far more volatile, amplified by decades of frustration over who profited from Harlem's commercial corridors and who merely lived there.

## Ten Minutes Past Ten

Roland James Smith Jr. arrived at Freddy's Fashion Mart around 10:12 a.m. on December 8, 1995. He carried a .38-caliber revolver and a container of paint thinner. After ordering Black customers to leave, he sprinkled the accelerant through the store and positioned himself near the only exit. When police arrived, Smith fired at them. He shot four people trying to escape the flames. The fire department contained the blaze by 12:07 p.m. and entered to find devastation: seven store employees had died of smoke inhalation, trapped by a fire escape that had been bricked up and a sprinkler system that had been shut down in violation of fire code. Smith was found dead of a self-inflicted gunshot wound. The victims -- workers who had simply shown up for their shifts that morning -- were of various backgrounds, including Honduran, Guyanese, and African American.

## The Man Behind the Violence

Smith's life before that December morning was marked by dislocation and conflict. Born in 1944, he lost his mother at age three. By his twenties he had renounced his American citizenship and refused to serve in Vietnam, earning a four-year federal prison sentence. Arrests for assault and harassment followed in subsequent decades. Ten days before the attack, the six-story Bronx apartment building where he lived burned to the ground. Whether he attended the protests outside Freddy's remains uncertain; Sharpton later stated that Smith had been an open critic of his nonviolent approach. Whatever personal grievances drove Smith into the store that morning, they collided with a neighborhood dispute and produced catastrophe.

## Reckonings and Regrets

In the aftermath, blame radiated outward. Critics pointed to Sharpton's inflammatory language as having stoked the climate that made violence possible. Sharpton himself denied responsibility for provoking the attack, though in 2002 he expressed regret for using the phrase "white interloper." Fire department investigators revealed that the building's safety violations -- the disabled sprinkler, the sealed fire escape -- had made the death toll far worse than it might otherwise have been. For Harlem, the attack became a painful touchstone. It did not begin or end the conversations about economic displacement, outside ownership, and racial solidarity on 125th Street. But it demonstrated, with terrible finality, where unchecked grievance could lead when infrastructure failed and rhetoric went unchallenged.
