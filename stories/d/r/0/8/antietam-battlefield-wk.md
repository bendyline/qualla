# Antietam Battlefield

Twelve hours of fighting near a small Maryland town produced more American dead, wounded and missing than any other day in the country's history - and made the Emancipation Proclamation possible.

**Location:** 39.4701, -77.7381

**Categories:** civil-war, battlefield, history, maryland, national-park, memorial

## Introduction

By the time the fighting stopped at Antietam Creek on the evening of September 17, 1862, 22,717 Americans were dead, wounded or missing. The number is so large it stops registering. It is helpful, then, to walk the Cornfield at dawn, on the actual ground, in the actual late-summer light, and try to make it register. Two armies stood in a forty-acre field of head-high corn. They were close enough to hear each other breathing. Over the next three hours, the corn was cut down completely. So were most of the men.

## How They Got to Sharpsburg

Robert E. Lee had brought his Army of Northern Virginia north for the first time, hoping a victory on Union soil would convince Britain and France to recognize the Confederacy. George B. McClellan, commanding the Army of the Potomac, was a brilliant organizer and an exceptionally cautious general. A few days before the battle, Union soldiers found a copy of Lee's invasion orders wrapped around three cigars in a fence corner. McClellan now knew his enemy's exact dispositions. He moved slowly anyway. On the morning of September 17, his army, twice the size of Lee's, attacked piecemeal along a four-mile front near the small Maryland farming town of Sharpsburg. Lee, outnumbered and pinned against the Potomac River, had nowhere to go. He fought.

## The Cornfield

The first attack came at dawn into a forty-acre field of standing corn on the farm of David Miller. Joseph Hooker's I Corps drove south through the rows; John Bell Hood's division and elements of Stonewall Jackson's corps drove north. For three hours the lines surged back and forth across the same forty acres. Hooker later wrote that every stalk of corn in the northern part of the field was cut as cleanly as if a knife had been drawn through it, and that the slain lay in rows precisely as they had stood in their ranks a few moments before. The 1st Texas Regiment lost 82 percent of its men in about twenty minutes. The Iron Brigade walked into the same fire from the other side. When the corn was gone, you could see across the entire field. The men who had been standing in it were gone too.

## Bloody Lane

South of the Cornfield ran a worn-down farm road, lower than the surrounding fields - the Sunken Road. Around 2,500 Confederates under D.H. Hill took position in it and used the embankment as a rifle pit. Federal divisions advanced toward the lane and were shot to pieces. Thomas Meagher led the Irish Brigade, four regiments of mostly Irish immigrants, across six hundred yards of open ground straight at the road. They were close enough to see the men they were trying to kill before the volley came. Fighting for the Sunken Road went on for three and a half hours. When it broke, federal soldiers came over the embankment and found a thousand Confederates lying where they had fired their last shots. From that afternoon on, no one called it the Sunken Road. They called it Bloody Lane. The casualty total along that 800-yard stretch was about 5,600.

## Burnside's Bridge and the Angel

Three miles south, Ambrose Burnside's IX Corps spent most of the day trying to take a three-arch stone bridge over Antietam Creek - a bridge held by perhaps 500 Georgians under Robert Toombs, firing from the wooded bluff on the far bank. The creek was shallow and easily fordable in several places nearby, but Burnside insisted on the bridge. It took three attempts and several hours. By the time his corps crossed and pressed toward Sharpsburg, A.P. Hill's Confederate division arrived from Harpers Ferry after a forced march and stopped them. Somewhere in the middle of all this, a forty-year-old former Patent Office clerk named Clara Barton arrived at the Cornfield with a wagon of medical supplies she had begged and organized herself. She tended wounded men under fire while a bullet passed through her sleeve and killed the soldier she was helping. The men called her the Angel of the Battlefield. She would later found the American Red Cross.

## What the Day Bought

By nightfall, both armies still held the ground they had started on. Tactically the battle was a draw. But Lee withdrew across the Potomac the next day, and McClellan - to Lincoln's lasting fury - let him go. The president visited the battlefield two weeks later, walked the lines with McClellan, and shortly afterward removed him from command. Five days after the battle, with the Confederate invasion turned back, Lincoln issued the preliminary Emancipation Proclamation. The Union now fought not only to preserve itself but to end slavery. The day at Antietam made that possible. Many of the men who lay in Miller's Cornfield and along Bloody Lane and on the slope above Burnside's Bridge were teenagers. About 3,650 of them died there. Walk the ground today and the fields look almost exactly as they did - the National Battlefield has preserved them carefully - and the only sound is wind across grass. It is the most American silence there is.

## Flight Context

Antietam National Battlefield is centered at 39.4701 N, 77.7381 W, near Sharpsburg in western Maryland's Washington County. From the air the battlefield reads as gently rolling farmland between Antietam Creek to the east and the Potomac River to the southwest - the trace of the creek is the orientation key. Burnside's Bridge crosses the creek at the southern end; the Dunker Church, Miller's Cornfield and the Sunken Road run roughly north-to-south through the middle. Closest airport is Hagerstown Regional (KHGR), about 12 miles north. Frederick Municipal (KFDK) sits 25 miles east. Recommended viewing altitude 2,000-3,000 feet AGL; the whole battlefield fits in a single low-altitude pass. Best light is early morning - the same light the men in the Cornfield fought in - when shadows from the Observation Tower and the Dunker Church stretch long across the fields.
