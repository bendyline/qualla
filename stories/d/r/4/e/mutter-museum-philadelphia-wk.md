# The Mütter Museum: A Reckoning Behind Glass

Philadelphia's famous medical museum, founded in 1858, holds the bones, brains, and bodies of thousands of people who never consented to being on display - and is now, finally, asking whether that should change.

**Location:** 39.9529, -75.1769

**Categories:** pennsylvania, philadelphia, museum, medical-history, ethics

## Introduction

In 2023, the Mütter Museum quietly pulled most of its YouTube videos offline. The museum that had spent 165 years displaying preserved human bodies, jars of fetal specimens, and shelves of skulls had begun, at last, to ask an uncomfortable question. Who were these people, and did they ever agree to this? Nestled inside the College of Physicians of Philadelphia at 19 South 22nd Street, the Mütter is one of the oddest institutions in American medicine. Over 37,000 specimens line its mahogany cases - around 10 percent on view at any time - many of them sourced in the 19th century from morgues, almshouses, dissection labs, and showmen. The bodies on those shelves belonged to people, mostly poor, who had no say in becoming exhibits. The museum is, in 2026, openly wrestling with that legacy. Its halls have not lost their power. They have only grown harder to walk through innocently.

## Dr. Mütter's Gift

Thomas Dent Mütter was a Philadelphia surgeon who pioneered reconstructive operations on people disfigured by burns and birth defects - what 19th-century medicine cruelly called 'monsters.' He kept teaching specimens to show his students how human bodies actually varied, and on December 11, 1858, he donated roughly 1,700 of them to the College of Physicians, along with an endowment to keep them in good order. He died only a year later, at 48. The collection grew with him absent. Doctors across the city bequeathed their own specimens; orphaned hospital materials drifted in. By 1909 the holdings filled a new building. What began as a teaching aid for one surgeon had become an archive of bodies the medical profession could not, or would not, let go.

## The Hyrtl Skulls

On a long wall, behind glass, sit 139 human skulls collected in central Europe by the Viennese anatomist Josef Hyrtl in the 1860s. Most belonged to working people from the Austro-Hungarian Empire - prostitutes, soldiers, suicides, the impoverished, the executed. Their names and occupations are inked onto their foreheads in Hyrtl's careful hand. He assembled them, ironically, to refute phrenology - to prove that European skulls did not vary by 'race' in the ways pseudoscientists claimed. He succeeded as science. But the people whose heads he bought, traded for, or quietly took from morgues were never asked. The Mütter has begun publishing what little is known about each individual. It is a slow act of acknowledgment, 160 years late.

## The Soap Lady and the Brain

Two specimens draw most of the crowds. The Soap Lady - a 19th-century Philadelphia woman whose buried body underwent adipocere, a chemical reaction that converted her fat into a hard, soap-like substance - lies in a long case, her open mouth still recognizably human. Albert Einstein's brain, sectioned into translucent microscope slides after his 1955 death by a pathologist who took it without family permission, sits in a small cabinet across the room. One was a woman whose name is lost. One was the most famous mind of the 20th century. Both ended up here for reasons neither chose. The museum no longer pretends this is merely educational. It is asking, in real time, what to do.

## Chang and Eng

Born in Siam in 1811 and joined at the sternum by a band of cartilage, Chang and Eng Bunker were exhibited around the world as a sideshow attraction, then settled in North Carolina, married sisters, fathered 21 children between them, and worked as farmers. Their bodies, the term 'Siamese twins' included, were claimed by an entertainment industry that profited off them long before it considered them men. When they died on the same day in 1874, autopsy revealed they could likely have survived surgical separation by then. The Mütter holds the plaster cast of their conjoined chest and the shared liver that had bound them. Visitors lean close. The exhibit text now reminds them: these were a husband, a father, a farmer, a brother.

## Visiting the Mütter

The Mütter Museum is at 19 South 22nd Street in Philadelphia, two blocks south of the Schuylkill River and a short walk from Rittenhouse Square. It is open daily; tickets are timed. The museum has reorganized exhibits since 2023, added context panels written with input from disability advocates and bioethicists, and removed some material from public display while research continues. Photography inside the galleries is generally not permitted. Allow ninety minutes; the cases reward slow looking, and the human stories now woven into the labels reward slow reading. The College of Physicians' library and historical garden are open by appointment. Philadelphia International Airport (KPHL) is eight miles south.

## Flight Context

Located at 39.9529°N, 75.1769°W in Philadelphia's Center City. From altitude the College of Physicians sits in a dense block of brownstones south of Market Street, near the eastern bank of the Schuylkill River and the looming presence of 30th Street Station across the water. The Philadelphia skyline rises to the east, capped by Comcast Technology Center. Philadelphia International (KPHL) is eight miles south-southwest; Wings Field (KLOM) and Northeast Philadelphia (KPNE) are alternates. The museum building itself is unremarkable from above - small, brick, indistinguishable from its neighbors. That ordinariness is part of the point: what is inside has always been more disturbing than the architecture suggests.
