# Philadelphia: Where America Was Born

The city where the Declaration of Independence was signed, where the Constitution was debated, where the Liberty Bell cracked, and where they still boo Santa Claus at Eagles games.

**Location:** 39.9526, -75.1652

**Categories:** pennsylvania, philadelphia, city, history, founding

## Introduction

On July 4, 1776, in a brick building on Chestnut Street, the Second Continental Congress adopted the Declaration of Independence. Eleven years later, in the same city, the Constitutional Convention created the document that still governs the nation. For a decade, from 1790 to 1800, Philadelphia served as the nation's capital while Washington D.C. was built. This was where America became America - Independence Hall, the Liberty Bell, Benjamin Franklin's city. Yet Philadelphia is not a museum piece. It's a gritty, loud, passionate city that booed Santa Claus at an Eagles game in 1968 and hasn't lived it down since. Philadelphians take fierce pride in their city, their sports teams, and their cheesesteaks - and equal pride in telling you exactly what they think.

## The Birthplace

Independence Hall witnessed both the Declaration of Independence (1776) and the Constitution (1787) - the only building in the world where both documents were debated and adopted. George Washington presided over the Constitutional Convention in the Assembly Room; the delegates included Benjamin Franklin, James Madison, and Alexander Hamilton. The Liberty Bell, which rang to summon citizens for the first public reading of the Declaration, cracked sometime in the early 1800s and hasn't been rung since 1846. It rests in a center across from Independence Hall, the most iconic crack in American history. The historical core is compact - Independence Hall, Congress Hall where Washington was inaugurated, Old City's colonial streets - preserved as Independence National Historical Park.

## Franklin's City

Benjamin Franklin arrived in Philadelphia in 1723, a 17-year-old runaway from Boston, and never really left. He became the city's most famous resident: inventor, publisher of the Pennsylvania Gazette and Poor Richard's Almanack, founder of the American Philosophical Society, the University of Pennsylvania, and the nation's first lending library. His image appears everywhere - a statue outside the Second Bank, his grave at Christ Church Burial Ground where visitors still toss pennies ('a penny saved is a penny earned'), the Franklin Institute science museum. Franklin was also the oldest signer of both the Declaration and Constitution, a diplomat who secured French support during the Revolution. Philadelphia without Franklin is unimaginable; he shaped the city's intellectual and civic character.

## The Cheesesteak Question

The Philadelphia cheesesteak was invented (probably) by Pat and Harry Olivieri at their South Philadelphia hot dog stand in 1930. Pat's King of Steaks and Geno's Steaks now face each other across 9th Street, a rivalry that has defined cheesesteak culture for decades. The correct order is debated fiercely: 'Whiz wit' means Cheez Whiz with onions; purists prefer provolone or American. The bread matters - Amoroso rolls from the Amoroso Baking Company, established 1904. The meat is thinly sliced ribeye, chopped on the grill. Whether Pat's or Geno's is better matters less than the ritual of choosing sides. Cheesesteaks became a political test when John Kerry ordered Swiss cheese in 2003, confirming suspicions about his authenticity.

## Rocky's Steps

The steps of the Philadelphia Museum of Art are better known as the Rocky Steps - the 72 stone stairs that Sylvester Stallone sprinted up in the 1976 film, pumping his fists as 'Gonna Fly Now' played. Visitors still run the steps and pose at the top; a bronze statue of Rocky stands nearby. The museum itself, America's third-largest art museum, contains masterpieces from medieval Europe to modern America, but many tourists never go inside. Rocky, the fictional boxer from Kensington who beat Apollo Creed, became Philadelphia's mascot - a working-class underdog who kept getting up. The city identifies with the character even though the films are increasingly dated. The steps remain a pilgrimage site.

## The City of Neighborhoods

Philadelphia International Airport (PHL) lies south of the city, with SEPTA regional rail connecting to Center City. Philadelphia is a city of distinct neighborhoods: Old City's colonial streets, Society Hill's row houses, the Italian Market in South Philly, Rittenhouse Square's elegance, Fishtown's breweries and artists. The SEPTA subway and trolley system serves the core. Reading Terminal Market, in a former railroad terminal, offers food stalls and local produce. The Mutter Museum displays medical oddities including Einstein's brain. From altitude, Philadelphia appears as dense development spreading along the Delaware and Schuylkill Rivers - Center City towers visible, the street grid William Penn designed in 1682 still shaping the core. The city that birthed America remains defiantly, loudly itself.
