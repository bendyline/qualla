# The Cloisters: Medieval Europe, Reassembled in Manhattan

Four medieval French cloisters were dismantled stone by stone, shipped across the Atlantic, and rebuilt on a hilltop in upper Manhattan - where the Unicorn Tapestries that once covered potato sacks now hang in rooms overlooking the Hudson.

**Location:** 40.8649, -73.9319

**Categories:** museum, medieval, art, architecture, landmark, new-york

## Introduction

The seven Unicorn Tapestries that hang in The Cloisters today spent part of their existence covering potatoes. During the French Revolution, the tapestries were looted from the Chateau de Verteuil, and for a period the French army used them to keep potatoes from freezing and to protect fruit trees from frost. Woven in the Southern Netherlands around 1500, depicting the hunt and capture of a mythical creature in thread of silk, wool, silver, and gold, they survived war, neglect, and agricultural service before John D. Rockefeller Jr. bought them in 1922 for about one million dollars. He hung six of them in his Manhattan apartment. Then he built them a home: a museum made of actual medieval cloisters, perched on the highest point in Manhattan, overlooking the river he had also arranged to protect.

## A Sculptor's Obsession

The Cloisters exists because George Grey Barnard could not stop collecting. The American sculptor lived in France in the early 1900s and began acquiring medieval architectural fragments - columns, capitals, archways, entire cloister arcades - from crumbling monasteries and abbeys across southern France. By 1914, he had assembled enough material to open a small museum in a brick building on Fort Washington Avenue in upper Manhattan. His collection included elements from four cloisters: Saint-Michel-de-Cuxa, Saint-Guilhem-le-Desert, Bonnefont-en-Comminges, and Trie-sur-Baise. Each had been dismantled, crated, shipped across the Atlantic, and reassembled. Barnard's museum was eccentric, crowded, and underfunded, but it contained something no other American institution possessed: the actual stones of medieval Europe.

## Rockefeller Builds a Castle

In 1925, John D. Rockefeller Jr. donated funds to the Metropolitan Museum of Art to purchase Barnard's entire collection. Then he went further. He bought 56 acres of land on the northern tip of Manhattan, including the hilltop site of Fort Tryon, and commissioned architect Charles Collens to design a new museum. Collens, who had designed Riverside Church, created a building that resembled a French Romanesque abbey, incorporating the four medieval cloisters into a coherent architectural whole. Frederick Law Olmsted Jr. designed the surrounding park. Rockefeller donated the land and the park to the city, and on May 10, 1938, The Cloisters opened to the public. The museum sits at the highest natural point in Manhattan, 250 feet above the Hudson River, with views across to the New Jersey Palisades.

## The View He Purchased Twice

Rockefeller understood that a medieval museum needed more than medieval stones. It needed a medieval feeling, and that meant controlling the view. Across the Hudson, the Palisades - sheer basalt cliffs rising 300 feet from the water - provided the perfect backdrop: wild, forested, and timeless. To ensure no one built on them, Rockefeller purchased several hundred acres of Palisades clifftop land on the New Jersey side and donated it to the Palisades Interstate Park Commission. The land preservation meant that visitors standing in The Cloisters' garden could look west and see nothing that would contradict the illusion of medieval Europe. No billboards. No apartment towers. Just river, cliff, and forest. Rockefeller had bought the museum, built the museum, and then bought the view from the museum.

## The Hunt of the Unicorn

The seven Unicorn Tapestries are the crown of The Cloisters' collection. Woven in the Southern Netherlands between approximately 1495 and 1505, they depict a hunt: nobles and their dogs pursuing a unicorn through forests and fountains, capturing it, killing it, and finally showing it alive again in a fenced garden. The tapestries are enormous, rich in botanical detail - researchers have identified more than 100 plant species in the backgrounds - and saturated with color that has survived five centuries. Their meaning remains debated. Are they religious allegory? A marriage celebration? A meditation on the cycle of death and resurrection? No one knows who commissioned them or exactly where they were made. Rockefeller donated six to the Metropolitan Museum in 1938 and secured two additional fragments from the La Rochefoucauld family, reuniting the complete set.

## Stones with Memory

Beyond the tapestries, The Cloisters holds a collection of medieval European art that spans from the ninth to the fifteenth century. The Merode Altarpiece, painted around 1422 by Robert Campin, places the Annunciation in a domestic Flemish interior so detailed that art historians study it for its depiction of everyday objects. Limestone tombs, carved ivory crucifixes, stained glass panels, and illuminated manuscript pages fill rooms whose walls are themselves artifacts. Walking through The Cloisters is disorienting in the best sense: the columns you touch were carved by hands that had never imagined the Americas, the garden herbs were planted according to medieval monastic tradition, and the light falling through Romanesque windows has traveled the same angle it struck in twelfth-century Languedoc. The building is not a replica. It is a rearrangement of real things, and the difference matters.

## Flight Context

The Cloisters (40.8649N, 73.9319W) sits atop the highest natural point in Manhattan, in Fort Tryon Park at the northern tip of the island. The museum's Romanesque architecture and surrounding gardens are visible from the air, perched on a bluff 250 feet above the Hudson River. The Palisades cliffs rise directly across the river on the New Jersey side. The George Washington Bridge is 1.5km to the south. Nearby airports: KTEB (Teterboro, 12km NW), KLGA (LaGuardia, 12km E), KEWR (Newark Liberty, 20km SW). Best viewed at 1,500-2,500 feet AGL approaching from the south along the Hudson. Fort Tryon Park's terraced gardens and the Heather Garden provide additional visual context.
