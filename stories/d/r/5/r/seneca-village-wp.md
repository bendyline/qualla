# Seneca Village: The Community Buried Under Central Park

In 1825, a Black shoeshiner named Andrew Williams bought three lots of Manhattan farmland for $125. Within a decade, an entire community of African American property owners stood here - until the city razed it all to build Central Park.

**Location:** 40.7808, -73.9710

**Categories:** history, african-american-history, civil-rights, lost-places, new-york, archaeology

## Introduction

Of the nearly 14,000 Black people documented in New York City in the 1820s, only 91 had the right to vote. The 1821 state constitution required African American men to own at least $250 in property to cast a ballot - a threshold that applied to no one else. Ten of those 91 voters lived in a small hilltop settlement on Manhattan's west side, between what are now 82nd and 89th Streets. They called it Seneca Village. For three decades, it was the most significant community of Black property owners in Manhattan - a place where families built houses, founded churches, buried their dead, and exercised a citizenship the rest of the city denied them. Then New York decided it needed a park.

## Three Lots and a Foothold

The story begins in September 1825, when John and Elizabeth Whitehead subdivided their farm into 200 lots and put them up for sale. Andrew Williams, a 25-year-old African American shoeshiner, bought the first three for $125. Within a week, Epiphany Davis had purchased a dozen more, and the African Methodist Episcopal Zion Church had secured additional parcels. The purchases were deliberate: in a city that restricted Black suffrage to property owners, buying land was not just shelter but citizenship. By the early 1830s, the Whiteheads had sold roughly half their parcels to African Americans. A community took shape on the rocky, wooded hillside. Houses went up. Gardens were planted. Seneca Village had begun.

## Churches, Schools, and Ballot Boxes

At its peak in the early 1850s, Seneca Village was home to roughly 225 residents. Three churches anchored the community: the African Methodist Episcopal Zion Church, the African Union Church, and All Angels' Church, which served a mixed congregation. Two schools educated the village's children. The community was not exclusively Black. By the 1840s, Irish and German immigrants had moved in, and the village had become a genuinely multi-ethnic settlement. But its core remained the African American families who had built it. About half of the village's Black residents owned their homes, a rate almost unheard of in mid-nineteenth-century New York. Property ownership meant stability. It meant roots. And for the men, it meant the vote.

## Eminent Domain and the Wrecking Crews

In 1853, the New York State Legislature authorized the creation of Central Park. The designated land encompassed Seneca Village entirely. Residents fought back. For two years they protested, filed lawsuits, and contested the sale of their land. It made no difference. Mayor Fernando Wood prevailed. In mid-1856, final notices went out. Compensation was offered, but at rates set by the city, not by the families who had invested decades of work. Police arrived to remove those who refused to leave. By the end of 1857, every structure in Seneca Village had been demolished. The churches were torn down. The cemeteries were paved over. Nearly 1,600 people across the entire park footprint were displaced, at least 225 of them from Seneca Village. The city's newspapers had made the erasure easier by calling the settlement a shantytown, but the property records told a different story.

## Buried but Not Gone

For over a century, Seneca Village vanished from public memory. History books described the Central Park site as empty swampland before construction began. It was not until the late 1990s that researchers began piecing the village back together from property records, census data, and church archives. In 2011, archaeologists from Columbia University and the City University of New York excavated the site. Beneath the carefully landscaped lawns, they found an iron tea kettle, a roasting pan, fragments of Chinese export porcelain, a stoneware beer bottle, and a small child's shoe with a leather sole and fabric upper. Each artifact was a fragment of a life that had been deliberately erased and accidentally preserved.

## What Remains in the Landscape

Today, a cluster of interpretive signs marks the Seneca Village site inside Central Park, near the western edge between 82nd and 89th Streets. The terrain is hilly and wooded, much as it was in the 1820s when the Whiteheads sold their farm. Joggers and dog-walkers pass through without knowing they are walking on the foundations of a community that once voted, worshipped, and owned its ground. Central Park is rightly celebrated as one of the great urban spaces in the world. But the land beneath it carries a cost that was paid by specific people with specific names - Andrew Williams, Epiphany Davis, and hundreds of others who built something real on a Manhattan hillside and watched the city take it away.
