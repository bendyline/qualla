# The Cotton Club: The Aristocrat of Harlem

The Prohibition-era nightclub at 142nd and Lenox where Black performers played for white-only audiences, Duke Ellington invented a sound, and CBS radio broadcasts carried Harlem's music into living rooms across America.

**Location:** 40.8224, -73.9387

**Categories:** music, jazz, nightlife, harlem-renaissance, african-american-history, new-york, history

## Introduction

The name tells you everything and nothing. The Cotton Club opened at the corner of 142nd Street and Lenox Avenue in 1923, in the heart of Harlem, and for seventeen years it served as America's most famous nightclub, its most glamorous showcase for Black musical genius, and its most brazen monument to racial segregation. Black performers played on the stage. White patrons filled the tables. The doorman turned Black customers away. Yet from that poisoned arrangement came some of the most transformative music of the twentieth century. Duke Ellington's orchestra, broadcasting live on the CBS radio network, sent the sound of Harlem into millions of American homes for the first time. Cab Calloway followed with a scat-singing style so infectious it made "hi-de-ho" a national catchphrase. The Cotton Club was a place of extraordinary art produced under conditions of extraordinary injustice, and its story is inseparable from both.

## A Gangster's Supper Club

The building at 644 Lenox Avenue began its nightlife career as the Douglas Casino, purchased in 1920 by Jack Johnson, the first Black heavyweight boxing champion, who renamed it Club Deluxe and ran it as a supper club seating 400. In 1923, Owen "Owney" Madden, a bootlegger and gangster freshly released from Sing Sing prison, bought the club from Johnson and rechristened it the Cotton Club. Madden wanted a venue to sell his signature beer, Madden's No. 1, and he wanted it in Harlem, where Prohibition enforcement was lax and the nightlife was booming. The club's interior was decorated in a plantation theme, with log cabins, painted cotton fields, and a backdrop of a southern mansion. The performers were Black; the audience was white; and the cover charge was steep enough to keep the clientele wealthy. Madden ran the operation through the mid-1930s, and the mob's presence guaranteed that the Cotton Club faced little interference from police or competitors.

## The Duke's Jungle Broadcast

On December 4, 1927, Duke Ellington's orchestra began a residency at the Cotton Club that would last until June 30, 1931, and transform American music. The band had developed a style built around muted brass growls and plunger-mute effects that the club's management and Ellington's agent, Irving Mills, marketed as "jungle music," a label designed to feed white fantasies of exotic Africa. The band was rebranded as Duke Ellington's Jungle Band, and the repertoire filled with titles like "Jungle Jamboree," "Jungle Blues," and "Echoes of the Jungle." The label was offensive, but the music was revolutionary. More importantly, the Cotton Club's live radio broadcasts on the CBS and NBC networks carried Ellington's sound nationwide, making him the first Black bandleader to reach a mass American audience through radio. By the time he left the Cotton Club, Ellington was one of the most famous musicians in the country.

## Tall, Tan, and Terrific

The Cotton Club's stage shows were lavish revues featuring singers, dancers, comedians, and chorus lines, produced with a budget and ambition that rivaled Broadway. But behind the spectacle lay rigid racial codes. The chorus girls were required to be at least five feet six inches tall, light-skinned, and under twenty-one years old. The club's slogan, "tall, tan, and terrific," made the colorism explicit. Fletcher Henderson, Jimmie Lunceford, Chick Webb, Louis Armstrong, Count Basie, Fats Waller, Ethel Waters, Bessie Smith, Billie Holiday, and Lena Horne all performed at the Cotton Club, yet none of them could sit at a table and order dinner. Cab Calloway's orchestra replaced Ellington's as the house band in 1931, and Calloway's scat-jive vocal style, with its call-and-response refrains, became the club's new signature sound. In June 1935, the Cotton Club finally opened its doors to Black patrons, a change that came far too late to erase the years of exclusion.

## Last Call on Lenox Avenue

The Harlem race riot of 1935 shook the neighborhood, and the Cotton Club closed its Lenox Avenue location the following year. It reopened in the Broadway theater district at 200 West 48th Street, but the magic of the original setting was gone. The midtown venue attracted a different crowd, and the entertainment shifted toward a more mainstream style. The Cotton Club closed for good in 1940, pressured by rising rents, changing musical tastes, and a federal investigation into tax evasion by Manhattan nightclub owners. The original building at 142nd and Lenox was demolished. No plaque marks the spot. What remains is the music: the recordings, the radio broadcasts, and the legacy of artists who turned an unjust stage into a proving ground for genius. The Cotton Club's contradiction, that it elevated Black art while degrading Black dignity, remains one of the defining tensions of the Harlem Renaissance.
