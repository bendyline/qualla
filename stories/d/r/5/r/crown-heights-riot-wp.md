# Crown Heights Riot

Three days of violence in August 1991 that laid bare the racial and ethnic fault lines running through a Brooklyn neighborhood where Black and Hasidic Jewish communities lived side by side.

**Location:** 40.6671, -73.9314

**Categories:** history, civil-rights, urban-conflict, community-relations

## Introduction

At 8:20 on the evening of August 19, 1991, a station wagon in the three-car motorcade of Rebbe Menachem Mendel Schneerson, leader of the Chabad Lubavitch movement, ran a red light at the intersection of President Street and Utica Avenue in Crown Heights, Brooklyn. The car struck another vehicle, veered onto the sidewalk, and pinned two seven-year-old children of Guyanese immigrants against the iron grate of a first-floor apartment window. Gavin Cato died. His cousin Angela survived with severe injuries. Within hours, Crown Heights was burning.

## A Neighborhood Already on Edge

Crown Heights had long been a place of proximity without understanding. Eastern Parkway served as an informal dividing line: Black residents, many of Caribbean descent, lived primarily to the north, while the Lubavitch Hasidic community occupied blocks to the south. Resentments had accumulated over years. Many Black residents perceived that the Hasidic community received preferential treatment from city government and law enforcement -- more police protection, better allocation of public resources, the Rebbe's motorcade itself traveling with a police escort. The Hasidic community, for its part, lived with the daily anxiety of being a visible religious minority in an increasingly tense urban neighborhood. The crash on President Street did not create these tensions. It detonated them.

## Three Days of Violence

Fury over Gavin Cato's death was compounded by what happened immediately after the crash. A Hatzolah volunteer ambulance, following police instructions, transported the injured driver and other Jews from the scene while city emergency workers were still trying to free the children pinned beneath the car. Rumors spread that the private ambulance crew had refused to help the Cato children because they were not Jewish. Crowds gathered. Bottles and rocks flew. About three hours after the rioting began, a group of roughly twenty young men surrounded Yankel Rosenbaum, a 29-year-old Australian Jewish doctoral student from the University of Melbourne, on a Crown Heights street. They stabbed him multiple times and beat him severely. Rosenbaum identified his attacker in a police lineup before dying that night at the hospital. Over three days, rioters looted stores, attacked Jewish homes, overturned police cars, and fired shots at officers. Property damage reached an estimated one million dollars, with 225 cases of robbery and burglary reported.

## Grief, Justice, and Politics

The legal aftermath was tortured. A grand jury found no cause to indict the driver, Yosef Lifsh, since losing control of a car did not constitute criminal negligence under New York law. Lifsh later moved to Israel, saying his life had been threatened. Lemrick Nelson Jr., the teenager Rosenbaum had identified as his attacker, was acquitted of murder at trial -- a verdict that stunned and enraged much of New York. Nelson was eventually convicted in federal court of civil rights violations and sentenced to ten years. Charles Price, who had incited the mob with the chant "Let's go get a Jew," received eleven and a half years. The riot became a defining issue in the 1993 mayoral election. Opponents of Mayor David Dinkins called the violence a "pogrom" -- a word chosen deliberately to suggest government complicity. An official report criticized both Dinkins and Police Commissioner Lee Brown for poor handling of the crisis, though it found no evidence they had intentionally delayed the police response.

## Toward Mending

The work of repair began almost immediately. Brooklyn Borough President Howard Golden convened leaders from both communities within days, creating the Crown Heights Coalition. Led by Edison O. Jackson, president of Medgar Evers College, and Rabbi Shea Hecht, the coalition operated for ten years as a forum where residents could voice grievances and attempt to resolve differences. One initiative sent paired Jewish and Black leaders into local schools to answer children's questions about each other's cultures. A week after the riot, Hatzolah volunteers helped repair an ambulance used by a Black-owned service. The Brooklyn Children's Museum mounted an exhibit on Black and Jewish contributions to New York. In 1998, the Crown Heights Mediation Center opened as a direct outgrowth of the coalition's work. In 2001, on the tenth anniversary, the families of Gavin Cato and Yankel Rosenbaum met and exchanged mementos at a street fair. The wounds had not closed, but neither community had walked away.
