# Providence: Where Religious Freedom Began and WaterFire Burns

Rhode Island's capital where Roger Williams founded America's first colony with true religious freedom, where Brown University anchors College Hill, where Federal Hill serves little Italy's best, and where bonfires float down the river on summer nights.

**Location:** 41.8240, -71.4128

**Categories:** rhode-island, providence, city, capital, college-town

## Introduction

Roger Williams was banished from Massachusetts in 1636 for his radical idea that government had no business in matters of conscience. He walked through winter snow to Narragansett Bay and founded Providence - a 'shelter for persons distressed for conscience,' the first place in the New World where Baptists, Quakers, Jews, and anyone else could worship freely. That spirit of refuge shaped the city for four centuries. Rhode Island's charter of 1663 guaranteed religious liberty a century before the Bill of Rights existed. The colony prospered through triangle trade, then textiles, then jewelry manufacturing that made Providence the 'Costume Jewelry Capital of the World.' Today the city's renaissance centers on College Hill's academic institutions, Federal Hill's Italian restaurants, and the Waterplace Park basin where WaterFire blazes - nearly 100 bonfires floating on the rivers while crowds gather along the banks, a civic celebration that captures something essential about this compact, quirky, fiercely independent city.

## College Hill

Brown University crowns College Hill, its Georgian brick buildings stepping up from downtown toward Prospect Terrace, where Roger Williams is buried overlooking the city he founded. The Rhode Island School of Design (RISD, pronounced 'RIZ-dee') sits beside Brown, its students' influence visible in galleries and studios throughout the neighborhood. Benefit Street preserves the finest concentration of colonial and Federal architecture in America - the 'Mile of History' lined with 18th-century homes painted in period colors, the First Baptist Church in America where Williams' congregation still meets. The RISD Museum holds everything from ancient Egyptian to contemporary, Japanese prints to French Impressionists. College Hill feels like a New England campus town within the city, students on bicycles, coffeehouses in old houses, and the intellectual energy of institutions that have shaped American thought.

## Federal Hill

The pineapple arch on Atwells Avenue marks entry to Federal Hill, Providence's Italian neighborhood, where red, white, and green lights string across the street and restaurants serve the kind of food that makes New Yorkers drive up from Manhattan. The neighborhood's Italianness is authentic - families who arrived in the early 20th century to work textile mills, whose grandchildren now run restaurants their grandparents founded. DePasquale Square, a pedestrian plaza, captures the atmosphere: espresso at outdoor tables, gelato shops, and the sound of Italian spoken between merchants. The neighborhood resisted gentrification that stripped identity from other cities' Italian quarters; Federal Hill remains a working neighborhood where nonnas still shop at Venda Ravioli and where Sunday dinner is serious business.

## WaterFire

On selected summer and fall evenings, nearly 100 bonfires blaze in iron braziers floating on the Woonasquatucket, Moshassuck, and Providence rivers as they converge in Waterplace Park. WaterFire began in 1994 as a temporary art installation; it became Providence's signature event, drawing hundreds of thousands annually. Volunteers in black boats tend the fires, adding wood as crowds gather on the riverwalks. Music echoes off the water - sometimes classical, sometimes world music, always atmospheric. The smell of wood smoke mingles with food vendors; couples dance; children point at flames reflected in dark water. WaterFire transformed Providence's downtown, encouraging the uncovering of rivers that had been buried beneath parking lots, creating public spaces where the city gathers to celebrate something beautiful and strange.

## Industrial History

Providence's Blackstone River Valley was birthplace of the American Industrial Revolution - Slater Mill in nearby Pawtucket was the first successful water-powered cotton spinning factory in America, built in 1793 by Samuel Slater who memorized British textile machinery designs. The industry spread along the rivers, transforming Rhode Island from maritime commerce to manufacturing. By 1900, Providence was the jewelry capital, producing costume jewelry, silverware, and fashion accessories that supplied the nation. The Jewelry District downtown preserves the factory buildings, now converted to offices and apartments. The What Cheer records building and the Superman Building (the tallest in Rhode Island, vacant for years but finally being renovated) mark the skyline of a city that made things, that still makes things, that carries its industrial heritage into an era of eds and meds.

## Ocean State Capital

T.F. Green Airport (PVD) in Warwick serves Providence with connections to major hubs. I-95 runs through the city; I-195 connects to Cape Cod; Route 146 leads to Worcester and Boston. Amtrak's Northeast Corridor stops at Providence Station, a quick ride to Boston or New York. The city anchors the northern end of Narragansett Bay; beaches and sailing are twenty minutes away. From altitude, Providence appears compact along the confluence of rivers - College Hill rising to the east, the State House dome (fourth-largest self-supporting marble dome in the world) gleaming white, downtown's modest skyline reflecting the scale of America's smallest state. What appears from the air as a minor Northeast city is where religious freedom was born, where American industry began, and where bonfires still float on rivers that once powered mills.
