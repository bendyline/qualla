# Saint Croix

The largest U.S. Virgin Island has been claimed by seven flags, sustained by sugarcane and enslaved labor, scarred by hurricanes and economic collapse, and shaped by waves of migration that made it one of the most culturally layered places in the Caribbean.

## Introduction

The Taino called it Ay Ay -- the river. The Kalinago called it Cibuquiera -- the stony land. Columbus landed here on November 14, 1493, called it Santa Cruz, and was immediately attacked by the Kalinago warriors living at Salt River on the north shore. It was the first recorded armed clash between the Spanish and an indigenous New World population, and Columbus named the battle site Cabo de la Flecha: Cape of the Arrow. That opening encounter set the tone for five centuries of contested ownership. Seven flags have flown over Saint Croix -- Spanish, Dutch, English, French, the Knights of Malta, Danish, and American -- and each change of sovereignty left its mark on the island's 84 square miles, its architecture, its language, and the complicated identities of the people who call themselves Crucian.

## Seven Flags and a Sugar Fortune

The colonial carousel began in earnest in 1625, when Dutch and English settlers landed alongside French refugees from Saint Kitts. The English expelled the others, then were themselves driven out by a Spanish force from Puerto Rico in 1650. The French established a colony of 300 that same year, and from 1651 to 1664, the Knights of Malta governed in the name of Louis XIV -- one of the more improbable chapters in Caribbean colonial history.

Denmark-Norway purchased the island from France in 1733 for 750,000 livres, and under Danish rule St. Croix became a sugar powerhouse. The wealth was staggering, and it was built entirely on the labor of enslaved Africans who planted, harvested, and processed the cane. At its peak, the plantation economy created a landscape of great houses, windmill towers, and regimented fields that still defines the island's interior. The slave trade was abolished in the Danish colonies in 1792, with the ban taking effect in 1802, but slavery itself persisted until the 1848 revolt led by General Buddhoe forced Governor-General Peter von Scholten to proclaim emancipation at Fort Frederik in Frederiksted.

## The Hamilton Connection

Alexander Hamilton arrived on St. Croix as a child in 1765, living with his mother Rachel Faucette and brother at 34 Company Street in Christiansted. Rachel ran a small shop on the ground floor selling food items, while the family occupied the upper story. Within two years, Hamilton's father abandoned the family, and Rachel died. His cousin, aunt, uncle, and grandmother all died shortly after. By the time he was thirteen, Hamilton was essentially alone in the world.

He became an apprentice clerk at the trading firm of Beekman and Cruger, at the intersection of King and King's Cross Streets, where he demonstrated the financial acuity that would later reshape a nation's economy. In 1772, local businessmen pooled funds to send the young man to New York for further education. The island that had taken everything from him -- family, stability, childhood -- also gave him the commercial education and the benefactors that launched one of the most consequential careers in American political history. Christiansted's colonial buildings, the ones Hamilton walked past every day, are now preserved as the Christiansted National Historic Site.

## Crucian Mosaic

What makes St. Croix unusual, even by Caribbean standards, is the sheer density of its cultural layering. Ancestral native Crucians -- those who can trace their lineage to before U.S. citizenship was granted in 1927 -- are largely descended from enslaved Africans and, in many cases, European planters whose surnames they still carry. Puerto Rican migration surged in the 1930s through 1950s, accelerated when the U.S. Navy's expropriation of Vieques during World War II displaced thousands of Viequenses who relocated to St. Croix for its similar size and geography.

In the 1960s and 70s, "down-islanders" arrived by the thousands from St. Kitts, Nevis, Antigua, St. Lucia, and Dominica, drawn by jobs in tourism, alumina production, and oil refining. Arab Palestinians established shops and supermarkets starting in the 1960s. Continental Americans settled primarily on the east end. The result is an island where Virgin Islands Creole English, Puerto Rican Spanish, French Creoles, Arabic, and standard American English coexist, where a single neighborhood might contain descendants of Danish planters, Antiguan laborers, Palestinian merchants, and Puerto Rican sugar workers.

## Boom, Bust, and Storm

The economic arc of modern St. Croix reads like a cautionary tale about dependence on single industries. The Hess Corporation opened one of the world's largest oil refineries on the island in 1966, bringing jobs and revenue that transformed the economy. When the refinery -- by then called Hovensa -- shut down in January 2012, the loss was devastating. A brief restart in January 2021 ended within months due to unsafe emissions. Meanwhile, the Cruzan Rum Distillery, founded in 1760 as Estate Diamond, has endured -- its single-barrel estate rums winning more than 30 Spirit Awards -- and the arrival of Diageo's Captain Morgan distillery brought controversy alongside investment.

Then there were the storms. Hurricane Hugo struck in 1989 with Category 4 winds, requiring federal troops to restore order. Hurricane Maria crossed St. Croix in 2017 with sustained winds exceeding 150 mph and gusts up to 250 mph on the island's western end, damaging or destroying 70 percent of the island's buildings, including schools and its only hospital. Each time, St. Croix rebuilt. The island was designated a National Heritage Area in 2023 -- the first in any U.S. territory -- an acknowledgment that its layered history is itself a resource worth preserving.

## Island of Two Towns

St. Croix's nickname is Twin City, for its two towns separated by roughly ten miles of rolling green hills. Christiansted, the former Danish capital on the northeast coast, clusters around its harbor and the yellow walls of Fort Christiansvaern. Frederiksted, on the western end, maintains its Victorian-era architecture and the original seven-by-seven-street grid, its pace more relaxed, its energy concentrated around cruise ship arrivals and the monthly Sunset Jazz on the beach.

Between the two towns, the landscape tells its own history: old plantation great houses and sugar mill ruins dot the interior, while the north coast drops off suddenly into a deep underwater trench where coral reefs, tropical fish, and migrating sea turtles draw divers from around the world. At Salt River Bay, a national park preserves the site of Columbus's 1493 landing, mangrove forests, and one of the Caribbean's only extant indigenous ball courts. Nearby, in the same bay, one of only seven year-round bioluminescent lagoons in the Caribbean glows with dinoflagellates whenever the water is disturbed. Cars drive on the left side of the road here, but nearly all are left-hand drive -- a quirk that confuses newcomers and delights everyone else.

## Flight Context

Located at 17.73N, 64.75W, Saint Croix is the largest U.S. Virgin Island at approximately 84 square miles, oriented roughly east-west. Clearly visible from cruising altitude, the island features hilly eastern and northern terrain with a plain sloping south. Henry E. Rohlsen International Airport (TISX/STX) is in the island's southwest. Christiansted is on the northeast coast, Frederiksted on the west. Buck Island Reef National Monument is visible as a small island just north of St. Croix. Salt River Bay, on the north shore, is identifiable from altitude. St. Thomas (TIST/STT) is approximately 40 nm to the north-northwest. Point Udall on the eastern tip is the easternmost point of the United States by travel.
