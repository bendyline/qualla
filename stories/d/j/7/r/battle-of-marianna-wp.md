# Battle of Marianna

On September 27, 1864, a Union cavalry raid across the Florida Panhandle collided with Marianna's home guard in a short, bloody street fight that burned a church, wounded a general and freed hundreds of enslaved people.

**Location:** 30.7764, -85.2381

**Categories:** Battles of the American Civil War in Florida, Union victories of the American Civil War, Jackson County, Florida, United States Colored Troops, 1864 in Florida

## Introduction

Alexander Asboth had already lived several lives before he rounded a corner in Marianna, Florida, on the morning of September 27, 1864. He had been a hero of the Hungarian Revolution of 1848, and in New York he had been one of the men who surveyed Central Park. Now a Union brigadier general at the head of a cavalry raid, he led a charge down the town's main road and straight into a volley from men waiting behind fences and a barricade of wagons and carts. A bullet struck him in the face, breaking his cheekbone. His wounds never healed properly, and when he died in 1868, they were the likely cause.

## The Last Prize in the Panhandle

Marianna mattered. It was the home of Florida's ardent secessionist Civil War governor, John Milton, and an important supply depot and recruiting center for Confederate militia and reserves. By late 1864 it was, by one account, the largest town in northwest Florida, besides Tallahassee, still in Confederate hands, and a July raid from St. Andrews had shown how exposed the region was. On September 18, 1864, Asboth rode east from Fort Barrancas, near Federal-occupied Pensacola, with a mounted column of 700 men. They included the 2nd Maine Cavalry, the Union's 1st Florida Cavalry, and detachments of the 82nd and 86th U.S. Colored Infantry. As they moved, the raiders fanned out across the countryside, destroying or confiscating food and supplies.

## A Raid That Hid Its Target

The thinly spread local Confederate cavalry could not report the raiders' size or direction, leaving the regional commander, Colonel Alexander B. Montgomery, guessing. Asboth made sure he kept guessing. On September 23 his troopers captured or scattered a small company of Confederate cavalry at Eucheeana. A detachment then destroyed Douglas' Ferry on the Choctawhatchee River, closing the direct road to Marianna, while the main column swung onto a route northwest of town. On September 26 they skirmished with Captain Alexander Godwin's cavalry around Campbellton, less than twenty miles from Marianna. Campbellton was a crossroads. From there the Federals could strike into Georgia or Alabama, into the richest farmland in northwest Florida, or back toward Marianna. Montgomery tried to picket every road, and in doing so he could not gather his meager reserves to defend any one of them.

## Ambush on the Main Road

Only when the Federals passed the Old Fort crossroads on the morning of the 27th could Montgomery be sure of their target. He called out the home guard. His cavalry contested the crossing of Hopkins' Branch, three miles out, planning to fall back into town along an old bypass, now Kelson Avenue. On the main road, now West Lafayette Street, conscripts, militia and home guard waited behind fences and a crude barricade of wagons and carts, a few feet from St. Luke's Episcopal Church. Then both plans unraveled. Asboth split his force, leading the main body in a headlong charge down the main road while sending another wing around the bypass behind the retreating Confederate cavalry. Montgomery saw the trap closing and tried to pull out, but the men at the barricade would not budge. Asboth's charge met their volley, which wounded him and cut down many of his senior officers. The Union troopers recovered quickly, overwhelmed the Confederate cavalry and swept into town from two directions.

## Fire at St. Luke's

Many Confederate riders forced their way past the flanking force and escaped, but many of the home guard and militia were trapped in town. Montgomery was captured trying to reach the Chipola River bridge. In town, defenders on the south side of the street broke and ran, but those near St. Luke's held out as the detachment of U.S. Colored Troops engaged them. A dismounted bayonet charge finally forced their surrender. Several Confederates kept firing from the church and nearby homes. The church was set ablaze, and some of the defenders were shot down or died in the fires as they were smoked out.

## What the Column Carried Away

Accounts of the losses differ widely. One common tally counts about ten Confederates dead or dying, 16 wounded and 54 captured, and Union losses of eight killed or mortally wounded, 19 wounded and ten captured; a Florida historical marker gives 26 Confederate and about 55 Federal casualties, and a newspaper report of the day put Union losses far higher. Among the Confederate wounded was a dentist, Thaddeus Hentz, son of the famed novelist Caroline Lee Hentz, shot not far from his mother's grave. The fighting had been fiercer than expected, so Asboth abandoned his plan to turn south toward St. Andrews Bay and withdrew toward Choctawhatchee Bay. With the column went hundreds of people liberated from slavery, by one estimate more than 600, along with 17 wagons of captured arms and stores, 200 horses and 400 head of cattle. The raiders brought back 96 prisoners in all, and many of them would die in prison. For northwest Florida, it would be decades before the region recovered from the damage the raid had done.
