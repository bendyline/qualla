# St. Vincent Island (Florida)

Once a private game preserve stocked with zebras and eland, this boat-only barrier island near Apalachicola is now a national wildlife refuge that serves as an island breeding site for endangered red wolves.

**Location:** 29.6610, -85.1552

**Categories:** Gulf Coast barrier islands of Florida, Islands of Franklin County, Florida, National Wildlife Refuges, Wildlife, Archaeological sites

## Introduction

In 1948, zebras came to a barrier island off the Florida Panhandle. Alfred Loomis Jr. and his brother Henry had just bought St. Vincent Island, and along with the zebras they brought in eland, blackbuck, ring-necked pheasants, Asian jungle fowl, bobwhite quail and semi-wild turkeys. Few barrier islands have a stranger history. St. Vincent is the westernmost of four barrier islands along this stretch of coast, lying just offshore in Franklin County near the mouth of the Apalachicola River, and it can be reached only by boat.

## Before It Was an Island

People were here before the sea was. Archaeologists on St. Vincent have found Paleo-Indian projectile points around 13,000 years old, left during the Pleistocene, when lower sea levels put this ground well inland. Pottery fragments about 4,000 years old have turned up on the north shore, though they may have been deposited while the island was still forming. The island's oldest sand ridge is itself only about 3,000 years old. Franciscan friars named St. Vincent around 1633 while visiting the Apalachee people of the region. By the mid-1700s Creek people had moved into the area, and the Seminole, who had broken away from the Creeks, also lived on the island. During the War of 1812, the British used it to unload military supplies as they prepared to invade the United States by way of the Flint River.

## A Private Menagerie

For a century, St. Vincent belonged to private owners with big ideas. George Hatch bought the island in 1868. In 1908, Dr. Ray V. Pierce imported Old World game animals, and by 1920 beef cattle were grazing here for the Apalachicola markets. In 1940 the first oyster lease was granted, the Pierce estate sold its first pine saw timber, and the St. Joe Lumber Company built a temporary bridge to haul the logs out. Then came the Loomis brothers and their exotic stock. The private era ended in 1968, when the Nature Conservancy bought the island for $2.2 million. The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service repaid the Conservancy with money from Duck Stamp sales and established the island as St. Vincent National Wildlife Refuge, which today covers roughly 12,500 acres, including a small neighbor, Pig Island, and a mainland tract.

## Ridges, Swales and Red Wolves

The island is a pattern of sand ridges and swales under live oaks and other hardwoods, laced with tidal marshes and freshwater lakes and streams. Alligators are abundant. Ospreys and bald eagles nest here, and peregrine falcons and wood storks join the shorebirds. Sambar deer, native to Southeast Asia, share the woods with native white-tailed deer, and the island is a haven for loggerhead sea turtles, indigo snakes and gopher tortoises. Since 1990 the island has also been an island propagation site for endangered red wolves, which roam free so their pups gain wild experience; the pups are often moved at around 18 months old to the wild red wolf population in eastern North Carolina. The program is small, and litters do not come every year.

## Oyster-Shell Roads

Visitors arrive by kayak or boat to fish and watch birds along the shore. Inland, more than 90 miles of mapped roads are open to hikers and cyclists, though not all are still in use; only the main route, B Road, is lined with oyster shells to make the sandy ground easier to cross. Four lakes are stocked with largemouth bass and bluegill. Each year, primitive hunts help keep the numbers of sambar deer, white-tailed deer, feral hogs and raccoons in check, with permits awarded by lottery and managed by the Florida Fish and Wildlife Commission; the refuge closes to other visitors during the hunts. There is no visitor center, drinking water or restrooms on the island.

## Flight Context

St. Vincent Island is centered near 29.661 N, 85.155 W, off the Franklin County coast of the Florida Panhandle. It makes a clear visual waypoint: a forested island just offshore, with Cape San Blas about 11 nm to the west, Cape St. George Island next in the barrier chain to the east, and the Apalachicola River mouth and the town of Apalachicola about 10 nm to the northeast. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet, look for the pattern of sand ridges and swales across its interior. As a national wildlife refuge, it warrants a respectful altitude to avoid disturbing wildlife. Apalachicola Regional Airport (KAAF) is about 8 nm northeast; Costin Airport at Port St. Joe (FAA identifier A51, no ICAO code) is roughly 10 nm northwest, and St. George Island Airport (FAA identifier F47) roughly 15 nm east. Watch for coastal haze and summer thunderstorms.
