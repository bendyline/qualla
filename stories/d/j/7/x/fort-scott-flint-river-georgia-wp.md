# Fort Scott (Flint River, Georgia)

Now drowned beneath Lake Seminole, this frontier post in Georgia's southwest corner was the staging ground for the destruction of a refuge for freedom seekers and the start of the First Seminole War.

**Location:** 30.7758, -84.7672

**Categories:** Forts in Georgia (U.S. state), First Seminole War, Native American history of Georgia (U.S. state), African-American history, Decatur County, Georgia, Military history

## Introduction

No one can stand inside Fort Scott today. Its site lies beneath Lake Seminole, the man-made reservoir in the southwest corner of Georgia. Two centuries ago this was the edge of the United States, where the Flint River joins the Chattahoochee to form the Apalachicola, and across the line lay Spanish Florida. The fort built here in 1816 was a fever-ridden outpost that saw only a few years of service. It still shaped the history of the region: its supply line became the pretext for destroying a fortified refuge for people escaping slavery, and it served as the foundation of the First Seminole War.

## A Line Drawn on Rivers

The case for a fort had been made during the War of 1812, when the British noticed how undefended this border was. In 1814 they built two forts on the Apalachicola River, into which the Flint flows: a strong one at Prospect Bluff and a smaller post, Nicolls' Outpost, at the river junction. Both stood in Spanish Florida, but Spain had neither the resources nor the inclination to act in so remote a place. The American answer came in 1816, when Fort Scott rose on the west bank of the Flint. Supplying it by land would have meant hauling goods through wilderness, a route never seriously considered, so the only practical supply line was the river, up the Apalachicola.

## The Fort at Prospect Bluff

That river route ran straight past Prospect Bluff. When the British withdrew in 1815, they deliberately left the fort, with all its weapons and ordnance, to the Black troops of the Corps of Colonial Marines who chose to stay. Around it grew a sizeable community of people who had escaped slavery, along with Red Stick Creeks, some of them forced out of Georgia and the Mississippi Territory. The U.S. Army called the place the "Negro Fort," the language of its era. It was known as a safe destination for freedom seekers from as far away as Virginia and Tennessee, and Georgia plantation owners, who feared it as a threat to their hold on the people they enslaved, wanted it gone. The Americans judged the fort, well sited and well built, very hard to take by land. When American supply boats bound for Fort Scott passed the fort and were fired upon, the United States had its justification. U.S. Navy gunboats destroyed the fort on July 27, 1816, when a heated cannonball set off its powder magazine, killing many of the roughly 330 people inside; estimates of the dead range from about forty to roughly 270. It is sometimes called the opening battle of the Seminole Wars.

## Burned, Rebuilt and Renamed

With the fort at Prospect Bluff gone, General Edmund Gaines ordered Fort Scott abandoned in November 1816, and concealed that decision from civilian leaders. Red Stick warriors soon burned it to the ground. In the spring or early summer of 1817 the Army sent Captain Samuel Donoho to rebuild it, joined in July by Brevet Major David E. Twiggs and his company of the 7th U.S. Infantry. The Red Stick chief Neamathla warned the Americans not to cross the Flint; the land to the south, he said, belonged to the Mikasuki Creeks, who had never agreed to the Treaty of Fort Jackson ceding it. After Secretary of War John C. Calhoun ordered Gaines to remove Neamathla and his followers, Twiggs set out on November 20, 1817, and reached Neamathla's town, Fowltown, the next morning. The Americans failed to capture the chief, but his Mikasuki people abandoned the land. Another clash followed on November 23, and a few days later Red Sticks attacked a boat carrying Lieutenant Richard W. Scott up the Apalachicola toward the post. Only seven of the roughly 50 soldiers, women and children aboard survived. The post, until then called Camp Crawford for Secretary of War William H. Crawford, became Fort Scott, in most accounts named in the lieutenant's honor, though he never visited it; a Georgia historical marker dates the Fort Scott name to 1816, before his death.

## Jackson's Staging Ground

News of the attack spread widely in the American press and helped prompt President James Monroe to order General Andrew Jackson to "punish" the Red Sticks whether they were in U.S. or Spanish territory. On December 15, 1817, at the Battle of Ocheesee, Red Stick warriors, in some accounts led by Josiah Francis, known as Francis the Prophet and an ally of Neamathla, fired from both riverbanks at a supply convoy headed for Fort Gaines and Fort Scott, pinning down its sailors. The siege held until mid-March 1818, when Jackson marched south from Fort Gaines to Fort Scott and then on to the ruins at Prospect Bluff, where he ordered Fort Gadsden built. Fort Scott had become, symbolically and practically, the foundation of the First Seminole War.

## The Deadliest Post

Fort Scott stayed garrisoned until it was abandoned in September 1821, after Spain turned Florida over to the United States, and its final years were grim. Beginning in 1820, its soldiers were overwhelmed by what is now recognized as malaria, yellow fever and the dysentery that followed; that year, by one account, 769 of the 780 soldiers at the post were ill. A historical marker at the nearby Camp Recovery, a medical camp set up in September 1820 on a ridge about three miles away, says soldiers considered the fort the deadliest military assignment in the country. Nobody yet understood that mosquitoes spread these diseases, and sending sick men to Fort Gadsden only carried the illness further. Today the water of Lake Seminole covers the place where they served.

## Flight Context

The Fort Scott site lies at about 30.776 N, 84.767 W, submerged beneath Lake Seminole in the southwest corner of Georgia, where the Flint and Chattahoochee rivers meet to form the Apalachicola. Nothing of the fort is visible; from 2,500 to 3,500 feet, use the lake's broad Flint River arm and the dam at its southern end as your references, with the Florida state line just to the south. Decatur County Industrial Air Park at Bainbridge (KBGE) is about 13.5 nm north-northeast. Donalsonville Municipal Airport (FAA identifier 17J, no ICAO code) is about 15 nm north-northwest, and Quincy Municipal Airport in Florida (FAA identifier 2J9) about 15 nm southeast. Expect summer thunderstorms and possible haze over the lake.
