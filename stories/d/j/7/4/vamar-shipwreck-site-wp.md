# Vamar Shipwreck Site

Off Mexico Beach, Florida, lies a ship that served the Royal Navy and carried Richard Byrd's first Antarctic expedition before sinking under suspicious circumstances in 1942 with a cargo of lumber bound for Cuba.

**Location:** 29.8989, -85.4633

**Categories:** Parks in Bay County, Florida, National Register of Historic Places in Bay County, Florida, Shipwrecks on the National Register of Historic Places in Florida, Florida Underwater Archaeological Preserves, 1919 ships, Maritime incidents in March 1942

## Introduction

Richard Byrd's sailors had a nickname for her: the "Evermore Rolling." On the rough seas of the Antarctic run, the steamer Byrd had named the Eleanor Bolling, after his mother, pitched and swayed enough to earn the pun. Fourteen years and several names later, she went down in just 25 feet of water off Mexico Beach, Florida, under circumstances that some locals found suspicious. Today the Vamar is one of Florida's Underwater Archaeological Preserves, a shallow wreck with an unusually long and wandering life story.

## A Gunboat with Many Names

The ship was built in 1919 by the Smiths Dock Company in England as a patrol gunboat for the Royal Navy, and she entered the world as HMS Kilmarnock. Her naval career was brief. From 1920 she sailed as the S.S. Kilmarnock, and by 1926 she was on the Canadian register. In 1928 she was sold to a private firm and renamed Chelsea. That might have been the last name anyone remembered her by, if not for an American naval officer with an ambitious plan for the bottom of the world.

## South with Byrd

In July 1928, Rear Admiral Richard E. Byrd acquired Chelsea as one of two support vessels for his Antarctic expedition and renamed her Eleanor Bolling, in honor of his mother, Eleanor Bolling Byrd. Her hold had two large cargo areas with a combined capacity of 800 tons, space that was used for carrying airplanes. The ship made several voyages between Antarctica and New Zealand before the expedition was completed in 1930, and the crew, rolling through heavy seas, gave her the nickname "Evermore Rolling." After the expedition, Byrd sold her to an Arctic sealing company for $15,000. A ship that had just served in the far south was headed for work in the far north.

## The Last Voyage from Port St. Joe

In 1933 the Vamar Shipping Company bought the ship and gave her the name she still carries. By 1942 she belonged to the Bolivar-Atlantic Navigation Company under Panamanian registry and worked as a tramp freighter. On March 21, 1942, Vamar left Port St. Joe, Florida, bound for Cuba with a load of lumber and a crew of 18 Yugoslavian, Cuban and Spanish sailors. She was overloaded, and the lumber stowed on her deck seemed to make her top-heavy; the pilot reported as much. As a harbor pilot guided her through the channel toward the Gulf, she listed to port and went down by the stern, coming to rest in 25 feet of water off Mexico Beach; some accounts instead say she ran aground and capsized in heavy seas, and the exact cause was never determined. Some local people suspected sabotage, believing the ship had been sunk deliberately to block the channel into Port St. Joe. A Coast Guard investigation could not verify the suspicions, but in wartime the question lingered.

## Twenty-Five Feet Down

The Vamar now lies about 3.7 nautical miles off Mexico Beach, where her wreckage rises several feet off the sea floor in 25 feet of water. The ship's remains have become a home for flounder, grouper, anemones and many other marine creatures, turning the site of a freighter's loss into a place full of life. In 2004 the wreck was dedicated as the ninth Florida Underwater Archaeological Preserve, and it was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 2006. Divers who swim over her are visiting the scattered remains of a single ship that served the Royal Navy, carried airplanes toward Antarctica, went to work for a sealing company and hauled lumber, and whose final sinking still carries an unanswered question.

## Flight Context

The Vamar wreck lies at approximately 29.899 N, 85.463 W in the Gulf of Mexico, about 3.7 nm off Mexico Beach, Florida. The wreck itself is underwater and not visible from altitude; at 1,500-2,500 ft, follow the beach at Mexico Beach and look east-southeast toward St. Joseph Bay and Port St. Joe, the port Vamar was leaving on her last voyage. Tyndall Air Force Base (KPAM) is only about 12 nm north-northwest and its military airspace dominates the area, so check restrictions and contact ATC. Apalachicola Regional Airport (KAAF) lies about 25 nm east-southeast, and Northwest Florida Beaches International (KECP) about 32 nm north-northwest. Summer and early-autumn afternoons often bring thunderstorms over the Gulf.
