# Tate's Hell State Forest

Named for a farmer who, legend says, staggered out of its swamps declaring he had come from Hell, this vast Panhandle forest is slowly being coaxed back to wetland after decades of ditching and pine plantations.

**Location:** 29.9204, -84.8248

**Categories:** Florida state forests, Protected areas of Franklin County, Florida, Protected areas of Liberty County, Florida, Wetlands, Folklore, Ecological restoration

## Introduction

"My name is Cebe Tate, and I just came from Hell." According to local legend, those were the last words of a homesteader who walked out of this swamp in the mid-1870s. Tate had gone in with a shotgun and his hunting dogs, chasing a panther that had been attacking his livestock. The story comes in several versions, but the most common one has him lost for seven days and seven nights, bitten by snakes and drinking the murky swamp water to ease his thirst. When he finally reached a clearing near Carrabelle, he lived only long enough to tell a passerby where he had been.

## The Country Tate Crossed

The land that gave the legend its setting now covers more than 200,000 acres of Franklin and Liberty counties, reaching toward the Gulf near Carrabelle off US 98 and onto St. James Island. At one time it held at least a dozen major habitats: wet flatwoods and wet prairie, seepage slopes and baygall, floodplain forest and floodplain swamp, basin swamp, upland hardwoods, sandhill, pine ridges, scrub, and dense thickets of titi swamp. Some of the forest's most remarkable trees are also its smallest. Stands of dwarf cypress include trees estimated at well over a century old, and by some accounts centuries old, that stand no taller than about 15 feet. An observation tower on the Ralph G. Kendrick Boardwalk looks out over one of the places where they grow most thickly.

## Where the Fresh Water Begins

Tate's Hell matters far beyond its own boundaries. It is an important hydrologic area, taking in a section of the New River, and its watershed sends fresh water into Apalachicola Bay, the Carrabelle River and the Ochlockonee River. What happens to the water on these flatwoods and swamps, in other words, shows up downstream in the bay. The wildlife reflects that watery richness. Gopher tortoises and red-cockaded woodpeckers, both endangered or threatened, live in the forest, along with alligators and wild cats. Rare plants grow here too, among them thick-leaved water-willow, white birds-in-a-nest, Florida bear grass, Chapman's butterwort and small-flowered meadow beauty.

## Eight Hundred Miles of Ditches

For decades before the state began buying it, the land was run for intensive commercial forestry. Its private owners cut more than 800 miles of roads and ditches, planted most of the area in pine monoculture and drained what had been wetlands. Habitat and wildlife were lost on a large scale. Downstream, Apalachicola Bay lost the steady flow of fresh water the wetlands had once provided, a blow that compounded the reduced flow coming down the Apalachicola River itself. That river's water is drawn upstream for uses such as Atlanta's drinking water and Georgia agriculture, and which user should come first has been the subject of a long-running feud among three southern states.

## Letting the Swamp Come Back

The state began buying the land in 1994, and the Florida Department of Agriculture and Consumer Services, which manages the forest, set out to undo some of the damage. Twelve restoration projects were planned to bring back natural drainage and natural communities, removing some road beds, planting others, restoring natural water flow and changing what grows on the land. By 2007, eight of those projects were complete, at a cost of $1.446 million, across 39,070 acres. The forest will need far longer than that to return to a more natural state, and the department and the Northwest Florida Water Management District continue to study and plan the work ahead. The place Cebe Tate called Hell is, slowly, being allowed to be a swamp again.

## Flight Context

Tate's Hell State Forest spreads across a large area centered near 29.920 N, 84.825 W, inland from Carrabelle on the Florida Panhandle's Gulf coast. From 2,500 to 4,000 feet the forest reads as a broad, flat expanse of pine plantation, swamp and dark titi thickets cut by straight old ditches and forest roads, with the New River winding through and the Gulf and Apalachicola Bay to the south. Carrabelle-Thompson Airport (FAA identifier X13, no ICAO code) is about 8 nm southeast, and Apalachicola Regional Airport (KAAF) about 16 nm southwest. Landmarks are few over the interior, so keep the coastline and US 98 in view. Expect summer thunderstorms and coastal haze.
