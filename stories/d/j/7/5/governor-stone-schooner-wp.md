# Governor Stone (schooner)

Built in Pascagoula in 1877, the little schooner Governor Stone has survived storm after storm that should have ended her and is described as the last two-masted coasting cargo schooner built on the Gulf Coast.

**Location:** 30.1675, -85.7026

**Categories:** Schooners of the United States, National Historic Landmarks in Florida, Ships on the National Register of Historic Places in Florida, Maritime history, 1877 ships, Panama City, Florida

## Introduction

Again and again the weather has tried to finish Governor Stone. A hurricane in 1906 washed her ashore in Alabama. A storm sank her in 1939, and another drove her into a bridge piling in 1956. In October 2018, Hurricane Michael rolled her over at her own dock. Each time, someone thought a 39-foot wooden workboat from 1877 was worth saving. That stubbornness explains why she is still here: by the usual account she is the only surviving two-masted coasting cargo schooner built on the Gulf Coast of the United States, and one of just five such American-built ships left anywhere.

## Pine, Cypress and Juniper

She was built in Pascagoula, Mississippi, for Charles Greiner, a merchant who ran a chandlery in town, and named for John Marshall Stone, then Governor of Mississippi. Her wooden hull is 39 feet long with a beam of 12.6 feet and a hold only 3.3 feet deep. The keel is pine, the frame cypress, and the decking and finished surfaces pine and juniper. Two pine masts rise from the deck, one 38.8 feet long and the other 39.8 feet, which carries a topmast that brings its total height to 52 feet. Aft sits a poop deck with a small cabin; the rest of the deck is divided into hatches that open into the hold. Nothing about her was meant to be remarkable. Her design is considered typical of Gulf schooners of the late nineteenth century, which is exactly what makes her rare now.

## Chandlery Boat, Buyboat, Queen of the Fleet

Greiner used her to carry supplies and materials out to deep-water ships waiting beyond the harbor. Later owners turned her into a buyboat, buying the oyster catch from working fishermen and bringing it ashore. Renamed Queen of the Fleet, she went into the tourist trade, taking visitors on excursions along the Mississippi coast. World War II brought another career change, when she became a training vessel. A string of owners and names followed in the postwar years, until she was given a careful restoration beginning in the 1960s.

## A Landmark That Floats

On December 4, 1991, Governor Stone was added to the National Register of Historic Places, and she is also a National Historic Landmark. In 2007 she was towed to a marina in Fort Walton Beach, and by 2014 she had moved to her home port at the St. Andrews Marina, in the historic St. Andrews district of Panama City, Florida, where the nonprofit Friends of the Governor Stone cared for her and took passengers sailing on St. Andrews Bay. Then came Michael. After the 2018 storm capsized her at the dock, the Friends raised money to salvage and repair her, and she was raised and stored ashore. Rebuilding began in May 2022 at the former St. Andrews School property in Panama City, supported by a $1.5 million FEMA grant that reimburses the group's spending rather than paying it up front, and the Friends' website has targeted a relaunch in late 2027. As of February 2026, however, work was at a temporary standstill: Bay County, which now owns the school property, has said it does not plan to continue the lease, and the Friends are working with the county, FEMA and the state on a long-term solution, which may mean moving the schooner.

## Flight Context

The story's map point is the St. Andrews Marina in Panama City, at about 30.168 N, 85.703 W on St. Andrews Bay, the schooner's home port since about 2014; she was berthed at Fort Walton Beach from 2007 and is now under rebuild ashore at the nearby former St. Andrews School property, not afloat. A 39-foot schooner is too small to pick out from altitude; from 1,500 feet, look instead for the marina basin along the bay shore in the St. Andrews district. Northwest Florida Beaches International Airport (KECP) is about 12 nm north-northwest of the marina, and Tyndall Air Force Base (KPAM) lies about 9 nm southeast, so respect military airspace. Expect sea breezes and summer thunderstorms along the Gulf Coast.
