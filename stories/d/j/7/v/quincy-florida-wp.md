# Quincy, Florida

Quincy grew rich on shade-grown cigar tobacco and early Coca-Cola stock, but the North Florida county seat also carries a history of racial terror and of the Black residents who kept organizing in spite of it.

**Location:** 30.5833, -84.5833

**Categories:** County seats in Florida, Cities in Gadsden County, Florida, Tobacco, Civil rights movement, Lynching in the United States, 1828 establishments in Florida Territory

## Introduction

For generations, Quincy's fortune grew in the shade. Gadsden County farmers learned to raise cigar-wrapper tobacco beneath cloth, because leaf that came up light in color and silky in texture fetched the highest prices. Later, a local banker steered his neighbors toward a second fortune in a soft drink. Founded in 1828 and named for John Quincy Adams, this county seat in the rolling hills of North Florida, 25 miles northwest of Tallahassee, holds both kinds of history: the prosperity people liked to talk about, and the terror that newspapers of the day too often let drop.

## Before the Guns

By 1840 the young town had two schools, the Quincy Male Academy and the Quincy Female Academy, and from 1839 to 1841 it had its own newspaper, the Quincy Sentinel, before the paper moved to Tallahassee. As secession loomed, Quincy hosted the state convention of the Constitutional Union Party on June 27, 1860. The party was one of the most important anti-secession organizations in Florida, urging "pacific, rational, and judicial methods" to settle the South's grievances. After secession, William Tennent Stockton, at one time Quincy's mayor, raised a cavalry company called the Gadsden Dragoons. Florida became a major supplier of beef, salt and other goods to the Confederacy, and the five commissary districts that moved those supplies answered to Major Pleasant W. White of Quincy.

## Silk Grown Under Cheesecloth

The tobacco story begins with two seeds. In 1828, Governor William P. Duval introduced Cuban tobacco to the Florida Territory. A year later John Smith arrived by covered wagon with his family and four related families; since the community already had a John Smith, he became John "Virginia" Smith. He planted Virginia seed meant for chewing and pipes beside Cuban seed, and over several years the two blended. In Florida soil the leaf grew thinner and lighter, and from the hybrid stalks came a new plant called "Florida Wrapper." After the Civil War, growers found that the lightest, silkiest leaf sold best and began shading their fields: first heavy wooden slats, then slats draped in cheesecloth, then ribbed cheesecloth, and finally, in 1950, a treated yellow cloth. New York companies bought land and packing houses multiplied, until the industry faded around 1970. The last crop of shade-grown wrapper was grown in 1977.

## Forty Dollars a Share

Quincy's other windfall came in a bottle. Local banker Mark Welch "Mr. Pat" Munroe, father of 18 children by two wives, and W. C. Bradley were among the stockholders of three banks that released 500,000 shares of new Coca-Cola common stock. They urged widows and farmers to buy in at $40 a share, and several did. The stock eventually split and made as many as 67 investors and Gadsden County residents rich, and Quincy was rumored to be home to many millionaires. By one estimate, a single share bought for $40 in 1919 would be worth millions of dollars today with all dividends reinvested. After tobacco, the local economy leaned on tomatoes, mushrooms and eggs, until the mushroom factory closed and the tomato farm laid off many workers in 2008.

## What the Newspapers Let Drop

Black residents who sought their rights in Quincy faced deadly violence. The Ku Klux Klan attacked and burned a local Knights of Pythias lodge and murdered members, reportedly because they were required to pay poll taxes and register to vote. In 1929, Will Larkins, indicted after being accused of attacking a white 13-year-old schoolgirl, was taken from the sheriff's custody by a mob of masked men, reportedly about 40, in some accounts while being moved toward the Duval County jail for his supposed safekeeping. A catalog summary of a November 1929 Tampa Times report says he was hanged with wire, shot, dragged through the streets of Quincy and burned; by one account his body was dragged behind a car. The governor promised an inquiry, yet apart from the NAACP's lynching lists, the case is said to have all but vanished from the newspapers of the time. In May 1941, A. C. Williams, a 20-year-old Black man, was arrested, and a white man then accused him of robbery and of sexually assaulting his 12-year-old daughter. Masked men took Williams from the Quincy jail and shot him repeatedly, leaving him for dead beside a pecan tree where the sheriff later found a plow line and blood. He was being sent without an escort toward the hospital for Black patients in Tallahassee, about 30 miles away, when masked, armed men stopped the ambulance and seized him again. His body was found the next morning on a bridge about five miles north of Quincy. No one was charged, and the national press called him the man who was lynched twice.

## Standing Up, Staying On

Quincy's Black residents kept organizing anyway. Residents repeatedly tried to form political organizations, vote and protest brutal labor conditions, and were suppressed by white violence. In the 1930s A. I. Dixie, a day laborer on a tobacco plantation, tried to organize workers, and when that effort collapsed in 1936 he took up arms in self-defense to keep white overseers from flogging him. In 1964 he hosted student activists from the Congress of Racial Equality, his daughter Linda led lunch-counter sit-ins, and Jewell Dixie became the first African American in the twentieth century to run for Gadsden County sheriff. Quincy was named an All-America City in 1996. At the 2020 census, 7,970 people lived here, and the historic district holds the Gadsden Arts Center, set in a renovated 1912 hardware store, and the Leaf Theater, a cinema said to be haunted. Jerrie Mock, who in 1964 became the first woman to fly solo around the world, died at her home in Quincy in 2014.

## Flight Context

Coordinates: 30.5833°N, 84.5833°W, in central Gadsden County in the rolling hills of North Florida, about 17 nm (19 miles) northwest of Tallahassee in a straight line. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet, U.S. 90 (Jefferson Street) runs through downtown, and Interstate 10 passes about three miles south of the town center. Nearby airports: Quincy Municipal Airport (FAA identifier 2J9, no ICAO code), about two miles northeast of downtown, and Tallahassee International (KTLH) to the southeast. Expect humid summers with afternoon thunderstorms.
