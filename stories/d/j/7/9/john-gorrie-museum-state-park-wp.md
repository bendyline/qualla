# John Gorrie Museum State Park

A small state museum in Apalachicola honors John Gorrie, the town doctor who set out to cool the rooms of fever patients, made ice by machine and received, in 1851, a patent usually described as the first in the U.S. for mechanical refrigeration.

**Location:** 29.7246, -84.9852

## Introduction

Long before air conditioning made a Gulf Coast summer bearable, a doctor in Apalachicola was trying to cool the rooms of his feverish patients. John Gorrie's efforts led him to build a machine that made ice, and in 1851 he received a patent usually described as the first in the U.S. for mechanical refrigeration. He never profited from it. Today a small Florida state park museum, a block off U.S. 98 at 46 Sixth Street, tells the story of the town and the man who was a pioneer in developing air conditioning.

## A Doctor Comes to the Gulf

Gorrie's journey to this port town began far away. He was born on October 3, most sources say in 1803 (the Architect of the Capitol gives 1802). Accounts differ on where: the Architect of the Capitol says the Island of Nevis in the West Indies, while other biographies say most accounts point to Charleston, South Carolina. In 1833 he moved to Apalachicola, then a port city on Florida's Gulf coast, and threw himself into its civic life. Over the years he served as a council member and postmaster, as president of the Bank of Pensacola's Apalachicola branch, as secretary of his Masonic lodge and as one of the founding vestrymen of Trinity Episcopal Church. He was, in short, a pillar of the community, and he was also its doctor.

## Cooling the Sickroom

Gorrie's medical research focused on tropical diseases, particularly yellow fever. His prescription went beyond medicine: he urged the draining of swamps and the cooling of sickrooms. That second idea pushed the doctor toward engineering. How do you cool a room on the Gulf Coast in the heat of summer? Gorrie's answer was to make cold by machine. He first produced ice mechanically in 1844, and on May 6, 1851, he was granted Patent No. 8080, titled "Improved process for the artificial production of ice," which is usually described as the first U.S. patent for mechanical refrigeration. A physician's concern for his patients had produced one of the ancestors of the modern refrigerator and air conditioner.

## Recognition That Came Too Late

The invention did not make Gorrie's fortune. His business venture failed when his partner died, and he was humiliated by criticism of his idea. Financially ruined, he died in seclusion on June 29, 1855, only four years after receiving his patent. The world came around eventually. In 1914, the state of Florida gave a statue of Gorrie by the sculptor C. Adrian Pillars to the National Statuary Hall Collection, a national honor for the Apalachicola doctor. The man who had been criticized for trying to manufacture cold had become one of the figures his state chose to remember.

## A Museum a Block off 98

Florida's tribute closer to home is the John Gorrie Museum State Park, established in the 1950s. It is a modest place with a focused mission, set on Sixth Street a block from U.S. 98 in the heart of Apalachicola. Its exhibits cover the history of Apalachicola alongside the life and inventions of John Gorrie, linking the town's past as a Gulf port to the doctor who tried to make its summers survivable for the sick. On a humid Gulf Coast afternoon, the connection is hard to miss: the cooled air of every modern building descends, in part, from an idea pursued here.

## Flight Context

The John Gorrie Museum sits at approximately 29.725 N, 84.985 W in downtown Apalachicola, Florida, a block off U.S. 98 near the mouth of the Apalachicola River on Apalachicola Bay. From 1,500-2,500 ft MSL, the small town grid on the bay shore is easy to spot, with the river mouth and the long barrier island of St. George Island offshore to the south. Apalachicola Regional Airport (KAAF) is only about 2 nm west and makes an easy stop for exploring the town on foot. Tallahassee International (KTLH) lies about 52 nm northeast, and Tyndall Air Force Base (KPAM) about 37 nm northwest is military airspace to avoid. Expect hot, humid summers with frequent afternoon thunderstorms, the very climate that set Gorrie to work.
