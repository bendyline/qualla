# Gulf Specimen Marine Laboratory

From a 1963 specimen business mentored by John Steinbeck, the Gulf Specimen Marine Laboratory in Panacea grew into a nonprofit aquarium, sea turtle hospital and supplier of marine life to scientists around the world.

**Location:** 30.0232, -84.3873

**Categories:** Marine biological stations, Public aquariums, Sea turtle conservation, Wakulla County, Florida, Natural history of Florida, 1980 establishments in Florida

## Introduction

An estimated 1,500 people came to Bald Point State Park on June 22, 2013, to watch a sea turtle go home. Allie, a roughly 230-pound, 50-year-old loggerhead, had been found sick and weak by a local fisherman at Alligator Point in May 2012, and thousands of people had followed her recovery online. The place that cared for her all that time was the Gulf Specimen Marine Laboratory in Panacea, Florida, an independent nonprofit whose touch tanks, turtle hospital and specimen shipments have connected the creatures of the northern Gulf with schoolchildren, cancer researchers and even NASA.

## A Specimen Business With a Famous Mentor

The lab began as a business. In 1963, the writer and naturalist Jack Rudloe founded the Gulf Specimen Marine Company to supply marine specimens, primarily to scientists, and John Steinbeck became an important mentor to the young company. The present site in Panacea was purchased in 1964. In 1971 Rudloe married the marine biologist Anne Eidemiller, and in 1980 Anne Rudloe founded the Panacea Institute of Marine Science. That same year the enterprise became a registered nonprofit, Gulf Specimen Marine Laboratories, Inc., with Jack and Anne Rudloe as cofounders. In 2010 their son Cypress took over as general manager. The lab's famous dock became the subject of Jack Rudloe's book The Living Dock.

## From the Gulf to the Lab Bench

Gulf Specimen ships marine creatures to scientists worldwide, including some that were the first specimens known to science, such as Chiropsella rudloei. It has supplied organisms that NASA sent into space and provides an ongoing source of specimens for The Photo Ark, the National Geographic Society-supported project. In 1968 it provided the first specimens of the bryozoan Bugula neritina that the National Cancer Institute used to develop the bryostatin family of compounds, later investigated as potential treatments for cancer, HIV, Alzheimer's disease and stroke. Authors tied to the lab and its predecessor institute have published more than 40 peer-reviewed articles on subjects ranging from horseshoe crabs and electric rays to algae fuel and astrobiology. The lab also took part in early efforts to build the now-successful jellyfish export industry on the East Coast, and it helped preserve 35,000 acres of wetlands in the Florida Panhandle and Big Bend.

## A Hospital in a Renovated Kitchen

Staff began tagging sea turtles under the guidance of Archie Carr, and the lab became one of the country's oldest sea turtle rescue programs; its own site calls it the third oldest in the United States. It has been described as one of 23 state-permitted captive sea turtle facilities and the northernmost in the state, handling everything from cold-stunned turtles and fish-hook removals to long-term rehabilitation. After joining the Responsible Pier Initiative in 2015, the lab went from rescuing one to three Kemp's ridley turtles a year to 17 that year. In March 2016 it opened a turtle hospital in a renovated kitchen, reducing transport stress on the animals, and the lab's veterinarian provides sea turtle care pro bono. Twice, in 2010 and again in 2018, the lab took part in rescuing many cold-stunned turtles. In May 2020, fishermen brought in a large female Kemp's ridley, a critically endangered species, caught in their net. Facebook followers named her Myrtle, and after treatment for a neck injury she went back to the wild.

## Hands in the Water

The public aquarium opened in 1990 and now draws more than 20,000 visitors a year. Its touch tanks invite people to handle marine creatures "to overcome fear and increase curiosity and thereby learning," and guided marsh walks and visits to the dock carry the lesson outdoors. Anne Rudloe's influence runs through all of it. In 2021 the Northwest Florida Water Management District posthumously named her its 2020 River and Bay Champion, and the board's chairman, George Roberts, said her biggest impact "may be found in the thousands and thousands of children who learned about marine biology from touring the Marine Lab she founded in Panacea." A $485,000 Volunteer Florida grant in July 2020 was awarded to build a 2,000-square-foot classroom complex, planned as the Anne Rudloe Memorial Education Center, on donated lots beside the original site.

## Weathering the Storms

Life on the edge of the Gulf has been hard on the lab. Hurricane Hermine made landfall nearby on September 2, 2016, damaging the building and badly battering the Living Dock, which was rebuilt with help from a social media campaign. On October 10, 2018, storm surge from Hurricane Michael severely damaged the dock again and flooded the main floor and grounds, yet the lab reopened the following week. Total damage was estimated at $200,000. Then came the COVID-19 pandemic, when revenues fell to 10 percent of normal and the staff kept going with virtual visits and Facebook tours until the doors could reopen. Each time, the lab found its way back.
