# Wes Skiles Peacock Springs State Park

Beneath a quiet patch of Suwannee County woods lies one of the largest underwater cave systems in the continental United States, a flooded limestone maze that trains cave divers from around the world and now carries the name of one of its greatest explorers.

**Location:** 30.1269, -83.1361

**Categories:** State parks of Florida, Caves of Florida, Springs of Florida, Cave diving, Suwannee River, Parks in Suwannee County, Florida

## Introduction

Walk down the stairs at Peacock I and you are standing at the doorway to one of the largest underwater caves in the continental United States. Above the waterline, this is a modest Florida state park off State Road 51, two miles east of the community of Luraville, with picnic tables, a nature trail and a pair of springs that feed the Suwannee River. Below it, more than seven miles of explored passage wind through the limestone. Cave divers arrive from all over the world to swim those tunnels, and many of them learn their craft here, in the dark water beneath the oaks.

## The Explorer's Name

Until 2010 this was simply Peacock Springs State Park. That November, Florida renamed it for Wesley Cofer Skiles, a Jacksonville-born cave diving pioneer, explorer and underwater cinematographer who had died in July of that year while diving off the state's southeast coast. Skiles began diving as a boy, made his home in High Springs, and carried his cameras on expeditions for National Geographic; his PBS series Water's Journey, made with Jill Heinerth, won a Suncoast Regional Emmy. The park that bears his name has grown as well. In 2006 the Trust for Public Land purchased roughly 481 acres for its expansion, more than doubling a park that had covered 252 acres. Today it spans about 761 acres of woods, sinkholes and springs.

## Mapping the Maze

Vasco Murray first explored the Peacock cave system in 1956. Turning exploration into a map took far longer. A National Speleological Society team led by Sheck Exley made more than 521 dives to complete the first survey, and a team led by Michael Poucher resurveyed the system in 1996. By June 2008 it ranked as the 24th longest underwater cave in the world, at 7,408 meters. Two years later, divers Agnes Milowka and James Toland found a connection between Baptizing Spring and Peacock, adding another 10,000 feet to the system's length. The network includes seven major springs and sinkholes, six of them inside the park, in a drowned karst landscape carved from limestone.

## Three Tunnels and a Gold Line

Peacock I is the most accessible and most popular entrance, reached by an elevated walkway. Three passages converge on its spring, and each carries a permanent guideline, called a gold line, to lead divers home. The Pothole tunnel is the deepest, dropping to 65 feet, with high ceilings, broad passages and a silt floor; now and then a flood scours the silt from the walls and exposes the white limestone beneath. The Peanut tunnel runs shallow and narrow, named for a section shaped like the two lobes of a peanut, and crossover tunnels link it to the Pothole. About 1,500 feet down the Pothole tunnel lies Olsen Sink. It was once a favorite shortcut into the heart of the cave, but to prevent erosion divers have been barred from entering there since 2002, though many still surface in it briefly mid-dive. That same year, the park banned diver propulsion vehicles to protect the cave.

## Sinks With Character

Every opening in this system has its own temperament. Orange Grove, a large sinkhole northeast of Peacock I, has stairs leading down to two tunnels: Lower Orange Grove plunges to 180 feet and is reserved for very advanced divers, while Upper Orange Grove twists in all three dimensions, rising from 70 feet to 40, and runs beyond the park boundary to Challenge Sink, the northernmost sinkhole in the system and the only one outside the park. Cisteen Sink offers a tiny, silty entrance and usually wears a thick blanket of duckweed. Peacock III works in reverse: it is a siphon, drawing water in rather than pouring it out. Its low, silty system is separate from the rest, except for one chamber, Henley's Castle, which drops past 200 feet.

## Life at the Surface

For those who stay dry, the park has its own quiet rewards. Deer, bobcats, raccoons, beavers and otters share these woods with wild turkeys, blue herons and barred owls, and a nature trail threads past the sinkholes. Swimmers and picnickers gather near the springs, which the park keeps open from 8 a.m. until sundown every day of the year. The water that surfaces at Peacock and Bonnet Springs eventually joins the Suwannee River, carrying the output of all that hidden limestone toward the Gulf.

## Flight Context

The park lies in Suwannee County, Florida, at approximately 30.1269°N, 83.1361°W, about 16 miles southwest of Live Oak along State Road 51 and two miles east of Luraville. The Suwannee River corridor to the west is the best visual reference; the springs and sinkholes themselves are small and hidden under tree canopy, so 1,500–2,500 feet AGL is a good altitude for picking out the river corridor and park clearings. Suwannee County Airport at Live Oak (FAA identifier 24J; no ICAO code in the OurAirports listing) is about 12 nm to the north-northeast, and Lake City Gateway Airport (KLCQ) is about 29 nm to the east. Summer afternoons often bring thunderstorms to North Florida; cooler months usually offer clearer skies.
