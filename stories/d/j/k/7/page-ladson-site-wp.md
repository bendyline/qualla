# Page–Ladson site

In a flooded sinkhole beneath Florida's Aucilla River, divers and archaeologists found mastodon bones and stone tools showing that people were here about 14,550 years ago, before the Clovis culture and some 2,000 years before the great Ice Age animals disappeared.

**Location:** 30.1702, -83.9577

**Categories:** Archaeological sites in Florida, Pre-Clovis archaeological sites in the Americas, Underwater archaeological sites, Paleontological sites of Florida, Ice Age

## Introduction

At the bottom of a sinkhole in the bed of the Aucilla River lie mats of chopped leaves, bark and wood, the fragments all of a similar length. That length matches the spacing between the cusps on a mastodon's teeth. Researchers have interpreted these "straw mats" as the Ice Age equivalent of the trampled elephant dung that builds up around water holes in Africa, and steroid traces linked to elephant dung have reportedly been identified in the deposits. Mastodons, in other words, once gathered here to drink. So did people. The Page–Ladson site, in Florida's Big Bend between Jefferson and Taylor counties, holds evidence that people were here before the Clovis culture, much earlier than had been believed.

## A Watering Hole in a Dry Land

At the height of the last ice age, sea level stood up to 100 meters lower than today, and Florida's coast reached much farther west. Most of Florida is a thick, porous limestone platform in which fresh water floats on top of salt water, so when the sea dropped, the fresh water table dropped with it, leaving the land far drier than it is now. The only reliable sources of fresh water at today's elevations were sinkholes and the deeper parts of river beds. Page–Ladson was one of those watering holes, a sinkhole holding a small pond in a ravine that later became the bed of the Aucilla River. Layer by layer, over thousands of years, its deposits recorded the animals and people who came to drink.

## Divers, a Test Pit and a Name

Discovery came from underwater. Starting in 1959, Dick Ohmes and other scuba divers began bringing up artifacts and Pleistocene animal bones bearing butcher marks from the lower Aucilla. In 1983 archaeologist James S. Dunbar and paleontologist S. David Webb began surveying a stretch called Half-Mile Run, where a former Navy SEAL named Buddy Page showed them a spot where he had found elephant bones. A 20-inch test pit produced elephant bones, bone tools and chips from toolmaking. The Ladson family, who owned the surrounding land, let the team camp along the river, and the site took both their names. From 1983 to 1997 the Aucilla River Prehistory Project excavated here, developing new ways to record exactly where each find lay in an underwater environment. The Aucilla has also reportedly yielded ivory spear foreshafts more frequently than any other sites in North America.

## Before Clovis

The lowest layer holds the late Pleistocene: bones of mastodon, mammoth, horse, ground sloth and palaeolama. Some bones bear what appear to be human-made cut marks, most notably a complete mastodon tusk. Excavations resumed from 2012 to 2014, and on May 13, 2016, a team led by Jessi J. Halligan, with Dunbar among the co-authors, reported in Science Advances that people had butchered or scavenged mastodons here about 14,550 years ago, roughly 2,000 years before the large mammals went extinct. Page–Ladson is described as the earliest well-documented site of human presence in the southeastern United States. Its early dates challenge theories that humans quickly wiped out big game once they arrived. As archaeologist Michael Waters put it in a Phys.org report, the people here "knew how to find game, fresh water and material for making tools."

## Hearths by the River

People kept returning long after the mastodons were gone. Above the Ice Age "straw mat" level lies a layer of mud with no bones of extinct animals at all. In 1996 excavators found an Early Archaic Bolen living level about 10,000 years old. It held at least three hearths, stone points, scrapers, adzes and gouges, and antler tools used to press flakes from stone. Three wooden stakes still stood upright in the ground, beside a cypress log that had been burned on its top side and hollowed out. Such fragile things survived because the river rose and flooded the site within a hundred years of its occupation, sealing the camp beneath the water.

## Flight Context

The Page–Ladson site lies at about 30.170 N, 83.958 W in the bed of the Aucilla River, which forms the line between Jefferson and Taylor counties in Florida's Big Bend region. Nothing is visible from the air, since the site is underwater; the landmark is the course of the Aucilla River itself. A viewing altitude of 2,000-3,000 feet follows the river well. Tallahassee International Airport (KTLH) is about 25 nm to the northwest, and Perry-Foley Airport (KFPY) is about 20 nm to the east-southeast. Expect summer thunderstorms and humid haze.
