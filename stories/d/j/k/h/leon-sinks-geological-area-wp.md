# Leon Sinks Geological Area

South of Tallahassee, a woodland of sinkholes opens onto one of the most extensive underwater cave systems in the world, a flooded labyrinth that divers have traced all the way to Wakulla Springs.

**Location:** 30.3093, -84.3464

**Categories:** Caves of Florida, Apalachicola National Forest, Landforms of Leon County, Florida, Karst landforms, Parks in Leon County, Florida

## Introduction

Stand at the observation platform above Big Dismal Sink and the forest simply stops. The ground falls away for roughly eighty to a hundred feet to a disk of dark water, and the water keeps going, another hundred feet down, with the mouth of a cave opening eighty feet below the surface. Big Dismal is the showpiece of the Leon Sinks Geological Area, in southern Leon County, Florida, but it is only one window into what lies under these woods: a flooded maze of limestone passages that ranks among the most extensive underwater cave systems in the world.

## A Forest Built on Sponge

Leon Sinks sits on the Woodville Karst Plain, a mature karst landscape laid over the Upper Floridan Aquifer. Here the carbonate bedrock is so permeable that the water moving through it is extremely vulnerable to pollution from above. The plain spreads across southern and southwestern Leon County, and beneath it the passages run far enough to reach Wakulla Springs. Many of the area's sinkholes are linked to one another through underwater caves, and they come in two kinds: wet sinks holding water, and dry ones. Besides Big Dismal, the wet sinks carry names like Black, Duckweed, Fisher Creek, Lost Stream, Magnolia and Natural Bridge, along with Hammock Sink, also called Little Dismal.

## The Day Two Caves Became One

Divers with the Woodville Karst Plain Project have done extensive mapping and exploration of these passages. The purpose goes beyond adventure: to understand the complex dynamics of the aquifer, and the proper ecological approach to keeping it clean. Farther south, the cave behind Wakulla Springs had its own survey: a branching network of conduits, 12 miles of it mapped, made of long tubes with a consistent diameter and depth of around 300 feet, broken here and there by larger chambers. Its largest conduit runs for more than 3.8 miles, and four secondary conduits, Leon Sinks among them, intersect it.

On December 15, 2007, project divers made the link between the two. The joined Wakulla-Leon Sinks Cave System, which had 31.99 miles of surveyed passage by a 2013 count (a more recent list gives about 44.8 miles), became the longest underwater cave in the United States and, by one ranking, the sixth largest in the world.

## Crayfish in the Dark and Chickadees Above

The flooded passages are full of life. Freshwater eels live here, and so do rare crustaceans found nowhere except the Woodville Karst Plain, including the Woodville Karst Plain crayfish and a swimming cave isopod, Remasellus parvus.

Up top, the Leon Sinks area is part of the Apalachicola National Forest, and three hiking trails lead to the different sinks: the 3.6-mile Sinkhole Trail, the 2.8-mile Gum Swamp Trail and the half-mile Crossover Trail, though the Forest Service says the area is only partially open while improvements continue and is subject to unscheduled closures. Along the way grow more than 20 species of trees and 75 kinds of plants. The wildlife runs from Carolina chickadees flitting overhead to gopher tortoises working the ground, all of it living above a landscape that water has hollowed out from below.

## Flight Context

Leon Sinks Geological Area lies at about 30.309 N, 84.346 W, in the Apalachicola National Forest just south of Tallahassee, Florida. From 2,000-3,000 ft the sinks appear as small, round openings and dark pools scattered through continuous pine and hardwood forest; Tallahassee's southern suburbs lie just to the north and the Wakulla Springs area to the south. Tallahassee International Airport (KTLH) is only about 5 nm to the north, so expect to work with Tallahassee's busy terminal airspace. Other nearby fields include Carrabelle-Thompson Airport (X13), about 34 nm south-southwest, and Cairo-Grady County Airport in Georgia (70J), about 36 nm north-northeast. Summer afternoons bring frequent thunderstorms.
