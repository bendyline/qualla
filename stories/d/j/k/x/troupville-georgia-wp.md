# Troupville, Georgia

Troupville was a thriving riverboat town and county seat at the meeting of two south Georgia rivers until the railroad passed it by, and its residents picked up their houses and carried them to a new town called Valdosta.

**Location:** 30.8492, -83.3375

**Categories:** Ghost towns in Georgia (U.S. state), Former county seats in Georgia (U.S. state), Lowndes County, Georgia, Railroad history, River towns

## Introduction

At three o'clock in the morning on June 22, 1858, the Lowndes County courthouse at Troupville was burning. Only five days earlier, the railroad had announced that its new line would not run through town. Accounts say the fire was set by a man named William B. Crawford, who was later released on bond and fled to South Carolina. Whatever his reasons, the flames made a fitting emblem for what came next. Within a few years, the county seat that had been built on the promise of river commerce was emptying out, its residents literally carrying their houses away.

## A Town Built for Boats

Troupville, occasionally recorded as Troupeville, was the third seat of Lowndes County by Wikipedia's count (other histories treat it as the renamed Lowndesville, the second seat) and was named after Governor George Troup. Its founders chose the site with water in mind. The town stood as a riverboat landing near the confluence of the Withlacoochee River and the Little River, at the uppermost navigable point. The county had already moved its seat once, in 1833, when Lowndesville replaced Franklinville, which lay east of modern Hahira. In 1834 a group of citizens was appointed to choose a new seat that could keep pace with settlement, and by July 1836 commissioners were advertising for merchants and mechanics to come to Troupville, convinced that the two rivers could be made navigable to this point at small expense. That October, newspapers carried calls for proposals to build a courthouse. The Georgia legislature incorporated the town on December 14, 1837, and designated it the county seat. Franklinville, left behind, declined.

## Cotton, Hotels and a Racetrack

For a time, geography delivered everything the commissioners had hoped. Troupville sat at the river junction, on the stage route from Thomasville to Waresboro, and along the planned route of the Brunswick and Florida Railroad. In 1839 town lots were being sold at $3,443; a decade later the figure had risen to $9,162. At its peak the town had three hotels, four stores, numerous shops, three churches and professionals of various kinds. A newspaper, the South Georgia Watchman, later renamed the Troupville Watchman, recorded local life. Set amid large cotton plantations, Troupville also served as a gateway to the new state of Florida. A mile north of town, a horse racetrack drew many of the area's men.

## The Railroad Chooses

Everything turned on the railroad, and in the 1850s two companies were fighting over the right to build it. The Brunswick and Florida Railroad held the older charter but lacked the money; the Savannah, Albany, and Gulf Railroad Company had the money but not the right of way through south Georgia. In February 1856 the Atlantic and Gulf Railroad Company was chartered to build a line through Lowndes County to the state's western border, and a law passed at the same time required the Brunswick and Florida to forfeit its right of way in exchange for state financial backing. After two years of wrangling, it did. Chief engineer E.L. Heriot surveyed three routes: one through northern Troupville, one across northern Lowndes County, and one crossing the Withlacoochee at a mineral springs. On June 17, 1858, the choice was published. Heriot picked the third, reasoning that it needed less curvature and fewer changes in grade than the shorter route through Troupville. Five days later, the courthouse burned.

## Houses on the Move

Riverboats were already giving way to railroads across the state, and establishing a town at the junction of the new rail lines had become crucial to the county's livelihood. On August 9, 1858, a meeting in Troupville's academy building discussed carving a new county, to be called Brooks County, from the part of Lowndes lying west of the Withlacoochee. After the new town of Valdosta was laid out on the rail line in 1859, many Troupville residents picked up their houses and businesses and moved them to the tracks. The county seat followed in 1860. A toll bridge across the Little River from Troupville to Brooks County was completed in early 1871, but it could not revive the place. By 1874 one Valdostan described Troupville as "a deserted village or rather an old field with scarcely a vestige of the village remaining." The town's municipal charter lingered on paper until 1995, when it was finally repealed.

## Flight Context

The Troupville site lies at about 30.849°N, 83.338°W, just west-northwest of Valdosta near the confluence of the Withlacoochee and Little Rivers. There is little on the ground to mark the vanished town, so navigate by the water: from 2,000–3,000 feet, look for where the two wooded river corridors meet west of Valdosta's street grid. Valdosta Regional Airport (KVLD) is about 5 nm to the southeast, and Moody Air Force Base (KVAD), about 10 nm to the northeast, is military airspace to respect. South Georgia's summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms can limit visibility, so cooler, drier months give the clearest view of the river junction.
