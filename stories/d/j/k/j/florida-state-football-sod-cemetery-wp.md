# Florida State Football Sod Cemetery

Just north of Doak Campbell Stadium, more than 100 bronze markers stand over buried patches of turf, each one carried home to Tallahassee from a stadium where Florida State won a game it was supposed to lose, or one too big to forget.

**Location:** 30.4399, -84.3047

**Categories:** Florida State Seminoles football, College football traditions, Florida State University buildings and structures, Tallahassee, Florida, 1962 establishments in Florida

## Introduction

More than 100 graves stand just outside Doak Campbell Stadium, and every one of them holds grass. Each bronze plaque in Florida State's Sod Cemetery marks a "sod game," one of the Seminoles' greatest victories away from Tallahassee, and beneath each marker lies a piece of turf pulled from the field where the win happened. On game days, thousands of fans drift through the gates to read the headstones. Some plots hold earth from Georgia's Sanford Stadium, Ohio Stadium or Notre Dame Stadium. And at least one road win reportedly ended with a $500 bill for a hole cut in an indoor stadium's turf.

## Sod From Between the Hedges

The tradition began with a challenge. In 1962, Florida State was preparing to face fifth-ranked Georgia at Sanford Stadium when Dean Coyle Moore, a longtime professor and member of the athletic board, spoke to the team after a Thursday practice. Moore had helped President Doak Campbell field the school's first football team in 1947; until that year Florida State had been a women's college, and Moore wanted to instill a scrappy spirit in the young program. He told the players to win and "bring back some sod from between the hedges at Georgia." On October 20, 1962, they won 18–0. Team captain Gene McDowell pulled up a small piece of the field. "We got a piece of sod and took it back to Tallahassee," McDowell later recalled. Moore and coach Bill Peterson buried it on the practice field, and a ritual was born.

## The Rules of Burial

A game earns its grave in several ways. Florida State wins on the road as the underdog against a higher-ranked opponent, or it wins at the University of Florida, home of perhaps its fiercest rival. A championship game counts, such as the ACC title or a national championship, and so does a bowl game. Finally, the head coach can simply declare one. In the early years, the ritual was reserved for upsets, but success changed the math. By the early 1980s every bowl win counted, starting with the Gator Bowl in 1982, and as a result the plaques trace the program's arc from scrappy underdog to national power. A season with few sod games can simply mean the Seminoles were favored in their big road games. In September 2023, the coach's discretion came into play at Clemson, where Florida State, ranked fourth against an unranked Clemson, had not won since 2013. The Seminoles won 31–24 in overtime, and a keepsake was dug up from Frank Howard Field.

## A Big Pair of Scissors

Bobby Bowden, an assistant from 1963 and head coach from 1976 to 2009, filled a large share of the cemetery, and he learned the tradition's practical limits on the artificial turf that covered many stadiums in his era. "It got to be a problem when we played on AstroTurf," he said. "I mean, you've got to have a big pair of scissors, boy! And I'm not sure they liked it." After Florida State beat Auburn in the Sugar Bowl following the 1988 season, defensive lineman Odell Haggins, later a longtime Seminoles assistant coach, reportedly cut a piece out of the Superdome's turf, and the university received a $500 bill. The next time the Seminoles played in the Sugar Bowl, officials handed them a precut sample of the carpet. In 2021, a bronze plaque joined the cemetery's brick column: "During the seasons of 1976-2009, Coach Bobby Bowden and his teams won 63 Sod Victories."

## Keepers of the Grounds

In 1988, Moore handed the cemetery's care to Tallahassee attorney Douglas Mannheimer, asking that Mannheimer's son, Andrew, one day take it on in turn. Mannheimer tends the markers and, every year, addresses the entire football team to explain what the sod means. His work earned him induction into the FSU Hall of Fame. "I'm a fan who has lived in Tallahassee and a fan who Dean Coyle Moore asked to carry on a tradition," he said. When new practice fields were built in the summer of 2013, the cemetery moved to its current spot, 200 feet north of the stadium's Gate M, outside the entrance to the Al Dunlap Seminole Practice Fields.

## Flowers in the Visitors' Colors

On game days the cemetery behaves like a real one, only happier. Fresh sod is sometimes laid in an open casket with sashes and flowers so fans can pay their respects, while an audio loop plays the cemetery's history, radio calls of great plays and comments from players. The flowers are color-coordinated to the visiting team. When Florida comes to town, the graves from past wins over the Gators bloom in blue and orange, while the rest of the cemetery wears garnet and gold. From 2014 until the pandemic season of 2020, fans gathered here 90 minutes before kickoff for Sod Talk, as former Seminoles such as Charlie Ward, Warrick Dunn, Derrick Brooks and Fred Biletnikoff returned to tell the crowd about their playing days. Since then, videos produced by the university's Seminole Productions have carried the stories on, featuring voices including Bobby Bowden himself.
