# Anhaica

The capital of the Apalachee people, seized by Hernando de Soto's expedition for the winter of 1539-1540, lay forgotten beneath modern Tallahassee until an archaeologist found Spanish chain mail and coins in a former governor's yard in 1987.

**Location:** 30.4340, -84.2734

**Categories:** Apalachee, Fort Walton culture, Archaeological sites in Florida, Geography of Tallahassee, Florida, Spanish Florida, Former Native American populated places in the United States, History of Tallahassee, Florida, Former populated places in Leon County, Florida

## Introduction

The chain mail turned up in a governor's yard. In 1987, state archaeologist B. Calvin Jones located a lost town on the grounds of the Gov. John W. Martin House in Tallahassee, a site that has since yielded early sixteenth-century Spanish coins, olive jars, chain mail and crossbow quarrels. He had found Anhaica, the principal town of the Apalachee people, and very likely the place where Hernando de Soto's expedition spent the winter of 1539-1540 in homes that were not its own.

## Capital of the Apalachee

Anhaica went by several names, among them Iviahica, Yniahico and the pueblo of Apalache. It became the Apalachee capital in the late pre-invasion era, after the people abandoned their former capital at what is now the Lake Jackson Mounds Site around 1500. The move may have marked a political change. No platform mounds have been found at Anhaica, suggesting either that the town was not occupied long enough for mound building to begin or that the Apalachee had stopped building them. Europeans may have played a part even before they settled in. Disease introduced by the Pánfilo de Narváez expedition, which passed through Apalachee in 1528, could have reduced the population and changed where villages stood and whether mounds were raised at all.

## A Winter Under Occupation

When Hernando de Soto arrived on October 6, 1539, Anhaica was a substantial town, with about 250 buildings by one account. De Soto set up camp there, forcing the Apalachee to abandon their own capital, and his expedition stayed through the winter, leaving only in March 1540. For the Apalachee families forced out, it meant losing their homes and the center of their nation to foreign soldiers for an entire winter. The Spanish did not forget the place. About 1633, Franciscans established a mission, La Purificacion de Tama, at the site of Anhaica, and in the early period of Spanish colonization the old Apalachee town served as the capital of the Apalachee Province.

## Found Beneath the Lawn

For centuries the town's location was lost, until Jones's discovery in 1987. The site is now known to archaeologists as the Martin Archaeological Site, catalogued as 8LE853B, and its finds of coins, olive jars, chain mail and crossbow quarrels have led it to be considered the location with the best claim to be the de Soto expedition's winter encampment. Today the ground where the Apalachee capital stood is part of the DeSoto Site Historic State Park, preserved within the modern city that grew up around it.

## Flight Context

The Anhaica site lies at 30.4341 N, 84.2734 W in Tallahassee, on the grounds of the Gov. John W. Martin House, now within the DeSoto Site Historic State Park. It sits in an urban setting with little visible from above beyond the park grounds, so use the city's layout for reference. The site is very close to Tallahassee International Airport (KTLH), about 5 nm to the west-southwest, so contact Tallahassee ATC before maneuvering nearby. A viewing altitude of 1,500 to 2,500 feet MSL, subject to ATC instructions, works well. Best seen in clear daytime conditions.
