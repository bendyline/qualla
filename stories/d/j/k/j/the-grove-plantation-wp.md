# The Grove Plantation

Built by enslaved craftsmen for a territorial governor around 1840, Tallahassee's brick Greek Revival house called The Grove sheltered generations of one family, from a Unionist who cursed secession on its porch to a governor who stood against segregation.

**Location:** 30.4503, -84.2819

**Categories:** National Register of Historic Places in Leon County, Florida, Plantation houses in Florida, Antebellum architecture, Historic house museums in Florida, Tallahassee, Florida

## Introduction

In January 1861, a group of secessionists came to The Grove looking for Richard Keith Call. They had just voted to take Florida out of the Union, and the old territorial governor, an ardent Southern Unionist, had planned to protest at the Capitol before deciding at the last minute to stay home. Call walked out onto his front porch, raised his cane above his head, and told them: "You have opened the gates of hell, from which shall flow the curses of the damned which shall sink you to perdition." The house behind him was barely two decades old. It would outlast every argument made on its steps.

## A Fortress of Brick

Call bought a large tract here in 1825, after a single term as Florida's territorial delegate to Congress. He belonged to Andrew Jackson's inner circle, and in 1836 Jackson appointed him territorial governor in the midst of the Second Seminole War. The exact date of construction is unknown, but in November 1838 a visitor wrote that the governor was building "a very fine brick house" that people said "will be the finest house in the Territory." War shaped its design. The thick exterior and interior walls, and even the four Tuscan columns of its portico, are brick, likely made on site, giving the house a fortress-like look. A cistern secured clean water. Wood flooring, marble fireplaces and sandstone windowsills most likely arrived on the new Tallahassee Railroad from St. Marks or Port Leon. Legend holds that the materials for a second-story balcony went down with a ship at sea, which is why an upstairs door opens onto only a small balconet.

## The Hands That Built It

The Grove was built by enslaved craftsmen of African descent, many of them likely hired out from other enslavers, and some perhaps the same men who had labored on Call's railroad. Their skill is written into the building itself: the walls still stand after more than 175 years. Despite its popular name, there is little evidence The Grove itself ever worked as a major agricultural plantation. Call made his money in land speculation, law, railroads and politics, and his fortunes suffered in the early 1840s, when the Port Leon debacle, the Tallahassee fire of 1843 and the failure of the Union Bank tangled him in lawsuits that dragged into the 1850s. He lost some land but kept the house. In 1851 he deeded it to his daughter Ellen, along with seven enslaved people.

## Silk and Fire

Ellen Call Long shared her father's Unionism, a conviction that strained her marriage to Medicus A. Long, an outspoken secessionist who left for Texas in the late 1850s. Of their eight children, only two lived to adulthood. When war came she backed the Confederacy anyway, opening The Grove to officers and wounded soldiers, and on May 1, 1865, less than a week after Tallahassee learned of Lee's surrender, she hosted the city's May Day festivities on the lawn. Afterward she reinvented herself. She raised silkworms on the property, published a well-regarded treatise on silk culture and saw a large American flag made from Grove silk presented at Governor Edward A. Perry's inauguration in 1885. Her interest in mulberry trees drew her into forestry, and she became one of the first Floridians to advocate controlled burning. Debt caught up with her all the same. Facing foreclosure in 1903, she signed the property over, under disputed circumstances, to her grandson-in-law.

## The Home of the Tallahassee Girl

Ellen's granddaughter, Reinette Long Hunt, kept The Grove alive by opening its doors. She taught art classes, hosted a salon-like circle, let the new Tallahassee Country Club use the house as its first clubhouse, and by the 1920s ran it as The Grove Hotel, marketed as "The Home of the Tallahassee Girl" after a Maurice Thompson novel whose heroine was widely believed to be modeled on her aunt. Future Florida chief justice Richard Ervin rented the whole second floor. At the city's 1924 centennial, a grandstand went up on the grounds, and separate plays were staged for white and Black audiences on different days in the segregated Tallahassee of the era; at least one was written by Reinette herself. On New Year's Day 1934, fire tore through the attic and roof, and Reinette's brother, Richard Call Long Jr., died of injuries suffered fighting it. She died in 1940, still battling creditors.

## The Governor's Other Mansion

In 1942 the badly run-down house was sold to state legislator LeRoy Collins and his wife, Mary Call Darby Collins, a great-granddaughter of Richard Keith Call. Restoration paused while Collins served in the Navy; years later, work on the old cistern turned up the dog tags of a World War II pilot, Lt. Joseph Azat, which were returned to his family. Elected governor, Collins made The Grove Florida's de facto executive residence from 1955 to 1957 while a new mansion rose nearby. When the legislature pushed a resolution of interposition against Brown v. Board of Education, Collins condemned it, and in 1965 he served as a federal intermediary during the Selma marches. Photographs of that role circulated back home and effectively ended his political career; he lost a bid for the Senate in 1968. The state acquired The Grove in 1985, granting the couple life leases. After Mary Call Darby Collins died in 2009, the state rehabilitated the house, and The Grove Museum opened to the public in 2017. A small family cemetery, older than the house itself, still holds several generations of the Call and Collins families.

## Flight Context

The Grove stands just north of downtown Tallahassee, Florida, at approximately 30.4503°N, 84.2819°W, beside the Florida Governor's Mansion. The tall tower of the Florida State Capitol, less than a mile to the south, is the easiest landmark to find; The Grove's white-columned house and lawn lie on the tree-covered blocks just north of it. A viewing altitude of 1,500–2,000 feet AGL gives a clear look at the downtown core. Tallahassee International Airport (KTLH) is about 5 nm to the southwest, and the area lies within its Class C airspace, so contact approach before overflying downtown. Summer afternoons in North Florida frequently bring thunderstorms; mornings and the cooler months usually offer the best visibility.
