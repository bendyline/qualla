# Frenchtown (Tallahassee)

Named for French settlers but built by people freed after the Civil War, Tallahassee's Frenchtown is often described as Tallahassee's oldest surviving historic African-American community, a place where a young Ray Charles is said to have lived and touring stars stopped to play.

**Location:** 30.4500, -84.2920

**Categories:** History of Tallahassee, Florida, Neighborhoods in Tallahassee, Florida, African-American history of Florida, Historic districts in Florida, Jazz history

## Introduction

The name points to France, but the neighborhood was made by people who had only just been freed. Frenchtown, tucked northwest of downtown in Florida's capital city of Tallahassee, is often described as the oldest surviving community of its kind in Tallahassee: a historic African-American neighborhood established during Reconstruction, in the aftermath of the Emancipation Proclamation of 1863. A young Ray Charles is said to have lived here. Touring bands played its clubs. Civil rights organizers worked its streets, close to the seat of state power they were trying to move.

## A Marquis's Township

The French part of the story begins on July 4, 1825. That day's Lafayette Land Grant gave Gilbert du Motier, Marquis de Lafayette, a township of more than 23,000 acres in central Leon County, and several of his acquaintances crossed the Atlantic to start new lives on it. Some later drifted west to New Orleans or went home to France. Those who stayed settled this corner of Tallahassee, and the name outlasted them. After the Civil War, the area became something else entirely. Newly freed African Americans moved in, drawn not by prestige but by availability: Frenchtown sat on low-lying, relatively undesirable land that nobody else was fighting over. On that overlooked ground, they built a community.

## Red Bird Nights

By the early twentieth century, Frenchtown had become a hub, its businesses growing along with its reputation. Local histories say that from 1940 to 1945 Ray Charles called the neighborhood home, though biographies also place him at the Florida School for the Deaf and the Blind in St. Augustine from 1937 to 1945. Nat Adderley and his brother Cannonball Adderley were known to have played here in their younger days, and touring stars such as Louis Armstrong and James Brown are said to have come through to perform. Two venues anchored the scene. The Red Bird Club and Cafe DeLuxe supplied a wealth of musical talent, and one local act, "Lawyer Smith and his Band," held down a residency there for thirty years. For a neighborhood built on land others had passed over, it was a remarkable roster of sound.

## Protest, Then a Long Decline

Frenchtown's proximity to the Capitol mattered during the civil rights movement. Organizations including the NAACP and the Urban League emerged here, organizing protests and advocacy aimed at the state government. The end of segregation, though, brought an unexpected cost. Local schools, healthcare facilities and public services were relocated or saw major funding cuts. Old Lincoln High School, at Brevard and Macomb Streets, closed in the late 1960s as Leon County's schools integrated, and its last classes finished at Lincoln-Griffin High; the main surviving building is today the Lincoln Neighborhood Center, which houses social services. Economist Patrick Mason of the University of Massachusetts Amherst has described stagnation and a decline in local commerce between 1974 and 1995. "Local businesses found it hard to compete with national chains in the grocery and the fast-food industries," he said. The neighborhood physically shrank, too. Until the 1970s it reached south of Tennessee Street to Park Avenue, onto land where the LeRoy Collins Leon County Public Library now stands.

## Jazz in a 1921 House

Revival began in April 2005, when the city completed the Frenchtown Renaissance Center. Three years later, the B Sharps Jazz Club opened inside the restored Woman's Working Band House of 1921, a building listed on the National Register of Historic Places, and international jazz talent often performs there, an echo of the Red Bird years. The pressures now come from a different direction. Some parts of Frenchtown lie less than a quarter-mile from Florida State University, and demand for housing and commercial space has brought a wave of development. Residents and community advocates have raised concerns about gentrification and displacement. The stakes are high: a 2018 Florida Chamber of Commerce study reportedly placed Frenchtown in the poorest zip code in the state. Today the neighborhood is generally bounded by Alabama Street to the north, Tennessee Street to the south and Woodward Avenue to the west, with its eastern edge drawn at Bronough Street or Martin Luther King Jr. Boulevard depending on the source.

## Flight Context

Frenchtown sits at 30.45°N, 84.292°W, just northwest of downtown Tallahassee. From 1,500–2,500 feet the neighborhood reads as a grid of small blocks between the Florida State University campus to the southwest and the downtown government district, marked by the high-rise Florida State Capitol, to the southeast. Tennessee Street, a major east-west artery, marks its southern edge. Tallahassee International Airport (KTLH) lies about 4.5 nm to the southwest and is the natural base for a local flight. North Florida summers bring frequent afternoon thunderstorms, so morning flights usually offer the smoothest air and clearest views.
