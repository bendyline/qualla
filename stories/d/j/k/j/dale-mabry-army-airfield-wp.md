# Dale Mabry Army Airfield

Carved out of Tallahassee swampland on a 90-day deadline, Dale Mabry Army Airfield trained thousands of World War II fighter pilots from the United States, Europe and China.

**Location:** 30.4375, -84.3372

**Categories:** Airfields of the United States Army Air Forces in Florida, World War II, Military history of Florida, History of Leon County, Florida, Aviation history

## Introduction

On November 16, 2016, a construction crew in Tallahassee dug into the roots of a tree and found a live bomb. A team from the 325th Explosive Ordnance Disposal Flight was dispatched from Tyndall Air Force Base in Panama City. It judged that the safest thing was to detonate it where it lay, and did so with no injuries reported. The find lay near Florida State University, a few miles east of the old field, but experts said it most likely dated from practice at a base that had closed seventy years before: Dale Mabry Army Airfield, where thousands of young pilots learned to fight in the sky.

## A Captain, a Swamp and Ninety Days

The field took its name from Captain Dale Mabry, a Tallahassee native who became an airship pilot in the Army Air Service. He was 30 when the Army dirigible Roma, with Mabry as its pilot, crashed and exploded at Norfolk, Virginia, on February 21, 1922. The Tallahassee airport that bore his name drew military attention in 1938, when the Army Air Corps opened a contract flying school there. In 1940 U.S. Senator Claude Pepper and Florida Governor Spessard Holland helped persuade the Army to turn it into a full Army airfield as the country built up its forces ahead of World War II.

The ground was swamp. In October 1940 work began on a railroad siding and drainage, and hundreds of laborers cleared land for temporary quarters. Because pilots were needed urgently, the federal government set a 90-day completion deadline. The field was activated in January 1941.

## A City Behind the Fence

Dale Mabry grew into a nearly self-sufficient city, with runways, barracks, officers' quarters, a mess hall, hangars, a hospital, a church and a bowling alley. It began at 530 acres and swelled to 1,720 acres and 133 buildings over the course of the war. When training peaked in mid-1944, the base averaged 1,300 officers, 3,000 enlisted men and women, and 800 civilian employees.

Its reach extended well beyond Tallahassee. Satellite fields at Thomasville and Harris Neck in Georgia, at Perry, and at a flight strip near Carrabelle all answered to it. Student pilots practiced gunnery at Alligator Point and bombing at a range near Sopchoppy on the Gulf. Through it all, even with the field closed to general aviation, Eastern Airlines and National Airlines kept flying in.

## Warhawks, Airacobras and Thunderbolts

Construction delays limited flying in 1941, but by early 1942 the field was busy. The 79th Fighter Group formed there that February with Curtiss P-40 Warhawks before moving on in May. The 81st Fighter Group followed with Bell P-39 Airacobras and trained until the end of June; bound for the South Pacific, it was sent to Egypt instead. The 58th Fighter Group became the base's first permanent Operational Training Unit, giving new graduates advanced combat training. When it too was chosen for combat, the 338th Fighter Group took over. Republic P-47 Thunderbolts arrived in the fall of 1943, and P-51 Mustangs as soon as they became available.

In 1942 and 1944, cadets of the Chinese Air Force and the Free French Air Force also trained here. The work was dangerous. More than a dozen pilots died in accidents while learning to fire at targets such as a giant plywood bull's-eye at Alligator Point.

## Seventy-Nine Thousand Visitors

The war swamped the small capital. In 1943 alone, some 79,000 family members came to Tallahassee, then a town of about 16,000, to visit the pilots in training. Late in the war the base took on a different kind of resident: in 1944 and 1945 Dale Mabry was one of several Florida bases that held German prisoners of war, up to 150 at its peak.

Fighter training ended on May 31, 1945. After Japan surrendered that August, the base's main business became sending people home. In the fall of 1945 its equipment began moving to other bases, and in 1946 the field was declared surplus and handed over for return to civil use. In all, more than 8,000 pilots from the United States, Europe and China had trained here in P-39s, P-40s, P-47s and P-51s, according to the state historical marker; a local museum director puts the figure at 6,000 to 8,000.

The airfield did not vanish entirely. A state historical marker installed in 2001 on Appleyard Drive says some sections of the asphalt runway are still visible, along with several concrete tie-down pads where aircraft were once secured, and it stands at the edge of the old northwest-southeast runway. Much of the field has been built over, though: a local museum director has said the north-south runway lay roughly where Appleyard Drive runs today, near Tallahassee Community College. And now and then, as that 2016 construction crew discovered, the ground gives up something more startling.

## Flight Context

The former Dale Mabry Army Airfield lies at about 30.4375 N, 84.3372 W, on the west side of Tallahassee, Florida, roughly 3 nm west of the state capitol. From 1,500-2,500 ft the old field is hard to pick out amid the developed west side of the city; look for the straight line of Appleyard Drive near Tallahassee Community College, roughly where a north-south runway once lay. The site sits just 2.4 nm north-northeast of Tallahassee International Airport (KTLH), so it lies inside Tallahassee's busy terminal airspace; coordinate with approach control. Other nearby fields: Cairo-Grady County Airport in Georgia (70J), about 29 nm north-northeast; Thomasville Regional (KTVI), about 37 nm northeast. Summer afternoons bring frequent thunderstorms.
