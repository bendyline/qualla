# Perry massacre

In December 1922, after the murder of a white schoolteacher in Perry, Florida, white mobs killed four Black men, none of whom was ever tried in a court, and burned the heart of the town's Black community.

**Location:** 30.1134, -83.5819

**Categories:** Lynching deaths in Florida, Racially motivated violence against African Americans in Florida, 1922 riots in the United States, Taylor County, Florida, African-American history

## Introduction

None of the four Black men killed in Perry, Florida, in December 1922 ever faced a court. One was shot at a roadblock. One was burned alive before thousands of spectators after a "confession" extracted by torture. One was dragged from the sheriff's custody and shot. One was killed in his own home over a note he was accused of writing. Over the same days, night after night, the buildings that held Black Perry together (its school, its lodge, its amusement hall and its church) went up in flames. It began with a murder, and with a mob that decided for itself who would pay.

## A Murder and a Manhunt

On the afternoon of December 2, 1922, the body of Annie "Ruby" Hendry, a young white schoolteacher, was found in Perry, in Taylor County. She had been brutally killed, so badly beaten that it took half an hour to identify her. By December 5 police had linked the weapons found at the scene, a double-barrelled shotgun and a bloody razor, to a Black man who had been living in the area under the name "Charley Wright" and who was identified as an escaped convict from neighboring Dixie County. No evidence linking Wright to the murder was ever made public.

Armed search parties with bloodhounds spread across the county. Authorities deputized local citizens and sealed the roads. And each night after Hendry's body was found, another building serving Perry's Black community burned: first the schoolhouse, then the lodge, then the amusement hall, and then the church.

## Hands in the Air

On December 6, Cubrit Dixon, a Black man traveling from Madison County into Taylor County, was stopped by armed citizens who had been deputized for the manhunt. They told him to put his hands up. When he did not comply, they shot and killed him. Witnesses said he had seemed to be reaching for a gun in his back pocket. When his body was examined, the only thing in that pocket was a closed pocketknife.

His killing came a day before the first suspect was arrested and two days before the second. He died on one of the roads that authorities had sealed and handed over to armed, newly deputized men.

## A Trial Without a Court

On December 7, Albert (or Arthur) Young, an escaped convict from Georgia and an acquaintance of Wright's, was captured near Valdosta, Georgia. The next day Wright, 21 years old, was arrested in Madison County. Wright was reported to have confessed, and Young to have admitted being with him, though Wright was also reported to have said Young took no part in the killing.

The prisoners never reached a courtroom. On December 8 a crowd of 3,000 to 5,000 white men, locals and out-of-staters alike, stopped their transport and seized them from the sheriff. They held what amounted to a kangaroo court. Wright was tortured until he produced a "confession," in which he claimed to have acted alone. The mob declared him guilty and burned him at the stake. Afterward, people in the crowd collected pieces of his body and clothing as souvenirs.

## The Toll

Young was returned to the sheriff and jailed in Taylor County. On December 12, as he was being moved from the jail, a smaller mob abducted him and shot him to death.

Three days later the Madison-Enterprise newspaper reported another killing: a Black man in Perry had been accused of writing "an improper note" to a white woman, and in retaliation he was shot to death in his home, which was then burned down on top of him.

By the time the violence ended, whites had burned Perry's Black school, its Masonic lodge, its church, its amusement hall and the homes of several families. Four men are documented as killed: Charles Wright, Cubrit Dixon, Albert Young and the man accused over the note, and some later accounts say more Black residents may have died. Wright and Young were killed within days of their arrests, and no court ever heard the case against either of them.

## Flight Context

Perry lies at about 30.113 N, 83.582 W in Taylor County, in Florida's Big Bend, some 15-20 nm inland from the Gulf coast. From 2,500-3,500 ft the small town appears as a grid of streets set in flat, heavily forested country, with US 19 running through it. Nearby airports: Perry-Foley Airport (KFPY; FAA identifier FPY), about 2.5 nm south of town; Suwannee County Airport at Live Oak (24J), about 31 nm east-northeast; Tallahassee International (KTLH), about 44 nm west-northwest; Valdosta Regional (KVLD), about 43 nm north-northeast. Summer afternoons bring frequent thunderstorms; winter mornings can be foggy in the low coastal country.
