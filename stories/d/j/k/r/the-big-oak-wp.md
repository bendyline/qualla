# The Big Oak

Rooted at a Thomasville street corner since around 1680, the Big Oak has survived trucks, storms and centuries of a growing town, and it has even stopped a president in his tracks.

**Location:** 30.8411, -83.9817

**Categories:** Individual oak trees, Individual trees in Georgia (U.S. state), Thomas County, Georgia, Tourist attractions in Georgia (U.S. state), Parks in Georgia (U.S. state)

## Introduction

In 1958, on his way to the Thomasville airport, President Dwight Eisenhower reportedly asked his driver to stop near the corner of Crawford and Monroe streets. In town on a hunting visit with his colleague George M. Humphrey, he climbed out, stepped onto the porch of Mrs. Rudolph Keyton's house, took a photograph of a tree and got back in the car. The tree was the Big Oak, a southern live oak that had already been spreading its limbs over this patch of southwest Georgia for close to three centuries. It is still there, still growing, and still stopping people in their tracks.

## A Seedling From 1680

The Big Oak dates to around 1680 and is described by Explore Georgia as one of the largest live oaks east of the Mississippi River. Its species, Quercus virginiana, grows from Texas to Florida to Virginia, and larger specimens tend to grow inland rather than along the coast, where live oaks stay smaller. This one grew enormous. When P.C. Andrews registered it with the Live Oak Society in 1936, it became only the forty-ninth tree on the society's rolls, measuring 49 feet 9 inches tall, 21 feet 6.5 inches around the trunk, with a limb span of 146 feet 8 inches. Nearly forty years later it had reached 68 feet in height, a girth of 22 feet and a span of 155 feet. The tree's longevity has drawn recognition several times over, including from the International Society for Agriculture in 1987.

## A Family Under the Branches

For most of the twentieth century, the Big Oak was somebody's backyard. John Albert Chastain and his wife owned the lot from 1906 until the mid-1960s, raising their family in a house beneath the tree for nearly sixty years. Mr. Chastain's mother could remember a time when the oak stood in a pond. In 1966, Elisabeth Ireland Poe, the owner of nearby Pebble Hill Plantation and a devoted guardian of Thomasville's heritage, joined with the city to acquire the property, which Pebble Hill says she generously donated, and turn it into a public park. That same year Mrs. Otto Carter of Meigs, Georgia, donated a Victorian-style gazebo, now restored and still the park's focal point. In 1969 the Thomasville Garden Club won a $5,000 appropriation, the Walter Hildebrand fund, which paid for a lattice-work brick wall on two sides of the lot that locals call "The Living Wall." In 1982 the city dedicated the site to Mrs. Poe's memory.

## Cables, Sprinklers and Low Clearance

Living at a downtown intersection has its hazards. Some of the Big Oak's limbs reach out just feet above the ground, and the tree now wears a web of support cables, along with an underground watering system, above-ground sprinklers and a tree surgeon on call. Trucks have been the persistent enemy. One struck the tree at 8:15 one morning in June 1973, doing minimal damage but earning its driver a charge for leaving the city truck route. In 1975 large trucks passing without enough clearance damaged two more limbs, and the Davey Tree Company worked for days to protect and maintain the tree. In 1978 crews surgically removed a 14-foot section of broken limb. "When we find a diseased or damaged part it should be cut off," explained parks superintendent Waymon Dekle, "or we could run the risk of losing the entire tree." On November 30, 2005, another truck wrenched two large limbs away, and police charged the driver with disobeying a traffic control device.

## Setting the Record Straight

A tree this old in a Deep South town attracts stories, and not all of them are true. Some have misidentified the Big Oak as a tree used for lynchings and other crimes against Thomasville's Black community. Jack Hadley, curator of Thomasville's Black History Museum, believes that claim is completely unsubstantiated. In a 2017 column in the local paper, Hadley wrote that in more than eighty years of living in and researching Thomasville, he had "never seen any proof, documentation and no one has provided me any documentation about the Big Oak Tree in downtown as a lynching tree."

## The Baby Big Oak

Today the oak mostly presides over happier occasions. Weddings are a favorite, with 23 held beneath its limbs in 1985 alone, and the gazebo hosts community receptions, Easter sunrise services, picnics and school field trips. In 2005, the tree did something it had not done in decades: it produced acorns. That fall, Carol Singletary, a granddaughter of John Albert Chastain, gathered more than a hundred of them and nursed them through germination into seedlings, which she shared with relatives connected to the old family lot. Her sister raised three. On Arbor Day 2009, Singletary presented one of them to Sue White of Pebble Hill Plantation, where it was planted and given a fitting name: "The Baby Big Oak." More than three centuries after its parent took root, the Big Oak had a new generation growing on the land of the woman who helped save it.
