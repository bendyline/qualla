# Lapham–Patterson House

Built in Thomasville, Georgia, by a survivor of the Great Chicago Fire, this deliberately asymmetrical Victorian cottage has two dozen doors to the outside, no square windows or doors, and a stained-glass window that casts a cow's head on the floor at the equinoxes.

## Introduction

Twice a year, the sun draws a cow on the floor. At the spring and fall equinoxes, light pours through a stained-glass window in the Lapham–Patterson House in Thomasville, Georgia, and passes through wooden fretwork cut with animal and abstract shapes. At the center of the colored pattern that lands on the floor sits the shadow of a cow's head, presumably that of Mrs. O'Leary's cow, the animal that legend blamed for the Great Chicago Fire of 1871. The joke was aimed at a fire the owner had lived through himself.

## A Winter Cottage for a Fire Survivor

Chicago businessman C.W. Lapham built the house between 1884 and 1885 as a winter cottage at what is now 626 North Dawson Street. Lapham, a Quaker, had been in the Great Chicago Fire, and the experience left him with a lasting fear of being trapped in a burning building. That fear shaped the design. By the Thomasville History Center's count, 24 of the house's 45 doors lead outdoors, and some accounts reckon at least 50 exits in all, a remarkable number for a three-story home. It was also thoroughly modern for its day, well appointed with a gas lighting system, hot and cold running water, indoor plumbing and modern closets. From the street the cottage glows a mellow yellow beneath a brick-red roof and chimneys, trimmed with fishscale shingles and an intricately designed porch.

## Nothing Is Square

What makes the house remarkable is not ornament but geometry. Its most significant feature is a complete and entirely intentional lack of symmetry. None of the windows, doors or closets are square, and at the core of the house is a hexagonal room. Even the building's placement is off-kilter by design. It was deliberately constructed slightly askew, so that sunlight would enter the third floor at the spring and fall equinoxes. Underfoot run inlaid floors of longleaf pine, and a double-flue chimney serves the rooms. The result feels playful rather than careless, a Victorian house in which every angle was a decision. Walking its rooms, from the dining room and formal parlor to the guest bedroom and sitting rooms, visitors find a home built to delight as much as to protect.

## The Cow in the Window

The finest joke waits in the gentlemen's parlor. The room has a small stage, and at its center is a stained-glass window. In the fretwork outside the room, over the balcony, the woodwork is cut into animal and amorphous shapes, and at the center is what is presumably the head of Mrs. O'Leary's cow. Most of the year it is simply decoration. At the equinoxes, though, the sunlight lines up, the stained glass throws its colorful pattern across the floor, and in the middle of it appears the shadow of the cow's head. If the cow is indeed Mrs. O'Leary's, it was a wry way for a man who had lived through the Chicago fire to carry its story into his southern retreat, written in light.

## A National Landmark on Dawson Street

The cottage has since been recognized well beyond Thomasville. It is a Georgia Historic Site and was designated a National Historic Landmark in the 1970s for its architecture, a significant example of the Victorian style. It also stands as a contributing building in the Dawson Street Residential Historic District, which is listed on the National Register of Historic Places. Visitors today can tour it with the Thomasville History Center, which operates the house, and walk through a home that still reflects the anxieties and humor of the man who built it.

## Flight Context

The Lapham–Patterson House stands at 30.8456 N, 83.9829 W on North Dawson Street in Thomasville, Georgia, in the Dawson Street Residential Historic District. From the air the house itself is small, so navigate by Thomasville's street grid and look for the yellow house with a brick-red roof. A low viewing altitude of 1,500 to 2,000 feet AGL, where permitted over the town, gives the best view. Thomasville Regional Airport (KTVI) is about 6 nm to the east-northeast, and Moultrie Municipal Airport (KMGR) about 17 nm to the north-northeast. Best seen in clear daytime conditions.
