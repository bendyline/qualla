# St. Marks Light

One of Florida's oldest light stations has guarded the shallow mouth of the St. Marks River since 1831, outlasting a rejected first tower, erosion, hurricanes and a Confederate attempt to blow it up.

**Location:** 30.0733, -84.1792

**Categories:** Lighthouses in Florida, National Register of Historic Places in Florida, Wakulla County, Florida, Maritime history, American Civil War

## Introduction

Samuel Crosby had reason to be afraid. He was keeper of the St. Marks Lighthouse when the Second Seminole War broke out in 1835, and news soon reached him of attacks on the lighthouses at Cape Florida and Mosquito Inlet. Crosby wrote to the authorities asking for a small detachment of troops to protect the tower and his family. They refused. He wrote again, asking only for a boat so his family could escape if trouble came. That was refused too. The attack never came, and Crosby kept the light burning for four more years at the mouth of the St. Marks River on Florida's Apalachee Bay.

## A Port in the Shallows

In the 1820s the town of St. Marks was an important port of entry, serving the prosperous planting region of Middle Florida and parts of South Georgia. Growers hauled their crops down by wagon on an early road from Tallahassee, then the territorial capital; that route would later be improved by the Tallahassee Railroad, one of the state's first two railroads. At St. Marks the goods went aboard boats bound for New Orleans or St. Augustine. Getting out to sea was the problem. Both Apalachee Bay and the St. Marks River were shallow in many places, and boats ran aground or bogged down in the muddy shallows often enough to make a light a pressing need.

## Walls That Were Not Solid

In 1828 territorial Governor William Pope Duval pressed the case in a letter to territorial delegate Joseph M. White, who took it to Senator Levi Woodbury's Committee on Commerce. On May 23, 1828, the House passed an act authorizing a lighthouse and appropriating $6,000. That proved too little, and the sum was raised to $14,000. By mid-1829 Winslow Lewis of Boston had a contract to build the tower for $11,765, but when it was finished, customs collector Jesse H. Williams refused to accept it because its walls were hollow. He wanted them solid. Calvin Knowlton was brought in to rebuild the tower, and in 1831 Williams, satisfied at last, accepted the work. That year Crosby, appointed keeper the year before, lit its whale-oil lamps for the first time.

## Moving Back from the Water

The sea proved a more persistent enemy than any army. By 1842 erosion threatened the tower, and Winslow Lewis, the Boston builder whose hollow-walled tower had been turned away, was called back, this time to move it. His contractors removed the lantern and lighting apparatus, tore down the original brick tower and built a new one on a site farther inland, then reinstalled the old lantern. The new tower soon faced its test. It survived the destructive hurricanes of the 1840s and 1850s, including the disastrous storm of September 1843 that destroyed most of the town of Port Leon and caused major damage to St. Marks itself.

## Shelled and Sabotaged

The Civil War brought a different kind of danger. The lighting apparatus was removed so that the tower could not guide the Union blockade ships patrolling Apalachee Bay, and in 1865 Confederate troops were stationed nearby. That March a Federal fleet of 16 ships appeared offshore and began shelling the area around the lighthouse in preparation for a landing. As they retreated, the Confederates tried to blow up the tower to keep Union forces from using it as a lookout. They failed to bring it down, but the damage was serious enough that the tower needed major repairs after the war. It was heightened, lifting the light to its present focal plane of 82 feet above sea level, and the light was relit in 1867.

## Relit

The Coast Guard automated the light in 1960 and reportedly spent $150,000 in 2000 to stabilize the tower. Around 2000 or 2001 the fourth-order Fresnel lens was switched off in favor of a solar-powered beacon mounted outside the lantern room, though the old lens stayed in place for more than a decade. In October 2013 the Coast Guard transferred the lighthouse to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, which operates the surrounding St. Marks National Wildlife Refuge. In 2019 a replica of the original fourth-order Fresnel lens was installed in the tower, and the light that once kept planters' cargo boats off the mudflats shines again, seasonally, over Apalachee Bay.

## Flight Context

St. Marks Light stands at about 30.073 N, 84.179 W on the east side of the mouth of the St. Marks River, where it meets Apalachee Bay, inside the St. Marks National Wildlife Refuge. The light sits 82 feet above sea level, and the lone tower at the river mouth is the landmark to look for. A low pass along the coast at 1,500-2,500 feet gives a good view; respect wildlife refuge overflight guidance. Wakulla County Airport (FAA identifier 2J0, no ICAO code) near Panacea is about 12 nm to the west-southwest, and Tallahassee International (KTLH) is about 21 nm to the north-northwest. Summer thunderstorms and coastal haze are common.
