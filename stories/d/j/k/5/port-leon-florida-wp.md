# Port Leon, Florida

A booming cotton port on the St. Marks River, Port Leon had a railroad, a newspaper and a new county to govern when a hurricane's storm surge erased it in 1843, about six years after it was founded.

**Location:** 30.1311, -84.1950

**Categories:** Former populated places in Wakulla County, Florida, Former county seats in Florida, Former municipalities in Florida, Ghost towns in Florida, Former populated places in Leon County, Florida

## Introduction

For six months in 1843, Port Leon was the seat of a brand-new county. On March 11 it was chosen to govern freshly created Wakulla County, and it had a railroad, wharves, warehouses and a newspaper to show for its ambitions. On September 13 a strong hurricane brought a storm surge of up to about 10 feet and destroyed the town. Its people did not stay to rebuild. Today the State Library and Archives of Florida lists Port Leon as an "extinct city," and only remnants mark the east bank of the St. Marks River where it stood.

## Cotton Down to the River

Port Leon was founded in 1837 (a state historical marker says 1838), largely by people who had left the neighboring port of Magnolia, and it lay about three miles south of St. Marks on the east bank of the St. Marks River. At the time the land belonged to Leon County. Like St. Marks and Magnolia, it lived on cotton. The bales came down from the plantations of the Red Hills Region of Florida and Georgia, including those of Leon County, where planters built their cotton operations on the labor of enslaved people. In 1839 the Tallahassee Railroad reached Port Leon, and by then freight wagons and trains were hauling some 30,000 to 40,000 bales a year to the area's ports. A bridge across the St. Marks River linked the railroad to the waterfront, where cotton was loaded onto ships bound for the east coast of the United States.

## Six Busy Years

For a short while, Port Leon had everything a young river town could want. A post office opened in 1840, and the town incorporated in 1841. At its peak several hundred people lived here, supporting eight to ten businesses, wharves and warehouses, a hotel, two taverns, a newspaper and an annual fair. Among its successful citizens was Daniel Ladd, who had married into the Hamlin family from Maine, the founders of Magnolia. Prosperity came with danger, though. In 1841, the same year the town incorporated, Port Leon and the other communities in the area endured a severe yellow fever epidemic. The town survived that ordeal, and two years later it reached its high point when it became the seat of the newly created Wakulla County.

## The Surge of September 1843

The hurricane that struck on September 13, 1843, drove a storm surge of up to about 10 feet into the area. Port Leon was destroyed outright, while nearby Magnolia and St. Marks suffered heavy damage. Port Leon was abandoned, and many of its residents moved inland to the new town of Newport. The whole life of the place, from founding to ruin, had lasted only about six years. What had been a county seat, a railroad town and a busy cotton wharf now survives mainly as a name in the state's list of extinct cities, and as scattered remnants along the quiet river it once served.

## Flight Context

The site of Port Leon lies at 30.1311 N, 84.1950 W on the east bank of the St. Marks River, about three miles south of the town of St. Marks in Wakulla County, south of Tallahassee. Little of the town remains, so navigate by the river itself, following it downstream from St. Marks. A low viewing altitude of 1,500 to 2,500 feet MSL gives the best look at the riverbanks. Tallahassee International Airport (KTLH) is about 18 nm to the north-northwest. Best seen in clear daytime conditions.
