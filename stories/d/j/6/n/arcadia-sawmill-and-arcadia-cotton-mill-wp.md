# Arcadia Sawmill and Arcadia Cotton Mill

Outside Milton, Florida, a boardwalk crosses the swamp-swallowed ruins of an antebellum water-powered mill complex that once ran what is described as the state's largest cotton mill on the labor of enslaved people.

**Location:** 30.6097, -87.0842

**Categories:** National Register of Historic Places in Santa Rosa County, Florida, Archaeological sites, Industrial history, Slavery in the United States, Museums in Santa Rosa County, Florida, Cotton mills in the United States

## Introduction

For more than a century, the swamp a mile southwest of Milton, Florida, kept the secret. Fire had gutted the cotton mill in 1855, the dam was reportedly breached or blown up during the Civil War so Union troops could not turn the site into a lumber mill, and the site was left to the swamp. Then, in 1964, Warren Weekes rediscovered the site. What lay under the undergrowth was the footprint of an industrial village: the Arcadia Sawmill and Arcadia Cotton Mill, also known as the Escambia Manufacturing Company, a water-powered complex that had once made this corner of the Florida Panhandle a place of saws, looms and machinery.

## A Dam, a Flume and a Mule Railroad

The land first belonged to Juan de la Rua, the Spanish mayor of Pensacola, who built a small dam here. In 1828 Joseph Forsyth, founder of the nearby community of Bagdad, bought the property, enlarged the dam and built a sawmill, going into partnership with the brothers Andrew and Ezekiel Simpson. The manufacturing tycoon Timothy Twitchell set up his own large complex near Arcadia, making shingles, silk and buckets. Getting the product out was the challenge. Lumber moved to the nearby Blackwater River by log flume or by mule railroad, operated by the Arcadia Railroad Company, which Forsyth, the Simpsons and Twitchell founded in 1838. It was an early railroad for territorial Florida, which did not become a state until 1845.

## Cotton and Forced Labor

Transportation troubles drove Forsyth and the Simpsons to move their lumber mills to Bagdad in 1840. In their place rose a cotton mill run by the Forsyth and Simpson firm and worked by enslaved people. By the early 1850s it was described as the largest cotton mill in the new state of Florida, part of what has been called the largest 19th-century water-powered industrial complex in northwest Florida. Arcadia grew into a small community with shops, a quarry, living quarters, a tannery, a blacksmith, a well and a gristmill. The site's stewards describe it as an industrial village of mixed ethnicity, where enslaved African Americans and Anglo-American laborers worked under Anglo-American owners and managers. The short histories of the site name its owners and partners, but not the enslaved people whose forced labor ran the mill. One account describes living quarters at the site for more than 40 enslaved women and their families, and their lives are part of what these ruins hold.

## Boardwalk Through the Ruins

The decline was swift. Joseph Forsyth died on March 10, 1855, and later that year fire largely destroyed the cotton mill, which was abandoned. Twitchell moved his operations to Bagdad as well. During the Civil War the dam is believed to have been deliberately breached or blown up so that Union troops, desperate for timber, could not turn Arcadia into a lumber mill. The site was added to the National Register of Historic Places on August 3, 1987. Today it is the Arcadia Mill Archaeological Site, managed by the University of West Florida Historic Trust (formerly West Florida Historic Preservation), which also runs the Pensacola Museum of History. The ticketed visitor center and its museum exhibit are open only on Fridays and Saturdays, while a free, elevated three-fourths-mile boardwalk, open daily from sunrise to sunset, carries visitors over the archaeological remains and into the adjacent swamp.

## Flight Context

The Arcadia Mill site lies at about 30.610 N, 87.084 W, roughly a mile southwest of Milton in Santa Rosa County, Florida. The ruins sit under swamp forest and are hard to see from the air; from 2,000 feet, use Milton and the Blackwater River to the east as your references. This is busy naval training airspace: Whiting Field Naval Air Station North (KNSE) is about 7 nm north-northeast, with several naval outlying fields close by, including Spencer Nolf (FAA identifier NRQ) about 3 nm west-northwest. Peter Prince Field at Milton (FAA identifier 2R4, no ICAO code) is roughly 4 nm east-northeast, and Pensacola International Airport (KPNS) about 10 nm southwest. Expect Gulf Coast haze and summer thunderstorms.
