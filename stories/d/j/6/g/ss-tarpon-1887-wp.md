# SS Tarpon (1887)

For three decades the steamer Tarpon was the Gulf Coast's weekly boat between Mobile and Carrabelle, until a late-summer gale in 1937 sent her to the bottom off Panama City, where she now rests as one of Florida's Underwater Archaeological Preserves.

## Introduction

At 2 a.m., the chief engineer could no longer keep ahead of the water. A leak in the bow was widening as the seas rose, and the Tarpon, heavy with 200 tons of cargo, was still short of Panama City. The forecast had promised calm. By dawn the wind was at gale force. The steamer that had worked this coast for decades, as familiar to its ports as a mail train, was about to become a shipwreck that divers now visit 7.8 nautical miles off Panama City, Florida.

## An Iron Hull from Delaware

She began life far from the Gulf. The shipbuilders Pusey and Jones built the twin-screw steamship at Wilmington, Delaware, in 1887 and christened her Naugatuck. She was 130 feet long with a 26-foot beam, her hull made of iron and her superstructure and passenger areas of wood, with twin steam engines turning iron screws. In 1889 she was bought by Henry Plant, whose railroad empire terminating at Tampa was one of the largest conglomerates in the United States. In 1891 the ship went back to her builders, where her hull was lengthened by 30 feet and she emerged with a new name: Tarpon. In 1902 she was sold to The Pensacola, St Andrews, and Gulf Steamship Company, and her real career began.

## The Weekly Boat

Captain Willis Green Barrow took command after the 1902 sale and stayed at her helm for some 30 years. Under him, Tarpon settled into a rhythm that coastal towns could set their calendars by: weekly runs linking Mobile, Pensacola, St. Andrews Bay, Apalachicola and Carrabelle. Freight and passengers moved along the northern Gulf in her holds and cabins, and Barrow is estimated to have made the run more than 1,700 times. For three decades, the arrival of the Tarpon was simply part of the life of the Panhandle's ports. The ship and her captain grew old together. By her final voyage, Barrow was 81 years old.

## The Last Run

In late August 1937, Tarpon left Mobile with 200 tons of cargo, called at Pensacola and headed east for Panama City. The forecasters had predicted calm seas, but the wind freshened after Barrow retired to his cabin for the night, leaving the second mate at the helm. By 2 a.m. the bow leak was gaining. Just before dawn on September 1, according to the National Park Service, the wind reached gale force, and the pounding seas poured through the wooden bulkheads until she listed to starboard. When Barrow gave the order to abandon ship, she had already settled by the stern. Only one boat got free, and it capsized, drowning the cook's wife. Those on deck were washed away, the old captain among them; he did not survive the day. According to Florida's preserve program and the National Park Service, eighteen of the thirty-one people aboard are believed to have lost their lives, some of them roustabouts trapped in the cargo hold who were never identified, though other accounts put the toll higher. The oiler Adley Baker spent some 25 hours in the water before he reached shore.

## A Museum Ninety-Five Feet Down

Tarpon came to rest in 95 feet of water on a sand and live-rock bottom, less than 10 miles from shore. The wreck that marked a tragedy has since become home to a crowd of sea life. Colorful angelfish and graceful jellyfish drift over the site, along with jacks, grunts and spadefish, while stony corals take hold on what remains of the ship. In 1997 the wreck was dedicated as the sixth Florida Underwater Archaeological Preserve, one of the state's "museums in the sea" open to divers, and in May 2001 it was added to the U.S. National Register of Historic Places. Divers who descend to it today are visiting a working ship's final port of call, and a memorial to those who never came home from it.

Nothing is visible from the air; the flight is about the setting.
