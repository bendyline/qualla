# Eglin AFB Site C-6

Built in the Florida Panhandle to catch Soviet nuclear weapons arriving from orbit, the AN/FPS-85 at Eglin Site C-6 is described as the first large phased array radar and now helps the Space Force keep count of everything circling the Earth.

**Location:** 30.5733, -86.2150

**Categories:** United States Space Surveillance Network, Equipment of the United States Space Force, Military electronics of the United States, Cold War, Radar stations

## Introduction

Picture a basketball drifting at the height of geosynchronous orbit, some 35,700 kilometers above the Earth. The U.S. Space Force says the radar at Eglin AFB Site C-6 can detect it. The AN/FPS-85, packed into a single transmitter and receiver building on the Florida Panhandle, puts out a peak radiated power of 32 megawatts, and the Space Force claims it is the most powerful radar in the world. When it began operating in 1969, it was described as the first large phased array radar, and it was built for a far grimmer purpose than cataloguing satellites.

## A Threat From Any Direction

In the 1960s, as Western nuclear missiles stood on its borders in Turkey, Europe and Asia, the Soviet Union developed a way to deliver nuclear weapons from Earth orbit: the Fractional Orbital Bombardment System, or FOBS. America's early-warning radars, such as BMEWS, looked north, because a conventional intercontinental ballistic missile from the Soviet Union would take the shortest great-circle route over the North Pole. An orbiting weapon broke that geometry. It could circle the planet before beginning its reentry and arrive from any direction at all. Soviet premier Nikita Khrushchev alluded to the developing capability in a speech in March 1962. That same spring, on April 2, the contract was signed for the AN/FPS-85, a phased array radar and computer system originally designed to detect and track orbital nuclear missiles.

## Steering a Beam Without Moving

The road to Site C-6 ran through Sputnik. After the Soviet satellite's launch on October 4, 1957, the Air Force's Missile Test Center at Patrick AFB set up a project to observe and collect data on satellites, and Eglin had its first satellite tracking facility operating that fall. Bendix Corporation built a linear array at its Baltimore facility, then a prototype wideband phased array paired with an IBM computer. In November 1960, its AN/FPS-46 Electronically Steerable Array Radar began transmitting, described as the first full-size pencil-beam phased-array radar system. As the name says, the beam was steered electronically rather than by swinging a dish. Bendix's success with that prototype led to the far larger FPS-85.

## Fire Before First Light

Construction began in October 1962 on a system that anticipated numerous tube failures and arranged for a large number of people to handle replacements. Radar testing was planned for May 1965. On January 5 of that year, arcing ignited dielectric material inside the transmitter and receiver building, and the fire almost totally destroyed the building and its contents. The system, at least, was insured. Rebuilding of the separate faces for transmitting and receiving began in 1967, and the replacements belonged to a newer era: low-loss diode phase shifters took over from the destroyed analog ones, and transistor receivers stood in for vacuum tubes. After technical problems, the completed site was turned over to Air Force Systems Command on September 20, 1968. Running its software in FORTRAN, it became operational during the winter of 1968 and 1969.

## Watching the Sea as Well as the Sky

Almost from the start, Site C-6 had a second job. In December 1965, NORAD decided the future radar would watch for submarine-launched ballistic missiles on an "on-call" basis, and by 1972 a fifth of its surveillance capability was dedicated to searching for them. From 1971 to 1984 the site hosted the Alternate Space Surveillance Center. It passed through Aerospace Defense Command and Strategic Air Command before moving to Space Command in 1983. In November 1988, a range-extension upgrade that integrated many pulses gave the radar a deep-space role. By 1994, when each transmitter module still used six vacuum tubes, Southwest Research Institute was redesigning the transmitters to replace five of them with solid-state components, and a PC-based system replaced the original central monitoring system that tested for failing modules.

## Counting a Crowded Sky

By 2002 the radar was tracking more than 95 percent of all Earth satellites daily. By 2011 it was logging 16 million observations of satellites a year, about 30 percent of the Space Surveillance Network's total workload, and its data had gone into a computer model of the February 2009 satellite collision. In the Mission Operations Center, personnel work at "a screen with [space] objects assigned numbers, similar to an air traffic control screen." Once every second, the radar sends out a calibration pulse for a nearby monitoring station to process. The site has its own power plant, fire station and two water wells, along with a softball field, a gymnasium and a helipad for logistics or emergency evacuation. The U.S. Space Force became an independent service in December 2019, and Site C-6 and its squadron became a Space Force facility; the squadron, the 20th Space Control Squadron until it was renamed the 20th Space Surveillance Squadron in 2022, is part of Mission Delta 2 and still catalogues the objects the Combined Space Operations Center tracks in orbit.

## Flight Context

Coordinates: 30.5733°N, 86.215°W, on the Eglin Air Force Base reservation about 6 miles from Freeport, Florida, north of the eastern end of Choctawhatchee Bay. The radar is housed in one large transmitter/receiver building with separate transmitting and receiving faces. Much of the Eglin reservation lies beneath restricted military airspace, so check charts, stay clear of restricted areas and observe from a respectful distance at 3,000 feet or higher. Nearby airports: Destin-Fort Walton Beach / Eglin AFB (KVPS), Bob Sikes Airport in Crestview (KCEW), Destin Executive (KDTS) and Northwest Florida Beaches International (KECP).
