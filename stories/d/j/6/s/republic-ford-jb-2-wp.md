# Republic-Ford JB-2

America's near-identical copy of the German V-1 never flew in combat, but from a Florida beach leased for a dollar a year it helped carry the United States toward its first operational cruise missiles.

**Location:** 30.3640, -86.3150

**Categories:** Cruise missiles of the United States, World War II guided missiles of the United States, Pulsejet-powered aircraft, Republic aircraft, Ford aircraft, Military history of Florida

## Introduction

For one dollar a year, the United States Army Air Forces leased a stretch of Florida Gulf shoreline from the Coffeen family and filled it with launch ramps. Between October 1944 and October 1945, 233 flying bombs were fired from that beach, then known as Range 64, out over open water. Each was a Republic-Ford JB-2, and each was very nearly a copy of Germany's V-1. The war ended before a single one was fired in anger, yet this American twin of an enemy weapon helped lead the way to the first operational cruise missile the United States would build.

## A Wreck on Bornholm

The American chapter of the story began on a Danish island. On August 22, 1942, a Danish naval officer discovered an early test version of the V-1 that had crashed on Bornholm, in the Baltic Sea, roughly 120 kilometers (75 miles) northeast of the German test launch ramp at Peenemünde. A photograph and a detailed sketch of that prototype, the Fieseler Fi 103 V83, were sent to Britain, setting off months of intelligence-gathering that traced the weapon to the top-secret test site on Germany's Baltic coast. As more evidence arrived through aerial photography and sources inside Germany, the United States decided in 1943 to develop a jet-powered flying bomb of its own. The effort spawned a whole family of Army Air Forces "Jet Bomb" projects, numbered JB-1 through JB-10. The JB-2 would prove the most successful of them.

## Copying Was the Easy Part

By September 8, 1944, Republic Aviation had assembled the first of thirteen complete JB-2s, reverse-engineered from material received at Wright Field that July. The copy differed from the V-1 only slightly in its dimensions, giving it 60.7 square feet of wing area against the German weapon's 55. After the first tests, American engineers replaced the V-1's preset guidance with command guidance: a radar beacon in the missile and radio commands from a tracking SCR-584 radar. Building it became a national collaboration. Republic's lines were full of P-47 Thunderbolts, so Willys-Overland built the airframes. Ford Motor Company copied the V-1's Argus pulsejet as the PJ31, Jack and Heintz of Cleveland made the guidance and flight controls, Monsanto worked on a better launching system, and Northrop supplied the launch sleds. The first order called for 1,000 missiles, then 1,000 a month, and deliveries began in January 1945.

## A Dollar a Year on the Dunes

The proving happened on the Florida Panhandle. Range 64, also called C-64, lay 22 miles east of Eglin Army Air Field on the coast near what is now Sandestin, and its test range stretched 150 miles offshore. It was a self-sufficient little world of launch and missile-preparation facilities, barracks, a mess hall and offices, with a training unit nearby for the men learning to field the weapon in combat. Nine launch ramps of varying length and propulsion eventually rose there, because while copying the V-1 proved easy, inventing a way to launch it proved very difficult. The 1st Proving Ground Group fired the first JB-2 from Range 64 on October 12, 1944. Others fell from an MB-17G bomber flying out of Hurlburt Field, echoing the German air launches of V-1s from Heinkel He 111s. Tests were cancelled in November 1945, everything was to be dismantled, removed or abandoned by December 12, and the land went back to the Coffeens.

## A Weapon in Search of a War

At one point, proponents envisioned producing 75,000 JB-2s, an absurd figure: launching them at the presumed rate would have consumed a quarter of the available shipping. Carl Spaatz, commander of the U.S. Strategic Air Forces in Europe, proposed a far smaller role as a harassment weapon for days when weather kept the bombers home. The real force behind the rush was a turf war between the Army Air Forces and the Army Ground Forces over who would control guided missiles, the opening round of a struggle that lasted until the late 1950s. The Air Forces won that round partly because the JB-2 had wings and flew, and it was decided the weapon "should be placed in combat, needed or not, as an insurance measure for AAF claims." After Germany surrendered in May 1945, planners aimed the JB-2 at Japan as part of a 180-day bombardment meant to precede the invasion. The war's end cancelled that plan. Production stopped on September 15, after 1,391 had been built.

## Rust, Loons and Matadors

Peace did not retire the JB-2. As essentially the only operational guided missile in the American inventory, it became a flying laboratory. Launches resumed on Santa Rosa Island in March 1947, from sites now recorded as archaeological sites 8OK246 and 8OK248, and a single crew could fire up to 30 missiles in a day. The Navy's version, named the Loon after the aquatic bird, was launched from a submarine off Point Mugu, California, on February 12, 1947. At Holloman in New Mexico, a JB-2 trailer ramp became the first step toward the Martin MGM-1 Matador, the first operational surface-to-surface cruise missile built by the United States. In mid-1992, military crews on an Air Force-owned section of Santa Rosa Island uncovered a well-preserved JB-2 wreck, a rare find among crash sites that were mostly flaky rust. Surviving JB-2s sit in museums around the country, among them reportedly the Air Force Armament Museum at Eglin, a short drive from the beaches where hundreds of its siblings were launched.

## Flight Context

Article coordinates: 30.364°N, 86.315°W, an area-level point on the Gulf coast of the Florida Panhandle in the Topsail Hill and Four Mile Village area near Sandestin, where the range is described below. The wartime Range 64 launch site, 22 miles east of Eglin, is now Topsail Hill Preserve State Park, the Coffeen Nature Preserve and Four Mile Village near Sandestin; the postwar launch sites lie farther west on Santa Rosa Island. A low pass along the beachfront at 1,500 to 2,500 feet shows the coastline the JB-2s once flew out over. Nearby airports: Destin Executive (KDTS), Destin-Fort Walton Beach / Eglin AFB (KVPS), Northwest Florida Beaches International (KECP) and Hurlburt Field (KHRT, military). Eglin's military airspace covers much of this coast, so check restricted areas before flying.
