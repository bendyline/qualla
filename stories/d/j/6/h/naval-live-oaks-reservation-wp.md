# Naval Live Oaks Reservation

Near Gulf Breeze, Florida, the United States bought a forest in 1828 to grow timber for its warships, creating what the National Park Service calls the nation's first tree farm; today its live oaks shelter trails within Gulf Islands National Seashore.

**Location:** 30.3642, -87.1308

**Categories:** Archaeological sites in Florida, National Register of Historic Places in Santa Rosa County, Florida, Wilderness areas of Florida, Protected areas established in 1971, Protected areas of Santa Rosa County, Florida, Gulf Islands National Seashore, 1971 establishments in Florida

## Introduction

In 1828 the United States government went shopping for warships and came home with a forest. The land near present-day Gulf Breeze, Florida, was bought for its live oaks, the strong timber that shipbuilders valued, and in January 1829 it began operating as what the National Park Service calls the country's first national tree farm. The Navy's need for wooden hulls has long since passed. The oaks have not. Today the Naval Live Oaks Reservation, also known as Deer Point Live Oaks Reservation or Deer Point Plantation, is part of Gulf Islands National Seashore.

## A Forest in Federal Hands

The reasoning behind the purchase was strategic. The government bought this land to reserve its valuable live oak resources for shipbuilders, keeping a supply of ship timber in public hands, and President John Quincy Adams is credited with authorizing the tree farm. It was a striking idea: a nation deciding that a stand of trees was worth protecting because its fleet might one day depend on it. Running the farm fell to its superintendent, Henry Marie Brackenridge, who lived on the property and did more than guard the trees. He experimented with cultivating live oaks, trying to coax along a resource the country expected to need. That work has earned him a notable, if carefully hedged, distinction: he was perhaps the United States' first federal forester. On this stretch of Gulf Coast shoreline, trees were being treated as a public resource to be studied and tended, not simply cut.

## The Wood Behind Old Ironsides

Live oak shipbuilding was well established in America by 1700, and some of the young nation's famous vessels were built of it, among them the USS Constitution, launched in 1797 with a frame of southern live oak. The Constitution saw action against the British during the War of 1812 and earned the nickname "Old Ironsides" for the strength of her live oak construction. Iron and steel warships eventually diminished the need for wooden ship timber. Yet the story took one more turn. In the 1920s, live oak timbers from the Pensacola area, a stockpile cut in the 1850s, proved useful in the restoration of the USS Constitution, a National Historic Landmark. The kind of timber that once built the Navy's ships helped restore Old Ironsides herself.

## Trails Beneath the Canopy

The National Park Service preserved the forest community here as part of Gulf Islands National Seashore on January 8, 1971, and on September 28, 1998, the reservation was added to the U.S. National Register of Historic Places. It now covers more than 1,300 acres owned by the Department of the Interior. U.S. Route 98 cuts through the southern portion. South of the highway are the seashore's park headquarters, which is closed to the public, a picnic area and water access to Santa Rosa Sound; the area's former visitor center has closed, and its exhibits were slated to move to Fort Pickens. To the north, a trail climbs to a bluff that overlooks Pensacola Bay. About seven miles of trails wind through the park, under the branches of trees once held in reserve for the Navy.

## Flight Context

The Naval Live Oaks Reservation lies at approximately 30.364 N, 87.131 W on the peninsula east of Gulf Breeze, Florida, between Pensacola Bay to the north and Santa Rosa Sound to the south. From 1,500-2,500 ft MSL, look for an unbroken block of dark green woodland straddling U.S. Route 98 just east of Gulf Breeze's neighborhoods, with Pensacola Bay's bluff on its north edge. Pensacola International Airport (KPNS) is about 7 nm north-northwest and the reservation sits within Pensacola's busy terminal airspace, so contact approach control before overflying. Naval Air Station Pensacola (KNPA) is about 10 nm west, and several Navy outlying training fields lie to the north and northeast, so expect heavy military training traffic. Summer afternoons often bring thunderstorms along the coast.
