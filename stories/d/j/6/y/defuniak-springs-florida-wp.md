# DeFuniak Springs, Florida

A railroad resort built around a nearly perfectly round spring-fed lake, DeFuniak Springs became a Victorian Chautauqua town of lectures, colleges and books, and the place where Florida's teachers first organized.

**Location:** 30.7167, -86.1167

**Categories:** County seats in Florida, Cities in Walton County, Florida, Chautauqua, Florida Panhandle, Populated places established in the 1880s

## Introduction

Lake DeFuniak looks as though someone planted a compass point in the middle of town and swung a circle. Fed by natural springs, it is billed as one of two spring-fed lakes in the world that are nearly perfectly round, and in the 1880s the railroad men who founded DeFuniak Springs made it the centerpiece of their plan. They were not building a farm town or a rail yard. They were building a destination, a place where vacationers would come to sit in lectures and classes along the shore of that improbably round lake.

## Laid Out by the Railroad

The town began as a resort development by officers of the Pensacola and Atlantic Railroad, a subsidiary of the Louisville and Nashville. In the 1880s the P&A was organized to link the L&N's terminus at Pensacola with the western end of a predecessor of the Seaboard Air Line Railroad at River Junction, now Chattahoochee, stitching the Florida Panhandle together by rail. The new town took its name from Frederick R. DeFuniak, a vice-president of the L&N. Like much of Northwest Florida, it was settled mainly by Scots who came from Virginia and the Carolinas. The railroad's mark is still visible downtown, where the former Louisville and Nashville depot now houses the Walton County Heritage Museum.

## Four Thousand Seats by the Water

To give their resort a purpose, the developers enlisted the cooperation of the Chautauqua Movement, the adult-education phenomenon that brought lectures and classes to vacationers. On the shore of Lake DeFuniak, at the center of town, rose the Chautauqua Hall of Brotherhood, with an auditorium, completed around 1909 to 1910, that seated 4,000 people. Seminars and classes were held there for people on vacation, who came to learn as well as to rest. The great auditorium did not survive the 20th century. Hurricane Eloise severely damaged it in 1975, and it was razed. The westerly portion of the building, facing Circle Drive, remained in use, and in 2003 a charitable foundation, the Chautauqua Hall of Brotherhood Foundation, launched a capital campaign to restore the historic building. The building, at 95 Circle Drive, was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1972 and has housed a branch of the Walton County Chamber of Commerce; restoration has proceeded in phases since 2016, and as of October 2025 its third phase, focused on the second floor, was under way.

## A Town That Went to School

Learning was the local industry. In the late 19th century, DeFuniak Springs gained a college and a private high school, Palmer College and Palmer Academy, along with a technical school, the Thomas Industrial Institute, and a teacher training school, Florida Normal College. That last one is reported to have later become part of Florida State University. The others closed during the Great Depression, when financial strains became too great, though a College Avenue that once led to Palmer College still runs through town. In 1886, DeFuniak Springs hosted a meeting that changed public education across the state: teachers from all over Florida gathered here and formed the Florida Education Association. That union remains the state's predominant voice for educators and is affiliated with both the National Education Association and the American Federation of Teachers.

## The Little Sister on Circle Drive

In 1887, a group of women formed the Ladies Library Association with a clear ambition: a library that would be the "little sister" to the Chautauqua center, the university and the school. They chose a plot of land beside the Chautauqua grounds and signed a lease for ninety-nine years. The library they founded, now the Walton-DeFuniak Library, opened on December 20, 1887, and it is often described as the oldest continuously run library in Florida, though historians dispute which Florida library is the oldest. In a town founded on the idea of self-improvement, it has outlasted Palmer College, Palmer Academy and the great Chautauqua auditorium itself.

## Crossroads of the Panhandle

Today DeFuniak Springs is the county seat of Walton County, home to 5,919 people at the 2020 census, up from 5,177 in 2010. It serves as a hub for rural communities such as Paxton, Mossy Head and Ponce de Leon. Interstate 10 runs just south of the city, U.S. 90 passes through town as Nelson Avenue, and U.S. 331 carries traffic 26 miles south toward the beaches near Santa Rosa Beach. Not every chapter has been a gentle one: a Perdue Farms chicken processing plant closed here in April 2004. Up in the Paxton area rises Britton Hill, the highest point in Florida. Down in the Historic District, the round lake still sits at the heart of things, with the surviving portion of the Chautauqua hall and the old library beside it.

## Flight Context

Coordinates: 30.7167°N, 86.1167°W, in the Florida Panhandle along Interstate 10 and U.S. Routes 90 and 331. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet, look for Lake DeFuniak, the nearly perfectly round lake at the center of town. Nearby airports: DeFuniak Springs Airport (FAA identifier 54J, no ICAO code), about two nautical miles west-northwest of downtown; Bob Sikes Airport in Crestview (KCEW) to the west; and Northwest Florida Beaches International (KECP) to the southeast. Check charts for Eglin's military airspace to the southwest. The climate is humid subtropical, with abundant summer rain and exposure to tropical storms.
