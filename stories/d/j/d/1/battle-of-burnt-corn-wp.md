# Battle of Burnt Corn

A botched ambush at a creek ford in 1813 turned a Muscogee civil war into a regional war with the United States, and set in motion the chain of violence that ran through Fort Mims to Horseshoe Bend.

**Location:** 31.1896, -87.1259

**Categories:** Battles of the Creek War, Battles of the War of 1812, Native American history of Alabama, Battles in Alabama, Escambia County, Alabama

## Introduction

Colonel James Caller organized the ambush, commanded it, and then vanished. For fifteen days after the Battle of Burnt Corn, the commander of the Mississippi Territory's forces east of the Pearl River was lost in the woods of what is now southern Alabama with one of his majors, until a search party finally found them. His embarrassment was the least of the fight's consequences. The skirmish at Burnt Corn Creek on July 27, 1813, lasted only a few hours and left a handful of dead, yet it transformed a civil war within the Muscogee Nation into a wider war that would end with the United States forcing most of the Muscogee people from their homeland.

## A Nation Pulled Two Ways

The Muscogee, also called the Creek, had long lived across most of present-day Alabama, parts of western Georgia and northern Florida, in Upper Towns along the Alabama, Tallapoosa and Coosa Rivers and Lower Towns along the Chattahoochee. Under the guidance of U.S. Indian agent Benjamin Hawkins, a centralized Creek National Council had taken shape, and it began selling tribal hunting lands to pay Muscogee debts. Many were angered. The old economy of hunting and trade was giving way to farming, and most Muscogee were forced to trade with the U.S. government or private firms such as Panton, Leslie and Company. One faction backed the centralized council and cooperation with the United States. The other, the Red Sticks, opposed the encroachment of American settlers and championed a return to the traditional Muscogee way of life. In the summer of 1813 a party of Red Sticks traveled to Spanish Pensacola to obtain weapons and supplies for that fight.

## An Army of Captains

Caller mustered the 15th Regiment of Mississippi Territorial Volunteers without waiting for orders from General Ferdinand Claiborne and set out from St. Stephens to intercept the returning party. Samuel Dale's militia joined him at Fort Glass. On July 24 the column crossed the Alabama River near present-day Gainestown and picked up militia from what is now Baldwin County, many of them Muscogee of mixed European and Muscogee ancestry, commanded by Captain Dixon Bailey. About 180 mounted men carried rifles, shotguns and muskets. Because many of them, Caller included, had political ambitions, the force was crowded with officers, and on the morning of the battle it elected William McGrew lieutenant colonel along with four new majors. Scouts found the Red Sticks at a ford where the Wolf Trail, a path toward Pensacola, crossed Burnt Corn Creek. They were preparing their midday meal, unaware that the Americans were approaching.

## Noon at the Ford

The Americans charged on horseback. In the first rush they killed a Muscogee woman and an enslaved African who was fleeing toward them, two people the surviving account does not name. One American fell, and eight to ten Red Stick packhorses were quickly seized. For up to an hour the fighting was scattered, until the Red Sticks were driven into a canebrake. There they rallied and began firing on the attackers, who were bunched on an exposed pine ridge with little cover. According to Colonel G. W. Creagh, who was there, Bailey's men pressed the pursuit while many of Caller's troops turned to looting the captured supplies. Red Sticks under McQueen and High-head Jim struck the looters, who fled. After two more hours, Caller ordered a retreat, covered by a small rearguard under Dale, Bailey and Benjamin Smoot. The Red Sticks, about 60 strong, lost 10 to 12 killed; the Americans lost two dead and roughly 15 wounded.

## A Humiliation in the Pines

The Americans came away with some ammunition, cloth and salt, but the battle was regarded as a humiliating defeat. George Stiggins, a Muscogee historian of mixed ancestry who wrote an early history of his people, reported that the Red Sticks had only 13 guns among them, alongside traditional weapons. Mississippi Territory governor David Holmes blamed Caller for the failure. For the first two days of his fifteen-day ordeal in the woods, Caller and Major James Woods had company in Private Abner Smith Lipscomb, who would go on to become the second Chief Justice of the Supreme Court of Alabama. Across the region, frightened settlers fled to Mobile, St. Stephens and Fort Stoddert, or threw up stockades around their homes. Claiborne, fearing retaliation north of Mobile, sent volunteers to reinforce the militia at Fort Mims, Fort Easley and Fort Peirce.

## The Road to Fort Mims

The violence did not stop there. Bailey led warriors against McQueen's homestead, then returned to Fort Mims, where the Tensaw militia elected him captain. Red Stick leaders William Weatherford and Francis planned attacks on Forts Mims and Peirce because many Burnt Corn participants, including the mixed-ancestry men who had guided the Americans and caused most of the Red Stick deaths, had taken refuge there. On August 30, 1813, Red Sticks killed most of the people inside Fort Mims. The war that followed brought a three-pronged American offensive, the Red Stick defeat at Horseshoe Bend, the Treaty of Fort Jackson and, eventually, the forced removal of most Muscogee from Alabama and Georgia. The battlefield itself nearly disappeared from memory. Only in 2011 did a grant from the American Battlefield Protection Program lead researchers to its approximate location on private land, and as of 2012 no definitive artifacts had been recovered.

## Flight Context

The approximate battlefield lies at 31.190N, 87.126W along Burnt Corn Creek in Escambia County, Alabama, on private land with nothing marked on the ground. From 3,000 to 5,000 feet there is nothing at the site itself to pick out, so trace the line of Burnt Corn Creek as your reference. Evergreen Regional Airport / Middleton Field (KGZH) is about 14 nm to the north and Monroe County Aeroplex (KMVC) at Monroeville about 20 nm to the northwest. Naval Air Station Whiting Field (KNSE) lies about 29 nm to the south-southeast; expect Navy training traffic in that direction.
