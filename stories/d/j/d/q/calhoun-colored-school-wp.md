# Calhoun Colored School

In the heart of Alabama's Black Belt, a school built with the hands of the families it served grew from basic industrial training into a college-preparatory academy with Latin classes and a library of more than 6,000 books.

**Location:** 32.0578, -86.5494

**Categories:** Historically segregated African-American schools in Alabama, School buildings on the National Register of Historic Places in Alabama, Educational institutions established in 1892, National Register of Historic Places in Lowndes County, Alabama, Defunct schools in Alabama

## Introduction

In October 1892, three hundred Black residents of Calhoun, Alabama, gathered to hear two women from New England explain their plan for a school. Many of the adults in that crowd did more than listen. Over the next four years they helped build it with their own hands: the teachers' cottages, the schoolhouses, the barn, the shop and the dormitories. The Calhoun Colored School served rural Black students in Lowndes County for half a century, and it traveled a long intellectual distance in that time, from a model designed to prepare children for farm and domestic work to a curriculum that sent graduates on to higher learning.

## A Plea at Hampton

Lowndes County, in the Black Belt southwest of Montgomery, had been a land of large cotton plantations before the Civil War. By 1890 it was reported to have the highest proportion of Black to white residents of any county in Alabama, and most Black families worked as sharecroppers, raising cotton whose price kept falling. Though they were the majority, they lived under white political and social domination. Conservative white Democrats in the legislature were already stripping Black citizens from the voter rolls, and the state's 1901 constitution would raise barriers that disenfranchised most Black voters and tens of thousands of poor whites. Into this world came an appeal. Booker T. Washington, president of Tuskegee Institute, returned to his alma mater, Hampton Institute, to recruit teachers, and spoke of the people of Calhoun and their great desire to educate their children. Two Hampton teachers, Charlotte Thorn and Mabel Dillingham, answered and traveled with him to find a site.

## Raised by Many Hands

N. J. Bell of Montgomery donated the first 10 acres. Thorn and Dillingham drew on wide networks of family and friends for money and donated goods, and publicized the school in Hampton's publication, The Southern Workman. By 1896 Calhoun had a working farm of 100 acres, 300 pupils, 40 of them boarders, and 13 teachers. Dillingham did not live to see much of it; she died in 1895, and Thorn led the school for more than thirty years after her death. Calhoun's reach extended past its classrooms. It sponsored a land bank that helped 85 families buy land, a meaningful step in a county where so many worked someone else's fields. It also joined with the county to improve a local road so that farmers could get their products to market.

## Learning Within the Lines

The school followed the Hampton-Tuskegee model. Students received a basic elementary education and then industrial training, meant to fit the work available in rural Alabama. Boys were prepared to farm or enter skilled trades; girls to keep homes or work as laundresses, dressmakers and domestic workers. The best students were encouraged to become teachers, a calling taken seriously in a push to raise literacy among children and adults alike. The model was also deliberately apolitical. Washington feared that a Black school seen as challenging the politics of the day might never be allowed to open, or would be shut down. Critics, most prominently the Harvard-educated scholar W. E. B. Du Bois, argued that the approach was too limited and that the most talented students deserved a full academic education. Others saw it as conforming to white expectations of low aspirations for Black Southerners.

## From Word Games to Latin

Calhoun did not stand still. Literacy instruction in the primary grades was doubled to fill half the school day, and by 1909 the kindergarten was teaching reading, writing and arithmetic through games. The school began asking for donated books by African American authors for its library. In 1912 Jessie Guernsey, holder of two degrees from Columbia Teachers College, arrived to head the academic department, bringing high-interest reading books, creative writing, literary societies, debates and music, and adding a tenth grade. In the mid-1920s Edward Allen, the first man in that post, shifted the focus to higher-order thinking, a major departure from the Hampton-Tuskegee model, and greatly expanded the library. Late in the decade R. Luella Jones pushed for accreditation. She added Latin, more science and mathematics, made industrial arts an elective, and added the eleventh and twelfth grades so students could graduate from Calhoun. The library grew past 6,000 volumes.

## The Principal's House

Charlotte Thorn died on August 29, 1932. By then many Calhoun graduates were teaching in the county's own schools for Black children, which were growing more popular even as the private school strained to pay for its expanding programs. The Great Depression cut its support, farm mechanization reduced the need for rural labor, and the Great Migration drew families north to industrial cities, shrinking the community until the school could not survive. In 1943 the State of Alabama acquired it. A public high school run by the Lowndes County Board of Education now stands on the grounds. Of the original campus, only the principal's house on County Road 33 remains, listed on the National Register of Historic Places for the school's role in African American education in Lowndes County.

## Flight Context

The Calhoun school site lies at 32.058N, 86.549W in rural Lowndes County, Alabama, about 26 miles southwest of Montgomery. From 2,500 to 4,000 feet, look for the modern public high school campus that now occupies the grounds; the small historic principal's house on County Road 33 needs a low, slow look in clear conditions to pick out. Montgomery Regional Airport (KMGM) is about 17 nm to the northeast, and Mac Crenshaw Memorial Airport (KPRN) at Greenville about 13 nm to the south. Maxwell Air Force Base (KMXF) lies about 22 nm northeast, so expect military traffic around Montgomery.
