# Eufaula, Alabama

A Chattahoochee River cotton port that briefly sheltered Alabama's fleeing government in 1865, Eufaula still keeps one of the state's largest historic districts, a lake famed for bass, and an oak that owns the ground it stands on.

**Location:** 31.8894, -85.1538

**Categories:** Cities in Alabama, Barbour County, Alabama, Chattahoochee River, American Civil War, Civil rights movement, Historic districts

## Introduction

In April 1865, with Union troops closing on Montgomery, Alabama's government packed up and fled southeast to the Chattahoochee. Governor Thomas H. Watts and other state officials fled to Eufaula, a river town in the state's southeast corner, and set up what the New York Daily Tribune called "the fugitive seat of Government of Alabama." Few records from those years survive. The town that sheltered them, though, has survived in remarkable completeness, with streets of historic houses, a lake known as the "Big Bass Capital of the World," and an oak tree that holds title to its own patch of ground.

## From Creek Country to Irwinton

Long before it had a downtown, this stretch of the Chattahoochee River was home to three Muscogee Creek tribes, among them the Eufaulas, whose name the city now bears. By the 1820s the land lay inside the Creek Indian Territory and was supposedly closed to white settlement. The 1832 Treaty of Cusseta changed that. The Creeks ceded all their land east of the Mississippi, and white settlers could now legally buy land from Creek owners, although the treaty's terms did not require anyone to relocate. By 1835 settlers had bought most of the townsite, and a store partly owned by William Irwin gave the new place its first name: Irwinton. Jewish settlers arrived in significant numbers in the mid-nineteenth century, from Germany and from neighboring states, and founded a cemetery whose first burial took place in 1845. By the late 1850s the river had made the town a major shipping center, sending cargo down to the Port of Apalachicola and on to Liverpool and New York City.

## Silence and the Railroad

That prosperity rested on slavery, and the town policed any dissent. In 1850 secessionists formed a vigilante committee that terrorized white residents with abolitionist sympathies. Captain Elisha Bett was driven out of town and allowed back only after he signed a written agreement never to express his views again. In November 1859 the local railroad company authorized its president to buy $150,000 worth of enslaved people to build its line, and grading of the track bed began in January 1860. When war became certain in 1861, those laborers were sent instead to lay track between Montgomery and Pensacola, carrying Confederate troops toward the Gulf of Mexico. The Eufaula line had to wait. Work resumed after the war, and the rails did not reach the city limits until October 1871, when John O. Martin was appointed agent of the new terminal station.

## The Fugitive Capital

Alabama seceded on January 11, 1861, and by the end of that month soldiers were camped at Eufaula, ready to move out wherever they were needed. Six Confederate companies were raised in Eufaula and Barbour County, including the Eufaula Zouaves, who modeled their uniforms on French light infantry. The Confederacy ran a military hospital in the town, and at least one ironclad warship was built here on the river. After Montgomery fell on April 12, 1865, the state's leaders arrived as refugees. On April 29, Union general Benjamin Grierson reached nearby Clayton, and word finally came that the war was over. An Atlanta paper later reported 10,000 Union troops occupying Eufaula, and in the immediate aftermath a food riot broke out. By the end of May, a postal agent carried mail in from Providence, Rhode Island, without needing any of the twenty-five armed guards he had brought along.

## Ballots Counted, Ballots Erased

Freedom changed the arithmetic of power. When the first Reconstruction elections came in October 1867, Barbour County had about 5,000 registered voters, roughly 3,500 of them Black. In March 1870 two Black candidates, Washington Burke and Melvin Patterson, won seats as fourth-ward aldermen. Election officials threw out both victories, citing fraud, and seated their white opponents instead. Black Baptists had already sought their own path, winning permission in 1866 to separate from white churches; their congregation became the basis of the Eufaula Association, one of only two Black Baptist associations formed in Alabama before the statewide association was founded in 1868. A century later the struggle continued. In 1955 the housing authority moved to condemn, by eminent domain, land where Black families had lived since emancipation; residents believed the real aim was to keep their children out of a new high school once integration came. In 1966 a local man, Daddy Bone, organized voter registration and boycotts for SNCC, and so many protesters were arrested and convicted under new anti-picketing laws that the county jail was overwhelmed.

## Old Houses, Big Bass and a Self-Owned Oak

The 2020 census counted 12,882 people in Eufaula, and many of its historic buildings are listed on the National Register of Historic Places. The Seth Lore and Irwinton Historic District, with 667 contributing properties, is the second-largest historic district in Alabama. Sheppard Cottage, built in 1837, is the oldest known residence. The Italianate Fendall Hall went up between 1856 and 1860, and the Shorter Mansion, built in 1884 and remodeled in 1906, houses a museum honoring the six Alabama governors from Barbour County and Admiral Thomas Moorer, a former chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff. Filmmakers noticed the streetscape too: the historic homes in the 2002 film Sweet Home Alabama were shot here. On one lot stands the Tree That Owns Itself, an oak deeded ownership of its own land by Eufaula's mayor in 1936, a title that has passed to each replacement tree since. At the city's edge spreads Walter F. George Lake, which locals simply call Lake Eufaula.

## Flight Context

Eufaula sits at about 31.889 N, 85.154 W on the west bank of the Chattahoochee River, which forms the Alabama-Georgia line here. The river is impounded here as Walter F. George Lake (Lake Eufaula), the dominant landmark from the air; Georgetown, Georgia lies directly across the water, and the Walter F. George lock and dam that forms the lake lies downstream. U.S. Highways 82 and 431 run through town, with 431 heading north toward Phenix City and southwest toward Dothan. A viewing altitude of 3,000-4,500 feet shows the historic street grid against the lakeshore. Weedon Field (KEUF) is about 4 nm from town. Larger fields nearby include Dothan Regional (KDHN, about 37 nm) and Columbus Airport, Georgia (KCSG, about 39 nm). Lawson Army Airfield (KLSF) at Fort Benning, about 28 nm north, is military. Expect summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms in this humid subtropical climate.
