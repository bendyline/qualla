# Jimmy Carter rabbit incident

A swamp rabbit swam toward President Jimmy Carter's fishing boat on a farm pond in Plains, Georgia, and months later a casual retelling turned it into the "killer rabbit" story that dogged his presidency.

**Location:** 32.0453, -84.3997

**Categories:** Carter administration controversies, 1979 in Georgia (U.S. state), Animal attacks in the United States, Rabbits and hares in popular culture, Plains, Georgia

## Introduction

"Bunny Goes Bugs: Rabbit Attacks President." That was the front-page headline The Washington Post ran on August 30, 1979, beside a parody of the Jaws movie poster retitled "PAWS"; Jody Powell later remembered the headline as "President Attacked by Rabbit." The incident behind it had happened four months earlier, on a quiet farm pond outside Plains, Georgia, and involved no injuries, no witnesses from the press and one very wet swamp rabbit. It might have stayed a private joke among White House staff. Instead it became one of the stranger news stories of the Carter years, remembered by the press as the "killer rabbit attack."

## A Johnboat on a Georgia Pond

On April 20, 1979, President Jimmy Carter was spending a few days of vacation in his hometown of Plains. He went fishing in a johnboat, a flat-bottomed craft sometimes wrongly described in later retellings as a canoe, on a pond on his farm. A swamp rabbit, Sylvilagus aquaticus, appeared in the water and began swimming straight toward him, "hissing menacingly, its teeth flashing and nostrils flared," as the story was later told. Carter splashed water at it with his paddle, and the animal turned away. He later speculated that it had been fleeing a predator. A White House photographer captured the moment, and nobody was hurt. The rabbit's fate is unknown.

## Tea, or Perhaps Something Stronger

Back in Washington that spring, Carter was making small talk on the Truman Balcony with staff members, including press secretary Jody Powell, when he mentioned the swimming rabbit. His staff were skeptical. Carter showed them a print of the photograph, but while it clearly showed him and the boat, the rabbit was too small to identify, so he produced a larger version, and that convinced them. In August, Powell passed the story on to Associated Press White House correspondent Brooks Jackson. Just how it happened depends on who is telling it. Powell remembered a cup of tea. Carter recalled that it was "in a bar after a lot of drinking had gone on." A shortage of other news that summer may have helped, and once the story broke, coverage ran for more than a week. The New York Times weighed in with "A Tale of Carter and the 'Killer Rabbit.'"

## Shrouded in Mystery

No news photographers had been close enough to see the encounter, and the administration refused to release its picture. Deputy press secretary Rex Granum explained that "there are just certain stories about the president that must forever remain shrouded in mystery." Cartoonists filled the gap with their own drawings, exaggerating the story further. Carter tried to deflate the tale, telling reporters the animal "was just a nice, quiet, typical Georgia rabbit." University of Maryland zoologist Vagn Flyger doubted there had been any attack at all: "If anything, he was probably scared and trying to find a dry place to get to." The damage lingered anyway. The press used the episode as a metaphor to portray Carter negatively, and in the 1980 election he lost to Ronald Reagan while Republicans won a Senate majority they had not held since 1954. It was Reagan's administration that finally released the photograph, setting off a fresh round of coverage.
