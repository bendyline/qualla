# Atsena Otie Key

A small island half a mile off Cedar Key served as an Army depot in the Second Seminole War and a pencil-wood mill town until a hurricane in 1896 swept most of it away, leaving a ghost town inside a wildlife refuge.

**Location:** 29.1225, -83.0268

**Categories:** Ghost towns in Florida, Islands of Levy County, Florida, Gulf Coast barrier islands of Florida, Archaeological sites in Florida, Cedar Key, Florida

## Introduction

A walking trail on Atsena Otie Key leads past the ruins of a mill that once cut cedar for Eberhard Faber's pencil factory in New York. Little else survives of the town that stood here. The island lies just half a mile off Cedar Key on Florida's Gulf coast, and in its time it has been an Army depot, a planters' summer retreat, a Civil War target and a busy lumber port. Then, on September 29, 1896, a powerful hurricane pushed a ten-foot storm surge across it. Today the ghost town is managed as part of the Cedar Keys National Wildlife Refuge, open to anyone who makes the short crossing by boat.

## Depot Key

People lived on the island long before any army arrived; a skeleton found here in 1999 proved to be more than 2,000 years old. After Spanish explorers reached Florida's Gulf coast in the early 16th century, European diseases devastated the native population, and by the 19th century the U.S. government had forced most of the area's Indigenous people onto reservations in the West. The island served as a trading post during the First Seminole War. During the Second Seminole War the U.S. Army made the island a supply depot, and it became known as Depot Key. In 1840 General Walker Keith Armistead ordered a hospital built here, and by some accounts the Army's Florida headquarters moved to the island too. In August 1842, Colonel William J. Worth met Seminole leaders on the key and struck an agreement: if the Seminoles withdrew to the southernmost part of Florida and stayed there, the Army would leave them alone. On that basis Worth declared the war over. Two months later a hurricane wrecked the post, and the Army abandoned it.

## Summer Cottages and a Chartered Town

In 1843 Augustus Steele claimed the island under the Armed Occupation Act and renamed it Atsena Otie Key, for reasons that remain unclear, as does the meaning of the name. He built summer cottages for wealthy planters from Florida and Georgia and became the first postmaster when the Cedar Key Post Office opened in 1845. Trade grew through the 1850s. A lighthouse on nearby Seahorse Key began guiding ships into the Cedar Keys harbor in 1854, and in 1855 A. W. Faber began buying large tracts of Levy County timberland, and a small parcel on the island, later the site of a lumber mill, was bought the following year. The Florida Legislature chartered the town of Atsena Otie in January 1858. By the 1860 census, 215 men, women and children lived here in 30 households, and the Florida Railroad, running across the state from Fernandina, reached the Cedar Keys in 1861 with a terminal on neighboring Way Key.

## Blockade and Raid

The Civil War shut the harbor down. Union ships of the Gulf Blockade Squadron sealed off the Cedar Keys, and shipping and fishing soon ground to a halt. On January 16, 1862, nine days after the USS Hatteras arrived off the Cedar Keys, she landed sailors and Marines to attack the railhead. A company of Florida cavalry and local civilian workers drove them back at first, but the raiders destroyed tracks, engines and buildings before withdrawing. The Hatteras then sank or burned seven blockade runners in the harbor and sent men ashore to burn the harbor facilities, an episode remembered as the Battle of Cedar Key. That June the USS Somerset captured the blockade runner Curlew off the island, and in October her crew destroyed the salt works on nearby James Island, with some two thousand bushels of salt. They also seized horses and took many of the workers there, both free and enslaved, into their custody.

## The Pencil Mill Years

Peace brought the island a busy era. In 1868 the Eberhard Faber Pencil Company built a lumber mill on Atsena Otie Key to supply wood for its pencil factory in New York, and the area revived as a shipping port. Oysters, green turtles and fishing added to the economy through the 1870s. By the 1890s, lumber production was reportedly turning a profit of almost $900,000, and the Faber mill alone produced casing wood for hundreds of thousands of pencils. More than 50 households lived on the island by 1895. But the boom carried the seeds of its end: by 1896 the cedar forests that fed the mills had mostly been cut over.

## After the Storm

The 1896 hurricane destroyed the Faber mill and wrecked nearly everything else, leaving only a few houses. With the forests cut over, the mill companies judged that rebuilding was not worth it. Residents floated a business building and several houses across the water to Cedar Key and used salvaged mill lumber to build there. A few residents stayed on into the new century, but by the middle of the 1900s the last houses were gone. The island reportedly sold for $500 in 1923. In the late 1990s the Suwannee River Water Management District bought it and arranged for the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service to manage it as part of the Cedar Keys refuge. Visitors now come by boat for the walking trails, wildlife viewing and the quiet ruins of the mill.

## Flight Context

Atsena Otie Key lies at 29.122N, 83.027W, about half a mile south of the town of Cedar Key on Florida's Gulf coast. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet it appears as a small island among the scattered Cedar Keys, with the town of Cedar Key immediately to the north and the open Gulf of Mexico to the west and south. George T. Lewis Airport (KCDK) on Cedar Key is only about 1.4 nm to the west-northwest, and Cross City Airport (KCTY) about 31 nm to the north. The island is part of a national wildlife refuge, where the FAA asks pilots to stay at least 2,000 feet above the surface; expect sea breezes and afternoon thunderstorms in summer.
