# Island Hotel

Built of oyster-shell tabby on the eve of the Civil War, Cedar Key's Island Hotel has been a general store, a post office, possibly a barracks, and since 1946 a small hotel with a bullet-scarred King Neptune in the bar.

**Location:** 29.1369, -83.0314

## Introduction

In 1993, when restorers worked on the painting of King Neptune in the Island Hotel's lounge, they found bullet slugs embedded in the wall behind it. Nobody needed to be told the building had stories. It had already lived through the Civil War, two hurricanes, a mysterious death, rumors of a bootleg still, and more than a century of Cedar Key's comings and goings - all behind tabby walls ten inches thick at 224 2nd Street.

## Walls of Oyster Shell

Major John Parsons and Francis E. Hale put the building up between 1859 and 1861 as a general store. According to the hotel's own history, the builders mixed oyster shell, limestone, and sand to pour its tabby walls, and they set oak beams twelve inches wide to carry the structure. Parsons and Hale's General Store sold a little of everything, from furniture, dry goods, and groceries to naval stores, hardware, and building supplies. In the 1860s the building also housed the Cedar Key Post Office and the customs house. Then came the war. Cedar Key changed hands more than once during the Civil War, and the building may have housed both Union and Confederate troops at various times; the hotel's history suggests it served the Union side as a barracks and warehouse, likely because it was so sturdily built.

## Storms, a Still, and a Stranger's End

By the end of the 1880s part of the building was being used as a boarding house. In 1896 a devastating hurricane destroyed much of the town and severely damaged the store. Hale died in 1910, and the building changed hands. What came next is the stuff of island legend. The hotel's history credits Simon Feinberg with converting the old store into lodgings between 1915 and 1919, rebuilding the interior stairs and adding a second-floor balcony around the south and west sides. Feinberg died in 1919 under circumstances the hotel still calls mysterious, and rumors of bootlegging lingered - kept alive when workers later turned up copper pipe from a still. Whatever the truth, it is the kind of question an old building on a small island can keep for a very long time.

## Bessie's Island Hotel

The building became the Island Hotel in 1946, when Bessie and Loyal "Gibby" Gibbs bought it and gave it the name it carries today. Bessie made the dining room a destination, buying fresh seafood straight from local boats at the Big Dock, and her Heart of Palm Salad is still on the menu. She was a force beyond the kitchen, too, serving Cedar Key as city commissioner, judge, and mayor and helping organize the Cedar Key Arts Festival. The guest list grew famous: the hotel counts author Pearl Buck, singers Vaughan Monroe and Tennessee Ernie Ford, actresses Frances Langford and Myrna Loy, and actor Richard Boone among its visitors. In 1948 the artist Helen Tooker painted the King Neptune mural in the lounge. Two years later Hurricane Easy blew the roof off and filled the upstairs rooms with water, leaving stains that still mark the painting.

## Ten Rooms, No Televisions

The Island Hotel was added to the National Register of Historic Places on November 23, 1984. It remains a small place by design: ten rooms, original hand-cut wooden walls and floors, and no televisions or phones in the rooms. The Neptune Lounge still displays its mural - bullet holes and all - and a speakeasy-style dining area called Bair's Cave nods to the building's rumored past. More than a century and a half after Parsons and Hale poured those shell walls, the old store is still open for business, still serving seafood, and still keeping a few of its secrets.

## Flight Context

The Island Hotel stands in the small island town of Cedar Key on Florida's Gulf coast, at approximately 29.137°N, 83.031°W. George T. Lewis Airport (KCDK) sits on the island only about 1 nautical mile to the west, so a flight to Cedar Key puts you within walking distance of the hotel. From 1,000-1,500 feet AGL, the town's compact streets and waterfront make easy references against the surrounding water. Watch for coastal fog and summer thunderstorms along the Gulf. Gainesville Regional Airport (KGNV) is about 52 nautical miles to the northeast.
