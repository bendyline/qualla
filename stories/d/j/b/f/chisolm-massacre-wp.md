# Chisolm massacre

In April 1877, less than a month after Reconstruction ended, a Ku Klux Klan mob stormed the Kemper County jail in Mississippi and killed Judge William Chisolm, two of his children and two of his friends; no one was ever convicted.

## Introduction

Reconstruction had been over for less than a month. On April 29, 1877, in Kemper County, Mississippi, a mob estimated at around 300 men, described as Ku Klux Klan members, stormed the local jail. Inside were William Chisolm, a judge and former sheriff accused of murder, and, held in protective custody alongside him, his son, his daughter and two of his friends. When the mob was done, five of the judge's party were dead or dying, including a boy of thirteen or fourteen and a young woman of eighteen or nineteen. No one was ever convicted. The Chisolm Massacre became a national story and a bitter argument, and it remains a stark marker of the violence that followed Reconstruction in Mississippi.

## The Judge in the Jail

William Chisolm was a judge and a former sheriff. According to the Yorkville Enquirer, he was also a Republican candidate for a seat in the U.S. Congress. In the spring of 1877 he was accused of killing John Gully, a leading local Democrat, and he was being held in the local jail. He was not alone there. His son and daughter and two of his friends were also in the jail, held in protective custody. The arrangement was meant to keep them safe. It did the opposite, gathering in one building the people a mob would come looking for.

## April 29, 1877

The mob that came to the jail was estimated at around 300 men, described as Klansmen. They killed William Chisolm. They killed his son John, aged 13 or 14, and fatally wounded his daughter Cornelia Josephine, aged 18 or 19. They also killed his friends J. P. Gilmer, a former state senator, and Angus McLellan, who was shot as he left the jail. Contemporary accounts say Chisolm fought back, and that Dr. David Rosser, who led the mob into the building, was himself killed in the struggle. It is worth pausing on the names of the dead. They belonged to a father and his two children and to two family friends, and they were killed not after any trial but by a crowd that had decided the outcome for itself. Much of what is remembered about the massacre concerns politics and newspapers. The people who died that day in De Kalb should come first.

## Silence from Above

The response from those with power was muted, or worse. Southern papers applauded the lynching. The Yorkville Enquirer ended its report on the "Tragedy in Mississippi" by noting that "Other hangings will probably follow." Governor John Marshall Stone reportedly refused to launch an investigation, and President Rutherford Hayes did not comment on the killings. The massacre was one of several reprisal actions in Mississippi in the period after Reconstruction. Later that year a freedman, Walter Riley, confessed to killing Gully and was executed for the murder on December 7, 1877; Wells's account says Riley had been seized in Tennessee and brought back to Kemper County without a lawful requisition. He maintained that he alone was responsible, though on the gallows he indirectly implicated the judge, saying he had obtained the gun from a former servant of Chisolm's who had persuaded him to commit the murder. Newspapers asked why that man would have done so unless prompted by the judge. Yet Riley also said that, as far as he knew, Chisolm and Gilmer had no involvement.

## Two Books, Two Stories

The killings did not stay local. The New York Times wrote about them, and the massacre soon became the subject of dueling books. James Monroe Wells, a deputy revenue collector and U.S. Army veteran, published The Chisolm Massacre: A Picture of "Home Rule" in Mississippi, criticizing the local people of Kemper County. James Daniel Lynch answered with Kemper County Vindicated, And a Peep at Radical Rule in Mississippi, an account that blamed Radical Republicans instead. The two titles still frame the argument. One saw murder sheltered by "home rule"; the other saw a county defending itself. Between them lie five people killed in and around a jail where they had been held for their own protection.
