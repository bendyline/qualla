# Duck Hill, Mississippi

A small Mississippi railroad town named for a Choctaw chief, Duck Hill gave the world gospel composer Lucie E. Campbell, and it also carries the memory of a 1937 lynching whose brutality drove a national fight over federal anti-lynching law.

**Location:** 33.6319, -89.7147

**Categories:** Towns in Montgomery County, Mississippi, Lynching deaths in Mississippi, Gospel music, Choctaw, Railroad towns

## Introduction

Each summer, Duck Hill, Mississippi, a town of a few hundred people on U.S. Route 51, has traditionally held a gospel and heritage festival named for its most celebrated daughter. Lucie E. Campbell was born here on April 30, 1885, and became a gospel composer and music director for the National Baptist Convention, publishing more than a hundred songs, among them "He'll Understand and Say Well Done." Her music is one thread in Duck Hill's story. Others run through a Choctaw chief's council ground, a Civil War train wreck, a train robbery and a 1937 lynching condemned across the nation, and the town deserves to be remembered whole.

## The Chief's Hill

The town takes its name from a large hill to the northeast, where a Choctaw chief named Duck held war councils. Chief Duck was also a medicine man who treated his people, and a statue of him stood, at least into the late 2010s, along U.S. 51 beside an old Illinois Central caboose. The Choctaw were forced to cede their lands to the United States, and after their removal the first European-American settler arrived. John A. Binford came in 1834, built the area's first home and developed a cotton plantation worked by enslaved African Americans. He became one of the region's most successful planters and a large slaveholder and was elected to the Mississippi Legislature, while Duck Hill grew into a trading center for the cotton planters around it.

## Rails, Wrecks and Robbers

In 1856 the Illinois Central Railroad completed its line from Chicago to New Orleans and built a depot at Duck Hill, and business picked up. The line brought tragedy too. In the early morning hours of October 19, 1862, two trains collided head-on at Duck Hill, killing about 34 men, most of them Confederate soldiers. It has been described as the South's worst loss of life in a train accident up to that time. In 1887, the discovery of iron ore nearby set off hopes of a mill town and a burst of financial speculation. In 1888 Duck Hill became the scene of a railroad robbery. Two armed men, Rube Burrow and Joe Jackson, clung to the outside of a departing train, climbed into the engine cab, forced the engineer to stop about a mile north of town, and took a reported $3,000 from the express car's safe, killing a man who tried to intervene.

## The Binford Name

John Binford's sons, James R. and John A. Jr., helped lead the Confederate "McClung Rifles," Company E of the 15th Mississippi Infantry Regiment, from Duck Hill. James R. Binford was later elected to the Mississippi State Senate, and after white Democrats regained control of the legislature following Reconstruction, he is credited with writing Jim Crow laws for Mississippi. In 1930 the town opened Lloyd T. Binford High School, named for his son, a Memphis insurance executive and film censor noted for his white supremacist views. The town also built a segregated agriculture education facility for its Black students. Declining population has since closed the schools. The high school gymnasium serves as a community center, and in 2012 volunteers organized to preserve the vandalized building.

## April 13, 1937

Roosevelt Townes and Robert "Bootjack" McDaniels, two Black men, had pleaded not guilty in the county courthouse at Winona to the December 1936 murder of George Windham, a Duck Hill grocer. As a crowd gathered outside, a mob of white men took them from the courthouse and drove them by school bus to woods near Duck Hill. Hundreds of white people followed, and a crowd estimated at several hundred, by some accounts as many as 500, watched as the two men were chained to trees and tortured with a blowtorch to force confessions. Townes was burned to death. McDaniels was shot to death. No one was ever charged. Their killings were among nine lynchings of African Americans by whites in Montgomery County after Reconstruction. Newspapers carried a photograph of McDaniels's tortured body chained to a tree, the lynchings were condemned nationwide, and the publicity helped Representative Joseph A. Gavagan of New York win support for a federal anti-lynching bill. It passed the House, but Southern Democrats blocked it in the Senate.

## Songs That Outlast

Anger at segregation surfaced again during World War II, when many Black soldiers trained in the South resented the segregated public facilities they were forced to use. In 1943, fifteen armed Black soldiers from nearby Camp McCain, upset over a recent white assault on a group of Black soldiers at Starkville, came to Duck Hill at night and fired into the town. No one was injured. Today Duck Hill counts 619 residents, about seven in ten of them Black, according to the 2020 census. Freight trains of the Grenada Railroad (formerly the Grenada Railway) still run on the old Illinois Central line, and a Mississippi historical marker on U.S. 51 recounts the achievements of Lucie Campbell. The festival in her name, held each summer, brings gospel music back to the town where her story began.

## Flight Context

Coordinates: 33.6319°N, 89.7147°W, in northwestern Montgomery County, Mississippi, on U.S. Route 51 midway between Grenada and Winona, with Big Bogue Creek flowing east and north of town. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet, follow U.S. 51 and the rail line through town; Interstate 55 runs about five miles to the west. Nearby airports: Grenada Municipal (KGNF), three nautical miles north of Grenada and about 13 nautical miles north-northwest of Duck Hill, and Winona-Montgomery County Airport (FAA identifier 5A6, no ICAO code), just south of Winona and about 10 nautical miles south. Summers are hot and humid, with afternoon thunderstorms.
