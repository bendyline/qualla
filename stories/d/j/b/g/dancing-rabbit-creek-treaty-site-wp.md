# Dancing Rabbit Creek Treaty Site

In a quiet stretch of Mississippi woods, a granite marker and a small Choctaw cemetery mark where, in September 1830, Choctaw leaders signed away their ancestral homeland in the first treaty of the Indian Removal era.

**Location:** 33.0099, -88.7542

**Categories:** Choctaw, Trail of Tears, National Historic Landmarks in Mississippi, Native American history of Mississippi, Treaty signing historic sites

## Introduction

No building stands here now. In a remote wooded corner of southwestern Noxubee County, Mississippi, a granite marker and a small Choctaw cemetery sit near a freshwater spring above the floodplain of Dancing Rabbit Creek. In September 1830, several thousand Choctaw gathered at this spot, around a meeting house that has long since vanished, to hear what the United States wanted from them. What it wanted was their ancestral homeland. The treaty signed here sent the Choctaw west to what is now Oklahoma, and it became the first treaty negotiated under the Indian Removal Act.

## A Nation Under Pressure

The Choctaw were one of the southeastern nations long labeled the "Five Civilized Tribes," because they had to some extent adopted Euro-American customs. They had also built an independent tribal government with its own constitution. None of it protected their land. Their territory had historically included parts of Mississippi, Alabama and Louisiana, and pressure from white settlers had already pushed the Choctaw to cede some of it during the 1820s. Andrew Jackson, elected president in 1828, sought the removal of the Choctaw and the other southeastern nations to what was then called Indian Territory. Then Congress passed the Indian Removal Act, and the Choctaw became the first nation to negotiate a treaty in its wake.

## Thirteen Days in September

Negotiations ran from September 15 to 27, 1830. Several thousand Choctaw attended, along with the federal commissioners John H. Eaton and John Coffee and a number of opportunistic white entrepreneurs who offered gambling, drink and other vices to the crowd. When the proposed treaty was presented on September 22, many Choctaw voiced their disapproval, and a large number left the encampment over the following days. Three major chiefs, including Greenwood LeFlore and Mushulatubbee, signed the treaty on September 27, under a questionable set of conditions that may have included threats, bribery and extortion. Under its terms, the Choctaw surrendered their ancestral lands in exchange for territory in what is now Oklahoma.

## The Road West

The treaty was ratified in February 1831; sources date the action to February 24 or 25. Under its Article 14, Choctaw who wished to remain in Mississippi could register for allotments of land within six months of ratification, a window that closed in late August 1831. The United States forced the vast majority west to Oklahoma between 1831 and 1833, on the journey that became the Choctaw Trail of Tears. The agreement did not stop with them. As the first treaty negotiated after the Indian Removal Act, it served as a model for the removal treaties that followed with other nations. The terms written near this spring echoed across the Southeast for years afterward.

## Remembering in the Woods

Nothing from 1830 survives above ground. In the late 19th century, the historian Henry S. Halbert researched where the treaty had been made, gathering oral histories from witnesses and from people who had known witnesses to the proceedings. His research guided the local chapter of the Daughters of the American Revolution when it placed the granite marker in 1928. The monument soon became a focus of Choctaw commemoration, and some Choctaw dead were buried beside it, turning a site of loss into a place of remembrance. Reached by Monument Road, the site was designated a National Historic Landmark in 1996.

## Flight Context

Coordinates: 33.0099°N, 88.7542°W, in rural southwestern Noxubee County, Mississippi, on the southeast side of Dancing Rabbit Creek. The site is a marker and small cemetery in remote woods, so it will not be visible from altitude; at 2,000 to 3,000 feet, use the creek's floodplain as your reference. Nearby airports: Golden Triangle Regional (KGTR), about 28 nautical miles north-northeast, and Meridian Regional / Key Field (KMEI), about 41 nautical miles south; Columbus Air Force Base (KCBM) lies about 41 nautical miles north-northeast and is military.
