# Meridian, Mississippi

A railroad junction that Sherman burned and that for a time was Mississippi's largest city, Meridian gave the world Jimmie Rodgers and James Chaney and still spins one of the rarest carousels in America.

**Location:** 32.3643, -88.7037

**Categories:** Cities in Mississippi, County seats in Mississippi, Railroad towns, Mississippi Blues Trail, Civil rights history, Country music

## Introduction

For a while, the town could not settle on its own name. When merchant John T. Ball put up a station house on the Mobile and Ohio Railroad, the sign on it switched every day between "Meridian" and "Sowashee." The argument ended only when the railroads kept growing and brought in a wave of railroad workers, who overruled everyone else and left "Meridian" up for good. It was a fitting settlement for a city the trains had made. Established in 1860 where the Mobile and Ohio met the Southern Railway of Mississippi, Meridian built its fortunes on rails and on the timber and cotton they carried.

## Burned and Rebuilt

The first settler, Richard McLemore, received a federal land grant of about 2,000 acres. In 1853 most of his land was bought by Lewis A. Ragsdale, a lawyer from Alabama, while Ball, a merchant from Kemper County, took the remaining 80 acres, and the two men laid out the lots that became the city. Its value as a rail junction also made it a target. In February 1864, during the Battle of Meridian, General William Tecumseh Sherman's forces burned much of the city to the ground. Workers repaired the rail lines with remarkable speed, and trains were running again 26 working days after the battle. What followed was a "Golden Age." Meridian was the largest city in Mississippi at the 1910 and 1920 censuses and a leading manufacturing center of the South, served by five major rail lines and 44 trains arriving and departing every day.

## Opera, Skyscrapers and Merchants

Prosperity left its mark in brick and stone. Immigrant Jews from Germany and eastern Europe were influential in the city's commercial growth; Congregation Beth Israel was founded in 1868, and by 1927 Meridian's 575 Jewish residents made up one of the largest Jewish communities in the state. In 1889 the half brothers Israel Marks and Levi Rothenberg built the Grand Opera House, which opened with a German company performing Johann Strauss II's The Gypsy Baron. Union Station went up in 1906, a string of hotels followed for passengers and railroad workers, and in 1929 the 16-story Threefoot Building rose as the city's signature skyscraper. Loeb's Department Store on Front Street has been selling clothing since Alex Loeb opened it in 1887. West of the core, in the African American business district, the E.F. Young Hotel of 1931 was one of the only places in segregated Meridian where a Black traveler could find a room.

## The Singing Brakeman and the Flying Keys

Jimmie Rodgers, the "Father of Country Music," was born here in 1897. In Highland Park, a museum keeps the original guitar of "The Singing Brakeman" among his memorabilia, beside railroad equipment from the steam era, and a festival has marked the anniversary of his death every May since 1953. A Mississippi Blues Trail marker in Meridian honors Rodgers and notes that more than a third of his recordings were blues. The same park shelters the Highland Park Dentzel Carousel, built around 1896 by Gustav Dentzel of Philadelphia and turning since 1909. A National Historic Landmark, it is the world's only two-row stationary Dentzel menagerie. Meridian's airport has its own legend. When Depression-era residents considered abandoning the field as too costly to maintain, its managers, the brothers Fred and Al Key, took their plane Ole Miss aloft over the city on June 4, 1935, and stayed up until July 1. Their 653 hours and 34 minutes in the air drew enough publicity and money to save the field, which now bears their name.

## Two Libraries, One Long Road

In 1913 Meridian opened two Carnegie libraries, one for white residents and one for Black residents, the latter built on land donated by St. Paul Methodist Church. They stayed separate until 1967, when the Civil Rights Act of 1964 brought their collections together. James Chaney was a son of this city. In June 1964 he, Michael Schwerner and Andrew Goodman drove to Neshoba County to meet members of a Black church that had been bombed and burned, and all three disappeared that night on their way back to Meridian. They had been murdered. At the federal trial held in Meridian's courthouse in October 1967, an all-white jury convicted seven of eighteen defendants, including a deputy sheriff, of conspiring to violate the victims' civil rights. The state brought its own charges only in 2005, when Edgar Ray Killen was convicted of manslaughter and sentenced to 60 years. Meridian later renamed part of 49th Avenue for Chaney and holds an annual memorial service in his honor.

## Curtain Up Again

As highways pulled residents toward new subdivisions after the 1960s, downtown lost ground, and the city answered by designating historic districts; nine are now on the National Register. The Grand Opera House, dark and abandoned for nearly 70 years after closing in the late 1920s, reopened in 2006 as Mississippi State University's Riley Center, helped by a $10 million grant from the local Riley Foundation. In 2018 the Mississippi Arts and Entertainment Experience opened downtown, home to a state hall of fame whose first class included Rodgers alongside Faulkner, Welty and B.B. King. At the 2020 census, 35,052 people lived in Meridian, and its aviation story continues: student naval aviators train in T-45C Goshawks at Naval Air Station Meridian, while Key Field is home to the Air National Guard's 186th Air Refueling Wing.

## Flight Context

Meridian lies at about 32.364 N, 88.704 W in the East Central Hills of Mississippi, where Interstates 20 and 59 join. From 3,000 to 4,000 feet, look for the downtown grid along the rail lines, with the Threefoot Building rising above it, oak and pine forest rolling away on all sides, and the broad water of Okatibbee Lake nearby. Key Field / Meridian Regional Airport (KMEI), with its 10,004-foot runway, is about 3 nm southwest and hosts Air National Guard tanker operations. Naval Air Station Meridian / McCain Field (FAA identifier NMM) lies about 14 nm northeast; expect military jet training traffic throughout the area. Summer thunderstorms are common, and spring storms can bring hail and tornadoes.
