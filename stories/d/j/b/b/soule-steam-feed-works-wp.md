# Soulé Steam Feed Works

Founded in Meridian in 1892 by a self-taught inventor, Soulé Steam Feed Works built steam engines for the world's sawmills for nine decades, and its intact factory, line shafts and all, now lives on as a museum and steam festival.

**Location:** 32.3655, -88.6964

**Categories:** Steam engine manufacturers, Steam museums in the United States, Museums in Meridian, Mississippi, Mississippi Landmarks, Industrial buildings and structures on the National Register of Historic Places in Mississippi

## Introduction

Every one of the roughly 6,600 engines Soulé Steam Feed Works built between 1892 and 1984 carried the name of its hometown cast into the metal: Meridian. The engines went out mostly to the lumber industry, and the company's products were sold around the world. The factory that made them never moved. With its foundry, forges and line shaft of roughly 100 feet, the complex in Meridian, Mississippi, is believed to be America's last intact steam engine factory, and each year its Live Steam Festival brings the age of steam back to life.

## A Missed Boat to Cuba

George Wilberforce Soulé was born in Buffalo, New York, in 1849, a descendant of the George Soule who came to America on the Mayflower. When he was one, he and his father, Isaac, moved to Wisconsin. His father died when George was 20, and he left the family farm to make his own way. Despite less than a year of formal schooling, he taught school for one three-month term before heading south. He had meant to sail to Cuba, but he missed the ship, and in 1875 he landed instead in Morton, Mississippi. After running businesses in Morton and Shubuta, he moved to Meridian in 1879, where he operated a turpentine company, a lumber company, a cotton gin, and worked as a manufacturer's representative.

## An Inventor Out of Necessity

Soulé's first great invention was born of a shortage of cash. Short of money after a railroad accident, he decided to build his own cotton press for his fledgling cotton gin. The machine turned out simpler and cheaper than anything on the market, and it attracted wide attention. He called it the Southern Standard Cotton Press and built a company around it, and in 1881, two years after moving to Meridian, the business sold 750 presses across the South. He would go on to earn at least 25 patents. In 1892 he founded Soulé Steam Feed Works, incorporating it in 1893, and the company became known for its many patented innovations in steam engine technology, reaching its height around the turn of the 20th century.

## Feeding the Saw

The company's name described its specialty. In a sawmill, a steam feed engine drives the log and its carriage into the saw blade, and Soulé built engines to do exactly that. The Soulé Rotary stayed in production for about three decades, with the last one built in 1923. By then its successor was ready. Designed in 1922 and patented in 1923, the Soulé Spee-D-Twin was a two-cylinder reciprocating steam engine, much more efficient than the engine before it, with a valve that let it run both forward and in reverse. It was small enough to be retrofitted onto an existing carriage, and it became the favorite of sawmill operators. The Spee-D-Twin remained in production until 1984.

## A Factory Driven by One Shaft

At the heart of the plant runs a line shaft roughly 100 feet long (sources give 100 to 106 feet), turned by an electric motor dating from the early 1920s. The shaft powered the blacksmith shop and its two forges. A belt-driven wooden freight elevator carried finished castings up to the assembly room and brought finished products back down, while a system of rails and cranes moved heavy machinery through the building, to the foundry built in 1917, to the machine shop and out to the street. Upstairs, in the steam engine factory, a 25-foot shaft driven by the main shaft below turned fans that kept the area cool through Mississippi summers.

## Steam Days in Meridian

Soulé Steam Feed Works shut down as a business in 2002, and a nonprofit that became the Mississippi Industrial Heritage Museum took over the complex, receiving the deed in December 2003 or in 2004, depending on the source; it has operated the site ever since. The buildings were already a contributing property to Meridian's Union Station Historic District, listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1979, and the complex became a Mississippi Landmark in 2003. Each year the museum hosts the Soulé Live Steam Festival, which draws thousands of people from around the nation to see the engines that made the company famous, along with an antique print shop in operation. For years, Alabama Art Casting also staged annual molten iron pours at Soulé.
