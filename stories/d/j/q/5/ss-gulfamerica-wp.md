# SS Gulfamerica

On her maiden voyage in April 1942, the new tanker Gulfamerica was torpedoed within sight of Jacksonville Beach, silhouetted by a resort town that had not yet learned to turn out its lights.

**Location:** 30.2333, -81.3000

**Categories:** Ships sunk by German submarines in World War II, Shipwrecks of the Florida coast, World War II tankers of the United States, Maritime incidents in April 1942, World War II shipwrecks in the Atlantic Ocean

## Introduction

"There was moonshine, how you say, moonlight. There were lights on the shore. And many people." That is how the German U-boat commander Reinhard Hardegen remembered the night of April 10, 1942, off Jacksonville, Florida. The lights belonged to the Jacksonville Beach resort, which was not yet observing a blackout. Against that glow, about five miles offshore, steamed a brand-new tanker on her very first voyage, outlined by the glow.

## A Maiden Voyage

SS Gulfamerica was an American steam tanker built by Bethlehem Shipbuilding at Sparrows Point, Maryland, and completed in March 1942. Operated by the Gulf Oil Company of New York City and homeported in Philadelphia, she set out on her maiden voyage from Port Arthur, Texas, bound for New York with 101,500 barrels of furnace oil. On the night of April 10 she was traveling alone, without an escort, off the Florida coast. Just after 10 p.m. the decision was made to stop steering an evasive zigzag course. Twenty minutes later, at 10:20, the German submarine U-123 sighted her and fired a torpedo.

## Fire on the Water

The torpedo struck the No. 7 tank on the starboard side, setting off a large explosion and fire. The engines were stopped, the order came to abandon ship, and Gulfamerica sent out distress calls. U-123 then opened up with its deck gun, firing about 12 shells into the engine room on the port side in an effort to bring down the radio antenna and the anti-aircraft gun. The evacuation dissolved into confusion. One lifeboat capsized. Another, carrying the master and ten crewmen, pulled away within ten minutes, and ten minutes after that a boat left with only three men aboard. Three more escaped on a life raft and later pulled two men from the water. Five men were killed by the torpedo blast or the gunfire, and 14 drowned after entering the sea. In all, two officers, two armed guards and 15 crewmen died. Coast Guard patrol boats rescued the survivors and carried them to Mayport. The tanker settled by the stern, listing about 40 degrees to starboard, but did not sink until April 16.

## Between the Ship and the Shore

After the torpedo hit, Hardegen brought his boat to the surface to finish the tanker. By one account, he realized that the brightly lit, crowded Jacksonville coast lay just beyond her, and that any shells passing over Gulfamerica could land among civilians. So he maneuvered around the burning ship and placed U-123 between the tanker and the shore, where any misses would fall into the sea. The maneuver cost him valuable time. The distress call had been received, and the destroyer USS Dahlgren later engaged U-123, which was damaged but made a narrow escape back to Europe. Hardegen survived the war. In 1990 he returned to Jacksonville, where he was received as an honored guest. "The town was very friendly to me," he said.

## Lights Out

Gulfamerica was one in a string of merchant ships lost along the North American coast as U-boats of Operation Drumbeat hunted there, and a wave of hysteria took hold, with fears of spies and saboteurs being landed all along the shore. Yet even though the lit-up coastline made ships easy targets, it took the government several months to organize an effective blackout, and only then did the losses drop. Two of the men who died that night were later honored in steel. The Liberty ships SS Robert J. Banks and SS James Kyron Walker were named for crewmen lost aboard Gulfamerica. They were two of the 17 or 18 Liberty ships (counts differ) named for African Americans during the war.

## Flight Context

The Gulfamerica attack took place about five miles off Jacksonville Beach; the recorded position is 30.2333°N, 81.3000°W, over open Atlantic water east of the beaches. From 2,000–4,000 feet, the Jacksonville Beach shoreline to the northwest makes a clear coastal reference, with Mayport farther north. Nearby airports include Naval Station Mayport (KNRB, military, about 13 miles north-northwest), Jacksonville Executive at Craig (KCRG, about 15 miles west-northwest) and Northeast Florida Regional (KSGJ) at St. Augustine, about 19 miles south. Sea breezes and summer thunderstorms are common along this coast.
