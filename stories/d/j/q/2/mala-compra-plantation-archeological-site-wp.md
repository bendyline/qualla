# Mala Compra Plantation Archeological Site

Beneath a county park on the Matanzas River lie the foundations of Mala Compra, the cotton plantation of Florida's first congressional delegate, worked by enslaved people for twenty years until it burned in the Second Seminole War.

**Location:** 29.6161, -81.2033

**Categories:** Archaeological sites on the National Register of Historic Places in Florida, Cotton plantations in Florida, Slave cabins and quarters in the United States, Seminole Wars, Palm Coast, Florida

## Introduction

In 1831, John James Audubon spent ten days as a guest at Mala Compra, a cotton plantation on the east bank of the Matanzas River in what is now Palm Coast, Florida. He shot dozens of American coots there, and from one male he drew the figure for the plate that appeared in The Birds of America. His host was Joseph Marion Hernández, a former delegate to Congress who would later command militia troops as a general. The fields around them were worked by enslaved people. Five years after Audubon's visit, the plantation was in ashes, and today its foundations lie beneath Bings Landing County Park.

## A Bad Bargain

Mala Compra is Spanish for "bad bargain" or "bad purchase," a name an earlier owner had already given the land. Hernández bought it on March 11, 1816, when Father Miguel Crosby sold him a tract described as 800 acres on the coastal barrier island (the county's park page says 724), "with its adjoining marshes on the Matanzas River," for 1,500 pesos. That same year the Spanish government granted him a large tract across the river, where he grew about 700 acres of sugarcane and called the place St. Joseph plantation. In 1818 he added Bella Vista, 375 acres bought from Francis Ferreira, a sea captain who trafficked enslaved Africans in Florida. Mala Compra became part of one of northeastern Florida's largest plantation systems, totaling 2,265 acres. Mala Compra itself was high hammock land once covered almost entirely in Southern live oak, and its fields, it was said, eventually turned as white and sandy as the Atlantic beach, yet still produced abundant crops of Sea Island cotton.

## The People Who Worked the Land

From 1816 to 1836, Mala Compra ran primarily as a cotton plantation built on slavery. Its buildings included a corn crib, a driver's house and a privy, along with cabins for the people who were forced to labor there. By 1836, 80 people were enslaved on the plantation, where cotton and corn were grown. Their labor made the place run; the main house belonged to the Hernández family, who lived there when they were not at their home in St. Augustine. Hernández rose high. In 1822 and 1823 he served as Florida's first delegate to the U.S. Congress, the first Hispanic member of Congress, and in 1830 he wrote from Mala Compra to Dr. William H. Simmons about cultivating Cuban tobacco. In 1832 the territory even chartered a company to dig a canal beginning at the plantation and running south about eleven or twelve miles toward Smith's Creek.

## Burned in the War

The Second Seminole War ended all of it. In the winter of 1835 and 1836, the United States took both Mala Compra and St. Joseph into military possession, fortifying the houses as military posts, and troops consumed the large stores of corn, hay, sugarcane and cattle. That spring, during a temporary evacuation, the Seminoles struck. They burned every building of consequence on the sugar plantation and, at Mala Compra, the cotton warehouse that had been turned into a fortification. They captured three of the 80 enslaved people and drove the rest off. In an 1837 account to the county court, Joseph S. Sanchez reported that his militia troops had found oxen, cattle and horses, garden enclosures and "an extensive and thriving sweet orange grove, said to contain 1,500 trees." Hernández went on to command Florida militia in the war, and in October 1837, while Osceola was negotiating under a white flag of truce, Hernández took the Seminole leader captive on the orders of General Jesup.

## What the Ground Remembers

Archaeologists began preliminary investigations in 1999 and found the family's house and its detached kitchen. Historical accounts had described a framed house of a story and a half on a masonry foundation, but the excavation revealed a building almost twice that size, with a coquina block foundation on its eastern side, masonry footers on its western side, traces of wooden floors and surfaces of tabby concrete. More than 14,000 artifacts came out of the ground: fragments of ceramics and wine bottles, kitchen utensils, kaolin pipes, hinges, nails and gun parts. The site was added to the National Register of Historic Places on March 5, 2004, and the house and kitchen are now interpreted for visitors at Bings Landing, west of State Road A1A.

## Flight Context

The site lies at about 29.616°N, 81.203°W at Bings Landing County Park in Palm Coast, Flagler County, on the east bank of the Matanzas River just west of State Road A1A. From 1,500–2,500 feet, follow the narrow Matanzas River and the coastal barrier island with A1A and the Atlantic beach to the east; the park sits on the island's river side. Flagler Executive Airport (KFIN) is about 9 nm to the south, and Northeast Florida Regional Airport (KSGJ) at St. Augustine is about 22 nm to the north. Summer afternoons bring frequent thunderstorms along this coast, and sea breezes can add turbulence at low altitude.
