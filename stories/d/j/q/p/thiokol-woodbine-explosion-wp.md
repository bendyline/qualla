# Thiokol-Woodbine explosion

In February 1971 a flare-making building at a coastal Georgia chemical plant exploded, killing 29 workers, after an Army order reclassifying its contents as explosive failed to reach the plant.

**Location:** 30.9241, -81.5152

**Categories:** Industrial fires and explosions in the United States, Chemical plant explosions, 1971 disasters in the United States, Camden County, Georgia, Explosions in 1971

## Introduction

On October 29, 1970, the U.S. Army issued an order that should have changed everything inside building M-132. The trip flares being made there, it said, were to be classed once again with the most dangerous materials in the government's hazard system. Through a communications error, the order never reached the Thiokol chemical plant near Woodbine, Georgia, in time. It arrived three weeks after the building blew apart, on February 3, 1971, killing 29 workers.

## A Rocket Plant Without Rockets

Thiokol Chemical built its Woodbine complex in 1964 on 7,400 acres of the former Floyd Plantation, about 12 miles southeast of Woodbine and 30 miles north of Jacksonville, Florida. Its 36 buildings were meant to test and build solid-propellant rocket motors for NASA, but the space agency changed course and chose liquid fuel, so Thiokol retooled to make other products. In 1969 the company won an Army contract to manufacture 750,000 trip flares for use in the Vietnam War. The work was concentrated in M-132, a large, windowless, T-shaped concrete block building where all three of the flare-making processes took place. About 80 employees worked there, most of them Black women.

## The Chemistry of Danger

The heart of a flare is magnesium. Its shavings can ignite at 950°F, and it burns with a brilliant white light at around 2,500°F. Ordinary firefighting cannot put it out: water reacts with burning magnesium to release hydrogen, which burns too, and carbon dioxide does not work either. In the open, magnesium burns by deflagration, a controlled spread of flame. Confined, it behaves very differently, as heat and pressure build until it detonates in an uncontrolled explosion driven by a shock wave. Before 1967, trip flare components were rated Class 7 hazardous material, a category reserved for the most dangerous substances short of biological and nuclear materials. That year, for reasons that remain unknown, they were downgraded to Class 2, a fire hazard. The Army's 1970 order to restore Class 7 was the correction that never arrived.

## 10:53 in the Morning

At 10:53 a.m. on Wednesday, February 3, 1971, a fire in M-132 ignited large quantities of flares and their components, and they detonated. The building was leveled, three nearby buildings were badly damaged, and the flames spread into the surrounding pines, eventually scorching 200 acres. Windows shattered 11 miles away, and the blast was heard for 50 miles around. Twenty-nine workers died. At least 50 more suffered debilitating injuries, among them severe burns and lost limbs. Another building holding the same materials was already burning, so the site was evacuated quickly. Co-workers and neighbors loaded the less seriously hurt into their own cars and pickups and drove them to hospitals. The gravely injured were carried to the plant entrance, where ambulances rushed them to St. Marys, Folkston, Brunswick and Jacksonville, and four Navy helicopters from NAS Cecil Field and NAS Jacksonville flew the most critical to Duval Medical Center, now UF Health Jacksonville.

## Justice, Slowly

Twenty-five suits were filed in federal court under the Federal Tort Claims Act, seeking a combined $717,526,391. They argued that Army procurement employees had been negligent in failing to tell Thiokol that the flares had been reclassified from a Class 2 fire hazard to a Class 7 explosive. The court agreed, finding that failure to be a proximate cause of the explosion, and entered judgment for the plaintiffs. The Army appealed to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit, which affirmed the verdict 2–1 on October 15, 1979. Even then the wait was not over: the victims received compensation only after another nine years. A granite monument about the size of a full-size bed, bearing the names of those who died, stands at Maple Ford Park. The Thiokol Memorial Museum, established in 2017 and now at 115 South Lee Street in Kingsland, preserves the history of the plant.

## Flight Context

The former Thiokol plant site lies at 30.9241°N, 81.5152°W in Camden County, Georgia, about 12 miles southeast of Woodbine and roughly 30 miles north of Jacksonville. From 2,000–3,000 feet the site blends into the surrounding pine woodland, so use the Atlantic coastline to the east as the main reference. Nearby airports include Jekyll Island Airport (FAA identifier 09J, about 10 miles north-northeast), St. Simons Island (KSSI, about 17 miles north-northeast) and Brunswick Golden Isles (KBQK, about 23 miles north); Fernandina Beach Municipal (KFHB) is about 22 miles south. Coastal fog can form on cool mornings, and summer afternoons bring thunderstorms.
