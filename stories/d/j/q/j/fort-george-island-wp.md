# Fort George Island

At the mouth of the St. Johns River, an island built on Timucua oyster shells holds a Spanish mission site, a vanished British fort, one of the country's best-preserved rows of slave quarters, and the ghost of a Gilded Age resort.

**Location:** 30.4258, -81.4297

**Categories:** Fort George Island, Florida Native American Heritage Trail, Slave cabins and quarters in the United States, State parks of Florida, Parks in Duval County, Florida, Northside, Jacksonville

## Introduction

The walls of the old slave quarters on Fort George Island are made of oysters. Centuries of Timucua meals left enormous mounds of shells here, and in the 19th century those shells were burned and mixed into tabby, a durable concrete found in the foundations of several island buildings. Nearly every era of this small island near the mouth of the St. Johns River, in far northeast Jacksonville, is built on the one before it, sometimes literally.

## The Village of Alicamani

In the early historical period the island was home to Alicamani, a major village of the Timucua chiefdom known as the Saturiwa. Its people left middens, great mounds of shells and discarded food, and here the shells were mostly oyster. Because the native villages were so large, the Spanish chose a nearby site for the Mission of San Juan del Puerto, founded in the later 16th century. It dominated the area until 1702, when the English destroyed it. For years the Franciscan friar Francisco Pareja lived at the mission, studying the Timucua language and compiling books about it that were published in Mexico. Those writings preserve most of what is known about the language today, a precious record of a people later devastated by diseases the Europeans brought.

## A Fort Nobody Can Find

In 1736 James Oglethorpe built Fort George, and the island has carried its name ever since. The fort itself was razed, and no one knows precisely where it stood. A writer in 1887 offered a guess, describing "a long, wide sand-spit" at the island's southeastern tip, flanked by knolls rising above the beach and the salt meadows. "On one of these knolls," he wrote, "was, in my opinion, the spot where the fort stood." When Florida became an English colony from 1763 to 1783, British planters began developing the island, setting the stage for the man who would dominate its next chapter.

## The King of Fort George

Zephaniah Kingsley leased and then owned the entire island from 1814, and he was later remembered as the "King of Fort George." He was a slave trader who owned and captained slave ships, helped smuggle enslaved Africans into the United States through Florida after the 1808 ban, and trained and sold enslaved people from his plantations. Kingsley publicly defended slavery. He also had four wives, including Anna Madgigine Jai Kingsley, each with her own house on the island. He freed his Black wives, and fearing for their and his children's future safety, he eventually moved his family to Haiti, became a Haitian citizen, and sold the plantation to his nephew Kingsley Beatty Gibbs. His main house, kitchen and barn survive, restored. So do the tabby quarters where the enslaved people of the plantation lived, among the best-preserved slave quarters in the country and a lasting record of the lives spent in bondage here.

## Hotel, Freeze and Fever

Later in the 19th century, tourists replaced planters. The luxurious Fort George Hotel opened in 1875, and the island also had a club for locals. St. George Episcopal Church, built in 1882 and 1883, is now on the National Register of Historic Places. Fire destroyed the hotel in 1889, and it was never rebuilt, because the new railroad was delivering tourists to St. Augustine instead. A freeze in 1895 wiped out the island's oranges, and a yellow fever epidemic in 1897 drove away the remaining visitors. In 1928 the Ribault Club opened on the old hotel site as a clubhouse for the affluent, and it hosted grand parties through the late 1920s and 1930s.

## An Island of Layers

Today part of the island belongs to the 46,000-acre Timucuan Ecological and Historic Preserve, which honors the Native people who once lived here, and Kingsley Plantation is preserved on its federal land. A small state park includes the restored Ribault Club, a popular wedding venue that has also served as a visitor center for the island. On its grounds stands the historical marker for the lost Spanish mission. The Mayport–Fort George ferry still carries travelers across the river, and the island has one more distinction: Mount Cornelia, billed as the highest point on the Atlantic coast south of New Jersey (some sources say south of North Carolina). Shell mound, mission marker, plantation, hotel site and club: on Fort George Island, the layers are all still within reach.

## Flight Context

Fort George Island lies at 30.4258°N, 81.4297°W on the north side of the mouth of the St. Johns River in far northeast Jacksonville. From 1,500–2,500 feet, the island stands out as a distinct landmass surrounded by water, with the river mouth, the Mayport ferry crossing and the naval station immediately to the south. Naval Station Mayport (KNRB, military) is only about 2.5 miles away across the river; Jacksonville Executive at Craig (KCRG) is about 8 miles southwest, and Jacksonville International (KJAX) is about 16 miles west-northwest. Respect the military airspace around Mayport, and expect sea breezes and summer afternoon thunderstorms.
