# Gregor MacGregor

The Scottish adventurer who seized Amelia Island in 1817 went on to invent an entire Central American country called Poyais, selling its land and bonds to British investors and sending some 250 settlers to a jungle where most of them died.

**Location:** 30.6887, -81.4569

**Categories:** Confidence tricksters, Impostors, Micronational leaders, Amelia Island, Venezuelan War of Independence, People from Stirling (council area)

## Introduction

On June 29, 1817, a Scottish general led a landing party onto Amelia Island with a line worthy of the stage: "I shall sleep either in hell or Amelia tonight!" Hell could wait. The Spanish commander at Fort San Carlos, badly overestimating the invaders, surrendered without a shot, and Gregor MacGregor raised a green cross on a white field over Fernandina. It was a strange, brief chapter in a life full of them. Five years later, the same man would sell British investors land in a country that did not exist, and send hundreds of ordinary people to find it.

## A Soldier in Borrowed Glory

MacGregor was born on Christmas Eve 1786 at Glengyle, on the north shore of Loch Katrine, into the Clan Gregor, whose famous kinsman Rob Roy was his great-great-uncle. He joined the British Army at sixteen and fought in the Peninsular War, but left in 1810 after a quarrel with a superior officer, by one hostile account, having already made himself unpopular with an obsession for uniforms and medals. Back in Britain he styled himself "Colonel," then "Sir Gregor MacGregor, Bart.," a baronetcy he did not hold. In 1812 he sailed for Venezuela's war of independence, and there, for a while, the pose became real. He married a cousin of Simón Bolívar, helped break out of besieged Cartagena in 1815, and in 1816 led 1,200 men on a 34-day fighting retreat across Venezuela to Barcelona. Bolívar called it "superior to the conquest of an empire," and the retreat earned him the title "Xenophon of the Americas."

## Hell or Amelia

In 1817, self-described "deputies of free America" commissioned MacGregor to take the Floridas from Spain. He raised $160,000 by selling scrip that promised investors fertile Florida acres, and sailed from Charleston with fewer than 80 men. After the easy capture of Fernandina, he proclaimed the "Republic of the Floridas," but few residents rallied to him; most simply left. His recruits stayed stuck in American ports, and money ran short. He tried to tax pirates' plunder, and he tried to raise funds by seizing and selling dozens of enslaved people found on the island. His troops were paid in "Amelia dollars" he had printed, and later not at all. On September 4 he boarded ship as an angry crowd hurled insults, then had medallions struck in Nassau reading "Amelia, I Came, I Saw, I Conquered." He never repaid those who had funded him.

## Out the Window at Porto Bello

What followed was worse. In 1819 MacGregor led British and Irish volunteers against Spanish-held Porto Bello, on the Isthmus of Panama. His second-in-command, Colonel William Rafter, took the town without a fight, but MacGregor made few plans and devoted himself to designing a new chivalric order. When Spanish troops marched back in on April 30, he was still in bed. He threw his bedding out of the window, jumped after it, and tried to paddle out to his ships on a log before being hauled aboard unconscious. Then he sailed away, abandoning Rafter and the garrison; Rafter was later shot with eleven other officers for conspiring to escape. Months later at Rio de la Hacha, MacGregor again boarded ship just as the Spanish attacked, and General Thomas Eyre and the troops left behind were killed. A furious Bolívar ordered him hanged if he ever set foot on the South American mainland again.

## A Country Made of Paper

In 1820, on the Mosquito Coast of Central America, King George Frederic Augustus granted MacGregor some 8,000,000 acres in exchange for rum and jewelry. The land was pleasing to the eye but unfit for farming. In London, MacGregor turned it into Poyais, a nation he claimed to rule as "Cazique." He invented a parliament, an army with custom uniforms, a coat of arms and a legation in the City of London. A 355-page guidebook described a healthy climate, soil yielding three maize harvests a year and a capital, St Joseph, with an opera house, a domed cathedral and up to 20,000 inhabitants. Its rivers, the book claimed, held "globules of pure gold." Buyers snapped up land certificates, some with their life savings, and a respected bank underwrote a £200,000 Poyaisian government loan. MacGregor aimed his recruiting at fellow Scots, invoking the failed Darien colony and promising to right an old national wrong.

## The Black River

The first settlers reached the Black River in November 1822 and found no city, only jungle and some forgotten foundations. A second ship arrived in March 1823. When the rainy season came, malaria and yellow fever swept the camp. "It seemed to be the will of Providence that every circumstance should combine for our destruction," wrote James Hastie, a Scottish sawyer who had brought his wife and three children. The man who had signed on as official shoemaker to the Princess of Poyais, a man who had left a family behind in Edinburgh, took his own life. Of roughly 250 people who sailed, at least 180 died; fewer than 50 ever saw Britain again. MacGregor was tried for fraud in France and acquitted, and kept peddling smaller Poyais schemes until 1837. He died in Caracas in 1845, aged 58, and was buried a war hero in Caracas Cathedral. His obituaries never mentioned Poyais.

## Flight Context

MacGregor's 1817 landing and his brief Republic of the Floridas centered on Fernandina at the northern tip of Amelia Island, Florida; this point lies at approximately 30.6887°N, 81.4569°W, at Fernandina Plaza and the old Fort San Carlos site on the Amelia River waterfront of Old Town Fernandina. From the air, the island's northern tip, the river mouth and the grid of Old Town Fernandina on its bluff above the Amelia River are the key references. A viewing altitude of 1,000–2,000 feet AGL shows the sound, the marshes and the old town together. Fernandina Beach Municipal Airport (FAA identifier FHB) is about 4.6 nm to the south, and Jacksonville International Airport (KJAX) lies about 17 nm to the southwest under Jacksonville's Class C airspace. Summer sea breezes often build afternoon thunderstorms; mornings and the cooler months are usually clearest.
