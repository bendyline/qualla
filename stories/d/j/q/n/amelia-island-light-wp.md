# Amelia Island Light

Florida's oldest existing lighthouse began life on a Georgia island, was taken down and rebuilt on Amelia Island in 1838, and still sweeps the shoals of Nassau Sound with a lens installed in 1903.

**Location:** 30.6732, -81.4424

**Categories:** Lighthouses completed in 1838, Lighthouses on the National Register of Historic Places in Florida, Transportation buildings and structures in Nassau County, Florida, National Register of Historic Places in Nassau County, Florida, 1838 establishments in Florida Territory, Amelia Island

## Introduction

The oldest existing lighthouse in Florida started out in Georgia. In 1820 a tower went up on the southern tip of Cumberland Island, just north of the inlet. Eighteen years later it was taken down and rebuilt on a hill near the northern end of Amelia Island, using materials from the old Georgia light. The recycled tower has been guiding ships ever since. Every ten seconds its white beam flashes across the water, and when it sweeps between 344 and 360 degrees it turns red, a warning to mariners that they are looking toward the shoals around Nassau Sound.

## A Lighthouse That Moved

The Cumberland Island light had stood for less than two decades when its tower was dismantled in 1838 and carried south to Amelia Island, where it rose again as a brick tower about 50 feet tall. Placing it on a hill gave the modest structure extra reach. That original Cumberland station is remembered as the predecessor of today's Little Cumberland Island Light. The Amelia Island light was first lit in 1839. There is something fitting in a lighthouse built from secondhand brick at the border of two states, as if the coast itself had decided the beacon was more useful on the other side of the inlet. Nearly two centuries later, the tower's pedigree remains its most unusual feature, a Florida landmark whose oldest pieces were first laid in Georgia.

## Fourteen Lamps to a Fresnel Lens

When it first shone in 1839, the light came from 14 lamps, each backed by a 14-inch reflector. By 1848 the reflectors had grown to 15 inches. The tower itself grew in 1881, when a new lantern was installed on top, raising the structure to 64 feet and lifting the light's focal plane to 107 feet above sea level. A major change came in 1903. That year the lamps-and-reflectors arrangement was replaced by a third-order Barbier Benard Fresnel lens, and remarkably, that same lens is still in use today. Each ten-second flash comes through glass that has been doing the job for more than a century, including the red sector that guards the shoal water of Nassau Sound.

## Keepers and Kin

More than twenty head keepers tended this light and its Georgia predecessor, and their roster reads like a small family saga. Robert Church kept the original Cumberland Island light from 1820 to 1829, and Amos Latham then served from 1829 to 1842. Some keepers stayed only a year or two; Christopher C. Morse's tenure is listed simply as 1859. Others dug in for decades. Thomas Patrick O'Hagan kept the light from 1905 to 1925, and his son, Thomas John O'Hagan, took over and stayed until 1954. The final entries on the roster belong to Otho O. Brown, a Coast Guard keeper who served two separate terms, in the late 1950s and again from the mid-1960s until 1970.

## The Light Goes Automatic

The year Brown's second term ended, 1970, the Amelia Island Light was automated, and the long line of keepers came to a close. The light did not. It remains in operation. After about 18 months dark because of mechanical failures, the beacon was restored in autumn 2024 with help from the Coast Guard Auxiliary, and it again flashes white every ten seconds from its hilltop near the northern tip of the island, still turning red over the shoal water of Nassau Sound. For a structure that has been dismantled, moved across a state line, heightened, re-lensed and finally left to run on its own, it has proved remarkably steady. An old oil building still keeps the tower company, and the Fresnel lens that arrived in 1903 keeps sending out the beam that generations of keepers once tended by hand.

## Flight Context

The Amelia Island Light stands near the northern end of Amelia Island in northeastern Florida, at 30.6732N, 81.4424W, close to the Georgia line and the inlet that separates Amelia Island from Cumberland Island to the north. From about 1,500 feet, look for the tower on its rise near the island's north end. Fernandina Beach Municipal Airport (FAA code FHB, GPS ident KFHB) is only about 3.8 nm south-southwest. (The former St. Marys Airport across the river in Georgia closed in 2017 and should not be used.) Jacksonville International (KJAX) is about 17 nm southwest and Naval Station Mayport (KNRB) about 17 nm south, so check the Jacksonville-area airspace before planning a low pass. Coastal fog is possible on winter mornings.
