# Fort San Carlos

On a bluff above the Amelia River, a small Spanish fort of wood and earth watched Fernandina change hands again and again, flying the flags of Spain, rebel patriots, a Scottish adventurer's republic, revolutionary Mexico and the United States before the river itself carried most of it away.

**Location:** 30.6887, -81.4569

**Categories:** Forts in Florida, Spanish forts in the United States, Amelia Island, State parks of Florida, Spanish Florida, 1816 establishments in North America

## Introduction

In the space of six months in 1817, Fort San Carlos answered to Spain, to a self-proclaimed "Republic of the Floridas," to revolutionary Mexico and finally to the United States. At the end of June the Spanish colors came down and a green cross on a white field went up. In September the flag of revolutionary Mexico was raised, and two days before Christmas the fort was surrendered to American forces. That dizzying year was only one chapter in the story of a bluff at the northern end of Amelia Island, where a small fort of wood and earth guarded the Spanish town of Fernandina and its deep harbor on the Amelia River.

## Four Thousand Years on the Bluff

Long before any fort, people lived here. Archaeology that began in the early 1950s has revealed intermittent use of the site for as long as 4,000 years, beginning in the Orange period, and nearly all of Old Town was built over a Timucua village and its shell heaps. In 1696 the Spanish built a sentinel house at that village. The site's value was obvious: a deep harbor near the northern edge of Spanish Florida. After Carolina governor James Moore's raids of 1702, though, the place stood largely empty for decades. When Britain handed East Florida back to Spain in the 1780s, a widow named Maria Mattair and her family were the only known occupants left on the island, living on a 200-acre grant along the bluff. The Spanish government took possession of the land in 1788, and in 1795 a Spanish force under Colonel Charles Howard drove off American rebels who had attacked the garrison, built a battery and raised the French flag.

## A Town by the Laws of the Indies

On May 10, 1811, acting governor Juan José Estrada ordered the new public surveyor, George J. F. Clarke, to lay out Fernandina according to the Spanish Laws of the Indies, whose 1573 town-planning ordinances prescribed how Spanish towns should be sited and arranged in a classical grid with places reserved for a fort and parade ground. Clarke's plan survives. An 1821 map shows a street layout nearly identical to Old Town's today, and the fort's parade ground fronted a street, Estrada, that still exists. The town sat on high ground behind the bluff, on a wide peninsula bounded by Egan's Creek and salt marsh. A strong picket with a blockhouse mounting two 4-pounder cannon guarded the narrow neck of land to the southeast. Fort San Carlos itself, finished in 1816, was a lunette of wood and earthworks backed by a palisade. It commanded the anchorage with four long Spanish 16-pounders, five 4-pounders and a 6-pound carronade.

## Patriots and Gunboats

Fernandina was a smugglers' haven and a threat to American trade, and in 1812 the United States moved against it. With President James Madison's approval, General George Mathews backed a band of Americans calling themselves the "Patriots of Amelia Island." On March 16, nine American gunboats under Commodore Hugh Campbell lined up in the harbor with their guns trained on the town. The Spanish commandant, Don Justo Lopez, acknowledged the superior force and surrendered. The Patriots raised their own standard, and the next day they handed the town to Mathews, who brought over 250 regular U.S. troops and ordered the Stars and Stripes raised at once. The annexation scheme soon collapsed. Congress feared war with Spain, Secretary of State James Monroe relieved Mathews, and on May 6, 1813, the last American troops lowered their flag and crossed back into Georgia. Spain then completed the new Fort San Carlos in 1816.

## The Summer of Flags

By 1817 the garrison numbered only about fifty men, most of them aging veterans, and the town held fewer than two hundred residents. Fernandina was booming on smuggling, especially the importation of enslaved Africans in defiance of the American ban on the trade. On June 29, the Scottish adventurer Gregor MacGregor attacked with a small force after spreading rumors of an army of more than a thousand. His men crossed Egan's Creek in small groups from several directions, and commander Francisco Morales struck his flag and fled. MacGregor raised his Green Cross of Florida and proclaimed the "Republic of the Floridas," but he sailed away in September. On September 13, about 300 Spanish troops backed by two gunboats shelled the town from a battery on McLure's Hill. The fort's guns answered, killing two Spanish soldiers, and after firing until dark the Spanish commander withdrew. Days later the French privateer Luis Aury sailed in, and on September 21 he raised the flag of revolutionary Mexico. His privateers preyed on Spanish slave ships, but the captives aboard did not gain their freedom; most appear to have been smuggled into Georgia and other states.

## What the River Took

Aury's raids, worth more than $500,000 in Spanish goods in two months by Lloyd's of London's reckoning, threatened Monroe's negotiations to buy Florida, and American forces arrived to end them. On December 23, 1817, Aury surrendered the island and the fort, which the United States held "in trust" for Spain until the Adams-Onís Treaty took effect on February 22, 1821. The U.S. Army made little use of Fort San Carlos and soon abandoned it. Local Confederate militia occupied it during the Civil War, and American troops used it briefly in 1898. Meanwhile the Amelia River kept eating at the bluff, and archaeologists estimate that two-thirds of the fort's site has washed away. What remains lies within Fernandina Plaza Historic State Park, the largest known undeveloped piece of the Spanish town and military post on Amelia Island.

## Flight Context

The site of Fort San Carlos lies in Old Town Fernandina, at the northern end of Amelia Island, Florida, at approximately 30.6887°N, 81.4569°W, on a bluff above the Amelia River. Nothing of the fort is visible from the air; look instead for the grid of Old Town on its peninsula north of modern Fernandina Beach, with the Amelia River to the west and the mouth of the St. Marys River, marking the Georgia state line, just to the north. A viewing altitude of 1,000–2,000 feet AGL shows the river, the marshes and Cumberland Sound. Fernandina Beach Municipal Airport (FAA identifier FHB) is about 4.5 nm to the south, and Jacksonville International Airport (KJAX) lies about 17 nm to the southwest beneath Jacksonville's Class C airspace. Sea breezes often build afternoon thunderstorms in summer; mornings and the cooler months are usually clearest.
