# Oldest Wooden School House

A cypress-and-cedar schoolhouse near St. Augustine's city gate, chained to the ground against hurricanes, that guides trace to tax records from 1716 and say taught girls alongside boys from 1788.

**Location:** 29.8973, -81.3136

**Categories:** Educational institutions established in the 1700s, Defunct schools in Florida, Buildings and structures in St. Augustine, Florida, Schoolhouses in the United States, Museums in St. Augustine, Florida, History museums in Florida, Education museums in the United States, Wooden buildings and structures in the United States

## Introduction

A heavy chain wraps around the little building at 14 St. George Street. It is not there to keep anyone in. It was fastened in place in 1937 to anchor the house to the ground if a hurricane ever tried to carry it off, and it has become the most memorable feature of a structure that sells itself on age. The Oldest Wooden School House, standing near St. Augustine's old city gate, is touted as the oldest wooden school building in the United States. Nobody knows exactly when it was built. Visitor guides say it shows up on tax records in 1716, though the museum's own history starts with property records from 1740, which makes it old enough for the claim to be interesting and cautious enough for a careful visitor to read the fine print.

## What the Fire Left Behind

The building's age is bounded by a catastrophe. In 1702 the British burned St. Augustine, and no wooden building in the city from before that fire still stands. The schoolhouse, by the guides' reckoning, belongs to the rebuilt town that rose from the ashes, sometime before its reported first appearance on the tax rolls in 1716. The property is said to have originally belonged to a man named Juan Genoply (the museum spells it Genopoly), although the museum's own history has him buying the home in 1780. Its walls are bald cypress and red cedar, fastened together with wooden pins and iron spikes. Not every board is original; recent maintenance has replaced the roofing, among other repairs. Still, those pinned and spiked walls of cypress and cedar are what visitors step inside today, and the chain outside is a reminder of how seriously the town takes the job of keeping them upright.

## Upstairs, Downstairs, Out Back

The house began as a family home and was later arranged around a classroom. Lessons happened on the ground floor, and the schoolmaster and his family lived on the second floor directly above. Cooking was banished to a separate building, a precaution that kept the heat of the hearth away from the living quarters and reduced the danger of fire in a town that knew exactly what fire could do. The classroom has one more distinction. Guides say it educated both boys and girls from 1788, making it one of the first co-educational classrooms in what is now the United States, while the museum's own history describes a school that taught Minorcan Quarter children until its last class in 1864. Picture a single room of mixed pupils bent over their lessons, the teacher's household creaking overhead and the kitchen standing apart in the yard behind.

## A Title With an Asterisk

The superlative deserves a little scrutiny, and the history supports it only in a narrow sense. Rival claimants exist, and the dating is shaky: the museum's own history says the house that stands today was built around 1800 to 1810. The Portsmouth Historical Society in Rhode Island owns a schoolhouse dated to 1725 that is described as one of the oldest surviving in the country, and the Voorlezer's House on Staten Island, New York, long dated to before 1696, is now thought to have been built in the mid-18th century. St. Augustine's building claims the more specific title of oldest wooden school building, and it is only "touted" as holding even that. None of this makes the place less charming. It simply makes it more honest to visit as what it is: a very old, very small wooden schoolhouse that has come through storms, fire risk and three centuries of city life around it.

## Animatronic Lessons

Today the school runs as a museum, open every day except Christmas, with longer hours on Fridays and Saturdays. The tour is self-guided, and the history lesson comes from an unexpected pair of narrators: an animatronic teacher and student built by Sally Corp. Informational signs and period items fill the rooms. Behind the house, the gardens hold exhibits on the old kitchen, a rebuilt outhouse, an old well and a sculpture garden of busts depicting famous educators. Those busts came from an ambitious project called the Grove of Educators, which hoped to gather statues of educators from every country in the Americas. Only a few countries took part, so the garden remains a small, slightly wistful gathering of teachers from across the hemisphere.
