# Villa Zorayda

A Boston millionaire's 1883 winter home modeled on Spain's Alhambra, Villa Zorayda was billed as Florida's first Moorish Revival house and St. Augustine's first poured-concrete building, and it still holds the collection of the Syrian-born merchant whose family has owned it since 1913.

## Introduction

Above the front entrance of a house on King Street in St. Augustine, Arabic script spells out a phrase copied from the walls of the Alhambra in Granada: "There is no conqueror but God." Behind it lies Villa Zorayda, a Moorish palace in miniature built in 1883 as the winter home of an eccentric Boston millionaire and amateur architect. It is described as the first house in Florida built in the Moorish Revival style, and more than a century later it still holds the treasures of two very different collectors.

## A Bostonian's Alhambra

Franklin W. Smith was a founder of the Boston YMCA and a man drawn to reform efforts and schemes for public improvement throughout his long life. For his winter home he borrowed from the Alhambra Palace in Granada, and he named the house after one of the princesses in Washington Irving's Tales of the Alhambra. Villa Zorayda is also described as the first poured-concrete building in St. Augustine. Smith used crushed coquina shells as the aggregate and poured the walls in successive levels, a method that Henry Morrison Flagler, the Standard Oil partner and Florida developer, adopted on an even grander scale for his nearby hotels and churches. Smith kept building. One block east stands the Casa Monica Hotel, a larger project of his, which Flagler later bought and renamed the Cordova. Smith is buried in Mount Auburn Cemetery in Cambridge, Massachusetts.

## The Merchant from Syria

In 1913, two years after Smith's death, the house, along with part of his art and antique collection, passed to Abraham Mussallem, a rug and antiquities merchant originally from Syria. The villa went on to lead several lives. Beyond its role as a private residence, it served at various times as a restaurant, a nightclub and gambling casino, and a hotel. On September 23, 1993, it was added to the National Register of Historic Places. Renovations began in 2003, and the building reopened to the public in 2008 as the Villa Zorayda Museum, still owned by the Mussallem family. Audio tours in English, Spanish and French walk visitors through the house, its place in St. Augustine's history and the collection that Smith and Mussallem assembled.

## Lace Made of Plaster

Inside, the walls do the talking. Reliefs of cast plaster mixed with ground alabaster, known as traceries or arabesques, replicate walls of the Alhambra and match the originals in Granada. Hand-painted wood panels and tiles frame the rooms, while elaborately designed fireplaces and doorways, geometric windows and stained glass catch the light. The collection fills the spaces between: hand-pierced brass lamps from Damascus and elsewhere in the Middle East, Oriental rugs, sculptures, carved furniture, decorative tiles and Egyptian artifacts. The result could be considered the first example of fantasy architecture in Florida: a Boston reformer's dream of Spain, kept alive by a family from Syria.

## Flight Context

Villa Zorayda stands at 29.8917°N, 81.3151°W at 83 King Street in historic downtown St. Augustine, one block west of the Casa Monica Hotel. From 1,500–2,500 feet, the old city's tight street grid runs down to the Matanzas Bay waterfront a short distance east; the villa itself is small and best appreciated at street level. Northeast Florida Regional Airport (KSGJ) is about 5 miles north-northwest. Coastal haze and summer afternoon thunderstorms are common; clear winter days offer the best views over the old city.
