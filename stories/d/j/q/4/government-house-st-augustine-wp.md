# Government House (St. Augustine)

On the same lot beside St. Augustine's central plaza since 1598, Government House sheltered Spanish and British governors, reportedly hosted Daniel Boone and Nathanael Greene, fell to ruin, and was rebuilt by the future architect of the Washington Monument.

**Location:** 29.8926, -81.3130

**Categories:** Museums in St. Augustine, Florida, Spanish Florida, British Florida, University of Florida, Colonial architecture

## Introduction

By one account, Daniel Boone came to East Florida looking into buying land, and while he was there he became a guest in this house, hosted by James Grant, the British royal governor who lived here from 1764 to 1771. Boone was one visitor among many. For more than a century, the coquina building at 48 King Street, beside St. Augustine's Plaza de la Constitución, was where power in Florida lived, whichever flag happened to fly over it.

## A Governor's Lot Since 1598

The site's story as a governor's home begins in 1598, when Governor Gonzalo Méndez de Canzo became the first governor to build his residence here. In 1706 a new structure went up to serve as a residence, an office, a courthouse and the social center of the town, and the east wing of today's building dates to that construction, completed by 1713. From about 1710 it served as the governor's official residence, through the rest of the First Spanish Period, which had begun in 1565 and lasted until 1763, and on through every change of ownership that followed. Built of coquina, it stood at the heart of the colonial town, facing its central plaza.

## Three Flags, One Front Door

The 1763 Treaty of Paris handed Florida to Britain, and Grant moved in. Among his guests were Boone and Patrick Tonyn, the man who would succeed him as governor. Twenty years later another Treaty of Paris returned Florida and St. Augustine to Spain at the close of the American Revolution. In 1784 the Revolutionary War general Nathanael Greene reportedly visited, and Governor Vicente Manuel de Zéspedes hosted him for an elaborate seven-course meal. The house underwent major renovation between 1785 and 1787. The last governor to use it was Enrique White, who died in 1811, and its use as the governor's residence ended in 1812.

## From Ruin to a Library

By the time the United States annexed Florida in 1821, only the walls were left standing. In 1833 and 1834 the building was rebuilt with federal funds from plans drawn by Robert Mills, the architect later famous for designing the Washington Monument. Its next public role was a quieter one. In 1870, Francis L. Wilson began negotiating to run a subscription library in two rooms of what was then the U.S. Customs House, and when he received permission in 1874, the old governor's house officially became home to what the St. Johns County library system calls the oldest library in Florida. It stayed for twenty-one years before moving in 1895 to Wilson's property at 12 Aviles Street, the Segui-Kirby Smith House.

## Kings on the Balcony

A library returned in the modern era. Today's Governor's House Library began as the research collection of the St. Augustine Historical Restoration and Preservation Commission, later the Historic St. Augustine Preservation Board, which studied how the city's colonial houses were constructed and furnished, who lived in them and how they worked and played, gathering books, photographs, maps, archives and artifacts. After the state abolished the board in 1997, the city ran the library for more than a decade, and since 2010 it has been managed by UF Historic St. Augustine, Inc., with the University of Florida's George A. Smathers Libraries on the second floor. Spain has not forgotten the house. On April 1, 2001, King Juan Carlos and Queen Sofía appeared on its east balcony, and King Felipe VI and Queen Letizia visited on September 18, 2015. Government House was added to the National Register of Historic Places on January 7, 2014.

## Flight Context

Government House stands at about 29.893°N, 81.313°W at 48 King Street, on the edge of the Plaza de la Constitución in downtown St. Augustine. From 1,000–2,000 feet, find the open rectangle of the plaza among the tight colonial street grid; the star-shaped coquina Castillo de San Marcos on the western shore of Matanzas Bay, a few blocks to the north, makes the best reference point. Northeast Florida Regional Airport (KSGJ) is about 4 nm to the north. Coastal summers bring frequent afternoon thunderstorms, and sea breezes can make low-level air bumpy.
