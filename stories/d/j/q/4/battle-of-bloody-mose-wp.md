# Battle of Bloody Mose

In 1740, free Black militiamen who had escaped slavery helped Spanish troops retake Fort Mose, a refuge north of St. Augustine, in one of the bloodiest actions of the War of Jenkins' Ear.

**Location:** 29.9278, -81.3253

**Categories:** Battles in Florida, Battles of the War of Jenkins' Ear, Conflicts in 1740, Sieges involving the Kingdom of Great Britain, Sieges involving Spain, Spanish Florida, African-American history of Florida, 1740 in North America

## Introduction

Francisco Menéndez had escaped from slavery in South Carolina. By 1740 he was a captain in the Spanish militia of Florida, commanding men who, like him, had fled bondage in Georgia and South Carolina. In June of that year, when a British force seized their home at Fort Mose, just north of St. Augustine, Menéndez and his militia joined Spanish regulars in a dawn counterattack to take it back. The battle that followed is remembered as Bloody Mose.

## A Refuge Two Miles North

Spain founded Fort Mose in 1738, two miles north of St. Augustine, as a refuge for people escaping slavery in Georgia and South Carolina. The policy behind it went back to 1693, when King Charles II ordered his Florida colonists to grant freedom and protection to runaways who converted to Catholicism and agreed to serve Spain. The settlement was modest: a church, a timber wall with a few towers, and some twenty houses where about a hundred people lived. Governor Manuel de Montiano commissioned its men as Spanish militia under Menéndez, who was born in West Africa and was of Mandinka descent. For Spain, Mose was both a colony of free people and the front line of Florida's defense against the British colonies. It is recognized as the first legally sanctioned free Black settlement in what would become the United States.

## Word Travels North

News of a free Black town in Spanish Florida did not stay in Florida. It reached the Province of South Carolina, and it is believed to have helped inspire the Stono Rebellion in September 1739; during that revolt, several dozen people headed for Spanish Florida and were recruited into the militia. The same year, the War of Jenkins' Ear broke out between Britain and Spain. Georgia's governor, General James Oglethorpe, raised a major expedition to capture St. Augustine, the capital of Spanish Florida. His force combined regular troops from South Carolina and Georgia, militia volunteers, hundreds of Muscogee (Creek) and Yuchi allies and a large number of enslaved Black laborers, with Royal Navy ships in support at sea; accounts of the numbers vary widely. Oglethorpe understood that he needed Fort Mose, and his men took it. Colonel John Palmer and 170 British soldiers held the fort while the siege of St. Augustine went on.

## Dawn at Mose

Inside the besieged city, Montiano weighed his options. Mose was strategically vital and, in British hands, vulnerable, so he ordered a counteroffensive. At dawn, Captain Antonio Salgado led a column of 300 Spanish regulars, while Menéndez led the free Black militia and allied Indian auxiliaries in a surprise attack on the fort. The British garrison was caught off guard. The fighting was hand to hand, with swords, muskets and clubs, and it was brutal: roughly 70 British soldiers were killed and 34 captured. Palmer's garrison was virtually annihilated, and the fort itself was destroyed in the struggle. For the men of the militia, the fight was for more than a strategic position. The ground they retook was the place where they had built lives as free people.

## What the Marsh Remembers

Spain did not rebuild Fort Mose until 1752. When Florida passed to Britain in 1763, Mose's residents left for Cuba with the departing Spanish settlers, along with other free and enslaved Black Floridians (one count puts those who left at some 400). Centuries later, from 1986 to 1988, a team led by Kathleen Deagan of the Florida Museum of Natural History carried out an archaeological and historical investigation of the site. Researchers found remains of the second fort underground; the exact location of the original 1738 fort has never been pinned down. Fort Mose was designated a National Historic Landmark on October 12, 1994. Today, at the edge of a salt marsh, a visitor center, a small museum and a replica of the 1738 fort tell the story of the people who defended it.

## Flight Context

The Fort Mose site lies at 29.9278°N, 81.3253°W on the edge of the salt marshes just north of St. Augustine. From 1,500–2,500 feet, look for open marsh and tidal creeks along the Tolomato River, with the old city of St. Augustine and its Castillo de San Marcos about two miles to the south. Northeast Florida Regional Airport (KSGJ) in St. Augustine is only about 2 nm north-northwest, so the site sits under its traffic pattern area and Class D airspace; contact the tower before overflying. Jacksonville International (KJAX) is about 39 nm north-northwest. Summer afternoons bring frequent thunderstorms, and coastal fog can linger on winter mornings.
