# Siege of St. Augustine (1702)

For roughly fifty days in 1702, English Carolinians and their Native allies besieged St. Augustine, but their cannon barely marked the coquina walls of the Castillo de San Marcos, and a relief fleet from Havana sent Governor James Moore home in disgrace.

**Location:** 29.8978, -81.3114

**Categories:** Battles of the Queen Anne's War, Sieges involving Spain, Sieges involving the Kingdom of England, Spanish Florida, Castillo de San Marcos, 1702 in North America

## Introduction

As the English closed their circle around St. Augustine in November 1702, a Spanish foraging party did something audacious. It drove 163 head of cattle straight through the enemy lines and into the dry moat of the Castillo de San Marcos. Inside the walls waited the town's people, gathered by a governor who had learned of the invasion in time and meant to outlast it. Outside stood Governor James Moore of Carolina, with hundreds of colonists and Native warriors and a few small cannon. What followed was a siege of roughly fifty days, a test of whether stone could beat iron.

## Rival Empires

Trouble had been building for decades. The English founding of the Province of Carolina in 1663 and Charles Town in 1670 raised tensions with the Spanish, who had long held Florida, and traders and slavers from Carolina pushed south, provoking raids and reprisals on both sides. When news of Queen Anne's War reached the colonies in 1702, Moore persuaded Carolina's assembly to fund an expedition against St. Augustine. His force, perhaps 500 colonists and 300 to 400 Yamasee, Tallapoosa and Alabama warriors led principally by a Yamasee chief named Arratommakaw, gathered at Port Royal that autumn. Their target was a fortress built for exactly this threat. The Castillo de San Marcos had risen in the later seventeenth century, partly because earlier English raids had shown wooden fortifications to be inadequate, and partly in answer to Charles Town itself.

## Everyone Into the Fort

Native people friendly to the Spanish heard about the recruiting, and word reached Governor José de Zúñiga y la Cerda on October 27. He ordered the townspeople into the fort, seized the food stores for what he reckoned would be a three-month siege, and sent messengers to Pensacola, Havana and the French at Mobile. His defenders were a mixed and uncertain lot: 174 regular soldiers and 14 artillerymen, plus 44 European townsmen fit for action, 123 Native men, most with poor or useless weapons, and 57 Black men, free and enslaved, of whom only twenty had any experience with arms. Zúñiga distrusted the Native and Black defenders and judged that only about 70 men in the whole force were ready for battle. He even complained that his gunners "lacked discipline." He chose to wait behind the walls. Meanwhile, Moore's deputy, Daniell, had landed on Amelia Island on November 3 and swept south, overrunning Spanish settlements and driving a flood of refugees ahead of him.

## Cannon Against Coquina

The English entered the undefended town on November 10 and began their siege. The fort's guns opened fire at once, though one old Spanish cannon exploded that first day, killing three defenders and wounding five. A few days later, Zúñiga sent men out to destroy the parts of town within range of his guns, costing more than 15,000 pesos in property by later accounts. Moore's four small cannon made little impression on the fort's coquina walls, and the longer-ranged Spanish guns kept most of his men at a distance. Around November 22 he sent Daniell to Jamaica for heavier artillery, and his troops dug trenches close enough to fire from musket range. Nothing worked, so Moore burned more of the town, including the Franciscan monastery. On December 14 he tried deception. A Yamasee couple entered the fort posing as refugees, apparently intending to blow up the powder magazine. Zúñiga grew suspicious, and by his own account they were tortured until they admitted the plot.

## Sails From Havana

Zúñiga's messengers did their work. Word reached Havana, where Governor Pedro Nicolás Benítez convened a war council on December 2 and dispatched more than 200 infantry under Captain López de Solloso aboard a small fleet led by General Estevan de Berroa in the Black Eagle. The ships appeared off St. Augustine in late December (the National Park Service says December 26, Wikipedia December 28). Berroa, apparently believing the siege already over, landed no one. The next day Zúñiga smuggled men out of the fort to reach him, and Solloso and about 70 raw recruits went ashore on Anastasia Island, three miles below the fort, and Spanish vessels blocked the southern inlet to Matanzas Bay. Moore lifted the siege the following day. With eight of his ships trapped in the bay, he burned them and retreated north, returning to Charles Town in disgrace. The expedition's cost to Carolina, including compensation for the lost ships, sparked riots in Charles Town.

## The Longer Shadow

The casualty counts from both sides are probably unreliable. Moore reported two dead; Zúñiga claimed more than 60 English killed against three or four of his own. The king commended Zúñiga and promoted him to the governorship of Cartagena, while he filed highly critical complaints that Berroa had landed only his weakest troops and sailed home within about a week. But the defense of one fortress did not save Spanish Florida. In 1704 Moore returned with a few Carolinians and a large band of Native allies to destroy Spanish missions, and by August 1706 the Carolinians had destroyed nearly everything in Spanish Florida from the Apalachicola to the St. Johns River. St. Augustine was the only colonial settlement left in Spanish hands, and the province never truly recovered from the loss of its people. The Castillo endured another siege in 1740 and still stands, a National Monument managed by the National Park Service.

## Flight Context

The Castillo de San Marcos stands on the waterfront of St. Augustine, Florida, at approximately 29.8978°N, 81.3114°W. Its star-shaped bastioned outline beside the Matanzas River is one of the most recognizable historic landmarks on the Florida coast, with the St. Augustine Inlet and Anastasia Island, where the Havana relief force landed, lying to the east. A viewing altitude of about 1,000–1,500 feet AGL, as regulations permit over the city, shows the fort, the old town and the inlet together. Northeast Florida Regional Airport (KSGJ) is only about 4 nm to the north-northwest, so remain mindful of its traffic and airspace. Summer sea breezes often trigger afternoon thunderstorms; winter mornings usually offer the clearest views.
