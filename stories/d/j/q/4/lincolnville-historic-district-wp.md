# Lincolnville Historic District

Founded by freed people on land leased for a dollar a year, St. Augustine's Lincolnville produced baseball pioneers and a beach of its own, served as the base of the 1964 civil rights campaign, and still fights to keep its historic streets standing.

**Location:** 29.8847, -81.3144

**Categories:** Historic districts on the National Register of Historic Places in Florida, St. Augustine, Florida, African-American history of Florida, Civil rights movement, Populated places established by African Americans

## Introduction

Peter Sanks, Matilda Papy, Harriet Weedman, Miles Hancock, Israel McKenzie, Aaron DuPont and Tom Solana had only recently been freed from slavery when, in 1866, they leased land for $1.00 a year on the west bank of Maria Sanchez Creek, across the water from the developed part of St. Augustine. The settlement they started on that southwest peninsula of the city came to be called Little Africa, and later Lincolnville. Over the next century it would give the country baseball pioneers and a beach resort of its own, and serve as the base of a civil rights campaign that drew national attention.

## A Dollar a Year

In those early years the rest of the peninsula belonged to two large orange-grove plantations, the Dumas plantation "Yalaha," from a Seminole word for orange, at the northern end and "Buena Esperanza," Spanish for "Good Hope," to the south. In the 1860s the northwest corner of what is now Lincolnville was a 5-acre orange grove owned by John Hay, Abraham Lincoln's private secretary and later Theodore Roosevelt's Secretary of State. The freed families called their settlement Africa, or Little Africa, and after streets were laid out in 1878 it became known as Lincolnville. Over the decades it spread out from its first blocks around Washington, Oneida, Dumas, St. Francis, St. Benedict and DeHaven streets. Its narrow streets, small lots and houses built close to the street line echoed the old colonial pattern of St. Augustine itself.

## In Flagler's Shadow

When Standard Oil magnate Henry Flagler arrived in the 1880s to remake St. Augustine into a "Winter Newport" for the wealthy, Lincolnville's geography changed with it. Flagler filled in the northern reaches of Maria Sanchez Creek to create high ground, using fill that included dirt, and archaeological remains, dug from the site and surroundings of Fort Mose. His partner William Warden dredged the southern part of the creek into what is now Maria Sanchez Lake, pushing Lincolnville's eastern edge to the Ponce de Leon Barracks at 172–180 Cordova Street. In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, the Barracks housed African-American servants and other workers from Flagler's hotels. In the 1940s the Flagler estate converted it into the Lakeside Apartments and, under the state's segregation laws, rented only to white tenants. Today the Barracks is considered one of the historic district's major buildings. The building was later redeveloped as condominiums.

## Ballplayers, a Beach and a Movement

Some of the African-American waiters from those hotels played for the Cuban Giants, billed as the first fully salaried professional Black baseball team in the United States. Some accounts say that in St. Augustine they played as the Ponce de Leon Giants. Frank Grant, who joined the Cuban Giants in 1889, was later elected to the Baseball Hall of Fame. Lincolnville produced entrepreneurs, too. During segregation, when African Americans were barred from the area's other beaches, Lincolnville businessman Frank B. Butler established Butler Beach as a beach resort where they were welcome. During the civil rights movement, Lincolnville was the base for activists fighting to end segregation in St. Augustine's schools and public facilities. As Ku Klux Klan violence rose, local activists appealed in 1964 to the Southern Christian Leadership Conference, led by Martin Luther King Jr. King and activists from across the country came to join them in nonviolent protests. Hundreds were arrested, filling the jails, and their struggle brought national attention to the issues and helped Congress pass the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965.

## Holding On

After legal segregation ended, many residents moved to newer suburban housing, part of a national postwar trend, and employment and population declined. In 1991 the Lincolnville Historic District, bounded by Cedar, Riberia, Cerro and Washington streets and DeSoto Place, was listed on the National Register of Historic Places with 548 historic buildings. Recognition did not bring protection. As migration to Florida grew, the city supported demolishing deteriorating buildings for redevelopment, and in the 1990s extensive demolitions markedly reduced the number of surviving historic structures. The pressure has continued into the 21st century. In 2014 a plan to build the St. Augustine Aquarium on an empty landfill in the neighborhood was blocked over traffic concerns.

## Flight Context

Lincolnville lies at about 29.885°N, 81.314°W on the southwest peninsula of St. Augustine, just south of the historic downtown. From 1,000–2,000 feet, look for Maria Sanchez Lake, the dredged remnant of Maria Sanchez Creek, along the neighborhood's eastern side, with the dense colonial grid and the plaza a few blocks to the north. Northeast Florida Regional Airport (KSGJ) is about 5 nm to the north-northwest. Coastal summers bring frequent afternoon thunderstorms, and sea breezes can make low-level air bumpy.
