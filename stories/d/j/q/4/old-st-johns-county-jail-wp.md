# Old St. Johns County Jail

Henry Flagler put up $10,000 for St. Augustine's 1891 jail to clear ground for his hotel, and for six decades the sheriff's family lived in one wing while prisoners endured harsh conditions in the other.

**Location:** 29.9078, -81.3189

**Categories:** Museums in St. Augustine, Florida, National Register of Historic Places in St. Johns County, Florida, Prison museums in Florida, Jails in Florida, Jails on the National Register of Historic Places in Florida, 1891 establishments in Florida

## Introduction

Henry Flagler wanted a piece of land, and a jail was standing on it. The old St. Johns County lockup occupied ground he needed for his Ponce de León Hotel, so the industrialist struck a deal with the county: he would pay $10,000 toward a new jail somewhere else. The result, completed in 1891 at what is now 167 San Marco Avenue, held prisoners for more than sixty years. It outlasted its usefulness, survived a second life as a roadside curiosity, and today operates as the Old Jail Museum, a place where the grim routines of a Southern county jail are laid out for anyone willing to be "booked" at the door.

## A Jail Bought With a Hotel

Flagler did not build the jail himself. He hired specialists: the Pauly Jail Building and Manufacturing Company of St. Louis, Missouri, designed and constructed it in 1891, delivering a finished facility to the county. The trade suited everyone with a vote in the matter. Flagler got his hotel site in the heart of St. Augustine, and the county got a purpose-built jail. The building went on to serve as the St. Johns County Jail until 1953, when the county moved its prisoners into a newer, more modern facility. Its next chapter would depend on the town's visitors rather than its prisoners.

## Two Wings, Two Lives

The jail was really two households under one roof. Its two-story northern wing was built to hold up to 72 prisoners, divided into a general population area, a maximum security section, a women's section and a kitchen on the lower level. Maximum security held the men considered most dangerous, and it included a death row cell for those condemned to die. The two-story southern wing held something very different: an office for the sheriff and living quarters for his family. The sheriff's family made a home a wall away from the cells. That arrangement is part of what makes the building so striking to walk through now, since the ordinary domestic rooms and the cellblocks sit side by side, each making the other harder to forget.

## Buckets, Fields and the Gallows

By modern standards, conditions for the people held here were very poor. Prisoners serving sentences of varying length were typically sent out as free farm labor during the day. Baths came rarely. Each cell had a single bucket for a toilet, and the diet was so thin that inmates often supplemented it with whatever animals they could catch while working in the fields. Racial segregation was strictly enforced throughout the jail, and disease, violence and death were commonplace. The compound also held gallows. Accounts of the jail's history say eight men were hanged there, on the same compound where the sheriff's family kept house. Those deaths remain the heaviest part of the building's story, and the museum does not hide them.

## Slim McDaniel's Second Act

When the prisoners moved out in 1953, the empty building might easily have been torn down. Instead it was sold to an entrepreneur, Henry "Slim" McDaniel, who began running it as a roadside tourist attraction. The gamble held up long enough for the building to earn formal recognition: on August 27, 1987, it was added to the National Register of Historic Places. Today's Old Jail Museum shows the restored cells and sheriff's quarters, along with a display of weaponry and a pictorial history of the hangings, focused on the years when Sheriff C.J. Perry lived here with his family. Visitors can enter only on guided tours, where costumed guides "process" them as new prisoners. After dark, the jail is one of the stops on Old Town Trolley's Ghosts and Gravestones tour.

## Flight Context

From 1,000-2,000 feet, follow the old city's waterfront north from the fort to pick it out.
