# Huguenot Cemetery

Just outside St. Augustine's old City Gate lies the city's first Protestant burying ground, opened in 1821 as Florida became American and filled over sixty years with soldiers, statesmen, children and strangers who came south for their health and never went home.

**Location:** 29.8982, -81.3140

**Categories:** Cemeteries in Florida, St. Augustine, Florida, Protestant Reformed cemeteries in the United States, National Register of Historic Places in St. Johns County, Florida, Florida Territory

## Introduction

The Huguenot Cemetery is not known to hold a single sixteenth-century Huguenot. The name recalls the French Protestants of the sixteenth century, but the people buried here arrived much later, from New York and Massachusetts, from Charleston and coastal Georgia, from Scotland, Ireland, Germany and the Bahama Islands. They came because in 1821 Spanish St. Augustine became an American town, and its new Protestant residents needed somewhere to be buried. Their answer was a plot of ground just outside the old City Gate. It served for sixty-three years, and it has a plainer name too: the St. Augustine Public Burying Ground.

## Outside the Gate

Under Spain, St. Augustine had been a predominantly Catholic city, and its only burial ground within the city, the Tolomato cemetery, was reserved for Catholics. The Adams-Onís Treaty of 1819 made Florida an American possession, and the physical handover came in 1821. Recognizing the need for a Protestant burial ground, the new American administration chose a site just beyond the city gate. The first burials took place in 1821, the year a yellow fever epidemic claimed the lives of a large number of the city's inhabitants; the state describes the ground as established in response to it. Some of the oldest stones date from that year. William R. Whilden was four years old when he died that October. Samuel Fleischman was fourteen. Edward S. Robinson, twenty, lies beneath a broad marble headstone raised by "a fond father and mother" in memory of "their dutiful and affectionate son."

## Far From Home

Read the inscriptions and a pattern emerges: a great many of these people were born somewhere else. Absalom H. Valentine of Brooklyn "died on a tour for his health" in 1835, and his stone promises that "his tender care will long be remembered by his bereaved widow and mourning friends." Colonel Lucious D. Mower of Granville, Ohio, died in 1834 while visiting "for the benefit of his health." Hardinia M. Burnley of Virginia died of consumption at 24; James Burr Griswold of New York died of the same disease at 22. Others simply record a hometown: Southampton, Massachusetts; Painesville, Ohio; Perthshire, Scotland; Ballymore in County Antrim. A native of Germany, "long a respected citizen of Charleston," lies here, and so does a young woman born in 1801 at Grand Caicos, then counted among the Bahama Islands (now part of the Turks and Caicos Islands). Some, at least, had come south hoping the climate would heal them.

## Names From the Territory

Some graves belong to people who shaped early American Florida. Charles Downing, a Virginian who served in the Territorial Legislature and was twice elected to Congress, died in 1841, "beloved in life" by the account of his epitaph. Joseph L. Smith, a colonel in the War of 1812 and a judge of the territory's Superior Court, followed in 1846. Colonel Charles W. Bulow of Charleston was buried here in 1823. Buckingham Smith raised a granite obelisk to his wife, Julia. One stone tells a story of frontier war in the words of its own time: Lieutenant J. W. S. McNeil of the 2nd Regiment of Dragoons, grandson of New Hampshire's Governor Pierce, was killed at twenty "in battle with the Indians of Florida" in September 1837, by a Seminole leader the inscription names as Uchee Billy. McNeil was later reinterred at the St. Augustine National Cemetery.

## Grief in Marble

The sorrows here come in every size. One large marble slab, which the 1893 survey found tilted with a corner sunk into the ground, records a family undone in a single summer: in 1830, three-year-old Josias Campbell died on July 4, his mother Elizabeth the next day, and his father Josias that September. The Reverend Wilbur F. Nields was elected rector of Trinity Church in St. Augustine, but his stone explains that "he came but God called him before he assumed the duties of his office"; he was 26. There are infants with wooden headboards and toddlers beneath small marble crosses, and simpler wooden markers whose inscriptions had already faded when they were recorded. Josias Campbell's children placed their family's stone as "this last tribute" to their parents, a phrase that could stand for the whole ground.

## What the Cedars Kept

When the inscriptions were surveyed in 1893, the cemetery had begun to reclaim itself. Cedars, many apparently planted at the time of burial, had grown into the stones. Roots pressed against the base of one headstone; at another, a cedar's trunk had flattened and spread until it overlapped the marble at both upper corners. Oleanders, osage orange, a magnolia ringed with narcissi, beds of lilies and a rose at a young clergyman's feet grew among paling fences and coquina curbs, some of them marking graves that no longer had stones at all. Estimates of the burials run from roughly 430 to at least 436 people. The Reverend Thomas Alexander acquired title to the ground and turned it over to the Presbyterian Church in 1832, and burials continued until 1884, when both this cemetery and the Tolomato were closed.

## Flight Context

The Huguenot Cemetery lies just outside the historic City Gate on the north edge of old St. Augustine, Florida, at approximately 29.8982°N, 81.3140°W. The most obvious landmark is the star-shaped Castillo de San Marcos on the Matanzas River waterfront immediately to the east; the small cemetery sits across the street from the gate at the head of the old town. A viewing altitude of about 1,000–1,500 feet AGL, as regulations permit over the city, shows the fort, the gate and the historic district together. Northeast Florida Regional Airport (KSGJ) is only about 4 nm to the north, so stay mindful of its traffic and airspace. Coastal sea breezes often build afternoon thunderstorms in summer; mornings and winter days are usually clearest.
