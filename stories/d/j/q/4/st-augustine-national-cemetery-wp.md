# St. Augustine National Cemetery

On 1.4 acres inside an active military post in St. Augustine, three coquina pyramids cover the remains of more than 1,400 soldiers who died in the Seminole Wars, among them Major Francis Dade and most of his command.

## Introduction

On December 28, 1835, a column of about 110 American soldiers under Major Francis L. Dade was attacked in central Florida, and only three of them were reported to have survived the fight. Seven years later, when the Second Seminole War finally ended in 1842, the remains of Dade's men were dug up from the place they fell and carried to St. Augustine. They lie there still, beneath three low pyramids of coquina stone in a cemetery of barely 1.4 acres. The St. Augustine National Cemetery is small, closed to new burials and tucked inside a working military post, yet it holds one of the most sobering monuments of the long American war for Florida.

## A Post Cemetery Grows Up

The first burial here came in 1828, when this ground served as the post cemetery for St. Francis Barracks. The earliest graves belonged to soldiers stationed at the barracks and to veterans of the Indian Wars. The cemetery's status climbed with the country's changing fortunes. During the Civil War, St. Augustine was first claimed by the Confederacy but was quickly occupied by Union forces, and it stayed in Union hands for the rest of the conflict. After the war the cemetery was expanded and improved, and in 1881 it became a National Cemetery. St. Francis Barracks is still an active installation today, serving as the state headquarters of the Florida National Guard, so the dead here rest within an army post that never really stopped working.

## Three Pyramids of Coquina

The Dade Monument is the cemetery's heart. Its three coquina pyramids were raised in 1842 to mark the end of the second of the Seminole Wars, and beneath them lie the remains of more than 1,400 soldiers who died during those wars. Major Dade himself is here, born in 1792, whose name would later be given to Dade County, Missouri, Dade County, Georgia, and Miami-Dade County, Florida. So is Captain George W. Gardiner, born in 1795, his second in command, who was also killed in 1835. Another grave belongs to Lieutenant John Winfield Scott McNeil, a nephew of President Franklin Pierce, killed in the Seminole Wars in 1837 at the age of about twenty. His dates, 1817 to 1837, say everything about how brief that life was.

## The Other Side of the War

The pyramids tell the story from one side, and the other side belongs in any visit. The Second Seminole War grew out of the United States' effort to force the Seminole people to leave their land in Florida and relocate to Indian Territory under the Treaty of Payne's Landing, part of the wider Indian Removal policy of the era. Seen from the Seminole side, the attack on Dade's column was resistance to that removal, fought by people defending their homeland. The earliest graves on this ground are themselves a trace of that conflict, since many were moved here from burial grounds in what was then Seminole-controlled territory. The soldiers under the coquina were real men with families who grieved them. So were the Seminole families whose world the war was meant to uproot, and a thoughtful visitor can keep both in mind while reading the monument's inscriptions.

## Strangers and Generals

Not every grave here belongs to Florida's frontier wars. The cemetery holds five British Commonwealth servicemen of World War II: a soldier of the Royal Corps of Signals and four aviation officers of the Royal Naval Volunteer Reserve, men who died far from home and were laid to rest in an American military ground. Brigadier General Martin Davis Hardin, a Union general of the Civil War who lived from 1837 to 1923, is buried here too. By the end of 2005 the cemetery counted 2,788 interments, and it is now closed to new ones. Administered by the Department of Veterans Affairs, it was folded into a National Historic Landmark historic district covering the old city of St. Augustine in 1970, and listed on the National Register of Historic Places in its own right in 2016.

## Flight Context

St. Augustine National Cemetery lies within the grounds of St. Francis Barracks in St. Augustine, Florida, at 29.8860N, 81.3096W, about 1.3 km south of the Castillo de San Marcos. The cemetery covers only 1.4 acres, so look for the barracks complex and the orderly rows of headstones beside it; a slow pass at 1,000-1,500 feet works best. Northeast Florida Regional Airport (KSGJ) is about 4.7 nm north-northwest. Jacksonville Executive at Craig (KCRG) is about 29 nm north-northwest and Jacksonville International (KJAX) about 41 nm north-northwest. Summer afternoons often bring thunderstorms along the coast; mornings are usually clearer.
