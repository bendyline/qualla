# Fort Caroline

On the St. Johns River in 1564, French Huguenots built Fort Caroline to claim Florida ahead of Spain; within fifteen months a Spanish dawn attack and a massacre on the beach at Matanzas ended the colony, and the fort's exact site has never been found.

**Location:** 30.3869, -81.5006

**Categories:** French forts in the United States, Huguenot history in the United States, National memorials of the United States, Lost cities and towns, Spanish Florida, Parks in Jacksonville, Florida

## Introduction

The storm was still blowing when Pedro Menéndez de Avilés sent his soldiers marching overland toward the French fort on the St. Johns River. The French commander had taken his fleet out to sea to strike the Spanish, and the storm had caught it at sea. At dawn on September 20, 1565, the Spanish attacked. Fort Caroline held some 200 to 250 people. About 50 to 60 women and children were taken prisoner, and a few defenders, including the colony's founder, René Goulaine de Laudonnière, escaped. The rest were killed. The French colony, barely fifteen months old, had been destroyed.

## The River of May

The story began two years earlier. In May 1562 a French expedition organized by the Protestant leader Admiral Gaspard de Coligny and led by Jean Ribault landed on what the French called the May River, now the St. Johns, and met the Timucua led by Chief Saturiwa. Ribault took some 28 troops north to present-day Parris Island, South Carolina, where they built a settlement called Charlesfort. When he sailed home to arrange supplies, he was captured and briefly imprisoned in England on suspicion of spying related to the French Wars of Religion, and could not return. After a year without supplies or leadership, and facing hostility from Native people, all but one of the Charlesfort colonists set out for Europe in an open boat. During the voyage they were reduced to cannibalism before the survivors were rescued in English waters.

## A Fort Beneath the Bluff

Laudonnière, who had been Ribault's second-in-command in 1562, returned with around 200 new settlers and founded Fort Caroline on June 22, 1564, on a small plain at the western slope of the high, steep bank later called St. Johns Bluff. The colonists relied heavily on the Timucua for food and trade. Chief Outina twice persuaded the French to join attacks on villages of his rivals, the Potano, to seize their surplus corn, and French deserters who raided Timucua settlements soured relations further. Menéndez later wrote that his own settlers had been preceded by free Africans at the French settlement, and the fort also used the labor of enslaved Black people. On July 20, 1565, the English adventurer John Hawkins arrived looking for fresh water and traded his smallest ship for four cannons and powder and shot. That ship and his provisions let the French survive and prepare to sail home. Laudonnière wrote that Hawkins had "wonne the reputation of a good and charitable man, deserving to be esteemed as much of us all as if hee had saved all our lives." The French also introduced Hawkins to tobacco, which he is traditionally credited with bringing to England.

## Two Fleets and a Storm

In late August 1565, Ribault, released from English custody, arrived with a large fleet and hundreds of soldiers and settlers and took command. Within days, Menéndez, Spain's newly appointed governor of Florida, arrived with orders to remove the French. After a brief skirmish at sea, the Spanish withdrew 35 miles south and on September 8 founded St. Augustine. Ribault chased them with most of his ships and troops, and a violent storm lasting several days caught his fleet at sea. Every ship sank or ran aground south of St. Augustine, and many men were lost. It was during that same storm that Menéndez marched on the weakened fort.

## The Inlet Called Slaughters

Ribault and his marooned sailors began walking north. Menéndez's troops found them and summoned them to surrender, and Menéndez then executed Ribault and several hundred Huguenots as heretics at the inlet now called Matanzas, Spanish for "slaughters." Even in that bloody era of religious war, the atrocity shocked Europe. A fort built much later, Fort Matanzas, stands in the vicinity of the site. The Spanish renamed the fort San Mateo and held it, but violence answered violence. In April 1568, Dominique de Gourgues led a French force that captured and burned the fort and slaughtered its Spanish prisoners in revenge. The Spanish rebuilt it, then abandoned it for good in 1569.

## A Fort No One Can Find

No one knows exactly where Fort Caroline stood. In 1953 the National Park Service created the Fort Caroline National Memorial on the south bank of the St. Johns, near the spot that commemorates Laudonnière's first landing; scholars generally accept it as near the original fort, though probably not on the exact site. An outdoor reconstruction built in 1964 was destroyed by Hurricane Dora that same year. A second, near full-scale "interpretive model" on the riverfront shows how modest the French defenses were, though chronic flooding has led the Park Service to plan to remove it and build a partial reconstruction on higher ground closer to the visitor center. The mystery invites challengers. In 2014, researchers Fletcher Crowe and Anita Spring argued that the fort actually stood on the Altamaha River in Georgia, but other scholars remain skeptical. University of North Florida archaeologist Robert Thunen notes that the Spanish marched overland to the fort in two days amid a hurricane, which makes a site so far from St. Augustine implausible.

## Flight Context

Fort Caroline National Memorial lies at about 30.387°N, 81.501°W on the south bank of the St. Johns River in Jacksonville, part of the Timucuan Ecological and Historic Preserve. From 1,500–2,500 feet, follow the broad, winding St. Johns east of downtown toward its mouth; the memorial sits along a wooded stretch of the south shore beneath St. Johns Bluff. Jacksonville Executive at Craig Airport (KCRG) is about 3 nm to the south, and Naval Station Mayport (KNRB), a military field, is about 4 nm to the east. Summer afternoons bring frequent thunderstorms along this stretch of coast.
