# Prince George Winyah Parish Church

Built of English red brick and oyster-shell mortar and first used for worship in 1747, Georgetown's Prince George Winyah is one of South Carolina's oldest churches in continuous service, its walled churchyard holding patriots, a governor and a Confederate general.

## Introduction

The mortar that holds Prince George Winyah together was made from oyster shells. Georgetown's colonists bound English red bricks with mortar made from local oyster shells, and on August 16, 1747, they held the first service inside the walls they had raised, though the church was not completed until 1755. The congregation has been worshiping here ever since. Prince George Winyah is one of the oldest continuous congregations in South Carolina, and its church is one of the oldest in the state still in continuous service. Box pews, a barrel-vaulted ceiling and a flagstone floor evoke the colonial interior, which British troops burned during the Revolution and which was repaired afterward, while a brick-walled churchyard outside holds several notable South Carolinians.

## A Parish Before a Church

In colonial South Carolina, a parish was more than a religious community. Starting in 1716, Anglican parishes also served as electoral and administrative units of government, so a new parish meant a new district. As colonists spread across the Lowcountry, the area north of St. James Santee Parish needed one, and Prince George Parish was established on March 10, 1721. It was named after the Prince of Wales, who later became King George II. The first question was where to build. One option was an inland settlement on the Black River; the other was Winyah, on the Sampit River and Winyah Bay, where Georgetown stands today. In 1726 a wooden church went up inland, on a bend of the Black River near Brown's Ferry, twelve miles from the present town. It did not stay theirs for long.

## Bricks, Taxes and Oyster Shells

On April 9, 1734, the parish was divided to create St. Frederick's Parish, and the wooden church on the Black River went with the new parish. Prince George's congregation had to start again, this time at Winyah. A subscription campaign for a new church began in 1737, and it was supplemented by an import tax collected at the port and £1,000 from the colonial Assembly. Bricks were collected starting in 1740. The land was donated by William Screven, the first Baptist minister in Carolina, and his son Elisha Screven, a generous gift for an Anglican church. The cornerstone was laid on October 30, 1745. Less than two years later, on August 16, 1747, the congregation gathered for its first service in the new building of English red brick and oyster-shell mortar, which the church's own history says was completed in 1755.

## Inside the Box Pews

The church that first held services in 1747 was a model of colonial restraint. Its nave follows a rectangular plan of five bays. The center bays hold side doors topped with fanlights, while the others carry fanlit windows, and brick pilasters frame the side doors and mark the corners of the building. Outside, a hipped roof covers the sanctuary, finished at the entrance with a Jacobean or Dutch gable. Inside, the nave rises to a plastered, barrel-vaulted ceiling above paneled wooden box pews, a center aisle and a cross aisle running between the side doors. The floor is flagstone. Plain, orderly and lit by its fanlights, the room still reads as a colonial space, shaped by the same brick walls raised in the 1740s.

## A Tower, an Organ and a New Allegiance

The church kept growing for generations. Around 1809 a chancel with a semielliptical apse and a gallery was added. In 1824 came a vestibule entrance with double doors and a brick tower, square in plan below and rising to an octagonal section with a cupola and a large cross. The upper tower holds a clock and a balustraded belfry, and it gives the old church its presence on Georgetown's skyline. A Mohler organ was installed in 1850. On May 6, 1971, the church and churchyard were named to the National Register of Historic Places. A more recent change was institutional rather than architectural. In 2012, Prince George disaffiliated from the Episcopal Church and, by its own account, is now affiliated with the theologically conservative Anglican Church in North America, carrying its colonial Anglican heritage into a new denomination.

## Behind the Brick Wall

A brick wall encloses the churchyard and its cemetery, where generations of parishioners and clergy rest alongside several notable South Carolinians. Paul Trapier, a patriot, was elected to the Second Continental Congress, though he never took his seat. James H. Trapier became a brigadier general in the Confederate Army. Governor Robert F. W. Allston is buried here too. Other graves are said to include those of the Rev. Dr. Anthony T. Porter and Capt. Roger Shackelford, born in North Carolina and a member of Georgetown's early Shackelford family. Walking among their stones, visitors can trace Georgetown's history from the Revolution through the Civil War in a single enclosed yard. The wall that surrounds them has marked this ground for the dead of one of the state's oldest congregations, and the church beside it remains in continuous service.
