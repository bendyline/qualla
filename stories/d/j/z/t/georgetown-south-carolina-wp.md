# Georgetown, South Carolina

South Carolina's third-oldest city grew rich on indigo and rice grown by enslaved Africans, then reinvented itself around lumber, paper and steel on the shores of Winyah Bay.

**Location:** 33.3675, -79.2939

**Categories:** Cities in South Carolina, Port cities, Lowcountry, History of slavery in South Carolina, Rice cultivation, Georgetown County, South Carolina

## Introduction

Members of the Winyah Indigo Society paid their dues in indigo. Agricultural profits here were so great between 1735 and 1775 that the society, incorporated in 1757, opened and maintained what is described as the first public school for white children anywhere between Charles Town and Wilmington. The town those planters served, Georgetown, is the third-oldest city in South Carolina. It sits on Winyah Bay where the Black, Great Pee Dee, Waccamaw and Sampit rivers meet, laid out in 1729 by Elisha Screven as a grid four blocks by eight.

## A Port for the Revolution

The Revolution ran straight through Georgetown. Thomas Lynch Jr., a Georgetown planter, signed the Declaration of Independence. In the war's final years the town served as the important port supplying General Nathanael Greene's army, while Francis Marion, the "Swamp Fox," led many guerrilla actions nearby. After the war, Georgetown's thriving economy kept drawing newcomers, from planters and shipowners who migrated from Virginia to a large Jewish-American population in the early 1800s. John Shackelford, a veteran of Virginia's Continental Army forces, arrived in the late eighteenth century, and his descendants became prominent planters, lawyers and judges. After the Revolution, rice overtook indigo as the staple crop, and it would remake the riverbanks and even the regional diet.

## Rice, and the People Who Made It Grow

The rice fields spread through the swampy lowlands along Georgetown's five rivers, and they were built by enslaved African and African-American laborers, who raised the dams, gates and canals that flooded and drained the fields. Planters often chose to import captives from the rice-growing regions of West Africa precisely because those men and women already knew how to cultivate and process the crop. By 1840 the Georgetown District produced nearly half of the entire rice crop of the United States, and the town became what is described as the largest rice-exporting port in the world. The profits bought stately manor houses, town residences and fine Charleston-made furniture and silver for a small planter elite. Joshua John Ward, who kept a townhouse in Georgetown, held more than 1,000 people in bondage on several plantations, and is described as the largest enslaver in the country at his death in 1853. His Brookgreen Plantation is now the heart of Brookgreen Gardens, and since the late twentieth century historic sites here have worked to tell the lives and skills of the enslaved people as well.

## When Rice Was No Longer King

The Civil War brought fortifications to the bay. Battery White, built south of town in 1862-1863 to guard the harbor and Winyah Bay, drove off two Union gunboats in November 1862, but by February 1865 it was found abandoned with its guns spiked, and Union forces took Georgetown without a fight. Emancipation then upended the economics of rice, whose soft-soil harvest had always depended on hand labor, and the rice crops of 1866 to 1888 were largely failures. Freedpeople made their own choices. Some left to find family members sold away in the domestic slave trade, many pulled women and children out of field work, and many sought to farm for themselves rather than labor in gangs. Several African Americans from Georgetown represented the county in the state legislature during Reconstruction, among them Joseph Haynes Rainey, the first African-American member of the U.S. House of Representatives. By the early twentieth century, Jim Crow laws had shut African Americans out of elections and office again. In 1900, an officer's attempt to arrest barber John Brownfield over an unpaid poll tax ended with the officer shot dead, rumors of a planned lynching, and a murder conviction, later appealed to the U.S. Supreme Court, by an all-white jury with ties to the deceased.

## Sawdust, Paper and Steel

Timber replaced rice. By 1900 several lumber mills lined the Sampit River, and the Atlantic Coast Lumber Company ran what was then the largest lumber mill on the East Coast; around 1905, according to historian Mac McAlister, Georgetown reached its peak as a lumber port. Under Mayor William Doyle Morgan the town added electricity, telephones, sewers, rail connections and some paved streets. The Great Depression bankrupted Atlantic Coast Lumber and put almost everyone out of work, until International Paper opened a mill in 1936 that by 1944 was described as the largest in the world. A German company, Korf, founded a steel mill here in 1973, and that mill closed and reopened under a succession of owners until it went idle under Liberty Steel; a planned sale of the site to a developer fell through in September 2026. On October 31, 2024, International Paper announced it would close its Georgetown paper mill by the end of that year, eliminating 526 hourly and 148 salaried jobs. In March 2026 the company agreed to sell the mill property to the developer River Development Equities, and it has said it will close its remaining container plant in the area later in 2026.

## Storms and a Changing Town

Water shapes Georgetown's modern story as much as its colonial one. When Hurricane Hugo came ashore to the south in September 1989, Georgetown sat under the storm's northern eyewall and took harder winds than Charleston, while an intense surge along the rivers flooded the town. Hurricane Ian made landfall nearby in September 2022. In 2013 a fire engulfed seven historic buildings on the 700 block of Front Street, and more than 200 firefighters from ten departments, joined by the Coast Guard, fought it for hours. Heritage tourism now drives much of the town's retail life, and the Georgetown Historic District holds more than fifty sites on the National Register of Historic Places. In 2018 Georgetown inaugurated its first African-American mayor, and in 2022 its first woman and first Republican to hold the office.

## Flight Context

Georgetown lies at 33.37N, 79.29W at the head of Winyah Bay, where the Sampit, Great Pee Dee, Waccamaw and Black rivers converge before the bay opens to the Atlantic. From 2,000-3,000 feet the historic grid along the Sampit River waterfront is easy to pick out, and traces of old rice-field embankments may show in the river marshes upstream. Georgetown County Airport (KGGE) is about 3.6 nm south-southwest of town; Robert F. Swinnie Airport (KPHH) at Andrews is about 13 nm west-northwest, and Myrtle Beach International (KMYR) about 26 nm northeast. Coastal haze and summer thunderstorms are common.
