# Friendfield Plantation

On the Sampit River near Georgetown, Friendfield Plantation's rice fields were worked by hundreds of enslaved Gullah people, among them Jim Robinson, born into slavery in 1850 and an ancestor of former First Lady Michelle Obama.

**Location:** 33.3682, -79.3226

## Introduction

Jim Robinson was born into slavery on Friendfield Plantation in 1850. He was one of the Gullah people forced to work the rice fields along the Sampit River near Georgetown, South Carolina, and he was fifteen years old when freedom came. Generations later, his descendant Michelle Robinson would become First Lady of the United States as Michelle Obama. That single line of family history, running from a Lowcountry rice plantation to the White House, gives Friendfield a place in the national story. The plantation itself, a 3,305-acre property assembled from parts of six former plantations, holds a longer and harder history, one that begins with a Charleston brick maker in 1734.

## Bricks, Indigo and Bondage

The founder was James Withers, a brick maker in Charleston who became a planter. From 1734 onward he developed a plantation along the Sampit River for indigo and rice, a venture built from the start on enslaved labor. Withers bought enslaved people from Barbados, where they had been brought from Africa and where some were "seasoned" for a period of time. The plantation passed down through his family. In 1818, his grandson Francis Withers built a new house on the property. Every harvest at Friendfield, then as before, rested on the work of people who were never paid and never free to leave.

## Hundreds of Lives

By the 1850s, an estimated 230 African Americans were enslaved at Friendfield (other accounts say more than 300 worked its fields), and they reportedly produced about 900,000 pounds of rice each year. Behind that figure were individual lives and families, and every pound of that harvest came from their unpaid labor. Friendfield's enslaved workers included Gullah people, and Jim Robinson was one of them, born into slavery in 1850. Michelle Obama's paternal ancestors, including Robinson, were among Friendfield's Gullah enslaved laborers. When the Civil War ended slavery, Robinson was fifteen. His story is one of hundreds at Friendfield, each a life lived under bondage, most of them never recorded in the plantation's history.

## Sharecroppers and the Great Migration

Freedom did not restore the plantation's fortunes. After the Civil War, Friendfield fell into disrepair. A long agricultural depression drove down commodity prices, and planters struggling with the change to free labor found themselves short of resources. The land came to be worked mostly by sharecroppers, freedmen who paid a portion of their crop for the right to farm it. Jim Robinson is believed to have been among them. African-American sharecroppers lived on the plantation and worked its fields until the 1950s. Meanwhile, thousands of African Americans left the South in the Great Migration, seeking better opportunities in northern industrial cities and an escape from Jim Crow oppression. Michelle Obama's paternal grandfather, Fraser Robinson Jr., was among those who went north, moving from the Georgetown area to Chicago. He later returned to the Lowcountry after retiring.

## Duck Blinds and a New House

Friendfield left the Withers family line in stages. It passed out of the bloodline in 1847, and in 1897 Elizabeth Hunt Warham Forster, Francis Withers's stepdaughter, sold it to B. Walker Cannon. Patrick C. McClary Sr. later bought the property and used it as a duck hunting club, a common fate for plantations owned by the wealthy in the early 20th century. In 1926 the Friendfield House burned. Four years later, in 1930, Radcliffe Cheston Jr., an investment banker from Philadelphia, bought the plantation, and around 1931 he had a new Friendfield House built to designs by the noted Philadelphia architect Arthur Ingersoll Meigs. The estate has remained in private hands ever since, changing ownership in 1989 and again in 2015.

## A Landscape on the Register

In 1996 Friendfield Plantation was listed on the National Register of Historic Places as a historic district. The listing counts 23 buildings, 15 other structures and 14 sites as contributing elements, a record of how many layers of history the property holds. Some of those layers are architectural, beginning with the 1930s house. Others belong to the land itself, the rice fields and riverbanks where enslaved people worked for more than a century before emancipation and where freed families labored as sharecroppers into the 1950s. Friendfield is private property, but its history reaches well beyond its gates, into Chicago, into the White House, and into the family trees of Americans who trace their roots to the Sampit River.

## Flight Context

Friendfield Plantation lies at 33.3682°N, 79.3226°W along the Sampit River on the western outskirts of Georgetown, South Carolina, whose center is about 1.5 nm east-northeast. From 1,500–2,500 feet, follow the Sampit River west from Georgetown's waterfront; the plantation lies along the river just west of town. The property is privately owned. Georgetown County Airport (KGGE) is only about 3.4 nm south, so expect traffic in its pattern; Robert F. Swinnie Airport at Andrews (KPHH) is about 11 nm west-northwest. Expect humid coastal haze, morning fog over the river marshes and summer afternoon thunderstorms.
