# Joseph H. Rainey House

From this 1760s house on Prince Street in Georgetown, Joseph H. Rainey, born into slavery in 1832, launched the political career that made him the first Black member of the U.S. House of Representatives.

**Location:** 33.3688, -79.2847

**Categories:** National Historic Landmarks in South Carolina, National Register of Historic Places in Georgetown County, South Carolina, Houses on the National Register of Historic Places in South Carolina, Houses completed in 1760, Houses in Georgetown County, South Carolina, Buildings and structures in Georgetown, South Carolina, Historic district contributing properties in South Carolina

## Introduction

On December 12, 1870, Joseph Hayne Rainey took his seat in the United States House of Representatives, the first Black person ever to serve there. He had been born into slavery in Georgetown, South Carolina, in 1832. The road between those two facts runs through a modest clapboard house at 909 Prince Street. Local tradition holds that Rainey was born here. What is more surely known is that it was from this house that he launched his political career in 1867, and that during his years in Congress it served as his district headquarters and his home when the House was in recess. The house was designated a National Historic Landmark in 1984.

## Freedom Bought and Paid For

Rainey's parents were enslaved, but his father, Edward, a barber who was leased out for work, saved enough to buy the family's freedom in the early 1840s. It was an extraordinary act of determination, paying for liberty that should never have been for sale. The family was said to have lived in the Prince Street house until 1846, when they moved to Charleston. There his parents built a reasonably prosperous barbershop business, and they made sure their son received some private education. Hard-won freedom, a family trade and an education gave young Joseph a foundation. None of it could protect him from the upheaval that was coming, and the Civil War would test the family's freedom almost as soon as it began.

## Conscripted, Then Gone

In 1861, with the outbreak of the Civil War, Rainey was among the free Black people pressed into Confederate service, first digging trenches and later working as a cook and steward on a blockade runner. Being free on paper did not mean being free to refuse. In 1862 Rainey and his wife, Susan, escaped to the British colony of Bermuda. There he worked as a barber, the trade his family knew, and ran a successful shop. Bermuda gave him distance from the war and time to prepare for what came next. The couple returned to South Carolina in 1866, and in 1867, with the war over and slavery abolished, Rainey became the Republican county chairman in Georgetown, ready to enter public life. The house on Prince Street became his political base.

## From Prince Street to the Capitol

Rainey launched his political career from this house in 1867. Three years later he won a seat in Congress as a Republican, and on December 12, 1870, he was seated in the House of Representatives. He was the first Black person to serve in the House and the second Black person in Congress, after Senator Hiram Revels. Rainey was re-elected and served four terms, leaving office on March 3, 1879, after losing the 1878 election. In the spring of 1874 (the exact date is disputed, though most sources say May) he broke another barrier, becoming the first African American to preside over the House of Representatives. Through all those years the Prince Street house was his anchor, serving as his district headquarters while he served in Washington and as the home he returned to during congressional recesses.

## Cypress, Pine and Memory

The house itself was built in the 1760s, long before Rainey was born. It is a single-family clapboarded house, two and a half stories tall, with two chimneys and a hipped roof broken by hipped dormers. Much of the interior is original, including cypress paneling, pine floors and wainscoting, so the rooms still carry the materials that surrounded Rainey during his years here. Also known as the Rainey-Camlin House, it stands in Georgetown's historic district a short walk from the Prince George Winyah Church. Rainey died in Georgetown, the city of his birth, in August 1887. A 2017 news report described the house as privately owned. The house on Prince Street endures as a tangible link to his life, a quiet building at the center of a remarkable political journey from slavery to the Speaker's chair.
