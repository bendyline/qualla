# Mepkin Abbey

At the fork of the Cooper River, Trappist monks keep a monastery and garden on land that once belonged to Founding Father and slave trader Henry Laurens and later to publisher Henry R. Luce, who gave it to the monks in the late 1940s.

**Location:** 33.1167, -79.9500

**Categories:** Trappist monasteries in the United States, Buildings and structures in Berkeley County, South Carolina, Tourist attractions in Berkeley County, South Carolina, Roman Catholic church buildings in South Carolina, Diocese of Charleston

## Introduction

Henry Laurens asked that his body be cremated and his ashes interred at his estate on the Cooper River. More than a century and a half later, publisher Henry R. Luce and his wife, Clare Boothe Luce, were buried on the same estate. Between and after them came an unlikely succession of owners, ending with a community of Trappist monks who arrived from Kentucky to pray and work where the two forks of the Cooper River meet. Mepkin Abbey, near Moncks Corner in Berkeley County, carries all of those lives at once: a Founding Father's fortune and its source in human bondage, a publishing magnate's retreat, and a monastery that has had to rethink how it earns its keep.

## A Name Older Than the Grants

The name came first. The area has been known as Mepkin for centuries, a name said to date back to the Native peoples who lived here. The first written record of it appears in a 1681 grant to the sons of Sir John Coleton, one of the Lords Proprietors of Carolina. In 1762, one of his descendants sold it to Henry Laurens of Charleston. Laurens built his home on the property, and it became known as the Mepkin Plantation. It sat where the Cooper River splits into two forks, northwest of Charleston, a riverside estate within reach of the city. Owners would come and go over the next three centuries, but the old name stayed with the land.

## The Founding Father's Fortune

Laurens was a Founding Father, a merchant, a rice planter and a slave trader. He earned great wealth as a partner in Austin and Laurens, a firm described as the largest slave-trading house in North America, which by one count oversaw the sale of more than 8,000 enslaved Africans in the 1750s alone. Each of those thousands was a person taken from home and sold, and their suffering underwrote the fortune of the man who bought Mepkin. Laurens also became a political leader of the Revolution. He succeeded John Hancock as president of the Continental Congress, and after being captured at sea by the British he was imprisoned for about fifteen months in the Tower of London, then released in exchange for General Charles Cornwallis. During their occupation of Charleston, British forces burned his home at Mepkin. After returning in 1784 he lived on the estate for the rest of his life.

## The Publisher's Gift

After a few generations the Laurens family sold Mepkin, and the property passed through several hands. The plantation house was burned again, this time by the Union army during the Civil War, and by the time Henry R. Luce, the well-known publisher, bought the property in 1936, the Laurens house was long gone; the house then standing dated from 1906. Assembled from several smaller plantations, the estate covered more than 7,200 acres, and Luce paid a reported $150,000 for it. He and Clare Boothe Luce made it a country retreat with landscaped gardens. Then, in the late 1940s, the Luces donated a large part of the property, including the garden, to the Trappist Order's Gethsemani Abbey. Twenty-nine monks of the Order of Cistercians of the Strict Observance came from Gethsemani, Kentucky, to found the new Mepkin Abbey; the order's own history has them arriving on November 22, 1948, with the abbey founded in 1949.

## Live Oaks and Camellias

The monks inherited more than land. The Luces' landscape gardens became the abbey's botanical garden, and they remain open to visitors today. Great live oaks shade the lawns, and a camellia garden designed by landscape architect Loutrel Briggs adds color in its season. Henry and Clare Boothe Luce are buried on the site, their graves a quiet reminder of the couple who gave the land away. The abbey lists the gardens as open to the public every day from 9 a.m. to 6 p.m., though the gift shop is closed on Mondays and the abbey church can be seen only on a guided tour, so visitors can walk the paths while the monks keep their own rhythm nearby. Beneath the oaks near the river, the whole arc of Mepkin's history comes into view: an old Native American name, a Revolutionary's plantation, a publisher's estate, and now a place set apart for prayer.

## From Eggs to Mushrooms

A monastery needs an income, and for years Mepkin's main income came from eggs. In February 2007, People for the Ethical Treatment of Animals released video of the abbey's battery cage egg operation, showing debeaked hens crowded inside cages. The abbey defended itself by citing its compliance with the animal welfare standards of the United Egg Producers, and it soon emerged that many scenes in the video, including images of dead chickens on the floor, had actually been filmed at a separate facility. Still, the episode left its mark. In December 2007, Mepkin announced that it would phase out the egg operation, citing the concerns raised and the disturbance to the monks' monastic way of life. The community eventually turned to growing mushrooms, a quieter trade for a community whose days are built around prayer.

## Flight Context

Mepkin Abbey is at 33.1167°N, 79.9500°W in Berkeley County, at the junction of the two forks of the Cooper River northwest of Charleston. From 2,000–3,000 feet, follow the Cooper River north from the Charleston area and look for the point where it divides; the abbey's lawns and live oaks sit near that fork, surrounded by woods and wetlands. Cypress Gardens lies about 4 nm due south, and the town of Moncks Corner about 6 nm northwest. Nearby airports: Berkeley County Airport at Moncks Corner (KMKS) about 6 nm northwest, and Charleston International (KCHS) about 14 nm south-southwest. Please keep well clear and avoid low passes over the monastery out of respect for the community's silence. Expect humid Lowcountry haze, morning river fog and summer afternoon thunderstorms.
