# Cypress Gardens (South Carolina)

A rice plantation's freshwater reservoir turned duck-hunting retreat turned public garden, Cypress Gardens near Moncks Corner lets visitors glide by flat-bottom boat through an 80-acre blackwater swamp of bald cypress and tupelo.

**Location:** 33.0478, -79.9489

**Categories:** Botanical gardens in South Carolina, Protected areas of Berkeley County, South Carolina, Swamps of South Carolina, Nature reserves in South Carolina, 1932 establishments in South Carolina, Landforms of Berkeley County, South Carolina

## Introduction

The black water at Cypress Gardens was put there on purpose. Long before flat-bottom boats carried visitors between the trunks of bald cypress and tupelo, this 80-acre swamp was a working reservoir, dug out and fitted with water gates to feed the rice fields of Dean Hall plantation. Nature took the engineering back. Today the reservoir is the dark, still centerpiece of a 170-acre preserve in Berkeley County, laced with boat channels and walking trails, bright with azaleas and camellias in their seasons, and familiar to moviegoers who never knew they were looking at a South Carolina swamp.

## Water Gates for Rice

The land was part of Dean Hall, a rice plantation whose wetlands were first turned into rice-field water preserves in the 1720s. The plantation depended on fresh water from the Cooper River, and to hold that water the area that is now the swamp was dug out and equipped with water gates, creating a freshwater reservoir. It was a feat of earthmoving on a large scale, all in service of a single crop, grown in rice fields worked by enslaved people. In time, the property fell into disuse. The reservoir remained, slowly becoming the blackwater bald cypress and tupelo swamp that defines the garden today. A small rice field planted near the swamp still recalls the crop that shaped this landscape, a reminder that the tranquil scene began as infrastructure for an agricultural enterprise.

## From Duck Blind to Public Garden

In 1909, Benjamin Kittredge bought the neglected property to use as a duck hunting reserve. The Kittredge family's vision grew into something larger. During the Depression, more than 200 workers built about 4.5 miles of trails around the swamp, work that gave the garden the shape visitors still follow today. Cypress Gardens opened to the public in the early 1930s (sources give 1931 or 1932). Three decades later, on June 1, 1963, Kittredge's son, Benjamin Rufus Kittredge Jr., donated the property to the City of Charleston. The plantation house itself did not stay. In the 1970s, the house at Dean Hall was moved to Beaufort County for preservation. Eventually the City of Charleston no longer wanted to support the garden, and in 1996 it gave the property to Berkeley County, which keeps the gates open for a new generation of visitors.

## Gliding Through the Cypress

The best way to see the swamp is from the water. Flat-bottom boats slip along the channels while footpaths trace the shoreline, and between them the preserve offers miles of walking paths and nature trails. The plantings reward repeat visits: azaleas, blueberries, a camellia garden, daffodils, a daylily island, dogwoods, pitcher plants, redbud and a rose garden. The garden also keeps its creatures close. The Swamparium, opened in 1998, holds about 24,000 gallons of water and displays native and exotic fish, reptiles and amphibians from swamp habitats, including common local venomous snakes and large aquatic salamanders called sirens. An aviary has housed African grey parrots and a sulfur-crested cockatoo, all former pets, and the 2,500-square-foot Butterfly House, opened in 1997, shelters butterflies, birds, koi, goldfish and turtles.

## Hollywood's Blackwater

That otherworldly look has drawn filmmakers again and again. Cypress Gardens has hosted more than 16 major movies and television series. Among them are Swamp Thing (1982), the television miniseries North and South, The Patriot (2000) and The Notebook (2004). The appeal is easy to see: the mirrored water, the ranks of cypress rising from it, the sense of a landscape older than any story told in front of it. For a place that began as practical infrastructure, it is a striking career. The reservoir that once regulated water for rice has become a stand-in for the romantic, mysterious South, lit and framed for the camera. Yet off camera it remains a working garden, with picnic tables, rental facilities and quiet boats drifting past the knees of the cypress.

## The Flood and the Return

In October 2015, historic flooding struck South Carolina, and Cypress Gardens took a heavy blow. On October 13, officials announced that the attraction would close indefinitely after an assessment found millions of dollars in damage. They planned to apply for FEMA assistance and hoped to reopen by the summer of 2016. Recovery took far longer. The park remained closed for about three and a half years, its boats idle and its trails silent. Then, on April 13, 2019, Cypress Gardens reopened, and more than 3,000 people came for the grand opening. Berkeley County made the homecoming easy for its own residents, keeping the park free for them until June 30. The swamp that had once been built to hold water had survived too much of it.

## Flight Context

Cypress Gardens lies at 33.0478°N, 79.9489°W in Berkeley County, near the Cooper River in the Lowcountry north of Charleston; its mailing address is Moncks Corner, whose town center is about 9.5 nm north-northwest. From 1,500–2,500 feet, look for the dark, irregular sheet of the 80-acre cypress swamp set within the surrounding forest. Mepkin Abbey is about 4 nm due north. Nearby airports: Berkeley County Airport at Moncks Corner (KMKS) about 9.5 nm north-northwest, Charleston International (KCHS) about 10 nm south-southwest, and Mount Pleasant Regional-Faison Field (KLRO) about 12 nm southeast. Watch for Charleston International's arrival and departure traffic to the south. Expect humid Lowcountry haze, morning fog over the swamps and summer afternoon thunderstorms.
