# Murrells Inlet, South Carolina

A fishing village on the Grand Strand, Murrells Inlet sits on land shaped by Waccamaw burial bluffs, rice plantations, a catastrophic 1893 hurricane and a sculptor's seaside studio.

**Location:** 33.5517, -79.0489

**Categories:** Grand Strand, Coastal communities, Plantations, Hurricanes, Georgetown County, South Carolina

## Introduction

Mapmakers never quite settled on the name. On later maps this salt-marsh inlet on South Carolina's Waccamaw Neck appears as Morrall's Inlet or Murrays Inlet, but it was most likely named for the family of Captain John Murrell, who bought 2,340 acres here and built a house on a bluff around 1733. Today Murrells Inlet is a onetime fishing village turned tourist and retirement community, 13 miles south of Myrtle Beach, best known for its Marshwalk, a boardwalk strung along the edge of the salt marsh.

## Place of Great Weeping

People lived here thousands of years before anyone wrote it down. Shell mounds and archaeological finds scattered between the Atlantic and the Waccamaw River record the cultures of the Indigenous peoples who lived along these creeks. When Europeans arrived, the inhabitants included the Waccamaw people, who drew on the resources of the creeks and rivers. One of their place names survives on the modern map. Wachesaw translates loosely as "Place of Great Weeping," a reference to burial grounds, and burial mounds found along the high bluffs there held European beads, urns and other artifacts, evidence of contact with the Europeans whose land grants would follow.

## Long, Narrow Plantations

The Lords Proprietors carved the Waccamaw Neck into baronies of tens of thousands of acres, granting them to men willing to bring workers and settle the land. The first grants went to Robert Daniell in 1711, and the estates were divided into long, narrow plantations running from the ocean to the river: The Oaks, Brookgreen, Springfield, Laurel Hill, Richmond Hill and Wachesaw. John Murrell raised indigo as his cash crop, and after his death in 1771 his land became Wachesaw and Richmond Hill plantations. In the nineteenth century rice replaced indigo, and families such as the Allstons, Westons, Flaggs and Wards grew wealthy on a crop whose heavy labor fell on enslaved people. The planters generally backed the Revolution; William Allston served as a captain under his brother-in-law, Francis Marion. They filled seats in the legislature and the governor's office, and founded institutions including the Hot and Hot Fish Club and All Saints Waccamaw.

## The Flagg Flood

Emancipation ended the forced labor rice depended on, and after the Civil War, as planters relied on freedmen to work the fields, the dikes and water-control works began to decline. Then came the storms. A series of powerful hurricanes uprooted trees and broke the rice-field dikes, climaxing with the hurricane of October 1893, when the Atlantic was reported to have met the Waccamaw River. On Magnolia Beach, by local accounts, the storm surge swept away members of the Flagg family: Arthur Flagg, his wife Georgeanne, their son Arthur Jr., and Arthur Jr.'s wife and six children. The storm became known as the Flagg Flood. Ward Flagg, one of three sons, survived. He retired to the miller's cottage at Brookgreen and reportedly never visited the ocean again. The storms ultimately ended rice production on the Waccamaw Neck.

## Hunters, a Novelist and a Sculptor

Cheap land drew wealthy outsiders in the new century. In 1920 Julius A. Mood of Sumter and a group of sportsmen bought Brookgreen, Springfield, Laurel Hill and The Oaks as a hunting preserve. His daughter, Julia Mood Peterkin, visited Brookgreen several times and used the area as a setting for some of her novels; she won the Pulitzer Prize for Fiction for Scarlet Sister Mary. In 1930 the philanthropist Archer Milton Huntington, heir to a railroad fortune, bought Brookgreen. He and his wife, the sculptor Anna Hyatt Huntington, first imagined a winter resort, then turned the plantation into an outdoor gallery for her award-winning sculpture. On the beach they built Atalaya, a Spanish-style, fortress-like home that served as her studio and their living quarters, in what is now Huntington Beach State Park. That same year William A. Kimbel bought Wachesaw, and when he added Richmond Hill for a large hunting estate, he restored the boundaries of John Murrell's original plantation.

## From Kings Road to the Marshwalk

For most of its history the inlet was reached by water or by rough dirt roads: the Waccamaw River and the Atlantic for boats, and for horses and wagons the Kings Road, used during George Washington's visit, and the River Road past the plantation houses. The steamship Comanche called at Wachesaw Landing with passengers and mail. Between Reconstruction and World War I, families from Marion, Conway, Southport and nearby counties settled here, and their names still appear around the inlet. Around 1933, Highway 17 was paved, and the Grand Strand's growth eventually reached the fishing village. By the 2020 census the community held 9,740 people. Atalaya, Richmond Hill and the Murrells Inlet Historic District are all listed on the National Register of Historic Places. The half-mile Marshwalk is free to visit and is maintained by Georgetown County Parks and Recreation.

## Flight Context

Murrells Inlet is centered near 33.55N, 79.05W on the southern Grand Strand, where a narrow salt-marsh inlet cuts behind the barrier beach. From 1,500-2,500 feet, follow U.S. 17 southwest from Myrtle Beach; the marsh-lined inlet and its waterfront appear just inland of the beach, with Huntington Beach State Park and Brookgreen Gardens to the south and the Waccamaw River a few miles west. Myrtle Beach International (KMYR) is about 10 nm northeast, Conway-Horry County (KHYW) about 17 nm north-northwest, and Georgetown County (KGGE) about 20 nm southwest. Expect busy low-level traffic along the beach in summer and sea-breeze thunderstorms on hot afternoons.
