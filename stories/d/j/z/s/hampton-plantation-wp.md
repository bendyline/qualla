# Hampton Plantation

A Santee River rice plantation worked by hundreds of enslaved people, Hampton's Georgian house gained what is believed to be America's first domestic temple front in time for George Washington's visit, and later became home to the state's first poet laureate.

## Introduction

Some of the windows at Hampton Plantation are fakes. When Daniel Horry enlarged the house in the 18th century, adding a ballroom on one side and a master suite on the other, he had false shuttered windows set into the front walls of the new wings so the facade would look perfectly balanced. Appearance mattered here. A few decades later, the house received a grand temple front, believed to be the first of its kind on an American home, finished in time for a visit from George Washington. Behind all that careful display stood a rice plantation whose prosperity depended on hundreds of enslaved people, forced to work its fields against their will.

## A Refugee's House on Hampton Creek

Hampton began with a newcomer. In 1735, Noe Serre, a French Huguenot refugee, built the original core of the house, a two-story structure with a central hall, on the banks of Hampton Creek, a tributary of the Santee River in what is now northern Charleston County. From that modest start, the house grew over more than half a century, its construction continuing in stages until about 1790 and 1791. Today the state site covers 274 acres west of U.S. Route 17, north of McClellanville. Most of the land is no longer farmed, and it has reverted to woods and swamp. The main house, a large two-and-a-half-story wood-frame building with clapboard siding, a dormered hip roof and a raised brick foundation, stands near the creek, one of the finest wood-frame Georgian plantation houses in South Carolina.

## Symmetry at Any Cost

Daniel Horry acquired the property in 1757 and transformed it. He greatly expanded Serre's house, adding a two-story ballroom on one side and a master bedroom suite on the other. The additions made the house larger and grander, but they also threatened the balanced front the house was meant to present. Horry's solution was theatrical. He placed false shuttered windows on the front walls of the new wings, so that from the lawn the facade appeared regular and orderly, whatever the rooms behind actually required. The Horrys, like the later owners from the Pinckney and Rutledge families, came from politically and economically prominent South Carolina families. A house like this was a statement of status, and Horry made sure that statement read clearly from the front.

## A Temple Front for the President

The last major change was the most striking. In 1790 and 1791 the house gained a projecting temple front: eight Doric columns, six across the front and one more on each side, supporting a carved Adamesque frieze and a pedimented gable. The portico is believed to be the first of its type in the United States and is one of the earliest known examples of a temple front in American domestic architecture. It was completed in time for a visit to the plantation by George Washington. In 1970 Hampton was declared a National Historic Landmark. Its grandeur, though, is only part of what the house has to say, because the wealth that paid for columns and friezes came from the rice fields along the Santee.

## The People Who Grew the Rice

Hampton was a very prosperous rice plantation, and that prosperity was produced by people who had no share in it. The plantation held hundreds of enslaved Black people, forced to grow rice against their will. Their forced labor produced the crop that paid for the ballroom, the careful symmetry and the temple front that welcomed a President. Their names are largely absent from the architectural story told about the house, which focuses on builders and owners. Any honest visit to Hampton has to hold both truths at once: the house is an important work of early American architecture, and it stood at the center of a system that held human beings in bondage.

## The Poet Comes Home

The last private owner of Hampton was Archibald Rutledge, South Carolina's first poet laureate. In 1937 he returned to the plantation after a thirty-three-year absence, and his 1941 book, Home by the River, describes that homecoming. The house drew other writers too. Pat Conroy, in his book My Reading Life, recalled meeting Rutledge at Hampton when Conroy was still a teenager with ambitions to write. In 1971 the state bought 274 acres from the Rutledge family, and it is now Hampton Plantation State Historic Site, where the grounds are free to walk and the state lists guided mansion tours Friday through Tuesday. Visitors walk grounds that have gone back to forest and swamp, and face the columns that once greeted Washington, in a place where the stories of planters, poets and the enslaved share the same ground.

## Flight Context

Hampton Plantation lies at 33.1983°N, 79.4378°W on Hampton Creek, a tributary of the Santee River in northern Charleston County, just west of U.S. Route 17. From 1,500–2,500 feet, follow U.S. 17 north from McClellanville, about 7 nm to the south, and look west of the highway for the plantation grounds along Hampton Creek, surrounded by woods and swamp near the Santee's lower reaches. Nearby airports: Georgetown County Airport (KGGE) about 9 nm northeast, Robert F. Swinnie Airport at Andrews (KPHH) about 16 nm north-northwest, and Mount Pleasant Regional-Faison Field (KLRO) about 25 nm southwest. Expect humid haze, morning fog over the river swamps and summer afternoon thunderstorms.
