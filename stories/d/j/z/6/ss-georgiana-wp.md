# SS Georgiana

A fast, secretive Scottish-built steamer was run aground and scuttled off Charleston on her maiden voyage in 1863, and her wreck, now a living reef, still fuels the argument over whether she was a merchantman or a Confederate warship.

**Location:** 32.7797, -79.7597

**Categories:** Shipwrecks of the American Civil War, Shipwrecks of the Carolina coast, Cruisers of the Confederate States Navy, Archaeological sites in South Carolina, Maritime incidents in March 1863, Ships built on the River Clyde

## Introduction

On the night of March 19, 1863, with solid shot passing clean through his hull and his propeller and rudder damaged, Captain A. B. Davidson flashed a white light at the Union blockaders off Charleston: the signal of surrender. It bought him the time he needed. Davidson ran the new steamer Georgiana aground in 14 feet of water, three-quarters of a mile from shore, scuttled her, and escaped on the land side with all hands. The blockading crews, who would have shared in the prize money, called it "the most consummate treachery."

## Swift, Black and Secret

The Georgiana was a brig-rigged, iron-hulled propeller steamer of 120 horsepower, with a jib, two heavily raked masts and a hull and stack painted black. On her clipper bow rode the figurehead of a "demi-woman." She was built at the Lawrie shipyard in Glasgow, perhaps under subcontract from Lairds of Birkenhead, and was registered there in December 1862. Reportedly pierced for fourteen guns, she could also carry more than four hundred tons of cargo, and secrecy surrounded her construction, loading and sailing. Union diplomats watched her closely anyway. The U.S. consul at Tenerife judged her "evidently a very swift vessel." A consular dispatch of January 6, 1863, noted that while lying at Liverpool "she had the Rebel flag flying at her mast," and the London American remarked that her officers wore gold lace on their caps, taken as a sure sign she was being groomed for a man-of-war. It was her maiden voyage that brought her, finally, to the waters off Charleston.

## Close Enough to Hear the Orders

The armed U.S. yacht America, the craft behind the famed America's Cup, spotted her first and alerted the rest of the blockading fleet with colored signal flares. A desperate chase followed. At one point the Georgiana ran so close to the big guns of a Union warship that her crew could hear the orders being given aboard the enemy vessel. After Davidson's white-light ruse and escape, Lieutenant Commander John L. Davis of the Wissahickon decided to set the wreck on fire so that guerrilla bands from shore could not salvage her or her cargo. She burned for several days, punctuated by large black powder explosions. Captain Thomas Turner, the station commodore, reported to Admiral S. F. du Pont that the ship had evidently been "sent into Charleston to receive her officers, to be fitted out as a cruiser there. She had 140 men on board, with an armament of guns and gun carriages in her hold, commanded by a British naval retired officer."

## Cruiser, Privateer or Merchantman?

What exactly was she? Opinion split then and still does. One contemporary report called her so lightly built that "she would shake from stem to stern if a gun were fired from her decks." Historian Stephen Wise describes her as a merchantman, noting that the Union consul Thomas Dudley investigated her while she loaded at Liverpool and judged her too frail for a warship, fit only to run the blockade. Others saw a raider. After her loss, the U.S. Secretary of the Navy wrote that "she was a splendid craft, peculiarly fitted for the business of privateering," and the London Times declared there was "not the least doubt of her being intended as a privateer." Thomas Scharf, who had served in the Confederate navy, later wrote that she was faster and stronger than any Confederate cruiser afloat and "would have made a superb man-of-war." The underwater archaeologist E. Lee Spence, who found and identified the wreck, sides with the cruiser theory, pointing to the naval guns aboard, her deep-draft hull, heavier-than-standard iron planking and doubled-up Z-beam framing.

## A Reef on Top of a Wreck

Spence discovered the Georgiana in 1965, and her huge boiler reportedly lies only 5 feet below the surface. Large sections of hull remain intact, the starboard side rising more than 9 feet from the sand in places, though the white pine deck has long since been eaten away. Sea fans, sea whips and living corals now cover the iron, sea urchins and anemones crowd its surfaces, and sea bass, grouper, flounder, stingrays, seahorses and toadfish move through it. Resting on top of her shattered remains is a second wreck, the sidewheel steamer Mary Bowers, which struck the Georgiana's wreck in August 1864 while trying to run the blockade herself. The pairing matters to archaeologists: one is a heavily reinforced, deep-draft screw steamer, the other a light, shallow-draft sidewheeler built for slipping over shoals, both built and lost within about two years. In 1967 the site was granted what is described as South Carolina's first salvage license, and artifacts by the tens of thousands (accounts range up to hundreds of thousands, and Spence's own site claims more than a million) have come up, from boxes of pins and buttons near the forward cargo hatch to munitions and medicines. A cache of gold, said to weigh 350 pounds, is rumored to be hidden aboard; none has ever been found.

## Flight Context

Listed coordinates: 32.7797 N, 79.7597 W, in shallow Atlantic water just off Isle of Palms, northeast of the entrance to Charleston Harbor, South Carolina. The wreck itself lies underwater and is not visible from the air; use the barrier-island beaches and the harbor entrance as references and fly the shoreline at 1,000-2,000 ft, staying clear of Charleston's busy terminal airspace or coordinating with ATC. Nearby airports: Mount Pleasant Regional, Faison Field (KLRO) about 7 nm north; Charleston Executive on Johns Island (KJZI) about 13 nm west-southwest; Charleston International (KCHS) about 16 nm west-northwest. Sea breezes, coastal haze and summer afternoon thunderstorms are typical.
