# Hampton Park (Charleston)

Charleston's largest peninsula park began as a plantation and a planters' race course, became a prison camp where Union soldiers died, hosted a May 1865 procession of freedpeople often cited as the first Memorial Day, and later an exposition, a zoo and an Olmsted-designed park.

**Location:** 32.8000, -79.9561

**Categories:** Parks in Charleston, South Carolina, Buildings and structures completed in 1906, 1906 establishments in South Carolina

## Introduction

At 9 a.m. on May 1, 1865, according to historian David W. Blight of Yale, a procession stepped off across the old planters' race course in Charleston, led by three thousand Black schoolchildren carrying armloads of roses and singing "John Brown's Body." They were marching to a cemetery that Black Charlestonians had built for Union soldiers who died as prisoners on this ground. That day has been cited as the first Memorial Day celebration. The land where it happened is now Hampton Park, at 60 acres the largest park on the Charleston peninsula, and a remarkable share of the city's history has passed across it.

## The Planters' Track

By 1769 the land was part of a plantation owned by John Gibbes, known as The Grove or Orange Grove Plantation. Part of it became the Washington Race Course, where racing began in 1792, and the South Carolina Jockey Club acquired land there in 1835. Every February, an annual race drew thousands of spectators, who watched from an Italianate grandstand designed by Charles F. Reichardt. In Blight's words, this was where the Lowcountry planter aristocracy displayed its wealth, leisure and influence. The track's outline survives today: Mary Murray Drive, a one-mile parkway, circles Hampton Park almost exactly where the race track ran. The Civil War brought a steep loss of thoroughbred horses, and the economic decline of Reconstruction made racing less viable. The famous racing seasons ended by about 1882, and the land was later leased for farming.

## Martyrs of the Race Course

In the war's closing days the Confederates turned the race course into an outdoor prison camp. More than two hundred Union soldiers died there; Blight puts the toll at no fewer than 257, dead of exposure and disease and hastily buried in a mass grave behind the grandstand. After Union troops entered the city, some twenty-eight Black workmen, by his account, went to the site, reburied the dead properly and built a whitewashed fence around the cemetery, with an arch inscribed "Martyrs of the Race Course." On May 1, Black Charlestonians, most of them formerly enslaved, joined white missionaries and teachers in a procession Blight numbers at 10,000 people. Women carried baskets of flowers, wreaths and crosses, and members of the 54th Massachusetts marched around the graves. There were scripture readings, speeches and picnics in the infield. The claim is disputed: Bellware and Gardiner credit Mary Ann Williams and the Ladies Memorial Association of Columbus, Georgia, as the holiday's true originators. The cemetery did not last, and the soldiers were later reinterred in national cemeteries, including Beaufort.

## Exposition Grounds

The Jockey Club disbanded in 1899, and its assets passed to the Charleston Library Society in 1900. The racing structures were removed, although August Belmont was given the course's gates, which he installed at Belmont Park in New York. The old track then became part of something grander. The South Carolina Inter-State and West Indian Exposition opened on December 1, 1901, on a large tract that included the former race course, and drew 674,086 visitors. President Theodore Roosevelt attended on April 9, 1902. Still, the exposition was a financial failure and closed on May 31, 1902. One piece reportedly endures: a building that served as a tea house during the fair and now houses city Parks Department offices.

## An Olmsted Plan and a Contested Name

Charleston acquired part of the exposition land for a park and hired the Olmsted Brothers landscape architecture firm. John Charles Olmsted, adopted son of Frederick Law Olmsted, drew up a plan after his first visit to the city in 1906. Some of his ideas, including long parkways along the Ashley River, were disrupted when the city sold roughly 200 acres, the Rhett Farm tract, to The Citadel for a new, expanded campus. The park was named for Confederate General Wade Hampton III, who at the time of the Civil War was one of the largest enslavers in the South. After the war he promoted the Lost Cause, joined the Red Shirts and became governor of South Carolina. His name now marks the same ground where, in 1865, freedpeople honored the Union dead.

## Lions, Joggers and a Rose Pavilion

In 1932 the park gained a zoo, and an aviary followed about six years later. Most of the animals, a lion among them, were donated or bred there. By the mid-1960s the zoo was run-down, and it closed in 1975, its animals largely moving to the new Charles Towne Landing state park. After years of decline, the city began redeveloping the park in the early 1980s, reducing crime, refocusing on landscaping and adding a small snack stand designed by Sandy Logan. The park reopened in June 1984 with the Piccolo Spoleto finale. Today walkers, joggers and cyclists circle the one-mile perimeter road, close to where the racehorses once ran, and the park hosts the MOJA Festival, weddings and other events. In 2019 the renovated snack stand reopened as the Rose Pavilion, a public gathering place.

## Flight Context

Hampton Park lies at about 32.8000 N, 79.9561 W on the upper Charleston peninsula, bordered by The Citadel's campus to the west. Look for Mary Murray Drive, the one-mile loop that traces the old race course, a distinctive shape among the surrounding grid of streets from 1,500 to 2,500 feet. Marion Square lies about 1.3 nm to the southeast, and the cable-stayed Arthur Ravenel Jr. Bridge over the Cooper River rises about 2 nm to the east. Charleston Executive Airport (KJZI) is about 6 nm south-southwest, Charleston International Airport (KCHS) about 7 nm northwest and Mount Pleasant Regional Airport (KLRO) about 10.5 nm northeast; the park lies within busy Charleston airspace. Expect summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms.
