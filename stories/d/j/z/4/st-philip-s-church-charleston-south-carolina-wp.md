# St. Philip's Church (Charleston, South Carolina)

Home to South Carolina's oldest European-American congregation, St. Philip's Church on Church Street has burned, been rebuilt, doubled as a harbor range light and sheltered the graves of a vice president and signers of the nation's founding documents.

**Location:** 32.7792, -79.9292

**Categories:** Churches in Charleston, South Carolina, National Historic Landmarks in South Carolina, Anglican Church in North America church buildings in the United States, Historic American Buildings Survey in South Carolina, English-American culture in South Carolina

## Introduction

For twenty-two years, ships finding their way into Charleston Harbor took their bearings from a church. From 1893 to 1915 the tower of St. Philip's Church served as the rear light of a pair of range lighthouses that guided mariners into port, with the front light standing on Fort Sumter, out in the harbor. One account adds that the church was briefly pressed back into service in 1921, when the regular light needed repairs. It was a practical role for a building whose National Historic Landmark description praises its "imposing tower designed in the Wren-Gibbs tradition" and calls the whole a building "of the highest quality and sophistication." St. Philip's stands at 142 Church Street, and its congregation reaches back to 1680.

## Three Churches, Two Sites

Established in 1680, St. Philip's is the oldest European-American religious congregation in South Carolina, and the church describes itself as the oldest in the United States south of Virginia. Its first church, a wooden building raised between 1680 and 1681, stood at the corner of Broad and Meeting Streets, the site where St. Michael's Church stands today. A hurricane damaged it in 1710, the site proved inadequate, and the congregation began a new church a few blocks away on Church Street. After delays it was finished in 1723, with first services on Easter Day that year, only to be lost to a major fire in 1835. Work on the present church began that same year, and the congregation was worshipping in it again by 1838. The stuccoed brick building, fronted by three Tuscan pedimented porticoes, waited more than a decade for its crowning feature: the steeple, adorned with clocks, was added between 1848 and 1850.

## Rectors, Painters and a Miniaturist

The church's early records hold remarkable lives. Henrietta Johnston, the wife of the early rector Gideon Johnston, became the first recorded female artist in the American colonies. Mary Roberts, the first woman recorded as working as a miniaturist in the American colonies, was also connected to the church, and the register records her burial here in 1761. After the Revolution, the Rev. Thomas Frost, a fellow of Caius College, Cambridge, became rector in 1785 and faced a new challenge: with Anglicanism disestablished in the new United States, he had to invent a new role for the church. He died in 1804 at the age of 46. His son, the Rev. Thomas Downes Frost, was chosen as assistant minister on March 12, 1815, but died young as well, at just 26, in 1819.

## Patriots in the Graveyard

The churchyard reads like a chapter of American history. John C. Calhoun, the seventh vice president of the United States, is buried here. So are Christopher Gadsden, a signer of the Continental Association; Charles Pinckney, a U.S. senator, governor of South Carolina and signer of the United States Constitution; and Edward Rutledge, a Continental Congressman who signed both the Declaration of Independence and the Continental Association. Other graves hold Captain John Christie of the 60th Royal American Regiment, founder and first Worshipful Master of Lodge No. 1 at Detroit, Michigan, and the colonial figures Judith DuBose and Judith Smith Ladson. The Alexander Christie House, built around 1805, has long served as the church's rectory.

## A Congregation Divided

In recent years St. Philip's has been at the center of a very different kind of history. On November 17, 2012, the church hosted a convention that sought to legitimize the disassociation of the Anglican Diocese of South Carolina from The Episcopal Church and to strip all references to the Episcopal Church from the diocesan constitution and canons. The title to the building then became the subject of a dispute between the Anglican Diocese and the Episcopal Diocese. On April 20, 2022, the South Carolina Supreme Court ruled that St. Philip's was among fifteen parishes of the Anglican Diocese of South Carolina that could keep their property. The congregation that began in a wooden church in 1680 remains in its Church Street home, today a parish of the Anglican Church in North America.

## Flight Context

St. Philip's Church stands on Church Street at 32.7792°N, 79.9292°W, in the lower Charleston peninsula near the Cooper River side. Its imposing, clock-adorned steeple makes an easy reference point from 1,000–2,000 feet; picture it lined up with Fort Sumter out in the harbor, as mariners once used the pair. Charleston Executive Airport (KJZI) is about 6 nm southwest, Charleston International Airport (KCHS) about 9 nm northwest, and Mount Pleasant Regional Airport (KLRO) about 10 nm northeast. Watch for KCHS traffic; summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are common.
