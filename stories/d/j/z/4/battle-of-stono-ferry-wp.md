# Battle of Stono Ferry

On June 20, 1779, an American force under General Benjamin Lincoln marched through the night to attack a British rear guard dug in at Stono Ferry near Charleston, only to pull back from a position its defenders were already planning to leave.

**Location:** 32.7570, -80.1339

**Categories:** 1779 in the United States, Conflicts in 1779, Battles of the American Revolutionary War in South Carolina, Battles involving the United States, Battles involving the Kingdom of Great Britain, Charleston County, South Carolina, Battles of the southern theater of the American Revolutionary War, 1779 in South Carolina

## Introduction

Two companies of Highlanders held out at the edge of the British works until only 11 of their men were left standing. Around them, on the morning of June 20, 1779, a Hessian battalion had broken and American troops had pushed up to the abatis, the barrier of felled trees that ringed the redoubts at Stono Ferry. For a moment, the attack that General Benjamin Lincoln had launched against the retreating British looked like it might succeed. Then the Hessians rallied, reserves came across the bridge, and Lincoln ordered his men to withdraw from a fight over a position the British had already decided to abandon.

## A Chase to Charleston's Gates

Britain's campaign to regain its rebellious southern colonies had begun badly in June 1776, when an attempt to capture Charles Town, now Charleston, stalled after the failure to take a fort on Sullivan's Island. By December 1778, though, Savannah had fallen, and Charleston, home of the Continental Army's southern command under Lincoln, was exposed again. In the spring of 1779 Lincoln felt strong enough to act. On April 23 he marched from Purrysburg toward Augusta, apparently unaware that the British supply situation was somewhat desperate. His move left coastal South Carolina guarded by a minimal militia force. British General Augustine Prevost saw the opening and marched out on April 29 with 2,500 men. General William Moultrie's militia, about 1,000 strong, fell back toward Charleston, losing men along the way as local soldiers deserted to protect their homes and plantations. Prevost chased them to the gates of the city, and on May 10 the two sides skirmished near Ashley Ferry, about 7 miles from Charleston.

## The Rear Guard at the Ferry

Two days later, Prevost intercepted a message revealing that Lincoln was marching rapidly back to Charleston, and he decided to retreat. His army was slowed by the supplies it had taken along the way, so he left a rear guard at Stono Ferry, between Johns Island and the mainland, and moved most of his force to Savannah by boat on June 16. Lieutenant Colonel John Maitland commanded the roughly 900 men who stayed behind. They built a bridgehead on the north side of what is now known as New Cut Church Flats to cover the ferry, with three strong redoubts circled by an abatis. The garrison was a mix of nations and loyalties: Highlanders of the 71st Foot, Hessians from the Regiment von Trumbach, and companies of Loyalists from North and South Carolina. Lincoln decided to strike. Although he commanded five to seven thousand men, he could raise only about 1,200 for the expedition, most of them poorly trained local militia, while Moultrie led a smaller secondary effort against British soldiers on Johns Island.

## Dawn in the Thick Woods

Lincoln's men marched through the night, 8 miles from Ashley Ferry, near today's Drayton Hall historic site. They arrived at dawn and immediately began struggling through thick woods. The Americans advanced in two wings. On the right, General Jethro Sumner led Carolina militia with two guns, their flank covered by light infantry under the Marquis de Malmady. On the left, Continental troops under General Isaac Huger brought four guns into the fight, and with them was a body of light infantry under John Henderson. Henderson's men made first contact shortly before sunrise. For an hour the Americans poured small arms and cannon fire into the British positions, then advanced to the abatis. That was when the Highlanders made their stand and the Hessians gave way. Maitland shifted his forces to meet the larger threat from Huger's wing, the Hessians rallied and returned, and reserves hurried across the bridge. Lincoln chose that moment to pull back.

## The Price of a Morning

Casualty returns vary slightly by source. One tally has the Americans losing 34 killed, 113 wounded and 155 missing, and many of the missing were probably deserters; the British lost 26 killed, 93 wounded and 1 missing. Among the Americans was Hugh Jackson, the elder brother of future President Andrew Jackson, who had served under Colonel William Richardson Davie. Felled by heat and exhaustion, he died after the battle, and the future president lost a brother to this river crossing near Charleston. The bitter irony of Stono Ferry lies in its timing. By one account, Maitland had decided almost a week before the battle to withdraw, and only a lack of boats had kept him there. On June 23, three days after the fighting, he finally began moving toward Beaufort, with little prompting from Lincoln's attack. Part of the battlefield, now hard to recognize along the Stono River near Hollywood, has been preserved by the South Carolina Battleground Preservation Trust, which has secured about 20 acres and plans public access.

## Flight Context

The Stono Ferry battlefield lies at about 32.7570 N, 80.1339 W on the mainland side of the crossing to Johns Island, roughly 10 nm west of downtown Charleston. The community of Rantowles is about 2 nm north-northwest, and Drayton Hall, near the start of Lincoln's night march, is about 7.5 nm north-northeast. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet, look for the waterway separating the mainland from Johns Island, with the town of Hollywood on the mainland shore. Charleston International Airport (KCHS) is about 10 nm north-northeast and Charleston Executive Airport (KJZI) about 7 nm east-southeast, so the area sits under busy Charleston airspace. Expect summer haze, heat and afternoon thunderstorms.
