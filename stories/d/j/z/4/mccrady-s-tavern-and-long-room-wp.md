# McCrady's Tavern and Long Room

A barber turned tavern keeper built Charleston's last surviving 18th-century long room, where the Society of the Cincinnati feted George Washington in 1791; the complex later served as a warehouse, a print shop and an acclaimed restaurant.

**Location:** 32.7781, -79.9272

**Categories:** National Register of Historic Places in Charleston, South Carolina, Drinking establishments in South Carolina, Commercial buildings completed in 1788, 1788 establishments in South Carolina

## Introduction

On May 4, 1791, George Washington sat down to a banquet in a second-floor hall off Unity Alley. His hosts were the Charleston branch of the Society of the Cincinnati, and around him gathered South Carolina governor Charles Pinckney, several members of Congress and the mayor of Charleston. The room had been built only three years earlier by Edward McCrady, a local barber turned tavern keeper, and it was made for exactly this kind of evening. McCrady's Long Room, with its stage and its sixteen-foot ceiling, hosted plays and banquets for the city's elite. It is the last room of its kind in Charleston, and it has outlived nearly every use the city has found for it.

## The Barber of East Bay Street

The lot's recorded history begins with a merchant named Jonathan Amory, who received the original grant and sold it in 1723. By 1767 the building that is now the main tavern stood on the site, noted in a city survey that year. In August 1778, Edward McCrady, a local barber, bought the building and began running it as a tavern. It was a risky moment to go into business. McCrady was a leader in Charleston's militia during the American Revolution, and when the city fell to the British in 1780 he was arrested and taken to St. Augustine. He was held there until 1781. It was the same fate that befell Thomas Heyward, Jr., the Declaration signer whose house stands a couple of blocks away on Church Street. After the war, McCrady returned to his tavern and set about expanding it.

## A Hall Built for Evenings

In the years after the Revolution, McCrady's tavern became a hub of Charleston's social life. McCrady bought the lots next door, and in 1788 he built the Long Room, a banquet hall and small-scale performance venue. The main room occupies most of the Long Room building's second story. It originally held a stage beneath a sixteen-foot ceiling, tall enough to lend performances and banquets a sense of occasion. The complex took on a distinctive plan. The main tavern building, a rectangular three-story brick structure four bays wide with a flat roof, faces East Bay Street and runs east to west. Behind it, a courtyard connects to the Long Room, which faces Unity Alley and runs north to south. Together the two buildings form a "T," a shape still legible in the old city's tight grid.

## The President's Dinner

Washington's banquet in May 1791 came during the same Charleston visit for which the city rented the Heyward house on Church Street as his lodging. The Charleston branch of the Society of the Cincinnati chose McCrady's Long Room for its tribute. The guest list reflected the building's status. Governor Charles Pinckney attended, along with several members of Congress and Charleston's mayor, all gathered in a room raised by a former barber and militia leader who had spent part of the war as a prisoner. For McCrady, the dinner capped a remarkable rise. He would die only three years later, in 1794, leaving behind a tavern whose most celebrated evening had already come and gone.

## Warehouse, Print Shop, Ruin

After McCrady's death the tavern changed hands several times, and its glamour faded. In 1884 the complex was converted into a warehouse. In 1913 the Daggett Printing Company bought it for use as a print shop, and the rooms that once held banquets rang instead with the business of printing. After the building had been abandoned for several years, McCrady's Tavern and Long Room was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1972, and plans took shape to restore it to its late-18th-century appearance. The recognition mattered. It marked the complex as more than an old warehouse on East Bay Street, and it preserved the last surviving long room of its kind in a city that once had others.

## A Restaurant's Moment

The old tavern returned to food and drink as McCrady's restaurant, and for a time it drew national attention. In 2010 its chef, Sean Brock, won the James Beard Foundation Award for Best Chef Southeast. Sam Sifton, a New York Times food editor, singled out McCrady's as one of the best restaurants outside the "first tier" of American cities. The revival did not last. On April 30, 2020, the Neighborhood Dining Group announced that McCrady's would remain closed permanently after its shutdown during the COVID-19 pandemic. The building has seen quiet spells before, as a warehouse and as an abandoned shell. In 2021 the property was sold to the restaurateur Martin Sprock, who announced plans for a new restaurant downstairs and a music venue in the Long Room, and the website of the new restaurant, Clarence Foster's Cookery & Saloon, lists the address as opening soon. The Long Room still stands, waiting for its next act.
