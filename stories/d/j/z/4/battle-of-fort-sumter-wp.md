# Battle of Fort Sumter

For thirty-four hours in April 1861, Confederate batteries ringing Charleston Harbor pounded an unfinished federal fort, and the first shots of the Civil War killed no one until a salute to the flag went wrong.

**Location:** 32.7524, -79.8750

**Categories:** American Civil War, Battles, Charleston, South Carolina, Military history, History of South Carolina

## Introduction

At 3:20 in the morning on April 12, 1861, Major Robert Anderson was handed a note inside an unfinished fort at the mouth of Charleston Harbor. The Confederate batteries around the water, it said, would open fire on him in one hour. Anderson walked the messengers back to their boat and shook hands with each of them. "If we never meet in this world again," he told them, "God grant that we may meet in the next." Seventy minutes later a single mortar shell climbed into the dark and burst over Fort Sumter, and the American Civil War began.

## Six Days After Secession

South Carolina declared its secession on December 20, 1860. Six days later, under cover of darkness, Anderson abandoned the indefensible Fort Moultrie on Sullivan's Island, ordered its guns spiked and its carriages burned, and slipped his command across the water in small boats to Sumter. Governor Pickens answered by seizing nearly everything else. State troops took Moultrie and its 56 guns, a 150-man force captured Castle Pinckney near downtown on December 27, and the federal arsenal fell on December 30, handing the militia more than 22,000 weapons. On January 9, 1861, the unarmed merchant ship Star of the West arrived with supplies and 200 soldiers. A battery on Morris Island, crewed by cadets from The Citadel who were then the only trained artillerymen in South Carolina's service, opened fire and drove her off. Anderson held his guns rather than start a war.

## A Fortress Facing the Sea

Sumter was meant to mount 135 guns worked by 650 men. Decades of delays and budget cuts had left it only about ninety percent finished, and Anderson had 85 soldiers, plus 43 workmen, to hold it through a hungry winter of short rations and scarce fuel. Worse, its guns faced the Atlantic, built to fight warships rather than batteries on the surrounding shore. The man arranging those batteries knew Anderson well. Brigadier General P. G. T. Beauregard had been his artillery student at West Point and later his assistant, and now he ringed the harbor with mortars, Columbiads and an iron-shielded floating battery, backed by about 6,000 men. In Washington, Abraham Lincoln learned on taking office that Sumter had six weeks of food left. He openly offered to evacuate if it would guarantee Virginia's loyalty, remarking, "A state for a fort is no bad business." In Montgomery, Confederate Secretary of State Robert Toombs reportedly warned that an attack would "strike a hornet's nest."

## Shells Over the Water

At 4:30 a.m., Lieutenant Henry S. Farley fired the signal round from Fort Johnson on James Island. The honor had first been offered to Virginia secessionist Roger Pryor, who declined: "I could not fire the first gun of the war." Then 43 guns and mortars opened in a counterclockwise sequence around the harbor, two minutes between shots, to stretch ammunition Beauregard expected to last only 48 hours. Charleston's residents, the diarist Mary Chesnut among them, rushed out into the predawn darkness to watch shells arc across the water. Inside the fort, Anderson's men waited for daylight and ate breakfast. At 7 a.m. Captain Abner Doubleday fired back at Cummings Point, and missed. Only 21 guns on the lowest tier could be worked safely, and just 700 cloth powder cartridges existed when the fight began. Workmen sewed more frantically, some from Anderson's own socks, until shortages cut the garrison's fire to six guns.

## Fire in the Barracks

Sumter's walls were masonry, but its barracks and officers' quarters were wood, and the Confederates fed them heated shot, cannonballs baked red in furnaces. A rain shower at 7 p.m. put out the first fires. By noon on April 13 most of the wooden buildings and the main gate were burning, and flames crept toward a magazine holding 300 barrels of gunpowder. Soldiers hauled barrels out until Anderson judged it too dangerous and ordered the doors shut on two-thirds of them. Barrels thrown into the sea came back, the tide nudging them into clusters, some of which incoming rounds set alight. Across the harbor, many Confederate gunners admired the stubborn defense, cheering whenever the fort resumed firing. At 1 p.m. the flagpole fell. Louis Wigfall, a former U.S. senator, took a small boat out from Morris Island waving a white handkerchief from his sword and asked on what terms Anderson would evacuate. Evacuate, Anderson noted, not surrender. After more than 3,000 rounds without losing a man, he accepted.

## Fifty Guns of a Hundred

The garrison formally surrendered at 2:30 p.m. on April 13. No one had died in the bombardment. Anderson's one condition was a 100-gun salute to the United States flag, and during it, on April 14, a spark set off a pile of cartridges. The blast killed Private Daniel Hough, usually counted as the first death of the war, and mortally wounded Private Edward Galloway; the salute stopped at about fifty shots. Hough was buried on the parade ground within two hours, and Galloway died days later in a Charleston hospital. Anderson carried the Fort Sumter Flag north, where it became a Union rallying symbol. Frederic Edwin Church's painting Our Banner in the Sky, made in the weeks after the attack, was sold as a chromolithograph to help soldiers' families. Lincoln called for 75,000 volunteers, and four more slave states seceded. Four years later, on April 14, 1865, an ailing Anderson returned to the ruined fort and raised the same flag he had lowered.

## Flight Context

Fort Sumter sits at 32.752N, 79.875W on a small island in the mouth of Charleston Harbor. From 1,500-2,500 feet the whole 1861 battlefield fits in one view: Sullivan's Island and Fort Moultrie to the north, Cummings Point at the tip of Morris Island to the south, James Island (Fort Johnson) to the west, and the Charleston peninsula about 3 nm to the west-northwest. Charleston International (KCHS) lies about 12 nm northwest; Charleston Executive (KJZI) on Johns Island is about 7 nm west-southwest, and Mount Pleasant Regional-Faison Field (KLRO) about 10 nm north-northeast. Busy harbor shipping and humid coastal haze are common; the fort's low masonry walls stand out best at low sun angles.
