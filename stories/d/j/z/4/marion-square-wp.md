# Marion Square

Once a line of colonial fortifications and then The Citadel's parade ground, Charleston's Marion Square has survived plans to pave it, outlived its bandstand, lost its Calhoun statue and still keeps its center open for soldiers who no longer drill there.

**Location:** 32.7869, -79.9358

**Categories:** Parks in Charleston, South Carolina

## Introduction

By the terms of its lease, the middle of Marion Square has to stay empty. The center of this downtown Charleston park is kept open as a parade ground, a legal echo of the decades when this lawn served a military college next door. Today the open lawn is more likely to hold sunbathing college students, Saturday farmers market stalls or festival tents than marching columns. Yet the rule endures, and so does the square, bounded by King, Calhoun and Meeting Streets and a pedestrian way called Tobacco Street. Its roughly ten acres have been a fortification, a military green, a battleground over parking lots and a stage for the city's hardest arguments about memory.

## Walls of Lime and Oyster Shell

The story begins with a land sale. When Joseph Wragg died, his son John inherited 79 acres along King Street, and in 1758 John Wragg sold 8.75 acres to the provincial government for 1,230 pounds. The colony wanted a defensive wall, built for protection against feared attacks by Native Americans and later by the British. At its center stood the hornwork, whose inner wall was two feet thick and made of lime and oyster shells. Two ditches fronted the wall, and 93 cannon were mounted along the works. By 1783 the fortifications were no longer needed, and the land passed to Charleston's newly created city government. In 1789 the state took back a strip along the northern edge for a tobacco inspection complex. An error later surfaced: the city had absorbed an extra 1.40 acres. Wragg's heirs petitioned the state Senate, and because the tobacco complex already stood on the land, the state paid them instead of returning it.

## The Citadel's Green

The square took its modern shape as a parade ground for the state arsenal built along its north side. That arsenal gave the square its most famous era. From 1843 until 1922, The Citadel occupied the building, and the lawn in front became known as Citadel Green, the parade ground of the military college's cadets. When The Citadel moved to the city's west side in 1922, the green stayed behind. The name changed too. Marion Square honors Francis Marion, the Revolutionary War hero whose name also graces a historic hotel on nearby King Street. The old arsenal, now known as the Old Citadel, still faces the square from the north, and Citadel Square Church rises directly across the way. Stand on the lawn today and the architecture still frames it as a military ground, even though the cadets are long gone.

## Guardians of the Grass

Marion Square is not city property. It is jointly owned by the Washington Light Infantry and the Sumter Guards, and the city operates it as a public park under a lease. That arrangement has saved the square more than once. The owners' objections stopped city officials from paving the park as a parking lot in the 1940s and again in 1956, and blocked a plan to turn it into a shopping center. Not every addition lasted. In 1944 a bandstand in the Art Moderne style went up to plans by Augustus Constantine. It was first conceived to house restrooms for white servicemen, a reminder of the segregated era in which it was built, and the bandstand was added to that plan. It became a spot for political rallies, but vandals plagued the restrooms. City council approved its removal as early as 1961, but it was not razed until a park refurbishment in 2000 and 2001.

## Monuments and Reckonings

The square holds many monuments, including a Holocaust memorial and an obelisk dedicated to Wade Hampton. The most contested was a bronze statue of John C. Calhoun atop a giant pillar. It was the second Calhoun monument on the site. Charlestonians disliked the first, and its organizers struggled with the delivery of its pieces. In 1894 a new artist, J. Massey Rhind, was commissioned to create a new figure, the replacement memorial rose in 1896, and the original was sold for scrap. More than a century later, concerns over Calhoun's views on slavery came to a head. In June 2020, after a unanimous City Council vote, the statue was taken down. The past had one more surprise waiting. In January 2021, workers removing the statue's base found a time capsule dating to 1858, a message from an earlier Charleston surfacing just as the city rewrote the square's meaning.

## Charleston's Front Lawn

For all its history, Marion Square is above all a place people use. It is a favorite of College of Charleston students because of its proximity to campus, and warm days bring them out onto the lawn. That habit even drew political fire. In 2003 a city council member proposed banning sunbathing in the square, but the idea found little support. During the summer a farmers market fills the square on Saturdays, and festivals such as the Food and Wine Festival and the Spoleto arts festival set up here as well. Winter has its own tradition. For well over a century a Christmas tree has been placed in Marion Square for the holiday season, a tradition of holiday trees on the old parade ground that the city still keeps. The soldiers may be gone, but the square still gathers the city in.

## Flight Context

Marion Square is at 32.7869°N, 79.9358°W in the middle of the Charleston peninsula, between the Ashley and Cooper Rivers. From 1,500–2,500 feet it is one of the easiest downtown landmarks to spot: an open rectangle of lawn bounded by King Street on the west, Meeting Street on the east and Calhoun Street on the south, with the Old Citadel on its north side. The diamond-shaped towers of the cable-stayed Arthur Ravenel Jr. Bridge over the Cooper River rise about 1.4 nm northeast, the Battery marks the peninsula's southern tip about 1.1 nm south, and the present Citadel campus lies about 1.4 nm west-northwest near the Ashley River. Nearby airports: Charleston Executive Airport on Johns Island (KJZI) about 6 nm south-southwest, Charleston International (KCHS) about 8.5 nm northwest, and Mount Pleasant Regional-Faison Field (KLRO) about 10 nm northeast. Low flight over downtown falls within the Charleston International terminal area; coordinate with approach control. Expect sea breezes, coastal haze and summer afternoon thunderstorms.
