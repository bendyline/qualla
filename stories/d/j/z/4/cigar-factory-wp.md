# Cigar Factory

Built as a cotton mill in 1881 and later one of Charleston's largest private employers, the Cigar Factory on East Bay Street is where striking workers in 1945 and 1946 sang the hymn that became "We Shall Overcome."

**Location:** 32.7975, -79.9347

**Categories:** National Historic Landmarks in South Carolina, National Register of Historic Places in Charleston, South Carolina, Industrial buildings and structures on the National Register of Historic Places in South Carolina, Cigar factories on the National Register of Historic Places, 1882 establishments in South Carolina, Industrial buildings completed in 1882, Victorian architecture in South Carolina

## Introduction

During the long strike at the Cigar Factory in 1945 and 1946, a striker named Lucille Simmons sang to signal the end of a day's picketing. Her song was a modified version of a gospel hymn. The Atlantic has called her singing the first widely acknowledged performance of the modern song that would become "We Shall Overcome," the anthem of the civil rights movement. The building where those workers stood their ground still fills a block at 701 East Bay Street in Charleston, though today it holds restaurants, shops and offices instead of cigar makers.

## Steam and Cotton

The building began as something else entirely. Constructed in 1881 by A. D. Lockwood & Company, it opened in 1882 as the Cotton Mill of Charleston, and on December 29 of that year a local newspaper announced, "The Charleston Steam Cotton Mill Now in Operation." Its design followed the Victorian commercial style of the era, applied to a building made for industry. By 1912, the American Cigar Company (the plant is also described as an American Tobacco Company factory) had bought the mill and converted it into a cigar factory. In time the building became one of the city's economic engines: during the 1930s, the Cigar Factory was reportedly the largest private employer in Charleston.

## The Strike

On October 22, 1945, about 1,000 workers at the Charleston plant walked out. A majority of them were Black women. Organized as Local 15 of the Food, Tobacco, Agricultural and Allied Workers union of the CIO, they demanded a 25-cent hourly raise, backpay owed to them and a closed shop. The strike lasted more than five months, through the winter, until it ended on April 1, 1946. The workers came away with their backpay, a raise and agreements that eased the racial barriers keeping Black workers out of skilled positions. They had held out through the winter, and their endurance on the picket line, day after day, is what gave the song its first stage.

## How a Hymn Traveled

Lucille Simmons's song did not stay in Charleston. In 1947, two members of Local 15, Anna Lee Bonneau and Evelyn Risher, carried it to the Highlander Folk School in Tennessee and taught it to Zilphia Horton. Horton passed it on to the folk singer Pete Seeger, who popularized it as "We Shall Overcome," and from there it became a major anthem of the civil rights movement. The path is remarkable for how human it was. A hymn adapted by a striking worker, shared by two union members, taught to Zilphia Horton at Highlander and handed to a folk singer: a chain of people who heard something worth keeping and passed it along.

## A Landmark Reborn

The cigar factory closed for good in 1973. Seven years later, in 1980, the building was listed on the National Register of Historic Places, but a listing alone did not give it a new purpose. That came with a $30 million redevelopment that began in 2014. By 2017 the old factory was mostly leased, and its tenants today include restaurants, an event venue, retail and offices, among them the Clemson University Design Center, the largest tenant, and a law firm that announced a 15-year lease for a full floor in July 2026. In 2024 the building was designated a National Historic Landmark. Diners today eat where cotton was spun and cigars were made, in the same building where, on a picket line outside, a song began its long journey.

## Flight Context

The Cigar Factory stands at about 32.7975 N, 79.9347 W at 701 East Bay Street on the east side of the Charleston peninsula, near the Cooper River waterfront. Look for the old factory building near the waterfront; the cable-stayed Arthur Ravenel Jr. Bridge over the Cooper River rises about 1 nm to the east-northeast, and Marion Square is a little over half a nautical mile to the south. A viewing altitude of 1,500 to 2,500 feet works well. Expect summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms.
