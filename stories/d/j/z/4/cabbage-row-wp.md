# Cabbage Row

Two eighteenth-century buildings at 89 and 91 Church Street in Charleston became, in DuBose Heyward's imagination, the Catfish Row of Porgy and later of Gershwin's Porgy and Bess.

**Location:** 32.7752, -79.9292

**Categories:** Houses in Charleston, South Carolina

## Introduction

Catfish Row, the crowded tenement courtyard where Porgy and Bess unfolds, never existed on any Charleston map. Cabbage Row did. The pair of eighteenth-century buildings at 89 and 91 Church Street gave DuBose Heyward, who lived nearby on the same street, the model for the fictional home of his most famous characters. From these two old structures came a novel, a play and, eventually, George Gershwin's opera Porgy and Bess.

## From Counting Houses to a Sailors' Resort

The buildings are old, described by most accounts as older than the United States itself, though some sources date them to about 1783. When they went up, they were perhaps operated as counting houses, the offices where merchants kept their accounts. Their fortunes did not hold. After the earthquake of 1886, their condition deteriorated steadily, and by the end of that decline they had become a well-known "resort for sailors," a place with a reputation very different from its mercantile beginnings. The row had come down in the world, and that worn, lived-in state is the Cabbage Row that passed into fiction.

## Heyward's Neighbors, Porgy's Home

DuBose Heyward lived nearby on Church Street, and the row became his model for Catfish Row. His novel Porgy, published in 1925 by the George H. Doran Company, is set in the Black tenements of 1920s Charleston, and its title character was based on a real Charlestonian, Samuel Smalls. In some passages, Heyward's characters speak Gullah, the creole language that developed among enslaved African Americans in the region. In 1927 Heyward and his wife, the playwright Dorothy Heyward, adapted the novel for the stage, and in 1935, after discussions between Heyward and George Gershwin, the story reached its most famous form as the opera Porgy and Bess. Behind the fiction stood real Charlestonians, Samuel Smalls among them, whose community gave the story its setting and its voice.

## An Artist's Vision for the Row

The buildings' next chapter came quickly. In February 1928, the year after the stage adaptation, landscape architect Loutrel Briggs bought the property, intending to restore it and envisioning the buildings as residences suited to artists. It was a fitting ambition for a place that had already inspired a novel and a play, with an opera still to come. The property changed hands more than once in the decades that followed, and in 1955 Charles H. Gibbs and his business partners bought it. Today the two buildings still stand at 89 and 91 Church Street, among the eighteenth-century structures of downtown Charleston, and visitors who know the opera can stand on the sidewalk and look at the real place that the imagined Catfish Row grew from.

## Flight Context

Cabbage Row stands at about 32.7752 N, 79.9292 W on Church Street near the southern end of the Charleston peninsula. The buildings are far too small to pick out individually, so orient on nearby landmarks: Rainbow Row, the line of colorful historic houses on East Bay Street, lies about a block to the east, and the cable-stayed Arthur Ravenel Jr. Bridge over the Cooper River rises about 2 nm to the north-northeast. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet the dense grid of the historic district is easy to read. Charleston Executive Airport (KJZI) is about 6 nm southwest, Charleston International Airport (KCHS) about 9 nm northwest, and Mount Pleasant Regional Airport (KLRO) about 10 nm northeast; downtown lies within busy Charleston airspace. Expect summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms.
