# Unitarian Church in Charleston

A church that calls itself the oldest Unitarian church in the South has survived war, fire, bombardment, a hurricane and an earthquake that dropped its tower into the nave, and it now honors the enslaved workers who made its bricks.

**Location:** 32.7786, -79.9344

**Categories:** Churches, Unitarian Universalism, Gothic Revival architecture, National Historic Landmarks, Charleston, South Carolina

## Introduction

The man who wrote "Fair Harvard," the alma mater of Harvard College, spent nearly four decades preaching in a brick church on Archdale Street in Charleston. Samuel Gilman arrived in 1819 and stayed until his death in 1858, and under him the congregation grew substantially. His church, which calls itself the oldest Unitarian church in the South and the second-oldest church building on the Charleston peninsula, still stands beside a leafy churchyard that reaches toward King Street, its fan-vaulted ceiling rebuilt after an earthquake tried to bring it down.

## A Meeting House Interrupted

It began as overflow space. The Independent Church in Charleston, also known as the Society of Dissenters, had outgrown its Meeting Street home and in 1772 began a second meeting house here: Georgian, plain brick, two doors and a tower in front. It was nearly finished in 1776 when the Revolutionary War began, and both Colonial and British forces quartered militia inside, leaving it in need of considerable repair. Only in 1787 was the Archdale Street Meeting House finally dedicated. For the next thirty years the two buildings operated as a single church, sharing two ministers and even the same sermon. Drs. Hollinshead and Keith, co-pastors for most of that period, each preached one sermon in both houses every Sunday, alternating between morning and afternoon services.

## Seventy-Five Walk Out

The partnership broke over theology. In 1815 one co-pastor was the Reverend Anthony Forster, whose wife, Altona, was the daughter of printer Joseph Gales, a close associate of the English scientist and Unitarian minister Joseph Priestley. Forster had moved from trinitarian to unitarian belief by 1816, and in 1817, after Dr. Hollinshead died, the congregation split. Seventy-five of its 144 members left the mother church and made the Archdale Street building their own, chartered that year as the Second Independent Church of Charleston. The original Meeting Street site is now home to the Circular Congregational Church. As Forster's health failed from tuberculosis, the congregation hired Gilman, an avowed Unitarian, to complete the transition. His wife, the author and poet Caroline Howard Gilman, was inspired by Mount Auburn Cemetery in Massachusetts to plant the churchyard in the 1830s as a garden meant for the living. In 1839 the church joined the American Unitarian Association and took the name it carries today.

## Westminster on Archdale Street

In 1852 church member Francis D. Lee, a 26-year-old Charleston architect, was hired to enlarge and remodel the building, working with the more experienced Edward C. Jones. Their models were English chapels: the Chapel of Henry VII at Westminster Abbey and St George's Chapel at Windsor Castle. They raised the entire roof four feet, added a chancel, built false flying buttresses to give the Georgian building Gothic proportions and raised the tower to hold a minister's office. Inside, a fan-vaulted ceiling spread over the nave in close imitation of its English prototypes. The great chancel window, made in 1854 by the Henry E. Sharp glass works of Brooklyn, shows Moses and Aaron flanking the Ark of the Covenant, with the four Gospel writers arranged above. Pews, pulpit, lectern and communion rail were carved from or capped with walnut, and gas lighting was installed.

## Spared by Fire, Struck by the Earth

War closed the doors until 1865. The Great Charleston Fire of 1861 destroyed several churches and stopped within two blocks, and the 18-month Federal bombardment that began in August 1863 left the building unscathed. Peace brought poverty instead: pew rentals collapsed, and seven ministers came and went in ten years, while member Alva Gage kept the church afloat with his own money. Nature finished what war had not. A hurricane in August 1885, rated a Category 2 storm with winds of about 105 mph in modern reanalysis, blew out every nave window; Unitarian churches in Boston donated Art Nouveau replacements inscribed "The Lord is One" in Hebrew and "Spirit of God" in Greek. The next year the great Charleston earthquake sent the whole top of the tower, pinnacles and medieval-style finials included, crashing into the nave, tearing open the roof and destroying part of the fan vaulting. Unitarians across the country gave $17,000. Architect Thomas Silloway restored Lee's interior, but rebuilt the tower with lowered, simplified pinnacles and parapet so it would better withstand another quake.

## Bricks That Remember

Today the churchyard is an oasis in the middle of the city, reached by a walkway from King Street or through gates on Archdale and part of Charleston's Gateway Walk. Hurricane Hugo destroyed about 30 mature trees in 1989, and dogwood, silverbell, crepe myrtle and Japanese maple grew in their place. Despite local myths, nobody famous is buried here. Across the yard, Gage Hall, built in the early 1890s with Alva Gage's money, once hosted radio-broadcast debates on controversial topics in the 1960s and one of Charleston's first Head Start programs. In 2013, cutting a wheelchair passage through the entry wall meant removing many original bricks. The congregation used them to build a memorial near the entrance, topped by a metal Sankofa bird looking backward, an African symbol for learning from the past in order to move forward. Its inscription honors "the enslaved workers who made these bricks and helped build our church."

## Flight Context

The church stands at about 32.779N, 79.934W on Archdale Street in downtown Charleston, several blocks inland from the Cooper River waterfront and just east of King Street. From 1,000-1,500 feet, look for its Gothic tower and the green churchyard block among the dense rooflines of the historic peninsula, north of the Battery and Broad Street. Charleston International (KCHS) is about 9 nm northwest, and Charleston Executive (KJZI) on Johns Island about 6 nm southwest. Downtown is congested with steeples and harbor traffic; hazy summer afternoons reduce contrast over the peninsula.
