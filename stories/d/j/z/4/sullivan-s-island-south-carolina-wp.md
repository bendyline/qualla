# Sullivan's Island, South Carolina

At the mouth of Charleston Harbor, Sullivan's Island holds the memory of the enslaved Africans quarantined on its shore, the palmetto-log fort that turned back a British fleet in 1776, and the beaches where a young Edgar Allan Poe found the setting for "The Gold-Bug."

**Location:** 32.7633, -79.8378

**Categories:** American Revolution, Edgar Allan Poe, Populated coastal places in South Carolina, Populated places established in the 17th century, Towns in Charleston County, South Carolina, Towns in South Carolina

## Introduction

In 1989, the writer Toni Morrison lamented that there was no memorial to the enslaved Africans brought to America: "There is no suitable memorial, or plaque, or wreath or wall, or park or skyscraper lobby." In 2008 the Toni Morrison Society answered in a quiet way, dedicating a small black steel bench on Sullivan's Island to their memory. The choice of place was deliberate. This barrier island at the entrance to Charleston Harbor was the quarantine station where enslaved Africans arriving sick were held, before being sold in Charleston. It is also where a palmetto-log fort defied the Royal Navy, where Edgar Allan Poe served as a young soldier, and where some 1,900 people now live beside the Atlantic.

## Captain O'Sullivan's Lookout

The island was first known as O'Sullivan's Island, named for Captain Florence O'Sullivan, who was stationed here as a lookout in the late 17th century. It is easy to see why the spot mattered. Sullivan's Island sits along the Atlantic Ocean, bordered on the west by the entrance to Charleston Harbor, on the north by Cove Inlet and the Intracoastal Waterway, and on the east by Breach Inlet and Swinton Creek. Ships bound for Charleston passed right by it. Today the Ben Sawyer Bridge links the island to Mount Pleasant on the mainland, and a bridge over Breach Inlet connects it to the Isle of Palms. The town covers about 3.4 square miles, roughly a quarter of it water, and the 2020 census counted 1,891 residents in 821 households. Its children attend Sullivan's Island Elementary, Moultrie Middle and Wando High schools.

## The Island of Arrival

Sullivan's Island's darkest role came with the slave trade. From 1707 to 1799 the island served as a quarantine station, and enslaved Africans on ships carrying infectious disease were held in "pest houses" there before being taken to Charleston for public auction. The National Park Service says Charleston was the entry point for nearly half of the captive Africans shipped to North America, though historians cannot say how many of them were held on the island itself, and the island has been likened to Ellis Island, the gateway for later generations of immigrants in New York. The comparison cuts both ways. Ellis Island received people seeking new lives; Sullivan's Island received people torn from theirs. For generations there was little on the landscape to say so. A South Carolina historical marker beside Fort Moultrie went up in 1999, and in 2008 the Toni Morrison Society placed its bench here, so the island now names what happened on its shore.

## Palmetto Logs and Sand

On June 28, 1776, the island became a battlefield. The first fort on Sullivan's Island, built of palmetto logs and sand, was still unfinished when Commodore Sir Peter Parker and nine British men-of-war attacked it. The spongy palmetto wood absorbed British cannonballs, and after a nine-hour battle the British ships were forced to withdraw. The fort was named in honor of its commander, Colonel William Moultrie, and Fort Moultrie remained a harbor defense for generations afterward. Later fortifications still line the island. Several of its districts and properties are listed on the National Register of Historic Places, including Fort Moultrie and its Quartermaster and Support Facilities Historic District, the Moultrieville, Atlanticville and Sullivan's Island historic districts, the U.S. Coast Guard Historic District, and the artillery emplacements of Battery Gadsden and Battery Thomson. Near the south shore stands the modern Charleston Light, first lit in 1962.

## Poe's Gold-Bug Island

Edgar Allan Poe was stationed at Fort Moultrie from November 1827 to December 1828, and the island stayed with him. It is the setting for much of his short story "The Gold-Bug" (1843). In "The Balloon-Hoax," Poe described a gas balloon piloted by eight men on a 75-hour trip from northern Wales to Fort Moultrie. Written in a dry, scientific style, it was published as fact in the New York Sun in April 1844 and retracted two days later. The island has returned the favor. Its town library, housed in a refurbished military battery, is named for Poe, and Raven and Gold Bug avenues recall his work. Later writers followed. Dorothea Benton Frank set her novel Sullivan's Island here, Pat Conroy used the island in The Boo, Beach Music and South of Broad, and Lawrence Hill's The Book of Negroes sends its heroine, Aminata Diallo, through Sullivan's Island as an eleven-year-old captive in 1757.

## Wrecks Offshore, Storms Overhead

The waters around the island hold their own history. The underwater archaeologist E. Lee Spence, who lived on the island, is credited with locating many shipwrecks along its shores in the 1960s and 1970s, including the Civil War blockade runners Flora, Beatrice, Stono, Flamingo, Prince Albert and Celt. In 1981, novelist and marine archaeologist Clive Cussler and his National Underwater and Marine Agency are credited with finding the wreck of the blockade runner Raccoon off the island. The sea can also turn on the island. Hurricane Hugo came ashore at Sullivan's Island around midnight on September 22, 1989, and its eye passed directly over the island. The Ben Sawyer Bridge broke free of its locks, one end in the water and the other pointing at the sky. Today the island turns up in song as well as story, named in Jason Isbell's "Palmetto Rose" on his 2015 album Something More Than Free.

## Flight Context

Sullivan's Island is centered near 32.7633°N, 79.8378°W, a narrow barrier island on the north side of the entrance to Charleston Harbor. From 1,500–3,000 feet along the beach, look for Fort Moultrie about 1 nm west-southwest at the island's harbor end, and the modern Charleston Light near the south shore about 0.4 nm southwest. Fort Sumter sits in the harbor mouth about 2 nm west-southwest, and the Charleston peninsula and the Arthur Ravenel Jr. Bridge lie about 4.5 nm west and west-northwest across the harbor. Breach Inlet separates the island from the Isle of Palms to the east, and the Ben Sawyer Bridge crosses to Mount Pleasant on the north. Nearby airports: Mount Pleasant Regional-Faison Field (KLRO) about 8.5 nm north-northeast, Charleston Executive Airport (KJZI) on Johns Island about 9 nm west-southwest, and Charleston International (KCHS) about 13 nm northwest. Expect sea breezes, beach haze and summer afternoon thunderstorms; Hurricane Hugo showed how exposed this coast is to tropical storms.
