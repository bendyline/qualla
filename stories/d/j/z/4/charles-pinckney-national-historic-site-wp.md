# Charles Pinckney National Historic Site

The surviving heart of Snee Farm in Mount Pleasant honors Constitution framer Charles Pinckney, though its house was built after his time and its richest evidence lies in the ground, in more than 173,000 artifacts that include pottery made by enslaved people.

## Introduction

The National Park Service site that bears Charles Pinckney's name does not contain a single building Charles Pinckney would have known. The house at the center of Snee Farm was built in 1828, after the family had lost the property, by a planter who tore down everything standing before him. What survives of the Pinckney years lies underground and in documents, pieced together through archaeology and research. It is a quiet place to remember a Founding Father who helped draft the Constitution, and an unusually honest one about how much of the past is gone.

## A Framer's Country Retreat

Colonel Charles Pinckney acquired Snee Farm in 1754 from the widow Ann (Scott) Allen and her second husband, John Savage, a Charleston merchant. He developed its 715 acres for the commodity crops of rice and indigo, and he left it to his son, also named Charles, who inherited it in 1782. The younger Pinckney, born in 1757, came from a prominent South Carolina political family. He fought in the American Revolutionary War, was captured when the British took Charleston in 1780 and held as a prisoner until 1781, when he was sent north for a possible exchange, and returned to the state in 1783. As a delegate to the Constitutional Convention, he contributed to drafting the United States Constitution, and he later served as governor. Snee Farm was both his working plantation and his country estate, conveniently reachable from Charleston by boat. Its work was done by enslaved people: the 1790 census recorded Pinckney as enslaving 111 people, about 40 of them at Snee Farm. Debt caught up with him, and around 1816 he placed the property in trust to settle what he owed. He died in 1824.

## Mathews's House and the Enslaved Quarters

Francis G. Deliesselines acquired the property in 1817 and had it surveyed. In 1828, William Mathews, another Lowcountry planter, bought Snee Farm, demolished the existing buildings and built a new main house that year, apparently on the site of the original foundations. That house, the Coastal Cottage, is typical of its time: rectangular, with a side-gable roof, a full-width front porch and a brick pier foundation, and inside, elaborate molding, paneling and some refined spaces for entertaining. Period maps show a line of cabins for enslaved workers perpendicular to the main road, though archaeology suggests the quarters were more loosely clustered than a neat row. Mathews owned other plantations as well and held a total of 352 enslaved people across them. He kept Snee Farm into 1848. The cottage with its refined rooms and the row of cabins for enslaved workers were two halves of the same place, and neither can be understood without the other.

## Saved From the Subdivisions

For well over a century after Mathews, the 715-acre estate and its outbuildings stayed largely intact. That changed in the 1970s, when the owners subdivided the land to profit from development spreading out from Charleston. Preservationists stepped in and in 1988 bought the central portion of the estate, including the house. After Congress passed enabling legislation, the National Park Service acquired it. Snee Farm had already been designated a National Historic Landmark in 1973, and in 1988 it became a National Historic Site. Today the grounds include the main house, a barn, a corncrib and a caretaker's residence, set among wooded and swampy tracts to the east and west and a manicured lawn with ornamental plantings around the house. A marble cenotaph, a twentieth-century reproduction of one the younger Pinckney designed, honors his father, Colonel Charles Pinckney; the original is now at Christ Church in Mount Pleasant.

## What the Ground Gives Back

Because no Pinckney-era structures remain, the family's story here comes from archaeology and documents, and the museum on the site makes that work visible. Its collection spans almost 200 years of Snee Farm, from the Pinckneys through the later owners, and holds more than 173,000 objects. Among them are artifacts of plantation life: 18th- and 19th-century tableware, and pottery made by enslaved people. Those pieces matter. Shaped by the hands of people held in bondage, the pottery sits in the same collection as the dishes and household goods of the plantation, and together they tell Snee Farm's story from more than one side. A visit here tells the story of a framer of the Constitution, and, alongside it, the story of the people whose forced labor made plantations like this one run.

## Flight Context

The Charles Pinckney National Historic Site lies at about 32.8463 N, 79.8247 W at 1254 Long Point Road in Mount Pleasant, about 10 miles northeast of downtown Charleston on Wando Neck, the peninsula formed where the Wando and Cooper rivers meet. The historic site is small and surrounded by suburban development, so look for its patch of lawn and woods amid the subdivisions; the cable-stayed Arthur Ravenel Jr. Bridge over the Cooper River is about 5 nm to the west-southwest. A viewing altitude of 1,500 to 2,000 feet works well. Mount Pleasant Regional Airport (KLRO) is only about 4 nm northeast, so expect traffic there; Charleston International Airport (KCHS) is about 11 nm west-northwest and Charleston Executive Airport (KJZI) about 13 nm southwest. Expect summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms.
