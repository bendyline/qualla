# Circular Congregational Church

Founded by the dissenters of 1680s Charles Towne and the reason Meeting Street has its name, Circular Congregational Church survived mockery, fire and war, and still worships on its original site in a cloverleaf church built partly from the bricks of its predecessor.

## Introduction

"Charleston is a pious place / And full of pious people / They built a church on Meeting Street / But could not raise the steeple." For a time the rhyme dogged the congregation of Circular Church, whose great round sanctuary stood without a spire. Then, in the late 1830s (1838, by most accounts), they answered it. The members raised a New England-style steeple that towered 182 feet above Meeting Street, and the laughter stopped. It was one chapter in the long life of a congregation as old as the city itself, one that gave the street outside its name.

## The Dissenters' Meeting House

The congregation was established in 1681 and co-founded with Charles Towne itself in the years between 1680 and 1685 by English Congregationalists, Scots Presbyterians and French Huguenots among the original settlers. These dissenters built a Meeting House in the northwest corner of the walled city, and the present sanctuary occupies that exact site. The road leading to it was called Meeting House Street, later shortened to Meeting Street. The early church was comfortable with more than one identity. It suffered itself to be called Presbyterian, Congregational or Independent, sometimes by one of those names, sometimes by two, and at other times by all three. That flexibility has never entirely left. Centuries later the church joined the Congregational Association in 1882, its successor the United Church of Christ in 1954 and the Presbyterian Atlantic Presbytery in 1968, and it remains one of the few congregations in the South to belong to two denominations.

## A Pantheon on Meeting Street

By 1804 the congregation needed a larger building. Martha Laurens Ramsay proposed a circular form, and the architect Robert Mills completed the plans. The result was a Pantheon-type building 88 feet in diameter, with seven great doors and 26 windows, and it gave the congregation the name it still carries. The missing steeple inspired the mocking rhyme until a steeple, added in 1838 by most accounts, settled the matter. The decades from 1820 to 1860 were the church's glory days. It had a large congregation of white and Black members, and in 1816, reportedly, it started the first Sunday School for religious education in South Carolina. Members founded the Charleston Bible Society, described as a prototype of the later American Bible Society. Among them were two governors of the state, prominent senators and the editor of the Charleston Courier. The congregation also included many enslaved people and poor whites whose names are now lost, their faith as much a part of the church as that of its famous members.

## Fire, War and Ruins

The round church did not last. On December 11, 1861, a fire broke out near the Cooper River and swept all the way across the city during the night, gutting the building and leaving only its brick walls standing. The Civil War followed with its own devastation. Photographs of the ruins in 1865 still show the clear circular layout of Mills's design. In 1867, with the war over, the church's Black members withdrew to form their own congregation, Plymouth Congregational Church. After the 1886 earthquake made the ruined walls unsafe, the remaining congregation began removing the materials of the old church in January 1888, and the new sanctuary was dedicated in 1892 (one account gives 1890).

## Not Circular, Still Circular

The building that stands today is the fourth house of worship on the site, designed in the Richardsonian Romanesque style by Stephenson & Greene of New York. It is not actually round. Its plan is a modified cloverleaf, yet everyone still calls it the Circular Church, and it was built using bricks from the earlier building, so the old sanctuary lives on in its walls. The parish house, with its twin stairways and wrought iron railings, was declared a National Historic Landmark in 1973. Today the congregation is international and multiracial, and it follows a progressive Christianity. Its members have founded the city's first marriage and family counseling center, Charleston's crisis intervention hotline, Hospice of Charleston and the Elder Shelter, and the church has given space to groups such as Alcoholics Anonymous and the Charleston Interfaith Crisis Ministry. The church says it is open to visitors when tour guides are available, and its historic graveyard holds graves dating to 1695.

## Flight Context

Circular Congregational Church stands at about 32.7789 N, 79.9311 W at 150 Meeting Street in the heart of Charleston's historic district, directly beside the Gibbes Museum of Art. St. Philip's Church lies about a tenth of a nautical mile east and the Fireproof Building a similar distance south, so the cluster makes a compact target. Rainbow Row, the line of colorful houses on East Bay Street, is about a quarter of a nautical mile to the southeast, and the cable-stayed Arthur Ravenel Jr. Bridge over the Cooper River rises about 1.6 nm to the north-northeast. A viewing altitude of 1,500 to 2,500 feet shows the downtown grid well. Charleston Executive Airport (KJZI) is about 6 nm southwest, Charleston International Airport (KCHS) about 9 nm northwest and Mount Pleasant Regional Airport (KLRO) about 10 nm northeast; downtown lies within busy Charleston airspace. Expect summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms.
