# Kahal Kadosh Beth Elohim

Founded in 1749 and rebuilt after the fire of 1838, Charleston's Kahal Kadosh Beth Elohim worships in a white Greek Revival temple on Hasell Street, a landmark of early American Reform Judaism that now openly acknowledges the enslaved people who built it.

## Introduction

In 1838, fire tore through Charleston's central business district. It destroyed more than 1,100 structures, and among the losses was the synagogue of Kahal Kadosh Beth Elohim, a Georgian-style building that had served the congregation for more than four decades. Within a few years, a new house of worship stood at 90 Hasell Street: a gleaming white Greek Revival temple fronted by six fluted Doric columns. It is still there, and still in use. Behind that serene portico lies a deeply layered chapter of American Jewish history, a story of Sephardi merchants from London, early experiments in reform, and the enslaved workers whose labor raised the building.

## Holy Congregation, House of God

The name means "Holy Congregation House of God," and the congregation behind it was founded in 1749, making it one of the oldest Jewish congregations in the United States. Its founding members were Sephardi Jews of Spanish and Portuguese origin who came to Charleston from London. For decades they worshiped according to Portuguese Sephardic ritual. The congregation began as Orthodox Sephardic. Its first purpose-built synagogue, in the Georgian style, went up between 1792 and 1794 and gave the community a permanent home in the city. That building stood until the fire of 1838 swept it away along with hundreds of other structures in the heart of the city.

## An Experiment in Reform

In 1824, a group of Portuguese Jews in Charleston founded the Reformed Society of Israelites, a splinter group originally led by Isaac Harby. The society borrowed ideas from the European Reform movement and contributed ideas of its own to the later, much wider American Reform movement, yet it remained distinct from both, with a Reform prayer book of its own, the first in America. Beth Elohim later reabsorbed the splinter group and adopted a reformed ritual. That history has made the congregation nationally significant as the place where ideas resembling Reform Judaism first appeared in the United States. It is sometimes called the originator of American Reform Judaism, though the movement as it spread across the country was built later, largely by immigrants from Germany. Charleston's contribution came first, and it was distinctly its own, a homegrown attempt to reshape an ancient tradition.

## The Temple on Hasell Street

The synagogue, built between 1839 and 1841, was designed in the Greek Revival style, with the New York architect Cyrus L. Warner credited as architect of record and the design itself credited to Tappan and Noble. It is a single-story brick building on a raised granite foundation, its brick stuccoed, painted white and scored to resemble blocks of stone. Six fluted Doric columns, equally spaced and made of stucco over molded brick, form a portico that approximates the order of the Greek Theseion and carries a gabled pediment. From the street the result reads as cut stone, though it was made from humbler materials. The building is the second oldest synagogue building in the United States and the oldest in continuous use. The congregation also owns the Coming Street Cemetery, listed separately on the National Register of Historic Places and described as the oldest Jewish burial ground in the South.

## The Hands That Built It

The white temple was raised by people who had no choice in the work. Enslaved people of African descent, working for the builder David Lopez Jr, a prominent slaveowner and later a defender of the Confederacy, built the synagogue; only two of its enslaved carpenters, Kit and George, are known by name. That labor is now acknowledged on the site itself. In September 2021 the congregation installed a brick monument outside the sanctuary, with an inscription recognizing the forced labor of the enslaved Black workers who constructed it. Rabbi Stephanie Alexander explained the decision plainly: "We're being honest and transparent about what has enabled us to come together and has enabled us to come to this space." Other reminders of the South's contested memory remain inside. According to a guide from the Jewish Historical Society of South Carolina, a mural in the synagogue includes a Jewish Confederate soldier seated with a broken sword, an image shaped by the Lost Cause view of the Confederacy. It hangs in the same building whose builders were enslaved.

## Pulpits and Conscience

The twentieth century brought its own tests. Rabbi Jacob S. Raisin led the congregation from 1915 to 1944, nearly three decades at the pulpit. In 1961, Rabbi Burton Padoll took the pulpit and became an outspoken activist for the rights of African Americans. His stand came at a cost. Prominent members of the congregation objected to his support for the civil rights movement, and he left Charleston in 1967, reportedly forced to resign. Read together, these episodes make Beth Elohim more than a handsome landmark. The congregation has carried the full weight of Charleston's history, including its ambitions and its injustices, for more than two and a half centuries, and in recent years it has chosen to name that history openly rather than let the white columns speak for it alone.

## Flight Context

Kahal Kadosh Beth Elohim stands at 32.7822°N, 79.9328°W on Hasell Street in the Charleston peninsula, where the Ashley and Cooper Rivers meet to form Charleston Harbor. From 1,500–2,500 feet, orient on Marion Square, the open green about 0.3 nm northwest, and the long rooflines of the City Market a couple of blocks to the south. The diamond-shaped towers of the cable-stayed Arthur Ravenel Jr. Bridge rise about 1.5 nm northeast, and the Battery marks the peninsula's southern tip about 0.8 nm south. The synagogue's white portico is small from the air, so use these larger landmarks to find the block. Nearby airports: Charleston Executive Airport on Johns Island (KJZI) about 6 nm southwest, Charleston International (KCHS) about 9 nm northwest, and Mount Pleasant Regional-Faison Field (KLRO) about 10 nm northeast.
