# Joseph Manigault House

Designed in 1803 by Gabriel Manigault for his brother, this refined Adam-style house nearly fell to a gas station in 1920, sparked the founding of the Preservation Society of Charleston, and served coffee and donuts to troops before becoming a museum.

**Location:** 32.7886, -79.9355

## Introduction

In 1920, the threat to an exquisitely detailed Charleston house was a gas station. The Joseph Manigault House, a three-story brick residence at the corner of Meeting and John Streets, faced demolition so that motorists could fill their tanks. Its slender pilasters, bowed porch and delicately carved mantels would have been swept away. Instead, a group of Charlestonians organized to stop it, and the organization they formed became the Preservation Society of Charleston. The house survived that scare, a tax auction, years of empty rooms and a wartime stint serving coffee and donuts to service members. Today the Charleston Museum owns and operates it as a historic house museum.

## A House Designed by a Brother

The house was built in 1803, and it was a family project. Gabriel Manigault designed it as the home of his brother, Joseph. Gabriel's hand shows elsewhere in the city too: he is also credited with designing the building that Charleston took over in 1818 and has used as its City Hall ever since. For Joseph, he produced a three-story brick house set on a raised brick foundation, with a main facade organized around a two-story porch spanning its center three bays. Elaborate doorways on both floors are framed by slender pilasters and sidelight windows. The sides of the house are just as inventive. A semicircular stairwell projects from one sidewall, and a bowed porch swells from the other, giving the building the rough shape of a parallelogram. Walk around it and the house refuses to be a simple box.

## The Adam Touch

The Joseph Manigault House is nationally significant as a well-executed and well-preserved example of Adam style architecture, and it was declared a National Historic Landmark in 1973. The style takes its name from Robert Adam, who promoted a distinction in scale between the details of domestic architecture and those of civic buildings. In a house, ornament should be delicate. That idea comes to life inside. The fireplace mantels, the moulding around doors and windows, and the cornices all display delicately refined woodwork, scaled for rooms where people lived rather than for halls of state. The effect is lightness. Where a courthouse might press down with heavy classical details, the Manigault interiors feel finely drawn, almost like engravings translated into wood. It is exactly the quality that made the house worth fighting for when demolition loomed.

## Saved from the Gas Pumps

The 1920 fight over the house became a founding moment for Charleston preservation, since the group that organized to save it grew into the Preservation Society of Charleston. The victory was not clean. A series of complicated legal entanglements followed, and in 1933 the house was auctioned for non-payment of taxes. Rescue came from Princess Henrietta Guerard (Pollitzer) Hartford Pignatelli of Wando Plantation, the former wife of A&P heir Edward Hartford. She became interested in saving the house, bought it, and presented it to the Charleston Museum. E. Milby Burton, then the museum's director, completed the picture by persuading Standard Oil Company to donate the garden property for restoration. It was a fitting twist. An oil company, the kind of business that had once threatened the house, handed back the ground around it.

## Coffee, Donuts and a Dormitory

Ownership did not bring money, and the house sat idle for lack of funds. Then World War II gave it a new purpose. The U.S.O. occupied and restored the building, and each floor took on a wartime job. The first floor served coffee and donuts, the second floor was given over to recreation, and the third became a dormitory. Service members passing through Charleston ate and slept among Adam-style mantels and cornices designed for a Manigault household nearly a century and a half earlier. After the war, the house finally became what its rescuers had intended. It opened to the public in 1948, and over the next thirty years furnishings were added slowly, piece by piece. The Joseph Manigault House is now one of the city's major attractions.
