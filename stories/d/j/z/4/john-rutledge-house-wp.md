# John Rutledge House

Built in 1763 for the lawyer who would lead South Carolina through the Revolution and sign the U.S. Constitution, the John Rutledge House on Broad Street now serves afternoon tea in its ballroom as a historic inn.

**Location:** 32.7761, -79.9336

## Introduction

Breakfast at 116 Broad Street is served in the ballroom of a house built for a signer of the United States Constitution. The John Rutledge House, completed in 1763 by an architect whose name has been lost, spent more than two centuries as a family home, a remodeled mansion and a law office before it began welcoming overnight guests. Its stuccoed walls, corner quoins and ornate iron balcony make it one of the handsome faces of Broad Street. Behind that facade sits the story of John Rutledge, the lawyer who helped organize South Carolina's Patriot forces, governed the state through much of the Revolutionary War and later put his name to the nation's founding law.

## A Young Lawyer's Address

When the house went up in 1763, it was a two-story structure built for John Rutledge, who had by then already established a successful law practice. Broad Street suited him. The house stands on the north side of the street, opposite its junction with Orange Street, and faces the Edward Rutledge House, the home of John's brother. Two brothers from one Charleston family would each leave a house on the same stretch of road, and both would become part of the city's historic record. The builder of John's house remains unknown, but the work has lasted. Today the house rises from a raised basement to a hip roof with a front-facing gable. Its walls are stuccoed and its corners marked by quoining, giving it the settled formality of a prosperous town house.

## The Governor at War

Rutledge's legal career soon gave way to something larger. During the American Revolutionary War he played a significant role in organizing South Carolina's Patriot forces, and he served as the state's executive for much of the conflict. For a Broad Street lawyer, it was an extraordinary burden: steering a state through war as its chief executive. His service did not end with independence. In 1787 Rutledge attended the Constitutional Convention, and he is among the signers of the United States Constitution. That signature places him among the Founding Fathers and gives his Broad Street home its national significance. In 1973 the house was declared a National Historic Landmark, honoring the man who lived there as much as the building itself.

## A Third Story and an Iron Balcony

The house did not stay in the Rutledge family. After it passed to other owners, Thomas M. Gadsden enlarged it in 1853 by adding a third story, turning the original two-story house into the tall building seen today, made taller still by its raised basement. The front facade is distinguished by an ornate two-story wrought-iron balcony, believed to have been made by Christopher Werner, the German-American master ironworker whose gates also guard Hibernian Hall, a short walk away on Meeting Street. The iron gives the facade its lace-like character, a contrast with the solid stucco behind it. In the 20th century the house returned, in a way, to its first owner's profession. It served as a law office, and lawyers once again worked in the rooms where Rutledge had lived.

## Afternoon Tea on Broad Street

In 1989 the house was renovated and opened to the public as the John Rutledge House Inn. It now offers 19 guest rooms, ranging from standard hotel rooms to large suites. Eleven are in the main house, and the remaining eight occupy the carriage houses at the rear of the property. The ballroom is used for afternoon tea as well as breakfast. The inn has received a four-diamond rating from AAA and belongs to the Select Registry and Historic Hotels of America. For visitors, the effect is a rare kind of access. Many historic homes are seen from behind velvet ropes. Here, a guest can sleep under the roof of a Constitution signer and take afternoon tea in the house of the man who governed South Carolina through the Revolution.
