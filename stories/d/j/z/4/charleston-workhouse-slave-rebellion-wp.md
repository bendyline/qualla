# Charleston Workhouse Slave Rebellion

On July 13, 1849, an enslaved plasterer named Nicholas Kelly led an uprising inside Charleston's Workhouse, a city-run prison and slave market, freeing some three dozen people for a few desperate hours; a plaque now marks the vacant lot on Magazine Street where it happened.

## Introduction

On the morning of July 13, 1849, a slave trader named John M. Gilchrist walked into the Charleston Workhouse to take custody of an enslaved woman, believed to have been close kin of a man imprisoned there named Nicholas (accounts differ on whether she was his sister or his wife). Nicholas refused to let it happen quietly. "There [will] be war today," he told the people around him, calling on "every man, who called himself a man, to be a man." By the afternoon, the Workhouse courtyard had become the scene of what historians regard as the most significant slave uprising in South Carolina since the Denmark Vesey Conspiracy of 1822.

## The Building on Magazine Street

The Charleston Workhouse stood on Magazine Street in downtown Charleston. It was a prison and a slave market, a place where enslavers confined Black people to be sold or punished, and where they paid the city a fee to have the people they enslaved tortured. Guards carried out public floggings and locked victims in the stocks. They also forced people onto the treadmill, a huge corn-grinding device that worked like a stepping machine, and made them walk it until they collapsed from exhaustion or fell and were hurt. The Workhouse is often confused with the Old Charleston Jail next door, which shared notable architectural features, but the two operated independently. Every person held there had a name, a family and a life far larger than the uses enslavers made of them.

## A Plasterer's Broken Bargain

Nicholas was born into slavery in 1822 or 1823 in the St. Andrews District, a few miles north of Charleston. His mother, Ellen, was an enslaved woman with Native American ancestry; his father was her enslaver, an unnamed planter. At eight years old Nicholas was hired out to William Kelly, an Irish-American building contractor from Philadelphia, who trained him as a plasterer and bought him a few years later. In 1845 Kelly took him to New Orleans, where Nicholas earned a stellar reputation as a skilled artisan and attended a Roman Catholic church. He was fiercely independent, and in 1846 a court sentenced him to 25 lashes for striking a white man. Kelly offered to let him buy his freedom for $1,000, and Nicholas later said he had paid at least $200 or $300. Then, in 1847, Kelly forcibly brought him back to Charleston. Newspapers speculated that Kelly had cheated him out of the bargain, and Kelly publicly denied it. During a sixteen-month incarceration in the Workhouse, Nicholas worked as a cook and overseer and was given special privileges.

## War in the Courtyard

When Gilchrist came for the woman, Nicholas and others resisted. The insurgents took over the Workhouse courtyard and armed themselves with whatever they could find: sledgehammer handles, pickaxes, axes and hammers. At about two o'clock, the Workhouse keeper, James C. Norris, entered the courtyard with Mayor Hutchinson and three white guards and tried to seize Nicholas. He fought back, and twenty other enslaved people came to his defense. In the brawl that followed, about half a dozen white men were seriously wounded. In the confusion, some thirty-seven enslaved people (other counts say 35 or 36) scattered and ran for freedom. For many, liberty lasted only hours. Eighteen were captured within a day, Nicholas among them. He had made it only one mile from the Workhouse when militiamen with rifles forced him to surrender on Cannon's Bridge.

## Judgment, Fear and a Plaque

Punishment came fast. On July 14, Nicholas stood before the Charleston Court of Magistrates and Freeholders and was sentenced to death. His two lieutenants, George Holmes and John Toomer, were tried on July 16 and also condemned. On July 20 all three men were publicly hanged, and even in death they were denied dignity: their bodies were sent to a nearby medical college for dissection. Six freedom seekers evaded capture long enough for the governor to offer a $600 reward. Three of them, Edward Farrar, Adam Kelly and Edward Graves, reached the area near Kingstree before they were caught on July 24, and all six were later sentenced to flogging and solitary confinement. The uprising frightened white South Carolina. Vigilance and safety committees formed across the state to control the enslaved population and root out abolitionists, and the alarm fed the state's secession crisis of 1850 and 1851. On July 13, 2022, the City of Charleston, at Mayor John Tecklenburg's request, installed a commemorative plaque at the vacant lot on Magazine Street where the Workhouse once stood.

## Flight Context

The site of the Charleston Workhouse lies at about 32.7786 N, 79.9372 W on Magazine Street in downtown Charleston, now a vacant lot marked by a commemorative plaque, right beside the Old Charleston Jail. Nothing of the Workhouse survives to be seen from above, so orient on nearby features: Colonial Lake lies about a third of a nautical mile to the west-southwest, Marion Square about half a nautical mile north, and the cable-stayed Arthur Ravenel Jr. Bridge over the Cooper River about 2 nm northeast.
