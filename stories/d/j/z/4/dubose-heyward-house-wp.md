# Dubose Heyward House

A modest two-story house at 76 Church Street in Charleston, home from 1919 to 1924 to DuBose Heyward, the author of Porgy, is a National Historic Landmark because of the writer who lived there rather than the building itself.

**Location:** 32.7739, -79.9289

**Categories:** National Historic Landmarks in South Carolina, Houses in Charleston, South Carolina, Houses on the National Register of Historic Places in South Carolina, Houses completed in 1919, National Register of Historic Places in Charleston, South Carolina

## Introduction

Architecturally, the house at 76 Church Street is not distinguished. It is a modest two-story building with a tile roof and a stuccoed exterior, and it no longer even stands entirely on its own: it has become a wing of the larger house next door. Yet in 1971 it was declared a National Historic Landmark. The reason is the man who lived here from 1919 to 1924, DuBose Heyward, whose novel Porgy grew out of this stretch of Charleston and went on to become Porgy and Bess.

## A Modest House on Church Street

The Heyward House sits in the Charleston Historic District on the east side of Church Street. It is smaller than the three-story house beside it, and its most significant changes came after Heyward's time. The house is now attached as a wing to the three-story building immediately to its left, and walls between the two were removed to create enlarged dining rooms and bedrooms. What remains recognizable is the simple two-story form under its tile roof and stucco walls. It is the kind of building a passerby could easily walk past without a second look, which makes its landmark status all the more telling: the history here is not in the architecture but in the work of the person who lived inside.

## A Family Fallen on Hard Times

DuBose Heyward was born in 1885 into an aristocratic Charleston family, but the family had fallen on hard times, and as a young man he worked a series of low-wage jobs. Writing became his way forward. He emerged as a significant early figure in the Southern literary revival of the early 1920s, and in 1920, while living in this house, he helped found the Poetry Society of South Carolina with Hervey Allen and John Bennett. Those were years of apprenticeship and ambition in a modest home, a short walk from the old buildings that would soon find their way into his fiction. The street outside his door was about to become literary territory.

## Up the Street to Catfish Row

Just up Church Street, at numbers 89 and 91, stands Cabbage Row, a pair of pre-Revolutionary buildings that became the model for Catfish Row in Heyward's most famous book. Porgy was published in 1925 by the George H. Doran Company. Set in the Black tenements of 1920s Charleston, its title character was based on a real Charlestonian, Samuel Smalls, and in some passages its characters speak Gullah, the creole language that developed among enslaved African Americans. One biographer called it "the first major Southern novel to portray African Americans without condescension," though later critics have noted that its Black characters still reflect the stereotypes and the primitivism fashionable in the 1920s. In 1927 Heyward and his wife, the playwright Dorothy Heyward, adapted it for the stage, and in 1935 it became George Gershwin's folk opera Porgy and Bess, cementing the character's place in American culture.

## A Landmark for a Writer

Heyward died in 1940, and in 1971 the house where he lived during those formative Charleston years was declared a National Historic Landmark. The designation honors a literary life rather than a building. Visitors who stand on Church Street today can trace, in a single short walk, the geography behind Porgy and Bess: the modest house where the author lived, and a little way up the street, the old row that he transformed into Catfish Row. The characters were fiction, but the neighborhood, and the Charlestonians like Samuel Smalls who inspired them, were real.

## Flight Context

The Dubose Heyward House stands at about 32.7739 N, 79.9289 W at 76 Church Street near the southern end of the Charleston peninsula, a short walk south of Cabbage Row. The house is far too small to pick out from the air, so orient on landmarks: Rainbow Row, the line of colorful historic houses on East Bay Street, is a few blocks to the northeast, and the cable-stayed Arthur Ravenel Jr. Bridge over the Cooper River rises about 2 nm to the north-northeast.
