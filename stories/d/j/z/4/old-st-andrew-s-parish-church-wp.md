# Old St. Andrew's Parish Church

Built in 1706 on the west bank of the Ashley River, Old St. Andrew's is billed as the oldest surviving church building south of Virginia, and it has outlasted fire, war, earthquake, hurricane and more than half a century of silence.

**Location:** 32.8383, -80.0497

**Categories:** Churches, Colonial architecture, Charleston, South Carolina, National Register of Historic Places, History of slavery in South Carolina, Religious history

## Introduction

Near the end of the Reverend Ebenezer Taylor's troubled tenure, the churchwardens and vestry of St. Andrew's Parish Church nailed the windows shut and locked the door, so that their own rector could not get in to lead worship. In 1717 he was banished to North Carolina. The little brick church he was shut out of still stands on the west bank of the Ashley River, in what is now Charleston. Built in 1706, it is billed as the oldest surviving church building south of Virginia, and its congregation still worships there.

## The Great Door and the Small Door

The Church Act of 1706 made the Church of England Carolina's official, tax-supported religion and divided the colony into ten parishes. St. Andrew's served the people along the Ashley, across roughly 280 square miles that included the spot where English settlers first landed in 1670, today's Charles Towne Landing. The first church was modest: brick walls forty feet by twenty-five, a pine roof, five small square windows and two doors that sorted worshippers by rank. The wide "great" door, facing the river, was for the gentry; a narrower door facing west served commoners and clergy. It is likely that enslaved Africans built it. Taylor, a Presbyterian recruited into Anglican orders in the hope he would draw local Presbyterians into the fold, instead alienated the Anglicans he was sent to serve. His ministry to enslaved people on the parish plantations, which few ministers then undertook, was one more sore point.

## Brick Arms of a Cross

Taylor's successor, the Reverend William Guy, arrived in 1718 and served until his death in 1750, a beloved rector in an age when illness and transfer usually cycled clergy through quickly. He began the parish register and opened a chapel on James Island. Beginning in 1723 and continuing for more than a decade, the rectangular church grew into the shape of a cross, tripling its floor space. Visitors still see that work in the chancel and transept at the east end, compass-headed windows recessed into the walls, a barrel-vaulted ceiling over the crossing and three doors of equal size. The walls were stuccoed to hide the seam between old and new brick and suggest grander stone. Today it is the only remaining colonial cruciform church in South Carolina. A fire in the early 1760s was severe enough to consume anything wooden, leaving only the 1706 and 1723 walls standing. Parishioners paid to reopen it in 1764, adding a costly reredos whose tablets carry the Lord's Prayer, the Ten Commandments and the Apostles' Creed.

## Rice, Indigo and the People Who Grew Them

The wealth behind those pews came from rice, and none of it would have been possible without enslaved labor. In 1728 Guy estimated the parish held 800 free whites and 1,800 enslaved Black people. Then Eliza Lucas Pinckney perfected indigo cultivation at her father's plantation on Wappoo Creek, inside the parish, and rice and indigo together made St. Andrew's one of the wealthiest areas in colonial British North America. By 1777 the enslaved population had reached 3,460 people. Three years later war arrived at the churchyard gate. In March 1780, British and Hessian troops met colonial cannon fire at Church Creek, just south of the church. Hessian Captain Johann Ewald was sent across to silence the guns before heavier cannon had to be brought up, fire that might have destroyed the church, but his men bogged down in the mud and the colonials escaped. The invaders camped in the churchyard overnight, then went on to capture Charles Town. They left the church "much Injured and pulled to pieces," and burned the parsonage.

## Eating Dinner From His Pocket

The Reverend John Grimké Drayton, born a Grimké, changed his surname in 1836 to inherit Magnolia plantation. A wealthy planter, noted horticulturalist and devoted priest, he served St. Andrew's for forty years, longer than any other rector. The Civil War left the parish devastated; one observer counted only three residences between the Ashley and Stono rivers. Walking the parish in 1867, Drayton met a group of freedmen who asked him to restart religious services. Nowhere else in South Carolina did freedmen reunite with their white prewar minister. He preached to overflowing congregations, and though he was in his sixties and living with tuberculosis, he walked sixteen to eighteen miles on Sundays from Charleston to the chapels, "eating my dinner from my pocket." Black communicants now outnumbered white ones eight to one, some arriving by steamer at Bees Ferry and walking on. As Drayton's health failed, that congregation took up its own leadership at Magwood's Chapel, which became St. Andrew's Mission Church.

## Fifty-Seven Quiet Years

Drayton was never replaced. The church stood dormant for fifty-seven years, a historical curiosity pictured on postcards and documented for the Historic American Buildings Survey in 1940. It reopened in 1948. In the postwar years termites closed it again for almost two years of repairs, and when workers stripped the old plaster they found, on the north wall of the nave a pew plan inscribed by Colonel William Izard Bull during his 1855 restoration, hidden for almost a century. The electricians installing the church's first lights etched their own names above it. Hurricane Hugo spared the church in 1989 but toppled more than 200 trees in the churchyard. As the tercentennial approached, engineers found the gabled walls tilting outward, and repairs estimated at $1.2 million closed the building for about a year before it reopened on Easter Day 2005. It remains an active parish; in 2013 it left the Episcopal Church to align with the Anglican Diocese of South Carolina, which belongs to the Anglican Church in North America.

## Flight Context

Old St. Andrew's stands at 32.838N, 80.050W on the west bank of the Ashley River in West Ashley, Charleston. From 1,500-2,000 feet, follow the winding Ashley northwest from the Charleston peninsula; the small church and its wooded churchyard sit just inland of the river, with Charles Towne Landing a few miles downstream toward the city and Drayton Hall, Magnolia and Middleton Place strung along the river upstream. Charleston International (KCHS) is only about 3.5 nm north, so expect busy airspace and arriving traffic overhead; Charleston Executive (KJZI) on Johns Island is about 9 nm south-southeast. Summer haze over the marshes can flatten contrast.
