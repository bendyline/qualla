# Charleston Library Society

Born in 1748 when a group of young Charleston gentlemen pooled their money to buy the latest periodicals and books from England, the Charleston Library Society has survived fire, war and hurricane to become, by common ranking, the third oldest subscription library in the United States.

**Location:** 32.7783, -79.9328

**Categories:** Libraries in South Carolina, Subscription libraries in the United States, Buildings and structures in Charleston, South Carolina, Education in Charleston, South Carolina, 18th century in Charleston, South Carolina

## Introduction

In 1748, a group of young Charleston gentlemen agreed to pool their money for a simple luxury: the latest periodicals shipped from England. One account dates the first ten-pound subscriptions to June 13, 1748, with a decision to buy books as well by December; the Society itself marks its founding on December 28, 1748, and counts nineteen founders. That modest subscription became the Charleston Library Society, usually ranked the third oldest subscription library in the United States, preceded only by Benjamin Franklin's Library Company of Philadelphia, founded in 1731, and the Redwood Library and Athenaeum of Newport, Rhode Island, founded in 1747. It is older than the Providence Athenaeum, the New York Society Library and the Boston Athenaeum. Nearly three centuries on, members still pay a small subscription so that everyone can share the books it buys.

## A Charter Hard Won

Respectability took time. The founders sought a charter, and twice the Colonial Assembly adopted one, only for the Governor to refuse his signature both times. An appeal for aid to the Royal government in London was turned down as well, and the Society temporarily stopped its activities. It recovered by adding members, and in 1754 Governor Glen finally signed a bill chartering the Charles Town Library Society; the Crown ratified it in 1755. The Society was founded as a white-only institution. Its early shelves also reflected the limits of colonial life. Most books had to be purchased from England, because the American printing industry was still in its infancy in the 18th century.

## Seeds of a College and a Museum

The members soon looked beyond reading. In 1759 the Society began investigating the creation of an educational institution and started setting money aside each year for it, accumulating about 20,000 pounds sterling by 1778. The Society played a crucial role in the founding of the College of Charleston in 1770 and the Charleston Museum in 1773. For decades the collection itself had no fixed home. Elected librarians safeguarded the books in their own houses until 1792, when they moved to the upper floor of the Statehouse, today the Charleston County Courthouse. From 1835 to 1914 the Society occupied a building at the corner of Church and Broad Streets, 50 Broad, bought with the help of "brick" memberships, several of which Charleston families still use generations later.

## Fire and War

Twice, disaster reached the collection. On January 15, 1778, a fire destroyed all but about 185 of the 5,000 or 6,000 volumes the Society owned. The library rebuilt patiently and held 20,000 volumes by 1860. Then came the Civil War, and the collection was divided: much of it was sent to Columbia, to South Carolina College (today the University of South Carolina), for safekeeping, and the rest stayed in Charleston. Accounts differ on which part was lost. The South Carolina Encyclopedia says the books sent to Columbia were destroyed there in 1865, while Wikipedia says the small portion left behind was destroyed. In 1874 the Apprentices' Library Society merged into the Charleston Library Society, adding its own history to the one that had already survived so much.

## A Home on King Street

By 1910 the building at Broad and Church was too small, not fireproof and hemmed in by neighbors to the west and north. A gift from James Murdoch seeded a building fund, and the hunt began. A site on Washington Square fell through when it became clear the City would not give up part of the park, and lots on Meeting Street between Broad and Market proved too expensive. The leading option was to buy 59 Meeting Street, site of the Branford-Horry House, and sell off the house. The membership preferred King Street, raised $14,020 for it, and chose that location at a special meeting on May 13, 1910. In 1914 the Society moved into its Beaux Arts building at 164 King Street, designed by the Philadelphia architects McGoodwin and Hawley. It later added the Barnwell Annex next door in 1963 and, in the 1990s, the Carolina Rifles Armory of about 1888. Only the armory's facade had survived Hurricane Hugo, so the Society kept that, renamed the property the Ripley-Ravenel Building, and built fireproof storage behind it for its rarest and most valuable holdings.

## Menders of Old Books

A library this old needs doctors as well as readers. In December 2011 the Society opened Dorothy the Bookbinder's Bindery and Archival Lab (now called Dorothy's Book Bindery), which works alongside the Jerrold and Ann Mitchell Conservation Lab. From 2012 to 2016 its director, Brien Beidler, oversaw the repair and conservation of the collection and taught courses and workshops on the art of the book and traditional binding. Today its conservators stabilize the rarest volumes so that members, researchers and visitors can go on turning pages that have already outlasted a fire, a war and a hurricane.

## Flight Context

The Charleston Library Society stands at 164 King Street, 32.7783°N, 79.9328°W, in the historic heart of the Charleston peninsula between the Ashley and Cooper Rivers. Individual buildings are hard to pick out, so orient on the peninsula's grid of low rooftops and church steeples and the Battery seawall at its southern tip; 1,500–2,000 feet gives a good overview. Charleston Executive Airport (KJZI) is about 6 nm southwest, Charleston International Airport (KCHS) about 9 nm northwest, and Mount Pleasant Regional Airport (KLRO) about 10 nm northeast. Expect busy traffic around KCHS and frequent summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms.
