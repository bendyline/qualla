# Huguenot Church

Founded by French Protestant refugees in the 1680s, Charleston's Huguenot Church is the only independent Huguenot church in the United States, worshipping in a Gothic Revival sanctuary on Church Street with an 18th-century French liturgy spoken in English.

**Location:** 32.7805, -79.9292

**Categories:** Churches in Charleston County, South Carolina, Huguenot history in the United States, Gothic Revival church buildings in South Carolina, National Historic Landmarks in South Carolina, French-American culture in South Carolina

## Introduction

Every April, French returns to Church Street. Once a year the Huguenot Church at 136 Church Street in Charleston holds a service featuring readings in French from its liturgy, marking the month in 1598 when King Henry IV of France issued the Edict of Nantes and granted the Huguenots a measure of safety. For the rest of the year the words are spoken in English, but they are still the words of an 18th-century French liturgy. The congregation, also known as the French Protestant Church, traces its origins to the 1680s and is the only independent Huguenot church in the United States, a living link to a people who crossed an ocean to worship as they chose.

## Refugees of Conscience

The Huguenots were French Calvinists, Protestants in a predominantly Catholic kingdom, and through the 16th and 17th centuries they faced persecution. Some looked abroad early. In the 16th century Huguenots founded short-lived colonies such as Fort Caroline in Florida and Charlesfort in what is now South Carolina, and others settled in Britain, South Africa, New Netherlands and Virginia. The Edict of Nantes brought a reprieve in 1598, but in 1685 Louis XIV revoked it, and an exodus followed. Many of those refugees found their way to Charleston, where they formed the early congregation of this church, and their descendants went on to play a role in its affairs for many decades. In 1687 Elias Prioleau became the church's first regular pastor, and he served until his death in 1699.

## Blown Up to Save a City

The congregation has worshipped on the same ground through three buildings, and the first met a startling end. In 1796 city authorities blew it up in an effort to stop a spreading fire, sacrificing the church to save the streets around it. A simple brick church replaced it in 1800. That building was torn down in 1844 to make way for the present church, designed by the architect Edward Brickell White and completed the following year. It was the first Gothic Revival church built in Charleston, and it is often described as the oldest in South Carolina, which makes this small congregation the keeper of a first in the city's architecture as well as its religious history.

## An Organ That Answers Quickly

The sanctuary's voice arrived in 1845, when the church purchased an organ built by the New York organ maker Henry Erben, who lived from 1801 to 1883. It is a tracker organ, described by the church as Charleston's most historically intact working organ from the mid-19th century. In a tracker instrument, a mechanical linkage connects the keys to the pipe valves, so the organ responds to the organist faster than modern mechanisms do. Its tone is similar to that of organs used during the Baroque period, a fitting match for a congregation that has kept its liturgy close to its 18th-century roots. Long before the organ arrived, the refugees' music would have come from the Genevan Psalter, the Calvinist songbook whose metrical psalms, such as Psalm 25, "A toi, mon Dieu, mon cœur monte," were the kind of music sung in the congregation's earliest days.

## Old Words in a New Tongue

The church began affiliated with the Calvinist Reformed Church of France, and it still teaches Calvinist doctrine. Its services derive from liturgies developed by the churches of Neufchâtel and Vallangin, in editions of 1737 and 1772, and the congregation published its own English translations in the 19th century, adapted for worship in the United States. Today the church is non-denominational, governed by a board of directors and a body of elders. In the churchyard lies Peter Manigault, once reckoned the wealthiest man in the British North American colonies. On April 14, 1912, the church marked the 225th anniversary of its founding with commemorative addresses, and today the building is a National Historic Landmark, still used for the purpose its refugee founders intended.

## Flight Context

The Huguenot Church stands at 136 Church Street, 32.7805°N, 79.9292°W, on the Cooper River side of the Charleston peninsula, a short walk from St. Philip's Church, whose tall steeple is the easiest nearby landmark. From 1,500–2,000 feet the church itself is small and hard to isolate, so orient on St. Philip's and the line of Church Street. Charleston Executive Airport (KJZI) is about 6 nm southwest, Charleston International Airport (KCHS) about 9 nm northwest, and Mount Pleasant Regional Airport (KLRO) about 10 nm northeast. Watch for KCHS traffic; summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are common.
