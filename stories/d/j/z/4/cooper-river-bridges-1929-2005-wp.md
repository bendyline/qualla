# Cooper River Bridges (1929–2005)

For 76 years, a pair of steel cantilever bridges known for their dips, curves and narrow lanes carried Charleston across the Cooper River, through bankruptcy, a deadly ship collision and a hurricane, until the Ravenel Bridge replaced them.

**Location:** 32.8018, -79.9312

**Categories:** Demolished bridges in the United States, Bridges in Charleston, South Carolina, Former toll bridges in South Carolina, History of Charleston, South Carolina, U.S. Route 17

## Introduction

When the Cooper River Bridge opened in 1929, engineers and critics reached for colorful language. They called it "the first roller-coaster bridge," and one description warned that its "steep approaches, stupendous height, extremely narrow width, and a sharp curve at the dip conspire to excite and alarm the motorist." For 76 years, first alone and later beside a younger twin, the steel cantilevers carried traffic across the Cooper River between Charleston and Mount Pleasant. They survived bankruptcy, a deadly collision and a hurricane before the Arthur Ravenel Jr. Bridge replaced them in 2005.

## A Bridge Nobody Could Afford

In June 1926, a group of businessmen led by Harry F. Barkerding and Charles R. Allen announced plans to bridge the river with steel. Not everyone welcomed the idea; Charleston Mayor Thomas Stoney was among those who feared a bridge would mar the city's skyline. One plan called for a tunnel under the shipping channel emerging at Castle Pinckney before continuing over the water to what is now Patriot's Point, but it proved too expensive. The private company Cooper River Bridge, Inc. took shape that month, and a consortium of four firms built the bridge in 17 months for $6 million. Its 1,050-foot main span ranked among the longest of its kind in the world when it opened, its total length about 2.7 miles, its two lanes just 10 feet wide. Col. James Armstrong started the ribbon cutting at 1:12 p.m. on August 8, 1929, and between 30,000 and 50,000 people crossed that first day. After four free hours, the toll began: fifty cents per car and driver, plus fifteen cents for each extra passenger.

## Paying Off the Toll

The tolls were supposed to pay off the bonds. Six weeks after opening, too few drivers were willing to pay, and on September 20, 1929, the bridge was offered for sale for delinquent taxes. It was saved when a 100-mile stretch of Route 40 near Georgetown was paved on October 1. The finances never really recovered. In 1936 the company filed for reorganization under bankruptcy laws with a $487,879 deficit. Locals resented the tolls as a drag on growth and tourism. "The entire future of Charleston absolutely depends upon the Cooper River Bridge being made free," Mayor Maybank said in 1933. The county bought the bridge in 1941 for $4.4 million, it was renamed the John Patrick Grace Memorial Bridge in 1943, and the state bought it in 1945. In June 1946, with the last bonds paid, the tolls ended and traffic grew.

## The Lawsons

Months before the tolls came down, on February 24, 1946, a 12,000-ton cargo ship called the Nicaragua Victory broke loose from its mooring. With its engines off, it drifted from the city toward Mount Pleasant until its bow stuck fast in the mud. Its stern kept swinging, and it slammed into the Grace Memorial Bridge while traffic was crossing. The impact damaged a pier and brought down a 240-foot section of the structure, snapping power cables on the deck, which exploded. Most drivers saw what had happened and stopped in time. Five members of the Lawson family did not: Elmer Lawson, his wife, his mother and their two children went down with their car, and none survived the fall. Their car and remains were not recovered until about a month later. A temporary Bailey truss went up in April, and by June the collapsed spans had been rebuilt. The federal government, which had chartered the ship, was sued and paid for the $300,000 repair.

## Green Arrows and Red X's

Free passage turned the Grace into a notorious bottleneck. By the late 1950s it was deemed functionally obsolete, its 10-foot lanes built for Ford Model As and its ramps climbing grades of up to six percent. The answer, opened on April 29, 1966, was a $15 million second span named for Silas Nathaniel Pearman, who had risen from surveyor to the highway department's chief engineer and chief commissioner. The Pearman Bridge had three wide lanes, one of them reversible so it could follow the rush hour to and from Mount Pleasant, then largely a bedroom community. Signs told drivers to "Use lanes with green arrow" and "Do not use red X lane." During its construction, workers found that marine worms had badly eaten one of the Grace's piers; they wrapped it in wire and filled it with concrete. After 1979, when trucks were banned from the aging Grace, the Pearman's reversible lane became permanently southbound. With oncoming traffic always beside them, drivers suffered many head-on collisions, deaths and injuries.

## Burning the Bridges

By 1995 the Grace scored just 4 out of 100 in a safety rating, and in 1998 the Pearman, battered by Hurricane Hugo in 1989 and repaired, scored 55. Three of Charleston's four shipping terminals sit up the Cooper and Wando rivers, and with ships growing ever larger, the old bridges' limited clearance risked losing business to other ports. Arthur Ravenel Jr. ran for the state Senate as a way to solve the problem, pushing for an eight-lane replacement. The cable-stayed bridge that bears his name was begun in 2002 and opened in mid-July 2005. Charleston said goodbye with a "Burn The Bridges" run and parade, as vintage cars typical of 1929 rolled across the empty Pearman deck. Demolition started that August. The Pearman's last pier was imploded on February 28, 2007, and the Grace's final pier fell on March 27. By April 10, 2007, the cleanup was complete and the old roller coaster was gone.

## Flight Context

Coordinates: 32.8018 N, 79.9312 W, over the Cooper River at Drum Island, between downtown Charleston and Mount Pleasant, South Carolina. The 1929 and 1966 cantilevers are gone; their successor, the cable-stayed Arthur Ravenel Jr. Bridge, now spans the same crossing and is the landmark to look for, with Charleston Harbor and the downtown peninsula to the south and west. This is inside Charleston's busy terminal airspace, so plan the flyover at around 1,500-2,500 ft with ATC coordination. Nearby airports: Charleston International (KCHS) about 8 nm northwest; Charleston Executive on Johns Island (KJZI) about 7 nm southwest; Mount Pleasant Regional, Faison Field (KLRO) about 9 nm northeast. Sea breezes, haze and summer afternoon thunderstorms are common over the harbor.
