# Miles Brewton House

Built in 1765–1769 for a wealthy slave trader, Charleston's Miles Brewton House is one of the most complete Georgian townhouse complexes in America, a Palladian double house that served as headquarters for occupying armies in two wars.

**Location:** 32.7725, -79.9329

**Categories:** National Historic Landmarks in South Carolina, Houses in Charleston, South Carolina, Houses completed in 1765, Slave cabins and quarters in the United States, Historic district contributing properties in South Carolina

## Introduction

Somewhere inside the Miles Brewton House, on a marble mantelpiece, is the scratched outline of a British general's face. The profile is reportedly that of Henry Clinton, for whom the British took over the house as headquarters after occupying Charleston in 1780. It is a small, strange mark in one of the grandest houses in the city, a private home that is also a National Historic Landmark on King Street, near the southern tip of the Charleston peninsula, and one of the most complete Georgian townhouse complexes in America. Its story holds a fortune built on the slave trade, a family lost at sea and two armies of occupation.

## A Palladian Double House

Miles Brewton was a wealthy slave trader and planter, and in 1765 work began on a house worthy of that fortune, credited to Ezra Waite, a carver from London whom the National Park Service calls its architect, and finished in 1769. It is one of the finest examples in Charleston of a double house, with four main rooms on each floor divided by a central stair hall, and it was designed on principles articulated by Andrea Palladio. The two-story brick house rises on a high stone basement. Marble steps climb to a projecting two-story Classical porch, with Doric columns below and Ionic columns above, crowned by a fully pedimented, dentillated gable. Inside, the rooms are richly finished in wood, plaster and marble. The quality was recognized long ago, and the well-preserved house was declared a National Historic Landmark in 1960.

## Behind the Walls

The two-acre property is ringed by walls topped with iron fencing, lower at the front, where an iron gate opens into a courtyard enclosed by brick walls to the north and south and by the house itself to the west. Along the north side runs what is known locally as a "plantation lane," and it is there that the complex reveals how the household really functioned. The outbuildings include period slave quarters, a kitchen and a pavilion, all joined by an arcade. The quarters housed the enslaved people whose labor kept this household running, and their surviving dwellings are part of what makes the complex so complete. Behind the house, the large garden still keeps its basic 18th-century layout.

## Lost at Sea, Taken by War

Brewton did not enjoy his house for long. Elected a delegate to the Second Continental Congress, he was traveling to Philadelphia with his family in late August 1775 when their ship went down, and all of them were lost at sea. The house passed to his sisters, Frances, the wife of Charles Pinckney, and Rebecca, the wife of Jacob Motte. Rebecca Motte was living there in 1780 when the British took the house for Clinton. She left the city with her daughters and the enslaved people who worked in her household for Mt. Joseph, a plantation on the Congaree River, about 95 miles away, which she had also inherited from her brother.

## Headquarters Again

The house stayed in the family. One of Rebecca Motte's daughters married Captain William Alston of Marion's Brigade, who bought the house as his town residence, an alternative to his plantation on the Waccamaw River. Their daughter, Mrs. William Bull Pringle, inherited it, and ever since it has also been called the Pringle House on King Street. War returned in 1865, when federal forces occupying Charleston at the end of the Civil War made it a Union headquarters, used by Brigadier General Alexander Schimmelfennig. Two occupations passed through the house, yet it endured, and it remains a private residence, reported to be still owned and lived in by descendants of the Brewton-Motte family.

## Flight Context

The Miles Brewton House stands at 27 King Street, 32.7725°N, 79.9329°W, near the southern tip of the Charleston peninsula, a few blocks north of the Battery seawall and White Point Garden where the Ashley and Cooper Rivers meet. From 1,000–2,000 feet, use the Battery and the harbor as your reference and look for the walled compound with its long garden on the west side of King Street. Charleston Executive Airport (KJZI) is about 6 nm southwest, Charleston International Airport (KCHS) about 9 nm northwest, and Mount Pleasant Regional Airport (KLRO) about 11 nm northeast. Keep clear of KCHS traffic; summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are common.
