# Exchange and Provost

Built in 1767–1771 at the foot of Broad Street, Charleston's Old Exchange has been a custom house, a revolutionary council chamber, a British military prison, the stage for George Washington's welcome, and the backdrop to generations of slave auctions.

**Location:** 32.7753, -79.9272

**Categories:** National Historic Landmarks in South Carolina, Museums in Charleston, South Carolina, Prison museums in the United States, Government buildings completed in 1767, History museums in South Carolina

## Introduction

In 1965, something older than Charleston's Old Exchange turned up underneath it: the Half-Moon Battery, a fortification dating to 1698. It was a fitting discovery for a place built in layers of history. The Old Exchange & Provost Dungeon, also known as the Custom House, stands at East Bay and Broad Streets, where the city meets its harbor. Since South Carolina's provincial government raised it between 1767 and 1771, it has served as a custom house, a public market, a meeting hall and a jail, and it has witnessed revolution, occupation, celebration and human cruelty in turn. It was designated a National Historic Landmark in 1973.

## Tea and Revolution

The Exchange was built as the working heart of a trading colony, a place for collecting customs duties, holding markets and gathering in public. Within a few years it had become something more dangerous. In 1774, as the quarrel with Britain deepened, confiscated tea was stored inside, and the building became the place where South Carolina's revolutionary leadership councils met. A building raised for the colony's commerce became the meeting room for the men leading its break with Britain.

## Prisoners Below

The tables turned in 1780. After British forces captured Charleston, they used the Exchange as a barracks and turned its basement into a military prison, the space remembered today as the Provost Dungeon. A study completed in 2012 examined the building's use as a British prison during the Revolutionary War and found that prisoners were held throughout the Exchange, not only in the dungeon. The investigation documented at least 120 of them, but there were many more whose identities could not be discovered. Those unnamed prisoners belong to the building's story too, captives held in a hall that had so recently echoed with talk of liberty.

## Washington on the Balcony

Independence brought the Exchange back into civic life. In 1788 the building hosted the South Carolina convention that ratified the United States Constitution. When George Washington spent a week in Charleston, the Exchange was the setting for many of the events of his stay, and a crowd greeted him while he stood on its balcony. The building continued as an exchange into the 19th century, when it also became a post office, which meant that residents and visitors alike called there daily for their mail. During that century the postmaster defended a shipment of abolitionist pamphlets passing through the Exchange from angry Charleston rioters.

## The Plaza to the North

Those daily visits carried people past one of the city's slave markets. The historian Frederic Bancroft wrote in Slave-Trading in the Old South that from colonial days until after the middle of the nineteenth century, "from several hundred to many thousand slaves were annually sold to the highest bidders, in front or just north of this building," and that anyone collecting mail on sale days after about 10 a.m. was sure to notice the crowd gathered around them. One such sale took place at 11 a.m. on March 10, 1853, when the auctioneer Alonzo J. White offered ninety-six people on the plaza north of the Exchange. The sale described them only as a "very prime gang" who had cultivated rice on the Combahee River and, for the previous five years, Sea Island cotton. Behind that language stood ninety-six human beings with lives, skills and bonds of their own, reduced by the auctioneer's words to a single lot for sale.

## Shells, Wars and Three Cupolas

During the Civil War the Exchange served as a Confederate post office until several shells struck it and it was abandoned. In 1913 it was granted to the Daughters of the American Revolution, who have preserved it ever since. The old building kept finding new wartime jobs. In World War I it served as the army headquarters of General Leonard Wood and housed the United States Lighthouse Service, which had been there since the late 1800s. In World War II it was a USO facility and canteen for troops and the Coastal Picket Station for the Sixth Naval District of the United States Coast Guard. Even its rooftop has a history. A hurricane damaged the first cupola in the early 1800s, the second had deteriorated before the great earthquake of 1886, and the third was not put in place until 1981, when the building opened as a museum. Today the South Carolina Society of the Daughters of the American Revolution still owns the Exchange, which is open to the public as a museum and historic site, and docents now lead visitors into the dungeon where animatronic pirates once told their stories.

## Flight Context

The Old Exchange stands at the foot of Broad Street on East Bay Street, 32.7753°N, 79.9272°W, facing the Cooper River waterfront on the eastern edge of the Charleston peninsula. From 1,500–2,000 feet, trace Broad Street east from the steeple of St. Michael's Church to the harbor; the Exchange sits where Broad meets East Bay, close to the water. Charleston Executive Airport (KJZI) is about 6 nm southwest, Charleston International Airport (KCHS) about 9 nm northwest, and Mount Pleasant Regional Airport (KLRO) about 10 nm northeast across the harbor. Watch for KCHS traffic; summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are common.
