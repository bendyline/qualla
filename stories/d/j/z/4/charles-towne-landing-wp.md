# Charles Towne Landing

On the banks of the Ashley River, a 664-acre state historic site guards the ground where the first permanent English settlement in South Carolina began, complete with a palisade, a sailing ketch and a zoo stocked by the calendar of the 1670s.

**Location:** 32.8075, -79.9869

**Categories:** Parks in Charleston, South Carolina, South Carolina state historic sites, Zoos in South Carolina, Archaeological sites in South Carolina, Living museums in South Carolina

## Introduction

The animals at Charles Towne Landing were chosen by the calendar. Every creature in the park's Animal Forest belongs to a species that lived in Carolina in the 1670s, which is why a visitor on a trail in the West Ashley area of Charleston can meet bison and pumas, animals long gone from the South Carolina Lowcountry. The zoo is only one thread of a 664-acre state historic site that preserves the original site of the first permanent English settlement in South Carolina, founded here in 1670. Three centuries later, in 1970, the park opened to commemorate South Carolina's tricentennial, and it has been gathering exhibits ever since: an exhibit hall, miles of trails, ongoing archaeological excavations, a replica tall ship and six replica cannon that can still be fired.

## A Wall Against the Spaniards

The colonists' greatest fear came overland. They raised a palisade wall to defend the young colony from a land-side attack by the Spanish, and today a reconstructed palisade bounds the park's Fortified Area once more, partially rebuilt on the original wall's archaeological footprint. The earthwork fortifications have been reconstructed the same way. The settlers mounted one battery of cannon facing the Ashley River and a second to defend Towne Creek, the stream known today as Old Towne Creek, and six replica cannon now stand guard inside the walls. A short walk away, the Experimental Crop Garden grows the plants early colonists hoped would feed them and turn a profit. The offerings change with the seasons, but two of the cash crops the settlers attempted, sugarcane and indigo, are frequently visible in the beds.

## A Ketch Called Adventure

Moored at the landing for most of the year (she goes off-site for annual maintenance from January to April) is Adventure, a replica of a ketch, a popular style of 17th-century cargo vessel. Ships like her carried commercial goods, foodstuffs and even livestock between New York, Barbados and everywhere in between. The naval architect and historian William Avery Baker designed Adventure in 1969, and the first vessel to carry the name served Charles Towne Landing from 1970 until 2004. Her successor was built in 2008 by Rockport Marine in Maine and sailed south to Charles Towne Landing in October of that year, a working reminder of the modest wooden hulls that once linked colonies up and down the Atlantic.

## Creatures of the 1670s

The Animal Forest is a natural habitat zoo, and its roster has read like a field guide to the colonial Lowcountry. The birds have included great blue herons, great egrets, white ibis and brown pelicans, black-crowned and yellow-crowned night herons, wild turkeys, and both turkey vultures and black vultures. Among the mammals have been red wolves, black bears, bobcats, white-tailed deer, striped skunks and North American river otters, along with the bison and pumas that show how different this coast was when the settlers arrived. The animals on view change over time, and the park's website has noted that the puma habitat is temporarily closed for improvements, so check before visiting. One of those big cats left a mark far beyond the park. During the 1970–71 school year, College of Charleston students voted to change their teams' nickname from the Maroons to the Cougars, in honor of a cougar that had recently arrived at the Animal Forest.

## Digging Down, Building Up

Beneath the trails, the ground is still talking. Professional archaeology began at the site in 1967 and continues to the present day, recovering Native American, English and African artifacts. The park's own beginnings were bumpier. In 1968 the Tricentennial Commission selected Corkern and Wiggins of Hilton Head to design an exhibition pavilion, and when the public criticized a drawing of it, a commission member released a second drawing that he claimed presented the structure better, noting that it would rise near the Old Town egg factory site rather than on the Waring gardens. Roof problems proved harder to fix than expected, and talks with the contractor and engineers ran into June 1970 before everyone agreed to add columns. The pavilion finally opened on July 16, 1970, after the park itself. Damaged by Hurricane Hugo and never repaired, it was cleared for demolition in November 2002.

## Flight Context

Charles Towne Landing sits at 32.8075°N, 79.9869°W on the west bank of the Ashley River in West Ashley, just northwest of the Charleston peninsula. Look for the wooded tract along the river and Old Towne Creek; from 1,500–2,500 feet the clearings, trails and the waterfront where Adventure is moored stand out against the surrounding suburbs. Charleston International Airport (KCHS) is about 6 nm to the north-northwest, Charleston Executive Airport (KJZI) on Johns Island about 6.5 nm to the south, and Mount Pleasant Regional Airport (KLRO) about 12 nm to the east-northeast. Expect heavy traffic into KCHS, and summer afternoons often bring thunderstorms and haze over the Lowcountry marshes.
