# Magnolia Cemetery (Charleston, South Carolina)

Laid out in 1850 as Charleston's rural cemetery, Magnolia holds governors, novelists, judges and the lost crews of the Confederate submarine H.L. Hunley, along with a hard history of whom the city allowed through its gates.

**Location:** 32.8183, -79.9422

**Categories:** Cemeteries in Charleston, South Carolina, Rural cemeteries, Cemeteries on the National Register of Historic Places in South Carolina, 1850 establishments in South Carolina, Tourist attractions in Charleston, South Carolina

## Introduction

On April 17, 2004, Charleston buried the final crew of the H.L. Hunley, 140 years after they died. Their Confederate submarine had attacked and sunk the USS Housatonic on February 17, 1864, and did not survive the attack. When the crew's remains were at last laid to rest beneath the live oaks and Spanish moss of Magnolia Cemetery, they joined Horace Lawson Hunley himself, the lawyer and financier whose name the vessel carried, and generations of Charlestonians already resting there. Dedicated in 1850, Magnolia is a place where grand monuments, war dead and an uncomfortable history of exclusion lie side by side.

## A Garden for the Dead

Magnolia was planned as a rural cemetery, a designed landscape rather than a churchyard. Its first board was assembled in 1849, and the architect Edward C. Jones laid out the grounds, which were built during 1850. That same year the cemetery was dedicated, with Charles Fraser delivering the address. Jones also designed a Gothic chapel near the central lake, and construction on it continued into early 1851. The chapel no longer stands, but the setting that framed it survives: graves shaded by southern live oaks and hung with Spanish moss, and monuments such as the Wm B Smith Monument, one of the most unusual and striking crypts on the grounds. In 1978 Magnolia was listed on the National Register of Historic Places as a historic district.

## Occupation and Aftermath

The Civil War reached even this quiet ground. Federal forces occupied the cemetery, and both the chapel and the porter's lodge at the entrance suffered very heavy damage. The lodge was demolished in 1868, while the chapel remained in use until at least 1876 before it, too, disappeared. The war left other marks. Cannons stand in the cemetery's Confederate section, and rows of markers commemorate unknown sailors of the Confederate States Navy, men whose names were lost even as their service was remembered. Confederate generals Micah Jenkins, Roswell S. Ripley and James Conner are buried here as well.

## The Men of the Hunley

The Hunley's story is written across several graves here. Horace Lawson Hunley, the lawyer who financed and helped develop early Confederate submarines, died on October 15, 1863, when the vessel failed to surface after a mock attack, killing all eight men aboard; he had joined the crew for the exercise. He lies at Magnolia, as does George E. Dixon, the submarine's commander, who died in 1864. Four months after the fatal exercise, on February 17, 1864, the Hunley sank the Housatonic, a steam-powered United States Navy sloop-of-war, and was lost with her crew. Those men were finally laid to rest at Magnolia in 2004. Their gravestones, and an H.L. Hunley memorial marker, are now among the cemetery's landmarks.

## A Line Drawn at the Gate

By the early 20th century, Magnolia was, in a newspaper's words, "the principal burying place of the white citizens," and that boundary was enforced. A 1909 account reported that "there is a rule in Charleston that colored people shall not be allowed to parade through Magnolia cemetery," using the language of its era. The rule applied regardless of social status; Dr. Crum, for one, was prohibited from even driving through. Among those buried within the gates is Ziba B. Oakes, a slave trader whose business depended on selling human beings. Yet the same ground also holds Julius Waties Waring, the federal judge whose work linked him to the American civil rights movement, a reminder that the cemetery's history is not one story but several.

## Writers, Judges and Reformers

Walk the paths and the names read like a roll call of Southern public life. William Gilmore Simms, poet, novelist and historian, is here, along with the novelist and poet Josephine Pinckney, the author and illustrator John Bennett, and Frank Bunker Gilbreth Jr., the author. Hugh S. Legaré, the 16th U.S. Attorney General, shares the ground with Langdon Cheves, a president of the Second Bank of the United States, and with governors including William Aiken Jr., Andrew Gordon Magrath and Burnet Rhett Maybank. The naturalist and physician John Edwards Holbrook studied fish and reptiles. Sallie F. Chapin organized the Charleston Woman's Christian Temperance Union in 1881, the first in the state, and became its first state president in 1883. Far from home, five Royal Navy and Merchant Navy personnel of World War II rest in British war graves.

## Flight Context

Magnolia Cemetery lies at 32.8183°N, 79.9422°W on the upper Charleston peninsula, roughly three miles north of the historic district. From 1,500–2,500 feet, look for a broad, tree-covered tract with a lake at its center. Charleston International Airport (KCHS) is about 7 nm northwest, Charleston Executive Airport (KJZI) about 8 nm south-southwest, and Mount Pleasant Regional Airport (KLRO) about 9 nm east-northeast. Traffic into KCHS passes nearby; expect summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms.
