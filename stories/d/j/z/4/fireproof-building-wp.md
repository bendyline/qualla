# Fireproof Building

Designed by Charleston-born Robert Mills, the future architect of the Washington Monument, and completed in 1827 to protect the county's records, Charleston's Fireproof Building was the most fire-resistant building in America in its day and now houses the South Carolina Historical Society's museum.

## Introduction

Years before he won the competition to design the Washington Monument, Robert Mills built a building in his hometown whose whole purpose was to keep paper from burning. The Fireproof Building, completed in 1827 at 100 Meeting Street in Charleston, was made to house the office and records of Charleston County, and it was the most fire-resistant building in America at the time. Nearly two centuries later, it is believed to be the oldest fire-resistant building in the country, and it now serves as the headquarters and museum of the South Carolina Historical Society, whose archive moved to the College of Charleston's library in 2014.

## The Architect Who Feared Fire

Robert Mills was born in Charleston in 1781 and has been called the nation's first domestically trained architect. He was also an early advocate of fire-resistant buildings, a conviction that paid off elsewhere in South Carolina: when fire broke out in a building he designed in Kingstree, the county records on its first floor survived thanks to his fireproofing measures. Charleston already knew his work. Mills had completed the plans for the great round sanctuary of Circular Church on Meeting Street, just up the block, which a citywide fire destroyed in 1861. His national fame came later. He designed the Treasury Building in Washington and won the 1836 competition for the Washington Monument, which was dedicated in 1885, thirty years after his death in 1855. The Fireproof Building, built under construction supervisor John G. Spidle (spelled Spindle in some sources), who changed some of Mills's specified details, belongs to the practical side of his career, where the goal was not a monument but a vault for a county's memory.

## Stone, Stucco and a Floating Stair

The building is a two-story masonry structure raised on a tall stone basement, whose arcade of round-arch openings runs along the ground level. Its walls are brick, stuccoed to resemble stone, and Doric porticoes stand on the north and south sides. The design has clean, crisp lines and relatively little ornamentation, a restraint that suits a building meant to be sturdy rather than showy. The most memorable space is inside. An oval stair hall rises through the building, lit from above by a cupola, and its stone stairs are cantilevered through three stories, climbing the curved walls with no visible support beneath them. Standing at the bottom and looking up toward the light, you see Mills's balance of practicality and elegance at its clearest.

## Records Into Museum

The Fireproof Building, also known as the County Records Building, stands at the northwest corner of Washington Square. It was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1969 and declared a National Historic Landmark in 1973. Today it is the home of the South Carolina Historical Society, which leased it in 1955 and made it its headquarters in 1968. In 2016 and 2017 the society completed a major renovation to modernize all of the building's systems and add an events venue and modern museum space. In September 2018 the building reopened as a museum dedicated to the history of South Carolina and to the history of the Fireproof Building itself. It is a fitting second life: a structure designed to keep records safe from flames, which protected the society's archive for decades before it moved to the College of Charleston in 2014, now tells the state's stories and invites the public in to explore them.
