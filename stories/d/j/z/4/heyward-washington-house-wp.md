# Heyward-Washington House

Built in 1772 as the town home of a signer of the Declaration of Independence, it housed George Washington for a week in 1791, and in 1930 it became Charleston's first historic house museum.

## Introduction

For one week in May 1791, the President of the United States slept in a rented house on Church Street. The City of Charleston had leased the place for George Washington's use during his visit, and the association proved so durable that the building has carried his name ever since, paired with the name of the patriot who owned it. The Heyward-Washington House at 87 Church Street has been many things across two and a half centuries: a signer's town home, a President's lodging, a boarding house, a billet for occupying soldiers. In 1930 it became something new for the city: Charleston's first historic house museum, a window into the late 18th century that the Charleston Museum still keeps open.

## The Signer's Double House

The house was built in 1772 as the town home of Thomas Heyward, Jr., a Georgian-style double house on Church Street (the Charleston Museum credits his father, the rice planter Daniel Heyward, with building it). Four years later he became one of four South Carolinians to sign the Declaration of Independence. His commitment went well beyond a signature. Heyward was a patriot leader and an artillery officer in the South Carolina militia during the Revolutionary War, and he paid a price for it. When the British took Charleston in 1780, Heyward was captured and sent into exile at St. Augustine, Florida. He regained his freedom only through a prisoner exchange in 1781. While he was held far to the south, his house stood in a captured city, a reminder that the Founders' homes were never safely distant from the war they had declared. The property stayed with the Heyward family until it was sold in 1794.

## A President Moves In

In May 1791, George Washington came to Charleston for a week-long stay, and the City rented Heyward's house for the President's use. It was a short visit, but it rewrote the building's identity. Charleston has traditionally called it the Heyward-Washington House ever since, joining the name of the family that owned it to the name of the man who slept there. The city did not let the memory fade. On May 23, 1901, a bronze marker was unveiled at the house, reciting the history of Washington's visit, 110 years after the President walked through its door. A century on, Charleston still wanted passersby to know exactly who had once taken up residence behind these walls, and that a single week could define a house for generations.

## Grimkés, Boarders and Soldiers

In 1794, Heyward sold the house to John F. Grimké, another Revolutionary War officer. His daughters Sarah and Angelina Grimké would become famous as abolitionists and advocates for women's rights. In 1824, Mrs. Margaret Munro bought the property and ran it as a boarding house. She left it to her granddaughter, Elizabeth Jane Hervey, who married Tobias Cambridge Trott in 1857, and the couple made their home at 87 Church Street. Then the Civil War came. Tobias died unexpectedly in 1863, and with Union guns bombarding Charleston, Elizabeth fled to the upstate with her three young children. After the city fell, Union soldiers were billeted in the house. A widow's escape and an occupying army's quarters share the same rooms where a President had once been hosted, and the building carried all of it into the next century.

## Charleston's First House Museum

The Charleston Museum acquired the property in 1929 and opened it the following year as Charleston's first historic house museum. It is now a National Historic Landmark, furnished for the late 18th century with a collection of Charleston-made furniture. The star piece is the Holmes Bookcase, considered one of the finest examples of American-made colonial furniture. Step out back and the story reaches even further into the past. The kitchen building dates to the 1740s, older than the house itself, and it is described as the only 1740s kitchen building open to the public in Charleston. A carriage shed stands on the grounds as well, and the formal gardens are planted with species commonly grown in the South Carolina Lowcountry in the late 18th century. Taken together, garden, kitchen, carriage shed and furnished rooms, the property reads as a whole town household of its era rather than a single showpiece.
