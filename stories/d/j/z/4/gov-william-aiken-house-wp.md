# Gov. William Aiken House

Built in 1820 and later home to Governor William Aiken Jr., the Aiken-Rhett House in Charleston's Wraggborough neighborhood is considered the city's best-preserved complex of antebellum domestic buildings, and a 2015 dig beneath its former laundry, in the service yard where enslaved people worked, recovered more than 10,000 artifacts.

## Introduction

In 2015, archaeologists excavating beneath the floor of the former laundry at the Aiken-Rhett House in Charleston recovered more than 10,000 artifacts: buttons and pins, fragments of bottles and pottery. They are small things, drawn from the place where enslaved people worked, and they are part of why this address at 48 Elizabeth Street matters. The house is considered the best-preserved complex of antebellum domestic structures in Charleston, and its caretakers have made the lives of the people enslaved here central to the story it tells.

## Robinson's House, Aiken's Name

Despite the name, the Aikens did not build the house. John Robinson bought several lots in the Mazyck-Wraggborough area in 1817 and built the house in 1820. He configured it as a Charleston double house, with its entrance on the south side along Judith Street. The property later became the home of William Aiken, the owner of the South Carolina Canal and Railroad Company, and then of his son, William Aiken Jr., who served as governor of South Carolina. That layered history explains why the house answers to several names. It is known as the Gov. William Aiken House, the Aiken-Rhett House and the Robinson-Aiken House, each one a record of a different family's claim on it. It stands today in the Wraggborough neighborhood, just north of Marion Square.

## Slavery in the City

Southern slavery is often imagined on sprawling plantations, but it was also woven into the life of cities like Charleston, and the Aiken property shows how. Census figures cited for the house put the number of people Aiken enslaved inside the urban residence at seven in 1850, while the family reportedly enslaved 878 people across the Charleston and Colleton districts combined. By 1860 the number at the city house had reportedly risen to nineteen. When the Civil War broke out, those living on the property included at least six children. Behind each number were individual lives, children among them, lived out in the slave dwellings that archaeologists would later study. When the Historic Charleston Foundation bought the property, what attracted it, the foundation has said, was the chance to understand and present antebellum urban life and the African American heritage of Charleston to the public.

## What the Ground Held

Frances Dill Rhett donated the house to the Charleston Museum in 1975, and it was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1977. In 1995 the Historic Charleston Foundation acquired the property and took a more traditional approach to its preservation. The foundation also turned to the ground itself. In 2015 a team of archaeologists led by Andrew Agha and Nicole Isenbarger of the Archaeological Research Collective excavated beneath the floor of the former laundry and uncovered those 10,000-plus artifacts, and an exhibit installed in the laundry space in 2018 now interprets their cultural significance. In a separate survey in 2017, archaeologists searched the property for evidence of siege lines that British soldiers reportedly dug in the spring of 1780, a reminder that the ground here holds history older than the house.

## Stories the Old Tour Left Out

For years, visitors followed a guide created in the mid-1990s, one that "left out many of the stories they had discovered since then about the lives of the enslaved on the property." In the fall of 2018 the foundation launched an app with a self-guided audio tour lasting roughly 45 minutes, with exhibit descriptions and photographs. The decision to update the tour reportedly came from Lauren Northup, the foundation's director of museums. The app has since expanded to other historic sites around Charleston, including the Nathaniel Russell House and Magnolia Cemetery, and it now includes women's history and the history of the LGBTQ community alongside the stories of enslaved people and the African American community. People once counted only as census tallies are now at the center of the tour.

## Flight Context

The Aiken-Rhett House stands at about 32.7914 N, 79.9350 W at 48 Elizabeth Street in Charleston's Wraggborough neighborhood, on the upper part of the peninsula. Marion Square, the large open green, lies about a third of a nautical mile to the south, and the Cigar Factory on East Bay Street is roughly 0.4 nm to the north. The cable-stayed Arthur Ravenel Jr. Bridge over the Cooper River rises about 1.2 nm to the northeast. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet, Marion Square is the easiest reference. Charleston Executive Airport (KJZI) is about 6 nm south-southwest, Charleston International Airport (KCHS) about 8 nm northwest and Mount Pleasant Regional Airport (KLRO) about 10 nm northeast; downtown lies within busy Charleston airspace. Expect summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms.
