# St. Michael's Anglican Church (Charleston, South Carolina)

Built in the 1750s and opened in 1761 on one of Charleston's Four Corners of Law, St. Michael's is the city's oldest surviving religious structure, with London-cast bells, a Tiffany window and the pew where George Washington worshipped.

**Location:** 32.7761, -79.9308

**Categories:** Churches in Charleston, South Carolina, National Historic Landmarks in South Carolina, 18th-century Episcopal church buildings, Anglican churches in South Carolina, Colonial South Carolina

## Introduction

The bells of St. Michael's have crossed the Atlantic more than once. Cast in London in 1764 by Lester and Pack, they were carried off by the British as a prize of war and later recovered in London, then cracked in the burning of Columbia in 1865 and sent back to London to be recast; the church's own table dates most of that recasting to 1868. They hang in the tower of the oldest surviving religious structure in Charleston, a church that stands at Broad and Meeting Streets on one of the city's Four Corners of Law, where it represents ecclesiastical law. Built in the 1750s by order of the South Carolina Assembly, St. Michael's is listed on the National Register of Historic Places and was declared a National Historic Landmark in 1960.

## Where the Wooden Church Stood

Long before St. Michael's rose, this corner belonged to another congregation. In the early 1680s St. Philip's Church built its original wooden church here. A hurricane damaged it in 1710, and a new St. Philip's went up several blocks away on Church Street, and in 1727 what was left of the old wooden church was demolished. Work on St. Michael's began on the same site in the early 1750s, with the cornerstone laid in 1752, and the church opened in 1761. No one knows who designed it, but the church clearly shows the influence of St Martin-in-the-Fields in London, designed in the 1720s by James Gibbs. Samuel Cardy was the builder. He raised brick walls that were stuccoed over and painted white, and the two-story portico facing Broad Street, with its Tuscan columns, is described as the first of its size in colonial America. The present portico dates to the late 1880s, a replica of the original that the 1886 earthquake damaged.

## Carved Flowers and an Old Organ Case

The details were lavish. Between 1754 and 1760 the Charleston artisan Henry Burnett carried out £1,000 worth of architectural carving, including ovolo moldings, seventy-two modillions for a Corinthian entablature on the facade, fifty-four double flowers for the portico and a large "foliage flower" at the center of the ceiling. In 1768 the church fitted an organ by John Snetzler. Only its case remains today; a new organ was built for the church in 1994 by Kenneth Jones of Bray, Ireland. The tower also houses a clock that strikes the hours and quarters, made by Aynsworth Thwaites, and a set of change ringing bells dating from colonial times.

## A Window Hidden and Revealed

One of the church's windows was lost for most of a century. The chancel originally had a window, but in 1788 the opening was enclosed to keep fires in adjacent buildings from spreading to the church. In 1865, during the Civil War, shelling damaged the wall, and the location of the old window became apparent again. A new stained glass window filled it in 1893. Made in New York, it depicts St. Michael conquering Satan and is based on a work by Raphael in the Louvre. The north wall holds another treasure, a window given in 1898 as a memorial to Mrs. E.A. Simons. A copy of "Easter Morning," it uses between 1,800 and 2,000 pieces of glass in a panel six feet by ten, and was made for Tiffany Studios, with the flesh tones painted by Louis Lederle. In 1905 Tiffany carried out the chancel's decorative panels and dome; one account says Louis Tiffany himself spent two months on the work.

## Pew Forty-Three

St. Michael's has seated famous worshippers. During his 1791 visit to Charleston, President George Washington worshipped in pew no. 43, and Robert E. Lee also worshipped here. In the graveyard lies the jurist John Rutledge. The setting around the church has changed over the years. A photograph taken between 1886 and 1896 shows a full front view that is no longer possible, because a post office was later built across Meeting Street. The artist Alice Ravenel Huger Smith painted "The Rector's Kitchen and View of St. Michael's" around 1910 to 1915, pairing the rectory's kitchen with a view of the church beyond.

## Flight Context

St. Michael's stands at the corner of Broad and Meeting Streets, 32.7761°N, 79.9308°W, in the lower Charleston peninsula. Its steeple is a useful visual marker in the historic district; from 1,000–2,000 feet, follow Meeting Street south toward the Battery and look for the church tower at the Broad Street intersection. Charleston Executive Airport (KJZI) is about 6 nm southwest, Charleston International Airport (KCHS) about 9 nm northwest, and Mount Pleasant Regional Airport (KLRO) about 10 nm northeast. Watch for traffic into KCHS; summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are common.
