# Powder Magazine (Charleston, South Carolina)

Completed in 1713 and engineered so that an explosion would blow out through its roof, Charleston's Powder Magazine is the oldest surviving public building of the former Province of Carolina.

**Location:** 32.7794, -79.9300

## Introduction

The Powder Magazine was designed to fail in the right direction. Its builders knew that a store of gunpowder might one day ignite, so they shaped the little square building on Cumberland Street to aim the blast upward. The walls thin as they rise, doors are few, and sand held in the roof was meant to smother whatever fire followed. Completed in 1713, this squat stuccoed structure with its red tile roof is the oldest surviving public building in the former Province of Carolina. It has held gunpowder for a colony at war, wine for a Charleston gentleman and, for well over a century now, the artifacts of a museum.

## A Fortress Piece from Queen Anne's War

The Province of Carolina authorized the magazine in 1703, during Queen Anne's War, as part of a series of fortifications meant to protect Charleston. Building it took a decade. The magazine was not completed until 1713. By the museum's account it stored gunpowder from 1713 to 1748 and again during the American Revolution, and it was retired from that work in 1780. For a good part of the 18th century, then, this small building held the explosive heart of the town's defenses. It sits on the south side of Cumberland Street, between Church and Meeting Streets, in what is now the historic center of Charleston. Its age gives it a distinction that no other surviving public building from the old province can claim.

## Engineered for Catastrophe

The magazine is a single-story square building with stuccoed brick walls 32 inches thick and its original red tile roof, pyramidal in form with intersecting gables. Each of its four walls carries a large arch, and the arches hold the secret of the design. The walls grow thinner as they climb, from their full thickness near the ground to just a few inches at the top of each arch. That taper was intentional. With only a few doors in the building, the force of any explosion would find its easiest path upward, and the arches would act like funnels, channeling the blast out through the roof rather than sideways into the neighboring streets. Sand stored in the roof would then fall to smother and put out the fire. It is a building that assumes disaster and plans around it.

## From Gunpowder to Wine

Once the Revolution wound down and the magazine's military job ended, the building entered a long second life. It saw a variety of uses, and one of them has a pleasing irony. For a time the magazine served as a wine cellar for Gabriel Manigault. The same thick walls that had guarded the colony's gunpowder now guarded bottles of wine, and a building made to contain danger became a place for storing pleasure. Those later lives helped carry a colonial military building into the 20th century in the middle of a busy city, long after its first purpose had passed.

## The Dames Take Charge

In 1902 the local chapter of the National Society of the Colonial Dames of America acquired the building, saving it from demolition, and it opened to the public within a year. The museum closed in February 2025 for repairs and a review of its future, and in 2026 the City of Charleston agreed to operate it; the museum's website now lists it as open Monday through Saturday. Visitors find historic artifacts and displays about the building during the Colonial and American Revolution periods, from its years as the town's powder store through the war for independence. The magazine is a National Historic Landmark, a recognition of its age and its rarity. Step inside and the scale is intimate. Stand beneath the arches and the logic of the builders becomes clear: everything about the room points the danger skyward.
