# Hibernian Hall (Charleston, South Carolina)

Built for an Irish benevolent society by the architect who later led the building of the U.S. Capitol dome, Hibernian Hall housed the Stephen Douglas faction during the fractured 1860 Democratic convention and is the only building left from that fateful gathering.

## Introduction

In 1860, the second floor of Hibernian Hall became a dormitory. Hundreds of cots filled the upstairs rooms, and on them slept delegates to the Democratic National Convention, men who had come to Charleston to choose a presidential nominee and who left without one. Downstairs, the faction backing Stephen A. Douglas made its headquarters, pressing for a nomination that never came. The party's failure in Charleston helped open the road to Abraham Lincoln's election. Of all the places connected with that convention, only this white-columned hall on Meeting Street survives, still hosting gatherings more than a century and a half later.

## An Irish Society's Temple

The building began as a home for community rather than politics. The Hibernian Society of Charleston, an Irish benevolent society, organized in 1801, drawing its members largely from Irish immigrants to the city. Nearly four decades later, in 1840, the society completed a hall worthy of its ambitions: a two-story brick building clad in white-painted stucco, fronted by a Greek temple portico. Six Ionic columns hold up an entablature and pediment, giving the modest street front a monumental calm, though the portico was rebuilt after the 1886 earthquake brought it down, and the original Ionic pediment was replaced with one in a Corinthian style. The hall stands today as a fine example of Greek Revival architecture and as a lasting record of the Irish community that paid for it, a society of immigrants who built something permanent in their adopted city.

## The Capitol Architect's Only Charleston Building

The society looked north for its designer. Thomas Ustick Walter of Philadelphia drew the plans, and Hibernian Hall is the only known work of his in Charleston. Walter modeled the columns and entablature on those of a Greek temple, the source of the portico's Ionic dignity. He was a prolific architect, credited with more than 400 projects, including Girard College in Philadelphia. His greatest commission lay ahead. Walter went on to serve as the fourth Architect of the Capitol in Washington, where he led the addition of the building's north and south wings and its central dome. Charleston's Hibernian Hall is therefore a modest work by the architect who would later lead the building of the Capitol's dome. At the entrance, the craftsmanship is local. Christopher Werner, a German-American master ironworker in Charleston, made the wrought-iron gates.

## Cots Upstairs, Crisis Downstairs

The 1860 Democratic National Convention ranks among the most critical political gatherings in American history, and Hibernian Hall stood at its center. The building served as headquarters for the faction backing Stephen A. Douglas. Its first floor became a meeting space, while the second floor housed delegates on hundreds of cots set up for the occasion. The party arrived divided by opinions on slavery, and the division proved fatal to the convention's central task. None of the candidates could gather enough support to win the nomination, and Charleston ended without a nominee. That failure mattered far beyond the city. The party's divisiveness contributed to its defeat by the Republicans and Abraham Lincoln in the 1860 presidential election. The hall's role earned it national significance and designation as a National Historic Landmark in 1973.

## At the Four Corners of Law

Hibernian Hall stands at 105 Meeting Street, just north of the intersection of Meeting and Broad Streets, a crossroads Charlestonians know as the "Four Corners of Law." Its location puts it in the civic heart of the old city, a few blocks from the Cooper River waterfront. The building is not a museum piece. It continues to serve as a function and meeting space, so the same rooms that once heard the arguments of 1860 now host events and gatherings. The white stucco, the six Ionic columns and Werner's ironwork still greet visitors on the street, and behind them sits the only surviving building of a convention whose outcome reshaped the nation.
