# City Market (Charleston, South Carolina)

Charleston's four-block City Market grew on donated marshland in the 1790s, survived fires, tornadoes, hurricanes and repeated demolition plans, and still runs from a temple-fronted Market Hall down to East Bay Street.

**Location:** 32.7803, -79.9314

## Introduction

Visitors often arrive at Charleston's City Market believing a story that is not true. Enslaved people were never sold here; that trade happened elsewhere in the city, at places such as the Old Slave Mart, now a museum, and the confusion between the two is a common misconception. The real City Market has a different history, one of beef and produce, and of neighbors meeting beneath the sheds. Established in the 1790s, it stretches four city blocks and 1,240 feet, from the columned Market Hall facing Meeting Street through a continuous line of one-story sheds that ends at East Bay Street. Today the stalls sell souvenirs, jewelry and Gullah sweetgrass baskets, but the market's bones are more than two centuries old.

## A Gift of Reclaimed Marsh

The market began as land that had once been water. In 1788 the Revolutionary War general Charles Cotesworth Pinckney and other landowners along an existing canal donated a strip of recovered marshland for a "Centre Market" that would run from the docks of Charleston Harbor to Meeting Street. The gift came with three conditions: the City had to use the land for Market Street and market buildings in perpetuity, it had two years to build them, and the donors' remaining land fronting the new street could not be specially taxed to pay for construction. The donor also reserved the right to take the land back if it ever stopped being a market. The first sheds went up around 1790 and spread along most of the strip by 1806. The market was developed to replace the city's Beef Market, which stood where City Hall now stands at 80 Broad Street and burned in 1796.

## A Temple for Beef and Produce

Fire shaped the market's grandest building too. In 1838 flames destroyed the headhouse, and the city hired the local architect Edward B. White to design a replacement. His Market Hall, completed in 1841, is a Greek Revival building with one raised story resting on a rusticated, open arcade at street level. Its high base and frontal portico take their cue from ancient temples such as the Temple of Portunus and the Temple of Athena Nike, and the building has been described as being of the "highest architectural design quality." Behind it, through the 19th century, farms and plantations brought beef and produce to sell, and townspeople came as much to gather and socialize as to shop. Enslaved workers were among the sellers, bringing meat and produce from nearby plantations to the stalls, which is the likely source of the market's mistaken "slave market" nickname. The hall's upper room first served as a large assembly room, and at the outbreak of the Civil War it became a military recruiting office. From 1899 the United Daughters of the Confederacy Chapter 4 used Market Hall to house a Confederate Museum.

## The Sheds That Refused to Fall

By the early 1900s the number of food merchants had dropped dramatically, and the city began debating the sheds' future. In January 1901 City Council endorsed a report to replace the sheds between East Bay and Anson Street with a small park, then reversed course after historical preservationists objected. The idea returned on November 8, 1904, when a motion called for demolishing the same sheds and laying the ground out "in an ornamental fashion." Through mid-1905 council weighed letters praising the buildings as unique examples of their architecture in America. Preservationists proposed a covered walkway, or a public bath house in the last building; business leaders petitioned for a freight station. On September 12, 1905, a special committee recommended whitewashing the eastern sheds, stripping their woodwork and opening them as a covered walkway. The work began in December 1906, and the remaining sheds were reorganized, meat vendors along the north side, fruit and vegetables along the south.

## Storms, Spotters and Ochre Paint

On the morning of September 29, 1938, a series of tornadoes swept through Charleston, killing 32 people, injuring 100 and destroying $2 million in property, and one of them badly damaged the City Market. Sixteen years later, on July 30, 1954, the market gained an unusual rooftop feature: a plane spotting tower, complete with telephone, opened atop one of the sheds for the Charleston Ground Observers Corps. Hurricane Hugo struck in 1989, partially tearing away Market Hall's roof and closing the Confederate Museum, which was housed temporarily in a Charleston kindergarten before it reopened. The City restored the hall, and in January 2003 it won a Carolopolis Award from the Preservation Society. The restorers repainted it in its original colors, a strong ochre with bright green ironwork, a choice that, by one account, displeased many locals, the mayor of Charleston among them.

## Flight Context

City Market runs east–west through downtown Charleston at 32.7803°N, 79.9314°W, a long, narrow ribbon of low rooftops between North and South Market Streets stretching from Meeting Street to East Bay Street near the Cooper River waterfront. From 1,500–2,000 feet the unbroken line of shed roofs is distinctive among the peninsula's grid. Charleston Executive Airport (KJZI) is about 6 nm southwest, Charleston International Airport (KCHS) about 9 nm northwest, and Mount Pleasant Regional Airport (KLRO) about 10 nm northeast across the harbor. Keep watch for traffic into KCHS; summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are common.
