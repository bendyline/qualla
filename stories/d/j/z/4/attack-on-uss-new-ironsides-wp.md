# Attack on USS New Ironsides

On an October night in 1863, a fifty-foot Confederate semi-submersible slipped out of Charleston and exploded a spar torpedo against the Union ironclad New Ironsides, in one of the first successful torpedo boat attacks in history.

**Location:** 32.7250, -79.8430

**Categories:** Naval battles of the American Civil War, Confederate victories of the American Civil War, History of South Carolina, Charleston County, South Carolina, Battles of the lower seaboard theater of the American Civil War, 1863 in South Carolina, Battles of the American Civil War in South Carolina, October 1863

## Introduction

"A very peculiar looking steamer." That was how Captain Stephen C. Rowan described the thing his lookouts spotted off Charleston at 9 p.m. on October 5, 1863. At first it looked like a small boat, heading toward his ship's starboard side from seaward. His men hailed it rapidly four times and got no answer. Moments later the strange craft struck, and a torpedo exploded against the hull of USS New Ironsides, throwing a column of water high enough to rain down on her decks. It was one of the first successful torpedo boat engagements in history.

## A Secret Weapon Against the Blockade

The Union blockade was a great impediment to Confederate commerce and warfare, and the defenders of Charleston were willing to try almost anything to lift it. CSS David was one of the South's secret weapons. Like the submarine H.L. Hunley, she was designed to creep up on enemy ships and place an explosive against their hulls. The newly built semi-submersible was only fifty feet long, with a beam of six feet and a draft of five feet, and her crew numbered just four officers and men under Lieutenant William T. Glassell. Her weapon was a spar torpedo, an explosive charge mounted on a spar. The target was the fourteen-gun ironclad USS New Ironsides, the Union's powerful broadside ironclad off Charleston. She had already survived one failed torpedo boat attack, in August 1863, when the Confederate boat Torch, hampered by engine trouble, failed to place its torpedoes.

## Fifty Yards Out

On the night of October 5, David headed out from the pier toward the ironclad. About an hour later she was closing in when Union lookouts spotted her some fifty yards away, just short of her goal. Rowan's report captures the confused final moments. After the hails went unanswered, the Union crew fired into the approaching craft with musketry, and the Confederates fired back, dangerously wounding Ensign C. W. Howard, who was in charge of the deck. Then David struck near the ironclad's No. 6 port on the starboard side. The torpedo exploded, shaking the whole vessel and throwing up an immense column of water, part of which fell back onto New Ironsides' decks. For the handful of men aboard the tiny boat, the most dangerous part of the night was only beginning.

## Water Down the Smokestack

The same column of water that drenched the Union decks came down on David too. Some of it poured down her smokestack and put out the fire in her engine, leaving the heavily damaged boat without power within reach of the ship she had just attacked. Her commander ordered the crew to abandon ship. One crewman could not swim, so he stayed aboard, while the others went into the water under fire and tried to swim for nearby Morris Island. Then Assistant Engineer J. H. Tomb made a choice that saved the boat. He went back to David, restarted the fire in her engine and got her under way again, escaping into the night. Glassell and one other crewman were not so fortunate. After leaving the boat, the two surrendered to the men of New Ironsides.

## A Victory Without a Sinking

The explosion ripped a large fissure in the ironclad's starboard quarter, and her crew had to hurry to repair the hole, though the ship remained leaky. The blast also damaged her armory bulkhead and some storerooms. Casualties were light: two men were wounded, one of whom later died, and a third man was also hurt. New Ironsides was apparently never in danger of sinking, and the damage proved mostly superficial. She stayed on blockade duty off Charleston for months afterward, then went north to Philadelphia in mid-1864 for repairs and a general overhaul before returning to service. The attack is usually classed as a Confederate victory, though the ironclad stayed on station. It demonstrated that a tiny, low-lying boat could reach and strike one of the Union's most formidable ships. David was repaired as well and went on to attack USS Memphis and USS Wabash. Several David-class torpedo boats were captured at the end of the war, and David herself may have been among them.

## Flight Context

The attack site is marked at about 32.725 N, 79.843 W, in the approaches to Charleston Harbor off Morris Island, which lies about 2.5 nm to the west. Fort Sumter sits about 2.3 nm northwest at the harbor mouth and Sullivan's Island about 2.2 nm north-northwest, so all three landmarks frame the scene from 2,000 to 3,000 feet. Nothing of the engagement is visible on the water today; picture a fifty-foot semi-submersible slipping across this open stretch at night. Charleston Executive Airport (KJZI) is about 8 nm west, Mount Pleasant Regional Airport (KLRO) about 11 nm north-northeast, and Charleston International Airport (KCHS) about 14 nm northwest. Coastal haze and summer thunderstorms are common.
