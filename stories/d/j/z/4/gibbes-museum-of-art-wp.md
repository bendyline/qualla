# Gibbes Museum of Art

Built after a New York court upheld James Shoolbred Gibbes's contested $100,000 bequest, Charleston's Beaux Arts Gibbes Museum of Art opened in 1905 under a Tiffany-style dome and holds more than 10,000 works, many tied to Charleston and the South.

## Introduction

James Shoolbred Gibbes left $100,000 to Charleston's Carolina Art Association for the "erection of a suitable building for the exhibitions of paintings." His nieces and nephews had other ideas. They contested the will, and the fight over whether Charleston would get its art gallery was argued in the courts of New York. Only after a judge upheld the gift did the money arrive, and only then could the Beaux Arts building at 135 Meeting Street rise. Today the Gibbes Museum of Art holds more than 10,000 works, many with a connection to Charleston or the South.

## A Gift Worth Fighting Over

The Carolina Art Association had been established in 1858, nearly half a century before its gallery would open. Gibbes's bequest promised a building, until his relatives challenged it. Their case was heard in the state court of New York during 1900 and 1901. On December 6, 1901, the New York Supreme Court, which despite its name is the state's trial-level court, issued an opinion declaring the gift to Charleston valid. Even then the money took time to arrive. The Association finally received it in 1903 and moved quickly. It hired the architect Frank Pierce Milburn, who planned a building with a Tiffany-style dome, Doric columns, and windows and doors capped with pediments. His drawings were finished by mid-1903, and on June 5 of that year the Charleston Evening Post published a drawing of the proposed gallery.

## From Agricultural Hall to Art Gallery

In August 1903 notices went out seeking contractors' bids, and in September H.T. Zacharias won the job with a contract for $73,370. On September 28 his crews began by clearing away the remains of the South Carolina Agricultural Hall, which had occupied the lot. Work on the foundations had already begun by the time a ceremony on December 8, 1903, laid the cornerstone at the building's northeast corner. The Gibbes Art Gallery formally opened on April 11, 1905. Visitors that first day found more than 300 pictures, many bronzes and about 200 miniatures on display, along with what was described as an "instructive collection" of Japanese prints. For a city that had waited nearly half a century since the Association's founding, it was a grand arrival.

## Charleston on the Walls

The collection that grew from those beginnings now exceeds 10,000 works of fine art, principally American, and many of them are rooted in Charleston or the wider South. The collection includes work by numerous artists with connections to the city, among them Henrietta Johnston, Jeremiah Theus, Charles Fraser, Mary Roberts, William Melton Halsey, Ned I.R. Jennings and Merton Simpson. The museum has also collected photographs by George LaGrange Cook, including images he made after the 1886 Charleston earthquake, a visual record of the city in the aftermath of disaster. Together these works make the Gibbes as much a portrait of Charleston as a museum of art. Walking its rooms, you meet the city through the eyes of the people who painted, drew and photographed it across generations.

## Blueprints From the Archives

In the early twenty-first century the museum closed for an extensive two-year renovation that cost $13.5 million. A lucky find guided the work. In 2008 the building's original blueprints had turned up in the City of Charleston archives, and the design teams used them to return the building to its 1905 Beaux Arts layout. The museum reopened to the public on May 28, 2016. Its first floor holds classrooms, artist studios, lecture and event spaces and a museum store, and when the museum reopened in 2016 the ground floor was made free to enter. At the back, the reception area opens onto a garden that forms part of Charleston's historic Gateway Walk, founded by the Garden Club of Charleston, so a visit can end outdoors in the garden.
