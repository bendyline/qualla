# Santee Canal

One of the earliest canals in the United States, the 22-mile Santee Canal linked the Cooper and Santee rivers in 1800 to connect Charleston with Columbia, failed as a business, and now lies largely beneath Lake Moultrie.

## Introduction

Most of the Santee Canal is underwater now, though not in the way its builders intended. Opened in 1800 as one of the earliest canals in the United States, it was dug to carry boats between Charleston and Columbia, South Carolina's new state capital, by linking the Cooper River to the Santee. For roughly half a century it did just that, lock by lock, through the Lowcountry. Then drought, railroads and sparse traffic finished it off. In the 20th century the reservoir called Lake Moultrie rose over much of its route, and today only fragments of this ambitious waterway remain to be seen.

## A Waterway to the New Capital

The plan took shape soon after independence. In 1785 or 1786 (sources differ), the South Carolina General Assembly chartered a company to build and maintain an inland canal joining the Cooper River near Charleston with the Santee River. The logic was geographic. The Santee connects to the Congaree River and the City of Columbia, so a cut between the Cooper and the Santee would give the port city a direct water route to the state's new capital in the interior. Construction began in 1793 under the direction of engineer Col. John Christian Senf, and it took seven years to finish. When the canal opened in 1800, it ranked among the earliest canals built anywhere in the United States, a bold bet on water as the link between coast and interior.

## Twenty-Two Miles and Ten Locks

The finished canal ran 22 miles. Its engineers raised and lowered boats along the route with two double locks and eight single locks, a sequence of chambers that stepped traffic between the rivers. The channel was modest by later standards: 35 feet wide at the water's surface, narrowing to 20 feet at the bottom, and only 4 to 5 and a half feet deep, depending on the account. Those dimensions tell the story of the vessels it could serve, shallow craft suited to a narrow ditch cut through the coastal plain. Every boat that passed through depended on enough water to fill the locks and float the hulls. That dependence would prove to be the canal's weakness, because the Lowcountry did not always supply the water it needed.

## Undone by Drought and Rail

The Santee Canal was never a financial success. Traffic was sparse, the construction was poor, and droughts left the channel short of water. Then the railroads arrived, and their construction sealed the canal's fate. The state withdrew its charter in the early 1850s, and the canal slid into abandonment; by 1865 it was out of use entirely. For decades it lay abandoned. The final chapter came with a new kind of water project. Much of the old route was eventually covered by Lake Moultrie, the reservoir created in the early 1940s when the state utility Santee Cooper built its dams, begun in 1939, to bring electricity to rural South Carolina. The canal that once lacked water ended up beneath far more of it than it ever held.

## What Survives at Stony Landing

Not everything disappeared. Old Santee Canal Park in Moncks Corner sits at Stony Landing, the canal's former southernmost section, keeping the old waterway's name alive at the place where its route once ended. The 195-acre park is owned by Santee Cooper and lists daily hours of 9 a.m. to 5 p.m. The canal was named to the National Register of Historic Places in 1982, and the Historic American Buildings Survey documented it with photographs and records now held at the Library of Congress. Those records preserve something that time and the reservoir have largely erased: the scale of an early American attempt to reshape geography with a channel and ten locks. At Stony Landing, visitors can picture the boats that once passed through, and ponder how a waterway built for the future fell out of use within sixty-five years.
