# USS Keokuk (1862)

An experimental all-iron warship, USS Keokuk was riddled by about ninety Confederate shots at Charleston in 1863, limped away with the help of pilot Robert Smalls, sank off Morris Island, and then surrendered her great guns to a daring Confederate salvage crew.

**Location:** 32.6933, -79.8719

**Categories:** Ironclad warships of the Union navy, Shipwrecks of the American Civil War, Shipwrecks of the Carolina coast, Maritime incidents in April 1863, Semi-submersibles

## Introduction

The pilot who helped the riddled Keokuk limp out of range of Fort Sumter's guns had made an even bolder run through the same harbor eleven months earlier. In the early hours of May 13, 1862, Robert Smalls, then enslaved and piloting the Confederate steamer Planter, took her past Charleston's harbor defenses, Fort Sumter among them, with his wife Hannah, their children and the families of other crewmen aboard, about sixteen people in all, and steered for the Union blockade and freedom. By April 1863 he was the pilot of an experimental Union ironclad named Keokuk. She would last one month in commission, and her guns would end up fighting for the other side.

## An Iron Experiment

Named for the city of Keokuk, Iowa, the ship was laid down in New York City by her designer, Charles W. Whitney, at J.S. Underhill Shipbuilders at the head of 11th Street. She began life under another name, Moodna, sometimes misspelled "Woodna," and was renamed while under construction, launched in December 1862 with Whitney's wife as sponsor, and commissioned in early March 1863 under Commander Alexander C. Rhind. Keokuk was one of the first warships built entirely of iron, with wood used only for deck planks and as filler in her armor. Her bow and stern were flooding spaces that let her crew raise and lower her waterline. Two stationary, cone-shaped gun towers, each pierced with three gun ports, housed one 11-inch Dahlgren gun apiece, a layout that often led observers to mistake her for a double-turreted monitor. Her composite armor, iron bars alternating with yellow pine and sheathed in iron plate, totaled 5.75 inches. Nine steam engines powered her various systems.

## Two Towers Bound for Charleston

Keokuk left New York on March 11, 1863, to join the South Atlantic Blockading Squadron for the attack on Charleston, South Carolina. The voyage was not smooth: after a stop at Newport News, her port propeller fouled in a buoy's anchor line, forcing her back to Hampton Roads for repairs, and she did not reach Port Royal until March 26. A soldier who watched her pass wrote home about the "curious looking affair," describing "two turrets" and adding a sketch, though he admitted he was no artist. At Charleston she helped lay buoys to guide Rear Admiral Samuel Francis Du Pont's ironclad flotilla, seven monitors, the broadside ironclad New Ironsides and Keokuk, into the heavily fortified harbor. The ships crossed the bar on April 6, but hazy weather that obscured targets and blinded pilots held off the attack for another day.

## Half an Hour Under the Guns

The First Battle of Charleston Harbor began at noon on April 7. Clearing torpedoes from the ironclads' path slowed them, and only shortly after 3 p.m. did they come within range of Fort Moultrie and Fort Sumter. Obstructions and a strong flood tide made the ships almost unmanageable while the forts fired at will. When a shot struck the pilothouse of the monitor Nahant, killing one man and wounding others, Keokuk had to run ahead of the crippled ship in the narrow channel. That put her less than 600 yards from Fort Sumter, where she stayed for half an hour under the undivided attention of the Confederate cannons. About ninety projectiles struck her, many at or below the waterline, and her thin armor proved as inadequate as her chief engineer had predicted. Rhind called her "completely riddled." Yet she withdrew under her own power and anchored out of range, thanks in part to Smalls's skill. Her crew kept her afloat through the night, but when a breeze rose on the morning of April 8, she filled rapidly and sank off Morris Island. Fourteen of her crew were injured, Rhind among them, and Acting Ensign Mackintosh later died of his wounds. Quartermaster Robert Anderson received the Medal of Honor, in part for his actions that day.

## Guns Raised by Night

A Union survey concluded that the wreck, almost completely filled with sand, could be neither refloated nor destroyed, and her two 11-inch Dahlgrens, which could easily be replaced from stockpiles, were written off as unsalvageable. The Confederates saw it differently. None of their naval or coastal defense guns was as large as the sunken Dahlgrens, and General P.G.T. Beauregard, commanding the Charleston defenses, authorized an immediate salvage led by a civilian, Adolphus W. LaCoste. A modified lightship, Rattlesnake Shoals, was towed into place as a work platform. To avoid attracting attention, the crews worked at night and at low tide, guarded by the Confederate ironclads Palmetto State and Chicora. The Union suspected nothing until the Charleston Mercury announced that the guns had been recovered. Both served the Confederacy for the rest of the war. One was lost, probably destroyed during the evacuation of Charleston in 1865, though accounts differ. The other still stands on the Charleston waterfront at White Point Garden, mounted on an iron fortress carriage that is not its own.

## Flight Context

The wreck site is marked at about 32.6933 N, 79.8719 W, just off Morris Island at the southern entrance to Charleston Harbor; Fort Sumter lies roughly 3.5 nm to the north, and White Point Garden, where one of Keokuk's guns survives, is on the Charleston waterfront further up the harbor. From 1,500 to 2,000 feet, the harbor mouth, Fort Sumter and the shoreline of Morris Island make clear landmarks, though nothing of the wreck is visible. Charleston Executive Airport on Johns Island (KJZI) is about 7 nm west, Mount Pleasant Regional Airport (KLRO) is about 13 nm north-northeast, and Charleston International (KCHS) is about 15 nm northwest. The harbor sits under Charleston's busy terminal airspace, so coordinate with approach control. Sea fog can form over the harbor on cool mornings.
