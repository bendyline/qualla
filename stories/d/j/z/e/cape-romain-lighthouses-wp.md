# Cape Romain Lighthouses

Two brick lighthouses stand side by side on remote Lighthouse Island off McClellanville, South Carolina: an 1827 tower whose light proved too dim and its taller 1858 successor, which began leaning during construction and has never stopped.

**Location:** 33.0189, -79.3736

**Categories:** Lighthouses on the National Register of Historic Places in South Carolina, Lighthouses completed in 1827, Lighthouses completed in 1857, Inclined towers, Buildings and structures in Charleston County, South Carolina

## Introduction

According to local lore, Hepburn Morrison had a trick for visitors. The assistant keeper at Cape Romain from 1893 to 1896, listed on the official keeper roll as Robert H. Morrison, would climb to the very top of the lighthouse and stand on his head, his legs wrapped around the tip of the lightning rod. His granddaughter is said to have recalled that "the girls loved" his uniform, and it is easy to believe it. His stage was one of a pair of brick lighthouses on Lighthouse Island, southeast of McClellanville, South Carolina, now within the Cape Romain National Wildlife Refuge. The two towers, one short and one tall, were named to the National Register of Historic Places in 1981, and together they tell a story of danger, disappointment, earthquakes and isolation on the edge of the Atlantic.

## Shoals and a Dim Red Light

Cape Romain has worn several names. In the days of Spanish exploration it was known as Cape Roman, and then briefly as Cape Carteret. What never changed were its shoals, which reached seven to nine miles off the coast and were treacherous. Before there were lighthouses, many vessels ran onto them, and many people died as wind and waves claimed the ships for the sea. When lighthouses were finally built to warn mariners away, they saved many lives. The first lighthouse went up in 1827 on Northeast Raccoon Key, built by Winslow Lewis for about $12,000, after Congress first appropriated $10,000. It was a short, conical brick tower about 65 feet tall, with a focal height of about 87 and a half feet and a stationary red light fitted with 11 lamps and 21-inch reflectors. It was also a functional disappointment. Its red, whale oil wick lamp could not be seen beyond 9 to 14 nautical miles. Yet the old tower has outlasted its shortcomings, and today it is one of the few remaining lighthouses of its period in the United States.

## The Tower That Leans

In 1853, $20,000 was appropriated to build a second lighthouse nearby and to remove an old windmill on Mill Island, and a wick house, a boat house and two more dwellings joined the station. As the new brick tower rose, the builders noticed that it was leaning toward the mainland. The lean persists, and by some accounts the tower stands more than three feet off plumb. First lit on January 1, 1858, and about 150 feet tall, it carried a first-order Fresnel lens that keepers reached by climbing 212 spiraling cast-iron steps. The oil lamp threw a revolving beam visible for 19 miles. The tower was barely finished when the Civil War began and lighthouses along the Atlantic coast went dark. The light at Cape Romain was restored in 1866.

## The Night the Island Shook

On August 31, 1886, the great earthquake of that year reached the island. The keeper was in his house when a gradually increasing rumbling began, "sounding something like a battery of artillery or a troop of cavalry crossing a long bridge." Then the shocks came, the first lasting about two minutes, the next one as long, at intervals of about two minutes. The tower escaped injury, but its vibration was great. About a thousand birds described as "cranes" nested on the key during the summer months, and during the shock they were flying about "making a fearful noise." The account survives in Carlyle McKinley's descriptive narrative of the earthquake.

## Cape Parties and a Refuge

Life on the island was lonely. Seven miles from the mainland, lighthouse families found opportunities for socializing rare, and children had to be sent away to attend school. In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, villagers from McClellanville began holding "Cape Parties" on Lighthouse Island, playing games, swimming, fishing and collecting crabs and oysters to roast on the beach. In 1932 Lighthouse Island, Cape Island, Raccoon Key and thousands of acres of salt marsh and tidal creeks became part of the new Cape Romain Migratory Bird Refuge, managed then by the U.S. Biological Survey and now by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service. Because refuge funding goes to habitat and species rather than historic preservation, the towers have depended on partners; in 1983 a local restoration professional worked with refuge staff and volunteers to repair and repaint them. The refuge says it runs six guided boat tours to Lighthouse Island a year, though a 2023 breach has since divided the island. Visitors can walk the grounds, but the staircase of the taller tower still awaits repair.

## Flight Context

The Cape Romain Lighthouses stand on Lighthouse Island at 33.0189°N, 79.3736°W, in the marshes and barrier islands of the Cape Romain National Wildlife Refuge southeast of McClellanville, roughly 32 nm east-northeast of Charleston. From 1,500–2,500 feet, look for two towers, one short and one tall, close together amid salt marsh and tidal creeks. Nearby airports include Georgetown County Airport (KGGE), about 18 nm north, and Mount Pleasant Regional Airport (KLRO), about 22 nm west-southwest; Charleston International (KCHS) is about 34 nm west-southwest. This is a wildlife refuge, so fly high enough not to disturb nesting birds, and watch for sea fog and summer thunderstorms along the coast.
