# Myrtle Beach Pavilion

For nearly six decades an 11-acre amusement park at Ocean Boulevard and 9th Avenue North was the heart of Myrtle Beach, home to a 1912 carousel of dragons and ostriches and a band organ built for the 1900 Paris Exposition.

**Location:** 33.6925, -78.8816

**Categories:** Defunct amusement parks in the United States, Amusement parks in South Carolina, Buildings and structures in Myrtle Beach, South Carolina, Amusement parks closed in 2006, Carousels

## Introduction

Long before it played for beachgoers in Myrtle Beach, the Pavilion's band organ was built for the 1900 World Exposition in Paris. Hand-crafted by A. Ruth & Sohn in Waldkirch, Baden, Germany, it traveled around Europe on a wagon after the fair and landed on the South Carolina coast in 1954. For decades its twirling figures and cymbal-playing cherubs entertained crowds at the Myrtle Beach Pavilion, the 11-acre amusement park at the corner of 9th Avenue North and Ocean Boulevard that anchored the heart of town until it was lost to redevelopment.

## Three Pavilions, Two Fires

The name belonged to a building long before it belonged to a park. Burroughs & Chapin, one of the companies that developed the Myrtle Beach area, built a series of Pavilions, each in a different architectural style and even different materials, but all meant as a place where the community could gather. The first, a one-story building put up around 1902 and expanded in 1908, belonged to Myrtle Beach's first hotel, the long-gone Seaside Inn. Fire destroyed it. A two-story Pavilion rose in 1923 and was expanded in 1938, only for another fire in 1944 to burn it to the ground. The third time, the builders used poured concrete, and the new Pavilion opened in 1949.

## The Carnival That Stayed

The amusement park grew up across Ocean Boulevard from the new Pavilion building, starting in 1948, and it began with a traveling carnival. The show had stopped in Conway, South Carolina, for the annual Tobacco Festival, then found a permanent home across from the Pavilion after signing an agreement with Myrtle Beach Farms. Ice skaters, bear acts and talent shows were brought in right away to supplement the carnival rides and build interest in the new park. In 1950, just two years in, the company now called Burroughs & Chapin bought out the park's owners, the Central Amusement Company, and added 14 new rides and new concessions. Over the decades the Pavilion swapped in carnival-style flat rides, weathered several hurricanes, and grew to roughly 40 attractions, eventually including the roller coaster Hurricane: Category 5. A few blocks away, Family Kingdom Amusement Park shared the heart of the resort.

## Dragons, Ostriches and One Horse

The park's two treasures were older than the park itself. Its Herschell-Spillman carousel dates to 1912, and alongside the jumping and standing horses it carries a menagerie of frogs, lions, ostriches, zebras, giraffes and even a dragon. Carousel registries count more than twenty of these menagerie figures. Despite the harsh seaside climate, the carousel was well maintained, and it was described as one of the few working Herschell-Spillman carousels left in the country. Then there was the band organ, bought by Myrtle Beach Farms in 1954. It was a giant: 20 feet wide, 11 feet high, 7 feet deep and two tons in weight, with more than 400 pipes and 98 keys, playing tunes punched into cardboard music. Its "twirling figures and cherubs that play cymbals, bells and drums" made it as much a show as an instrument.

## The Last Ride

The end came at the height of the park's popularity. The farewell 2006 season drew record attendance and profits, yet Burroughs & Chapin cited financial instability in shutting the park down, and citizens' efforts to save it failed. The season closed to the public on September 24, 2006, and a select group returned for a "Last Ride" event on September 30. Demolition of the oceanfront Pavilion and the amusement area ran from December 2006 into the summer of 2007. The Haunted Hotel and the multimillion-dollar Hurricane coaster were torn down, though Hurricane's trains found a second life at Kings Island in Mason, Ohio, running on Son of Beast until that coaster closed in 2009. The carousel and the organ moved to Broadway at the Beach, also owned by Burroughs & Chapin, where a smaller Pavilion Nostalgia Park opened on July 4, 2007. In June 2014 the organ was moved out of public view, and when people complained, the company said it planned to find it a new home.

## A Lot Full of Memories

For years afterward, the 11-acre site sat mostly empty, and as of early 2026 it still did, apart from the festival weekend each summer; the city and the property owner were exploring redevelopment options, with no timeline announced. Visitors kept coming to the neighborhood anyway, drawn by survivors such as the Gay Dolphin Gift Cove, and a historical marker commemorating the Pavilion now stands at the northeast corner of the oceanfront lot, along the new boardwalk. The land has tried out new roles. A zip line attraction was planned for the old site in 2012; by 2017 it was adding more lines and a ropes course, strung high enough that concerts could continue underneath, and it closed in January 2024. A proposal for a temporary carnival on part of the lot in 2013 was dropped after resistance from the city. Between 8th and 9th Avenues North, Burroughs and Chapin Pavilion Place has become an event and concert venue, and each year the Carolina Country Music Fest brings the crowds back to the Pavilion's old corner of town.

## Flight Context

Coordinates: 33.6925 N, 78.8816 W, on the oceanfront at Ocean Boulevard and 9th Avenue North in central Myrtle Beach, South Carolina. The Pavilion is gone; look for the open event lot along the boardwalk in the dense line of high-rise hotels. The site lies just 2.5 nm east-northeast of Myrtle Beach International Airport (KMYR), inside its Class C airspace, so coordinate with approach and fly the beach at the altitude ATC assigns. Other airports: Grand Strand Airport in North Myrtle Beach (KCRE) about 11 nm northeast; Conway-Horry County Airport (KHYW) about 15 nm northwest. Summer brings heavy beach traffic, sea breezes and afternoon thunderstorms.
