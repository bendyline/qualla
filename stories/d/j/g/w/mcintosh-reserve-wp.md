# McIntosh Reserve

A family park on the Chattahoochee in Carroll County, Georgia, occupies the plantation where Creek leader William McIntosh was executed in 1825 for signing away his nation's land.

**Location:** 33.4455, -84.9567

**Categories:** Protected areas of Carroll County, Georgia, Parks in Georgia (U.S. state), Chattahoochee River, Muscogee history, Native American history

## Introduction

On hot summer days, children dash through the sprinklers of a splash pad here, and riders on horseback play polocrosse in the big field. Across the road from a reconstructed log house lies a single grave. The man buried in it, the Creek leader William McIntosh Jr., died on this ground in 1825, executed by order of his own nation's council for signing away land that was never his alone to sell.

## Acorn Bluff

McIntosh called his plantation Lochau Talofau, which means "Acorn Bluff" in English, and it lay beside Acorn Creek above the Chattahoochee River. He was born in the Lower Creek town of Coweta, in present-day Georgia, to a Scottish-American soldier, also named William McIntosh, and to Senoya, a Muscogee woman of the Wind Clan. He became a prominent leader and a planter, and he had powerful connections: one of his cousins, George Troup, became governor of Georgia while McIntosh was a leading chief. His home, though, was modest. It was a two-story log house with an open central passage, a "dog run," on both floors, and it doubled as an inn for travelers. A reconstruction of that house stands in the park, and the county lists a log cabin among its historic sites.

## A Signature at Indian Springs

On February 12, 1825, McIntosh and a small group of other chiefs signed the Treaty of Indian Springs. Its effect was sweeping: it ceded almost all of the remaining Creek lands in Georgia, plus some 3 million acres in Alabama, to the United States government. In exchange for his signature, McIntosh was allowed to keep his plantation. The Creek National Council had opposed the treaty, and signing it broke Creek law as set down in the Code of 1818. Under that law, ceding the nation's communal lands was a capital offense against the Creek government. The Council ordered the execution of McIntosh and the other signatories. The sentence came not from a mob but from the nation's governing council, enforcing its own written law.

## The Law Menders Arrive

On April 30, 1825 (the county's park history says May 1), a force of Law Menders, put at 120 to 150 men in some accounts and far more in others, reached the plantation. They were led by Menawa, a Red Stick leader who had long been McIntosh's political opponent. The warriors lit bonfires around the buildings and then set McIntosh's house on fire, and McIntosh was killed at his home. His single-plot military grave lies just across the road from the reconstructed house. The treaty he died for did not stand. The U.S. government set the 1825 agreement aside and negotiated a new one, the 1826 Treaty of Washington, which allowed the Muscogee to keep about 3 million acres in Alabama.

## Trails Along the River

Carroll County acquired 527 acres of Lochau Talofau in 1978, and the old plantation now lies within McIntosh Reserve, a county park that the county lists at 1,750 acres and runs through its recreation department. Trails wind through the park for hikers, cyclists and riders on horseback. There are two ponds, primitive campsites, a scenic overlook on the Chattahoochee, and a broad, flat field popular with model airplane hobbyists. The river has not always been gentle: flooding in September 2009 closed the park for several months. Nearby Whitesburg offers access for rafting and canoeing. The park has hosted an Easter Festival, a Halloween Carnival, a Santa Program and a Fall Festival with a Native American pow-wow of traditional music and dance on the old Creek plantation; check the county's park calendar for current events. The county lists the park as open daily, year-round.

## Flight Context

McIntosh Reserve lies at 33.4455 N, 84.9567 W along the Chattahoochee River in Carroll County, Georgia, near the town of Whitesburg. Follow the Chattahoochee; the park's open grassy field and two ponds sit among woodland on the riverbank. A viewing altitude of 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL frames the river and the park well. Newnan Coweta County Airport (FAA identifier CCO) is about 12 nm to the southeast, and West Georgia Regional Airport / O V Gray Field at Carrollton (FAA identifier CTJ) about 15 nm to the northwest. Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International (KATL) is about 29 nm to the east-northeast, so expect busy Atlanta airspace in that direction. Best viewed in clear conditions.
