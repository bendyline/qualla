# Fort Mitchell Historic Site

A reconstructed 1813 stockade in eastern Alabama tells three chapters of the Creek nation's dealings with the United States - war, trade, and finally forced removal along the Trail of Tears.

**Location:** 32.3519, -85.0217

## Introduction

Beside a reconstructed stockade in eastern Alabama, a ceremonial flame memorial honors the Creek nation - the people this fort was built to fight, then to trade with, and finally to see removed from their land. Fort Mitchell Historic Site, a park and archaeological site declared a National Historic Landmark in 1990, holds all three chapters of that relationship in one place: the war post of 1813, the government trading factory that followed, and the garrison that stood here as the Creek were forced west along the Trail of Tears.

## A Stockade for a War

The first Fort Mitchell went up in 1813 as a United States outpost during the Creek War and the War of 1812. It was named for David Brydie Mitchell, a governor of Georgia, and it represented the military edge of American expansion into Creek territory. The Red Sticks - the traditionalist faction of the Creek Nation, drawn mostly from the Upper Towns, who opposed that expansion - were defeated, and in 1814 the Creek were forced to cede more than 20 million acres of land in Georgia and Alabama, a loss that reached both the Lower and the Upper Towns. Today the park's reconstruction of the 1813 stockade gives that first chapter a physical shape, complete with a blockhouse at one corner. Inside the walls stands a blacksmith shop where historical demonstrations are periodically presented.

## Traders, Agents, and a Tavern

In 1817 the post took on a new role. An Indian Factory - a government trading post staffed by the assistant U.S. Indian agent - was transferred here from Fort Hawkins in Georgia, operating until about 1820, and a post office ran at the site from 1818 to 1820 as well. Outside the stockade, the grounds now include a tavern, a pioneer garden and an 1840s cabin. The agency itself changed hands in 1821, when Col. John Crowell was appointed U.S. Indian agent to the Creek, replacing the fort's namesake: David Brydie Mitchell had been pushed out over a scandal involving the illegal smuggling of enslaved Africans. Crowell's brothers Thomas and Henry came with him to set up the agency, and by 1825 Thomas was running a tavern near the fort. That same year, the U.S. Army rebuilt Fort Mitchell, and soldiers would remain here until 1840.

## The Road West

The third chapter is the hardest. It represents the federal government's attempt to live up to its treaty obligations, including payment of annuities and supplies and recognition of sovereignty. Those promises frayed. Fort Mitchell was garrisoned during the Second Creek War of 1836, which erupted in Alabama after speculators and squatters defrauded Creek families of their allotted lands and the state moved to end tribal government and extend its laws over them. Afterward, the U.S. government forcibly removed most Creek people in Alabama to Indian Territory. Those who stayed behind had to give up their tribal membership and were considered citizens of the state and the United States instead. The Chattahoochee Indian Heritage Center, adjacent to the historic site, keeps that history in view: its interpretive panels recount the Creek War of 1836 and the forced removal of the Creek peoples along the Trail of Tears, beside the ceremonial flame memorial to the Creek nation.

## What the Ground Remembered

By 1971 only ruins remained of the old fort. That year professional archaeologists began excavating the site and found gravesites, the remains of a hospital, the old fort, outbuildings, offices, barracks, and storage rooms; the Alabama Archaeological Association published a report on the work in 1974. Today the park includes a visitor center and museum on the fort's history, a carriage house, an 1840s cabin and historic burial grounds, and it is free to visit, open Friday through Sunday according to the site's own listing. The site's connection to military service continues, too. In 1987 the Fort Mitchell National Cemetery was established beside the site for the burial of United States veterans from Alabama and Georgia, adding a modern resting place to ground that has seen so much history.
