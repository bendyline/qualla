# Fort McClellan

In the foothills above Anniston, an Army post trained half a million World War II soldiers, housed prisoners of war, schooled the Army's chemical troops and every woman entering the service, then gave much of its land back to mountain longleaf pine.

**Location:** 33.7108, -85.7372

**Categories:** Former installations of the United States Army, Forts in Alabama, Military history of Alabama, World War II sites in the United States, Anniston, Alabama

## Introduction

Some of the last mountain longleaf pine forests in the southeastern United States grow on the old reservation of an Army post where World War II recruits once crouched in foxholes as tanks moved overhead. The 9,016-acre Mountain Longleaf National Wildlife Refuge occupies part of what was Fort McClellan, a post in the Choccolocco foothills beside Anniston, Alabama, that over eight decades trained an estimated half-million World War II troops, housed enemy prisoners, schooled the Army's chemical warfare specialists and military police, and served as the home of the Women's Army Corps. Its story mirrors the twentieth-century Army itself.

## Hills Made for Artillery

Anniston began in 1872 as Woodstock, a private industrial town of the Woodstock Iron Company. The Army first came briefly in 1898, when Camp Shipp quartered nearly 10,000 troops, among them the 3rd Alabama Volunteer Infantry, during the Spanish–American War year, then served as a hospital during an influenza epidemic before closing in 1899. It honored Lieutenant William E. Shipp, killed leading a charge at San Juan Hill. In 1912, Congressman Henry D. Clayton Jr. pointed the War Department to the Choccolocco Mountains, and Army War College officials found the terrain highly suitable for artillery training. The government bought 18,952 acres in March 1917 and established Camp McClellan that July, named for Union general George B. McClellan and one of 32 camps built to train men quickly for World War I. About 1,500 hastily built structures went up, and by October more than 27,000 soldiers from New Jersey, Virginia, Maryland, Delaware and Washington, D.C., were training there with the 29th Infantry Division, alongside units such as the 1st Separate Negro Company of Maryland.

## Half a Million Soldiers

The camp became permanent Fort McClellan in 1929, and Depression-era WPA money built roads, an airfield and buildings largely in the Spanish Colonial Revival style. As war approached, the New York National Guard's 27th Infantry Division arrived in October 1940 and lived mostly in tents until December 1941. Then came an enormous expansion: housing for up to 50,000 personnel, 74 miles of new roads, and a general hospital of 80 wooden buildings linked by 4.5 miles of catwalks. A 22,168-acre tract to the west became Pelham Range for artillery and tank fire. At its peak the post held 2,170 officers and 42,126 enlisted soldiers. Recruits trained in simulated urban combat, advanced under live artillery fire and huddled in foxholes as tanks passed overhead. The African American soldiers of the 92nd Infantry Division trained here in 1942 and 1943 before fighting in Italy.

## Prisoners and Secret Tests

In 1943 the Army built a prisoner-of-war camp for 3,000 men, which also processed prisoners bound for three other Alabama camps. When the war in Europe ended it held 2,546 captives. A cemetery on the reservation marks 26 German and 3 Italian prisoners who died in captivity. Placed on inactive status in 1947, the post revived in 1951 during the Korean War, and in 1952 its Chemical Corps School opened. In September 1953, in a program highly secret at the time and very controversial once revealed, Operation Top Hat used Chemical Corps personnel to test decontamination methods for biological and chemical weapons, including sulfur mustard and nerve agents. The Department of Veterans Affairs has since acknowledged that some personnel may have been exposed to hazardous materials during service at Fort McClellan, likely at low levels, and a bill to create a health registry for those exposed was introduced in the 2017–2018 Congress.

## Where Women Joined the Army

The Women's Army Corps School was founded at Fort McClellan on September 25, 1952, and about two years later the post became the first permanent home of the WAC Center. For the next generation, every woman entering the Army as an officer or enlisted soldier was received, processed and trained here. When the Corps was disestablished in 1978 and its flag retired, Major General Mary E. Clarke, its last director, took part in the final ceremony. She soon became commanding general of Fort McClellan itself, the first woman ever to command a major U.S. Army installation. The post kept adding missions. An infantry training brigade prepared more than 30,000 men for Vietnam, and in 1969 Fort McClellan was the only Army installation in the country with three major missions. The Military Police School arrived in 1975, and in 1988 a single Border Patrol class trained at a satellite academy built here.

## Longleaf Takes the Ground Back

The 1995 Base Realignment and Closure Commission voted to shut the post, and the official closing ceremony came on May 20, 1999. The Chemical School and Military Police School moved to Fort Leonard Wood, Missouri. Part of the land remains in use by the Alabama National Guard for field training and its Officer Candidate School, and FEMA, part of the Department of Homeland Security, runs its Center for Domestic Preparedness here. Clearing unexploded ordnance took eleven years, finishing in 2014. The developed core has become McClellan, a master-planned, mixed-use community of about 10,000 acres that, by the development authority's late-2010s count, was home to more than 900 residents and a workplace for more than 3,000 employees; in November 2024 the authority's oversight committee recommended dissolving it and handing redevelopment to the City of Anniston, and in March 2025 its board voted to transfer its assets to the city while it kept overseeing the environmental cleanup funds. Another part belongs to the longleaf. Established in 2003, the wildlife refuge is still young, with only its eastern portion open to the public, reached through the eastern gate on Bain's Gap Road, while the rest stays closed pending unexploded-ordnance cleanup, and with ongoing work to heal land worn by decades of field training.

## Flight Context

Fort McClellan lies at 33.711N, 85.737W on the northeast edge of Anniston, Alabama, in the Choccolocco foothills of the southern Appalachians. From 3,500 to 5,000 feet, look for the old post's buildings and streets along the road north toward Jacksonville, with forested ridges rising to the east and the Anniston urban area to the southwest. Anniston Regional Airport (KANB) is about 9.5 nm to the southwest, and Northeast Alabama Regional Airport (KGAD) at Gadsden about 24 nm to the northwest. The Alabama National Guard still trains on part of the reservation and Pelham Range lies to the west, so check charts and NOTAMs for restricted or military activity before flying low over the area.
