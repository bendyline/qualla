# Tuskegee Veterans Administration Medical Center

Built in 1923 for Black veterans of World War I who were turned away elsewhere, the Tuskegee hospital became a battleground over whether Black doctors and nurses would be allowed to run it.

**Location:** 32.4453, -85.7129

**Categories:** National Register of Historic Places in Macon County, Alabama, Veterans Affairs medical facilities, Old soldiers' homes in the United States, African-American history, 1923 establishments in Alabama

## Introduction

Colonel Robert H. Stanley had already been in Tuskegee for two days when anyone thought to tell Robert Russa Moton. The Veterans' Bureau had promised the president of Tuskegee Institute, categorically, that he would be consulted before a superintendent was named for the new veterans' hospital rising beside his campus. Instead a white officer arrived to take charge, and Moton heard about it after the fact. That small humiliation was one moment in a much larger struggle over a hospital built for Black veterans of World War I, and over who would be permitted to care for them.

## Ground Given by a College

The institution opened in 1923 as the Tuskegee Home, described as an old soldiers' home, and the National Register of Historic Places classes it among the nation's second generation of veterans hospitals. It grew on 464 acres next to the Tuskegee Normal and Industrial Institute, now Tuskegee University, and the institute itself donated 300 of those acres. Over time the complex reached 27 buildings. The hospital opened with 600 beds and 250 patients. Its purpose was long-term care for the 300,000 African-American veterans of World War I in the segregated South, men who had served their country and then found care denied or neglected at other veterans' hospitals and old soldiers' homes. Two conditions shaped its early work: tuberculosis, and the mental illness that followed combat, then called shell shock. They were the two diseases most often diagnosed in veterans after the war.

## Whose Hospital?

Civil rights groups, the NAACP among them, had lobbied Washington to accept its responsibility for these veterans, from health care to employment and retraining. Yet once Congress authorized the Tuskegee hospital, white residents of the city and the state, who had resisted caring for Black veterans at all, began maneuvering to control its medical jobs. Black Americans saw the hospital differently. For Black doctors and nurses, whose opportunities were sharply restricted, especially in Alabama, it could be a rare place of professional work. Moton stood in the middle. He needed to keep the peace locally, but the NAACP and the National Medical Association, an organization of Black physicians, pressed him hard. Together with their representatives he went directly to President Warren G. Harding, warning that the Republican Party would lose the support of Black citizens, many of whom had backed the nation in the war, unless they were given jobs at the center.

## Threats and a Broken Promise

Harding directed regional and veterans' officials to recruit Black doctors and nurses, and eventually committed to reserving those positions for them. White opposition in Tuskegee grew so intense that Moton received threats on his life and made plans to leave on a lecture tour. The Harding administration had made him a sort of referee over the hospital's personnel on behalf of the nation's millions of Black Americans, which made Stanley's unannounced appointment all the more pointed. The Ku Klux Klan staged protests against the hospital, and by one account members of a local chapter marched on it. The intimidation backfired: it fed a broader campaign by the NAACP and the National Medical Association that drew national attention. By 1924, by one account, the situation had calmed, and Brigadier General Frank T. Hines, director of the Veterans Bureau, appointed Dr. Joseph H. Ward, an African American, to head the complex. Ward led it until 1936.

## A Campus That Endured

Ward was followed by Dr. Eugene Dibble, who served from 1936 to 1946, and then by Dr. Toussaint T. Tildon, who led the center until 1958. In 1930 three federal agencies were combined into the Veterans Administration, and the Tuskegee center passed to its authority. In the decades that followed, many additional residency programs were established on the grounds, training new generations of physicians where Black doctors had once had to fight simply to be hired. Since 1997 the complex has been part of the Central Alabama Veterans Health Care System, known as the East Campus among its sites in Alabama and Georgia, and the VA still lists it as an open medical center around the clock. On March 19, 2012, it was listed on the National Register of Historic Places, and it also appears on the Alabama Register of Landmarks and Heritage.

## Flight Context

The medical center sits at 32.4453 N, 85.7129 W on the edge of Tuskegee, Alabama, directly beside the Tuskegee University campus; look for the cluster of institutional brick buildings set among open grounds and trees. A viewing altitude of 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL shows the hospital and the university together. The closest field is Moton Field Municipal Airport (FAA identifier 06A; no ICAO code), about 2 nm away and home to the Tuskegee Airmen National Historic Site. Auburn University Regional Airport (KAUO) lies about 17 nm to the northeast, and Montgomery Regional (Dannelly Field) Airport (KMGM) about 36 nm to the west. Best viewed in clear conditions; summer haze can soften detail at a distance.
