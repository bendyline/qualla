# 1996 Summer Olympics opening ceremony

On a July night in 1996, Atlanta welcomed the Centennial Olympic Games with John Williams, a mechanical thunderbird, fifty-foot shadow athletes and, at the end of a flame's long journey, Muhammad Ali.

**Location:** 33.7356, -84.3894

**Categories:** 1996 Summer Olympics, Olympics opening ceremonies, Ceremonies in the United States, Atlanta Symphony Orchestra, Sports in Atlanta

## Introduction

The flame had passed through more than 10,000 hands on its way to Atlanta. On the night of Friday, July 19, 1996, it entered Centennial Olympic Stadium with Evander Holyfield, circled the track, and climbed a long ramp toward the stadium's northern end in the grip of swimmer Janet Evans. Waiting for it was Muhammad Ali, the former Olympic gold medalist and boxing champion, by then living with Parkinson's disease. He lit the cauldron before a crowd of more than 80,000 in the stadium and an estimated 3.5 billion watching around the world. It was the climax of a ceremony built to celebrate a century of the modern Games.

## Counting Down a Century

Directed and produced by Don Mischer, the evening began with a countdown on the big screen, each number paired with footage of an earlier Games, from Athens in 1896 at twenty-two down to Barcelona at one, before Atlanta appeared at zero with fireworks. A clip from Barcelona's 1992 closing ceremony showed IOC President Juan Antonio Samaranch inviting the world's athletes to Atlanta. In answer, spirits in the five Olympic colors rose from a corner of the stadium, each to its own percussion, and summoned tribes of performers onto the floor. U.S. Army paratroopers rappelled from the roof trailing long colored streamers while the tribes formed the Olympic rings and the Children of Atlanta, dressed in white, spelled out the number 100. Then John Williams raised his baton over the Atlanta Symphony Orchestra for "Summon the Heroes," the piece he wrote for the 1996 Games, as the children reshaped themselves into a flying dove.

## Pickup Trucks and Georgia on My Mind

President Bill Clinton made his entrance with organizing committee chief Billy Payne and Samaranch, the American flag rose to the national anthem, and Air Force Thunderbirds roared overhead. The artistic program opened loudly with "Welcome to the World," written by Jimmy Jam and Terry Lewis, a celebration of American inventions such as cheerleading, marching bands and stepping. College-football spirit and an all-American party spilled across the field, chromed Chevrolet pickups rolled through with spotlights in their beds, and the crowd joined in with the wave. The mood softened when Gladys Knight sang "Georgia on My Mind," the state's official song. Among the audience that night sat former president Jimmy Carter and Coretta Scott King, alongside dozens of world leaders from Jacques Chirac and Helmut Kohl to South Africa's deputy president, Thabo Mbeki.

## Butterflies and a Thunderbird

The longest artistic segment, "Summertime: The Beauty of the South," was meant to evoke the antebellum South and its rebirth after the Civil War, told through the ceremony's own allegory. James Earl Jones read from Zora Neale Hurston, William Faulkner and Mark Twain. Two performers in vast butterfly wings, the Sun and the Moon, met at center field to create an operatic Southern Spirit, and dancers dressed as butterflies and fireflies moved to jazz, gospel, "Ol' Man River" and "When the Saints Go Marching In." Then a huge mechanical thunderbird rose from the north end and smothered them under a gray cover, the ceremony's image of the war and Sherman's March to the Sea. Slowly the Spirit's voice revived the fallen, fifty multicolored butterflies emerged, and the audience lit the stadium with flashlights as the Morehouse and Spelman College glee clubs joined the Atlanta Symphony Orchestra Chorus in gospel.

## Giants on a Screen

Darkness fell again, and a temple to Zeus appeared at the center of the stadium: eight pillars of 2,500 pounds each hoisted an octagonal screen into place. Inside it, a 20,000-watt arc light cast the shadows of dancers as archers, discus throwers, wrestlers and runners, silhouettes reaching nearly fifty feet tall, to music by Basil Poledouris. Pierre de Coubertin's voice then proclaimed the rebirth of the modern Games, runners representing each past host circled the track, and the Parade of Nations began with Greece. Delegations from about 197 nations marched, the United States last as host. Eight former American Olympians carried in the Olympic flag to "Simple Gifts," a hundred children ran papier-mâché doves around the stadium, and the stadium heard portions of Atlanta native Martin Luther King Jr.'s "I Have a Dream" speech. The evening also honored Leon Štukelj, then the oldest living Olympic gold medalist, whose first gold came in Paris in 1924.

## The Flame Comes Home

Al Oerter carried the torch to the stadium and handed it to Holyfield, who emerged from a network of tunnels onto a stage in mid-field as Beethoven's "Ode to Joy" played. Greek athlete Voula Patoulidou joined him for a lap, a link between the first modern Games and this centennial edition, before Evans took the final run. After Ali lit the cauldron, basketball star Teresa Edwards spoke the Athletes' Oath and diving official Hobie Billingsley the officials' oath. Celine Dion sang David Foster's "The Power of the Dream," which became the unofficial anthem of the Atlanta Games, and Jessye Norman closed the night with "Faster, Higher, Stronger." The stadium itself went on to other lives, rebuilt as Turner Field, home of the Atlanta Braves for twenty seasons, and later as Georgia State University's football stadium, Center Parc Credit Union Stadium.

## Flight Context

The former Centennial Olympic Stadium, now Center Parc Credit Union Stadium, stands at 33.736N, 84.389W just south of downtown Atlanta, near the junction of the Interstate 75/85 Downtown Connector and Interstate 20. From 4,000 to 5,000 feet, look for the stadium bowl beside the interstate south of the downtown skyline. Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International Airport (KATL) is only about 6 nm to the south, and the stadium sits deep within Atlanta's Class B airspace, so a clearance is required and sightseeing is usually easiest from a departure or arrival over the city. Fulton County Executive Airport-Charlie Brown Field (KFTY, formerly Fulton County Airport-Brown Field) lies about 7 nm to the west-northwest and DeKalb-Peachtree Airport (KPDK) about 9.5 nm to the north-northeast.
