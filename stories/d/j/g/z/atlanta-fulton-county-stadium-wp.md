# Atlanta–Fulton County Stadium

Built on a mayor's gamble before Atlanta even had a team, the "Launching Pad" hosted the Braves for 31 seasons, Hank Aaron's 715th home run, and Atlanta's first World Series title before its implosion in 1997.

**Location:** 33.7394, -84.3894

**Categories:** Demolished sports venues, Atlanta Braves stadiums, Atlanta Falcons stadiums, Venues of the 1996 Summer Olympics, Baseball history, Atlanta, Georgia

## Introduction

In a parking lot south of downtown Atlanta, a wall has long marked a spot where nothing seemed to be happening. It stood where there was once a ballpark around it: the landing point of the 715th home run of Hank Aaron's career, the swing on April 8, 1974, that made him baseball's all-time home run leader. The stadium itself is gone, imploded in 1997, and only an outline in the pavement recalled where the stands once rose. Georgia State University has now begun building a baseball stadium on the footprint, with a new marker for Aaron's home run planned in left field. For 31 seasons, though, Atlanta–Fulton County Stadium was where the city learned to be a big-league town.

## Build It and They Will Come

The stadium began as a campaign promise. Running for mayor in 1961, Ivan Allen Jr. pledged to build a sports facility that could lure a Major League Baseball team. Once elected, he chose a 47-acre site in the Washington–Rawson neighborhood, close to the state capitol, downtown and major highways. Half a century earlier it had been a wealthy district, home to Georgia's governor among others, but by the 1960s it had fallen on hard times, and it was cleared for the stadium. A first courtship, of Charlie Finley's Kansas City Athletics, collapsed in July 1963 when the American League refused the move. Allen built anyway. Ground was broken on April 15, 1964; that November the Milwaukee Braves signed a 25-year agreement to play there from 1966. The $18 million stadium was completed on April 9, 1965, and that summer, on August 18, the Beatles played there.

## The Launching Pad

In 1966 the relocated Braves and the brand-new Atlanta Falcons moved in together, and on April 12 Joe Torre hit the first major league home run in the stadium's history. The building was one of the era's look-alike multipurpose bowls, and like the others it suited neither sport well: in baseball configuration, 70 percent of the seats sat in foul territory. It had one quirk, though. The football field ran along a line between first and third base, so a seat behind home plate was also on the 50-yard line. At 1,050 feet above sea level, it was the highest ballpark in baseball for 27 seasons, until the Colorado Rockies arrived in 1993. Thin air and Southern summer heat sent fly balls carrying, and the stadium earned the nickname "The Launching Pad." Until the Florida Marlins came along, it was also reputed to be the hottest park in the majors.

## Seven Hundred Fifteen

Its most famous night came on April 8, 1974, when Hank Aaron hit his 715th home run off the Los Angeles Dodgers' Al Downing and passed every hitter who had come before him. Aaron had already homered here in the 1972 All-Star Game, which the National League won 4–3 in ten innings. The stadium took on its compound name, Atlanta–Fulton County Stadium, after the county threatened to withdraw its financial support; by one account that happened on February 11, 1975, though the Braves date the change to after Ted Turner bought the team in 1976. The field saw odder moments, too. In August 1984 a beanball feud with the San Diego Padres set off a series of bench-clearing brawls, and on July 6, 1986, Bob Horner became only the eleventh major leaguer to hit four home runs in a single game.

## October Comes to Atlanta

The stadium hosted its first World Series in 1991, when the Braves met the Minnesota Twins in a series ESPN judged the best ever played. Atlanta won all three games at home, two of them in its final at-bat, and still lost in seven. The Toronto Blue Jays beat them in 1992. Then, on October 28, 1995, Tom Glavine threw eight innings of one-hit ball, and a 1–0 win over Cleveland gave the Braves their first championship in Atlanta, making them winners in each of the three cities they had called home. In 1996 the stadium hosted the Olympic baseball tournament while the Braves were away on a three-week road trip. Its last game was Game 5 of the 1996 World Series, a 1–0 loss to the New York Yankees. Former President Jimmy Carter, who had thrown out first pitches as governor, threw the final ceremonial one.

## Home Plate Crosses the Street

On April 4, 1997, home plate was removed and carried to the new Turner Field next door, the converted Olympic stadium, where Hank Aaron himself installed it. The old stadium's interior was stripped that spring and summer, and on August 2, 1997, the shell was imploded. Its outfield fence found a second life at the Georgia State Baseball Complex. Georgia State University agreed to buy the site and Turner Field in 2016 and closed the sale in January 2017, as the Braves departed for suburban Cobb County, and the old footprint, a parking lot since the demolition, then served the university's Center Parc Credit Union Stadium. In May 2024 the University System's Board of Regents approved a new baseball stadium on the site, first targeted for the 2026 season; construction began in late 2025, completion is now scheduled for fall 2026, with the first season expected in 2027, and the Aaron statue is to move to its entrance. It will bring the game back to the ground where Aaron's record ball came down.

## Flight Context

The former stadium site lies at about 33.739 N, 84.389 W, just south of downtown Atlanta. From above, look for Center Parc Credit Union Stadium (the former Turner Field) and, beside it, the old stadium footprint where Georgia State is building its baseball stadium, with the old stadium's outline built into the surrounding pavement. The site sits under Atlanta's busy terminal airspace, only about 6.5 nm north of Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International (KATL), so any sightseeing flight requires ATC clearance and close attention to arrival and departure traffic. Fulton County Executive Airport-Charlie Brown Field (KFTY, formerly Fulton County Airport-Brown Field, about 7 nm west-northwest) and DeKalb-Peachtree (KPDK, about 9 nm north-northeast) are the general-aviation reliever airports. Summer haze and thunderstorms are common.
