# Delta Flight Museum

Born from retirees' hunt for a lost DC-3, the Delta Flight Museum fills two 1940s hangars beside Atlanta's airport with a restored Ship 41, a 767 bought by employees, and the very first Boeing 747-400.

**Location:** 33.6561, -84.4223

**Categories:** Aerospace museums, Delta Air Lines, Aviation history, Hartsfield–Jackson Atlanta International Airport, Museums in Atlanta

## Introduction

In 1990 a group of Delta Air Lines retirees set out to find an airplane. They were looking for one of the five Douglas DC-3s the airline had bought new in the early 1940s, and after some searching they found Ship 41 in Puerto Rico, still earning its keep hauling cargo. Delta bought it in June 1993 and flew it home to Atlanta, setting in motion everything that followed. Their restoration project, combined with an effort to gather the company's scattered historic collections, grew into the Delta Flight Museum, housed today in two 1940s hangars at Delta's headquarters in Hapeville, Georgia, beside the airline's main hub at Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International Airport.

## Ship 41 Comes Home

The museum was incorporated as an independent nonprofit on May 23, 1995, and the work on Ship 41 was painstaking. Active and retired Delta employees and volunteers restored the DC-3 to its exact original configuration and appearance on the day it was first delivered to the airline, January 4, 1941, finishing on October 8, 1999. The result is considered one of the most faithfully restored passenger DC-3s in the world. In 2001 it became the first aircraft to receive an award from the National Trust for Historic Preservation, according to the museum. Ship 41 is the only Delta passenger DC-3 still in existence, and Delta is the only major carrier known to still own its first new passenger-carrying DC-3.

## Crop Dusters and a Café

The Legacy Hangar, formerly Hangar One and the Propeller Era, reaches back to the airline's roots in crop dusting and carries the story through the Golden Age of Travel. Before the museum's 2024-25 exhibit refresh, visitors stepped into the Monroe Cafe, a full-scale replica of Delta's former headquarters in Monroe, Louisiana, which served the company from 1928 until the move to Atlanta in 1941. Nearby is a replica Huff-Daland Duster biplane, representing the first aircraft operated by Delta's predecessor, alongside a Travel Air 6B Sedan that the museum lists among its aircraft, a type said to symbolize the airline's first passenger plane. A 1936 Stinson Reliant nicknamed the "Gull Wing" once trained Northeast Airlines pilots on instruments, and a Northwest Airlines Waco 125 is the only aircraft of its kind remaining. More than 40 airlines appear in Delta's family tree, and the museum's collections trace them all. The hangars themselves were designated a Historic Aerospace Site in 2011.

## The Airplane the Employees Bought

The Spirit Hangar, formerly Hangar Two and the Jet Era, takes the story from the 1960s to the present and is dominated by one that still sounds unlikely. In 1982 Delta acquired its first Boeing 767-200, Ship 102, and it was paid for by voluntary contributions from employees, retirees and community partners. Three flight attendants led the campaign, called Project 767, as a thank-you for what they described as "solid management and strong leadership during the first years following airline deregulation." Christened The Spirit of Delta, the jet wore a special livery for the airline's 75th anniversary in 2004 and was retired on February 12, 2006. After a two-week farewell tour in its original 1982 colors, it was towed across two roads to the museum on May 7, 2006. Visitors can board the jet. Around it sit the forward fuselage of the first Lockheed L-1011 TriStar ever built, the cockpit of a Convair 880, and a retired Boeing 737 simulator that visitors can book for an hour of flying.

## A Jumbo Across the Street

Outside waits one of the museum's biggest prizes. Ship 6301 was the first Boeing 747-400 ever built, flown first by Boeing as a test aircraft and delivered to Northwest Airlines on December 8, 1989. On October 9, 2002, operating as Northwest Flight 85 from Detroit to Tokyo, it made an emergency landing in Anchorage after a rudder malfunction. It joined Delta's fleet after the 2009 merger and flew passengers until September 9, 2015, having logged more than 61 million miles. The following April, the jumbo was moved across two streets from the airport tarmac to the museum lot. Inside, visitors pass through the intact first class cabin, walk out on a railed walkway over part of the wing, and look down from the upper deck through the lower deck and the emptied cargo hold to take in the aircraft's full cross-section. The museum went through a 2012-2014 renovation and a large exhibit refresh that ended with a reopening in April 2025. It is open to the public most days, but private events sometimes close the hangars or the 747, so check its published schedule before you go.

## Flight Context

The Delta Flight Museum sits at about 33.656 N, 84.422 W at Delta's headquarters in Hapeville, on the northern edge of Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International Airport (KATL), which lies about 1 nm away. From the air, look for the outdoor Boeing 747-400, 757 and DC-9 parked around the museum lot beside the historic hangars. This is some of the busiest airspace anywhere; overflight requires ATC clearance within Atlanta's terminal airspace, and the best views often come from an airline window on approach or departure. For general aviation, Fulton County Executive Airport-Charlie Brown Field (KFTY, formerly Fulton County Airport-Brown Field) is about 9 nm to the north-northwest and DeKalb-Peachtree (KPDK) about 14.5 nm to the north-northeast. Summer brings haze and afternoon thunderstorms.
