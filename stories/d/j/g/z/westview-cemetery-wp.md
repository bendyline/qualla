# Westview Cemetery

Billed as the Southeast's largest cemetery, it holds more than 125,000 Atlantans, from Joel Chandler Harris and the mayor the airport is named for to civil rights leaders buried on grounds that officially stayed segregated until 1970.

**Location:** 33.7462, -84.4431

**Categories:** Cemeteries in Atlanta, History of Atlanta, Cemeteries on the National Register of Historic Places in Georgia (U.S. state), 1884 establishments in Georgia (U.S. state)

## Introduction

The Burford holly is sold the world over as a landscape shrub, and gardeners who plant it rarely know that it began in a cemetery. Around 1895 Thomas Burford, head gardener at Atlanta's West View Cemetery, discovered the plant growing on the property. It is one of only two things left from the vast greenhouse business that once operated among the graves. Westview itself is still very much here: nearly 600 acres on the city's west side, billed as the largest cemetery in the Southeast and the resting place of more than 125,000 people.

## Three Sections From the Start

In May 1884 twenty-seven prominent Atlantans, among them L. P. Grant and Edward P. McBurney, petitioned the Fulton County court to form the West View Cemetery Association. McBurney, a financier, became its manager, and members assembled roughly 577 acres of farms and homesteads about four miles west of downtown. The first burial, Helen Livingston Haskins, took place on October 9, 1884. From the beginning the grounds were divided. The main sections bore names like Laurel Hill and Terrace Hill; Rest Haven was set apart for African Americans; and God's Acre served as the city's pauper burial ground until 1925. In 1888 a receiving vault was built into a hillside to hold bodies until families chose a plot, or until winter ground thawed enough for horse-drawn carriages; it also sheltered bodies during the 1918 influenza outbreak. In 1889 Confederate veterans raised a memorial and burial ground near where part of the Battle of Ezra Church had been fought.

## Greenhouses Among the Graves

A Romanesque Revival gatehouse by architect Walter T. Downing opened in 1890, with a waiting room and a secretary's office. The following year brought an unusual enterprise. The Westview Floral Company grew flowers in greenhouses on cemetery land, selling them to lot holders and the public and landscaping the homes and businesses of wealthy Atlantans. It grew into what was described as the largest greenhouse operator in the South before it closed, and by 1973 all its structures were gone. Two survivors remain. One is the Burford holly. The other is a 110-foot water tower built in 1921, topped with crenellations that make it look like a castle turret. It may be mistaken for a battlefield lookout, but it only ever held water.

## Asa Candler Jr.'s Abbey

Coca-Cola heir Asa Candler Jr. took full control of the cemetery in 1933, after the Great Depression loosened the grip of real estate brothers Frank and Forrest Adair. Candler built boldly. His Garden of Memories followed the memorial park model of Hubert L. Eaton, and in 1943 construction began on Westview Abbey, a Spanish Plateresque mausoleum by California architect Clarence Lee Jay and builder Cecil E. Bryan with 11,444 crypts. Twenty-seven stained glass panels in its chapel trace the life of Christ. Much of the third floor, however, long went unfinished, though Westview has recently opened a North West Wing in the Abbey, adding 234 crypts and 334 niches. Southwest of the abbey, Lake Palmyra opened in 1947 with one of four known versions of Harriet Hosmer's sculpture Zenobia in Chains on its shore. Candler also built an administration building housing his trophy room, billed as one of the largest private trophy rooms in North America. The lake was drained in the 1970s, and the building was demolished in 1973, its animal specimens donated to the Fernbank Museum of Natural History.

## Separate Ground, Shared Ground

Candler sold the cemetery in 1951, when West View became Westview, and since 1952 a nonprofit led by three generations of the Bowen family has run it. In 1970, under Frank Bowen, Westview officially ended segregation within its main grounds. The roll of those buried here now reads like a history of Atlanta, with all its contradictions. Journalist Henry W. Grady and Uncle Remus author Joel Chandler Harris lie here, as do Coca-Cola's Asa Candler and Frank Mason Robinson, creator of the Coca-Cola name and logo. So do civil rights leaders Joseph and Evelyn Lowery, C. T. Vivian and Vivian Malone Jones, attorney Donald L. Hollowell, and Horace T. Ward, Georgia's first African American federal district judge. So does Samuel Green, a Grand Wizard of the Ku Klux Klan in the late 1940s. Alice Hawthorne, killed in the 1996 Centennial Olympic Park bombing, rests here too, along with Chris Kelly of Kris Kross and producer Rico Wade.

## A Cemetery Still at Work

Westview is no museum piece. It remains an active cemetery, reportedly with as many as eight burials a day, and half of its land is still undeveloped. Since 2015, Atlanta Preservation Center guides have led walking tours, split into a Nineteenth-Century Tour and a Candler-Era Tour because the grounds are too large for one. A Friends group launched in 2020 is restoring the 1890 gatehouse, envisioned as a welcome center with exhibition space and public restrooms; the work is still in its first phase. Author Jeff Clemmons published a book on the cemetery in 2018 and is credited with pushing for its listing on the Georgia Register of Historic Places in 2019 and the National Register of Historic Places in 2020. Hollywood has found it too; film and television crews work among its monuments throughout the year.

## Flight Context

Westview Cemetery spreads across nearly 600 acres at 33.746N, 84.443W, about four miles west of downtown Atlanta along Ralph David Abernathy Boulevard. From 3,000 to 4,000 feet it reads as a large expanse of green lawns and woods amid the city's west-side neighborhoods, with the 110-foot crenellated water tower rising among them. Two nearby airports carry the names of men buried here: Fulton County Executive Airport-Charlie Brown Field (KFTY, formerly Fulton County Airport-Brown Field), named for Charles M. Brown, about 4.5 nm to the west-northwest, and Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International (KATL), named in part for mayor William B. Hartsfield, about 6.5 nm to the south. The cemetery lies within Atlanta's Class B airspace.
