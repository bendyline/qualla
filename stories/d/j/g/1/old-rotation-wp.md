# Old Rotation

On about an acre of Auburn ground, cotton has been planted every year since 1896 in the world's oldest continuous cotton experiment, the one that proved a winter legume could keep Southern soil alive.

**Location:** 32.5933, -85.4858

**Categories:** Auburn, Alabama, Auburn University, Agricultural research, Farms on the National Register of Historic Places in Alabama, National Register of Historic Places in Lee County, Alabama

## Introduction

From Lem Morrison Drive it looks like very little: a patchwork of small plots beside an arboretum, planted in cotton. But the crop in this ground has been sown, tended and measured since 1896, making the Old Rotation the oldest continuous cotton experiment in the world. It was set up to answer a question that had dogged Southern farmers for decades, and it answered it so clearly that the plots were simply never retired.

## Tired Ground

Through much of the late 19th century, cotton yields across the American South were sliding. Farmers planted the same crop on the same land year after year, and the soil paid for it, losing nutrients season by season. In 1896 Professor J. F. Duggar of the Agricultural and Mechanical College of Alabama, the school renamed Auburn University in 1960, laid out an experiment on a one-acre plot a mile south of the campus. His idea was simple. Cotton does not grow in winter. What if a crop of legumes used those months, growing on the same ground before the cotton went back in, and gave the soil back what the cotton had taken?

## An Answer Within a Few Years

It did not take long to see the result. Within a few years it was evident that the winter legume crop restored enough nutrients to support a cotton crop every year, indefinitely. The Old Rotation was among the first experiments to show that a cotton and legume rotation could keep soil producing cotton without end, and that original rotation has continued without interruption ever since. Over time, further systems were added in adjacent plots: cotton rotated with corn, cotton with corn and legumes, and cotton with corn, legumes and soybeans.

## Thirteen Plots, Six Systems

Today the Old Rotation consists of 13 plots divided among six planting systems. One is a control, where cotton goes in every year with no rotation and no fertilizer. Another rotates cotton with winter legumes but adds no fertilizer, while a third relies on fertilizer alone. The remaining three bring in corn with winter legumes, corn with legumes and fertilizer, and corn with legumes and soybeans. Side by side, the plots tell their story plainly. By its 110th year of continuous plantings, cotton yields remained high on the rotated plots, which were reported to produce nearly three times the yield of the unrotated ones.

## A Farm on the Register

The plot that once lay a mile beyond the campus now sits on the Auburn University campus, next to the Donald E. Davis Arboretum. It is the third-oldest ongoing field crop experiment in the United States. On January 14, 1988, it was placed on the National Register of Historic Places. Nearby, the Cullars Rotation runs a similar agricultural rotation experiment. The Old Rotation, meanwhile, keeps doing what it has done since the 1890s: growing cotton, and keeping score.

## Flight Context

The Old Rotation lies at 32.5933 N, 85.4858 W on the Auburn University campus in Auburn, Alabama, beside the Donald E. Davis Arboretum on Lem Morrison Drive. The plots cover only about an acre, so they are hard to pick out from altitude; use the university campus as your reference and descend to around 1,500 to 2,000 feet AGL where permitted. Auburn University Regional Airport (KAUO) is about 3 nm away, making this an easy overflight on arrival or departure. Moton Field Municipal Airport at Tuskegee (FAA identifier 06A; no ICAO code) is about 13 nm to the southwest, and Columbus Airport (KCSG) in Georgia about 28 nm to the east. Best viewed in clear conditions with low sun, when the plot boundaries stand out.
