# Battles of Emuckfaw and Enotachopo Creek

In January 1814 Andrew Jackson gambled an untried army on a winter march into Red Stick Creek country, and two bloody fights at Emuckfaw and Enotachopo Creek nearly undid him.

**Location:** 32.9727, -85.7366

**Categories:** Battles of the Creek War, 1814 in the United States, Native American history of Alabama, Battles of the War of 1812, Battles in Alabama, Tallapoosa County, Alabama, January 1814

## Introduction

At the start of 1814, Andrew Jackson's army was evaporating. His cavalry had deserted, his last regiment's enlistments were about to run out, and reinforcements would not bring him to full strength until the end of February. Then, on January 14, a draft of raw recruits arrived unexpectedly - men signed on for just sixty days. Jackson decided to use them before they were gone. The result was a pair of desperate fights in the Creek country of what is now east-central Alabama: the battles of Emuckfaw and Enotachopo Creek.

## An Army Running Out of Time

The trouble had been building since the Battle of Talladega. Supply shortages and discipline problems, both rooted in short-term enlistments, dogged Jackson's campaign in the Creek War. General John Coffee, who had gone back to Tennessee for fresh horses, wrote that the cavalry had deserted. By the end of 1813 Jackson was down to a single regiment. Governor Willie Blount had ordered a new levy of 2,500 troops, but that help was still weeks away. When the new recruits turned up in mid-January, Jackson's experienced core had dwindled to 103 men and Coffee, who had been "abandoned by his men." Allied Lower Creek and Cherokee warriors, around 200 of them, joined the force. Against them stood 400 to 500 Red Stick Creek warriors, led according to some accounts by Red Eagle, on their own ground.

## Dawn Attack at Emuckfaw

Jackson left Fort Strother on January 17 and marched toward the village of Emuckfaw, intending to cooperate with the Georgia Militia. It was a risky choice: a long march through difficult terrain, against a numerically superior force, with men who were inexperienced and insubordinate. A defeat could have prolonged the war. On January 22 his army was camped about 12 miles from Emuckfaw when, at dawn, a strong force of Red Sticks from a camp three miles away struck. They were driven off after about thirty minutes. Jackson sent Coffee with 400 men to burn the Red Stick camp, but Coffee, seeing how strong the position was, turned back without attacking. The Red Sticks attacked again, and Coffee was seriously wounded leading a small party to turn their flank. The Creek were driven off, with 54 killed by the commonly cited count. Jackson had no choice but to retreat to Fort Strother.

## Panic at the Ford

The first crossing of Emuckfaw Creek had been so difficult that Jackson chose a longer route home, yet the crossing was hard all the same. On the morning of January 24, 1814, his column began to re-cross the creek. Jackson's artillery was about to enter the ford when alarm shots rang out in the woods. He had anticipated an attack and had ordered his advance guard to counterattack and try to envelop the attackers. But the rear guard panicked and fled. For reasons that remain unknown, the Red Sticks could not take advantage of the collapse, and a handful of defenders drove them off. Across the two engagements, the commonly cited figures put Jackson's losses at 24 killed and 71 wounded, though accounts differ. The Creek lost 54 killed by that count, though a historical marker cites 189, with an unknown number of wounded. Behind every one of those figures, on both sides, was a family waiting for someone who would not come home.

## The Chance That Slipped Away

On paper the battles were indecisive. Jackson had been forced to withdraw, and he returned to Fort Strother and did not resume the offensive until mid-March. Yet the Red Sticks had lost their best opportunity for a decisive victory against the American forces - the moment when Jackson's army was at its weakest and least disciplined. The Battle of Horseshoe Bend followed in March 1814, a fight now commemorated at Horseshoe Bend National Military Park, and the Treaty of Fort Jackson in August of that year brought the conflict to a close. The January fights at Emuckfaw and Enotachopo are far less remembered. Still, they were the Red Sticks' best chance at a decisive victory over Jackson, and that chance slipped away at a creek crossing where a panicked rear guard should have cost him dearly.
