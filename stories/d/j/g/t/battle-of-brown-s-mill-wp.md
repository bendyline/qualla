# Battle of Brown's Mill

In July 1864 a Union cavalry raid meant to cut Atlanta's railroads and, if it could, free the prisoners of Andersonville collapsed near Newnan, Georgia - and the defeat at Brown's Mill pushed Sherman into the siege of Atlanta he had hoped to avoid.

**Location:** 33.3500, -84.7600

## Introduction

The Union troopers rode south with a bold objective: break the railroads feeding Atlanta, then, if all went well, push on to the Andersonville prison camp and free the roughly 30,000 prisoners held there. Instead, many of them ended up inside Andersonville themselves. Their raid came apart on July 30, 1864, at Brown's Mill, three miles south of Newnan, Georgia, in a defeat that reshaped the Atlanta Campaign.

## Sherman's Gamble

Gen. William T. Sherman did not want to lay siege to Atlanta. Hoping to avoid one, he sent two columns of Federal cavalry raiding south of the city to sever its supply and communication lines. Maj. Gen. George Stoneman led the cavalry of the Army of the Ohio to the southeast. Brig. Gen. Edward M. McCook, with the First Division of the Army of the Cumberland's cavalry, was to cut the railroads southwest of Atlanta, link up with Stoneman, and then, if Stoneman judged it feasible, go on to liberate the Union prisoners at Macon and Andersonville. On paper it was a daring plan. In practice it depended on two separate columns finding each other deep in hostile territory, and on their being able to fight their way home again.

## Fire Along the Rails

McCook's troopers crossed the Chattahoochee River on a pontoon bridge at Smith's Ferry and reached Palmetto, where they cut the Atlanta & West Point Railroad. At Fayetteville on July 28 they captured and burned more than 1,000 Confederate supply wagons. McCook also gained a reputation for condoning and encouraging the destruction of civilian property - a reminder that the war's raids fell on civilians as well as on armies. Early the next morning his men reached Lovejoy's Station, twenty-three miles south of Atlanta, and began wrecking the Macon & Western Railroad. But Stoneman never appeared. With no one to link up with, McCook called off the raid and turned back toward the river and the main Union army.

## Cornered at Brown's Mill

He did not make it. Near Brown's Mill, Confederate cavalry under Joseph Wheeler caught McCook's division and attacked. McCook reportedly wanted to surrender, but instead he let his officers lead their battalions out separately, each trying to break free on its own. The result was a rout. By commonly cited figures McCook lost about 1,300 men, nearly all of them taken prisoner, along with 1,200 horses, several ambulances, and two pieces of artillery, spiked so they could not be used. About 100 more were killed or wounded. Wheeler also freed some 300 Confederate prisoners that McCook's raiders had taken along the way. Against all of that, Wheeler's losses came to about 50 men. It was a stunning defeat at the hands of a smaller force, and it would remain the major blemish on McCook's record even though he served with distinction for the rest of the war.

## The Road to the Siege

Stoneman's column met its own disaster. Stoneman was captured, becoming the highest-ranking Union officer to be held as a prisoner of war during the Civil War. In a bitter irony, many enlisted men from both his command and McCook's wound up in Andersonville - the very prison they had set out to empty. The failure of the two raids changed the course of the Atlanta Campaign. Sherman had to abandon his effort to cut Atlanta's railroads with cavalry and began the lengthy siege he had hoped to avoid. McCook later led his remaining men north into Tennessee when Sherman sent the Army of the Cumberland after John Bell Hood. Brown's Mill stayed behind him as the one defeat his record could never quite shake.
