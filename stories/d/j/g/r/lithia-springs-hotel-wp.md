# Lithia Springs Hotel

A Connecticut promoter's grand hotel of 175 rooms drew guests to little Tallapoosa, Georgia, until Prohibition and the Depression hollowed out the town and the ruin was pulled down in 1943.

**Location:** 33.7436, -85.2878

**Categories:** Hotels in Georgia (U.S. state), Hotels established in 1881, 1881 establishments in Georgia (U.S. state)

## Introduction

In the fall of 1886, Ralph L. Spencer was riding a train north when it passed through Tallapoosa, Georgia, and he saw something that was not there yet: a grand hotel. Spencer, a transplant from Essex, Connecticut, moved his wife and son to Tallapoosa to build it. The Lithia Springs Hotel that followed grew into one of the largest hotels in the Southeast, a four-story palace of 175 rooms where guests from New York were said to arrive after 32 hours on the train. By 1943 it was a ruin, and it was pulled down as a fire hazard.

## A Born Salesman

Spencer was described as "above average" in many respects - handsome, personable, wealthy, and a born salesman. He needed all of it. In 1887 he and other contractors obtained charters for the Tallapoosa Water Works Company and the Tallapoosa Electric Light Company, funded largely by two prominent, fast-growing local businesses, the Tallapoosa Glass Factory and the Mountain City Athletic Association. Then Spencer left town. Many people assumed he had been daunted by the sheer scale of the hotel he had promised. They were wrong. He came back with Hungarian workers to build it, and those workers settled just southeast of Tallapoosa, in an area that was named "Budapest" for them. The hotel itself was presumably named for lithia water springs, as was the town of Lithia Springs, Georgia.

## Balls, Teas, and Nine Courses

Once the hotel was finished and the town settled into its new identity, Tallapoosa began to grow quickly. Guests came from all over the country for their health, for business, or simply for leisure, and a round-trip ticket to Tallapoosa cost $38.65. They found 175 rooms spread over four floors, a banquet hall, an elevator, a billiard hall, poolrooms, and a large ballroom. The accommodations also included tennis courts, bowling alleys, and riding trails. The hotel overlooked a 13-acre park with flowered walkways and four mineral springs. It hosted balls, teas, and lawn parties; its garden paths were lined with mimosas and magnolias, and its wide verandahs caught the scent of wisteria in the afternoons. At dinner, nine courses were served while an orchestra played classical music.

## Two Blows

The boom did not last. Wine had become an important source of income for the Haralson County area, where Hungarian settlers had planted vineyards, and for a time the area lived lavishly. Then the Georgia Prohibition Act of 1907 outlawed the trade that so much of the area depended on. Later came the Great Depression, and the two blows compounded each other, draining the money needed to keep a hotel of that size running. The economy forced the Lithia Springs Hotel to close its doors. For several years afterward the building stood in ruins, and it eventually became a fire hazard. In 1943 the grand hotel was dismantled. Spencer's vision from a passing train had filled a small Georgia town with guests, workers, and music; what remains now is the story.

## Flight Context
