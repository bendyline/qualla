# Battle of West Point

A week after Appomattox, Union cavalry stormed a tiny earthwork above the Chattahoochee at West Point, Georgia, where the last Confederate general killed in action fell to a sniper's bullet.

**Location:** 32.8817, -85.1872

**Categories:** Wilson's Raid, Battles of the American Civil War in Georgia (U.S. state), Union victories of the American Civil War, Troup County, Georgia, April 1865

## Introduction

Robert E. Lee had surrendered in Virginia seven days earlier, but on the morning of April 16, 1865, the war had not finished with West Point, Georgia. In a small square earthwork on a hill commanding the Chattahoochee River bridges, Brigadier General Robert C. Tyler waited with a garrison of convalescents and militia. He had lost a leg after being shot at Missionary Ridge and moved on crutches. The fort bore his name. Before the day was out he would be dead, the last Confederate general killed in action in the war.

## Two Bridges, One Gamble

The attack was part of Wilson's Raid, the Union cavalry campaign through Alabama and Georgia in the war's final full month. After defeating Lieutenant General Nathan Bedford Forrest at Selma on April 2 and capturing Montgomery on April 12, Brigadier General James H. Wilson turned east. He telegraphed Major General George H. Thomas in a confident mood: "There is no force to resist me, and I see no reasonable ground for fearing failures. My command is in magnificent condition." Rain had swollen the Chattahoochee, so Wilson needed a bridge. Two would do: one at West Point and a more heavily defended one downriver at Columbus, only 35 miles away. To improve his odds of taking one intact, he split his force. He led the push on Columbus, an important Confederate manufacturing center, himself, and sent Colonel Oscar Hugh La Grange's brigade against West Point.

## A Junction Worth Holding

West Point had no mills or foundries, but it was a critical rail center where two railroads of different gauges met, and its extensive yard held many locomotives and cars. Before Montgomery fell, the rolling stock of the Montgomery and West Point Railroad had been sent there along with commissary stores. Because of their narrow gauge, those trains were now trapped. Guarding the bridges was Fort Tyler, an earthwork 35 yards square with walls four and a half feet high, ringed by a ditch six to ten feet deep and seven to twelve feet across. It mounted a 32-pounder siege gun and two 12-pounder field pieces. Tyler had first come to West Point to recover in a hospital, and returned in late 1864 to command the fort. His defenders numbered somewhere between 120 and 265 men, armed with smoothbore muskets.

## The Fight for Fort Tyler

Against them came Union artillery and dismounted cavalrymen carrying Spencer repeating carbines. Artillery and sniper fire pinned the garrison inside its walls, and because the defenders had no headlogs to shelter behind, many of the Confederate dead and wounded were struck in the head. Tyler looked out over the field and was shot by a sniper firing from a nearby cottage. His second-in-command, Captain Celestino Gonzalez, was killed soon afterward, and Colonel James H. Fannin took command. To break the stalemate, the dismounted troopers crossed the ditch on planks. Running low on ammunition, some defenders hurled rocks and lit shells over the parapet. Then a cavalry bugle sounded the final charge, Union soldiers stormed over the embankment, and the fort surrendered at once.

## Fire in the Rail Yard

Federal cavalry took control of the town and its bridges. The raiders then burned the railroad's entire rolling stock, some 19 to 20 locomotives and 340 to 350 cars. Before the cars were set alight, some of the food aboard was taken off and handed to the mayor of West Point to feed the wounded of both sides and the town's destitute citizens, a small mercy amid the destruction. The bridges survived only briefly. La Grange held off until word came of the attack on Columbus, since Wilson might still need a crossing there. When news of the Union victory at Columbus arrived, his troops burned the bridge. The fight at West Point was one of the last battles of the Civil War, fought a full week after Lee's surrender.

## Flight Context

The battlefield is at 32.8817 N, 85.1872 W in West Point, Georgia, where the Chattahoochee River runs through town near the Alabama state line. Follow the river and the rail lines into West Point; the Fort Tyler site sits on high ground overlooking the river crossings. A viewing altitude of 2,000 to 2,500 feet AGL shows the town, the river and the rail corridor together. Lanett Municipal Airport in Alabama (FAA identifier 7A3; no ICAO code) is about 5 nm away, LaGrange Callaway Airport (KLGC) about 10 nm to the northeast, and Columbus Airport (KCSG), near the other target of Wilson's split attack, about 25 nm to the south-southeast. Best viewed in clear conditions.
