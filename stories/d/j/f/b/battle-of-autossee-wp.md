# Battle of Autossee

On November 29, 1813, Georgia militia and allied Creek warriors attacked and burned the Red Stick Creek towns of Autossee and Tallassee on the Tallapoosa River, an early Georgia offensive in the Creek War whose site now lies unmarked on private land near Shorter, Alabama.

## Introduction

Before sunrise on November 29, 1813, a Red Stick hunter spotted the soldiers. He warned Autossee, a Creek town near present-day Shorter, Alabama, where General Floyd later estimated some 1,500 Red Sticks had gathered, and his warning gave the town time to get its women and children out and to send runners to nearby settlements for help. General John Floyd's Georgia militia and their Creek allies were already closing in. Within hours, Autossee and the nearby town of Tallassee would be in flames. Most of their people escaped, helped by that warning and by an encircling line stretched too thin to hold them.

## A War Among Neighbors

The Creek War set Creek against Creek as well as against the United States. Tensions between white settlers and Creek factions had been rising in the Mississippi Territory, in what is today central Alabama, and the recent massacre at Fort Mims had hardened Floyd's resolve to mount a larger offensive. He raised a substantial force of militia in Georgia and marched along the Federal Road toward Autossee, home to High Head Jim and others responsible for Fort Mims. At the same time, Andrew Jackson, John Coffee and John Cocke were moving south from Tennessee with 3,500 men. Floyd's own column included some 900 militiamen and 450 allied Creek warriors under the command of William McIntosh of Coweta, many of them said to be from Tukabatchee.

## Plans Unravel in the Dark

Floyd's plan was to encircle the town. Before sunrise his militia split into two columns, but three things went wrong before the attack could begin. First, Floyd discovered a second, smaller Red Stick town, Tallassee, and had to spread his men thinner than intended. Second, McIntosh was supposed to block the western escape route, but he and his warriors could not cross the deep, freezing cold Tallapoosa River; they crossed Calebee Creek instead and blocked the routes to the north. Third, and most critically, the Red Stick hunter had already raised the alarm. By the time the militia moved in, the town knew they were coming, and many of its people were already slipping away.

## Three Hours

The attackers' muskets and cannon allowed them to storm Autossee quickly and set it ablaze. Most of the Creek warriors fled, but Floyd later reported that some stayed to fight to the end and died in the fires. The fighting lasted three hours. Floyd claimed that 200 to 250 Red Sticks were killed, although Red Stick reports put their dead nearer 80, and he reported about 11 of his own men killed, with accounts differing on whether that count includes his Creek allies, and was himself among the wounded. Between the two towns, 400 houses were burned to the ground. For the families who fled that morning, the victory the militia claimed meant the loss of their homes and their town, even as most of them escaped with their lives.

## The Long Road Back

Low on supplies, the militia turned back toward Fort Mitchell. The Creek warrior Paddy Walsh, who had arrived too late to help defend Autossee, harassed the column along the way and killed five more men. At the fort, Floyd's force spent two months regrouping and preparing another offensive, which led to the Battle of Calebee Creek in late January 1814. The site of Autossee now lies on private property in Macon County, near the town of Shorter, unmarked and inaccessible to the public. The river and the creek remain; the towns that stood here survive only in the record of the morning they burned.
