# Majestic Caverns

Burial ground, saltpeter mine, failed onyx quarry and Prohibition speakeasy, the great cave near Childersburg has been nearly everything before becoming the show cave now called Majestic Caverns.

**Location:** 33.2968, -86.3064

**Categories:** Caves of Alabama, Show caves in the United States, Landforms of Talladega County, Alabama, Tourist attractions in Talladega County, Alabama, Properties on the Alabama Register of Landmarks and Heritage

## Introduction

In the early 1920s, the great cave near Childersburg had a nickname that no tourism brochure would choose: "The Bloody Bucket." During Prohibition, someone opened an illegal speakeasy and dance hall inside it, and the shootings and fights were frequent enough that federal agents eventually shut it down. The speakeasy was just one chapter. Over the centuries this cavern in the Appalachian foothills of Talladega County has been a burial place, a saltpeter mine, an onyx quarry that never paid, and finally a show cave, known for decades as DeSoto Caverns and, since June 2022, as Majestic Caverns.

## A Room Ten Stories Tall

The entrance was once a hole in a hillside just 4 feet by 8 feet; it has since been enlarged. Beyond it opens a main room just over ten stories high and larger than a football field. People once believed the cave ran all the way from near Childersburg to Talladega, but exploration found only one real continuation. From the left end of the main room's rear wall, a passage runs some two or three hundred yards before ending at a blank wall and a small pool. Apart from a possible underwater exit through that pool, there are no other known unexplored routes. What the cave lacks in length it makes up for in decoration. It is billed as holding one of the largest accumulations of onyx formations in the United States, and because the formations are still growing, visitors may not touch most of them.

## The People of Coosa

Long before the tours, the cave held the dead. In the 1960s (sources say 1963 or 1965) archaeologists from the University of Alabama found a burial of the Copena culture, whose name joins the first letters of copper and the last of galena, two materials common in its graves. It held the remains of five people, one of them a child. In 1995 the cave's operators worked with a group of Native Americans who wished to rebury the five in an undisclosed part of the cave. When Hernando de Soto's expedition arrived in 1540, by the local account the Coosa chiefdom stretched roughly from Gadsden to Wetumpka along the Coosa River, with its capital, also called Coosa, near present-day Childersburg; other accounts place the capital in northwest Georgia and call the town at Childersburg Talisi. The cave, a few miles east, is described as the Coosa people's ancestral cave. De Soto is said to have stayed a little over five weeks. The Coosa chief, or Micco, welcomed him warmly and offered land, and de Soto answered by taking him hostage and enslaving Coosa people.

## Saltpeter and Onyx

In December 1796, Benjamin Hawkins, appointed by George Washington's administration as General Superintendent for Indian Affairs south of the Ohio River, visited the Upper Creek territory and described the cave in his report to the president, noting its saltpeter crystals. That report is the basis for the claim that this is the first officially recorded cave in the United States. Late in the Civil War the cave went to work. The Confederate Army encouraged families to mine caves for saltpeter, used to make gunpowder, and even paid young men to mine rather than enlist, turning the cavern into a saltpeter-mining center. Its spring-fed well mattered because leaching saltpeter from soil takes a great deal of water, and the well, a leaching trough and a reconstructed vat are still on display. In 1912 Ida Elizabeth Brandon Mathis, a businesswoman and nationally recognized expert on farm economics, bought the cave with friends to mine its onyx, but the onyx proved to be of poor quality, and by one account cheaper Mexican onyx undercut them, so the mine went dormant.

## Lights On

In the mid-1920s, Ida's son Allen Mathis bought out the other partners and secured the underground rights to the property. For years the cave remained a playground for young romantics and teenage spelunkers. In the 1960s (accounts give around 1960 or 1965), Mathis and Fred Layton began turning it into a show cave, installing high-powered electric lights to bring out the onyx colors, and it opened to the public as KyMulga Onyx Cave, echoing its old local name, Kymulga Cave. His son and grandson, Allen W. Mathis Jr. and Allen Mathis III, took over in 1975. The following year it was renamed DeSoto Caverns, and on July 19, 1976, it joined the Alabama Register of Landmarks and Heritage. Wider paths and new lighting followed, back areas opened in 1980, and the 1980s brought a light, sound and water show. Today, as Majestic Caverns, it bills itself as "Alabama's Big Cave," with a three-quarter-acre maze, gemstone panning, a climbing wall and rides above ground, and a show below that changes with the holidays and seasons.

## Flight Context

Majestic Caverns lies in the Appalachian foothills of Talladega County, Alabama, at approximately 33.297°N, 86.306°W, a few miles east of Childersburg and the Coosa River. The cave itself is underground, so use Childersburg, the river and the wooded ridges as references from 2,500 to 3,500 feet. Nearby airports: Merkel Field Sylacauga Municipal Airport (KSCD), about 7.5 nm south; St. Clair County Airport in Pell City (KPLR), about 16 nm north; and Talladega Municipal Airport (KASN), about 21 nm to the northeast. Birmingham-Shuttlesworth International Airport (KBHM) is about 27 nm to the northwest. Summer thunderstorms and morning haze over the ridges are common.
