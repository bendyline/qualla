# Red Mountain (Birmingham)

The rust-stained ridge south of Birmingham held the iron ore that built the city, and today it carries mansions, broadcast towers, a vast urban park, and the cast-iron Vulcan keeping watch over the valley.

**Location:** 33.4867, -86.8064

## Introduction

In the company towns of Birmingham's steel industry, the ridge above the city once appeared in pageants as a character in its own right. It had earned the role. Red Mountain, a long southwest-to-northeast ridge dividing Jones Valley from Shades Valley, takes its name from rust-stained rock faces and prominent seams of red hematite iron ore - the ore that, sitting close to nearby coal and limestone, gave the Birmingham District its reason to exist. The mines are gone now, but the mountain is still where Birmingham looks up.

## Iron in the Rock

Red Mountain belongs to the Ridge-and-Valley region of the Appalachian Mountains, and its hard Silurian strata, the Red Mountain Formation, lie exposed in several long crests. The iron locked inside them was the whole point. Mining along the ridge began in the early 1860s, when the Civil War created a demand for iron to sustain the Confederate war effort. In 1865 the Union army destroyed the Oxmoor, Irondale, and Tannehill furnaces, and mining along Red Mountain stopped. It did not stay stopped. After the war, iron production resumed on a commercial footing, and under industrialists such as DeBardeleben, Sloss, and Woodward, miners returned to the red seams. The proximity of that ore to coal and limestone was the impetus to develop and promote the Birmingham District as an industrial site, and the mountain took on a symbolic place as the source of the region's wealth.

## Above the Smoke

Wealth changed the ridge itself. In the Altamont and Redmont areas, abandoned mine sites became the locations for large estates and upper-class developments, prized for their cool breezes and for a panoramic view of the growing industrial city from above its constant layer of thick black smoke. Redmont Park, just southeast of downtown, was developed in the 1920s by Robert Jemison and became home to the city's early bankers and iron and steel industrialists. It grew into one of Birmingham's most prominent neighborhoods, holding many of the multimillion-dollar residences and estates within the city proper. Along Altamont Road lies the Woodward House, built in 1926 for the family of Alex Harvey "Rick" Woodward, which UAB's archives say became the official home of the university's president in 1969. The Altamont School and Saint Rose Academy, a Catholic school run by Dominican sisters, share the neighborhood.

## The Last Ore

Economics eventually did what the Union army could not. As the steel furnaces modernized, labor costs rose, and geological faults made the local ore harder to reach, it became cheaper to buy pelletized ore from distant sources than to keep digging on Red Mountain. The last ore mine on the ridge, operated by US Steel, closed in 1962. About a decade later, in 1971 or 1972 depending on the source, the Birmingham district's final ore mine - the Pyne Mine off Highway 150 near Bessemer, run by the Woodward Iron Company - shut down too. The ridge itself endures. Its long spine runs 33 miles, from Sparks Gap in the southwest to Trussville in the northeast, with communities strung along its length - Raimund, Muscoda, Lipscomb, Wenonah, Ishkooda, Greensprings, Irondale, and Ruffner among them. It is also home to Red Mountain Park, one of the largest urban parks in the United States at 1,500 acres.

## Vulcan on the Crest

In the late 1930s a giant cast-iron statue of Vulcan, the Roman god of the forge, went on display atop a sandstone tower built with Works Progress Administration funding on Red Mountain. The figure had first represented Birmingham at the 1904 St. Louis World's Fair, and it is billed as the world's largest cast-iron statue - a fitting emblem for a city built on iron from the very ridge it stands on. Today the crest also serves as a natural promontory for broadcasting: most of Birmingham's television and radio stations have their transmission towers here. In 1970, after many years of work, the Red Mountain Expressway was completed through a cut in the ridge, linking Birmingham with its southern suburbs and exposing geological strata spanning millions of years, including the very red ore seam that started it all.

## A Mountain in Fiction

The ridge has a literary life, too. The fantasy writer and paleontologist Caitlín R. Kiernan used Red Mountain, especially the area west of U.S. Route 31 and the expressway cut, as the setting for four of her novels: Silk, Threshold, Low Red Moon, and, to a much lesser extent, Murder of Angels. In Threshold the mountain's geography and geology are integral to the plot, and Kiernan later wrote an afterword on the ridge's Paleozoic strata. Fannie Flagg's 2010 novel I Still Dream About You also features Red Mountain prominently - one more sign that, for Birmingham, this ridge is never just scenery.

## Flight Context

Red Mountain runs southwest to northeast immediately south of downtown Birmingham; this point on the ridge sits at approximately 33.487°N, 86.806°W. From 2,500-3,500 feet AGL the ridge is easy to follow as a long line separating the downtown grid of Jones Valley from Shades Valley to the south. Look for the Vulcan statue on its tower along the crest and the notch of the Red Mountain Expressway cut. Caution: most of Birmingham's television and radio transmission towers stand on this ridge, so check charts for tower heights and keep a safe altitude. Birmingham-Shuttlesworth International Airport (KBHM) is about 5.4 nautical miles north-northeast, Bessemer Airport (KEKY) about 12 nautical miles south-southwest, and Shelby County Airport (KEET) about 19 nautical miles south.
