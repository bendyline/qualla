# Red Mountain Expressway Cut

Birmingham blasted a highway through Red Mountain instead of tunneling under it, and the gash it left exposes roughly 150 million years of geological time - now a National Natural Landmark.

**Location:** 33.4960, -86.7880

**Categories:** Freeways in the United States, Geology of Alabama, Geography of Birmingham, Alabama, Historic American Engineering Record in Alabama, National Natural Landmarks in Alabama, Transportation in Birmingham, Alabama, U.S. Route 31

## Introduction

Birmingham almost went under Red Mountain instead of through it. City and state highway engineers seriously considered a tunnel to link the city with its southern neighbors, and two competing routes were drawn up - one from 18th Street, one from 22nd. The tunnel never happened. Instead, in the 1960s, a section of the ridge was blasted and carted away, and in opening a road, the builders opened a book: roughly 150 million years of geological time laid bare within about 650 feet of exposed rock.

## The Tunnel That Wasn't

The Red Mountain Tunnel project was one of the original ideas for getting traffic from downtown Birmingham to the suburbs on the far side of the ridge. Two alternate routes were proposed, one starting at 18th Street and the other at 22nd Street, and in February 1947 a Birmingham News headline had a tunnel board "pondering two routes under Red Mountain." Both were eventually abandoned. A tunnel would have cost too much and taken too long to build, so the planners chose the blunter option. A section of Red Mountain was blasted and removed to let the Red Mountain Expressway enter downtown, and in 1970, after many years of work, the expressway was complete. What had been solid ridge became a gap wide enough for a highway - and deep enough to reveal exactly what the mountain was made of.

## Over the Mountain

The road did exactly what it was meant to do. The expressway links Birmingham with its southern suburbs of Homewood, Mountain Brook, and Vestavia Hills, and it has spurred suburban growth toward the south of the city. Two federal highways share the passage: U.S. Route 31, which continues south as the Montgomery Highway, and U.S. Route 280, which heads off to the southeast. For most drivers the cut is simply the way into town, a brief passage between walls of layered rock. Engineers and historians saw more in it. The Historic American Engineering Record documented the site as the Red Mountain Cut National Natural Landmark, at U.S. 280 on Red Mountain, adding a highway excavation to the national record of American engineering works.

## Reading the Walls

The walls of the cut are a cross-section of the Earth's history. Within about 650 feet of exposed strata lie roughly 150 million years of geological time, including the red iron ore seam that spurred Birmingham's development in the first place - the very reason there was a city here to build an expressway for. The rock has yielded discoveries of its own. A new species of Lower Silurian trilobite, a phacopid from the middle Llandovery, was first collected from exposures on Red Mountain. It was named for the city, Acaste birminghamensis, and formally published in May 1972. In 1987 the National Park Service designated the Red Mountain Expressway Cut a National Natural Landmark, covering about eight acres - an unusual honor for a man-made excavation.

## The Museum on the Slope

For a time the cut had its own science museum. The Red Mountain Museum opened on the slope beside it in 1971, and interpretive signs went up along one of the cut's terraces, with guardrails and fencing so visitors could inspect the exposed rock up close. From the late 1970s until 1994 the museum was busy with paleontological research, collecting fossils from hundreds of localities across Alabama; its staff cataloged tens of thousands of specimens, including many Cretaceous mosasaurs, Pleistocene ground sloths, and primitive Eocene archaeocete whales. The museum later joined a children's science museum, The Discovery Place, to form Discovery 2000, which moved downtown and became the McWane Science Center, opening on July 11, 1998. The collection is stored there today. Back at the cut, the interpretive trail was reported in disrepair and closed to the public as of 2023, with rock slides a concern, and by October 2007 the old museum building had been demolished.

## Flight Context

The cut lies on the crest of Red Mountain just south of downtown Birmingham at approximately 33.496°N, 86.788°W. From 2,500-3,500 feet AGL, follow the expressway south from downtown until it notches through the ridge; the exposed rock walls stand out against the ridge line on either side. Caution: many of Birmingham's television and radio transmission towers stand along Red Mountain, so check charts and keep a safe altitude. Birmingham-Shuttlesworth International Airport (KBHM) is about 4.5 nautical miles to the north-northeast, Bessemer Airport (KEKY) about 13 nautical miles south-southwest, and Shelby County Airport (KEET) about 19 nautical miles south.
