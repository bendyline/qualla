# Louise Wooster

When cholera emptied Birmingham in 1873, the madam Lou Wooster stayed to nurse the sick and bury the dead - and today a public health award in her name has traditionally ended with a carriage ride to her grave in Oak Hill Cemetery.

## Introduction

When cholera swept through Birmingham in 1873, several thousand people fled the city. Lou Wooster stayed. She was, at the time, a well-paid lady of the evening, and nothing obliged her to risk her life for the city. Yet through the epidemic she nursed the sick, fed the hungry, and prepared the dead for their funerals. She now rests in Birmingham's Oak Hill Cemetery, and the recipients of a public health award named in her honor have been driven to her grave in a horse-drawn carriage.

## Step by Step

Louise Catharine Wooster was born on June 12, 1842, in Tuscaloosa, Alabama, to William Wooster and Mary Chism Wooster. Her childhood came apart early. Her father died in 1851, and her mother remarried; a few years later her stepfather abandoned the family and took their money with him. Mary Wooster died a few years after that, virtually destitute. By her middle teens, Lou was an orphan with nothing to rely on but the mercy of relatives, and that mercy did not protect her. She suffered abuse in those years, and her older sister turned to prostitution. Looking back, Wooster described her own path into the same life in a single, unsparing sentence: she "fell, step by step, until at last I was beyond redemption." It is worth reading those words with the circumstances in mind - a girl with no parents, no money, and very few choices.

## The Year the City Emptied

In 1873 she was working in Birmingham when a deadly cholera epidemic arrived. Several thousand residents fled. Wooster did not. She organized the women of her brothel, as she later put it, because she was "determined to stay and help nurse the poor sick and suffering ones who needed me." Through the epidemic she nursed the sick, fed the hungry, and prepared the dead for their funerals, risking her own death to do it. Her colorful character and her care for the sick and dying endeared her to the Birmingham community, and one account of her life credits her courage with going a long way toward assuring there was a Birmingham for its leadership to come back to. It was an unlikely reputation for a woman in her line of work, and it would follow her for the rest of her life.

## Near City Hall

Her business did not survive the epidemic as well as her reputation did. Few of her clients remained in Birmingham afterward, so she moved to Montgomery to open a brothel there. By the 1880s she was back in Birmingham, running brothels near City Hall, where she could attract the wealthiest patrons. She made a fortune. By several accounts she also gave to charity and came to the aid of the women her era dismissed as "fallen" - women whose situation she understood from the inside - and she buried several of them, and at least two of their infants, in her own plot at Oak Hill. Wooster was a master at storytelling and self-promotion, and she wrote a book chronicling her life, published in 1911 as The Autobiography of a Magdalen. Historians treat it with caution: the Encyclopedia of Alabama calls it factually unreliable, so details of her early life that rest on it deserve the same care. Her story has kept attracting writers since: James Baggett published a book about her in 2005, A Woman of the Town, and one Alabama Heritage article even asked in its headline whether she had been John Wilkes Booth's lover, a claim that appears nowhere outside her own interviews and autobiography.

## Empty Carriages to Oak Hill

Lou Wooster died on May 16, 1913, at the age of 70. She was buried in Oak Hill Cemetery. Legend says hundreds of empty black carriages drove past the funeral home to pay their respects to a local hero, though the Encyclopedia of Alabama finds no evidence of such a procession and says few mourners attended. A carriage returned to her story, in a sense, nearly a century later. The School of Public Health at the University of Alabama at Birmingham has presented the Lou Wooster Public Health Hero Award since 2007 to individuals, groups, or organizations it considers unconventional public health heroes, and recipients have ridden in a horse-drawn carriage from the school to Oak Hill. The first award went in 2007 to state Representative Patricia Todd. Later honorees included American Electric Power in 2010, for working with environmental groups to reduce pollution from power production, and the VF Corporation in 2012, for rebuilding the Wrangler distribution center in Hackleburg, Alabama, after the April 27, 2011 tornado destroyed it.
