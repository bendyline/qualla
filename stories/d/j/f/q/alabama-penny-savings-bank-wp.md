# Alabama Penny Savings Bank

Alabama's first Black-owned bank financed homes and churches for thousands of Birmingham's Black citizens, and the six-story building that carries its name still stands on 18th Street North.

**Location:** 33.5150, -86.8103

## Introduction

The building went up in 1913. Within two years, the bank whose name it carries was gone. That short span hides a much longer story: the Alabama Penny Savings Bank, founded in 1890 by William Reuben Pettiford, was the first Black-owned bank in Alabama, and for a quarter century it lent the money that let thousands of Black citizens in Birmingham build homes and churches of their own. At its height it was reportedly among the largest Black banks in the United States. Its six-story tower of buff-colored brick still stands at 310 18th Street North.

## Money That Became Houses

Pettiford opened the bank in 1890, and its purpose showed up in the city's physical fabric. The Penny Savings Bank financed construction of homes and churches for thousands of local Black citizens - houses and congregations that owed their buildings to a Black-owned institution, in a state where no Black-owned bank had existed before it. Measured against its peers across the country, the bank did remarkably well: by 1907 it was reported to be the second largest Black bank in the United States, and the Encyclopedia of Alabama calls it the largest and strongest by the time of Pettiford's death in 1914. It also reached beyond Birmingham, establishing branches in Selma, Anniston, and Montgomery. In Montgomery, the branch was run by Victor Tulane and William J. Robinson, and Robinson also served as general manager of a newspaper called The Emancipator. The picture that emerges is of a network of Black enterprise, with banking, publishing, and community building bound up together across the state.

## Six Stories, Four Piers

The bank's building, completed in 1913 by the Windham Construction Co., is a six-story Commercial style office block faced in buff-colored brick. Its designers were after height, or at least the look of it. "It has a strong vertical appearance," as its architectural description puts it, "emphasized by its proportions and the unbroken rise of four vertical piers." Those piers run up the facade without interruption until they meet a projecting cornice at the top, a crisp horizontal line that caps the whole composition. The architect is not known for certain. Wallace A. Rayfield, a Black architect, may have been responsible for the design, though the record stops short of confirming it. Either way, the result is considered a distinctive local example of 1910s office building design - a confident, upward-reaching statement from an institution then more than two decades old.

## The Pythian Temple

Confidence could not outlast the founder. After Pettiford's death, the bank merged with another institution and then, in 1915, closed - just two years after the new building rose on 18th Street. The structure survived the institution. It has also been known as the Pythian Temple, a name tying it to the Knights of Pythias fraternal order, and it counts among the buildings associated with that organization. In 1980 the building was listed on the National Register of Historic Places, and it is also a contributing building in Birmingham's Fourth Avenue Historic District. Stand across the street today and the vertical piers still do what they were meant to do, pulling the eye upward from the sidewalk toward the cornice line.

## Flight Context

The building stands in downtown Birmingham at approximately 33.515°N, 86.810°W, on 18th Street North. From 2,000-3,000 feet AGL, look for the dense grid of downtown office towers; the six-story bank building is modest in scale beside them, so use the downtown core as your reference. Birmingham-Shuttlesworth International Airport (KBHM) is only about 4 nautical miles to the northeast, so expect airline traffic and coordinate with ATC before circling downtown. Bessemer Airport (KEKY) lies about 13 nautical miles to the south-southwest.
