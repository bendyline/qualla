# Birmingham Museum of Art

A museum that opened in five spare rooms of Birmingham City Hall in 1951 has grown into a 180,000-square-foot home for more than 24,000 works, from Renaissance altarpieces to Alabama quilts.

**Location:** 33.5222, -86.8101

## Introduction

In April 1951, the Birmingham Museum of Art opened its first exhibition in five unused rooms at city hall. It had a director hired just two months earlier, a modest collection gathered by a local art club, and a great many works borrowed from museums across the eastern United States. Its governing board set out to create "an institution of public service, educational and recreational, with all the people welcome." Seven decades later, that improvised gallery has become a museum of 180,000 square feet in the city's cultural district, holding more than 24,000 paintings, sculptures, prints, drawings and decorative objects from nearly every corner of the world.

## An Iron City Wants Art

The idea is older than the museum. In 1908 the Birmingham Art Club was founded to build a public collection for a city that had been established as a new industrial town only 37 years before. In 1927 the club finally displayed its holdings in the galleries of the new Birmingham Public Library, and for two decades it kept acquiring works while lobbying the press and city hall for a real building. A governing board formed in September 1950. The following February it hired Richard Foster Howard as the first director, and by April the opening exhibition was on view. Publicity brought a stream of gifts, among them Chinese ceramics and textiles, Japanese prints, old master prints, costumes, glass and oil paintings. In 1952 the Samuel H. Kress Foundation lent 29 Italian Renaissance paintings, which became the core of the European collection.

## A Building That Kept Growing

A large bequest in 1954 made a building possible. Land was bought the next year, the firm of Warren, Knight & Davis drew the plans, and the Oscar Wells Memorial Building opened on May 3, 1959. The Kress Foundation soon handed over the deed to the Italian paintings, along with several more works and the trusteeship of a collection of Renaissance furniture and decorative objects. Expansion became a habit. Upper galleries rose on the west wing in 1965, a new east wing followed in 1967 and was rebuilt to three stories in 1974, and 28,000 square feet of gallery space arrived in 1980. In 1986 New York architect Edward Larrabee Barnes, working with the local KPS Group, was chosen for a major renovation that added 50,000 square feet of exhibition space and an outdoor sculpture garden. It was completed in 1993. The museum's memorial garden is older still, established in 1955 by Red Mountain Garden Club president Fariss Gambrill Lynn.

## Jade, Bronze and Cast Iron

The Asian collection began with a gift of Chinese textiles in 1951 and now numbers more than 4,000 objects, including a rare Ming dynasty temple wall, Tang dynasty tomb figures, Jomon period pottery from Japan and a collection of Vietnamese ceramics. The Vetlesen Jade Collection, described as on long-term loan from the Smithsonian Institution, is billed as one of the most important jade collections in the country. Nearly 2,000 works of African art span the 12th century to the present, among them a Yoruba mask and a Benin bronze hip pendant. The Native American galleries hold fine Navajo blankets and rugs, while Pre-Columbian highlights include Sican ceremonial gold vessels from Peru. For a city built on iron, one holding feels especially apt: the museum's late 19th-century European cast iron is described as the only public collection of its kind in the United States. Its Wedgwood collection, more than 1,400 pieces, traces the factory's production from its early years through the 19th century.

## From Perugino to Kerry James Marshall

The Kress gift anchors a European collection that includes works by Pietro Perugino, Canaletto and Paris Bordone, 17th-century Dutch paintings by Jacob van Ruisdael and Ferdinand Bol, portraits by Thomas Gainsborough and Thomas Lawrence, and French paintings by Mary Cassatt, Gustave Courbet and Camille Corot. The American galleries run from Gilbert Stuart and Childe Hassam to Georgia O'Keeffe, with sculpture by Hiram Powers and Frederic Remington and decorative pieces from Tiffany Studios and Frank Lloyd Wright. Albert Bierstadt's Looking Down Yosemite Valley, California, painted in 1865, was chosen by the National Endowment for the Humanities as one of 40 American masterpieces that best tell the nation's story. Contemporary holdings reach from Andy Warhol and Joan Mitchell to Kerry James Marshall, and include civil rights era photographs by Danny Lyon, Spider Martin, Chris McNair and Charles Moore, images with particular weight in this city.

## Alabama's Own Hands

From its first year the museum has collected the art of its own state, beginning with the miniaturist Hannah Elliott and the landscape painter Carrie Hill. In 1995 it organized Made in Alabama, an exhibition of art created in the state during the 19th century. Alongside academically trained artists, it built a collection of folk painting, sculpture, quilts and pottery, and since 2009 a permanent display has featured Bill Traylor, Thornton Dial, Alabama's quilters and other self-taught artists. The Robert Cargo Folk Art Collection arrived in 2013. Outside, the multi-level Charles W. Ireland Sculpture Garden shows Auguste Rodin, Jacques Lipchitz and Fernando Botero beside works made for the site: Elyn Zimmerman's Lithos II, a water wall of textured granite blocks, Valerie Jaudon's tiled Blue Pools Courtyard, and Sol LeWitt's Bands of Color in Various Directions, commissioned for the museum's 50th anniversary.

## Flight Context

The Birmingham Museum of Art sits at 33.522N, 86.810W in the cultural district of downtown Birmingham, Alabama. From 3,000 to 4,000 feet, orient on the downtown towers and on Red Mountain, the ridge along the south side of the city topped by the Vulcan statue; the museum lies within the downtown grid and is best picked out in clear conditions. Birmingham-Shuttlesworth International Airport (KBHM) is less than 4 nm to the northeast, so the museum lies deep inside Birmingham's Class C airspace; contact approach before any low sightseeing. Bessemer Airport (KEKY) is about 14 nm to the southwest.
