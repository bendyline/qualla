# Bethel Baptist Church (Birmingham, Alabama)

Bombed three times between 1956 and 1962, Bethel Baptist Church in Birmingham's Collegeville neighborhood was the headquarters of Fred Shuttlesworth's Alabama Christian Movement for Human Rights.

**Location:** 33.5518, -86.8020

## Introduction

On Christmas Day 1956, a bomb destroyed the parsonage of Bethel Baptist Church in Birmingham's Collegeville neighborhood. It was the first of three attacks on the church's buildings. Another came on June 29, 1958, and the last on December 14, 1962. Bethel endured. From 1956 to 1961 it served as headquarters for the Alabama Christian Movement for Human Rights - the organization Bethel's own pastor, Fred Shuttlesworth, led against segregation.

## A Pastor and a Movement

Fred Shuttlesworth took over Bethel's pulpit in 1953 and served as its pastor until 1961. The congregation's own history calls him the central leader of the civil rights movement in Birmingham, and in 1956 he founded the Alabama Christian Movement for Human Rights, known as the ACMHR. The group worked on two fronts at once. It pursued legal challenges, and it organized nonviolent direct action, taking aim at segregated public accommodations, transportation, and schools, as well as discrimination in employment. For five years the movement's headquarters was not a downtown office but a neighborhood Baptist church in Collegeville. Its congregation lived with everything that came with that role, including the danger.

## Three Bombs, One Congregation

The first bombing, on December 25, 1956, destroyed the parsonage, the pastor's home, on Christmas Day. Eighteen months later, on June 29, 1958, the church was targeted again, when civil rights guards removed a bomb planted there before it could explode. The third attack came on December 14, 1962, more than a year after Shuttlesworth's pastorate had ended, so the violence did not end with his tenure. Each time, the congregation stayed. The church today describes itself in words that make its meaning plain: having been bombed three times, in 1956, 1958, and 1962, "Bethel stands as a testament of the power of God." For the people who kept coming back to worship on 29th Avenue North, perseverance was not an abstraction. It meant walking into a building that had been attacked, and choosing to walk in again.

## From Collegeville to the Freedom Rides

The ACMHR's reach extended well beyond its home neighborhood. The movement played a crucial role in the 1961 Freedom Rides, the campaign that resulted in federal enforcement of rulings by the U.S. Supreme Court and the Interstate Commerce Commission to desegregate public transportation. Those rulings had been on the books, but enforcement was another matter, and the pressure brought by the Freedom Rides helped close that gap. It is a striking arc for a single congregation. Work organized from a Baptist church in a north Birmingham neighborhood helped change how Americans could travel between states. The movement's leader is remembered in an unexpected place as well: in 2008, Birmingham's main airport was renamed Birmingham-Shuttlesworth International Airport in his honor, and its runways lie less than three nautical miles from Bethel.

## A Landmark and a Living Church

The complex where these events unfolded, on 29th Avenue North, was added to the Alabama Register of Landmarks and Heritage on November 13, 1996. In the 1990s the congregation moved into a new building a block away, leaving the original church as a historic site open to visitors. Recognition kept widening. Bethel is a National Historic Landmark, and in 2008 the United States government submitted it to UNESCO as part of an envisaged World Heritage nomination of Civil Rights Movement Sites, placing it on the UNESCO Tentative List alongside Montgomery's Dexter Avenue Baptist Church and Birmingham's Sixteenth Street Baptist Church. Bethel also stands just outside the boundary of the Birmingham Civil Rights National Monument, created in 2017, and partners with it.

## Flight Context

Bethel Baptist Church stands in the Collegeville neighborhood of north Birmingham at approximately 33.552°N, 86.802°W. It lies only about 2.6 nautical miles west-southwest of Birmingham-Shuttlesworth International Airport (KBHM) - the airport named for Bethel's former pastor - so any overflight here is inside busy airport airspace; coordinate with ATC and expect a low, brief view on arrival or departure. Downtown Birmingham's towers lie a couple of miles to the south as a reference point. Parker Field (AL18, private use) in Gardendale is about 8 nautical miles to the north-northwest, and Bessemer Airport (KEKY) is about 16 nautical miles to the south-southwest.
