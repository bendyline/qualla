# Shiloh Baptist Church stampede

In 1902 a single misheard word - "fight" taken for "fire" - sent 3,000 people rushing for the door of Birmingham's largest Black church, and 115 of them died.

**Location:** 33.5197, -86.8111

**Categories:** 1902 in Alabama, Disasters in Alabama, Human stampedes in the United States, History of Birmingham, Alabama, 1902 disasters in the United States, 20th century in Birmingham, Alabama, Booker T. Washington

## Introduction

A single misheard word turned a packed church into a disaster. On September 19, 1902, some 3,000 people filled Shiloh Baptist Church in Birmingham, Alabama, to hear Booker T. Washington address the National Convention of Negro Baptists. Washington finished his speech. Then a voice from the choir shouted, "There's a fight!" - and across the crowded room, people heard "fire." In the rush that followed, 115 people died by the most widely cited count, most of them women, many of them caught in a brick-walled stairwell that led to the street.

## Seventh Avenue and Nineteenth Street

Shiloh stood at the corner of 7th Avenue and 19th Street, and in 1902 it was the largest Black church in Birmingham. It was also known as the Shiloh Negro Baptist Church, a name that, like the convention it hosted that September, carries the vocabulary of its era. On the day Booker T. Washington came to speak, roughly 3,000 people crowded inside. Among them were convention delegates who had come from other cities - one of them, as the day would reveal, from Baltimore. They had come for a speech and for the business of a national convention, and nothing about the program hinted at danger. The speech itself went off as planned. Only after Washington concluded his remarks did the trouble begin, and it began not at the pulpit but somewhere near the choir, over something almost absurdly small.

## Fight, Heard as Fire

It started with something small. By the most widely told account, a delegate from Baltimore got into a dispute with the choir leader over an unoccupied seat - the kind of argument that, in an emptier room, might have ended with a few sharp words. Someone in the choir yelled, "There's a fight!" Booker T. Washington, who had just finished his lecture, later gave a different account, blaming a scuffle in which a man made a gesture as if to draw a gun after his foot was stepped on. In a hall holding thousands of people, the word did not carry cleanly. Many listeners heard "fire." No fire had broken out, but that hardly mattered once the word spread. Mistaking one alarm for the other, the congregation rose en masse and started for the door. Picture the scene from the back of the room: thousands of people standing at once, a shout you only half caught, and everyone around you moving the same way. The only real danger in the building was the one the crowd was about to create for itself, as people tried to reach the same exit at the same moment.

## Between the Brick Walls

The deadliest place was the stairwell. It led from the church to the street, and it was hemmed in by brick walls - walls that gave a surging crowd nowhere to spread out. As people pushed in from behind, many of those ahead became stuck between them. Physicians said that in many cases the victims had fainted and then died from suffocation. By the end, 115 people were dead. Most of them were women. They had come to a church to hear a speech, and they died within reach of the street, killed not by flames or by any fight but by the weight of a frightened crowd. A dispute over a seat, a shout, a single misheard word: none of it would have been deadly on its own. Together, inside a building packed with 3,000 people, they cost more than a hundred lives.

## Flight Context

The church site lay in downtown Birmingham at approximately 33.520°N, 86.811°W, near the corner of 7th Avenue North and 19th Street North, a few blocks from the Birmingham-Jefferson Convention Complex. From 2,000-3,000 feet AGL on a clear day, look for the dense street grid of downtown Birmingham; the site itself is a city corner rather than a visible landmark, so use the downtown core as your reference. Birmingham-Shuttlesworth International Airport (KBHM) is about 4 nautical miles northeast of the site, and Bessemer Airport (KEKY) is about 14 nautical miles to the south-southwest.
