# Aldrich Coal Mine Museum

In the old company store of Aldrich, a coal town now part of Montevallo, Alabama, a small museum keeps the original sales counter and cash register alongside relics of the mine, its churches, school and prison, with Alabama's only coal miner monument standing outside.

## Introduction

The sales counter is still there, and so is the cash register. Miners in Aldrich, Alabama, once did their buying at the Montevallo Coal Mining Company commissary, the company store of a town the mine operators built. The mines are long closed, and Aldrich is now part of Montevallo, but the commissary survives as the Aldrich Coal Mine Museum. Outside stands what is described as the only coal miner monument in Alabama, a tribute to the people who worked underground here.

## A Town with an Owner

Coal was mined around Aldrich beginning in 1839. In 1875, Truman H. Aldrich purchased the town surrounding the mines and gave it his name, then leased the mining operations to his younger brother, William F. Aldrich. What followed was a true company town. The churches, the school and the houses were all built by the mine operators, and the post office, opened in 1883, had William Aldrich as its first postmaster. It stayed open until 1965. For a few years, from 1895 to 1902, the town even had its own locally published newspaper, The Alabama Time-Piece. Nearly every part of life in Aldrich, from worship to schooling to the mail, ran through the company that dug the coal.

## Convicts Underground

Not everyone who worked these mines chose to. At some point during their operation, Aldrich's coal mines used convict labor, leased from state and county prisons, a history recorded in Douglas Blackmon's book Slavery by Another Name. A prison, in use from 1913 to 1928, was built to house the convicts, and the convict cemetery lies near the spot where that prison once stood. Those who lie there worked the mines as prisoners, not as free miners, and their graves are part of Aldrich's story as surely as the company store. The museum's displays include material from Aldrich's prison alongside the mine, the churches and the school, a reminder that the prison was as much a part of this company town as its chapels and its store.

## Inside the Commissary

The mines closed on July 5, 1942, but the commissary building endured. Today it holds historical photographs, artifacts and displays drawn from every part of Aldrich's past: the coal mine, the churches, the school, the prison, the stores and the post office. Photographs taken for the Historic American Engineering Record show the commissary interior filled with Henry Emfinger's collections of mining artifacts. That federal survey documented the building as part of the Birmingham District, and the Montevallo Coal Mine Company Store is listed on the Alabama Register of Landmarks and Heritage. Behind the original counter, the old cash register still waits, as if the next shift might walk in at any moment.

The commissary building is small; from 1,500-2,500 ft MSL, orient on Montevallo and look for the small cluster of the Aldrich community along the county road in the wooded hills.
