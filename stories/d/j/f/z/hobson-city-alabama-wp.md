# Hobson City, Alabama

Pushed out of Oxford and denied the schools their taxes paid for, the Black residents of Mooree Quarters incorporated their own town in 1899, becoming Alabama's first self-governed all-Black municipality.

**Location:** 33.6185, -85.8434

**Categories:** Towns in Calhoun County, Alabama, Populated places in Alabama established by African Americans, African-American history, Towns in Alabama

## Introduction

"The whole country will doubtless be interested in the result of this experiment," the Alabama newspaper The Peoples' Journal observed when a small community in Calhoun County incorporated in August 1899. The paper called the new town "the only municipality controlled and governed entirely by colored people anywhere in the United States." That town was Hobson City. Squeezed between Oxford and Anniston, it became Alabama's first self-governed all-Black municipality - though not the nation's first, as the paper believed, since Eatonville, Florida, had incorporated earlier - and more than a century later it still has an African American majority. At the 2020 census it was home to about 760 people.

## Silas Moore's Quarters

The town began as Mooree Quarters, a Black community near Oxford that took shape shortly after the Civil War. It was named for Silas Moore, a formerly enslaved man who became a prominent landowner and community leader. By 1868 he and other Black residents had acquired land in the area, and despite systemic racism and economic hardship they built homes, churches and schools. They paid for services they did not receive. Residents of Mooree Quarters contributed $1,200 a year in school taxes to Oxford, yet their children were barred from the white schools and no adequate schools for Black children were provided.

## Pushed Out of Oxford

In the late 1870s, Oxford's white leaders, including Mayor John J. Dungan, and Calhoun County officials began a campaign to force Black citizens out of Oxford proper and into segregated areas such as Mooree Quarters, part of a wider effort to limit Black land ownership and political influence. Resistance came at a cost. In 1888 Thomas Harris, Silas Moore's half-brother, ran for justice of the peace in Calhoun County to fight the theft of Black-owned land. His candidacy, and a speech he gave at Mount Zion Baptist Church, enraged white supremacists and sped up the drive to expel Black residents from Oxford. By 1890 many Black families had been forcibly confined to Mooree Quarters.

## A Town of Their Own

The people of Mooree Quarters decided to take control of their own future. A committee of residents sought advice from Ross Black, an attorney in Anniston, who recommended that they incorporate as an independent municipality. On July 20, 1899, about 125 residents petitioned Calhoun County Probate Judge E. F. Cook to establish their own town. Twenty Black property owners who met the voting requirements of the day played a key role in the incorporation, and on August 16, 1899, the town officially came into being. S. L. Davis was elected its first mayor, and with James Duran as the first police chief, police protection returned to the area. The town was named for Richmond P. Hobson, a white naval hero of the recent Spanish–American War who would later serve in Congress.

## Holding the Line

Self-government did not bring safety from terror. In 1919 Mayor Newman O'Neal received death threats and was assaulted by the Ku Klux Klan, and he was forced to flee the town he led. Hobson City endured anyway. In 1970 its mayor, J. R. Striplin, was the thirteenth Black mayor in succession. Today the town covers about 2.7 square kilometers in southern Calhoun County, bordered by Oxford to the south and Anniston to the north, and in 2010 the Alabama Historical Commission named it one of the state's "Places in Peril." Its population has declined, from 878 at the 2000 census to about 760 in 2020, but the experiment that newspaper watched in 1899 is still going.

## Flight Context

Hobson City lies at approximately 33.618°N, 85.843°W in southern Calhoun County, Alabama, between Oxford to the south and Anniston to the north; the small town blends into the surrounding urban area, so use Interstate 20 and the Oxford–Anniston corridor as references from 2,000 to 3,000 feet. Anniston Regional Airport (KANB) is only about 2 nm to the south-southwest, so expect traffic in its pattern, and Talladega Municipal Airport (KASN) is about 11 nm to the west-southwest. The ridges of the Talladega Mountains, including Cheaha Mountain (about 2,410 feet), rise to the south. Summer afternoon thunderstorms are common.
