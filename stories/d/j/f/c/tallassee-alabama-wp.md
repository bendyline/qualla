# Tallassee, Alabama

A Tallapoosa River mill town that grew on the site of the Creek town of Talisi, Tallassee made carbines for the Confederacy, woke to a 4:30 a.m. mill bell, and now lives beside the dams that power the region.

**Location:** 32.5394, -85.8931

**Categories:** Cities in Alabama, Tallapoosa River, Creek history, American Civil War, Textile mills, Hydroelectric power

## Introduction

Visitors to Tallassee have been known to wake in the dark certain the town was on fire. The alarm was only the "long bell," rung at 4:30 in the morning for the mill hands. According to a 1988 Chamber of Commerce history, it had sounded every day but Sunday since Thomas Barnett's time, falling silent only for six months in 1948 while the bell tower was repaired. The bell tells you what Tallassee has been: a town built around the water and the work of the Tallapoosa River, on ground that was a Creek town long before it was a mill town.

## Old Town

The name comes from Talisi, which means "Old Town" in the Creek language. Talisi was a town of the Coosa Province of the Mississippian culture, the mound-building civilization that flourished across the Southeast from about 1000 to 1450, and in 1540 Hernando de Soto's expedition passed through. Later the historic Creek people lived here. The area was the location of the Creek capital, Tuckabatchee, and of the seven sacred plates. Tallassee also counts the Creek chief Opothleyahola and the prophet and warrior Peter McQueen among its notable people. Osceola, who became a famed Seminole leader in Florida, is believed to have been born in Talisi to a mixed-race Creek mother and, most likely, a Scottish trader father, and a historical monument stands near his birthplace.

## War on the Tallapoosa

In 1813 a civil war among the Creek drew in the United States. After U.S. forces attacked a Red Stick party at Burnt Corn and were defeated, the Red Sticks struck Fort Mims, north of Mobile, killing or capturing most of the roughly 500 militiamen, settlers, enslaved people and mixed-race Lower Creek sheltering there. The war was marked by raids and civilian massacres on both sides. Its last major battle came in 1814 at Horseshoe Bend, on this same Tallapoosa River, where Andrew Jackson's coalition of militia, federal troops, Lower Creek and Cherokee crushed the outnumbered Red Sticks. The victory boosted Jackson toward the presidency and toward his policy of Indian removal. In the years that followed, many Creek were forced west to Indian Territory, while others went into hiding among the Cherokee and the Seminole. Creek people from Talisi and the neighboring town of Lochapoka carried the old name with them, founding a new Talisi that would later be known as Tulsa, Oklahoma.

## The Armory That Survived

The town was established in 1835; the Tallassee Falls Manufacturing Company was making cotton cloth by 1841, and the mill complex later listed on the National Register of Historic Places dates to 1844. When the Civil War came, the Barnett, Micou and Company mill supplied cloth for Confederate uniforms and tents. In June 1864 the Confederate army moved the Richmond Carbine Factory from Virginia into an old Tallassee cotton mill, where it began turning out carbines. Houses on King Street were built around 1863 for the families of the officers running the armory and the mill. Local legend says Union forces tried to raid the arms works in both 1864 and 1865, though records show only James H. Wilson's cavalry was ever sent against Tallassee, and the building is described as the only Confederate armory building to survive the war without being destroyed.

## Spindles and Children

The mills outlasted the Confederacy by generations. In 1900 the Tallassee Falls Manufacturing Company joined the Mount Vernon-Woodberry Cotton Duck Company, a consolidation of mills including ones in South Carolina, Maryland and Alabama that together ran fourteen plants and 227,000 spindles. The long bell called workers to those spindles, and some of the workers were children: child labor was common in the Tallassee mills, as in thousands of American industrial plants, until the 1930s. Thomas Barnett's sons, Thomas Jr. and Nicholas, built homes on the hill above the mill, and Nicholas's house survived to become known as the George D. Patterson home.

## Power, Fire and a Divided River

In 1923 Alabama Power Company began developing the Tallapoosa, and over the next decade it built three power plants on the river, at Thurlow, Yates and Martin dams, which remain important sources of the company's electricity. Thurlow Dam, completed around 1930 and named for Oscar G. Thurlow, an Alabama Power executive, is home to the major hydroelectric plant that still operates in Tallassee. The river also splits the town, with Tallassee proper in Elmore County and East Tallassee in Tallapoosa County. Not everything has endured. Early on November 30, 2009, an arson fire gutted the historic Hotel Talisi only weeks after investors had reopened it. In 2019 the city council voted to condemn the shell with plans to demolish it, and Alabama Heritage listed it among its Places in Peril. At the 2020 census, 4,763 people called Tallassee home.

## Flight Context

Tallassee lies at about 32.539 N, 85.893 W on the Tallapoosa River in east-central Alabama, about 23 nm east-northeast of Montgomery. From 3,000-4,500 feet, the key landmark is Thurlow Dam and its hydroelectric plant on the river, with the old Tallassee mill buildings on the Elmore County (west) bank. State Highway 14 crosses the river here, and Interstate 85 runs about 8 miles to the south. Moton Field Municipal Airport (FAA identifier 06A, no ICAO code listed) in Tuskegee lies about 12 nm to the east-southeast. Auburn University Regional (KAUO) is about 24 nm east, and Montgomery Regional (KMGM) about 29 nm to the west-southwest. Summers bring haze and afternoon thunderstorms.
