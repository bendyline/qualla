# First Baptist Church (Montgomery, Alabama)

Founded by 700 freedpeople who walked out of a segregated balcony, Montgomery's Brick-A-Day Church became a home of the civil rights movement and, one night in 1961, a besieged refuge for the Freedom Riders.

**Location:** 32.3819, -86.2986

**Categories:** Civil rights movement, African-American history in Montgomery, Alabama, Baptist churches in Alabama, Churches in Montgomery, Alabama, Romanesque Revival church buildings in Alabama

## Introduction

The congregation built its church with a simple request: every member, a brick a day. Between 1910 and 1915, when First Baptist Church rose again on North Ripley Street in Montgomery, members were asked to each bring a brick a day to build it, and the church earned its nickname: the Brick-A-Day Church. Those walls would be tested. On the night of May 21, 1961, about 1,500 worshipers and activists sheltered inside while some 3,000 white people massed outside, throwing bricks through the windows and threatening to burn the church down.

## Out of the Balcony

The church was born from a refusal to accept second-class treatment. Its first members had worshiped during slavery at the First Baptist Church on Perry Street, where Black worshipers were confined to the balcony and, in one account's words, "were never allowed on the main floor of the sanctuary unless they were sweeping or mopping." After emancipation they organized a congregation of their own in 1866. In 1867, 700 African American communicants marched to an empty lot at the corner of Ripley and Columbus streets and declared themselves the "First Baptist Church (Colored)," founding what became "the first 'free Negro' institution in the city." Their first wooden building faced north onto Columbus Street and was known as the Columbus Street Baptist Church.

## From Hundreds to Thousands

The first pastor, Nathan Ashby, also became the first president of the Colored Baptist Convention in Alabama, which was founded inside his church on December 17, 1868. Paralysis forced him to retire in 1870. After a brief tenure by J. W. Stevens, James H. Foster took the pulpit in 1871 and held it for twenty years, and he is credited with growing the membership from a few hundred to several thousand. His successor, Andrew Stokes, added still more, and within a few decades First Baptist was one of the largest Black churches in the South. When fire destroyed the original frame church, Stokes led the rebuilding. The new Romanesque Revival sanctuary, designed by Walter T. Bailey of what is now Tuskegee University, turned to face east toward Ripley Street.

## The Pastor on Ripley Street

From 1952 to 1961 the pastor was Ralph Abernathy, a close friend of Martin Luther King Jr., who preached a few blocks away at Dexter Avenue Baptist Church from 1954 to 1960. A confidant of Edgar Nixon, Abernathy was drawn quickly into the Montgomery bus boycott of 1955 and 1956, and First Baptist hosted its mass meetings. Victory brought retaliation. After the buses were desegregated, some were ambushed and shot at, and in January 1957 both the church and Abernathy's house were bombed. White men were charged, but no one was convicted: the city prosecutor dropped the charges against three of them, and all-white juries acquitted others who stood trial. In the spring of 1958 a young John Lewis, who had been active at American Baptist College and Fisk University in Nashville and hoped to desegregate Troy State, came to Montgomery. In the pastor's office in the church basement he met Abernathy and King, a meeting that formally initiated him into the movement.

## The Night of the Siege

On May 20, 1961, the Freedom Ride met with violence at the Greyhound Bus Station in downtown Montgomery. The next night First Baptist became their refuge. Some 1,500 people packed the church, among them King, Fred Shuttlesworth, Diane Nash and James Farmer. Outside, a crowd of about 3,000 white people besieged the building. In the basement, King, with Abernathy, Wyatt Tee Walker, Farmer and Lewis beside him, was on the telephone with Attorney General Robert F. Kennedy as bricks crashed through the windows and tear gas drifted in. According to Lewis, Kennedy jokingly suggested that King say a prayer, since he was in a church anyway. The people in the basement were not amused.

## Release at Dawn

Robert Kennedy and President John F. Kennedy both pressed Alabama governor John Malcolm Patterson to protect the people inside. Around 10 p.m., Patterson placed the city under "qualified martial rule." City police and more than a hundred Alabama National Guardsmen formed a shield around the church, the marshals on the scene were placed under Guard command, and the mob was finally dispersed. Even then, the people inside were not free to go; the Guardsmen kept them in the church. At around 4 a.m., Assistant Attorney General William Orrick worked out a deal with the Guard's adjutant general, Henry Graham, and National Guard trucks and jeeps carried the Freedom Riders and parishioners away. The events of May 20 and 21, including the siege of First Baptist, played a crucial part in the desegregation of interstate travel. In May 2000 the Alabama Historical Commission added the church to the Alabama Register of Landmarks and Heritage, and on June 25, 2024, it was listed on the National Register of Historic Places.

## Flight Context

First Baptist Church stands on North Ripley Street in downtown Montgomery, Alabama, at approximately 32.382°N, 86.299°W, a short distance northeast of the Alabama State Capitol and Dexter Avenue. The church itself is hard to pick out from the air; use the Capitol and the downtown grid along the Alabama River as references from 2,000 to 3,000 feet. Maxwell Air Force Base (KMXF) lies about 3.2 nm to the west, so respect its military airspace, and Montgomery Regional Airport (Dannelly Field, KMGM) is about 7 nm to the southwest. Summer afternoon thunderstorms are common.
