# Oakwood Cemetery (Montgomery, Alabama)

One of Montgomery's oldest burying grounds holds enslavers and the enslaved side by side, 78 young RAF airmen, a one-armed ferryman hailed as a hero, and Hank Williams, whose funeral drew the largest crowd in the city's history.

**Location:** 32.3844, -86.2950

**Categories:** Cemeteries, Montgomery, Alabama, Hank Williams, Commonwealth War Graves Commission cemeteries, History of Montgomery, Alabama

## Introduction

For more than a century and a half, Montgomery's Catholics were being buried in the wrong place. The deeds to St. Margaret's Cemetery had been drawn up in the 1850s, and from 1863 onward the Catholic Archdiocese of Mobile was accidentally burying its dead on city property, a mistake that went unnoticed through extensions in 1863, 1945 and 1981. Only when the records were checked for a fourth extension did the error surface, and in 2017 a land swap set it right. The boundaries were redrawn around the graves, and the dead stayed where they lay. It is a fitting tale for Oakwood Cemetery, a place where the boundaries, like the stories, run together.

## Scott's Free Burying Ground

Oakwood opened in 1819 on land donated by two of Montgomery's founders, John Scott of East Alabama and Andrew Dexter of New Philadelphia, whose settlements were combined into one town. Today the city-owned cemetery covers 120 acres, and by 2014 it held some 200,000 burial sites. Its oldest part, begun as Scott's Free Burying Ground, had no racial segregation rules, so many enslavers lie beside the people they held in bondage. Only two graves are marked as those of enslaved people. One belongs to a man remembered simply as Jim, enslaved by the Sebussier family, whose grave is ringed by a brick enclosure; beside him lies Susan Tooley, buried in November 1856, at the top of the old graveyard's horseshoe. Some graves are lined with quartz bottles of water, following an old superstition that they would give the dead something to drink in the afterlife.

## The Unnamed

Many of Oakwood's dead are marked only as "unknown." Some died in the 1918 influenza pandemic, when burials came so fast that records were not kept accurately. Others were strangers the city could not name: an infant found in Catoma Creek, a 13-year-old girl found in a railroad car in 1895, a victim of strychnine poisoning, someone found drowned near the wharf in 1883. Near the railroad lie 47 people executed by public hanging; their graves bear no names, and the records that hold them are sealed. In 2009 a forgotten pauper's cemetery was rediscovered in a vacant lot nearby and became the Children of God section. Elsewhere lie some 800 Confederate and 198 Union soldiers, on a hill beside the grave of William Lowndes Yancey, and a section called the Land of Peace, where many Jewish people are buried.

## Airmen Far From Home

In the Oakwood Cemetery Annex, near the Hank Williams Memorial, rest pilots from Britain, Canada and France who died while training at Maxwell Field during World War II. The cemetery holds 78 Royal Air Force students who died between June 1941 and February 1942. Some had first been buried elsewhere, in plots donated by local churches, veterans' groups and private citizens, before being reinterred here. Their wooden crosses gave way to granite headstones by October 1943. Since 1981, care of the RAF section has been handled under contract to the Commonwealth War Graves Commission, and each November, around Veterans Day, RAF officers stationed at Maxwell gather among the rows for a ceremony.

## One-Armed Bob

Bob Goodwyn had lost his right arm in an accident at a gin shop, yet he worked as a ferryman on the Alabama River. On April 11, 1908, he rescued a railroad agent, E. W. Bliss, from drowning, and that May the mayor awarded him a gold medal for heroism. At the ceremony, Sheriff Horace Hood told him the medal assured "all people of your race" of white goodwill so long as they showed goodwill to "the dominant race," remarks that a 2024 Montgomery Advertiser retrospective said pointedly othered him. On March 8, 1909, Goodwyn drowned in the same river along with four others when their small boat caught on the ferry cable and capsized; he let go of the hull to reach for a rescuer's rope with his one arm. Bliss paid for his lot at Oakwood. Eight white schoolboys carried the coffin, hundreds attended, and the paper reported that Black and white mourners mingled "with little distinction." His exact grave is unrecorded.

## Midnight in Montgomery

When Hank Williams was buried in 1953, the city had never seen anything like it. Estimates of the crowd ran from 15,000 to 25,000 people, and it is described as the largest funeral in Montgomery's history. Three trucks were needed to carry the wreaths to the Annex. In the segregated auditorium, 200 African American mourners sat in the section set aside for them. His memorial is a pair of 16-foot monoliths, one for Hank and one for Audrey Williams, yet the city long did little to point visitors toward it; in 1982 country music journalist Martha Hume noted that the Annex was not signposted and not even listed in the telephone directory. The grave found its own fame: Alan Jackson's 1992 hit "Midnight in Montgomery" is about a night-time visit to it, and F. Scott Fitzgerald modeled the Confederate burial ground in his story "The Ice Palace" on Oakwood.

## Flight Context

Oakwood Cemetery lies at about 32.384 N, 86.295 W, on the northeast edge of downtown Montgomery, Alabama, reached from Upper Wetumpka Road. From the air, look for the large tract of graves just northeast of the downtown grid, with a railroad along one side. An altitude of 2,000-3,000 feet AGL gives a good view while staying clear of busier airspace. Maxwell Air Force Base (KMXF, military) is about 3.5 nm to the west, and Montgomery Regional Airport (Dannelly Field, KMGM) is about 7 nm to the southwest; both sit in controlled airspace, so contact ATC. Wetumpka Municipal (FAA identifier 08A, no ICAO code listed) lies about 9 nm to the north. Summer haze and thunderstorms are common.
