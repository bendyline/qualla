# Holt Street Baptist Church

On the night of December 5, 1955, a crowd too big for its walls gathered at this Montgomery church and launched the year-long Montgomery bus boycott.

**Location:** 32.3647, -86.3203

**Categories:** Civil rights movement, African-American history in Montgomery, Alabama, Baptist churches in Alabama, Churches in Montgomery, Alabama, History of African-American civil rights

## Introduction

The leaflet ended with an address. Distributed to thousands of Black residents of Montgomery in early December 1955, it urged them: "don't ride the bus to work, to town, to school, or anyplace Monday, December 5." Its final line told them where to go next: "come to a mass meeting Monday at 7:00 pm at the Holt Street Baptist Church for further instructions." That Monday night, the church at the corner of Holt and Bullock streets became the place where the Montgomery bus boycott truly began.

## A Neighborhood Church

Holt Street Baptist Church was established in 1909, and its building at Holt and Bullock streets was completed in 1913. The Reverend I. S. Fountain was its first pastor, and Charles Kenzie Steele led the congregation from 1939 to 1952. In 1955 it was the largest and most central church in Montgomery's Black community, and its building suited a crowd: a spacious main auditorium, a large basement that could hold hundreds, smaller rooms where loudspeakers could carry the proceedings, and a large outdoor area where people could gather.

## Monday, December 5

On Thursday, December 1, 1955, Rosa Parks was arrested after refusing to give up her seat on a Montgomery bus to a white passenger. The Women's Political Council, an African American civil rights organization founded in Montgomery, responded by organizing a boycott of the city's buses. It began on Monday, December 5, the same day Parks appeared in court, and that evening a mass meeting gathered at Holt Street to judge how the first day had gone. Every space the church had was needed. Parks later wrote that by the time she arrived, "the church was so filled up that a crowd of hundreds spilled out into the street, and speakers had to be set up outside to accommodate everyone." She remembered the mood: "The excitement around the church was electrifying, and I remember having a sense that something powerful was being born." The meeting launched a boycott that would last a year.

## Voting to Ride Again

While the boycott went on, the fight moved into the courts. A case called Browder v. Gayle argued that bus segregation violated the Fourteenth Amendment, and in November 1956 the Supreme Court ordered Alabama to desegregate its buses. Mass meetings were held at both the Hutchinson Street Baptist Church and Holt Street. Martin Luther King Jr. spoke at both, and some 8,000 people at the two gatherings voted unanimously to end the boycott once the Supreme Court decision took effect. Holt Street's role was not finished. On December 3, 1956, King returned to deliver "Facing the Challenge of a New Age," the opening address of a week-long Institute on Nonviolence and Social Change organized by the Montgomery Improvement Association. The congregation carried on for decades in the building where the boycott began, until 1998, when it moved to a new complex called "The Village" on South Court Street and the old church at Holt and Bullock closed. After about three years of renovations, a December 2019 report said the building was set to open as a museum commemorating the boycott.

## Flight Context

Holt Street Baptist Church stands at the corner of Holt and Bullock streets in west Montgomery, Alabama, at approximately 32.365°N, 86.320°W, southwest of downtown. The church is hard to spot from the air; use the downtown skyline and the Alabama State Capitol to the northeast as references from 2,000 to 3,000 feet. Maxwell Air Force Base (KMXF) is only about 2.3 nm to the west-northwest, so respect its military airspace, and Montgomery Regional Airport (Dannelly Field, KMGM) lies about 5.4 nm to the southwest. Summer afternoon thunderstorms are common.
