# Exchange Hotel, Montgomery

For more than a century Montgomery's Exchange Hotel was where Alabama power gathered, from Jefferson Davis's Confederate headquarters to the backroom deals of the 20th century, until a highway bypass left it a 'faded rose.'

**Location:** 32.3777, -86.3099

**Categories:** Hotels in Alabama, Demolished buildings and structures in Alabama, Buildings and structures in Montgomery, Alabama, Confederate States of America, History of Alabama

## Introduction

In the words of Montgomery newspaperman Joe Azbell, the Exchange Hotel was where "determined men walked upon those tile floors, made deals in the chairs of the high-ceiled lobby, decided the future of Alabama government," and swapped "black bags of payoff dough for laws, for fat contracts, for jobs and appointments." For well over a century, in two successive buildings at the corner of Montgomery and Commerce streets, the Exchange was a hotbed of Alabama politics. It began as a luxury hotel and ended, as Azbell put it, as a "faded rose."

## Luxury on Commerce Street

A group of local businessmen launched the hotel in 1846, hiring the builders Robinson and Bardwell, the firm also responsible for the Alabama State Capitol, to work with the architect Samuel Holt. The Exchange was finished in the fall of 1847. In 1855 it passed to Sterling Lanier, who owned three hotels in the South, and a string of managers followed, among them his son William B. Lanier and his son-in-law Abram P. Watt. Meetings of the legislature and party conventions, one account noted, contributed "largely to the business of the hotel." Behind the hospitality was forced labor: on the eve of the Civil War, Watt held eight African Americans in slavery who labored in the hotel.

## The Man and the Hour

When Montgomery briefly served as the capital of the Confederacy, Jefferson Davis made the Exchange both his headquarters and his home. The secessionist William Lowndes Yancey introduced Davis to the people of Montgomery from the hotel's Commerce Street balcony, declaring that "the man and the hour have met," words later recorded on a plaque in the hotel. Davis kept coming back long after the war. He spoke from the piazza in April 1879 and stopped again in April 1886. The rebuilt hotel later carried a bronze plaque on its second floor commemorating his stay, and in 1913 the Daughters of the Confederacy placed another on the Montgomery Street side marking his inauguration speech. Politicians of the reunited nation used the balcony too. In 1887 President Grover Cleveland visited Montgomery and spoke from it.

## Clerks Who Wrote Poems

Sterling Lanier's grandsons, the poet Sidney Lanier and his brother Clifford, both worked at the Exchange as clerks. The job had its literary rewards. When a Lanier recognized the name of W. J. Scott, editor of the Atlanta magazine Scott's Monthly, in the guest register, he introduced himself, and Scott went on to publish a number of Lanier's poems. After the war Clifford managed the hotel and, in January 1872, became a co-owner with an R. L. Watt. It was still a proud establishment. The historian Matthew Powers Blue, whose history of Montgomery appeared in 1878, wrote that "few hotels have as high a reputation, well constructed, well officered, and complete in all of the appointments."

## Robes in the Lobby

The first Exchange came to an end in 1904, and by 1906 a new hotel had replaced the original four-story building. In its heyday it was the place where "congressmen, senators, commissioners, mayors, city commissioners" used the hotel as a "political tool," and in the mid-century it was also a favorite spot for weddings. Its darker guests are part of the record too. By one account, the Ku Klux Klan gathered there to "thwart the mongrelization of the races," and in the early 1950s the Klan's "Imperial Emperor," Lycurgus Spinks, held meetings there with dozens of robed Klansmen. Azbell, unimpressed, described Spinks as "doing mouth-to-mouth resuscitation on a corpse of a cause."

## Undone by a Bypass

The hotel's end came from the road. Governor Gordon Persons had promoted his political career from the Exchange, and his campaign mastermind ran the effort from there. Yet by one account, once Persons built Montgomery's Southern Bypass the old hotel's fate was sealed, because travelers began stopping at the motels along the new highway instead. The national chain Milner Hotels took over in 1966 and turned the Exchange into lodging for permanent residents and for railroad and bus company workers. It did not last. In 1974, with motels replacing hotels and Montgomery's nightlife in decline, the building was torn down. In the mid-1980s the Colonial Company raised a "handsome polished granite and glass building" on the site.

## Flight Context

The Exchange Hotel stood at the corner of Montgomery and Commerce streets in downtown Montgomery, Alabama, at approximately 32.378°N, 86.310°W, near Court Square and a short distance south of the Alabama River. Nothing of the hotel remains, but the downtown grid, the river bend and the Alabama State Capitol at the head of Dexter Avenue to the east make good references from 2,000 to 3,000 feet. Maxwell Air Force Base (KMXF) is only about 3 nm to the west, so respect its military airspace. Montgomery Regional Airport (Dannelly Field, KMGM) lies about 6 nm to the southwest. Summer afternoon thunderstorms are common.
