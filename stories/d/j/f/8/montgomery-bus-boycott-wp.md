# Montgomery bus boycott

For 381 days, the Black citizens of Montgomery walked, carpooled and rode mules rather than accept segregated buses, and their refusal carried a young minister named Martin Luther King Jr. onto the national stage.

**Location:** 32.3675, -86.3000

**Categories:** Montgomery bus boycott, African-American history of Alabama, Civil rights movement, Boycotts, 1955 protests

## Introduction

At rush hour in Montgomery in the winter of 1955, the sidewalks were crowded and the city buses rolled by nearly empty. The city's Black residents walked, rode bicycles, hitchhiked and climbed into volunteer carpools; some even rode mules or drove horse-drawn buggies. Black churches across the country reportedly collected new and gently used shoes to replace footwear worn out by all that walking. For 381 days, from December 5, 1955, until December 20, 1956, they refused to ride segregated buses, in one of the foundational campaigns of the American civil rights movement.

## Ten Seats at the Front

The rules on Montgomery's buses were precise and humiliating. The first ten seats were always reserved for white riders and the last ten for Black riders, with sixteen seats in between filled from opposite ends. If another white passenger boarded when the middle filled, every Black person in the nearest row had to stand, because it was illegal for Black and white passengers to sit side by side. Black riders often paid at the front, stepped off, and re-entered through the rear door, and drivers sometimes pulled away before they could climb back on. They were not hired as drivers, though they made up 75 percent of the system's riders. Many were assaulted, shortchanged or stranded after paying their fares. Rosa Parks knew these indignities personally. Twelve years before her arrest, driver James F. Blake ordered her to board at the rear door and then drove off without her. She vowed never again to ride a bus he was driving.

## Mrs. Parks Keeps Her Seat

Parks was a seamstress and secretary of the Montgomery NAACP, where she investigated cases of sexual assault; in 1944 she had gone to Abbeville to investigate the gang rape of Recy Taylor. She was not the first to defy the buses. Fifteen-year-old Claudette Colvin and eighteen-year-old Mary Louise Smith had been arrested for refusing to give up their seats. Four days before her arrest, Parks sat in the audience at Dexter Avenue Baptist Church as T. R. M. Howard spoke about the lynching of Emmett Till, and she later said Till was on her mind when she refused to move. On December 1, 1955, she was sitting in the first row of the middle section when a white man boarded and the driver told her row to move. She realized the driver was Blake. The others complied. Parks did not, and she was arrested, then convicted on December 5 and fined $10 plus $4 in court costs. She appealed.

## A Flyer in the Night

On the night of the arrest, the Women's Political Council, led by Jo Ann Robinson, printed and circulated a flyer through Montgomery's Black neighborhoods calling on everyone to stay off the buses on Monday. "The next time it may be you," it warned, "or your daughter, or mother." E. D. Nixon, the NAACP chapter president, had long wanted a test case, someone "above reproach" to challenge the law in court. He brought local ministers together, and his choice to lead the new Montgomery Improvement Association was Martin Luther King Jr., a young pastor so new to town, Nixon reasoned, that city leaders had not yet had time to intimidate him. On December 5 a mass meeting gathered at the Holt Street Baptist Church. King, given twenty minutes' notice, spoke, and the crowd voted enthusiastically to continue the boycott. The first demands were modest: courtesy, first-come seating and Black drivers on Black routes. Leaders judged a compromise likelier to succeed than full integration.

## A City on Foot

The boycott cost the bus system so many riders that it fell into serious economic distress. When the city pressured local insurers to drop the carpool cars, organizers bought policies from Lloyd's of London. When Black cab drivers charged a dime, the same as bus fare, officials ordered fines for any driver charging less than 45 cents. Women's groups such as the Club from Nowhere found new ways to raise money and keep spirits up, contributions some have described as essential to the boycott's success. Opposition hardened. White Citizens' Council membership doubled. King's and Ralph Abernathy's homes were firebombed, along with four Black Baptist churches, and boycotters were often physically attacked. King and 88 others were indicted under a 1921 ordinance for conspiring to interfere with a business, and they turned themselves in rather than wait for arrest. King was convicted and ordered to pay $500 or serve 386 days in jail, and the prosecution drew national attention. "I was proud of my crime," he said. "It was the crime of joining my people in a nonviolent protest against injustice."

## Victory, and What It Cost

On June 5, 1956, a federal court ruled in Browder v. Gayle that Alabama's bus segregation laws were unconstitutional, and on November 13 the Supreme Court upheld the decision. The boycott ended on December 20. White backlash followed swiftly. Someone fired a shotgun through King's front door, white men attacked a Black teenager as she stepped off a bus on Christmas Eve, and snipers fired on buses, wounding a pregnant woman in both legs. On January 10, 1957, bombs struck several Black churches and the homes of Ralph Abernathy and Robert Graetz, a white minister who had publicly supported the boycott. Death threats and blacklisting drove Parks from Montgomery. In almost every other setting, Montgomery remained overwhelmingly segregated. Yet the campaign made King a national figure and stirred activism across the South. In 2018 the National Memorial for Peace and Justice opened downtown with a sculpture by Dana King dedicated to the women who sustained the boycott.

## Flight Context

Downtown Montgomery sits at 32.368N, 86.300W on the south bank of a broad bend in the Alabama River. From 3,000 to 4,500 feet, the river bend and the downtown grid are the landmarks; the white-domed Alabama State Capitol at the head of Dexter Avenue marks the city center.
