# Maxwell Air Force Base

On the Montgomery field where the Wright brothers once taught people to fly, a congressman's stubborn lobbying built the Air Corps Tactical School and, in time, the Air Force's university.

**Location:** 32.3792, -86.3625

**Categories:** Military bases, United States Air Force, Aviation history, Montgomery, Alabama, Military education

## Introduction

On August 12, 1920, engine trouble forced Second Lieutenant William C. Maxwell to bring his DH-4 down into a sugarcane field in the Philippines. Below him, a group of children were playing. Maxwell maneuvered to avoid them, struck a flagpole hidden in the tall cane, and was killed instantly. Two years later the Army gave the name of the airman, who had grown up in Atmore, Alabama, to an airfield in his home state, at Montgomery. Maxwell Field would become something its namesake could hardly have imagined: the intellectual headquarters of American air power, home today to Air University and the Air Force's center for professional military education.

## The Wrights' Classroom

The site's aviation story begins before the Army arrived. In February 1910, the Wright brothers decided to open one of the world's earliest flying schools here, teaching students the fundamentals: take-offs, balancing, turns and landings. The school wound down in late May 1910, but the field kept its purpose. During World War I it served as an aviation repair depot, and on September 20, 1918, the depot exhibited what was billed as the first airplane built in Montgomery. The Army, which had leased the land during the war, bought it on January 11, 1920, for $34,327. On November 8, 1922, on the recommendation of his former commanding officer, the War Department renamed the depot Maxwell Field.

## Saved by a Freshman Congressman

Peace nearly finished the field. In 1919 the War Department announced plans to close thirty-two facilities nationwide, Montgomery's depot among them, even though its $27,000 monthly civilian payroll mattered enormously to the city. In June 1921, 350 civilian employees were laid off. Like most Army air stations of the era, the field made do with temporary wartime buildings meant to last two to five years, and by the mid-1920s such shacks had become a national disgrace. Help came from J. Lister Hill, a World War I veteran newly elected to Congress. In 1925 he attached an amendment to a military appropriations bill providing $200,000 for permanent buildings, without the blessing of the War Department or the Air Corps. Hill then pushed for an attack group. The Air Corps chief and his assistant asked him, as "a friend of the Air Corps," not to "embarrass" them, and in the end Shreveport, Louisiana, won the group. But in December 1928 the Assistant Secretary of War announced a different prize: the Air Corps Tactical School would move to Maxwell Field from Langley Field, Virginia.

## Forty-One Future Generals?

Montgomery leaders scrambled to assemble land, and the project kept growing, first to twice and then to four times its planned size. George B. Ford and Frederick Law Olmsted Jr. designed the campus layout, and Ford's plan clustered similar functions together, leaving plenty of open space. Before the school even opened, the field proved its worth in March 1929, when personnel from Maxwell brought relief to flooded Montgomery; it was one of the first times U.S. military forces airdropped food and supplies during a major civilian emergency. On September 17, 1931, forty-one students gathered for the first training session, and at the formal launch, Air Corps chief James E. Fechet declared they could be future generals. Early teaching reflected Billy Mitchell's belief in air superiority and pursuit aircraft, but by the mid-1930s the emphasis had shifted to bombardment. The school also produced two aerobatic teams, including Captain Claire L. Chennault's "Three Men on a Flying Trapeze" in 1932.

## Cadets and Superfortresses

The approach of World War II ended the Tactical School, suspending classes in June 1940. That July the training center at Maxwell was redesignated the Southeast Air Corps Training Center, handling basic, primary and advanced flying training at airfields across the eastern United States. A preflight school drilled aviation cadets in mathematics and the physics of flight before teaching them aeronautics, deflection shooting and thinking in three dimensions. Over the war years Maxwell hosted six different schools training aviators and their support teams. In July 1943 the Army Air Forces announced a specialized school for pilots of four-engine aircraft, and the first B-24 Liberator landed later that month. In early 1945, B-29 Superfortress training replaced the B-24 program, and wartime training at Maxwell ended on December 15, 1945.

## The Air Force Goes to School

Air University was established here in 1946, a year before the Air Force became an independent service, and it remains the heart of the base. Maxwell Field became Maxwell Air Force Base in September 1947. Construction of the Academic Circle began in the 1950s, centered on the Air University Library. Over the decades the base gathered Headquarters Civil Air Patrol-USAF, the Air Force Historical Research Agency, Air Force ROTC headquarters, and, from 1994, Officer Training School. In March 1992 nearby Gunter Air Force Base was folded in as a hedge against future base closures, creating the combined Maxwell-Gunter installation. Flying continues too: in January 2024 a detachment activated here to train crews on the MH-139A Grey Wolf, on the ground where the Wrights once taught their students to turn and land.

## Flight Context

Maxwell AFB lies at about 32.379 N, 86.363 W, a few miles west of downtown Montgomery, Alabama. It is an active military airfield (KMXF) with controlled airspace, so civilian pilots should view it from outside the base's airspace and coordinate with ATC; 3,000-4,000 feet over the city is a typical sightseeing altitude. Look for the base runway and the Air University campus with the downtown skyline to the east. The nearest civil airport is Montgomery Regional (Dannelly Field, KMGM), about 5 nm to the south-southwest. Prattville-Grouby Field (FAA identifier 1A9) and Wetumpka Municipal (FAA identifier 08A) are general-aviation options within about 10 nm; neither has an ICAO code listed. Summer brings haze and afternoon thunderstorms.
