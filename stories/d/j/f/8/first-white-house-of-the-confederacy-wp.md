# First White House of the Confederacy

Jefferson Davis lived in this rented Montgomery house for only a few months in 1861, but it has been bought, moved, restored and argued over ever since.

**Location:** 32.3762, -86.2999

**Categories:** Historic house museums in Alabama, American Civil War museums in Alabama, Houses in Montgomery, Alabama, Italianate architecture in Alabama, Lost Cause of the Confederacy

## Introduction

The first executive mansion of the Confederate States of America was a rental. In February 1861, shortly after choosing Jefferson Davis as president, the Provisional Confederate Congress, meeting in Montgomery, authorized the leasing of a residence, and Edmond Harrison of nearby Prattville offered his house fully furnished and staffed for $5,000 a year. The Davis family lived there for only a few months. The house they left behind has been bought, moved and restored in the decades since, and the way it tells its story has become a part of that story.

## A Merchant's House

The house predates the Confederacy by a generation. It is thought to have been built between 1832 and 1835 by William Sayre, one of Montgomery's merchants, at the corner of Bibb and Lee streets. A later owner, J. G. Winter, remodeled it and sold it to Harrison. What visitors see today is a large two-story wooden frame house painted white and green, five bays wide, set on brick piers and dressed in the Italianate style. A portico at the center rests on fluted columns behind a balustrade, and the wall inside it is made of wooden planks shaped to imitate stone. A heavy bracketed cornice wraps the hipped roof, and its ventilation openings are ornamented with a Liberty cap design; the cornice of the rear one-story wing uses a flower instead.

## Rooms for a President

Inside, a central stairhall opens onto a double parlor, two bedrooms and a back hallway. The parlors are joined by sliding doors, and the hall ends in an archway framed by Greek Revival doorways beneath an elaborate cornice. One detail is especially clever: the side porch connects to the dining room through a hinged window that doubles as a door. Upstairs are four more bedrooms and a room that was most likely a nursery. When the Davises moved in, they asked for extra silver and linen, which added $987 to the lease. The family entertained often. Varina Davis hosted the first of their gatherings for the ladies of Montgomery, and records describe the events as both lavish and well managed.

## Gone by Summer

The arrangement was short-lived. In May 1861 the Confederacy moved its capital to Richmond, Virginia, and on May 27 the Davises left the Montgomery house for what became the Second White House of the Confederacy in Richmond. The Confederate government sold its lease. After the Civil War the house passed to William Crawford Gibb and, in 1871, to the Tyson family. The United Daughters of the Confederacy began planning to take it over, but progress stalled until the White House Association of Alabama formed in 1900 to lead the effort. Even then the owner would not sell the land beneath the house, and the association could not afford to move it.

## A New Address

The breakthrough came around 1919. With $25,000 from the Alabama Legislature, the White House Association bought the house and moved it to its present site at 644 Washington Avenue. Accounts differ on whether the move came in 1919 or 1921, but the restored house was presented to the state there on June 3, 1921. Today it is open to the public, completely furnished with original period pieces from the 1850s and 1860s. It has been listed on the National Register of Historic Places since 1974 and on the Alabama Register of Landmarks and Heritage since 2012.

## Whose History?

The house has also drawn debate. In 2017, the state's subsidy of $100,000 a year drew criticism from several groups and individuals, among them State Senator Hank Sanders. They objected to the way the displays and materials presented Alabama's history and the ideology of the Lost Cause of the Confederacy, a presentation in which slavery, as an issue leading to the Civil War, seemed to play no part. The dispute shifted attention from the antiques in the rooms to the story those rooms are used to tell.
