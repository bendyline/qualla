# Avondale Mills

For 109 years Avondale Mills spun Alabama cotton into livelihoods, with progressive benefits and child labor side by side, until a chlorine disaster at a South Carolina plant helped bring the company down.

**Location:** 33.1827, -86.2585

**Categories:** Textile mills in the United States, Industrial history, Child labor, History of Birmingham, Alabama, Sylacauga, Alabama

## Introduction

Avondale Mills survived the Great Depression and a statewide textile strike. What helped finish it was a train wreck in another state. On January 6, 2005, a derailment outside the company's plant in Graniteville, South Carolina, ruptured a tank car of chlorine, and nine people died. Sixteen months later the company announced it would close for good. It was an abrupt end to a business that began in 1897 in a Birmingham neighborhood, grew into a network of mills across Alabama and beyond, and at its peak consumed 20 percent of all the cotton the state produced.

## Looking South for Cotton

Alabama had long lagged in textiles. One account counts only 12 mills and 12,580 spindles there in 1852, while Georgia ran 40 mills with 80,000 spindles. The Trainer family of Chester, Pennsylvania, already successful in the trade, wanted to move closer to the cotton fields, and Birmingham offered a wide labor pool and the transportation links to ship finished goods across the country. Birmingham civic leaders pledged $150,000, and Braxton Bragg Comer, a banker, miller and cotton farmer recruited as the mill's president, added $10,000. B. F. Roden of the Avondale Land Company supplied the land on one condition: the mill had to be called Avondale Mills. Money was scarce and interest rates high, so the founders borrowed from Wall Street banker DuMont Clark and sold $100,000 in bonds to J. M. Lewis of Talladega.

## A Governor's Mills

The early years were difficult, but the Comers held on. In 1906 B. B. Comer was elected governor of Alabama and kept the mill presidency anyway; by 1907, under his son Donald, the mills had turned a $55,000 profit. Their success, and the tax revenue that came with it, proved critical to the "Greater Birmingham" movement that folded outlying suburbs into the city. A single worker could earn up to $700 a year, and whole families could work. Comer's schools were far better than those at most American mills and often took in children whose parents did not work there, and while most mill villages had outhouses, his houses had interior sanitation. Donald set out to expand, opening the Eva Jane mill in Sylacauga in 1913, named for B. B. Comer's wife; its opening was called the most important event in the town's history. More mills followed in 1919 and 1926, often in rural areas where farm families could earn mill wages and keep their land.

## Children in the Lint

The progressive reputation had a darker side. A product of his time, Comer used child labor relatively extensively. Alabama law barred only children under 12 from mill work, and families arriving from farms were used to children working alongside adults. In 1910 the sociologist and photographer Lewis Hine visited Avondale and documented numerous child workers with "mere weeks of education if any." He wrote that mill bosses arrived at school at any time of day to take children to work. The jobs were fast and demanded nimble fingers more than skill, and the rooms where the children worked filled with lint. It settled in young lungs, and many of those children later developed brown lung disease as adults. Their conditions were common at the time; today they are considered abusive.

## Holding Together in Hard Times

When the Depression came, Avondale cut hours, production and pay like mills across the country. In 1933 labor organizers began unionizing many Birmingham industries, and in 1934 textile workers throughout Alabama walked off the job to protest steep wage cuts. Donald Comer reminded his employees of the quality of life at his mills, and accounts differ on how far the walkout reached into them in July 1934: one describes a single night shift, another the Birmingham, Stevenson and Sylacauga plants, which Comer closed until President Roosevelt called the workers back. Later, the company also shared profits with its employees, on a fifty-fifty basis by one account. After Donald handed control to his brother-in-law, James Craig Smith Jr., Avondale expanded into Georgia and South Carolina. Harder times followed: sales fell 20 percent, the company lost $14 million, and four chief executives came and went in five years before Walton Monroe Mills bought it in 1986 for $165 million.

## The Cloud Over Graniteville

The two companies merged as Avondale Incorporated in 1993, and in the mid-1990s Avondale acquired the Graniteville Company of South Carolina. There, on January 6, 2005, a Norfolk Southern train hit an improperly aligned switch and slammed into a parked locomotive near the Avondale plant, derailing 16 of its cars. One car, carrying 90 tons of chlorine, split open, and roughly 60 tons of gas escaped and rolled through the plant and the neighboring town. Nine people died that day, six of them Avondale employees. Weakened by the disaster and by growing overseas competition, the company's chief executive announced in May 2006 that Avondale would close all of its operations; a confidential settlement with Norfolk Southern followed in 2008. More than 4,000 people in four states lost their jobs.

## Flight Context

The story's coordinates, about 33.183 N, 86.259 W, fall in Sylacauga, Alabama, where Donald Comer opened the Eva Jane mill in 1913 and where the company was later headquartered; the original 1897 mill stood in Birmingham's Avondale neighborhood, roughly 40 miles to the northwest. From 3,000-4,000 feet, Sylacauga appears as a compact town set in open country southeast of Birmingham. Merkel Field Sylacauga Municipal Airport (KSCD) is about 2.5 nm to the west-southwest.
