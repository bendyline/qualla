# Talladega College

Alabama's oldest private historically Black college began when three formerly enslaved men built a one-room schoolhouse, then found a permanent home in the very academy building they had been forced to construct for white students.

**Location:** 33.4321, -86.1133

**Categories:** Historically black universities and colleges in Alabama, Talladega College, Schools supported by the Freedmen's Bureau, Schools supported by the American Missionary Association, Liberal arts colleges in Alabama

## Introduction

William Savery was a carpenter. Thomas Tarrant and Ambrose Headen were laborers. Before the Civil War, all three were enslaved, and their labor helped raise the Coosa River Valley Baptist Academy, a school founded in Talladega in 1852 for white students. Within two years of emancipation, those same men had become founders of a school for the children of freed families, and in 1867 it moved into the very building they had been forced to construct. Today that school is Talladega College, the oldest private historically Black college in Alabama, and the old academy, now Swayne Hall, still stands as the symbol of its beginning.

## A Pledge Made in Mobile

On November 20, 1866, Savery and Tarrant traveled to Mobile for a Freedmen's Bureau convention of newly freed men. The gathering produced a commitment that became their guiding purpose: "We regard the education of our children and youth as vital to the preservation of our liberties, and true religion as the foundation of all real virtue, and shall use our utmost endeavors to promote these blessings in our common country." Back home, joined by Headen, they set to work. Their first school was a single room built from lumber salvaged from an abandoned carpenter's shop, and it filled with pupils from the day it opened. It soon became clear the community needed something much larger.

## Reclaiming the Academy

Opportunity came in the form of a mortgage default. The Baptist academy nearby was about to be sold, and an urgent appeal went to Major General Wager Swayne of the Freedmen's Bureau. Swayne persuaded the Bureau to buy the land on the condition that the American Missionary Association purchase the building and organize the new school. In the end, the land and building cost $23,000. The AMA named it the Swayne School, and it opened in November 1867 with about 140 pupils. In 1869 the county granted a charter, and the Judge of Probate changed its name to Talladega College. The story's reversal is hard to overstate: a building raised by enslaved workers for white students became home to Alabama's first private historically Black liberal arts college. Swayne Hall, the oldest building on campus, was designated a National Historic Landmark on December 2, 1974.

## Fifty Cents a Month

By 1909 the college was an entire educational community of 797 students. There were theology students and college men and women, high schoolers preparing for college, students (most of them young women) training as teachers, children in the grammar department and the Cassedy School for the youngest grades, music conservatory pupils, nursing trainees and adults in night school. Tuition ranged from 50 cents to two dollars a month. Room and board cost ten dollars a month, plus an hour of work each day. The campus grew around them. De Forest Chapel, built in 1903, honors the Rev. Henry Swift De Forest, president from 1879 to 1896 and father of the inventor Lee De Forest. Silsby Science Hall, from 1926, is named for E. C. Silsby, who taught at the college for 37 years. Juliette Derricotte House remembers a 1918 graduate who was serving as a trustee when she died in 1931.

## The Ship on the Library Floor

Savery Library, completed in 1939 to replace a 1907 building funded by Andrew Carnegie, carries the name of William Savery, the carpenter who helped start it all. Set into its floor is an image of La Amistad, the ship whose enslaved African captives seized control and later won their freedom in a United States court. School tradition holds that it must never be stepped on. Hale Woodruff was commissioned in 1938 to paint murals for the new library's lobby, depicting the Amistad uprising, the Underground Railroad and the founding of the college, and they hung there for more than seventy years. After a restoration in partnership with Atlanta's High Museum of Art, they moved in 2020 to the Dr. William R. Harvey Museum of Art, founded in 2014, where they are the showcase of the collection. The library's mezzanine Galangue Room still holds an extensive collection of Angolan and Nigerian artifacts. Thirty-two campus buildings are listed together on the National Register of Historic Places as the Talladega College Historic District.

## The Great Tornado

Talladega's teams are the Tornadoes, competing in the NAIA across 16 varsity sports. Football, played from 1909 to 1943, has been gone for decades, though a club football partnership with Prep U. Collegiate, with some games on campus, was announced in May 2026 for the fall. Since 2012 the college has fielded something just as loud: the Tornado Marching Band, known as the Great Tornado. With more than 200 members, five drum majors and a danceline called the Dega Diamonds, it is said to be the largest organization on campus. It debuted at the Honda Battle of the Bands in 2015, marched in the 2017 presidential inauguration parade in Washington, D.C., played halftime shows for the New Orleans Saints, and on New Year's Day 2024 paraded through London. The 50-acre college keeps an open admissions policy, and in 2020 it opened the 47,000-square-foot Dr. Billy C. Hawkins Student Activity Center, named for its 20th president.

## Flight Context

Talladega College lies at 33.432N, 86.113W in the city of Talladega, in east-central Alabama. From 2,500 to 3,500 feet, look for the campus within the town; wooded ridges rise to the east and southeast. Talladega Municipal Airport (KASN) is about 9 nm to the north-northeast, beside the unmistakable oval of Talladega Superspeedway, which makes an excellent visual reference. Anniston Regional Airport (KANB) is about 16 nm to the northeast and Merkel Field Sylacauga Municipal (KSCD) about 18 nm to the southwest. Expect temporary flight restrictions around the speedway on race weekends.
