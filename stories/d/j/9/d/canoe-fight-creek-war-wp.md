# Canoe Fight (Creek War)

On November 12, 1813, three militiamen and a free Black paddler named Caesar met a canoe of Red Stick Creek warriors in the middle of the Alabama River, in a few minutes of hand-to-hand fighting that changed little in the Creek War but became an enduring frontier legend.

**Location:** 31.4318, -87.5767

**Categories:** Battles of the Creek War, Battles involving canoes, Native American history of Alabama, Alabama River, Battles of the War of 1812, Clarke County, Alabama

## Introduction

When the fighting began, the most important job in the little dugout belonged to the one man who was not trying to shoot anyone. Caesar, a free Black man, was paddling three Mississippi Territory militiamen out onto the Alabama River on November 12, 1813, toward a canoe of Red Stick Creek warriors. When the two boats met, Captain Samuel Dale ordered him to hold them together, and Caesar did, while the men around him fought hand to hand. The skirmish lasted only minutes and held little military value in the Creek War, yet the Canoe Fight was told, illustrated and painted for more than a century afterward.

## A Nation Divided

The Creek War grew out of a conflict among the Creeks themselves. One faction had championed the growth of the Creek governing body known as the National Council and paid off debts to the United States by selling Creek lands in present-day Alabama and Georgia. Their opponents, the Red Sticks, resented the council's growing power, believed governing should remain with the Creek clans, and were angered by the loss of traditional lands and the settlers arriving on them. The quarrel had already turned into civil war. The United States, fearing the Red Sticks would ally with Great Britain during the War of 1812, sided with the Creeks who supported it, along with Choctaw and Cherokee warriors. As Red Stick warriors began attacking settlers who encroached on Creek lands, residents of the Mississippi Territory built protective forts.

## Harvest on a Dangerous River

After the Fort Mims massacre, militias formed in what is now southern Alabama to protect settlers as they brought in the fall harvest, and Red Stick attacks continued. Dale led a band of volunteers up the Alabama River, with six to eight men in canoes under Jeremiah Austill and the rest marching along the eastern bank. Near the mouth of Randon's Creek, Dale's men surprised a band of warriors preparing food and killed one, a métis man whom Dale identified as Will Milfort, son of Le Clerc Milfort and Jeannet McGillivray, sister of Alexander McGillivray. The going on the east bank was hard, so Dale ordered his men to cross to the west bank. Twelve, including Dale, Austill and James Smith, stayed behind to prepare some of the captured food. Then shouts came across the water: they were surrounded by Creek warriors.

## Eleven Men in a Canoe

The twelve rushed to the riverbank, but neither canoe had come back from ferrying the others. What they saw instead was a canoe carrying eleven Red Stick warriors coming downstream, which turned back the moment its paddlers spotted the militiamen. For reasons no one knows, the warriors surrounding the men on the east bank never attacked. Two men in the canoe jumped into the river and swam for shore, and Smith shot and killed one of them. Dale called for eight soldiers to cross in the larger canoe, but when they saw how many warriors were in the Red Stick boat, they turned back. Only the smaller canoe, with Caesar at the paddle, reached the eastern shore. Dale decided to take it out himself, with Austill and Smith beside him.

## Rifles and Oars

As Caesar drove the dugout toward the warriors, all three militiamen tried to fire. Dale's and Austill's guns failed because their priming was wet, and Smith's shot missed. The canoes came together, and the Red Stick leader knocked Austill down with his rifle. Dale ordered Caesar to hold the two boats side by side, and in a few minutes of close fighting, with rifles and oars swung as clubs, the three militiamen killed the warriors remaining in the canoe. Austill, struck on the head, was the only one of Dale's men wounded, and he recovered. The Red Stick warriors who died in the canoe, men who had taken up arms over the loss of Creek lands and power, go unnamed in the accounts that made the fight famous, which came from the militiamen themselves.

## Murals and a Marker

The fight made its participants famous. Dale became, in the words of the Encyclopedia of Alabama, as legendary to early Alabamians as Daniel Boone and Davy Crockett were to Kentuckians and Tennesseans. In the mid-1930s the painter John Augustus Walker painted murals for Mobile's Old City Hall, now the History Museum of Mobile, and one of them is said to show Austill and Caesar in the fight. Austill's handwritten account, with a sketch of the field, also survives. The exact site of the canoe fight is unknown, though it happened near where Randon's Creek joins the Alabama River, and the place where warriors surrounded the twelve men on the east bank has been confirmed on private property. Today only a roadside historical marker, which sources place near Barlow Bend or Gainestown, tells passersby what happened on the river.
