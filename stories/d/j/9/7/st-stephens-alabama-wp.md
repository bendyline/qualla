# St. Stephens, Alabama

On a limestone bluff where the Tombigbee's shoals stopped the boats from Mobile, St. Stephens rose from Spanish outpost to Alabama's territorial capital, then emptied so completely that its streets are now an archaeological site.

**Location:** 31.5403, -88.0553

**Categories:** Former colonial and territorial capitals in the United States, Ghost towns in Alabama, History of Alabama, National Register of Historic Places in Washington County, Alabama, Archaeological sites in Alabama

## Introduction

In 1819, St. Stephens had more than 500 homes, two hotels, some twenty stores and Alabama's first chartered bank. It was the capital of the Alabama Territory, and its population numbered in the thousands. Within little more than a decade, most of its people had left. Today the old town site on the Tombigbee River lies within St. Stephens Historical Park, listed on the National Register of Historic Places and counted among the most important archaeological sites in Alabama, where researchers work to map the foundations of a capital that simply emptied out.

## Where the Boats Had to Stop

Geography made St. Stephens. The town grew on a limestone bluff that Native Americans called Hobucakintopa, at the fall line of the Tombigbee River, where rocky shoals ended navigation for boats coming north from Mobile, 67 miles to the south. As early as 1772, the British surveyor Bernard Romans saw the potential: "sloops and schooners may come up to this rapid; therefore I judge that here some considerable settlement will take place." The Spanish got there first, building Fort San Esteban on the bluff in 1789. Ten years later, after a treaty set the boundary between Spanish and American territory, the fort proved to lie on the American side, and on May 5, 1799, an American officer raised the U.S. flag over it.

## Deerskins for Cloth

In 1803 the United States opened the Choctaw Federal Trading House at St. Stephens, named for the dominant Native American nation in the area, where Americans traded manufactured goods such as tools and cloth for deerskins. In 1805 the federal government appointed George Strother Gaines to head the Choctaw Agency as its representative to the Choctaw people. Gaines made practical use of what the Spanish had left behind, running the agency's store out of the old blockhouse, setting up a land office in the former warehouse and living in the Spanish commandant's house. In 1811 he built what may have been the first brick building constructed by Americans in what is now Alabama, a warehouse.

## A Capital in a Hurry

Settlers around the fort asked the Mississippi Territorial legislature for official recognition, and it chartered the town of St. Stephens on January 8, 1807. An amended charter of December 18, 1811, officially renamed it Saint Stephens. By 1815 the town site had been surveyed and lots were sold, and in 1816 there were about 40 houses. Growth came fast. St. Stephens served as the seat of government of the new Alabama Territory from 1817 to 1819, and the first territorial assembly met at the Douglass Hotel in January 1818. By 1819 the town counted more than 500 homes, several thousand residents and the Tombeckbe Bank, the first chartered bank in Alabama.

## Bypassed by History

The collapse came almost as quickly. In 1819 the capital moved to Cahaba. New shallow-draft boats could carry trade farther upstream, past the town that had grown up where navigation once ended, and yellow fever epidemics struck in the 1820s and 1830s. By 1833 most residents had moved about two miles west, and a "new" St. Stephens took shape inland, eventually growing around a railway station beside the old site. It has the post office, Baptist and Methodist churches and homes; the St. Stephens Methodist Church building, dated to the 1850s, joined the Alabama Register of Landmarks and Heritage in 1976. The village appeared in the 1890 census, then vanished from the counts until 2010. The old capital on the bluff now belongs to the archaeologists.

## Flight Context

Coordinates: 31.5403°N, 88.0553°W, on the west bank of the Tombigbee River in Washington County, Alabama, with Mobile about 51 nautical miles to the south in a straight line (sources usually quote 67 miles, a longer, non-straight distance). From 2,000 to 3,000 feet, follow the Tombigbee north from Mobile and look for the limestone bluff at the river's old fall line, where St. Stephens Historical Park now lies. Nearby airports: Jackson Municipal (FAA identifier 4R3, no ICAO code), about 9 nautical miles east-southeast, across the river two miles south of Jackson, Alabama, and Mobile Regional (KMOB), about 52 nautical miles to the south. Summers are hot and humid with frequent afternoon thunderstorms.
