# Pensacola Light

The light at the mouth of Pensacola Bay began as a ship that could not be seen from sea and grew into a tower of roughly 150 feet that survived a Civil War artillery duel and still guides vessels today.

**Location:** 30.3464, -87.3081

**Categories:** Lighthouses in Florida, Lighthouse museums in Florida, Buildings and structures in Pensacola, Florida, National Register of Historic Places in Escambia County, Florida, Lighthouses completed in 1859

## Introduction

The first Pensacola Light floated. In 1823 the lightship Aurora Borealis came east from her old post at the mouth of the Mississippi River, where a lighthouse had just been completed, to mark the entrance to Pensacola Bay. The Gulf did not cooperate. Seas were so often rough that she had to anchor inside the bay entrance, tucked behind Santa Rosa Island, where ships outside the bay could not reliably see her. More than two centuries later the light she started is still working, now shining from a tower of roughly 150 feet that has outlasted a war, an artillery duel and automation.

## Reaching for Height

The first solution was a tower on land. In late 1824 a 40-foot lighthouse was lit on a bluff at the entrance to the bay, but it too had a problem: trees close to the tower and on Santa Rosa Island partially blocked its beam. The second solution was to build much higher. In 1858 work began on a new tower on the north side of the bay entrance, and it was lit on January 1, 1859. This is the tower that stands today, between 150 and 160 feet tall depending on the source, on its own bluff of about 40 feet, which puts the light roughly 190 feet above sea level. The bluff now lies within Naval Air Station Pensacola, so the old lighthouse shares its ground with the aircraft of a working military base.

## Lining Up the Bar

The new site gave the light a second job. From the north shore it could serve as the rear range light, working with a lower front light to mark the passage across the Pensacola Bar. Little is known about the first front light. Around 1879 a new one, the Pensacola Bar Beacon, was built 448 feet southeast of the tower. It was a square, pyramidal wooden tower 26 feet tall, standing on a point 29 feet above sea level so that its lamp burned 55 feet above the water. A sixth-order Fresnel lens threw a fixed white light visible for 11 miles. Sometime in the early 1900s the beacon was taken out of service and torn down, leaving the great tower to stand alone.

## Caught Between the Guns

When the Civil War began, the lighthouse ended up on the front line. Confederate forces held Pensacola, while Fort Pickens, across the bay, stayed in Union hands. Confederate authorities removed the lighthouse's lens, and most of its supplies were requisitioned for the war effort. In November 1861 the two sides fought an artillery duel across the water, and the tower was damaged in the exchange. The Confederates later evacuated Pensacola and Union troops took their place. In December 1862 the light shone again, this time through a smaller fourth-order Fresnel lens, and in 1869 a new first-order lens returned it to full strength.

## Black Above, White Below

Through the war years the tower was entirely white. Later its upper two-thirds were painted black, giving the tower a two-tone look. The daily work of keeping the light changed slowly. Until electricity arrived in 1939, someone had to rewind the clockwork that turned the light every four and a half hours. In 1965 the light was automated, and in 1974 the tower and its associated buildings were placed on the National Register of Historic Places.

## Keepers of a Different Kind

The Pensacola Light remains an active aid to navigation, but it now welcomes visitors as well as ships. Coast Guard Auxiliary Flotilla 17 once led tours, until they were discontinued in 2007. Tours resumed on a limited basis within a year or two, and the lighthouse now keeps daily hours. Today the Pensacola Lighthouse Association, a nonprofit with a small staff and a large corps of volunteers, maintains the tower and runs the tours. The 1869 keeper's quarters beside it hold exhibits, and a renovated carriage house serves as the visitor center and museum shop.

## Flight Context

Pensacola Light stands at approximately 30.346°N, 87.308°W on the north shore of the Pensacola Bay entrance, inside Naval Air Station Pensacola. The light sits about 190 feet above sea level, making the tall tower a prominent mark on the bluff; look across the entrance toward Santa Rosa Island and Fort Pickens. The lighthouse is only about 0.7 nm from Naval Air Station Pensacola Forrest Sherman Field (KNPA), so expect busy military training traffic and controlled airspace, and coordinate with ATC before descending for a closer look. Pensacola International Airport (KPNS) lies about 10 nm to the northeast. Coastal haze and summer afternoon thunderstorms are common.
