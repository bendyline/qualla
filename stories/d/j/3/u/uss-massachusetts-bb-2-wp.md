# USS Massachusetts (BB-2)

One of America's first modern battleships, Massachusetts missed the great sea battle of 1898 while taking on coal, suffered groundings and deadly accidents, and ended as a gunnery target sunk off Pensacola, where she now lies as a reef.

**Location:** 30.2969, -87.3114

**Categories:** Indiana-class battleships, Spanish–American War battleships of the United States, Shipwrecks in the Gulf of Mexico, Shipwrecks on the National Register of Historic Places in Florida, Artificial reefs

## Introduction

On the night of July 2, 1898, the battleship Massachusetts left the blockade of Santiago de Cuba and steamed to Guantánamo Bay to load coal. The next morning, the trapped Spanish fleet tried to break through the blockade and was completely destroyed. Massachusetts missed it all. She returned a day later to find the decisive battle of the Spanish-American War already over. It was not the last time the ship would find herself in the wrong place. Her career ran through reefs and fatal accidents, and it ended, strangely, in the shallow Gulf water off Pensacola, where she rests today.

## Too Much on Too Little

Massachusetts grew out of a failed dream. An 1889 policy board proposed a fleet of more than 30 battleships and 167 smaller ships, and Congress, seeing an attempt to end American isolationism, refused. A year later it funded three coast defense battleships instead, authorized on June 30, 1890. Massachusetts was built by William Cramp & Sons of Philadelphia; the contract was $3,020,000, but the finished ship cost nearly twice that. She was 351 feet long, displaced 10,288 long tons as designed, made 15 knots, and carried an armored belt 18 inches thick. Her class pioneered an intermediate battery, eight 8-inch guns in four twin wing turrets. Yet the ships came out badly overweight, handled poorly, and rode so low that their decks were not safe from high waves on the open ocean. Conway's All the World's Fighting Ships judged the design as "attempting too much on a very limited displacement." They were nevertheless counted as the first modern battleships of the American fleet. Thousands, including Captain George Dewey, watched her launch on June 10, 1893, and she was commissioned exactly three years later.

## The Blockade and the Battle She Missed

In March 1898 Massachusetts joined Commodore Winfield Scott Schley's Flying Squadron for the blockade of Cuba. The squadron first searched the harbor of Cienfuegos, spending several days confirming that Admiral Cervera's Spanish ships were not there, then moved on to Santiago de Cuba, where on May 29 a Spanish ship was spotted inside the harbor. For the next month Massachusetts held her place in the blockade, occasionally bombarding the harbor forts. Then came the coaling trip, and the battle of July 3 went on without her. On July 4 she returned and fired on a disarmed ship the Spanish were scuttling in an attempt to block the harbor channel. After supporting the American occupation of Puerto Rico, she steamed home and reached New York on August 20.

## Reefs, Rocks and a Turret Explosion

Peace brought a run of misfortune. Leaving New York Harbor on December 10, 1898, she struck Diamond Reef and flooded five forward compartments, and repairs took about three months. In March 1901 she grounded in Pensacola harbor but was able to continue. During target practice in January 1903, an explosion in an 8-inch turret killed nine crewmen, reported as the first fatalities aboard a U.S. battleship since the loss of Maine in 1898. That August she hit a rock in Frenchman Bay, Maine, and was seriously damaged. In December 1904 a broken gasket filled a boiler room with steam, killing three men and badly burning others. She was modernized after 1906, but by 1910 the Secretary of the Navy called her "obsolete and worthless, even for the second line of defense." She carried midshipmen on summer cruises, trained Naval Reserve gun crews during World War I, and in March 1919 was renamed Coast Battleship Number 2 so that her name could go to a new dreadnought.

## Target, Wreck, Reef

Struck from the Navy's register in November 1920, the old battleship was loaned to the War Department for artillery experiments. That year she was retroactively given the hull number BB-2. On January 6, 1921, she was scuttled in shallow water off Pensacola and shelled by the coastal batteries of Fort Pickens and by railway artillery. In 1925 the Navy took the wreck back and offered it for scrap, but no acceptable bids came in, so she stayed where she lay. The Supreme Court of Florida eventually declared her state property. On June 10, 1993, exactly a century after her launch, the site became Florida's fourth Underwater Archaeological Preserve, and in 2001 it was added to the National Register of Historic Places. The battleship that missed her great battle now serves a gentler purpose, as an artificial reef and a destination for divers.

## Flight Context

The wreck lies at about 30.297 N, 87.311 W in shallow Gulf water just outside Pensacola Pass, Florida, south of the gap between Fort Pickens on Santa Rosa Island and the tip of Perdido Key. From 1,000-2,000 feet the pass, Fort Pickens and the Pensacola Lighthouse on the mainland make good references; the wreck itself is underwater and may be hard to spot. NAS Pensacola Forrest Sherman Field (KNPA) is only about 3.4 nm north, a busy military training field with its own controlled airspace, so contact the tower or stay clear. Pensacola International (KPNS) is about 12 nm north-northeast and Gulf Shores International Airport (Jack Edwards Field, KJKA) about 19 nm west. Expect sea breezes, summer thunderstorms and the June-November hurricane season.
