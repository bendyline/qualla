# Presidio Santa Maria de Galve

Founded in 1698 to hold Pensacola Bay against the French, Spain's wooden presidio of Santa María de Galve survived rot, hurricanes and an eight-year siege before changing hands three times in a single year.

**Location:** 30.3471, -87.2972

**Categories:** Spanish forts in the United States, Colonial forts in Florida, Spanish Florida, Pensacola, Florida, 1698 establishments in the Spanish Empire

## Introduction

Sand and humidity breached the walls of Fort San Carlos de Austria before any army did. Spanish builders raised the fort in 1698 on a bluff above Pensacola Bay, laying pine logs horizontally against upright posts and packing the space between the inner and outer walls with sand. The damp climate and that constant contact with sand rotted the timbers so quickly that by 1707 most of the walls had been replaced with vertical logs. The fort was the heart of the Presidio Santa María de Galve, the first European settlement at Pensacola since Tristan de Luna's colony of 1559 to 1561, and it would spend its two decades fighting weather, rivals and the sea.

## A Line Drawn Against France

The presidio existed because of the French. Late in the 17th century, French expansion down the Mississippi River spurred Spain to protect its interests along the northern coast of the Gulf of Mexico, and in 1698 the Viceroy and Audiencia of New Spain founded Santa María de Galve to guard the western approaches to Spanish Florida. At the time, Spanish Florida exercised effective control only from the Atlantic coast to the Apalachicola River. The threat Spain feared arrived almost at once: the French built Fort Maurepas near present-day Biloxi, Mississippi, in 1699, and founded Mobile, now in Alabama, in 1702. A small wooden outpost on a sandy bluff suddenly found itself on the front line between rival empires.

## Eight Years Outside the Walls

The first assault came from another direction entirely. In August 1707, a force of English colonists from the Province of Carolina and Tallapoosas attacked the settlement, and the village outside the fort was abandoned and burned. The Spanish held the fort, and they held it again when the attackers returned that November. With English support, the Tallapoosas kept up the pressure for eight years, raiding and capturing more than 70 Spaniards they found outside the fort during that time. For the garrison, every trip beyond the walls carried risk. Then, in 1715, the Tallapoosas broke their alliance with the English and established friendly relations with the Spanish at Fort San Carlos de Austria. With the siege over, a new village grew up beside the fort.

## Three Flags in One Year

Peace lasted only a few years. In May 1719, during the War of the Quadruple Alliance, the French attacked Santa María de Galve, and the Spanish surrendered Fort San Carlos de Austria after a five-hour battle. They recaptured it in August, only to lose it to French forces again the following month. France held the presidio for three years, until a 1722 treaty returned the Pensacola Bay area to Spain, and the French burned the fort and village before leaving. Spain did not rebuild the presidio on the bluff, though it later returned to the high ground nearby to build Fort San Carlos de Barrancas, completed in 1797. After the loss of 1719, the seat of government for West Florida moved to the Presidio Bahía de San José on the St. Joseph Peninsula. In 1722 it shifted again, to the Presidio Isla Santa Rosa Punta de Sigüenza on Santa Rosa Island, where it remained for about three decades, until hurricanes, notably in 1752, drove the Spanish to the mainland, where the Presidio San Miguel de Panzacola was established in the mid-1750s.

## A Settlement Built of Wood and Thatch

The fort measured 100 by 100 varas, a Spanish unit whose length varied but was generally somewhat less than a meter, with walls three varas high above a 3.3-foot-deep moat. Inside stood a church, a hospital, a warehouse, the governor's house and three barracks, one each for officers, soldiers and convict laborers. There was no local stone suitable for building and no brick kiln, so everything was wood. Walls and roofs began as palmetto thatch, and the more important buildings later gained plank walls and roofs of lead sheeting or shingles; some had solid floors, others bare dirt. Several hurricanes struck during the settlement's two decades, eroding the bluff beneath the fort. Eventually the south wall, closest to the water, had to be rebuilt behind its original line, pulling the fort out of its original square.

## Beneath a Naval Air Station

The presidio stood in the area of Fort Barrancas, on ground that is now Naval Air Station Pensacola. The French burned the fort and village on their way out in 1722, and Spain chose to rebuild elsewhere, first on Santa Rosa Island and later on the mainland. Archaeologists from the University of West Florida investigated the site in a study that ran from 1995 through 1998, and what they studied is the footprint of a short, hard-fought chapter: a thatched village, a wooden fort perpetually being rebuilt, and a garrison that held a contested bay for as long as it could.

## Flight Context

The presidio stood on the bluff near Fort Barrancas, at about 30.348°N, 87.298°W on Naval Air Station Pensacola, facing the entrance to Pensacola Bay and about 1.3 nm north-northwest of Fort Pickens at the western end of Santa Rosa Island. Viewing from 2,000 to 3,000 feet shows the bay mouth, the barrier island and the air station together. Nearby airports: NAS Pensacola / Forrest Sherman Field (KNPA, military; about 1 nm west-northwest, so avoid its airspace unless cleared) and Pensacola International (KPNS), about 9.5 nm to the northeast. Summer afternoons along the Gulf coast often bring thunderstorms.
