# Capture of Pensacola (1719)

In the summer of 1719 the Spanish outpost on the bluffs above Pensacola Pass changed hands three times in four months, as France and Spain traded it back and forth during the War of the Quadruple Alliance.

**Location:** 30.3478, -87.2972

**Categories:** Conflicts in 1719, Battles in Florida, Battles involving France, Battles involving Spain, Battles of the War of the Quadruple Alliance, Battles and conflicts without fatalities, History of Pensacola, Florida, 1719 in North America

## Introduction

Between May and September of 1719, the Spanish outpost on the red bluffs above Pensacola Pass changed hands three times. First the French took it. Then a Spanish force took it back, helped along by a French ship that sailed into Havana under a flag of truce. Then the French returned. The Capture of Pensacola was a small episode in the War of the Quadruple Alliance, but for the soldiers holding this bluff it was a season in which the flag overhead never seemed settled.

## Red Bluffs Facing the Pass

The prize was the Presidio Santa María de Galve, which Spain established on Pensacola Bay in 1698. It stood on the red bluffs facing Pensacola Pass, a height the Spanish called the Barranca de Santo Tomé, and its fort bore the name San Carlos de Austria. The ground would matter again long after 1719: the same bluff later became the site of Fort Barrancas. In May 1719, with France and Spain at war, a French force led by Jean-Baptiste Le Moyne de Bienville took and occupied the settlement in the Spanish colony of Florida. Among those who fell into French hands was the Spanish governor, Juan Pedro de Matamoros de Isla. The French now held Pensacola, but only with a small garrison, and Spain had not given up on it.

## A Flag of Truce in Havana

The turn came by sea. A French ship carrying prisoners of war, the governor among them, arrived in Havana under a flag of truce. The Spanish fleet there captured it instead, returned the prisoners to Santa María de Galve, and in turn captured the French garrison. In August 1719, a large Spanish force arrived at Pensacola and compelled the small French garrison to surrender. Men who had been shipped away as captives came back with the expedition that retook their post. For a few weeks, Spain held the bluff again, and the French occupation that had begun in May was over almost as soon as it had started.

## The French Come Back

The Spanish occupation did not last long either. In September, a French squadron under Admiral Desnos de Champmeslin arrived to reassert French control, and after an attack on the presidio the Spanish surrendered once more. Fort San Carlos de Austria did not survive the tug-of-war; it was destroyed. When the war ended with the Treaty of The Hague, Pensacola was officially returned to Spanish control in exchange for French commercial benefits. Even so, the French held the bay for roughly three years in all, until about 1722, and Spain won the town back on paper before it had it back in fact. Three changes of hands in a single summer had settled nothing that a treaty could not.

## Flight Context

The site lies at approximately 30.348 N, 87.297 W on the bluff above Pensacola Pass, the later location of Fort Barrancas, inside Naval Air Station Pensacola. From 2,000-3,000 ft MSL, look for the narrow pass between the mainland and the western tip of Santa Rosa Island, with the bay opening to the north. Naval Air Station Pensacola Forrest Sherman Field (KNPA) is only about 1 nm west-northwest, so this is busy military airspace with heavy training traffic; coordinate with ATC before overflying. Pensacola International (KPNS) lies about 9 nm northeast, and Gulf Shores International Airport (Jack Edwards Field, KJKA) about 20 nm west. Gulf coast summer afternoons frequently bring thunderstorms.
