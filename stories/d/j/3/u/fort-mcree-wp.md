# Fort McRee

Built on a shifting sandbar to guard Pensacola Bay, Fort McRee was battered to silence in 1861, burned, stripped for bricks and finally surrendered to the sea, leaving only scattered foundations on Perdido Key.

**Location:** 30.3258, -87.3169

**Categories:** American Civil War forts in Florida, Florida in the American Civil War, Gulf Islands National Seashore, Buildings and structures in Escambia County, Florida, Military installations established in 1839

## Introduction

Nobody can say for certain exactly where Fort McRee stood. An 1882 survey recorded two different sets of coordinates for it, one placing the fort just onshore of what remains of Foster's Bank and the other putting it under water off the beach. The ground itself has not stayed still: Foster's Bank, the sandy tip of Perdido Key, has shrunk and grown with the weather and the sea for more than 170 years. War, fire, storms and salvage have done the rest. Of the three forts that once guarded Pensacola Bay, this is the one that nearly vanished, reduced to a few scattered foundations at the edge of the Gulf.

## A Fort Shaped by a Sandbar

After the War of 1812, the United States built three major installations to defend Pensacola and its natural harbor: Fort Pickens on Santa Rosa Island, Fort Barrancas on the mainland, and Fort McRee on the eastern tip of Perdido Key, a stretch of beach called Foster's Bank. McRee went up between 1834 and 1839 as a three-tiered fort with a detached water battery near sea level, and because it sat on a small, narrow barrier island, it took a highly unusual shape. Its landward walls, the gorge, held a 12-foot-wide sally port flanked by powder magazines. Named in April 1840 for the Army engineer Colonel William McRee, it did not receive its 122 guns until sometime between 1843 and 1845, and rot in the second-tier wooden decking likely delayed many of them further. The first troops arrived on May 2, 1842. After the Mexican-American War, the fort was manned only for drills, maneuvers and target practice.

## Slipping Away to Fort Pickens

In January 1861, with only about 50 men to hold all three forts, First Lieutenant Adam J. Slemmer decided to concentrate his small force where it could best defend itself. On January 8, Union soldiers at Fort Barrancas fired warning shots at Florida militia who demanded the fort's surrender, an incident some accounts count among the first shots of the war. Over January 9 and 10, Slemmer's garrison spiked the guns at Barrancas and McRee and crossed the bay to Fort Pickens. They moved just in time: on January 12, Florida and Alabama militia took over the empty forts. Through the months that followed, both sides built batteries and brought in troops, trading cannon fire many times with little effect. By the end of the year, about 7,000 Confederates faced some 2,000 Union soldiers around Fort Pickens.

## Two Days Under the Guns

After General Braxton Bragg's attack on Fort Pickens failed on October 9, 1861, the Union commander, Colonel Harvey Brown, planned his answer, and Fort McRee, the closest Confederate work and a roadblock to any assault on Pensacola, was the primary target. On the morning of November 22, the guns of Fort Pickens and the warships Niagara and Richmond opened fire. McRee's gunners held their own at first and heavily damaged the Richmond, but by 5 p.m. the fort's guns had fallen silent. After the day's fight its commander, Colonel John B. Villepigue, reported half his guns dismounted and his powder unprotected, and asked to destroy the fort and withdraw. Bragg, worried about the effect a retreat would have on his men and on the enemy, refused. On November 23 the bombardment resumed, and McRee never answered. Shells blew away large chunks of wall and punched holes clean through others, a section collapsed, most of the wooden decking burned, and a powder magazine caved in, killing six Confederates. When the Confederates abandoned Pensacola in May 1862, they burned the fort.

## Mined for Bricks

For three decades the fort stood battered and burned. An expense report for 1871-1872 records $191.29 spent maintaining it. In 1875 the War Department let the commander at Fort Barrancas remove 50,000 of its bricks for walkways and repairs, and in 1885 the Army Corps of Engineers turned down a group of businessmen who offered $500 for the ruins, reasoning that the bricks were worth more as repair material. Renewed interest in coastal defense brought new batteries in 1898 and 1899, named for Slemmer and for Lieutenant J. P. Center, but a hurricane on September 26 and 27, 1906, destroyed most of the newer structures. The guns of Battery Slemmer went to Europe during World War I to be mounted on railway cars. During World War II, foundations were poured for a battery of two 6-inch guns, but the war ended before the guns were installed.

## Foundations in the Surf

The Navy took over the site with Fort Barrancas in 1947, and in 1971 the land passed to the National Park Service as part of Gulf Islands National Seashore, ending nearly 140 years of military ownership. Forts Pickens and Barrancas survived through continued use and later preservation, but McRee, reachable only on foot or by boat, was left to the storms and erosion. Today visitors who make the trip find a remote stretch of beach with no facilities of any kind and only a few scattered foundations. Metal detectors are prohibited, and any artifacts found must stay where they lie.

## Flight Context

The site is listed at 30.326 N, 87.317 W on the eastern tip of Perdido Key at the western side of Pensacola Pass, the entrance to Pensacola Bay, Florida. From 1,500-2,500 feet, Fort Pickens on the western end of Santa Rosa Island lies directly across the pass, and the Pensacola Lighthouse and Fort Barrancas stand on the mainland shore to the north at Naval Air Station Pensacola; nothing substantial of Fort McRee remains to see. The site is only about 1.6 nm from NAS Pensacola Forrest Sherman Field (KNPA), a busy military training field with its own controlled airspace, so contact the tower or stay well clear. Pensacola International (KPNS) is about 11 nm northeast and Gulf Shores International Airport (Jack Edwards Field, KJKA) about 18 nm west. Expect sea breezes, summer thunderstorms and hurricane season from June through November.
