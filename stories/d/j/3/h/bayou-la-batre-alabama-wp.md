# Bayou La Batre, Alabama

A French-named shrimping town on the Mississippi Sound, rebuilt by Vietnamese, Cambodian and Laotian newcomers and battered by Katrina, that Hollywood made famous without filming its shrimp boats.

**Location:** 30.4033, -88.2481

**Categories:** Cities in Alabama, Fishing communities in the United States, French-American culture in Alabama, Populated coastal places in Alabama, Vietnamese American culture, Hurricane Katrina

## Introduction

Movie audiences know Bayou La Batre as the place Forrest Gump became a shrimp boat captain. In Winston Groom's novel and the 1994 film, it is the home of Benjamin Buford "Bubba" Blue, Forrest's army buddy, and later of Forrest himself. Here is the twist: none of the film's shrimping scenes were shot here. The cameras set up in South Carolina's Lowcountry instead, mostly around Beaufort and Port Royal. The real Bayou La Batre never needed a stand-in. This small Mobile County city, home to 2,204 people at the 2020 census, is built around a working waterway that runs straight through its center and out toward the Gulf of Mexico.

## The Bayou of the Battery

The name is a leftover from French cannon. During the French settlement of the Gulf Coast, the bayou was known as the Riviere D'Erbane, but a battery of artillery the French maintained on its west bank gave it a new identity: the bayou of the battery, Bayou La Batre. Permanent settlement came in 1786, when Joseph Bouzage (the name also appears as Bosarge) received a Spanish land grant of 1,259 acres on the west bank. Bouzage was born in Poitiers, France, reached the Gulf Coast around 1760, and on June 5, 1762, married Catherine Louise Baudreau. The couple raised seven children, among them a single son, Jean Baptiste. His grant made this the first permanent settlement on the mainland of southern Mobile County. The modern city took far longer to arrive; Bayou La Batre was not incorporated until 1955.

## Shrimpers from Across the Pacific

The waterway called Bayou La Batre runs about two miles southwest from the middle of town to Portersville Bay, an arm of the Mississippi Sound. Mobile lies 27 miles up the road to the north-northeast, and the Mississippi state line is 12 miles west. After the Vietnam War, that water drew a large influx of Vietnamese American shrimpers, joined by Cambodian and Laotian refugees and their children. The attraction was familiar work: the town's shrimping industry resembled the one many had known in Vietnam. At the 2000 census, a third of the city's residents, 33.29 percent, were Asian; in 2010 the figure was 22.8 percent. In December 2007 a new library opened on Wintzell Avenue, inside the local office of Boat People SOS, a national organization that assists Vietnamese Americans, with $2,500 from the Mobile County Commission. Its purpose was practical and generous at once: a place where elderly immigrants could learn computer skills, children could practice Vietnamese after school, and day laborers could get online after work.

## Sixteen Feet of Water

On August 29, 2005, Hurricane Katrina drove a storm surge of nearly 16 feet into Bayou La Batre, with higher waves riding on top of it. The water engulfed the town. When it drew back, more than 23 shrimp boats sat stranded on shore beside a cargo ship, the 179-foot M/V Caribbean Clipper, owned by Caribbean Shipping Inc. Its captain had ridden out the storm aboard. The Clipper stayed on land for six months before a large crane returned it to the sea. Students at Alba Middle School turned cameras on the wreckage around them, and their photographs were exhibited in Alabama and the Chicago region; some were gathered into a book, Eyes of the Storm: A Community Survives after Katrina. Even the schools carry storms in their history. Booth Elementary, built in 2006, was delayed by Katrina, just as Alma Bryant High School had been slightly delayed by Hurricane Georges in 1998. Both stand on a street called Hurricane Boulevard.

## Adopted from Afar

Help arrived from the far side of the country. Right after the hurricane, students at Sierra High School in Truckee, California, adopted Bayou La Batre. They gathered donations, sold raffle tickets and filled their school gym with bedding, clothes and basic household goods, then sent roughly $15,000, the supplies and 15 students to help rebuild homes. A pen-pal program linked them with students at a school in Bayou La Batre. In October 2005, seven weeks after the storm, the city of Santa Monica, California, adopted the town as well. Its city council approved lending Bayou La Batre 18 vehicles for the cleanup, among them six pickups, two trucks with large cranes, utility vehicles with smaller cranes, a dump truck, street sweepers and a riding lawnmower, along with six chainsaws.

## Pirate Ships and Shrimp Boats

Stranger vessels than trawlers have set out from Bayou La Batre. For the sequels to Pirates of the Caribbean: The Curse of the Black Pearl, Disney had a floating Black Pearl built in the town's shipyards; by local accounts it was launched in April 2005. The town's shrimpers were also the subject of Big Shrimpin', a History channel reality series that ran for one season in 2011 and 2012, and the city counts Regina Benjamin, a former Surgeon General of the United States who founded its rural health clinic, among its notable people. Fame has found Bayou La Batre by several routes, from a novel and a film franchise to a cable reality series. The shrimp boats tied up along the bayou are the part that was never fiction.

## Flight Context

Bayou La Batre sits at 30.4033 N, 88.2481 W in southern Mobile County, Alabama. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet, follow the bayou itself: the channel cuts through the middle of town and runs about two miles southwest to Portersville Bay and the open Mississippi Sound, with shrimp boats lining its banks. Mobile lies to the north-northeast and the Mississippi line about 12 miles west. Roy E. Ray Airport (FAA identifier 5R7, no ICAO code) is about 3.6 nm northeast. Larger fields nearby include Trent Lott International Airport at Pascagoula (KPQL, about 15 nm west), Mobile International Airport (KBFM, formerly Mobile Downtown Airport, about 16 nm) and Mobile Regional Airport (KMOB, about 17 nm). Expect coastal haze and warm-season convection over the Sound.
