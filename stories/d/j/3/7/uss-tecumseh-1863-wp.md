# USS Tecumseh (1863)

Leading the Union ironclads into Mobile Bay in August 1864, the monitor Tecumseh struck a mine and capsized within sight of Fort Morgan, taking more than ninety men with her; she still lies upside down off the fort, a protected war grave.

**Location:** 30.2317, -88.0258

**Categories:** Ships of the Union navy, Canonicus-class monitors, Shipwrecks of the American Civil War, Shipwrecks of the Alabama coast, Ships sunk by mines, Shipwrecks on the National Register of Historic Places in Alabama

## Introduction

Water was already up to their waists when Commander Tunis A. M. Craven and his pilot, John Collins, reached the foot of the ladder to the main deck at the same moment. Craven stepped back. "After you, pilot," he is said to have told him. Collins climbed out, and Craven never followed; the monitor capsized before he could. It was the morning of August 5, 1864, at the mouth of Mobile Bay, and USS Tecumseh, the monitor leading the Union ironclads into the channel, had just struck a mine. She went down with more than ninety of her crew, the exact toll varying by account, and she lies upside down northwest of Fort Morgan to this day.

## Iron in Layers

Tecumseh was a Canonicus-class monitor, built for the U.S. Navy during the Civil War under a contract awarded to Charles Secor & Co., and named after the Shawnee leader Tecumseh. She was launched on September 12, 1863, and commissioned on April 19, 1864, with Craven in command. She was 223 feet long with a 43-foot beam, displaced 2,100 long tons and could make 8 knots. Her punch came from two 15-inch Dahlgren smoothbores in a single turret. Armor defined her. Five layers of one-inch wrought-iron plate, backed by wood, covered the exposed hull, while the turret and pilot house carried ten layers. A soft iron band ringed the base of the turret to keep shells and fragments from jamming it, a lesson learned when older monitors had jammed at the First Battle of Charleston Harbor in April 1863.

## A Detour Up the James

Though meant for Rear Admiral David Farragut's squadron at Mobile Bay, Tecumseh was first sent to Virginia. She guarded the transports carrying Major General Benjamin Butler's Army of the James upriver at the start of the Bermuda Hundred Campaign in May 1864, and in mid-June she helped block the river against Confederate warships, sinking four hulks and a schooner and laying booms across the channel. On June 21, Craven spotted Confederates building breastworks at Howlett's Farm and opened fire. A four-gun battery answered, and Confederate ships near Dutch Gap joined in, firing blindly at the Union monitors. Tecumseh fired forty-six 15-inch shells and was not hit once. Her trip south was harder. Her wire steering ropes, burned halfway through by boiler heat, broke and ran her aground, and on the tow to Pensacola her engine overheated and needed a week's repairs at Port Royal, South Carolina. She arrived off Mobile Bay on the evening of August 4.

## Past the Black Buoy

Farragut gave Tecumseh a central role. She and her sister ship Manhattan were to keep the Confederate ironclad ram Tennessee away from the vulnerable wooden ships as they passed Fort Morgan, and then sink her. The four Union monitors would form the starboard column, closest to the fort, with Tecumseh in the lead. The eastern side of the channel near the fort was clear, but "torpedoes," as mines were then called, were known to lie west of a prominent black buoy. Tecumseh opened fire at 6:47 a.m. to test her guns, and the Confederates began shooting at 7:05. By 7:30 she was about 600 yards from Tennessee. Craven judged that his clumsy monitor could not intercept the ram in time unless it passed through the minefield, and he ordered the pilot to steer straight for her. Ten minutes later, 100 yards from Tennessee, Tecumseh struck a torpedo and rolled over.

## The Men in the Water

More than ninety of the crew, Craven among them, went down with the ship; the sources' figures for her complement and losses differ, but roughly 20 men survived. Commander James Jouett of the gunboat Metacomet sent a boat under Acting Ensign Henry C. Nields to search for survivors, and it pulled ten men from the water, including the pilot, and delivered them to the monitor Winnebago. Seven others reached one of Tecumseh's own boats, and four swam ashore, where they were captured. A ship that had fired on Confederate positions along the James without taking a single hit was lost to a hidden mine, and most of her crew were lost with her.

## A Grave Beneath the Channel

In the mid-1960s the Smithsonian Institution formed a team to raise Tecumseh as the centerpiece of a planned National Armed Forces Museum Park in Washington, D.C. Divers found her in February 1967, capsized and buried just off Fort Morgan, and recovered an anchor, dishes and other artifacts before the primary donor withdrew the funding. Later plans fared no better. In 1974 Mobile naval historian Jack Friend estimated the cost of raising her at $10 million; more modern estimates put salvage and conservation at $80 million. A 1993 survey by East Carolina University archaeologists found the hull covered by a calcareous crust, with only nominal deterioration. Her engine room gong has been displayed at the Hampton Roads Naval Museum. The wreck is marked and watched by the U.S. Coast Guard, and as a war grave it may not be disturbed without permission from the Secretary of the Navy.

## Flight Context

The wreck of Tecumseh lies at approximately 30.23 N, 88.03 W in the main ship channel at the mouth of Mobile Bay, northwest of Fort Morgan. Nothing of the ship is visible from the air, but the setting is: from 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL, the channel between Mobile Point and Dauphin Island, Fort Morgan on the eastern shore and the Sand Island Light to the south-southwest outline the ground over which the battle was fought. Jeremiah Denton Airport (formerly Dauphin Island Airport; FAA identifier 4R9; no ICAO code) is about 5.5 nm west-northwest, Gulf Shores International Airport (Jack Edwards Field, KJKA) about 19 nm east, and H. L. Sonny Callahan Airport at Fairhope (KCQF) about 16 nm north-northeast. Expect summer thunderstorms, sea haze and tropical weather risk in hurricane season; over-water flight calls for appropriate altitude.
