# Sand Island Light (Alabama)

The third lighthouse to rise on a shrinking island at the mouth of Mobile Bay, this 132-foot tower has outlasted Civil War shelling and hurricanes, but not the Gulf's steady appetite for the sand beneath it.

**Location:** 30.1878, -88.0506

**Categories:** Lighthouses on the National Register of Historic Places in Alabama, Transportation buildings and structures in Mobile County, Alabama, Lighthouses, Civil War, Mobile Bay

## Introduction

In December 2011, workers finished building a new island around the Sand Island Lighthouse, a $6,000,000 effort to restore ground the Gulf had been steadily eroding at the mouth of Mobile Bay. Less than a year later, shifting sand and Hurricane Isaac had stripped it away. The 132-foot tower still stands roughly three miles offshore from the main bay entrance, at the southernmost point of Alabama, with Mobile Point to the east and Dauphin Island to the west. It has been dark since 1971, and it is the third lighthouse to rise on this restless patch of sand.

## A Monopolist's Lamps

The first lighthouse on Sand Island was a 55-foot tower built by Winslow Lewis, begun in 1837 and completed at the end of the decade. Its light came from 14 lamps set in 16-inch reflectors, the so-called Lewis lamp, a poorly designed version of the Argand-style lamp. Lewis kept winning such work because of his relationship with Stephen Pleasonton, a monopoly that lasted until 1853. A new lighthouse replaced his tower in 1859, but it would stand only a few years.

## Caught Between the Lines

The Civil War reached Sand Island in 1863. On February 23 of that year, the 1859 lighthouse was destroyed by a Confederate, John W. Glenn. Confederates at nearby Fort Morgan had seen U.S. Army soldiers in the lighthouse reconnoitering the fort. By one account Glenn rowed out and set about seventy pounds of gunpowder beneath the tower; by another, the fort's guns fired on the island. A tower built to guide ships safely into Mobile Bay had become a lookout for Union soldiers, and the Confederates would not leave it standing.

## On 171 Pilings

The lighthouse that replaced it was built to last. Its base, 28 feet in diameter and 6 feet thick, rests on 171 interconnected wood pilings covered with 12 feet of concrete, and the tower lifts its light to a focal height of 125 feet. A two-story keepers' dwelling stood beside it, and at the time the island covered approximately 400 acres. The current tower was first lit in 1873 and served until it was deactivated in 1971.

## Holding Back the Gulf

The sea has been the lighthouse's real adversary. Erosion ate at Sand Island so relentlessly that granite blocks were added to try to stave off the loss of the island and its tower. Hurricane Ivan in 2004 and Hurricane Katrina in 2005 damaged both Sand Island and the Mobile Bay lighthouse, and the repairs further delayed restoration. Then came the rebuilt island of 2011 and its quick loss to shifting sand and Isaac. Lighthouse Digest has placed the tower on its Doomsday List as one of the most endangered lighthouses in the country. The Dauphin Island Foundation, founded in 1991, works with the Alabama Lighthouse Association as agent and administrator for the Sand Island Restoration Project, and its board includes Dauphin Island's mayor alongside the leaders of the island's civic organizations. As of 2025 the tower stands in about 20 feet of water and is closed to visitors; the U.S. Interior Department donated the site to Dauphin Island in 2023, and the town has weighed restoring the tower in place or dismantling it and rebuilding it on Dauphin Island, with little money to do either.

## Flight Context

Sand Island Light stands at approximately 30.19 N, 88.05 W in the Gulf of Mexico off the mouth of Mobile Bay, roughly three miles offshore from the main bay entrance between Mobile Point to the east and Dauphin Island to the west. From 1,000 to 2,000 feet AGL the lone tower rising from the water is unmistakable, framed by Mobile Point on one side and Dauphin Island on the other. Jeremiah Denton Airport (formerly Dauphin Island Airport; FAA identifier 4R9; no ICAO code) is about 6 nm northwest. Gulf Shores International Airport (Jack Edwards Field, KJKA) is about 20.5 nm east-northeast, and Mobile International Airport (KBFM, formerly Mobile Downtown Airport) about 26 nm north. Over-water flight here calls for appropriate altitude and equipment; expect summer thunderstorms, sea haze and tropical weather in hurricane season.
