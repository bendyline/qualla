# Fort Bowyer

A fort of sand and logs at the mouth of Mobile Bay turned back a British squadron in 1814, then fell in what is usually counted as the War of 1812's last land engagement, fought after the peace treaty had already been signed.

**Location:** 30.2281, -88.0231

**Categories:** War of 1812 forts, Battles of the War of 1812, Forts in Alabama, Military history of Alabama, Buildings and structures in Baldwin County, Alabama

## Introduction

The last land engagement between British and American forces in the War of 1812, by the usual reckoning, was fought after the peace treaty had already been signed. On Christmas Eve 1814, diplomats signed the Treaty of Ghent, but word crossed the Atlantic slowly, and in February 1815 a British army was still digging siege lines against a small fort of sand and logs on Mobile Point, at the mouth of Mobile Bay in what is now Baldwin County, Alabama. Fort Bowyer fell on February 12. Within a day or two, a British ship arrived carrying news of the treaty.

## Sand, Logs and a Narrow Channel

Mobile had been a Spanish possession, but after the War of 1812 began, Congress declared it American territory, and when Spanish forces evacuated the town in April 1813, the Americans built a redoubt on Mobile Point, then part of the Mississippi Territory. Colonel John Bowyer completed the fort that June. It was fan-shaped, built of sand and logs, with its curved face aimed at the ship channel into Mobile Bay and a bastion flanked by two demi-bastions guarding the landward side. Its job was simple: command the narrow entrance to the bay and stop any British invasion there. It started with 14 guns. About a year later the Americans abandoned it, but in August 1814, Major William Lawrence and 160 men of the 2nd U.S. Infantry moved back in.

## The Burning of the Hermes

In September 1814, Royal Navy Captain William Percy set out to take the fort as a first step toward Mobile, from which the British could block Louisiana's trade and move overland to Natchez to cut New Orleans off from the north. He believed Fort Bowyer was a low wooden battery with perhaps six to 14 small guns. He brought four ships, HMS Hermes, Sophie, Carron and Childers, while Lieutenant Colonel Edward Nicolls landed about 60 Royal Marines and about 60 Native American allies with a howitzer some nine miles east on September 12. When the ships finally engaged and the fort opened fire at 3:20 p.m., Hermes was within musket-shot range; Sophie joined at 3:40, but the other two ships could not get into position. After two hours of fruitless bombardment, Hermes ran aground and lay helpless under the fort's guns. Sophie's boats took off her crew, Percy set her on fire, and she blew up when the flames reached her magazine.

## A Detour to New Orleans

The next morning the surviving ships crossed back over the bar and sailed away. The British had lost 34 men killed in the land and naval attacks, among them Royal Marine Charles Butcher, killed on shore; the Americans lost four killed. A court-martial later concluded that the circumstances had warranted Percy's attack, but the defeat changed British strategy. Rather than try Mobile again, they turned against New Orleans. Andrew Jackson, recognizing the fort's strategic importance, ordered it strengthened. Lawrence was less confident. For all Jackson's bravado, he described his position as precarious, because the landward approaches to the fort were undefended. After their defeat at New Orleans, the British commanders, Admiral Cochrane and General John Lambert, received considerable reinforcements and went back to their original plan: take Mobile first. That meant taking Fort Bowyer.

## Siege After the Peace

On February 8, 1815, Lambert landed a force of at least 1,000 men (some sources say 1,300 or more) about seven miles east of the fort, troops from regiments that had fought at New Orleans. The Royal Engineer Colonel Burgoyne surveyed the defenses and chose the method of attack. Over the following nights the British extended their siege works and completed four batteries, enduring constant American fire and taking light casualties. They brought 18-pounder cannon and howitzers, and a 25-man Royal Marine Artillery detachment arrived with Congreve rocket launchers. Lawrence's fear about the landward side proved right. On February 12, after an artillery barrage, Lambert called on the fort under a flag of truce to surrender, and Lawrence reluctantly did so after resisting for five days. One American had been killed and ten wounded, and 374 were taken prisoner. Around February 13, HMS Brazen arrived with news of the Treaty of Ghent. When word of its ratification followed, the British withdrew.

## Fort Morgan Rises

The fort the British captured was far stronger than the one Percy had imagined, mounting three long 32-pounders, eight 24-pounders, six 12-pounders, five 9-pounders, a mortar and a howitzer. Its weakness had always been the land behind it. The United States kept the ground. Fort Bowyer itself had lasted barely six years. Between 1819 and 1834 the United States built a new masonry fortification, Fort Morgan, on its site. The war itself changed little on the map; the final attachment of Mobile to the United States from the Spanish Empire was the only permanent exchange of territory during the War of 1812.

## Flight Context

Fort Bowyer's site, now occupied by Fort Morgan, lies at 30.2281°N, 88.0231°W on Mobile Point at the mouth of Mobile Bay, Alabama. From 1,500-2,500 feet AGL, look for the masonry fort on the point, with the ship channel into the bay beside it and Dauphin Island to the west. Nearby airports: Jeremiah Denton Airport (formerly Dauphin Island Airport; FAA identifier 4R9, no ICAO code), about 6 nm west-northwest across the bay entrance; Gulf Shores International Airport (Jack Edwards Field, KJKA), about 19 nm east-northeast; and Mobile International Airport (KBFM, formerly Mobile Downtown Airport), about 24 nm north. Coastal haze, sea breezes and summer thunderstorms are common along the Gulf shore.
