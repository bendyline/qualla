# Magnolia Springs, Alabama

At the headwaters of the Magnolia River, a Baldwin County community with roots in the 1700s became a town only in 2006, and some of its residents have long received their mail by boat.

**Location:** 30.3997, -87.7761

**Categories:** Towns in Baldwin County, Alabama, Towns in Alabama, French-American culture in Alabama, Rivers, Creole communities

## Introduction

In Magnolia Springs, some of the mail has long come by boat. The United States Postal Service has described the town as one of the few places in the country with year-round boat delivery, on a river route that dates to 1915. It suits a place whose mayor once called its river "the lifeblood of the whole community." Magnolia Springs sits at the headwaters of the Magnolia River in south Baldwin County, and although its story reaches back to the 1700s, it did not become a town until 2006. At the 2020 census it was home to 811 people.

## A River of Many Names

Before it was the Magnolia, local residents knew the river by other names: River de Lin, Cataract Creek, River del Salto. In the 1700s French and British settlers laid out plantations along its banks and along the shores of Weeks Bay, and the economy ran on cattle, fishing and masonry. Later one of the area's biggest businesses was turpentine distillation. That industry ended in smoke. In 1865, as Union soldiers began massing in the area, the owners of the stills burned them rather than let them be captured.

## A Community in a Scholar's Notebook

In 1931 the scholar Horace Mann Bond described Magnolia Springs in The American Journal of Sociology, in an article titled "Two Racial Islands in Alabama." He told readers to leave the Old Spanish Trail at the eastern head of the Cochrane Bridge and drive south through Fairhope along Mobile Bay; ten or fifteen miles beyond lay "the pleasant little village of Magnolia Springs," set in sandy Gulf Coast soil where a Creole community kept its farms and its community life. They called themselves Creoles. Their white neighbors, Bond reported, attached a racial slur to the name, and to the county's dominant white class the word meant only one thing: some degree of African ancestry, or "Negro extraction" in the language of the time. At a crossroads store a young Creole clerk, one of the few local men with a French family name, told Bond he was the great-grandson of an officer in Napoleon's Grande Armée. He had crossed the bay from Mobile County, he said, because the Creole community there was disintegrating, a decay he described as almost complete.

## Two Hundred Twenty-Four to Ninety-Six

For most of its history Magnolia Springs was a community without a town government. That changed in May 2006, when residents voted 224 to 96 to incorporate. Baldwin County Probate Judge Adrian Johns certified the results on June 29, 2006, and the town still marks that date as its anniversary. Self-government started modestly. The first council members served without pay, and an early proposal to pay the mayor and council $50 a month failed on a 3–3 vote. In February 2008 the council voted salaries of $100 a month for incoming members; the original version would have given the mayor $200, but it was altered to comply with tax reporting laws. The town contracts with the Baldwin County Sheriff's Department for law enforcement, and its children attend Baldwin County Public Schools.

## The Lifeblood

Everything circles back to the water. "The river is, if you would, the lifeblood of the whole community," Mayor Charles Houser told the Press-Register in 2008. "Whether you're using it for recreation or for whatever else, the river and its health has an impact on the whole community." In 2007, activists were working to have the Magnolia River named an Outstanding Alabama Water, the state's highest level of environmental protection, and the effort succeeded: the river had won that status by early 2010. The town has also declared itself a bird sanctuary.

## Flight Context

Magnolia Springs lies at approximately 30.400°N, 87.776°W in south Baldwin County, Alabama. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet, follow the winding, tree-lined Magnolia River west from town toward Weeks Bay, which opens south into Bon Secour Bay on the eastern side of Mobile Bay. Nearby airports: Foley Municipal Airport (FAA identifier 5R4, no ICAO code), about 4 nm east-northeast; H. L. Sonny Callahan Airport in Fairhope (FAA identifier CQF), about 6 nm northwest; and Gulf Shores International Airport (Jack Edwards Field, KJKA), about 8 nm southeast. Coastal haze and summer afternoon thunderstorms are common.
