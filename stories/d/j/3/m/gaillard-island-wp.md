# Gaillard Island

Built in Mobile Bay from the spoil of a ship channel, a 1,300-acre triangle of clay and sand became the place where Alabama's brown pelicans came back.

**Location:** 30.5056, -88.0361

**Categories:** Mobile Bay, Islands of Alabama, Artificial islands of Alabama, Bird sanctuaries, Landforms of Mobile County, Alabama

## Introduction

The engineers doubted it would hold together in open water. The environmentalists feared what it might do to the bay. Gaillard Island proved both camps wrong. Raised from the mud of Mobile Bay beginning in 1979, this 1,300-acre triangle of clay and sand has become an important nursery for the seabirds and shorebirds of coastal Alabama, and it has been the state's only brown pelican nesting site since the birds were first discovered here in 1983.

## Thirty-One Million Cubic Yards

The island began as a place to put something unwanted. The U.S. Army Corps of Engineers was dredging a deep-draft ship channel to connect Mobile Bay with the Theodore Industrial Park, a cut about 5.2 miles long, 300 feet wide and 40 feet deep, and all that excavated material needed a home. The solution was to build land where there had only been water. Hard red clay dug from shore and silty sand flecked with shell and gravel from the bay bottom traveled by barge and was hydraulically pumped into place to form the island's dikes. In all, thirty-one million cubic yards went into it. The Corps ran tests to learn how to keep its creation from simply washing back into the bay. The answer was a triangle, shielded from waves by floating breakwaters made of tires and anchored by marsh plants whose roots would bind the edges. That technique has since been widely studied, found effective and put to use nationwide.

## The Dentist's Island

Not everyone believed in the project. Engineers questioned whether an island could survive in an open body of water, and environmentalists worried about the ecological cost to the bay. The island's name honors a man who pictured it as something more than a dumping ground. Dr. M. Wilson Gaillard was a Mobile dentist and an environmentalist, and he envisioned the new land as a nesting haven for shorebirds and seabirds alike. Locals know it by a simpler name: Pelican Island. Birds were already settling in by the time the island was completed in 1981, and it has been a success from the start, an environmental showcase that is home to thousands of birds. Today it is owned by the Alabama State Docks and managed by the Corps of Engineers, the Alabama Department of Conservation and Natural Resources and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service.

## Four Pelicans in 1983

In 1983 a biologist found four brown pelicans nesting on the island. It was the first such sighting in Alabama since the species had been driven into decline. Hunting did the early damage in the 1900s, when pelican feathers were used to decorate women's hats, and the widespread use of the pesticide DDT, beginning in the 1940s, pushed the birds down further, until by the 1960s they had almost vanished from the Gulf Coast. The brown pelican ended up on both the Alabama and the federal endangered species lists. Four birds on a man-made island were a modest beginning, but the nesting there kept growing. Partly because of the pelicans raised on Gaillard Island, Alabama took the brown pelican off its endangered species list in 1995, and the federal government removed it from the national list in 2009.

## A Crowded Nursery

Pelicans are only part of the crowd. Skimmers, stilts, terns, egrets, herons and ibis all use the island, and it is the only place in Alabama where Caspian terns, Sandwich terns, royal terns and laughing gulls nest. Herring gulls were reported nesting here by 1986. The Alabama Department of Conservation and Natural Resources has surveyed the colonies annually since 1998, and on that first count it estimated some 10,000 nests. The plants tell a quieter version of the same story. Smooth cordgrass was the only species anyone planted, yet saltmeadow cordgrass, saltmarsh bulrush, salt marsh cattail and American threesquare have all flourished on their own behind the berms, turning an engineered dike into living marsh.

## Storms, Boats and Birdwatchers

People are not the island's main threat; weather is. Hurricanes Danny, Georges and Katrina all damaged it, and on August 29, 2005, Katrina's tidal surge eroded and destroyed its levee and berm system. The Federal Emergency Management Agency approved $8.6 million to restore it. Visitors are kept at a respectful distance. The island is closed to the public, and walking on it is prohibited because footsteps would disturb the nesting grounds, though boats can approach its beach and the perimeter is good for birding. Birdwatching is the main draw, and the Mobile Bay Audubon Society leads tours for local enthusiasts and conservationists, with spring the best season for watching the nests. Anglers have their own reason to come. Marine life is abundant around the island, making it a favorite spot for speckled trout, flounder and mullet, and shrimp are harvested here as well.

## Flight Context

Gaillard Island sits at approximately 30.506°N, 88.036°W in the middle of Mobile Bay, roughly 13 miles south of downtown Mobile and several miles east-southeast of the Theodore Industrial Park on the bay's western shore. Its triangular outline stands out clearly against open water; 1,500 to 2,500 feet gives a good view of the shape, the berms and the marsh behind them. Please avoid low passes, since the island is a major bird nesting colony, especially in spring. Nearby airports: Mobile International Airport (KBFM, formerly Mobile Downtown), about 7.5 nm north-northwest; H. L. Sonny Callahan Airport in Fairhope (FAA identifier CQF), about 9 nm to the east-southeast across the bay; and Mobile Regional Airport (KMOB), about 15 nm northwest. Gulf Coast haze and summer afternoon thunderstorms can reduce visibility over the bay.
