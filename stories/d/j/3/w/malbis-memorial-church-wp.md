# Malbis Memorial Church

A Greek monk's dying wish became a marble-and-mosaic Byzantine church in the Alabama woods, raised by the self-sufficient Greek community he founded and painted by iconographers working 75 feet above the floor.

**Location:** 30.6519, -87.8511

**Categories:** Greek Orthodox church buildings in the United States, Eastern Orthodox church buildings in Alabama, Churches completed in 1965, 20th-century Eastern Orthodox church buildings, Properties on the Alabama Register of Landmarks and Heritage, Byzantine Revival architecture in Alabama, Churches in Baldwin County, Alabama, Church buildings with domes, Tourist attractions in Baldwin County, Alabama

## Introduction

Jason Malbis left his instructions in writing: "build a Greek Orthodox church for me in Malbis." He died in 1942, on a trip back to Greece, and it took the community he had founded twenty-three more years to do it. When they finished, they had built something far grander than a country chapel. Malbis Memorial Church, formally the Sacred Patriarchal and Stavropegial Monastery of the Presentation (also rendered Entrance) of the Theotokos, rises in Baldwin County, Alabama, with white Pentelic marble, mosaics made in Italy, and an interior in which almost every available surface is painted. Yet, as the Alabama Historical Commission has described it, it has never had an active congregation.

## The Monk from Doumena

Malbis was born Antonios Markopoulos in the settlement of Doumena and spent the first half of his life in a monastery. Around 1900 he emigrated to Chicago, where he changed his name to Malbis. He did not stay. With his friend William Papageorge, he traveled around the country looking for a place to establish a Greek community, and in 1906 the two men found it: 120 acres near Daphne, Alabama, bought at $5 an acre. The land was thickly wooded and unsettled. It was, in other words, a blank page, and the former monk intended to write a whole village on it.

## A Village That Needed Nothing

Other Greek families joined Malbis and Papageorge, and together they began clearing and building. In 1909 they bought another 600 acres. By 1920 the community numbered 85 people, farming the land and supplying their own needs. Self-sufficiency became something close to an empire. Over the years Malbis Plantation came to run its own power plant, dairy, cannery, bank, garage, nursery and timber company, along with a motel and a bakery in nearby Mobile, and, by the plantation's own accounting, its holdings grew to more than 10,000 acres. What began as 120 acres of woods at $5 apiece had grown into a working world of its own, built by immigrants who had come a very long way to make it.

## Bread into Marble

Keeping the founder's promise required money, and the community found it in the bakery. Sam George Papas, the plantation's president at the time, sold the Malbis bakery on Broad Street in Mobile, and the proceeds financed the church. The Mobile firm Frederick C. Woods and Associates designed it in the Byzantine Revival style, taking inspiration from the Church of Ayia Paraskevi in Athens. Built of brick, limestone and marble on a cruciform plan, it has domed towers flanking a western facade with a stone arcade. Above the three sets of entrance doors, arched mosaics show Theotokos (Mary), Jesus and Saint Paul, with the four evangelists above and the Dove of Peace crowning the pediment. The Italian artist Sirio Tonelli made every exterior mosaic in his workshop in Pietrasanta and shipped them to Alabama. Archbishop Iakovos of America conducted the opening service, and the church was dedicated on January 3, 1965. Jason Malbis was laid to rest in a crypt inside, to the right of the iconostasis.

## Seventy-Five Feet Up

Step inside and the walls seem to speak in color. The Greek iconographer Spyros Tziouvaras and two assistants, Haralambos and Chris Tziouvaras, covered almost every surface with hand-painted iconography. Their most daring work floats in the dome above the transept: a Pantocrator, Christ the Almighty, painted while the team lay on a scaffold 75 feet above the floor. Corinthian columns and pilasters of red marble carry the nave, while the iconostasis, bishop's throne and pulpit are carved from white Pentelic marble. In 1979 Hurricane Frederic damaged much of this iconography, especially in the dome. After a series of failed restorations, Mobile-area artist David Riall took on the job, using research and experimentation to match the Tziouvarases' original work so closely that his repairs cannot be told from theirs.

## A Church Without a Parish

Malbis Memorial Church is one of only a handful of Greek Orthodox churches in Alabama, and it stands apart even among those. It does not belong to the Greek Orthodox Archdiocese of America; it answers directly to the Patriarchate of Constantinople. The Alabama Historical Commission has described it as lacking an active congregation; it hosts religious observances, special services and events such as weddings instead. The state listed it on the Alabama Register of Landmarks and Heritage on November 30, 1977, and in 2008 the Alabama Historical Commission designated the Malbis Plantation Historic District, which includes the church. That same year, the last of the plantation's original Greek settlers died.

## Flight Context

Malbis Memorial Church stands at approximately 30.652 N, 87.851 W in Malbis, Baldwin County, Alabama, near Daphne on the eastern side of Mobile Bay. From 1,500-2,500 ft MSL, look for the church's domed towers; the waters of Mobile Bay lie a few miles to the west and the city of Mobile beyond them. Mobile International Airport (KBFM, formerly Mobile Downtown) is about 11 nm west across the bay, and H.L. Sonny Callahan Airport (KCQF) in Fairhope about 12 nm south. Mobile Regional (KMOB) lies about 20 nm west. Navy training aircraft use outlying fields in the county, including Barin Field (KNBJ) about 19 nm southeast, so watch for military training traffic; the old Silverhill field (KNQB), about 6 nm south-southeast, has closed. Summer afternoons often bring thunderstorms over the bay.
