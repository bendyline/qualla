# Lynching of Michael Donald

In 1981 Klansmen murdered 19-year-old Michael Donald in Mobile, and his mother's lawsuit went on to bankrupt the United Klans of America.

**Location:** 30.6903, -88.0633

**Categories:** Civil rights history, Ku Klux Klan crimes in Alabama, Lynching deaths in Alabama, Racially motivated violence against African Americans in Alabama, 1981 in Alabama, Mobile, Alabama

## Introduction

For twenty-five years after the murder, the street kept its old name, Herndon Avenue. In 2006, Mobile renamed it Michael Donald Avenue, and the city's first Black mayor, Sam Jones, helped dedicate the renamed street at a ceremony that May. The renaming answered what had happened on that street on March 21, 1981, when members of the Ku Klux Klan left the body of a 19-year-old African American hanging from a tree. It stands as one of the last reported lynchings in the United States. It also became the case in which a grieving mother took on the Klan in court, and won.

## Nineteen Years Old

Michael Anthony Donald was born on July 24, 1961, the youngest of six children of Beulah Mae and David Donald. He grew up in Mobile and went to local schools, and in 1981 he was studying at a technical college while working at the city's newspaper, the Mobile Press Register. That spring, Mobile was hosting the trial of Josephus Anderson, an African American man charged with killing a white Birmingham police officer during an armed robbery. The case had been moved to Mobile on a change of venue, and the first trial ended with a mixed white and Black jury deadlocked. At a meeting held while the jury was still deliberating, Bennie Jack Hays, the second-highest-ranking official in the United Klans of America, reportedly said that if a Black man could get away with killing a white man, Klansmen ought to be able to get away with killing a Black man. His son Henry, 26, and James Llewellyn "Tiger" Knowles, 17, acted on that idea.

## A Walk Home

Armed with a gun and a rope borrowed from Hays's brother-in-law, the two men drove through Mobile looking for a Black person to attack. In one of the city's mostly Black neighborhoods they found Michael Donald, walking home after buying a pack of cigarettes for his sister at a nearby gas station. They asked him for directions to a local club, then forced him into the car at gunpoint and drove to woods near Mobile Bay. Donald fought back, knocking away Hays's gun and trying to run, but they caught him, beat him and strangled him, and Hays cut his throat. They left his body hanging from a tree on Herndon Avenue, across the street from Hays's house. That same night, two other Klan members burned a cross on the lawn of the Mobile County courthouse.

## The Case That Would Not Close

Police officers first floated the idea that Donald had been involved in a drug deal that went wrong. The FBI investigated and was ready to close the case, until Thomas Figures, an Assistant United States Attorney in Mobile, asked the Justice Department to authorize a second investigation. Figures worked closely with FBI agent James Bodman, and in 1983 Knowles confessed to Bodman. He pleaded guilty to federal civil rights violations, testified against Hays and other Klansmen, and was sentenced to life in prison. Henry Hays was convicted of capital murder. His jury voted for life imprisonment, but the judge overruled it and sentenced him to death. Benjamin Franklin Cox Jr., who had supplied the rope and a pistol, was convicted in 1989 as an accomplice and also received a life sentence. Bennie Jack Hays was indicted for inciting the murder, but his trial ended in a mistrial when he collapsed in court, and he died before he could be tried again.

## A Mother Against the Klan

Beulah Mae Donald went further than the criminal courts could. In 1984, with Morris Dees and the Southern Poverty Law Center, and with Michael Figures as her attorney, she filed a wrongful death suit against the United Klans of America itself. Judge Alex T. Howard Jr. presided over a trial that rested on the legal theory of agency, which held the Klan responsible for the acts of its members. In 1987 an all-white jury found the entire organization at fault and ordered it to pay $7 million in damages. To satisfy the judgment, the Klan turned over its two-story national headquarters building in Tuscaloosa, and the sale brought Michael Donald's mother a reported $51,875. The civil trial also brought out evidence that helped the criminal case against Cox, and payment of the judgment bankrupted the United Klans of America. Beulah Mae Donald died the following year, on September 17, 1988.

## What the Record Keeps

Henry Hays died in Yellow Mama, Alabama's electric chair, on June 6, 1997. Michael Donald's brother Stanley was among the witnesses. It was the state's first execution for a white-on-Black crime since 1913, and Hays is the only known Klan member executed in the twentieth century for the murder of an African American. He maintained his innocence in public to the end, but at his funeral the Reverend Bob Smith, president of the Mobile NAACP, revealed that Hays had tearfully confessed to him in private two days before the execution. The story has since been retold in Ravi Howard's novel Like Trees, Walking, Laurence Leamer's book The Lynching, Ted Koppel's documentary The Last Lynching and CNN's series The People v. The Klan. At its center remains a young man who walked to a gas station for his sister and never came home.
