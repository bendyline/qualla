# Bienville Square

Set aside by Congress in 1824 to be a park forever, downtown Mobile's central square has heard a president, hosted wartime shipyard rallies and weathered the loss of its old canopy.

**Location:** 30.6922, -88.0425

**Categories:** Parks in Mobile, Alabama, Urban parks, History of Mobile, Alabama, Public squares

## Introduction

The promise was written into federal law: forever. In 1824 the United States Congress passed an act transferring a large plot of land to the city of Mobile and specifying that the property be used forever as a city park. Two centuries on, Bienville Square still fills the entire block bordered by Dauphin, Saint Joseph, Saint Francis and North Conception streets, a green room at the center of downtown Mobile named for the city's founder, Jean-Baptiste Le Moyne, Sieur de Bienville.

## From Hospital Grounds to Shade Trees

Before it was a park, the block held the old Spanish Hospital, which stood on its southwestern corner at Dauphin and North Conception. The square took on its familiar shape in the 1850s, when walkways and benches were laid out, a cast-iron fence went up around it (long since removed), and live oaks were planted to give the city some shade. In the 1890s a large cast-iron fountain decorated with an acanthus-leaf motif was installed at the very center, where the paths meet. The bandstand came later. In 1941 the Sears and Roebuck Company donated a new one, complete with public restrooms, to replace the square's Victorian-era original.

## The City's Soapbox

A square in the middle of a port city draws people with something to say. In 1905 Theodore Roosevelt stood here and spoke about what the Panama Canal would mean for the port of Mobile. Four decades later the voices belonged to working people. During World War II, as the Alabama Drydock and Shipbuilding Company went through labor disputes, its shipyard workers gathered in Bienville Square for many mass meetings, turning the shady park into a place where wartime labor tensions played out in public.

## Two Brothers Remembered

Mobile was settled by two Le Moyne brothers, and the square honors both. A plaque near the northwest corner commemorates d'Iberville, the elder brother. Near the southern edge stands a large granite cross dedicated to Bienville, the younger of the two, who went on to govern Louisiana and whose name the square carries. Another historic marker at the northwest corner has nothing to do with French explorers: it commemorates the Salvation Army.

## Open Sky Where the Canopy Was

Like much of downtown, the square had its ups and downs. When the city center began to revive in the 1980s, Mobile renewed the park and its popularity grew. It became the stage for the city's cultural calendar: Jazz in Bienville, presented by the Gulf Coast Ethnic and Heritage Jazz Festival; the annual Lighting of the Trees, when Mobile's official Christmas tree is lit; and Kids Day in Bienville Square. It also served as the epicenter of Mobile's Bayfest Music Festival. Then, in September 2020, Hurricane Sally badly damaged many of the square's large trees, and the city lost 11 of them. The park's Ketchum Fountain was taken out for restoration in 2021, and the square reopened in April 2024 with the refurbished fountain back at its center. Where a green, shady canopy once closed overhead, there is now much more open sky, a reminder that even a park meant to last forever has to be tended through every storm.

## Flight Context

Bienville Square occupies one full block of downtown Mobile, Alabama, at approximately 30.692°N, 88.043°W, a few blocks west of the Mobile River waterfront. It is small, so look for a single green block amid the downtown high-rises; 1,500 to 2,500 feet gives the best chance of picking it out. Nearby airports: Mobile International Airport (KBFM, formerly Mobile Downtown), about 4 nm south, and Mobile Regional Airport (KMOB), about 10 nm west. Gulf Coast haze and summer afternoon thunderstorms can limit visibility.
