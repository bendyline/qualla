# Alabama Drydock and Shipbuilding Company

Mobile's great wartime shipyard built Liberty ships and tankers for the Allies, and in May 1943 became the scene of a riot when twelve Black workers were promoted to welder.

**Location:** 30.6754, -88.0319

**Categories:** Shipyards, Companies based in Mobile, Alabama, Defunct shipbuilding companies of the United States, World War II, African-American history, Civil rights history

## Introduction

Twelve promotions set it off. In the spring of 1943, following directives from President Roosevelt's Fair Employment Practices Committee, the Alabama Drydock and Shipbuilding Company moved twelve of its African American workers into welding jobs, positions that until then had been held only by white employees. On the evening of May 24, white workers at the yard turned on their Black coworkers. The riot exposed the fault line running through one of the largest marine production facilities in the United States, a Mobile yard that was building ships for a world war while its own workforce was divided against itself.

## Ships from the Keel Up

ADDSCO, as the company was known, was formed in December 1916 and began operating in 1917. By 1922 its yard had moved to Pinto Island, where the Mobile River meets Mobile Bay, and during World War II it expanded dramatically, building Liberty ships, tankers and other vessels from the keel up, often at great speed. Twenty Liberty ships came out of Mobile before wartime losses shifted the yard toward tanker work, and between 1943 and 1945 ADDSCO produced 102 T2 tankers. At its height the company reportedly employed some 30,000 people (the 1943 figure given below is lower), including many African Americans and women, which made it the largest employer in Mobile. A few miles upstream, Gulf Shipbuilding turned out destroyers and cargo ships. The defense buildup remade the city around them: Mobile became the second-largest city in Alabama, behind only Birmingham.

## Beds That Never Cooled

Thousands of people made their way to Mobile looking for work, and the city was not ready for them. Housing ran so short that a single bed might be rented to a night-shift worker by day and to a day-shift worker by night. By 1943 some 18,500 men worked at ADDSCO, about 6,000 of them African American. The company had begun hiring Black workers in 1941, but only for unskilled jobs, while white workers held the skilled trades such as welding. The NAACP and local Black leaders had pushed for years to open those trades. Even after the Fair Employment Practices Committee ordered African Americans elevated to skilled positions, ADDSCO was reluctant to act. When it finally promoted twelve Black workers to welder in the spring of 1943, the resentment of white coworkers boiled over.

## The Night of May 24

An estimated 4,000 white shipyard workers attacked Black workers at the yard, while others in the city threw bricks at Black housing. Governor Chauncey Sparks ordered every liquor and beer outlet in Mobile and Baldwin counties closed until order returned, and National Guard troops joined all available law enforcement officers to protect the workers. The mobs killed no one, but they injured more than 50 people. Even after the situation was declared under control, every African American worker at the plant was sent home, and the company ordered some of them to leave the job. The settlement, reached by federal, city, union and NAACP leaders, was a compromise shaped by segregation: four separate shipways where African Americans could fill every position except foreman. The men and women working those skilled jobs earned wages equal to white defense workers.

## A Victory at Home

Newspaper commentary at the time warned that the riot played directly into Hitler's hands, since racial hatred threatened the very industrial production the war depended on. Mobile was not alone. The Detroit race riot that followed in June 1943 killed 34 people. Across the country, life was starting to change for African Americans: membership in the NAACP increased nine-fold, and the Pittsburgh Courier campaigned for a double victory over the enemies of freedom at home as well as overseas. At ADDSCO, equality came slowly. Federal defense contractors were not supposed to practice segregation, yet legal segregation of facilities in Alabama did not end until the late 1960s. Management and trade unions addressed racial tensions repeatedly, but one account says a smaller race riot after the war forced the yard to close temporarily.

## New Names on the Gate

Shipbuilding declined across the United States in the later twentieth century, and ADDSCO's business slowed sharply after the mid-1970s. Its ship repair operation closed in 1988 and the company shut down for good in 1989. Atlantic Marine leased part of the facility in March 1989 and bought the site outright in December 1992; an investment company headed by former Navy Secretary John Lehman operated the yard from 2006 until 2010, when BAE Systems acquired it. BAE closed the yard in 2018. On June 18, 2019, Alabama Shipyard became its owner, and today the yard is a major shipyard in Mobile, offering vessel maintenance, repair, overhaul and conversion, marine construction, and ship dismantling and disposal. Ships still come to the island where twelve welders once changed the yard.

## Flight Context

The yard lies at about 30.675 N, 88.032 W on Pinto Island, directly across the Mobile River from downtown Mobile, Alabama. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet, look for dry docks, shipways and cranes along the island's riverfront, with the downtown towers just to the northwest and the broad head of Mobile Bay opening to the south. Mobile International Airport (KBFM, formerly Mobile Downtown) is only about 3.5 nm south-southwest, so expect busy airspace close to the field; Mobile Regional Airport (KMOB) is about 11 nm west. Gulf Coast haze and summer thunderstorms can reduce visibility over the bay.
