# Battle of Fort Charlotte

In the spring of 1780 a Spanish army besieged Mobile's crumbling British fort, and its two commanders traded courteous letters while the guns did their work.

**Location:** 30.6861, -88.0415

**Categories:** Battles in Alabama, History of Mobile, Alabama, West Florida, Sieges of the Anglo-Spanish War (1779–1783), Battles of the American Revolutionary War involving Spain

## Introduction

Even as his guns crept closer, Bernardo de Gálvez kept writing letters to the man he was besieging. The Spanish governor of Louisiana politely chided the British commander for burning houses in Mobile to deny the attackers cover. Captain Elias Durnford replied, just as politely, that the Spaniards would find a better vantage point on the far side of his fort, away from most of the town. Then the trenches deepened and the bombardment went on. In March 1780, after a siege of about two weeks, Fort Charlotte fell, and Britain lost its post nearest New Orleans, which one account calls the last British frontier post capable of threatening the city, leaving only Pensacola in British hands.

## A Governor Goes to War

Spain entered the American Revolutionary War in 1779, and Gálvez, Louisiana's energetic governor, wasted no time. In September 1779 he captured Fort Bute and then, after the Battle of Baton Rouge, secured the surrender of the remaining British forces on the river, giving Spain complete control of the lower Mississippi. That left two British strongholds in the province of West Florida: Mobile and its capital, Pensacola. Gálvez wanted both. His requests for extra troops from Havana had been turned down in 1779, so he gathered a mixed force of Spanish regulars and militia in New Orleans and sent one of his lieutenants to Havana to ask one last time.

## Storm and Sandbars

The expedition sailed in January 1780, twelve ships carrying 754 men, soon joined by the brig sloop Gálveztown under Captain William Pickles with a crew of 58. On February 6 a storm scattered the fleet, yet every ship had reached the waters outside Mobile Bay by February 9. Getting in proved harder than getting there. Several vessels ran aground on the sandbars, and at least one, the Volante, was wrecked. Gálvez made the best of the loss, salvaging the wreck's guns and mounting them on Mobile Point to guard the entrance to the bay. His luck turned on February 20, when reinforcements arrived from Havana and brought his army to about 1,200 men. By February 25 they were ashore on the Dog River, about ten miles from the fort, where a deserter told them the garrison numbered 300.

## An Old French Fort

What they faced was a fort with a long and tired history. The French had built it as Fort Condé when Mobile belonged to French Louisiana. By 1763, when the British took over after the French and Indian War, it lay in ruins. The British repaired it and renamed it Fort Charlotte, but by the time war with Spain approached in 1779 it had fallen into disrepair again. Its roughly 300 defenders were a patchwork: regulars mostly from the 60th Regiment, Loyalists from Maryland and Pennsylvania, and local volunteers. Ever since word of Gálvez's victories on the Mississippi reached Mobile, Durnford had been working to strengthen the defenses.

## Letters Under Fire

On March 1, Gálvez sent Lieutenant Colonel Francisco Bouligny to Durnford with an offer to accept his surrender. Durnford politely declined. The next day the Spanish began setting up gun batteries around the fort, and Durnford wrote to General John Campbell at Pensacola for help. On March 5 and 6, most of the Pensacola garrison set out on the march toward Mobile, but difficult river crossings slowed them, and they never arrived in time to make a difference. Through it all, the courteous correspondence between the two commanders continued alongside the digging and the shelling. Around March 13 (accounts say March 12 or 13), Spanish guns breached the walls of Fort Charlotte. Durnford, having waited in vain for relief, surrendered his garrison the following day.

## The Long Road to Pensacola

The victory secured the western shore of Mobile Bay and confined British military power in West Florida to Pensacola, but Gálvez did not rush on. Pensacola was strongly defended and armed with powerful cannon, so he again asked Havana for large-scale naval support. In April he learned that British reinforcements, including Royal Navy ships, had reached Pensacola. He left a garrison in Mobile and sailed to Havana to raise the troops and equipment he needed. That garrison held when the British counterattacked in January 1781, and only then did Gálvez launch his successful assault on Pensacola. Fort Charlotte itself was demolished by the early 1820s. Its foundations were rediscovered in the late 1960s, when archaeologists dug ahead of the George Wallace Tunnel, and a reduced-size replica, dedicated in 1976 as Fort Condé, now stands on part of the site. The History Museum of Mobile operates it, and the site has been on the National Register of Historic Places since 1969.

## Flight Context

The site of Fort Charlotte lies in downtown Mobile, Alabama, at approximately 30.686°N, 88.042°W, near the west bank of the Mobile River where it meets the head of Mobile Bay. The replica fort is small, so look instead for the downtown high-rises and the riverfront; 2,000 to 3,000 feet gives a good view of the city's layout and the bay opening to the south. Nearby airports: Mobile International Airport (KBFM, formerly Mobile Downtown), about 4 nm south-southwest, and Mobile Regional Airport (KMOB), about 10 nm west. Watch for Gulf Coast haze and summer afternoon thunderstorms.
