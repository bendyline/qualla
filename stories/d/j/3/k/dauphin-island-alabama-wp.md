# Dauphin Island, Alabama

A thin barrier island at the mouth of Mobile Bay, Dauphin Island was misnamed for a massacre that never happened, anchored French Louisiana, watched Farragut run the torpedoes, and still offers migrating birds their first landfall.

**Location:** 30.2542, -88.1125

**Categories:** Barrier islands of Alabama, Towns in Mobile County, Alabama, Beaches of Alabama, French-American culture in Alabama, Bird sanctuaries, Populated places established in 1702

## Introduction

When Pierre Le Moyne d'Iberville anchored off this island on January 31, 1699, a large pile of human skeletons was discovered there. The French explorer named the place Île du Massacre, Massacre Island, and the grim name stuck. But there is no evidence of a massacre. By one account, the bones came from a Mississippian burial mound that a hurricane had broken open; another puts the number at some 60 skeletons gathered in mounds. It was a fitting first misunderstanding for Dauphin Island, a 15-mile ribbon of sand at the mouth of Mobile Bay where storms have been rearranging the evidence for as long as anyone has been keeping records.

## Shell Middens and a French Harbor

People were here long before d'Iberville. Serpentine shell middens, perhaps 1,500 years old, show at least seasonal occupation by the Mississippian mound-building culture, and Shell Mound Park lies along the island's north shore. In 1519 the Spanish explorer Alonso Álvarez de Pineda became the first documented European visitor, mapping the island with remarkable accuracy. The French valued it for its timber, fresh water and deep-water harbor, and d'Iberville made it the port for Fort Louis de La Louisiane, with a fort, a chapel, government warehouses and homes. Enslaved Africans were first brought to Alabama through this island under French rule; local histories date the first arrival to 1719. The island changed hands with the empires around it, passing to Britain in 1763 after the French and Indian War and then to Spain as part of West Florida until the United States took it in 1813. The town's coat of arms still carries a fleur-de-lis for France and a Spanish galleon for Spain.

## Torpedoes Off the Point

Fort Gaines, the walled fort on the island's eastern tip, was begun in 1821 and took decades to finish. Confederate forces occupied it in 1861, and Union troops captured it during the Battle of Mobile Bay. Just a few hundred yards off the island's shore, Admiral David Farragut supposedly uttered the words "Damn the torpedoes, full speed ahead." Offshore, the story of the Sand Island Light followed the war's arc. The first light was authorized in the 1830s, and its 150-foot replacement, built for $35,000, was destroyed in 1863 by Confederate forces. The present lighthouse dates from 1873 and was used until 1971. It is listed on the National Register of Historic Places, and its ownership has passed from the Department of the Interior to the town of Dauphin Island.

## First Landfall for Tired Wings

For most of its length the island is less than a quarter-mile wide, though its eastern quarter swells to more than a mile. That wider eastern end is shaded by thick pines and saw palmettos, while the narrow west carries scrub and few trees. For many birds migrating north from South America, Dauphin Island is the first land they meet, and many species rest here before flying on; the main refuge is the Audubon Bird Sanctuary, a tract of well over a hundred acres of forest, marsh and beach (counts run from about 137 to 164 acres). The Dauphin Island Sea Lab, on grounds once occupied by an Air Force radar squadron, studies the surrounding waters, and the Estuarium, near the ferry dock, displays fresh- and saltwater species native to Alabama. The island's 1,778 residents, counted in 2020, share it with fishermen, beachgoers and the occasional surprise: in 2023, a large alligator was photographed riding the waves and sunbathing on the beach. There is even a large public pier that sits on dry land.

## Living in the Storm Track

Hurricanes are part of the island's calendar. Camille flooded 70 percent of it in 1969, and Frederic in 1979 destroyed the bridge to the mainland that had opened in 1955; today the Gordon Persons Bridge, along with a ferry from Fort Morgan, links it to the rest of Alabama. Georges destroyed 41 houses in 1998, and Ivan in 2004 left nearly a quarter of the island under about two feet of water. Katrina in 2005 destroyed homes on the west end and carved the Katrina Cut, a channel that split off the island's western part, while an offshore sandbar broke the force of the waves before they reached the Gulf-front homes of DeSoto Landing. Katrina also pushed an oil drilling platform, the Ocean Warwick, nearly 60 miles before it grounded near the island. In 2020, storm surge from Hurricane Sally, passing offshore, flooded the island yet again.

## Flight Context

Dauphin Island lies at 30.254 N, 88.113 W, a long, narrow barrier island running east-west between the Gulf of Mexico and Mississippi Sound, with its wide, wooded east end marking the western side of the mouth of Mobile Bay. From 1,500-3,000 feet the Gordon Persons Bridge to the mainland, the walled Fort Gaines on the eastern point, Fort Morgan across the bay entrance and the Sand Island Light offshore make unmistakable landmarks, and the island's thin western tail stretches toward Mississippi. Dauphin Island Airport (Jeremiah Denton Airport, FAA 4R9) sits on the island itself; Mobile International (KBFM, formerly Mobile Downtown) is about 23 nm north, Gulf Shores International (Jack Edwards Field, KJKA) about 23 nm east, and Mobile Regional (KMOB) about 27 nm north-northwest. Expect sea breezes, summer thunderstorms and the June-November hurricane season.
