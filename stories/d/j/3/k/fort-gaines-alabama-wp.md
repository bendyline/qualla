# Fort Gaines (Alabama)

Guarding the entrance to Mobile Bay from the east end of Dauphin Island, Fort Gaines fell during the 1864 Battle of Mobile Bay and survives today as one of the nation's best-preserved Civil War masonry forts, even as the Gulf erodes the beach at its walls.

## Introduction

On August 8, 1864, the Confederate colonel commanding Fort Gaines did the one thing his superior had forbidden. Union soldiers had brought their artillery right up to his walls behind the sand dunes of Dauphin Island, and the fort's weaknesses were plain. So Colonel Anderson opened talks under a flag of truce and surrendered. Three days earlier, Admiral David Farragut's fleet had forced its way into Mobile Bay past the forts guarding its entrance. Today Fort Gaines is still standing, one of the best-preserved Civil War era masonry forts in the nation.

## Guarding the Bay

Established in 1821 and named for Edmund Pendleton Gaines, the fort sits on the east end of Dauphin Island, Alabama, facing Fort Morgan across the main channel into Mobile Bay. Together with a third work, Fort Powell, they were meant to shut the bay to enemy ships. By the summer of 1864, Fort Gaines mounted 26 guns and held a garrison of roughly 800 men (estimates run from about 600 to 860). On August 5, a Union fleet under Rear Admiral Farragut, supported by a contingent of soldiers, attacked the forts and the smaller Confederate fleet under Admiral Franklin Buchanan. It was during this battle that Farragut, by long-told tradition, gave his most famous order. The exact words are uncertain and possibly apocryphal, but they have come down in paraphrase as "Damn the torpedoes, full speed ahead!"

## Siege in the Dunes

Fort Gaines did not face the fleet alone. On August 3, some 1,500 Union troops had landed on Dauphin Island, miles west of the fort, and marched toward it. After Farragut's ships entered the bay, the monitor Chickasaw was sent to support the soldiers ashore, and the bombardment revealed how vulnerable the fort was to fire from the rear. Union troops, heavily outnumbering the garrison and shielded by the dunes, brought their artillery to close range. Colonel Anderson recognized that his position was hopeless. Ignoring orders from his superior, he opened communication with the Union commanders and surrendered on August 8, a decision that cost him his command but ended the fighting at Fort Gaines.

## An Anchor and a Blacksmith's Forge

Visitors today find the fort remarkably complete. The most striking exhibit is the huge anchor from USS Hartford, the flagship from which Farragut gave his celebrated command. Original cannons used in the battle are on display, and five pre-Civil War brick buildings still stand in the interior courtyard, along with an operational blacksmith shop and kitchens. Tunnels lead out to the fortified corner bastions. A museum traces the fort's history and reaches further back to the French colonial presence that began in the late seventeenth century. The fort was also partially modernized for the Spanish–American War, adding another layer to its story. Tours and historical reenactments bring the place to life, and the fort has been on the National Register of Historic Places since 1976.

## Under Siege Again

The fort's greatest enemy now is the Gulf of Mexico. Hurricanes and tropical storms have inflicted significant damage on its masonry over the years, and although that damage has largely been repaired, the fort stands only feet from the water, where erosion has stripped away dunes and beach at up to about ten feet a year. On March 18, 2009, the Civil War Preservation Trust placed Fort Gaines on its History Under Siege list of the ten most endangered Civil War battlefields in the United States. In 2011 the National Trust for Historic Preservation added it to its list of America's 11 Most Endangered Historic Places. A fort that surrendered to an army in 1864 is now holding out against the sea.

## Flight Context

Fort Gaines sits at 30.2483°N, 88.0769°W on the east end of Dauphin Island, Alabama, at the western side of the entrance to Mobile Bay. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL, look for the masonry fort at the island's eastern tip, with Fort Morgan about 3 nm to the east-southeast across the channel. Mobile International Airport (KBFM, formerly Mobile Downtown) lies about 23 nm north, and Gulf Shores International Airport (Jack Edwards Field, KJKA) about 21 nm east. Naval outlying fields dot the mainland to the northeast, so watch for military training traffic. Expect over-water flying and changeable Gulf weather.
