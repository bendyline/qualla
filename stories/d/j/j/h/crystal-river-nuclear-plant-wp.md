# Crystal River Nuclear Plant

A 2009 upgrade meant to make Florida's Crystal River 3 reactor more powerful instead opened a gap inside its concrete containment wall, and the plant never ran again.

**Location:** 28.9575, -82.6983

**Categories:** Former nuclear power stations in the United States, Energy infrastructure, Crystal River, Florida, Buildings and structures in Citrus County, Florida, Engineering failures

## Introduction

The plan was to make Crystal River 3 better. In September 2009 the reactor on Florida's Gulf coast went offline for refueling, new steam generators and an upgrade that would raise its power by 20 percent. The old generators could only come out through the side of the containment building, so crews began loosening the steel tendons inside its concrete walls and cutting an opening. As the concrete came away, workers found a large gap inside the wall itself. The repairs that followed produced new cracks, the new cracks produced new estimates, and in February 2013 Duke Energy announced that the plant would be permanently shut down.

## A Reactor Among the Stacks

CR-3, as it is known, was the third plant built on the 4,700-acre Crystal River Energy Complex, the only nuclear unit on a site it shared with four fossil-fuel units. It was an 860-megawatt pressurized water reactor, completed and licensed to operate in December 1976 and commissioned the following March. It ran for 33 years, until the 2009 outage.

Its ownership changed hands along the way. Florida Progress Corporation originally owned the plant and operated it through its subsidiary, Florida Power Corporation. In 2000 Carolina Power & Light bought Florida Progress, forming Progress Energy, which owned 91.8 percent of the reactor; nine municipal utilities held the rest. On July 2, 2012, Duke Energy bought Progress Energy, which meant the final decision about the damaged plant fell to Duke.

## Anatomy of a Shell

A reactor like this one is guarded by three main barriers between its radiation and the public, and one of them is the containment building that houses the fuel, the reactor and its cooling system. At Crystal River that building was a steel-lined cylinder of prestressed concrete about 157 feet tall and about 138 feet across, with walls 42 inches thick, a flat foundation mat and a shallow dome.

The concrete's strength came partly from tension. Two arrays of steel tendons ran through the walls, horizontal ones on the outside and vertical ones just inside them, embedded about 15 inches from the outer surface. Pulled tight, they squeezed the concrete together. There were 426 tendons in all. To cut a hole big enough to swap steam generators, some of them first had to be slackened, and how many, and in what order, turned out to matter.

## Counting Tendons

The engineering firm Sargent & Lundy, which developed the plan, specified loosening 97 tendons. Progress rejected that as excessive. A second proposal called for 74, a number typical of other nuclear plants doing the same job. According to a Progress employee, "de-tensioning the tendons is a very expensive and time-consuming effort," and the figure was cut again, to 65. Bechtel, brought in for an outside review, agreed that 65 was appropriate.

When the work was actually done, only 27 tendons were loosened, and a foreman and a supervisor sent emails questioning how it was being carried out. Later engineering analysis found that the gap in the wall was a large delamination, a separation within the concrete itself, and attributed its main cause to the scope and sequence of the detensioning.

## Cracks That Kept Coming

Crews repaired the first crack, and more appeared. They fixed the delaminated section and re-tensioned the concrete, and then the same problem turned up in adjacent bays. The plant had been due back in service in April 2011. In June 2011 Progress Energy said it did not expect a restart until 2014.

The numbers climbed with the delays. Preliminary repair estimates ran from $900 million to $1.3 billion. In October 2012 an independent review put the cost at $1.5 billion, with a worst case of $3.4 billion. In February 2013, after several months of weighing the options, Duke's senior executives announced that Crystal River 3 would never restart, and that the company would recover $850 million in insurance claims. The fossil-fuel units on the site were not affected by the containment problem.

## A Goodbye That Sped Up

Shutting the plant down was only the beginning. In 2013 the decommissioning was expected to take 60 years, at an estimated cost of $1.18 billion. That plan did not hold. In 2019 the owner switched from long-term storage to prompt dismantling, and in October 2020 the license passed to the decommissioning contractor Advanced Decommissioning Partners, a joint venture of NorthStar and Orano. By 2026 the reactor building was the last major structure left, with its demolition scheduled to finish by the end of August; Duke Energy still owns the site.

The plant's surroundings had changed a great deal during its working life. In 2010, 20,695 people lived within ten miles, an increase of 50.9 percent in a decade, and more than a million lived within fifty. Florida is rarely shaken, but on September 10, 2006, a magnitude 5.9 earthquake (first reported as 5.8) struck about 300 miles to the southwest, and the plant came through undamaged. An NRC study published in 2010 estimated the yearly risk of an earthquake strong enough to damage the reactor core at 1 in 45,455. In the end, the threat to Crystal River 3 came not from the ground but from within its own walls.

## Flight Context

Crystal River 3 sits at 28.9575 N, 82.6983 W on the Gulf of Mexico shore of Citrus County, Florida, part of the large Crystal River Energy Complex several miles northwest of the town of Crystal River. From 3,000-4,000 ft the industrial complex stands out sharply against the low coastal marsh and islands, with open Gulf water to the west. Respect any airspace restrictions over power-generating facilities. Nearby airports: Crystal River Airport (KCGC), about 9 nm southeast; George T. Lewis Airport at Cedar Key (KCDK), about 21 nm west-northwest; Inverness Airport (KINF), about 22 nm east-southeast; Ocala International-Jim Taylor Field (KOCF), about 28 nm east-northeast. Expect sea-breeze thunderstorms on summer afternoons and coastal haze.
