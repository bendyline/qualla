# Extraterrorestrial Alien Encounter

Co-created with Lucasfilm, the Magic Kingdom's darkest attraction strapped guests into harnesses, killed the lights and let an escaped alien breathe down their necks, until too many frightened families sent it packing in 2003.

**Location:** 28.4186, -81.5797

**Categories:** Former Walt Disney Parks and Resorts attractions, Tomorrowland (Disney Parks), Magic Kingdom, Audio-Animatronic attractions, Outer space in amusement parks

## Introduction

The lights went out, and the harness across your shoulders held you in place. Somewhere in the blackness a creature shrieked, a maintenance worker was attacked, and a spray of fluid hit your face. Then came warm breath on the back of your neck. For eight years in the Magic Kingdom's Tomorrowland, Extraterrorestrial Alien Encounter set out, quite deliberately, to terrify its audience, and it did most of that work in total darkness.

## Born from a Xenomorph

The idea began with the 1979 film Alien. An early concept called Nostromo, after the film's spacecraft, would have featured the Xenomorph itself, but the Alien series was R-rated, clashing with a general rule of thumb that Disney attractions should draw on G or PG properties. The name was dropped, an original alien was created, and the fictional company became X-S Tech. George Lucas joined the project as the story developed; the attraction was a co-production between Walt Disney Imagineering and Lucasfilm, then separate from Disney. One early version, in which the alien turned out to be sentient and freed the captive guests, was reworked for its dark tone. The show was first proposed for Disneyland's "Tomorrowland 2055" makeover during CEO Michael Eisner's "Disney Decade," but financial issues after the opening of Euro Disney, now Disneyland Paris, sank that plan. In late 1993 Disney announced the attraction for the Magic Kingdom instead.

## Not Scary Enough

Alien Encounter opened for previews on December 16, 1994, in the building that had housed Mission to Mars. Guests criticized its violence, its storyline and the jokey pre-show that clashed with the grim main event. On January 12, 1995, the attraction closed for retooling, and here the story takes a surprising turn: Eisner, who ordered the retooling, felt it was not intense enough. Disney spent an extra $10 million, reworked the second pre-show to match the darker tone, adjusted the show so guests understood what was happening, and reprogrammed every computer system. The original pre-show robot, TOM 2000, voiced by Phil Hartman, gave way to a new one. The attraction officially opened on June 20, 1995, as part of the Magic Kingdom's New Tomorrowland.

## An Open House at X-S Tech

Guests entered the "Tomorrowland Interplanetary Convention Center," where monitors advertised a "Championship Pet Show" and a talk called "Mission to Mars: History or Hoax," a wink at the building's previous tenant. X-S Tech's chairman, L.C. Clench, played by Jeffrey Jones, summed up his philosophy: "If something can't be done with X-S then it shouldn't be done at all." Next, a robot called SIR, voiced by Tim Curry, demonstrated teleportation on a small alien named Skippy, who arrived charred and dazed, a clear hint that the technology was flawed. In the main chamber, guests sat in harnesses around a huge plastic teleportation tube while technicians Spinlok, played by Kevin Pollak, and Dr. Femus, played by Kathy Najimy, appeared on video. Clench impatiently decided to beam himself in, the signal was diverted through an unknown planet, and a towering, winged, carnivorous alien materialized in the tube instead. It broke free, and the power failed.

## Engineering Fear

Most of the effects lived in the shoulder restraints behind each guest's head. Speakers set beside each ear delivered binaural sound, placing the monster precisely in space and layering in murmurs, screams from other audience members, pink noise and heartbeats. Moving restraints and sound made the alien seem to travel overhead, while banks of 1,800-watt-per-channel subwoofers, repurposed from Mission to Mars, joined seat transducers to pound out its footsteps. Warm, moist air played the creature's breath, sometimes gently and sometimes in a sudden blast. Sprinklers in the row ahead simulated drool or the blood of the attacked worker, a cast member with a flashlight performing to recorded dialogue in the scaffolding above. Soft fabric tubes slapped against the backs of heads while hot air and scent emitters fired, suggesting the alien was licking its captives. At last the creature was forced back into the tube and exploded.

## Exit, Stage Stitch

Released from their harnesses while the two technicians resumed the search for the misplaced Clench, shaken guests filed out into the Merchant of Venus shop. Reactions split. Some Disney fans admired the attraction's darker edge, a sharp contrast to the rest of the Magic Kingdom, but its reception from guests was largely negative, since it was considered too scary for younger children, and its popularity dwindled. Plans to bring it to Disneyland, Tokyo Disneyland and Disneyland Paris were cancelled amid poor reviews and budget cuts. On September 21, 2003, Disney announced that an attraction based on its 2002 film Lilo & Stitch would take over the building, and on October 11 (some sources say October 12) Alien Encounter closed so work could begin on Stitch's Great Escape! Its successor would keep much more of its show in the light.

## Flight Context

The former attraction building stands in Tomorrowland at the Magic Kingdom, near 28.4186 N, 81.5797 W, about 20 miles southwest of Orlando; Bay Lake lies immediately east of the park. Walt Disney World lies under a permanent FAA flight restriction (a 3-nautical-mile radius from the surface up to 3,000 feet AGL, centered very close to this site, with limited exceptions), so view the park only from outside the restricted area and check current NOTAMs. Kissimmee Gateway Airport (KISM) is about 11 nm to the southeast, Orlando International (KMCO) about 14 nm to the east, and Orlando Executive (KORL) about 15 nm to the east-northeast. Summer afternoon thunderstorms are frequent over central Florida.
