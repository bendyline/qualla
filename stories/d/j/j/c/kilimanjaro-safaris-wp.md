# Kilimanjaro Safaris

At Disney's Animal Kingdom the ruts are poured concrete and the mud is painted cement, but the animals on this open-sided East African safari are real, fulfilling a wish Walt Disney never managed at Disneyland.

**Location:** 28.3608, -81.5936

**Categories:** Amusement rides introduced in 1998, Disney's Animal Kingdom, Walt Disney Parks and Resorts attractions, Africa (Disney's Animal Kingdom), Audio-Animatronic attractions, 1998 establishments in Florida

## Introduction

The tire ruts are poured into the road. The mud caked on the trucks is spray-painted cement. The animals, though, are real, and that is the whole point of Kilimanjaro Safaris, the open-sided ride at Disney's Animal Kingdom that carries visitors on a simulated photo safari through the savanna of East Africa. Long before Walt Disney World existed, Walt Disney had wanted living African animals for the Jungle Cruise at Disneyland. For several reasons he settled for Audio-Animatronic replicas. In Florida, his company finally let the elephants and hippos stand in for themselves.

## Welcome to Harambe

The story places riders in the Harambe Wildlife Reserve in the story's town of Harambe, East Africa. Guests climb aboard a safari vehicle, and a game driver narrates as the truck lurches across open country, pointing out animals and keeping up a running patter. The illusion depends on what you cannot see. Animals appear close enough to walk right up to the vehicle, yet cleverly disguised fences and boundaries keep them apart from people and from each other. Between each ecosystem, chain road sensors and bars stop animals from wandering into the next section, and the trucks simply drive over them. Look carefully at the flamingo island along the route and you may notice that its outline forms a huge Hidden Mickey, one of several tucked along the ride path.

## The Elephant They Could Not Save

The original ride had a plot, and a dark one. The truck, callsign "Simba-1," would be radioed by a warden and a scientist and sent after poachers who had captured a mother elephant named Big Red and her calf, Little Red. At first the chase ended with a cast member, playing a warden, capturing the poachers at gunpoint. Later versions had the truck simply scare them off. During cast previews before the park opened there was a bleaker version still, a "Dark Ending" in which the safari came upon the body of Big Red. It was only a statue, but families found the scene too shocking, complaints poured in, and Disney removed it. Between 2007 and 2009 the script moved away from the Little Red storyline toward the animals themselves, and in 2012 the Little Red scene was replaced entirely.

## Concrete Mud and Propane Trucks

Almost nothing about the road is what it seems. The roadbed is dark brown concrete with tire ruts built in permanently, so every truck bounces through the same washed-out tracks. The vehicles themselves are custom-built GMC Topkicks and Ford Super Duty trucks dating from 1990 to 2002, running on liquid propane, and between 2007 and 2009 they were stretched with an extra row of seats to carry more people. Keeping a working savanna in Florida has its own demands. In 2004 much of the drainage system had to be replaced, and the ride stayed open throughout, with green tarps screening the construction sites to keep the animals out. Some experiments failed. Zebras introduced in the new exhibit in 2012, reported as Hartmann's mountain zebras, were removed four months after their arrival over "acclimation" issues, and addax antelope took their place.

## The Savanna After Sundown

The safaris normally ran until sundown, but in the 1998 holiday season Disney tried something new. Kilimanjaro Night Safaris had its own script, extra animal sounds, reflectors hidden in the foliage to mimic glowing eyes, and an African dance troupe performing around a bonfire in the area where the elephants usually stood. Many animals were asleep or hard to see, guests complained, and the added cost sank the idea after one season. In 2016, as part of the park's expansion, night safaris returned, this time with a simulated sunset, animal sounds, and hyenas and painted dogs added to the reserve. The nighttime route is shorter, skipping areas where nothing would be visible, and Disney offers it only when park hours allow.

## Growing Pains on the Reserve

The reserve's beginnings were not easy. Early on, a number of animals died from disease, toxic exposure, maternal killings and collisions with park vehicles. A United States Department of Agriculture investigation into the deaths (29 between September 1997 and April 1998 by one count; other reports give different totals) is reported to have found no violations of the Animal Welfare Act. In June 1998 a hippopotamus died, reportedly of pneumonia, closing the ride for 40 minutes. A decade later, in February 2008, a small fire broke out at the front of a ride vehicle; a woman who jumped from the truck was taken to hospital, and three other people suffered minor injuries.

## Flight Context

Kilimanjaro Safaris lies at 28.3608 N, 81.5936 W in the Africa section of Disney's Animal Kingdom, south of the Magic Kingdom. From the air the reserve shows as open grassland crossed by the ride's brown concrete road, with the Tree of Life, at the park's center, a few hundred meters to the southeast. Walt Disney World sits beneath a permanent flight restriction (a 3-nautical-mile radius from the surface to 3,000 feet above ground level, centered near the Magic Kingdom about 3.3 nm north-northeast of this site, so Animal Kingdom lies near its southern edge); keep well clear of the circle and check current NOTAMs. Kissimmee Gateway Airport (KISM) is about 9 nm to the east-southeast, Orlando International (KMCO) about 16 nm to the east-northeast, and Orlando Executive (KORL) about 18 nm to the northeast. Best seen in clear daytime conditions.
