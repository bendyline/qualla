# 20,000 Leagues Under the Sea: Submarine Voyage

For 23 years, a fleet of forty-ton Nautilus replicas carried Magic Kingdom guests past sea serpents, sunken ships and a giant squid, before the lagoon was drained and most of the submarines were scrapped and buried.

**Location:** 28.4208, -81.5800

**Categories:** Former Walt Disney Parks and Resorts attractions, Fantasyland, Magic Kingdom, Audio-Animatronic attractions, Amusement rides based on works by Jules Verne

## Introduction

Every passenger had a porthole of their own, and tucked into its recess was a small button meant to clear the glass if it fogged. It rarely worked. Above it, a tiny speaker carried the voice of Captain Nemo, and beyond the glass a curtain of bubbles rose as the Nautilus appeared to dive. From 1971 to 1994, 20,000 Leagues Under the Sea: Submarine Voyage took Magic Kingdom guests on a 20-minute journey through a Fantasyland lagoon, and it did so with a showman's cheerful illusion: the submarines never actually submerged.

## Commissioned in California

The ride's roots lay at Disneyland, where an ambitious 1959 expansion of Tomorrowland added the Matterhorn Bobsleds, the Monorail and the Submarine Voyage. That attraction was "commissioned" on June 6, 1959, in front of Richard Nixon, Walt and Lillian Disney, and officers of the U.S. Navy. Eight grey submarines glided through a lagoon and into a show building hidden behind two waterfalls, where early animatronics and forced perspective made the underwater world feel real. It was so popular that Walt Disney Imagineering planned a more elaborate version for the project in Florida that became Walt Disney World. The Florida ride was a re-theming, dressed in the characters and settings of Disney's 1954 film 20,000 Leagues Under the Sea, itself adapted from Jules Verne's 1870 novel.

## Forty Tons of Victorian Fantasy

The fleet was the signature. Imagineer George McGinnis adapted Harper Goff's Nautilus design from the 1954 film for theme park use, and veteran Imagineer Bob Gurr oversaw the project. Morgan Yachts in Clearwater, Florida, built the basic hulls before the final work moved to Tampa Ship partway through. Delivered to Walt Disney World in August 1971, each vehicle weighed some forty tons and rode on a concrete guide track fitted with a mechanism to limit bumping. They were really boats, with guests seated below the waterline. Inside were metal panels, rivets and bolts, Victorian-style flip-out seats and armrests beneath the portholes. Twelve vehicles each carried forty riders, usually cycling through in packs of three. Up in the "sail," a helmsman stood and ran the vessel, while Captain Nemo's narration came from voice actor Peter Renaday, doing an impression of James Mason.

## Beneath the Lagoon

Guests ducked through a low rear hatch and found their seats as an eerie organ version of the film's theme looped endlessly. Bubble machines and the waterfalls produced the dive. Nemo then introduced the underwater plains around Vulcania, where moray eels, crabs, lobsters, sea bass, clams, turtles and small tropical fish appeared. An underwater party of animatronic divers, wearing replicas of Goff's diving gear, worked kelp beds and wrestled with rebellious turtles. When bubbles from the waterfall simulated a storm on the surface, Nemo took the submarine deeper, into the show building and the Graveyard of Lost Ships, where wrecks from various centuries lay under patrolling sharks. The voyage passed beneath the polar ice cap, among icebergs stabbing down through the water, then into the Abyss and its strange deepwater fish. Near the end came the ruins of Atlantis, a two-armed sea serpent, mermaids and a treasury of jewels and gold. Last of all, in tribute to the film's most famous scene, a giant squid gripped a smaller sister Nautilus marked XIII before its tentacles lashed at the guests' own vessel.

## Temporary Maintenance

Guests loved it to the end, but the ride was expensive to maintain and moved few people per hour for an attraction of its size. On September 5, 1994, it closed without advance notice for what was described as a temporary maintenance period. A reopening was planned for the summer of 1996; in April of that year the closure was made permanent. The fleet was pulled out in 1996 and shuffled around backstage for years until, around 2004, most of the submarines were stripped and, by most accounts, scrapped and buried in a landfill. Two escaped that fate and were sunk in the snorkeling lagoon at Castaway Cay, Disney's private island, draped in cargo netting to help sea life cling to them; hurricane weather later destroyed one. A third shell, once used in the backlot tour's special-effects tank at what is now Disney's Hollywood Studios, survives in storage and still appears at Disney events. The lagoon lingered as "Ariel's Grotto" until it was drained in 2004. Pooh's Playful Spot followed, then the Seven Dwarfs Mine Train. A Nautilus silhouette is said to hide in the rockwork outside Under the Sea: Journey of The Little Mermaid.

## Flight Context

The former lagoon lay in Fantasyland at the Magic Kingdom, near 28.4208 N, 81.5800 W, where the Seven Dwarfs Mine Train now stands. Walt Disney World, about 20 miles southwest of Orlando, lies under a permanent FAA flight restriction (a 3-nautical-mile radius from the surface up to 3,000 feet AGL, with limited exceptions), so plan to view the area from outside the restriction; Bay Lake, a natural lake about a mile across lying immediately east of the Magic Kingdom, and the man-made Seven Seas Lagoon joined to it are the unmistakable landmarks. Kissimmee Gateway Airport (KISM) lies about 11 nm to the southeast, Orlando International (KMCO) about 14 nm to the east, and Orlando Executive (KORL) about 15 nm to the east-northeast. Expect heavy Orlando traffic and frequent afternoon thunderstorms in summer.
