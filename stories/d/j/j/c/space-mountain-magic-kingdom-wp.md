# Space Mountain (Magic Kingdom)

Walt Disney's idea for a space-age roller coaster was impossible to build at Disneyland in the 1960s, so it rose instead in Florida in 1975 as a cone 300 feet across that became the model for Space Mountains around the world.

**Location:** 28.4192, -81.5772

**Categories:** Roller coasters at the Magic Kingdom, Space Mountain, Tomorrowland (Disney Parks), Walt Disney Parks and Resorts attractions, 1975 establishments in Florida

## Introduction

Blue lights pulse down a long tunnel, flashing faster and faster as a warping hum builds, as if the train were charging up for launch. Then the strobes cut out and a field of stars appears. Riders have been making that same run into the dark at Walt Disney World's Magic Kingdom since January 15, 1975, when Space Mountain opened in Tomorrowland. It was the original version of an attraction later reproduced at every Disney resort except Shanghai, one of the first roller coasters ever operated by computer, and today described as the oldest operating roller coaster in Florida.

## Too Big for Disneyland

The idea descended from the Matterhorn Bobsleds, which opened at Disneyland in 1959 and convinced Walt Disney that thrill rides belonged in his park. In 1964 he brought designer John Hench a concept for a space-themed coaster to anchor a renovated Tomorrowland planned for 1967. Working with Arrow Development, the company behind the Matterhorn's coaster systems, the designers first imagined four separate tracks. The technology of the time and the limited space in Disneyland made that impossible. By 1969, brochures and concept models were already promoting Space Mountain as a headline attraction for the new Walt Disney World in Florida. Money came through a clever contract. RCA was supplying the resort's communications hardware, and its agreement stated that if Disney presented an attraction of interest, RCA would provide $10 million to support it. Disney president Card Walker, later its chief executive, persuaded RCA chairman Robert Sarnoff that this was the one.

## A Mountain Beyond the Berm

Planners considered placing the mountain in the southern part of Tomorrowland, where Disneyland would later build its own version. Instead, Florida's Space Mountain went outside the park's perimeter berm, roughly due east of Cinderella Castle, and guests reach it through a "star corridor" tunnel beneath the Walt Disney World Railroad. The hollow cone measures 300 feet across. Inside are two tracks, Alpha on the left and Omega on the right, each about 65 feet high and each a mirror image of the other, while the PeopleMover glides through the building between them. The original dedication plaque, set below the entrance pylon in 1975 and presented by "missile, space and range pioneers," honored the men and women whose "skills, sacrifice, courage and teamwork" opened the door to space exploration.

## Launch Bay to Wormhole

After the strobe tunnel, trains swing around and climb a lift hill designed as a launch bay, where a long, narrow spaceship modeled on the Discovery One from 2001: A Space Odyssey hangs overhead. Full-size astronaut figures dangle upside down beneath its engines to suggest zero gravity, and projections of the planets, comets, asteroids and the Milky Way appear beyond the bay's open ceiling. Then the train crests, dips and plunges into near-total darkness, twisting around the mountain and taking a steepest drop of 39 degrees before rushing through a swirling red and orange wormhole to the brakes. Each train is two rocket-shaped cars with three single-rider rows, six passengers in all. Until 1989 the cars had two rows in which one rider sat in front of the other, carrying eight.

## Sponsors in Orbit

Corporate sponsors shaped much of what riders saw off the coaster. RCA's post-show, the Home of Future Living, imagined how consumer electronics would transform a typical home, and in the late 1970s the company briefly swapped the attraction's theme song for a jingle promoting its Colortrak televisions. A model RCA satellite gave way to a flying saucer carrying the company's dog mascot, Nipper. FedEx took over in 1994, and waiting riders watched SMTV, a mock broadcast that included commercials for Crazy Larry's Used Spaceships, with Charles Fleischer, the voice of Roger Rabbit, as the salesman. The post-show's space colony was redressed so that its astronauts teleported packages across space to a robot dog called the "lab retriever." When FedEx left in 2004, most of its logos came down the next year.

## Still Counting Down

From April to November 2009, Space Mountain closed for a major renovation. The ride path stayed the same, but the loading station was roofed over to keep stray light out of the mountain, the trains were repainted blue and gray, and the computer system was upgraded to reduce breakdowns. In 2010 new "Starry-O-Phonic" sound arrived, with a score by Mike Brassell that borrows motifs from Michael Giacchino's music for Disneyland's version. The post-show picked up tributes to Epcot's departed Horizons ride, including a suitcase marked "Mesa Verde" and an undersea scene labeled "20,000 Light-years under the Sea." In 2018, the moving walkway that had carried riders out since 1975 was controversially removed and carpeted over. Half a century on, the rockets still launch.

## Flight Context

Space Mountain stands at 28.419N, 81.577W on the east side of the Magic Kingdom at Walt Disney World, southwest of Orlando, Florida. Its large white cone, 300 feet across, sits just outside the park's berm east of Cinderella Castle, with Bay Lake beyond it to the east and the Seven Seas Lagoon to the south of the park. Walt Disney World is protected by permanent flight restrictions from the surface to 3,000 feet AGL within 3 nm, so stay above 3,000 feet AGL or outside that ring; from about 4,500 to 5,500 feet the cone is easy to spot in clear weather. Orlando's Class B airspace overlies much of the area, so contact Orlando Approach. Orlando International Airport (KMCO) is about 14 nm to the east and Kissimmee Gateway Airport (KISM) about 11 nm to the southeast.
