# Mission to Mars (attraction)

When real astronauts made Disney's moon rocket old news, Tomorrowland simply pointed it at Mars, launching nearly two decades of air-cushion seats, animatronic mission controllers and a bird that crashed every show.

**Location:** 28.4186, -81.5797

## Introduction

In 1975, Disney's spaceship changed destinations for a reason no imagineer could argue with: humans had already been to the Moon. So the voyage that had opened in Florida as Flight to the Moon on Christmas Eve 1971 was retooled to aim for Mars instead. In Tomorrowland at Walt Disney World's Magic Kingdom, and at Disneyland in California, guests took their seats in a round theater, felt their seats shudder, and watched the red planet swim up on the screen beneath their feet. Mission to Mars flew until the early 1990s, and its building has been passing through new identities ever since.

## A Rocket from Walt's Childhood

The idea had deep roots. The technology behind the original Rocket to the Moon recalled Hale's Tours of the World, amusement rides that simulated travel by train, and Walt Disney had encountered one as a child at Electric Park in Kansas City. Rocket to the Moon opened with Disneyland in 1955 and was refurbished there as Flight to the Moon in 1967. Florida's Magic Kingdom got its own Flight to the Moon, which welcomed its first travelers on Christmas Eve 1971. Then reality overtook the fantasy. With the Moon no longer beyond human reach, the destination changed to Mars in 1975, on March 21 at Disneyland and on June 7 at the Magic Kingdom, in a revised and updated show designed in cooperation with NASA. McDonnell-Douglas served as the first sponsor.

## The Bird on the Launch Pad

Every mission began in Mission Control. Guests filed into a room modeled on a real control center, where about ten seated Audio-Animatronic technicians worked their consoles with their backs to the audience, turning their heads and moving their arms. Facing the crowd stood the flight director, an Audio-Animatronic figure called Mr. Johnson. Through talk and film clips he explained how people had advanced in space travel, learned to manufacture things in microgravity and coped with the effects of spaceflight. Once per show, without fail, an intruder alarm cut him off: a large bird had crash-landed near the spacecraft launch pad. Mr. Johnson's voice belonged to George Walsh, the narrator of the television show Gunsmoke, who also narrated Disney specials such as The Magic of Disneyland.

## Seats That Breathed

From Mission Control, travelers moved into the spacecraft itself, a circular theater with stadium-like seating. Round flat screens on the ceiling and the floor showed the view outside the ship, while side screens ran film clips and graphics. The tour guide, Third Officer Collins, was voiced by Peter Renaday, and he talked guests through the flight as the craft explored space and approached Mars. The most memorable trick was underneath them. At take-off and landing, the seats filled with compressed air to simulate the vibrations and G-forces of a jump into "Hyper-space." Things did not go entirely to plan. The ship was damaged, possibly by a volcanic eruption, and had to race home. After a safe landing, Collins sent everyone off with an invitation to come back, since, as he put it, "there's a lot more to see on Mars."

## Aliens, Stitch and an Empty Stage

Disneyland's version closed on November 2, 1992, to make way for an ambitious Tomorrowland overhaul that was cancelled in early 1993 when CEO Michael Eisner began cutting costs; the building eventually reopened in 1998 as Redd Rockett's Pizza Port. The Magic Kingdom's mission lasted about a year longer, closing on October 4, 1993. On June 20, 1995, the Florida building reopened as ExtraTERRORestrial Alien Encounter, part of the Magic Kingdom's New Tomorrowland alongside The Timekeeper. Alien Encounter closed in 2003 and gave way to Stitch's Great Escape!, which ran from 2004 to 2018 and reworked many of its predecessor's elements in a lighter, comic key. As of the most recent reports, the show space has not been given a new attraction. The dream of simulated spaceflight did not vanish, though. Mission: Space opened at Epcot in 2003, and in 2000 Disney turned Mission to Mars into a feature film.

## Flight Context

The former Mission to Mars building stands in the Magic Kingdom's Tomorrowland at 28.4186 N, 81.5797 W, north of the Seven Seas Lagoon. Cinderella Castle rises just to the west-northwest, and the monorail loop circles the lagoon to the south. The Magic Kingdom lies within Walt Disney World's permanent flight restriction (a 3-nautical-mile radius from the surface to 3,000 feet above ground level), so view it from above that ceiling, around 4,000 to 5,000 feet MSL, or from outside the ring, and check current NOTAMs. Kissimmee Gateway Airport (KISM) is about 11 nm to the southeast, Orlando International (KMCO) about 14 nm to the east, and Orlando Executive (KORL) about 15 nm to the east-northeast. Best seen in clear daytime conditions.
