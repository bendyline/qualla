# Battle of Wahoo Swamp

In November 1836 an American army pushed into the unmapped Wahoo Swamp to crush a Seminole and Black Seminole stronghold, and the swamp itself turned it back.

**Location:** 28.6899, -82.2228

**Categories:** Second Seminole War, Battles of the Seminole Wars, Swamps of Florida, Black Seminoles, 1836 in Florida Territory

## Introduction

In 1836 no map showed the inside of the Wahoo Swamp, and for the people sheltering there, that was the point. A maze of wetlands, dense hardwood hammocks and scattered wet prairies along the Withlacoochee River, in what is now Sumter County, Florida, it had become a refuge for Seminole and Black Seminole families resisting the United States' campaign to expel them. That November a mixed American army under Florida's territorial governor, General Richard K. Call, went in after them: Florida militia, Tennessee volunteers, Creek fighters in the Army's pay, and some regulars and Marines. Facing them were Seminole forces led by the chiefs Osuchee and Yaholooche. By the end of the month, the Americans had withdrawn.

## A Refuge on the Withlacoochee

The war had turned hot the previous December, when Major Francis Dade's column was destroyed on the Fort King road and Osceola killed the Indian agent Wiley Thompson that same day. Afterward, many Seminoles and Black Seminoles of north and central Florida withdrew into the swamp. The Black Seminoles were Maroons, Africans who had escaped enslavement and lived in their own towns near those of the Seminoles. For them the threat was double: the Army and territorial militias meant to relocate the Seminoles and hand the refugees back to the slaveholders who claimed them.

On patches of dry ground along the river they raised corn, pumpkins, squash, beans and sugar cane, and stored provisions in hammocks ringed by water. Cattle and families were hidden deep inside. Small raiding parties went out from here against soldiers and plantations between Fort Brooke and Fort King, so the Americans made destroying the villages and seizing the food a priority.

## Houses Left Burning

The first clash came on November 14. Brigadier General Robert Armstrong, riding toward the settlements with five hundred mounted men, ran into a well-armed force of Seminoles and Maroons, and in less than an hour eleven of his men fell. He drove the defenders from the field, but they carried off their dead and wounded as they went.

Four days later Call went in himself. Leaving his baggage train under strong guard, he marched with 550 Tennesseans, most of them on foot. About three miles out they struck a large trail that wound through two dense hammocks and over two creeks into a broad field, walled on three sides by more hammock. Ahead of them the Seminoles were falling back to stronger positions, leaving their own houses in flames.

## Hand to Hand in the Hammock

Colonel William Trousdale spread his foot soldiers in open order to charge the hammock, with horsemen held back on each flank as a reserve. Before the order to advance ever came, a tremendous volley tore along the whole American front. The Tennesseans edged forward, trading shots, and then charged. This time the defenders did not break and run. They stood and fought hand to hand while their wings struck both flanks and a band of about fifty men fell on the rear.

After nearly half an hour, a general charge scattered them. Twenty-five Seminole dead lay on the field; the Americans counted three killed and eighteen wounded. Six hundred Seminoles were said to have been engaged. With the hour late and the men spent, the army fell back to meet Colonel Pierce near Dade's battleground, where the war had opened eleven months before.

## Waist-Deep at the Stream

On November 21 Armstrong ordered a combined attack. Three columns entered the swamp on a line a full mile long, with Tennesseans, regulars and Colonel Warren's mounted men on the right, Pierce in the center and the Creek regiment on the left. They found the battleground of the 18th back in Seminole hands. The Tennesseans and regulars held their fire under a heavy volley until they were among the defenders, then charged, and the Seminoles gave way.

The ground was another matter. Many of the regulars and Tennesseans followed a trail into an almost impassable morass where no horse could move and men waded waist-deep in mud and water. The Creeks' leader, Major David Moniac, a Creek of mixed ancestry and the first Native American to graduate from West Point, was shot and killed at a stream, reportedly while trying to find out how deep it was. Several Creek warriors died with him. That day cost Call's army twenty-five casualties, nine of them killed.

## Back Into the Wilderness

Call could not follow. With supplies dwindling and the swamp impassable, he fell back to Volusia, and the Seminoles slipped away into the unmapped interior, from which war parties kept raiding plantations. The autumn offensive, led by a protégé of President Andrew Jackson and a veteran of earlier Indian wars, had failed to break resistance to removal.

On December 9 Call was relieved of overall command and replaced by Major General Thomas Jesup. When the volunteers' short enlistments ran out later that month, they went home. The war went on. Osceola led hit-and-run raids against Army detachments and outposts until October 21, 1837, when he was seized under a white flag of truce near Fort Peyton, a few miles south of St. Augustine, on Jesup's orders.

## Flight Context

The battle site is given as 28.6899 N, 82.2229 W, in the Withlacoochee River lowlands of Sumter County, Florida, about 5.5 nm west-northwest of the Dade Battlefield near Bushnell. From 2,000-3,000 ft AGL the area shows as a dark band of swamp forest and wet prairie along the winding Withlacoochee, with farmland and pine on the drier ground around it. Nearby airports: Inverness Airport (KINF), about 8.5 nm northwest; Brooksville-Tampa Bay Regional (KBKV), about 18 nm southwest; Crystal River Airport (KCGC), about 21 nm west-northwest; Leesburg International (KLEE), about 23 nm east-northeast. Summer brings daily afternoon thunderstorms; low-lying swamp can hold morning fog in winter.
