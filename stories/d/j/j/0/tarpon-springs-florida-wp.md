# Tarpon Springs, Florida

Named for fish that were mostly mullet, Tarpon Springs became Florida's sponge-diving capital when divers arrived from the Greek islands - and every January 6 its young men still plunge into Spring Bayou after a wooden cross.

## Introduction

Tarpon Springs owes its name to a case of mistaken identity. When settlers arrived on this stretch of bayous feeding into the Gulf of Mexico around 1876, some of them saw fish leaping from the water and took them for tarpon - the name, the story goes, began with one early settler's exclamation, "See the tarpon spring!" Most of the fish splashing here were actually mullet. The name stuck anyway, and the city that grew up around it would become famous for something else entirely: sponges, and the Greek divers who came across an ocean to harvest them.

## A Winter Town Takes Shape

The first settlers were farmers and fishermen. In 1882, Hamilton Disston, who had bought the land the year before, ordered a town plan drawn up, and on February 12, 1887, Tarpon Springs became the first incorporated city in what is now Pinellas County. Less than a year later, on January 13, 1888, the Orange Belt Railway arrived - the first railroad built in what is now Pinellas County - and the town developed as a wintering spot for wealthy northerners. Other chapters of the town's early life are recorded in its burial grounds. The city's Rose Cemetery, where Black residents are buried, is believed to hold burials dating to the late 1800s, and its earliest legible marked grave is from 1904.

## Divers from the Islands

Around 1890, John K. Cheyney founded the first local sponge business, and the trade grew through the 1890s. Many people from Key West and the Bahamas settled in Tarpon Springs to hook sponges from the water and process them, and a few Greek immigrants arrived to join the work. Then, in 1905, John Cocoris transformed the trade. He introduced sponge diving to Tarpon Springs, recruiting divers and crew members from Greece. The first came from the Saronic Gulf islands of Aegina and Hydra, but they were soon outnumbered by men from the Dodecanese islands of Kalymnos, Symi, and Halki. Sponging became one of the leading maritime industries in Florida and the most important business in Tarpon Springs, generating millions of dollars a year. The first Greek immigrants depended on the sea and their boats for their livelihoods, and the community they built still shapes the city.

## Red Tide and the Long Recovery

In 1947 a red tide algal bloom wiped out the sponge fields in the Gulf. Many sponge boats and divers switched to shrimping to survive, and others left the business altogether. The sponges eventually came back, and with them a smaller but steady industry. Then, in the 1980s, fortune turned in an unexpected direction: a disease killed off Mediterranean sponges, and Tarpon Springs enjoyed a boom. A small, active sponge industry remains today, and sponge diving is still a key part of the city's culture and economy. Its history is written into the historic landscape as well, with the E.R. Meres and N.G. Arfaras sponge packing houses among the city's landmarks. Hollywood noticed, too: the 1953 film Beneath the 12-Mile Reef, about a family of Greek sponge fishermen, was set and filmed here.

## The Cross in Spring Bayou

Tarpon Springs is best known for its Greek influence, earning the nickname "Little Greece." It once had the highest percentage of Greek Americans of any city in the United States, and of its 25,117 residents counted in the 2020 census, somewhere around one in ten still reports Greek ancestry. St. Nicholas Greek Orthodox Cathedral hosts elaborate religious ceremonies, including the Epiphany celebration each January 6. The Metropolitan of Atlanta usually presides over the blessings, sometimes joined by the Archbishop of America. The blessings conclude at the city's Spring Bayou, where a wooden cross is thrown into the water and boys aged 16 to 18 dive in after it. Whoever brings it back is said to be blessed for a full year. The old ties run both ways: in 2007 and 2008 the city formed sister-city relationships with Kalymnos, Halki, Symi, and Larnaca in Cyprus, and a pairing with Hydra was still being arranged as of 2020.

## Museums and Main Streets

The past is easy to find here. The Tarpon Springs Heritage Museum in Craig Park tells the history and culture of the Greek community and holds a permanent exhibition of work by artist Christopher Still. The 1883 Safford House Museum and the Historic Train Depot Museum round out the list. The Tarpon Springs Greektown Historic District and the Tarpon Springs Historic District are both on the National Register of Historic Places, alongside the Tarpon Springs Depot, built in 1909, the Safford House, the Arcade Hotel, the old high school, and the Old Tarpon Springs City Hall, now home to the Tarpon Springs Cultural Center. Downtown underwent a beautification project in 2010.

## Flight Context

Tarpon Springs lies on the Gulf coast in northern Pinellas County at approximately 28.15°N, 82.75°W. From 1,500-2,500 feet AGL, look for the bayous threading into the Gulf; water covers nearly half of the city's area, so the shoreline makes an easy reference. Clearwater Air Park (KCLW) is about 10 nautical miles to the south. St. Pete-Clearwater International (KPIE) is about 15 nautical miles south-southeast and Tampa International (KTPA) about 16 nautical miles southeast - Tampa's Class B airspace lies close by, so check your chart and coordinate with ATC. Summer afternoons bring frequent thunderstorms, occasionally with hail or waterspouts off the Gulf.
