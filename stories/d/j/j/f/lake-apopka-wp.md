# Lake Apopka

Once a magnet for trophy bass anglers, Florida's fourth-largest lake was nearly ruined by farm runoff and pesticides, and has spent decades being painstakingly nursed back to health.

**Location:** 28.6206, -81.6219

**Categories:** Lakes of Florida, Environmental restoration, Water pollution, Superfund sites, Orange County, Florida

## Introduction

Through the 1940s, anglers came from all over the United States to fish Lake Apopka. They were after trophy-sized bass, and 21 fish camps lined the shore to serve them. The lake, fed by a natural spring, rainfall and runoff, was one of Central Florida's main attractions. Within a few decades it would be described as the state's most polluted large lake, clouded by algae, its sandy bottom buried in muck and its wildlife sickened by pesticides. The story of how Florida's fourth-largest lake was broken, and of the long, painstaking effort to mend it, plays out just 15 miles west-northwest of Orlando.

## Where the Water Goes

Lake Apopka lies mostly in Orange County, with its western edge in Lake County. It feeds a chain of lakes: water leaves through the Apopka-Beauclair Canal into Lakes Beauclair and Dora, then passes through Lake Eustis and Lake Griffin before heading north into the Ocklawaha River and on to the St. Johns. People have been reshaping the lake for more than a century, beginning with that very canal in 1888. The decisive change came in 1941, when a levee was built along the north shore so that 20,000 acres of shallow marsh could be drained for farming. The marsh became farm fields, and the lake became the place their water drained.

## Too Much of a Good Thing

The farms flooded their fields with lake water during the hot summer months to limit erosion, then pumped it back into the lake before the growing season, using a network of canals and high-capacity pumps. That returning water was loaded with phosphorus. Historically, more than 85 percent of the phosphorus entering Lake Apopka came from the north shore farms. The result was a hypereutrophic lake. Chronic algal blooms robbed the water of the oxygen and sunlight that plants on the lake bottom needed, and over the decades the sandy bottom disappeared beneath a deep layer of muck. The lake lost its recreational value and much of the game fish population that had once drawn anglers from across the country.

## Poison on the Shore

In July 1980, Tower Chemical Company, a local pesticide manufacturer, improperly disposed of significant amounts of DDE, a known endocrine disruptor, along with other toxic chemicals, which spilled into the lake. The company shut down that year, and in 1981 the EPA began investigating; in 1983 the site was added to the Superfund National Priorities List. Some of the chemicals seeped into the Florida aquifer and spread into interconnected lakes and waterways. They caused health problems across the lake's wildlife, including infertility and other sexual disorders in several species, among them alligators. Restoration brought its own tragedy. In late 1998 and early 1999, an estimated 676 birds died on the former farms, most of them American white pelicans, wood storks and great blue herons, killed primarily by pesticide residues they absorbed from eating contaminated fish.

## Buying Back the North Shore

In 1991 real estate interests in west Orange County organized the Friends of Lake Apopka to reclaim the lake from the farm discharges. In 1996 Governor Lawton Chiles signed legislation funding the purchase of the offending farmland, and between 1988 and 2001 the St. Johns River Water Management District and the U.S. Department of Agriculture bought almost all the north shore farms, more than 19,000 acres in all. The land needed healing before it could be flooded. Crews used modified farm equipment to plow as deep as a meter and flip the soil, burying pesticide-laden topsoil beneath clean earth on about 4,000 acres, work completed in May 2009. A drinking-water treatment residue was spread on about 7,000 acres to lock up phosphorus. Only then, phase by phase and under close monitoring of fish and birds, did the old marsh begin to return.

## A Lake Slowly Clearing

Other tools went to work on the lake itself. Since November 2003, the Marsh Flow-Way, a 760-acre constructed wetland on the northwest shore, has pumped lake water through four wetland cells, where it slows and drops its suspended particles; through 2010 it removed about 28 percent of the incoming phosphorus and about 90 percent of the suspended solids. Crews have also harvested gizzard shad, removing about 18.5 million pounds of fish by the fall of 2011, and with them the phosphorus locked in their bodies. The results are measurable. By September 2011 phosphorus had fallen 62 percent from the baseline and water clarity had improved 53 percent, despite droughts that drained large portions of the lake's volume. In February 2014 the Lake Apopka Loop Trail opened through the restored north shore, inviting people back to its shore. By March 2026 the district reported phosphorus down 66 percent and water clarity up 52 percent since the late 1980s, though a drought that began in the summer of 2025 has pushed phosphorus levels back up.

## Flight Context

Lake Apopka is centered at about 28.621 N, 81.622 W, some 15 miles west-northwest of Orlando, Florida. The lake is the obvious landmark in the area, with the restored north shore marshes beside it and the Apopka-Beauclair Canal leading north from its northwest shore toward Lakes Beauclair and Dora. A viewing altitude of 2,500-4,000 feet shows the contrast between open water and the reflooded wetlands. Orlando Apopka Airport (FAA identifier X04) is about 5.5 nm to the north-northeast of the lake's center. Leesburg International (KLEE) is about 16 nm to the northwest and Orlando Executive (KORL) about 16 nm to the east-southeast; Orlando International (KMCO) and its busy terminal airspace lie about 20 nm to the southeast. Summer afternoon thunderstorms are frequent.
