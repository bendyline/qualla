# Yulee Sugar Mill Ruins Historic State Park

A 40-foot stone chimney and rusting iron gears in Homosassa mark the steam-driven sugar mill of Senator David Levy Yulee - and the labor of the enslaved African Americans, commonly estimated at about 1,000, who worked his land.

**Location:** 28.7833, -82.6067

## Introduction

David Levy Yulee made history in 1845 as the first American of Jewish heritage to serve in the United States Senate. The fortune he built in Homosassa, Florida, rested on other people's lives. His farm of some 5,000 acres was worked, by the figure usually cited, by about 1,000 enslaved African Americans, and what survives of its sugar mill - a 40-foot stone chimney, a well, a foundation, and iron gears - now stands in a small state park just off U.S. 19.

## Senator and Enslaver

Yulee's public career was a long climb. He served as a delegate to the Florida Territorial Legislative Council, and after Florida became a state, the legislature elected him to the U.S. Senate in 1845. He is credited with developing a network of railroads that tremendously boosted the state's economy. When Florida seceded from the Union, Yulee went with it, and some accounts place him in the Confederate Congress, though sources disagree. Alongside all of this, he was an enslaver. At Homosassa he established a forced-labor farm where the people he held in bondage raised sugarcane, citrus, and cotton. The history of this place counts them rather than naming them, but their work is everywhere in what remains. Every stalk of cane that passed through the mill's press depended on people who had no choice in the matter and were never paid for it.

## Steam, Cane, and Molasses

The mill at the heart of the operation was large and steam-driven, and it ran from 1851 to 1864. It turned the farm's sugarcane into sugar, syrup, and molasses, and the molasses in turn was used to make rum. When the Civil War came, the farm put its output to work for the Confederacy, supplying Confederate soldiers with sugar products. In May 1864, Union forces burned Yulee's plantation and his home, but the mill itself was spared. It survived the raid only to be abandoned because of it. Left behind, it slowly fell into ruin over the years that followed.

## What the Ruins Hold

Today the park preserves the mill's stonework - its foundation, its well, and its 40-foot chimney - along with iron gears, a cane press, and some of the other machinery. The site was added to the National Register of Historic Places on August 12, 1970. Standing beside the cane press and the tall stone chimney, it is easy to picture the machinery at work. It is harder, and more important, to picture the people who did that work. By the commonly cited estimate, about 1,000 enslaved African Americans labored on this land for a senator's profit, and the mill itself is thought to have employed more than 100 of them. The ruins are a monument to industry, and just as surely a record of the human cost that industry depended on.

## Flight Context

The ruins lie in Homosassa, Florida, just off U.S. 19, at approximately 28.783°N, 82.607°W. From 1,000-2,000 feet AGL, follow U.S. 19 as your reference; the site is small, so it is easiest to spot on a slow pass in good light. Crystal River Airport (KCGC) is about 5 nautical miles to the north-northeast and makes the closest landing field. Tampa International (KTPA) lies about 49 nautical miles to the south, and Leesburg International (KLEE) about 42 nautical miles to the east. Expect afternoon thunderstorms in summer along Florida's Gulf coast.
