# Dade battle

On a December morning in 1835, Seminole and Black Seminole fighters ambushed Major Francis Dade's column in the pine barrens south of present-day Bushnell, and the Second Seminole War began.

**Location:** 28.6522, -82.1267

**Categories:** Second Seminole War, Battles of the Seminole Wars, Seminole history, 1835 in Florida Territory, Last stands

## Introduction

"We had been preparing for this more than a year." That is how Halpatter Tustenuggee, the Seminole leader the Americans called Alligator, remembered the morning of December 28, 1835. Just as day was breaking, he and the other warriors moved out of the swamp into the pine barren beside the Fort King military road. At Jumper's direction he counted them: one hundred and eighty. Each man chose a position on the west side of the road and sank out of sight among the palmettos. Somewhere to the south, more than a hundred American soldiers were marching north, their muskets buried under heavy winter coats, believing the most dangerous ground was already behind them.

## A Road Through Contested Land

The road ran through a quarrel over land. Under the Indian Removal Act of 1830, the United States was trying to force the Seminoles off the Florida lands the Treaty of Moultrie Creek had set aside for them and send them to Indian Territory under the Treaty of Payne's Landing. On December 23, 1835, two companies drawn from the 2nd Artillery, 3rd Artillery and 4th Infantry left Fort Brooke, on Tampa Bay, under Major Francis Langhorne Dade, bound for Fort King near present-day Ocala with men and supplies. Scouts reportedly watched the sky-blue uniforms along every foot of the route. Dade knew he might be attacked, but he expected it at a river crossing or in the thicker woods to the south. Once those were behind him, he called in his flanking scouts so the column could move faster.

## The Signal Shot

The warriors had let the earlier ambush sites pass because they were waiting for Osceola. He was busy elsewhere: on that same December 28 he killed the U.S. Indian Agent Wiley Thompson at Fort King. Finally the others gave up waiting. The ground they chose was a high hammock of oaks, pines, cabbage palms and saw palmetto, just south of present-day Bushnell and roughly 35 miles short of Fort King. Open pine and palmetto could hide no one on horseback, but it could hide a man crouched or lying flat.

Even the hour is disputed. Private Ransom Clark put the start at 8 a.m., while Alligator recalled the column approaching about nine. On the signal itself the story is clear. Jumper gave the whoop and Micanopy fired the first rifle, and Dade, riding on horseback, was killed by that first shot.

## Behind Two Logs

Alligator said the opening volley "laid upon the ground, dead, more than half the white men." The soldiers were strung out in two single-file lines, and few managed to free their flintlocks from beneath their coats. Command passed to Captain George W. Gardiner, a West Point graduate of the class of 1814, and he too was killed. The column's cannon fired several times, but "the men who loaded it were shot down as soon as the smoke cleared away."

The survivors threw together a low log breastwork. When Alligator returned with Jumper and ten warriors, he found six men behind two logs. "They had guns, but no powder," he said; the ammunition boxes were empty. He reported three warriors killed and five wounded. The warriors took food, some clothing and ammunition. By Clark's account, fifty or sixty Black allies of the Seminoles then rode onto the field and killed wounded soldiers. Clark was hauled up by the heels, dropped, pronounced "dead enough," and stripped.

## The Ones Who Came Back

Three soldiers are reported to have survived the battle itself. Private Edward Decourcey had lain hidden beneath the dead. He and Clark set out together, then split up to avoid capture, and the next day a mounted Seminole caught Decourcey and killed him. Clark collapsed within a mile of Fort Brooke, and a friendly Native woman helped him the rest of the way. His became the only narrative from the Army's side. The third man, Private Joseph Sprague of Vergennes, Vermont, reached Fort Brooke on New Year's Day 1836 with a wounded arm. He could not read or write and left no report.

Luis Pacheco, an enslaved man of mixed ancestry who guided and interpreted for the column, told his own story in 1837. He said he had been ahead of the troops and was taken prisoner; some suspected him of betrayal, and he spent the rest of his life trying to clear his name. Shipped west with the Seminoles, he came home to Florida in 1882 and died there in early 1895.

## Under the Coquina Pyramids

Emboldened, Seminole fighters burned plantations across the territory; by the end of 1836 all but one house in what is now Miami-Dade and Broward counties had been burned. Florida dominated national news until the Alamo later that year. President Andrew Jackson called for volunteers, and General Winfield Scott was ordered south. Not until February 20, 1836, did General Edmund P. Gaines reach the site with 1,100 men, identify the dead and bury them where they fell.

The war lasted until 1842 and ended without a formal treaty. Afterward the soldiers' remains were moved to St. Augustine National Cemetery, where they rest beneath three coquina pyramids alongside more than 1,300 other soldiers who died in the war. A Dade Monument at West Point followed in 1845, and near Bushnell the battlefield is now a state historic site where the ambush is reenacted each year.

## Flight Context

Dade Battlefield lies at 28.6522 N, 82.1267 W, just south of the town of Bushnell in Sumter County, Florida. From 2,000-2,500 ft AGL the site reads as a patch of pine woods and hammock in flat, low country; the Withlacoochee River swamps lie to the west and Lake Panasoffkee to the north. Nearby airports: Inverness Airport (KINF), about 14 nm northwest; Leesburg International (KLEE), about 20 nm northeast; Brooksville-Tampa Bay Regional (KBKV), about 20 nm southwest. Expect summer afternoon thunderstorms and morning haze or fog in the cooler months.
