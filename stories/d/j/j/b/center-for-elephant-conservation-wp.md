# Center for Elephant Conservation

From 1995 to 2021, the Ringling circus's parent company ran a 200-acre elephant breeding and retirement farm in Polk City, Florida - a place its admirers called "wonderful" and its critics an "elephant puppy mill."

## Introduction

Depending on whom you asked, the Center for Elephant Conservation was "wonderful," "the leading elephant-breeder in the Americas," "a stark, sterile-looking place," or an "elephant puppy mill." All of those descriptions were aimed at the same 200 acres in Polk City, Florida, where from 1995 to 2021 Feld Entertainment - the company that operated the Ringling Bros. and Barnum & Bailey Circus from the 1960s until 2017 - bred Asian elephants and retired its performers.

## A Herd Apart

Feld Entertainment was the center's sole sponsor, and it promoted the facility as a gathering place for researchers studying elephant behavior and conservation. Its herd set it apart. Traditional zoos usually keep only a handful of elephants, and Feld described the CEC as the largest Asian elephant gene pool outside the Indian subcontinent. One early count put the herd at 27 elephants, including four breeding bulls and six babies; between the traveling circuses and the center, the combined herd numbered more than 70 animals. By 2010 the center claimed 23 births, the most recent a female the staff named April. The CEC also loaned both elephants and semen to zoos and cooperative breeding programs around the world, extending its bloodlines well beyond Polk City.

## Pasture and Barn

Life at the center looked very different depending on where you stood. Much of the property was largely undisturbed natural habitat, where elephants were permitted to graze and stroll. Yet some of the elephants were kept confined and chained in cement barns. Photos of the facility, and of staff training newborn elephants, became public after the death of a former employee, sharpening the debate over how the animals were treated. The center's record with regulators was mixed. In 1999 it failed a USDA inspection under the Animal Welfare Act because of its restraint policy, and at least one case of tuberculosis was noted at the time. In 2003 and 2004 the center's publicists announced that USDA inspectors had given it a clean bill of health. Then, in 2006, the center was quarantined for a case of tuberculosis; only one animal in the herd appears to have been affected.

## Fourteen Years in Court

In 2000, animal-welfare organizations - among them the American Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals, the Animal Protection Institute, the Animal Welfare Institute, and the Fund for Animals - joined a former Ringling employee in a lawsuit against Feld Entertainment under the Endangered Species Act. In late 2009 the case was dismissed after the court ruled that the plaintiffs lacked standing to sue. Accounts of that outcome differ on how far the ruling went: some describe the judge as rejecting the abuse claims after the 2009 trial, while others stress that the court did not rule on the merits, and legal scholars have disputed the ruling's rigor. The former employee was later found to have been paid at least $190,000 by the groups that helped bring the suit, and the judge called him "essentially a paid plaintiff" who lacked credibility. Between 2012 and 2014, Feld Entertainment won $25.2 million in settlements from several animal-rights groups, including the ASPCA and the Humane Society of the United States, ending the 14-year legal battle.

## Closing the Gates

The center operated until 2021, as the elephants passed from Feld to White Oak Conservation, which began moving them that year to a 2,500-acre habitat near Jacksonville, and its story does not resolve neatly. Its supporters could point to a rare concentration of Asian elephants, a breeding program that reached zoos around the world, and a long run of births at the center. Its critics could point to the chains, the cement barns, the failed 1999 inspection, and the tuberculosis cases. Both sides were describing the same place and the same animals. From the early herd of 27 to April and the calves born before her, the elephants themselves are the ones whose lives the whole debate was ultimately about.
