# Green Swamp (Florida)

Four Florida rivers are born in the Green Swamp, a 560,000-acre mosaic of cypress, pine, and marsh that recharges the aquifer most Floridians drink from - and, by one account, the last place in the state where an ivory-billed woodpecker was reported.

## Introduction

In 1967, observers reported an ivory-billed woodpecker in the Green Swamp, in what some accounts call the last confirmed sighting of the bird in Florida, though others place those late-1960s reports in Highlands County and the evidence has never been universally accepted. The species may have hung on until 1969, and a single secondary wing feather, recovered at a roost site, was identified as an ivory-bill's by the ornithologist Alexander Wetmore. That the bird's last Florida refuge may have been here says a great deal about this place: a vast, flat, easily overlooked expanse of cypress, pine, and marsh in the middle of the state that turns out to matter enormously.

## Where Four Rivers Begin

The Green Swamp covers approximately 560,000 acres across parts of Hernando, Pasco, Sumter, Lake, Polk, and Hillsborough counties, lying between the southern end of the Brooksville Ridge to the west and the middle of the Lake Wales Ridge to the east. It is a relatively flat mosaic of wet and dry habitats, and when rain falls on it, the water goes in several directions at once. Some drains across the surface to form the headwaters of four major rivers: the Withlacoochee, the Ocklawaha, the Hillsborough, and the Peace. Much of it also percolates down through a thin soil over limestone bedrock to replenish the Floridan aquifer system, the primary source of drinking water for most Floridians. Because the region sits higher than the land around it, and the aquifer rises very close to the surface here, the Green Swamp acts as the pressure head for the aquifer - one reason it is considered vital to the quality and quantity of Florida's water supply.

## Cypress Domes and Blackwater

Walk across the Green Swamp and the landscape keeps changing: xeric hammocks, pine sandhills, oak scrub, wet prairies, marshes, pine flatwoods, cypress domes, bottomland forests, hydric hammocks, bayheads, and riverine swamps. Nearly circular cypress swamps sit in shallow sinkholes surrounded by pine flatwoods, and in periods of high water neighboring domes often merge into cypress strands. Along the Withlacoochee and Hillsborough rivers, blackwater swamps of cypress and mixed hardwoods can stretch up to a mile wide, and Devils Creek Swamp in Sumter County alone covers 6,305 acres. The ground beneath has a karst character. At Dobes Hole on the Withlacoochee, water moves both ways: when the river is high, the hole swallows some of its flow underground, and when the water table is high, it works as a spring, adding water back. Elsewhere, siphons in the riverbed pull part of the flow into underground channels that later rejoin the river.

## Timber, Floods, and a Decision

People have used the Green Swamp for a very long time - evidence dates back to about 6000 BC - yet no trace of a permanent settlement before the arrival of Europeans has been found. In the 20th century, cattle ranching and lumbering grew here, and a logging railroad was built across the swamp to haul out cut timber. Then came 1960, when a heavy rainy season aggravated by Hurricane Donna caused widespread flooding across west-central Florida. The disaster led to the creation of the Southwest Florida Water Management District to manage flood control, and the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers developed the Four Rivers Basin project of dams and retention areas. Initial plans to build such structures in the Green Swamp met opposition, and the decision was made to keep the swamp in its natural condition for flood management. The wetland, in other words, was judged more useful left alone.

## Disney Next Door

A new pressure arrived in the early 1970s, when Walt Disney World opened just to the east and development pushed toward the swamp. Florida responded in 1974 by designating 322,000 acres of the Green Swamp region as an Area of Critical State Concern. Large pieces are now protected. Some 110,000 acres make up the Green Swamp Wilderness Preserve, managed by the Southwest Florida Water Management District in five units, from the 5,067 acres of Colt Creek State Park to the 51,149-acre East Tract. Within the preserve, nearly 36 miles of the Withlacoochee River's 110-mile length are protected as an Outstanding Florida Water. The Green Swamp Wildlife Management Area covers 50,692 acres in the east, the Lower Green Swamp Nature Preserve 12,800 acres in northeastern Hillsborough County, and more than 58,000 acres of the western swamp fall within the Richloam Wildlife Management Area of the Withlacoochee State Forest.

## Running with the Skunk Ape

For visitors, the swamp is open country to explore. The General James A. Van Fleet State Trail runs for 29.2 miles through it, and the Green Swamp has become popular with trail runners, who turn out every year for the Skunk Ape Night Run and the Skunk Ape's Revenge; an earlier race, Dances With Dirt, was once a favorite here too. Out among the cypress at night, with the theme parks somewhere off to the east, it is easy to understand why an ivory-billed woodpecker might have found its last Florida hiding place in this quiet, watery heart of the state.

## Flight Context

The Green Swamp sprawls across west-central Florida; this marker sits in northern Polk County near Polk City at approximately 28.207°N, 81.774°W. From 2,000-3,000 feet AGL the swamp shows as a flat patchwork of cypress domes, pine flatwoods, and wet prairie, while the sandy Lake Wales Ridge rises to the east and the Brooksville Ridge lies to the west. Winter Haven's Gilbert Airport (KGIF, also called Winter Haven Regional) is about 9 nautical miles to the south, Lakeland Linder International (KLAL) about 18 nautical miles southwest, and Kissimmee Gateway (KISM) about 18 nautical miles east-northeast. Orlando International (KMCO) is about 28 nautical miles to the east-northeast, so mind Orlando's Class B airspace. Summer afternoons bring frequent thunderstorms.
