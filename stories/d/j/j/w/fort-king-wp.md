# Fort King

Built in 1827 on ground people had lived on for eight thousand years, Fort King in Ocala anchored the Seminole Wars, was burned, rebuilt, dismantled for lumber, and finally raised again as a replica in 2017.

**Location:** 29.1889, -82.0822

**Categories:** Forts on the National Register of Historic Places in Florida, National Historic Landmarks in Florida, Archaeological sites in Florida, Second Seminole War, Buildings and structures in Ocala, Florida

## Introduction

The soldiers were the newcomers. Archaeologists working at the site of Fort King, in what is now Ocala, have found that people lived on this ground during two long stretches of time: from about 6500 to 2000 B.C., and again from about A.D. 200 to 1500. The first of those began more than 8,000 years ago. Against that span, the United States Army's stay was brief, less than twenty years of stockades and treaties and war. Yet those years made the place a National Historic Landmark, and in 2017 the fort rose again, rebuilt at the historic site to be as historically accurate as possible.

## A Crossroads in the Wilderness

The Army built the fort in 1827 as a buffer. To the south lay the reservation the Treaty of Moultrie Creek had set aside for the Seminole; to the north, European Americans were settling. The post was named for Colonel William King, commander of the 4th Infantry Regiment and military governor of West Florida in 1818 and 1819, and it was also known as Camp King or Cantonment King.

Its real value was location. Fort King sat at the hub of a web of military roads. The Fort King Road ran south to Fort Brooke on Tampa Bay, and other routes linked it to Fort McCoy, Palatka, Jacksonville and a ford on the St. Johns River where the town of Astor later grew. Even so, the fort fell into disuse after 1829. It took the government's push to remove the Seminole to bring it back to life.

## December 28, 1835

In 1832 Fort King was reactivated as a base for moving the Seminole to Indian Territory, west of the Mississippi, under the Treaty of Payne's Landing. When the Second Seminole War broke out in late 1835, its strategic position made it a center of American military activity for the next seven years.

The war's opening blows landed on a single day. On December 23, 1835, Major Francis L. Dade marched out of Fort Brooke with a column meant to reinforce and resupply Fort King's garrison. Five days later, on December 28, Seminole warriors ambushed the column on the road in what became known as the Dade battle. That same day, at Fort King itself, Osceola and a group of Seminole raiders killed the U.S. official Wiley Thompson, who had been organizing the removal. Together the two attacks marked the beginning of the war.

## Burned, Rebuilt, Taken Apart

The fort did not have an easy war. In July 1836, after the Army abandoned it, the Seminoles burned it down. Troops returned and rebuilt it in April 1837. In May 1839 the Macomb Treaty, a peace agreement negotiated between General Alexander Macomb and the Seminole, was made at Fort King, but it collapsed only two months later after the Battle of the Caloosahatchee.

When peace finally came, the fort found a civilian use. After Marion County was organized, the building served in 1844 as the county's first courthouse. Eventually it was abandoned, and early settlers took it apart piece by piece, salvaging its materials for their own buildings. For decades afterward, the site that had anchored a war was simply land.

## Guardians of an Empty Field

Memory came back slowly. In October 1927 the founding members of the Ocala Chapter of the Daughters of the American Revolution bought the fort's memorial property and raised money for a granite monument. The 1953 film Seminole was set around Fort King, though the events it portrayed were historically inaccurate. In February 2004 the site was designated a National Historic Landmark, and in 2013 the Fort King Heritage Association formed to develop, promote and protect it.

Today the site covers roughly 40 acres surrounded by a residential neighborhood near the corner of East Fort King Street and 39th Avenue, owned jointly by the City of Ocala and Marion County, with a visitor center and an interpretive trail that descends to Seep Spring. Three historical markers stand there, including one at the old fort cemetery site. In the fall of 2017 a replica of the fort was built within the historic site, and in December 2022 the Festival of Fort King, with its living-history events, returned to the park.

## Flight Context

The Fort King site is at 29.1889 N, 82.0822 W, on the east side of Ocala, Florida, near East Fort King Street and 39th Avenue. The roughly 40-acre park is a small green and open patch inside residential neighborhoods; from 1,500-2,500 ft AGL, look for the reconstructed fort in the clearing. Downtown Ocala lies a few miles to the west. Nearby airports: Ocala International-Jim Taylor Field (KOCF), about 7.5 nm west; Williston Municipal (X60), about 23 nm west-northwest; Leesburg International (KLEE), about 26 nm south-southeast; Gainesville Regional (KGNV), about 32 nm north-northwest. Summer afternoons bring frequent thunderstorms; winter mornings can be foggy.
