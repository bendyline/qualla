# Souther Field

A World War I flying school outside Americus, Georgia, where surplus Jennys sold for a song, Charles Lindbergh made his first solo, and RAF cadets learned to fly before the field took Jimmy Carter's name.

**Location:** 32.1106, -84.1886

**Categories:** World War I airfields in the United States, Airfields of the United States Army Air Forces in Georgia (U.S. state), Aviation history, Charles Lindbergh, Sumter County, Georgia

## Introduction

By one account, propellers went for around twenty-six cents. Altimeters sold in lots of 200 at about a dime apiece, and a big-time buyer could snap up a load of Curtiss Jennys for roughly fifty dollars each, though the Georgia Encyclopedia puts the average price nearer $1,000. After World War I, the Army flying field outside Americus, Georgia, turned into something stranger than a base: a continuous auction of surplus airplanes, a mecca for would-be barnstormers hunting for a bargain still in the crate. In May 1923 one of those bargain hunters was a young man named Charles Lindbergh, who had never flown an airplane solo. He did it here, above the cotton fields.

## A Small City for Flyers

Souther Field was one of thirty-two Air Service training camps established after the United States entered World War I in April 1917. On 19 January 1918 the War Department acquired 407 acres north of Americus through Sumter County, as a lease by Wikipedia's account and a deeded gift by the Georgia Encyclopedia's, for a primary flying school and an aviation supply depot. The field took its name from Henry Souther of Hartford, Connecticut, head of the Air Service's Aircraft Engineering Division and a consulting engineer on many wartime aviation projects, who had died in August 1917. The J.B. McCray Company of Atlanta won the construction contract on 7 February and started work on the 19th. About 1,200 men cleared and graded the ground before anything could be built. What rose there was a small city: warehouses, 15 wooden hangars, quarters for 2,000 officers and men, a hospital, a YMCA, schools, a fire department, a post office, electric lighting, underground sewers, telephones and a railroad spur.

## Twenty-Five Cadets a Week

The first airplanes to land were Standard J-1s flown in from Maxwell Field, Alabama, but the first ones assigned to Souther were Canadian-built Curtiss Jennys, soon joined by 71 more from the Curtiss factory in Buffalo, New York. The first 25 cadets reported on 29 May 1918, and a fresh class of 25 arrived every week until the armistice. The Army formally accepted the field and opened it on 4 July 1918. At its high-water mark Souther held 125 officers, 1,400 men and 147 aircraft, and by the field's own history it logged more flying hours in 1918 than any other field in the country. Then the war ended, the War Department deactivated the field, and the auctions began.

## Solo Above the Cotton

Lindbergh paid $500 for a Jenny with a brand-new OX-5 engine, a new paint job and an extra 20-gallon gasoline tank, choosing it from more than 100 on the field. He had some dual instruction time to his credit, but he did not advertise that he had never actually soloed. He taxied around for a while to get the feel of the plane, then nearly damaged it on an attempted takeoff. Back at the hangars, he asked a pilot who had been watching to ride with him; Lindbergh's own memoir calls the man Henderson, while Glenn Messer later claimed the role. The pilot climbed out satisfied that his student would do fine, though he suggested waiting until later in the afternoon, when the wind had calmed. Later that day, Lindbergh made his first solo flight over the fields near Americus. After more practice, he set off for Montgomery, Alabama, to begin his barnstorming career.

## Stearmans and the Royal Air Force

Sumter County bought the property in 1928. The field's second life began with William J. Graham of Pittsburgh, whose Graham Aviation Company managed eight airports in western Pennsylvania by the end of 1940. When the Army Air Corps asked him to establish a contract pilot school, a pilot who had trained at Souther during World War I suggested the old field. Graham leased it, and construction began in December 1940, slowed by the need to remove the concrete foundations of the earlier buildings. Instructor training opened on 1 February 1941 with five PT-15s, and the first ten PT-17 Stearmans arrived in March. Officially the 56th Army Air Forces Flying Training Detachment, the school was soon training British Royal Air Force cadets, who came by train from Canada in early June and were warmly greeted as their buses rolled through Americus. One of them, Jack Currie, soloed at Souther in early 1942, survived the war and recounted his training here in his book Wings Over Georgia.

## Prisoners, Classrooms and a President's Name

American entry into the war brought expansion. The Defense Plant Corporation bought Graham's investment, and the school grew to 644 acres with three hangars and two auxiliary fields. The British departed in the fall of 1942, and American cadets took their place. Between January 1943 and January 1944, 2,073 students trained here. The school peaked in the winter of 1943–44 with 600 cadets, more than 450 civilian employees including over 100 flight instructors, and 122 PT-17s. The last class graduated in October 1944, and the school closed. That year Souther also became a site for German prisoners of war, who worked on farms in the area. After the war the land passed to the city of Americus. Most of it became a civil airport, and in 1948 part was chartered for the vocational school that grew into South Georgia Technical College. In 2009 the field was renamed Jimmy Carter Regional Airport, honoring the former president who lived in nearby Plains.

## Flight Context

Souther Field is today's Jimmy Carter Regional Airport (KACJ), at approximately 32.111°N, 84.189°W and about 470 feet elevation, roughly 4 miles northeast of downtown Americus, Georgia. Overflying at 2,000–3,000 feet AGL shows the airfield against the flat farm country of Sumter County, with Americus to the southwest. Plains, Jimmy Carter's hometown, lies about 10 miles west of Americus. Other nearby fields include Crisp County–Cordele Airport (KCKF), about 22 nautical miles east-southeast, and Southwest Georgia Regional Airport (KABY) at Albany, about 35 nautical miles to the south. Expect summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms; spring and fall usually bring the best visibility.
