# Albany, Georgia

Founded on the Flint River after the removal of the Creek, Albany grew rich on cotton, steamboats and railroads, hosted bombers and Marines, became a crucible of the civil rights movement, and has learned to live with a river that keeps rising.

**Location:** 31.5822, -84.1656

**Categories:** Cities in Georgia (U.S. state), County seats in Georgia (U.S. state), Civil rights movement, Flint River, Populated places established in 1836

## Introduction

In 1858, Nelson Tift, the founder of a Georgia cotton town, hired Horace King, a bridge builder who had once been enslaved, to build a toll bridge across the Flint River. The toll house from King's bridge still stands in Albany, Georgia. It is a fitting emblem for a city whose history runs along the Flint: built by the labor of Black Southerners, enriched by the river's commerce, and repeatedly battered by its floods.

## A Northern Name on a Southern River

Long before Tift, the Creek people lived here and called the area Thronateeska, from their word for flint, the valuable stone found in beds near the river and shaped into arrowheads and tools. After Congress passed the Indian Removal Act in 1830, the U.S. Army forcibly removed most of the region's Native people to lands west of the Mississippi. In October 1836, Tift, who came from Groton, Connecticut, took land along the Flint. He and his colleagues named the town after the capital of New York, hoping that a settlement near the headwaters of the Flint would thrive the way Albany, New York, had at the head of the Hudson. It proved to be nowhere near as prosperous. Alexander Shotwell laid out the town in 1836, and the Georgia General Assembly incorporated the city on December 27, 1838. Tift remained its leading booster for decades, and during the Civil War he provided naval supplies and helped build two ships.

## Cotton by River and Rail

Planters developed the surrounding land for cotton, forcing large numbers of enslaved African Americans to clear it and process the crop. By 1840 enslaved African Americans outnumbered whites in the Albany region, and Dougherty County, created in 1853, had Albany as its seat. Albany became the market center for the plantations, and scheduled steamboats connected it with the busy port of Apalachicola, Florida. When railroads replaced the riverboats, seven lines met here, making Albany a center of trade in the Southeast; the former railroad station now houses the Thronateeska Heritage Center. After the war, Carey Wentworth Styles founded the Albany News, and in 1868, dissatisfied with Atlanta's newspapers, he started a Democratic daily in Atlanta called The Constitution. The river that fed all this commerce was also a threat, flooding the city in 1841 and again in 1925, and on February 10, 1940, a severe tornado killed at least eighteen people.

## Bombers, Marines and Vigilantes

World War II transformed Albany. In 1941 the Army Corps of Engineers began building an Air Corps training base on city land leased for $1 a year. After the war the field became Turner Air Force Base, home to heavy bombers such as the B-52 Stratofortress. In the early 1950s the Marine Corps established a logistics base on the eastern outskirts, and so many white servicemen and workers arrived in the mid-20th century that the city briefly became majority white for the first time since 1870. In 1967 the Air Force left and the Navy took over, basing nearly all of its RA-5C Vigilante carrier reconnaissance jets here until Naval Air Station Albany closed in 1974. Part of the property later became a Miller brewery.

## The Albany Movement

In 1961 and 1962, Black residents of Albany played a prominent role in the civil rights movement. Through what became known as the Albany Movement, they led protests and nonviolent demonstrations to end segregation in public facilities, win the right to vote and advance social justice, with support from the SCLC, CORE, SNCC and the NAACP. The city repealed its Jim Crow laws in 1963, but Black citizens could not fully exercise their voting rights until the Voting Rights Act of 1965. Segregation had shaped even the city's Carnegie Library, which opened downtown in 1906; protesters, including a 12-year-old girl, were arrested at its steps in 1962, and Black residents were admitted only about a year later. Today the Albany Civil Rights Institute and Albany Civil Rights Memorial keep the story alive, and Albany State University, founded in 1903 as a pre-collegiate school, remains one of the few historically Black universities in the University System of Georgia.

## Living With the Flint

The late 20th century was hard. Military cutbacks and railroad restructuring cost jobs, many white residents moved to the suburbs, and Albany became majority African American in the 1970s. In 1988 it drew national headlines as the "Murder Capital of America," a title other cities have since taken. Then the river rose again. In 1994, rain from Tropical Storm Alberto sent the Flint above 43 feet at Albany and forced more than 23,000 residents to evacuate, and another serious but less damaging flood followed in 1998. Albany chose not to rebuild homes and businesses on the riverfront floodplain, improving it instead with a riverfront walkway and an aquarium built over a tributary creek. The city has since weathered tornadoes in 2017 and Hurricane Michael in 2018. Today health care, education and the Marine Corps Logistics Base are its largest employers, and the pecan groves and farms around it still frame a city that grew up on both banks of the Flint.

## Flight Context

Albany sits at about 31.582°N, 84.166°W on the Flint River in southwest Georgia, with the city spread across both banks. From 3,000–4,000 feet, follow the Flint through downtown and look north for Lake Worth, also called Lake Chehaw, behind its hydroelectric dam; the Liberty Expressway loops around the city's north and east sides. Southwest Georgia Regional Airport (KABY) lies about 3 nm to the south-southwest. Albany averages thunder on about 86 days a year, more than twice the national average, so summer afternoons call for caution.
