# Jefferson Davis Memorial Historic Site

In a quiet corner of Irwin County, Georgia, a small park marks the creekside camp where Union cavalry captured Confederate president Jefferson Davis at dawn on May 10, 1865, ending his five-week flight from Richmond.

**Location:** 31.6646, -83.3867

**Categories:** American Civil War sites in Georgia (U.S. state), American Civil War museums in Georgia (U.S. state), National Register of Historic Places in Irwin County, Georgia, Monuments and memorials to Jefferson Davis, Protected areas established in 1920

## Introduction

Early on the morning of May 10, 1865, a pop of gunfire woke a small camp near a creek at Irwinville, Georgia. Within minutes, Union cavalry from Michigan and Wisconsin had surrounded it. In that camp was Jefferson Davis, president of the collapsing, slaveholding Confederacy, who had fled his capital more than five weeks earlier. He had thrown his wife's raglan, or overcoat, over his shoulders as he tried to get away. He did not get far. The ground where his flight ended is now a historic site of about 13 acres in Irwin County, run by the county, with a museum, a nature trail and a granite monument marking the spot where he was taken.

## Five Weeks on the Run

Davis left Richmond, Virginia, on April 2. From April 3 through 10, Danville, Virginia, served as the capital of the rapidly collapsing Confederacy, and then what remained of its government kept moving south: Davis, Cabinet members John H. Reagan, Judah P. Benjamin and John C. Breckinridge, his aide Burton Harrison and a military escort. They passed through Greensboro and Charlotte in North Carolina, then Fort Mill, York and Abbeville in South Carolina. The news caught up with them in pieces. On April 10, at Danville, Davis learned of the surrender at Appomattox, and on April 19, in Charlotte, of Abraham Lincoln's assassination. On May 3 his party crossed the Savannah River into Georgia. He still meant to reach the Western and Trans-Mississippi theaters, regroup the Confederate military and continue the war.

## The Last Camp

By May 6 Davis had reached Sandersville, and on May 7 he met his wife, Varina, and their children. With Union troops in close pursuit, the family fled on through Wilcox County and made camp near Irwinville on the evening of May 9. At first light the 4th Michigan and 1st Wisconsin cavalry regiments closed in from different directions. In the half-dark, the two Union units briefly mistook each other for the enemy and exchanged fire, and two cavalrymen were killed before the shooting stopped. Davis was taken prisoner on the spot. Charged with treason, he was held at Fort Monroe, Virginia, for two years before being released.

## Jeff in Petticoats

Even the garment is told two ways. Some accounts describe Varina's raglan, or overcoat, thrown over his shoulders; others, her black shawl. Either way, it gave rise to a persistent rumor that the Confederate president had tried to escape disguised as a woman, and the Northern press seized on it to ridicule him as a coward. A popular song of the era was titled "Jeff in Petticoats," and illustrated papers pictured him in everything from a wig to a hoop skirt. Davis and Varina told it differently: he was ill, they said, and she had lent him her wrap to guard his health on a hard journey.

## A Judge's Gift, a Daughters' Monument

The land where Davis was captured later belonged to Judge James Bagley Clements (1869–1936), who wrote a history of Irwin County that included a chapter on the capture and the site. In 1920 he deeded four acres to the State of Georgia "for the purposes of creating a state park." The United Daughters of the Confederacy erected a monument on the exact spot of the capture: a granite stele on a stepped granite base, carved with relief panels and topped by a bronze bust of Davis modeled by Atlanta sculptor Laurence Tompkins. The front relief shows the captured Davis flanked by two Union soldiers; the back shows a battle scene, and the sides bear the Confederate flag. The monument was dedicated on June 3, 1936. Three years later the Works Progress Administration completed a Civil War museum on the grounds, and added parcels brought the park to nearly 13 acres by 1952.

## What Remains at Irwinville

The site ceased to be a Georgia state park in the mid-1970s and was deeded to Irwin County in 1976; it is still listed by the state as a historic site, and the county runs it. It was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1980. Today the monument sits in a square bed of gravel inside a low iron fence. The museum shows a film alongside Civil War-era weapons, uniforms and artifacts, and one exhibit traces Davis's flight from Richmond to Irwin County. Around it are a nature trail a third of a mile long, thirteen picnic sites, a group shelter that seats 100, a gift shop and a playground, all gathered around the place where the Confederate government's last flight came to an end.

## Flight Context

The capture site lies at approximately 31.665°N, 83.387°W near the small community of Irwinville in Irwin County, south-central Georgia. From 2,000–2,500 feet AGL the park is a small green patch in flat farm country; the city of Fitzgerald, about 8 miles to the east-northeast, is the best nearby landmark. Fitzgerald Municipal Airport (KFZG), about 6 nautical miles east-northeast, is the closest public field, and Henry Tift Myers Airport (KTMA) at Tifton lies about 15 nautical miles to the south-southwest. Summer brings haze and afternoon thunderstorms to this part of Georgia; cooler months usually offer clearer air.
