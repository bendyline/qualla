# Koinonia Partners

A Christian farm outside Americus, Georgia, that endured dynamite, gunfire and a Klan motorcade for daring to be interracial in the 1950s, and later planted the idea that became Habitat for Humanity.

**Location:** 31.9828, -84.2979

**Categories:** Christian communities, Intentional communities in the United States, Civil rights movement, Habitat for Humanity, Georgia history, Sumter County, Georgia

## Introduction

"Help us ship the nuts out of Georgia!" It reads like a joke, and it was one: the wry first slogan of a small mail-order catalog selling pecans and peanuts. But the joke carried a hard truth. In the mid-1950s Koinonia Farm was under boycott, its roadside produce stand had been dynamited, and shots had been fired into its compound. So the farm's members looked past Sumter County and sold their harvest around the world instead. The catalog, which grew to include treats from the farm's bakery, outlasted the boycott. It is still a mainstay of Koinonia's earned income, a sweet and stubborn monument to a community that refused to leave.

## A Demonstration Plot for the Kingdom

In 1942 two couples, Clarence and Florence Jordan and Martin and Mabel England, set out to build what they called a "demonstration plot for the Kingdom of God." Their model was the earliest Christian communities described in the Acts of the Apostles, and they planted it in the rural South, amid poverty and racism. The name they chose, Koinonia, is an ancient Greek word that appears often in the New Testament. It means deep fellowship. Members gave up their personal wealth and pooled everything into a "common purse." From their reading of the New Testament they drew four commitments: treat all human beings with dignity and justice, choose love over violence, share all possessions and live simply, and be stewards of the land and its natural resources. Above all, they imagined a place where Black and white people could live and work together as partners.

## Shared Tables, Equal Wages

Other families joined, and visitors came to "serve a period of apprenticeship in developing community life on the teachings and principles of Jesus." Koinonians, guests and neighbors farmed together, worshipped together and ate at the same tables. They gathered for Bible studies and ran summer youth camps. When the farm could afford to hire seasonal help, Black and white workers were paid the same. On a ledger that looks like a small thing. In Jim Crow-era Georgia it was a clear break with the prevailing culture. Conrad "Con" Browne and Will Wittkamper were among the community's spiritual stewards in those early years.

## A Motorcade at the Gate

Many citizens of Sumter County pushed back, and the pressure was most intense in the 1950s. In the middle of the decade the farm was boycotted, and the local Chamber of Commerce met with its full members to ask that Koinonia sell its property and disband. Then came acts of terrorism: the dynamiting of the produce stand, gunfire into the compound, threatening phone calls and letters. In February 1957 the Ku Klux Klan drove a motorcade out to the farm to intimidate the people living there, a caravan reported at more than seventy cars. Koinonia's members concluded that their faith called them to bear all of it nonviolently. They wrote editorials to the local newspaper explaining the farm's position, and through the nights they kept an unarmed watch at the entrance to the community. The hostility faded only slowly, with diminishing intensity for years afterward.

## A Revolving Fund and Some 200 Houses

By the late 1960s the threats of violence had dwindled, but the strain had emptied the farm of many of its people, and the remaining members considered closing the experiment unless they found a new focus. It came through Millard and Linda Fuller, who had spent a month at Koinonia several years earlier. Millard had been an extremely successful businessman before the couple rededicated their lives to Christianity and gave away their wealth. A series of meetings between Clarence Jordan, Millard Fuller and other allies produced a new direction. Koinonia Farm became Koinonia Partners, a social service organization working with its neighbors. Its centerpiece, Koinonia Partnership Housing, built homes for low-income families who had been living in shacks and dilapidated houses. Volunteers gave the labor and donors the money; families bought the houses with 20-year, no-interest mortgages, and their payments went into a revolving Fund for Humanity that financed still more houses. From 1969 to 1992 Koinonia built about 200 homes. Sixty-two of them form two neighborhoods on Koinonia's own land, surrounding the central community area.

## From Sumter County to the World

The Fullers guided the housing program for its first four years, then moved to Zaire (now the Democratic Republic of Congo) for three years to establish a similar program there. In 1976 they returned to Americus and founded Habitat for Humanity International. Habitat's own history says the idea that became the organization first grew from the fertile soil of Koinonia Farm. The farm kept changing too. In 2005 it reorganized, ending the distinction between staff and volunteers and recommitting to the intentional Christian community model. According to one account, the common purse did not return, and each member instead receives an allowance based on needs, family and responsibilities. That same year the community, once again called Koinonia Farm, was honored with a Georgia Historical Society marker, and in 2008 it received the Community of Christ International Peace Award.

## Flight Context

Koinonia Farm lies at approximately 31.983°N, 84.298°W in Sumter County, Georgia, a few miles south-southwest of Americus. The surrounding land is flat, open South Georgia farm country; 2,000–3,000 feet AGL gives a good view of the fields and the two neighborhoods of partnership houses around the community's center. Americus is the nearest town to the north-northeast, with Plains, where more of Koinonia's partnership houses stand, to the northwest. Jimmy Carter Regional Airport (KACJ) at Americus is about 9 nautical miles away, and Southwest Georgia Regional Airport (KABY) at Albany is about 28 nautical miles to the south-southeast. Summer afternoons in this region commonly bring haze and pop-up thunderstorms; mornings usually offer the clearest visibility.
