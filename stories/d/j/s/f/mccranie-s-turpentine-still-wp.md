# McCranie's Turpentine Still

Beside U.S. 82 west of Willacoochee, Georgia, a family-run turpentine still built in 1936 on old-fashioned lines went cold in 1942 when two of the three brothers who ran it left for war.

**Location:** 31.3639, -83.0636

**Categories:** Buildings and structures in Atkinson County, Georgia, Industrial buildings and structures on the National Register of Historic Places in Georgia (U.S. state), National Register of Historic Places in Atkinson County, Georgia, Distilleries on the National Register of Historic Places

## Introduction

In 1942, two of the three McCranie brothers went to war, and the fire under their turpentine still west of Willacoochee went out for good. It had been burning for only six years. The brothers had built the still in 1936, deliberately following designs and methods from earlier eras, just as the industry around them was moving on to something new. Today it stands beside U.S. 82 in south Georgia, damaged by Hurricane Helene in 2024 but still a relic of an older way of making turpentine, retired by a war and a change in technology. It was added to the National Register of Historic Places on June 28, 1976.

## A Family in the Pines

The McCranies were turpentine people long before this still existed. The family worked in the industry before 1900 and stayed with it for generations, so when three McCranie brothers set up their own operation in 1936 they were carrying on a family trade rather than starting fresh. Turpentine belongs to what the trade called naval stores, the chemicals collected from conifers. Workers gashed pine trees with a curved blade called a hack, cutting through the bark, and a strip of tin set below each wound steered the oozing sap into a cup fastened to the trunk. That sap was then distilled into spirits of turpentine, and the rosin that remained in the still after the turpentine and water boiled off became a product of its own.

## Fire, Copper and Risk

McCranie's was a fire still, built on the older model. At stills of this kind across Georgia, log fires heated the apparatus while the stiller and his crew charged a copper kettle with five to eight barrels of gum. Each barrel of crude resin yielded six to seven gallons of spirits of turpentine, and the leftover rosin was drained off and barreled. It was dangerous work. Accidental fires at stills were frequent and often seriously injured the workers who tended them. The industry's labor was also sharply divided: superintendents and woodsriders were usually white men, while most of the woodsmen who worked the trees were African American. It was their labor in the woods that kept the stills supplied.

## Georgia's Resin Economy

The McCranies worked in a state where turpentine mattered enormously. By 1890 Georgia led the nation in naval stores production, a ranking it held until 1905. Florida led from 1905 to 1923, and then Georgia regained its predominance and kept it until the 1960s. Savannah and Brunswick were the leading ports for naval stores products, and Savannah is credited with setting the world price until 1950. Yet by the time the brothers built their still, the ground was shifting. New methods introduced in the 1930s, above all large-scale steam distillation, were modernizing production, and small operations declined after 1940 as large chemical companies took over the market.

## When the Brothers Went to War

Two pressures closed McCranie's still. Steam distillation was replacing the fire process, and World War II brought a labor shortage that small operations could not easily absorb. In 1942 the two elder brothers left for the war, and the still ceased operation. Much of it survived anyway, though Hurricane Helene did significant damage to the site in 2024, and the Georgia Trust for Historic Preservation put it on its 2026 Places in Peril list, warning that fallen trees and water intrusion threaten the fragile structures. The still stands on its roadside site west of town, with a log cabin beside it and a historical marker to explain what passing drivers are looking at. For travelers on U.S. 82, it offers a close look at the equipment of an industry built on Georgia's pines, preserved at the moment one family's part in it stopped.
