# SS Republic (1853)

A Baltimore sidewheeler that carried gold rushers, passed through both navies of the Civil War under four earlier names, and sank in an 1865 hurricane off Georgia with $400,000 in coins that stayed on the seabed until 2003.

**Location:** 30.9566, -79.6769

**Categories:** Shipwrecks in the Atlantic Ocean, Shipwrecks of the Georgia (U.S. state) coast, Steamships, American Civil War ships, California Gold Rush, Maritime archaeology

## Introduction

By the time she went down in a hurricane off the coast of Georgia, the sidewheel steamer had worn five names. She was launched as Tennessee, seized by the Confederacy as CSS Tennessee, taken into the Union Navy as USS Tennessee, renamed USS Mobile, and finally sold as SS Republic. She had carried gold rushers and mercenaries, Mexican silver and an admiral's flag. On her last voyage, in October 1865, she carried passengers and, by her captain's account, $400,000 in coins bound for a city starved of hard money. The passengers mostly survived. The coins waited nearly 140 years on the ocean floor.

## A Steamer for the Gold Rush

She was built for James Hooper, a War of 1812 veteran and president of the Baltimore and Southern Steam Packet Company, and launched in 1853 as Tennessee. She began on the run between Baltimore and Charleston, but was soon sent on the first transatlantic crossing by a Baltimore steamship, to Southampton, England, and Le Havre, France. A short time later she opened the first regular passenger steamship service between New York City and Central America. During the California Gold Rush she carried "49ers" to the eastern shores of Panama and Nicaragua, where they set out for the Sierra Nevada. She also delivered the last group of volunteers heading to fight as mercenaries for William Walker in Nicaragua, and after Walker's defeat she brought hundreds of the dejected survivors home. For several years she ran between Veracruz, Mexico, and New Orleans, often carrying immigrants and large sums of Mexican gold and silver.

## Under Both Flags

Tennessee was tied up at New Orleans when the Civil War began in April 1861. The Confederacy seized her for use as a blockade runner, but she never managed to slip out of the blockaded harbor. After the Union captured New Orleans, she was armed for Federal service and became the flagship of Admiral David G. Farragut for the conclusion of the Mississippi campaign. As USS Tennessee she served as a fast blockade ship in the West Gulf Squadron and as a powerful gunship that bombarded Fort Morgan during the Battle of Mobile Bay. In September 1864 she was renamed USS Mobile so that a famous Confederate armored ram, captured at Mobile Bay, could carry the name Tennessee. A month later a hurricane off the mouth of the Rio Grande damaged her. Judged too expensive to refit, she left Navy service in December 1864 and was sold at auction the following March.

## Hard Money for New Orleans

Repaired and renamed SS Republic, she went back to hauling passengers and cargo between New York and New Orleans. On October 18, 1865, she left New York on her fifth civilian voyage. According to her captain, she carried $400,000 in coins, mostly gold $10 and $20 pieces, meant for use as hard currency after the war. New Orleans had been captured largely intact by the Union in 1862 and had become the southern hub of the Federal war effort, a thriving, busy city. But the war had left gold and silver coin in very short supply. Bringing treasure into New Orleans was nothing new for this ship, which had once hauled Mexican gold and silver into the same port.

## Forty-Foot Seas

On the fifth day out, a hurricane off the Georgia coast proved too strong for the ship. By evening her hull was leaking so badly that the water put out the fire in her boiler, and she stalled in heavy seas, taking on water faster than crew and passengers could bail. At 4 p.m. on October 25, 1865, she sank. The people aboard escaped in four lifeboats and a makeshift raft, but seas of 40 feet through the night made simply staying afloat a struggle. Not until October 27 did the sailing ship Horace Beals find the survivors, by then desperate with thirst. The steamer General Hooker, sent to look for the Republic, met the Horace Beals two days later and carried the passengers to Charleston. Most of those aboard survived, but several people were lost before rescue came, on the raft by one account and in a lifeboat by another. All of the coins went down with the ship.

## Treasure From 1,700 Feet

In August 2003, Odyssey Marine Exploration, a deep-ocean exploration company based in Tampa, Florida, located the wreck about 100 miles southeast of Savannah, in roughly 1,700 feet of water. Most of the hull is gone, but the rudder, parts of the paddle wheel and the steam engine remain. The salvage brought up more than 51,000 rare 19th-century gold and silver coins, along with thousands of other artifacts; estimates of the coins' collector value have ranged from about $75 million to $180 million. The search and recovery were chronicled in the National Geographic documentary Civil War Gold, and artifacts and coins from the Republic have been displayed in museums.

## Flight Context

The wreck of the SS Republic lies at approximately 30.957°N, 79.677°W, in open Atlantic water about 100 miles southeast of Savannah, Georgia, with nothing on the surface to mark it. This is an overwater flight far beyond gliding distance of land, so carry appropriate survival equipment and fly high: 8,000–10,000 feet gives the best glide range and radio coverage. The nearest airports are on the Georgia coast, including McKinnon St. Simons Island Airport (KSSI), about 89 nautical miles to the west, Brunswick Golden Isles Airport (KBQK), about 94 nautical miles to the west-northwest, and Hunter Army Air Field (KSVN) at Savannah, about 98 nautical miles to the northwest. The Gulf Stream runs near this area, and it can spawn fast-building convective weather; Atlantic hurricane season peaks in late summer and fall, the season that sank the Republic.
