# Charleston Tea Garden

On Wadmalaw Island near Charleston, 127 acres of cloned tea bushes are harvested by a homemade machine called the Green Giant, the survivor of more than two centuries of attempts to grow tea in South Carolina.

**Location:** 32.6197, -80.1856

**Categories:** American tea, Tea estates, Agriculture in South Carolina, Food and drink tourism, Wadmalaw Island

## Introduction

The machine that harvests the tea on Wadmalaw Island is called the Green Giant, and it looks like what it is: a cross between a cotton picker and a tobacco harvester. Mack Fleming is credited with inventing it while working for the Lipton company, and it has long done the harvesting, rolling along straight, carefully manicured rows of Camellia sinensis and clipping off the top layer of leaves. Those leaves become black tea at the Charleston Tea Garden (called the Charleston Tea Plantation until 2020), roughly twenty miles southwest of Charleston, South Carolina. Owned by the Bigelow Tea Company, the garden grows the tea sold under the American Classic Tea and Charleston Tea Garden names, and it is the end point of a story that began with a French botanist more than two hundred years ago.

## Two Centuries of False Starts

In the late 1700s the French botanist André Michaux brought the tea plant to the United States and gave it to Henry Middleton, and the two planted it at Middleton's plantation. Tea seemed to thrive around Charleston and Georgetown, yet turning that promise into a lasting business proved stubbornly difficult. Company after company and grower after grower tried. Plantations in Georgetown, Greenville and Summerville all failed. The most successful, Dr. Charles Shepard's Pinehurst in Summerville, founded in 1888, sold tea commercially but was abandoned after Shepard died in 1915. The breakthrough came from a large company worried about supply. Fearing that unrest in tea-producing countries, and the inability to import Chinese tea, could cut off its imports, the Thomas J. Lipton Company decided to create a research and development center in South Carolina. In 1960 it bought the former Pinehurst Tea Plantation in Summerville, and in 1963 it moved to Wadmalaw Island, where it ran a research station for about twenty-five years.

## The Professor and the Tea Taster

In 1987 William Barclay Hall, known as Bill, a third-generation tea tester from England, bought the land and the research station from Lipton, in partnership with Mack Fleming according to some accounts, and founded the Charleston Tea Garden as it is known today. Fleming, a horticulture professor at Trident Technical College, had been running the garden for Lipton. Bigelow bought the garden in 2003 and partnered with Hall, who has kept it running and growing tea for sale across the United States. Under Bigelow the garden has updated its machinery and processes and worked to expand both its grounds and its production. Drawing on his many years in South America, Hall brought in more modern tea manufacturing equipment for the factory, and a newer, more efficient irrigation system now reaches all 127 acres of tea plants.

## From Bush to Cup

Every bush in the garden grows from a clone rather than a seed, so that each plant is identical and the tea tastes the same from season to season. Harvest runs from May through the fall. After the Green Giant gathers the leaves, they go to the factory, where the real work is drying them. The leaves are first spread out for twelve to eighteen hours to wither, then oxidized for a little under an hour, and finally baked to drive off the remaining moisture. Stray sticks and fibers are removed before the tea is packaged. Visitors can follow the whole process. The garden is open seven days a week, with a free factory tour, a ticketed trolley tour of the growing fields and a greenhouse, and tastings at the tea bar; check its website for current hours and holiday closures.

## The First Flush

For nine years, the garden marked the start of each harvest with the First Flush Festival. The name refers to the new leaves that appear on the bushes at the start of the season, ready to be picked. Beginning in 2006, visitors were offered the season's first tea for free, in the hope of winning new customers, while local food vendors set up booths, local musicians played, and children had a playground or jump castle of their own. The festival ended after its ninth year, but the garden still welcomes visitors, weddings and private events among its rows of tea.

## Flight Context

The Charleston Tea Garden lies at about 32.6197 N, 80.1856 W on Wadmalaw Island, roughly twenty miles southwest of Charleston, South Carolina. From 1,500 to 2,000 feet, look for the unusual pattern of long, straight, closely trimmed rows of tea bushes, which stand out from the surrounding countryside. Charleston Executive Airport (KJZI) on neighboring Johns Island is about 10.5 nm to the east-northeast, and Charleston International Airport (KCHS), with busy controlled airspace, is about 18 nm to the north-northeast. Summer brings heat, haze and afternoon thunderstorms; spring and fall usually offer the clearest views.
