# Fig Island

In the salt marsh off Edisto Island, three shell rings raised roughly four thousand years ago include one of the largest and most complex in North America.

**Location:** 32.5703, -80.2153

**Categories:** National Historic Landmarks in South Carolina, Archaeological sites on the National Register of Historic Places in South Carolina, Shell rings, Geography of Charleston County, South Carolina, Late Archaic

## Introduction

At the crest of the great ring on Fig Island, where the shell stands up to six meters high, there is an odd hollow dug into the mound. Local residents suggested it was a Civil War gun emplacement. If they were right, the gunners were digging into something far older than their war: the ring beneath them had been heaped up from shell some 3,500 to 4,000 years earlier. Here on the South Carolina coast, wrapped in salt marsh, three ancient shell rings still hold their shape, and the main ring has been described as perhaps the largest and most complex in North America.

## When the Marsh Was Land

Fig Island today sits on a peninsula reaching out from Edisto Island along the North Edisto River, surrounded by salt marsh. It did not always look this way. When the rings were built and occupied, the sea along this coast stood as much as 1.2 meters lower than it does now, and as the water rose, the marsh spread over what was probably dry land linking Fig Island to Edisto. The three rings cover an area of about 300 by 275 meters. They belong to a wider tradition: more than 30 shell-ring sites are known along the Atlantic coast from South Carolina to Florida, most dating from between 4,500 and 5,000 years ago for the earliest to about 3,000 years ago for the latest. Oddly, the largest ring here was the last to be recognized. When Fig Island 2 and 3 were listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1970, Fig Island 1 had not yet been discovered. Investigations in the early 2000s found it, and in 2007 the site became a National Historic Landmark.

## Rings Within Rings

A shell ring is a curved midden of shell wrapped wholly or partly around an open central plaza, and Fig Island 1 takes the form to an extreme. Its main ring rises up to six meters, with steep sides and a base about 40 meters wide. At least five smaller rings are attached to it, one tucked entirely inside another, and a separate shell mound connects to the main ring by a shell causeway or ramp. The complex runs 157 meters along its longest axis. By mapping the site and probing to gauge the depth of the deposits, archaeologists estimate it holds 22,114 cubic meters of shell. As at other shell rings, the enclosed plazas are nearly empty, with little or no shell or everyday debris. Fig Island 1 dates to between 4,000 and 3,500 years ago, but one of its small attached rings produced the site's youngest radiocarbon dates and more artifacts than anywhere else on the island. It may record a later occupation of a different nature.

## A Path Beneath the Marsh

Fig Island 2 is the study in symmetry. Circular or hexagonal in outline, it is more regular than most shell rings and has been described as being in "an excellent state of preservation." The ring is 82 meters across, 10 to 25 meters wide at the base and one to two meters high, and archaeologists calculate it contains 2,178 cubic meters of shell. A ramp on its north side meets a shell path that once led to Fig Island 3; that path now lies below the surface of the marsh. Opposite the ramp, a small gap breaks the circle. Fig Island 3, the smallest of the three, is a C-shaped half circle 49 meters across and just under two meters tall, with no evidence that it was ever complete. Half-circle rings have turned up at several other sites in South Carolina and Georgia. A ramp from its center joins the shell path, and the shared pottery, radiocarbon dates and connecting walkway all indicate that the two rings were in use during the same period.

## Monument or Midden?

By the Late Archaic period, coastal groups had become mostly settled, living in permanent villages and making occasional foraging trips. The pottery at Fig Island is mainly Thoms Creek, with some Stallings, both Late Archaic traditions; the earliest pottery on the South Carolina coast dates to about 4,200 years ago. The diet was not what the mounds suggest. Careful excavation of shell middens has revealed large quantities of fish bones, indicating that the people behind them got more of their protein and calories from small fish than from shellfish. There is no evidence they grew crops, but they gathered nuts, fruits and seeds. The deeper question is why the rings exist at all. Archaeologists have debated whether they are simply middens that accumulated around circular villages or monuments built on purpose. Mound building had begun in the lower Mississippi River valley and in Florida by about 6,000 years ago, which makes the monument idea more plausible. Fig Island adds weight to it: excavations found little evidence of people living on the rings, and circumstantial evidence that large quantities of shell were piled up rapidly and deliberately.

## Flight Context

Coordinates: 32.5703 N, 80.2153 W, in the salt marsh at the north end of Edisto Island along the North Edisto River, Charleston County, South Carolina. The rings rise only one to six meters above their surroundings, so they are subtle from the air; look for raised ground in the marsh on the Edisto Island side of the North Edisto River. A viewing altitude of 1,000-2,000 ft AGL works best. The rings are reported to be no longer open to the public, so enjoy them from the air. Nearby airports: Charleston Executive Airport on Johns Island (KJZI) about 13 nm northeast; Charleston International (KCHS) about 21 nm north-northeast; Hilton Head (KHXD) about 32 nm southwest. Coastal haze, sea breezes and summer afternoon thunderstorms are typical.
