# Ebenezer Creek

At a Savannah River tributary in December 1864, Union troops pulled up their pontoon bridge and left hundreds of people who had escaped slavery stranded between an icy creek and Confederate cavalry.

**Location:** 32.3619, -81.2373

**Categories:** American Civil War sites, Georgia (U.S. state) in the American Civil War, African Americans in the American Civil War, Massacres of African Americans, History of Effingham County, Georgia, Tributaries of the Savannah River

## Introduction

On the morning of December 9, 1864, more than 600 people stood on the western bank of Ebenezer Creek, waiting to cross. They had escaped slavery and followed the Union army across Georgia in the hope of food and protection, and the pontoon bridge in front of them was their way forward. The soldiers they had followed crossed first. Then, as the last of them reached the eastern bank, army engineers cut the bridge loose and hauled it up onto the shore. What happened next at this tributary of the Savannah River, in Effingham County about 20 miles north of Savannah, is one of the most painful episodes of Sherman's March to the Sea.

## Following the Columns

Throughout the March to the Sea, thousands of people escaping slavery attached themselves to the Union army's infantry columns. The army called them "contrabands," a wartime term for people who had fled enslavement. Most eventually turned back, but as the army neared Savannah in December 1864, those who remained were regarded as "a growing encumbrance." They were also in danger. Confederate cavalry under General Joseph Wheeler were harassing the Union rear guard, and Wheeler's scouts shadowed the march, preying on stragglers among the refugees. On December 8, the XIV Corps under Brigadier General Jefferson C. Davis, a Union officer who shared a name with the Confederate president, reached the creek. While his engineers assembled the pontoon bridge, Wheeler's cavalry came close enough to shell the Union lines sporadically.

## The Bridge Pulled Up

The bridge was ready by midnight, and early on December 9 Davis's 14,000 men began to cross. The refugees were anxious to cross with them, but Davis forbade it, citing the possibility of combat ahead. In reality, no such force existed. When the engineers drew the bridge ashore, panic swept the western bank, where everyone knew Confederate cavalry were near. The people there "hesitated briefly, impacted by a surge of pressure from the rear, then stampeded with a rush into the icy water, old and young alike, men and women and children, swimmers and non-swimmers, determined not to be left behind." In the terrified crush, many drowned quickly. On the far bank, some soldiers waded in as far as they dared to reach whoever they could, and others felled trees into the water. Several of the freedpeople lashed logs into a crude raft and used it to pull others from the creek and ferry them across.

## When the Cavalry Came

Wheeler's scouts arrived while the rescue was still underway, fired briefly at the soldiers on the eastern bank, and rode off to summon the full force. XIV Corps officers ordered their men to leave, and the march resumed. The freedpeople kept working the raft, trying to bring as many across as they could. When Wheeler's cavalry arrived in force, those who had not reached the eastern bank and had not drowned were captured, and by some accounts killed or crushed beneath the horses. Those who survived were returned to slavery. They had escaped once, followed an army across Georgia, and come within about 20 miles of Savannah. At this creek, that escape was undone.

## Witnesses and Reckoning

Davis's orders infuriated several Union men who watched the disaster unfold, among them Major James A. Connolly and Chaplain John J. Hight. Connolly described the events in a letter that found its way into the press. Secretary of War Edwin M. Stanton questioned Sherman about the cut bridges when he reached Savannah in January 1865. Davis defended his decision as military necessity, and Sherman backed him, but the episode sharpened the question of what would become of the freedpeople who followed the army. After Stanton and Sherman met 20 Black church leaders in Savannah on January 12, Sherman issued Special Field Orders No. 15 on January 16, 1865, which set aside coastal land, from the islands south of Charleston to abandoned rice fields thirty miles back from the sea and country along Florida's St. Johns River, to be divided into 40-acre plots for freed families. In 2010 the Georgia Historical Society placed a marker near the site, titled "March to the Sea: Ebenezer Creek," recognizing the tragedy and its consequences.

## Flight Context

Ebenezer Creek meets the Savannah River at about 32.3619 N, 81.2373 W in Effingham County, Georgia, about 20 miles north of Savannah. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet, look for the mouth of the creek on the broad Savannah River, which forms the Georgia-South Carolina line here. Savannah/Hilton Head International Airport (KSAV) is about 14 nm to the south, and Ridgeland-Claude Dean Airport (FAA identifier 3J1) is about 15 nm northeast across the river in South Carolina. Hunter Army Airfield (KSVN) lies about 22 nm south, so mind its controlled airspace. Summer haze and thunderstorms are common; winter, the season of the 1864 crossing, usually brings the clearest air.
