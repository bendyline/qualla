# Fort Frederick, South Carolina

A small tabby fort on the Beaufort River once guarded the southern edge of British America, sheltered Georgia's first colonists, and later became the doorway through which crowds walked to hear the Emancipation Proclamation read.

**Location:** 32.3849, -80.6781

**Categories:** Colonial forts in South Carolina, British forts in the United States, Buildings and structures in Beaufort County, South Carolina, Tabby architecture, Emancipation Proclamation history

## Introduction

Part of Fort Frederick now lies beneath the Beaufort River. Erosion has carried away a section of the old walls, and the rest survives as heavily worn tabby, the coastal building material made with shell, now shielded by a fresh protective layer of the same stuff. It may be the oldest tabby structure in South Carolina. For such a modest ruin, measuring just 125 by 75 feet, it has an outsized place in history. It guarded the southern frontier of British America, gave shelter to the colonists who founded Georgia, and on the first day of 1863 became the gateway through which people walked to hear freedom proclaimed.

## Guarding the Edge of an Empire

Until Georgia was founded in 1733, South Carolina was the southernmost British colony in North America, and Fort Prince Frederick stood near its southern tip on Port Royal Sound, one of the deepest natural harbors on the continent's east coast. Controlling the sound mattered. Spain claimed the region, and France was interested in pushing east from the lower Mississippi. The fort also protected Beaufort, founded in 1711 as the second settlement in the Province of Carolina after Charles Town, today's Charleston. Rice planting and trade with Native Americans, bound into the Atlantic triangular trade that included the traffic in enslaved Africans, had made the region prosperous. The fort was built in the early 1730s with tabby walls, and sources date its completion between 1733 and 1735. Inside stood a barracks and a magazine, while a battery of cannon lined the eastern wall and a prominent bastion projected from the southwest side. An independent company of British regular infantry garrisoned it until the soldiers were transferred to Georgia in 1736.

## A Resting Place for Georgia

In January 1733, colonists bound for the new colony of Georgia arrived at Fort Frederick after crossing the Atlantic. They rested in barracks that had just been finished and prepared for the work ahead, while their leader, the Georgia Trustee James Oglethorpe, went out with South Carolina scouts to the Savannah River, forty miles away by water, to choose the site of Savannah. Georgia was founded on February 12, 1733, and its success undid the little fort's purpose. New forts rose farther south, including Fort Frederica on St. Simons Island, and with a strong military presence in Georgia under Oglethorpe, Fort Frederick became obsolete.

## Two Forts for One Prince

Both Fort Frederick and Fort Frederica were named for Frederick, Prince of Wales, the son of George II and Queen Caroline. Frederick had grown up in Hanover, now part of Germany, and only moved to England in 1728 after his father inherited the throne. When the South Carolina fort took his name he was still new to Britain. By the time Oglethorpe named Fort Frederica for him in 1736, he was emerging as a progressive opposition figure within the royal family. Oglethorpe and the 4th Earl of Shaftesbury, great-grandson of Carolina's founder, were among those linked to the opposition country party, and the writer Lord Bolingbroke cast Frederick as a national redeemer in his work On the Idea of a Patriot King.

## Walking Through to Freedom

On January 1, 1863, the Emancipation Proclamation was read aloud to the public on a site next to Fort Frederick. Many of those who came to hear it arrived by water, landing at the fort's dock and walking through the old fort on their way to the reading. That passage has led one historian of the region to call the site "hallowed ground." The ruin long sat on the grounds of Naval Hospital Beaufort and was closed to the public for decades, until it was donated to the State of South Carolina in 1997 through the National Park Service's Federal Lands to Parks Program. In 2015 archaeologists investigated the site, and in February 2021 it opened as Fort Frederick Heritage Preserve, a six-acre park beside the naval hospital with parking, a picnic pavilion and interpretive panels. The state Department of Natural Resources owns the tabby ruins and Beaufort County the public-access half. Before it opened, Port Royal town manager Van Willis had observed, "It's wonderful to have this type of history, but when it's behind a fence, then it's difficult to expose people to it."

## Flight Context

Fort Frederick sits at about 32.3849 N, 80.6781 W on the Beaufort River in Port Royal, South Carolina, between the town of Beaufort to the north and Port Royal Sound to the south. The fort itself is tiny and hard to spot, so use the river and the adjoining naval hospital grounds as your guide from 1,500 to 2,000 feet. Beaufort Executive Airport (KARW) is only about 3 nm away. Marine Corps Air Station Beaufort (KNBC) is about 6 nm to the north-northwest, with busy military airspace, so check charts and contact the appropriate controllers. Hilton Head Airport (KHXD) is about 10 nm to the south. Expect summer haze and afternoon storms; fall and spring usually bring the clearest views over the sound.
