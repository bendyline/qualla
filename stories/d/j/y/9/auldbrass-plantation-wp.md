# Auldbrass Plantation

Frank Lloyd Wright's only plantation complex took more than a decade to build, sank into neglect as a gun club's lodge, and has been painstakingly rebuilt from his original drawings in the South Carolina Lowcountry.

**Location:** 32.6795, -80.8133

**Categories:** Frank Lloyd Wright buildings, Plantation houses in South Carolina, Houses on the National Register of Historic Places in South Carolina, Houses in Beaufort County, South Carolina, Houses completed in 1951

## Introduction

At the low point of its life, Frank Lloyd Wright's only plantation complex had boats parked in the living room. According to Wright's grandson Eric Lloyd Wright, the gun club that used the house in the 1980s also turned the bedrooms into bunk rooms and the main kitchen into a slaughter room. Auldbrass, a cluster of more than 20 structures near Yemassee in the South Carolina Lowcountry, took well over a decade to build, was reshaped by its patron's later marriages, and has spent the years since being coaxed back toward the design Wright drew for it.

## Old Brass, Made Auld

The land carried a long paper trail before Wright ever saw it. In the mid-18th century the site held as many as 16 land grants. One of them, Mount Pleasant, was issued in 1736 to Charles Barker and later passed to John Deas, whose house was completed in 1758 on the spot where the main building now stands; none of those colonial structures survive. In the 20th century, the industrial consultant Charles Leigh Stevens pieced together 4,253 acres from several old tracts, among them a grant called Old Brass, though nobody knows how that name came about. Stevens wanted a fully functioning farm, and Wright answered with some 500 drawings. A revised plan in August 1939 added cabins and a swimming pool, with farm buildings sized for 40 dogs, 15 horses or mules, 500 chickens and five cows. Around then Wright renamed the place using the Scottish word for old: Auldbrass.

## Walls That Lean Like Live Oaks

Wright designed Auldbrass in his organic style, shaping it to the landscape and arranging the buildings so that no single structure dominates the rest. There is no grand entrance. The plantation house and its companions sit on hexagonal floor plans, and an unsubstantiated rumor holds that Stevens challenged Wright to leave out right angles altogether. The walls are the signature: slanting cypress facades, at an angle often said to echo the lean of the property's live oaks, backed by vertical brick piers and pierced by vertical windows that swing outward to catch the breeze. Copper roofs spread across some 17,000 square feet. Inside the main house, two bedrooms, a study and two bathrooms gather around a living room, warmed by a heating system fed with water from 200-foot wells. Outside, gravel drives and landscaped esplanades link the buildings, Thomas D. Church designed the landscape, and 500 lamps light the grounds at night.

## A Plan That Kept Changing

Two of Wright's senior apprentices finished the working drawings in August 1940, and construction began the following month. Materials were short, Wright did not know the site well, Stevens was often unavailable, and local contractors struggled to make sense of the unconventional details. A reporter from the Atlanta Journal who visited in early 1942 found that supervisor T. F. Haddock "had no idea when it would be completed" and predicted that the job, first estimated at around $90,000, would cost $200,000. Somewhere along the way the plantation house and guesthouse swapped positions, for reasons never documented, and Stevens divorced and remarried twice, changing the plans each time. World War II slowed everything further; the complex was not completed until 1952 or 1953. His third wife, Nina Katherine Lunn, made extensive changes and reportedly tried to burn some of the Wright furniture. They divorced in June 1957, and a month later Stevens married Barbara, whose only known alteration was a pool outside the master bedroom.

## Tours for Anyone Who Came

Stevens was still living at Auldbrass when he died in 1962. His daughter, Jessica Loring, and her husband kept it going for the next 17 years, buying out the stakes held by Stevens's widow and her brother, and Jessica moved into the plantation house in 1966. The farm animals were long gone, and the farmland had shrunk to 200 acres. The Lorings raised crops without earning much from them or finding enough hands to help, and the scattered buildings were hard to maintain. They showed the place to everyone who turned up anyway, even producing a brochure on its history. Visitors included the journalist Herbert Jacobs, for whom Wright had designed two houses in Wisconsin, the architectural writer William Allin Storrer, and local church groups. On June 3, 1976, Auldbrass was added to the National Register of Historic Places, reportedly the first piece of 20th-century architecture in South Carolina to be listed. In 1979 Jessica Loring sold the western section of the land, including the plantation house, to Boise Cascade, keeping an adjoining tract of more than 1,850 acres.

## Under Fifteen Coats of Paint

Boise Cascade soon sold the property to the paper company Westvaco, which sold the house and about 100 acres to a Yemassee man in 1981, and the gun club years followed. In December 1986 the film producer Joel Silver decided to buy, eventually securing 55 acres for $130,000. He hired Eric Lloyd Wright as a consultant and set about building what Wright had drawn but never built, starting with the long-planned swimming pool. Crews uncovered the original cypress beams beneath 15 coats of paint, and 6,000 feet of underground ducts and wiring replaced the overhead lines. Silver hosted Sylvester Stallone, Bruce Willis and Demi Moore here, dredged out the original lake that had dried up in Stevens's day, and rebuilt the outbuildings lost to fire. The bill climbed from $7 million by 1992 to $10 million by 1995 and past $20 million by the mid-2010s, a cost attributed to his determination to stay close to the original design. In October 2024 an outbuilding burned, causing $2 million in damage. Public access remains rare; since 2018 the Beaufort County Open Land Trust has reportedly offered tours once a year.

## Flight Context

Coordinates: 32.6795 N, 80.8133 W, near Yemassee in Beaufort County, South Carolina, just southwest of the Combahee River in the ACE Basin. The low, copper-roofed buildings are tucked among trees and hard to pick out; fly the Combahee's winding course through the Lowcountry marshes at 1,500-2,500 ft AGL and look for the clearings on the river's southwest bank. The property is private. Nearby airports: Marine Corps Air Station Beaufort, Merritt Field (KNBC, military) about 13 nm south-southeast, so check its airspace; Ridgeland-Claude Dean Airport (FAA identifier 3J1, no ICAO code) about 14 nm southwest; Lowcountry Regional in Walterboro (KRBW) about 17 nm north-northeast; Hilton Head (KHXD) about 28 nm south; Charleston International (KCHS) about 41 nm east-northeast. Expect summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms.
