# Battle of Eutaw Springs

On September 8, 1781, the last major battle of the Revolution in the Carolinas ended with the British holding the field but soon losing most of the South.

**Location:** 33.4073, -80.2987

**Categories:** Battles of the American Revolutionary War in South Carolina, Battles of the southern theater of the American Revolutionary War, Conflicts in 1781, Orangeburg County, South Carolina, Military history

## Introduction

South Carolinians who sing their state song still "Point to Eutaw's Battle Bed," a line that reaches back to a single September day in 1781. On the 8th of that month, near springs about two miles east of present-day Eutawville, Nathanael Greene's Continentals and militia attacked a British force under Colonel Alexander Stewart in what became the last major engagement of the Revolutionary War fought in the Carolinas. When it was over, both sides claimed a victory. In a sense, both of them were right.

## A Summer of Hard Marching

Greene, commander of the Continental Army's Southern army, had spent 1781 trying to break British control of the South Carolina backcountry. His siege of the fortified village of Ninety Six, begun on May 22, ended when an assault on June 18 was repelled and reinforcements under Lord Rawdon approached from Charleston. Greene fell back toward Charlotte. Rawdon chased him for several days before his exhausted, undersupplied men gave out, then burned and abandoned Ninety Six, even though it was the last inland British outpost after the fall of Augusta, and withdrew the garrison to Charleston. In poor health, Rawdon sailed for England in July (a French fleet captured his ship on the way), leaving Stewart in command. Greene, meanwhile, rested his worn-out army in the High Hills of Santee from July 16 while Francis Marion and Thomas Sumter harassed the British in a "war of posts." In late August he moved again, crossing the Wateree and the Congaree. Stewart, with 2,000 to 2,300 men, had fallen back to Eutaw Springs on August 27.

## Seventeen Volleys

Greene's army numbered 2,400, and after 200 were left to guard the baggage, about 2,200 marched on Stewart's camp. Among them were 1,256 Continental infantry and 300 cavalry: Virginians under Lieutenant Colonel Richard Campbell, Marylanders under Colonel Otho Holland Williams and Lieutenant Colonel John Eager Howard, Robert Kirkwood's Delaware companies and William Washington's horsemen. Warned by one of his officers, Coffin, Stewart deployed in time. Greene formed three lines, with the militia in front beside two small 3-pounder guns, the Maryland, Virginia and North Carolina Continentals behind them with two 6-pounders, and the Delaware troops and Washington in reserve. At 9 a.m. the Americans opened with artillery and sent the militia forward, a line that included Andrew Pickens, Marion and Henry Lee's infantry and cavalry. The fighting closed to hand-to-hand combat. Some militiamen panicked. Others held firm and fired 17 times before they were ordered back and replaced by the North Carolina Continentals in a "passage of lines."

## The House That Held

The North Carolinians stopped the British advance, were driven back by a bayonet charge, re-formed and stopped it again. Greene then sent in the Marylanders and Virginians, forcing the British back toward their camp. Two pockets of resistance saved Stewart. One was a brick house held by Major Henry Sheridan, armed with a swivel gun; the other was a force under Major Marjoribanks on the northern flank. Washington's cavalry charged Marjoribanks and failed, and Washington himself was unhorsed, wounded and taken prisoner, sitting out the rest of the war. An American artillery assault on the brick house also failed, and the building became a rallying point where the British regrouped and returned to the fight. Marjoribanks struck the American flank before he was mortally wounded. Greene ordered a withdrawal, and in the confusion some Americans plundered the British camp, but the army marched off in column with cavalry covering an orderly retreat. Among the badly wounded British officers was Lord Edward Fitzgerald, later famous as a United Irish rebel.

## Holding the Field, Losing the South

Hundreds of men lay dead or wounded on each side. Stewart reported 85 killed, 351 wounded and 257 missing; Greene's own casualty returns were revised more than once, the final version of September 25, 1781, counting 119 killed, 382 wounded and 78 missing. The British took 60 prisoners, Washington among them, and two cannon. Stewart kept the field, but rain prevented any renewal of the fight. He buried his dead, destroyed supplies including 1,000 muskets, and retreated toward Moncks Corner, leaving dozens of wounded behind, with his rear apparently under fire until he met reinforcements. Greene followed as far as Martin's Tavern near Ferguson's Swamp. The tactical win meant little. Unable to stop Greene, the British abandoned most of their conquests in the South and were left holding isolated enclaves at Wilmington, Charleston and Savannah. Their effort to pacify the region with Loyalist support had failed even before Cornwallis surrendered at Yorktown. The Continental Congress awarded Greene a gold medal, and Eutaw Springs Battleground Park joined the National Register of Historic Places in 1970. The American Battlefield Trust and its partners have preserved more than 18 acres of the field.

## Flight Context

Coordinates: 33.4073 N, 80.2988 W, about two miles east of Eutawville in Orangeburg County, South Carolina, near the southern shore of Lake Marion, which makes an unmistakable reference from the air. The small battleground park is easy to miss among woods and farmland; circle at 2,000-3,000 ft AGL and use Eutawville and the lake shore to orient. Nearby airports: Santee Cooper Regional in Manning (KMNI) about 12 nm north-northeast; Berkeley County Airport in Moncks Corner (KMKS) about 19 nm southeast, toward the town where Stewart retreated; Orangeburg Municipal (KOGB) about 28 nm west; Charleston International (KCHS) about 33 nm south-southeast; Shaw Air Force Base (KSSC) about 35 nm north-northwest, so check military airspace. Summer brings haze and afternoon thunderstorms over the lakes.
