# Colonial Dorchester State Historic Site

On the Ashley River near Summerville, a tabby fort and a lonely brick bell tower are almost all that remain of Dorchester, a colonial trading town founded by New England Congregationalists and abandoned after the Revolution.

**Location:** 32.9495, -80.1719

**Categories:** South Carolina state historic sites, Archaeological sites on the National Register of Historic Places in South Carolina, Tabby buildings, American Revolutionary War sites, Protected areas of Dorchester County, South Carolina, Summerville, South Carolina

## Introduction

The bell tower survived because people were afraid of it. After the town of Dorchester emptied out following the American Revolution, scavengers carried off bricks from its ruined church and from building after building around it. The tower they left alone. "Rumor says, they were afraid to touch the tower, because there was a chance of its falling on them," an 1858 magazine article noted, "and therefore it remains." It still does. The brick tower of St. George's Anglican Church stands today at Colonial Dorchester State Historic Site, on the Ashley River near Summerville, South Carolina, alongside one of the best-preserved oyster-shell tabby forts in the country and the traces of a town that lasted about a century.

## Missionaries from Massachusetts

The town grew on a neck of land between the Ashley River and Dorchester Creek, first called Boshoe Creek. On November 20, 1676, the Earl of Shaftesbury granted 1,800 acres here to John Smith, who had arrived from England with Mary Smith the year before. Twenty years later, in January 1696, a small group of settlers from Dorchester, Massachusetts, acquired 4,050 acres that included the Smith land. With permission from their home church, the Reverend Joseph Lord and his Congregationalist parishioners traveled south, regarding themselves as missionaries ready to "settle the gospel." Once their church was established, the town developed quickly and more settlers followed from Massachusetts. The community's restlessness did not end there: around 1752, Congregationalists who had lived in Dorchester moved on to Georgia and founded the settlement of Midway.

## A Market on the River

Dorchester's location served it well. Roads led to Charleston and into the colony's interior, and the Ashley River offered a convenient highway for shipping goods and produce. Wealthy planters and merchants profited from the trade that passed through, and the town remained a bustling commercial center even though it stayed relatively small for most of its existence. A fair was established in 1723 and a Free School in 1734. At the heart of town stood St. George's Anglican Church, completed around 1719; the bell tower was added in 1751. By 1781 Dorchester had about 40 houses. Visitors to the park can still see the remains of a log shipping wharf that once served this river trade, visible at low tide, along with burial grounds and cemeteries.

## Camp, Outpost, Ruin

The Revolution turned the trading town into a military prize. At the start of the war, Dorchester became an armed camp for American forces, and someone drew up a plan to fortify St. George's Church, though exactly how remains a mystery because the plan has never been found. After Charleston fell to the British in May 1780, Dorchester became an outpost for British and Loyalist troops, who used the large church. In December 1781, American forces led by Colonel Wade Hampton and General Nathanael Greene advanced on the town, and before the British were driven out they burned the church. It was eventually repaired, but like the town around it, it was soon abandoned. A new town, Summerville, grew up nearby on higher, better-drained ground, and bricks from old Dorchester were carried off to build it.

## Digging Up Dorchester

The site was added to the National Register of Historic Places on December 2, 1969, and today it forms a 325-acre state park. Archaeologists are still unearthing the settlement's history, and digs and archaeological programs run throughout the year. Living history programs, colonial demonstrations at the fort and "Garrison Weekends" bring the eighteenth century back to life, while trails, an interpretive kiosk and a self-guided tour of the cemetery at St. George's lead visitors through the grounds. The riverside setting also draws wildlife watchers: American alligators, white-tailed deer, foxes, raccoons, bald eagles and hawks can all be seen here.

## Flight Context

Colonial Dorchester State Historic Site lies at about 32.9495 N, 80.1719 W on the Ashley River near Summerville, South Carolina. From 1,500 to 2,000 feet, follow the Ashley and look for the neck of land where Dorchester Creek meets the river; the tabby fort and the bell tower are small, so the river bend is the best guide. Summerville Airport (KDYB) is about 9 nm to the northwest, and Charleston International Airport (KCHS) is about 7 nm to the east-southeast, so expect busy controlled airspace and coordinate with approach control. Summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are common in the Lowcountry; fall through spring usually brings the clearest views.
