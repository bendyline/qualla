# Fort Motte

The British turned Rebecca Brewton Motte's South Carolina plantation house into a fortified supply depot, and in 1781 Patriot forces under Francis Marion and Henry Lee forced its surrender by setting the mansion's roof ablaze.

**Location:** 33.7392, -80.6925

**Categories:** American Revolutionary War forts, Forts in South Carolina, Colonial forts in South Carolina, Buildings and structures in Calhoun County, South Carolina, American Revolution on the National Register of Historic Places

## Introduction

Rebecca Brewton Motte is remembered for helping to set fire to her own house. In 1781, with a British garrison fortified inside the mansion on her family's plantation in the South Carolina backcountry, Patriot forces under General Francis "Swamp Fox" Marion and Colonel Henry Lee laid siege. Tradition says Motte gave them arrows from East India that would ignite on impact. However the fire was started, and accounts differ, the roof caught, the British were driven out, and their commander surrendered. The fortified plantation took her name, Fort Motte, and so did the small crossroads community that grew up nearby, near the meeting of the Congaree and Wateree rivers.

## Before the Plantation

Long before any mansion stood here, the land lay near the Cherokee Path, a route that Indigenous peoples had used for generations for trade and travel. The first Anglo-European colonists in the area were Scots and English traders, who set up trading posts to deal with the Cherokee and other Native American nations of the region, and some of those posts were fortified as early forts in the colonial period. A settlement called Amelia Town was established in the area around 1735. In 1767 Miles Brewton of Charleston built Mt. Joseph Plantation here as an up-country estate, close to where the Congaree and Wateree rivers meet.

## A Widow's Refuge Seized

Brewton's sister, Rebecca Brewton Motte, who lived from 1737 to 1815, inherited some of his property, including Mt. Joseph. In 1780 she was widowed when her husband, Jacob, died of illness. The British had taken over the Miles Brewton House in Charleston as their headquarters, so Motte left the city for the relative safety of Mt. Joseph, 95 miles away. That safety did not last. The British commandeered the plantation too, and after the soldiers took over the mansion, the Motte family moved into the overseer's house (an old farmhouse nearby, in some accounts). The British fortified the big house and its grounds, and a garrison of regular, Hessian and Provincial troops turned it into a depot for their supply convoys between Camden and Charleston. Waterways were still the key transportation routes, and Mt. Joseph sat near a strategic crossing of the Congaree River, giving the British access to an important chain of transport from Charleston to points north and west.

## Palisades, Ditch and Flaming Arrows

By May 1781 the British had built serious defenses of wood and earth around the house: palisades nine feet tall, ramparts ten to eleven feet wide, and a six-foot-deep ditch in front, with a row of abatis twenty to thirty feet beyond the ditch. Inside were roughly 140 to 185 British regulars, Hessians and Provincials (counts differ by account) under Lieutenant Donald McPherson, a name some sources give as Daniel. Marion and Lee laid siege to the post on May 8, 1781. On May 12 their forces set the roof on fire, by tradition with flaming arrows (William Dobein James, who was present, recalled a ball of rosin and brimstone slung onto the roof instead), and fired artillery to keep the defenders from putting the fire out. Driven from the burning house, McPherson surrendered. Lord Rawdon had already evacuated Camden on May 10, and his campfires were visible from the fort the night before it fell. Still, the loss rippled outward: according to one account, "The British surrender of the fort alarmed Lord Rawdon and hastened his retreat from Camden to Charleston."

## A Capital That Wasn't

After the war, the site is said to have been considered as the capital of the newly formed state of South Carolina, before Columbia was chosen instead. Fort Motte never became a city. It is now an unincorporated village at the crossroads of SH 419 and State Road S-9-13. The community has its own place in the state's later political history: Samuel L. Duncan was born in Fort Motte, and Edward Israel Cain, who represented the area and surrounding Orangeburg County in the state legislature, was buried there. Memorials to both men stand at Lang Syne Cemetery, and on February 8, 2023, the South Carolina General Assembly passed a resolution in their honor. The battle site itself is listed on the National Register of Historic Places and is privately owned, a quiet patch of countryside where a widow's house once burned to end a siege.

## Flight Context

Fort Motte lies at about 33.7392 N, 80.6925 W in Calhoun County, South Carolina, near where the Congaree and Wateree rivers join, about 23 nm southeast of downtown Columbia. From 2,500 to 3,000 feet, the meeting of the two rivers makes the clearest landmark; the village sits at a rural crossroads nearby. Orangeburg Municipal Airport (KOGB) is about 19 nm south-southwest, Jim Hamilton L.B. Owens Airport (KCUB) in Columbia is about 20 nm northwest, and Columbia Metropolitan Airport (KCAE) is about 24 nm west-northwest. McEntire Joint National Guard Base (KMMT), about 12 nm north-northwest, and Shaw Air Force Base (KSSC), about 18 nm northeast, are military fields; check for military operations and restricted airspace before flying the area. Summer brings haze and afternoon thunderstorms.
