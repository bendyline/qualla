# Rivers Bridge State Historic Site

On the Salkehatchie River near Ehrhardt, South Carolina, still-intact earthworks mark where Confederate defenders tried to stop Sherman's march in February 1865, until two Union brigades waded through the swamp around them.

**Location:** 33.0561, -81.0959

**Categories:** Monuments and memorials on the National Register of Historic Places in South Carolina, 1865 establishments in South Carolina, Protected areas of Bamberg County, South Carolina, South Carolina state historic sites, Buildings and structures in Bamberg County, South Carolina, National Register of Historic Places in Bamberg County, South Carolina, American Civil War on the National Register of Historic Places

## Introduction

The Confederates held the crossing, so the Union soldiers went through the water instead. In February 1865, two brigades of Major General Francis P. Blair's XVII Corps waded into the swamp along the Salkehatchie River and came out on the flank of the defenders at Rivers Bridge. After two days of fighting, the Confederates were forced to withdraw, and General William T. Sherman's army pushed on through South Carolina. The earthworks those defenders dug are still standing near the small town of Ehrhardt in Bamberg County, preserved today as Rivers Bridge State Historic Site.

## A Line on the Salkehatchie

Sherman's army had come up from Savannah and was advancing through South Carolina when it reached this river crossing. The battle here marks one of the Confederacy's early attempts to stop that sweep across the state. The odds were lopsided. Roughly 5,000 Union troops (the South Carolina Encyclopedia says about 7,000), mostly from Blair's XVII Corps with Major General John A. Logan's XV Corps nearby, faced about 1,200 Confederates under Major General Lafayette McLaws, with Colonel George P. Harrison's brigade holding the bridge itself. The Union regiments came from Illinois, Ohio, Indiana, Missouri, Wisconsin and New Jersey. The Confederate side included Harrison's, Kirkland's and Logan's brigades along with the 3rd Arkansas Cavalry, dug in behind earthen fortifications at the crossing. Outnumbered by four to one or more, the defenders had the river and its swamp in front of them.

## Through the Swamp

The decisive move came through the water. Two brigades of Blair's corps waded through the swamp and flanked the Confederate position. Once they were around it, the defense could not hold, and McLaws withdrew. The way north was open, and Sherman's forces continued on toward Columbia. The cost fell on both sides, and accounts differ on how heavy it was: some histories put losses at about 100 on each side, while one detailed tally lists 124 Union casualties (18 killed, 106 wounded) and 97 Confederate (8 killed, 44 wounded and 45 captured or missing). Behind those numbers were individual soldiers, many far from home, who fought and suffered in the swamp for a single river crossing in the last months of the war.

## Keeping the Ground

The battlefield did not stay untouched. In the late nineteenth century, logging railroad operations altered parts of the bluff. Even so, much survived, and the Rivers Bridge Memorial Association eventually acquired the battlefield before turning it over to South Carolina in 1945 for a state park. The site was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on February 23, 1972. Today it covers 390 acres bordering the Salkahatchie River and Three Mile Creek, and its still-intact earthen fortifications are its centerpiece. The grounds also hold four historic cemeteries, including a Confederate cemetery, and four county Civil War monuments, among them the Colleton County Memorial. A guided trail 0.56 miles long follows the story of the battle through interpretive panels, and about 3 miles of hiking trails wind through the property's natural and cultural landscape.

## Flight Context

Rivers Bridge State Historic Site lies at about 33.0561 N, 81.0959 W on the Salkehatchie River in Bamberg County, South Carolina, about 5 nm west-southwest of the town of Ehrhardt. From 1,500 to 2,000 feet, follow the Salkehatchie's course and look for the park where Three Mile Creek borders the river; the earthworks themselves are low and best appreciated on foot. Allendale County Airport (KAQX) is about 9.5 nm west-southwest, Hampton County Airport (FAA: 3J0) about 11 nm south, and Bamberg County Airport (FAA: 99N) about 15 nm north. Several small private strips lie closer. Expect summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms.
