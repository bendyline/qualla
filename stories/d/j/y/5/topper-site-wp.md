# Topper site

A flooded-out expedition moved to a chert quarry on the Savannah River, dug below the Clovis layer, and reopened the question of when people first reached the Americas.

**Location:** 33.0053, -81.2444

**Categories:** Archaeological sites in South Carolina, Pre-Clovis archaeological sites in the Americas, Geography of Allendale County, South Carolina, Paleoindian archaeology, Savannah River

## Introduction

The dig that unsettled a long-standing idea about the first Americans was never supposed to happen here. In 1998, the Allendale Paleoindian Expedition, directed by archaeologist Albert Goodyear, was scheduled to work the Smiths Lake Creek site on the Savannah River. Floodwater got there first. Forced to improvise, the team moved to a hillside quarry called Topper, on the South Carolina bank of the same river, and kept digging past the depth where most archaeologists of the era would have put down their trowels. What came out of the ground below that line has been argued over ever since.

## Stone Worth the Journey

Topper is, at heart, a chert quarry. On the slope above the Savannah River, the chert-bearing Flint River Formation breaks the surface at the northernmost point of its range. North and east of Topper, rock suitable for shaping into tools is scarce, which made this stretch of riverbank a draw for hunter-gatherers from that direction. The site itself is compact, roughly a tenth of a mile east to west and a little more than half that north to south, though the full extent of its buried deposits has never been pinned down. The ground climbs from 80 or 90 feet of elevation beside the river to more than 130 feet at the eastern edge, and a chute channel cuts through toward the water. In the late Pleistocene this was borderland: a cool, moderately moist deciduous forest stretched to the north, while a warmer evergreen forest grew to the south.

## A Diary Written in Sand

On the terrace above the river, the earth keeps a layered record. The deepest unit uncovered is sand and sandy clay, believed to have been laid down by a meandering river more than 55,000 years ago. Above it lies about a meter and a half of gravel, sand and silty clay, thought to have been spread by braided streams before 15,000 years ago. Then, beginning around 13,000 years ago, a meter of silty sand built up as wind, running water and slow soil creep carried sediment down from the slope. That hillside tells its own story. Its soil shows no visible layering, yet when excavators opened a 40-square-meter block there between 2005 and 2008, the artifacts sorted themselves anyway: Woodland pieces near the top, Archaic tools in the middle, Clovis material at the bottom. Patination, signs of heat treatment and the placement of each find all pointed to a sequence that had barely been disturbed.

## Below the Line Nobody Crossed

Since the 1930s, the prevailing theory held that the first people in the Americas were the Clovis culture, thought to have appeared around 13,000 years ago and whose artifacts turn up across most of the United States and into northern Mexico. Because nothing older was expected, it was unusual for archaeologists to dig beneath the Clovis layer. Topper's first excavations, carried out by the South Carolina Institute of Archaeology and Anthropology in 1985 and 1986, followed that logic. They recovered material ranging from the 15th-century Mississippian culture back to presumed Paleoindian times, including evidence of fluted points around the one-meter mark, and nothing below it. Goodyear's 1998 crew went deeper on purpose. Well beneath the Clovis level they found chert fragments, some interpreted as the work of human hands, along with lumps of charcoal. At 180 centimeters, a cluster of about 20 rocks looked as though it might be a deliberate cultural feature.

## Fifty Thousand Years, or Not

The most dramatic claim came in 2004. Goodyear, of the University of South Carolina, announced at a November news conference that carbonized plant remains, found as a dark stain in pale soil at the lowest excavated level, had been radiocarbon dated to roughly 50,000 years ago, some 37,000 years before Clovis. He had reached that level by digging 4 meters deeper than the Clovis artifacts that were readily found at the site, and he argued that the stone objects there were rudimentary tools. CNN reported the news under the headline "Man In Americas Earlier Than Thought." Other archaeologists were unconvinced. Some argued the stones were natural rather than made. Others proposed that the stain came from a natural fire, and noted that 50,000 years is the theoretical upper limit of radiocarbon dating, so the layer might simply be "radiocarbon dead" rather than truly that old. Waters and colleagues pointed out that the supposed tools stayed unchanged from 50,000 to 15,000 years ago, unlike the shifting toolkits in the younger layers above.

## A Roof Over the Question

There is a quieter claim at Topper, too. Before reaching the deepest layer, Goodyear had recovered objects he identified as tools about 16,000 years old, roughly 3,000 years older than Clovis. According to Dean Snow, that figure is far more plausible than the radiocarbon ceiling, and it fits what is popularly called a "pioneer" stage of Clovis: the birth of a culture and its toolkit, whose earliest traces would be sparse because new techniques took time to spread. Monte Verde in Chile, Cactus Hill in Virginia and Buttermilk Creek in Texas have challenged the old model too, and most researchers have since abandoned the idea that Clovis came first. Today the site, recorded as 38AL23, belongs to the Allendale Chert Quarries Archeological District on the National Register of Historic Places. Its land was owned by Clariant until 2013, when it passed to Archroma. Since 2006 a pavilion has shielded the Pleistocene terrace from sun and rain, with a viewing deck overlooking the dig. By 2013, excavators had taken 840 square meters down to the Clovis level or deeper.

## Flight Context

Listed coordinates: 33.0053 N, 81.2444 W, in Allendale County, South Carolina. Note that the dig itself sits on the east bank of the Savannah River near the community of Martin, west of the town of Allendale, so look toward the river rather than the listed point. The site is a small excavation under a pavilion and is not distinguishable from the air; treat this as a flyover of the Savannah River valley at 2,500-3,500 ft AGL, following the river that forms the Georgia-South Carolina line. Nearby airports: Allendale County Airport (KAQX) about 1.4 nm southwest of the listed coordinates; Barnwell Regional (KBNL) about 17 nm north-northwest; Augusta Regional at Bush Field (KAGS) about 42 nm northwest; Savannah/Hilton Head International (KSAV) about 53 nm south. Expect summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms across the coastal plain.
