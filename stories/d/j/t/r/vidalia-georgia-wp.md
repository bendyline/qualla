# Vidalia, Georgia

A South Georgia railroad town that gave its name to a sweet onion first noticed by a Depression-era farmer, now protected by state and federal law and crowned Georgia's official state vegetable.

**Location:** 32.2153, -82.4100

**Categories:** Cities in Georgia (U.S. state), Cities in Toombs County, Georgia, Agriculture, Vidalia onions, Populated places established in 1890

## Introduction

In 1931 a Toombs County farmer named Mose Coleman noticed something odd about his onions. They were not hot. They were mild, sweeter than other onions, and he managed to sell them for $3.50 per fifty-pound bag, a very good price at the time. His neighbors, who had struggled through the Depression years to get a fair price for their produce, thought he had found a gold mine, and they began planting the same crop. Nearly a century later, the town at the center of that discovery has lent its name to a vegetable protected by state and federal law, and Georgia has made it official.

## Before the Onions, a Railroad

The settlement was first called Jenkins Station, after local landowner Warren T. Jenkins. Its present name likely came from a daughter of Samuel Hawkins, president of the Savannah, Americus and Montgomery Railroad, though the exact origin remains unclear. Vidalia was incorporated on January 1, 1890, and grew up around a rail yard that served farmers growing pecans and tobacco. By the 1950s it had become an important interchange where the Seaboard Air Line Railroad met the Georgia and Florida Railroad, with a seven-track yard and an engine servicing facility. The city lies mostly in Toombs County, with a small western portion in Montgomery County, and U.S. Route 280 runs six miles east to Lyons, the Toombs county seat.

## A Sweet Surprise at the Crossroads

Coleman's discovery spread from field to field as other farmers took up the crop. In the 1940s the state of Georgia built a farmers' market in Vidalia to help growers sell what they raised, and the location proved a stroke of luck. Vidalia sat at the crossroads of some of the most important north-south highways, and word spread among travelers about the sweet onions from Vidalia. By that decade the onion had become something tourists sought out and bought. Growers learned, too, that sulfur makes these onions more pungent, so they limit the use of sulfur fertilizer on the crop. Harvest season is short: the onions mature from mid-April to mid-June.

## Guarding a Name

A famous name is worth protecting, and Vidalia's growers set out to do exactly that. In 1986 the Vidalia Onion Act granted a state trademark and protection to the onions of the Vidalia and Toombs County area. In 1989 the U.S. Department of Agriculture's Federal Marketing Order No. 955 extended federal protection to the onion's name and production, and gave growers and handlers the legal right to establish the Vidalia Onion Committee. Today an onion may be labeled "Vidalia" only if it was grown in one of thirteen Georgia counties or in specific portions of seven others. Their taste and reputation let growers command a higher price in the marketplace, and in 1990 the Vidalia onion was named Georgia's official state vegetable. Agriculture remains a major industry in a city of 10,785 people at the 2020 census.

## Festival Days and Hometown Names

Each spring Vidalia throws its Vidalia Onion Festival, a four-day event in 2026 with related events beginning earlier in April, which draws visitors with a wide variety of activities. Year-round, the Altama Museum of Art & History presents regional history and art inside the restored Brazell House. The town's famous names reach well beyond the onion fields. Mel Blount, born in Vidalia, became a Pittsburgh Steelers defensive back, a five-time Pro Bowler and a Pro Football Hall of Fame inductee, and Fred Stokes, another native, played ten seasons as a defensive end in the National Football League. Don Harris, an NBC News correspondent born near Vidalia, was killed at the Port Kaituma airstrip in Guyana on November 18, 1978, in an attack by Peoples Temple members on the day of the Jonestown deaths. Olympic weightlifter and strongman Paul Anderson founded the Paul Anderson Youth Home here, which offers faith-based preparatory education and substance abuse treatment to at-risk young people.

## Flight Context

Vidalia sits at approximately 32.215°N, 82.410°W in the flat coastal-plain farm country of southeast Georgia. From 2,000–3,000 feet AGL, look for the town where U.S. Route 280 runs east to Lyons, about six miles away, with open farmland spreading out around it. Vidalia Regional Airport (KVDI) lies about 2.4 nautical miles from the city center, making it the obvious stop. Other nearby fields include Swinton Smith Field at Reidsville Municipal Airport (KRVJ), about 16 nautical miles to the southeast, and Telfair-Wheeler Airport (KMQW) at McRae, about 25 nautical miles west-southwest. Expect summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms typical of the Georgia coastal plain.
