# Mulberry Grove Plantation

Planned for silk, worked for rice by enslaved people, given to General Nathanael Greene and visited by George Washington, Mulberry Grove is where Eli Whitney conceived the cotton gin, and today only brick ruins and historical markers remain.

**Location:** 32.1900, -81.1600

**Categories:** Plantations in Georgia (U.S. state), Rice plantations in the United States, History of Georgia (U.S. state), History of slavery in Georgia (U.S. state), Greene family (Rhode Island)

## Introduction

The name is the last trace of a plan that failed. Mulberry Grove, north of present-day Port Wentworth in Chatham County, Georgia, was established as part of the Joseph's Town settlement to be a silk plantation, and its mulberry nursery belonged to that venture. The silk venture did not last. Rice did, grown by enslaved people, and the estate went on to host a Revolutionary War general, a visiting president and an inventor whose idea would help drive the spread of slavery across the South. No house stands now. Surrounded by industrial land, Mulberry Grove has gone to ruin, with only moss-covered brick remains on land closed to the public, and the place where Eli Whitney conceived the cotton gin is marked by roadside historical markers.

## From Silk to Rice

By 1740 the plantation was already experimenting with rice. When slavery was introduced to Georgia, the mulberry nursery was abandoned, and rice production became the estate's main purpose. From that point on, Mulberry Grove's fortunes rested on the forced labor of the enslaved people who worked its fields. A description from 1798 gives a sense of the scale they labored on: some 500 acres of river swamp "under good dams and well drained," another 200 acres of upland suited to cotton or provisions, and a tract of more than 2,000 acres in all, with oak, hickory and well-timbered pine. There was a "large and complete water machine for cleaning out rice," along with barns and overseers' houses, a well-finished dwelling house and an excellent garden of shrubs and trees.

## A Gift for a General

The Revolutionary War left the estate deserted. On April 13, 1782, the state legislature passed an act setting aside five thousand guineas to buy it for General Nathanael Greene, in recognition of his contributions to the war. Greene did not enjoy it long; he died in 1786. His widow remained, and in time President George Washington came calling. In his diary, Washington recorded that he "called upon Mrs. Greene the Widow of the deceased Genl. Greene, (at a place called Mulberry Grove) and asked her how she did." When he later left Savannah for Augusta, he noted the pleasure of "dining at Mulberry Grove the Seat of Mrs. Greene."

## A Machine in His Mind

Eli Whitney came to Mulberry Grove as a guest, and while he was there he conceived a new machine. He described the moment himself: "I... struck out a plan of a Machine in my mind which I communicated to Miller (who is agent to the Executors of Genl. Greene and resides in the family, a man of respectability and property)." Miller was pleased, and he offered to pay the whole expense of an experiment; Whitney would lose nothing but his time, and if he succeeded, they would share the profits. Whitney created his cotton gin in 1793 and patented it in 1794. The consequences reached far beyond the plantation. Across the United States, the number of enslaved people rose along with cotton production, from around 700,000 in 1790 to around 3.2 million in 1850.

## A Marker by the Old Road

The prosperity of Mulberry Grove, built largely on slavery, ended with the Civil War. Afterward the estate was gradually broken up, and its buildings fell into ruin. Industry grew up around it. In 1938 a bronze marker was placed on the spot where the plantation's old avenue once met the Augusta Road, and a Georgia Historical Society marker erected in 1956 on Georgia Highway 21 says the foundation of the large ginning machine built there in 1794 still stands. The site itself, listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1975, is on land owned by the Georgia Ports Authority and is not open to the public. The dams, the rice machine, the dwelling house and its garden are gone. The enslaved people whose labor made the plantation run left little visible trace on the land, though theirs is the story it holds most deeply.

## Flight Context

The site is given at approximately 32.19 N, 81.16 W, north of Port Wentworth in Chatham County, in an area now largely industrial; little of the plantation can be made out from the air beyond the surrounding river swamp and industry; the site is on Georgia Ports Authority land closed to the public, and its historical markers stand along Georgia Highway 21. Savannah/Hilton Head International Airport (KSAV) is only about 4.3 nm south-southwest, so this area lies close to the airport's approach paths and inside its busy terminal airspace; coordinate with Savannah approach control.
