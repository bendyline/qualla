# Bethesda Academy

Founded in 1740 as George Whitefield's "House of Mercy" for orphans south of Savannah, Bethesda survived debt, fire and revolution - and carries a history entangled with the slavery its founder fought to bring into Georgia.

## Introduction

George Whitefield chose the name with care. He called his orphanage Bethesda, meaning "House of Mercy," because he hoped many acts of mercy would take place there. Yet when the orphanage ran short of money, the famous evangelist campaigned to make slavery legal in Georgia, and bought enslaved people to work Bethesda's land. That contradiction runs through the story of an institution that began when Georgia itself was a newly founded colony.

## Twenty Rooms on a Frontier Grant

In 1740, Georgia was a newly founded colony, and Whitefield held a 500-acre land grant about 10 miles south of Savannah. Construction on the orphanage began on March 25, 1740. The main house rose two stories and held twenty rooms, and two smaller buildings went up behind it, one intended as an infirmary and the other as a workhouse. Whitefield wanted a place of strong Calvinist influence, with a wholesome atmosphere and firm discipline. Children were meant to leave able to support themselves. Younger children learned spinning and carding, boys learned trades so they could earn a living as adults, and every boy was taught mechanics and agriculture. Whitefield also dreamed bigger than an orphanage. He hoped Bethesda would one day become the foundation of a university.

## Franklin's Advice

The children grew most of their own food, but the enterprise still cost far more than Whitefield had expected, and he fell into debt. Benjamin Franklin offered a practical suggestion. Workmen and materials were scarce in Georgia, he pointed out, so perhaps the orphanage and its children should move to Philadelphia. Whitefield refused. His contributors had given money specifically for the Georgia project, and he would not redirect it. The decision kept Bethesda rooted in the coastal land south of Savannah, where it remains nearly three centuries later.

## The Cost Borne by Others

Slavery had been outlawed in early Georgia. In the late 1740s, Whitefield began campaigning for its legalization, arguing that the territory would never prosper unless farms could use enslaved labor. He came back to America for his fourth visit in 1751 still pressing the case, convinced that re-legalization was necessary to make his plantation profitable. Partly through his campaigns and his written pleas to the Georgia Trustees, slavery was made legal again in 1751. Whitefield, who had been buying enslaved people for a South Carolina plantation since 1747, also bought enslaved people to work at Bethesda, and he put others to work at his Providence Plantation to raise money for the orphanage. Their forced labor, taken without consent, helped sustain a house built in the name of mercy. When Whitefield died, he left them, along with the orphanage, to an English patron.

## Fire, Revolution and Renewal

That patron was Selina Hastings, Countess of Huntingdon, a charitable sponsor in England. Whitefield asked her to keep the orphanage running on its existing principles and to establish a college. From 3,000 miles away, she could not provide the oversight it needed, and Bethesda nearly closed. In 1773, fire destroyed the home. Three years later, the American Revolution stymied the plans for a college. The mission did not end there. After several administrative changes, a new building and a new society emerged on the same site as the Bethesda Home for Boys. Its historic building was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1973. Bethesda has not been an orphanage for many years, but it still devotes itself to young people from the greater Savannah area. In April 2011 the Bethesda Home for Boys was renamed Bethesda Academy to better reflect its focus on educating young men, and today it describes itself as a private day school for young men in grades six through twelve. The campus includes Whitefield Chapel, bearing the founder's name. In 2015, Bethesda began celebrating its 275th anniversary.

## Flight Context

Bethesda Academy lies at 31.9594 N, 81.0953 W in unincorporated Chatham County, roughly 8 miles in a straight line south of downtown Savannah, Georgia. From 1,500-2,500 feet, look for the campus among the tidal waterways south of the city: Wormsloe State Historic Site is about 1.7 nm northeast, the Isle of Hope community about 2.5 nm northeast, and the tidal Skidaway River about 2 nm south-southeast. Hunter Army Airfield (KSVN), a military field, is just 4 nm northwest, so stay clear of its airspace and coordinate accordingly. Savannah/Hilton Head International Airport (KSAV) is about 11 nm north-northwest, and Hilton Head Airport (KHXD) about 26 nm northeast. The terrain is low-lying coastal marsh, so plan around haze and convective weather rather than obstacles.
