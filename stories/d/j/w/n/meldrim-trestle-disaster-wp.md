# Meldrim trestle disaster

On a June Sunday in 1959, heat-warped rails threw a freight train off a trestle over Georgia's Ogeechee River, and the gas tank cars that fell burned through a crowded swimming and picnic spot below, killing 23 people.

## Introduction

Nobody on the riverbank below was watching the rails. On Sunday, June 28, 1959, some 175 people had gathered at a recreation area beside a railroad trestle over the Ogeechee River at Meldrim, Georgia. Some were on the bank; others were bathing in the water. Overhead, the summer heat had been quietly working on the steel, and when a Seaboard Air Line freight train rolled onto the bridge, the track gave way beneath it.

## A Swimming Hole Under the Bridge

People came to the spot by the bridge to swim and to picnic, and on that Sunday the recreation area was crowded. Reaching it was another matter. The only way in was a single-lane dirt road, a detail that would soon matter terribly. Among the crowd were entire families, gathered together on the shore, and among them was a woman who was eight months pregnant. Some people stayed on the bank; others waded out and bathed in the river. Above them ran the trestle, carrying the Seaboard Air Line's tracks across the Ogeechee. It was an ordinary summer scene at a popular spot, and it gave no hint of the danger building in the steel overhead.

## When the Rails Bent

The train was a mixed freight, and among its cars were tank cars loaded with liquefied petroleum gas. As it crossed the trestle, the rails deflected, pushed out of line by heat-induced expansion. The wheels of one car dropped between the rails, and the train derailed. Loaded LPG tank cars fell from the bridge onto the riverbank below and ruptured, and the fire that followed reached the people gathered at the shore. Some were caught on the bank. Others were in the water when the flames came. The narrow dirt road that had brought them in was the only way out.

## Twenty-Three Lives

The fire killed 23 people. Entire families died together, and so did the woman who was eight months pregnant. Rescue was hampered from the start: with only that single-lane dirt road leading to the site, emergency services found it very hard to reach the scene at all. The blaze also consumed part of the structure that had failed, destroying a further 110 feet of the bridge and damaging another 300 feet. Behind the number were households in which a single outing on a hot Sunday had taken parents and children at once, leaving absences that no investigation could repair. A bronze plaque at the entrance of Meldrim Memorial Park lists the names of all 23 victims.

## What the Investigators Found

The Interstate Commerce Commission examined the wreck and named the deflection of the rail beams as the proximate cause of the derailment. It went further, faulting the railroad for failing to install guard rails along the trestle - rails that might have helped keep the derailed equipment on the bridge deck instead of sending it onto the riverbank. Meldrim itself is a small unincorporated community in Effingham County, roughly 19 miles west-northwest of downtown Savannah, most likely named for the nineteenth-century Georgia politician, judge and army officer Peter Meldrim. Its best-known recent industry was a sawmill, which its owner, Interfor, now lists among its former facilities. It is also, and perhaps above all, remembered for what happened at the river in 1959.
