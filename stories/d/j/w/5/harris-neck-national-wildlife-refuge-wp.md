# Harris Neck National Wildlife Refuge

Egrets, herons and wood storks now nest on a Georgia peninsula where Black families built homes after emancipation, lost them to a wartime airfield in 1942, and were paid less per acre than their white neighbors.

**Location:** 31.6333, -81.2667

**Categories:** National Wildlife Refuges in Georgia (U.S. state), Protected areas of McIntosh County, Georgia, Wetlands of Georgia (U.S. state), Peninsulas of Georgia (U.S. state), African-American history of Georgia (U.S. state)

## Introduction

Two weeks. In mid-1942, by most accounts, that was all the time the families of Harris Neck were given to leave their homes after the Army Air Force decided to build a base on their coastal peninsula. Black families had settled here after 1865, when a plantation owner deeded the land to a formerly enslaved man, and they had built houses and oyster and crab processing factories. Today the same ground is Harris Neck National Wildlife Refuge, roughly 2,800 acres of saltwater marsh, grassland, mixed deciduous woods and cropland where thousands of egrets and herons nest every summer. The quiet here is real. So is the history beneath it.

## From Dickinson's Neck

Harris Neck is a coastal peninsula in McIntosh County, about 30 miles south-southwest of Savannah, with the small town of South Newport six miles to the west. It was not always called Harris Neck. The peninsula was originally named Dickinson's Neck, and it took its present name when William Thomas Harris became its principal landowner in the mid-18th century. The more consequential change came a century later. In 1865 a plantation owner deeded land here to a formerly enslaved man, and Black families settled on the peninsula in the years that followed. They built homes, and they built a working economy out of the surrounding waters, processing oysters and crabs in their own factories.

## Intermediate Field Site Number Eight

Aviation reached the peninsula early. Sometime between 1929 and 1932, a small airfield was built here and named Harris Neck Intermediate Field Site #8. It was an emergency landing ground for commercial planes flying the Richmond to Jacksonville air route, an irregular 93-acre parcel of sod with two sod runways, one of 2,600 feet running east to west and one of 2,550 feet running north to south, laid out in a criss-cross. The field was said to be lighted, though it offered no services. On January 1, 1942, it closed to the public as the Civil Air Patrol began flying anti-submarine patrols. It was evidently abandoned later that year, when a new military airfield was built half a mile to the north. There is no trace of the original field today.

## Two Weeks' Notice

The new base did not rise on empty land. When the Army Air Force moved in, the land was expropriated and families were given two weeks to remove themselves. The payments they received were not equal. According to a 1985 federal report, Black families, who owned 1,102 acres, were paid an average of $26.90 an acre. White families, who owned 1,532 acres, received an average of $37.31 an acre, more than ten dollars an acre more for the same peninsula. The taking also swept in the 225-acre Livingston estate, which included the Lorillard mansion and a deep-water dock. For the families who had built their lives around the oyster and crab houses, the war arrived not as a distant headline but as an eviction notice with a deadline of about two weeks. Former residents, their descendants and some white landowning families later organized as the Harris Neck Land Trust, which has asked Congress and the Fish and Wildlife Service to return the land or let them return to part of it; in its most recent public updates, covering 2019 to 2024, the Trust reported no resolution. In 2020 the Fish and Wildlife Service signed a memorandum of understanding with a separate group, the Direct Descendants of Harris Neck Community, to collaborate on projects and land use.

## Wings Over the Old Airfield

The refuge was established in 1962, created by the transfer of federal lands and the World War II Army airfield, which had been managed by the Federal Aviation Administration. Where military planes once flew, birds now hold the ground. In summer, thousands of egrets and herons nest in the swamps. In winter the marshland and freshwater pools fill with large concentrations of ducks, especially mallards, gadwall and green-winged teal. Harris Neck is also an important nesting area for the wood stork. More than 15 miles of paved roads and trails give visitors easy access to its different habitats, from open grassland to mixed woods and saltwater marsh. Chosen for its accessibility and the diversity of its birds, Harris Neck is one of 18 sites on the Colonial Coast Birding Trail, which was inaugurated in 2000.

## Flight Context

The refuge centers on about 31.6333 N, 81.2667 W, on a peninsula of marsh and woodland in McIntosh County roughly 30 miles south-southwest of Savannah. Look for the tidal creeks wrapping the neck and the patchwork of grassland, woods and marsh on the peninsula itself; the town of South Newport lies about six miles to the west. A viewing altitude of 2,000 feet or higher keeps the marsh in view while avoiding disturbance to the nesting colonies below. The nearest public airports with ICAO codes are MidCoast Regional Airport at Wright Army Airfield (KLHW) near Hinesville, about 22 nm northwest; Hunter Army Air Field (KSVN) in Savannah, about 23 nm north; and Brunswick Golden Isles Airport (KBQK), about 25 nm south-southwest. Savannah/Hilton Head International (KSAV) is about 30 nm north. Coastal haze and summer afternoon thunderstorms are common along this stretch of the Georgia coast.
