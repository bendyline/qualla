# Dorchester Academy

A lone 1930s dormitory outside Midway, Georgia, outlived the school built for freed African Americans and became headquarters for the civil rights movement's campaign to teach Black Southerners how to claim the vote.

**Location:** 31.8015, -81.4652

**Categories:** National Historic Landmarks in Georgia (U.S. state), Buildings and structures completed in 1935, African-American museums in Georgia (U.S. state), Museums in Liberty County, Georgia, Education museums in the United States, History museums in Georgia (U.S. state), National Register of Historic Places in Liberty County, Georgia, Schools supported by the American Missionary Association

## Introduction

When Dorchester Academy closed in 1940, nearly everything on its campus came down. The building left standing was a two-story Georgian Revival dormitory for boys, only a few years old. Twenty-one years later, that dormitory found a purpose its builders could never have planned: it became headquarters for a civil rights program that taught Black Southerners their rights and prepared them for the tests that stood between them and the ballot box.

## A School for the Newly Free

The American Missionary Association, a forerunner of today's United Church of Christ, founded Dorchester Academy in the aftermath of the Civil War, in 1869 by some accounts and 1871 by others. Its mission was to educate the freed African Americans living around Midway, Georgia. For roughly seven decades, the academy did exactly that. A photograph from around 1874 shows one of its early buildings, and an arch on the grounds still carries the mark of the class of 1927. In the 1930s, fire destroyed many of the academy's buildings, and the complex was rebuilt. The boys' dormitory, completed in 1934 or 1935 (sources differ) and designed by architect George Awsumb, was part of that recovery. It would also be the only part to last. When the school shut its doors in 1940, most of the remaining buildings were torn down.

## Lessons in Citizenship

In 1961 the Southern Christian Leadership Conference adapted the empty dormitory as the headquarters of its Citizenship Education Program. The program had just moved to the SCLC that year, although Martin Luther King Jr. was at first hesitant about taking it on. Its purpose was practical and radical at once. Becoming a registered voter meant passing a required test, so the program taught African Americans their rights and helped them acquire the knowledge they needed to pass it. From 1961 to 1970, Dorchester was the program's primary site. The goal reached well beyond any single exam. By turning students into voters, the program worked toward equality for Black citizens across the American South - one classroom, and one registration, at a time.

## The Mother of the Movement

The program was overseen by Septima Poinsette Clark, known as the "mother of the movement." Clark became the SCLC's director of education and teaching, and she was reportedly the first woman to hold a seat on its board. Under her guidance, the effort established hundreds of education centers across the American South. With the SCLC's larger budget behind it, the citizenship school project reportedly trained more than 10,000 teachers, who led citizenship schools throughout the South. By 1965, Clark estimated, the citizenship schools had reached more than 25,000 students and helped produce roughly twice that number of registered voters. Those figures describe the program as a whole, not Dorchester alone - but this quiet dormitory in Liberty County was where the effort was headquartered.

## Holding On to What Remains

In 2006, the boys' dormitory was designated a National Historic Landmark, honored for its role in the civil rights movement and its association with Clark. Recognition did not guarantee survival. In 2009, the National Trust for Historic Preservation placed Dorchester Academy on its list of America's Most Endangered Places. Today the campus serves as a museum and community center, open by appointment, with a research center in development. It sits at the corner of Lewis Fraser Road and East Oglethorpe Highway, U.S. Route 84, a short way west of Midway's city hall, wrapped around a church that stands directly at the corner. A small concrete-block cottage facing the highway houses the main museum. Behind it rises the dormitory, joined by a single-story annex, a barbecue pit and the site of a former swimming pool with its pool house.

## Flight Context

Dorchester Academy sits at 31.8015 N, 81.4652 W on U.S. Route 84, a short way west of Midway's city hall in Liberty County, Georgia. From 1,500-2,500 feet, follow U.S. 84 as it runs through Midway; the campus lies at the Lewis Fraser Road intersection, with the two-story dormitory behind a small cottage and a church at the corner. The nearest public airport is MidCoast Regional Airport at Wright Army Airfield (KLHW), about 7 nm northwest near Hinesville; the former Liberty County Airport (2J2) at Hinesville is listed as closed, so plan on KLHW instead.
