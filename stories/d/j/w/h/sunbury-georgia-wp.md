# Sunbury, Georgia

A colonial port on the Medway River that once rivaled Savannah, Sunbury was burned by the British, drained by storms and fever, and, by one account, finished off by Sherman's troops, leaving little more than a cemetery on its bluff.

**Location:** 31.7681, -81.2806

**Categories:** Ghost towns in Georgia (U.S. state), Province of Georgia, Former ports and harbours, American Revolutionary War sites, Former county seats in Georgia (U.S. state), Destroyed populated places

## Introduction

When the British ordered the garrison at Fort Morris to surrender, Colonel John McIntosh sent back a slogan instead: "Come and take it." The fort guarded Sunbury, a busy colonial port on a bluff above the Medway River in what is now Liberty County, Georgia, and a town so fervent in the American cause that it counted two signers of the Declaration of Independence among its residents and regulars. The British did eventually take it, in 1779, and they burned the town, in January 1779 by one account and as they left near the war's end by another. Sunbury never truly recovered. Established in 1758 and mostly abandoned by the middle of the next century, it is now a ghost town, with its cemetery among the only survivors of a place that once challenged Savannah itself.

## Squares on a Bluff

Among the first European settlers were the families of Benjamin and Samuel Baker, who arrived in 1752 from the Province of South Carolina at a bluff several miles upriver from St. Catherine's Sound. In 1757 King George II granted 500 acres here to Mark Carr, a wealthy military officer, and wharves and a trading post soon followed. On June 20, 1758, Carr and several neighbors agreed to found a town on his land. Carr oversaw much of the planning himself, down to the architectural style and the price of each lot. There were to be 496 lots, each 70 by 130 feet, arranged around three squares, King's Square, Meeting Square and Church Square, much like Savannah's distinctive plan, with buildings of wood and tabby concrete. The name most likely came from Carr's ancestral home of Sunbury-on-Thames in England. It was the only town in Saint John's Parish, which covered all of present-day Liberty County. The parish's one other settlement was Midway, founded around 1752 by rice-farming New England Puritans who had come by way of Dorchester, South Carolina.

## Rivaling Savannah

Sunbury rose quickly. Five merchant wharves lined the river, and around 1761 the town became Georgia's second port of entry, soon rivaling Savannah in economic importance. It had a shipyard, a works turning out shingles and staves, and a lumber trade reaching ports across the West Indies. In 1772 alone, 56 ships were recorded calling at the port. Visiting in 1773, the naturalist William Bartram found the town "beautifully situated on the main," with a "capacious and safe" harbor holding "water enough for ships of great burthen." Planters who owned plantations nearby kept houses in town, typically arriving in June and leaving in November. That prosperity rested on forced labor. Each fall, enslaved people requisitioned by the local government repaired the causeway to Colonels Island, a regional center of indigo production, and on at least one occasion Africans who had been carried across the ocean in bondage were sold at one of Sunbury's wharves.

## A Hotbed of Rebellion

During the Revolution, Sunbury was a center of pro-American sentiment in Georgia. Lyman Hall, a future signer of the Declaration of Independence and governor of Georgia, lived in the town. Button Gwinnett, another signer, conducted his business here as the parish's justice and owned a large plantation on nearby St. Catherine's Island. In June 1775, when British customs officers seized a ship in the harbor for carrying illegal cargo, townspeople gathered at the liberty pole and intimidated the officials into letting it go. Fort Morris, which grew out of earlier forts built to protect the settlers, became one of the last strongholds against the British. After McIntosh's defiant reply, both fort and town fell in 1779, and the British held the area for much of the rest of the war, renaming the fort Fort George. George Walton, a third signer, was held here as a prisoner of war before being paroled. At its Revolutionary-era peak, by one estimate, some 1,000 people lived in Sunbury, its businesses spread across 300 acres by the river. The British burned the town and the fort, in January 1779 by one account and, by another, as they evacuated near the end of the war.

## The Long Unraveling

Sunbury tried to rebuild. In 1784 it became the first county seat of Liberty County, and the Sunbury Academy, authorized in 1788 and opened in King's Square in 1793, became one of the best-known schools in southern Georgia for about the next forty years, with the Reverend Dr. William McWhir, a friend of George Washington, as principal for thirty of them. The town was incorporated on December 8, 1791. The next year brought the Sunbury Road, which ran all the way to Greensboro and was described as one of the longest vehicular routes laid out in Georgia after the Revolution. But the county seat moved to Riceboro in the late 1790s, a strong hurricane struck in 1804, and by the early 1800s much of the town's trade had gone to Savannah. During the War of 1812, British warships off the coast disrupted commerce, and a Navy plan to base patrol gunboats in Sunbury's deep harbor failed for lack of resources. A new Fort Defiance was begun on the site of Fort Morris in 1813 or 1814, but the blockade left the town poorer still.

## What the Bluff Remembers

An outbreak of yellow fever and further hurricanes deepened the decline. On December 8, 1841, the post office closed, and by 1848 Sunbury was more or less abandoned, though a few residents lingered. On December 13, 1864, during Sherman's March to the Sea, Union cavalry under Judson Kilpatrick seized the town, and a boy who lived there remembered the Sunbury Baptist Church being set on fire; by one account, whatever remained was destroyed. Today the Sunbury Cemetery is one of the only structures left from the town's long history, though newer homes have been built on some of the old town plots. In 1957 the Georgia Historical Commission placed a marker for "Sunbury and Fort Morris" about 11 miles to the west, in Midway, pointing travelers toward a port that has all but disappeared.

## Flight Context
