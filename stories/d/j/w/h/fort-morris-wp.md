# Fort Morris

On a bend of Georgia's Medway River, a succession of earthen forts guarded the town of Sunbury from 1741 to the Civil War, and in the Revolution its commander answered a British surrender demand with "Come and take it!"

**Location:** 31.7679, -81.2812

**Categories:** Forts in Georgia (U.S. state), State parks of Georgia (U.S. state), American Revolutionary War museums, National Register of Historic Places in Liberty County, Georgia, Military and war museums in Georgia (U.S. state)

## Introduction

"Come and take it!" During the American Revolution, when a British force demanded the surrender of Fort Morris, Colonel John McIntosh sent back that dare on behalf of the fewer than 200 Americans under his command. The British declined to attack. They pulled back, and they returned in January with a larger force. The earthworks where McIntosh made his stand still rise on a bend in the Medway River in Liberty County, Georgia, and they are only one chapter in a long story. For more than a century, from 1741 until the end of the Civil War in 1865, forts on or near this spot stood watch over the river and the southeast Georgia coast.

## A Planter's Fort

The first fort here went up in 1741, during the War of Jenkins' Ear, to protect a plantation owned by Captain Mark Carr. Carr held 500 acres in the area, granted to him by the Georgia Trustees, and he commanded a company of marine rangers. On March 18, 1741, his plantation came under attack by Native groups allied with the Spanish colonial forces in Florida. The site's value as a defensive position outlasted that first crisis. In 1756, as local settlers faced attacks during an uprising by the Creek in the era of the French and Indian War, a new fort was built at their encouragement. Two years later it was expanded to protect a new settlement, Sunbury, which was laid out on land that Carr owned.

## The Dare and the Reckoning

By the Revolution, the post was known as Fort Morris, and it guarded Sunbury and the river against the British. McIntosh's defiant reply bought time, but not safety. On January 9, 1779, the British attacked in greater strength, and after a short but heavy bombardment the fort surrendered and was theirs. The number of lives lost is not well documented. Historians suppose that fewer than twelve American soldiers died in the siege, and fewer British. The victors renamed the post Fort George. Whatever its name, it remained what it had always been: earthen walls on a river bend, guarding the approach to Sunbury.

## A Fort That Kept Changing Hands

The British occupied Fort George for most of that year, until September 1779, when its garrison was ordered to Savannah to help defend that city. Colonial forces moved back in and found an empty fort, with only a few damaged guns left behind. It was a hollow prize, and their hold on it lasted barely a month. In October the British retook the post, and Fort Morris, or Fort George, stayed under British control until 1782. In about ten months the fort had been besieged, surrendered, renamed, abandoned, reoccupied and captured again.

## Defiance Left Unfinished

After the Revolution the fort fell into disrepair again, until the War of 1812 renewed the need to defend Sunbury and the river. In 1814 a new work, Fort Defiance, was begun on the site of the old Fort Morris. The war ended before it was finished, and it was left incomplete. The Civil War brought only a minor role. A small group of Confederate soldiers was stationed at Sunbury and may have used the fort, and when General William T. Sherman's March to the Sea brought Union troops into the area in 1864, they carried off some of its cannons to Union-held forts on the Atlantic coast. On May 13, 1970, Fort Morris was added to the National Register of Historic Places. Today it is Fort Morris State Historic Site and Museum, where visitors can walk the earthworks, see a museum and film on the colonial port of Sunbury, and sometimes catch re-enactments. One of the fort's cannons is displayed at the Liberty County Courthouse.

## Flight Context

Fort Morris sits at about 31.7679 N, 81.2812 W on a bend of the Medway River in Liberty County, where the river winds through tidal marsh toward the coast. From 1,500 to 2,000 feet, follow the Medway's bends and look for the small historic site on its bank; downtown Savannah lies roughly 20 nm to the north-northeast. The nearest airports with ICAO codes are MidCoast Regional Airport at Wright Army Airfield (KLHW) near Hinesville, about 16 nm west-northwest, and Hunter Army Air Field (KSVN), a military field in Savannah, about 16 nm north-northeast; check the military airspace around both before planning the route. Savannah/Hilton Head International (KSAV) is about 22 nm north. Coastal haze and summer afternoon thunderstorms are common over the marshes.
