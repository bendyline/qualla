# St. Simons Island Light

Blown up by Confederates in 1861 or 1862 and rebuilt in brick about a decade later, the St. Simons Island Light still sweeps Georgia's St. Simons Sound with one of the few third-order Fresnel lenses left in service.

**Location:** 31.1331, -81.3917

**Categories:** Lighthouses in Georgia (U.S. state), Lighthouse museums in the United States, Lighthouses completed in 1872, St. Simons, Georgia, National Register of Historic Places in Glynn County, Georgia

## Introduction

Count slowly to sixty on a dark night at the southern tip of St. Simons Island, and the sky over the sound answers you. The rotating lens atop the St. Simons Island Light throws four beams across the water, with one strong flash every minute, guiding ships into St. Simons Sound and warning them off the sandbars that crowd its approaches. The glass doing that work is a third-order biconvex Fresnel lens, one of fewer than 80 such lenses still operating in the United States, by one count. Fifteen of them shine in the South, and just two in Georgia. The brick tower that carries it was completed in 1872, and it is not the first lighthouse to guard this shore. Its predecessor was destroyed on purpose.

## A Light Put Out on Purpose

The first St. Simons light went up in 1810, a 75-foot octagonal tower in the early federal style, crowned by a 10-foot oil-burning lamp. For half a century it did its quiet work. Then came the Civil War. U.S. naval forces clamped a blockade on the coast, and Confederate soldiers destroyed the lighthouse so that it could not help U.S. Navy warships find their way in. The Coastal Georgia Historical Society dates the destruction to September 29, 1861, while other accounts say 1862, as an invasion by U.S. Army troops forced the Confederates to abandon the area. The roster of head keepers records the loss in its silence. James Gould took charge around 1810 and served until 1837. Lachlan McIntosh and then Alexander D. McIntosh followed him, and John F. Carmon's tenure ended in 1861. After that the list goes blank for eleven years, and when the next head keeper, Bradford B. Brunt, arrived in 1872, he was tending an entirely new tower.

## Brick, Glass and 129 Steps

The U.S. government rebuilt a little to the west of the original site. The new lighthouse rose 104 feet in brick and was fitted with the Fresnel lens that still sweeps the sound, and at its base stood a two-story Victorian cottage for the lightkeepers. The keepers came and went across the decades: Frederick Osborne, George W. Ashbell, Isaac L. Peckham, Joseph Champagne. Carl O. Svendsen held the post longest of all, from 1907 to 1935, twenty-eight years of climbing the same stairs to the same light. On July 1, 1939, the United States Lighthouse Service was placed under the jurisdiction of the U.S. Coast Guard. Arthur F. Hodge kept the light from the mid-1930s to 1945, and David O'Hagan's term, ending in 1953, closes the list of head keepers. Anyone willing to pay a fee can now follow in their footsteps. The climb is 129 steps, and at the top the view opens across St. Simons Sound and the country around it.

## Bubble Wrap for a Crown Jewel

In 2010 the tower closed to the public for several months for a significant renovation. Crews sandblasted away every layer of paint, inside and out, and then repainted the whole structure. All of the ironwork was blasted and repaired where needed, and eight iron handrail posts at the top were replaced with new ones recast from one of the originals. The most careful work went into protecting the Fresnel lens. It was bubble-wrapped, then shrink-wrapped, and finally enclosed in a plywood box, a precious antique packed like fragile freight while the dust flew around it. The ships, meanwhile, still needed guiding into the sound. A temporary spotlight fixed to the tower's gallery kept watch over the water until the main light could return to service.

## New Keepers, Same Watch

The light's later caretakers were historians rather than lamplighters. In the early 1970s the keepers' cottage passed to Glynn County, and the Coastal Georgia Historical Society spent three years restoring the Victorian building before opening it as a museum. In 1984 the society leased the lighthouse itself, and in 2004 it took ownership under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act. It now runs the St. Simons Lighthouse Museum in the 1872 keeper's house, with its headquarters next door in the A. W. Jones Heritage Center, which holds exhibits, the society's archives, a research library, an event hall and a museum shop. The society also operates the World War II Home Front Museum elsewhere on the island. The tower, meanwhile, does double duty. Together with the northernmost water tower on Jekyll Island, it marks the line that separates St. Simons Sound from the Atlantic Ocean. It has also become a beloved symbol of St. Simons Island and of Glynn County, painted and sketched again and again, a working aid to navigation that people simply like to look at.

## Flight Context

The lighthouse stands at 31.1331 N, 81.3917 W on the southern tip of St. Simons Island, where St. Simons Sound opens to the Atlantic. The tall brick tower and the keeper's cottage beside it sit near the shoreline; Jekyll Island lies directly across the sound to the south. A low pass along the sound at about 1,500 feet gives a good view of the tower and the channel it guards. St Simons Island Airport (KSSI) is only about 1 nm north of the lighthouse, so plan the sightseeing leg with that field's traffic in mind. Jekyll Island Airport (FAA identifier 09J, no ICAO code) is about 4 nm south-southwest, and Brunswick Golden Isles Airport (KBQK) is about 8.5 nm north-northwest on the mainland. Watch for haze over the marshes and for afternoon thunderstorms in the warm months, which can quickly cut visibility along the coast.
