# Naval Air Station Glynco

North of Brunswick, Georgia, a Navy base built for World War II blimps went on to train the officers in the back seats of Phantoms and Tomcats before becoming the Golden Isles' airport and a federal law enforcement academy.

**Location:** 31.2588, -81.4665

**Categories:** United States Navy air stations, Airships, Former installations of the United States Navy, Airports in Georgia (U.S. state), Buildings and structures in Glynn County, Georgia, Military aviation history

## Introduction

Each hangar was more than a fifth of a mile long. When the United States Navy came to the northern part of Glynn County, Georgia, in August 1942, workers spent fourteen months raising two enormous wooden hangars, each 1,058 feet long, 297 feet wide and 182 feet tall. They were built for airships. Naval Air Station Glynco, its name a contraction of Glynn County, was designed as an operational base for blimps, able to house a fleet of six to eight airships, by differing accounts, and to maintain airships from other bases. The hangars are gone now, and the Navy left in 1974. But the base's single long runway still carries travelers to and from the Golden Isles, and its old blimp grounds have found a second purpose.

## Blimps Over the Convoys

The station spread across 2,400 acres. Its principal wartime unit was Airship Patrol Squadron 15, or ZP-15, part of Fleet Airship Wing ONE headquartered at NAS Lakehurst in New Jersey. The blimps' great advantage was endurance. Capable of sustained flight and carrying state-of-the-art electronic equipment for detecting submarines, they flew thousands of hours on coastal and maritime patrols and escorted convoys, shielding vulnerable ships that carried essential war materials. That convoy escort program has often been cited as one of the most successful defense operations of the war. A runway for fixed-wing aircraft was added as well, and units at Glynco contributed to significant advances in communications and electronics equipment and in new search and rescue procedures.

## A Reprieve, Then a Slow Goodbye

Peace nearly ended Glynco's story early. The base was slated for decommissioning in 1949, until tensions in Korea persuaded the Navy to reconsider the value of airships for anti-submarine warfare. Glynco stayed active, and a full complement of blimps returned, mainly with Airship Patrol Squadrons 2 and 3. In 1953 or 1954 the Airship Training Unit moved in from Lakehurst to teach airship flying to Naval Aviators who had already earned their wings in airplanes and helicopters. The revival did not last. The Navy gradually dismantled its airship program, disbanding both squadrons and the training unit in 1959. The two giant hangars, unique landmarks by any measure, eventually succumbed to time and climate and were demolished in 1971.

## The Officers Beside the Pilots

As the airships faded, fixed-wing aircraft took their place, and the runway was lengthened to slightly over 8,000 feet. In the 1960s the Navy expanded the role of the officers who flew alongside the pilots, the Naval Aviation Observers later renamed Naval Flight Officers, and Glynco took on their advanced training. Training Air Wing EIGHT was established here, and under it Training Squadron 86 prepared Navy and Marine Corps officers for crew positions in some of the era's best-known aircraft: Radar Intercept Officers for the F-4 Phantom II and F-14 Tomcat, Bombardier Navigators for the A-6 Intruder, and specialists for the E-2 Hawkeye, the S-3 Viking and the EA-6B Prowler. The squadron flew 24 T-39 Sabreliners, 20 A-4C Skyhawks, two EC-121K Warning Stars and 12 TS-2A Trackers. From March 1962 Glynco was also home to the Navy's enlisted air traffic control schools, moved from Olathe, Kansas, which trained Navy air controllers and their Marine Corps counterparts. The Navy even studied adding three more runways to make Glynco a Master Jet Base, an expansion it never carried out.

## The Town Takes the Controls

Glynco was marked for closure by the end of 1974, one of a wave of post-Vietnam budget closures. Training Squadron 86 moved to Pensacola, Florida, and the air traffic control schools left as well. After more than 31 years, the base had become part of Glynn County's economy and identity, and the closure took the community by surprise. Nineteen local leaders formed the Glynco Steering Committee to attract civilian users. Their work paid off in 1975, when the Federal Law Enforcement Training Center chose the former blimp site for a consolidated academy for federal law enforcement personnel, and the airfield was designated the official county municipal airport the same year. Both proved invaluable to Brunswick and the Golden Isles, and the 8,000-foot runway has remained an important community asset.

## From Jetport to Golden Isles

Airline service reached back to the Navy years. In 1973 Delta Air Lines flew McDonnell Douglas DC-9-30 jets through NAS Glynco, with direct flights to Atlanta and Orlando and nonstops to Augusta, Jacksonville and Macon. After the Navy left, the field became Glynco Jetport, managed from 1980 by the Glynn County Airport Commission, and in 2003 it was renamed Brunswick Golden Isles Airport. A new passenger terminal opened in 2005 on the site of the old Navy control tower and base operations building. The FAA never replaced that tower, and the airport has no active control tower today. In 2026, Delta Connection flights operated by Endeavor Air link it nonstop to Atlanta; SkyWest has also flown Delta Connection service there.

## Flight Context

The former NAS Glynco is today's Brunswick Golden Isles Airport (KBQK), at 31.2588 N, 81.4665 W, about 5 miles north of Brunswick, Georgia. Its single runway of roughly 8,000 feet stands out clearly on the flat coastal plain, and the Federal Law Enforcement Training Center occupies the old blimp grounds beside the field. The airport has no operating control tower, so use standard non-towered procedures. McKinnon St. Simons Island Airport (KSSI) is about 7.5 nm to the southeast and Jekyll Island Airport (FAA identifier 09J, no ICAO code) about 11 nm south. A pattern-altitude pass shows the runway and the base layout; climb to 2,500 to 3,500 feet to take in the barrier islands of the Golden Isles to the east. Expect summer thunderstorms and coastal haze, and occasional morning fog near the coast.
