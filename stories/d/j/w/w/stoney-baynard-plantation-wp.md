# Stoney-Baynard Plantation

On the south end of Hilton Head Island, the tabby ruins of a plantation house built around 1793 that may have changed hands over a lost poker bet stand among the traces of an overseer's house, a slave house and a Union troop camp.

**Location:** 32.1289, -80.8147

**Categories:** Archaeological sites on the National Register of Historic Places in South Carolina, Georgian architecture in South Carolina, Houses in Beaufort County, South Carolina, National Register of Historic Places in Beaufort County, South Carolina, Plantations in South Carolina, Plantation houses in South Carolina

## Introduction

Nobody knows for certain how the house changed hands. One version says a Stoney heir wagered the home in a late-night poker game and lost it. The other is duller and perhaps more likely: the Stoneys went bankrupt, and William Baynard bought the estate from the bank. Either way, by the 1840s (accounts give dates from 1837 to 1845) the plantation house above Calibogue Sound belonged to the Baynards, and within a generation of that transfer it would be occupied by Union soldiers and then burned to the ground. What remains on Hilton Head Island today are walls made of oyster shell, standing in the preserved green space of Sea Pines.

## Walls of Oyster Shell and Lime

Captain Jack Stoney, a Revolutionary War Patriot and privateer, built the house around 1793 as part of Braddock's Point Plantation, though some accounts say it was not finished until about 1805. It covered 1,885 square feet and was made of timber combined with a mixture of oyster shells, lime and sand, the material known as tabby, which formed its foundations and exterior walls. The builders worked in the Georgian architectural style, and the house was placed to overlook Calibogue Sound. Because the Stoneys were the first to live here, the site carries both family names, and it is often called simply the Stoney-Baynard ruins. Tabby is a coastal recipe built from shell, and it has proved durable: more than two centuries after Stoney raised his house, parts of those shell-studded walls still stand.

## A Bet or a Bank

However the Baynards acquired it, William Baynard was a very successful cotton planter, and he lived in the house from 1840 to 1849. The ruins make clear who else lived here. Alongside the main house and the footing of an overseer's house, the site includes the remains of a slave house, evidence of the enslaved people who lived and labored on this plantation. Their names do not appear in the site's brief written history, but their presence is built into the ground as surely as the planter's. An overseer's house and a slave house standing a short walk from the owner's Georgian home describe the order of the place more plainly than any ledger could: one family's fortune in cotton, and the people forced to produce it.

## Soldiers, Then Fire

William Baynard died, and the Civil War reached the island. Union forces raided the plantation, and the property housed many soldiers during the war. They left their own mark in the archaeological record: among the ruins are footings for a tent used by Union troops, a fourth structure alongside the house, the overseer's quarters and the slave house. The house did not outlast the conflict by long. Soon after the war ended, in 1867 or 1869 by differing accounts, it was burned to the ground. What had been a planter's residence overlooking the sound became a shell of tabby, its timber gone and its walls open to the weather, a ruin some seventy-five years after Jack Stoney built it.

## What the Ground Keeps

The Stoney-Baynard Plantation was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on February 23, 1994. The listing covers one contributing site on 6 acres, and much of its value lies below the surface: archaeological testing has documented intact sub-surface features and clear horizontal patterning of artifacts, the kind of intact record that lets researchers read how the plantation was laid out and lived in. Above ground, the standing ruins include portions of the main house and the chimney footing of what may have been the overseer's house. The land is now part of the green space owned by the Sea Pines Community Services Administration Association, which keeps it preserved. Set within the private Sea Pines community, the ruins remain a stretch of the island's plantation past that was never paved over.

## Flight Context

The Stoney-Baynard ruins lie at about 32.1289 N, 80.8147 W near the south end of Hilton Head Island, inside the Sea Pines community and close to the eastern shore of Calibogue Sound. The ruins themselves are small and hard to spot, so use nearby landmarks: the red-and-white striped Harbour Town Lighthouse at Harbour Town Marina stands a little over half a nautical mile to the north. A viewing altitude of 1,500 to 2,000 feet shows the sound, the marina and the south end of the island together. Hilton Head Airport (KHXD) is about 8 nm northeast, Beaufort Executive Airport (KARW) about 19 nm north-northeast, and Savannah/Hilton Head International Airport (KSAV) about 20 nm west. Expect summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms along the coast.
