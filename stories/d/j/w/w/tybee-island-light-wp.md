# Tybee Island Light

Four towers have stood at the mouth of the Savannah River since 1736; the one on Tybee Island today rests on the lower 60 feet of a colonial brick tower that Confederates set on fire, has survived a hurricane and an earthquake, and still shines as a working aid to navigation.

## Introduction

Four towers have stood on the north end of Tybee Island, and two of them never showed a light at all. The station watches the point where the Savannah River meets the Atlantic Ocean, and its story reaches back to 1736. The tower standing today is the fourth. It has been burned, rebuilt, battered by a hurricane and cracked by an earthquake, yet its base is colonial brickwork from 1773, which ties the Tybee Light to the colonial era even though the mid-nineteenth century remade much of it.

## Towers Without a Flame

The first tower went up in 1736 at the direction of James Oglethorpe. It was built of wood, and in 1741 a storm knocked it down. The next year a replacement rose, this time of stone and wood, but it still carried no light. Instead of a lamp, it was topped with a flagpole. That second tower did not fall to weather. It fell to the shoreline itself, as erosion ate away the ground beneath it. Two towers, then, and not a single lamp between them. For its first decades, the station at the river's mouth was a landmark to be seen rather than a light to be followed.

## Candles, Oil and Glass

The third tower changed that. John Mullryne built it in 1773 of brick, originally 100 feet tall, and it was lit at first with reflectors and candles. After the light was ceded to the federal government in 1790, the candles gave way to oil lamps. In 1822 a second, lower tower was added in front of the main one, creating a range light, a pair of lights that pilots could line up to hold their course. Better optics followed in 1857, when both towers received Fresnel lenses: a 4th order lens for the lower front tower and a larger 2nd order lens for the main tower. By the eve of the Civil War, the colonial brick tower had become a modern light station, with a partner light and glass optics to match.

## Burned, Rebuilt, Shaken

In 1861, Confederate forces took out the lens and set fire to the light, burning its wooden stairs and landings, as they withdrew to Fort Pulaski. Reconstruction began in 1866. The builders did not start from scratch. They raised a new tower on top of the first 60 feet of the old one, so the colonial brick became the foundation of the new light, and in 1867 it was lit again, now with a 1st order lens. Nature tested the work almost at once. A hurricane cracked the main tower in 1871, and in 1886 the Charleston earthquake displaced the lens and extended the cracks, though not to a dangerous degree. The lens damage was repaired promptly. The twentieth century brought quieter changes, with electricity in 1933 and automation in 1972.

## A Tower That Keeps Changing Its Coat

Over the years the tower has worn a surprising number of outfits. It began all white. In 1887 the base and lantern were painted black, and repaintings in 1914 and 1916 each brought the black at the top further down the shaft. In 1967 the whole tower was redone with a white base and a gray top, but the gray faded badly and was painted black in 1970. The modern chapter began in the late 1990s, when a major restoration got under way, and in 2002 the Tybee Island Historical Society took possession of the light station under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act. As part of that work, the tower returned to the black-white-black daymark it wore from about 1916 to the mid-1960s. Another round of masonry and lantern-room repairs, about $1.9 million, ran from November 2023 to August 2024. It remains a functioning aid to navigation, still using a first-order Fresnel lens, with a range of about 16 nautical miles. The site is open to the public, and the keepers' houses and auxiliary buildings still stand beside the tower.

## Flight Context

Tybee Island Light stands at about 32.0222 N, 80.8456 W on the north end of Tybee Island, overlooking the mouth of the Savannah River where it opens into the Atlantic Ocean. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet, look for the black-white-black tower with its keepers' houses and outbuildings beside it. About 2.5 nm to the west lie Fort Pulaski and the small Cockspur Island Light, and downtown Savannah is roughly 13 nm upriver to the west-northwest. Hilton Head Airport (KHXD) is about 14 nm north-northeast, Hunter Army Air Field (KSVN), a military field, about 15 nm west, and Savannah/Hilton Head International Airport (KSAV) about 19 nm west-northwest. Coastal haze, sea fog and summer thunderstorms can reduce visibility.
