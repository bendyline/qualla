# 1958 Tybee Island mid-air collision

After an F-86 struck their B-47 during a 1958 night exercise, an Air Force crew jettisoned a 7,600-pound Mark 15 nuclear bomb off Tybee Island, and it has never been found.

**Location:** 32.0000, -80.8500

**Categories:** Military nuclear accidents and incidents, Mid-air collisions involving military aircraft, Aviation accidents and incidents in Georgia (U.S. state), 1958 in military history, Chatham County, Georgia

## Introduction

Nobody knows exactly where the bomb is. The government's best estimate, based on a 2001 hydrographic survey, is that the 7,600-pound Mark 15 lies buried under 5 to 15 feet of silt at the bottom of Wassaw Sound, off the shores of Tybee Island near Savannah, Georgia. It has been down there since the early hours of February 5, 1958, when an Air Force bomber crew, their aircraft crippled by a mid-air collision during a night practice exercise, let it fall into the sea to make an emergency landing possible. Every search since has come up empty, and the official records still disagree about whether the weapon carried its nuclear capsule.

## Collision in the Dark

The B-47 was a six-engine bomber flying a simulated combat mission out of Homestead Air Force Base in Florida, and it carried a single 7,600-pound bomb. At about 2:00 a.m. Eastern Standard Time, an F-86 fighter collided with it. The fighter went down. Its pilot, Lieutenant Clarence Stewart, ejected and parachuted to safety near Estill, South Carolina, about 10 miles north of the spot east of Sylvania, Georgia, where his F-86 came down. The bomber stayed in the air, but only barely. Damaged in the collision, it plunged from 38,000 feet, and its pilot, Colonel Howard Richardson, did not regain control until the aircraft had fallen to 20,000 feet. He now had a wounded airplane, a crew to bring home and a very large weapon in the bomb bay.

## Letting It Fall

The crew asked for permission to jettison the bomb. Dropping it would reduce the bomber's weight and keep the weapon from exploding if the emergency landing went wrong. Permission came, and the bomb was released at 7,200 feet while the plane was traveling at about 200 knots. The crew saw no explosion when it struck the water. They then brought the B-47 down safely at nearby Hunter Air Force Base, just south of Savannah, and Richardson was awarded the Distinguished Flying Cross for landing the damaged bomber. The base still operates today under a different name: in 1967 it was transferred to the Army and renamed Hunter Army Air Field.

## Was It Armed?

The Air Force maintains that the bomb's nuclear capsule, the physics package used to initiate the nuclear reaction, was removed before the flight. An Atomic Energy Commission form, the "Form AL-569 Temporary Custodian Receipt (for maneuvers)," signed by the aircraft commander, records that the bomb carried a simulated 150-pound cap made of lead. Yet in 1966 congressional testimony, Assistant Secretary of Defense W. J. Howard described the Tybee Island bomb as a "complete weapon, a bomb with a nuclear capsule," and one of two lost weapons that contained a plutonium trigger. A study of Strategic Air Command documents points the other way again: Alert Force test flights in February 1958 carrying the older Mark 15 were not authorized to fly with nuclear capsules on board. That approval came only in June 1958, when safer weapons with sealed-pit nuclear capsules began to be deployed.

## The Search and the Silt

The search began on February 6, 1958. The Air Force's 2700th Explosive Ordnance Disposal Squadron and 100 Navy personnel swept the sound with hand-held sonar and with galvanic drag and cable sweeps. On April 16, the military announced that the search had been unsuccessful, and the bomb was declared lost. What about contamination? As of 2007, the Georgia Department of Natural Resources had detected no undue levels of unnatural radioactive contamination in the region's Upper Floridan aquifer, beyond the already high natural levels thought to come from the mineral monazite. The lost bomb has also taken on a life of its own. In February 2015, a fake news website claimed it had been found and removed by vacationing Canadian divers, and the invented story spread widely on social media.

## Flight Context

The story is placed at about 32.00 N, 80.85 W on Tybee Island; the bomb is believed to lie in Wassaw Sound, the inlet just south and southwest of Tybee, off its shores. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet, Tybee's beaches, the mouth of the Savannah River to the north and the marshy islands framing Wassaw Sound to the south make clear landmarks, though there is nothing of the bomb to see. Hunter Army Air Field (KSVN), the former Hunter Air Force Base where the damaged B-47 landed, is about 15 nm west; it is a military field. Hilton Head Airport (KHXD) is about 15.5 nm north-northeast, and Savannah/Hilton Head International (KSAV) is about 19.5 nm west-northwest, so coordinate with Savannah approach control. Sea fog and haze can settle over the sound, especially on cool mornings.
