# Butler Island Plantation

On a rice island in the Altamaha delta, Fanny Kemble recorded the cruelty of slavery, and two decades later her former husband put 436 people on the auction block in the sale remembered as the Weeping Time.

**Location:** 31.3547, -81.4465

**Categories:** Plantation houses in Georgia (U.S. state), Rice plantations in the United States, History of slavery in Georgia (U.S. state), Protected areas of McIntosh County, Georgia, Buildings and structures in McIntosh County, Georgia

## Introduction

Before Fanny Kemble ever reached the Altamaha River, she had been given two assurances about the people her husband's family held in bondage on Butler Island: they were well treated, and they were never sold. She realized very quickly that this was not true. Twenty years later her former husband would prove it beyond any argument, putting 436 enslaved men, women, children and infants up for auction in what was long considered the largest slave sale in United States history. Butler Island, a former rice plantation on the Altamaha delta just south of Darien, Georgia, is quiet today. The old rice-field ditches are still visible, and the remains of a steam-operated rice mill still stand. The story behind them is one of the most documented, and most painful, in the history of the Georgia coast.

## A Founding Father's Rice Island

Major Pierce Butler, one of the Founding Fathers of the United States and a supporter of slavery, owned the property in 1790. He ran the plantation until he died in 1822. His unmarried daughter Frances then became a trustee, with the overseer, Roswell King, serving as her co-administrator. When Frances died in 1836, controlling ownership passed to the two heirs Major Butler had chosen: grandsons of his eldest daughter, who had made their permanent homes far away in Philadelphia. The people who actually lived on Butler Island and worked its rice fields appear in this chain of inheritance only as property, passed from one owner to the next. In 1838 the King overseers resigned, and one of the grandsons, Pierce Mease Butler, traveled south to the plantation.

## What Fanny Kemble Wrote Down

Pierce Mease Butler arrived in December 1838 with his wife and their two daughters. His wife was Frances Anne Kemble, known as Fanny, and she was opposed to slavery. She wanted to see and learn how the enslaved people lived. Her husband hoped the visit would change her views. It did not. During her time on the plantation she kept a diary, recording the horrific treatment and living conditions she witnessed among the people enslaved there. The visit also marked the beginning of a deep rift between husband and wife. Kemble eventually published her writing as Journal of a Residence on a Georgian Plantation in 1838–1839, a book that some believe helped turn British opinion against supporting the Confederacy during the Civil War. The marriage did not survive. On September 22, 1849, a Pennsylvania court granted Butler's petition for divorce and gave him custody of their daughters.

## The Weeping Time

In the years before the Civil War, Pierce Mease Butler sank deep into debt. In 1859 he put 436 enslaved people up for auction at what had been the Ten Broeck Race Course, just outside Savannah. Some of them were held in buildings meant for stabling horses. Buyers spent days inspecting them. Then came two days of agonizing sales, in which men, women, children and infants were sold off and families who had lived their whole lives together were separated for the first time. The sale came to be known as the Weeping Time. Until the 2022 discovery of an 1835 Charleston auction of about 600 people, it was considered the largest slave sale in the history of the United States, though some accounts say only 429 of those advertised in Savannah were actually sold. Behind that grim record stand hundreds of individual people, each torn from someone they loved.

## Two Daughters, Two Diaries

When the Civil War began, the plantation was abandoned. In 1866 Butler returned with his daughter Frances to try to restore its former productivity. The Kemble daughters had chosen opposite sides: Sarah aligned with her mother, while Frances had adopted her father's pro-slavery views. Like her mother, Frances kept a diary, and in 1883 she published it as Ten Years on a Georgian Plantation since the War. An account of the island written in November 1873 paints a picture of an estate of about 1,600 acres ringed by the muddy Altamaha, with a modest frame house, and a grove of orange trees so laden with fruit that the writer expected to fill more than 100 barrels for market. Behind the house lay Settlement No. 1, where, in the writer's words, the "coloured people" lived in rows of wooden houses along an avenue of orange trees. Half of the old hospital had become a church. New barns and a threshing mill were nearly finished, but the old mill had burned the year before. Without enslaved labor, and amid the postwar depression across the South, the plantations failed. The fifth generation of Butlers sold the last of the family's lands in 1923.

## Dairy, Lettuce and Fire

Tillinghast L'Hommedieu Huston bought the plantation in 1926 and turned it into a dairy and lettuce farm. In 1927 he completed the Huston House, which became the main structure on the property. After his death the land was sold to R. J. Reynolds Jr. Today the Georgia Department of Natural Resources owns and manages Butler Island as part of its Altamaha Wildlife Management Area, where visitors can watch wildlife from a nature trail and observation towers on the managed impoundments, subject to the agency's wildlife-area rules. The Huston House is gone, destroyed by fire on the night of June 26, 2024, after local residents had hoped to turn it into a museum or memorial honoring the people enslaved here. What remains are older traces: the brick cistern and hearth of a boiler, the ruins of the steam-operated rice mill, historical markers, and the ditches that still cut across the old rice fields.

## Flight Context

Butler Island lies at about 31.3547 N, 81.4465 W on the Altamaha River delta, immediately south of the town of Darien. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet the old rice-field grid and its drainage ditches stand out against the surrounding marsh, and the braided channels of the Altamaha make an unmistakable landmark running east to the sea. Brunswick Golden Isles Airport (KBQK) is about 6 nm south, and St Simons Island Airport (KSSI) is about 12.5 nm south. Low-level flight over the delta should respect the wildlife that uses these marshes; keep well above the wetlands and avoid circling at low altitude. Coastal haze and summer afternoon thunderstorms can reduce visibility over the river mouth.
