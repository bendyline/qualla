# Cathedral Basilica of St. John the Baptist (Savannah, Georgia)

In a city whose colonial charter once barred Catholics, refugees from Haiti planted a congregation that grew into Savannah's soaring, twice-built Gothic cathedral on Lafayette Square.

## Introduction

When Savannah was young, Catholics were not allowed to settle in it. The colony's English trustees worried that Catholics would feel more loyalty to the Spanish authorities in Florida than to Georgia's English government, so the city's charter shut them out. Today the spires of the Cathedral Basilica of St. John the Baptist rise over Lafayette Square as the mother church of the Roman Catholic Diocese of Savannah - an unlikely outcome for a faith once kept out of the city.

## Refugees Build a Parish

The prohibition faded shortly after the American Revolution, and the congregation reorganized around 1796. Its real foundation came from people fleeing upheaval. French Catholic émigrés who had fled Haiti after the slave rebellions that began on the Caribbean island in 1791 established the first church, around 1799. The congregation's first building stood on Liberty Square, and in the early 19th century it became the main church for free Black Haitians who had made new lives in Savannah. From the start, then, this was a parish shaped by exile and by the aftermath of a revolution in which enslaved people had risen against their bondage. In 1811, the growing congregation chose a new site at Drayton and Perry Streets for a larger building.

## A Diocese and a Gothic Dream

Bishop John England of the Diocese of Charleston, whose territory then included Savannah, consecrated the church at Drayton and Perry on April 1, 1839. In July 1850, Pope Pius IX created the Diocese of Savannah. Two decades later, under Savannah's fourth bishop, the Right Reverend Ignatius Persico, a new cathedral was planned in 1870. Construction began in 1873 on a design attributed to architect Ephraim Francis Baldwin. On April 30, 1876, the Most Reverend James Roosevelt Bayley, Archbishop of Baltimore, presided over the dedication of the Neo-Gothic sanctuary. It was not quite finished. The brick building had no spires until 1896, when they were finally added and the building received a coating of stucco and whitewash.

## Only the Walls and Spires

Within a couple of years of the spires going up, disaster struck. On February 6, 1898, fire swept through the cathedral, leaving only its walls and those new spires standing. The wind direction spared the neighboring Cathedral Rectory and St. Vincent's Academy. The congregation moved quickly, and by 1899 it was celebrating Christmas Mass inside the rebuilt cathedral. Finishing the work took far longer. The renovation continued for another 13 years, and the stained glass windows were installed by 1905. Later generations kept up the labor of care. Renovations in 1959-1963 brought new heating, cooling and lighting systems along with fresh decoration. In 1984-1985 the parish reinforced the structural foundations and made changes suggested by the Second Vatican Council, and from 1998 to 2000 it installed new roofing and restored the original interior colors and decorations. The most recent project began in fall 2024, when the steel supports inside the spires were found to be corroding rapidly; crews replaced more than 500 tons of steel rods at a cost of $2.5 million, and in November 2025 the spires were unveiled and the bells tolled again.

## By the Numbers

The cathedral rewards anyone who likes counting. By one count it holds 81 stained glass windows and 16 gargoyles. It stands roughly 214 feet tall, with interior ceilings reaching 96 feet, and building it reportedly took more than 90,000 copper nails and 45,000 slates. Inside, an organ loft overlooks the rear of the nave, and there is a Blessed Virgin Mary Chapel and a decorated window in the south transept. In 2020, Pope Francis raised the church to the rank of minor basilica, giving it the title it carries today. Its address, 222 East Harris Street, sits beside Lafayette Square in the heart of a city that once refused Catholics a place to live - and now looks up at their cathedral as one of its landmarks.

## Flight Context

The Cathedral Basilica stands at 32.0733 N, 81.0914 W beside Lafayette Square in Savannah's historic district, Georgia. At roughly 214 feet, its spired silhouette is a good landmark to pick out over the downtown grid of squares from 1,500-2,500 feet; the Savannah River runs along the north edge of downtown. Hunter Army Airfield (KSVN), a military field, lies about 4.7 nm southwest, and Savannah/Hilton Head International Airport (KSAV) about 6.4 nm west-northwest - expect controlled airspace over the city. Hilton Head Airport (KHXD) is about 22 nm east-northeast. The terrain is low and coastal, so haze is the main visibility concern.
