# Siege of Savannah (1779)

In the fall of 1779, French and American forces tried to wrest Savannah back from the British, shelling the town for days before a fog-bound dawn assault on October 9 ended in one of the bloodiest defeats of the Revolutionary War.

**Location:** 32.0756, -81.0995

**Categories:** American Revolutionary War, Battles of the American Revolutionary War in Georgia (U.S. state), History of Savannah, Georgia, Sieges, Haitian history

## Introduction

"I had the pleasure of planting the American flag on the last trench," the Swedish count Curt von Stedingk wrote in his journal, "but the enemy renewed its attack and our people were annihilated by cross-fire." On the morning of October 9, 1779, Stedingk commanded one of the French assault columns against the British lines at Savannah, and he reached the last trench. Overwhelming numbers drove him back with some 20 men, every one of them wounded, Stedingk included. "The moment of retreat with the cries of our dying comrades piercing my heart," he wrote, "was the bitterest of my life." That morning ended the joint French and American attempt to retake Savannah, in one of the bloodiest battles of the Revolutionary War.

## A Southern Gamble

After failing to win the war in the North, British planners turned south, counting on Loyalist support. In December 1778 an expedition under Lieutenant-Colonel Archibald Campbell took Savannah against weak resistance. The South's rice, indigo and tobacco were paying for American military supplies from Europe, and the British promised freedom to enslaved African Americans who fought for them. To take the city back, American General Benjamin Lincoln needed a navy, and he turned to France, which had entered the war as an American ally in 1778. Admiral Charles Henri Hector, Count of Estaing, spent the first half of 1779 in the Caribbean, capturing Grenada in July. Then, on September 3, while the hurricane season still posed real danger, a few French ships reached Charleston with news that Estaing was sailing for Georgia with twenty-five ships of the line and 4,000 French troops. Lincoln marched from Charleston with more than 2,000 men on September 11.

## Twenty-Four Hours

Estaing began landing troops below the city on September 12. Confident of victory, and believing Lincoln would stop British reinforcements under Colonel John Maitland from reaching the town, he offered the British commander, General Augustine Prevost, the chance to surrender. Prevost asked for a 24-hour truce. Because of confusion over who was responsible for blocking Maitland, the waterways between Hilton Head Island and the mainland were left unguarded, and Maitland's men reached Savannah hours before the truce expired. Prevost then politely refused. His defenses were formidable. Captain Moncrief of the Royal Engineers used between 500 and 800 enslaved African Americans, working up to twelve hours a day, to build an entrenched line with redoubts on the plains outside the city. On the river, the British scuttled the leaking warship Rose in a narrow part of the channel, blocking it so the French fleet could not help in an assault.

## A Town Shot Through

Rejecting an assault at first, Estaing unloaded cannons from his ships and bombarded Savannah from October 3 to 8. The shells fell hardest not on the British works but on the town. "There was hardly a house that had not been shot through," one British observer wrote. An American officer admitted that "the poor women and children have suffered beyond description," and one of Prevost's aides recalled that "many poor creatures were killed trying to get to their cellars, or hide themselves under the bluff of the Savannah River." The bombardment did not break the defenses. With scurvy and dysentery spreading on his ships and supplies running low, Estaing was unwilling to wait out a traditional siege. He decided to storm the lines.

## Fog at Spring Hill

Against the advice of many of his officers, Estaing launched the assault on the morning of October 9. Parts of the plan had already been betrayed to Prevost. Fog made the troops attacking the Spring Hill redoubt lose their way in the swamps, and it was nearly daylight before the attack began. Estaing had chosen that redoubt partly because he believed militia alone held it. In fact, regulars of Maitland's 71st Regiment of Foot stood with the militia, whose riflemen easily picked off the white-clad French soldiers. Estaing was wounded twice. Count Casimir Pulaski, the Polish nobleman leading the combined cavalry on the American side, was mortally wounded. By the time the second wave arrived, the trenches below the redoubt were filled with bodies. After an hour of carnage Estaing ordered a retreat, and the allies soon abandoned the siege. They lost 244 killed, nearly 600 wounded and 120 captured; the British lost 40 killed, 63 wounded and 52 missing.

## From Savannah to Haiti

About 545 of the men in the allied ranks belonged to the Chasseurs-Volontaires de Saint-Domingue, a unit raised six months earlier from the Black population of Saint-Domingue (now Haiti) and led by white officers. It included free people of color and enslaved men offered freedom in exchange for military service. Henri Christophe, later King of Haiti from 1811 to 1820, possibly served with it at Savannah, and many less famous veterans of the unit became officers in the rebel armies of the Haitian Revolution. The British held Savannah until July 1782. In 2005 archaeologists found portions of the original Spring Hill redoubt, and a later survey found another segment of the British defenses, a ditch, beneath Madison Square. Each year a presidential proclamation marks General Pulaski Memorial Day in memory of the battle.

## Flight Context

The story is anchored at approximately 32.076°N, 81.100°W in Savannah, Georgia, at the Spring Hill Redoubt site on the western edge of the historic district, where the main French and American assault struck in 1779; archaeologists have also found traces of the 1779 British lines in Madison Square. From 1,500–2,500 feet AGL, the Savannah River and port frame the old city to the north; Tybee Island, where the French fleet first appeared, lies about 15 miles east at the river's mouth, and Hilton Head Island lies across the state line to the northeast. Hunter Army Air Field (KSVN) is about 4.6 nautical miles to the south-southwest and Savannah/Hilton Head International Airport (KSAV) about 6 nautical miles to the west-northwest; KSAV sits in Savannah's Class C airspace and Hunter has its own tower, so expect busy controlled airspace. Expect coastal haze and afternoon thunderstorms in summer; autumn, the season of the siege, often brings clearer skies.
