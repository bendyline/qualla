# The Marshall House (Savannah, Georgia)

Opened in the early 1850s by a widow who lived to 93, the Marshall House on East Broughton Street survived Union occupation, a string of new names and four decades of empty upper floors to become what it bills as Savannah's oldest hotel.

**Location:** 32.0784, -81.0906

**Categories:** Hotels in Savannah, Georgia, Hotels established in 1852, Hotel buildings completed in 1852, Savannah Historic District

## Introduction

In the lobby hangs a portrait of Mary Marshall, painted in 1830, more than two decades before she opened the hotel that still carries her name. The painting came out of the estate of Jim Williams, the Savannah figure at the center of John Berendt's Midnight in the Garden of Good and Evil, and it presides over a building with a long memory of its own. The Marshall House on East Broughton Street opened in 1851 or 1852, depending on the source, housed Union soldiers at the end of the Civil War, changed names and fortunes more than once, and sat half-empty for four decades. Today it bills itself as Savannah's oldest hotel.

## The Widow Who Built on Broughton

She was born Mary Magdalene Leaver in 1783, the daughter of Gabriel Leaver, a cabinet-maker from London. At seventeen she married James Marshall, who later commanded in the Savannah Volunteer Guards and who died in 1845. Six or seven years after his death, Mary Marshall opened her hotel (the hotel dates itself to 1851, Wikipedia to 1852), one of the city's first, arriving three decades after the City Hotel had claimed the title of Savannah's earliest. The hotel was only one piece of a real-estate career. She also built Mary Marshall Row on East Oglethorpe Avenue, across from Colonial Park Cemetery, and owned the Mary Marshall Houses on Oglethorpe Square as rental property. Remembered as an investor and philanthropist, she supported an asylum for female orphans. She died in Savannah in 1877 at the age of 93 and was buried in Laurel Grove Cemetery beside her husband, her daughter and her parents. Even then the Broughton Street hotel kept working for her family: her estate collected rent on it until 1914.

## Iron Lace and Blue Coats

The hotel's most striking feature arrived early. In 1857 Ralph Meldrim, who ran the hotel, raised an iron veranda across the front of the second floor, 12 feet deep and 120 feet long, turning the facade into a long shaded gallery above the street. The building was worth guarding, and a volunteer fire company, the Marshall Hose Company, was later organized to protect it and its neighbors. War reached the hotel in 1864, when the Union Army occupied the building and used it as a hospital, and the soldiers stayed into 1865. Peace brought growth. In 1880 the adjoining Florida House was absorbed into the Marshall House, and several of the hotel's claw-foot baths date to that same year. The hotel closed between 1895 and 1899, then reopened with a modern boast: electric lights and hot and cold plumbing on every floor. Among the people who lived there at one time was Joel Chandler Harris, the author of the Uncle Remus stories.

## Many Names, Empty Floors

The twentieth century was less kind. Herbert W. Gilbert leased the building in 1933 and renamed it the Gilbert Hotel. When the property sold in 1941, it held 66 guest rooms, one suite, an apartment and a set of public rooms, and it later operated as the Geiger Hotel. A 1952 postcard shows it lit up at night beside the Avon Theatre. Then, in 1957, an economic downturn closed it. The upper three floors were abandoned, left to dust and silence, while shopkeepers kept the ground floor busy with street-level trade. That split existence lasted more than forty years: storefronts below, empty guest rooms above. Only in 1998 did the last shops give way, and after restoration the hotel reopened in 1999, once again welcoming overnight guests to Broughton Street.

## What the Walls Remember

Much of what guests touch is original. Philadelphia pressed brick faces the exterior, while Savannah grey brick runs throughout the interior, and the staircases, wooden floors, fireplaces and the doors to each guest room all survive from earlier eras. The famous veranda and the gas lights are reproductions, made in the likeness of the originals that once lined the front. A bronze plaque marks the site as the former home of The Georgia Gazette, described there as the first publication in colonial Georgia. The hotel also carries a reputation for being haunted, a fixture of Savannah's many ghost stories. It was long owned by Savannah's HLC Hotels, Inc., whose Historic Inns of Savannah portfolio also included the Olde Harbour Inn, the Eliza Thompson House, the East Bay Inn, the Gastonian and the Kehoe House. HLC sold that portfolio in 2026, with the Marshall House the last sale to close, reportedly in June, so the hotel has changed hands; its website now credits the Indigo Road Hospitality Group.

## Flight Context

The Marshall House stands at about 32.0784 N, 81.0906 W on East Broughton Street in downtown Savannah, a few blocks south of the Savannah River waterfront. The historic district's grid of green squares is easy to pick out from 2,000 to 2,500 feet; Broughton Street runs east-west through the heart of it, and Colonial Park Cemetery, across from Mary Marshall's other rental row, is a large green block a short distance to the south. Hunter Army Air Field (KSVN), a military field, lies about 5 nm southwest, and Savannah/Hilton Head International Airport (KSAV) is about 6 nm west-northwest, so downtown sightseeing flights operate in busy controlled airspace. Hilton Head Airport (KHXD) is about 22 nm east-northeast. Expect summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms.
