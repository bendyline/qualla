# Battle of the Rice Boats

In March 1776, Georgia and South Carolina militia fought a Royal Navy squadron on the Savannah River over a cargo of rice meant for British troops in Boston, a clash that ended royal rule in Georgia for nearly three years.

**Location:** 32.0813, -81.0858

**Categories:** Battles of the American Revolutionary War in Georgia (U.S. state), Naval battles of the American Revolutionary War involving Great Britain, History of Savannah, Georgia, Georgia (U.S. state) in the American Revolution, 1776 in Georgia (U.S. state)

## Introduction

The rice was meant to feed an army in Boston. By the time the British ships carrying it finally sailed north from the Georgia coast at the end of March 1776, that army had already abandoned the city. The voyage had begun months earlier, when General William Howe, his troops besieged in Boston and short of provisions, ordered an expedition south to buy rice and other supplies in Georgia. What followed, on March 2 and 3, 1776, was a strange little battle on the Savannah River, fought by Patriot militia from Georgia and South Carolina against a small Royal Navy squadron. It is remembered as the Battle of the Rice Boats, or the Battle of Yamacraw Bluff, and it ended British rule in Georgia for nearly three years.

## A Colony Slipping Away

Georgia had stayed relatively neutral as tensions boiled over into war at Lexington and Concord in April 1775. News of that fighting and of the Battle of Bunker Hill in June fanned the flames of independence, and by the summer of 1775 radicals in Georgia's provincial congress had taken power and were steadily stripping Royal Governor James Wright of his authority. Wright asked for a naval presence at Savannah, the colony's capital, but Patriots in Charleston intercepted his request and substituted a dispatch saying he did not need one. Warships began arriving off Tybee Island anyway in January 1776. Wright told Joseph Clay and others that he believed the fleet had come to punish the rebels. In fact it was the provisioning expedition, commanded by Captain Andrew Barclay, and it included two transports, Whitby and Symmetry, carrying about 200 regulars of the 40th Foot under Major James Grant.

## The Governor Escapes

The fleet's arrival alarmed the Patriots who controlled Georgia's government, and they placed Governor Wright under arrest. Harassed even in confinement and fearing for his life, Wright slipped out of his mansion on the night of February 11 and made his way to the plantation of a Loyalist supporter, from which he was taken out to the warship Scarborough. Once aboard, he wrote to the remaining members of his council, frustrated that he could obtain neither assurances of safety nor access to the supplies. Georgia, along with the other twelve colonies, had adopted the Continental Association in 1774, banning trade with Great Britain. Meanwhile the provincial assembly had elected delegates to the Second Continental Congress and begun raising regiments for the Continental Army. With negotiations effectively dead, Barclay ordered his fleet into action on February 29.

## Four O'Clock on Hutchinson Island

On March 1, four warships, Scarborough, Tamar, Cherokee and Hinchinbrook, sailed up the Savannah River to an anchorage called Five-Fathom Hole with transports carrying two to three hundred of Grant's men. Hinchinbrook tried to take a position above the town by way of the Back River but ran aground on a sandbank. Joseph Habersham's militia cleared her decks with gunfire, yet without suitable boats they could not board her, and she floated free on the next high tide. Late on the evening of March 2, Grant's troops landed on Hutchinson Island, crossed it in the dark, and at 4 a.m. on March 3 seized a number of the rice boats anchored nearby. They worked so quietly, possibly with help from the ships' own captains, that Savannah did not raise the alarm until 9 a.m. Colonel Lachlan McIntosh then placed three 4-pound cannon on Yamacraw Bluff, defended by roughly 300 militiamen. Two officers he sent out under a flag of truce, Lieutenant Daniel Roberts and Major Raymond Demeré II, were promptly arrested. A second, larger party turned violent when its leader, Captain Rogers, was insulted and fired at the ship; the British fired back, wounding one man and nearly sinking the boat.

## Fire on the River

The Patriots' Committee of Safety decided the supply ships should be burned rather than lost. Militia set the ship Inverness alight and let her drift toward the captured vessels, and the British scrambled to abandon them ahead of the flames. From the bluff, militia muskets and the battery's grapeshot raked the confusion. Two of the occupied ships escaped downstream. Two more fled upstream, but were forced to dock and their crews were taken prisoner. Three ships burned well into the night. Several hundred South Carolina militia, sent in answer to an earlier appeal, arrived to help; accounts differ on how many reached Savannah before the fighting ended.

## Rice Without a Destination

Even so, the British got most of the merchant ships down the shallow Back River, some only after dumping part of their cargo. At Tybee Island they loaded 1,600 barrels of rice onto the two transports. While the fleet lay at anchor negotiating a prisoner exchange, it seized several arriving vessels as prizes, and on March 25 militia from Savannah burned every house on the island so Wright and the ships' officers could not use them. Barclay sailed north on March 30 or 31, depending on the account, but the British had already abandoned Boston, so he put into Newport, Rhode Island, where local Patriots refused him help and fired on his ships with field artillery. He finally rejoined British forces at Halifax, Nova Scotia, in May. Wright's flight and the battle ended British control of Georgia until British forces recaptured Savannah on December 28, 1778. Wright returned, and the city stayed in British hands until July 1782.

## Flight Context

The battle centered on the Savannah River at about 32.0813 N, 81.0858 W, where Yamacraw Bluff on the city's riverfront faces Hutchinson Island across the water. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet you can trace the main channel and the Back River around Hutchinson Island, then follow the river downstream toward Tybee Island at its mouth, where the British fleet anchored. Savannah/Hilton Head International Airport (KSAV) is about 6.5 nm to the west-northwest and Hunter Army Airfield (KSVN) about 5 nm to the southwest; both have busy controlled airspace, so coordinate with approach control. Summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are common; morning fog can settle over the river in the cooler months.
