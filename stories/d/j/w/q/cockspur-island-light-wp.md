# Cockspur Island Light

The smallest of Georgia's historic lighthouses stands on an oyster bed in the Savannah River's south channel, its base shaped like a ship's prow, and it came through a Civil War battle and nearly a century of darkness to shine again.

**Location:** 32.0227, -80.8799

**Categories:** Lighthouses in Georgia (U.S. state), Lighthouses completed in 1839, Buildings and structures in Chatham County, Georgia, Tourist attractions in Chatham County, Georgia, Fort Pulaski National Monument

## Introduction

Its foundation is a bed of oysters and mussels. Its base is shaped like the prow of a ship, a design meant to soften the blows of the waves. The Cockspur Island Light is the smallest of Georgia's five surviving historic lighthouses, a stubby sentinel on an islet off Cockspur Island in the south channel of the Savannah River, near Lazaretto Creek and northwest of Tybee Island. It went dark as a working beacon in 1909. It was relit in 2007, and again in 2022 with a new solar-powered light, not to guide ships but to honor a history that includes a hurricane, a Civil War battle that put it directly in the line of fire, and almost a century of darkness.

## A Daymark Learns to Shine

The structure began as a daymarker, an unlit tower meant to show mariners the entrance to the South Channel of the Savannah River. Construction ran from March 1837 to November 1839. In 1848 the New York City architect John S. Norris, whose work in the region also included Savannah's U.S. Custom House, was hired to retrofit it with lights and reflectors, and by 1849 it showed a fixed white light backed by 14-inch reflectors. With the light just 25 feet above sea level, it could be seen 9 miles away. In 1854 a hurricane destroyed the tower. A larger replacement went up on the same foundation in 1855, though some sources date it to 1856.

## In the Line of Fire

Cockspur Island is also home to Fort Pulaski, and in the Civil War that proximity put the little lighthouse in harm's way. The light was extinguished at the start of the war, in 1861, and in April 1862 the tower stood directly in the line of fire during the roughly thirty-hour bombardment that ended in the defeat of Fort Pulaski, yet it came through with little or no damage. It was relit in 1866. For the next four decades it marked the south channel. But that channel was used only infrequently compared with the deeper, wider North Channel of the Savannah River, and on June 1, 1909, the light was finally put out.

## The Keeper's Sister

The keepers of the Savannah River lights left behind one of the city's famous stories. Florence Martus, remembered as the Waving Girl, was the sister of the lighthouse keeper. She was born on Cockspur Island, where her father served as an ordnance sergeant at Fort Pulaski, and her brother George later became keeper of the Elba and South Channel lights. The two moved together to Elba Island. From there, between 1887 and 1931, Florence greeted the ships passing on the river, waving a handkerchief by day and a lantern by night, a greeting she kept up for more than four decades.

## Relit for Memory

On August 14, 1958, the U.S. Coast Guard relinquished control of the lighthouse to the National Park Service. Today it is part of Fort Pulaski National Monument and is listed, with the rest of the monument, on the National Register of Historic Places. From 1995 to 2000 the upper portions of the tower were restored, though its foundations still need protection from wave action and tidal erosion. On the evening of March 18, 2007, the National Park Service relit the light in a ceremony; in early 2013 more rocks were placed around it to shield it from strong currents at high tide; and in 2021 the masonry was stabilized and the door and windows were replaced. In November 2022 a new solar-powered light was installed, with a relighting event to mark it. Because of ongoing preservation work, the Park Service has closed both the lighthouse and its island to the public. Visitors can look from the Lighthouse Overlook Trail, which has an observation deck, or from the water, keeping their distance.

## Flight Context

The lighthouse stands at about 32.0227 N, 80.8799 W on a tiny islet in the south channel of the Savannah River, just off Cockspur Island and Fort Pulaski, with Tybee Island to the southeast and the river mouth opening to the Atlantic. Fort Pulaski on Cockspur Island makes the best visual anchor; the little tower sits in the channel nearby. From 1,000 to 1,500 feet the lighthouse, the fort and the river channels line up clearly. Hunter Army Air Field (KSVN), a military field, is about 14 nm west; Hilton Head Airport (KHXD) is about 15 nm northeast; and Savannah/Hilton Head International (KSAV) is about 17 nm west-northwest. The river mouth carries heavy ship traffic and the area lies near Savannah's terminal airspace, so coordinate with approach control. Sea fog and haze can settle over the river mouth, especially on cool mornings.
