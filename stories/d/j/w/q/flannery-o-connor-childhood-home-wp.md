# Flannery O'Connor Childhood Home

On Savannah's Lafayette Square stands the 1856 house where Flannery O'Connor spent her first thirteen years - within earshot of the cathedral bells and home to a chicken that walked backward into the newsreels.

## Introduction

"When I was six I had a chicken that walked backward and was in the Pathé News," Flannery O'Connor remembered. "I was in it too with the chicken. I was just there to assist the chicken but it was the high point in my life. Everything since has been an anticlimax." The chicken, and the girl, lived in a house on Savannah's Lafayette Square. It was the future writer's first home, and today it is a museum devoted to her childhood.

## Little Mary O'Connor

Mary Flannery O'Connor was born in Savannah on March 25, 1925, the only child of Edward Francis O'Connor, a real estate agent, and Regina Cline. She lived at 207 East Charlton Street, in a house built in 1856, from her birth until 1938. By her own later account, she was a "pigeon-toed child with a receding chin and a you-leave-me-alone-or-I'll-bite-you complex." Her mother worried about mosquitoes, and she sometimes placed her daughter in a "Kiddie's Coop," a protective screened-in crib for infants and toddlers. At around five or six, the girl became something of a local celebrity when Pathé News reported on how "Little Mary O'Connor" had trained a chicken. Years later she was still telling the story, and still keeping a perfectly straight face about it.

## Within Sight of the Spires

The house stands just across Lafayette Square from the Cathedral of St. John the Baptist, where the young O'Connor regularly attended Mass. It was so close that she could see the cathedral's spires from her house and hear its bells tolling. The Savannah years did not last. Her father was diagnosed with lupus in 1937, and she left the Charlton Street house the following year. By 1940 the family had relocated to Milledgeville, Georgia, where her father died on February 1, 1941. The same disease would later shape her own life.

## The Writer She Became

From those beginnings came two novels, Wise Blood in 1952 and The Violent Bear It Away in 1960, along with 31 short stories. Her collection A Good Man Is Hard to Find appeared in 1955, and Everything That Rises Must Converge followed in 1965. She did not live to see that second collection published. O'Connor died on August 3, 1964, at the age of 39, from complications of a new attack of lupus following surgery. Recognition kept coming after her death. In 1972, eight years after she died, her Complete Stories won the U.S. National Book Award for Fiction - an honor for a writer whose life ended early, and whose first memories belonged to this house on the square.

## A Home Saved for Her Readers

In 1989, the Flannery O'Connor Childhood Home Foundation bought the house for a reported $132,500, closing the sale on August 1. The effort was led by Armstrong State College President Robert Burnett and professors Hugh Brown and Robert Strozier. The foundation set out to find period furniture and furnishings, and it asked "anyone with photographs or memorabilia from Miss O'Connor's childhood in Savannah" to get in touch. Savannah landscape architect Clermont Huger Lee designed a historically accurate garden for the home, now called the Lee Garden, and both Lee and O'Connor have been inducted into the Savannah Women of Vision. The house also hosts events through the year. Its best-known program, the free Ursrey Memorial Lecture, was founded in 2009 by Alene Ursrey, John Hunt and Betsy Cain in memory of brothers Terry and Ashley Ursrey, native Georgians who were, like O'Connor, "lifelong devotees of all things Southern, particularly the art of storytelling."
