# Immortal Six Hundred

Caught in the collapse of Civil War prisoner exchanges over the Confederacy's refusal to treat Black soldiers as prisoners of war, 600 Confederate officers were held on Morris Island off Charleston and on retaliation rations at Fort Pulaski.

**Location:** 32.0272, -80.8903

**Categories:** American Civil War prisoners of war held by the United States, Confederate States Army soldiers, Fort Pulaski National Monument, American Civil War sites in Georgia (U.S. state), Prisoner exchange

## Introduction

Why couldn't they simply be exchanged? The Union and the Confederacy had traded captured soldiers back and forth, but by 1864 that system had broken down, and the six hundred Confederate officers who came to be known in the South as the Immortal Six Hundred were caught in the wreckage. The reason lay at the heart of the war itself: whether Black men in Union uniform would be treated as soldiers at all. The officers' captivity took them to Morris Island at the entrance to Charleston Harbor, then to the casemates of Fort Pulaski near Savannah, where for forty-two days they lived on moldy cornmeal and soured pickles. Their story is one of retaliation answering retaliation, with prisoners on both sides paying the price.

## A Resolution and a Refusal

On May 1, 1863, the Confederate Congress passed a resolution declaring that captured African-American soldiers, and the white officers of colored troops, would not be exchanged. The resolution allowed captured officers of colored troops to be executed and captured Black soldiers to be sold into slavery. The Union answered by demanding that all its soldiers be treated equally, and exchanges of prisoners broke down. Order 252 made the Union position formal: there would be no exchanges until the Confederacy agreed to treat Black and white prisoners of war alike. General Ulysses S. Grant held to that line. When Major General John G. Foster later planned an exchange of the six hundred, Grant refused, reportedly writing, "In no circumstances will he be allowed to make exchanges of prisoners of war."

## Human Shields at Charleston

The chain of events that created the six hundred began in June 1864, when the Confederate Army imprisoned five Union generals and forty-five other Union officers in Charleston, South Carolina, using them as human shields in an attempt to stop Union artillery from firing on the city. In retaliation, Secretary of War Edwin M. Stanton ordered fifty captured Confederate officers of similar ranks taken to Morris Island. Correspondence between the opposing commanders, Major General John G. Foster for the Union and Major General Samuel Jones for the Confederacy, turned toward an exchange, and on August 4, 1864, the fifty officers were traded. But Jones then brought 600 more Union prisoners to Charleston, in part to press for a larger exchange. Foster asked for a like number of Confederate prisoners to be placed on Morris Island in retaliation. They arrived in the first week of September 1864.

## Forty-Two Days of Cornmeal

In early October 1864, with yellow fever breaking out in Charleston, Jones removed the Union prisoners from the city. Once officially informed, Foster moved the Confederate officers off Morris Island and sent them to Fort Pulaski, outside Savannah, where about 540 arrived on October 23. There they were crowded into the fort's casemates. For 42 days, the only food issued to them was a "retaliation ration" of ten ounces of moldy cornmeal and half a pint of soured onion pickles, a punishment imposed in response to the conditions Union forces had found at the Confederate prisons at Andersonville, Georgia, and Salisbury, North Carolina. Starving men supplemented their rations with the occasional rat or stray cat. Thirteen of them died at Fort Pulaski of diseases such as dysentery and scurvy.

## Eleven Dollars and an Oath Refused

Even in those conditions, the prisoners tried to look after one another. On December 13, 1864, they organized "The Relief Association of Fort Pulaski for Aid and Relief of the Sick and Less Fortunate Prisoners" and elected Colonel Abram Fulkerson of the 63rd Tennessee Infantry Regiment as president. According to Fulkerson's report of December 28, the association collected and spent eleven dollars out of the men's scant funds. Five more of the six hundred later died at Hilton Head Island, South Carolina. On the voyage north, a group of thirteen officers, among them Colonel Paul F. DeGournay and Colonel George Woolfolk, tried to escape from the gunboat carrying them, but the captain noticed a man missing and sent the prisoners below to the brig. The survivors were returned to Fort Delaware on March 12, 1865, where another twenty-five died. Across the South they became known for refusing to take the Oath of Allegiance under duress.

## Flight Context

This story is anchored at Fort Pulaski National Monument, about 32.0272 N, 80.8903 W, on Cockspur Island at the mouth of the Savannah River, where the six hundred were held in the fort's casemates. The fort sits between the river's channels with Tybee Island to the southeast; Morris Island, the prisoners' first place of captivity, lies about 65 nm to the northeast at the entrance to Charleston Harbor. From 1,000 to 1,500 feet the fort and the marsh around it are easy to pick out. Hunter Army Air Field (KSVN), a military field, is about 13 nm west; Hilton Head Airport (KHXD) is about 15 nm northeast; and Savannah/Hilton Head International (KSAV) is about 17 nm west-northwest. The river mouth lies near Savannah's terminal airspace, so coordinate with approach control, and watch for sea fog and haze over the marshes.
