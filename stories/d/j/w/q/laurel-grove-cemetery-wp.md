# Laurel Grove Cemetery

Opened in 1853 on part of a former plantation, Savannah's Laurel Grove was laid out as two cemeteries divided by race, and holds Victorian stonework, Confederate veterans, the founder of the Girl Scouts and the man credited with writing "Jingle Bells."

**Location:** 32.0647, -81.1067

**Categories:** Cemeteries in Savannah, Georgia, Protected areas of Chatham County, Georgia, 1853 establishments in Georgia (U.S. state), National Register of Historic Places in Savannah, Georgia, Cemeteries established in the 1850s

## Introduction

Even in death, antebellum Savannah kept people apart. Laurel Grove Cemetery, which opened for burials in 1853, took shape as two burial grounds. The original cemetery, now called Laurel Grove North, was for white people. A companion ground, Laurel Grove South, was reserved for enslaved people and free people of color. Walking the two sections today means walking through the city's history of division as much as its history of mourning.

## From Plantation to Burial Ground

Savannah was thinking about a new cemetery as early as 1818, but the plan took decades to realize. By around 1850, other burial grounds were running out of room, and Laurel Grove was developed on the west side of town, on part of the former Springfield Plantation. The north cemetery covers roughly 55 to 67 acres, depending on the count, designed around a natural ravine, with a large Central Green and curving roadways. By 1853, a Keeper's House, Porter's Lodge, Gazebo and Public Holding Vault were in place. The cemetery was dedicated in 1852, when the lawyer and poet Henry Rootes Jackson delivered the address, and it opened for burials the following year.

## Carved Stone and a Sold-Out Era

With moss-draped live oaks, winding roads and beautifully carved stones, both sections of Laurel Grove follow the Victorian-era model of the cemetery as a park. The timing explains much of that character. According to the city, every available lot in the north cemetery was sold during the Victorian Era, which is why, in the city's words, it likely has the highest concentration of Victorian period cemetery architecture in the Southeast. Many of Savannah's Confederate veterans of the Civil War are buried in the original cemetery, their graves woven into the same Victorian landscape. The two sections were recognized separately on the National Register of Historic Places: Laurel Grove South in 1978, and the north section in 1983.

## Names Among the Graves

Some of the people buried at Laurel Grove are known far beyond Savannah. Juliette Gordon Low, founder of the Girl Scouts of the USA, lies here. So does James Lord Pierpont, credited as the writer and composer of "Jingle Bells." Edward Clifford Anderson Sr., who served eight terms as mayor of Savannah before and after the Civil War, is buried here, as is James Moore Wayne, an associate justice of the U.S. Supreme Court. So is W. W. Law, the civil rights leader and president of the Savannah NAACP - a figure whose life's work pushed back against the very separation that shaped this cemetery's founding. Laurel Grove North is open to the public daily from 8 a.m. to 5 p.m., and Laurel Grove South keeps the same hours.

## Flight Context

Laurel Grove Cemetery lies at 32.0647 N, 81.1067 W on the west side of Savannah's midtown, Georgia, about 0.6 miles west-southwest of Forsyth Park. From 1,500-2,000 feet, look for a large, tree-filled green space west of the historic district's grid of squares; Forsyth Park's rectangle makes a good reference to the east. Hunter Army Airfield (KSVN), a military field, is about 3.8 nm south-southwest, and Savannah/Hilton Head International Airport (KSAV) about 6 nm northwest, so expect controlled airspace. Hilton Head Airport (KHXD) lies about 23 nm east-northeast. Low coastal terrain means haze is the main visibility concern.
