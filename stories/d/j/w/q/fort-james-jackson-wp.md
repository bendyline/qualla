# Fort James Jackson

Built between 1808 and 1812 to guard Savannah from the sea, Old Fort Jackson served the Confederacy, fell to Sherman's army without a siege, and now fires its cannon twice a day for visitors on the Savannah River.

**Location:** 32.0819, -81.0361

## Introduction

Twice a day, at 11 a.m. and 2 p.m., weather permitting, a cannon fires on the bank of the Savannah River just east of the city. The gunners are historical interpreters in period military dress, and the fort around them was built more than two centuries ago for exactly this purpose: to answer any enemy that came up the river toward Savannah. When the fort's great test finally came, it arrived from an unexpected direction.

## A Young Engineer's Fort

The land for the fort was sold in 1808, and construction ran from that year until 1812. Its purpose was straightforward: to protect Savannah from attack by sea. The engineer was William McRee, who had only just graduated from West Point, and the fort took its name from James Jackson (1757-1806), a British-born political figure in Georgia. An earlier structure on the site, known as Fort Mud, preceded it. Fort Jackson stood on the river itself, about three miles downriver from the city, facing the water route an attacker from the sea would have to use. It remained part of Savannah's defenses for the next half century and more.

## One of Three

During the Civil War, Fort Jackson was among the Confederate forts defending Savannah from Union forces, alongside Fort McAllister and Fort Pulaski. Together they gave the city a defense in depth. That defense proved beside the point. In December 1864, the Union army commanded by William T. Sherman approached Savannah by land, and Fort Jackson, built to face the river, was evacuated and occupied by Union troops almost immediately. A fort designed for the threat of ships had little answer to an army arriving overland, and its Confederate service ended with the city it had been meant to shield.

## Quiet Decades and a New Name

In the mid-1880s (sources give 1885 or 1886), the fort was renamed Fort Oglethorpe, in honor of Georgia's founder, Lieutenant-General James Edward Oglethorpe. The new name came with little new purpose. Until 1905, the U.S. military made little use of the fort, and it slipped out of active service. Its second life came later, as history rather than defense. Today the fort, which the state of Georgia once ran before a nonprofit took over in 1976, is a National Historic Landmark, known once again as Fort Jackson and, informally, as Old Fort Jackson.

## Powder Smoke on the River

The Coastal Heritage Society operates Old Fort Jackson as a museum, open seven days a week from 9 a.m. to 4 p.m., closed only on a few holidays. Historical interpreters in military impressions stage daily cannon firings and historical weapons demonstrations, with activities meant for all ages. Exhibits in the fort's casemates help visitors understand its history, the people who served there and its role in Savannah's defense. The fort's address, 1 Fort Jackson Road, lies along the Islands Expressway, the road that links Savannah with Fort Pulaski and the town of Tybee Island - a reminder that Fort Pulaski, its Civil War partner, lies farther down the same road.

## Flight Context

Old Fort Jackson sits at 32.0819 N, 81.0361 W on the south bank of the Savannah River, about 2.8 nm east of downtown Savannah, Georgia. From 1,500-2,500 feet, follow the river east from the downtown waterfront; the fort lies beside the water along the Islands Expressway, the road toward Fort Pulaski and Tybee Island. Savannah/Hilton Head International Airport (KSAV) is about 9 nm west-northwest and Hunter Army Airfield (KSVN), a military field, about 7 nm southwest, so expect controlled airspace. Hilton Head Airport (KHXD) lies about 19 nm east-northeast. The river carries large commercial shipping, which makes a useful visual cue for the channel. Low coastal terrain means haze is the main visibility concern.
