# Savannah Protest Movement

From a 1960 lunch-counter sit-in on Broughton Street to the night marches of 1963, Savannah's Black citizens used boycotts, ballots and nonviolent protest to win a desegregation so thorough that Martin Luther King Jr. called it the most desegregated city south of the Mason-Dixon line.

**Location:** 32.0781, -81.0894

**Categories:** Civil rights movement, African-American history in Savannah, Georgia, Sit-ins, 1960 protests, 1963 protests

## Introduction

Before he sat down at the Azalea Room, the lunch counter inside Levy's Department Store, Ernest Robinson wrote part of Psalm 23 on his palm. It was March 16, 1960. He, Carolyn Quilloin and Joan Tyson, all members of the NAACP Youth Council, kept trying to order after the owner told them to leave, and they were arrested. In jail they sang "We Shall Overcome." One of the students later told the Savannah Morning News that the verse uplifted them: they knew what had happened to Emmett Till, and "while we thought that we were safe in Savannah, we knew that anything could happen." That afternoon began three and a half years of protest that would remake the oldest city in Georgia.

## Ballots Before Boycotts

The ground had been prepared for two decades. In 1942 Ralph Mark Gilbert, pastor of First African Baptist Church, revived the Savannah NAACP. With his wife, Eloria, he led mass voter registration drives, and by 1946 about half of Savannah's 45,000 African Americans were registered to vote. That bloc helped win a new high school, road repairs and, in 1947, the hiring of the city's first Black police officers, the nine men later known as the Original Nine. In 1950 leadership passed to W. W. Law, a mail carrier who believed firmly in nonviolence. His vice president, Hosea Williams, was a chemist for the U.S. Department of Agriculture, spurred to activism in part after one of his children was refused service at a lunch counter in the city.

## A Mouthful of Freedom

On the day of the first sit-ins, the NAACP announced a boycott of downtown stores. Its demands went beyond lunch counters: jobs above menial positions, desegregated fountains and restrooms, and the courtesy of "Mr.," "Mrs." and "Miss" instead of "boy" or "girl." Pickets outside Levy's carried signs reading "You can buy a $50 suit, but not a 10 cent cup of coffee" and "We want a mouthful of freedom." When the city banned picketing by two or more people, Time quoted Mayor Lee Mingledorff Jr. saying he did not care whether it was "especially constitutional or not." At Easter, Black marchers wore last year's clothes rather than shop downtown. On August 17, activists waded into the water at segregated Tybee Island beach and swam for about 90 minutes before police arrested 11 of them. Four days later students knelt in at ten white churches. Five turned them away, three offered segregated seats, and two, Christ Church and Tabernacle Baptist, seated them without separation.

## Pressure That Held

Williams organized the Chatham County Crusade for Voters, and by the end of 1960, 57 percent of Savannah's eligible Black citizens were registered, a higher share than among white residents, in a city about one-third Black. The boycott bit, too. Five businesses declared bankruptcy at least partly because of it, and the chamber of commerce blamed it for a 15 percent drop in downtown revenue. In June 1961 the bus company agreed to hire Black drivers. That July the post office fired Law for his activism, then reinstated him after pushback from national NAACP leaders and President John F. Kennedy. By October 1, 1961, a biracial committee assembled by Mayor Malcolm Maclean had agreed to hire Black workers into higher positions and to use courtesy titles, the city had desegregated buses, golf courses, movie theaters and restaurants, and the downtown boycott ended. Protests continued wherever segregation remained.

## The Summer of Night Marches

On June 4, 1963, under pressure from the white community, Savannah's movie theaters resegregated. Williams, now aligned with the Southern Christian Leadership Conference, answered with marches along Broughton Street by day and by night, sometimes leading up to 1,000 people after dark. Law feared the night marches would provoke violence. Within a week about 500 protesters had been arrested, and the SCLC sent James Bevel, Dorothy Cotton and Andrew Young to train marchers in nonviolence. On June 19 about 3,000 people held a mass prayer outside a segregated Holiday Inn, and 400 were arrested. Williams was jailed on July 8 and held for more than a month. On July 10 (some accounts say July 11), after the arrest of SNCC field secretary Bruce Gordon, about 2,000 people gathered outside the city jail, and a two-hour clash with police followed; officers used tear gas and high-powered hoses and beat several protesters with clubs. At least one store burned, 75 people were arrested, and the Georgia National Guard was mobilized.

## The Most Desegregated City

By then the protests had cut the city's trade by 30 percent. White business owners helped secure Williams's release, and 100 businessmen assembled by Mayor Maclean agreed to desegregate the city's remaining public and private facilities in exchange for a 60-day pause in protests, and the city would rescind its remaining segregation ordinances. On October 1, 1963, bowling alleys, hotels, motels, movie theaters and swimming pools opened to everyone. Local leaders had even declined an offer from Martin Luther King Jr. to join them, believing they were progressing well without him. On New Year's Day 1964, King came to Savannah and praised it as "the most desegregated city south of the Mason–Dixon line." Schools were ordered to fully integrate in 1964. In 2016 a Georgia historical marker was placed beside the old Levy's building, now a Savannah College of Art and Design library, and Quilloin and Tyson were among the guests.

## Flight Context

The story is anchored at approximately 32.078°N, 81.089°W, at the former Levy's Department Store on Broughton Street in Savannah's historic downtown, a few blocks from the Savannah River. From 1,500–2,500 feet AGL, the downtown grid of squares is unmistakable beside the river and the port, with the Talmadge Memorial Bridge arching over the water to the northwest; Tybee Island, site of the 1960 wade-in, lies about 15 miles east at the river's mouth. Hunter Army Air Field (KSVN) is about 5 nautical miles to the southwest, and Savannah/Hilton Head International Airport (KSAV) is about 6.5 nautical miles to the west-northwest; both are towered fields, and KSAV sits in busy Class C airspace. Coastal Georgia summers bring haze, humidity and afternoon thunderstorms; spring and fall usually offer the clearest views.
