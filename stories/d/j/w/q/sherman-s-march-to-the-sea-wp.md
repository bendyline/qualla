# Sherman's March to the Sea

In the fall of 1864, 62,000 Union soldiers cut loose from their supply lines and carved a 300-mile path of destruction from Atlanta to Savannah, breaking the Confederacy's war economy while thousands of enslaved people followed them toward freedom.

**Location:** 32.1287, -81.1519

**Categories:** American Civil War, Sherman's March to the Sea, Military operations of the American Civil War in Georgia (U.S. state), Emancipation, 1864 in Georgia (U.S. state)

## Introduction

The campaign got its name from a prisoner of war. S. H. M. Byers, a Union soldier held at Camp Sorghum near Columbia, South Carolina, wrote a poem in late 1864 about the campaign, and a fellow prisoner named Rockwell set it to music. When Columbia fell and Byers was freed, he handed General William T. Sherman a scrap of paper with the poem on it. Sherman read it later that day, was so moved that he brought Byers onto his staff, and the two became lifelong friends. The song became an instant hit with the army and then the public. Its title was "Sherman's March to the Sea."

## Go as You Propose

Sherman and Ulysses S. Grant believed the war would end only when the Confederacy's capacity to fight was decisively broken. After taking Atlanta in September 1864, Sherman proposed to abandon his supply lines entirely, march through the heart of Georgia to the sea, and live off the land. President Abraham Lincoln and Grant had serious reservations, but on November 2 Grant telegraphed: "Go as you propose." Sherman assembled 62,000 men, including 55,000 infantry and 64 guns, weeding out the physically weak and relying heavily on veterans. They carried 20 days of rations. To route them, Sherman studied livestock and crop data from the 1860 census. On November 15 the army left Atlanta in two wings, moving so that the Confederates could not tell whether it was bound for Macon, Augusta or Savannah. Sherman's personal escort was the 1st Alabama Cavalry, a regiment of Southerners who had stayed loyal to the Union.

## Bummers and Neckties

Foragers known as "bummers" stripped farms of food while the army wrecked Georgia's railroads, factories and agricultural infrastructure. Soldiers heated rails over fires and twisted them around tree trunks, leaving behind what became known as Sherman's neckties. His Special Field Orders No. 120 barred soldiers from entering homes and let the cavalry and artillery appropriate horses, mules and wagons freely while discriminating between the rich, "usually hostile," and the poor, "usually neutral or friendly," though the campaign still destroyed a great deal of civilian property. Most Confederate troops in Georgia had gone into Tennessee with John Bell Hood, and Georgia's militia was made up largely of boys and elderly men. At Griswoldville on November 22, militia troops launched hours of badly coordinated attacks and retreated with about 1,100 casualties, against about 100 for the Union. The next day Union troops took the state capital at Milledgeville, where Governor Joseph Brown and the legislature had hastily fled, and held a mock session in the capitol, jokingly voting Georgia back into the Union.

## A Christmas Gift

On December 10 the army reached the outskirts of Savannah and found 10,000 men under William J. Hardee entrenched behind flooded rice fields, with only narrow causeways leading to the city. To reach the Navy's supply ships, Sherman needed Fort McAllister on the Ogeechee River. On December 13, William B. Hazen's division stormed it and took it within 15 minutes, though some of the 134 Union casualties were caused by "torpedoes," crude land mines rarely used in the war. With supplies and siege guns arriving, Sherman demanded surrender. Hardee chose to escape instead, slipping across the Savannah River on a makeshift pontoon bridge on December 20. The next morning Mayor Richard Dennis Arnold rode out to surrender the city in exchange for protection of its people and property, and Union troops occupied it that day. Sherman telegraphed Lincoln: "I beg to present you as a Christmas gift the City of Savannah, with one hundred and fifty heavy guns and plenty of ammunition and about twenty-five thousand bales of cotton."

## The Road Toward Freedom

For enslaved Georgians, the army's passage was both an opening and a danger. Some welcomed Sherman as a liberator and followed his columns; others suffered alongside their enslavers as soldiers ransacked the plantations. A Confederate officer estimated that 10,000 liberated people followed the army, and hundreds died of hunger, disease or exposure along the way. At Ebenezer Creek, north of Savannah, many of the freed people following the army drowned trying to cross after a Union general had the pontoon bridge removed behind his troops; accounts put the toll at hundreds, but no one knows the number. In 2010 the Georgia Historical Society placed a marker nearby that tells what happened there. In Savannah, Sherman organized relief for the refugees who had flooded the city, and with Special Field Orders No. 15 he assigned them land, orders popularly remembered as the origin of "40 acres and a mule" and reversed later in 1865 by President Andrew Johnson. He also arranged for 50,000 bushels of captured rice to be sold in the North to raise money to feed Savannah.

## The Hard Hand of War

Sherman himself estimated the damage at $100 million, about a fifth of which "inured to our advantage," while the "remainder is simple waste and destruction." A 2022 economic study found that the destruction caused a large contraction in agricultural investment, farm values and manufacturing, with parts of the agricultural decline persisting through 1920. Some historians call the march an early example of total war, while others prefer "hard war," arguing that Sherman targeted food, industry and military property rather than civilian lives. Sherman framed it bluntly: his men were "not only fighting hostile armies, but a hostile people." The song that came to symbolize the campaign was written afterward: Henry Clay Work's "Marching Through Georgia," from 1865. Sherman came to dislike it, partly because he was never one to rejoice over a fallen foe, and partly because it was played at almost every public appearance he made.

## Flight Context

This story is anchored at approximately 32.129°N, 81.152°W on the west side of Savannah, Georgia, where the march ended in December 1864. From 2,500–3,500 feet AGL the Savannah River, the port and the historic downtown grid lie to the east; Fort McAllister stands on the Ogeechee River about 16 miles to the south, and Ebenezer Creek joins the Savannah River some miles upstream to the north.
