# Savannah: The Belle of the South

The city Sherman spared on his March to the Sea, where Spanish moss drapes over 22 perfect squares, where 'Midnight in the Garden of Good and Evil' made murder literary, and where ghosts are a tourist attraction.

**Location:** 32.0809, -81.0912

**Categories:** georgia, savannah, city, historic, southern

## Introduction

General Sherman burned Atlanta and intended to burn Savannah too, but when his army arrived in December 1864, the city surrendered without a fight. Sherman telegrammed Lincoln, offering Savannah as a Christmas present. The city that survived became a time capsule - 22 original squares laid out by founder James Oglethorpe in 1733, antebellum architecture that escaped the torch, live oaks dripping Spanish moss over cobblestone streets. Savannah is the South as people imagine it should be: beautiful, haunted, slightly decadent, hiding secrets behind wrought-iron gates. The book 'Midnight in the Garden of Good and Evil' captured this atmosphere in 1994, describing a city where eccentricity is tolerated and the line between society and scandal is deliberately blurred.

## The Squares

James Oglethorpe founded Savannah in 1733 and designed it around a unique system of public squares - open spaces surrounded by buildings, creating outdoor rooms throughout the city. He laid out 24 squares; 22 survive. Each has its own character: Chippewa Square where Forrest Gump sat on his bench (the bench was a prop, moved to the Savannah History Museum), Monterey Square from 'Midnight in the Garden of Good and Evil,' Reynolds Square with its statue of John Wesley, Johnson Square as the city's commercial heart. The squares create a rhythm for walking - shade and rest between blocks, the city organized for pedestrians before anyone thought to call it urban planning. Nothing like this grid of green rooms exists anywhere else.

## The Midnight Tour

John Berendt's 'Midnight in the Garden of Good and Evil' was published in 1994 and spent 216 weeks on the New York Times bestseller list - longer than any other book in the list's history. The non-fiction account of a 1981 murder and the four trials that followed introduced readers to Savannah's eccentric characters: Jim Williams, the antiques dealer who shot his assistant at the Mercer House; Lady Chablis, the transgender performer; the man who walked an invisible dog. The book transformed Savannah's tourism; visitors now take 'The Book' tours, seeking locations from the narrative. The Mercer House remains private, but its Spanish moss-draped exterior is among the most photographed in the city.

## Ghost City

Savannah claims to be America's most haunted city - a marketing claim that's unprovable but lucrative. Ghost tours depart nightly, visiting sites where spirits allegedly linger: the Pirate's House (built 1753, mentioned in 'Treasure Island'), the Marshall House (a Civil War hospital where limbs were buried in the basement), the Moon River Brewing Company (abandoned for decades, now serving haunted pints). Yellow fever epidemics killed thousands in the 1800s; mass graves lie beneath modern streets. The colonial cemetery on Oglethorpe Avenue contains more bodies than markers. Whether ghosts exist is beside the point - Savannah's atmosphere invites belief in the uncanny. The live oaks and Spanish moss and centuries-old buildings look like they should be haunted.

## River Street

River Street runs along the Savannah River waterfront, its cobblestones descended from the ballast stones that English ships dumped after unloading cargo. The cotton warehouses that lined the bluff have become restaurants, shops, and hotels, connected by steep stone stairways to the city above. It's touristy now - taffy shops and T-shirt stores mixing with genuinely good restaurants - but the bones are authentic. River Street was where cotton was king, where fortunes were made shipping the South's primary crop. Container ships still pass on the river, heading to the port facilities downstream. The waterfront that built Savannah's wealth now sustains its tourism.

## Living in the Past

Savannah/Hilton Head International Airport (SAV) lies 15 miles northwest of the Historic District. The city is walkable - the entire Historic District covers about one square mile, perfect for exploring on foot. Horse-drawn carriages offer tours; trolleys circulate among major sites. The SCAD (Savannah College of Art and Design) presence has enlivened the city with students and galleries. Tybee Island, 18 miles east, offers beaches. Bonaventure Cemetery, made famous by the book's cover image, contains graves dating to 1846 beneath moss-draped oaks. From altitude, Savannah appears as a dense historic core near the river, the grid of squares visible even from above - the city Sherman spared, where the South preserved itself, and where ghosts walk nightly for tourist dollars.
