# Massie School

Paid for by a Scottish planter's patient $5,000 bequest, Savannah's first public school opened in 1856, served as a Union hospital and a Freedmen's school, and now teaches the city's history as the Massie Heritage Center.

**Location:** 32.0702, -81.0924

**Categories:** Schools in Savannah, Georgia, Museums in Savannah, Georgia, History museums in Georgia (U.S. state), Civil rights movement museums, Taylor Square (Savannah, Georgia) buildings

## Introduction

Peter Massie never saw the school he paid for. The Scottish-born planter, who made his fortune on the Georgia coast, died in 1840 and left $5,000 to the City of Savannah with a patient request: invest it, let it grow, and when it was large enough, use it to build a free school for the city's poor children. Savannah did as he asked. For some fifteen years the money sat in railroad and gas company stocks, and in 1855 the city hired an architect, and the school rose beside what was then Calhoun Square, today's Taylor Square. The Massie School opened in 1856 as the first public school in Savannah, part of what is described as Georgia's oldest chartered public school system.

## Gray Brick, Greek Revival

The bequest had been invested in the Central Rail Road and Canal Company and the Savannah Gas Light Company. When the time came to build, the city hired John S. Norris, a successful New York architect who had first come to Savannah in 1846 to build the U.S. Custom House. Norris gave the schoolhouse the same Greek Revival style, raising it in Savannah gray brick at 201–213 East Gordon Street. When it opened in 1856, the school welcomed 150 students and three teachers in its first semester, and by the end of the first year enrollment had grown to 240. The school was progressive for its day in admitting both boys and girls, though they were separated in the classroom and given separate outdoor spaces, each with its own outhouse. The school kept growing: architect John Hogg designed wings added in 1872 and 1886, both with facades that replicate Norris's original design.

## Hospital in a Cold Winter

In December 1864, General William T. Sherman and his army of 60,000 Union soldiers captured Savannah, and the school closed its doors to students. The building became a hospital for his troops. It suited the purpose well: it was large, and a powerful coal-burning furnace in the basement carried heat throughout the building. But the Union naval blockade of the Savannah River had cut the city off from vital supplies, coal among them. With no coal to be had, the occupying troops burned whatever came to hand for warmth, including the school's own furniture, and in the process irreparably broke the furnace.

## A Brief Door Opened

In May 1865 the Massie School briefly became a Freedmen's school. The Reverend S. W. Magill led the effort, drawing on teachers from the American Missionary Association, and the classrooms filled with recently emancipated people eager to learn. So many enrolled that the program had to expand beyond the building, moving into several churches and other locations formerly operated by the Savannah Education Association. The opening did not last. In October of that same year, the Massie School was designated a white school once more, and it kept that status until public schools were integrated in the 1960s.

## A Schoolhouse That Became a Lesson

The Massie School served as a public elementary school until the end of the 1974 school year, when its ongoing costs could no longer be justified against falling enrollment. Its survival after that owed much to two people. In 1975, Emma Adler, a Savannah native and the wife of fellow preservationist Lee Adler, joined with former school superintendent Saxon Pope Bargeron to plan a future for the empty schoolhouse. Together they formed the Friends of Massie Committee to restore the buildings and grounds and to create a heritage education program. Their research uncovered the site's rich history, and in November 1976 Bargeron nominated the building to the National Register of Historic Places; it was listed in April 1977. A heritage classroom opened there in 1976, and the Massie Heritage Center was under way by 1978. It includes a re-created 19th-century schoolroom and exhibits on local history, public education, coastal Georgia culture, the civil rights movement and Savannah's architecture. Still managed by Savannah-Chatham Public Schools and open to the public, it is reported to welcome more than 10,000 student visitors a year.

## Flight Context

The Massie School stands beside Taylor Square, formerly Calhoun Square, at about 32.0702 N, 81.0924 W, in the southern part of Savannah's historic district, a short distance north of Forsyth Park. From 2,000 to 2,500 feet, the grid of tree-filled squares is easy to read; the large green rectangle of Forsyth Park is the best landmark, with the school a few blocks to its northeast. Savannah/Hilton Head International Airport (KSAV) is about 6.5 nm west-northwest, and Hunter Army Air Field (KSVN), a military field, is about 4.5 nm southwest, so downtown sightseeing flights operate in busy airspace; coordinate with Savannah approach control. Summer brings haze and afternoon thunderstorms.
