# Candler Oak Tree

Growing beside Savannah's Forsyth Park for an estimated 250 to 450 years, the Candler Oak spreads twice as wide as it is tall - and in 1984 became what is billed as the first single tree in the nation protected by a conservation easement.

**Location:** 32.0676, -81.0963

**Categories:** Individual oak trees, Savannah, Georgia, Individual trees in Georgia (U.S. state)

## Introduction

The Candler Oak is twice as wide as it is tall. Its crown spreads an average of 110 feet, while the tree itself rises only 54 feet, so that its great limbs reach far out to the sides beside Drayton Street in Savannah, Georgia. Estimated to have been growing since the 1700s or earlier, with age estimates running from about 250 to 450 years, it is one of the oldest living landmarks in the area - and it owes its survival to people who decided, again and again, that a single tree was worth defending.

## Measuring a Neighbor

Numbers only begin to describe the Candler Oak. Its trunk has a circumference of 17 feet and a diameter of 63 inches, and its crown, averaging 110 feet across, spreads far wider than the tree is tall. The oak stands at 516 Drayton Street, on property now owned by the Savannah College of Art and Design, right beside Forsyth Park. That location has been both its good fortune and its risk. A tree beside a beloved park in a growing city draws admirers, but it also sits on land that someone, sooner or later, will want to use for something else.

## An Easement for a Single Tree

In 1984, the Savannah Tree Foundation found a way to put the oak's protection in writing. It secured what is described as the nation's first conservation easement on a single tree, establishing a protected area of about 6,804 square feet around the Candler Oak to keep it from being lost to development. A legal safeguard, in other words, was wrapped around one living thing. Recognition followed. In 2001, the Georgia Urban Forest Council designated the Candler Oak a Georgia Landmark and Historic Tree. The designation treated the oak as a piece of history in its own right, not just a fine specimen of scenery.

## Roots Under Pavement

Protection on paper did not undo every harm. According to the Savannah Tree Foundation, one of the Candler Oak's toughest historical hurdles was a thick, three-inch layer of asphalt paved right up against its trunk. Decades of trapped roots and suffocated soil left the tree under heavy stress, with dead twigs appearing throughout its canopy. The foundation's early management program for the oak became what it calls a real turning point, setting a new precedent for how Savannah handled its historic canopy. In that sense, the tree has taught the city something about caring for all its old giants.

## A Law School's Emblem

In 2012, Savannah Law School purchased the historic property where the Candler Oak grows, and the school took its stewardship seriously. It put up fences, reserved space for the tree's lowest branches and placed the oak under security surveillance, as if it were a treasure in a museum. The tree even became the law school's logo. The building was sold to the Savannah College of Art and Design in 2018, the law school later closed, and SCAD owns the property today. Through each change of hands, the oak has stayed put, still spreading its crown beside Drayton Street as it has for an estimated 250 to 450 years.

## Flight Context

The Candler Oak stands at 32.0676 N, 81.0963 W on Drayton Street along the eastern edge of Forsyth Park in Savannah's historic district, Georgia. From 1,000-2,000 feet, Forsyth Park is the unmistakable landmark: a 30-acre green rectangle in the historic district, bordered by Drayton Street on the east. A single tree is hard to pick out from altitude, so use the park's east edge as the reference. Hunter Army Airfield (KSVN), a military field, is about 4.3 nm southwest, and Savannah/Hilton Head International Airport (KSAV) about 6.4 nm northwest, so expect controlled airspace over downtown. Hilton Head Airport (KHXD) lies about 22 nm east-northeast. Coastal haze is the usual visibility limit.
