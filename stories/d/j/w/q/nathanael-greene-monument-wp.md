# Nathanael Greene Monument

The marble obelisk in Savannah's Johnson Square began as Lafayette's 1825 tribute to two Revolutionary generals, became Nathanael Greene's alone, and since 1902 has also been his grave.

## Introduction

In October 2018, someone stuck googly eyes on the bronze face of a Revolutionary War general in the middle of Savannah, and the prank made national news. CNN, CBS News and USA Today all covered it. The face belonged to Nathanael Greene, and the monument it decorates is far more than a backdrop for a joke. The marble obelisk in Johnson Square has been a political stage, a fundraising headache and, for more than a century, a tomb.

## Lafayette Lays the Stone

Nathanael Greene was born in Rhode Island in 1742 and raised as a pacifist Quaker, yet as the Revolution approached he helped found a militia and rose to become a general in the Continental Army. Late in the war he commanded the Continental army in the South, and his strategy helped drive the British out of Georgia. The state rewarded him with Mulberry Grove Plantation, seized from loyalists, and in 1786 named Greene County in his honor. He died at Mulberry Grove later that same year and was buried in Savannah's Colonial Park Cemetery. On March 21, 1825, the Marquis de Lafayette, visiting the United States, laid the cornerstones for two Savannah monuments, one for Greene in Johnson Square and one for Casimir Pulaski in Chippewa Square. At the Greene ceremony, held with local Masonic lodges, Lafayette declared that "in the very name of Greene are remembered, all the virtues and talents which can illustrate the patriot, the statesman, and the military leader."

## One Obelisk for Two Generals

Lofty words did not pay for marble. Fundraising for both monuments went poorly, and in November 1826 the Georgia General Assembly authorized a lottery to raise $35,000 for the cause. Money remained hard to come by, so the commissioners decided to build only the one in Johnson Square and make it a joint monument to both Greene and Pulaski. Designed by William Strickland, the 50-foot marble shaft was completed in 1830. It remained a shared tribute until 1853, when enough money had been raised to erect a separate Pulaski monument in Monterey Square, and the Johnson Square obelisk became Greene's alone. Seven years later, it took on a very different role. After the 1860 presidential election, the monument became the site of secession celebrations, draped with a large banner showing a rattlesnake and the words "Don't Tread on Me."

## Finishing the Monument

In 1879, Savannah's city council formed a committee with the Georgia Historical Society to plan the "finishing" of the monument. They agreed to add two bronze plaques and split the cost evenly. The plaques were unveiled in May 1886. One carries a bas-relief portrait of Greene; the other records his life, calling him "Soldier Patriot" and "The Friend Of Washington" and noting that the shaft was "reared by the People Of Savannah." The ceremony's speaker was Jefferson Davis, the former president of the Confederacy, whose father had served in the Continental Army during the Revolution. Davis praised Greene, and he also used the occasion to defend the Lost Cause of the Confederacy while urging reconciliation after the Civil War - a reminder that even a Revolutionary memorial could be pressed into the arguments of its own era.

## The General Comes Home

For years, the exact location of Greene's grave in Colonial Park Cemetery was debated. In 1901 his body was finally identified in a vault there, and on November 14, 1902, it was reinterred beneath the monument. The remains of his son, George Washington Greene, were interred with him; according to a 2014 Savannah Morning News article, the two had been buried in the same vault and could not be told apart. The Savannah chapter of the Daughters of the American Revolution placed a bronze wreath directly above Greene's body, and the Governor of Rhode Island and the president of the Society of the Cincinnati attended the ceremony. A Georgia historical marker followed in 1953, and in 2012 a local garden club raised money for a wrought iron fence. Then came 2018, the googly eyes, and a police report for trespassing - proof, perhaps, that the old general still draws attention.

## Flight Context

The Nathanael Greene Monument stands at 32.0799 N, 81.0916 W in the center of Johnson Square, the largest square in Savannah's historic district, Georgia, a few blocks south of the Savannah River waterfront. From 1,500-2,000 feet, find the grid of green squares along Bull Street; the obelisk marks the middle of the northernmost, largest square. Hunter Army Airfield (KSVN), a military field, lies about 5 nm southwest, and Savannah/Hilton Head International Airport (KSAV) about 6.3 nm west-northwest, so expect controlled airspace over downtown. Hilton Head Airport (KHXD) is about 22 nm east-northeast. Low coastal terrain means haze is the usual visibility limit.
