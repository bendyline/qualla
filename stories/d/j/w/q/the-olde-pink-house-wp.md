# The Olde Pink House

Built in 1789 for merchant James Habersham Jr., Savannah's Olde Pink House survived the fire of 1796, became one of Georgia's first banks, and now serves dinner beside 18th-century kitchen hearths rediscovered in its basement.

**Location:** 32.0797, -81.0898

**Categories:** Restaurants in Savannah, Georgia, Houses completed in 1789, Georgian architecture in Georgia (U.S. state), Reynolds Square (Savannah, Georgia) buildings, Savannah Historic District, Taverns in Georgia (U.S. state)

## Introduction

The fireplaces had been hidden since the 1800s. When Herschel McCallar Jr. and his partner Jeffrey Keith began restoring an old house on Savannah's Reynolds Square in 1970, they uncovered a pair of fireplaces in the basement that had been covered over at some point in the nineteenth century. The twin hearths had been the house's original cooking kitchen in the 18th century. Today they are the highlight of the bar at The Olde Pink House, a restaurant and tavern in a building that has been a merchant's home, one of Georgia's first banks, an architect's office, a bookstore and a tea room since it was completed in 1789.

## Habersham's House

The building was first known as Habersham House, after its owner, James Habersham Jr., a Savannah merchant, planter and slave trader from one of the colony's founding families. The lot itself had begun as a land grant from the British Crown. Habersham lived in the house until his death in 1799, and in 1796 he saw it survive the Savannah fire that destroyed 229 buildings in the city. It stands on the northwestern trust lot of Reynolds Square, bounded by East Bryan Street to the north, Abercorn Street to the east and East Saint Julian Street to the south. One of its key features is a Palladian window above the portico; the portico itself, along with one section of the building, was added in the early 1800s (sources give 1812 or around 1820). Directly across East Saint Julian Street stands the Oliver Sturges House, built 24 years after its neighbor.

## Banknotes, Blueprints and Tea

In 1812 the house became home to Planters Bank, one of the first banks in Georgia. After the Civil War it changed hands several times, and each new owner found a different use for its rooms. It became the architectural practice of Henrik Wallin, then an attorney's office, then a bookstore. Between 1930 and 1943 it was Alida Harper Fowlkes' Georgian Tea Room, where the portico and Palladian window greeted a new kind of guest. In 1968 the building was bought and restored by Jim Williams, the owner of Mercer House. Each chapter left the structure standing, and by the late 1960s the old merchant's home had been a bank, a workplace, a shop and a dining room, a sequence that anticipated what it would become next.

## Lifting the House

In 1970 McCallar and Keith bought the building for around $60,000 and spent a year restoring it. The work was ambitious. They jacked up the entire building, installed I-beams to upgrade the foundation, and set it back down, and when they did, the doorways and mouldings settled back into place as if new. They found the twin basement fireplaces. They removed a Victorian staircase and lowered the original 18th-century section of stairs to the first floor, the staircase visitors still see when they walk in, and they added a staircase down into the tavern so no one had to go outside to reach it. New plumbing, wiring and a kitchen followed. The partners made several buying trips to England for the 18th-century antiques and paintings that still furnish the rooms, and Keith opened an antique store on the second floor. The restaurant opened in 1971.

## Thirteen Dining Rooms

McCallar died in 1990, and two years later Keith sold the building to William and Elizabeth Balish, who kept the restoration intact. In 2006 an expansion added the Arches Bar on the building's southern side. Below ground, the cellar tavern, Planters Tavern, holds a single-table wine vault for special occasions, and in all the house has thirteen dining rooms. It has not been spared trouble. In December 2018 a fire broke out in the upstairs ballroom, and the damage closed the restaurant for four months; in 2020 the building was repainted. Savannah even has a near-twin: in 1928 an almost identical house was built at 102 East Gaston Street, near Forsyth Park.

## Flight Context

The Olde Pink House stands at about 32.0797 N, 81.0898 W on Reynolds Square in the northern part of Savannah's historic district, only a couple of blocks south of the Savannah River waterfront. From 2,000 to 2,500 feet, the historic district's grid of tree-filled squares is easy to read; Reynolds Square lies on Abercorn Street, just east of the Bull Street spine and close to the river. Savannah/Hilton Head International Airport (KSAV) is about 6.5 nm west-northwest, and Hunter Army Air Field (KSVN), a military field, is about 5 nm southwest, so downtown sightseeing flights operate in busy airspace; coordinate with Savannah approach control. Summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are common.
