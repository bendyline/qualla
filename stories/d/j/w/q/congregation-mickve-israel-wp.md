# Congregation Mickve Israel

Founded by Jews who reached Savannah in 1733, Mickve Israel still uses, on special occasions, the Torah scroll they carried across the Atlantic - inside a Gothic Revival synagogue built, to one letter-writer's astonishment, in the shape of a cross.

## Introduction

"You are the first Israelites I have ever heard of to build a church in the shape of a cross." So wrote a woman from Pennsylvania in the 1870s, not long after the plans for Savannah's new synagogue were confirmed. She was right about the shape. The Gothic Revival building on Monterey Square is laid out in a cruciform plan, and its tower ends in something closer to a gazebo than a steeple. What she misread was the meaning. The congregation inside was not drifting toward her fold; it was one of the oldest Jewish congregations in the United States, and it had been in Georgia since the colony's first year.

## A Ship from London

On July 11, 1733, the ship William and Sarah reached Savannah from London carrying 41 Jews, though some counts say 42. The colony James Oglethorpe had founded was only a few months old. Most of the passengers were Sephardic Jews of Spanish and Portuguese descent, many from families that had fled persecution in Portugal and Spain, and Georgia offered them yet another new beginning. They had brought a Sefer Torah with them. In July 1735, many of these settlers established Kahal Kadosh Mickva Israel - the Holy Congregation, the Hope of Israel. The scroll that crossed the ocean with them is still used at the synagogue on special occasions, a physical thread running back nearly three centuries to that first voyage.

## Fire and Brick

For decades the congregation had no purpose-built home. That changed on July 21, 1820, when a small wooden building at the northeast corner of Liberty and Whitaker Streets was consecrated as the first synagogue built in the State of Georgia. It lasted less than ten years. On December 4, 1829, fire destroyed the building, but the congregation managed to save its Torah scrolls. The members rebuilt on the same site, this time in brick, and dedicated the new synagogue in 1841.

## A Cross-Shaped Synagogue

The cornerstone of the present synagogue was laid on March 1, 1876, and the building was consecrated in 1878. Its architect was Henry G. Harrison of New York, whose work also included the Cathedral of the Incarnation in Garden City, New York, begun the same year. Harrison set out the synagogue in a cruciform plan more typical of Christian churches. In an unusual flourish, the tower is topped with an open, gazebo-shaped belvedere crowned by an almost Islamic-inspired cupola. The design prompted that letter from Pennsylvania, whose writer took comfort in the idea that the congregation had "taken this step to enter our fold" and marveled that its members had consented to a building "so ultra-Christian in form." The congregation's answer was the building itself, which has served as a synagogue ever since.

## Two Hundred Years in Georgia

In 1902, part of the synagogue's lot became the site of the Mordecai Sheftall Memorial, a building with meeting rooms and space for a religious school. On January 10, 1904, the congregation joined the Union of American Hebrew Congregations, and today it is a Reform congregation. On May 7, 1933, a capacity crowd of Jews and prominent Christians gathered at the synagogue to mark the 200th anniversary of the arrival of Jews in the colony of Georgia. The planned speaker, Harold Hirsch of Atlanta, was unable to attend, but the celebration went on. As the congregation's needs grew, the original Sheftall Memorial became too small. An expanded replacement was dedicated on January 11, 1957.

## Inside the Sanctuary

The synagogue stands at 20 East Gordon Street on Monterey Square, within the Savannah Historic District, and it welcomes visitors on weekday tours of about forty-five minutes, except on public and Jewish holidays. Inside, the bimah and ark stand at the front of the sanctuary. On the northern side, facing Wayne Street, a stained-glass window memorializes Moses Ferst. A museum component displays the congregation's artifacts and Judaica, the tangible record of a community that has worshipped in Savannah since 1733.
