# Wright Square (Savannah, Georgia)

One of Savannah's four original 1733 squares, Wright Square holds the grave of the Creek leader Tomochichi, whose original stone marker was lost by 1883 and who was honored anew in 1899 with a granite boulder billed, famously, as payable on Judgment Day.

## Introduction

The bill came to a dollar, or perhaps fifty cents, depending on which account you trust, and it was payable on Judgment Day. The Stone Mountain Monument Company had offered its granite for free, but Mrs. Gordon, working with the Colonial Dames, insisted on paying. So the company sent an invoice that could not come due until the end of the world, and she paid it anyway, attaching a note explaining that on Judgment Day the Dames "would be too busy attending their own duties on that momentous day." The monument she paid for still stands in Wright Square, in the heart of Savannah, honoring Tomochichi, the Creek leader buried there, who helped James Oglethorpe found Georgia.

## Percival Square

Wright Square was laid out in 1733, one of the first four squares in Oglethorpe's new town. It did not start out with its present name. It was first called Percival Square, for John Percival, 1st Earl of Egmont, and in 1763 it was renamed to honor James Wright, the third and final royal governor of Georgia. The square sits in the second of Savannah's five rows of squares, where Bull Street meets President Street. Johnson Square lies to the north, Chippewa Square to the south, Oglethorpe Square to the east and Telfair Square to the west. Around it, following the pattern now known as the Oglethorpe Plan, are eight blocks: four residential tything blocks and four civic trust blocks.

## A Friend of the Colony

Tomochichi was a leader of the Creek nation and a trusted friend of Oglethorpe, and he assisted him in founding the colony. When Tomochichi died in 1739, Oglethorpe ordered him buried with military honors in the center of what was then Percival Square. In keeping with the customs of Tomochichi's people, the grave was marked with a pyramid of stones gathered from the surrounding area. Accounts differ on how long the pyramid lasted: the Georgia Historical Society says it was gone by the 1830s, while another account has a remnant surviving into the 1880s. Either way, the burial at the center of the square was a reminder that the colony had not been founded by English settlers alone.

## Payable on Judgment Day

In 1883, citizens who wished to honor William Washington Gordon replaced what was left of Tomochichi's original marker with an elaborate and highly allegorical monument to Gordon at the center of the square. The change upset Nellie Gordon, William's daughter-in-law, and she joined the Colonial Dames in planning a new monument to Tomochichi, to be made of granite from Stone Mountain. That was when the Stone Mountain Monument Company offered the stone at no cost, and Mrs. Gordon, feeling she was being condescended to, insisted on paying. The famous Judgment Day bill followed. The new monument was erected in 1899 in the southeast corner of the square, where it still eulogizes Tomochichi as a great friend of James Oglethorpe and of the people of Georgia. The Gordon Monument holds the center of the square; the Creek leader's memorial stands in a corner of the square where he was laid to rest.

## Courthouses and Keys

Around the square, Savannah's civic and commercial life crowd together. The oldest building is the William Waring Property at 12 West State Street, dating to 1825. Along Bull Street stand the Tomochichi Federal Building and United States Court House at 125 Bull, the Old Chatham County Court House at 124 Bull and the Lutheran Church of the Ascension, while the late 19th century left a run of commercial blocks: the John Schwarz Building of 1890, and the A. R. Altmeyer Building and the Peter Ott Property, both from 1892. Bradley Lock and Key, founded in 1883 and doing business on the square, is described as the oldest operating business in Savannah. Film fans may recognize 6 East State Street, which doubled as Dixie's Flowers, the flower shop where Mandy, played by Alison Eastwood, works in Midnight in the Garden of Good and Evil.

## Flight Context

Wright Square lies at about 32.0779 N, 81.0923 W in the heart of Savannah's historic district, the second square south of the river along Bull Street, the district's north-south spine. From 2,000 to 2,500 feet, follow Bull Street south from the Savannah River waterfront: Johnson Square comes first, then Wright Square. Savannah/Hilton Head International Airport (KSAV) is about 6 nm west-northwest, and Hunter Army Air Field (KSVN), a military field, is about 5 nm southwest, so downtown sightseeing flights operate in busy airspace; coordinate with Savannah approach control. Summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are common.
