# First African Baptist Church (Savannah, Georgia)

Built by free and enslaved Black Savannahians who, by tradition, made its bricks after the day's labor was done, First African Baptist Church is said to have hidden freedom seekers beneath its floor and traces its congregation to 1773.

**Location:** 32.0814, -81.0963

**Categories:** Baptist churches in Georgia (U.S. state), Churches on the Underground Railroad, African-American history in Savannah, Georgia, History of slavery in Georgia (U.S. state), African-American museums in Georgia (U.S. state), Churches on the National Register of Historic Places in Georgia (U.S. state)

## Introduction

At first glance, the holes in the sanctuary floor look like a decorative pattern. They were air holes. Beneath the floorboards of First African Baptist Church in Savannah is a four-foot space where, according to the church's tradition, people escaping slavery were hidden, and those openings are said to have let them breathe. The church above them was built in the 1850s by free African Americans and enslaved people, who, by long-told accounts, made its bricks and raised its walls after the day's labor was done. Completed in 1859, it was the first brick building in Georgia owned by African Americans. The congregation that gathers inside is older still. It claims descent from the first Black Baptist congregation in North America, with roots reaching back to 1773.

## A Preacher Licensed in 1773

The story begins with George Leile, an enslaved man who in 1773 became the first African American licensed by the Baptists to preach in Georgia. His license was to preach to enslaved people on the plantations along the Savannah River, in Georgia and South Carolina. Leile's master, a Baptist deacon, freed him before the Revolutionary War, and Leile went on converting and baptizing enslaved people across the region. One of them was David George, among eight people baptized who formed the Silver Bluff Baptist Church in Aiken County, South Carolina, across the river from Augusta. George became an elder and preacher there, and over the next few years he drew nearly 30 members. Leile's preaching also reached the people who would become the first members of the Savannah church.

## Freedom Behind British Lines

The Revolution scattered and remade these congregations. The British had offered freedom to enslaved people who escaped from rebel masters, and in 1778 Leile made his way to British-occupied Savannah, where he would be secure behind British lines. When the British took the city, David George's Patriot master fled, and all the members of the Silver Bluff church went to Savannah to claim their freedom, joining with some of Leile's group. Leile kept preaching. Among those he converted were Andrew Bryan and his wife, Hannah. In 1782 the British evacuated hundreds of Black people from Savannah, carrying many to Nova Scotia and other colonies and some to London. Leile and his family sailed to freedom in Jamaica. David George and his family went with the Loyalists to Nova Scotia and later to Sierra Leone. Both men founded Baptist congregations in their new homes.

## The One Who Stayed

Andrew Bryan, who purchased his own freedom, was the only one of the three early Black Baptist preachers to remain in Savannah and the new United States. He became the congregation's leader, and it was officially recognized in 1788, with Bryan as pastor until 1812; the church itself dates its formal constitution as a body of believers to December 1777. In the 1790s the members built a frame church on land Bryan had bought and named it Bryan Street African Baptist Church. The congregation's later brick church is remembered as a refuge for people escaping slavery. The congregation's claim to be the first of its kind is contested: Silver Bluff Baptist Church dates its founding to 1773, and the First Baptist Church of Petersburg, Virginia, was officially organized in 1774. Seventeen pastors have led the church over its long history, among them Andrew Cox Marshall, Bryan's successor, who led the congregation until 1856.

## Bricks Made After the Fields

The present sanctuary stands at the corner of West Bryan and Montgomery streets, across from Franklin Square in Savannah's historic district. Its builders were free and enslaved, and by tradition the enslaved among them worked on the church only after the day's labor was done. Upstairs, the balcony still holds some of the original pews made by enslaved people, carved with West African Arabic script. The stained-glass windows, dating to the 1880s, depict African-American subjects, including former pastors. A hurricane destroyed the original, much taller bell tower, in the 1890s by most accounts. The building is a contributing property of the Savannah Historic District on the National Register of Historic Places, and it carries its history in its fabric: in the floor said to have sheltered freedom seekers, in the pews, and in bricks that tradition says were made by hands that had already given a full day's labor.

## A Living Archive

The church's public role did not end with emancipation. Participants in the early Civil Rights Movement in Savannah held weekly meetings here. Today the church runs a museum whose archives and memorabilia reach back to the congregation's beginnings in 1773. It holds pictures of the church's seventeen pastors, written records from the 1800s onward, communion sets dating to 1814, newspaper articles from 1861 and handmade quilts displayed with the stories behind their designs. The church began offering public tours in the early 1970s, and an estimated 20,000 to 25,000 visitors now come through each year. The congregation is affiliated with the National Baptist Convention, USA, one of the largest Baptist organizations in the world.
