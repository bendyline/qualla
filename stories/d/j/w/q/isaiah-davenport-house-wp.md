# Isaiah Davenport House

A master builder's 1820 showpiece on Columbia Square slid into decline, came within reach of the wrecking ball in 1955, and became the first rescue of the Historic Savannah Foundation.

**Location:** 32.0773, -81.0880

**Categories:** Houses completed in 1820, Museums in Savannah, Georgia, Historic house museums in Georgia (U.S. state), Historic American Buildings Survey in Georgia (U.S. state), Houses in Savannah, Georgia, National Register of Historic Places in Savannah, Georgia, Columbia Square (Savannah, Georgia) buildings, Savannah Historic District

## Introduction

In 1955, the Isaiah Davenport House was slated to come down for a parking lot. By then the once-stately Federal home on Columbia Square had become a rundown rooming house in a part of town that had lost its shine. A group of community-minded Savannahians decided otherwise. They pooled their resources, bought the house, and in doing so took the first action of the Historic Savannah Foundation - an organization that would go on to save hundreds of buildings across the city.

## A Builder's Calling Card

Isaiah Davenport was a master builder from New England, an upwardly mobile artisan who had made his way in Savannah. Around 1820 he and his crew finished a Federal-style house at 324 East State Street, on the northwest corner of Columbia Square, and it first appeared on the tax rolls in 1821. The house served two purposes. It sheltered his growing household, and it advertised his craft: the house itself was a demonstration of what Isaiah Davenport could build. The household that filled it was large. Davenport lived there with his wife, Sarah Clark Davenport, their growing family (ten children were born, and seven lived to adulthood) and, by the museum's accounts, nine or ten enslaved people whose labor kept the house and his business running.

## The People Who Worked the House

The museum today tells their stories by name. By its account, the number of people Davenport held in slavery grew from two in 1810 to nine or ten by 1827. Among them were Ned and Dave, and Bella with her children Jack, Jacob, Isaac and Polly, as well as two other children named Peggy and Nancy. Most of the women labored as domestics, caught in the constant round of cooking, washing, child care and cleaning. Dave worked as a waggoner, and others were listed as "mechanic," probably skilled laborers put to work in Davenport's building trade. Where they slept is uncertain: perhaps in the ground-floor workspace, in the garret, at the foot of their enslavers' beds, or above the carriage house. The comfort of the family rooms rested, in part, on their unpaid and unfree labor.

## Boarders, Baynards and Decline

Isaiah Davenport died in 1827, and Sarah turned the house into a boarding house. She stayed on Columbia Square until 1840, when she sold the property to the Baynard family of South Carolina, who kept it for the next 109 years. Over that long stretch, the neighborhood changed around it. The fashionable address slipped, and the house became a rundown rooming house in a seedy part of town. Even in that neglected state, its quality showed through. In the 1930s, New Deal surveyors with the Historic American Buildings Survey recognized its architectural significance and carefully identified and measured it, leaving a detailed record of the building at its lowest ebb.

## Where Preservation Began

After the 1955 rescue, the house became the office of the Historic Savannah Foundation and a family services agency. In 1957, Savannah landscape architect Clermont Huger Lee drew up a period-specific plan and wall detail for the garden. The first floor opened to the public as a museum on March 9, 1963, and in later years the upper floors followed as the foundation moved its offices elsewhere. From the mid-1980s, the museum's leaders worked to meet professional museum standards. In the mid-1990s, a restoration funded through a drive led by Cornelia Groves and Clare Ellis brought back period wallpaper and room furnishings based on the inventory taken when Isaiah Davenport died in 1827. Groves went on to establish the Friends of Davenport House in 2003. The foundation that began here, meanwhile, became known for its revolving loan fund and a long list of rescues across the city.
