# Beach Institute African-American Cultural Center

Built in 1867 as one of Savannah's first schools for African Americans after the Civil War, the Beach Institute survived fire, closure and auction to become a cultural center for Black history and art.

**Location:** 32.0727, -81.0883

## Introduction

The name on this Savannah schoolhouse belongs to the editor of Scientific American. Alfred E. Beach donated money for the property, and in return a school built for African Americans in the first years after the Civil War carried his name. In the century and a half since, the two-story frame building on Harris Street has returned again and again to the same job: teaching.

## Eight Classrooms and a Chapel

The Beach Institute opened in 1867 as one of the first schools for African Americans in Savannah after the war. The Freedmen's Bureau built it, with major funding from the American Missionary Association, and a marker at the site credits the labor of newly freed people. Architect John Boutell designed a two-story frame building with eight classrooms, each meant for eighty students, and a chapel that could hold five hundred. He gave it a raised basement, wooden pilasters and a hipped roof, crowned on the Harris Street side by a prominent pedimented gable. Demand was immediate. When the school opened, about 600 students enrolled, taught by nine women and a male principal. Most of the teachers were white. By 1873, tuition was one dollar a month.

## Fire, Transfers and a Basement Full of Boys

In 1874 the institute was leased to the Savannah Board of Education and became a free public school for African American children. Four years later, in 1878, a fire damaged the building and temporarily halted classes. The American Missionary Association then took back control, aiming to raise academic standards beyond those the local school authorities maintained. For several decades afterward, the Beach Institute remained a leading place of learning for Savannah's African American students. The building found new uses as well. In 1917, the Savannah Boys Club began meeting in a single basement room. The club soon spread through the entire basement, holding educational programs for African American boys throughout the week.

## Closed, Auctioned, Reborn

By 1919, local African American students were attending newer schools, including Savannah's first African American public high school and the Georgia State Industrial College at Thunderbolt. Enrollment at the Beach Institute declined, and it closed. The building still had work to do. In 1939, the Chatham County Board of Education took it over from the AMA and ran it as a public school for Black children until 1970. In 1988, the local school board put the building up for auction, and Paula Wallace, a co-founder of the Savannah College of Art and Design who became its president in 2000, bought it. Inspired by her friend W. W. Law, a Savannah civil rights leader, she donated it to the King-Tisdell Cottage Foundation, which Law headed. SCAD also paid for the labor and materials to renovate the exterior, restoring its original architectural character. In 1990, the building reopened as the Beach Institute African-American Cultural Center.

## Carved Wood and Living Memory

Today the building's galleries hold a crowd of carved figures. The permanent Davis Collection, housed in the John B. and Mozelle D. Clemmons Galleries, holds somewhere between 200 and 240 wood sculptures by folk artist Ulysses Davis, depending on the count. Nearby, the Grand Gallery hosts changing exhibitions and cultural programs, and rotating shows feature contemporary artists exploring African American themes. Past exhibitions have taken on Black cultural traditions, spiritual folk art and historical narratives from the Savannah area. The teaching mission continues too, through guided tours, lectures, workshops and seminars on Gullah-Geechee heritage, food justice and historic preservation. The center even offers genealogy help, so that visitors can trace their own family histories in a building where generations of Savannah's Black children went to school.

## Flight Context

The Beach Institute stands at 32.0727 N, 81.0883 W in Savannah's Historic District, Georgia, on Harris Street about 0.2 miles east of Lafayette Square. From 1,500-2,000 feet, the downtown grid of squares is distinctive; use the spires of the Cathedral Basilica of St. John the Baptist on Lafayette Square as a reference, with the Savannah River along the north edge of downtown. Hunter Army Airfield (KSVN), a military field, lies about 4.8 nm southwest, and Savannah/Hilton Head International Airport (KSAV) about 6.5 nm west-northwest, so expect controlled airspace over the city. Hilton Head Airport (KHXD) is about 22 nm east-northeast. Low coastal terrain means haze is the usual visibility limit.
