# Green–Meldrim House

In this Gothic Revival mansion on Savannah's Madison Square, Sherman offered Lincoln the city as a Christmas gift, and twenty Black ministers and church leaders met with him in the conversation that led to the promise of forty acres and a mule.

**Location:** 32.0738, -81.0948

**Categories:** National Historic Landmarks in Savannah, Georgia, Historic house museums in Georgia (U.S. state), Gothic Revival architecture in Georgia (U.S. state), Madison Square (Savannah, Georgia) buildings, American Civil War sites in Georgia (U.S. state)

## Introduction

On January 12, 1865, twenty Black ministers and church officers came to a Gothic Revival mansion on Savannah's Madison Square to meet General William T. Sherman and Secretary of War Edwin Stanton. What was said in the house that day led directly to the promise remembered as forty acres and a mule. Only weeks earlier, in the same rooms, Sherman had written to President Lincoln offering him the city itself as a Christmas present. The Green–Meldrim House was built for a wealthy cotton merchant, and it is still admired for its ironwork and battlements. Its place in history, though, was secured by what happened inside during a few winter weeks near the end of the Civil War.

## A Cotton Merchant's Castle

The house was designed and built by the architect John S. Norris, with construction beginning around 1850 and, depending on the source, finishing anywhere from 1853 to 1861, at a cost of $93,000. Its first owner was Charles Green, a wealthy cotton merchant who would become the grandfather of the writer Julien Green. The house's Gothic Revival design is counted among the finest and most lavish examples of the style in the American South, and in 1976 the house was designated a National Historic Landmark. It stands at 14 West Macon Street, a spur off Whitaker Street, at the southwest corner of West Harris and Bull streets on the west side of Madison Square. Next door is St. John's Episcopal Church, which owns the house today.

## Iron Lace and Battlements

The principal facade faces south, with a porch and garden looking onto the square. The exterior is stuccoed brick, dressed in cast iron: an iron porch, an imposing cast-iron fence along the front, and oriel windows projecting from the walls. The main entrance has an iron portico believed to be unique in the United States, its octagonal posts supporting a pair of arches. Along the roofline runs a crenellated parapet, giving the house the silhouette of a small castle. Inside, a center-hall plan preserves the original woodwork, plaster and ironwork, including a freestanding staircase.

## Sherman at Madison Square

After Union troops captured Savannah in December 1864, Sherman occupied the house and used it as his headquarters. It was here that he composed his famous message to Lincoln, offering the president "as a Christmas Gift the City of Savannah, with one hundred and fifty heavy guns and plenty of ammunition, and also about twenty-five thousand bales of cotton." For Savannah, it meant that a Union general was now writing dispatches from one of the city's most lavish private homes, a cotton merchant's mansion turned into the command post of the army that had just taken the city. The meeting of January 12, 1865, gave the house a deeper significance. Sherman and Stanton sat down with twenty Black ministers and church officers, among them Garrison Frazier, Ulysses L. Houston and William Gaines. The discussion, remembered as the Savannah Colloquy, led directly to Sherman's Special Field Orders No. 15, which included the famous land allotment of forty acres and a mule. A conversation in a merchant's house became a military order, and the order gave Americans a phrase they still know.

## The Judge, the Church and the Garden

In 1892 the local politician and judge Peter Meldrim bought the property, and he lived there for decades. In 1943 his heirs sold the house to St. John's Church next door. During the 1950s, the Savannah landscape architect Clermont Huger Lee drew up period-appropriate designs and planting plans for the garden. Today the house serves as the church's parish house and also offers daytime tours, ticketed events and community programs. Tours focus on the architecture and on how the house's uses and interiors have changed, not on original furnishings. What visitors see is the architecture itself, the ironwork and the freestanding stair, and the rooms where a general, a secretary of war and twenty ministers and church officers once met.

## Flight Context

The Green–Meldrim House stands at about 32.0738 N, 81.0948 W on Madison Square in Savannah's historic district, one of the leafy squares strung along Bull Street, the district's north-south spine. From 2,000 to 2,500 feet, count the squares south from the Savannah River along Bull Street to find Madison Square; St. John's Episcopal Church stands beside the house. Savannah/Hilton Head International Airport (KSAV) is about 6 nm west-northwest, and Hunter Army Air Field (KSVN), a military field, is about 5 nm southwest, so downtown sightseeing flights operate in busy airspace; coordinate with Savannah approach control. Summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are common.
