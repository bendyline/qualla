# Hilton Head Island, South Carolina

A shoe-shaped Lowcountry island of shell rings, Gullah communities, Civil War history and resorts built around the trees, where dolphins drive fish onto the mud to feed.

**Location:** 32.1789, -80.7431

**Categories:** Islands of South Carolina, Seaside resorts in the United States, Gullah history, Towns in Beaufort County, South Carolina, American Civil War history, Archaeological sites

## Introduction

In the waters around Hilton Head Island, dolphins do something they rarely do anywhere else. They herd schools of fish up onto the mud banks, then lie on their sides to feed before sliding back into the water. The waters here are among the few places on Earth where dolphins routinely use this technique, called strand feeding. It is a fitting introduction to a place that is both busy and wild. Hilton Head is a Lowcountry resort town and barrier island in Beaufort County, South Carolina, about 20 miles northeast of Savannah, with 12 miles of Atlantic beachfront, 37,661 year-round residents at the 2020 census, and a summer peak that can swell the population to 150,000.

## Shells and Sweet Air

People gathered here long before any resort. Near the east entrance of the Sea Pines Forest Preserve lies a ring of shells 150 feet across, believed to be more than 4,000 years old and one of at least 50 such rings known. Archaeologists believe it was a refuse heap left by Native people who lived inside the circle and kept its center clear as a common area. Two other shell rings on the island were destroyed when their shells were hauled away to make tabby for roads and buildings. Europeans arrived much later. A Spanish expedition led by Francisco Gordillo is traditionally said to have explored the area in 1521, and in 1663 Captain William Hilton sailed the Adventure from Barbados to scout lands King Charles II had granted to the eight Lords Proprietors. He noticed a headland near the entrance to Port Royal Sound, named it "Hilton's Head" after himself, and stayed several days, admiring the trees, the crops, the "sweet water" and the "clear sweet air."

## Freedom on the North End

The island's waters were known, occupied and fought over in turn by the English, Spanish, French and Scots. In the 1740s a South Carolina half-galley was stationed in a cove at the southern tip to guard against the Spanish at St. Augustine, and the point still bears its commander's name, Captain David Cutler Braddock. The island's few farmers stayed Loyalist through the Revolution. The Civil War changed everything. On November 7, 1861, Hilton Head fell to a Union naval attack, and more than 12,000 Union troops soon occupied it as an important base for the Union blockade of Southern ports. Hundreds of formerly enslaved people came to the island, where they could buy land, go to school, live in government housing and serve in the First Regiment of South Carolina Volunteers. A community called Mitchelville, named for General Ormsby M. Mitchel, was built on the north end to house them. Martin Delany, commissioned a major in 1865 as the first Black field officer in the U.S. Army, served here too. Many of those freedpeople's descendants, known as the Gullah or Geechee, still live on Hilton Head and have held on to much of their cultural identity.

## Hunting Grounds and Beach Patrols

Wealthy outsiders long valued the island as a place to hunt. In 1890 the shipping magnate William P. Clyde bought 9,000 acres as a private hunting preserve. Three years later, on August 27, 1893, the Sea Islands Hurricane came ashore near Savannah with a 16-foot storm surge and swept north across South Carolina, killing more than 1,000 people and leaving tens of thousands homeless. In the early 1930s the financier and physicist Alfred Lee Loomis and his brother-in-law Landon K. Thorne bought 17,000 acres, more than 63 percent of the island, for a reported $120,000. During World War II, large concrete gun platforms went up along the Atlantic shore, and a training center taught Coast Guard beach patrols to guard the southeastern coast with horses and dogs. Then, in the early 1950s, three lumber mills logged 19,000 acres of the island.

## A Town Built Around the Trees

The resort era began in 1956, the year the James F. Byrnes Bridge was built and Charles E. Fraser began developing Sea Pines Resort. Fraser was a committed environmentalist who reconfigured the entire marina at Harbour Town to save an ancient live oak, and when he died in 2002 he was buried beside it. The Heritage golf tournament was first played at Sea Pines in 1969 and has been a regular PGA Tour stop ever since, now as the RBC Heritage at Harbour Town Golf Links. Hilton Head incorporated as a town in 1983 and wrote conservation into its rules. A Land Management Ordinance governs building styles and how structures sit among existing trees, which is why the island keeps an unusual amount of tree cover for its level of development. About 70 percent of the island lies inside gated communities, but residents have approved multimillion-dollar bond referendums to buy land and control commercial growth.

## Two Islands in One

Seen on a map, Hilton Head is shaped like a shoe, and geologically it is only half a barrier island. The north end is a sea island dating to the Pleistocene, while the south end is a barrier island that formed as recently as the Holocene, and Broad Creek, a land-locked tidal marsh, separates the two halves. Alligators, deer, manatees and hundreds of bird species share the island with its visitors. From May through October, the Coastal Discovery Museum and the South Carolina Department of Natural Resources patrol the beaches to inventory and monitor the nests of endangered loggerhead sea turtles, moving them to safer spots when necessary.

## Flight Context

Hilton Head Island centers on about 32.1789 N, 80.7431 W, roughly 20 miles northeast of Savannah by air. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet the island's shoe shape is easy to read: 12 miles of Atlantic beach on the seaward side, Broad Creek cutting into its middle, and Port Royal Sound off the northern end. Hilton Head Airport (KHXD) is on the island, about 3.5 nm from the town center, and Beaufort Executive Airport (KARW) is about 15 nm north. Marine Corps Air Station Beaufort (KNBC) lies about 18 nm north, so check military airspace, and Savannah/Hilton Head International (KSAV) is about 23.5 nm west. Expect sea breezes, summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms; fall and spring bring the clearest views of the marshes and sounds.
