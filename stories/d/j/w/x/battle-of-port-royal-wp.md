# Battle of Port Royal

On November 7, 1861, a Union fleet steaming in circles battered two Confederate forts into silence at the mouth of South Carolina's Port Royal Sound, seizing a harbor that became the base for the blockade of the southern coast.

**Location:** 32.2500, -80.6600

**Categories:** Naval battles of the American Civil War, Battles of the American Civil War in South Carolina, Beaufort County, South Carolina, Hilton Head Island, 1861 in the American Civil War

## Introduction

Shortly after noon on November 7, 1861, a Union warship that had been held up by a storm finally reached the mouth of Port Royal Sound and swung into position to rake Fort Walker from the flank. Her captain was Commander Percival Drayton of the United States Navy. The Confederate general commanding the forces ashore was his brother, Brigadier General Thomas F. Drayton. The fort they were fighting over stood on Hilton Head Island, guarding a broad harbor between Savannah and Charleston that the Union Navy badly wanted and the Confederacy had never quite finished defending. By early afternoon, its gunners were walking away.

## A Harbor Worth the Risk

Early in the war, the Navy's blockade of the Southern coast depended on coal-burning steamships that had to return to Northern ports for fuel and supplies. A commission chaired by Captain Samuel Francis Du Pont studied the problem and judged Port Royal the best of the harbors it considered, though it expected the place to be strongly defended. Confederate General P. G. T. Beauregard doubted the sound could be held at all, since forts on opposite shores would be too far apart to support each other. Overruled by Governor Francis Pickens, he drew plans for two forts anyway, Fort Walker on Hilton Head and Fort Beauregard on Phillip's Island. The engineer who built them, Major Francis D. Lee, had been an architect of Charleston churches. The labor came from enslaved people requisitioned from local farms and plantations. Work went slowly, the heavy guns never came, and to fit more small guns into Fort Walker's water battery the protective traverses were removed, leaving it open to flanking fire.

## The Worst-Kept Secret

Du Pont's fleet of 77 vessels was billed as the largest that had ever sailed under the American flag. Each captain carried a sealed envelope to be opened at sea: "Port Royal, S. C., is the port of destination for yourself and the ships of your convoy." It hardly mattered. Two days before the main fleet sailed from Hampton Roads, the New York Times ran a front-page article, "The Great Naval Expedition," laying out the full order of battle, gathered by mingling with the troops. The Charleston papers reprinted it word for word, and on November 1 the acting Confederate Secretary of War, Judah P. Benjamin, telegraphed that "the enemy's expedition is intended for Port Royal." That same day a gale scattered the fleet off South Carolina. The transport Governor, carrying 300 Marines, went down, and seven men were lost. The storm cost Brigadier General Thomas W. Sherman's army its landing boats and much of its ammunition, so the battle would be ships against forts.

## Ships in Motion

On November 4 the Coast Survey vessel under her civilian captain, Charles Boutelle, charted the sound under gunboat escort, and Flag Officer Josiah Tattnall's small Confederate flotilla, a converted coaster and three former tugs mounting just two guns apiece, came out to interfere and was driven back. The next day the flagship Wabash, drawing 22 feet, ran aground on Fishing Rip Shoal, and on November 6 a storm forced another delay. During the wait, fleet captain Charles Henry Davis proposed keeping the ships moving in an ellipse, pounding one fort and then the other, a tactic recently used at Hatteras Inlet. November 7 dawned calm. At 9:26 a gun in Fort Walker opened fire, and its first shell exploded harmlessly just beyond the muzzle. The moving ships spoiled the gunners' aim, and many of their shots sailed high into masts and rigging.

## Out of Powder

The plan soon unraveled. Commander Sylvanus W. Godon of Mohican found a spot where he could enfilade Fort Walker's water battery without taking return fire, and the confused ships behind him dropped out too. Only Wabash and Susquehanna kept circling, yet the ships that had left the line did most of the damage. By midday only three guns in the water battery still worked. General Drayton left around 12:30 to gather reserves; when he returned around two o'clock (one account has the garrison leaving by 1:15), he found his men leaving the fort, nearly out of powder. Commander John Rodgers went ashore under a flag of truce, found the fort abandoned, and raised the Union flag. The survivors escaped to the mainland. Across the sound, Colonel Robert Gill Mills Dunovant heard the cheering in the fleet, feared being cut off, and quietly abandoned Fort Beauregard; the Union flag went up there the next morning. Losses were light: 11 Confederates killed, about 48 wounded and 4 to 7 missing, depending on the count, and 8 killed and 23 wounded in the fleet.

## A Base for the Blockade

Union forces quickly occupied Beaufort and moved north to St. Helena Sound, pressing up to the rivers south of Charleston, so the long siege of that city can be said to have begun here. Robert E. Lee, assigned to command the coast just two days before the battle and arriving the day after it, chose not to contest the Union gunboats, pulling his forces back from the coast to defend vital interior positions, including the railroad between Savannah and Charleston. In July 1862 Du Pont became the second officer, after David G. Farragut, promoted to rear admiral. He built extensive coaling, provisioning and repair facilities at Port Royal to keep the blockading squadron at sea, though his reputation did not survive the failed fleet attack on Charleston in April 1863. For enslaved people around Port Royal, the victory meant freedom, and their liberation in the battle's aftermath became the subject of John Greenleaf Whittier's poem "At Port Royal."

## Flight Context

Port Royal Sound opens to the Atlantic at approximately 32.25°N, 80.66°W, between Hilton Head Island to the south, site of Fort Walker, and Phillip's Island and the Sea Islands to the north, site of Fort Beauregard. From 2,500–3,500 feet AGL the wide mouth of the sound, the marshes and the resort shoreline of Hilton Head are unmistakable; the town of Beaufort lies up the sound to the north. Hilton Head Airport (KHXD) is only about 2.5 nautical miles away, and Beaufort Executive Airport (KARW) is about 10 nautical miles to the north. Marine Corps Air Station Beaufort (KNBC) lies about 14 nautical miles to the north-northwest, so watch for military jet traffic. Coastal haze, sea fog and summer thunderstorms are common; fall often brings the clearest days.
