# Santa Catalina de Guale

For roughly a century the Spanish mission of Santa Catalina de Guale anchored the Guale province from St. Catherines Island, until disease, slave raids, pirates and war drove it south island by island and finally destroyed it.

**Location:** 31.6252, -81.1734

**Categories:** Spanish missions in Georgia (U.S. state), Native American history of Georgia (U.S. state), 1602 establishments in the Spanish Empire, Spanish Florida, Archaeological sites in Georgia (U.S. state)

## Introduction

Santa Catalina de Guale lasted roughly a hundred years, and it spent the last two decades of them in retreat. Founded on St. Catherines Island, one of the Sea Islands of present-day Georgia, in the 1590s or early 1600s depending on the source (Wikipedia says 1602), this Spanish Franciscan mission served as the provincial headquarters of the Guale mission province, part of Spain's effort to convert the region's Native Americans to Catholicism. It was a town as well as a church, and it sent food and labor south to the colonial capital of St. Augustine. Then the raiders came. In 1680 the mission fled to Sapelo Island. Three or four years later it moved again, to Amelia Island in what is now Florida. In 1702 it was destroyed.

## A Village Beneath the Mission

The mission did not rise in empty country. It was probably associated with a Guale village known today to archaeologists as Wamassee Head, on St. Catherines Island. The Guale people who lived there and in neighboring towns would pay a terrible price for the century that followed. During the 1600s their population fell dramatically, mainly because of epidemic diseases, and as communities shrank the Spanish consolidated the mission settlements. By 1675 the Guale mission village of San Diego de Satuache had been folded into Santa Catalina de Guale. The mission villages of Santa Clara de Tupiqui and San Joseph de Sapala were likewise merged. Behind each merger stood communities too diminished by sickness and death to carry on alone.

## The Stone Fort Falls

In 1680 two major slave-raiding attacks struck the Guale coast. The attackers, by one account about 300 Westo Indians, had been armed, supplied and encouraged to strike the Spanish missions by English colonial authorities in Carolina, and their purpose was to capture people; the Georgia Encyclopedia describes them simply as English raiders from Charleston. Santa Catalina de Guale was the first to fall. Its defenses were modest for what was coming: by one account, a recently built stone fort, six Spanish soldiers and about 40 Christianized Native Americans, with Captain Francisco de Fuentes rallying his Spanish soldiers and Guale musketeers in a failed defense. The Westo are said to have lost men and taken no captives there, but the mission could not be held. Other missions in the region fell quickly after it. The survivors of Santa Catalina abandoned St. Catherines Island and moved south to Sapelo Island, carrying their mission's name with them.

## Chiefs Without Villages

On Sapelo, the four original mission villages of Tupiqui, Sapala, Satuache and Santa Catalina were merged into one. The merger quietly dismantled a political world. The Guale chiefdom had been organized as a hierarchy among villages, with each community's leaders holding a place in relation to the others. Once everyone lived in a single settlement, that intervillage system was lost. The chiefly lineages tied to each village survived, however, and so Santa Catalina's population came to include many leaders who held their titles by inheritance but no longer had villages to lead. It is a small detail in the colonial record, and a telling one: an entire structure of authority reduced to names.

## Pirates, Then War

Sapelo offered only a few years of refuge. In 1683 the French pirate Michel de Grammont raided settlements in Spanish Florida, including St. Augustine and the Mocama mission province, forcing more migrations southward. In 1683 or 1684, depending on the source, Santa Catalina de Guale moved again, this time to Amelia Island in present-day Florida. Other missions were not so fortunate. When more pirates appeared in 1684, the nearby missions of Santo Domingo de Asao and San Buenaventura de Guadalquini were prevented from moving south, and both were burned. Their people fled to the mainland. By 1685 the Guale had either gone inland, some joining unconverted groups such as the Yamasee, or relocated to Amelia Island's three settlements: Santa Catalina de Guale, San Felipe and Santa Clara de Tupiqui. The end came in 1702, during Queen Anne's War, when Carolina's governor, James Moore, invaded Spanish Florida. The Amelia Island settlements, Santa Catalina among them, were destroyed. Those survivors who remained within the Spanish mission system moved to the vicinity of St. Augustine, the capital their labor had once helped to feed.

## Flight Context

The mission's first site is marked at about 31.6252 N, 81.1734 W on St. Catherines Island, one of Georgia's Sea Islands, facing the Atlantic. Sapelo Island, the mission's second home, lies roughly 13 nm to the south-southwest. A cruise altitude of 2,000 to 3,000 feet gives a good view of the island's forest, marsh and tidal creeks and lets you trace the mission's route south along the chain of Sea Islands. The nearest airports with ICAO codes are Hunter Army Air Field (KSVN) in Savannah, about 23 nm north; MidCoast Regional Airport at Wright Army Airfield (KLHW) near Hinesville, about 25 nm northwest; and Brunswick Golden Isles Airport (KBQK), about 27 nm south-southwest. Savannah/Hilton Head International (KSAV) is about 30 nm north. Expect coastal haze and summer afternoon thunderstorms over the islands.
