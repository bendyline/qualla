# Amos Deason Home

Billed as Jones County's oldest home, this is a pine house disguised as stone, and the bedroom where a Confederate major was shot in 1863 has given it a reputation as one of the most haunted houses in Mississippi.

**Location:** 31.6100, -89.2003

**Categories:** Houses on the National Register of Historic Places in Mississippi, National Register of Historic Places in Jones County, Mississippi, Antebellum architecture, Reportedly haunted locations, Free State of Jones

## Introduction

From Anderson Street in Ellisville, Mississippi, the walls of the Amos Deason Home look like pale, unpolished limestone or granite. They are pine. Builders cut thick weatherboards, beveled them to resemble blocks of masonry, brushed on paint, pressed sand into the wet coat and painted again, borrowing a technique George Washington had used at Mount Vernon to make wood pass for stone. Completed around 1845 (some sources date it into the 1850s), the house is described as the oldest home in Jones County, but its notoriety rests on something else: a bedroom where, on October 5, 1863, a Confederate major was shot in the back.

## Heart Pine and Wooden Pegs

The house's beginnings are partly legend. Construction is believed to have started in the 1830s under Ed Chapman, who died before it was finished, and Boyles McManus is said to have completed it; a historical marker dates the house to about 1845, though the National Register lists it as built between 1855 and 1860 and no one knows the exact date. What is accepted is that Amos and Eleanor Deason were the first family to live here, on an original homestead of 700 acres. The frame was hand-hewn from the hearts of the virgin pine forests that then surrounded the property, pinned together with wooden pegs, since nails as we know them were not available. Bricks for the chimney were made from native clay and fired on the homestead, while window glass and hardware had to be hauled in from Mobile. Up in the attic, the underside of the original lath and hand-split shingles can still be seen.

## A House Without Hallways

Guests arrived at a hexagonal vestibule with French doors opening onto a porch shaped to match it and carried on six columns. Inside were just four rooms, with twelve-foot ceilings for relief against the Jones County summer heat, and no interior doors at all: to move from one room to the next, you stepped out onto the wraparound porch and came back in. The master bedroom held a rare luxury, a closet, which by local tradition was taxed as a room. Beside the parlor fireplace was a hidden passage, sometimes called a safe spot. The kitchen stood apart behind the house, where the family cooked over an open fire and ate their meals, and where the women of the household spun, wove and quilted. The moldings, doors and trim were made on site by skilled craftsmen and indentured labor, and the outbuildings are said to have included slave quarters, a reminder that enslaved people also lived on this homestead.

## The Winter Morning Room

The master bedroom was called the "winter morning room" for the warmth of its fireplace. In the autumn of 1863 it was lent to a guest. Major Amos McLemore, a Confederate officer and a friend of the Deasons, had been sent to Jones County to round up a band of deserters led by Newt Knight, whose band of Confederate deserters, later organized as the Knight Company, resisted the Confederacy. McLemore was using the Deason house as his headquarters. On the night of October 5, 1863, an attacker burst in and shot him in the back; most people believed the man who pulled the trigger was Knight himself. McLemore fell to the pine floor, gravely wounded. Local tradition says he was carried home to Eastabuchie and died a few days later, though other accounts have him dying that night.

## Stains That Would Not Scrub Out

According to the house's lore, McLemore's blood soaked into the floorboards and ran down the joists, and no amount of scrubbing by Mrs. Deason could remove the stains. Later residents laid a new floor over the old one, but the story did not go away. The bedroom became known as the "Murder Room," ghosts are said to haunt the house, and it has gained a reputation as one of the most haunted homes in Mississippi. The stains, it is said, can still be seen from beneath the house. Whatever one makes of the ghosts, the tale ties this modest cottage to a remarkable Civil War story, in which armed deserters in Jones County turned against the Confederacy they had once served.

## Between Two Schools

Eleanor Deason died in 1888, and around 1890 Amos Deason's grandson, Isaac Anderson Jr., and his wife, Sarah Rebecca "Sallie" Pool, acquired the house and made additions. They raised ten children here, three of them born in the house, and the basic structure still stands much as it did in the 1890s. Today the French Raised Cottage sits at the corner of Deason and Anderson Streets, tucked between two modern schools and looking a little out of place among them. It is listed on the National Register of Historic Places and owned by the Tallahala Chapter of the National Society Daughters of the American Revolution, which opens it for occasional open houses and tours and, fittingly, ghost hunts.
