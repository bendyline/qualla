# Sullivans Hollow

A Mississippi valley settled by one prolific family around 1820 earned a reputation for lawlessness, a reputation its residents never minded if it kept outsiders away, and one that the facts only partly support.

**Location:** 31.8043, -89.5084

**Categories:** Landforms of Smith County, Mississippi, Valleys of Mississippi

## Introduction

Forty men, the stories said. Fifty, according to some. That was the number of killings attached to William Cicero "Wild Bill" Sullivan, the outlaw of Sullivan's Hollow, a valley near Mize, Mississippi. Historians who consulted long-time residents of the county went looking for those forty, and they could confirm only one. Sullivan's Hollow has always lived in that gap between legend and record, and the people who lived there seem to have been perfectly content to let outsiders believe the legend.

## One Family's Valley

The hollow takes its name from Thomas Sullivan, usually dated 1775 to 1855, who settled here early in the 1800s; accounts range from about 1807 to 1821. In its early days, Sullivan's Hollow was considered to be about six miles long and three miles wide. It did not stay that size, because the Sullivans did not stay a small family. Thomas reportedly had 22 children by two wives, and as they grew up and established families of their own, the area known as Sullivan's Hollow grew with them. Today its unofficial boundaries form a triangle running from Mize, in Smith County, to Mount Olive and Hot Coffee in Covington County. The name on the land is the name of the family that filled it.

## Wild Bill and the Arithmetic of Legend

Some historians came to regard the hollow as an area of great lawlessness, and Wild Bill Sullivan, who lived from 1851 to 1932, is the usual example. He was reported to have killed forty men, some said fifty. Yet historians who talked with long-time residents of the county were never able to confirm more than one killing. One charge is on the record, and it is grave enough: Sullivan was indicted for the murder of his brother, Wilson. Local residents, asked about the clan, offered a different kind of explanation for the reputation. "The Sullivans and their neighbors were strong independent people who wanted to be left alone and did not take any guff from anyone," they said. "If reports of lawlessness kept outsiders away it was just fine with them." A fearsome reputation, in other words, could be useful to a people who prized their privacy.

## The Courthouse Fires

The Smith County Courthouse has burned three times, and one of the hollow's most enduring stories attaches itself to those fires. According to the tale, shortly before the current courthouse was built in 1912, two Sullivan boys killed a man and were indicted by a grand jury for murder. The legend holds that they then burned down the courthouse and hid out for several years. When they finally turned themselves in, there was no record left of any grand jury indictment, and so they went free. Whether it happened just that way, no one can now say, and tellings differ on the year; the story has always been told as legend rather than history. Such stories are common in Smith County, and in Sullivan's Hollow they are part of the landscape.
