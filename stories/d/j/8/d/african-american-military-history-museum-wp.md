# African American Military History Museum

In Hattiesburg, Mississippi, a 1942 USO club built for African American soldiers from Camp Shelby now tells the story of Black service members from the Revolutionary War onward, having survived an EF-4 tornado in 2013.

## Introduction

Soldiers came here to read, to write letters home, to develop photographs in a darkroom and to hear music. Between 1942 and 1946, the East Sixth Street USO Club in Hattiesburg, Mississippi, served as a home away from home for African American soldiers stationed at nearby Camp Shelby. The wood-frame building was put up specifically to house an African American USO, shortly after the United States entered World War II. Today it is the African American Military History Museum, and it honors those soldiers and the generations who served before and after them.

## Built for Camp Shelby's Soldiers

The East Sixth Street USO was unusual from the start. It was constructed in 1942 specifically to house an African American USO club, and it had the support of both Black and white residents of Hattiesburg. For the men stationed at Camp Shelby, it was a place for recreation, relaxation and socialization. The services offered read like a list of small comforts: a library, a darkroom, a lounge, counseling, music, mail service, religious literature and even a sewing service. None of it was grand. All of it helped turn a building into a home away from home for soldiers in the middle of a world war. The building itself is a T-shaped wood-frame structure built around a central brick chimney. Inside were a lobby, meeting rooms, dressing rooms, men's and women's restrooms and, at its heart, an auditorium measuring 34 by 55 feet. Its stage was raised 3 feet above the floor and measured 20 by 22 feet, with dressing rooms on either side. The plain design speaks of wartime practicality. It was built to serve the soldiers of Camp Shelby, and it did so until 1946.

## From Community Center to Museum

After its USO years, the building found new purpose. It housed the N.R. Burger Center, which served as a museum and community space commemorating the allegiance of African American soldiers through historical documents, artifacts and photographs. In 2004 the structure was added to the National Register of Historic Places. A renovation completed in 2009 transformed it into the African American Military History Museum, which opened to the public on May 23 of that year. The old USO auditorium was subdivided into exhibit spaces spanning American military history: the Revolutionary War, the Buffalo Soldiers, both World Wars, Korea, Vietnam, Desert Storm and the Global War on Terrorism, along with a Hattiesburg Hall of Honor. About 13,000 visitors toured the museum between 2009 and 2012, and in 2010 the building was designated a Mississippi Landmark.

## Weathering the Storm

On February 10, 2013, an EF-4 tornado passed through Hattiesburg. The museum's roof was severely damaged, and rain soaked the exhibits and artifacts inside. Staff moved the collection to another facility for storage and braced for a closure of at least a year. The damage was estimated at $450,000 to the building and $600,000 to the museum's contents. Reconstruction took about a year, and on February 6, 2014, the museum officially reopened. A building raised in wartime to shelter soldiers had needed rescuing itself, and it was brought back. The museum, at 305 East Sixth Street, lists its hours as Wednesday through Friday from 10 a.m. to 4 p.m. and Saturday from noon to 4 p.m.
