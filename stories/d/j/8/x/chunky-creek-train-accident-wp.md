# Chunky Creek train accident

On February 19, 1863, a train carrying Confederate reinforcements plunged from a rain-damaged bridge into Chunky Creek in Mississippi, killing an estimated 75 people, as Choctaw soldiers camped nearby dove into the flood to pull survivors out.

**Location:** 32.3189, -88.9722

**Categories:** Railway accidents in 1863, Bridge disasters in the United States, Mississippi in the American Civil War, Newton County, Mississippi, Choctaw history

## Introduction

At four o'clock on the morning of February 19, 1863, the locomotive Hercules pulled out of Meridian, Mississippi, bound west on the Southern Rail Road toward Vicksburg. Behind its tender rode Confederate soldiers headed for the Vicksburg garrison, packed into cars they had broken open themselves before the train left. Ahead, heavy rain had damaged the bridge over Chunky Creek. The local section-master had put a warning sign on the track and given orders to stop incoming traffic, but those instructions were not followed. The train derailed and fell into the creek, and an estimated 75 people died in the winter water, though accounts of the toll range from more than 40 to nearly 100.

## The Worst-Kept Railroad

The Southern Rail Road Company was chartered in 1850, and by 1861 its line ran 145 miles from Vicksburg to Meridian, built to a wide five-foot gauge. At Jackson it met the New Orleans, Jackson & Great Northern, and at Meridian the Mobile & Ohio. It also had a reputation as the worst-maintained railroad in the country. The Daily Southern Crisis, a Jackson newspaper, claimed that accidents on the line were almost a daily occurrence. Poor repairs were blamed for the constant wrecks. In February 1863 the winter rains added one more hazard, and on the day of the disaster a telegraph repairer reported the line badly washed out between Newton and Hickory.

## The Engine Called Hercules

The Hercules was an American-type 4-4-0 locomotive that had been based in New Orleans until April 1862. Its engineer, Isaiah P. Beauchamp, had been living in New Orleans in 1860. That morning it likely pulled one tender and four cars. The telegraph called it a freight train, but it was carrying Confederate reinforcements to Vicksburg, and one newspaper report said two of the cars held about seventy soldiers and a horse. Beauchamp was forward on the engine, keeping a lookout, and the conductor rode with him. Some modern accounts mislabel the locomotive as the "Mississippi Southern," but a telegram sent that very day names it clearly as the Hercules.

## Out of Sight in the Water

The locomotive went completely under, and the wooden boxcars behind it were demolished. Barrels, boxes and supplies floated off on the cold current. Of nearly one hundred passengers, about 75 are usually reported killed (other accounts say anywhere from more than 40 to nearly 100), some by the violence of the impact and others drowned after being trapped beneath the wreckage. That day J. M. Booker telegraphed the railroad's superintendent from Meridian: "The engineer, fireman and a large number of the passengers are supposed to be drowned. Eight bodies have been recovered. The engine and four cars are out of sight in the water." Early reports disagreed on the toll. The Athens Post in Tennessee relayed an estimate of between twenty-five and fifty lost, mostly soldiers, and reported that the conductor, though hurt, had swum out.

## The Choctaw Soldiers Dive In

Close by camped what was most likely the 1st Choctaw Battalion, Choctaw men serving in the Confederate army, and its soldiers rescued some of the passengers. A writer named Spann later described the scene in a magazine article, in the vocabulary of his era. When the cry reached the camp, he wrote, "every Indian was at the scene." He singled out two men, Jack Amos and a man called Elder (whose surname is given as Jackson in Wikipedia's transcription of Spann and as Williams by the Newton County historical society), calling them "dauntless braves," and said the Choctaw soldiers stripped and plunged into the raging water after the drowning men. By Spann's count, ninety-six bodies were brought out onto a strip of high ground, and twenty-two of those people were revived and returned to their units. The rest were buried in crude graves along the railroad right of way, in view of passing trains, except nine whom friends later moved to a more fitting grave. Spann wrote that both men were then living in Newton County, and that the second had become an ordained Baptist minister.

## A Monument That Never Came

Years later, the United Confederate Veterans' Camp Dabney H. Maury tried to place a memorial at the wreck site. A New Orleans newspaper urged that a lasting monument should honor both the dead soldiers and "the dauntless courage and fraternal heroism of the Choctaws." The paper meant that lone burial spot beside the tracks, where, as Spann wrote, the dead lay "in full view of the passing train." For unknown reasons, it was never built. Instead, on February 19, 2005, exactly 142 years after the crash, a small historical marker was placed at the scene, although the Historical Marker Database now lists a state marker near U.S. 80 as missing. In 2015 and 2016 the historian and writer Robert Bruce Ferguson investigated the presumed wreckage area, photographing it for study.
