# Iron Horse (sculpture)

A two-ton steel horse that University of Georgia students once vandalized found refuge on a professor's farm, became a beloved roadside icon, and in 2024 was restored and set back in its field on a new pedestal.

## Introduction

The campus did not want it. When sculptor Abbott Pattison finished a twelve-foot, two-ton steel horse at the University of Georgia in 1954 and set it outside Reed Hall, the reaction was hostile, and the sculpture was vandalized by disgruntled students. It was soon moved into a barn, and it might have stayed there had a horticulture professor not wanted a conversation piece. Instead the Iron Horse, also called Pegasus Without Wings, ended up standing alone in a farm field south of Athens, where over the following decades the object nobody wanted became a curiosity visited by many tourists and students.

## The Sculptor in Residence

Abbott Pattison was the university's sculptor in residence when he produced the horse in 1954. The work was built of steel, stood twelve feet tall and weighed two tons, and it also goes by a second name, Pegasus Without Wings. Outside Reed Hall, students made their feelings about it known. After the vandalism, the sculpture was moved off the grounds to a barn. The story of those first turbulent years was compelling enough to become film material. It was depicted in a National Educational Television film titled Pegasus Without Wings, and again in 1980 in Iron Horse, a documentary by William VanDerKloot.

## A Professor's Conversation Piece

Rescue came from L.C. Curtis, a horticulture professor, who obtained the sculpture around 1959. He had wanted it from Lamar Dodd, then chairman of the art department. Curtis explained his motives to The New York Times in 1979 with disarming frankness: "I collect conversation pieces. I'm a little bit of an eccentric." He moved the horse to his farm near Watkinsville, and there it stood in the open fields, far from the students who had rejected it. In its new setting, alone in a field, the horse slowly found an audience. Visitors began to seek it out, and over time the horse turned from a campus embarrassment into an attraction drawing tourists and University of Georgia students alike.

## Knocked Down, Patched Up

Fame did not make the horse safe. It was vandalized again in 2011, and afterward a secret group quietly restored it. People periodically knocked the sculpture over, so the Curtis family eventually bolted it to a concrete pad. Around 2012, the family sold roughly 660 acres of the farm to the university, keeping only the twenty-foot by twenty-foot square where the horse stood. In 2014 the university named its new land the Iron Horse Plant Sciences Farm and began using it for agricultural research. The Curtis family later offered to deed the horse and its remaining 400 square feet to the university, on one condition: the horse had to stay where it was. The university, for its part, insisted the sculpture belonged back on campus, and for a time the horse's future was uncertain.

## A New Plinth Under the Hooves

In 2024 the family transferred the sculpture and its patch of ground to the university, and that spring crews broke up the concrete pad and carried the Iron Horse to Athens for restoration. Art conservator Amy Jones Abbe and metalsmith Don Cope led the work. Because Pattison had departed from his own sketches while building the horse, the team relied on a small number of photographs to guide them, and they added, by one account, 32 pieces that had been lost to weather or vandalism. The restored horse was painted and sealed in epoxy. On November 22, 2024, it returned to the Iron Horse Plant Sciences Farm and was installed on its old site, now standing on a new plinth. On February 16, 2025, a storm toppled the horse off its pedestal, and it was reportedly set upright again two days later. The sculpture that students once rejected had come home to the field that made it famous.

## Flight Context

A lone sculpture in a field makes a small target, so a slow pass at 1,000-1,500 feet in good light is best; look for the isolated figure on its plinth amid the research fields.
