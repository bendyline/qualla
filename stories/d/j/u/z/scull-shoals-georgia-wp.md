# Scull Shoals, Georgia

A lost rapids and mill town on Georgia's Oconee River, Scull Shoals lies buried under some fourteen feet of silt washed down from the cotton farms that once made it prosper.

**Location:** 33.7283, -83.2928

**Categories:** Ghost towns in Georgia (U.S. state), Mounds in Georgia (U.S. state), Geography of Greene County, Georgia, Oconee National Forest, Soil erosion

## Introduction

Somewhere beneath the Oconee River in Greene County, Georgia, lies a set of rapids that nobody alive has ever seen. They are buried under an estimated fourteen feet of silt, and the town that grew up beside them has sunk into the same mud, leaving only the ruins of a mill and a four-story brick warehouse standing in the woods of Oconee National Forest. Scull Shoals is a ghost town, and its killer came downstream from the very cotton fields that gave it life.

## A River That Fell Four Feet

In the Southeastern Piedmont, "shoals" usually means a stretch of river where rocks block boat traffic. In 1775 the shoals here were probably a rocky place where the Oconee dropped at least four feet in a short distance, and the river itself was stable. European settlement changed it fast. Upstream, farming, and cotton farming above all, stripped soil from the land and sent it into the river. By 1860 the rapids were completely buried. Silt kept piling up until cotton farming ended in the 1920s, and only then did the river settle down again, now flowing over rapids buried some fourteen feet down.

## Skulls in the Riverbank

The name most likely comes from prehistoric earthworks upstream. When European settlers arrived, erosion was uncovering the skulls of Indigenous people buried in those mounds, and the name most likely recalls what those eroding banks laid bare. The earthworks have gone by other names, too, including the Watson Springs Mound Site and the Brightwell Mounds. Archaeologist Mark Kelly estimated that most of the mound building happened in the late Etowah to Savannah periods, around A.D. 1250, with work continuing into the Lamar, or late Mississippian, period. Today the mounds are protected within Oconee National Forest, a resting place once more.

## A Pioneering Paper Mill

Settlers saw power in the falling water. Water-powered mills were running at the shoals before 1809, and over the years the community had a grist mill, a paper mill, a cotton gin and a textile mill. The paper mill, which began operating shortly after 1810, has been described as the first in Georgia. Scull Shoals flourished for a time, largely on the strength of the cotton grown around it. But the same upstream farms that fed its mills also sent down floods and silt, and the problem grew year after year. By the mid-1880s water power was no longer workable, and the town declined.

## Undone by Its Own Success

Summing up the demise of Scull Shoals, Bruce K. Ferguson saw the town's end not as the fading of an old antebellum civilization but as a lesson in how land use works. The mill, he argued, was an integral part of the region's cotton economy, and it was killed by the effects of the same economic system that had given it life before that system "disintegrated from its own abuses." The federal government acquired the site in the 1930s, it became part of the Oconee National Forest, and the U.S. Forest Service now runs it as a historic recreation area, still battling floods and silt. Nearby, part of the national forest serves as the Scull Shoals Experimental Forest, where scientists have studied problems such as littleleaf disease in shortleaf pine.

## Flight Context

Scull Shoals lies at 33.7283°N, 83.2928°W on the Oconee River in Greene County, Georgia, within Oconee National Forest. From 1,500–2,500 feet, follow the Oconee's winding, forest-lined course; the ruins are small and hidden under tree cover, so the river bends and the surrounding national forest are the main references. Greene County Regional Airport (FAA identifier CPP; GPS code KCPP) is about 13 miles southeast near Greensboro, and Athens–Ben Epps Airport (KAHN) is about 15 miles north. Summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are common in the Georgia Piedmont.
