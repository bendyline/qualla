# South-View Cemetery

Founded in 1886 by Black businessmen who wanted their families buried with dignity, Atlanta's South-View Cemetery holds the King family, John Lewis, Julian Bond, Hank Aaron and generations of the people who built Black Atlanta.

**Location:** 33.7024, -84.3737

**Categories:** Cemeteries in Atlanta, African-American history in Atlanta, African-American cemeteries in Georgia (U.S. state), Civil rights movement, 1886 establishments in Georgia (U.S. state)

## Introduction

In February 1886, nine African-American businessmen in Atlanta set out to secure something that should never have been in doubt: a safe, secure place where their families could be buried with dignity. They were acting in the midst of the backlash to Reconstruction. Among the founders were Jacob McKinley, George W. Graham, Robert Grant, Charles H. Morgan, John Render and Albert Watts, and in April the State of Georgia approved their charter. The cemetery they created, South-View, now covers more than 100 acres south of downtown, and it is still an active burial ground. Martin Luther King Jr. was first laid to rest here. So, in time, were Hank Aaron and John Lewis.

## Owned by Its Own

South-View is described as the oldest African-American-controlled cemetery in Atlanta and, by the cemetery association's own account, the oldest African-American "non eleemosynary" corporation in the country, meaning it was organized as a business rather than a charity. It has stayed close to its roots. Albert H. Watts, grandson of co-founder Albert Watts, served as president-treasurer from 1977 until his death in 2001, and today the president, several stockholders, board members and staff members are descendants of the founders. For many years the city of Atlanta paid South-View to provide graves for African Americans buried at city expense. Not every corner has fared equally well. New lots are sold with perpetual care, but many historic family plots were not, and some older sections have suffered from neglect. In 2004 a nonprofit foundation was created to raise money, carry out preservation projects and care for those historic grounds.

## Where King Was First Laid to Rest

After his assassination in 1968, Martin Luther King Jr. was buried at South-View. On January 13, 1970, he was moved to a tomb at the site of what became the King Center for Nonviolent Social Change. Much of his family remains here: his father, Martin Luther King Sr.; his mother, Alberta Williams King; and his brother, civil rights leader Alfred Daniel Williams King. As of 2016, every deceased pastor of Atlanta's historic Ebenezer Baptist Church except King Jr. was buried at South-View, beginning with John Andrew Parker, the church's first pastor. King was not the only one to leave. Henry Ossian Flipper was reburied in his hometown of Thomasville, Georgia, in 1978, and Benjamin Mays and his wife, Sadie Gray Mays, were moved to the Morehouse College campus in 1995.

## The Movement's Own Ground

The civil rights movement is written across these grounds. John Lewis chaired the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee from 1963 to 1966 and was one of the "Big Six" leaders who organized the 1963 March on Washington. In 1965 he led the first of three Selma to Montgomery marches across the Edmund Pettus Bridge, where state troopers and police attacked the marchers, Lewis among them, on the day remembered as Bloody Sunday. He went on to serve in Congress. Julian Bond helped found SNCC, served in the Georgia House of Representatives and later chaired the NAACP. His father, historian and educator Horace Mann Bond, who became the first African-American president of Lincoln University in Pennsylvania in 1945, lies here too. So do activist Ruby Doris Smith-Robinson; Fred C. Bennette, an aide to King; William Holmes Borders, pastor of Wheat Street Baptist Church; and Jesse Hill, civil rights activist and president of the Atlanta Life Insurance Company.

## The Builders of Black Atlanta

Walk the rows and you meet the people who built Black Atlanta. Alonzo Herndon was one of the first Black millionaires. John Wesley Dobbs was the unofficial "mayor" of Auburn Avenue. Grace Towns Hamilton was the first African-American woman elected to the Georgia General Assembly, and Henry McNeal Turner, minister and politician, was one of the Original 33, the first African Americans elected to that body in 1868. Ludie Clay Andrews was the first African American granted a nursing license by Georgia, and Moses Amos was the state's first licensed African-American pharmacist. The music runs from opera singer Mattiwilda Dobbs and the vaudeville Whitman Sisters to singer Chuck Willis and William and Eleanor Guest of Gladys Knight & the Pips. Hank Aaron, who broke Babe Ruth's record of 714 home runs and finished with 755, is buried here. Twenty-two people at South-View have had Atlanta public schools named for them, and veterans of every war since World War I rest here, including Tuskegee Airmen Horace A. Bohannon and Walter Drake Westmoreland.

## Names That Carry Grief

Some graves hold the city's hardest memories. Frank Smith and George Wilder were victims of the Atlanta Massacre of 1906. William Barrett, Yusuf Bell, Milton Harvey, Aaron Jackson, Patrick Rogers, Curtis Walker and Aaron Wyche were among those killed in the Atlanta murders of 1979 to 1981, and here each is remembered by name. Larry Brown, a graduate of Archer High School who played football for Marshall University, died in the crash of Southern Airways Flight 932. A cell-phone tour created with Atlanta's Oakland Cemetery has offered these stories at 14 stops marked with granite, where callers dial the stop number to hear them. The cemetery has held a Wreaths Across America ceremony each December since 2010. When South-View turned 125 in 2011, Mayor Kasim Reed gave it the city's Phoenix Award for its contributions to Atlanta. In May 2026 it was added to the Georgia Register of Historic Places, and its leaders say a national-level review comes next.

## Flight Context

South-View Cemetery lies at approximately 33.702°N, 84.374°W, about three miles south of downtown Atlanta, Georgia. From 2,500–3,500 feet AGL it appears as a broad green space amid the neighborhoods of south Atlanta, with the downtown skyline to the north. This is the heart of Atlanta's Class B airspace and the arrival and departure paths of Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International Airport (KATL), just under 5 nautical miles to the southwest, so any low flying here requires ATC coordination. Fulton County Airport-Brown Field (KFTY) is about 9 nautical miles to the west-northwest, and DeKalb-Peachtree Airport (KPDK) about 11 nautical miles to the north-northeast. Summer brings haze and afternoon thunderstorms.
