# Cabbagetown, Atlanta

Built in 1881 for Appalachian families who worked Atlanta's Fulton Bag and Cotton Mill, Cabbagetown turned an insult into a badge of pride and, after the mill closed, reinvented itself as one of the city's street-art centers.

**Location:** 33.7497, -84.3664

**Categories:** Neighborhoods in Atlanta, Company towns in Georgia (U.S. state), Historic districts on the National Register of Historic Places in Georgia (U.S. state), Culture of Appalachia, Shotgun architecture

## Introduction

Outsiders meant it as an insult. The mill families on Atlanta's east side, many of them transplanted from the Appalachian mountains of north Georgia, grew cabbages in their front yards, and, as one explanation goes, the smell of cooking cabbage drifted through the narrow streets so strongly that the neighborhood got a name to match. The people who lived there took the jab and kept it. Today Cabbagetown wears the name with pride.

## A Mill on a Battlefield

The neighborhood grew out of war and reconstruction. The Atlanta Rolling Mill was destroyed after the Battle of Atlanta, and on its site the Fulton Bag and Cotton Mill began operations in 1881, one of the first textile mills built in the South. It opened soon after the International Cotton Exposition, held in Atlanta to draw investment to the region, at a time when many industries were moving to the post-Reconstruction South in search of cheap labor. The owner, Jacob Elsas, was a German Jewish immigrant, and his main product was cotton bags for packaging crops. His workforce was made up of poor white workers recruited from Appalachian north Georgia. Around the mill Elsas built a village of one- and two-story shotgun houses and cottages, first called Factory Town or Fulton Mill Village, on the extremely narrow streets, short blocks and frequent intersections typical of mill towns.

## Cabbages, a Model T and Pearl Park

The cabbage-yard story is only one of several explanations for the name. In a variation of the legend, a Ford Model T took a sharp turn at one of the main intersections, rolled over and spilled a load of cabbages across the street. Another version credits a neighborhood baseball team. Records at the Atlanta History Center also mention a different name, Pearl Park, after the daughter of a developer who built houses just east of the mill houses, near today's Pearl Street. Whatever its origin, the name stuck. Many of the residents were of Scots-Irish descent, and for more than half a century they formed a tight-knit, semi-isolated community whose lives were anchored by the mill.

## Strike, Closure and Decline

At its height the mill employed as many as 2,600 people, and a protracted strike in 1914 and 1915 failed to win a union for the workforce. The mill kept running until 1977, a year after it was named to the National Register of Historic Places, and when it closed the neighborhood around it fell into a steep decline. Revival started with artists in the 1980s. Among them was Panorama Ray, who ran a photo gallery on Carroll Street, the main commercial strip; since his death in 1997 that street has filled with popular restaurants and become a neighborhood gathering spot. Growth accelerated in the early 1990s as Atlanta's intown neighborhoods began to revive, and from the mid-1990s the mill itself was converted into the Fulton Cotton Mill Lofts, billed by its developer as "the nation's largest residential loft community."

## Fire, Wind and Paint

The conversion had its setbacks. In April 1999 a five-alarm fire badly damaged the mill's east building, still under renovation, and destroyed several nearby homes; the lofts opened anyway the following year. In March 2008 a tornado damaged parts of the complex along with many historic homes and businesses. Today Cabbagetown's walls speak loudest. The Krog Street Tunnel, linking the neighborhood to Inman Park, has become Atlanta's centerpiece for street art, and the annual Forward Warrior live-painting event, founded by Atlanta artist Peter Ferrari, has covered walls along Wylie and Tennelle streets with dozens of murals. Even the art has its own local stewards: the Wallkeepers Committee of the Cabbagetown Initiative manages the neighborhood's walls, and in 2012 it invited La Pandilla from Puerto Rico and Trek Matthews of Atlanta to paint two murals for the Living Walls street art organization. Each November the neighborhood throws Chomp and Stomp, a bluegrass and chili festival with a 5K run that draws thousands of people into the old mill village's narrow streets; the 2026 edition is set for Saturday, November 7.

## Flight Context

Cabbagetown lies at 33.7497°N, 84.3664°W on Atlanta's east side, directly beside historic Oakland Cemetery and a short distance east of downtown. From 2,500–3,500 feet, the large mill complex of the Fulton Cotton Mill Lofts and the green expanse of Oakland Cemetery make good references, with the CSX Hulsey rail yard in neighboring Reynoldstown close by. The area sits under busy Atlanta airspace: Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International (KATL) is about 9 miles south-southwest, Fulton County Airport–Brown Field (KFTY) about 9 miles west-northwest, and DeKalb-Peachtree (KPDK) about 9.5 miles north-northeast. Summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are common.
