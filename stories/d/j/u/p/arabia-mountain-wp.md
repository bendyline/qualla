# Arabia Mountain

A swirled granite dome east of Atlanta that once supplied building stone and chicken grit to the nation, and now shelters some of the rarest plants in the United States in shallow pools on its bare rock.

**Location:** 33.6650, -84.1183

**Categories:** Mountains of Georgia (U.S. state), Inselbergs of North America, National Heritage Areas of the United States, Nature reserves in Georgia (U.S. state), Quarries, Geology

## Introduction

The oddest product ever to leave Arabia Mountain was not a building block. It was chicken grit. An unlikely boost for the quarries came with the discovery that granite grit mixed into chicken feed helped the birds digest, and the Davidson family, which owned several quarries in the area, reportedly became the largest supplier of poultry grit in the world. They claimed the sparkling flecks of mica in their Stonemo grit caught a chicken's eye and helped its gizzard break down food. The business did so well that, according to a 1941 Time magazine article, the U.S. government let the company keep operating during World War II in the name of national defense. Today the same rock is a nature preserve, and what grows in the puddles on its surface is rarer than anything its quarries ever sold.

## Two Summits and a Case of Mistaken Identity

Arabia Mountain is the northern of two peaks in the Davidson-Arabia Mountain Nature Preserve in DeKalb County, Georgia, due east of Atlanta. A low saddle separates it from Bradley Mountain, several hundred feet to the south, and together the two form a monadnock, a hump of erosion-resistant rock left standing after softer rock around it wore away. The summit reaches 955 feet above sea level and rises 172 feet above the Arabia Lake reservoir. It is modest as mountains go, and even finding it takes some care: because Bradley Mountain sits closer to the visitor trails, visitors often mistake it for Arabia Mountain. The Atlanta area holds several of these granite domes, including Stone Mountain and nearby Panola Mountain, but each has its own character.

## Taffy Turned to Stone

Arabia Mountain's character is written in swirls. More than 400 million years ago, as the rock was cooling, heat and pressure gave it a taffy-like consistency. Bands of different minerals folded and twisted, and they froze into the "tidal swirl" pattern still visible across the rock today. Panola Mountain, by contrast, cooled at a different rate and has flakier, darker rock with less compressive strength and different mineral grains. Differences in mineral composition between Panola and Stone Mountain even point to different sources of magma. The swirls are a record of how the stone was stirred long before anyone thought to cut it.

## Raising a Ledge

By the time Anglo-American settlers arrived in the early 19th century, the area was sparsely populated by Muscogee (Creek) and Cherokee people, and it is believed to have served as a buffer and a trade and transportation corridor between the two nations. The Muscogee ceded the land to Georgia in 1821, and the state distributed it to settlers through its land lotteries. The country stayed thinly settled, with many roads unpaved until the 1950s, but the stone drew industry. Before 1880 it was quarried by hand. Starting in 1879, workers used drills, dynamite and air compressors to "raise a ledge," severing large blocks from the mass so they could be cut to size for building construction. The railroad carried the stone known as Tidal Grey across the country, and in 1949 the Lithonia district produced nearly 1.5 million tons of granite, valued at the time at $3 million. Quarry office ruins, rock ledges and drill holes still mark the landscape.

## Gardens in the Solution Pools

The mountain looks barren, but it teems with plants specially adapted to its harsh surface. Life here started with lichens, which draw nutrients from dust and rainwater. Their acids, and those of mosses, slowly pitted the rock; wind, freeze-thaw cycles and even lightning strikes cracked it further. Shallow depressions called solution pools formed where weaker rock wore away faster, and sand washed into them along with rainwater and decaying leaves. In these sandy hollows Small's stonecrop, or diamorpha, takes hold, and at Arabia Mountain it blankets the pools in red. Rarer still are two species protected by Georgia and federal law: the endangered black-spored quillwort and the threatened little amphianthus. Counts vary by source, but black-spored quillwort is known from only about a dozen sites in the United States, and Arabia Mountain is said to hold one of its largest populations. Little amphianthus is somewhat less scarce, with a few dozen known populations, and Arabia Mountain is among them.

## A Family's Gift, a Nation's Landscape

In the 1970s the Davidson family, whose quarries had worked this stone, donated more than 500 acres, including Arabia Mountain, to DeKalb County as a nature preserve for local residents. Expanded several times since, the preserve now covers 2,550 acres of granite outcrops, wetlands, pine and oak forest, streams and two lakes, with a multi-use bike path, hiking trails and fishing. Red diamorpha colors the rock in winter, and yellow daisies bloom in the fall. On October 12, 2006, the mountain became part of the Arabia Mountain National Heritage Area, some 40,000 acres stretching from the historic commercial center of Lithonia to the Monastery of the Holy Spirit in Conyers, taking in Panola Mountain, the historic Flat Rock community and old farms such as the Lyon Farm and Vaughters Farm.

## Flight Context

Arabia Mountain sits at approximately 33.665°N, 84.118°W in southeastern DeKalb County, about 15 nautical miles east-southeast of downtown Atlanta. From 2,500–3,500 feet AGL, look for pale, bare granite domes and the small Arabia Lake reservoir amid the woods; Stone Mountain, far larger and unmistakable, rises about 9 nautical miles to the north, and Panola Mountain lies a few miles to the southwest. The site sits under the busy Atlanta Class B airspace, so check altitudes carefully. Nearby airports include DeKalb-Peachtree Airport (KPDK), about 16 nautical miles to the northwest; Covington Municipal Airport (KCVC), about 14 nautical miles to the east; and Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International Airport (KATL), about 16 nautical miles to the west. Summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are common; clear winter days show off the red diamorpha on the rock.
