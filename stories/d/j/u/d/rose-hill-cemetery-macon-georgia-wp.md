# Rose Hill Cemetery (Macon, Georgia)

Laid out in 1840 as a garden cemetery on the Ocmulgee River, Macon's Rose Hill holds governors, generals, enslaved people in unmarked ground, and four members of the Allman Brothers Band, who once came here for inspiration.

**Location:** 32.8478, -83.6336

## Introduction

Simri Rose asked for one thing in payment. The horticulturist and newspaper editor, who had assisted in the original survey of the city of Macon, agreed to design its new cemetery in return for the right to choose his own burial plot. He got it, and the cemetery got his name. Rose Hill opened in 1840 on 50 acres along the banks of the Ocmulgee River, and more than a century later it would become a hangout and a source of inspiration for the Allman Brothers Band.

## A Garden for the Dead

In 1836 Rose, Ambrose Baber, Levi Eckley and R. W. Willis were commissioned to find a site for what would become Macon's third cemetery, after Fort Hill Cemetery and Old City Cemetery. They chose land outside the city, where it was cheaper. Rose, the horticulturist, personally decided where the trees and flowers would go and which kinds to plant. The plan followed the garden-cemetery ideal of Mount Auburn Cemetery, a landscaped ground meant to serve the living as a park as much as it served the dead as a burial place. Photographs from around 1877 show a visitors' spring and Central Avenue running in from the gate, the kind of place people came to stroll. In 1973 the cemetery was listed on the National Register of Historic Places. Time has not left the garden untouched. In 1881 a new railroad cut off the cemetery's access to the Ocmulgee River, and much later Interstate 16 added the constant hum of traffic. On March 13, 1954, a tornado uprooted many of its trees. Yet the cemetery endures, with the historic Riverside Cemetery immediately beside it, still holding generations of Macon side by side.

## Many Faiths, Many Stories

Rose Hill is divided into sections that trace Macon's communities. There is a Catholic section for Saint Joseph's Catholic Church and seven Jewish sections, among them the Hebrew Lot, the Polish Lot, the Russian Lot and Sherah Israel. Oak Ridge, created in 1851, is the final resting place of an unknown number of enslaved people. In Soldier's Square lie around 600 Civil War soldiers, Confederate and Union. The notable burials read like a history of Georgia: Blanton Winship, a U.S. Army major general who served as Judge Advocate General and then as Governor of Puerto Rico from 1934 to 1939; James Jackson, a congressman, Civil War judge advocate and chief justice of the Supreme Court of Georgia; Buck Melton, mayor of Macon from 1975 to 1979; and Peter J. Bracken, engineer of the locomotive Texas in the Great Locomotive Chase. Simri Rose lies here too, in the plot he bargained for.

## In Memory of Elizabeth Reed

In their early years, the Allman Brothers Band made Rose Hill a hangout and a place of artistic inspiration. Among the headstones is that of Elizabeth Reed Napier, who lived from 1845 to 1935 and inspired the band's song "In Memory of Elizabeth Reed." Today four members of the band rest here: slide guitarist Duane Allman, keyboardist and vocalist Gregg Allman, bassist Berry Oakley and drummer Butch Trucks. Their neighbors span the whole of Macon's past, with names such as Augustus Octavius Bacon, Alfred H. Colquitt, Eugenius Aristides Nisbet and J. Neel Reid carved nearby, so that rock musicians and figures from Georgia's earlier history share the same quiet ground along the river.

## Flight Context

Rose Hill Cemetery lies at 32.8478°N, 83.6336°W on the banks of the Ocmulgee River just north of downtown Macon, Georgia, with Riverside Cemetery immediately adjacent. From 1,500–2,500 feet, look for the wooded grounds along the river, bordered by the rail line and Interstate 16. Macon Downtown Airport (KMAC) is about 4.5 miles east-southeast, and Middle Georgia Regional Airport (KMCN) is about 11 miles south. Summers bring haze and afternoon thunderstorms; fall and winter give clearer views.
