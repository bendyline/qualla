# Hazel Jane Raines

Georgia's "First Lady in Flight" learned to fly in Macon despite asthma and a heart condition, ferried Spitfires for Britain in wartime, and served in the WASP and the Women's Air Force before her death at 40.

**Location:** 32.8513, -83.6381

**Categories:** Aviators, Women in aviation, Air Transport Auxiliary pilots, Women Airforce Service Pilots personnel, People from Waynesboro, Georgia, Macon, Georgia

## Introduction

On March 2, 1943, a Spitfire's engine malfunctioned over the English countryside, and the fighter crash-landed in Wiltshire on top of a cottage, at Collingbourne Kingston by most accounts, though the Air Transport Auxiliary's own record says near Upavon. It lost its wings. The cottage was severely damaged, yet no one on the ground was killed. The pilot, Hazel Jane Raines, a 26-year-old from Georgia, is said to have pulled herself out of the wreck and, following her Air Transport Auxiliary instructions for securing an aircraft, commanded, "London – guard that plane!" (the ATA record says only that she stayed in the cockpit until rescued, with concussion and minor injuries). Only then was she taken to a medical facility. It was one moment in a flying life that began at a small airport in Macon and ended, far too early, in London.

## Asthma, a Weak Heart and a License

Raines was born in Waynesboro, Georgia, on April 21, 1916, to Frank and Bessie Raines, joining sisters Frankie and Martha. She was born with an unspecified heart condition and chronic asthma, and she chased flying anyway. In 1936 she graduated from Wesleyan College in Macon, a liberal arts school chartered a century earlier, in 1836, as the Georgia Female College. She took her first lessons at Herbert Smart Airport in Macon, and when she earned her pilot's license in 1938 she became the first woman in Georgia to do so. Afterward she worked as a stunt pilot at local aviation shows. Much of what is known about these early years comes from a 1996 biography, Hazel Jane Raines: Pioneer Lady of Flight, written by her niece Regina Trice Hawkins from family letters inherited after Bessie Raines died in 1971.

## Teaching America to Fly

As war approached, the United States began training civilians who could help defend the country. President Franklin D. Roosevelt signed the Civilian Pilot Training Act on June 27, 1939, and a provision from Illinois congressman Everett Dirksen declared that "none of the benefits of training or programs shall be denied on account of race, creed, or color." The program opened doors that the segregated armed forces of the day kept shut, and the Tuskegee Airmen were a direct result. Raines earned her flight instructor's rating in 1941 and in 1942 began teaching for the program in Georgia and Florida. By then she also held a commercial pilot's license, had more than 1,000 hours of flying time, and belonged to the Ninety-Nines, the Macon Aero Club and the National Aeronautical Association.

## Twenty-Five Americans in a War Zone

Jacqueline Cochran was recruiting women for Britain's Air Transport Auxiliary, whose civilian pilots ferried aircraft between air bases for the Royal Air Force. Women made up roughly one in eight of the force's pilots, and the American recruits needed at least 300 hours of flying time. Cochran was impressed enough with Raines's credentials to interview her in New York in 1942, and Raines was accepted. Training began at White Waltham Airfield, where each pilot received Ferry Pilots' Notes on index cards: instructions for flying any plane they were assigned, whether or not they had any prior experience with that type. Raines and 24 other American women serving with the ATA at that time became the first women from the United States to fly military aircraft, and they did it in a theater of war, without radio contact and without ammunition. Raines flew Hawker Hurricanes, Supermarine Spitfires and de Havilland Tiger Moths.

## Grounded, Then Airborne Again

The crash left injuries, described in one account as head and eye injuries, severe enough to force a mandatory medical leave; the ATA's record has her off sick from March to June. Raines returned to flying in June 1943, piloting Airspeed Oxfords and Fairchild 24s, though her injuries still kept her from wearing a helmet. Her ATA contract ended on medical grounds on July 24, 1943, and she came home in August; the ATA lists her as a second officer, though some biographies say she left as a captain. By then the U.S. Army Air Forces had combined two women's flying programs under Cochran as the Women Airforce Service Pilots, or WASP, training at Avenger Field in Sweetwater, Texas. Raines joined in November 1943 and was made a squadron commander at Avenger Field, where she learned to fly the Martin B-26 Marauder. She went on to serve as a squadron commander at Pecos Army Air Field in Texas and at Kingman Army Airfield in Arizona. Then, on December 20, 1944, the WASP program was terminated.

## A Commission at Last

Raines came out of the war with no job and no benefits. She spent a year with the Air Ministry of Brazil in São Paulo, teaching instrument flying on the Link Trainer, an early flight simulator. When President Harry S. Truman signed the Women's Armed Services Integration Act of 1948, the military finally opened to women like her. In 1949 she was commissioned a second lieutenant in the Women's Air Force reserves at Lackland Air Force Base in Texas; in 1950 she returned to active duty at Maxwell Air Force Base in Alabama, and later she was posted to London and sent on to several European locations. Health problems surfaced in 1956, and after hospital stays in Wiesbaden and London she died in London on September 4 of a heart attack, aged 40, with about 6,400 flying hours to her name. She was brought home to Georgia and buried in Macon's Riverside Cemetery. In 1989, the year the Georgia Aviation Hall of Fame was founded, she was inducted as "Georgia's First Lady in Flight," and in 1995 she was named to Georgia Women of Achievement.

## Flight Context

This story is pinned to approximately 32.851°N, 83.638°W, at Riverside Cemetery beside the Ocmulgee River on the north side of Macon, Georgia, where Raines is buried. From 1,500–2,500 feet AGL, the river and downtown Macon make easy landmarks. Macon Downtown Airport (KMAC), formerly Herbert Smart Downtown Airport and the field where Raines took her first flying lessons, lies about 4 nautical miles to the southeast. Middle Georgia Regional Airport (KMCN) is about 9.5 nautical miles to the south, and Robins Air Force Base (KWRB) at Warner Robins is about 13 nautical miles to the south. Summer afternoons often bring haze and thunderstorms to central Georgia.
