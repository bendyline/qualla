# International Cherry Blossom Festival

A Macon realtor's puzzle over an unknown tree in his yard grew into hundreds of thousands of Yoshino cherries and a ten-day spring festival that turns a Georgia city pink.

**Location:** 32.8328, -83.6148

**Categories:** Cherry blossom festivals, Festivals in Georgia (U.S. state), Macon, Georgia, Spring festivals in the United States, Flower festivals in the United States

## Introduction

William A. Fickling Sr. had a tree in his yard in Macon, Georgia, that he could not name. He loved its blossoms, but he did not know its species, or that it was not native to the South. The answer came in 1952, on a trip to Washington, D.C., when he spotted a tree that looked exactly like his own. That chance resemblance would eventually turn Macon into a city of hundreds of thousands of Yoshino cherry trees (the festival cites more than 350,000) that bloom across the city every late March.

## Macon's Johnny Appleseed

Fickling was a local realtor, and he tested his hunch the practical way. He compared cut samples from his own tree with those from the cherries at Washington's Tidal Basin, and they matched: his mystery tree was a Yoshino cherry. After that he began propagating the trees and giving them away to people around Macon, and he became known as the city's Johnny Appleseed, a fitting title for a man who handed out saplings the way the folk hero scattered seeds. By 1982 he had donated about 30,000 of them. Even today, after his death, the Fickling Family Foundation donates the Yoshinos that the community keeps planting every year.

## A Bargain Made at a Picnic

Carolyn Crayton was new to Macon when she first noticed the trees and thought they were beautiful. At a picnic she approached Fickling and talked with him about planting more of them across the city. They struck a deal: Fickling would donate the trees if Crayton would organize the planting. On November 24, 1973, the first of those Yoshinos went into the ground, and the community planted around 500 along Wesleyan Woods Drive, Guerry Drive and Oxford Road. Year by year the trees spread through the city's neighborhoods until the pink blossoms became an identifying symbol of a city now known as the "Cherry Blossom Capital of the World."

## A One-Time Party That Stayed

In 1982 Crayton had the idea of celebrating Fickling and the trees he had given the city, and she worked with the Keep Macon-Bibb Beautiful Commission to create the event. That spring, on March 23, Fickling's birthday, Macon held its first International Cherry Blossom Festival, a three-day affair held in Third Street Park by the festival's own account, with a birthday celebration at Wesleyan College in other tellings. It was meant to happen only once. It was so well received that it became an annual celebration, now running ten days to match the typical bloom in late March. Macon's sister city, Kurobe, Japan, a partnership dating to 1977, is a festival partner; by Wikipedia's account its representatives came to that first festival and it has sent gifts every year since, from lanterns to flowers. The festival has run every year except 2020, when the COVID-19 pandemic forced its cancellation and the 39th edition was deferred to 2021. Despite the shared flower, it has no connection to the National Cherry Blossom Festival in Washington.

## Painting the Town Pink

Planning starts months ahead, with a board, staff and community volunteers such as the Think Pink committee. As opening day approaches, fountain water is dyed pink and cherry blossoms are painted around the festival area. Each year begins with a ribbon cutting, and what follows can include concerts, animal shows, theater, international showcases, house tours, fashion shows, galas, marathons, dances, fair rides and bus tours along the Cherry Blossom trails. A fixture for decades is the Pink Pancake Breakfast, served by Macon-Bibb County firefighters inside Luther Williams Field in Central City Park; in 2026 the proceeds went to United in Pink, a Central Georgia breast cancer support organization. The crowds are real: the festival drew an estimated 282,000 attendees in 2026, up 4.6 percent from 2025, and Macon-Bibb County put that year's economic impact at $6.9 million. The festival itself, a nonprofit funded mainly by sponsors, brought in about $3 million in 2018, and it has been recognized by the International Festivals and Events Association, winning Haas & Wilkerson Pinnacle awards in gold, silver and bronze for its promotion, programming and outreach.

## Flight Context

The festival is centered in Macon, Georgia, at about 32.8328°N, 83.6148°W, near Central City Park. From 2,000–3,000 feet in late March, the blooming Yoshino cherries can tint whole neighborhoods pink. Macon Downtown Airport (KMAC) is about 3 miles east-southeast, and Middle Georgia Regional Airport (KMCN) is about 10 miles south-southwest; Robins Air Force Base (KWRB) lies about 13 miles to the south, so check its military airspace before flying that way. Spring brings changeable weather, with fronts and occasional thunderstorms.
