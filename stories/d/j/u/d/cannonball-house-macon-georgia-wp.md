# Cannonball House (Macon, Georgia)

A Greek Revival planter's townhouse on Macon's Mulberry Street took its name from a Union shell that crashed through its front in 1864 and came to rest, unexploded, in the hallway.

**Location:** 32.8398, -83.6322

## Introduction

On July 30, 1864, a Union shell fired by troops under George Stoneman struck a handsome Greek Revival house on Macon's Mulberry Street. It bounced through the front of the building and came to rest in the hallway without exploding. The house has carried the name of that shell ever since. The Cannonball House, built in 1853 and standing at 856 Mulberry Street, is now a museum listed on the National Register of Historic Places, and few buildings owe their name so completely to a single afternoon.

## A Planter's Town House

Judge Asa Holt, a local planter, built the house in 1853 as a planter's townhouse, his family's foothold in the city. It has gone by other names over the years, including the Home of Judge Asa Holt and the Mansion of the Old South, but the cannonball won the argument. Holt was living here during the Civil War, so it was his family's front wall that the shell passed through in the summer of 1864. Today the rooms are furnished in the style of the period from 1853 to 1870, spanning the house's first years, the war that named it and the uneasy years that followed. Out in the front yard sits a bronze cannon forged at the Macon Arsenal in 1864, a reminder that the city was making weapons of its own even as Union guns found it.

## Saved From a Parking Lot

The Holt family held on to the house for nearly a century after the war, keeping it until 1962. By then it was in trouble. The property had been liened for taxes, and the City of Macon was interested in tearing it down to make room for a parking lot. The United Daughters of the Confederacy stepped in and bought the house from the last family member still living there, Miss Elizabeth Martin. Rather than turn her out, the new owners made her an apartment upstairs, and she became the Cannonball House's very first tour guide. There is something quietly remarkable in that arrangement. The woman who had lived in the house became the one who explained it to strangers, her private home turned into a public story while she still slept under its roof.

## Parlors of Two Early Sororities

Some of the house's most surprising rooms have nothing to do with the war. The Adelphean Society, founded in 1851 at Macon's Wesleyan College and known today as Alpha Delta Pi, is described by the college as the oldest secret society for college women in the world, and the house's parlor holds some of the society's original furnishings and window treatments, along with a marble mantel. A second collection belongs to the Philomathean Society, founded at the same college in 1852 and known today as Phi Mu, and includes window treatments, furniture and another marble mantel. Standing among them, visitors meet a different nineteenth century than the one the cannonball suggests, one in which young women were organizing societies of their own, institutions that still exist under new names today.

## Uniforms, Quilts and Quarters

The Civil War museum within the house displays uniforms worn by Georgian officers and the weapons they used, including a Richmond Depot Type II jacket, along with company flags such as that of the Jackson Artillery. Elsewhere, the general collection rotates textiles through its displays because they are too fragile to stay out for long: quilts handmade between 1850 and 1920, nineteenth-century wedding dresses, original day dresses and children's clothing. Then there are the servant's quarters, furnished with two beds, trunks, spinning wheels, three tables and tea sets, a wardrobe, straight chairs, a weasel, a pie safe, a grain bin and a bathtub. The inventory is precise about the objects and silent about the people. Who slept in those beds and worked those spinning wheels, and under what terms they came to serve a planter's household, is a question worth carrying out of the house.
