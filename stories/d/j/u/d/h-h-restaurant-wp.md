# H&H Restaurant

A Macon soul food kitchen opened in 1959 by two women, where a plate of food extended on credit to a broke young band named the Allman Brothers turned Louise Hudson into the "Mother of Southern Rock."

**Location:** 32.8357, -83.6348

## Introduction

The musicians had pooled their money for two meals. There were more of them than that, and the woman running the restaurant noticed. Louise Hudson, known to everyone as Mama Louise, felt sorry for the hungry young players and brought each of them a plate of their own, telling them to pay her when they had the money. The band was The Allman Brothers Band, then struggling young musicians recording for Capricorn Records, and that act of kindness at the H&H Restaurant grew into a friendship that lasted for decades. The band never forgot it. Neither has Macon.

## Two Women, One Kitchen

The H&H began with Inez Hill, born on October 13, 1913, in or near Warrenton, Georgia. She moved to Macon in 1950 when her husband took a job at Robins Air Force Base, and in 1959 she opened a restaurant with her goddaughter and cousin, Louise Hudson. The two women became Mama Hill and Mama Louise to the people they fed. The restaurant wandered before it settled. Its first home was at Hayes and Third Street, then Cotton Avenue, and finally 807 Forsyth Street in downtown Macon. Along the way it became more than a lunch counter. The H&H served as a meeting place for civil rights activists and for the NAACP, including Georgia state presidents and officers, giving the dining room a role in the city's history well beyond its menu.

## Plate Lunch, Rotating Daily

The H&H is a soul food restaurant built on the "meat and three" formula, and the lineup changes by the day. Today's plate lunches rotate, with fried chicken and meatloaf on offer every day and other choices such as beef tips, fried pork chops, catfish and chicken and dumplings. An older menu published by the restaurant under its founders laid out a fuller schedule. Its meats ran from roast beef, fried chicken and smothered chicken to fried pork chops, stew beef, baked ham, barbecue ribs and fried fish. As at traditional Southern diners and lunch counters, the vegetables changed with the day of the week: Mondays brought macaroni and cheese, lima beans, okra, rice and squash; Tuesdays turned to corn, boiled okra, snap beans and black-eyed peas; Friday added rice and gravy. Collard greens appeared on every day's list, Monday through Saturday. For dessert, there was peach cobbler, apple cobbler, sweet potato pie and bread pudding. It was a menu that read like a Sunday dinner served all week.

## On Tour With the Band

Once the Allman Brothers became famous, they repaid Mama Louise in their own way. In 1972, as the story goes, they invited her along on tour to California to cook their meals, although she never cooked a single thing. The friendship covered the restaurant's walls, which filled with Allman Brothers memorabilia and posters, along with one of the awards for their Fillmore East album. Other Southern rock bands found the place as well; Molly Hatchet and the Wet Willie Band were regular customers. In 2007, by the restaurant's own account, hundreds of people gathered at the Macon Armory Ballroom for Mama Louise's birthday. Gregg Allman told stories of the band's days at the H&H and on the road with her, and the politician Jim Marshall delivered a proclamation from Washington honoring her as "Lady Mama Louise." The mayor's office declared July 26 Mama Louise Day, and she received a brick for the Georgia Music Hall of Fame walkway naming her the Mother of Southern Rock. To close the evening, Allman and fellow musicians took the stage for "These Days," "Melissa" and "Midnight Rider," her favorite song.

## Oprah, Farewells and a Second Life

In November 2007, Oprah Winfrey came to Macon to record an episode of her television show, Oprah's Favorite Things. By the restaurant's own account, she stopped at the H&H, slipping in through the back door past security guards and a crowd of fans, and talked with Mama Louise and Mama Inez, signing autographs and posing for pictures before she left. A few weeks later, on December 18, 2007, Inez Hill died. She had collapsed while working at the restaurant she founded. She had lived to be a mother of seven and a grandmother of sixteen, and a great- and great-great-grandmother besides. Her funeral in Macon was closed to the public. The H&H itself closed in 2013, when Mama Louise retired, and for a moment it seemed that its story might end. It reopened the following year under the Moonhanger Group, a Macon business that owns several local restaurants, and the fryers were soon lit again. Louise Hudson died on November 8, 2022, at 93; the H&H still opens for breakfast and lunch on Forsyth Street.
