# Sidney Lanier Cottage

The High Street cottage in Macon where poet, flutist and soldier Sidney Lanier was born in 1842, now honored as both a Landmark of American Music and a Landmark of American Poetry.

**Location:** 32.8376, -83.6363

**Categories:** Houses on the National Register of Historic Places in Georgia (U.S. state), Houses in Macon, Georgia, Literary museums in the United States, Museums in Macon, Georgia, Historic house museums in Georgia (U.S. state), National Register of Historic Places in Bibb County, Georgia

## Introduction

Mary Jane Anderson Lanier was living in Griffin, Georgia, when her first child was due, but she did not have the baby there. She traveled to Macon, to the High Street home of her husband's parents, and in that small frame house in 1842 she gave birth to Sidney Lanier. The boy would grow up to be a soldier, a concert flutist and a poet remembered above all for his regional verse, and his life would be cruelly short. The cottage where it began still stands on High Street, a white Gothic Revival house honored for music and for poetry alike, though it is now a private home rather than a museum.

## A House That Kept Changing

The house belonged to Sidney's grandfather, Sterling Lanier. It was built in 1840 as a four-room cottage, and it did not stay that way for long. The family altered it extensively over the years. In 1879 the entire building was moved fifty feet, and the Laniers took the opportunity to add two rooms on the second floor along with a porch. A year later, in 1880, came the renovation that gave the house its present Gothic Revival style. So the white frame home that stands there today is not quite the cottage where Sidney was born but a record of a family adapting its house across four decades. It is fitting, in a way, for the birthplace of a man whose own life moved through so many forms: soldier, prisoner, musician, scholar and poet.

## Soldier, Prisoner, Flutist

Lanier's youth was consumed by the Civil War. He served in the Confederate Signal Corps, mostly in the tidewater region of Virginia, and on November 3, 1864, the ship he was aboard, the Lucy, was captured by the USS Santiago de Cuba. He was imprisoned at the military prison at Point Lookout in Maryland, and there he contracted tuberculosis, the illness that would shadow the rest of his life. In 1867 he married Mary Day of Macon. Music became one of his callings. Lanier signed on to play flute with the Peabody Orchestra in Baltimore shortly after it was organized and quickly rose to first flautist. That silver alto flute, the one he played with the Peabody, later found its way home to the cottage in Macon as part of the museum's collection while the museum operated.

## Marshes, Rivers and Sunrise

Lanier is best remembered for his regional poems. "The Song of the Chattahoochee" was written in 1877 and "The Marshes of Glynn" in 1878, and together with "Sunrise" they are the poems for which he is best known. He also became a student, then a lecturer and finally a faculty member at Johns Hopkins University in Baltimore. The tuberculosis never let go. On September 7, 1881, while he was convalescing with his family near Lynn, North Carolina, Lanier died of complications from the disease. He was 39. Even their titles turn toward river, marsh and morning light, which makes it all the more poignant that the man who wrote them spent his last years ill with the disease he had caught in a prison camp.

## Landmark for Music and Verse

For many years the cottage was simply a private residence. It joined the National Register of Historic Places in 1972, and in 1973 the Middle Georgia Historical Society bought it. The honors kept coming. The house became a Landmark of American Music in 1976 and a Landmark of American Poetry in 2004, recognizing both halves of Lanier's artistic life. As a museum run by the Historic Macon Foundation, it displayed artifacts of his work, including that silver flute, first editions of his books, photographs of Sidney and Mary, and the wedding dress Mary Day wore in 1867. The foundation also organized the Lanier Center for Literary Arts, and the cottage, part of the Macon Historic District, hosted book signings and writers' workshops called Sidney's Salon, keeping a house of poetry busy with new writers. That chapter has closed. In May 2020 the foundation announced that it would close the cottage and sell it as a single-family residence, subject to strict guidelines, with the proceeds going to a new Lanier education fund. The museum operated until 2021, and the house is now a private home, no longer open to the public.
