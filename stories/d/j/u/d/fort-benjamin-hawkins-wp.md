# Fort Benjamin Hawkins

On the Fall Line above the Ocmulgee River, a frontier fort where the Lower Creek, the young United States and Georgia met in trade and war is slowly giving up its secrets to the archaeologists digging beneath Macon.

**Location:** 32.8489, -83.6116

**Categories:** Forts in Georgia (U.S. state), 19th-century fortifications, Buildings and structures in Macon, Georgia, Archaeological sites, Muscogee (Creek) history, Forts on the National Register of Historic Places in Georgia (U.S. state)

## Introduction

On paper, Fort Hawkins sounded modest: a log palisade enclosing about 1.4 acres by one account (the National Park Service gives 4), a blockhouse at two corners, and quarters where soldiers and officers lived and worked. The ground told a bigger story. When archaeologists from the Lamar Institute began digging here in 2005, they found a second palisade wall and the remains of several brick buildings, and by the time the work wound down in 2013 they had recovered nearly 40,000 artifacts. Built by the United States Army between 1806 and 1810, during Thomas Jefferson's administration, the fort stood on the Fall Line on the east side of the Ocmulgee River, in what is now Macon. For the Lower Creek, for the United States and for the State of Georgia, it mattered for reasons of trade, war and politics, and each of those relationships left something behind in the soil.

## Where the Trading Path Crossed

The site was chosen with an eye to the land. From its rise the fort overlooked the Ocmulgee Old Fields, and the Lower Creek Trading Path ran right past its northwestern blockhouse before continuing west to a natural ford on the Ocmulgee River. Anyone moving goods or messages along that route passed under the soldiers' eyes. The fort took its name from Benjamin Hawkins, a former United States Senator from North Carolina who from 1801 served as the government's principal agent for Indian affairs south of the Ohio River. Commerce was part of the design from the start. Besides housing troops, Fort Hawkins has been described as "a major government fur trade factory for regulating the Creek economy", a place where the federal government could shape the terms on which the Creek bought and sold.

## Headquarters of a Frontier

For a time this outpost served as the Southeastern Command of the U.S. Army, described as "a major troop garrison and bivouac point for regular troops and state militia in several important campaigns." During the War of 1812 against Great Britain and the Creek War, it was where troops were gathered and supplied: in October 1814 more than 2,500 militiamen were organized and equipped here to join General Andrew Jackson at Mobile, and in February 1818 Jackson himself arrived with 1,000 Tennessee volunteers to await reinforcements for the First Seminole War. Then the frontier moved on. As settlement pushed farther west, the military threat to interior Georgia essentially receded, and the fort era here closed around 1821. For the Creek people whose trading path ran past the walls, the cost of that westward shift was their homeland: through the treaties of 1825 and 1826 they were forced to move west of the Chattahoochee River.

## Reading the Ground

Interest in the old fort revived in the twentieth century. In 1928 work began on a replica of the southeastern blockhouse, based on plans by Curran Ellis and Henry Behr, though it was not finished until 1938, and a small excavation took place in 1936. The real revelations came later. Funded by the Peyton Anderson Foundation and the City of Macon, principal archaeologist Dan Elliott found evidence of two palisade walls, the second enclosing several brick buildings, which made the complex more substantial than its sparse historical descriptions suggested. More palisades turned up in 2007, probably built in 1809 and 1810 by the Army's Regiment of Rifles. Broken ceramics, including English-American style dishes dating from about 1779 to about 1834, hint at the tables where the fort's residents ate. The northwestern blockhouse proved elusive. Erosion and grading have erased every outward sign of it, and a 2012 dig found only indirect evidence: the western palisade wall stops abruptly at its northern end, just where the blockhouse should have stood.

## A Fort Still Being Rebuilt

Recognition came in stages. The Fort Hawkins Archeological Site joined the National Register of Historic Places in 1977, became part of the Fort Hill Historic District in 1993, and gained a state highway marker in 2002. From 1990 to 2018 the Fort Hawkins Commission, chaired by Bob Cramer, ran the site and launched the modern excavations. A replica log cabin dedicated in 2014 serves as the visitors' center. Since 2018 the non-profit Fort Hawkins Foundation has managed the grounds, with plans to reconstruct the entire fort complex so visitors can step into its history and archaeology, along with that of the Woolfolk Plantation and the Fort Hawkins School, which both occupied the property at different times. The site keeps its community close. The Ocmulgee Archaeological Society holds its monthly meetings in the visitors' center, and each May for about two decades it has hosted Fort Hawkins Archaeology Day on the grounds, inviting the public to see how much a single hill above the river still has to say.

## Flight Context

Fort Hawkins sits at 32.8489 N, 83.6116 W on the east side of the Ocmulgee River in Macon, Georgia, with the replica blockhouse on a rise overlooking the river valley and downtown Macon just across the water to the west. The Ocmulgee Old Fields, which the fort once overlooked, lie in the river lowlands nearby. A pass at roughly 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL keeps the river bend, the Old Fields and the fort's hill in one view. Macon Downtown Airport (KMAC) is about 3 nm away, Middle Georgia Regional Airport (KMCN) about 10 nm, and Robins Air Force Base (KWRB) about 13 nm south, so stay clear of its controlled military airspace. Central Georgia summer afternoons often bring haze and pop-up thunderstorms; mornings and cooler months usually give the clearest views.
