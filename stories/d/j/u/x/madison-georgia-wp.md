# Madison, Georgia

A small Georgia county seat famous for antebellum houses and the legend that Sherman found it too beautiful to burn, whose real history includes political bargaining, a prosperous Black entrepreneur and documented lynchings its polished reputation long obscured.

## Introduction

Ask about Madison, Georgia, and someone will probably tell you that General William Tecumseh Sherman spared it on his March to the Sea because it was simply too beautiful to burn. It is a lovely story, and it is not quite true. Madison was home to Joshua Hill, a pro-Union congressman who later became a senator. Hill had served in the House of Representatives at the same time as Sherman's brother John, who went on to the Senate from Ohio, and he reportedly appealed to Sherman himself to spare the town. The town's survival owed more to politics than to admiration. That gap between the legend and the record runs through Madison's history, a place whose elegant streets have always carried more than one story.

## Culture on the Stagecoach Road

Madison was incorporated on December 12, 1809, and named for James Madison, the fourth president of the United States. It did not take long for the town to acquire a reputation. A nineteenth-century issue of White's Statistics of Georgia called it "the most cultured and aristocratic town on the stagecoach route from Charleston to New Orleans." By 1849 the same publication was more effusive still: "In point of intelligence, refinement, and hospitality, this town acknowledges no superior." Some of the houses from that era remain. Heritage Hall's original portion dates to 1811, and Bonar Hall has been described as one of the first of the grand-style Federal homes built in Madison. Today the town is featured on Georgia's Antebellum Trail and is designated one of the state's Historic Heartland cities, and its historic district, listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1974, has been described as one of the largest in the state.

## Mills, Gins and a Carriage Maker

By the 1890s, Madison had become a busy small industrial town. An 1895 report listed an oil mill with $35,000 in capital, a soap factory, a fertilizer factory, four steam ginneries and what it called a mammoth compress. There were two carriage factories, a furniture factory, a grist and flouring mill, a bottling works, a distillery that could produce 120 gallons a day, an ice factory, a canning factory and a bank with $75,000 in capital. One of those carriage factories is said to have belonged to H. R. Goldwire, a prominent African American businessman and entrepreneur. His success is worth dwelling on, because it came in the Jim Crow era and in a town where, as the record shows, the danger to Black residents was anything but abstract.

## What Refinement Hid

Behind that prosperity, white Madisonians took part in at least three documented lynchings of African Americans. In February 1890, Brown Washington, a fifteen-year-old boy, was found guilty of the murder of a nine-year-old white girl after a rushed trial in which jurors carried knives. The sheriff, with the governor's approval, called up the Madison Home Guard to protect him, but only three militiamen and none of the officers answered. A posse of ten men organized by a "leading local businessman" took Washington from the jail and promptly handed him to a mob of more than 300 people waiting outside the courthouse, and the mob killed him. Afterward, a sign was posted on the telegraph pole where he died: "Our women and children will be protected." In 1919, a few days before Thanksgiving, a mob took another man, Wallace Baynes, from his home, and he was shot near the Little River.

## Remembering the Whole Town

It would be easy to tell Madison's story only through its porches and parlors. The White's Statistics boasts about refinement, the legend of a town too pretty to burn and the lynching of a fifteen-year-old boy by men described as "among the best citizens" all belong to the same place and the same century. Brown Washington and Wallace Baynes were people, not footnotes, and their deaths are as much a part of Madison's past as Heritage Hall. Madison today is a town of 4,447 people by the 2020 census, roughly half white and more than forty percent Black. Its historic district remains the town's leading attraction, and walking it with the full history in mind makes the beauty more complicated, and the visit more honest.

## Flight Context

Madison lies in Morgan County, Georgia, at 33.5878N, 83.4722W, east of Atlanta just off Interstate 20, which makes a clear line to follow from the west. From 2,000-3,000 feet, look for the grid of the historic district, with U.S. Route 278 running through the center of town. Madison Municipal Airport (FAA code 52A) is only about 1.6 nm north-northeast of town. Athens Ben Epps Airport (KAHN) is about 23 nm north-northeast, and Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International (KATL) about 48 nm west; stay clear of Atlanta's Class B airspace when approaching from that direction. Lake Oconee lies to the south. Summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are common across the Georgia Piedmont.
