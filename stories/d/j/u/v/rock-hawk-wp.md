# Rock Hawk

Near Lake Oconee in Putnam County, Georgia, thousands of quartzite stones laid by Woodland-period people 1,000 to 3,000 years ago form a great bird whose species, and whose original shape, remain open questions.

**Location:** 33.3449, -83.1750

**Categories:** Adena culture, Native American history of Georgia (U.S. state), Rock art in North America, Indigenous culture of the Southeastern Woodlands, Protected areas of Putnam County, Georgia, Archaeological sites in Georgia (U.S. state)

## Introduction

Everyone calls it a hawk, but no one actually knows what bird it is. Somewhere between 1,000 and 3,000 years ago, people living in what is now Putnam County, Georgia, gathered thousands of pieces of quartzite and laid them on the ground in the shape of a large bird. The name Rock Hawk came much later, and scholars are careful to say they cannot tell what kind of bird the builders meant to portray. What survives near the shores of Lake Oconee is one of only two such bird effigy mounds found east of the Mississippi River, and it asks more of its visitors than most monuments do.

## Two Birds in One County

Rock Hawk has a single sibling, and it lives close by. The other bird effigy east of the Mississippi, Rock Eagle, lies about thirteen miles to the west-northwest, also in Putnam County. Rock Eagle is by far the more famous of the two. Its stone bird measures 102 feet from head to tail and 120 feet from wing tip to wing tip, and the University of Georgia administers the site, using much of the surrounding land for a 4-H camp and environmental education. Rock Hawk has had a quieter life. Its physical condition is far below that of its sister, and the difference is itself a puzzle. Scholars do not know whether Rock Hawk suffered greater degradation, whether Rock Eagle benefited from better preservation or reconstruction, or whether some combination of those and other factors explains why one bird is so much clearer than the other.

## Seeing the Bird

Standing at Rock Hawk, you have to work a little. Viewers must use a degree of imagination to see a bird in the remains, let alone a hawk. The stones are there, thousands of pieces of quartzite, but the outline is far harder to read than at Rock Eagle. Photographs of the site still mark out one end as the bird's "head," and an observation tower stands nearby. There is something honest about a monument that refuses to perform. Rock Hawk does not offer a crisp silhouette. It offers a pattern of stones that rewards patience and invites visitors to picture the bird as its makers might have seen it.

## The Builders

Current archaeology points to Woodland-period Native Americans as the likely builders, working sometime between 1,000 and 3,000 years ago. They may have been connected to the Adena or Hopewell cultures, though scholars think it more likely that the people who built Rock Hawk represented a unique group of their own. Their names, their language and the meaning the bird held for them are not recorded. What remains is the labor itself: thousands of stones gathered and set, piece by piece, into a single figure. Whatever the bird was meant to be, it mattered enough to its builders to be worth that effort, and it has long outlasted any memory of their names.

## A Fence, a Trail and a Lake

Today Rock Hawk is protected rather than displayed. Access to the mound itself is prohibited, and a tall fence surrounds it. The Georgia Power company, whose Wallace Dam created the lake, maintains the effigy and its hiking trails, which cover about 15 miles, with parking at the trail entrances and other recreational amenities. Visiting costs nothing. The site sits along Wallace Dam Road, off State Route 16, about 12 miles east of Eatonton, close to the shores of the manmade Lake Oconee. That juxtaposition is part of the experience: a modern reservoir and its recreation on one side, and on the other, behind a fence, a stone bird laid down by people who knew this land long before any dam or lake existed.

## Flight Context

Rock Hawk lies near the shores of Lake Oconee in Putnam County, Georgia, at about 33.345N, 83.175W, roughly 12 miles east of Eatonton along Wallace Dam Road. Lake Oconee is the best landmark from altitude. The effigy itself is a low stone figure in poor condition, so a slow pass at 1,000-1,500 feet gives the only real chance of picking out the site; Rock Eagle, the better-preserved sister effigy, lies about 13 statute miles to the west-northwest. Madison Municipal Airport (FAA code 52A) is about 21 nm northwest, and Baldwin County Regional Airport (KMLJ) at Milledgeville about 12 nm south-southwest. Athens/Ben Epps Airport (KAHN) is about 37 nm north. Expect haze on summer afternoons over the Georgia Piedmont.
