# Eatonton, Georgia

Georgia's self-styled Dairy Capital is also the birthplace of the Uncle Remus stories' author and of Alice Walker, a small Piedmont city whose history runs from Barbary War namesake to New Deal homesteads.

**Location:** 33.3264, -83.3878

**Categories:** Cities in Georgia (U.S. state), County seats in Georgia (U.S. state), Cities in Putnam County, Georgia, Dairy farming, Literary history, 1807 establishments in Georgia (U.S. state)

## Introduction

It is hard to think of two books farther apart than the Uncle Remus stories and The Color Purple, yet their authors share a hometown. Joel Chandler Harris and Alice Walker were both born in Eatonton, a Piedmont city of 6,307 people at the 2020 census that likes to introduce itself another way entirely: as the "Dairy Capital of Georgia." The county seat of Putnam County sits where U.S. Routes 129 and 441 meet, 22 miles south of Madison and 21 miles northwest of Milledgeville. Its story takes in a Barbary War hero, a New York banker with fifteen cows, a planned farm community called Briarpatch, and five hours of fire in 1919 for which no one was ever charged.

## A Name from the Barbary Coast

Eatonton's history begins with the Creek. In 1790, by Wikipedia's account, Birdtail, chief of the Cusseta, was among the twenty-seven Muscogee leaders, led by Alexander McGillivray, who signed the Treaty of New York on behalf of their people, an agreement meant to bring peace and friendly relations between the Creek Nation and the United States. After the Revolutionary War, settlers pushed west into the upland Piedmont to plant cotton, and in 1807 (1808 in some local accounts) Eatonton was founded as the seat of newly formed Putnam County. Its name came from far away. William Eaton was an officer and diplomat who served in the First Barbary War, and the town simply added the English suffix "ton," meaning town, to his surname. Eatonton was incorporated as a town in 1809 and as a city in 1879.

## Fifteen Cows and a Banker

The Civil War left Putnam County's plantation wealth diminished or gone, and for the rest of the nineteenth century the county searched for a new economy. It found one in 1876, when Benjamin Hunt, a former New York banker, settled in Putnam with fifteen dairy cows and established Panola Farm. The experimental dairy became a cornerstone of a new industry. By 1901 Putnam ranked third among Georgia's dairy counties, shipping cream to Athens and butter to Atlanta. By 1909 local farmers had founded the Eatonton Co-Operative Creamery to produce milk, cream and butter together, and in 1946 it sold $1,406,000 worth of dairy products. The town began celebrating its farmers with a Dairy Festival in 1953, and the tradition continues with an annual parade on the first Saturday of June; the 2026 festival, billed as the 65th, was held on June 6.

## The Briarpatch Experiment

In the northeastern corner of the county, a homestead project first called Chancellorsville Homestead, Inc. was renamed by the FSA as the Briarpatch Farm Project. Spread across 12,000 acres, it gave each settling family land, a home, farm buildings and government stock, along with running water and electricity. Getting in was not easy. Applicants had to be "thrifty, honest, have civic pride," and bring three references from local citizens. The first families moved into their homes on February 7, 1935, and fifty more had arrived by June. The settlers farmed cotton, dairy and timber, ran a workshop, a cannery and a poultry house, and celebrated holidays like the Fourth of July together. They fielded a baseball team, opened a small library, and by 1937 had a Woman's Club, a farmers' organization, a girls' cooking and sewing club and a non-denominational Sunday school. That year, the government contracted to build the New Phoenix School on Briarpatch land for $2,500.

## Fire and Wind

Eatonton's past has darker pages. In 1893 only 12 percent of Putnam County's children attended school, in a system divided into 14 schools for white children and 24 for Black children, and segregation did not end in Eatonton's schools until 1969. On the night of May 27 into May 28, 1919, at least five Black churches and two Black lodges in and around Eatonton were burned to the ground, by some counts six or more churches. The community raised a reward of $1,100 for information, but no one is known to have been charged with arson. For the Black congregations and lodge members who lost their places of worship and gathering, there was no reckoning. Nature struck too. On November 22, 1992, an F4 tornado, a rating that covers winds of roughly 207 to 260 miles per hour, tore through the southern part of the city, killing five people by most counts, injuring 86 and causing $27 million in damage to homes and businesses.

## A Town of Storytellers

Alongside Harris, the author and journalist behind Uncle Remus, and Walker, the novelist of The Color Purple, Eatonton claims the nineteenth-century poet Louise Prudden Hunt and Henry Grady Weaver, author of The Mainspring of Human Progress. David Driskell, the artist, scholar and curator who championed African American art, was a native, as was S. Truett Cathy, founder of Chick-fil-A. Thomas Adiel Sherwood, born here, sat on the Missouri Supreme Court from 1873 to 1902. North of town lies an older kind of storytelling: the Rock Eagle Effigy Mound, probably built by Woodland-period peoples perhaps 1,000 to 3,000 years ago and now set within a 1,500-acre park run by the University of Georgia beside its 4-H camp.

## Flight Context

Eatonton is centered at about 33.3264 N, 83.3878 W in Georgia's Piedmont. Follow U.S. 129/441, which meet at the city's western edge: the combined highway runs north 22 miles to Madison, and south of town the routes split toward Gray and Milledgeville. Georgia routes 16 and 44 cross the center of town. The Rock Eagle Effigy Mound and its park lie just north of the city. A cruise at 2,500 to 3,500 feet MSL takes in the town grid and the surrounding farm country. Baldwin County Regional Airport (KMLJ) at Milledgeville is about 13 nm southeast, and Madison Municipal Airport (FAA identifier 52A, no ICAO code) is about 17 nm north-northwest. Expect summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms; fall and spring bring the best visibility.
