# Old State Capitol (Milledgeville, Georgia)

Georgia's Gothic Revival statehouse in Milledgeville hosted the legislature for six decades, the 1861 vote for secession and a Union occupation, before the capital left for Atlanta and the building became the heart of a military college.

## Introduction

On January 19, 1861, delegates gathered in Milledgeville's statehouse voted 208 to 89 to adopt an Ordinance of Secession, and Georgia became the fifth state to leave the Union. Less than four years later, Union troops were occupying the same town and the building where that vote was cast had been damaged. The building sat squarely in the path of Georgia's nineteenth-century history. The Old State Capitol, a Gothic Revival statehouse on Greene Street, served as Georgia's seat of government for six decades. Today its halls belong to cadets and tour groups rather than legislators.

## A Capital Drawn on a Map

Milledgeville was not Georgia's first capital; the seat of government had moved among several towns, most recently Louisville. On December 12, 1804, the legislature voted to designate centrally located Milledgeville as the capital instead. In 1805 lawmakers appropriated $60,000 for a capitol building, and planners proposed a city that borrowed elements from both Savannah and Washington, D.C. Jett Thomas was among the builders. Lawmakers did not wait for the work to be finished; they met in the incomplete building for the first time in 1807. The statehouse kept growing for decades. A north wing was completed in 1828 and a south wing in 1834, and in 1835 east and west porticoes with granite steps were added. The result was a capitol in the Gothic Revival style, grown wing by wing over nearly three decades.

## Lafayette, Secession and Sherman

In 1825 the building received a famous guest, the Marquis de Lafayette, then on his tour through the United States. Thirty-six years later came a far more consequential moment. Georgia's Secession Convention met in the building in January 1861, and on January 19 the vote to leave the Union carried. During the Civil War, Governor Joe Brown operated from the statehouse. The war came to the statehouse's door in November 1864, when General William T. Sherman's army reached Milledgeville on its March to the Sea. Union forces occupied the city on November 23. The town escaped widespread destruction, but the Old Capitol was damaged, and the armories and magazines on Statehouse Square were destroyed. Grain and livestock were seized and homes looted, a hard reckoning for the town that had hosted the vote to leave the Union.

## Old South, New South

Milledgeville's time as capital ended in 1868, during Reconstruction, when the legislature moved the seat of government to Atlanta. The choice carried symbolism. Atlanta was emerging as the emblem of the New South and a growing transportation and commercial hub, while Milledgeville was seen as tied to the Old South. The statehouse did not sit empty for long. From 1871 to 1879 it served as the courthouse for Baldwin County. After that it was given over to education, becoming home to the Middle Georgia Military and Agricultural College, which took the name Georgia Military College in 1900. The building that had once housed the debates of a state government now echoed with the routines of a school, a second life that has lasted far longer than its first.

## The College on Statehouse Square

Today the Old State Capitol still stands at the center of Georgia Military College's campus. Visitors can walk the grounds, and interior tours are given privately by a college guide and arranged through the Milledgeville Visitor Center. It was added to the National Register of Historic Places on May 13, 1970. Walking up to it, visitors approach by way of porticoes and granite steps, features that first appeared in 1835, framing the Gothic Revival building that grew up behind them. Inside these walls Georgia's legislators met for more than half a century, the Marquis de Lafayette was welcomed, and a state chose to leave the Union. That so much of the state's early story passed through one building in centrally located Milledgeville is the best reason to stop and look.
