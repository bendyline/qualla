# Rock Eagle

In the Georgia Piedmont, thousands of quartzite stones laid out as a great bird with a 120-foot wingspan have kept their builders' purpose secret for perhaps as long as 3,000 years.

**Location:** 33.4175, -83.3881

**Categories:** Archaeological sites in Georgia (U.S. state), Native American history of Georgia (U.S. state), Effigy mounds, Protected areas of Putnam County, Georgia, National Register of Historic Places in Putnam County, Georgia

## Introduction

Everyone calls it an eagle, but no one actually knows what kind of bird it is. The figure spreads across a patch of Putnam County, Georgia, built from thousands of pieces of quartzite piled into the shape of a great bird, 102 feet from head to tail and 120 feet from wing tip to wing tip. Its makers left no word about what they meant to portray, or why. Current archaeology places its construction somewhere between about 1000 BC and AD 1000, which means the Rock Eagle Effigy Mound may have stood for centuries, or even millennia, before the rise of the Mississippian, Creek and Cherokee cultures in this part of the Southeast. Today it rests behind a tall fence on land administered by the University of Georgia, watched over by a stone tower built so that visitors could see it the way it seems made to be seen: from above.

## A Bird of Stone

Seen from the tower, the effigy resolves into the clear silhouette of a bird with its wings spread wide. Up close it is a low mound, rising roughly 8 to 10 feet above the ground and made of thousands of small to medium-sized rocks. It appears to have grown from a large dome-shaped central mound that was later expanded into the bird. Only two bird effigy mounds like this are generally recognized east of the Mississippi River, and both are in Putnam County. The other, called Rock Hawk, lies about thirteen miles to the east-southeast. In 1954 the archaeologist A. R. Kelly reported that both effigies showed signs of once having been enclosed by walls made of material similar to the rocks in the birds themselves, which may link their builders to those who raised similar walls at Stone Mountain, destroyed in 1923, and at Fort Mountain, where the wall still stands.

## Older Than Anyone Thought, Then Younger

Rock Eagle was once believed to be as much as 5,000 years old. There is strong evidence that Archaic peoples lived in the area at that time, but scholars no longer credit them with the mound. The current view is that Woodland peoples built it between 1,000 and 3,000 years ago. They may have been part of the Adena or Hopewell cultures, though it is more likely they represented a distinct group of their own. Even with its revised age, Rock Eagle has been described as the next oldest Native American mound in Georgia after the Sapelo Shell Ring Complex. The question of why these early inhabitants of Middle Georgia heaped stone into the shape of a bird remains open. The mound clearly held ceremonial significance, but beyond that the builders kept their reasons to themselves.

## What the Diggers Found

Early settlers suspected the mound might hide buried treasure and dug into it from time to time. Twentieth-century archaeologists found the real secret underneath: the eagle was built on top of a natural rock outcrop. Kelly, a University of Georgia archaeologist, had excavated much of the site in the 1930s, uncovering a single set of human and animal bones and a projectile point that may or may not relate to the effigy. A research project in the 1950s turned up one quartz tool and evidence of cremated human burials within the mound, a reminder that this was a place where people were laid to rest. Otherwise, digging has yielded surprisingly few artifacts beyond the stones themselves, and the structure has revealed very little about its purpose.

## Watching from the Tower

The Scott family sold the property to the United States government in 1938, and Depression-era work crews, in association with the University of Georgia, built a granite tower at the foot of the effigy to give visitors an aerial view. In 1940 the Georgia Society of the Colonial Dames of the XVII Century placed a bronze marker near the bird's head. An extensive renovation cleared plants from the mound and put back rocks that had scattered nearby. The United States Department of the Interior listed Rock Eagle on the National Register of Historic Places in 1978. Visitors are welcome free of charge, though stepping onto the mound itself is strictly prohibited. A paved path circles the effigy, with interpretive panels along the way, inside a 1,500-acre park beside U.S. 441 between Madison and Eatonton. The adjoining Rock Eagle 4-H Center, where young people come for day and residential environmental education, takes its name from the bird and prints its image on the masthead of its newsletter, Wingspan.

## Flight Context

Rock Eagle lies at 33.4175 N, 83.3881 W, beside U.S. 441 between Eatonton, about 6 miles to the south, and Madison to the north. The effigy itself is only about 120 feet across and fenced, so it is hard to pick out from the air; look instead for the University of Georgia's Rock Eagle 4-H Center complex with its cottages and buildings alongside the highway. A slow pass at 2,000 to 3,000 feet MSL is a good way to see the park and highway corridor. Madison Municipal Airport (FAA identifier 52A, no ICAO code) is about 12 nm north, and Baldwin County Regional Airport (KMLJ) at Milledgeville is about 17 nm to the south-southeast. Summer brings haze and afternoon storms to middle Georgia; cooler months usually offer the clearest air.
