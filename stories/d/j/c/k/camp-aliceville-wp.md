# Camp Aliceville

From 1943 to 1945, a small Alabama town hosted the largest prisoner-of-war camp in the Southeast, where German captives staged plays, planted elaborate gardens and took university classes, while hardline Nazis among them terrorized the rest.

**Location:** 33.1172, -88.1734

**Categories:** World War II prisoner-of-war camps in the United States, Defunct prisons in Alabama, 1942 establishments in Alabama, 1945 disestablishments in Alabama, Pickens County, Alabama

## Introduction

The prisoners named their newspaper Der Zaungast. According to historian Chip Walker, the term is untranslatable: it suggests someone peering through a peephole in a fence, watching activities nearby but separate from them, and in colloquial German it means a goof-off or loafer. To the German prisoners of war held at Camp Aliceville, it carried another meaning too: "fence-guest," or "guest behind the fence." From 1943 to 1945, thousands of such guests lived behind the fences of this camp in Aliceville, Alabama, the largest World War II POW camp in the Southeastern United States.

## A Town of Barracks

Construction began in August 1942 and was finished by December. The camp was built big: 400 wood-frame barracks able to hold as many as 6,000 prisoners and 900 Army personnel, though its population rarely exceeded 3,500 at a time. Of the eleven POW camps in the Southeastern U.S., Aliceville was the largest. The first prisoners arrived in June 1943, most of them at first from Erwin Rommel's Afrika Corps. As the war went on the camp's population grew more varied, and by its end Camp Aliceville held German prisoners captured in many different places.

## Gardens, Plays and English Lessons

Behind the wire, the prisoners built a rich cultural life. There were musical groups and theatre productions, and Der Zaungast was one of 80 local POW camp newspapers. Camp authorities held landscaping contests, and the prisoners threw themselves into them, creating spectacular topiary and intricately designed flower gardens. Education thrived as well. Faculty from the University of Alabama in Tuscaloosa taught college-level classes in the camp, and English was the most popular subject. Prisoners could even arrange to have their transcripts sent to the Reich Ministry of Education in Germany, which would award them academic credit. For some, the camp became a classroom as much as a prison.

## Fear Inside the Wire

Not every danger came from outside the fence. According to Randy Wall, "fewer than 10 percent of all German POWs were devoted Nazis," but in many American camps, Aliceville among them, those prisoners harassed, injured or killed fellow prisoners they believed had grown too comfortable with their American captors. Stephen Fleck, a medical officer at the camp, estimated that there were probably two or three such deaths a month, and said that nobody would inform on the killers for fear of meeting the same fate. To protect prisoners from this violence, American military authorities set up so-called "segregation camps" for "ardent Nazis." Aliceville began as an ordinary POW camp, but by 1944 it had become a segregation camp itself. Some prisoners simply tried to leave. Across American camps there were more than 2,000 escape attempts during the war, and one group of six from Aliceville got as far as Memphis, Tennessee, where the FBI caught them after they stole a car.

## Neighbors on the Other Side

The prisoners at Camp Aliceville were treated humanely, in part because the United States government hoped Germany would treat American prisoners of war the same way. That policy was not always easy for the camp's neighbors to accept. The people of Aliceville were living with shortages of goods and mandatory rationing of many necessities, and the contrast with the humane treatment of enemy soldiers led to some hard feelings against the prisoners. A small Alabama town found itself tied, through the camp, to battlefields and homes an ocean away.

## A Chimney and a Collection

Camp Aliceville is usually said to have closed on September 30, 1945. After the war it was dismantled and sold for scrap, and by most accounts its only remaining trace is an old stone chimney. The memory proved more durable than the buildings. The Aliceville Museum and Cultural Arts Center, in its permanent Camp Aliceville exhibits, houses papers, letters, documents, maps and other material from Camp Aliceville and other POW camps, in what the museum describes as the largest collection of World War II POW artifacts in the United States. It has also brought the camp's two sides back together: the town held a Friendship Reunion of former prisoners and guards in 1989, a 50th-anniversary reunion followed in 1993, and the museum, which opened in 1995, has hosted more since. Men who had once faced each other across a fence came back to the town that had held them.

## Flight Context

The site of Camp Aliceville lies at approximately 33.117 N, 88.173 W on the edge of Aliceville in Pickens County, west Alabama. By most accounts nothing of the camp survives except an old stone chimney, so from 1,500-2,500 ft MSL the flight is about the setting: the small town and the open farm country around it. George Downer Airport (KAIV) is just 1.4 nm west-southwest, making this an easy stop. Tuscaloosa National Airport (KTCL) is about 29 nm east-northeast, and Golden Triangle Regional (KGTR) about 29 nm northwest. Columbus Air Force Base (KCBM), about 34 nm north-northwest, generates busy military training traffic. Summer afternoons bring thunderstorms, and spring can bring severe weather.
