# Wilson's Raid

In the last weeks of the Civil War, 13,500 Union cavalrymen with repeating rifles swept across Alabama and Georgia, burning ironworks, a university and an arsenal city while outmaneuvering Nathan Bedford Forrest.

**Location:** 33.1596, -87.3423

**Categories:** Wilson's Raid, Cavalry raids of the American Civil War, Military operations of the American Civil War in Alabama, Military operations of the American Civil War in Georgia (U.S. state), 1865 in the United States

## Introduction

On April 4, 1865, soldiers of a Union brigade marched into Tuscaloosa and set fire to the University of Alabama. The school had been run as a military school since 1860, and its Rotunda and the halls around it were destroyed. The burning was one small episode in a much larger campaign. Brigadier General James H. Wilson was leading some 13,500 cavalrymen across Alabama and Georgia on a mission to wreck what remained of the Confederacy's industrial heart. Standing in his way was Lieutenant General Nathan Bedford Forrest, with a far smaller force of Confederate cavalry and militia.

## Seven Shots to Every Man

The raid grew out of the Union victory at Nashville, which left Major General George H. Thomas facing almost no organized opposition in the Deep South. He sent Wilson to destroy the arsenal at Selma, one of the few Confederate military bases still in Southern hands. Selma held a naval foundry, gun factories, a powder mill, warehouses and railroad shops. Wilson's three divisions, under Edward M. McCook, Eli Long and Emory Upton, carried seven-shot Spencer repeating rifles, a formidable advantage. By the usual account Forrest had about 2,500 troopers, two partial brigades and some local militia, though some sources put his total strength nearer 5,000. Delayed by the rain-swollen Tennessee River, Wilson finally left Gravelly Springs on March 22, 1865. He split his command into three columns to mask his intentions, and the ruse worked. Forrest learned only late in the raid that Selma was the real target. The columns reunited at Jasper on March 27.

## Furnaces Go Dark

Much of the raid's damage fell on iron. At Elyton, in what is now Birmingham, Union troops skirmished on March 28 and destroyed the Oxmoor and Irondale furnaces. On March 31 a detachment of Upton's division wrecked a foundry in Columbiana and the Shelby Iron Works. Wilson also sent a 1,500-man brigade under Brigadier General John T. Croxton south and west. It burned the Roupes Valley Ironworks at Tannehill and the Bibb Naval Furnace at Brierfield, then turned on Tuscaloosa and its university. The detour did more than destroy property. It also drew James Chalmers's Confederate division away from Forrest's main force. Croxton kept moving across Alabama, burning more ironworks and fighting a Confederate brigade at Munford, and did not rejoin Wilson until May 1, far to the east at Macon, Georgia.

## The Line at Selma

Forrest could not wait for Chalmers. At Montevallo on March 31 he was routed, and Union troops overran his headquarters, seizing papers that revealed his plans. He tried again the next day near Ebenezer Church and was routed once more. His men raced into Selma and formed a three-mile semicircle anchored at both ends on the Alabama River. On April 2 the divisions of Long and Upton attacked on foot. After brief bouts of hand-to-hand fighting the line broke, chiefly because inexperienced militiamen abandoned their positions, and Wilson personally led a mounted charge of the 4th U.S. Cavalry against an unfinished stretch of works. Long fell badly wounded in the head. Forrest, also hurt, escaped to Marion. The night that followed was chaotic, with street fighting and fires, among them 35,000 bales of cotton and the Central Commercial Warehouse, fired by Confederates as the city fell. Some Union soldiers, and some people newly freed from slavery, took to plundering until Wilson restored discipline after the first night.

## Easter Sunday, After Appomattox

Wilson spent over a week destroying Selma's military works, then occupied Montgomery on April 12. Robert E. Lee had surrendered three days earlier, but Confederate forces in the Carolinas, Georgia and Florida were still in the field, so Wilson pushed into Georgia after the bridges over the Chattahoochee. On Easter Sunday, April 16, Colonel Oscar Hugh La Grange's 3,700 men attacked Fort Tyler at West Point, an earthwork held by somewhere between 120 and 265 convalescents, militia and boys under Brigadier General Robert C. Tyler. Outnumbered and poorly armed, the defenders threw primitive hand grenades as the attackers crossed the ditch. Eighteen or nineteen of them were killed (sources differ) and 28 wounded, and the rest were captured; the attackers suffered 36 casualties. Tyler was shot by a sharpshooter and died, the last Confederate general killed in action. The same day, Upton's division took Columbus, Georgia, and its naval works, burning and then scuttling the unfinished ironclad CSS Jackson, in a fight sometimes called the last battle of the war.

## The Final Reckoning

Wilson's troopers entered Macon without resistance on April 20, and the raid was over, just six days before Joseph E. Johnston surrendered the remaining Confederate forces in the Carolinas, Georgia and Florida to William Tecumseh Sherman. The numbers were lopsided, though accounts of the totals vary. Wilson's men are credited with capturing five fortified cities, 288 cannon and 6,820 prisoners at a cost of about 725 Union casualties, while casualties among Forrest's much smaller force are put at about 1,200. Unlike Sherman's March to the Sea the year before, the raid is said to have spared plantation property. Its riders still had one more role to play. As they fanned out across the region hunting fleeing Confederate officials, Jefferson Davis was captured near Irwinville, Georgia, on May 10, 1865.

## Flight Context

The raid's marker coordinates are 33.160N, 87.342W in Tuscaloosa County, roughly 12 nm east-southeast of downtown Tuscaloosa. The raid itself spanned hundreds of miles from northwest Alabama to Macon, Georgia, so there is no single site to spot; from 4,000 to 6,000 feet, orient on Tuscaloosa and the Black Warrior River to the west and the Birmingham urban area to the northeast, the Elyton of 1865. Tuscaloosa National Airport (KTCL) lies about 14 nm to the west, Bessemer Airport (KEKY) about 23 nm to the northeast, and Birmingham-Shuttlesworth International (KBHM) about 38 nm to the northeast.
