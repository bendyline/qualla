# Friendship Cemetery

In Columbus, Mississippi, an Odd Fellows cemetery laid out in three linked circles became the scene of an 1866 Decoration Day when local women laid flowers on both Confederate and Union graves, a gesture that inspired the poem 'The Blue and the Gray.'

## Introduction

On April 25, 1866, by most accounts (the ceremony had been called for April 26), a procession of Columbus women, young and old, walked into Friendship Cemetery carrying flowers. The graves they came to decorate held soldiers from both sides of the Civil War. They laid their flowers on the Confederate dead, and they laid them on the Union dead too. That simple act of treating fallen enemies as equals travelled far beyond Mississippi, inspiring a poem in The Atlantic Monthly and joining the grave decoration days that eventually grew into Memorial Day.

## A Hospital Town's Burial Ground

The graves were there because of the war's wounded. During the Civil War, Columbus served as a military hospital center, particularly after the Battle of Shiloh, and many of the men brought here did not survive. Sources differ on the totals, from roughly 1,500 soldiers in all to more than 2,000 Confederates alone, and they put the Union soldiers buried in Friendship Cemetery at somewhere between 40 and 150. In 1866, four women who became known as the Decoration Day Ladies organized a formal procession and ceremony so that the women of Columbus could place flowers on all of those graves. It was an act of mourning, and it was also an act of generosity toward soldiers who had fought on the other side.

## The Blue and the Gray

Word of the tribute reached the poet Francis Miles Finch, and it moved him to write "The Blue and the Gray," published in an 1867 edition of The Atlantic Monthly. The poem carried the story of Columbus's flowers to readers far beyond Mississippi. That same year, the Union dead began to leave Friendship Cemetery: in 1867, most of the Union soldiers' graves were moved to Corinth National Cemetery, though a handful have never been accounted for, and no Union graves are marked here today. The Union graves the women had decorated were largely emptied, but the gesture outlived them. Over time, grave decoration days like this one, honoring those who died in military service, eventually developed into Memorial Day.

## Three Linked Circles

The cemetery itself is older than the war. The Independent Order of Odd Fellows established it in 1849 on 5 acres, laying it out as three interlocking circles, the emblem of the Odd Fellows. That symbolic plan was the seed of something much larger. By 1957 the cemetery had grown to 35 acres, and the City of Columbus acquired it. Friendship Cemetery was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1980 and designated a Mississippi Landmark in 1989. As of 2015 it held some 22,000 graves across 70 acres, and it was still in use, a working cemetery as well as a historic one.

## Unexpected Neighbors

Walk its paths and the headstones tell stories far beyond the Civil War. Confederate generals rest here, among them Lieutenant General Stephen Dill Lee, and there is a cenotaph for Brigadier General William Barksdale, whose remains lie in Jackson. Two governors of Mississippi, James Whitfield and Henry Lewis Whitfield, are buried here. So are some surprising figures of the twentieth century: Cornell Franklin, a judge who chaired the Shanghai Municipal Council from 1937 to 1940; Joshua Lawrence Meador, a Disney animator; Clyde S. Kilby, an author and English professor; and Henry Edward Warden, a career Air Force officer known as the father of the B-52.

## Voices After Dark

In April, the cemetery has traditionally come alive in an unusual way. The Mississippi School for Mathematics and Science has hosted a public evening event here, "Tales from the Crypt," part of the city's spring Pilgrimage. Before it, each student researches someone buried in Friendship Cemetery; then, dressed as that person, the student performs their story among the graves. It is a fitting tradition for a place whose best-known moment was an act of remembrance. The women who carried flowers here in 1866 insisted that the dead be honored. The students who come each spring make sure they are also remembered as people, one life at a time.

## Flight Context

Friendship Cemetery lies at approximately 33.481 N, 88.431 W in Columbus, Mississippi. From 1,500-2,500 ft MSL, look for the broad green expanse of a 70-acre cemetery set within the city. Columbus-Lowndes County Airport (KUBS) is less than 3 nm east-southeast, and Golden Triangle Regional Airport (KGTR) about 8 nm west-southwest. Columbus Air Force Base (KCBM), about 9 nm north, is a busy military training base, so expect heavy training traffic and coordinate with ATC. Summer afternoons bring thunderstorms, and spring can bring severe weather, including around the April anniversary of the 1866 Decoration Day.
