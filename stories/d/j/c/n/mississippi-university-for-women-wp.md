# Mississippi University for Women

The first state-supported college for women in America, by its own account, founded in Columbus in 1884, has admitted men since a 1982 Supreme Court ruling and still wrestles with what to call itself.

**Location:** 33.4931, -88.4186

**Categories:** Public universities and colleges in Mississippi, Former women's universities and colleges in the United States, Columbus, Mississippi, Women's history, Universities and colleges established in 1884

## Introduction

Joe Hogan wanted a bachelor's degree in nursing. He was already a registered nurse and a nursing supervisor in Columbus, Mississippi, and in 1979 he applied to the baccalaureate program at a nursing school in his own city. The obstacle was the school's admissions policy, because the school was Mississippi University for Women. Hogan's application became a case, the case went to the U.S. Supreme Court, and on July 1, 1982, the justices ruled 5 to 4 in his favor. The decision changed a campus that had been built, nearly a century earlier, around the idea that women deserved a public college of their own.

## Three Women and a Legislature

That idea took shape through the efforts of three Mississippi women, Sallie Eola Reneau, Annie Coleman Peyton and Olivia Valentine Hastings. On March 12, 1884, the Mississippi Legislature created the Industrial Institute and College for the Education of White Girls, a name that carried the segregation of its era in plain words. By its own account, it was the first state-supported college for women in America. It later became the Mississippi State College for Women, and in 1966 three local women from Hunt High School, among them Laverne Greene-Leech, became its first Black undergraduates. In 1974, with graduate studies and a broader curriculum, the school took the name Mississippi University for Women, as the state's other colleges were likewise designated universities.

## The Hogan Decision

In Mississippi University for Women v. Hogan, Justice Sandra Day O'Connor wrote for the majority that the nursing school's single-sex admissions policy violated the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. The state's Board of Trustees of State Institutions of Higher Learning then ordered the university to admit qualified men to all of its programs. Men have been admitted ever since, and as of 2022 they made up 23 percent of the student body. The university did not abandon its roots. In 1988 the board reaffirmed its mission as a place offering quality programs for all qualified students, with an emphasis on distinctive opportunities for women. Writing in Innovative Higher Education in 1997, Dale Thorn described how the university successfully fought off a merger with another institution and remained independent.

## Champions and a Storm

Long before men arrived, the school's women were winning. In 1971, as the Mississippi State College for Women, the school won what it counts as the national championship in women's basketball, the National Invitational Women's Intercollegiate Basketball Tournament, beating West Chester State in a close final (reported as 57 to 55) in only the third such tournament ever held. A year later it finished fourth at the AIAW national championship, falling in the semifinals to the famed Immaculata team. Then, on November 10, 2002, an F3 tornado tore through the southern half of campus, damaging 26 of its 60 buildings and leveling the Edna Pohl gymnasium. Athletics were shut down until 2017. When the teams returned, now called the Owls rather than the Blues, men's sports arrived for the first time, and by the 2022-23 season the program was competing in NCAA Division III.

## A Name Still Being Written

What to call "The W" was left an open question when the 2024 effort halted, and the university still goes by its 1974 name. In 2009 President Claudia Limbert floated renaming it Reneau University, after one of its founders, but the legislature did not approve. Nora Roberts Miller, inaugurated in 2019 as the university's first alumna president, proposed Mississippi Brightwell University in January 2024, a nod to the motto "We study for light to bless with light." After backlash from alumni and local residents, a task force returned with Wynbridge State University of Mississippi, announced on February 13 from the steps of Poindexter Hall. Eight days later the effort was halted. Whatever the name, the alumni roll speaks for itself: Eudora Welty, nystatin co-discoverer Elizabeth Lee Hazen, Lenore Prather, the first woman on the Mississippi Supreme Court, and Toni Seawright, the first African American Miss Mississippi.

## Flight Context

Mississippi University for Women sits at about 33.493 N, 88.419 W in Columbus, Mississippi, near the east bank of the Tombigbee River. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet, look for the campus beside downtown Columbus, with the river and the Tennessee-Tombigbee Waterway just to the west. Columbus Lowndes County Airport (KUBS) is about 2.5 nm southeast, Golden Triangle Regional Airport (KGTR) about 9 nm west, and Columbus Air Force Base (KCBM) about 9 nm north, so expect military training traffic. Spring and summer thunderstorms are common.
