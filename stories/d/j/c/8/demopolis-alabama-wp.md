# Demopolis, Alabama

Exiled Bonapartists named this Alabama river town the City of the People, lost it to a surveyor's error within a year, and left behind a name that outlasted their failed dream of vineyards and olive groves.

**Location:** 32.5094, -87.8372

**Categories:** Cities in Alabama, Vine and Olive Colony, French-American culture in Alabama, Cities in Marengo County, Alabama, Populated places established in 1817

## Introduction

Count Lefebvre Desnouettes had served in the doomed invasion of Russia and, by older accounts, ridden in Napoleon's own carriage. A few years after the emperor's fall, the old cavalry general was lending his name to a very different campaign: a plan to plant grapevines and olive trees on a chalk cliff above the Tombigbee River in the Alabama Territory. The exiles who gathered around that plan, a mix of Bonapartists and French refugees who had fled Saint-Domingue after its enslaved people overthrew the colonial government, chose a Greek name for their settlement. Demopolis, the City of the People, was meant to honor the democratic ideals behind the venture. The vines and olives never took. The name did.

## Vines, Olives, and a Surveyor's Error

The scheme began in Philadelphia, where the expatriates petitioned Congress for land. An act of March 3, 1817, allowed them to buy four townships in the Alabama Territory at two dollars an acre, on the condition that they cultivate grapes and olives. Pioneers and geographers had advised that Alabama's climate would suit those crops, which proved not to be the case. By July 14, 1817, a small party had settled at White Bluff, where the Black Warrior River meets the Tombigbee. Many of the most prominent backers had little appetite for frontier life, sold their shares and stayed in Philadelphia, and by 1818 the Vine and Olive Colony counted only 69 settlers. Then came the survey of August 1818. The land they had cleared lay outside their grant, which began less than a mile to the east. The colonists moved on and founded two new towns, Aigleville and Arcola.

## Steamboats Below the Chalk

Americans moved quickly to buy the abandoned site. The White Bluff Association formed in 1819 to purchase the land and lay out a town, with George Strother Gaines as its spokesman; Desnouettes himself sat among its commissioners. By the 1830s Demopolis had become a regional river hub, drawing American and European-born craftsmen and merchants, while planters from the surrounding Canebrake built townhouses nearby. Marengo County held a large number of slaveholders, and the cotton trade that filled the riverboats rested on the labor of the people they enslaved. By the 1850s palatial steamboats such as the Forest Monarch, the Alice Vivian and the ill-fated Eliza Battle called regularly on the route between Mobile and Columbus, Mississippi. A Catholic congregation had been present since the French arrived, and in 1858 Jewish residents founded B'nai Jeshurun, the fourth Jewish congregation in Alabama, later raising a Moorish Revival temple in 1893. A yellow fever epidemic struck in 1853, yet by 1860 about 1,200 people lived within the town limits.

## A Camp Swollen With Soldiers

Two navigable rivers and a railroad made Demopolis useful to the Confederacy. The town hosted commissary and quartermaster warehouses, a large ordnance depot, two large hospitals and a sprawling encampment at the fairgrounds in Webb's Bend. After Vicksburg and Port Hudson fell in July 1863, regiments of paroled Confederate soldiers were sent here to await exchange, and thousands at a time poured into a town that had held only about a thousand people before the war. Residents struggled to feed and house them. Many hundreds of soldiers who died in the hospitals were buried in a cemetery at the south end of Webb's Bend, a site now lying underwater since the river was dammed below Demopolis in the twentieth century. Reconstruction brought a different fight. The county seat moved here from Linden in 1868, a Presbyterian church on the square was bought for $3,000 as a courthouse, and after disputed referendums marred by irregularities, Linden won the seat back in February 1871.

## Opera Houses and The Little Foxes

That former church, now called Rooster Hall, spent 1876 to 1902 as the Demopolis Opera House, staging dramas, civic lectures and minstrel shows. The ornate Braswell Opera House, with its private box galleries, opened on October 23, 1902, and survived until its demolition in 1972 and 1973. The Elks Theater, the town's first cinema, opened in 1915, became the Si-Non a year later and was restored in the 1990s. Demopolis also reached Broadway. Lillian Hellman's maternal ancestors were all natives of the town, and the local Marx and Newhouse families were reputedly the inspiration for her 1939 melodrama The Little Foxes, with Alabama-born Tallulah Bankhead as Regina. William Wyler's 1941 film version, starring Bette Davis, was nominated for Best Picture, and Hellman returned to her Demopolis ancestors, loosely, in Another Part of the Forest.

## What Still Stands on the Bluff

The boll weevil infestations of the 1920s and the Great Depression finally ended cotton's long reign, and beef cattle, catfish farming, cement, lumber and paper filled the gap. The river still matters: the Port of Demopolis connects, by way of the Tennessee-Tombigbee Waterway, to the Gulf of Mexico. The past endures in brick and plaster. Gaineswood, a National Historic Landmark built between 1843 and 1861 in an asymmetrical Greek Revival style, still holds its domed ceilings, ornate plasterwork and much of the Whitfield family's antebellum furnishings, and the Alabama Historical Commission owns it. Bluff Hall, built in 1832 and reworked in the Greek Revival style in the 1840s, is also a house museum. At Laird Cottage hang works by Geneva Mercer, a Marengo County native who interned with Giuseppe Moretti, sculptor of Birmingham's colossal Vulcan. The 2020 census counted 7,162 residents in the City of the People.

## Flight Context

Demopolis sits at 32.509N, 87.837W atop White Bluff, a cliff of Demopolis Chalk on the east bank of the Tombigbee River, at its confluence with the Black Warrior River. From 3,000 to 4,500 feet the meeting of the two rivers is the key landmark for finding the town. Demopolis Regional Airport (KDYA, formerly Demopolis Municipal), with its 5,000-foot runway, lies about 6 nm southwest of town. Tuscaloosa National Airport (KTCL) lies about 44 nm to the north-northeast, and Key Field / Meridian Regional Airport (KMEI) about 48 nm to the west-southwest. The rivers are easiest to trace in clear conditions; low haze can blur the pale bluff face.
