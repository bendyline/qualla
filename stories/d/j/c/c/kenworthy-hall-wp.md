# Kenworthy Hall

A Black Belt cotton factor wrote to a famous New York architect for a house plan, and got an Italian villa with a four-story tower that survived war, ruin, vandals and a ghost story to become a National Historic Landmark.

**Location:** 32.6351, -87.3522

**Categories:** National Historic Landmarks in Alabama, Richard Upjohn buildings, Italianate architecture in Alabama, Reportedly haunted locations in Alabama, Houses in Perry County, Alabama

## Introduction

"Desiring to build a house, a country residence, and at a loss for a plan, we address you as a well known Architect." Edward Kenworthy Carlisle mailed those words to Richard Upjohn on May 4, 1858, asking for nothing grander than "a rough sketch at first." What came back, after months of letters, was one of the most distinctive houses in the American South: a red-brick Italian villa with a four-story tower, rising from the cotton country two miles west of the courthouse square in Marion, Alabama. Kenworthy Hall is one of the last asymmetrical Italianate villas left in Alabama and one of the few houses Upjohn designed in the South, and Wikipedia calls it the only surviving residential example of his Italian villa style designed especially for the Southern climate and the plantation way of life.

## A Cotton Factor's Ambition

Carlisle was born near Augusta, Georgia, in 1810 and came to Perry County as a young man with his mother, Susan Curry Carlisle, whose relatives were already established cotton planters. He married Lucinda Wilson Walthall in 1841, acquired large landholdings and set himself up as a cotton factor. The house he commissioned in 1858 was meant to announce his family's standing in the Black Belt, as the centerpiece of a 440-acre estate. Planning continued by correspondence even as brownstone trim shipped from New York began arriving on site. Skilled labor was the harder problem. Carlisle searched until November 1858 before hiring a master mason, Philip Bond, and only then did work begin. The family had moved in by 1860, and the estate ran on the labor of people Carlisle held in slavery. Bondage ran through the family fortune: his mother's 1844 will is said to have left him a woman named Daphney along with what the document called her "future increase," any children she might bear, claimed as property before they were born.

## An Italian Villa Tuned for Alabama Heat

Upjohn had built Italian villas in the Northeast, most notably the Edward King House in Newport, Rhode Island. Here he adapted the form to a hot, humid climate: a wide cross hall behind the entrance hall, a detached brick kitchen and a full-width rear porch. The walls are brick throughout, even inside, and the original standing-seam terne roof survives. Sixty-four windows carry brownstone headers and sills, most under round or segmental arches. A bracketed balcony with a metal canopy, a signature of Upjohn's villas, projects from the tower, and the eaves overhang three feet on flat wooden brackets. Inside, oak arches frame the halls, an octagonal library holds built-in arched bookcases, and heart pine boards cover every floor. The house also contained one of the Deep South's first uses of ornamental stained glass, believed to be the work of Henry Sharp and Company of New York City. The plan divided family from public spaces, and gave servants their own hall, staircase and entrance.

## Fortune, Blockade and Reversal

The Civil War did not immediately hurt Carlisle. One of his cotton trading firms, Carlisle and Humphries, actually saw profits rise during the Union blockade. Defeat changed that. After the war his taxable property was valued at less than $20,000, and in 1867 Kenworthy Hall itself was appraised at only $9,000. Carlisle regrouped in nearby Selma with his son, Edward Carlisle Jr., and his son-in-law, Alexander Jones, and in 1871 they founded the City National Bank. He died in 1873. Lucinda divided her time between Kenworthy and a home in Selma, gradually treating the villa as a summer house, and in 1899 she gave it to her only surviving child, Augusta Carlisle Jones. Lucinda died in 1912. Two years later Augusta sold the property, and the house began a long slide through a succession of owners, losing both of its original arched porches along the way.

## The Vigil in the Tower

For much of the 1950s Kenworthy Hall stood completely empty, and vandals moved in. They mutilated the plasterwork, broke many of the marble mantels and destroyed the stained glass, which survives only partly in documentation made by the Historic American Buildings Survey in 1934. Empty houses gather stories, and this one gathered a famous ghost. Local tellings were later recorded by Kathryn Tucker Windham as "The Faithful Vigil at Carlisle Hall" in her first book of ghost stories, 13 Alabama Ghosts and Jeffrey. The tale places the spirit of a young woman in the fourth-floor tower room, still waiting for her lover to come home. It is a fitting stage for a vigil. The small room at the top of the tower has three arched casement windows on each wall, twelve in all, looking out in every direction.

## Saved Room by Room

In 1957 Karen Klassen of Birmingham bought the house and 19 acres for $4,000 and spent the next decade doing what she could to restore it. The Martin family took over in 1967 and devoted thirty years to restoration. Kenworthy Hall joined the National Register of Historic Places in 1990, and in the summer of 1997 the Historic American Buildings Survey returned to draw and photograph it once more. On August 18, 2004, the house was declared a National Historic Landmark along with four surviving outbuildings: the brick kitchen, a smokehouse, a partly buried cistern and a well house. The rear piazza came down in the 1940s, but some of its components were stored and survive, and rebuilding the porches has been contemplated.

## Flight Context

Kenworthy Hall stands at 32.635N, 87.352W on the north side of Alabama Highway 14, about two miles west of the Marion courthouse square in Perry County. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL, follow Highway 14 west out of Marion and look for the four-story brick tower among trees on the north side of the road; the house is small from the air and best picked out in clear, low-sun conditions. Craig Field (KSEM) at Selma lies about 25 nm to the southeast, Tuscaloosa National Airport (KTCL) about 37 nm to the north-northwest, and Demopolis Regional Airport (KDYA, formerly Demopolis Municipal) about 32 nm to the west-southwest.
