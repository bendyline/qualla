# Eutaw massacre

In October 1870, Klansmen opened fire on a Republican rally of 2,000 Black citizens at the Eutaw courthouse, swinging an Alabama county's election and testing whether federal law could protect freed people from private violence.

**Location:** 32.8403, -87.8878

**Categories:** Reconstruction era, Ku Klux Klan crimes in Alabama, Racially motivated violence against African Americans in Alabama, Massacres in 1870, Greene County, Alabama, Voting rights

## Introduction

In 1868, Greene County, Alabama, gave Ulysses S. Grant a margin of roughly 2,000 votes. Two years later, in the race for governor, the county went for Democrat Robert B. Lindsay by a reported 43. Much of the explanation lies in what happened on the courthouse square in Eutaw, the county seat, on October 25, 1870. That day white Klansmen attacked a Republican rally of some 2,000 Black citizens, killing as many as four people and wounding 54. It was terror with a purpose, and in Greene County it worked.

## A Season of Terror

As in other states of the former Confederacy, Alabama had been terrorized again and again by the Ku Klux Klan in the run-up to the 1870 election for governor. In Calhoun County in July, Klansmen killed five or six people at Cross Plains, among them a schoolteacher, according to differing accounts. Greene County had its own victims. Gilford Coleman, a Black Republican leader, was taken from his own house, lynched and shot dead, and his body was mutilated. His killing was the first of two political assassinations of Black men in the county that summer and fall. The goal of all this violence was plain: white Democrats were using racial terror to keep Black Republicans away from the polls.

## The Courthouse Square

On October 25, Republicans held a rally at the county courthouse in Eutaw, and some 2,000 Black citizens came. Klansmen, supporting the Democrats, first harassed the crowd with words and then began shooting. When it was over, between two and four Black citizens lay dead and 54 people were injured. State officials reportedly took no action against the attackers; the only people they arrested were some of the Black victims. On election day Eutaw was calm, but it was the calm of fear. Many Black voters stayed home, and some voted Democratic. Lindsay carried Greene County, and with similar intimidation in other heavily Republican counties, the Democrats won the governorship of Alabama.

## A Question for the Supreme Court

The federal government did act. The U.S. Commissioner in Demopolis issued arrest warrants and set $4,000 bonds to make sure defendants appeared in court. A local man, Samuel B. Brown, likely a low-ranking Republican politician, testified before U.S. Circuit Court Judge William Woods, and his testimony produced a complaint charging fourteen white defendants with violating the First Amendment and the constitutional rights of Brown and six others. On December 24, 1870, a federal grand jury indicted twenty of the rioters. Woods, meanwhile, had written to Supreme Court Justice Joseph P. Bradley to ask whether federal law, particularly the Enforcement Act of 1870, reached events like these. Bradley's January 1871 reply went to the heart of it: had the rioters violated their victims' constitutionally protected right to free speech? The Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments guarded individuals against the states, but in Eutaw it was private individuals who had trampled those rights.

## The Precedent That Never Came

The stakes reached beyond one county. Despite the Enforcement Acts passed in May 1870 to end Klan violence, Democrats in the readmitted Southern states kept suppressing the Black Republican vote, costing Republicans heavily in Congress. United States Attorney General Amos T. Akerman, a former Confederate and slaveholder who had become one of the Klan's most outspoken enemies, directed the prosecution. It fell short. In United States v. Hall, brought against one of the white defendants, the jury failed to convict. Judge Woods's opinion overruling the defense demurrer did give future federal prosecutors broad room to charge private violations of fundamental rights under the Enforcement Acts, but Bradley later backed away from using the Fourteenth Amendment to protect individuals' natural rights. Had the government won, it has been argued, the case could have set an important precedent for protecting African Americans under that amendment. The people shot on the courthouse square never saw that protection.

## Flight Context

Eutaw, the seat of Greene County in west-central Alabama, lies at about 32.840 N, 87.888 W. The historic courthouse square sits at the center of the small town grid, which is easy to pick out from 2,000 to 3,000 feet amid farmland and woods. Eutaw Municipal Airport (FAA identifier 3A7, no ICAO code) is less than 2 nm southeast of town. Tuscaloosa National Airport (KTCL) is about 27 nm northeast, and George Downer Airport at Aliceville (KAIV) about 22 nm northwest. Summer thunderstorms and spring severe weather are common in this part of Alabama.
