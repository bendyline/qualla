# Safe House Black History Museum

In neighboring shotgun houses in Greensboro, Alabama, where Martin Luther King Jr. took refuge from the Ku Klux Klan two weeks before his assassination, a small, modestly funded museum honors the grass-roots activists of the rural Black Belt.

## Introduction

On March 21, 1968, Martin Luther King Jr. needed somewhere to hide. Hunted by the Ku Klux Klan, he found refuge in Greensboro, Alabama, in a modest three-room shotgun house that belonged to the Burroughs family, local activists who opened their door to him. Two weeks later, on April 4, King was assassinated in Memphis. The little house that sheltered him now forms part of the Safe House Black History Museum, a museum and cultural center that tells the story of the ordinary people in the rural Black Belt whose courage helped build the civil rights movement.

## Houses Built for the Cotton Gin

The museum occupies neighboring shotgun houses in Greensboro's Depot neighborhood. They were built as homes for employees of a local cotton gin, plain dwellings for working people. One of them, the three-room house where King took refuge, belonged to the Burroughs family. Theresa Turner Burroughs had been childhood friends with Coretta Scott King. Burroughs, who died in 2019, founded the museum in these buildings, in 2002 by the Encyclopedia of Alabama's account. Its focus is the grass-roots activism in the rural Black Belt that led to the civil rights movement: the organizing and risk-taking of local people, close to home.

## Faces from the Marches

Among the museum's reported displays is a pickup truck. King is said to have given a speech from it once, because local churches were afraid to let him speak inside their buildings, and the truck is a reminder of the fear that surrounded the movement. The collection also includes mugshots of local activists arrested in protests and marches during the civil rights era: the Greensboro marches, Bloody Sunday and the 1965 march from Selma to Montgomery. Taken to record arrests, those photographs now honor the people in them. The collection reaches back further still. It is also said to hold a desk made for a local landowner by one of the people he enslaved, an object that carries the skill of its maker and the injustice of the system that claimed their labor.

## Restored on a Shoestring

Auburn University's Rural Studio took on the museum as a project for its architecture students, who completed the work in 2010. The buildings were renovated, their exteriors restored to their original style, and a glass hallway was added to connect them, turning the small houses into a single place to walk through history. Recognition followed. In 2018, the museum was reported to be one of the 20 Alabama sites important to civil rights history that the World Monuments Fund placed on its watch list as a single consortium entry, a recognition that places like this need care to survive. The Safe House is not a large institution. As of 2021, it operated on an annual budget of $10,000, had a volunteer executive director and was open by appointment. Its funding comes from donations, grants, admissions fees and gift shop sales, and as of 2024 its supporters included the city of Greensboro, the Alabama Humanities Alliance and the Alabama African American Civil Rights Heritage Sites Consortium. That modest scale suits the story it tells. The movement it remembers was built by local people with few resources and a great deal of resolve, and the museum is carried forward the same way.
