# Bryant–Denny Stadium

Alabama's football home opened in 1929 before 6,000 fans and spent decades in the shadow of Birmingham's Legion Field before growing, deck by deck, into a stadium that seats more than 100,000.

**Location:** 33.2078, -87.5506

**Categories:** Alabama Crimson Tide football venues, Sports venues completed in 1929, Sports venues in Tuscaloosa, Alabama, College football stadiums

## Introduction

Six thousand people turned out on September 28, 1929, to watch Alabama beat Mississippi College 55–0 in a brand-new stadium that held 12,000. That original grandstand still exists, as the lower half of today's east side, but it has been swallowed by everything built around it since. Saban Field at Bryant–Denny Stadium now seats about 100,000 on the University of Alabama campus in Tuscaloosa, among the ten largest stadiums in the United States. The man it was first named for had imagined something big from the start. George H. Denny, the university's president, envisioned a full bowl seating 66,000, more than five times the capacity he opened with.

## A President's Bowl, Built in Pieces

Denny Stadium replaced Denny Field and was formally dedicated a week after that first game, at homecoming, with a 22–7 win over Ole Miss. Denny led the university from 1912 to 1936, and the stadium grew toward his bowl one piece at a time. Permanent seats along the east sideline pushed capacity to 24,000 before the 1937 season. End-zone bleachers in 1946 brought it to 31,000, new west-side seats in 1961 to 43,000, and fully enclosed end zones in 1966 to 60,210. In 1975 the Alabama legislature added a second name. Paul "Bear" Bryant, an alumnus and the Tide's longtime coach, then led the team for seven more seasons through 1982, making him one of the few Division I coaches to work in a stadium that bore his own name.

## Second Fiddle to Birmingham

For most of its life, Bryant–Denny was not where Alabama played its biggest games. Well into the 1980s, Legion Field in Birmingham seated almost 20,000 more people, so it hosted the marquee matchups, above all the Iron Bowl against Auburn. Tuscaloosa usually saw three or four games a season, mostly against non-conference opponents, though Mississippi State nearly always came here because Starkville sits so close. The balance tipped in 1998. A new east upper deck, built for $35 million with help from Tuscaloosa, Northport and Tuscaloosa County, lifted capacity to 83,818, past Legion Field. It debuted on September 5 that year, when Alabama beat BYU 38–31 and running back Shaun Alexander scored a school-record five touchdowns. Tennessee moved to Tuscaloosa in 1999 and the Iron Bowl followed a year later. After a 2003 game against South Florida, Alabama never played at Legion Field again.

## The Walk of Champions

A roughly $47 million north end zone expansion, finished days before the 2006 opener against Hawaii, added an upper deck and a three-level block of skyboxes called The Zone. Outside, the university graded away a hill, demolishing the Delta Kappa Epsilon fraternity house that stood on it, to create the Walk of Champions. A brick plaza leads from University Boulevard to the north gates, set with 16 granite monuments to Alabama's SEC and national championship teams. Along its west side stand five bronze coaches, each about nine feet tall and roughly 2,000 pounds: Wallace Wade, Frank Thomas, Bryant and Gene Stallings, unveiled in 2006, joined on April 16, 2011, by Nick Saban, honoring a 37–21 national title win over Texas. At the north entrance, two anonymous bronze players, one raising a Crimson Tide flag, wear jerseys 18 and 92. Together the numbers spell 1892, Alabama's first football season.

## A Field That Resisted Decoration

Alabama was slow to paint its grass. Into the 2002 season the field carried no midfield logo at all, only the markings the rules required and "ALABAMA" in plain white block letters in the end zones, apart from a special centennial logo in 1992. A white script "A" arrived in 2002, turning crimson two years later. Since 2009 the end zones have read "ALABAMA" and "CRIMSON TIDE" in white block letters on crimson. In 2011, houndstooth ribbons appeared on the 25-yard lines recognizing relief efforts after the tornado of April 27, 2011. Humor has its corner too. In 2008 the visitors' locker room was named the Fail Room for alumnus and donor James M. Fail, who said that when he spotted it as a naming opportunity, he figured it was "the most appropriate opportunity I would ever have to use my name."

## Past One Hundred Thousand

A south end zone upper deck, approved unanimously in February 2009 and built straight through that football season, opened for 2010. A weak economy lowered construction costs, and the project came in around $65.6 million, $15 million under estimate, lifting capacity to 101,821. During the work, welding equipment set fire to the inside of the old south scoreboard as it was being dismantled. A renovation budgeted at about $92.5 million for its first phase began in November 2019 and aimed to finish before the 2020 season, adding new premium areas, a relocated press box and a new team tunnel into an updated home locker room. In 2024 the playing surface took the name of Nick Saban, who won six national championships at Alabama between 2007 and 2023. The stadium also shares the state's high school football championships, the Super 7, with Auburn's Jordan–Hare Stadium and Birmingham's Protective Stadium.

## Flight Context

Bryant–Denny Stadium sits at 33.208N, 87.551W on the University of Alabama campus in Tuscaloosa, a short distance south of the Black Warrior River. The huge enclosed bowl is unmistakable from 3,000 to 5,000 feet. Tuscaloosa National Airport (KTCL) is only about 3 nm to the west, and Bessemer Airport (KEKY) lies about 32 nm to the east. On game days, the FAA's standing stadium flight restriction prohibits flight within 3 nm and below 3,000 feet AGL from one hour before until one hour after the event, so check NOTAMs before planning a low pass.
