# Silver Bluff Baptist Church

Founded in the 1770s by enslaved worshippers on the Savannah River, Silver Bluff Baptist Church is counted by one leading historian as the nation's first separate Black congregation, and its first elder carried its faith to Savannah, Nova Scotia and Sierra Leone.

**Location:** 33.4101, -81.8926

**Categories:** Baptist churches in South Carolina, African-American history of South Carolina, Buildings and structures in Aiken County, South Carolina, Black Loyalists, American Revolution

## Introduction

The congregation that began at Galphin's Mill traveled remarkably far. Around 1774 or 1775, a small group of enslaved men and women started meeting at the mill on the South Carolina side of the Savannah River, under an elder named David George. Within two decades, its first elder had carried that faith to Savannah, to Nova Scotia and to the new colony of Sierra Leone in West Africa. Its starting point was Silver Bluff, near today's Beech Island.

## Converts of the Awakening

During the Great Awakening, Baptist and Methodist preachers from the North traveled through the South, converting white people and enslaved and free Black people alike. The Baptists in particular offered Black members roles in their congregations, and some men were licensed as preachers and elders. At Silver Bluff, the founders were enslaved people converted by a white Baptist minister named Wait Palmer and perhaps by an enslaved preacher named George Leile. There were eight: David George and his wife, Jesse Peter, also known as Jesse Galphin, and five others. Palmer was so impressed by George's preaching that he made him the group's elder. They drew in more members and met at Galphin's Mill, owned by George's enslaver, George Galphin. The historian Albert Raboteau has identified Silver Bluff as the first separate Black congregation in the nation, though others, including the First Baptist Church in Petersburg, Virginia, contend for that distinction.

## From Savannah to Freetown

The American Revolution broke the congregation apart. After the British captured Savannah in 1778, Galphin, a Patriot, left his plantation. The British had promised freedom to enslaved people who escaped from rebel masters, and David George and some 30 to 50 enslaved members of the church, by differing accounts, went to Savannah to claim it behind British lines. There they joined George Leile and his followers. When the war ended, George and his family evacuated with the British in 1782, along with thousands of other freed people, and the Crown kept its promise, carrying them to freedom in Nova Scotia. Leile sailed with the British to Jamaica, where he founded a Baptist church in Kingston. In Nova Scotia, David George preached in Shelburne and planted another Baptist congregation among the Black Loyalists who had been resettled there. In 1792, with help from British abolitionists, the George family moved again, this time to Sierra Leone, the colony Britain had just founded in West Africa. George became one of the founders of Freetown and of its first Baptist church. A man who had begun preaching while enslaved beside the Savannah River helped plant churches in the American South, in Canada and in West Africa.

## The Church That Stayed

Not everyone left. Members who remained in Savannah after the war formed the core of what became the First African Baptist Church. Andrew Bryan, converted in 1782, was the only one of the three earliest Black Baptist preachers in Georgia to stay in Savannah. He later bought his own freedom and his wife's, kept preaching, and in 1788 organized the First African Baptist Church of Savannah. In 1793 or 1794 he bought land, and the congregation built its church there in 1794. By 1800 it had grown to roughly 700 members, one of the living branches of a faith first gathered at Silver Bluff.

## Flight Context

Silver Bluff Baptist Church is associated with the Beech Island area of Aiken County, South Carolina, at about 33.4101°N, 81.8926°W, on the South Carolina side of the Savannah River below Augusta, Georgia. From 1,500–2,500 feet, the broad Savannah River is the dominant reference, with Augusta's urban area to the northwest. Augusta Regional at Bush Field (KAGS) is only about 5 miles to the southwest, across the river, so expect its traffic; Daniel Field (KDNL) is about 9 miles west-northwest and Aiken Regional (KAIK) about 20 miles northeast. Summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are common.
