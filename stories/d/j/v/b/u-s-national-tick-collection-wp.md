# U.S. National Tick Collection

On the bottom floor of a science building at Georgia Southern University in Statesboro sits what may be the largest tick collection in the world, a Smithsonian-owned archive of around a million specimens that helps scientists track the parasites behind Lyme disease and Rocky Mountain spotted fever.

**Location:** 32.4194, -81.7767

**Categories:** Collection of the Smithsonian Institution, Georgia Southern University, Ticks, Parasitology

## Introduction

One of the largest collections of ticks in the world, and by the university's own description perhaps the largest, is not in Washington, D.C., or in a grand natural history hall. It sits on the bottom floor of the Math/Physics Building at Georgia Southern University in Statesboro, Georgia. The U.S. National Tick Collection holds more than a million specimens, covering most of the roughly 860 known tick species, and the Smithsonian Institution owns the collection. Few visitors think of ticks as treasures. Yet for scientists trying to understand the diseases these small parasites carry, this quiet archive in south Georgia is an essential reference library.

## From Montana to Statesboro

The collection's roots lie far from Georgia. Its historical core was assembled by Dr. Robert A. Cooley, who lived from 1873 to 1968, and his student Dr. Glen M. Kohls at Montana State College. In 1990 the collection was transferred to Georgia Southern University through a long-term enhancement loan. The Smithsonian still owns the collection, which has been entrusted to the university since 1990, and day to day it operates under the auspices of the university's James H. Oliver, Jr. Institute for Coastal Plain Science (older accounts place the collection under the Institute of Arthropodology and Parasitology, which James H. Oliver, Jr. directed). The arrangement gives a regional university in Statesboro stewardship of a national scientific treasure, and it makes an ordinary campus building the home of what may be the largest tick collection on Earth.

## Sorting the Dangerous From the Common

Why keep a million ticks? The collection's essential job is to help separate the ticks that matter medically and financially from those that are merely common. That distinction has real consequences. Some tick species carry Lyme disease and Rocky Mountain spotted fever, so research drawing on the collection is of interest to both human health and agriculture. The university describes the archive as a vital resource for scientists and public health officials working to understand and combat tick-borne diseases, and for the tick taxonomists whose careful identifications make that work possible. Each preserved specimen adds to a reference against which new finds can be compared, one species measured against another.

## A Million Specimens, Counted Carefully

Numbers for a collection like this shift as it grows. One account counts 850 separate species and almost one million specimens. The university's own current description puts it at more than a million specimens in more than 125,000 accessioned lots, covering most of the roughly 860 known tick species. Either way, the scale is staggering for creatures most people encounter only as an unwelcome discovery after a walk in the woods. Lorenza Beati was appointed the collection's curator late in 2011; she holds both an M.D. and a Ph.D. and is on the faculty of the university's College of Science and Mathematics. Visitors who want to see the archive itself can arrange tours by appointment, and a permanent exhibit outside the collection is open whenever the university is.

## Flight Context

The U.S. National Tick Collection is housed on the Georgia Southern University campus in Statesboro, Georgia, at 32.4194N, 81.7767W. The collection itself is indoors, so the campus and the town of Statesboro are the visual targets; a pass at 1,500-2,500 feet shows the campus layout clearly. Statesboro-Bulloch County Airport (KTBR) is about 4.3 nm north-northeast and is the obvious stop for a visit. Savannah/Hilton Head International (KSAV) is about 34 nm east-southeast, and Hunter Army Airfield (KSVN) about 40 nm southeast; its military airspace should be avoided. Southeast Georgia summers bring haze and afternoon thunderstorms, so mornings usually offer the best visibility.
