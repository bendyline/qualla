# Georgiacetus

Thirty feet beneath a nuclear plant construction site on the Savannah River, workers uncovered Georgiacetus, a four-legged, fluke-less Eocene whale that swam a sea which once covered southeastern Georgia.

**Location:** 33.1433, -81.7606

**Categories:** Paleontology, Prehistoric whales, Eocene mammals of North America, Fossil taxa described in 1998, Burke County, Georgia

## Introduction

In 1983, crews building the Plant Vogtle nuclear power plant in Burke County, Georgia, were cutting a drainage channel for the plant's river water intake on the Savannah River. About 30 feet below the surface, they hit bone. It belonged to a whale, but not one any sailor would recognize: an animal with large hips, well-developed hind legs and no tail fluke, which hunted in a warm sea that once rolled over this part of Georgia. Georgia Power halted construction in the area and helped recover it. The creature, eventually named Georgiacetus, the Georgia whale, is now an important clue to how whales spread across the ancient world.

## A Whale in the Intake Trench

Geologist E. A. Shapiro of the Georgia Geologic Survey reviewed the bones in the field and recommended calling in Georgia Southern University, where Richard Petkewich and Gale Bishop led the recovery. The site held three individuals of the same species. Two were known only from scattered bones, a few vertebrae, ribs and tooth crowns, but the main find was remarkable: an individual 75 to 80 percent complete, with more than 60 bones and teeth, including a well-preserved skull and lower jaw. The bones lay jumbled across an area about 3.3 meters square. It was an adult, its molars fully erupted and well worn. Scratches on one rib hinted at light scavenging, and only ten shark teeth turned up with it. There was no apparent cause of death. In 1998 a team led by Dr. Richard Hulbert, also of Georgia Southern, described it as a new genus and species.

## Built to Seize and Shear

The skull alone is more than 30 inches long, 12 inches wide and about 24 inches deep, and in life, fleshed out, it would have been decidedly larger. The bones of its inner ear settle any doubt about what it was: all whales and their land-dwelling ancestors share a distinctive, identifiable inner ear structure. Georgiacetus was an active predator equipped to take and process large prey. A prominent crest at the back of the skull anchored powerful jaw muscles. Its curved, banana-shaped front teeth, mostly buried in the jaw with only the tips exposed, were made to seize and hold struggling prey. Farther back, deeply rooted triangular teeth sheared, and sharp molars crushed. Large fish, birds or turtles could have been caught and cut into manageable chunks. Its nostrils sat halfway back along the snout, just in front of the eyes.

## Legs Where a Fluke Should Be

No hind leg bones survived, but both hip bones did, and they were relatively large with well-developed sockets, pointing to well-developed hind legs. Those hips were much larger than the hips of Basilosaurus, a far bigger later whale, so researchers cautiously concluded that Georgiacetus had much larger hind legs relative to its body. Yet the hips were not fused to the spine, which suggests the animal probably could not support its own weight out of the water. Material from Alabama and Mississippi supplied tail vertebrae, including one from the very end of the tail. It showed two muscle-supporting flanges but none of the compressed shape or fluke-supporting features seen in modern whales. Georgiacetus apparently had no fluke. It probably swam with its hind limbs, wiggling its hips and moving its trunk up and down, a way of moving that modern whales abandoned.

## A Sea Over Georgia

When Georgiacetus lived, global sea levels stood about 130 feet higher than today, and a warm coastal sea, driven by the Suwannee Current, covered much of the southeastern United States. The Plant Vogtle fossils were found 93 miles inland from the modern coastline. Invertebrate fossils collected with the bones, which also allowed the remains to be accurately dated, show that this whale died well offshore in shallow, open water, roughly 30 miles from the coastline of its day. That ancient shore ran along what is now Georgia's Fall Line. Fossils of Georgiacetus are known from Georgia, Alabama and Mississippi, and fossils of related early whales from the same time frame, not yet confirmed as Georgiacetus, have been found in Texas and South Carolina.

## The Long Swim From the Tethys

Whales emerged about 52 million years ago in the Tethys Ocean, between present-day India and Asia. How they reached North America is an open question. One idea is a direct deep-water crossing of the Atlantic; the other is a polar route up the European coast, across to a Greenland that was very likely green, and south along North America. Either way meant long deep-water crossings, longer than today's because seas were higher. Scientists once thought the tail fluke was what let early whales spread from South Asia. Georgiacetus, with its legs and fluke-less tail on the far side of the ocean, contradicts that idea. Its skull and teeth show a clear, direct relationship to the basilosaurids, which appeared later and led on toward modern whales, and the Georgia whale is thought to lie close to their ancestry. A reconstruction is on display at the Georgia Southern University Museum in Statesboro, which also shows some of the original fossils, including the animal's hip bones.

## Flight Context

The discovery site at Plant Vogtle lies at approximately 33.14°N, 81.76°W on the Georgia bank of the Savannah River in Burke County, with the Savannah River Site across the river in South Carolina. From 3,000–4,000 feet AGL the plant's large cooling towers beside the river make an unmistakable landmark; pilots are asked to avoid circling or loitering over power plants, so view it from a respectful distance. Burke County Airport (KBXG) at Waynesboro is about 14 nautical miles to the west-southwest, and Augusta Regional Airport at Bush Field (KAGS) lies about 17 nautical miles to the northwest. Summer brings haze and afternoon thunderstorms to the Savannah River valley.
