# Ellenton, South Carolina

A South Carolina railroad town that survived a Reconstruction-era massacre and the Depression was bought, emptied and erased starting in 1950 to make room for a plant producing materials for the hydrogen bomb.

**Location:** 33.1989, -81.7558

**Categories:** Ghost towns in South Carolina, Forcibly depopulated communities in the United States, African-American history of South Carolina, Savannah River Site, Cold War history, Former municipalities in South Carolina

## Introduction

In the end, most of what remained of Ellenton was pavement: streets, curbs, driveways and walkways tracing the outline of a town where, in the early 1950s, about 760 people lived among some 190 homes, five churches, two schools, a cotton gin, a city hall and jail, and a railroad station. Everything else is gone. Beginning in 1950 the U.S. Atomic Energy Commission acquired the entire town, every residence and every business, as part of the site for the Savannah River Plant. The people of Ellenton scattered, many of them to a new town built to replace the old one, and former residents have held reunions since 1973 to remember a place that exists now mostly in memory.

## Ellen's Town

Ellenton owed its existence to the railroad. The Port Royal and Augusta Railroad, later renamed the Charleston and Western Carolina Railway and eventually taken over by CSX Transportation, ran through the plantation of Robert Jefferson Dunbar, and part of his land became the right-of-way, the train station and the town. The name comes wrapped in a story. According to local oral tradition, Stephen Caldwell Millet, who superintended the railroad's construction and served as its president, boarded with the Dunbar family and was so struck by the beauty of their nine-year-old daughter, Ellen, that he asked his company to name the station "Ellen's Town." The records complicate the tale. In a note to Lucius Sidney O'Berry's book about the town, the Savannah River Archaeological Research Program points out that in 1870, when this was supposed to have happened, Mary Ellen Dunbar was twenty-two years old.

## Days in September

In September 1876, during Reconstruction, Ellenton became the scene of what is remembered as the Ellenton riot, and of mass killing. The violence began when a white posse set out to arrest two Black men accused of assaulting a white woman, an accusation later shown to be unfounded, and armed Black neighbors turned the posse back; accounts of the warrants differ. Over the following days, from about September 15 to 21, white gun clubs hunted through the area and the confrontation escalated into killing. Estimates of the dead vary widely: the South Carolina Encyclopedia puts the number of Black dead at thirty to more than one hundred and says at least two white men died, one historian concluded that one white man and up to 100 Black men were killed, and the official record of the Deputy U.S. Marshals counted 25 to 30 Black men. White leaders played down the numbers. Among the dead was Simon P. Coker, who served in the state legislature as a member from Barnwell. Whatever the exact count, it hides many individual lives, most of them unnamed in the surviving account, and their deaths make that week one of the darkest chapters in the town's history.

## A Town of Firsts

Ellenton was incorporated in 1880, and for most of its life it was an agricultural, trading and sawmill town. Cotton set its rhythm, and when cotton prices fell after World War I and the Depression of the 1930s followed, the town declined. Yet, by local account, it had a knack for being early. Ellenton reportedly had the first automatic telephone dialing system in South Carolina, and after the bank failures of the Great Depression it is said to have opened the state's first cash depository. By the early 1950s it was a settled small town with about 30 commercial buildings, Ellenton High School among its two schools, and the railroad station that had given it life still in place.

## The Announcement

On November 28, 1950, the Atomic Energy Commission and the E. I. du Pont de Nemours Company announced that the Savannah River Plant would be built on about 300 square miles of Aiken, Barnwell and Allendale counties. Its purpose was the production of plutonium and tritium for the hydrogen bomb. About 6,000 people and 6,000 graves had to be moved. Ellenton was not alone: the incorporated town of Dunbarton and the unincorporated communities of Hawthorne, Meyers Mill, Robbins and Leigh were also taken. In this relatively poor rural area, a significant share of those displaced were African-American farmers and sharecroppers. The government bought or condemned their property. Many residents moved themselves, and some moved their houses, to New Ellenton, built about 14 miles to the north on U.S. Highway 278, or to Jackson, Beech Island, Aiken, North Augusta and Augusta, Georgia. Some left the state altogether.

## I Don't Live There Anymore

Ellenton's disappearance did not erase it. Two new towns, New Ellenton and Jackson, were built as part of the upheaval, and New Ellenton in particular was developed to take the old town's place, carrying its name forward. Former residents began holding an annual reunion in 1973, and the tradition continues. The town's story became a musical, I Don't Live There Anymore: The Ellenton Story, which premiered in Dorset, England, in 1992 and was staged the following year at Piccolo Spoleto during the Spoleto Festival USA in Charleston. Ellenton's fate also shaped fiction: Pat Conroy is said to have drawn on it for the town of Colleton in his novel The Prince of Tides.

## Flight Context

The former townsite lies at about 33.1989 N, 81.7558 W on the Aiken County-Barnwell County line, inside the Savannah River Site, a secured federal nuclear facility; check current charts and NOTAMs for any airspace restrictions and do not loiter. There is little of the town left to see from the air, but the sheer size of the reservation tells the story. New Ellenton lies about 14 miles to the north on U.S. Highway 278 (13.8 nm, about 16 statute miles, in a straight line from the stored townsite pin). Augusta Regional Airport at Bush Field (KAGS) is about 15 nm to the northwest across the Savannah River, Barnwell Regional Airport (KBNL) about 19 nm east, and Aiken Regional Airport (KAIK) about 27 nm north. Summer haze and thunderstorms are common; winter and spring offer the clearest views.
