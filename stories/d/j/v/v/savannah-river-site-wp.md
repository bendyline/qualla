# Savannah River Site

A 310-square-mile Cold War bomb plant in South Carolina that erased two towns, made plutonium and tritium for nuclear weapons, helped prove the neutrino exists, and now wrestles with one of the nation's largest nuclear cleanups.

**Location:** 33.2500, -81.6500

**Categories:** Savannah River Site, Cold War, Nuclear weapons history, Military nuclear reactors, Atomic tourism, Buildings and structures in Aiken County, South Carolina

## Introduction

In July 1956, two physicists announced in the journal Science that they had caught a ghost. The neutrino, a particle Wolfgang Pauli had proposed in 1930 to explain energy that seemed to vanish during beta decay, was still doubted when Frederick Reines and Clyde Cowan of the Los Alamos Scientific Laboratory set out to find it. Their first attempt, at Hanford in 1953, failed. So they hauled several truckloads of equipment to P Reactor at South Carolina's Savannah River Plant, where a detector weighing about ten tons before shielding, fitted with 110 photomultiplier tubes, finally registered the particle. Reines later won the 1995 Nobel Prize in Physics; Cowan had already died. The reactor itself had been built for a far grimmer purpose.

## A Dollar for the Hydrogen Bomb

After the Soviet Union detonated its first atomic bomb on 29 August 1949, the Atomic Energy Commission launched an expansion, and on 31 January 1950 President Harry S. Truman directed it to continue work on all forms of atomic weapons, including the hydrogen bomb. That bomb was believed to need large quantities of tritium, which decays by about 5.5 percent a year and must be constantly replenished. The AEC wanted new heavy-water reactors to make it, and it turned to DuPont, builder of the wartime plutonium complex at Hanford. DuPont's president, Crawford Greenewalt, asked for a personal letter from Truman affirming the project's urgency, and got one on 25 July 1950. The contract paid DuPont a fee of one dollar. Of the final candidate sites, Site Number 5 on the Savannah River won out, with good water, land considered marginal for agriculture, and a place in the First Defense Zone, beyond the range of Soviet bombers.

## The Towns Inside the Fence

Land considered marginal was still home to people. By adjusting the boundary, planners spared the villages of Jackson and Snelling, but Ellenton, with 746 residents in 1950, and Dunbarton, with about 300, were taken and their residents forced to relocate. The government acquired 1,706 tracts totaling 200,742 acres. Farmers occupied 73 percent of them, most of them African American and many of them sharecroppers, and nearly half the land was taken through condemnation. Even the dead were moved: 126 cemeteries holding roughly 6,000 graves, some dating to the 18th century. About 120 Black and 30 white families from Ellenton rebuilt in a new town, New Ellenton. In the old town, Black and white neighborhoods had been intermingled; the new one was laid out with strict racial segregation, the two communities divided by the highway.

## Five Reactors and the Canyons

Within six months DuPont was hiring more than a hundred workers a day. R Reactor, begun in June 1951, became operational in December 1953, and P, L and K followed in 1954, with C Reactor going critical in 1955. Two chemical separation plants, called canyons, extracted plutonium and uranium from irradiated material. F Canyon, the world's first operational full-scale PUREX plant, began radioactive operations in April 1954, and the first plutonium shipment left on 28 December that year. Over thirty years of operation the plant produced about 36.1 metric tons of weapons-grade plutonium. It also made plutonium-238, which went to the Moon with the Apollo missions and to the outer Solar System aboard Voyager. Production ended in 1988. An incident during P Reactor's startup that August drew wide media attention, a congressional hearing in September received an internal report listing more than thirty significant incidents, and a temporary halt became permanent.

## A Forest Where Farms Had Been

The bomb plant also became an early center of ecological research in the United States. DuPont brought in Ruth Patrick and her team from the Academy of Natural Sciences of Philadelphia to survey the river before the reactors warmed it. They studied all the major plant and animal species and how they interacted, something Patrick later called revolutionary for its day, and, by one account, their methods were later used on the Amazon. University of Georgia ecologist Eugene Odum began work on the site in 1951 with a biological inventory for the AEC, and that effort grew into the Savannah River Ecology Laboratory, now a division of the University of Georgia. The Forest Service, also brought in in 1951, planted pines across land that farmers had worked for generations; some 75 million seedlings were in the ground by June 1960, and by 1999 forest covered 72 percent of the site. Red-cockaded woodpeckers, listed as endangered in 1970, grew from four birds on site that year to 120 in 2002.

## The Long Cleanup

Decades of production left a heavy inheritance. Seepage basins and underground tanks contaminated soil, groundwater and surface water, and in 1989 the site became a Superfund site. Liquid high-level waste has been held in 51 carbon-steel tanks built between 1951 and 1981; eight have since been operationally closed, leaving 43 in use. The Defense Waste Processing Facility melts the most radioactive portion into borosilicate glass and pours it into steel canisters ten feet tall, each weighing about 5,000 pounds when full, and about 7,800 are projected. A plant meant to turn surplus weapons plutonium into reactor fuel was halted in 2018 at about 70 percent complete and is being rebuilt to make plutonium pits, with construction now expected to finish around 2035. In 2020 the federal government agreed to pay South Carolina $600 million and to remove the remaining 9.5 metric tons of plutonium by 2037. The legacy turns up in odd places: in July 2025 a worker found a radioactive wasp nest beside a waste tank, and it was bagged as nuclear waste.

## Flight Context

The Savannah River Site centers on approximately 33.25°N, 81.65°W, covering 310 square miles of Aiken, Allendale and Barnwell counties, South Carolina, along the Savannah River about 25 miles southeast of Augusta, Georgia. From 4,000–5,000 feet AGL the reservation reads as a vast, mostly forested tract crossed by roads leading to scattered industrial areas, with the large cooling reservoirs of Par Pond and L Lake standing out as open water; across the river on the Georgia side, the cooling towers of Plant Vogtle are a strong landmark. Avoid loitering over the site's nuclear facilities and check current NOTAMs and airspace restrictions before flying nearby. Barnwell Regional Airport (KBNL) lies about 13 nautical miles to the east, Augusta Regional Airport at Bush Field (KAGS) about 17 nautical miles to the west-northwest, and Aiken Regional Airport (KAIK) about 24 nautical miles to the north. Summer brings haze and afternoon thunderstorms.
