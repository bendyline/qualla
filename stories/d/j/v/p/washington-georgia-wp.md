# Washington, Georgia

Once a Revolutionary War capital called Heard's Fort, the Wilkes County seat of Washington is where Jefferson Davis met his Confederate cabinet for the last time, and where legend says the missing Confederate gold still lies buried.

**Location:** 33.7368, -82.7393

**Categories:** Cities in Georgia (U.S. state), County seats in Georgia (U.S. state), Former state capitals in the United States, American Revolutionary War sites, American Civil War history, Cities in Wilkes County, Georgia

## Introduction

On May 5, 1865, fourteen officials of a collapsing government gathered in a building in a small Georgia town. Jefferson Davis had fled Richmond a month earlier, as Union troops under Ulysses S. Grant closed in on the Confederate capital, and had been moving south with his cabinet ever since. In Washington, Georgia, in the State of Georgia Bank building, he met with that cabinet for the last time. The town is remembered as the place where Davis's government held its last cabinet meeting and formally dissolved itself, a symbolic end to the Confederate government, though fighting would continue elsewhere for several more weeks. It is an odd bookend for a place that began, nearly a century earlier, as a fort that served for a time as the capital of revolutionary Georgia.

## Capital of a Rebel State

The colonist Stephen Heard established the settlement in the early 1770s (accounts give 1773 or 1774), and it went by the name Heard's Fort. When the Revolutionary War reached Georgia, the settlement became something grander than a fort. From February 3, 1780, it served as the temporary capital of the new state, though accounts differ on how long the government stayed. The war came close in other ways too. On February 14, 1779, about eleven miles from present-day Washington, Patriot and Loyalist forces fought the Battle of Kettle Creek, the first major Patriot victory in Georgia's back country. The Patriots won, killing somewhere between 40 and 70 Loyalists and wounding or capturing about 75 more, at a cost of fewer than ten of their own dead and a larger number wounded or missing. Today the town is the county seat of Wilkes County and is often called Washington-Wilkes, to tell it apart from the many other places named Washington.

## The Last Meeting

No major Civil War battles were fought in or near Washington, but the war's political story ended here. After abandoning Richmond in early April 1865, Davis and his cabinet escaped first to Danville, Virginia, then continued south. A hand-picked escort led by Given Campbell traveled with the Confederate president, including his personal bodyguard, Sergeant Joseph A. Higgenbotham Jr. of Amherst and Nelson counties in Virginia. The last cabinet meeting took place in the town's State of Georgia Bank building, which some accounts call the Heard house. The town already had a link to the Confederacy's leadership. As a child, Alexander H. Stephens had studied at a Washington school run by the Presbyterian minister Alexander Hamilton Webster. Stephens went on to become a politician and was elected vice-president of the Confederacy. Robert Toombs, a slaveholder who served as the Confederacy's first Secretary of State and later as a Confederate general, also counted Washington as home; his house is now a historic house museum.

## The Gold That Vanished

The end of the Confederate government left behind one of Washington's most lingering mysteries. The cabinet's journey south ended here, and so does the paper trail of the remaining Confederate gold: the Washington area is its last recorded location, and local legend holds that the treasure lies buried nearby. Accounts of the sum differ: one source estimates the original treasure at over half a million dollars in gold and says northern troops seized $100,000 of it from a Washington bank. The legend has drawn enough attention that the cable channel A&E produced a documentary about it.

## Walking the Old Town

Washington had 3,754 residents at the 2020 census, and its past is written into its buildings. Several are listed on the National Register of Historic Places, including the Wilkes County Courthouse, the Robert Toombs House State Historic Site, the Washington-Wilkes Historical Museum, the Mary Willis Public Library, the Cherry Grove Baptist Church Schoolhouse, and the Fitzpatrick Hotel, built in 1898. The town's notable names stretch well beyond the war. Among them are Ernie Harwell, the baseball broadcaster known as "The Voice of the Tigers," and the blues musician Frank Edwards, who recorded for the OKeh, Regal and Trix labels. The list also includes Edward Porter Alexander, a U.S. Army officer turned Confederate general and later a railroad executive; Mary Rankin Cranston, librarian, writer, social researcher and farmer; and Hillary Lindsey, a Grammy Award-winning songwriter.

## Flight Context

Washington sits at about 33.7368 N, 82.7393 W in the rolling Piedmont of northeast Georgia, the seat of Wilkes County. The courthouse square and historic downtown make a compact target from 2,000 to 3,000 feet MSL. Washington-Wilkes County Airport (KIIY) is about 5 nm from town and is the natural stop for a visit. Thomson-McDuffie County Airport (KHQU) lies about 17 nm to the southeast. The Kettle Creek battlefield is about eleven miles from town in Wilkes County. Expect warm, hazy summers with afternoon thunderstorms; fall and spring usually bring clearer skies.
