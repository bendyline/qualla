# Robert Toombs House State Historic Site

The columned Washington, Georgia, home of Robert Toombs, a senator who once opposed secession, became Confederate secretary of state and a general, fled abroad after the war, and never asked for a pardon.

**Location:** 33.7361, -82.7344

## Introduction

Robert Toombs never asked to be forgiven. After the Civil War, the former U.S. senator and Confederate general fled the country, returned to Georgia in 1867 and refused to request a pardon from the president. For the rest of the Reconstruction era he never regained the right to vote or to hold political office. He practiced law instead, successfully, from the columned house on East Robert Toombs Avenue in Washington, Georgia, that he had owned since 1837. The house is now a National Historic Landmark and a historic site listed by Georgia State Parks, though the park system says it is managed by Wilkes County, and it tells the story of a man whose political path took some very sharp turns.

## A Doctor's House, a Lawyer's Columns

The house was already decades old when its famous owner arrived. Doctor Joel Abbott built it sometime between 1794 and 1801, and Toombs acquired it in 1837. He made it his own. Toombs is credited with a number of alterations, including the west wing and the front colonnade that now defines the house: a two-story row of fluted Doric columns across the front of a two-story wood-frame building. The main entrance is framed by sidelight windows and topped by a transom. Inside, the rooms have been decorated to reflect the house as it was in the mid-nineteenth century, when Toombs lived here, and they include his private library, which also served as his law office. A bedroom, a ladies' parlor, a dining room and a staircase complete the picture of a prosperous lawyer-politician's household.

## The Orator and His Contradictions

Before the war, Toombs was one of the South's prominent orators, serving Georgia as a U.S. representative and then a senator. His politics resist a simple label. He supported the extension of slavery into the territories, yet he opposed the breakup of the country and was a major supporter of the Compromise of 1850. He shared that stage with his friend Alexander H. Stephens of Crawfordville, about fifteen miles away, and their friendship became a powerful personal and political bond through which the two men effectively defined Georgia's position on national issues in those decades. Like Stephens, Toombs was an enslaver. By 1860 he held 16 people in slavery at his Wilkes County plantation and 32 more on a 3,800-acre plantation in Stewart County on the Chattahoochee River.

## Secretary, General, Exile

When the Civil War began in 1861, the man who had once opposed disunion became the Confederate States of America's secretary of state. He did not last in the post. Differences with President Jefferson Davis led him to resign, and he entered the Confederate Army instead. At the Battle of Antietam he commanded the defense of Burnside's Bridge and was wounded in the hand. When the war ended, Toombs fled, making his way out of the country and reaching Paris, France, early in July 1865. He stayed away for two years. When he came back to Georgia in 1867, it was on his own terms, without the pardon that might have restored his rights, and he built a successful law practice from Washington.

## Rooms That Remember

Toombs died on December 15, 1885, and was buried at Resthaven Cemetery in Wilkes County. His house endured. Listed by Georgia State Parks but managed today by Wilkes County, it serves as a period house museum with exhibits about his life, and in 1973 it was declared a National Historic Landmark. Walking through it, visitors can see his library and law office, the bedrooms and the dining room, and a painting of Ophelia hanging among the furnishings. The house invites a complicated reckoning. It honors the architecture and the ambitions of a gifted orator, and it also stands as a record of a man who defended slavery's expansion, served the Confederacy and never sought his country's pardon.

## Flight Context

The Robert Toombs House stands on the east side of Washington, Georgia, on the south side of East Robert Toombs Avenue, at 33.7361N, 82.7344W. The house itself is small from the air, so aim for the town of Washington and its historic center from 1,500-2,500 feet. Washington-Wilkes County Airport (FAA code IIY, GPS ident KIIY) is only about 4.8 nm west-northwest. Liberty Hall, Alexander Stephens's home in Crawfordville, lies about 13 nm to the southwest. Athens Ben Epps Airport (KAHN) is about 32 nm west-northwest, and Augusta Regional at Bush Field (KAGS) about 44 nm east-southeast. Expect haze and afternoon thunderstorms in the Georgia summer.
