# Saint Paul's Church (Augusta, Georgia)

Augusta's mother church has built five churches on one riverside corner since 1750, burying founders and generals in its churchyard and ringing a chime that holds the remains of a bell pulled from the ashes of 1916.

**Location:** 33.4758, -81.9611

**Categories:** Episcopal church buildings in Georgia (U.S. state), Churches on the National Register of Historic Places in Georgia (U.S. state), Buildings and structures in Augusta, Georgia, Colonial history, Bells

## Introduction

The bell came from London's Whitechapel Bell Foundry, the same foundry that cast Philadelphia's Liberty Bell and Big Ben. From 1820 it hung in the belfry of Saint Paul's Church in Augusta, Georgia, until March 1916, when the Great Augusta Fire burned the church to the ground and the belfry collapsed. The bell was damaged beyond repair. Its remains were dug up from the ground beneath the burned church and saved, and when a new chime of eleven bells rang for services in 1924, the old bell was part of it. Saint Paul's has a long habit of rising from its own ruins. The church that stands on this corner today is the fifth.

## Five Churches on One Corner

The story begins in 1749, when Georgia's colonial governing council approved Augusta resident James Fraser's petition to build a church and burial ground. Established in 1750 by the Church of England at the site of Fort Augusta, Saint Paul's was the third Church of England congregation in the colony, after Christ Church in Savannah and Christ Church at Frederica, and formal services under the Saint Paul's name have been held since 1751. It is regarded as the oldest church congregation in Augusta. The first building was severely damaged when it sheltered refugees during the French and Indian Wars, and the second was destroyed in the American Revolution. William Mead completed the third in 1789, after work that began in the mid-1780s. The fourth, designed by John Lund, burned in the fire of 1916, which also destroyed the homes of many parishioners. The fifth, designed by Henry Ten Eyck Wendell and consecrated in 1919, blends Federal, Georgian and Greek Revival styles and seats up to 600 people at the corner of 6th and Reynolds Streets.

## Councils of Church and War

The Episcopal Diocese of Georgia was founded at Saint Paul's on February 24, 1823. Four decades later the church hosted a more fraught gathering. From November 12 to 22, 1862, the First General Council of the Episcopal Church in the Confederate States met here, adopting a constitution and canons for a separate Confederate church and electing Stephen Elliott, Bishop of Georgia, as its presiding bishop. The war left other marks. Leonidas Polk, Episcopal Bishop of Louisiana and a Confederate general, was buried at Saint Paul's after his death near Marietta in June 1864, and his remains lay beneath the chancel window of the fourth church until they were moved to Christ Church in New Orleans in 1945. That same month of June 1864, Bishop Elliott confirmed James Longstreet, the Confederate general raised in Augusta, and Joseph Wheeler, the Confederate general born in Augusta, was a member of the parish.

## The Churchyard's Two Histories

The churchyard holds men who helped found a nation. William Few, a signer of the U.S. Constitution who represented Georgia at the Constitutional Convention, was moved here in 1973. George Mathews served as governor of Georgia and in Congress. Commodore Oliver Bowen captured a British vessel laden with gunpowder near Savannah in 1775, and U.S. Marshal Robert Forsyth is considered the first federal law enforcement official killed in the line of duty. Ambrose Gordon, a Revolutionary War soldier, was the great-grandfather of Girl Scouts founder Juliette Gordon Low. But the churchyard also held enslaved people and free people of color, until their remains were moved in 1825 to the newly opened Cedar Grove Cemetery, which local government designated as the burying ground for Augusta residents classified as non-white. Two Black sextons who served the parish for many years, Abel Wright, who died on Christmas Day 1887, and Benjamin Whitehead, who died in 1894, were later buried in the churchyard as a sign of affection and respect.

## A Rector Who Carried a Cause

From 1937 to 1941, Saint Paul's was led by the Rev. John E. Hines, whose commitment to racial reconciliation and social justice was already evident in Augusta. He carried it with him to become Bishop of Texas and then Presiding Bishop of the Episcopal Church from 1965 to 1974. In 1968 Hines returned to the parish he had served three decades earlier, convening the House of Bishops of the Episcopal Church in joint session with the bishops of the Anglican Church of Canada. It was the first time in the Episcopal Church's history that its House of Bishops held a formal meeting in which the bishops of another Anglican church took part. They discussed the social problems facing both churches, as well as relations with the Roman Catholic and Orthodox churches.

## The Chime and the Lunch Hour

The new chime, cast by the Meneely Bell Foundry of New York, weighs 12,187 pounds. It was dedicated in January 1924 and first rang for services on Sunday, June 1, 1924, playing the Doxology, the Old 100th. The great bell reads, "The Living to the Church I call, To the grave I summon all." Rung by levers and ropes until 1956, the chime was later fitted with French-cast Paccard clappers and computer controls, and three matching Paccard bells brought it to fourteen. The church itself keeps ringing with music. Since 1988 Saint Paul's has hosted Tuesday's Music Live, billed as America's largest lunchtime concert series: twelve free thirty-minute noon concerts each season, September through March, drawing roughly 250 to 400 people apiece. The parish also runs a food pantry, hosts proms and galas in its River Room event facility, and feeds pasta to athletes before the Augusta Ironman each September.

## Flight Context

Saint Paul's Church stands at approximately 33.476°N, 81.961°W in downtown Augusta, Georgia, beside Riverwalk Augusta on the south bank of the Savannah River, which forms the Georgia–South Carolina line here. From 1,500–2,500 feet AGL, follow the river past downtown; the church sits near the water at 6th and Reynolds Streets, alongside the Riverwalk. Daniel Field (KDNL) is about 4 nautical miles to the west, and Augusta Regional Airport at Bush Field (KAGS) is about 6 nautical miles to the south. Expect haze and pop-up thunderstorms on summer afternoons.
