# Imperial Theatre (Augusta, Georgia)

Opened in downtown Augusta in 1918 as a vaudeville house, shut within months by the Spanish flu, and later a movie palace and then a revived performing arts venue, the 853-seat Imperial Theatre has outlasted every downturn thrown at it.

## Introduction

Less than eight months after it opened, Augusta's newest theater went dark. On Sunday, October 6, 1918, more than 3,000 cases of Spanish flu were reported, and 52 servicemen from a local military camp had died. The next day the city closed every public venue, including its new downtown vaudeville house, and the quarantine did not lift until late November. The man who founded the theater was forced to sell it. The theater itself carried on. Now called the Imperial, the 853-seat house has lived through a pandemic, the rise of the movies, the decline of downtown and a four-year closure, and it is still raising its curtain more than a century later.

## Jake Wells Opens His House

The Imperial began in 1917 as the Wells Theatre, a vaudeville showcase founded by impresario Jake Wells. Architect G. Lloyd Preacher designed it in the Victorian Renaissance style, and the whole project cost $47,792. It opened on February 18, 1918. The opening-night price list shows what an evening of vaudeville cost, and whom it was for. Matinee seats ran ten and twenty cents, evening seats fifteen, and the orchestra thirty-five. In the balcony, the first section cost thirty-five cents and the remainder a quarter, while the gallery was labeled the "Colored Section," at fifteen cents. Black Augustans were sent to the gallery, a reminder that segregation was written into the building's very first price list.

## Chaplin, Pavlova and the Flu

The early months brought remarkable visitors. In mid-April 1918, Charlie Chaplin appeared at the theater selling Liberty war bonds. Early bills also featured Leo Carrillo, who would later play Pancho, the Cisco Kid's partner, and the famous ballet dancer Anna Pavlova. Then came the flu. When the city ordered public venues closed on October 7, Jake Wells ran into serious financial trouble and sold his theater to Lynch Enterprises. The quarantine lifted on November 27, 1918, shortly after the sale, and two weeks later the theater reopened, still under the Wells name, with the B. F. Keith Supreme Vaudeville Company. After Lynch Enterprises acquired several other local theaters, the Wells became the Imperial.

## From Vaudeville to Technicolor

By 1929, vaudeville was fading and motion pictures were enjoying meteoric success, so the Imperial was renovated into a full-time movie house in the Art Deco style that was then in fashion. In March 1936 it showed its first color film, The Trail of the Lonesome Pine, starring Henry Fonda and Fred MacMurray. For more than fifty years, from that 1929 conversion until 1981, the Imperial served Augusta as a picture palace. The decline of the downtown area eventually caught up with it, though, and in 1981 the theater closed. For four years the house stood empty, but the Imperial's story had one more turn left.

## The Curtain Rises Again

In 1985 the Imperial was recognized for its architectural significance and reopened as a performing arts venue, with help from local groups including the Augusta Ballet and the Augusta Players. It stands in the Broad Street Historic District, where the National Register of Historic Places identifies it as important to the district's character. Today it stages musicals, dance, concerts and comedy, and its stage has hosted the Augusta Ballet and the Augusta Players, the Morris Museum of Art's Southern Soul + Song series, Storyland Theatre, Dance Augusta, the Columbia County Ballet, Westobou Festival events and the Poison Peach Film Festival. In January 2026 the Augusta Players and the Imperial announced their intent to merge into a single performing arts organization. One of the few historic theaters in Augusta still in regular use, the Imperial has turned a century of near-misses into a long second act.
