# Confederate Powderworks

On the Augusta Canal, a 150-foot obelisk chimney is all that remains of the Confederacy's great gunpowder factory, described as the second-largest in the world and the principal powder factory of the South.

**Location:** 33.4869, -81.9924

**Categories:** Georgia (U.S. state) in the American Civil War, Buildings and structures in Augusta, Georgia, Augusta Canal National Heritage Area, Manufacturing plants in the United States, Confederate States of America monuments and memorials in Georgia

## Introduction

Beside the Augusta Canal stands a 150-foot chimney shaped like an obelisk, one of the most recognizable features on the Augusta skyline. Its neighbor, the Sibley Mill, was built from the bricks of the very factory the chimney once served. That factory was the Confederate Powderworks, a gunpowder plant that, during the Civil War, has been described as the second-largest in the world and was the principal powder factory of the South.

## The Chemist from West Point

The works were the creation of George Washington Rains. He graduated from West Point in 1842, taught chemistry at the Military Academy, and served with gallantry in the Mexican War. In 1856 he resigned after marrying into the wealthy Ramsdell family and became president of an ironworks in Newburgh, New York, owned by his father-in-law. When the Civil War began, he sided with his native North Carolina and returned south. Confederate President Jefferson Davis assigned him to build a national gunpowder factory and gave him carte blanche. Rains picked the old United States Arsenal site in Augusta, Georgia, between the Augusta Canal and the Savannah River, a secure inland location with good rail and water connections.

## A Factory Built from a Pamphlet

Construction began in September 1861. Rains bought a 130-horsepower steam engine from an Atlanta flour mill owned by Richard Peters, and within seven months, in 1862, the works were turning out gunpowder. His guide was a pamphlet by a British artillery officer describing the powder works at Waltham Abbey, near London, and he also found a man who had worked there to advise him. As was customary for powder mills, the buildings were spread apart and designed to survive explosions. Raw materials entered at one end, and finished powder emerged a mile and a half away at the other. The largest building, the saltpeter refinery, was designed in the Gothic style as a replica of the British Houses of Parliament.

## Three and a Half Tons a Day

At its peak the Powderworks produced about 3.5 tons of gunpowder a day. Between April 1862 and April 1865 it made about 2.75 million pounds of first-quality powder, by Rains's own count, and a Georgia historical marker calls it the principal powder factory in the South. The Union, by contrast, spread its production across many mills; the Hazard Powder Company of Connecticut was one of its three chief suppliers, turning out up to 12,500 pounds a day. Where the North could draw on many mills, the South leaned heavily on this one. The Augusta works also stands out for what happened to it afterward. It is described as one of the only permanent structures completed by the Confederate government that was not destroyed by Union forces.

## The Chimney That Stayed

After the war the works were seized and dismantled. In 1872 Rains asked that the obelisk chimney be spared, since he had designed it to "remain a monument to the Confederacy should the Powderworks pass away." On June 2, 1879, the city gave custody of the chimney to the Confederate Survivors' Association, which repaired its square castellated base and set a large Italian marble tablet into the face looking toward the canal. The inscription calls the chimney the "sole remnant" of the works and dedicates it "in Honor of a fallen Nation" and to the memory of those who died in the Southern armies. It remains a Confederate memorial in the words of the veterans who dedicated it. Around it, between 1880 and 1882, the Sibley cotton mill was built on the site as a private venture, using the powder works' own bricks, and it became one of the largest and most successful cotton mills in the region.

## Flight Context

The Powderworks chimney stands at 33.4869°N, 81.9924°W at the Sibley Mill, 1717 Goodrich Street, in Augusta, Georgia, on the strip of land between the Augusta Canal and the Savannah River. From 1,500–2,500 feet, follow the canal alongside the river; the tall, slender chimney and the mill buildings beside it mark the spot. Daniel Field (KDNL) is about 3 miles southwest, and Augusta Regional at Bush Field (KAGS) is about 8 miles south. Summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are common.
