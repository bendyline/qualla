# Meadow Garden (Augusta, Georgia)

A modest Sand Hills cottage beside the Augusta Canal was the home of George Walton, an orphaned carpenter's apprentice who signed the Declaration of Independence, and the Daughters of the American Revolution have kept it as a museum since 1901.

**Location:** 33.4738, -81.9796

## Introduction

George Walton began life with almost nothing. Born in Cumberland County, Virginia, probably in 1749, he lost both parents as an infant and was taken in by an uncle, who put him to work as an apprentice carpenter. By 1776 that carpenter's apprentice was voting for American independence, one of three Georgians to sign the Declaration. His home in Augusta, Meadow Garden, is no mansion. It is a wood-frame Sand Hills cottage on a high brick basement, tucked between downtown and the Augusta Canal, and its modesty tells its own story about the man who lived there.

## The Carpenter Who Signed

On July 2, 1776, Walton voted in favor of independence for Georgia alongside Button Gwinnett and Lyman Hall, the other two Georgians whose names would appear on the Declaration. The Revolution soon demanded more than a signature. At the Battle of Savannah in 1778, Walton was hit in the thigh by a musket ball that threw him from his horse, and he was taken prisoner. He was released in a prisoner exchange in October 1779, and that same month Georgia elected him governor, a post he held for only two months. It was the beginning of a long public career that would carry him to the governor's office again, to the United States Senate and to the bench, but it is hard to forget where it started: an orphan learning a trade in Virginia.

## Governor, Senator, Judge

Walton won a second term as governor in 1789 and served for a year. From 1790 until his death he sat as a judge of the superior court, and during those years he also briefly served in the U.S. Senate, from November 16, 1795, to February 20, 1796. Meadow Garden was his home from the early 1790s until 1804, the year he died. A Georgia Historical Society marker says he died there, though other accounts place his death at College Hill, his country house. Even death did not end his public role. In 1848 his remains were reinterred beneath the Signers Monument in front of the courthouse on Greene Street in Augusta, placing him at the heart of the city's civic life once more. The house, meanwhile, remained on the city's western edge, where a founder of the nation had spent his final years in rooms of surprisingly human scale.

## A House Built in Stages

Meadow Garden grew a piece at a time. It began three bays wide and is now six, with three gabled dormers and two chimneys rising from its gabled roof. A shed-roofed porch runs across the front on slender Doric columns, and the facade is irregular, with two doors and four windows arranged without strict symmetry. The whole house sits on a high brick basement. It is classified as a Sand Hills cottage. Its lack of pretension is part of its appeal: this is not the grand house of legend but a practical home, altered and enlarged as needs changed.

## Saved by the Daughters

Meadow Garden might easily have disappeared. Instead the Daughters of the American Revolution saved it and established it as a museum in 1901, making it one of the organization's museum properties. Recognition followed over the decades. The house was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1976 and declared a National Historic Landmark in 1981. Today it stands at 1320 Independence Drive on the west side of downtown Augusta, separated from the Augusta Canal only by the Augusta Canal Historic Walking Trail, and just east of the Sutherland Mill. Visitors can walk the canal trail and step off it into the home of a signer, a juxtaposition of Augusta's industrial waterway and its Revolutionary past.
