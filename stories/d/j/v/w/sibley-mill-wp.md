# Sibley Mill

Built of Confederate Powderworks brick on the Augusta Canal in 1882, Sibley Mill wrapped a plain cotton factory in castle-like ornament, spun cloth and later Levi's denim for 124 years, and still makes electricity from the canal's water.

**Location:** 33.4873, -81.9920

**Categories:** Cotton mills in the United States, Augusta Canal National Heritage Area, Industrial history, Hydroelectric power, Buildings and structures in Augusta, Georgia

## Introduction

One of the tallest things at Sibley Mill never made a yard of cloth. The great chimney in front of the mill is a relic of the Confederate Powderworks, which stood on this ground beside the Augusta Canal before the mill did, and it has no manufacturing function at all. That is only the first puzzle. Completed in 1882, the mill wraps an interior typical of any textile mill of its day in an exterior of towers, crenellated rooflines, tall iron finials and colorful cast-iron coats of arms, a style described as both eclectic and neo-Gothic. No one knows why so much effort went into its looks. The King Mill, built shortly afterward, is quite austere.

## Bricks From the Powderworks

The Augusta Canal was greatly enlarged in 1875 to promote industry, and after the Enterprise Mill opened successfully in 1878, the Sibley Manufacturing Company was chartered in 1880. Jones S. Davis, the Enterprise's superintendent and architect, drew plans for a highly ornamental factory 528 feet long, with money raised from investors in Augusta, Savannah, Charleston, New York and Cincinnati. The mill was named for Josiah Sibley, a cotton broker, businessman and civic leader, and it rose on the site of the Powderworks refinery building. Half a million of the old Powderworks' bricks were bought for five dollars per thousand. Josiah's son, William Crapon Sibley, became the company's president. Construction began around June 1, 1880, and a fourth story was added along the way. William's daughter Pearl laid the ceremonial "corner brick" on October 13, 1880, and the last brick on January 27, 1882. At a grand opening on February 22, 1882, water was let into the wheel pit.

## To Be, Rather Than Seem

Two towers form the centerpiece of the facade, one holding the bell and the other a water tank. At the center of each wing hangs the Sibley family's coat of arms, bearing the motto Esse quam videri, "To be, rather than just seem," an odd creed for a building so devoted to appearances. The crenellations continue along the entire roofline and across the office building in front. Behind the ornament, the mill was built to run on water. Turbines in the wheel pit drove mechanical shafting that carried power throughout the building, and the pit was large enough to hold twice the capacity the mill required. The Augusta City Council allowed the mill up to 2,000 horsepower, at an annual water rate of $5.50 per horsepower.

## From the Lowell of the South

The mill opened in a time of overcapacity, and its first results were disappointing. It grew anyway. By 1884 it had 35,136 spindles and 672 looms, and after a fire forced a two-month shutdown, automatic sprinklers were installed. Its cotton consumption climbed from about 2.15 million pounds in 1883 to about 8.5 million pounds in 1894, and by 1895 it ran 40,250 spindles and 1,109 looms. Sibley came to be regarded as well managed and profitable, its reputation enhanced by its striking appearance. After William C. Sibley retired in 1896, that sound management broke down. Sales fell below capacity in 1911. An attempt to recruit immigrant workers from the North failed when most left after only a few days, and in 1914 the mill was criticized for failing to reinvest in new machinery. Its standing in the community slipped as Augusta tried to recast itself from "the Lowell of the South" to "the Garden City of the South" to attract northern winter tourists.

## Denim Until the End

In 1921 the Graniteville Company bought a controlling interest, and by 1940 it had fully absorbed both the Sibley and Enterprise mills. The last seven company-owned houses were sold in 1969. In 1977 the mill was making denim for Levi Strauss, running 32,700 spindles and 634 looms to produce 22 million pounds a year. Carding and spinning ended in 1999, but denim finishing went on until 2006, when the mill shut down completely and all its processing equipment was removed. Foreign competition was cited as one cause. Another was the 2005 Graniteville train derailment disaster, which damaged upstream processing operations in Aiken County, South Carolina.

## The Water Still Turns

One part of Sibley Mill outlived the looms: its hydropower. In 2010 the Augusta Canal Authority bought the idle building and kept its hydropower unit running, while testing and cleaning up the grounds so the site could be approved for redevelopment under Georgia's brownfield program. In 2011 a 77,000-square-foot Kroc Center opened directly across the canal, its architecture designed to complement the old mill. On May 4, 2016, the Canal Authority announced a 75-year lease of Sibley Mill to Cape Augusta, LLC, which planned a technology-oriented redevelopment; a data center plan did not prove feasible, and the Authority and Cape Augusta ended up in court in 2024. Some technology tenants did move into the old mill. On December 23, 2025, the Canal Authority completed the sale of the property for $2 million to CG Sibley, a partnership of Crossgate Partners and Cape Augusta, which plans a mixed-use project with about 215 apartments. In 2018 Cape Augusta's affiliate Augusta Cyberworks also bought the neighboring King Mill, though not the part holding its hydroelectric turbines. The Canal Authority kept the hydroelectric generation rights at Sibley Mill, so the water still turns turbines in the very buildings where cotton once turned into cloth.

## Flight Context

Sibley Mill stands at approximately 33.487°N, 81.992°W on the Augusta Canal, just northwest of downtown Augusta, Georgia. From 1,500–2,500 feet AGL, trace the canal as it runs parallel to the Savannah River; the long, towered mill and its tall front chimney sit on the canal bank, with the Kroc Center directly across the water and the King Mill nearby. Daniel Field (KDNL) is less than 3 nautical miles to the southwest, and Augusta Regional Airport at Bush Field (KAGS) is about 7 nautical miles to the south-southeast. Summer brings haze and afternoon thunderstorms to the Savannah River valley.
