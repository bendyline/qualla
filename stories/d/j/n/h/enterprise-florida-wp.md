# Enterprise, Florida

Once the head of steamboat navigation on the St. Johns River and the county seat of three successive counties, Enterprise hosted presidents at its famous hotel before fever, freezes and the railroad left it a quiet village fighting to keep its name.

**Location:** 28.8692, -81.2667

**Categories:** Unincorporated communities in Volusia County, Florida, Former county seats in Florida, Former municipalities in Florida, St. Johns River, Populated places established in 1843

## Introduction

Only by daylight would a prudent captain take a steamboat up the narrow, crooked upper St. Johns River. Snakes slid aboard from the Spanish moss overhead, and the crew shot alligators before they could foul the paddlewheel. The Darlington left Jacksonville at 8:00 on a Saturday morning, rested in Palatka over Sunday, and cast off again at 5:00 on Monday. That evening, 206 miles from where it started, it tied up at Enterprise, on the northern shore of Lake Monroe, at the head of navigation.

## A Mound of Shells

Long before steamboats, the Mayaca lived here in small villages, collecting snails and shellfish, hunting turtles, deer and alligators, and gathering roots, nuts and berries. Over thousands of years their ancestors' cooking and toolmaking left a shell mound, the Enterprise midden. Pedro Menéndez de Avilés explored the St. Johns in 1565, perhaps as far as Lake Monroe. Disease, forced labor, missionization and slave raids from the Carolinas followed, and by the 1760s the Mayaca and Florida's other native peoples had been devastated. Muscogee (Creek) and other Native peoples from Alabama and Georgia, later called Seminoles, moved into the emptied land. After the United States took Florida in 1821, the government tried to force them west. Early in the Second Seminole War the Seminole burned Palatka, the main river port, and in 1838 the United States built Fort Kingsbury on this shore, across the lake from Fort Mellon at Mellonville, now Sanford.

## An Inn on the Midden

Fort Kingsbury was abandoned after six weeks. In 1841, or 1842 by some accounts, Cornelius Taylor, a former timber agent from San Pablo (now Mayport) and a first cousin of General Zachary Taylor, founded Enterprise at the old fort with a band of other settlers. In 1842 Congress passed the Armed Occupation Act, offering 160 acres to settlers who would clear, cultivate and hold five acres for five years, and Taylor filed for his homestead. He built an inn on top of the shell mound to catch travelers coming upriver by shallow-draft steamboat. Settlers planted orange groves and set up a gristmill and a sawmill to cut Southern live oak, prized by the Navy for warships. In 1843 Enterprise became seat of Mosquito County, which was renamed Orange County in 1845, when the seat moved to Mellonville. An epidemic believed to be smallpox killed Taylor's oldest daughter and nine people he held in slavery in 1842. He left in 1847.

## The Captain's Wharf

Jacob Brock arrived in 1851, bought land a mile west of the first settlement, and laid out a wharf, streets and lots. He was a steamboat captain with "a notable reputation for the lavish and original nature of his profanity," and he had already carried many invalids upriver in search of the climate and the sulfur springs, believed to cure all manner of ailments. In 1854 he finished The Brock House, a hotel for more than 50 guests, and that same year the new Volusia County named Enterprise its seat. Brock ran the first regular passenger steamboat service from Jacksonville to Palatka and extended it here. The Civil War reached his wharf on March 14, 1864, when Union forces seized the Hattie Brock, named for his daughter, on Lake Monroe with 150 bales of cotton aboard. The New York Tribune reported that Miss Brock voiced her grief and indignation from the hotel veranda. The raiders took 2,000 pounds of sugar, demolished the mill two miles downriver, and left with five Black people, two men and three women, who were probably enslaved.

## Presidents on the Veranda

Peace brought tourists, and Enterprise became a fashionable resort for hunters and anglers. "No dreamland on earth," Harriet Beecher Stowe wrote in 1873, "can be more unearthly in its beauty and glory than the St. Johns in April." Sold and renovated in 1876, The Brock House became one of the most famous hotels in Florida, hosting Presidents Ulysses S. Grant and Grover Cleveland, General William Sherman, Jay Gould and members of the Vanderbilt family, along with guests from England, France and South America. The town incorporated in 1877. One guest, the champagne importer Frederick deBary, stayed in 1870 and the next year bought 400 acres to the west, where he built DeBary Hall. He founded the DeBary Merchants Line in 1876 to carry mail from Jacksonville, and helped pay for the Gothic Revival All Saints Episcopal Church, finished in 1883. A railroad arrived in 1885. Then came yellow fever in 1888, the loss of the county seat to DeLand, and the freezes of 1894 and 1895 that wiped out the citrus. Enterprise voted to disincorporate in 1895.

## Holding On to a Name

The shell mound that once appeared on the town seal eventually disappeared, along with the shell paving of its streets and sidewalks. A power plant rose near the lakeshore in 1924, and around the same time the owner, staff and guests of the Benson Springs Hotel petitioned to rename the community after it. The change was never popular. In 1937 Enterprise got its name back, and the decaying hotel was razed to make room for the Florida United Methodist Children's Home. The rail branch closed in the 1950s, and the power plant came down in 2007 and 2008. Today the village works to avoid being absorbed by Deltona. A preservation society formed in 2000, and in 2006 Volusia County designated Enterprise a historic village with strict development guidelines. An old elementary school building, slated for demolition, was moved to Main Street and reopened in October 2014 as the Enterprise Heritage Center & Museum.

## Flight Context

Enterprise lies at 28.8692°N, 81.2667°W on the north shore of Lake Monroe, flanked by the cities of DeBary and Deltona. From 2,000–3,000 feet, Lake Monroe and the St. Johns River are the defining landmarks; Sanford's waterfront faces Enterprise across the lake to the south. Orlando Sanford International Airport (KSFB) is about 7 miles south, just beyond the lake, so expect busy airspace nearby. DeLand Municipal – Sidney H. Taylor Field (KDED) is about 14 miles north. Central Florida summers bring afternoon thunderstorms; winter and spring mornings usually offer the clearest views.
