# DeBary Hall

A champagne importer's 1871 hunting lodge on the St. Johns River, built with comforts few Reconstruction-era Southern homes could match, and home to a family whose American line ended in two young deaths.

**Location:** 28.8747, -81.2972

**Categories:** Historic house museums in Florida, National Register of Historic Places in Volusia County, Florida, Museums in Volusia County, Florida, DeBary, Florida, St. Johns River

## Introduction

Breakfast was served at four-thirty in the morning. Long before the winter sun cleared Lake Monroe, the hunting parties at DeBary Hall were fed and heading out among the live oaks and pines after quail, deer and alligator. This was the retreat Frederick deBary built in 1871 on the St. Johns River in central Florida, a place where guests stayed by personal invitation only. The house still stands at 198 Sunrise Boulevard, in a town that now carries his name.

## A Fortune in Bubbles

Samuel Frederick deBary was born on January 12, 1815, in Frankfurt-am-Main. As a young man he befriended G. H. Mumm, whose family made champagne in Reims, and the Mumms proposed that he carry their wine to America. In 1840, at 25, deBary sailed to New York to sell Mumm's Champagne to the city's new wealthy class of merchants, financiers and manufacturers, men growing rich on a booming port. His firm, Frederick deBary Company, imported Mumm's and nothing else, and because America did not yet make much in the way of luxuries, it prospered. In 1844 he returned to Europe to marry Julie Scherpenhauser, and the couple settled permanently in New York, where they raised two children, Adolphe and Eugenie. Julie died in 1868. Afterward the widower took a steamboat trip on the St. Johns River, and the quiet of the place stayed with him. In January 1871 he bought three parcels from Oliver and Amanda Arnett, land on Lake Monroe shaded by live oaks and green pines.

## Luxury in the Wilderness

The lodge went up during Reconstruction, a lean time for the South, yet it was fitted out like a machine. Its 8,000 square feet held an elevator for guests' luggage, running water fed from a 500-gallon tank in the ceiling, a wired call system reaching every room, and a lightning protection system spread across the roof. Outside stood a water tower and what the source calls Florida's first spring-fed swimming pool, pumped full and drained every day. Such features let the household look after itself on a mostly remote estate, and they are the main reason the site joined the National Register of Historic Places on July 24, 1972. As the guest list grew, so did the house, gaining a second dining room, a wrap-around porch, two more bathrooms and three more guest bedrooms. Visitors may have included Presidents Grant and Cleveland, European royalty and General William Tecumseh Sherman.

## Oranges Rolling Downhill

DeBary could not leave a pleasure unprofitable. He built his holdings to roughly 10,000 acres by some accounts (the county credits the estate with more than 6,000), planted orange groves, and put up a packing house beside the lake pier. He was one of the first growers to sort oranges by size, rolling them down an inclined trough punched with holes of different sizes. He also entered the river trade with the DeBary Merchants Line, whose steamboats ran between Jacksonville and Enterprise, and until 1889 most steamers on the St. Johns were deBary boats. When he died on December 23, 1898, hard freezes had wrecked his citrus and faster railroads were taking the steamboat business. His son Adolphe, 53, stepped into the importing firm and the river line, and into his father's love of horses. He kept no fewer than six in the stable, along with a brougham, a surrey, a phaeton, a brake cart and pony carts for children.

## Two Heirs, Two Sudden Endings

Adolphe outlived all four of his children, and at his death in 1928 the Hall passed to his grandchildren, Leonie deBary Lyon and Adolphe deBary Lyon, still minors, with their father Cecil Lyon guarding the trust. Young Adolphe graduated from Harvard College in 1935. On September 19, 1937, as he was leaving a party with friends, a young woman stepped into the street without seeing an oncoming car. Adolphe rushed out, pushed her clear and was struck himself. He was 23. Leonie then owned the Hall with her husband, Benjamin Brewster, a childhood friend. She remodeled more than any owner before her, adding a tennis court, new floors and a larger master bath, and because the couple loved flying, they built a runway and a hangar on the grounds. On May 9, 1941, they reportedly set out to fly from Long Island to Philadelphia in weather unfit for flight. In blinding rain the plane struck a mountainside, killing both. Leonie was 31 and had no children.

## From Clubhouse to Museum

Leonie's will split her properties between her Lyon cousins and her German deBary relatives, and neither side wanted the Hall. In 1942 a buyer named P. V. Proctor acquired the deBary land and carried off most of the furnishings. The land changed hands again until, in late 1947, a developer turned it into Plantation Estates, a subdivision of small houses with the old Hall as its clubhouse. In 1959 the Florida Federation of Art took the Hall and five acres, including the pool and tennis courts, for one dollar. The state bought the property in 1967, but once the federation moved on, the house stood empty again. A major restoration in the 1990s brought it back, and today Volusia County runs it as a house museum on 10 acres of the original estate. The Hall, an icehouse, two servants' quarters and the restored stables survive, and bike trails lead more than 10 miles to Gemini Springs and Lake Monroe Park.

## Flight Context

DeBary Hall sits at 28.8747°N, 81.2972°W in residential DeBary, just north of Lake Monroe, the broad lake on the St. Johns River that the estate once fronted. From 2,000–3,000 feet the lake is the unmistakable landmark, with the river channel threading in and out of it; look for the wooded 10-acre grounds among the neighborhoods on its northern side. Orlando Sanford International Airport (KSFB) is about 8 miles to the south-southeast, across the lake; DeLand Municipal – Sidney H. Taylor Field (KDED) is about 13 miles north. Expect busy airspace around KSFB and typical central Florida summer afternoon thunderstorms; winter mornings are often the clearest.
