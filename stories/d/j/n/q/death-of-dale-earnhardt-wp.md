# Death of Dale Earnhardt

On the last turn of the last lap of the 2001 Daytona 500, Dale Earnhardt died in a crash while his own team's cars crossed the finish line first and second, and his loss reshaped safety in American stock car racing.

**Location:** 29.1855, -81.0692

**Categories:** Dale Earnhardt, 2001 in NASCAR, Daytona International Speedway, Motorsport safety, 2001 in Florida

## Introduction

In the closing laps of the 2001 Daytona 500, Dale Earnhardt ran third. Ahead of him, fighting for the win, were two cars from his own team: the blue No. 15 driven by Michael Waltrip and the red No. 8 driven by his son, Dale Earnhardt Jr. Behind him, Sterling Marlin kept trying to get by, and Earnhardt kept blocking him. With fewer than two laps left, Fox commentator Darrell Waltrip remarked that Marlin had "beat the front end off of that ol' Dodge" just trying to get around Earnhardt. Moments later, in turn 4 of the final lap, the sport lost one of its most famous drivers.

## A Race Built for Bunching

Earnhardt had not been shy about what he thought of racing at Daytona. NASCAR required carburetor restrictor plates there and at Talladega, and after it tightened rules on springs and shocks in 2000, he told reporters the changes had "killed the racing at Daytona." NASCAR answered with a new aerodynamic package, and in its first outing, at Talladega in the fall of 2000, Earnhardt passed 17 cars in four laps to win. It was the 76th and final victory of his career. The 2001 Daytona 500 was the first 500-mile race at the track under that package, which was designed to keep cars bunched closely together and allow more frequent passing at high speed. Two weeks earlier, for the first time in his career, he had run the Rolex 24 endurance race at Daytona, sharing a car with Dale Jr. and finishing fourth overall. On race morning, he appeared confident and relaxed.

## Lap 173

Earnhardt ran near the front all afternoon. On lap 173, a massive wreck on the back straightaway eliminated 18 cars; Tony Stewart's car was launched airborne and flipped twice before coming to rest in the infield. Earnhardt was among those who escaped, and the race was red-flagged for cleanup. During the pause, according to team owner Richard Childress, Earnhardt made a remark over the radio: "Richard, if they don't do something to these cars, it's gonna end up killing somebody." When racing resumed on lap 180, Waltrip and Dale Jr. were out front, and the lead passed back and forth between them. Earnhardt's Rolex 24 teammate Andy Pilgrim later said that Earnhardt spent those final laps cheering on his two drivers over the radio.

## Turn Four

The crash came in the last turn of the last lap. Earnhardt made light contact with Marlin and slid off course. As he tried to regain control and turned back up the track, his car crossed in front of Ken Schrader's, the two collided and Schrader's car was dragged up the track, and Earnhardt struck the retaining wall head-on at a critical angle. Both cars slid down into the infield grass as Waltrip took the checkered flag, with Dale Jr. second. Schrader climbed out uninjured and went to check on Earnhardt. He pulled down the window net himself, then frantically signaled to the paramedics arriving at the scene. Afterward he chose his words carefully: "I'm not a doctor, I'm telling you it don't look good." Years later he admitted that he had known at once. Earnhardt was taken to Halifax Medical Center, two miles from the speedway, and pronounced dead at 5:16 p.m. He had suffered a basilar skull fracture, determined to have killed him instantly.

## The Search for Blame

One newspaper called it "Black Sunday." In their grief, some fans went looking for someone to blame. Sterling Marlin received hate mail and death threats. Dale Jr. publicly absolved him and asked everyone who loved his father to stop assigning blame. Five days after the crash, NASCAR announced that Earnhardt's left lap belt had broken, and Bill Simpson, whose company made the belt, began receiving death threats too. An independent study by Dr. Barry Myers of Duke University, released on April 10, 2001, concluded that Earnhardt died because his inadequately restrained head and neck snapped forward, regardless of the belt. "The best news I've heard in seven weeks," Simpson said. "I've been living in daily hell." NASCAR's own report that August blamed a combination of factors. Meanwhile, Teresa Earnhardt went to court to keep her husband's autopsy photographs private. Newspapers and a website founder fought in court to see them, and in December 2003 the U.S. Supreme Court declined to hear the last appeal.

## The Legacy in the Walls

Earnhardt's death forced a transformation. On October 17, 2001, NASCAR mandated head-and-neck restraints in its top three series. It went on to require SAFER barriers at tracks hosting its top series and began developing the Car of Tomorrow, whose design drew on research conducted after the crash. Many teams moved from five-point to six-point harnesses. Richard Childress pledged that the black GM Goodwrench car would no longer carry the No. 3 and renumbered the team 29. For more than a decade a small No. 3 decal rode beside that number in Earnhardt's memory, until Childress's team announced in December 2013 that the No. 3 would return to the Cup Series for his grandson, Austin Dillon. At the 2021 Daytona 500, run four days before the 20th anniversary of his death, fans and crews at the speedway stood during lap 3 and held up three fingers in tribute.

## Flight Context

Daytona International Speedway lies on the west side of Daytona Beach, Florida; the final-lap crash site at turn 4 is at approximately 29.1855°N, 81.0692°W. The speedway's huge tri-oval, with its tall grandstands and infield lake, is unmistakable from the air and sits immediately beside Daytona Beach International Airport (KDAB). The area is inside KDAB's Class C airspace, so establish contact with Daytona approach before overflying, and check NOTAMs for temporary flight restrictions during race events. A viewing altitude of 1,500–2,000 feet AGL, as permitted, gives a clear view of the banking and infield. Ormond Beach Municipal Airport (FAA identifier OMN) is about 7 nm to the north. Afternoon sea-breeze thunderstorms are common in summer; February race season usually brings drier, clearer air.
