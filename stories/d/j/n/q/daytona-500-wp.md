# Daytona 500

Born on the sand of Daytona Beach and run every February since 1959, the Daytona 500 opens the NASCAR season with its marquee race, a 500-mile contest of drafting, slingshot passes, heartbreak and legend.

## Introduction

In February 1979, a blizzard kept much of the eastern and midwestern United States indoors, and many of those snowbound viewers turned on CBS, which for the first time was showing the Daytona 500 live from start to finish. On the final lap, leader Donnie Allison tried to block Cale Yarborough's slingshot pass down the backstretch. Yarborough refused to give ground, dropped his left-side tires into the muddy infield grass and lost control; the cars touched again and again, locked together and crashed into the turn three wall. In the grass, the two drivers began to argue. Donnie's brother Bobby pulled over, a fight broke out, and Richard Petty, more than half a lap back, drove through to win. The story made the front page of The New York Times sports section.

## From the Sand to the Speedway

The race grew out of shorter events on the Daytona Beach Road Course, a long loop run partly on the sand and partly on the highway near the beach, where stock cars raced 200 miles. In 1959 the new Daytona International Speedway opened with a 500-mile race of its own, only the second 500-mile NASCAR race after the Southern 500. The track is 2.5 miles around, so a full race takes 200 laps. Lee Petty, patriarch of the racing family, won that first 500 Mile NASCAR International Sweepstakes on February 22, 1959, defeating Johnny Beauchamp. A year later Junior Johnson won in a slower, year-old car by using the draft, then a little-understood phenomenon, in a field of 68 cars, the largest in the race's history. By 1961 people were calling it the Daytona 500, and it has been held every year since.

## The Great American Race

Most sports save their marquee event for the end of the season. NASCAR, unusually, starts with it. Since 1982 the Daytona 500 has opened the Cup Series schedule, and it is regarded as the most important and prestigious race on the calendar, with by far the largest purse, though it awards no more championship points than any other Cup race. Fans call it "The Great American Race" or the "Super Bowl of Stock Car Racing." From 1995 through 2020, its U.S. television ratings were the highest of any auto race of the year, and in 2006 it drew an average live global audience of 20 million. Since 1997 the winner has lifted the Harley J. Earl Trophy in Victory Lane, and the winning car is displayed in race-winning condition for a year at the Daytona 500 Experience next to the speedway.

## Seven Wins and a Slingshot

Richard Petty won it seven times, in 1964, 1966, 1971, 1973, 1974, 1979 and 1981, and remains the only driver to win it in three different decades. In 1968 Cale Yarborough used one out of turn three with five laps to go to beat LeeRoy Yarbrough; the next year Yarbrough, having learned the lesson, did the same thing to Charlie Glotzbach on the final lap. In 1976 Petty was passed by David Pearson on the last lap, and when he tried to get back under him off the final corner, the two cars spun into the infield grass short of the finish line. Petty's car would not restart. Pearson limped across the line to win, in a finish many fans consider the greatest in NASCAR history. Petty survived even his worst day here: in 1988 his car rolled about eight times in the tri-oval, and he walked away.

## Twenty Years of Trying

Dale Earnhardt was a perennial contender at Daytona who, for two decades, could not win its signature race. In 1990 he was leading on the final lap when he ran over a piece of another car's blown engine and blew a tire, handing a stunning upset to Derrike Cope. By 1993 he had led the 500 inside the final ten laps four times without winning, and in 1997 he rolled on the backstretch, then got back in his car and finished 31st. In 1998 he finally won, after "20 years of trying, 20 years of frustration." Three years later, on the final lap of the 2001 race, Earnhardt crashed into the outside wall in turn four and died of a basilar skull fracture, a loss so heavy that the day is still called "Black Sunday." His son, Dale Earnhardt Jr., won the 500 in 2004. On the tenth anniversary of his father's death, the third lap was run in a moment of silence while fans held up three fingers for Earnhardt's car number, and in 2018 Austin Dillon, who had been photographed beside Earnhardt as a child after the 1998 win, took the No. 3 back to Victory Lane.

## Rain, Fire and Photo Finishes

The race has a habit of refusing to end on schedule. In 2012 rain pushed it to a Monday night, the first Daytona 500 run as a night race, and after Juan Pablo Montoya crashed into a jet dryer and set off a fire, it finally ended after midnight on Tuesday. In 2014 heavy rain and a tornado warning stopped the race for a record six hours and 22 minutes. Overtime finishes have stretched it past 500 miles again and again, and in 2023 it ran 212 laps, or 530 miles, the longest ever. Denny Hamlin beat Martin Truex Jr. in 2016 by 0.010 seconds, the closest finish in the race's history. Trevor Bayne won in 2011 at 20 years and one day old, becoming the youngest winner ever. William Byron won in both 2024 and 2025, and in 2026 Tyler Reddick passed Chase Elliott on the final lap to win.

## Flight Context

Daytona International Speedway sits at about 29.186°N, 81.069°W in Daytona Beach, a few miles inland from the Atlantic beaches where stock cars once raced on the sand. From 2,000–3,000 feet the 2.5-mile tri-oval and its grandstands are unmistakable. Daytona Beach International Airport (KDAB) lies immediately next door, less than 1 nm away, so any viewing flight means operating in the airport's airspace: contact air traffic control and check NOTAMs for race-day flight restrictions. Ormond Beach Municipal Airport (KOMN) is about 7 nm to the north. Summer brings frequent afternoon thunderstorms, and February race days have seen their share of rain delays.
