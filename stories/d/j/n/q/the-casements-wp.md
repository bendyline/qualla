# The Casements

John D. Rockefeller spent his last winters at this Ormond Beach mansion, handing out dimes and verse, and the city now keeps it open to everyone.

**Location:** 29.2892, -81.0458

**Categories:** Rockefeller family residences, Houses on the National Register of Historic Places in Volusia County, Florida, Shingle style houses, Ormond Beach, Florida, Shingle style architecture in Florida, Gilded Age mansions

## Introduction

Harvey Firestone once hit such a good golf shot that John D. Rockefeller decided he deserved a reward and handed him a dime. The tire magnate was somewhat embarrassed. It was a very Rockefeller gesture. At The Casements, his winter home in Ormond Beach, the aging oil magnate became known to neighbors and visitors alike for giving away dimes, often with a small poem to go with them.

## Named for Its Windows

The house came before the oil magnate. It was built around 1913 for the Reverend Dr. Harwood Huntington of New Haven, Connecticut, in the Shingle style, and named for its many casement windows, which helped keep the interior cool in Florida's subtropical climate. The setting helped, too. The mansion stands on a barrier island within Ormond Beach, overlooking the Halifax River, a stretch of water that is now part of Florida's Intracoastal Waterway. In 1918, Rockefeller bought the house as his winter residence. He was seventy-eight years old when he moved in.

## Dimes, Golf and Verse

In Ormond Beach, Rockefeller became known for three things: elaborate Christmas parties, a love of golf, and those dimes. Guests who received one also got a poem, believed to have been written by Rockefeller himself: "I was early taught to work as well as play / My life has been one long, happy holiday / Full of work and full of play / I dropped the worry on the way / And God was good to me every day." Famous visitors came through the door over the years, among them the future Edward VIII, Henry Ford and Will Rogers. Rogers could not resist a joke about golf and oil. "I'm glad you won today, Mr. Rockefeller," he once quipped. "The last time you lost, the price of gasoline went up!"

## The Last Winter

Rockefeller died in his sleep at The Casements on the morning of May 23, 1937, at the age of 97. His family sold the house two years later, in 1939 by one account, and it began a very different life. On November 8, 1940, Maud van Woy bought it to establish Casements Junior College and Preparatory School. In 1972 the mansion was placed on the National Register of Historic Places, and the City of Ormond Beach purchased it soon afterward, in 1973 or 1974 depending on the account, for a reported $500,000. The city's ownership turned a private winter retreat into a public place, and in 2009 the house reportedly went through a $1.1 million renovation.

## Open to All Comers

Today The Casements serves as a cultural center and park, owned by the city rather than a single wealthy family. Its gardens are an invitation to linger, with citrus trees, a grand promenade, small streams crossed by little bridges, and seasonal flower displays that change through the year. Standing on the lawn above the Halifax River, it is easy to imagine an old man pressing a dime into a visitor's palm, and just as easy to see how far the house has traveled from that private world to a public one.

## Flight Context

The Casements stands at 29.2892°N, 81.0458°W on the barrier island in Ormond Beach, on the east bank of the Halifax River. From 1,500–2,500 feet, follow the Halifax River north from Daytona Beach; the mansion's grounds sit right on the riverfront, with the Atlantic beaches just to the east. Ormond Beach Municipal (KOMN) is about 3.5 nm west-northwest, Daytona Beach International (KDAB) about 6.5 nm south, and Flagler Executive (KFIN) about 14 nm northwest. The area lies under or near Daytona's Class C airspace, so contact approach before overflying. Summer afternoons often bring sea-breeze thunderstorms.
