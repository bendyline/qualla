# Museum of Arts and Sciences (Daytona Beach)

Born in a Quonset hut in 1955, Daytona Beach's Museum of Arts and Sciences holds a giant ground sloth, Cuban art, Coca-Cola history, a planetarium and a preserve of old-growth hammock, and in 2026 it closed to daily visitors to be rebuilt as The Brown.

## Introduction

The Museum of Arts and Sciences started out in a Quonset hut. In 1954, a Daytona Beach committee announced plans for a children's museum for the Halifax area, and the local Board of Public Instruction lent it a converted hut at the Mary Karl Vocational School, on what is now the campus of Daytona State College. Today the institution that grew from that hut spreads across a wooded preserve and holds a prehistoric giant, a planetarium and a pair of private railroad cars. Since March 29, 2026, however, the museum, which now calls itself the Cici and Hyatt Brown Museum of Art, Science and History ("The Brown"), has been closed to daily visitors while it prepares an expansion, with only select planetarium events continuing and no firm reopening date from the museum (reports point to 2028 or 2029), so check with the museum before you go.

## Out of the Hut

The Halifax Children's Museum was incorporated as a nonprofit in 1955. Six years later, in 1961, it took the name Museum of Arts and Sciences, known locally as MOAS, and widened its mission to teach science and history to adults as well as children. The broader ambition needed more room than a Quonset hut could offer, so in 1964 the museum moved to a new building in Daytona's Tuscawilla Preserve. Before the 2026 closure the main building held ten permanent exhibits on Florida's natural history, African and Cuban culture, Florida art and science, while roughly half of what visitors saw changed every few months, much of it curated in-house. The museum even has its own theater, Root Hall, with seats for 265.

## A Sloth, Cuban Art and a Coca-Cola Bottle

The collections swing wildly in subject, and that is part of the fun, though the galleries are off view while the museum is closed. In the Florida fossil gallery stands the skeleton of an Eremotherium, a giant ground sloth, described as among the most complete of its kind. Elsewhere hangs one of the most significant collections of Cuban fine and folk art outside Cuba, along with a gallery of Chinese art, international decorative arts and Early American furniture. Then there is the Root Family Museum, built from the lifetime collection of Chapman Root of the Root Glass Company. It gathers Americana of every scale: two private rail cars, the Skytop Lounge "Dell Rapids" and the observation dome car "Silver Holly," Indy race cars the family sponsored, and one of the largest Coca-Cola memorabilia collections in the world.

## Watching the Moon Shots

On October 11, 1972, the new MOAS Planetarium opened to the public. One of its earliest shows, "Trip to the Moon," ran during Apollo 17, the last crewed mission of the Apollo program, which launched that December just an hour south of Daytona. Working with the Volusia County School Board, the planetarium became a classroom under the dome for local students, and it has tracked the Space Coast's missions ever since, from Pioneer 10's flyby of Jupiter and the building of Skylab to the International Space Station and NASA's Artemis program. It was rebuilt in 2014 with a fully digital projection system and exhibit space, and in 2019 it was renamed the Lowell and Nancy Lohman Family Planetarium.

## Florida in Paint

The campus kept growing. On November 21, 2008, MOAS opened the Charles and Linda Williams Children's Museum, billed as the first hands-on science center in Volusia County, a space of roughly 9,300 square feet with exhibits where kids can build their own vehicles for a raceway, try out doctor and radiologist stations, peer through a video light microscope or run a make-believe pizza parlor. On May 7, 2012, the museum announced a $13 million gift for the Cici and Hyatt Brown Museum of Art, which opened in February 2015 as the permanent home of more than 2,600 paintings of Florida from the Browns' collection, a display the museum calls the largest collection of Florida art in the world.

## Wild Florida Next Door

Step outside and the museum becomes a nature walk. The Tuscawilla Preserve is about 62 acres of protected land in the middle of Daytona Beach, with more than half a mile of boardwalks and nature trails (the boardwalk is currently closed for redesign) through virgin Florida coastal hydric hammock, habitat for endangered plants and animals. The Klancke Environmental Education Complex opened there in 2005. Farther afield, MOAS has looked after Gamble Place, a historic district within the Spruce Creek Preserve that it restored with the Nature Conservancy and the City of Port Orange. The park has trails through five different ecosystems, shelters many endangered and threatened species, and tells its past through three historic house museums.

## Flight Context

The Museum of Arts and Sciences sits at 29.1918°N, 81.0372°W in Daytona Beach, within the green block of the Tuscawilla Preserve west of the Halifax River. It lies barely 1.3 nm east-northeast of Daytona Beach International (KDAB), so it is inside Daytona's Class C airspace and close to the airport's approach paths; contact approach and expect to view it only at pattern-compatible altitudes. Daytona International Speedway, beside the airport, is an easy landmark, and the Atlantic beaches lie a couple of miles east. Ormond Beach Municipal (KOMN) is about 8 nm north-northwest and New Smyrna Beach Municipal (KEVB) about 9 nm south-southeast. Summer thunderstorms build most afternoons.
