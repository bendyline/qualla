# Landing Zones 1 and 2

On the site of a demolished Cape Canaveral launch complex, two painted circles caught SpaceX's returning boosters for a decade, from the first Falcon 9 landing in 2015 to the retirement of the first pad in 2025.

**Location:** 28.4858, -80.5444

**Categories:** SpaceX facilities, Cape Canaveral Space Force Station, Buildings and structures completed in 2015, SpaceX related lists

## Introduction

On December 21, 2015, a Falcon 9 first stage called B1019 came back. It had just helped launch Falcon 9 flight 20 from a pad a few miles up the coast, and now it descended onto a circle marked with a stylized X on the Cape Canaveral shore and touched down. It was the first landing at what SpaceX called Landing Zone 1, and for the next decade this patch of old launch ground would be where rockets came home.

## An X Where a Launch Pad Stood

The site had a past. SpaceX leased the land in February 2015 on the grounds of the former Launch Complex 13, which was demolished and replaced with circular landing pads, the original one 282 feet across, each painted with the stylized X from the SpaceX logo. By March 2, 2015, the Air Force's LC-13 sign had briefly given way to one reading "Landing Complex," and before the first touchdown the name changed again to Landing Zone. One photograph taken there puts a single person in the middle of the main pad, and the tiny figure makes the scale of the target plain.

## Seven Tries Before Success

Nothing about that first landing was routine. SpaceX had already run seven landing tests: five ended with intentional descents into the open ocean, and two more were failed attempts to land on an ocean-going platform. With approval from the FAA, flight 20 became the eighth controlled-descent test and the first to succeed at the complex. Even then, Elon Musk was cautious. In January 2016 he estimated that roughly 70 percent of the year's landing attempts would succeed, hoped for 90 percent in 2017, and warned that the company expected a few more failures. The ground landings kept coming. A second booster touched down shortly after midnight local time on July 18, 2016, after the CRS-9 launch, and a third, from CRS-10, landed on February 19, 2017.

## Two Boosters at Once

Falcon Heavy needed more room. In July 2016, SpaceX applied to build additional pads for the heavy rocket's boosters, and in May 2017 construction began on a second, smaller pad about 1,017 feet northwest of the first. By June 2017 it had been coated with radar-reflective paint to sharpen landing precision. Landing Zone 2 made its debut on February 6, 2018, on Falcon Heavy's maiden launch, when the rocket's two side boosters came down at the same time, one on each pad. Ordinary Falcon 9 boosters usually stuck to LZ-1. The first one to land on LZ-2 did so only on December 11, 2022, after the Hakuto-R Mission 1 launch, and only because LZ-1 was still occupied by a booster from a OneWeb mission three days earlier.

## Last Touchdowns

The end came in stages. At a press conference ahead of the Crew-11 mission, William Gerstenmaier announced that booster B1094's return would be the last routine use of Landing Zone 1, and on August 1, 2025, it made that landing. SpaceX's lease on the Launch Complex 13 site had expired at the end of July, and the ground was passing to joint use by Vaya Space and Phantom Space as a reactivated Space Launch Complex 13. Landing Zone 2 kept catching boosters a little longer, including Falcon 9 boosters through the NROL-77 mission on December 9, 2025, and it was still in use in 2026: after Falcon Heavy launched NASA's Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope on August 30, 2026, its two side boosters landed at Landing Zone 2 and at Landing Zone 40, a new pad inside Space Launch Complex 40 that received its first booster in February 2026. Returning boosters now have somewhere else to land: SpaceX is moving its landings inside its own launch complexes, with Landing Zone 40 in service and two more pads planned at Launch Complex 39A.

## Flight Context

Landing Zones 1 and 2 sit at 28.4858°N, 80.5444°W near the Atlantic shore of Cape Canaveral Space Force Station, on the site of the old Launch Complex 13. From 2,000–4,000 feet, look for two large circular pads about 1,000 feet apart in the coastal scrub, close to the eastern tip of the cape. The Cape Canaveral Skid Strip (KXMR) is less than 2 nm to the southwest, the NASA Shuttle Landing Facility (KTTS) about 11 nm northwest, Space Coast Regional (KTIX) about 14 nm west, and Patrick Space Force Base (KCOF) about 15 nm south-southwest. The Cape's restricted airspace is activated for launches and recoveries; check NOTAMs before flying nearby. Summer afternoons bring frequent thunderstorms, so mornings give the clearest views.
