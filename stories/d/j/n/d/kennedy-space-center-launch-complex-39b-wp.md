# Kennedy Space Center Launch Complex 39B

Built for the Saturn V but home to just one, Pad 39B has launched Apollo, Skylab, Challenger's final flight and, in 2026, the first crew to leave low Earth orbit since 1972.

**Location:** 28.6272, -80.6209

**Categories:** 1969 establishments in Florida, Apollo program, Artemis program, Constellation program, Kennedy Space Center launch sites, Space Shuttle program

## Introduction

Pad 39B was built for the Saturn V, then the most powerful rocket the United States had, and only one Saturn V ever left it. That was SA-505, which launched Apollo 10 in May 1969, the pad's very first flight. Every other rocket to rise from this stretch of Merritt Island shoreline was something else: a smaller Saturn, a Space Shuttle, a prototype for a cancelled program, and eventually the Space Launch System that has carried NASA's astronauts back toward the Moon.

## A Pad for the Moon

The pad exists because of a deadline. In 1961 President Kennedy proposed to Congress that the nation land a man on the Moon by the end of the decade, and the Apollo program that followed demanded a vast expansion of NASA's operations, pushing launches from the Cape onto adjacent Merritt Island to the north and west. Launch Complex 39B, the second of Launch Complex 39's three pads, was designed alongside 39A to handle the Saturn V, the rocket that would propel Apollo spacecraft to the Moon. Apollo 10 gave the pad its debut in May 1969. It would never host another Saturn V.

## The Milk Stool

After Apollo 17 in 1972, the pad found a second job with a smaller rocket. The Saturn IB pads at Cape Canaveral, Complexes 34 and 37, had been decommissioned, so NASA adapted 39B instead. Engineers modified the Mobile Launchers with a "milk-stool" extension platform on the launch pedestal, lifting the shorter Saturn IB high enough that the swing arms built for a Saturn V could still reach its S-IVB upper stage and Apollo spacecraft. From that perch, 39B launched all three crewed Skylab flights and the Apollo–Soyuz mission. A 1975 photograph shows a Saturn IB standing on the milk stool, waiting to fly the Apollo–Soyuz Test Project.

## Challenger and the Shuttle Years

When the Space Shuttle arrived in the early 1980s, both pads were rebuilt around it, but 39A handled every Shuttle launch until January 1986. The first Shuttle to launch from 39B was Challenger, on mission STS-51-L. About a minute into the flight, Challenger was destroyed and its crew was killed. The pad went on to host 53 Shuttle launches in all, the last of them Discovery's STS-116 in December 2006, after which the program's remaining flights left from 39A. Pad 39B still had one quiet Shuttle role to play. In May 2009, when the final Shuttle mission to the Hubble Space Telescope launched from 39A, Endeavour stood on 39B, ready to launch the STS-400 rescue mission if it was needed.

## A Clean Pad Again

In October 2009, a prototype Ares I rose from 39B on the Ares I-X test flight, part of the Constellation program that was cancelled the following year. NASA then removed the pad's Fixed Service Structure, returning it to an Apollo-style "clean pad" for the first time since 1977. The idea is flexibility: rockets now arrive with their service structures on the mobile launcher rather than relying on towers fixed to the pad. From the Shuttle era, only the liquid hydrogen, liquid oxygen and sound-suppression water tanks remain. In 2014 NASA offered the pad to commercial users when the Space Launch System did not need it, and Orbital ATK planned to fly its OmegA rocket here. Northrop Grumman absorbed Orbital ATK in 2018 and cancelled OmegA in September 2020 before it ever launched.

## Back Toward the Moon

By 2019 the pad had sprouted new lightning towers and was holding the SLS mobile launcher. On November 16, 2022, at 06:47:44 UTC, the Space Launch System lifted off from 39B on Artemis 1. The next flight carried people. Artemis II launched from the pad on April 1, 2026, with Reid Wiseman, Victor Glover, Christina Koch and Canadian astronaut Jeremy Hansen aboard, becoming the first crewed flight beyond low Earth orbit since Apollo 17 in 1972. The crew flew farther from Earth than anyone before them, beating Apollo 13's distance record, and splashed down in the Pacific on April 10. Fifty-seven years after Apollo 10, the pad built for the Moon had sent a crew that way again.

## Flight Context

Launch Complex 39B stands at 28.6272°N, 80.6209°W on the Atlantic side of Merritt Island at NASA's Kennedy Space Center. From 3,000–6,000 feet, its tall lightning towers make it easy to pick out; Pad 39A lies roughly 3 km to the southeast along the same shoreline, and the crawlerway runs inland toward the Vehicle Assembly Building about 7 km to the south-southwest. The NASA Shuttle Landing Facility (KTTS) is about 4 nm west, the Cape Canaveral Skid Strip (KXMR) about 10 nm south-southeast, and Space Coast Regional (KTIX) about 12 nm southwest. Airspace over the Kennedy Space Center is restricted, and temporary flight restrictions surround launches; check NOTAMs before planning any sightseeing flight. Summer afternoons frequently bring thunderstorms.
