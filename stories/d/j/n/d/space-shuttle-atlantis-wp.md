# Space Shuttle Atlantis

Space Shuttle Atlantis flew 33 missions, from a narrow escape in 1988 to Mir, the ISS, Hubble and the program's final flight, and now rests at Kennedy Space Center tilted at a countdown-inspired 43.21 degrees.

**Location:** 28.5249, -80.6803

**Categories:** Space Shuttle orbiters, Crewed spacecraft, Kennedy Space Center, Museum exhibits in Florida, Individual aircraft

## Introduction

Space Shuttle Atlantis hangs in its hall at the Kennedy Space Center Visitor Complex with its payload bay doors flung open, tilted at exactly 43.21 degrees. The number is a tribute to the countdown that preceded every shuttle launch from KSC, and behind the orbiter a digital screen backdrop of Earth from orbit makes it look as if Atlantis were still in space. It earned the pose. Over 33 missions between 1985 and 2011, Atlantis circled the planet 4,848 times, covering more than 525 times the distance from the Earth to the Moon, and it flew the final mission of the entire Space Shuttle program.

## Named for a Research Ship

Rockwell International built Atlantis in Southern California, and the orbiter, designated OV-104, was delivered to Kennedy Space Center in April 1985. It was the fourth operational shuttle and the second-to-last one built. Its name honored RV Atlantis, a two-masted sailing ship that served as the primary research vessel of the Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution from 1930 to 1966.

Atlantis went to work quickly. Its maiden flight, STS-51-J, launched on October 3, 1985, as only the second shuttle mission dedicated to the Department of Defense. Just 50 days after STS-51-J landed, it launched again on STS-61-B, the second shuttle night launch and the fastest turnaround any of the five flown orbiters ever achieved. Then the Challenger disaster in 1986 grounded the entire fleet.

## Lights and Shadows

Atlantis's closest call came in 1988. During the launch of STS-27, a classified flight, a piece of insulation shed from the right solid rocket booster struck the orbiter's underside, severely damaging more than 700 tiles and knocking one off entirely. The crew used the robotic arm to inspect the right wing and found substantial damage, but because the mission was classified, the only images sent to Mission Control were encrypted and of extremely poor quality. Controllers judged the damage to be "lights and shadows" and told the crew to carry on, infuriating many of them.

Atlantis came home as the most heavily damaged shuttle ever to land successfully. The crew's survival is attributed to a steel L-band antenna plate that happened to sit directly beneath the missing tile. A similar situation, this time involving the more critical reinforced carbon-carbon, would later lead to the Columbia disaster in 2003.

## Probes, Telescopes and Mir

Between 1988 and 1992 Atlantis flew ten missions. In 1989 it sent the Magellan probe to Venus on STS-30, becoming the first shuttle to launch an interplanetary probe, and later that year dispatched Galileo toward Jupiter on STS-34. In 1991 it deployed the Compton Gamma Ray Observatory.

Beginning with STS-71 in 1995, Atlantis made seven straight flights to the Russian space station Mir. That first docking marked the 100th U.S. crewed spaceflight, the first joint shuttle-Mir operations and the first swap of crew members in orbit. Locked together, Atlantis and Mir formed the largest spacecraft in orbit at the time. Later, as the International Space Station took shape, Atlantis delivered some of its most important pieces: the Destiny laboratory in February 2001, on a flight that included NASA's 100th spacewalk, and the Quest Joint Airlock that July, which let station crews make spacewalks in either American or Russian suits.

## The Workhorse's Last Decade

Atlantis's first flight after the loss of Columbia was STS-115, to the space station in September 2006, carrying new truss segments and solar arrays. Its longest flight, STS-117 in June 2007, lasted almost 14 days, and in February 2008 it delivered Columbus, the European Space Agency's largest single contribution to the ISS. In May 2009 Atlantis carried seven astronauts to the Hubble Space Telescope for its final servicing mission, where they spent 37 hours over five spacewalks installing new cameras, batteries and a gyroscope.

The orbiter was once slated for early retirement in 2008, to serve as a source of spare parts. Instead it kept flying, and in 2009 shuttle launch director Mike Leinbach praised its ground team for setting a record low of just 54 interim problem reports. Atlantis flew what was meant to be its last mission, STS-132, in May 2010, but when the program was extended into 2011, it was chosen for one more.

## Wheels Stop

STS-135, the final mission of the Space Shuttle program, lifted off on July 8, 2011, and Atlantis landed at Kennedy Space Center for the last time on July 21. Over its career, about 200 crew members flew aboard it. On April 12, 2011, the 30th anniversary of the first shuttle flight, NASA Administrator Charles Bolden had already announced its future home: "here at the Kennedy Space Center where every shuttle mission and so many other historic human space flights have originated, we'll showcase my old friend, Atlantis." Ground was broken on the exhibit building in 2012, and Atlantis went on display on June 29, 2013.

## Flight Context

Atlantis is displayed near 28.525°N, 80.680°W at the Kennedy Space Center Visitor Complex in eastern Florida, about 7 miles east of Space Coast Regional Airport. View it from 3,000–4,000 feet and only from outside restricted areas. Airspace around Kennedy Space Center and Cape Canaveral can be restricted, and launch operations bring temporary flight restrictions, so check charts and NOTAMs carefully. Nearby airports: Space Coast Regional Airport (KTIX) about 7 miles west, the Space Florida Launch and Landing Facility (KTTS) about 6 miles north, and Merritt Island Airport (KCOI) about 13 miles south. Afternoon thunderstorms are common in summer.
