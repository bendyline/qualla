# Cape Canaveral Space Launch Complex 40

The Cape Canaveral pad that sent Cassini toward Saturn on a Titan IV was torn down, rebuilt by SpaceX, nearly destroyed in 2016, and reborn as a weekly Falcon 9 workhorse.

**Location:** 28.5619, -80.5772

**Categories:** Cape Canaveral Space Force Station, Launch complexes of the United States Space Force, SpaceX facilities, 1965 establishments in Florida

## Introduction

On October 15, 1997, a Titan IV-B rose from this pad carrying Cassini-Huygens, bound for Saturn and its moons. It was the only civilian launch a Titan IV ever made. A quarter-century later, the same ground was sending up a Falcon 9 as often as once a week, most of them loaded with Starlink satellites. Space Launch Complex 40, nicknamed "Slick Forty," has lived two very different lives, and the break between them was total: the old towers came down before the new era could begin.

## Built for Titans

The Air Force built Launch Complex 40 as one of two pads at the Integrate-Transfer-Launch Complex, alongside neighboring Launch Complex 41, to fly Titan III rockets with strap-on solid boosters. The first launch came on June 18, 1965, a Titan IIIC carrying a mass simulator to test its Transtage upper stage. Titan IIICs flew through the 1960s and 1970s, and in the 1980s the pad was retrofitted for the Titan 34D. In the 1990s Martin Marietta and the Air Force upgraded it for the Commercial Titan III, a rocket that never found much success. Its launches here included Intelsat 603 in 1990, which suffered a partial failure and was later repaired by Space Shuttle Endeavour, and Mars Observer in 1992, which fell silent before it could enter orbit around Mars. The Titan IV carried the pad into the 2000s, flying Cassini and, finally, the Lacrosse-5 reconnaissance satellite at the end of April 2005.

## Clearing the Ground

With the Titans retired, the Air Force leased the pad to SpaceX in April 2007 for a rocket that had not yet flown, the Falcon 9. The old Titan tower was dismantled in early 2008, and the Mobile Service Structure came down in a controlled demolition later that year. SpaceX built a hangar for preparing rockets and payloads, installed new fuel tanks, and bought from NASA a spherical liquid oxygen tank that had once served Launch Complex 34. The first Falcon 9 launch from the pad came in June 2010 with a dummy payload, and a Dragon spacecraft demonstration followed that December. From 2012, SLC-40 became the primary launch site for Dragon cargo flights to and from the International Space Station, a job the Space Shuttle had done until its retirement in 2011.

## The Fireball of 2016

In September 2016, while being fueled for a static fire test, a Falcon 9 exploded on the pad, destroying the rocket and its payload, the AMOS-6 satellite. The blast badly damaged the launch complex. After a thorough investigation and cleanup, repairs and upgrades did not begin until early 2017. SpaceX was not grounded in Florida during that time, because it had already leased Launch Complex 39A at the nearby Kennedy Space Center in April 2014, and launches shifted there. Slick Forty finally returned to service in December 2017, with the successful launch of CRS-13.

## The Workhorse Gets a Tower

In the 2020s, SLC-40 settled into its role as SpaceX's workhorse, handling less complex satellite launches as often as every week and logging 50 launches in 2023 alone. Most of its missions have served the Starlink constellation, and by September 2026 the pad had hosted more than 340 Falcon 9 launches. One thing it lacked was an access tower, so once the original Dragon capsule retired in 2020, Dragon missions stopped flying from here. SpaceX began building one at SLC-40 in early 2023, and by early 2024 it was being used to load late cargo into spacecraft. Its first crewed launch, Crew-9, followed in September 2024. That flight had been planned for 39A, but delays caused by trouble with Boeing's Starliner Calypso, docked at the station, would have collided with 39A's conversion for the Falcon Heavy that was set to launch NASA's Europa Clipper in October. In February 2026, after the Crew-12 launch, a booster made the first landing at Landing Zone 40, a new landing zone inside the complex built to replace Landing Zones 1 and 2. In August 2026 the pad hosted SpaceX's last planned Falcon 9 Starlink launch from Florida, as the company shifts future Florida Starlink flights to Starship, while Falcon 9 and Falcon Heavy missions continue.

## Flight Context

SLC-40 lies at 28.5619°N, 80.5772°W on the Atlantic shore of Cape Canaveral Space Force Station, with its twin, SLC-41, just to the north along the beach. From 3,000–5,000 feet, look for the pad's towers and the large processing hangar set back from the dunes, with the Banana River beyond to the west. The Cape Canaveral Skid Strip (KXMR) is about 6 nm south, the NASA Shuttle Landing Facility (KTTS) about 7 nm west-northwest, and Space Coast Regional (KTIX) about 12 nm west-southwest. Because this pad launches so often, restricted airspace over the Cape is activated frequently; check NOTAMs and launch schedules before flying near it. Summer afternoons bring frequent thunderstorms, so mornings offer the clearest views.
