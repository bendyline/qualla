# Wekiwa Springs State Park

Just north of Orlando, a 72-degree spring pours about 42 million gallons a day into the Wekiva River, feeding a state park of more than 7,000 acres that shelters black bears, bobcats and two snails found nowhere else.

**Location:** 28.7125, -81.4596

## Introduction

Two of the rarest residents of Wekiwa Springs are small enough to overlook. The Wekiwa hydrobe and the Wekiwa siltsnail live in these springs and nowhere else, and both are described as critically imperiled. Two more snails, the smooth rib hydrobe and the armored siltsnail, are found in only three springs each, and this is one of them. All around those tiny lives, a spring of crystal-clear water wells up at a steady 72°F, feeding the headwaters of the Wekiva River at the heart of a state park of more than 7,000 acres in Apopka, Florida, just north of Orlando.

## Two Spellings, One Spring

Visitors notice the name problem quickly. The springs are spelled Wekiwa, but the river, along with most subdivisions and streets in Seminole County, uses Wekiva. Wekiwa is the Creek word for a spring. Contrary to a popular belief that Wekiva means "flowing water," that spelling was actually an alternate one used by developers.

Whatever the spelling, the water is the point. The spring supplies roughly 42 million gallons a day to the Wekiva River, and it holds its temperature at 72°F all year. The park gives access to Wekiwa Springs, Wekiwa Springs Run, Rock Springs Run and the Wekiva River itself.

## From Turpentine Camp to State Park

In the 1800s, Central Florida was mostly agricultural. After the Civil War, a tourist trade grew up around Florida's temperate winters, long summers and natural beauty, and Wekiwa Springs emerged from that growth. Part of the land had an industrial past. The Wilson Cypress Company kept a small turpentine camp on what is now parkland, and in 1941 the Apopka Sportsmen's Club bought the property from the company and maintained it for recreation.

One of the club's co-owners fought to protect it. John H. Land, the mayor of Apopka, spent three years campaigning the Florida Legislature to preserve the land. By 1969 the state was interested in making it a state park, and since 1970, visitors have come for the natural spring, its crystal-clear water and the area's wildlife.

## Into the Clear Water

The spring is the park's main attraction. Its swimming area ranges from under a foot to five feet deep, and at the source a small cavern opens into a cave that has been explored in great detail, to a distance of about 50 meters. That exploration is over for visitors: scuba and cave diving are strictly prohibited, though snorkeling and fishing are allowed.

Most people explore on the surface instead. Canoes and kayaks can be rented at the nature center, and some backcountry campsites in the park can be reached only by canoe or kayak. On shore, picnic pavilions come with charcoal grills, electricity and water, and the campgrounds support everything from tents to recreational vehicles, including family, primitive and youth cabin areas. The full-facility campground has 60 campsites.

## Bears, Bobcats and Long Trails

Beyond the spring, the land stays largely undeveloped, acres of untouched Florida ecology. Coyotes, rabbits, deer, gray foxes, bobcats, raccoons, opossums, alligators and black bears all live in the park. A network of trails is open to bicycling, hiking and horseback riding. The main hiking trail runs 13.5 miles, the 5.3-mile Volksmarch trail is marked with orange diamonds, and another trail links the main parking area to a distant lot at Sand Lake inside the park.

The park also works as a base for its keepers. It has served as headquarters for one of the state's AmeriCorps Florida State Parks chapters, and like other Florida state parks, it is open from 8 a.m. until sundown every day of the year.

## Flight Context

Wekiwa Springs State Park surrounds the spring at about 28.71°N, 81.46°W in Apopka, Florida, north of Orlando, where the Wekiva River begins. From 2,000–3,000 feet, look for the largely undeveloped, wooded parkland and the river corridor running out of the spring. Orlando Sanford International Airport (KSFB) is about 14 statute miles east-northeast, Orlando Executive Airport (KORL) about 14 miles south-southeast, and Leesburg International Airport (KLEE) about 22 miles west-northwest. The park lies north of the Orlando Class B airspace's core, but check sectional charts for its outer shelves. Summer afternoon thunderstorms are common; mornings offer the calmest air.
