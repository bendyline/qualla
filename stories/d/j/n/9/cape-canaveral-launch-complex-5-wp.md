# Cape Canaveral Launch Complex 5

In five busy years, this Cape Canaveral pad went from launching Army missiles to sending the first two Americans into space, and its original consoles are still there.

**Location:** 28.4394, -80.5733

**Categories:** Cape Canaveral Space Force Station, Project Mercury

## Introduction

Two dates bracket the working life of Launch Complex 5. The first launch, on July 19, 1956, was a Jupiter-A flown by the United States Army. The last, on July 21, 1961, carried Gus Grissom into space in a capsule called Liberty Bell 7. In those five years, this patch of Cape Canaveral went from military test range to the place where America first sent people into space. No rocket has left the pad since.

## Twenty-Three Countdowns

LC-5 hosted 23 launches in all, and the list reads like a family tree of early American rocketry: one Jupiter-A, six Jupiter intermediate-range ballistic missiles, a single Jupiter-C, four Juno Is, four Juno IIs and seven Redstones. Until October 1958, every one of them was an Army operation. After that, NASA did the flying. A photograph from May 16, 1958, shows Army troops preparing the first PGM-11 Redstone for its launch the following day. Within three years, a Redstone from this same pad would carry an astronaut. The pad was never a lonely one, either. LC-5 shared its blockhouse, the control building for its countdowns, with neighboring Launch Complex 6.

## Before Shepard, Ham

Project Mercury's road to a crewed flight ran straight through LC-5. The first attempt, Mercury-Redstone 1, ended in a pad abort, and Mercury-Redstone 1A followed. In January 1961, Mercury-Redstone 2 lifted off with a chimpanzee named Ham aboard, flying the route months before any astronaut did. Then, at 9:34 a.m. Eastern time on May 5, 1961, Mercury-Redstone 3 rose from this pad carrying Alan Shepard in the capsule Freedom 7. The flight lasted fifteen minutes and twenty-eight seconds and climbed to about 116 statute miles. Shepard splashed down in the North Atlantic, where the USS Lake Champlain recovered both him and his spacecraft. It was a suborbital arc rather than an orbit, but it made Shepard the first American in space.

## The Capsule That Sank

Eleven weeks later, Gus Grissom rode Mercury-Redstone 4 from the same pad. His flight in Liberty Bell 7 lasted fifteen minutes and thirty-seven seconds, and the trouble came only after it was over. As the spacecraft bobbed in the ocean, its hatch cover blew away and salt water splashed inside. Flooded, the capsule weighed more than 5,000 pounds, about 1,000 pounds beyond what the recovery helicopter could lift, and it went under. Grissom was rescued, but Liberty Bell 7 stayed on the floor of the Atlantic for nearly four decades, until Oceaneering International lifted it onto the deck of a recovery ship on July 20, 1999. That July 1961 flight was also LC-5's final launch. After twenty-three countdowns, the pad went quiet.

## Consoles in the Blockhouse

Quiet is not the same as forgotten. LC-5 sits beside the Cape Canaveral Space Force Museum, which is based at Launch Complex 26, and the old LC-5 and LC-6 blockhouse is now part of that museum. Inside, the original launch consoles and computers remain on display. Photographs from 2010 show the blockhouse, its firing button, and a display Redstone standing on the pad outside. Seeing it takes some planning. The station is a working military facility, so the museum and its grounds can be visited only on a certified tour, and the four-hour Premier Tour from Canaveral Tours advertises stepping inside the LC-5 blockhouse.

## Flight Context

Launch Complex 5 sits at 28.4394°N, 80.5733°W on Cape Canaveral, the low cape that juts into the Atlantic on Florida's Space Coast. From 3,000–5,000 feet the historic pads dot the flat coastal scrub near the shoreline, with Port Canaveral's harbor to the south and the Banana River to the west. The Cape Canaveral Space Force Station Skid Strip (KXMR) is under 2 nm to the north-northeast; the NASA Shuttle Landing Facility (KTTS) is about 12 nm north-northwest, Space Coast Regional (KTIX) about 13 nm west-northwest, and Melbourne Orlando International (KMLB) about 21 nm south. Airspace over the Cape is frequently restricted for launch operations, so check NOTAMs and restricted areas before planning a pass. Sea breezes often build afternoon cumulus along this coast in summer; mornings usually offer the clearest views.
