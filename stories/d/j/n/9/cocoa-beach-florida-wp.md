# Cocoa Beach, Florida

A barrier-island town once called Oceanus, remade by a space-age population boom and now defined by surfing, festivals and a 45-foot height cap.

**Location:** 28.3311, -80.6269

## Introduction

Cocoa Beach's most famous fictional resident is a genie. The 1960s sitcom I Dream of Jeannie was set here, yet no episode was ever filmed in town, and its star, Barbara Eden, visited only twice during production, both times in 1969 and both for publicity. The fiction was borrowed. The boom behind it was real: in a single decade, the space program drove a population surge of more than 1,000 percent and remade this strip of Atlantic sand as a space-age beach town.

## A Place Called Oceanus

The first non-native settlement here was made by a family freed from slavery, who settled after the American Civil War. In 1888 a group of men from nearby Cocoa bought the entire tract, then left it undeveloped for thirty-five years. In 1923 one of them, Cocoa's city attorney Gus Edwards, bought out the others. His holdings came to roughly 600 acres, and he gave up practicing law to pour all his effort into developing them. Back then the area went by Oceanus. On June 5, 1925, it became the Town of Cocoa Beach, and on July 27 the new town held its first official meeting at the Cocoa Beach Casino, where it adopted a city seal. Edwards was elected mayor.

## Lights Out on the Shore

Civic life stayed small for years. In 1938 the town appointed a deputy marshal "to act in emergencies at night or at other times," for twenty-five cents an hour. World War II then arrived at the surf line. On May 1, 1942, the German submarine U-109 torpedoed the La Paz off Cocoa Beach, and local boys were recruited to help with salvage and to clear the debris that came ashore. Soon afterward the federal government recognized that lights along the coast were silhouetting passing ships and making them easy targets, and it ordered a blackout for the rest of the war. Money was scarce, too. Through the war years the town struggled to pay its employees and to repair its roads.

## The Space Age Moves In

Between 1950 and 1960, Cocoa Beach's population grew from 246 to 3,475, an increase of more than 1,000 percent, mainly because of the U.S. space program. NASA's Kennedy Space Center lies about 15 miles north, and newcomers arrived for space jobs and fresh opportunities. In September 1959 the city voted to add sidewalks, improve streets and pave more roads. After crewed flights, the town threw parades for the astronauts. Ron Jon, the New Jersey surf shop, opened a store here in the early 1960s, and the pier, originally called the Cape Canaveral Pier, went up in 1962. In 1965 the high school asked that Cocoa Avenue be renamed Minutemen Boulevard after its mascot. The boom had a hard edge, though. When the Apollo program ended and the Space Shuttle was not yet in full swing, layoffs rippled through the local economy, and in 1975 unemployment reached 14.3 percent.

## Surf Town Rules

Today the beach itself is the draw. Ron Jon Surf Shop was welcoming 2 million visitors a year as of 2006, and the city is home to the East Coast Surfing Hall of Fame. An annual Easter Surfing Festival began in 1964 and is estimated to draw 100,000 spectators, while around 10,000 people turn out each Christmas for the Surfing Santas. Labor Day weekend has brought the NKF Rich Salick Pro-Am Surf Festival, a benefit for the National Kidney Foundation of Florida and one of the largest charity surfing festivals, since the mid-1980s. Eleven-time world champion Kelly Slater is among the notable people linked to the city. Popularity has a price. An estimated 2.4 million day trippers a year compete for fewer than 2,400 downtown parking spaces near the beach. In the early 2000s, voters capped new buildings at 45 feet, though earlier construction and later variances left about 80 buildings between 45 and 70 feet tall as of 2018.

## Land Made From River Mud

On a map, Cocoa Beach is more water than land. Of its 15 square miles, 10.1 are water, about two-thirds of the total. The city runs along 5.6 miles of Atlantic oceanfront, with Cape Canaveral to the north, Crescent Beach to the south and the Banana River to the west. Centuries ago a powerful hurricane drove the ocean through the barrier islands and created the Thousand Islands in the Banana River. People have been reshaping the water ever since. Boating channels are dredged and numbered, from the 100 Channel to the 200 Channel for houseboats and on up to the 600, and many homes stand on mud and sand dredged from the river. The city maintains 37 canals totaling 9 miles, plus 17 miles of channels. Summers are hot and humid, with July highs averaging 91°F and frequent thundershowers, while January highs settle at a mild 72°F.

## Flight Context

Cocoa Beach is centered near 28.3311°N, 80.6269°W on the barrier island between the Atlantic and the Banana River. From 2,000–4,000 feet, look for the pier reaching into the surf, State Road 520 crossing the Banana River from Merritt Island, the scattered Thousand Islands in the river just west of town, and the launch complexes of Cape Canaveral on the horizon to the north. Patrick Space Force Base (KCOF) lies about 6 nm south, Merritt Island Airport (KCOI) about 3 nm west-northwest, the Cape Canaveral Skid Strip (KXMR) about 9 nm north-northeast, Melbourne Orlando International (KMLB) about 14 nm south, and Space Coast Regional (KTIX) about 14 nm northwest. Expect military and launch-related airspace restrictions nearby; check NOTAMs. Summer afternoons bring frequent thunderstorms, so morning flights usually offer the clearest views.
