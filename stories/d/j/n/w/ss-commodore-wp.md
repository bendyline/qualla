# SS Commodore

The gun-running steamer Commodore sank off Florida on January 2, 1897, and one of the men who fought through the surf to Daytona Beach was Stephen Crane, who turned the ordeal into "The Open Boat."

**Location:** 29.2038, -80.7740

## Introduction

"None of them knew the color of the sky." So begins "The Open Boat," Stephen Crane's story of four men in a dinghy fighting their way toward the Florida shore. Crane knew the scene firsthand. He was one of those four men, a war correspondent for the Bacheller-Johnson syndicate, and the ship that left them adrift was the steamer Commodore, which went down off this coast on January 2, 1897. Its remains still lie on the seafloor offshore.

## A Bad Start From Jacksonville

The Commodore sailed from Jacksonville on New Year's Eve, 1896, carrying 27 or 28 men and a cargo of supplies and ammunition for Cuban rebels. It barely got going. Less than two miles out on the St. Johns River, it struck a sandbar in dense fog and damaged its hull. The ship was towed off the next day, only to be beached again at Mayport and damaged further. That evening a leak opened in the boiler room, and with the water pumps malfunctioning, the Commodore came to a standstill about 16 miles from Mosquito Inlet, now Ponce de León Inlet. As the water rose, Crane described the engine room as resembling "a scene at this time taken from the middle kitchen of Hades."

## Ten Feet of Boat

In the early hours of January 2, 1897, the crew lowered the lifeboats, and at 7 a.m. the Commodore sank. Crane was among the last to leave, climbing into a ten-foot dinghy with three other men, including the captain, Edward Murphy. For a day and a half they struggled off the Florida coast before trying to bring the little boat in at Daytona Beach. It overturned in the surf, and the exhausted men had to swim for shore. Not all of them made it. Billie Higgins, the ship's oiler, died in that last stretch of water. News of the disaster ran on front pages across the country, along with rumors that the ship had been sabotaged. Those rumors were widely repeated but never substantiated.

## From Headline to Classic

Crane wrote about the sinking twice. His newspaper account, "Stephen Crane's Own Story," appeared in the New York Press on January 7, 1897. Then, in June of that year, Scribner's Magazine published "The Open Boat," and the story became a classic. In it the men are known by their roles: the correspondent, standing in for Crane himself, the captain, the cook and the oiler. Only one of them is given a name. It is Billie Higgins, the oiler who did not survive the surf, and whose name the story has carried to readers ever since.

## Proving the Wreck

One question lingered: were the remains off this coast really the Commodore? Between 2002 and 2004, three seasons of archaeological work set out to document the exposed wreckage and settle the question. In May 2002, over two weeks, graduate student Kimberly Eslinger Faulk of East Carolina University's Maritime Studies Program, technical divers from the Cambrian Foundation, representatives of the Ponce Inlet lighthouse and videographer Rick Allen of Nautilus Productions carried out an archaeological survey of the site. On April 7, 2004, divers from the Lighthouse Archaeological Maritime Program at the St. Augustine Lighthouse and Museum measured the engine remains: a 26-inch bore and a 30-inch stroke. Those numbers matched the single-expansion steam engine built by the Neafie and Levy yard in Philadelphia, as recorded when the Commodore was first registered in 1882. The archaeologists judged the identification beyond reasonable doubt.

## Flight Context

The wreck of the Commodore lies offshore at about 29.2038°N, 80.7740°W, roughly 11 nm northeast of Ponce de León Inlet and about 12 nm east of the Daytona Beach shoreline. Nothing is visible from the air, but the setting is: from 2,000–4,000 feet over open water, look west to the long, straight line of Daytona Beach, where Crane and his companions came ashore, and southwest to the inlet and its tall lighthouse. Daytona Beach International (KDAB) is about 15 nm west and New Smyrna Beach Municipal (KEVB) about 13 nm southwest. Stay within gliding distance of shore in a single-engine aircraft, and watch for Daytona's Class C airspace near the coast. Summer sea-breeze storms form over land in the afternoon, leaving mornings clearest offshore.
