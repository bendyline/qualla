# New Smyrna Beach, Florida

A surf town that calls itself a swan among ducks began as one of the most ambitious and troubled colonies in British Florida, and now shares its waves with more sharks than almost anywhere on Earth.

**Location:** 29.0306, -80.9253

**Categories:** Cities in Volusia County, Florida, Greek-American culture in Florida, Populated coastal places in Florida on the Atlantic Ocean, Beaches of Florida, Populated places established in 1768

## Introduction

The city motto is Latin, cygnus inter anates: "a swan among ducks." Its other title is less elegant. Experts at the University of Florida have given Volusia County the "dubious distinction" of being the world's "shark-bite capital," and New Smyrna Beach sits at the heart of that reputation. Yet surfers keep paddling out, and National Geographic is credited with naming New Smyrna one of the world's top 20 surf towns in 2012. Between the dunes and the downtown on the Indian River lies a history older and stranger than the beach life suggests.

## A Greek Name on a British Coast

Europeans first settled here in 1768, when the Scottish physician Andrew Turnbull, a friend of East Florida's British governor James Grant, founded the colony of New Smyrna. Turnbull had married Gracia Dura Bin, the daughter of a Greek merchant in London who came from the Ottoman city of Smyrna, today's İzmir in Turkey, and he named the settlement after it. He recruited roughly 1,400 settlers to grow hemp, sugarcane and indigo and to make rum. Most came from Menorca in the Balearic Islands and spoke Catalan, while others were Greeks from the Mani peninsula. Few ventures in North America had tried to settle so many people at once in a single town. For its first few years the colony turned out relatively large amounts of processed indigo.

## Seventy Miles North

The venture could not hold. Insect-borne disease swept through the settlement, raids by Native peoples added to the losses, and Turnbull and his overseers mistreated the colonists until tensions broke the colony apart. About 600 survivors walked nearly 70 miles north along the King's Road to St. Augustine, where their descendants still live. Turnbull eventually retired to Charleston, South Carolina. The Greeks among those settlers are honored as the first colony of Greek people in America, and today the St. Photios Greek Orthodox National Shrine on St. George Street in St. Augustine tells the story of their hardship and their contributions to the city. Like St. Augustine, New Smyrna has lived under British, Spanish, United States and Confederate rule, returning with Florida to the Union after the Civil War.

## Gunboats, Railroads and Rum Runners

Through much of the 19th century few white settlers lived in central Florida, and the Seminole defended their territory; the Army fought them through the Seminole Wars but never fully dislodged them. On March 23, 1862, part of the 3rd Florida Infantry Regiment defeated a small U.S. naval force trying to land near New Smyrna, and in 1863 Union gunboats shelled the town's Stone Wharf. When New Smyrna incorporated in 1887 it had just 150 residents. Henry Flagler's Florida East Coast Railway arrived in 1892, and the population climbed quickly as tourism joined citrus and commercial fishing. During Prohibition, moonshine stills hid on the river islands while rum runners slipped in from the Bahamas through Mosquito Inlet, now Ponce de León Inlet. In 1947 the city annexed seaside Coronado Beach and became New Smyrna Beach.

## Fins Among the Surfboards

According to the International Shark Attack File at the University of Florida, Volusia County recorded more confirmed shark bites in 2007 than any other region in the world, and the trend continued into 2008. On September 18, 2016, sharks bit three different surfers at the same beach within a few hours. In July 2024, two men and a 14-year-old boy were bitten in three separate incidents in less than a week, the first two at New Smyrna Beach and the boy's at nearby Ponce Inlet. Numbers like these explain the nickname, but they come with an important footnote: very few of these bites prove fatal. One Orlando Sentinel photographer even caught a four-foot spinner shark leaping over a surfer, a literal reversal of "jumping the shark."

## Roosters, Artists and Storms

Downtown, roosters strut along Flagler Avenue, a favorite with tourists. They are thought to have been abandoned by locals, since residents may keep only hens. The city has been named one of "America's Top Small Cities for the Arts," and it hosts the Atlantic Center for the Arts, an artists-in-residence community, along with the Little Theatre and the Harris House. The New Smyrna Speedway, a half-mile paved track opened in 1967, still draws stock car racing. Storms have left their marks. Hurricane Charley crossed Florida from Punta Gorda and exited over New Smyrna Beach on August 13, 2004, damaging the beachside and toppling many historic oaks downtown and along Flagler Avenue. In 2022 Hurricane Ian dropped almost 21 inches of rain on the town and forced about 180 residents to evacuate rising floodwaters, and Hurricane Milton in 2024 caused heavy damage across Volusia County. By the 2020 census, 30,142 people called the swan among ducks home.

## Flight Context

New Smyrna Beach lies at 29.0306°N, 80.9253°W on Florida's central Atlantic coast. From 2,500–3,500 feet, look for the Indian River lagoon separating the downtown on the mainland from the barrier island beach, with the Coronado Beach Bridge crossing the Intracoastal Waterway just south of Ponce de León Inlet, the break in the barrier island to the north. New Smyrna Beach Municipal Airport (KEVB) is about 2 miles northwest of the city center; Daytona Beach International (KDAB) is about 13 miles northwest, and Massey Ranch Airpark (FAA identifier X50) is close by to the south. Summer afternoon thunderstorms are common, and hurricane season runs June through November; winters bring frequent sunny, dry days.
