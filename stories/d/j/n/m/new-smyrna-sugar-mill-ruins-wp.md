# New Smyrna Sugar Mill Ruins

Long mistaken for a Spanish mission that never existed, these coquina walls are the remains of an 1830 steam sugar mill worked by enslaved people and burned on Christmas Day 1835.

**Location:** 29.0089, -80.9411

## Introduction

For years, people believed these weathered stone walls in New Smyrna Beach were the ruins of the Mission of Atocuimi, a Spanish mission to the Timucua. There was never any such mission. The arches and chimney belong to something far less romantic and far more revealing: a steam-powered sugar mill built in 1830, run on the labor of enslaved people, and burned just five years later in the opening days of the Second Seminole War. The myth has proved stubborn, still repeated by the misinformed, but the real story is written into the stone.

## Walls Made of Shells

In 1830, a steam-operated sugarcane mill and a sawmill rose on this site. Their masonry buildings were made of coquina, a sedimentary rock composed of tiny fossilized mollusk shells, quarried nearby. The centerpiece was a crushing house with a chimney and large arched doors and window openings. Inside stood steam-driven grinding machinery, built by the West Point Foundry of Cold Spring, New York, that crushed the sugarcane to extract its juice. It was an ambitious industrial operation for its time and place, and it was built as a speculation. William Kemble contracted to build it for William DePeyster and Henry Cruger, merchant speculators from New York, which is why the site is also known as the Cruger and DePeyster Sugar Mill.

## Land, Money and Forced Labor

Henry Cruger's wife, Eliza Cruger, along with outside investors, financed the mills on six hundred acres near New Smyrna. Cruger had bought the land from Ambrose Hull, an Episcopal minister who received it as a grant from the Spanish crown during Florida's Second Spanish period. Before that, it had been part of the original grant the British made to Andrew Turnbull during their twenty-year occupation of Florida. The work itself was done by enslaved people, together with draft animals, under the management of the plantation overseer, John Dwight Sheldon. Every stage of turning cane into sugar here depended on people held in bondage, whose forced labor made the investors' speculation possible.

## Christmas Day, 1835

The Second Seminole War began in late December 1835. It grew out of a treaty calling for the Seminoles to move west, which most Seminoles rejected, and in its opening phase sugar plantations along the Atlantic coast south of St. Augustine were destroyed. This one fell on Christmas Day. Sheldon and his family fled across the Halifax River to the mainland; accounts differ on whether the enslaved people living on the plantation went with them, and many enslaved people on these coastal plantations joined the Seminoles. A band of Seminoles then pillaged the plantation, and that night they set the sugar mill and other buildings on fire. Only the walls survived. The machinery inside was left mostly undamaged, and it was later removed and installed at the Dunlawton Sugar Mill. Soldiers garrisoned here afterward to keep watch on the Seminoles altered the site further.

## What the Ruins Keep

Today the ruins stand at 600 Old Mission Road, one mile west of the Intracoastal Waterway, and were added to the National Register of Historic Places on August 12, 1970. The site came under the Florida Park Service's administration as a historic site, and with that came a correction. Today Volusia County lists it among its historical parks, free and open daily from sunrise to sunset. The walls were never a mission. They are the shell of a short-lived industrial venture, built of coquina and powered by steam but ultimately dependent on the people forced to work it.

## Flight Context

The New Smyrna Sugar Mill Ruins lie at 29.0089°N, 80.9411°W in New Smyrna Beach, about a mile west of the Intracoastal Waterway. The ruins are small and hard to pick out from the air, so use the Intracoastal Waterway and the barrier-island beaches to the east as references, with Ponce de Leon Inlet a few miles to the north. New Smyrna Beach Municipal (KEVB) is about 3 nm north, Daytona Beach International (KDAB) about 12 nm north-northwest, and DeLand Municipal (KDED) about 18 nm west. A low pass at 1,500–2,500 feet over the town gives the best perspective; keep clear of the Daytona Class C airspace to the northwest. Summer afternoons bring frequent thunderstorms.
