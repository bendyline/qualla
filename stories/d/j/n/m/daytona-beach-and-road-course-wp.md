# Daytona Beach and Road Course

Half highway, half hard-packed sand, the Daytona beach course hosted land speed records and chaotic stock car races, and it helped give birth to NASCAR.

## Introduction

When the ticket-takers arrived for the race on March 8, 1936, they found thousands of fans already at the track. It was an early sign of how badly things would go. The sandy turns became virtually impassable, scoring disputes and protests piled up, and officials stopped the event after 75 of its 78 laps. The city of Daytona Beach lost a reported $22,000. Yet out of that mess, and the races that followed on this strip of highway and sand, came the National Association for Stock Car Auto Racing.

## Highway Out, Beach Back

The course was half road and half shoreline. It began on the pavement of State Road A1A, at what is now 4511 South Atlantic Avenue in Ponce Inlet, where a restaurant called "Racing's North Turn" stands today. Drivers ran south on A1A, parallel to the ocean, to the end of the road, swung onto the beach at the south turn by the Beach Street approach, and roared back north on the sand before rejoining the highway at the north turn. Early laps measured 3.2 miles, and in the late 1940s the course was stretched to 4.2. Local racer Sig Haugdahl, who organized that first chaotic 1936 race for the city, is credited with designing the track.

## Two Hundred Miles an Hour on Sand

Before stock cars, the beach was famous for raw speed. Fifteen world land speed records were set on the Daytona Beach and Road Course. On March 29, 1927, Major Henry Segrave drove his Sunbeam 1000 hp Mystery to a record 203.79 miles per hour, touching a top speed of 211. Speed never entirely left the sand, either. Even after road racing ended, Daytona Speed Week carried on along the beach through 1961, with time and distance record attempts over the standing mile and flying mile in multiple classes. Chrysler's 1960 300G Specials are reputedly among the fastest stock cars ever timed on the beach.

## The Newcomer From Washington

William France Sr., a Washington, D.C., resident who knew Daytona's racing history, moved here in 1935 to escape the Great Depression and opened a car repair shop. He drove in the 1936 race and finished fifth, behind winner Milt Marion, whom the sanctioning AAA declared victor despite protests from second-place Ben Shaw and third-place Tommy Elmore. Rather than give up, Haugdahl and France persuaded the Daytona Beach Elks Club to host another race in 1937. It went better but still lost money, and in 1938 France took over running the course himself, staging two events that year as the finances improved.

## Talks at the Streamline

Drivers of the era were frequently cheated by unscrupulous promoters who left events with all the money before anyone got paid. On December 14, 1947, France began talks at the Ebony Bar of the Streamline Hotel, and those talks ended with the formation of NASCAR on February 21, 1948. The beach course was central from the start. At the first NASCAR event, a modified-division race held here on February 15, 1948, Red Byron beat Marshall Teague. When the Strictly Stock series began in 1949, its second race came to the beach in July, and Byron won again. From 1950, the race moved to February, a timing that became a lasting tradition.

## Crowds Outgrow the Sand

Success created its own problem. Hotels were rising all along the beachfront, and France knew the large crowds needed a permanent racetrack. He negotiated with the city of Daytona Beach for a site near the Daytona airport, arranged financing, and in 1957 construction began on Daytona International Speedway, a 2.5-mile tri-oval with steep banking that allowed higher speeds. The beach course held its last race in 1958, won by Paul Goldsmith. The next year, in 1959, the first Daytona 500 ran at the new superspeedway, and Daytona's racing moved inland for good.
