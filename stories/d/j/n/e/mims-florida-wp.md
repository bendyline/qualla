# Mims, Florida

A citrus-and-railroad crossroads on Florida's Space Coast that gave up being a city in the Depression, and the home of civil rights pioneers Harry and Harriette Moore.

**Location:** 28.6686, -80.8481

**Categories:** Census-designated places in Brevard County, Florida, Census-designated places in Florida, Populated places on the Intracoastal Waterway in Florida, Former municipalities in Florida

## Introduction

Mims takes its name from three brothers, Britton J., Robert and Casper Neil Mims, among its earliest settlers, who were reputedly distant relatives of Jesse James. Around 1876, Casper opened the first Mims general store. The family story may be the most colorful thing about the town's founding, but it is not the most important story Mims has to tell. That belongs to a teacher and his wife who lived here for decades and refused to accept the rules of a segregated Florida.

## Citrus by Rail

The railroad made Mims. Railroad building along the Indian River began in the 1880s, with Henry Flagler's Florida East Coast line following in the early 1890s, and the community grew along with it. By 1893, with the line in place, Mims and neighboring Titusville were shipping citrus and lumber to cities in the North, and by the end of the nineteenth century the citrus groves had come to dominate the local economy. The settlement organized itself as a city in the early 1900s and grew slowly from there. Then the Great Depression hit. In its first years, large numbers of residents reportedly left to look for work elsewhere, and Mims, unable to keep a municipal government running, reverted to an unincorporated township within Brevard County. It has remained unincorporated ever since, a community rather than a city.

## The Moores of Mims

Harry T. Moore and his family lived in Mims from at least the 1920s. He was a teacher and the founder of the Brevard County NAACP, and he became a civil rights leader known across the nation. His wife, Harriette, was an educator and civil rights worker in her own right. In 1946 both of them lost their teaching jobs because of their activism; Harry had been pushing for equal pay for Black teachers in Brevard County's segregated schools. He also led the Progressive Voters League, and between 1944 and 1950 he helped raise Black voter registration in Florida to 31 percent of those eligible, markedly higher than in any other Southern state.

## Christmas, 1951

At Christmas in 1951, on the Moores' 25th wedding anniversary, a bomb exploded beneath their home in Mims. Harry was killed. Harriette was gravely injured and died on January 3, 1952. Both of their daughters survived. The murders were racially motivated and are believed to have been carried out by members of the Ku Klux Klan. The FBI investigated from 1951 to 1955, the county and state took up the case again in 1978 and 1991, and the Florida attorney general's office reinvestigated from 2004, implicating four Klansmen, all long dead, in 2006. No one was ever charged. Mims and Brevard County have not let the couple be forgotten. Several sites are dedicated to them, among them a memorial park and cultural center that the county created at their home site in Mims by 2004.

## A Crossroads Today

About 7,336 people lived in Mims at the 2020 census, spread across roughly 58 square kilometers, nearly a quarter of it water. The community sits on the Intracoastal Waterway and marks the eastern end of State Road 46, which meets Interstate 95 at exit 223 and ends at U.S. 1, where West Main Street becomes East Main Street. It is the first major junction east of Seminole County. Wikipedia's list of notable residents includes NFL player Dick Yelvington, who was born in Mims, and Melanie L. Campbell, president and CEO of the National Coalition on Black Civic Participation.

## Flight Context

Mims is centered near 28.6686°N, 80.8481°W on the mainland shore of the Indian River, just north of Titusville. From 2,000–4,000 feet, trace Interstate 95 and U.S. 1 running north-south through town, with State Road 46 heading west toward Seminole County; across the Indian River to the east, the Kennedy Space Center and its Vehicle Assembly Building are visible on Merritt Island in clear weather. Arthur Dunn Airpark (X21) in Titusville is about 3 nm south-southeast, Space Coast Regional (KTIX) about 10 nm south-southeast, and Orlando Sanford International (KSFB) about 22 nm west-northwest. Stay clear of the restricted airspace over Kennedy Space Center to the east and check NOTAMs on launch days. Summer afternoons bring frequent thunderstorms.
