# Horizons (Epcot)

Epcot's Horizons carried riders through imagined 21st-century homes on land, under the sea and in space, then vanished in 2000, leaving behind a planter and a trail of tributes.

**Location:** 28.3739, -81.5467

**Categories:** Epcot, Dark rides, Former Walt Disney Parks and Resorts attractions, Future World (Epcot), Audio-Animatronic attractions, Buildings and structures demolished in 2000

## Introduction

Just west of Epcot's Mission: Space stands a concrete planter with an oddly familiar outline. It marks the spot where the main sign for Horizons once stood, and its shape echoes the distinctive building that once stood here. Horizons opened on October 1, 1983, carrying guests through visions of how people might live in the 21st century: on land, under the sea and in outer space. It closed for good in 1999, and a year later the building was gone. The future it imagined is now the present, and the ride has become a piece of the past.

## Century 3, Futureprobe, Horizons

The idea began with General Electric, whose then-CEO Reginald Jones and future CEO Jack Welch first proposed an attraction about Thomas Edison and the company's origins. That became a story about America's future, then shifted again to suit EPCOT Center's global audience. Show designer George McGinnis presented early concepts in 1979.

The name changed nearly as often. In the concept phase it was Century 3, honoring America's third century from 1976 to 2076, but planners worried international guests would not understand it. Futureprobe replaced it, until the medical connotations of "probe" killed that too. After much debate, GE and Disney settled on Horizons. Before construction began, the budget was cut by $10 million, the building was reduced and the ride shrank by 35 percent, losing 600 feet of track. Site work started on August 5, 1981, and construction of the building, designed to resemble a spaceship and give the impression of an infinite horizon, began in January 1982.

## The Family From the Carousel

Horizons was proposed as a sequel to the Carousel of Progress, the ride Disney created for GE's pavilion at the 1964 New York World's Fair and now in Magic Kingdom's Tomorrowland, where it closed in July 2026 for an update expected to reopen in 2027. The Carousel follows a family through the changes of the 20th century; Horizons continued their story into the 21st. The link was audible. In the ride's Looking Back at Tomorrow section, a television in an Art Deco scene played "There's a Great Big Beautiful Tomorrow," sung by Larry Cedar, the exact recording heard on a radio in the Carousel's long-running 1990s version.

Riders traveled in a suspended omnimover through a dark ride that was the only Future World attraction to showcase all of the section's themes: communication, energy, transportation and anatomy, along with humanity's relationship with the sea and the land. Its imagined communities carried names like Mesa Verde, Sea Castle and the Brava Centauri space colony, and the finale included an ending sequence for each.

## Losing the Sponsor

In 1993, GE announced it would not renew its sponsorship, which expired that September 30, and every reference to the company was removed. Without a sponsor, the ride began to deteriorate. Several animatronic figures wore out, breakdowns became frequent, and ideas floated for turning the building into a space-themed pavilion with individual harnesses that let guests control their own pitch and yaw.

In December 1994, Horizons closed with no explanation. It reopened in December 1995, because World of Motion was about to close for its conversion into Test Track and Universe of Energy was being refurbished, which would otherwise have left Wonders of Life as the only attraction open in the east half of Future World. Horizons stayed running until Test Track was ready to open, and closed permanently on January 9, 1999. No reason was ever publicly given. The loss of corporate sponsorship, around the time of Euro Disneyland's financial troubles, is widely believed to have been the main factor, and structural and roof problems were also rumored.

## Echoes in Other Rides

Demolition began in the spring of 2000, and by late July the building was down. On the same site, Mission: Space opened in 2003, with its grand opening on October 9, and it carries several tributes to its predecessor: the Horizons logo at the center of the gravity wheel in the queue, a stylized version on the gift shop counter and, since a 2017 refurbishment, an entrance mural showing Brava Centauri orbiting Earth.

Other parks remember it too. Space Mountain's post-show includes a bag stamped "Mesa Verde," an undersea scene and a robot butler before a futuristic skyline, all recalling Horizons. At Tokyo Disneyland, a kiosk outside Star Tours plays the full 31-second Mesa Verde, Brava Centauri and Sea Castle finale sequences, unedited, as advertisements for other tours. The ride's exit corridor originally featured The Prologue and the Promise, a mural by the renowned space artist Robert T. McCall.

## Flight Context

The Horizons site, now occupied by Mission: Space, lies near 28.374°N, 81.547°W on the east side of Epcot at Walt Disney World, southwest of Orlando, Florida. From 3,000–4,000 feet, use Epcot's 180-foot geodesic sphere, Spaceship Earth, as the reference point. Check current NOTAMs and charts for flight restrictions over the Walt Disney World parks and stay outside any restricted area. Nearby airports: Kissimmee Gateway Airport (KISM) about 9 miles southeast, Orlando International Airport (KMCO) about 15 miles east-northeast, and Orlando Executive Airport (KORL) about 18 miles northeast. Summer afternoon thunderstorms are frequent over central Florida.
