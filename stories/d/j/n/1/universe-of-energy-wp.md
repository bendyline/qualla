# Universe of Energy

For 35 years, Epcot's solar-roofed Universe of Energy carried whole theaters on battery power through a smoking, swampy world of Audio-Animatronic dinosaurs, first as a serious 1982 showcase and later as a Jeopardy!-themed comedy starring Ellen DeGeneres and Bill Nye.

## Introduction

It began with the seats. Guests filed into what looked like an ordinary theater at Epcot, and then the entire seating area swung 180 degrees to face three enormous screens. After a short film about the birth of fossil fuels, the audience turned again, a curtain lifted, and the theater broke apart into six vehicles that rolled into a primeval jungle. The air smelled of swamp. A Brontosaurus family waded nearby, a Stegosaurus battled an Allosaurus on a cliff overhead, and Pteranodons perched on an erupting volcano that came with a smell of its own. This was the Universe of Energy, one of the attractions that opened with Epcot in 1982.

## A Roof That Drank the Sun

The pavilion was itself a demonstration of its subject. Its roof carried 80,000 photovoltaic solar cells that partially powered the ride vehicles, large battery-powered "traveling theatre cars" that followed guide-wires buried in the floor instead of running on tracks. Exxon sponsored it from the start. The pre-show was an eight-minute film on the forms of energy found in nature, projected onto a Kinetic Mosaic designed by the Czech film director Emil Radok: 100 rotating, prism-shaped flip screens arranged 25 across and four high. Each had two white projection faces and one matte black face, and as a computer turned them in time with five synchronized projectors, the images rippled and sometimes seemed to leap into three dimensions. After the dinosaurs came a twelve-minute live-action film on current and future energy resources around the world, and then a two-minute animated finale reflected off mirrored walls.

## Ellen Behind the Rocks

In January 1996 the pavilion closed for a makeover, but filming problems delayed the new show. With World of Motion already closed for its conversion into Test Track and Horizons running inconsistently, Epcot faced a summer with little open in its eastern Future World. So from June 14 to September 2, 1996, the Universe of Energy reopened in a strange half-finished state. The original films ran, but the Kinetic Mosaic was gone and the finale's mirrors had already been removed. New figures had to be hidden. An Audio-Animatronic Ellen DeGeneres stood in the diorama behind temporary rockwork, and the Elasmosaurus, already reprogrammed for the new show, lunged awkwardly at rocks instead of at the passing vehicles. On September 15 the new version opened as Ellen's Energy Crisis and, for reasons that remain unknown, was immediately renamed Ellen's Energy Adventure.

## Jeopardy! in the Jurassic

The new show turned the lesson into a comedy. In the pre-show, Ellen settles in to watch Jeopardy! and is unenthused to see her college rival, Judy Peterson, played by Jamie Lee Curtis, who knows a lot about energy, on the show. Her neighbor, Bill Nye the Science Guy, tries to explain why energy matters, but Ellen naps instead and dreams she is a contestant, facing Judy and Albert Einstein in categories that are all about energy. Bill Nye rescues her by taking her back to the Big Bang and into a jungle 220 million years in the past, where the dinosaurs, now repainted in much brighter colors, were waiting. One Brontosaurus sneezed water on riders, and Ellen's figure fought off the Elasmosaurus with a tree branch until it stopped working and was removed after November 2014. In the Final Jeopardy! round, asked what energy will never run out, Ellen answers "brain power" and wins with $35,600. In 2011 this version surpassed the original as the longest-running version of the attraction.

## Changing Colors

The building changed with its shows. The 1996 refurbishment repainted the exterior in pastel rainbow tones, and ExxonMobil, as the sponsor had become, put its new name on the marquees in 2001, only to end its 22-year sponsorship in 2004, after which every reference to the company was stripped from the signage and show. A lengthy refurbishment beginning in 2008 refreshed the diorama scenes, upgraded the audio and computer systems, repainted the traveling theaters from purple to light blue, and restored the exterior to its original 1982 reds, oranges and yellows. The pavilion reopened on March 28, 2009. But its popularity had been dwindling for years, and on July 15, 2017, Disney confirmed that it would close and give way to a Guardians of the Galaxy attraction.

## Out of Power

The final day came on August 13, 2017, and the last show ended the way no one would have scripted it. The attraction broke down mid-show, and one of the vehicles in a pavilion built to celebrate energy ran out of battery. Guests had to be evacuated. Cast members at first reassured them they would get to ride again and let some linger in the primeval diorama to take photos with the dinosaurs, but the breakdown proved too complex, and no one got another ride. For an attraction that had opened under a roof of 80,000 solar cells, it was a fittingly ironic farewell. The show building lives on, reused for Guardians of the Galaxy: Cosmic Rewind.

## Flight Context

The former Universe of Energy pavilion, now the show building for Guardians of the Galaxy: Cosmic Rewind, sits at about 28.375°N, 81.548°W on the eastern side of Epcot's former Future World (now World Discovery), at the Walt Disney World Resort in Bay Lake, southwest of Orlando. Walt Disney World lies under a permanent FAA flight restriction, so view the resort only from outside the restricted area and check current NOTAMs before planning a nearby route. Kissimmee Gateway Airport (KISM) lies about 8 nm to the southeast and Orlando International (KMCO) about 13 nm to the east. Central Florida summers bring near-daily afternoon thunderstorms; mornings and the drier winter months are clearer.
