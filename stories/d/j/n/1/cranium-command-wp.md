# Cranium Command

Epcot's Cranium Command put audiences inside a 12-year-old boy's head, launched the directors of Beauty and the Beast, and helped inspire Pixar's Inside Out.

**Location:** 28.3748, -81.5465

**Categories:** Epcot, Former Walt Disney Parks and Resorts attractions, Audio-Animatronic attractions, Future World (Epcot), Amusement rides introduced in 1989, Amusement rides closed in 2007

## Introduction

The show was supposed to teach you how to manage stress, so its creators decided the host should scream at you. "We thought it would be funny," animator Kirk Wise explained, "if the character who was supposed to teach you how to manage your stress screamed at you incessantly like a psychotic drill sergeant." That joke became General Knowledge, the bellowing commander of Cranium Command, a humorous show about the human brain that ran in Epcot's Wonders of Life pavilion from 1989 to 2007. Tucked into a back corner of the pavilion, it was overshadowed from the start, yet it quietly left its mark on some celebrated animated films.

## A Starship Inside the Skull

The pavilion itself nearly never happened. When Disney could not find a sponsor for a planned Life and Health pavilion, construction was delayed indefinitely, though an expansion pad between Universe of Energy and Horizons was held in reserve. In the mid-1980s, CEO Michael Eisner finally secured MetLife as sponsor, and the project, renamed Wonders of Life, came back to life.

Imagineer Steve Kirk and Barry Braveman developed an idea called "The Head Trip" into a full attraction. Partly inspired by Disney's 1943 propaganda film Reason and Emotion, they first imagined a Star Trek parody, with the human head as the bridge of a starship crewed by a captain, a Mr. Spock type and officers for reason and emotion. Budget cuts reduced the cast to a single Audio-Animatronic figure and one robot, and the concept turned military: a pilot named Captain Cortex steering a young boy under the command of General Knowledge.

## Rewritten in a Week

Imagineering outsourced the film segments to Colossal Pictures, but halfway through, Disney executives scrapped that version. Animator Steve Moore, who worked on both, recalled that the script read "like an educational film from the '40s - very dry and condescending." In May 1989, Feature Animation president Peter Schneider brought director Jerry Rees to meet Eisner, Jeffrey Katzenberg, Marty Sklar and Tom Fitzgerald, who had unanimously loathed the first cut. Rees recommended starting over from scratch. He was given full creative freedom, provided he kept General Knowledge and the sponsor's stress-management theme, and just five months to meet an October opening.

Rees recruited Feature Animation artists including Gary Trousdale, Kirk Wise and Tom Sito, and together they built a new storyline in under a week, with Jenny Tripp writing the script. General Knowledge was remodeled on R. Lee Ermey's drill instructor from Full Metal Jacket, and Captain Cortex became Buzzy, a bumbling young recruit. When Rob Minkoff left to direct a Roger Rabbit short, Trousdale and Wise took over the animated pre-show, their first directing job. It was the last project at Disney Feature Animation to be traditionally inked and painted on cels.

## Piloting Bobby

Guests waited beneath posters urging them to enlist in the Cranium Command, then watched General Knowledge, voiced by Corey Burton, assign Buzzy one of the hardest missions of all: piloting a twelve-year-old boy. Doors swung open onto a dim, 200-seat theater designed as the inside of a human head, with the outside world visible on screens where the eyes would be.

Buzzy, an Audio-Animatronic on an articulated seat, guided Bobby through a rough day: skipped breakfast, a missed bus, a crush on a new girl named Annie, bullies, a lunchroom food fight, a trip to the principal and finally a kiss. A comic crew argued at every turn. Charles Grodin played the logical Left Brain, Jon Lovitz the wacky Right Brain, George Wendt the hungry Stomach and Bobcat Goldthwait the overreacting Adrenal Gland, while Dana Carvey and Kevin Nealon reprised their Saturday Night Live characters Hans and Franz as the heart's ventricles. Kirk Wise himself voiced the monotone robot Hypothalamus.

## The Hidden Gem's Long Shadow

Cranium Command opened on October 19, 1989, overshadowed by the pavilion's flight-simulator thrill ride, Body Wars, and handicapped by its tucked-away location. Still, it stayed well regarded for its humor and pace, and the Unofficial Guide to Walt Disney World called it "Epcot's hidden gem."

Its most lasting legacy came off the stage. At the grand opening, Katzenberg was so impressed by the pre-show that he called it almost more entertaining than the main show. Soon after, needing a new director for Beauty and the Beast, he thought of Trousdale and Wise and hired them, and their film became one of Disney's largest hits. One of Pete Docter's first jobs was as an animator on Cranium Command, and he later credited the attraction as an inspiration for Pixar's Inside Out.

## Lights Out Upstairs

The show began operating seasonally in January 2004 and closed permanently on January 1, 2007. It was left intact behind closed doors, and a 2017 urban-exploration video showed the theater and figures still in good condition. The Walt Disney Archives marked several animatronic elements, Buzzy among them, for preservation. In December 2018, Buzzy was reported stolen. Two men were later charged over thefts of Disney property that included Buzzy's clothing, and both pleaded no contest in 2020 and received probation, but the animatronic itself has never been recovered. A 2019 plan to turn the pavilion into a Play! Pavilion was put on indefinite hold during the COVID-19 pandemic, and in January 2023 Disney said it was reevaluating the concept and removed it from park maps.
