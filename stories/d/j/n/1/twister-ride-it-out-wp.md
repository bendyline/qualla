# Twister...Ride It Out

From 1998 to 2015, Universal Studios Florida's Twister...Ride It Out spun a five-story indoor tornado out of wind machines, 65,000 gallons of water and a roar built from camel sounds.

**Location:** 28.4757, -81.4695

**Categories:** Universal Studios Florida, Former Universal Studios Florida attractions, Amusement rides based on film franchises, Amusement rides introduced in 1998, Amusement rides closed in 2015

## Introduction

The roar was the strangest part. To give its indoor tornado a voice, Universal blended camel sounds, lion roars, and human and animal screams played backward. Twister...Ride It Out, an indoor special-effects attraction in the New York area of Universal Studios Florida, put audiences on a sheltered platform while a storm five stories tall tore apart a small-town drive-in in front of them. Based on the 1996 film Twister, with its stars Bill Paxton and Helen Hunt appearing on video throughout, it opened on May 4, 1998, and ran for 17 years before closing on November 2, 2015.

## Building a Storm

The building already had a history. Since the park's opening on June 7, 1990, it had housed Ghostbusters Spooktacular, a show whose popularity had declined sharply by the mid-1990s; its final performance came on November 8, 1996. Twister had reached theaters that May 10, and on February 6, 1997, Universal announced a new attraction themed to it for the 1998 season. Construction began that spring.

To get the experience right, Universal talked with tornado meteorologists about the real sights, sounds and feel of a twister. The full-size effect needed constant winds of 35 miles per hour. Thunder came through 54 speakers powered by 42,000 watts, enough to run five average homes. More than 65,000 gallons of water created the rainstorm, and the system could be ready for another show every six minutes. Behind it all, 20 laserdisc players, 300 speakers and 60 monitors were linked by 50 miles of wire and run by 20 computers.

## A Storm Too Close to Home

Twister...Ride It Out was supposed to open in March 1998. Just a few weeks before, the Kissimmee tornado outbreak struck nearby, and a ride built around a simulated tornado was about to open near a community that had just lived through real ones. Universal delayed the opening to May 4, 1998, and its officials donated $100,000 to the victims.

The opening arrived during a year of change for the resort. Universal was preparing to open Islands of Adventure and CityWalk in 1999, when the whole resort would be branded Universal Studios Escape, and during the attraction's first year staff members ran demonstrations of the experience inside a wind truck.

## Waiting in Wakita

The queue led guests past director's chairs for Steven Spielberg, Bill Paxton and Helen Hunt and out into a small mock-up of the Oklahoma town of Wakita. Screens played real tornado footage, each clip labeled with its place and date, and signs explained the Fujita scale. A plastic cow mooed on command, "Drink Esmoo Milk. Fresh from the farm to your fridge," and DOROTHY II sat near the waiting area. In the early years the outdoor queue was far larger, with vehicles such as Dusty's Bus and Beltzer's Van, crop fields, a windmill and the Wakita water tower.

Inside Soundstage 50, the opening of the film played before Paxton and Hunt appeared to talk about making it. The next room recreated Aunt Meg's wrecked house, televisions impaled in its walls, and there the stars described the ordeal: jet-engine blasts, chopped ice fired at them as hail, a gas tanker dropped and exploded in front of them, and real tornadoes touching down south of the filming locations. Then thunder cracked, the screens dissolved into static and tornado sirens began to wail.

## Five Stories of Wind

Staff with flashing red glowsticks led guests onto a tiered platform under a corrugated metal roof, facing the Galaxy drive-in theater and the Rocket Hamburgers diner at dusk. Lightning struck a tree. A projected tornado dropped from the sky and destroyed the drive-in, and then the real centerpiece formed on stage, a vortex five stories tall and twelve feet wide. The diner window shattered as water sprayed behind the audience, a replica of Jo's truck rolled into a fire hydrant, the drive-in sign crashed into a garage and a prop cow flew past, a nod to a scene in the film. A pickup slid into a gas pump, sparks lit the leak and a fireball rose three stories into the storm. As the tornado died away, the roof overhead dropped and the floor gave one short, sudden drop. Paxton then thanked everyone for surviving and sent them out through the "Aftermath" gift shop.

## Blown Away

In 2008 the outdoor queue was cut back to make room for the Hollywood Rip Ride Rockit coaster. In February 2009 the attraction switched to peak seasons only, but guest demand brought it back within weeks. The attraction even made a brief cameo in the 2015 movie Sharknado 3: Oh Hell No!.

In late October 2015, Universal announced Twister's retirement. By then it had become outdated, the film behind it had lost its cultural pull, and the show was expensive to operate. It closed on November 2, 2015, and in 2017 Race Through New York Starring Jimmy Fallon opened in the same building. A window display there still carries references to the old attraction, including one honoring Bill Paxton, who died on February 25, 2017, shortly before the new ride's debut.

## Flight Context

The former Twister building, Soundstage 50, stands near 28.476°N, 81.470°W in the New York area of Universal Studios Florida, part of the Universal Orlando Resort in Orlando, Florida, with Universal Islands of Adventure next door. From about 2,500–3,500 feet, look for the large soundstage buildings of the studio park. Nearby airports: Orlando Executive Airport (KORL) about 10 miles east-northeast, Orlando International Airport (KMCO) about 10 miles east-southeast (check the Orlando terminal-area chart for airspace before overflying), and Kissimmee Gateway Airport (KISM) about 13 miles south. Central Florida's summer afternoon thunderstorms are real ones; give them a wide berth.
