# Nickelodeon Studios

From 1990 to 2005, Nickelodeon Studios at Universal Studios Florida turned tourists into game-show contestants and produced more than 2,000 episodes of slime-soaked kids' television.

**Location:** 28.4744, -81.4686

**Categories:** Nickelodeon, Universal Studios Florida, Television studios in the United States, Nickelodeon in amusement parks, 1990 establishments in Florida, 2005 disestablishments in Florida

## Introduction

Every 10 minutes, a 17-foot fountain in front of the building erupted with green water. The Slime Geyser, also known as the Gak Geyser, was unveiled on October 27, 1990, with Russell Johnson, the Professor from Gilligan's Island, on hand for the ceremony, and the network later used a recording of the eruption as the sign-on for its broadcast day. Behind the geyser stood Nickelodeon Studios, billed as "The First World Headquarters for Kids," a working television studio inside Universal Studios Florida where young tourists could become audience members or even contestants. In just under fifteen years, it produced more than 2,000 episodes of original programming.

## A Studio Nickelodeon Didn't Have to Pay For

In November 1988, Nickelodeon contracted for space in the not-yet-built Universal Studios Florida. Universal's market research had convinced it that a working studio was crucial to drawing guests to a movie-themed park, so it offered the network a sweetheart deal: a custom-built studio at no expense and rent-free production. That lured Nickelodeon away from negotiations with Disney-MGM Studios. In return, the network agreed to promote the park on-air 1,000 times a year and broadcast its commercials.

The deal also solved a real problem. Nickelodeon had been shooting its live-action shows on rented soundstages in multiple cities, which was costly and limited its growth; its game shows, for instance, had been taped at WHYY-TV in Philadelphia. Super Sloppy Double Dare and Think Fast were shot on Universal's Soundstage 21 even before the new complex was finished.

## Opening Day and the Gak Kitchen

Universal Studios Florida opened to the public on June 7, 1990, and Nickelodeon celebrated with a live broadcast from the studio hosted by Marc Summers of Super Sloppy Double Dare, with celebrity guests including Steven Spielberg. Daily 45-minute tours took guests upstairs through the control room, through a viewing area overlooking Soundstages 18 and 19, and downstairs through the wardrobe department and the Gak Kitchen, ending in a large audition room. The contract promised guests they could watch shows being filmed "seven days a week, 52 weeks a year," a promise that proved awkward when sitcoms such as Welcome Freshmen rehearsed all week and shot only on weekends.

On April 30, 1992, a time capsule was buried near the geyser, filled with objects Nickelodeon viewers had voted important to the children of 1992: a Game Boy, Rollerblades, Reebok Pump sneakers, Nickelodeon Gak, a piece of the Berlin Wall and news reports on the dissolution of the Soviet Union.

## The Shows That Came Through

The list of productions reads like a map of a generation's afternoons: Clarissa Explains It All, Legends of the Hidden Temple, Nickelodeon Guts, Nick Arcade, Family Double Dare, All That, Kenan & Kel, Gullah Gullah Island and Slime Time Live among them. An episode of Weinerville shot on February 14, 1994, marked the studio's 1,000th episode of live-action programming. By then Nickelodeon Studios had brought $110 million in business to Florida, and at its peak it employed 400 people, making it, by the Wikipedia account, the largest production studio in the state.

The honors followed. Eureeka's Castle won a CableACE Award for best children's series, Legends of the Hidden Temple won one for best game show series, and Clarissa Explains It All and Gullah Gullah Island both earned Emmy nominations.

## The Slow Drift West

Cracks appeared by the mid-1990s. In 1994 Universal drew 7.7 million guests to Disney World's 28.9 million, and in the mid-1990s it moved ahead with Islands of Adventure, recommitting itself primarily to being a theme park, with the studio side now secondary to thrill rides; its slogan shrank from "See the Stars. Ride the Movies" to simply "Ride the Movies." Roundhouse had already left for Los Angeles in 1993, its unionized cast and crew unhappy that working in Orlando, in right-to-work Florida, cost them residuals. In 1996 the producers of All That asked to move their hit show to Los Angeles so they would not have to leave home, and Nickelodeon agreed.

The network was also pouring money into animation after Rugrats became an unexpected success in reruns. It opened Nickelodeon on Sunset in Los Angeles in 1997 and the Nickelodeon Animation Studio in 1998. Layoffs in Orlando began in March 2001. The final program taped there, Nickelodeon Splat!, aired live on August 17, 2004, and the studio closed for good on April 30, 2005.

## What Remains

Nickelodeon came back briefly in 2008 to film My Family's Got Guts, using some of the old facilities, including the green room. Universal left much of the Nickelodeon branding in place for years, and the empty complex drew urban explorers until most of the remnants were removed in a 2018 renovation. Soundstage 18 became a 1,000-seat theater for Blue Man Group, whose residency ran from 2007 until February 1, 2021. The time capsule has moved twice, and it has lain since 2016 at Nickelodeon's animation studio in Burbank, California, and is scheduled to be opened on April 30, 2042. Its original cover has already resurfaced, selling at auction in 2022 for $19,200.
