# McCoy Air Force Base

Before it was Orlando International Airport, this was McCoy Air Force Base, a Strategic Air Command bomber base where B-52s stood on alert, U-2s flew over Cuba during the missile crisis, and a colonel's name was written into three letters every traveler still carries.

**Location:** 28.4294, -81.3089

**Categories:** Installations of Strategic Air Command, Installations of the United States Air Force in Florida, Cuban Missile Crisis, Defunct airports in Florida, Orlando International Airport, Military airbases closed in 1975

## Introduction

Every bag tag printed at Orlando International Airport carries a small memorial. The letters MCO stand for McCoy: Colonel Michael Norman Wright McCoy, commander of the 321st Bombardment Wing, who died in October 1957 when his B-47 Stratojet suffered wing failure near Orlando during preparations for the Strategic Air Command's annual bombing competition. Three others died with him, including Group Captain John Woodroffe of the Royal Air Force. Seven months later, the base where they served was renamed in McCoy's honor. The Air Force left in 1975, but the name stayed, printed on every airline ticket and baggage tag.

## Fifteen Minutes From War

The field opened in 1940 and served as a training base during World War II, known at various times as Orlando Army Air Field #2 and Pinecastle Army Airfield. From 1951 it became something far more serious: a frontline Strategic Air Command base in the Cold War. When the new name was dedicated on May 21, 1958, an estimated 30,000 Floridians turned out for the open house. From 1961, B-52 Stratofortresses began replacing the B-47s. Under SAC's strategy of dispersing its bombers across many bases, half of the bombers and tankers were kept on fifteen-minute alert, fully fueled, armed and ready for combat. Later the 306th Bombardment Wing maintained constant ground alert and flew frequent airborne alert missions, and between 1966 and 1973 it also deployed to Guam, Okinawa and Thailand for bombing missions in the Vietnam War, including the Linebacker raids over North Vietnam in 1972. Until Walt Disney World opened, the base was reportedly one of Orlando's largest employers.

## Over Cuba

On October 14, 1962, Major Richard S. Heyser flew a U-2 over Cuba and photographed Soviet nuclear missiles being installed on the island. He landed his spy plane at McCoy, and the missile crisis began. Overnight, the base was on the front line. A U-2 operating location at McCoy flew at least 82 missions over Cuba between October 22 and December 6, while the Air Force rushed in 67 F-105 fighter-bombers, 63 F-100s and 20 aerial refueling tankers to prepare for strikes on the missile sites. On the morning of October 27, Major Rudolf Anderson Jr. took off from McCoy on another overflight. Hours later, a Soviet-manned surface-to-air missile site near Banes brought down his U-2, and Anderson was killed. He became the first recipient of the Air Force Cross, awarded posthumously. The crisis ended without the air strikes, but U-2 detachments kept watching Cuba from McCoy until 1973 or 1974.

## Airliners on a Bomber Runway

In the early 1960s, Orlando had a problem. Its civilian field, Herndon Airport, had World War II-era runways of 6,000 feet, too short to safely handle the Boeing 707s and Douglas DC-8s that airlines were beginning to fly. McCoy had two runways of 12,000 feet. In 1962 the Air Force and the city agreed to share one of them, and the city bought two former Hound Dog missile storage hangars in the northeast corner of the base to turn into a passenger terminal. The Orlando-McCoy Jetport opened in 1964, with Delta Air Lines flying the first jet service in a DC-8. By 1968 every airline had moved over from Herndon. Then Walt Disney World opened in 1971, air traffic climbed sharply, and the Air Force agreed to give Orlando more land for the growing civilian airport alongside the bombers.

## Short of the Runway

On March 31, 1972, a B-52D took off from McCoy on a routine training mission carrying no weapons. Shortly after departure it suffered multiple engine failures and an engine pod fire. The crew turned back toward the base, but the bomber came down 3,220 feet short of the runway in a residential neighborhood just north of the airfield, where burning fuel swept much of a two-block area. All seven crew members died, and so did a 10-year-old boy on the ground, who succumbed to his injuries three days later. In 2012, Orange County placed a historical marker near the crash site to honor the flight crew. A year after the crash, with the Vietnam cease-fire in place, Secretary of Defense Elliot Richardson announced that McCoy would close, noting that it lay near the east coast and was therefore "subject to short warning time attacks by (Soviet) submarine-launched ballistic missiles."

## Sold for a Dollar

The last Air Force operations ended in early 1975. The federal government transferred the remaining property, airfield included, to the City of Orlando for $1.00, the standard price for such transfers at the time, with a clause letting the Air Force return if national security ever required it. The base became Orlando International Airport, now among the busiest in the United States by passenger numbers. Traces of McCoy linger everywhere. A B-52D has stood on permanent display at the airport's B-52 Memorial Park since 1984, a portrait of the colonel hangs near the terminal chapel, and an instrument departure procedure from the field has carried the name MCCOY ONE. Less welcome relics turn up, too: World War II practice bombs, rockets and machine-gun rounds from old Army bombing ranges, which have been dug up beneath a nearby middle school and the neighborhoods around it, with cleanups under way since 2007.

## Flight Context

The former McCoy Air Force Base is today Orlando International Airport (KMCO), at approximately 28.4294°N, 81.3089°W, about 9 miles southeast of downtown Orlando. Its long parallel north-south runways and sprawling terminal complex are unmistakable from the air. KMCO sits inside busy Class B airspace, so any overflight requires clearance from Orlando approach; many pilots view it from outside the Class B shelf or while arriving on an instrument procedure. Orlando Executive Airport (KORL), the former Herndon Airport, lies about 7 nm to the north, and Kissimmee Gateway Airport (KISM) about 11 nm to the southwest. Central Florida summer afternoons bring frequent thunderstorms; mornings are typically clearer.
