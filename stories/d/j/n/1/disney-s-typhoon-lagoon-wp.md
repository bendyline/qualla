# Disney's Typhoon Lagoon

Built around the tale of a storm that flung a shrimp boat onto a mountaintop, Typhoon Lagoon pairs one of the world's largest outdoor wave pools with some serious surf engineering.

**Location:** 28.3650, -81.5278

**Categories:** 1989 establishments in Florida, Amusement parks opened in 1989, Buildings and structures in Lake Buena Vista, Florida, Tourist attractions in Greater Orlando, Walt Disney World, Water parks in Florida

## Introduction

A shrimp boat sits skewered on the peak of a mountain, and every half hour the mountain erupts. The boat is "Miss Tilly," the mountain is "Mount Mayday," and the eruption is a 50-foot geyser of water that shoots skyward right before the bells of the watch ring out on deck. According to the Disney legend behind it all, a typhoon once tore through a pristine tropical paradise and left this wreckage behind. The paradise, of course, is Disney's Typhoon Lagoon, a water park at the Walt Disney World Resort that opened on June 1, 1989, and still looks as if the storm passed through last week.

## Wreckage by Design

Everywhere the storm supposedly struck, it left props. Ships, fishing gear and surfboards lie strewn where the wind flung them, and the resident mascot, Lagoona Gator, presides over the mess. Typhoon Lagoon was the resort's second water park, following Disney's River Country, which closed in November 2001, and today it is one of two operating water parks at Walt Disney World.

Mount Mayday is more than scenery. The man-made mountain behind the surf pool holds Miss Tilly, many of the park's waterfalls and the launch points for its slides, and it quietly conceals the pipework that keeps all that water moving. Walkways thread through it: Forgotten Grotto passes through a cave inside the mountain, and Overlook Pass crosses its slope past small waterfalls.

## Engineering a Wave Worth Riding

The heart of the park is its surf pool, one of the world's largest outdoor wave pools, and building it took real science. Typhoon Lagoon was one of the first wave-makers applied to a theme park, and a prime objective of the engineering work in 1988 and 1989 was to produce surfable waves. The waves are generated by a 12-cell prestressed concrete tank at the back of the lagoon, hidden behind the shipwreck scene. Engineers used computer modeling to study fluid-structure interaction, stress concentrations and fatigue. Before opening, they tested the pool for wave shape, for how long a surfboard ride lasted, and for how naturally the waves felt as they rolled in and broke on the artificial beach.

The result is a pool that sends 6-foot waves rolling in roughly every 90 to 110 seconds, depending on the source. Before the park opens, Craig Carroll's Cocoa Beach Surf School teaches two-and-a-half-hour surf lessons there, for an additional cost.

## Five Stories Down, Twenty-One Hundred Feet Around

For those who prefer gravity, Mount Mayday delivers. Humunga Kowabunga's three enclosed speed slides drop riders five stories in seconds, at speeds said to reach 39 miles per hour. The three Storm Slides, named Jib Jammer, Stern Burner and Rudder Buster, twist down from three stories up into a splash pool. Crush 'n' Gusher is a water coaster whose rafts are propelled by water jets through hairpin turns and drop-offs, with a choice of the Banana Blaster, Coconut Crusher or Pineapple Plunger. Beside it, the family raft ride Miss Adventure Falls, planned in 2016 and opened in March 2017, begins with a large conveyor belt that carries riders, rafts and all, to the top.

The gentlest ride circles everything. Castaway Creek is a continuously flowing, 2,100-foot lazy river that loops the entire park, drifting through waterfalls, rainforest, mist screens and Mount Mayday itself.

## The Reef That Went Quiet

For years, Typhoon Lagoon held something often considered one of the most unique attractions at any Disney park. At Shark Reef, guests spent five to fifteen minutes snorkeling across a man-made saltwater lagoon, unheated, alongside stingrays, bonnethead sharks, leopard sharks and tropical fish. The 362,000-gallon habitat was split into two tanks by an overturned "sunken tanker" that disguised an underwater viewing tunnel, where visitors could peer out through the ship's portholes.

Shark Reef closed permanently on October 3, 2016. Disney made no official statement on the reason, but analysts pointed to its unusually high operating costs, from animal care to water temperature control, filtration and lifeguards. The sunken tanker has since been walled off, the tank nearest the paths has been filled in and covered with sand for lounge seating, and the old dive shop now pours drinks as a bar.

## The Last Gate to Reopen

When the COVID-19 pandemic closed every Walt Disney World park in March 2020, Typhoon Lagoon stayed dark long after the four theme parks returned in July. It finally reopened on January 2, 2022, nearly two years later, the last part of the resort to come back. That year, by industry estimates, it reclaimed the title of the world's most popular water park, with about 1.92 million guests.

One more quirk sets it apart. Lake Buena Vista is the mailing address for the whole resort, yet Typhoon Lagoon is the only one of Disney World's major parks that actually lies within Lake Buena Vista's city limits. Blizzard Beach and the four theme parks sit in the neighboring city of Bay Lake.

Like Blizzard Beach, it closes periodically for refurbishment, and Disney announced a temporary closure starting September 9, 2026.

## Flight Context

Disney's Typhoon Lagoon lies at approximately 28.365°N, 81.528°W in Lake Buena Vista, Florida, on the Walt Disney World Resort southwest of Orlando. From 2,500–3,500 feet, look for the large wave pool and the rocky peak of Mount Mayday, topped by a shipwrecked shrimp boat, with Epcot's geodesic sphere, Spaceship Earth, a short distance to the west-northwest. Nearby airports: Kissimmee Gateway Airport (KISM) about 7.5 statute miles southeast and Orlando International Airport (KMCO) about 14 statute miles east-northeast, inside Orlando's Class B airspace. Check current NOTAMs for flight restrictions over the Disney parks. Summer afternoon thunderstorms build quickly over central Florida, and mornings are usually clearest.
