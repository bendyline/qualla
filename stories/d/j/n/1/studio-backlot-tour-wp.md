# Studio Backlot Tour

Once the premier attraction at Disney's Hollywood Studios, the Studio Backlot Tour drenched volunteers, rolled trams through a boneyard of movie vehicles and staged a flash flood in Catastrophe Canyon before shrinking away to make room for Galaxy's Edge and Toy Story Land.

**Location:** 28.3550, -81.5617

**Categories:** Amusement rides introduced in 1989, Amusement rides closed in 2014, Backlot sets, Disney's Hollywood Studios, Disney production studios, Former Walt Disney Parks and Resorts attractions, Streets of America, 1989 establishments in Florida, 2014 disestablishments in Florida

## Introduction

The tram rolled to a stop in a rocky canyon, and the host explained that the crew of this "live" movie set happened to be on break. Then filming started without warning. The ground shook, telephone poles swayed and threw sparks, a fuel truck burst into flame and sent a fireball into the air, and a flood of water came rushing down the canyon and from above the tram. Moments later it was over, and the set began to reset itself for the next tram. This was Catastrophe Canyon, the climax of the Studio Backlot Tour at Disney's Hollywood Studios, where from 1989 to 2014 guests got a peek behind the curtain of moviemaking.

## A Tour Too Popular for Its Own Route

The Backlot Tour began as the park's premier attraction, a combination walking and tram tour that was far longer and more elaborate than the version guests knew at the end. Trams loaded at the former entrance to The Magic of Disney Animation and drove through New York Street before heading to Catastrophe Canyon. After a break area, which later grew a children's play area called Honey, I Shrunk the Kids: Movie Set Adventure, a second, walking half led past a water effects tank, an effects shop and the park's soundstages, viewed from overhead walkways. Pieces of those walkways, including bridges near the last tour entrance, have been spotted in the park.

The park's surprise popularity kept eating the route. Within the first few years, New York Street was pulled from the tour and opened to pedestrians, and other former backlot areas became today's Mickey Avenue and southern Animation Courtyard. By 1991 much of the walking portion had split off into its own attraction, Backstage Pass, greatly shortening the tour.

## Harbor Attack

In its later form, the tour began under a large canopy lined with props from Pearl Harbor, Pirates of the Caribbean: The Curse of the Black Pearl, The Rock and Armageddon, while a looping film showed director Michael Bay explaining how some of Pearl Harbor's effects scenes were filmed. Four volunteers were plucked from the lines.

Those four soon learned why. Guests were led to a large water tank dressed as the deck and engine room of a patrol boat, where cast members explained how tanks like this were used in action films. In a demonstration called Harbor Attack, one volunteer sat in the engine room as 1,000 gallons of water from two dump tanks crashed through a window. The other three stood on deck amid underwater explosions, simulated torpedo bursts and fireballs. The footage was then cut together with previously recorded airplane attacks and dialogue and screened for the audience. From there, guests walked the aisles of a prop warehouse holding pieces from films such as The Santa Clause, Who Framed Roger Rabbit, The Rocketeer and George of the Jungle.

## Through the Boneyard

Trams carried guests past the Earffel Tower, the park's former icon, closer than they could get to it anywhere else in the park, then straight through the costume and materials building, where windows gave guests a look at costumes and the people at work on them, along with a room full of tires for the Lights, Motors, Action! Extreme Stunt Show.

Outside lay the boneyard, named after an aircraft boneyard. There sat the genuine steamroller Judge Doom drove in the climax of Who Framed Roger Rabbit, ships from the original Star Wars films, the motorcycles from Indiana Jones and the Last Crusade, a spaceship from The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy, cars from the Herbie the Love Bug films, bone cages from Pirates of the Caribbean: Dead Man's Chest and the alien drone ship from Flight of the Navigator. After Catastrophe Canyon, where the host revealed how the disaster was done as the tram passed behind the set, the ride rolled by Walt Disney's own private airplane, a Grumman Gulfstream I now displayed at the Palm Springs Air Museum. The exit led through an American Film Institute exhibit on AFI's 50 Greatest Villains.

## The Last Take

The tour kept giving ground. In 2003, Residential Street was walled off and demolished to make room for Lights, Motors, Action!: Extreme Stunt Show, which debuted in 2005. The trams were rerouted around the big stadium, the boneyard was relocated and downsized, and New York Street lost its arch so trams could turn around. In 2008, Imagineering told cast members that an automated narration was being developed to replace the live tram guides, although the water tank, with its volunteers, kept a live cast to the end.

In mid-September 2014, Disney confirmed that the tour would close. Its final day was September 27, 2014, with no replacement named. At the D23 Expo in 2015, Disney revealed that the tour's land and most of the Streets of America area would make way for Star Wars: Galaxy's Edge and Toy Story Land. Catastrophe Canyon was demolished first, in early 2016, and the rest followed after the area closed on April 2, 2016. Today the entire attraction, canyon and all, has been demolished.

## Flight Context

The Studio Backlot Tour ran through the backlot of Disney's Hollywood Studios, at approximately 28.355°N, 81.5617°W within the Walt Disney World Resort southwest of Orlando, Florida. Its land was cleared to make room for Star Wars: Galaxy's Edge and Toy Story Land. From 2,500–3,500 feet, look for the studio park about 1.6 statute miles south-southwest of Epcot's geodesic sphere, Spaceship Earth. Nearby airports: Kissimmee Gateway Airport (KISM) about 9 statute miles east-southeast and Orlando International Airport (KMCO) about 16 statute miles east-northeast, inside Orlando's Class B airspace.
