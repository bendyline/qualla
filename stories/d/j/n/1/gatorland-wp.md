# Gatorland

Founded on old cattle land in 1949 and still run by the founder's family, Gatorland bills itself as the Alligator Capital of the World, and its breeding marsh once doubled for Indiana Jones.

**Location:** 28.3556, -81.4042

**Categories:** 1949 establishments in Florida, Amusement parks in Greater Orlando, Amusement parks opened in 1949, Roadside attractions in Florida, Tourist attractions in Orlando, Florida, Zoos in Florida

## Introduction

Indiana Jones and the Temple of Doom went looking for danger all over the world, and part of it was found in a marsh on South Orange Blossom Trail, just south of Orlando. Footage from the breeding marsh at Gatorland appeared in the 1984 film (reportedly the alligators beneath the rope bridge), and it is no stretch to see why a movie crew would want it: the water there belongs to alligators, and the park that surrounds it bills itself as the "Alligator Capital of the World." Gatorland has held that stretch of road since 1949, and more than seventy-five years later, the reptiles are still the reason to stop.

## Cattle Land to Gator Land

Owen Godwin founded Gatorland in 1949 on land that had been used for cattle, and the park remains privately owned by his family. Today it covers 110 acres as a combined theme park and wildlife preserve. Its residents number in the thousands of alligators, along with crocodiles and many other animals, including rare leucistic alligators.

The program is old-school Florida roadside attraction and modern wildlife park at once. There are alligator feeding shows and alligator wrestling shows, an aviary, a petting zoo and educational programs. Visitors can take an off-road swamp vehicle tour, and a boardwalk and observation tower lead out over the breeding marsh, the same one that played its part in Indiana Jones. In the White Gator Swamp, an indoor area with the feel of a small museum, guests view white alligators through a glass wall while signs explain the origin of their color, how they differ from other gators, and when they were first spotted.

## A Second Chance for Nuisance Gators

Many of Gatorland's alligators arrived as trouble. The park is known for buying and rescuing nuisance alligators from trappers, animals that would otherwise be killed for their meat and skin. Instead of ending up as products, they end up as residents.

Gatorland's reach extends beyond its own gates. It has also managed the live alligator display at the Gaylord Palms resort in Kissimmee, and on May 11, 2015, it opened Gator Spot at Fun Spot America's Orlando park in the International Drive tourist area. That $1 million attraction was built to let visitors hold, photograph and feed alligators, and its star was Bouya, a leucistic alligator with white skin and blue eyes. Fun Spot still lists Gator Spot among its Orlando attractions.

## The Little Train That Came First

Not everything at Gatorland has teeth. The Gatorland Express, a narrow gauge railroad built by the Allan Herschell Company, first opened in 1961, and the park claims it was the first amusement attraction in Central Florida. It ran as the Ol' Iron Horse Express until 2001. Its original locomotive retired in 2000 and went on static display, and a brand-new engine from Custom Locomotive of Chicago took over the following year, the same model once used at the former Green Meadows Petting Farm in Kissimmee.

For decades that train, which carried an additional fee, was the only attraction in the park that had nothing to do with animals. That changed in the summer of 2011, when Gatorland added a zip line of roughly 1,200 feet, strung several stories high across a pool of alligators and past other attractions. In January 2016, the park made it wheelchair-accessible.

## Fire Before Dawn

Shortly before sunrise on Monday, November 6, 2006, a three-alarm fire broke out at Gatorland. It was believed to have started when a heating pad in one of the gift shop displays shorted out, and arson was not suspected. Crews brought the blaze under control within several hours, but the gift shop was completely destroyed and several walkways burned. A four-foot crocodile and two six-foot pythons died in the fire. The birds displayed in and around the shop were moved to the aviary at the back of the park and were not injured.

Inspectors found no structural damage to the park's walkways and displays, and Gatorland reopened less than three weeks later, on the day after Thanksgiving. Its new gift shop and main offices rose as a two-story concrete block building, and they carried a piece of the past forward: the park's historic concrete alligator's mouth, repainted and built into the new structure, which opened on May 22, 2008.

## Flight Context

Gatorland is at approximately 28.3556°N, 81.4042°W along South Orange Blossom Trail, south of Orlando, Florida. From 1,500–2,500 feet, follow the highway south from Orlando and look for the park's ponds and the open breeding marsh beside the road. Kissimmee Gateway Airport (KISM) is about 5 statute miles south-southwest, and Orlando International Airport (KMCO) is about 8 statute miles northeast, so the park sits beneath Orlando's Class B airspace and close to KISM traffic. Orlando Executive Airport (KORL) is about 14 miles north-northeast. Expect afternoon thunderstorms in summer; mornings usually offer the calmest air.
