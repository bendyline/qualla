# SeaWorld Orlando

Opened two years after Magic Kingdom, SeaWorld Orlando turned Central Florida into a multi-park destination, then remade itself from a marine show park into a coaster park, through ownership changes and a 2010 tragedy that changed how trainers work.

## Introduction

When SeaWorld Orlando opened on December 15, 1973, Walt Disney World's Magic Kingdom had been running for just two years. The new marine park, the third in the SeaWorld chain, gave visitors a second gate to buy tickets for, and with it Central Florida became a multi-park vacation destination. Half a century later the park sits just outside Orlando with a Shamu-themed lighthouse at its entrance and a skyline of steel coasters behind it, and in 2022 it welcomed an estimated 4.45 million guests, making it the tenth most visited amusement park in the United States.

## A Park That Kept Changing Hands

SeaWorld did not stay with one owner for long. In 1976 the chain was sold to Harcourt Brace Jovanovich, and in 1989 it passed to Anheuser-Busch, owners of Busch Gardens. Along the way, the Orlando park found its way onto movie screens: it was featured in the 1983 film Jaws 3-D.

The brewery left its own marks. For years the park had a Hospitality House, a Beer School and resident Clydesdales with their barn. After the Busch parks were sold off, those disappeared: the Hospitality House and Beer School closed, and the Clydesdales and their barn were removed. In 2013 the company went public in an initial public offering, with Blackstone selling part of its stake while keeping control. Today SeaWorld Orlando belongs to United Parks & Resorts, which also owns its neighbors Discovery Cove and Aquatica, along with Busch Gardens Tampa Bay.

## From Shows to Screams

Anheuser-Busch brought theme park experience and an aggressive competitive streak, and it steered SeaWorld from a show-based park toward a ride-based one. In 1992 the park joined the simulator-ride wave that Disneyland had started, opening Mission: Bermuda Triangle, later rethemed as Wild Arctic. In 1998 came Journey to Atlantis, the nation's first combination roller coaster and flume ride. Kraken, a floorless coaster from Bolliger & Mabillard, followed in 2000, and in 2009 the flying coaster Manta arrived and won the Theme Park Insider Award as best new attraction.

The building never really stopped. Turtle Trek brought a 360-degree, three-dimensional theater in 2012, and in 2013 Antarctica: Empire of the Penguin introduced the country's first trackless dark ride system alongside a renovated penguin exhibit. Ice Breaker, a launched steel coaster by Premier Rides, joined the lineup, and in 2023 Pipeline opened as the world's first surf coaster, a Bolliger & Mabillard revival of the stand-up coaster with better restraints and a more comfortable ride. In 2024 the penguin dark ride gave way to Penguin Trek, a launched family coaster, and in 2025 a Mack Rides flying theater called Expedition Odyssey took over the old Wild Arctic building, then reopened in May 2026 with a new film as Expedition Odyssey: Fire & Ice.

## February 24, 2010

The park's darkest day came during a "Dine with Shamu" show. On February 24, 2010, the orca Tilikum pulled trainer Dawn Brancheau into the water and killed her. An autopsy found that she died from blunt force trauma and drowning.

The consequences reshaped the job she had done. In August 2010, the U.S. Department of Labor's Occupational Safety and Health Administration cited SeaWorld of Florida LLC for three safety violations related to her death, with an initial total penalty of $75,000. After appeals, a judge downgraded the main citation from willful to serious and cut the total penalty to $12,000, and a federal appeals court upheld the ruling in 2014, which required SeaWorld to separate trainers from the whales during shows with barriers or equally protective measures. The risks did not vanish entirely. In March 2025, the Department of Labor fined the park $16,550 after a killer whale injured an employee in September 2024.

## Seas, a Street and a Sky Tower

In the summer of 2014, as part of the company's 50th anniversary, SeaWorld Orlando divided itself into themed areas called "seas," each with its own look. Visitors start at Port of Entry, a Florida-inspired entrance of tropical landscaping around a large artificial freshwater marina, watched over by that iconic Shamu-themed lighthouse.

Other corners carry longer histories. The Sky Tower, a 400-foot rotating gyro tower, lifts riders high above the grounds, and Flamingo Paddle Boats, dating to 1978, let visitors pedal around the park's central lake for an extra fee. In 2019 a cluster of children's rides was rethemed as Sesame Street Land: the Seven Seas Railway became Elmo's Choo Choo Train, the jellyfish-themed Jazzy Jellies became Abby's Flower Tower, and the spinning Swishy Fishies became Big Bird's Twirl 'N' Whirl.

The pandemic briefly emptied it all. In March 2020, the park temporarily shut down along with the other SeaWorld parks, reopening three months later. Next door, Aquatica Orlando, a water park that opened in 2008, rounds out the complex.
