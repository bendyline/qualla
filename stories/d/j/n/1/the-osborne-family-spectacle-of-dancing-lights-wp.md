# The Osborne Family Spectacle of Dancing Lights

A Little Rock father's Christmas lights for his six-year-old daughter grew so large they reached the U.S. Supreme Court, then found a new home at Disney's Hollywood Studios from 1995 to 2016.

**Location:** 28.3550, -81.5597

**Categories:** Disney's Hollywood Studios, Former Walt Disney Parks and Resorts attractions, Christmas in Florida, Christmas lights, 1995 establishments in Florida, 2016 disestablishments in Florida

## Introduction

In 1986, a six-year-old girl in Little Rock, Arkansas, asked her father whether they could decorate their house with lights. Jennings Osborne said yes and strung 1,000 bulbs around the family home. "Each year after that," he later recalled, "it got bigger and bigger." It got so big, in fact, that neighbors sued, the case reached the United States Supreme Court, and the whole display eventually left Arkansas in four 18-wheelers bound for Walt Disney World. For two decades, The Osborne Family Spectacle of Dancing Lights was one of the most popular attractions of the holiday season at what is now Disney's Hollywood Studios.

## A House That Outgrew Its Street

Jennings Osborne and his wife Mitzi founded the Arkansas Research Medical Testing Center in Little Rock in 1968, and its success let them buy a nearly 12,000-square-foot estate in the middle of town in 1976. Their daughter, Allison Brianne, nicknamed Breezy, was born in 1980, and it was her request that started it all.

Osborne eventually bought the two neighboring properties so the display could spread into them. By 1993 it held more than three million lights. An illuminated globe in the back yard marked both Little Rock and Bethlehem. Two rotating carousels of lights stood at either end of the circular driveway, a 70-foot tree made of 80,000 lights in three colored layers rose from atop the kitchen, and a canopy of 30,000 red lights arched over part of the drive. News crews came to film it, and the display drew fans in Arkansas and around the world.

## Lights Versus Neighbors

The house sat on one of the busiest streets in Little Rock, and the crowds brought severe traffic and many complaints. Osborne once knocked out the power for part of the neighborhood, after which the utility gave the home a transformer of its own. By 1993 the lights burned for 35 days each season, from sunset to about midnight.

Six neighbors filed suit, saying traffic turned a trip to the corner store into a two-hour ordeal and that emergency vehicles might not get through. Osborne's answer was to add more lights. A county court then limited the display to 15 days, lit only from 7 to 10:30 p.m. Osborne appealed to the Arkansas Supreme Court, which in December 1994 rejected his First Amendment argument and ordered the display cut back substantially. He then asked the U.S. Supreme Court, where Justice Clarence Thomas declined to halt the order. The Little Rock show could no longer go on at anything like its old scale.

## A Different Residential Street

The court fight made national news, and several cities offered to host the lights. Walt Disney World project director John Phelan reached out to Osborne's attorney and then to Osborne himself. At first Osborne understood that Disney wanted to set up the display on some other residential street in Orlando. What Phelan actually meant was Residential Street, a backlot set at what was then Disney-MGM Studios. Osborne, a fan of the resort, accepted.

In 1995, four 18-wheelers hauled in millions of lights, and the display was an immediate success. Guests normally saw Residential Street from Studio Backlot Tour trams, but during the holidays the trams stopped before sunset so visitors could walk among the lights. Disney's caretakers added hidden Mickeys and nods to films like One Hundred and One Dalmatians and Finding Nemo, eventually expanding the display to more than five million lights. The Osbornes returned to see it every year until Jennings Osborne died in 2011.

## Teaching the Lights to Dance

In the mid-2000s, Residential Street was demolished to make way for a new stunt-show arena, so the display moved to the park's New York Street set, later called the Streets of America. There it gained 33 snow machines that went through 100 gallons of fluid a night. The scale was staggering: more than 10 miles of rope lighting, 30 miles of extension cords, two million ties, 800,000 watts of power and 20,000 hours of labor to install each season, starting in September.

In 2006, hundreds of dimmer relay circuits and control switches let the lights fade on and off in choreography with music, and the display took its final name. A 2011 overhaul brought in LEDs, and the old all-red canopy became 21,600 pixels capable of more than 16 million colors.

## The Last Dance

On September 11, 2015, a month after Disney announced Star Wars: Galaxy's Edge and Toy Story Land, the company said the coming holiday season would be the display's last. The final performance, first set for January 3, 2016, was extended three more nights to January 6. The lights danced one last time to "Have Yourself a Merry Little Christmas" and the "Mickey Mouse Club Alma Mater." Most of them were then donated to a children's charity in Kissimmee, Florida, where for several years they lit its "Night of a Million Lights" event.

## Flight Context

The display's last home, the former Streets of America area of Disney's Hollywood Studios, lies near 28.355°N, 81.560°W at Walt Disney World, southwest of Orlando, Florida, about a mile and a half south-southwest of Epcot's Spaceship Earth. The lights were removed after the final 2015–16 season, so there is nothing of the display left to see. Check current NOTAMs and charts for flight restrictions over the Walt Disney World parks and stay outside any restricted area. Nearby airports: Kissimmee Gateway Airport (KISM) about 9 miles east-southeast, Orlando International Airport (KMCO) about 16 miles east-northeast, and Orlando Executive Airport (KORL) about 19 miles northeast. Summer afternoon thunderstorms are common over central Florida.
