# World of Motion

General Motors' World of Motion carried EPCOT Center riders from cave dwellers with hot feet to a gleaming city of the future, singing "It's Fun to Be Free" all the way, before giving its building to Test Track.

## Introduction

Riders on Test Track at Epcot can catch a sign bearing a cryptic string of letters: "FN2BFRE." It is a small tribute, easy to miss at speed, to the attraction that stood here first. From EPCOT Center's opening day in 1982 until 1996, the transportation pavilion was home to World of Motion, presented by General Motors, a gently comic ride through the whole history of getting from one place to another. Its theme song, played all the way through, was "It's Fun to Be Free."

## GM Answers Ford

General Motors signed a 10-year sponsorship contract in December 1977, a move to compete with Ford, which had sponsored a Disney-created attraction at the 1964 New York World's Fair. The pavilion went up as part of EPCOT Center's initial construction and was ready when the park opened on October 1, 1982, with its own opening ceremony for GM executives a few days later.

Ward Kimball and Marc Davis designed most of the ride, and their trademark visual gags were set against the straight-faced narration of actor Gary Owens. Guests boarded Omnimover vehicles holding four to six people and rolled past Audio-Animatronic figures and projection effects. X Atencio, who had written songs for Pirates of the Caribbean and The Haunted Mansion, wrote "It's Fun to Be Free" with composer Buddy Baker, and its arrangement shifted with each era the ride passed through.

## From Hot Feet to the First Traffic Jam

History began on foot. A caveman and cavewoman blew on their hot feet, and from there the scenes rolled forward: travelers on projected boats, a man asleep on a raft as a crocodile lunged at him, and an Assyrian tableau of a driver struggling with worn-out animals from camels to zebras, while a man with a crystal ball floated by on a flying carpet. In Babylon, the wheel was invented by contest. Men presented a square, a triangle and a pentagon before the circle won the king's laughter. Nearby, a used chariot dealer tried to sell everything on the lot, the Trojan Horse included.

The ages kept turning. Leonardo da Vinci attempted to fly while a clearly upset Mona Lisa looked on, a balloonist drifted over London, stagecoaches and a Mississippi riverboat carried the westward expansion, and a train robbery unfolded as a sheriff with a gleaming badge saw riders safely on their way. Then came one of the ride's most remembered scenes, the world's first traffic jam, a chaos of an upset horse, a spilling ice truck, screaming kids, a family picnic and a suspicious policeman.

## Speed Tunnels and a City of Tomorrow

After a parade of automobiles, the Omnimovers entered bullet-shaped "speed tunnels," a tribute to the Magic Kingdom's If You Had Wings, where screens rushed past with scenes of crop-dusting, rafting and snowmobile trails. The tunnels opened onto CenterCore, a sparkling metropolis of the future that seemed to be in perpetual motion. Just before unloading, riders were asked to "help shape tomorrow's mobility," and a Pepper's Ghost illusion, like the hitchhiking ghosts of the Haunted Mansion, placed them inside a futuristic car.

The ride emptied into the TransCenter, designed by Bob Rogers and BRC Imagination Arts. It held prototype cars such as the Lean Machine, a show called The Water Engine in which nine animated characters representing different fuel systems debated how cars should be powered, and "Bird and the Robot," in which a cigar-smoking toucan traded bad jokes with a GM assembly-line robot named Tiger.

## The Last Ride Stalls

After GM's sponsorship deal ended in 1992, with the economy slumping, the company agreed only to one-year contracts and wanted Imagineering to work on something new. GM insisted that the next ride focus on cars alone, not transportation in general, so its marketing could promote its vehicles. In November 1995, Epcot announced the closure, and the building would be gutted for Test Track.

World of Motion closed to the public on January 2, 1996, and its final ride did not go smoothly. Halfway through, the attraction ran into technical difficulties, and the GM executives aboard had to walk back to the loading station. Test Track was scheduled to open in May 1997, but it opened significantly behind schedule, in 1999. As World of Motion closed, Disney reopened Horizons, another future-minded attraction, which finally closed in 1999.

GM never left. It has sponsored Test Track since it opened, through its Chevrolet division from 2012 to 2024 and under GM branding again from 2025. And since the ride's 2025 reopening, the old World of Motion logo has served as Test Track's official symbol, on its main sign and on the signage directing guests to it.

## Flight Context

World of Motion's former building, now Test Track, sits just southeast of Spaceship Earth at Epcot, at approximately 28.3733°N, 81.5477°W, within Walt Disney World southwest of Orlando, Florida. From 3,000–4,000 feet, orient on Spaceship Earth's geodesic sphere, a few hundred yards to the northwest of the building, and look for Test Track's outdoor track looping around the building. Nearby airports: Kissimmee Gateway Airport (KISM) about 9 statute miles southeast and Orlando International Airport (KMCO) about 15 statute miles east-northeast, inside Orlando's Class B airspace. Check current NOTAMs for flight restrictions over the Disney parks. Summer afternoon thunderstorms are frequent over central Florida.
