# Big Bamboo Lounge

A World War II fighter pilot's South Pacific bar on the Kissimmee tourist strip became the after-work refuge of Disney cast members and Houston Astros players for more than a quarter century.

**Location:** 28.3335, -81.4766

**Categories:** Drinking establishments in Florida, Buildings and structures in Kissimmee, Florida, 1977 establishments in Florida, 2004 disestablishments in Florida, Restaurants established in 1977, Restaurants disestablished in 2004, Houston Astros, Defunct restaurants in Florida

## Introduction

The menu described the house drink in five words: "Rum, rum, and more rum." The Big Bamboo, as the cocktail was called, came out a light red, and it was the signature pour at a bar that never served a meal and never pretended to be anything but a hangout. For 27 years the Big Bamboo Lounge, known to its regulars simply as "The Boo," stood at 4849 W. Irlo Bronson Highway in Kissimmee, Florida, not far from Walt Disney World. It drew Disney employees year-round and, every spring, the players, coaches and fans of the Houston Astros.

## A Pilot's South Pacific

The bar began with one man's memories. Bruce Muir had been a fighter pilot in World War II, and in 1977 he bought a former doctor's house in Kissimmee and turned it into the kind of bar he had known in the South Pacific. The theme reached back to the 1930s. Bartenders wore Hawaiian shirts, swing music played, and the walls slowly disappeared beneath Disney employee name tags and business cards left behind by visitors.

Out front, the war stayed visible. A World War II-era ambulance and a spotting tower decorated the entrance, and they were kept up not by a paid crew but by volunteers drawn from the bar's own customers and staff. That detail says a great deal about the place. The Boo was the sort of bar people felt they partly owned.

## Where the Cast Members Clocked Out

For employees of the Walt Disney World Resort, the Big Bamboo was a popular after-work hangout, one of the places where the people who ran the magic went to put their feet up. Among the regulars was Ralph Kent, the Disney artist Walt Disney himself had hired in 1963.

The theme parks leaked in, piece by piece. Over the years employees from both Universal and Walt Disney World donated a limited amount of park memorabilia, and the best known pieces were ride vehicle seats from Disney's Test Track, which found a home in the small hallway outside the restrooms. Anyone waiting there could sit where park guests had once buckled in.

The kitchen, meanwhile, did not exist. Bagged chips were sometimes for sale, but customers were allowed and even encouraged to order pizza delivered to the bar. Alongside the Big Bamboo, the other famous order was the "Swamp Water."

## Spring Training Regulars

Each spring, the bar picked up a second crowd. The Houston Astros trained at nearby Osceola County Stadium, and players, coaches and fans made the Boo their after-work stop. The affection ran deep enough to travel. In 2003, Larry Dierker, the longtime Astros broadcaster and former manager, was so taken with the place that he built a replica of it at Minute Maid Park (now Daikin Park), the team's ballpark in Houston, Texas.

The bar found its way into fiction as well. Tim Dorsey's 2006 novel The Big Bamboo takes its title from the lounge, and his main characters return to it repeatedly to mark special occasions.

## Last Call

Bruce Muir died in 1999, and when longtime bartender Ray Guenther retired, the bar's essential character changed. A rowdier tradition took hold in their absence, including a photo album of patrons kept under the bar and bras stapled to the ceiling, a far cry from the swing-era hideaway Muir had built. The album's whereabouts are unknown today.

The end came in stages. Hurricane damage closed the Big Bamboo in 2004. In December 2005 a fire destroyed the building, which officials believed was started by vagrants trying to keep warm. The bar never reopened. On September 1, 2020, the vacant land sold for $1,150,000, and construction began there in December 2022 on a seven-story, 160-unit condo hotel called The Flats Resort and Spa (also marketed as The Flats Orlando), then expected to finish in 2025; the project's website still says it is not yet open to guests. The ambulance, the name tags and the Test Track seats are gone, and the Boo lives on in the memories of its regulars and in the pages of a novel.
