# Lake Eola Park

Downtown Orlando's favorite gathering place grew from an 1883 land gift around a sinkhole lake, and its color-changing fountain, knocked out by lightning in 2009, remains an official symbol of the city.

## Introduction

The lake at the heart of downtown Orlando is a sinkhole. About 100 feet east of its famous fountain lies the sinkhole itself, roughly 80 feet deep, hidden beneath a surface that gives no hint of it. On a calm evening the water is anything but ominous. A 0.9-mile path circles its 23 acres, the fountain changes colors at night like a light show, and Lake Eola Park has become the place where Orlando gathers, from Sunday farmers markets to Fourth of July fireworks.

## A Gift With Conditions

The park began with a generous man who wanted guarantees. Jacob Summerlin was a wealthy Orlando resident, owner of the Summerlin Hotel, the first City Council president and the lender who financed construction of Orlando's courthouse in the 1870s. In 1883, he came to a city council meeting and offered a large tract of land around the lake, on the condition that it be beautified and turned into a park.

He spelled out the terms. The city had to plant trees and build a "driveway" around the lake, and reverter clauses in the contract allowed Summerlin's heirs to reclaim the property if the city failed to keep its promises.

## The Fountain and the Lightning

The fountain rising from the lake is an official symbol of Orlando, and in late August 2009 it went dark. Lightning struck it, leaving it inoperable. The city carried a $1 million insurance policy on the fountain, but with a $500,000 deductible, and Orlando was then in a budget crisis. For a time, no one knew whether the city's icon would ever run again.

On October 15, 2009, Mayor Buddy Dyer answered the question. The city would not only repair the fountain but "also replace its cracked plastic skin and install a state-of-the-art system of lights and water jets," at a cost of $2.3 million. The work took nearly two years. The fountain was rededicated and resumed operation on Independence Day, July 4, 2011.

## A Park That Keeps Growing

Lake Eola Park has pushed outward more than once. In 1993, the city closed Washington Street, which had run between Lee's Lakeside restaurant and the park, pulling the restaurant and the Post Parkside Apartments inside the park's boundaries and creating the International Food Court.

In July 2013, the park expanded again to the southeast to take in East Washington Street, adding 1.36 acres of lawn, new LED lighting, wider sidewalks, nearly 4,000 square feet of paved patio and 7,500 more square feet of brick paving. That September, the city opened Eola House at 512 East Washington Street as the park's welcome center, gift shop and offices. The same year, the nonprofit See Art Orlando installed six sculptures in the park.

## Orlando's Front Yard

Four streets frame the park: East Robinson Street, Rosalind Avenue, East Central Boulevard and North Eola Drive. On the west side stands the Walt Disney Amphitheater, which hosts community events and performances year-round. On the east side are a Chinese pagoda and a playground.

The calendar keeps the lawns busy. In March, the Spin City Classic has brought professional and amateur bicycle racing to the surrounding streets. Fireworks Over the Fountain fills the Fourth of July, and in October the Come Out With Pride festival brings a parade, vendors and fireworks. Fiesta in the Park, on the first full weekend of November, spreads 600 booths of arts and crafts around the lake. From 2003 to 2020, Russian Ballet Orlando performed The Nutcracker at the amphitheater every December. And every Sunday, the Orlando Farmers Market sets up by the water.
