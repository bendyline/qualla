# Albin Polasek House and Studio

On the shore of Lake Osceola in Winter Park, a Czech-born sculptor's Mediterranean-style retirement home became a museum and sculpture garden, and later welcomed a neighbor's house that arrived across the water in two halves.

## Introduction

The house crossed Lake Osceola in two pieces. Slated for demolition in 2013, the Capen-Showalter House was saved when a coalition of Winter Park preservationists raised roughly half a million dollars, cut it in half and floated both halves across the lake to a new home on the grounds of the Albin Polasek Museum & Sculpture Gardens. It was an unusual arrival for an unusual place. The museum, known locally as The Polasek, occupies three lakefront acres at 633 Osceola Avenue in Winter Park, Florida, around the former home and studio of a sculptor who kept working long after his body tried to stop him.

## From Moravia to the Lakeshore

Albin Polasek was born in 1879 in Frenštát, Moravia, then part of Austria-Hungary and now in the Czech Republic. He immigrated to the United States in 1901 to work as a wood carver. He went on to head the sculpture department at the Art Institute of Chicago, where he remained for decades, and in 1950 he retired to Winter Park, Florida.

Retirement did not go as planned. Within months he suffered a stroke that left his left side paralyzed; most accounts date it to 1950, though the museum's own history says 1951. He kept sculpting anyway, and he went on to complete 18 major works using only his right hand. Toward the end of 1950 he also married a former student, the sculptor Ruth Sherwood, who died less than two years later.

## A Studio Opened to the Public

In 1961, Polasek opened his Mediterranean-style home and studio to the public, and the museum counts that year as its founding. He died in 1965. Many of the original furnishings are still on display in the rooms where he lived and worked.

The permanent collection holds about 200 pieces, focused mainly on American representational sculpture. It includes works by Polasek and by Ruth Sherwood, along with antiquities Polasek collected. Changing exhibitions of local artists fill the galleries, their works on view and for sale.

The Polasek is a member of Historic Artists' Homes and Studios, a program of the National Trust for Historic Preservation that, as of 2026, lists dozens of the living and working spaces of American painters, sculptors, ceramicists, photographers and furniture designers. Florida's other members include the Ann Norton Sculpture Gardens in West Palm Beach and Bonnet House in Fort Lauderdale. On May 2, 2000, it was added to the National Register of Historic Places as the Albin Polasek House and Studio.

## Gardens by the Water

Outside, the grounds overlooking Lake Osceola are as much a draw as the galleries. Visitors can wander a butterfly garden and two water gardens, admire the cycad collection that lines the museum's front drive, and find container gardens scattered throughout the property, with sculpture set among the plantings.

The museum is a nonprofit, funded by donations and memberships, and all of that money goes to the Albin Polasek Foundation, which preserves, maintains and runs the site. Docent-led tours of the grounds and of the Capen-Showalter House are included with regular admission, with the house tours by advance reservation, and a free audio tour is also offered.

## The House That Floated

The rescue of the Capen-Showalter House took a village. The Polasek teamed with the Winter Park History Museum, the Friends of Casa Feliz and community supporters to raise the money to relocate it rather than lose it. The move won the 2014 Organizational Achievement Award from the Florida Trust for Historic Preservation. Today the 4,200-square-foot house holds museum offices, exhibits and artwork, and it hosts workshops, meetings and special events.

Each April, the museum also turns the whole town into a studio. During the Winter Park Paint Out, more than twenty artists have seven days to create works in public on the streets of Winter Park. Their finished paintings hang in the gallery's "Wet Room" and are sold, with a portion of the proceeds supporting the museum, and for that week the museum has been free to the public.
