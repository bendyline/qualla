# E.T. Adventure

Built with Steven Spielberg and scored by John Williams, E.T. Adventure has been flying bicycles over a miniature city and on to the Green Planet at Universal Studios Florida since the park opened in 1990, and it still says goodbye to each rider by name.

## Introduction

At the end of the ride, E.T. says your name. Before boarding, every guest gives a name and receives an "interplanetary passport," and after the bicycles have soared over the city and out into space, an animatronic E.T. thanks each rider individually by the name they gave. Then, in a flash of light, they are sent back to Earth, and on the way out some hear his voice one last time: "I'll be right here." E.T. Adventure, a suspended dark ride at Universal Studios Florida in Orlando, has been delivering that personal goodbye since 1990.

## An Audition With a Giant Ape

The ride's story starts with King Kong. In 1986, as King Kong Encounter was nearing completion on the Studio Tour at Universal Studios Hollywood, Steven Spielberg met with producer Peter Alexander, a former Disney Imagineer. Alexander showed him the 30-foot animatronic Kong, and Spielberg was impressed. The attraction's success helped revive Universal's plans for a Florida park.

From 1989, Spielberg worked with Universal Creative on an attraction built around his 1982 film E.T. the Extra-Terrestrial, with Alexander leading development. Alexander knew the challenge. The emotional impact of a two-hour film, he observed, is not easily translated into a ride lasting six or eight minutes.

## Bicycles Over the City

The queue building is disguised as a film sound stage where E.T. is being shot. On screens, Spielberg explains the mission: E.T.'s homeworld, the Green Planet, is dying, and only E.T.'s healing touch can save it. Inside, the line winds through a dark forest of redwoods scented with pine, like the woods where the film's police chase took place, and guests pass E.T.'s communication device from the movie, including a Speak & Spell spelling out "H-E-L-P E-T."

Then riders climb onto bicycles. Suspended from above, they glide past police and NASA officials closing in, and just as capture seems certain, the bicycles lift off into the film's iconic flying chase, soaring over a miniature city with moving cars, a football stadium and baseball fields, and then out into space.

## The Green Planet

The story goes further than the film ever did. The designers drew on William Kotzwinkle's 1985 novel E.T.: The Book of the Green Planet, a sequel that shaped the look of E.T.'s home, its plant-like creatures and the character Botanicus. Spielberg wanted the story kept personal and the alien world friendly.

Through a portal of flashing lights, riders reach the Green Planet, where Botanicus pleads with them to help save E.T.'s friends. E.T.'s healing touch revives them, and the dark opening gives way to a joyful celebration of baby E.T.s drumming, swinging on ropes and singing, with water effects all around. Sally Corporation designed and built more than 300 animatronics and exotic plants for the ride, and John Williams, who scored the original film, composed an original score for it.

## The Last One Still Flying

E.T. Adventure was successful enough to travel. Another installation opened at Universal Studios Hollywood in 1991 and closed in 2003, making way for Revenge of the Mummy. A third opened at Universal Studios Japan in 2001 and closed in 2009. Only the Orlando original remains in operation.

That makes it something of a survivor. Of all the rides that opened with Universal Studios Florida, E.T. Adventure is the only one still operating. More than three decades on, the bicycles still fly, and E.T. still says goodbye by name.
