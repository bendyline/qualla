# Tinker Field

For a century, Orlando's Tinker Field hosted big-league spring training, minor-league seasons and, on one March day in 1964, Martin Luther King Jr.'s only public speech in the city, before its grandstands fell to a stadium renovation.

## Introduction

On March 6, 1964, Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. walked out to the pitcher's mound at Tinker Field and spoke to a crowd of roughly two thousand people. It was his only public speech in Orlando, and it happened not in a church or an auditorium but on a baseball diamond on the west side of downtown. That moment is one of the most historic non-baseball events in a ballpark whose story runs from 1914 to its demolition in 2015, and today both halves of its legacy, the civil rights and the baseball, are remembered at Tinker Field History Plaza.

## A Century of Springs

Baseball first came to the site in 1914. The first known stadium, built in 1923, was all wood and seated 1,500, and the park took its name from Joe Tinker, a Baseball Hall of Fame inductee. For the next decade the Cincinnati Reds held spring training there, until 1933. The Brooklyn Dodgers followed in 1934 and 1935.

Then came the longest tenancy. In 1936, Clark Griffith moved the Washington Senators' spring training to Orlando, and the franchise kept coming back, even after relocating and becoming the Minnesota Twins, until after the 1990 season. Tinker Field was also home to the minor-league Orlando Rays until they moved to Cracker Jack Stadium in 2000. Not every game was baseball. College football was played here too, including the 1937 Orange Blossom Classic.

## Seats From Washington

The stadium was rebuilt in 1963, and it came with a remarkable inheritance. When Griffith Stadium in Washington, D.C., was demolished, nearly 1,000 of its seats were moved to Tinker Field, where they remained until the ballpark's final days. Some things never changed. The old press box beside the home-side dugout was the original, and it can be seen in photographs as early as the 1920s.

By its final years, the ballpark held 5,100 people and sat directly against the western side of the Orlando Citrus Bowl football stadium. In 2004, Tinker Field was added to the National Register of Historic Places.

## Squeezed Out by a Neighbor

That proximity proved fatal. In late January 2014, at the groundbreaking for the Citrus Bowl's reconstruction, the city announced that Tinker Field's grandstands and every other remaining building would be torn down. The stadium's expansion would shorten right field so much that the ballpark would be unusable even if the entire complex were renovated. On March 9, 2015, the Orlando City Council approved an ordinance to demolish the grandstands and buildings and set aside money to re-create the area around the field. The grandstands came down in April 2015, and the city sold off the remaining seats that year. The football stadium next door was later renamed Camping World Stadium.

## History Plaza

The field did not simply vanish. In September 2015, the city held a public input meeting and unveiled preliminary plans to memorialize Tinker Field. The result, Tinker Field History Plaza, opened in May 2018, honoring both civil rights and baseball in Orlando, the two stories that meet on that pitcher's mound.

The open ground still draws crowds. Tinker Field has hosted the Electric Daisy Carnival each November, a three-day electronic dance music festival with celebrity DJs, visual effects, rides and art, and the one-day Orlando Vans Warped Tour rock festival came each June or July. On September 6, 2015, residents of the surrounding West Lakes neighborhoods held a Family Fun Day on the grounds ahead of the MEAC/SWAC Challenge.

## Flight Context

Tinker Field History Plaza is at approximately 28.5388°N, 81.4046°W in the West Lakes neighborhoods on the west side of downtown Orlando, Florida, immediately west of Camping World Stadium, formerly the Citrus Bowl. From 1,500–2,500 feet, the big football stadium is the obvious landmark; the old ballfield site lies against its western side. Orlando Executive Airport (KORL) is about 4.5 statute miles east, so remain clear of its Class D airspace unless in contact with the tower. Orlando International Airport (KMCO) is about 9.5 miles southeast within Orlando's Class B airspace. Summer afternoon thunderstorms are common over central Florida.
