# Bulow Plantation

Coquina ruins in the Florida woods mark a plantation built on enslaved labor, visited by Audubon, and burned after just fifteen years in the Second Seminole War.

**Location:** 29.4361, -81.1411

## Introduction

Bulow Plantation lasted only fifteen years. Founded in 1821, worked by enslaved people and once visited by John James Audubon, it was abandoned when the Second Seminole War broke out and burned in January 1836. What the fire could not destroy was the stone. Built of brick and coquina, much of the structure has survived, and today its ruins stand in the woods of a Florida state park, near a creek that has since become a kayaking route.

## Before the Bulows

Long before any plantation, this was Timucua country. The nearest known Timucua settlement was Nocoroco, to the south in what is now Ormond Beach, and the Timucua population died out during the eighteenth century because of disease. The first known European structure at the site was not a building but a road. The Old Kings Road, laid out in the eighteenth century during British colonial rule, connected St. Augustine with the settlements and plantations to the south, and it still leads to the plantation site today.

## Built on Forced Labor

Development began in 1821 under Major Charles Wilhelm Bulow, who acquired nearly 4,700 acres. After his death in 1823, the estate passed to his son, John Joachim Bulow, who was just seventeen. About 2,200 acres were cleared for indigo, cotton, rice and sugarcane, and Bulow became one of the largest plantations in East Florida. That scale rested on the forced labor of enslaved Africans and African Americans, men and women whose work the whole enterprise depended on. The plantation's sugar mill, built sometime between the late 1820s and about 1831 (accounts differ), was made of coquina, a rock composed of shells. At Christmas 1831, into January 1832, John Bulow hosted the artist and naturalist John James Audubon, who explored the area as part of his continuing study of American birds.

## Fire in January 1836

The Second Seminole War began in December 1835, after a treaty calling for the Seminoles to move west that most of them rejected. In the war's opening phase, sugar plantations along the Atlantic coast south of St. Augustine were destroyed, and many enslaved people on those plantations joined the Seminoles. Bulow, like its neighbors, was abandoned. The plantation was burned in January 1836. Because it was built of brick and coquina, much of the structure has survived, while other parts remain only as fragmentary ruins. The state acquired the property in 1945, and it was dedicated as a historic park in the 1950s.

## Two Parks and a Creek

Today Bulow is really two state parks joined together. Bulow Plantation Ruins Historic State Park holds the ruins and an interpretive center and is open Thursday through Monday, while Bulow Creek State Park runs south for several miles along the Intracoastal Waterway into Volusia County, linked to the ruins by a trail of nearly seven miles, parts of which have recently been closed for storm cleanup, so check with the park. Bulow Creek, which flows past the plantation on its way to the Halifax River, has become a kayaking route, with a boat ramp for launching. Both parks have picnic benches, but neither allows overnight stays; for camping, Tomoka State Park lies to the south in Ormond Beach. The one real hazard is alligators, so take care near the water after dark or when paddling.

## Flight Context

Bulow Plantation lies at 29.4361°N, 81.1411°W near the Flagler–Volusia county line, between Interstate 95 to the west and the Intracoastal Waterway and Flagler Beach to the east. From 1,500–2,500 feet, look for the winding line of Bulow Creek heading toward the Halifax River; the ruins themselves are hard to pick out, so use the creek and the Old Dixie Highway as references. Flagler Executive (KFIN) is about 4 nm west-northwest, Ormond Beach Municipal (KOMN) about 8 nm south, and Daytona Beach International (KDAB) about 16 nm south-southeast. The region sits between Central Florida's subtropical climate and North Florida's more temperate one, so expect humid summer heat with afternoon thunderstorms, and occasional winter cold snaps.
