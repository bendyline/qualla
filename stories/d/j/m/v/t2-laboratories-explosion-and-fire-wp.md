# T2 Laboratories explosion and fire

On December 19, 2007, a runaway reaction in a batch reactor at T2 Laboratories on Jacksonville's Northside exploded with the force of 1,400 pounds of TNT, killing four people and injuring 32 others.

## Introduction

Before the blast, people nearby heard a sound like a jet engine. It was the roar of high-pressure gas venting from a chemical reactor at T2 Laboratories on Jacksonville's Northside, a reactor already out of control. On December 19, 2007, it exploded with a force equivalent to 1,400 pounds of TNT, scattering debris up to a mile from the plant. Four people were killed, and 32 others were injured, including 28 people at neighboring businesses.

## A Specialty Chemical Plant

T2 Laboratories specialized in manufacturing specialty chemicals, primarily additives for gasoline. On the day of the disaster, workers were producing methylcyclopentadienyl manganese tricarbonyl, known as MCMT, in a batch reactor. Keeping that reaction under control depended on the reactor's cooling system, and the system had no backup. When it failed, there was nothing else to hold the reaction in check.

## Anatomy of a Runaway

In September 2009, the U.S. Chemical Safety and Hazard Investigation Board released its report on what had happened. When the cooling failed, the reaction entered a thermal runaway. Pressure inside the reactor rapidly reached 400 pounds per square inch and burst the reactor's rupture disc, but venting was not enough to slow the reaction, and that escaping gas produced the jet-engine sound witnesses described. As pressure rose, so did temperature, until the solvent in the reactor, diglyme, reached the point at which it began to decompose. Pressure and temperature kept climbing until the reactor detonated violently. The blast was so severe that four buildings in the immediate vicinity of the plant were condemned.

## Four Lives and a Hellish Fire

The explosion killed four people, aged 35 to 49, and injured 32 others, most of them at neighboring businesses. What followed was a major fire. Every hazardous materials unit in Jacksonville and more than 100 firefighters fought the blaze, which a spokesman called a "hellish inferno." For the families of those who died, the loss came less than a week before Christmas. The investigators' account of how it happened, a single cooling system with no backup, a pressure relief that could not keep pace, and a solvent pushed past its limits, is a sober record of how quickly a chemical reaction can turn deadly once its cooling is lost.
