# Battle of Alligator Creek Bridge

On June 30, 1778, Patriot horsemen chasing Loyalist rangers through the Florida backcountry ran straight into a British redoubt at Alligator Creek, the only major fight of Georgia's last, failed attempt to conquer British East Florida.

**Location:** 30.5664, -81.8333

**Categories:** Battles of the American Revolutionary War in Florida, Conflicts in 1778, Nassau County, Florida, British East Florida, Battles of the southern theater of the American Revolutionary War

## Introduction

Neither side wore regular uniforms, and at first that caused chaos. On June 30, 1778, a column of Loyalist rangers came running down the road toward a bridge over Alligator Creek, in what is now Nassau County, Florida, with Patriot horsemen hard on their heels. The British regulars dug in at the bridge saw a crowd of men without uniforms and assumed they were all friends. For a few confused moments, pursuers and pursued looked exactly alike. Then the regulars understood, and the firing began in the only major engagement of the American campaign to conquer British East Florida.

## A Border of Raids

For the first three years of the Revolutionary War, the frontier between rebel Georgia and loyal East Florida saw constant raiding. The St. Marys River formed the border then, as it does now. Georgia's leaders believed that St. Augustine, East Florida's capital, was vulnerable, and they kept pushing expeditions to take it. The first, in 1776, fell apart when Continental General Charles Lee was called north. The second, in 1777, was plagued by problems of command and supply, and the only unit that actually entered Florida was ambushed at the Battle of Thomas Creek. Meanwhile East Florida's governor, Patrick Tonyn, posted Lieutenant Colonel Thomas Brown's Loyalist rangers along the St. Marys. Sometimes supported by Creeks and Seminoles, Brown's men raided south Georgia plantations for cattle to help feed the province. Tonyn also squabbled with Major General Augustine Prevost, the British Army commander, over control of Brown's rangers and over strategy; Prevost was under orders to stay on the defensive, while Tonyn wanted a more vigorous defense.

## The Long March South

In February 1778 Georgia's assembly authorized Governor John Houstoun to try again, over the objections of General Robert Howe, the Continental commander in the South. The plan gained urgency when Brown's rangers captured and burned Fort Howe on the Altamaha River in a surprise attack. In early April, Georgia's 400 Continentals marched south and reoccupied the fort, and by early May militia and South Carolina Continentals had swelled the force to some 1,300 men. The camp was miserable. The weather was hot, desertions were frequent, and at least eleven men were executed. The army crossed the Altamaha on May 28 and crept south, reaching the St. Marys on June 26. There the expedition nearly broke down. Houstoun wanted to march straight for St. Augustine; Howe wanted to take Fort Tonyn first. At an impasse, Howe moved on the fort with his own troops while Houstoun's militia stayed put. Brown burned Fort Tonyn and slipped into the swamps, and Howe occupied its ruins on June 29.

## Chased Into the Redoubt

About fourteen miles south of the fort, the way south crossed Alligator Creek, a tributary of the Nassau River. There Major James Marcus Prevost, the general's younger brother, waited with about 200 men from the 16th and 60th Regiments and Daniel McGirth's Loyalist rangers behind a redoubt of logs and brush. On June 30, Howe sent James Screven south with 100 cavalry to find Brown. Brown laid a trap, sending one company to circle behind the horsemen while the rest hid along the road. Deserters betrayed the flanking party, and most of its men were captured or killed. Brown fell back toward the bridge, but Screven's riders caught his company just short of it and drove them straight into the British position. Once the confusion cleared, Prevost's regulars opened fire while some of Brown's men worked around Screven's flank. Men went down on both sides. Accounts differ on whether Screven or Colonel Elijah Clarke was among the wounded, and some of the Patriots narrowly escaped being trapped before the retreat was ordered.

## Out of Rice

The next day Major Prevost led his, Brown's and McGirth's men out and surprised a Patriot crew repairing a bridge, then withdrew, felling trees across the road behind them. There was no second push from the Americans. The Continentals ran out of rice and had to beg the Georgia militia for supplies. The militia did not cross the St. Marys until July 6, by which time disease and desertion had cut the Continental force to only 400 effective soldiers. Hunger and the endless command dispute finished the expedition, which began its retreat on July 14. It effectively ended Georgia's ambition to take East Florida. The war was not finished with the men who fought at the bridge, though. In November 1778, James Screven was shot and mortally wounded in a surprise attack by Thomas Brown's rangers. Even the battlefield remains uncertain. The bridge site has long been placed in central Callahan, where a historical marker now stands, but some historians believe the actual crossing lay somewhat farther east.

## Flight Context

The traditional battle site is marked at about 30.566°N, 81.833°W in central Callahan, Nassau County, Florida, northwest of Jacksonville; some historians place the actual bridge somewhat farther east. From 2,000–3,000 feet, look for the small town of Callahan amid low, wooded country, with the St. Marys River, the Georgia–Florida border, winding to the north. Jacksonville International Airport (KJAX) lies about 9 nm to the east-southeast, and Hilliard Airpark (FAA identifier 01J; no ICAO code) about 8 nm to the north-northwest. Summer afternoons bring frequent thunderstorms; fall and winter usually offer clearer air.
