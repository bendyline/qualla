# Miami Air International Flight 293

On a stormy night in May 2019, a Boeing 737 charter from Guantanamo Bay slid off the runway at Naval Air Station Jacksonville into the St. Johns River, and all 143 people aboard were rescued.

**Location:** 30.2318, -81.6703

**Categories:** Aviation accidents and incidents in the United States in 2019, Accidents and incidents involving the Boeing 737 Next Generation, Aviation accidents and incidents involving runway overruns, Airliner accidents and incidents in Florida, Jacksonville, Florida

## Introduction

The jet came to rest in the St. Johns River, beyond the end of the runway it had been unable to stop on. It never sank. But the brackish river found its way in through breaches in the fuselage, soaking many of the passengers seated in the front and middle of the cabin, while the rows at the back filled with several inches of water. It was the night of May 3, 2019, and a thunderstorm was pounding Naval Air Station Jacksonville. All 136 passengers and seven crew would come out of it alive.

## A Charter From Guantanamo

Miami Air International Flight 293 was a military charter from Guantanamo Bay to Naval Air Station Jacksonville. The aircraft was a Boeing 737-800, specifically a 737-81Q with manufacturer's serial number 30618 and line number 830, registered N732MA. It had first flown on April 12, 2001, and was delivered to Miami Air two weeks later.

It left Guantanamo with its left-hand thrust reverser inoperative, which the master minimum equipment list allowed, but which left the jet with only one working thrust reverser once it touched down. As it approached Jacksonville, the weather turned. Thunderstorms began at 9:04 p.m., and by 9:22 p.m. the airfield was reporting heavy rain and thunderstorms, with a light wind from 350 degrees at four knots. The 737 landed in the storm, overran the runway and skidded into the river.

## Everyone Out

Emergency crews, including more than 50 firefighters, rescued every one of the 136 passengers and seven crew members. Early reports said 21 people were taken to the hospital, none critically hurt, and the NTSB's final report counted a single minor injury among the 143 occupants. The losses were in the cargo hold: at least three pets carried there are presumed to have died. Authorities were also concerned about fuel spreading into the river and worked to contain it.

The aircraft could not be saved. Eighteen years after its first flight, it was written off, the 17th Boeing 737-800 to be lost.

## The Runway Had the Last Word

The National Transportation Safety Board investigated alongside Boeing and the United States Navy, and issued an update on May 23, 2019 and its final report on August 4, 2021. Early attention fell on the inoperative thrust reverser and on the pilot's request to change runways.

The Board found plenty that had gone wrong. The airline's guidelines for evaluating runways were inadequate. The crew did not abandon an approach that had become unstabilized under the captain's heavy workload, touched down with excessive airspeed and was slow to deploy the speed brakes, and the first officer had limited experience in jets as heavy as the 737.

None of it, the NTSB concluded, changed the outcome. The runway was ungrooved, and heavy rain on its surface caused the 737 to hydroplane. Even if the landing had been flown perfectly, the Board found, the aircraft would still have been unable to stop.

## Flight Context

The accident site is at about 30.232 N, 81.670 W, at the end of a runway at Naval Air Station Jacksonville (Towers Field, KNIP), where the airfield meets the St. Johns River on the west bank, south of downtown Jacksonville, Florida. KNIP is an active military field; civilian pilots should remain clear of its airspace unless authorized. From 2,000-3,000 ft the wide St. Johns and the runways jutting toward it are unmistakable. Nearby civil airports: Herlong Recreational Airport (KHEG), about 7.6 nm west-northwest; Jacksonville Executive at Craig (KCRG), about 10 nm northeast; Cecil Airport (KVQQ), about 11 nm west. Florida summer thunderstorms, like the one that night, can bring sudden heavy rain and standing water on runways.
