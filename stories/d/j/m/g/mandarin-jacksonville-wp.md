# Mandarin (Jacksonville)

On the St. Johns River at Jacksonville's southern edge, Mandarin grew from British plantations into an orange-shipping village that Harriet Beecher Stowe made her winter home and helped sell to the nation.

**Location:** 30.1603, -81.6594

**Categories:** Neighborhoods in Jacksonville, Florida, History of Jacksonville, Florida, Populated places on the St. Johns River, Harriet Beecher Stowe, African-American history

## Introduction

"A tumble-down, wild, panicky kind of life—this general happy-go-luckiness which Florida inculcates." Harriet Beecher Stowe, the author of Uncle Tom's Cabin, wrote those words about the Florida she came to love as a winter resident of Mandarin, a riverside village on the east bank of the St. Johns. She called the place "a tropical paradise." Today Mandarin is a neighborhood at the southern tip of Jacksonville, bounded by Beauclerc to the north, Julington Creek to the south and the broad river to the west. It is still known for its moss-draped old oaks, its parks and marinas, and its water views, and for a history that reaches back to the 1760s.

## A Plantation Shore

The British established the area in the 1760s. Among their holdings was a 10,000-acre plantation created by Francis Levett Sr., where the Levetts grew a variety of crops using the labor of enslaved people. The 1783 Treaty of Paris returned the region to Spain, and many more plantations followed under Spanish rule.

The planter Zephaniah Kingsley, whose Laurel Grove estate lay across the river, freed his wife, Anna Kingsley, in 1811; he had enslaved her. In 1813 she received a Spanish land grant of five acres in Mandarin. She went on to take an active part in managing the family's plantations, and acquired land, and enslaved people, of her own.

## Named for an Orange

In 1830 a prominent resident, Calvin Reed, named the settlement Mandarin, after the mandarin orange. Through the 19th century it was a small farming village that loaded oranges, grapefruit, lemons and other produce onto the steamships plying the St. Johns, bound for Jacksonville and points north. The river also brought war: in 1864 the Union steamship Maple Leaf struck a Confederate mine and sank just off Mandarin Point.

After the Civil War, the censuses of 1870 and 1880 recorded that most of Mandarin's residents were African American. Three churches were founded in the area, among them the Philip R. Cousin AME Church, established in 1886. For years most of the land was planted in oranges, until a freeze in the early 1890s destroyed much of the crop. Rather than replant, many growers sold their former groves to developers.

## Mrs. Stowe's Winter Summers

Harriet Beecher Stowe began wintering in Mandarin in 1867 and bought a cottage there; for seventeen winters she welcomed the tourists stepping off the river steamers. She had promised her Boston publisher another novel. Instead, captivated by northeast Florida, she produced a series of sketches of its land and people, written in 1872 and published in 1873 as Palmetto Leaves.

The book never approached the sales of Uncle Tom's Cabin, but it did something else. Its idyllic scenes of picnics, sailing and river excursions in a tropical "winter summer" land became, in one biographer's words, "the first promotional writing about Florida ever," and rich and fashionable visitors began arriving to see her. Her name lives on in Mandarin: the Episcopal Church of Our Saviour grew from a group that met for Bible readings with Calvin Stowe and his wife, and a life-size bronze of Stowe sits at the Walter Jones Historical Park. Her name did not land everywhere, though: when a new public high school opened in 1990, several prominent citizens urged that it be named for Stowe, but the idea never won wide support, and it became Mandarin High School.

## A Village Inside a City

In 1968 Jacksonville and most of Duval County merged into a single consolidated government, and Mandarin ceased to exist as a political entity. Its history did not disappear with it. The Mandarin Museum & Historical Society, founded in 1989, restored the Mandarin Historic Post Office and the Walter Jones General Store, and in 2000 the city opened the 10-acre Walter Jones Historical Park on the site.

The society kept collecting: an early 20th-century sawmill, and a late 19th-century log cabin that belonged to the Losco family, part of what Wikipedia describes as the largest wine operation in northeast Florida. By 2016 it had opened the 1898 St. Joseph's Mission Schoolhouse for African-American Children, moved onto museum property, with displays on the area's African American and Gullah Geechee heritage. Older still are the graves: headstones in the Lofton Cemetery date back to 1891, and the Mandarin Cemetery sits on a site that, by one account, was once part of the Seminole Wars.

## Flight Context

Mandarin is centered near 30.160 N, 81.659 W on the east bank of the St. Johns River at the southern edge of Jacksonville, Florida, opposite Orange Park, with Julington Creek marking its southern boundary. From 2,000-3,000 ft the river here is very wide, and Mandarin Point juts into it; residential neighborhoods under a heavy tree canopy line the shore. Jacksonville NAS Towers Field (KNIP, military) sits across the river about 4.7 nm to the north-northwest, so stay clear of its airspace. Other nearby airports: Herlong Recreational Airport (KHEG), about 10 nm northwest; Cecil Airport (KVQQ), about 12 nm west-northwest; Jacksonville Executive at Craig (KCRG), about 13 nm northeast. Summer afternoons bring frequent thunderstorms.
