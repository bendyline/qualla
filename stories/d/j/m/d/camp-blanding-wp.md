# Camp Blanding

In Clay County, a National Guard camp born in 1939 swelled during World War II into one of the largest training bases in the country, briefly holding a population said to rival Florida's fourth-largest city.

**Location:** 29.9519, -81.9799

**Categories:** Military installations in Florida, Installations of the United States Army National Guard, World War II sites in the United States, Military and war museums in Florida, Buildings and structures in Clay County, Florida

## Introduction

At one point during World War II, Camp Blanding was said to hold as many people as the fourth-largest city in Florida. It was a wartime city in all but name of 10,000 buildings laced with 125 miles of paved roads, served by a hospital described as the largest in the state and by camp newspapers with names like the Bayonet. More than 800,000 soldiers trained at Camp Blanding during the war. Today the city is gone, but the camp remains, sprawling across some 73,000 acres near Starke and taking in Kingsley Lake as the primary training ground of the Florida National Guard.

## A New Home for the Guard

Camp Blanding exists because the Navy wanted the Guard's old home. Florida's National Guard had trained at Camp Foster on the St. Johns River near Jacksonville until the Navy took that site for Naval Air Station Jacksonville. In 1939 the Guard started over on 30,000 acres here, naming the new post for Albert H. Blanding, who had been commissioned in the Florida National Guard in 1899 and had risen to major general and Chief of the National Guard Bureau. Its quiet beginnings did not last. In 1940, as the threat of war grew and the Army expanded, Camp Blanding became a federal facility housing two infantry divisions and their supporting units. Although it uses a Starke mailing address, the installation lies entirely within Clay County, just across the line from Starke's Bradford County.

## Nine Divisions and a Parachute Regiment

Between 1940 and 1943, nine Army infantry divisions trained here, including the 1st, 29th, 30th, 31st, 36th, 43rd, 63rd, 66th and 79th. In October 1942 the 508th Parachute Infantry Regiment was stood up at the camp. The next year, Camp Blanding became an infantry replacement training center, preparing soldiers to fill gaps in divisions already in the field, and it supplied a high percentage of the replacements sent to Army combat units. Among the trainees were Japanese American volunteers bound for the 442nd Regimental Combat Team. After the war, the camp served as headquarters of the Military District of Florida under Brigadier General Charles S. Kilburn.

## Behind the Wire

Not everyone at Camp Blanding came by choice. In 1942 a separate stockade at the base held German and a few Italian civilians brought from Costa Rica, Guatemala and Panama and interned as enemy aliens, between about 170 and 300 people depending on the count, before they were moved to permanent internment camps that summer. From 1942 to 1946 the base also ran a prisoner-of-war camp for Germans, the base camp for fifteen smaller side camps, and by one count its main camp held about 1,200 men at its peak. Six or seven German prisoners died here, depending on the count, between about July 1944 and October 1945 and were buried in a small cemetery on the grounds. In April 1946 their remains were moved to Fort Benning, Georgia, but the original grave markers still stand. Older graves lie scattered across the reservation as well: five settler-era cemeteries, unmaintained and heavily overgrown. One of them, Oak Grove Cemetery, took burials from 1829 until 1968, well after the impact area was established around it, and now sits unreachable inside the range's buffer zone.

## Still Training

Camp Blanding never stopped being busy. In the 1970s an expeditionary airfield with two gravel runways able to handle C-130 Hercules transports was added, and the artillery range and parachute drop zones were reactivated. The post is now the main training ground for the 53rd Infantry Brigade Combat Team, home to the headquarters company of the 3rd Battalion, 20th Special Forces Group, and to several non-flying Air National Guard units, and it serves as Florida's alternate Emergency Operations Center. In 2008 it began hosting an Army air assault course to meet demand from Iraq and Afghanistan. Navy, Marine Corps and Air Force aircraft use a bombing and strafing target on the south end. Each April, Army ROTC cadets from more than a dozen universities in Florida, Georgia and Puerto Rico gather for a five-day field exercise named for Audie Murphy, and Civil Air Patrol cadets from the Florida Wing continue to use the site for encampments.

## Memory at the Gate

Just outside the main entrance, the Camp Blanding Museum and Memorial Park has been open to the public since 1990. The museum occupies a restored World War II building and is free, with displays covering Florida's military history from World War I to the present. Outside, a self-guided memorial park lines up vehicles and aircraft from World War II onward, along with memorials to every unit that trained here during that war and to Florida Guard members who died in service. There is an airpark of fixed-wing and rotary aircraft, a recreated Vietnam-era artillery post called "Firebase Florida," and armored vehicles captured during the 1991 Gulf War and Operation Iraqi Freedom, both conflicts in which the Florida National Guard deployed.

## Flight Context

Camp Blanding lies in Clay County, Florida, at approximately 29.9519°N, 81.9799°W, east of Starke and north of Keystone Heights. Kingsley Lake, within the reservation, is a useful visual reference, and the post's expeditionary airfield, Camp Blanding Army Airfield (FAA identifier 2CB, military), lies within the post. The southern portion of the installation contains an active bombing and strafing target and an artillery impact area, so check current charts for restricted airspace and NOTAMs and remain outside any active restricted areas. Where airspace permits, 2,500–3,500 feet AGL gives a broad view of the reservation and its lakes. Keystone Heights Airport (FAA identifier 42J) is about 7 nm to the south-southwest, and Gainesville Regional Airport (KGNV) is about 22 nm to the southwest. Summer thunderstorms are common in the afternoons.
