# Lake Pithlachocco Canoe Site

When drought lowered Newnans Lake near Gainesville in 2000, it exposed more than 100 dugout canoes, many of them made with fire between 2,300 and 5,000 years ago.

**Location:** 29.6772, -82.2153

**Categories:** Archaeological sites in Florida, National Register of Historic Places in Alachua County, Florida, Dugout canoes, Archaic period in North America, Newnans Lake

## Introduction

In the first half of 2000, drought pulled the water back from Newnans Lake, just east of Gainesville, Florida, and the lake bottom began giving up boats. Surveys would eventually count the remains of more than a hundred dugout canoes lying in the mud, most along the northeast shore, some hidden beneath others. Most had been shaped more than 2,300 years ago, and some as long as 5,000 years ago, by people who hollowed logs with fire.

## A Fleet in the Mud

The drought exposed plank-built skiffs as well as dugouts. A few fragmentary canoes turned up on the opposite, southwest shore, and as archaeologists worked they kept finding more canoes lying beneath the ones they were excavating.

In June and July 2000, the Florida Bureau of Archaeological Research excavated 53 of the canoes for study, and when low water returned in May 2001 it revealed two more. Each canoe was measured and photographed, and samples were taken for radiocarbon dating and to identify the kind of tree it had been carved from. On March 27, 2001, the site, also known as the Newnan's Lake Canoe Site, was added to the National Register of Historic Places. It lies within the Newnans Lake Conservation Area.

## Boats Made With Fire

Every one of the canoes had been made by using fire to hollow out a log, and some showed signs that fire had also shaped their sides and ends. No tool marks were found. That fits what is known of Florida's Indigenous peoples at the time of first European contact, who used fire together with scraping tools to shape their canoes.

The builders favored conifers. All of the older canoes were made from conifer logs, most of them yellow pine, and one from a bald cypress. Nineteen of them, dated to between about 2,310 and 4,160 years before the present, had one or more thwarts, ridges of wood running across the interior. Nobody knows exactly what the thwarts were for.

## Five Thousand Years, Gone Soft

Radiocarbon dating showed that the lake had been used across an enormous span of time. Samples from 41 canoes returned dates between 5,000 and 2,300 years before the present, the Middle Archaic and early Late Archaic periods. Another thirteen were far younger, between 1,300 and 500 years old.

The researchers, Wheeler and colleagues, concluded that the canoes' positions on the lake bottom were random, likely the work of natural forces such as prevailing winds rather than a deliberate arrangement. And the oldest boats looked much like the newer ones. The Archaic canoes from Newnans Lake could not be distinguished from Post-Archaic canoes, made after 1000 B.C., found elsewhere in Florida.

Time had not been kind to the wood. Most of the Archaic canoes were fragmentary, and only five were judged to be at least three-quarters intact. The ends tended to survive best, because the wood there was thicker and held more of the tough heartwood. Everywhere else it had turned soft and spongy. In the end, none of the canoes was judged suitable for removal and preservation. What survives is the record: the measurements, the photographs and the dates.

## A Peninsula of Paddlers

The Newnans Lake find is part of a larger Florida story. Similar Archaic canoes have turned up elsewhere in the state, pointing to a peninsula-wide tradition of canoe-building going back 5,000 years. Outside Florida, Archaic canoes are rare in the eastern United States.

The reason may lie in rising water. Around 7,000 years ago sea levels rose rapidly, lifting the water table across the peninsula and creating new wetlands. The spread of canoes in Florida is likely tied to the cultures that grew up to use those wetlands, people who, on one shallow lake near Gainesville, left behind an entire fleet.

## Flight Context

The canoe site lies on the northeast shore of Newnans Lake at about 29.677 N, 82.215 W, east of Gainesville, Florida, off State Road 26. From 1,500-2,500 ft Newnans Lake is an unmistakable broad lake ringed by forest just east of the city. Gainesville Regional Airport (KGNV) is only about 3 nm to the west-northwest, so the lake lies under Gainesville's terminal airspace; coordinate with approach. Williston Municipal (X60) is about 24 nm southwest. Summer afternoons bring frequent thunderstorms.
