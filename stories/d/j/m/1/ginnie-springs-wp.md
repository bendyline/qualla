# Ginnie Springs

On the Santa Fe River in north Florida, Ginnie Springs draws cave divers, tubers and campers to its clear, cold water, and its popularity has made it a flashpoint in the fight over Florida's springs.

**Location:** 29.8360, -82.7000

**Categories:** Springs of Florida, Parks in Gilchrist County, Florida, Cave diving, Santa Fe River, Water conservation

## Introduction

Deep inside the cavern called the Ginnie Ballroom, an iron grate bars the way forward. It was not put there to keep the curious out of the spring altogether; Ginnie Springs, on the Santa Fe River in north Florida, is famous for letting people in. It was put there because of a rising toll of diver deaths. Owner Bob Wray installed it over the most dangerous part of the cave, and posted warning signs at the entrances to the nearby Devil's Spring system. The grate tells you two things at once about this place: how inviting the water is, and how much respect it demands.

## A Window Into the Aquifer

Ginnie Springs lies in Gilchrist County, about six and a half miles northwest of High Springs, on the south side of the Santa Fe River. The main spring pool is about 90 feet across and about 12 feet deep, big enough for swimmers to move around comfortably in the open water. From it a run roughly 35 feet wide and three feet deep flows for about 500 feet before reaching the river's floodplain. That steady flow keeps the water clear and moving. It is cold, about 72 degrees Fahrenheit near the springs, and beneath it lie caverns with sand and limestone floors that divers can reach.

According to the Springs Eternal Project, the Santa Fe River and its springs are classified by the Florida Department of Environmental Protection as an "Outstanding Florida Waterway," a designation that requires them to receive water-quality protection.

## The Wrays and the Divers

The Wray family has owned Ginnie Springs since 1971. As scuba diving grew in popularity, Bob Wray opened the springs to the public, and Ginnie's reputation took off. It became a nationally renowned diving destination and remains a popular one today.

That popularity came with a grim cost. As the number of divers rose, so did the number of deaths in the caves, which is what led Wray to close off the most dangerous part of the Ballroom with iron and to post warnings at Devil's Spring. The divers still come, but the grate marks the place where exploring stops.

## An Hour on the River

Not everyone comes to go under. Ginnie is one of the most popular places in Florida for river tubing, and the Ginnie Springs tube run on the Santa Fe takes about an hour from start to finish, the water warming as it leaves the springs for the river. Because the park is privately owned, tubers have long faced fewer restrictions than at state parks, and Ginnie was one of the few springs in Florida that allowed alcohol on the tube run. That changed on April 21, 2026, when a new policy confined open containers of alcohol to campsites.

Many visitors stay the night. The park has 123 campsites with water and electric hookups, across from the park's store, which can be reserved, and primitive sites that are first-come, first-served.

## Who Gets the Water

All that activity carries a price. Nitrogen pollution is a problem for every Florida spring, and at Ginnie the crowds make it worse by stirring up sediment and damaging native plants. The effects build over time, making it harder for the ecosystem to recover during busy seasons.

A larger fight ran from 2019 to 2023. Seven Springs Water Company, whose water is bottled at a plant owned by BlueTriton Brands (formerly Nestlé Waters North America), sought to renew a 1994 permit to draw water from the aquifer that feeds Ginnie and other nearby springs. The Suwannee River Water Management District signaled in 2020 that it would deny the permit, then approved it in 2021 after a judge sided with the company. After the Florida Springs Council challenged that approval, an administrative law judge backed the permit in October 2023, and in December 2023 the district's Governing Board signed off on renewal at an average of 984,000 gallons a day, down from the 1.152 million gallons a day the old permit allowed. The decision sparked an outcry. Environmentalists argued that the pumping would further degrade the state's springs and rivers, threaten minimum flow levels, harm the land and river, and add stress to springs already under pressure.

## Flight Context

From 1,500-2,500 ft follow the dark, tree-lined Santa Fe River as it winds west; the clear spring runs stand out against the darker river, with the park's campground clearings nearby.
