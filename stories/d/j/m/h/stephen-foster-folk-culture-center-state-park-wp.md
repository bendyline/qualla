# Stephen Foster Folk Culture Center State Park

On the banks of the Suwannee River in White Springs, Florida honors the composer of its state song, a man who never visited the state, with a museum and a 97-bell carillon first built for the 1939 World's Fair.

**Location:** 30.3311, -82.7669

## Introduction

Stephen Foster never set foot in Florida, and he never saw the Suwannee River. Yet on the river's banks at White Springs, the state has built him a memorial, complete with a museum and a bell tower built to ring out his songs through the day. The reason is a single song. "Old Folks at Home," better known by its opening line, "Way Down Upon the Swanee River," is Florida's state song, and Stephen Foster Folk Culture Center State Park exists because of it.

## A River Picked from an Atlas

Foster wrote the song in 1851 as a minstrel number, commissioned by E. P. Christy for his troupe, Christy's Minstrels. The river in the title came almost by chance: Foster's brother Morrison consulted an atlas and pointed out the "Swanee," a small river in Florida emptying into the Gulf of Mexico, and the name suited the melody. The original lyrics were written in the first person, in dialect, from the perspective of an enslaved African narrator longing for "the old plantation," and they included words now recognized as offensive slurs. Florida adopted the song as its official state song in 1935. In 2008 the state approved revised lyrics, and at the same time named a new state anthem, "Florida (Where the Sawgrass Meets the Sky)," while the revised "Old Folks at Home" remained the state song.

## A Memorial Proposed by Josiah Lilly

The idea of honoring Foster in Florida came from Josiah K. Lilly Sr., son of Eli Lilly, who proposed the memorial in 1931. The park that grew from that proposal, established in 1950, sits off U.S. 41 in White Springs, along the Suwannee in north Florida. Its Stephen Foster Museum honors the composer's accomplishments with dioramas and exhibits devoted to his famous songs, including the one that brought him here. A spring house survives from White Springs' days as a health resort, a reminder of an earlier reason to visit this stretch of the river.

## Ninety-Seven Bells

The park's most striking feature began life far from the Suwannee. J. C. Deagan, Inc. built the carillon for the spire of the Florida exhibit building at the 1939 World's Fair. At the fair it had 75 bells, weighed 25 metric tons and was billed as the largest carillon in the world by number of bells. It was a gift of Florida's Stephen Foster Memorial Association, which planned to install it at a new Foster memorial in White Springs once the fair closed. That took nearly two decades. The carillon was finally installed at the park in the summer of 1958, by which time its bell count had grown to 97. Deagan craftsmen needed more than a year to build the huge set, perhaps the greatest single manufacturing project in the firm's 78-year history. When the carillon is in service, the bells play Foster's songs through the day, and a second museum inside the tower tells the story of the composer and the instrument. In 2017 an electrical storm damaged the carillon, and repairs to restore the bells were begun afterward; the park's citizen support organization describes a full restoration as still pending.

## Flight Context

Stephen Foster Folk Culture Center State Park lies at 30.3311 N, 82.7669 W in White Springs, north Florida, off U.S. 41 on the banks of the Suwannee River. From the air, follow the winding Suwannee to White Springs and look for the park's carillon tower. A viewing altitude of 1,500 to 2,500 feet MSL shows the river and park grounds well. Lake City Gateway Airport (KLCQ) is about 13 nm to the southeast. Best seen in clear daytime conditions.
