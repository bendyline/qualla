# Paynes Prairie Preserve State Park

South of Gainesville, a 21,000-acre savanna that has been a Spanish cattle ranch, a Seminole stronghold and a steamboat lake now shelters wild bison, Cracker horses and more than 270 kinds of birds.

**Location:** 29.5831, -82.3331

**Categories:** State parks of Florida, National Natural Landmarks in Florida, Grasslands of Florida, Florida Native American Heritage Trail, Parks in Alachua County, Florida

## Introduction

For twenty years, steamboats crossed a prairie. Around 1871, Alachua Sink, the drain at the heart of this vast grassy basin south of Gainesville, became blocked, and the savanna filled into a lake. Shallow-draft steamers plied Alachua Lake until 1891, with a line running between Chacala Pond and Sweetwater Branch. Then, in late July and August of that year, the water simply left. At one point the water fell eight feet in ten days, the banks were littered with dead fish and alligators, and one steamer was left marooned. Paynes Prairie has been shape-shifting between marsh, meadow and lake ever since.

## A Basin That Breathes

The prairie sits in the center of the Paynes Prairie Basin, a 21,000-acre savanna between Gainesville and Micanopy whose water drains into the Floridan aquifer through Alachua Sink. When the sink cannot keep up, the basin fills. Heavy rains did it in 1868, and similar sudden drainings struck in 1823 and 1870 as well as 1891. The most recent flood came in September 2017, when Hurricane Irma dropped several inches of rain, damaged a levee and turned much of the basin back into open water for months. The plant life shifts with the depth of the water. Freshwater marsh covers the open prairie, bald cypress and swamp tupelo fill the wetter forests, and on the higher ground southern live oak hammocks give way to longleaf pine sandhills on the driest, sandiest soils.

## Cowkeeper's Country

People have long prized this grass. In the seventeenth century the Menéndez Márquez family, beginning with Francisco Menéndez Márquez, royal treasurer of Spanish Florida, ranched at La Chua nearby; said to cover 87 square miles, it became by the late seventeenth century the largest cattle ranch in the colony. Native groups and French buccaneers raided it several times, and by 1706 it lay abandoned. By the mid-1700s the prairie was the stronghold of the Alachua band of the Seminole, led by Ahaya, known as the Cowkeeper. Their town of Cuscowilla stood near today's Micanopy, and in 1774 the naturalist William Bartram described the Seminole grazing their herds on what he knew as the Alachua Savannah. By the 1790s the town had moved east of Lake Wauburg and taken the name of the Cowkeeper's eldest surviving son, Payne. Tennessee Volunteers destroyed Paynes Town in 1813. After the Seminole were forced out under Indian Removal, the land that still bears Payne's name was settled by European Americans, the people they held in slavery, and a very small number of free people of color.

## Ditches, Dikes and Highways

By the 1920s, most of the prairie belonged to Camp Ranch, Inc., which set about re-engineering its water. In 1927 the company built a canal system that diverted water bound for the basin through the River Styx to Orange Lake, cutting the prairie's surface water supply in half. That same year the state pushed U.S. 441 across the basin, and a decade later Camp Ranch began channeling Sweetwater Branch straight into Alachua Sink. Interstate 75 arrived across the western prairie in 1964. Change came in 1970, when Florida bought the land from Camp Ranch; in December 1974 it was designated a National Natural Landmark. The restoration has been slow and deliberate. In 1975 the park service temporarily restored half the historic flow from Newnans Lake, and in May 2015 Sweetwater Wetlands Park opened on the northern section of the prairie, a 125-acre constructed wetland designed to restore sheetflow and reduce the pollution reaching Alachua Sink.

## The Return of the Bison

Plains bison roamed this part of Florida until the late eighteenth century. In 1975, as part of an effort to restore the land to its pre-European-settlement condition, the park brought them back from the Wichita Mountains Wildlife Refuge. Their comeback has not been simple. An outbreak of brucellosis in the mid-1980s cut the herd from thirty-five animals to seven, and inbreeding concerns forced another reduction in the late 1990s. By 2011 the herd had grown to a peak of 70, and in 2012 the males were removed after years of debate. A July 2026 magazine feature put the herd at about thirty females, with plans to bring in bison from ranches across the Southeast. Visitors who spot them usually do so along trails such as Cone's Dike or Bolen Bluff. Small herds of Florida Cracker horses and Cracker cattle, introduced in 1985, share the grass, while alligators patrol the wet margins and more than 270 species of birds pass through.

## The View From the Tower

The best way to take in the prairie is from above it. A 50-foot observation tower near the visitor center offers a panoramic sweep of the preserve, and a scenic outlook ramp off U.S. 441 lets drivers pause mid-crossing. Eight trails carry hikers, cyclists and riders on horseback into the basin, and the Gainesville-Hawthorne State Trail runs through it, with viewpoints where wildlife often wanders close. In the years of the Space Shuttle, observers here could see a launch from Cape Canaveral about a minute into its flight. Ranger-led programs run on weekends from November through April, anglers and paddlers can launch on Lake Wauburg, and the park serves as a gateway site for the Great Florida Birding Trail.

## Flight Context

Paynes Prairie lies immediately south of Gainesville, Florida, centered at approximately 29.5831°N, 82.3331°W. It is one of the most distinctive sights in north-central Florida from the air: a broad, flat, open grassland or shallow lake several miles across, crossed by the straight lines of Interstate 75 on the west and U.S. 441 through the middle, with the small town of Micanopy a few miles beyond its southern rim. An altitude of 2,000–3,000 feet AGL shows the whole basin, its dikes and the Alachua Sink area on the north side. Gainesville Regional Airport (KGNV) is about 7 nm to the north-northeast, so stay clear of its airspace or coordinate with approach; Williston Municipal Airport (FAA identifier X60) is about 15 nm to the south-southwest. Summer afternoon thunderstorms are common; winter brings the clearest views, and after heavy rains the prairie may appear as open water.
