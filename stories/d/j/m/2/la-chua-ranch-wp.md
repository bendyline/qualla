# La Chua ranch

On the rim of what is now Paynes Prairie, the La Chua ranch grew into the largest cattle operation in 17th-century Spanish Florida, financed with borrowed royal money and battered by rebellion, pirates and raids until its defenders burned their own blockhouse and fled.

**Location:** 29.6069, -82.2991

**Categories:** Spanish Florida, Ranches in Florida, Colonial history, Archaeological sites in Florida, Alachua County, Florida

## Introduction

The warning came in a letter Juan Menéndez Márquez could not read. It was 1656, and the Timucua chief Lúcas Menéndez, who was probably the godson of Juan's father, had written to him in St. Augustine telling him to stay away from the family's cattle ranch. The letter was in the Timucua language. Unaware of the danger, Juan went to the ranch anyway, straight into a rebellion. The ranch was La Chua, on the broad wet grassland now called Paynes Prairie, and it would become the largest cattle ranch in 17th-century Spanish Florida.

## Ranching on the King's Money

Juan's father, Francisco Menéndez Márquez, belonged to a prominent criollo family related to Pedro Menéndez de Avilés, founder of Spanish Florida. Francisco served as royal treasurer, and while acting as co-governor of the colony he began ranching in Potano Province with the approval of Lúcas Menéndez, probably in 1646 or 1647. By 1649, the year Francisco died, that ranch was worth 8,000 pesos and earning 700 a year. Then the auditors came. A few years after his death, between 16,000 and 20,000 pesos were found missing from the royal treasury in St. Augustine, against a treasurer's salary of 1,470 pesos a year. By historian Bushnell's calculation, 6,000 pesos would have bought about 200 head of cattle, five horses and two enslaved people to work as ranch hands. Francisco had financed his ranch with the King's money. His family reportedly repaid about three-quarters and was allowed six years to settle the rest, though other accounts record no repayment and say the ranch stayed in the family.

## Rebellion at the Hacienda

When the Timucua Rebellion broke out in 1656, Lúcas Menéndez was among its leaders, and he ordered his people to kill all Spaniards except missionaries. The couriers carrying that order met two La Chua workers on the road, a Spaniard and a Tabasco Mexican, and killed them. Lúcas then led a raiding party to the hacienda itself. There the Timucuas killed a Spanish soldier who had traveled with Juan from St. Augustine, along with two enslaved Africans who had been forced to labor on the ranch. The raiders killed all the cattle they could find and burned the hacienda. Juan himself was spared. Lúcas protected him, apparently because Francisco had been generous to the chief, and sent Juan back to St. Augustine under escort.

## Hides for Havana

Even so, in the latter part of the 17th century, cattle ranching in Spanish Florida boomed. Francisco's son Tomás Menéndez Márquez and grandson Francisco II founded or bought most of the ranches between the St. Johns River and the Potano missions, and several of Tomás's children married into other ranching families. Accounts of La Chua's founding vary, with some popular histories giving 1637 and the documentary record allowing no earlier than the late 1640s, but the first mention of it in the records comes in 1675. Cattle went overland to St. Augustine and sometimes to Apalachee Province. From San Martin, a port founded in the early 1670s on the Suwannee River, fourteen leagues from La Chua, Tomás shipped hides, dried meat and tallow to Havana, and he owned a ship that traded between San Martin, Havana and San Marcos in Apalachee. The colony's taxes reveal the scale. Ranches paid two and a half percent of their calves each year, and in 1698 and 1699 Florida's 34 permanent ranches paid 222 head, 77 of them from La Chua alone, a figure that implies some 3,080 calves born there in two years. By century's end the ranch held one-third of the colony's cattle.

## Pirates at Two in the Morning

Wealth drew raiders. In 1682, French pirates based on Anclote Key worked their way up the Suwannee and reached the La Chua hacienda at 2:00 a.m. They seized Tomás, his son-in-law Juan de Hita and four servants, demanded 150 head of cattle and money as ransom, and began withdrawing toward Anclote Key. Before any ransom was paid, three Timucua chiefs ambushed the pirates and freed Tomás and Juan de Hita. Two years later pirates struck La Chua again, this time by way of the Amajuro River, probably the Withlacoochee. Long afterward, in 1763, the British official James Robertson recalled that cattle had once abounded in Florida and that one Spaniard, presumably Tomás, had owned 7,000 head.

## Burning the Blockhouse

The economics were already turning. A beef steer worth 21 pesos in 1651 fetched only six in 1689, and between 1651 and 1682 a horse fell from 100 pesos to 25 and a pair of draft oxen from 80 to 25. From the 1680s, the English of the Province of Carolina and their Native allies raided into Florida, while people who had escaped slavery and Native people living outside the missions took cattle for food. Spain lost control of the Apalachee and Guale provinces to English-backed raids. At La Chua, a blockhouse went up and soldiers were stationed there to help work the ranch and defend it. It was not enough. By 1706, under the pressure of further raids, the defenders had burned the blockhouse themselves and retreated to St. Augustine, and the ranch was abandoned. The hacienda headquarters has been identified with an archaeological site on the northern rim of Paynes Prairie, overlooking the Alachua Sink that drains it.

## Flight Context

The La Chua hacienda site lies at about 29.607°N, 82.299°W on the northern rim of Paynes Prairie, southeast of Gainesville, overlooking the Alachua Sink. From 2,000–3,000 feet the prairie is easy to find: a 21,000-acre open savanna, now Paynes Prairie Preserve State Park, crossed by I-75 and U.S. 441, with the Alachua Sink near its northern edge. Gainesville Regional Airport (KGNV) is about 5 nm to the north-northeast. Morning light and dry-season air give the best view across the grassland; summer afternoons in north-central Florida often bring thunderstorms.
