# 34th Street Wall

A plain concrete retaining wall beside a Gainesville highway became the city's ever-changing bulletin board, painted over so often that its center carries an estimated 250 layers of paint.

**Location:** 29.6477, -82.3722

**Categories:** Graffiti in the United States, Buildings and structures in Gainesville, Florida, University of Florida, Public art, Memorials

## Introduction

At the middle of the 34th Street Wall in Gainesville, Florida, the paint is estimated to be about 250 layers thick. Every one of those layers was a message once: a marriage proposal, a birthday wish, a graduation shout-out, a victory celebration, a cause, a memorial. Each was painted over by the next. Underneath all of it is a 1,120-foot slab of poured concrete that the Florida Department of Transportation built in 1979 for the most unglamorous of reasons, to keep a hillside from eroding. Nobody planned a landmark. Gainesville made one anyway.

## The Wall Nobody Could Keep Clean

When SW 34th Street was widened from two lanes to four, the new roadway sliced through a small hill on the edge of the University of Florida golf course. The state built the retaining wall to hold the hill in place. Because it adjoins a state highway, the wall technically belongs to the state.

Graffiti appeared soon after the wall was finished, and at first the city of Gainesville removed it. In the 1980s there were several more attempts, including painting the whole wall white and adding a graffiti-resistant coating. None of it worked. Gradually the painted wall came to be seen as a local landmark, and state and local officials, bowing to public sentiment, stopped fighting it. Although painting government property generally violates vandalism or criminal mischief laws, neither the Alachua County Sheriff's Office nor the Gainesville Police Department has ever charged anyone for painting here. In the 1990s the city even installed trash bins along the sidewalk so painters could properly dispose of their supplies.

## Gainesville's Bulletin Board

Since the 1980s the wall has worked as a community message board that rewrites itself constantly. Most of the painting is done by university students and other residents, with occasional tagging and the odd piece of professional art. The city and the University of Florida have sometimes used it themselves, to promote public events.

When musician and Gainesville native Tom Petty came home for a concert in 2006, a message on the wall welcomed him. During the visit he was given a photograph of that section of the wall along with the key to the city.

By the early 2000s, the wall had enough defenders to change a road project. When the city planned to resurface 34th Street and widen its bicycle lanes, the original design called for removing part of the wall. Public outcry changed the plan: the median was narrowed for about a block so that the wall could stay.

## Five Names That Stay

Amid all the change, one panel is kept. In 1990 five students were killed in the crimes known as the Danny Rolling murders. That year Adam Byrn Tritt painted a memorial on the wall in black, red and white, listing their names. It has been painted over or defaced several times, and each time it has been repaired quickly; while most of the wall around it changes continuously, the memorial is regularly touched up. The city helped make sure it was left intact until 2000, when the UF Interfraternity Council took over its care.

On the memorial's twentieth anniversary, a plaque was added. It reads: "In remembrance of those individuals who lost their lives in 1990: Sonja Larson, Christina Powell, Christa Hoyt, Tracy Paules, Manuel Taboada. You will never be forgotten."

## Thanks, Tommy

Tom Petty died on October 2, 2017. Within hours, a large new panel appeared on the wall, right beside the memorial to the five students. It called him "Gainesville's No. 1 Son" and said simply "Thanks, Tommy," beneath the logo of his band, the Heartbreakers.

Like its neighbor, it has long been looked after, kept up by a local artist and by local and visiting members of a Facebook group called Tom Petty Nation, though it has been tagged and painted over more than once. In February 2026 a student club accidentally covered it with an announcement for a charity event, and the club apologized; by early March an artist was recreating the mural. Together the two panels are the wall's most protected memorials, fixed points on a surface that otherwise never stops changing.

## Flight Context

The 34th Street Wall runs along SW 34th Street (State Road 121) at about 29.648 N, 82.372 W, on the western edge of the University of Florida campus in Gainesville. It is far too small to pick out from altitude, but from 1,500-2,500 ft the UF golf course on the east side of the road and the dense campus beyond make good references. Gainesville Regional Airport (KGNV) is about 5.8 nm east-northeast; Williston Municipal (X60) is about 18 nm south-southwest. Summer afternoons bring frequent thunderstorms.
