# Ben Hill Griffin Stadium

Dug into a waterlogged ravine in 1930 and paid for partly with personal loans, the University of Florida's stadium grew into "The Swamp," Florida's largest stadium and one of college football's most punishing places to play.

**Location:** 29.6500, -82.3486

**Categories:** College football venues in Florida, Florida Gators football, University of Florida buildings and structures, Sports venues completed in 1930, Tourist attractions in Gainesville, Florida

## Introduction

The ground fought back almost as soon as the digging began. In the spring of 1930, crews excavating a shallow ravine on the University of Florida campus in Gainesville struck water from a previously unknown underground stream, and it poured into the site from the north, turning the future playing field into mud. The builders tamed it with massive culverts, but they never lifted the field out of its hollow. About thirty rows of seats still sit below the level of the surrounding land, and that sunken bowl became the foundation of a legend: Ben Hill Griffin Stadium, known to nearly everyone as "The Swamp."

## Built on Borrowed Money

Florida's football program first earned national prominence in the 1920s, and when John J. Tigert arrived as university president in 1928 he began a drive for a proper on-campus stadium. The timing was terrible. With state funding unavailable on the cusp of the Great Depression, the semi-independent University of Florida Athletic Association was organized to raise money, and Tigert and ten supporters took out personal loans to speed construction of the $118,000, 22,000-seat stadium. Work began on April 16, 1930. The culverts that saved the project diverted the underground stream south to Graham Pond, two blocks away, and ultimately to Lake Alice on what was then the undeveloped southern side of campus. In 1934 Florida Field, as it was called, was dedicated to the memory of Florida servicemen who died in World War I, with a plaque on the outside wall behind the north end zone.

## A Bowl That Kept Rising

The original concrete grandstand was a gently sloping U that held 21,769 people and covered roughly the lower half of today's seating. In 1949 and 1950 a second tier on the west side and bleachers in the open south end zone nearly doubled capacity to about 40,000, and new lights let the 1950 season open with the Gators' first home night game, a 7–3 win over The Citadel. A double-decked south end zone followed in 1982. In 1991 the north end got its upper deck of "Sunshine Seats" and the "Touchdown Terrace" club level, completing the enclosed bowl and lifting capacity past 85,000. Official capacity is now 88,548, making it the largest stadium in Florida, though crowds for key games have regularly topped 90,000. The name changed as the stadium grew. It was simply Florida Field until 1989, when the university honored citrus magnate and benefactor Ben Hill Griffin Jr., and in 2016 the playing surface was named for Steve Spurrier.

## Where Gators Live

Spurrier, the former Florida quarterback who returned as head coach in 1990, coined the nickname in 1992. "A swamp is where Gators live," he explained. "We feel comfortable there, but we hope our opponents feel tentative. A swamp is hot and sticky and can be dangerous." The design backs him up. Steep stands close in on every side, fans sit within a few feet of the action, and crowd noise on the field has been measured at 115 decibels, just short of the threshold of pain. The sunken, enclosed field also traps Gainesville's humid heat, and game-day temperatures at field level have been known to exceed 100°F. That heat was the impetus for University of Florida researcher Robert Cade to develop Gatorade to combat dehydration. On sunny afternoons the press box shades Florida's west sideline, while visitors bake on the east. In the twenty years from 1990, Florida went 113–13 at home, a record that ranked among the best in the nation over that span.

## Two Bits and a Chomp

Ritual fills the spaces between plays. For almost sixty years George Edmondson, known as "Mr. Two Bits," roamed the stands in a yellow oxford shirt, seersucker trousers and saddle shoes, leading the old "Two Bits" cheer he began in 1949. Public address announcer Jim Finch, at the microphone from 1966 through 2001, stretched his "Heeeeeeere come the Gators!" into a baritone signature that is still replayed or imitated today. The Pride of the Sunshine, the university's marching band, wheels out an enormous bass drum called the "Biggest Boom in Dixie" and plays a two-note theme borrowed from Jaws while the crowd answers with the two-armed Gator chomp. At the end of the third quarter, rows of fans sway and sing "We Are the Boys from Old Florida," a football tradition since at least the 1930s, from when the university was still an all-male school. Before kickoff, a video ends in the jaws of a real alligator and a deep voice warns: "Only Gators get out alive!"

## Not Only Football

For decades the field also hosted Gator Growl, a student-produced pep rally held the Friday before homecoming and long billed as the largest student-run pep rally in the world. Its headliners included Robin Williams, Jerry Seinfeld, Bob Hope and George Burns before it left the stadium after 2013. The Swamp has stood in for other venues, hosting the 1973 Tangerine Bowl while construction to expand Orlando's stadium stalled and the 1994 Gator Bowl while Jacksonville's stadium was renovated for the NFL's new Jaguars. Bob Dylan, Joan Baez, Elton John and Jimmy Buffett all played concerts here, but worries about damage to the turf have kept the gates mostly closed to music since 1990. Only two non-Growl shows have happened since: the Rolling Stones in 1994 and Garth Brooks in 2019.

## Flight Context

Ben Hill Griffin Stadium sits at 29.65°N, 82.349°W on the northern edge of the University of Florida campus in Gainesville. From 1,500–2,500 feet the enclosed, double-decked bowl is unmistakable, with West University Avenue along its north side and the Stephen C. O'Connell Center arena and football practice fields just to the west across Gale Lemerand Drive. Lake Alice lies on the southern part of campus. Gainesville Regional Airport (KGNV) is about 5 nm to the northeast. Check NOTAMs for flight restrictions over the stadium on football game days, and plan around the frequent afternoon thunderstorms of north-central Florida summers.
