# Battle of Gainesville

On August 17, 1864, Confederate cavalry caught a Union raiding column in downtown Gainesville, Florida, and in about two hours of fighting in the town square nearly destroyed it.

**Location:** 29.6520, -82.3251

**Categories:** Battles of the American Civil War in Florida, Confederate victories of the American Civil War, Alachua County, Florida, 1864 in Florida, Military history of Florida

## Introduction

On August 17, 1864, the people of Gainesville, Florida, watched a battle through window glass. As Union and Confederate troops fought in the town square, many townspeople followed the fighting from the windows of the nearby Beville house. What they saw was a rout. In roughly two hours, a Confederate force caught a Union column off balance and nearly destroyed it, and Gainesville stayed in Confederate hands for the rest of the Civil War.

## Fifty-Six Hours in February

Gainesville mattered because of the railroad. The town held a rail junction and depot in north central Florida, and that made it a target. On February 14, 1864, about 50 Union troops entered the town, reportedly intending to capture two trains. Two companies of the 2nd Florida Cavalry under Captain John Jackson Dickison, reported at about 130 men, tried to drive them out and failed. The raiders held the town and then returned safely to Jacksonville.

Three days later, Brigadier General Truman Seymour issued a general order singling out the raid's leader. He wrote that he "especially desires to praise Capt. George E. Marshall, Co. G. 40th Mass. Mounted Volunteers, and his small command of forty-nine men who captured and held Gainesville for fifty-six hours, receiving and repulsing an attack from more than double his force." Gainesville had shown that it could be taken. Six months later, the Union tried again.

## Dickison's Riders

The second column set out from Baldwin, west of Jacksonville, under Colonel Andrew L. Harris of the 75th Ohio Mounted Infantry, with men from his regiment, the 4th Massachusetts Cavalry and some Florida Unionists, somewhere between 310 and 400 troops in all, depending on the account. When they reached Gainesville on August 17, they found the town held by a single company of the 2nd Florida Cavalry.

That company was not alone for long. Captain John Jackson Dickison arrived and struck the Union troops from the rear with Companies H and F of the 2nd Florida Cavalry, backed by local militia, elements of the 5th Florida Cavalry and a small battery of two cannons. Dickison had about 290 men, though only 175 of them actually entered Gainesville and joined the fight.

## Two Hours in the Square

The Union troops were taken by surprise and had not fully deployed when the attack began. The fighting centered on the town square. After about two hours, Harris ordered a retreat, and the Confederates kept closing in on his disorganized columns.

The cost was lopsided. By the commonly cited tally, Union losses came to 28 dead, 5 wounded and 188 captured (some accounts add 86 more missing, and one gives more than 200 prisoners), along with a 12-pound howitzer and about 260 horses. Confederate losses were slight: accounts give about three killed and several wounded, two of them mortally. About 40 Union soldiers escaped, Harris among them. He reported that his column had been destroyed by a large Confederate force of 600 to 800 men with three cannon, roughly double to nearly triple the number Dickison actually brought.

On hearing his account, the remaining Union forces in north central Florida pulled back to their garrisons at Jacksonville and St. Augustine. Gainesville stayed in Confederate control until the war ended.

## Flight Context

The battle was fought around the old town square of downtown Gainesville, Florida, at about 29.652 N, 82.325 W. From 1,500-2,500 ft the compact downtown grid sits just east of the University of Florida campus; the historic core is small, so use the downtown high-rises and the university as references. Gainesville Regional Airport (KGNV) is only about 3.6 nm to the northeast, so the area lies within Gainesville's terminal airspace. Williston Municipal (KX60) is about 20 nm south-southwest. Summer afternoons bring frequent thunderstorms.
