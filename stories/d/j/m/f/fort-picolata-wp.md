# Fort Picolata

Guarding the ferry crossing where the St. Johns River narrows on the road west from St. Augustine, Fort Picolata was burned, rebuilt in stone, handed to the British and left behind a treaty that reportedly took more than two million acres from the Creek and Seminole.

**Location:** 29.9231, -81.6008

**Categories:** Government buildings completed in 1737, Forts in Florida, Pre-statehood history of Florida, 1737 establishments in the Spanish Empire

## Introduction

In his Travels, William Bartram described Fort Picolata as "dismantled and deserted" when he stopped there in April 1774. His own report from that year said something different. Writing to his patron, Dr. John Fothergill, Bartram noted that he had called "at Picolata Fort. which I observed was newly repared." By the time the book appeared in 1791, seventeen years had passed, and memory may have rewritten the scene. The small fort on the St. Johns River deserves a truer account, because for much of the 18th century it guarded a strategic river crossing in Spanish Florida.

## Holding the Narrows

Fort Picolata stood on the east bank of the St. Johns River, about eighteen miles from St. Augustine, the capital of Spanish Florida. Here the river narrows considerably, forming a natural pass the Native people called "Salamatoto," and here the old trail to the Spanish province of Apalachee in western Florida met the water. Picolata and its sister outpost, Fort San Francisco de Pupo, controlled all traffic at the ferry. Their purpose was plain. In a letter to King Philip V dated January 31, 1740, Governor Montiano wrote that the two forts "were constructed solely for the purpose of defending and sheltering from the continual attacks of Indian allies of the English, the mails that go to and come from Apalachee." The first defenses, built soon after 1700, were little more than a sentry box inside a palisade.

## Burned and Rebuilt in Stone

The frontier around the crossing was growing dangerous. Tensions between Spain and Britain had sharpened after James Moore, the governor of Carolina, invaded La Florida in 1704 and 1706. According to archaeologist Carl D. Halbirt, Picolata and Pupo were hexagonal wooden towers 16 feet high, enclosed by a palisade, but by 1737 both were dilapidated. In 1738 they were rebuilt more substantially as wooden towers 30 feet high and 16 feet square, with machicolations, inside an 8-foot palisade. It was not enough. In late December 1739 and early January 1740, during the War of Jenkins' Ear, General Oglethorpe, governor of the British Province of Georgia, invaded Florida with Scottish Highlanders and Native allies, who captured and burned Fort Picolata before Oglethorpe besieged St. Augustine. In 1755 the Spanish rebuilt Picolata as a coquina stone tower two and a half stories high, hauling the stone to the spot where El Camino Real, the Royal Road, crossed the river. There is no record that Pupo was ever rebuilt.

## The Treaty at Picolata

When Britain acquired Florida under the Treaty of Paris in 1763, its officials quickly saw the fort's value to the defense of St. Augustine and kept a garrison there, as the Spanish had. Picolata also became a meeting ground. From November 15 to 18, 1765, British officials met a delegation of Lower Creek and Seminole leaders at the fort, in a congress organized by John Stuart, the Indian superintendent of the Southern Department, and summoned by Governor James Grant to set the boundaries between Native and British lands. The treaty signed there reportedly transferred more than two million acres of northeast Florida to the British, a strip reaching thirty-five miles inland from the coast and westward past the St. Johns, plus all the tidewater land on the rest of the peninsula up to ten miles from the coast. For the Creek and Seminole, the treaty meant surrendering a vast territory. Another congress followed at the fort in 1767.

## A Lieutenant Named Sherman

By the 1840s Picolata had dwindled, but soldiers still came through. During the Second Seminole War, a first lieutenant named William T. Sherman was stationed at Picolata between 1841 and 1842. In his memoirs, he recalled that the place then consisted of just two buildings, one "which had been built for a hospital, and the dwelling of a family named Williams." From a fortified river crossing on a colonial road to a pair of buildings in a wartime post, Picolata's story traces the changing masters of northeast Florida across nearly a century and a half.

## Flight Context

The site of Fort Picolata lies at 29.9231 N, 81.6008 W on the east bank of the St. Johns River in northeast Florida, about eighteen miles west of St. Augustine. From the air, follow the broad St. Johns and look for the stretch where the river narrows at the old Picolata crossing. A viewing altitude of 1,500 to 2,500 feet MSL gives a good view of the river and its banks. Northeast Florida Regional Airport (KSGJ) at St. Augustine is about 14 nm to the east, and Naval Air Station Jacksonville (KNIP) about 19 nm to the north-northwest, so check military airspace and coordinate as required. Best seen in clear daytime conditions.
