# Fort San Francisco de Pupo

A tiny Spanish outpost guarding a St. Johns River ferry crossing, Fort Pupo grew from a wooden sentry box into a blockhouse before James Oglethorpe's raiders captured it in 1740.

**Location:** 29.9386, -81.6039

**Categories:** Spanish forts in the United States, Forts in Florida, Pre-statehood history of Florida, War of Jenkins' Ear, St. Johns River

## Introduction

"A sentry box built of boards, eight feet in diameter… surrounded by a palisade." That was how the Spanish Royal Engineer Antonio de Arredondo described Fort San Francisco de Pupo in a letter dated January 22, 1737. It was hardly a fortress. Yet this little post on the west bank of the St. Johns River, about eighteen miles from St. Augustine, sat astride one of the most important routes in Spanish Florida, and within three years it would be fought over, rebuilt, and captured by a British force.

## Guarding the Crossing

Pupo's value lay in the river. The old trail west from St. Augustine to the Spanish province of Apalachee crossed the St. Johns here by ferry. Fort Pupo on the west bank and its sister outpost, Fort Picolata, on the east bank, together controlled all traffic across the crossing.

By one account both were built in 1734 on the orders of Governor Francisco del Moral y Sánchez, though a Florida historical marker says only that Pupo was first mentioned in 1716 and was built sometime before 1737. Tension had been building with the English since Carolina forces under James Moore invaded La Florida early in the century, and the Spanish expected more attacks by the English and their Indian allies. Today the remains of Fort Pupo lie in Clay County, about three miles south of Green Cove Springs near the end of Bayard Point, in a hammock of southern live oak, southern magnolia and pignut hickory. The site is on private property, and the Clay County Clerk's archives report that it is eroding into the river.

## From Sentry Box to Blockhouse

The attacks came. In 1738 a party of Yuchi warriors, allies of the British and the Upper Creeks, struck Fort Pupo, damaging the stockade and killing two soldiers. The same year, Governor Manuel de Montiano ordered the little fort replaced. Engineer Pedro Ruiz de Olano pulled carpenters, sawyers and axemen off the construction of the Castillo de San Marcos, St. Augustine's great fortress, and put them to work building a new wooden blockhouse with barracks and storehouses. His drawing survives under the title "Plano y perfil del nuevo fortín de San Francisco de Pupo."

The enlarged post was a blockhouse behind a rampart of timber and earth, held by ten soldiers and a sergeant with seven cannon. Across the border in Georgia, meanwhile, Governor James Oglethorpe was gathering militia, regulars and Indian allies at Fort Frederica for an invasion of Florida.

## The Ferry That Turned Back

The War of Jenkins' Ear gave Oglethorpe his opening. After a Spanish attack on the British outpost at Amelia Island, in which two British soldiers were decapitated, he resolved to raid the outposts that shielded St. Augustine. On January 6, 1740 (Old Style), his force set out from Fort Saint George at the mouth of the St. Johns, joined by an armed English privateer's sloop.

They went upriver, landed five miles below Picolata on the east bank and advanced through the night, reaching the fort at 2 a.m. on the 7th. Darien Highlanders, infantry from Fort Frederica and Indian allies led the assault, and by daybreak Picolata had been taken and burned. Across the river, Pupo's garrison saw Oglethorpe's Indian auxiliaries and took them for friendly Yamasee. The Spanish sent the ferry across. It hastily reversed course and returned once its crew realized the mistake.

## Surrender at Sunset

Oglethorpe then landed his regulars a mile north of Pupo and marched on it with four field guns. An advance party of Indians and Rangers attacked while the regulars, under Ensign Sanford Mace, opened fire with the artillery. The defenders surrendered, in one account after the second volley and just before sunset, in another after a day-long artillery barrage. The Highlanders and Indian scouts took the fort and made its garrison prisoner.

Oglethorpe left 50 Highlanders at Pupo under Captain Hugh MacKay Jr., along with the sloop, and returned to Fort Frederica. His campaign took Pupo, Picolata, San Diego and Fort Mose, preliminary blows ahead of a planned land and sea attack on St. Augustine. Those outposts were the campaign's gains; the effort to take the city itself in 1740 failed.

Centuries later, in 1950 and 1951, the anthropologist John Goggin and University of Florida students excavated the site, and their stratigraphic tests indicated that the original Fort Pupo had been little more than a sentry box.

## Flight Context

The Fort Pupo site lies at about 29.939 N, 81.604 W, near the tip of Bayard Point on the west bank of the St. Johns River in Clay County, Florida, about 3 miles south of Green Cove Springs and directly across the river from Picolata. From 2,000-3,000 ft the St. Johns runs broad here, and Bayard Point is a wooded point projecting into the river. Nearby airports: Northeast Florida Regional at St. Augustine (KSGJ), about 14 nm east; Palatka Municipal-Lt. Kay Larkin Field (K28J), about 17 nm south-southwest; Jacksonville NAS Towers Field (KNIP, military), about 18 nm north-northwest; Cecil Airport (KVQQ), about 22 nm northwest. Expect afternoon thunderstorms in summer and occasional river fog on cool mornings.
