# Friendship Fountain

Taylor Hardwick's Friendship Fountain opened in 1965 billed as the world's largest and tallest fountain, and Jacksonville has spent decades fighting to keep it running.

**Location:** 30.3200, -81.6598

**Categories:** Parks in Jacksonville, Florida, Downtown Jacksonville, Southbank, Jacksonville, Taylor Hardwick buildings, Modernist architecture in Jacksonville, Florida, Fountains

## Introduction

On a calm evening, the nine nozzles at the heart of Friendship Fountain were built to throw water 120 feet into the air. Let the wind pick up past 15 miles an hour, though, and an anemometer quietly caps those center jets at 50 feet, so the spray does not blow across the park and drench the people gathered around the basin. It is a small, considerate piece of engineering on a structure built to be enormous. When it opened in 1965, Friendship Fountain was billed as the world's largest and tallest fountain. Today it anchors St. Johns River Park, also known as Friendship Fountain Park, at the west end of Downtown Jacksonville's Southbank Riverwalk, and it remains one of the city's most recognizable attractions.

## A Gift of Fourteen Acres

The fountain began with a donation. The Southside Business Men's Club, an organization founded in 1932 to improve Jacksonville's Southside, gave 14 acres of land for the project. Jacksonville architect Taylor Hardwick, who also designed the Haydon Burns Library, drew up the fountain and its surrounding park in 1963. Work began that year, the finished project cost $1.7 million, and the park opened in 1965.

The machinery was on a scale that shaped the architecture itself. Three pumps with a combined 750 horsepower could push 17,000 gallons of water a minute, sending some streams as tall as a 10-story building. The enclosure for the pumps and controls was so large that Hardwick had to make it part of the design.

## Three Rings of Water

Seen from above, the fountain is a study in concentric circles. Its basin stretches 200 feet across and holds 500,000 gallons of water, treated to swimming-pool sanitation standards. Around the outer rim, 36 evenly spaced nozzles angle inward at 45 degrees, arcing their streams 35 feet high on the power of a 250-horsepower motor. Inside them, a middle ring 120 feet in diameter holds 18 nozzles firing straight up to 40 feet. At the very center, a ring just 15 feet across carries nine vertical nozzles, driven by a 350-horsepower motor pumping 5,500 gallons a minute. These are the jets that reach 120 feet.

After dark, color takes over. Each nozzle is fitted with a cluster of four lights in dark red, light red, turquoise and amber, washing the rising columns of water in warm and cool tones against the night sky.

## The Park That Shrank

The land around the fountain has not always fared as well as the fountain itself. When the Acosta Bridge was rebuilt beginning in 1990, the city agreed to turn a big portion of Friendship Park into a new restaurant and parking lot, cutting the park by more than half. Essentially every design structure in the park was removed, with the single exception of the fountain.

The restaurant, named Harbormasters, became a boondoggle. The city guaranteed a $2.9 million federal building loan on its own land, and early success gave way to lawsuits, a new owner, missed rent payments, foreclosure, loan default and finally closure in 1992. The city had to pay off the nearly $3 million loan. The River City Brewing Company replaced Harbormasters in November 1993 and proved successful, but because the city owns the land, the restaurant paid no property taxes. In October 2020, the company that owned it submitted a plan to raze the building and put up an eight-story tower with 325 apartments. The restaurant closed in July 2021 and was demolished. A later deal with the Related Group for an apartment building on the site was canceled in January 2023, and in April 2026 Mayor Donna Deegan said the developer would break ground that May.

## Running on One Pump

Age caught up with the machinery. Severe corrosion and deteriorating equipment led to periodic closures in the 2000s. On April 29, 2002, a power outage and voltage spike damaged the fountain's computer controller and blew out 40 lights and lenses. Once repaired, it ran at full strength for nearly three years and was a major attraction during Super Bowl XXXIX in February 2005.

Two months later, two of the three original pumps, by then 40 years old, failed, and parts for them were no longer available. Replacements were estimated at $400,000, money the city's tight budget could not spare, so the fountain carried on with a single pump and a greatly reduced flow. For several years its future was genuinely in doubt. Proposals included moving Kids Kampus from Metropolitan Park to the site and eliminating the fountain entirely, building a smaller fountain, or renovating the original.

## The Water Rises Again

Renovation won out. As of May 2008, the city's capital budget held $15 million to improve the Southbank Riverwalk, including the area around the fountain, though Mayor John Peyton felt that was not enough. On February 9, 2010, the City Council approved final plans for a renovation of the fountain and park costing roughly $3 million, and ground was broken that September 1. The fountain reopened amid a celebration on June 18, 2011.

A second overhaul began in 2020 with a budget of nearly $8 million. After several delays, the attraction reopened on February 15, 2024. Today the water flows every day from 8 a.m. to 10 p.m., more than half a century after Hardwick's three rings first rose beside the river.

## Flight Context

Friendship Fountain sits at 30.32°N, 81.66°W on the south bank of the St. Johns River in downtown Jacksonville, Florida, at the west end of the Southbank Riverwalk near the Acosta Bridge. The 200-foot circular basin is best picked out from about 1,500–2,500 feet; look for the ring of water on the riverbank directly across from the downtown skyline. The fountain runs daily from 8 a.m. to 10 p.m., and its colored lights make it easiest to spot after dusk. Nearby airports: Jacksonville Naval Air Station (Towers Field, KNIP, military) about 6 miles south-southwest, Jacksonville Executive at Craig Airport (KCRG) about 9 miles east, and Jacksonville International Airport (KJAX) about 12 miles north. Coastal haze and summer afternoon thunderstorms can reduce visibility.
