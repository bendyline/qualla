# Treaty Oak (Jacksonville)

Jacksonville's sprawling Treaty Oak got its name from a newspaper story invented in the 1930s to save it from developers, and the ruse worked: the live oak, perhaps the oldest living thing in the city, still spreads its limbs across a small downtown park.

**Location:** 30.3170, -81.6581

**Categories:** History of Jacksonville, Florida, Individual oak trees, Tourist attractions in Jacksonville, Florida, Parks in Jacksonville, Florida, Downtown Jacksonville, Southbank, Jacksonville, Individual trees in Florida

## Introduction

The treaty that gave the Treaty Oak its name almost certainly never happened. The name was a rescue plan. In the 1930s, with developers threatening the great live oak on Jacksonville's Southbank, Florida Times-Union journalist Pat Moran wrote an article claiming that Native people and early European settlers had once signed a treaty in its shade. Until then the tree had been known simply as the Giant Oak (the Great Oak in some accounts). The story stuck, the tree survived, and today it spreads its twisting limbs over a small park in the heart of downtown Jacksonville.

## A Legend Written to Order

The name is generally linked to local apocryphal stories of peace accords between Native Americans and Spanish or American settlers, signed beneath the oak's branches. But the name and the history attached to it were created in the 1930s by Moran, and with a clear purpose: to make the tree too important to cut down. It worked. Nearly a century later, the tree still carries the name he gave it, and the old oak itself still stands in the park that grew up around it. The legend may be fiction. The tree, very much, is not.

## The Octopus on the Southbank

The Treaty Oak is a Southern live oak, Quercus virginiana, and it looks less like a single tree than an octopus caught mid-sprawl. Its trunk measures more than 25 feet around, it rises 70 feet high, and its crown spreads more than 145 feet, with twisting branches that bow down to the ground and then curl back up again. The tree is estimated to be about 250 years old, and it may be the single oldest living thing in Jacksonville. If the estimate holds, the oak was already growing when Isaiah Hart founded the city in the 1820s, and it has stood through the city's entire history since.

## Electric Lights and Babe Ruth

The oak's surroundings have not always been quiet. Most of the land around it was once the Dixieland Amusement Park, which opened in 1907. In those days the tree was festooned with electric lights, and it stood by as Babe Ruth played baseball and John Philip Sousa performed a concert. The amusement park is long gone. The tree that presided over it remains, its limbs still reaching across the same ground.

## The Smallest Park, a Giant Tree

Today the oak stands in Jessie Ball duPont Park, also known as Treaty Oak Park, a seven-acre green space on the south side of the St. Johns River. The city acquired additional land for the park over time, and after the death in 1970 of Jessie Ball duPont, a philanthropist and part-time Jacksonville resident, the preserve was named in her honor. There is a pleasing irony here: the smallest nature park in Jacksonville is said to hold its largest tree, though it is not the largest live oak in Florida. Paved paths, a score of benches, a handful of picnic tables and informational plaques make it an easy place to linger, and workers from the nearby buildings come at lunch to eat, read a book or simply sit and talk in the shade of its canopy.

## Flight Context

The Treaty Oak stands at 30.3170 N, 81.6581 W in Jessie Ball duPont Park on the Southbank of downtown Jacksonville, on the south side of the St. Johns River. From the air, look for a small patch of green among the Southbank buildings with a single enormous canopy some 145 feet across. Downtown Jacksonville is surrounded by busy civil and military airspace, so coordinate with Jacksonville approach. Naval Air Station Jacksonville (KNIP) is about 5 nm to the south-southwest, Jacksonville Executive at Craig (KCRG) about 7.5 nm to the east, and Jacksonville International (KJAX) about 10.8 nm to the north. A viewing altitude of 1,500 to 2,500 feet MSL, as cleared, works well. Best seen in clear daytime conditions.
