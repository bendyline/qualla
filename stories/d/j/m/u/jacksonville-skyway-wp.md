# Jacksonville Skyway

Jacksonville's free, elevated Skyway has carried downtown riders across the St. Johns River since 1989, though its operator has announced that service will pause on November 2, 2026.

**Location:** 30.3271, -81.6623

**Categories:** Transportation in Jacksonville, Florida, Monorails in the United States, People mover systems in the United States, Railway lines opened in 1989, Downtown Jacksonville

## Introduction

Most transit agencies worry about fares, schedules and crowding. The Jacksonville Skyway has had a stranger problem: trains so old that their parts can no longer be replaced. Since 1989 this automated people mover has carried riders above the streets of Downtown Jacksonville, and today its two routes run across 2.5 miles of elevated track, serving eight stations and crossing the St. Johns River on the Acosta Bridge. The ride costs nothing. Yet since 2015 the Jacksonville Transportation Authority (JTA) has openly weighed a wide range of futures for the line, from buying new cars to turning the guideway into a walking path. In September 2026 JTA announced budget cuts that include pausing Skyway operations on November 2.

## One of Seven Cities

The Skyway began as a federal bet on small, automated downtown transit. After several stops and starts, the federal transit agency UMTA selected Jacksonville in 1985 as one of seven cities to receive funding for what was called the "Automated Skyway Express." Miami's Metromover and Detroit's People Mover are related projects from the same era. UMTA's approved plan called for a 2.5-mile Phase I system built in three segments, and the agency awarded JTA $23.5 million for the first piece, a 0.7-mile stretch known as Phase I-A.

That short segment opened in 1989 with just three stations. Its trains were VAL 256 vehicles designed by Matra, running on rubber wheels rather than the monorail beam riders know today.

## From Rubber Tires to a Monorail Beam

The Matra contract expired in 1991, and negotiations for a new one failed. In October 1994, Bombardier Transportation won a contract to revamp the existing segment with new technology and complete the remaining Phase I extensions. The Skyway shut down on December 15, 1996, for the conversion and reopened exactly a year later, on December 15, 1997, as a version of Bombardier's UM III monorail with a fleet of 10 vehicles. The system grew again in 1998 and 2000.

The trains now straddle beams 34 inches wide and 28 inches tall, fixed on an 11-foot-wide guideway lined with parapet walls. Five stations sit in the Downtown Core and LaVilla, and three lie across the river on the Southbank. Every train passes through Rosa Parks Transit Station, James Weldon Johnson Park and Central, where the routes split: one heads west to LaVilla, the other swings south over the St. Johns to Kings Avenue.

## What Happened When the Fare Disappeared

In February 2012, JTA made the Skyway free to ride, only temporarily, until a new payment system could be installed. Ridership jumped 61 percent, to 481,000 a year. By 2013 the system averaged nearly 4,000 riders on weekdays, even though it normally closes on weekends except for special downtown events, and JTA extended the fare-free policy through the end of 2016. The Skyway has remained free ever since.

Buoyed by that momentum, JTA director Nat Ford announced that the agency would seek grants for a new station in the fast-growing Brooklyn neighborhood. On March 3, 2021, JTA approved converting part of its Skyway maintenance property off Leila Street into that Brooklyn station, with construction planned to begin that September; JTA's current list of eight stations does not include a Brooklyn station. Meanwhile the old Convention Center station was stripped of original elements such as its overhead canopy, and its platform was tied into the new Jacksonville Regional Transportation Center, built partly over the existing tracks, which opened on May 4, 2020. Because of a COVID-19 pause in Skyway service, trains did not reach it until that July.

## A Fleet Running Out of Time

In December 2015, JTA announced a review of the system. The agency's staff reported that the Skyway's vehicles were so old that parts could no longer be replaced, that four of the 10 were out of commission, and that industry experts had not responded favorably to the idea of overhauling them. The options on the table ranged widely: refurbish the existing cars, buy replacements, expand the system, tear the structure down, or convert it to another use such as a walking path. In January 2017, JTA said it was trying to keep the Skyway running for another five years while it decided the system's future.

## Driverless on the Guideway

The answer JTA favored in December 2016 was the Ultimate Urban Circulator, or U2C: shared autonomous vehicles that would use the Skyway's elevated route, then drop to street level and continue on an extended network. In December 2017 the agency opened a Test and Learn track as an outdoor classroom for autonomous shuttles, and by 2020 vehicles from three different suppliers had been trialed there. A 2019 plan proposed converting one of the monorail's two beams for autonomous vehicles while the Skyway kept running on the other.

Plans unveiled in 2020 envisioned a 10-route-mile network reaching San Marco, Springfield and Brooklyn/Riverside. In May 2021, the Jacksonville City Council removed $132 million from the project's budget, leaving $240 million. In December 2024 JTA's board approved a multimillion-dollar study that would weigh whether the conversion should happen at all, partly because the city could owe roughly $100 million to the federal government for the existing Skyway. By early 2026 JTA was reportedly asking the public to choose among six options, from running the aging system until it fails to demolishing it, and in September 2026 it announced that Skyway operations will pause on November 2.

## Flight Context

The Skyway's midpoint lies near 30.327°N, 81.662°W in Downtown Jacksonville, Florida. From about 1,500–2,500 feet, follow the elevated guideway from the downtown core as it rides the Acosta Bridge across the St. Johns River to the Southbank; Friendship Fountain's round basin at the west end of the Southbank Riverwalk makes a useful nearby landmark. Nearby airports: Jacksonville Naval Air Station (Towers Field, KNIP, military) about 6 miles south-southwest, Jacksonville Executive at Craig Airport (KCRG) about 9 miles east, and Jacksonville International Airport (KJAX) about 12 miles north. The system normally runs on weekdays only, and JTA has announced that operations will pause on November 2, 2026.
