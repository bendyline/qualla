# Kona Skatepark

Born in the 1970s skateboarding boom and nearly killed by an unrideable 30-foot pipe, Jacksonville's Kona Skatepark outlasted its rivals, inspired Danny Way's Mega Ramp and was named the oldest surviving commercial skatepark.

## Introduction

In 1991, at a World Cup contest in Jacksonville, Danny Way launched himself across the huge gap between Kona Skatepark's bowl and its snake run. It was the kind of jump that stays with a skater, and it stayed with Way: a decade later, it became the inspiration for his Mega Ramp. Twelve years after that first flight he came back to the same gap and spun a full 360 over it. Kona's story, though, began long before that first jump.

## Born in the Boom

Kona opened on June 4, 1977, in the Arlington area of Jacksonville, one of many parks built during the 1970s skateboarding boom, when cities across the country were getting their first skateparks. Martin and Helen Ramos founded it with a group of investors. The original layout offered a pro bowl with a six-foot vertical "tombstone," a freestyle area, and snake and J-runs full of banked curves and speed runs. Kona quickly became a stage. It hosted the U.S. Open of Skateboarding in 1977 and 1978, reportedly the first time West Coast skaters traveled east to compete against their East Coast rivals. The contests drew the top professionals of the day, and crowds packed the stands to watch tricks in person and pick up new moves.

## The Pipe That Nearly Sank It

Ambition almost ended the story early. Inspired by Skateboarder magazine, the park set out to build a 30-foot concrete pipe. The project bankrupted Kona, and the finished structure proved unrideable. The park closed from late 1978 until the summer of 1979, and around that time trespassers on skateboards, motorcycles, BMX bikes and even cars found their way in for so-called "epic sessions." The Ramos family reacquired the property and reopened on June 27, 1979. Kona kept innovating. In 1980 it built one of the first half-pipe ramps with a flat bottom and added a pool, and around 1983 a mini-spine ramp followed. Professional skateboarders Clyde Singleton and Mike Peterson both learned to skate at Kona.

## Last Park Standing

Kona endured while other parks closed. When Del Mar Skate Ranch closed in 1987 and Upland Skatepark shut in 1989, Kona was, by some accounts, the only active skatepark in the United States. Derby Skatepark in Santa Cruz, California, had opened a year before Kona, in 1976, but it has since closed, and in 2019 Guinness World Records named Kona the "Oldest Surviving Commercial Skatepark." The park's fame spread well beyond Jacksonville. In 2002 it became a location in the video game Tony Hawk's Pro Skater 4. The next year, in 2003, Danny Way returned for his 360 over the same gap he had cleared in 1991. Age brings its own challenges. By 2024 Kona's vertical ramp was more than 20 years old, riddled with holes and unsafe to ride, and rebuilding it required $60,000. For a park that has come through bankruptcy, closure and the loss of its rivals, it was one more test, and the park scheduled the unveiling of a rebuilt concrete vert ramp for Go Skateboarding Day in June 2025. Nearly half a century after it opened in 1977, Kona remains a place where skaters learn, compete and fly.

## Flight Context

Kona Skatepark lies at 30.3233 N, 81.5649 W in the Arlington area of Jacksonville, east of downtown. From the air, look for the pale concrete of the bowls and snake runs set among the neighborhood's streets. The park sits only about 2.7 nm west-southwest of Jacksonville Executive at Craig Airport (KCRG), so coordinate with ATC before maneuvering nearby. Naval Air Station Jacksonville (KNIP) is about 8 nm to the southwest, Naval Station Mayport (KNRB) about 8 nm to the east-northeast, and Jacksonville International (KJAX) about 12 nm to the north-northwest. A viewing altitude of 1,500 to 2,500 feet MSL, as cleared, works well. Best seen in clear daytime conditions.
