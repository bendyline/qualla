# Hotel Roosevelt fire

On a Sunday morning in December 1963, a fire in the ballroom ceiling of Jacksonville's 13-story Hotel Roosevelt killed 22 people, while Navy helicopters, bedsheet ropes and neighbors helped nearly 475 others survive.

**Location:** 30.3284, -81.6582

**Categories:** 1963 fires in the United States, 1963 in Florida, Hotel fires in the United States, History of Jacksonville, Florida, Fires in Jacksonville, Florida, Laura Street, December 1963 in the United States

## Introduction

Shortly before 8 on the morning of Sunday, December 29, 1963, hotel doorman Alton Joseph Crowden made the first call to the Jacksonville Fire Department. Smoke was already moving through the 13-story Hotel Roosevelt on Adams Street, and some of its residents were still in bed. By the time the fire was out less than two hours later, 22 people had died, most of them from carbon monoxide poisoning. It was the worst fire in Jacksonville since the Great Fire of 1901, and its 22 deaths were far more than the seven lives lost in 1901.

## Out Through the Windows

The fire began as an electrical fire in the ballroom's ceiling. When a new ceiling had been installed, the old one, already judged a fire hazard, was left in place above it, and it gave the flames ready kindling. As smoke filled the corridors, guests found their own ways out. Some climbed from the building with help from other patrons, and others knotted bedsheets together into makeshift ropes and climbed down. It was desperate, improvised work, carried out by strangers helping strangers through the smoke. By 9:30 a.m. the fire was extinguished, and nearly 475 people had been saved from the burning building.

## Helicopters over Downtown

Mayor W. Haydon Burns immediately called on the U.S. Navy for help, and eight helicopters lifted off from Cecil Field and Naval Air Station Jacksonville and flew to downtown. The airmen helped hotel guests out of the building and carried them to a nearby parking lot, where ambulances stood waiting. Among those who survived the morning were Donna Axum, Miss America 1964, along with two basketball coaches, Ken Norton of the Manhattan Jaspers and Norm Sloan of the Florida Gators.

## The Twenty-Two

Not everyone could be reached in time. Twenty residents died of smoke inhalation in their beds. A woman trying to escape from her room on the 11th floor slipped from the rope she had made of bedsheets and did not survive. The fire also claimed one of the men fighting it: Assistant Chief J.R. Romedy collapsed of a heart attack during the first rescue efforts and died at the scene. Each of them left behind people who mourned them. Memorial gatherings have been held over the years to remember those who were lost, among them one in December 2003, on the fortieth anniversary of the fire.

## A City Takes In Its Guests

In the hours after the fire, Jacksonville opened its doors. Local residents, churches and businesses took in the displaced hotel guests and gave them food and clothing. The Roosevelt itself never fully recovered as a hotel. It closed in 1964, and the businesses it had housed moved to the Hotel George Washington. The building later reopened as a retirement home, the Jacksonville Regency House, which closed in 1989. In February 1991 the old hotel was placed on the National Register of Historic Places. It still stands downtown on Adams Street. Built in 1926 as the Carling Hotel and renamed the Roosevelt in 1936, it was converted by Vestcor into The Carling, a 100-unit apartment building that opened in July 2005, and it carries its original name again.
