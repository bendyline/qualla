# James Weldon Johnson Park

Described as Jacksonville's oldest park, this square has been a village green, the site of a Confederate monument, the scene of the violence of Ax Handle Saturday and, since 2020, a park named for the city's own James Weldon Johnson.

**Location:** 30.3292, -81.6592

**Categories:** Parks in Jacksonville, Florida, Downtown Jacksonville, Civil rights movement, African-American history of Florida, Removed Confederate monuments

## Introduction

Few places in Jacksonville have worn as many names as this 1.54-acre square downtown. It has been City Park, St. James Park, Hemming Park and Hemming Plaza, and since 2020 it has been James Weldon Johnson Park. Each name marks a turn in the city's story: a frontier town's village green, a monument raised to Confederate soldiers, a civil rights struggle fought in the stores around it, and finally a decision about whom the heart of downtown should honor. It is described as the first park in Jacksonville and its oldest.

## A Green for a Young Town

Isaiah Hart, the founder of Jacksonville, established the area as a public square in 1857. After Hart's death in 1861 and the end of the Civil War, his family deeded the land to the city for $10. It was known at first simply as City Park. In 1869 the grand St. James Hotel went up across the street, and the green took the hotel's name. At its center stood a popular fountain. Among the town's residents in those years were the Hemings. John C. Heming, an Englishman who spelled the name with one "m," arrived in the mid-1840s, worked in real estate and as a bookkeeper, and served as town auctioneer and city councilman before his death in 1886. His son Charles moved to Jacksonville from St. Augustine at the age of two and grew up in the city before heading west, where he became a banker in Colorado Springs and made a fortune.

## A Soldier on a Granite Shaft

In February 1896, Charles C. Hemming announced at a reunion of the United Confederate Veterans in Ocala that he would erect a memorial to Florida's Confederate soldiers. He chose the center of St. James Park, and the city, though reluctant to lose the fountain, moved it aside. The monument, supplied by George H. Mitchell of Chicago, cost about $20,000 and was a joint gift from Hemming and his wife, Lucy Key Hemming. It rose sixty-two feet in Vermont granite, topped by a bronze soldier at ease in winter uniform, with "J.L.I." for the Jacksonville Light Infantry on his cap. It was unveiled on June 16, 1898, in the middle of the Spanish-American War, with General Fitzhugh Lee in the reviewing stand and a grandson of Ulysses S. Grant watching from the piazza of the Windsor Hotel. Troops from both North and South attended, and much of the oratory was about reunion. In 1899 the city renamed the green Hemming Park, and two years later the monument was one of the few structures to survive the Great Fire of 1901.

## Ax Handle Saturday

The park's visibility made it a stage. John F. Kennedy and Richard Nixon both spoke here, a few hours apart, on October 18, 1960, and Lyndon B. Johnson followed in 1964. In the same decade, the park and its surrounding stores became the site of civil rights demonstrations against segregated public facilities in a city with a large Black population. Rutledge Pearson, a local high school teacher, and the NAACP organized students for sit-ins, and Rodney Hurst, just 16, led the NAACP Youth Council. Beginning on August 13, 1960, students asked to be served at segregated lunch counters. On August 27, a group of about 200 white men, some thought to have Ku Klux Klan ties, armed themselves with axe handles and baseball bats, and beatings followed. Organizers had warned police when they saw the armed men, but officers did not intervene against the mob; when the Boomerangs, a group of Black men, and other Black residents fought back to stop the beatings, police arrested them. More than 50 people were injured, by one count, and 62 were arrested, most of them Black.

## Starlings and Concrete

By the mid-1970s the park had changed again. Thousands of starlings had descended on downtown, and the city removed the shade trees to drive them out. A 1977 renovation costing $648,000 converted the green into a concrete and brick-paved square. Four decades after the sit-ins, on August 26, 2000, the NAACP, the Jacksonville Urban League, the Jacksonville Historical Society and other groups gathered to commemorate what had happened here. In 2014 the city handed management to a nonprofit, Friends of Hemming Park, charged with bringing the square back to life, and on the first Wednesday of each month it became the centerpiece of Jacksonville's Downtown Art Walk.

## Lift Every Voice

On June 9, 2020, during local protests following the murder of George Floyd, the Confederate monument and its plaque came down after 122 years at the center of the park, the end of a three-year removal campaign by Take 'Em Down Jax. Two months later, on August 11, the City Council voted to rename the park for James Weldon Johnson. Born in Jacksonville in 1871, Johnson became principal of the city's Stanton School, and in 1897 or 1898 he became the first African American admitted to the Florida Bar since Reconstruction ended. In 1900 he wrote the poem "Lift Every Voice and Sing," first performed by 500 schoolchildren at Stanton School for a celebration of Abraham Lincoln's birthday, and his brother later set it to music. From 1920 to 1930 he led the NAACP as its executive secretary. The square where students once faced axe handles now carries his name.

## Flight Context

The park sits at 30.329°N, 81.659°W in downtown Jacksonville on the Northbank of the St. Johns River. From 1,500–2,500 feet, find the downtown towers on the river's north shore; the park is a small open block among them. Airspace here is busy: Jacksonville Naval Air Station (KNIP) lies about 6 nm to the south, Jacksonville Executive at Craig Airport (KCRG) about 7.5 nm to the east, and Jacksonville International (KJAX) about 10 nm to the north. Summer afternoons bring frequent thunderstorms; cooler months are usually clearer.
