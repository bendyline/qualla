# Palatka, Florida

Palatka grew up where the St. Johns River narrows, passing from failed English plantation to Seminole War fort, steamboat resort, burned-out town and brick-built river city.

**Location:** 29.6478, -81.6514

**Categories:** Cities in Putnam County, Florida, County seats in Florida, Populated places on the St. Johns River, Steamboat era, Seminole Wars, Utopian communities in the United States

## Introduction

The Seminole called this place Pilo-taikita, "crossing over" or "cows' crossing." The name describes the river. Here the St. Johns narrows and begins a shallower, winding course upstream toward Lake George and Lake Monroe. Over time the word was contracted to Pilatka, and in 1875 the post office settled on the spelling Palatka. Long before any of that, the Timucua lived here under chiefs Saturiwa and Utina, fishing for bass and mullet and farming beans, corn and squash, until disease that came with European contact, and war, ended them as organized peoples by the mid-18th century. Some survivors joined other tribes; others left with the Spanish for Cuba in 1763.

## Rolle's Ideal Society

In 1767, an English gentleman and philanthropist named Denys Rolle founded Rollestown on the river's east bank, at the head of deepwater navigation. His 78,000-acre plantation was a commercial experiment, and he stocked it with people recruited off the streets of London: paupers, vagrants, pickpockets and, in the phrase of the day, "penitent prostitutes." He paid their passage, and those who survived their indenture could receive land. Some two hundred arrived. Unaccustomed to the work and the subtropical climate, most left. Rolle then bought enslaved Africans, people taken captive in West Africa and forced to tend livestock and grow cotton, indigo and citrus. In 1770 an overseer sold more than 1,000 of Rolle's cattle and vanished with the money, and despite new overseers and more enslaved workers, the plantation never prospered. When Spain resumed control of Florida in 1783, Rolle shipped his household, livestock and the people he enslaved to an estate on Great Exuma in the Bahamas. That point of land in East Palatka is still called Rollestown.

## A Fort Becomes a Town

After the United States acquired Florida in 1821, Nehemiah Brush started a ferry across the river, and the landing became a distribution point for settlers pushing inland. Their hunger for land collided with the Seminole. When the government moved to force the tribe west of the Mississippi under Indian Removal starting in 1833, the Second Seminole War began, and in 1835 the Seminole attacked and burned Pilatka. Recognizing the site's command of the St. Johns, the main artery into Central Florida, the Army built Fort Shannon here in 1838, with a garrison, supply depot and hospital. By 1842 the Army had forced most of the Seminole to Indian Territory, and it left the following year. Settlers simply moved into what the soldiers abandoned, turning military piers and eight blockhouses into the bones of a town. Pilatka became seat of the new Putnam County in 1849 and was incorporated on January 8, 1853, with the help of Judge Isaac H. Bronson.

## Steamboats, Gunboats and a Spy

By the 1850s, Pilatka had a reputation as a winter haven for invalids and others escaping northern cold, and steamboats brought them upriver in growing numbers. One visitor listed the amusements: "sailing, fishing, rowing, walking, riding in buggies and on horseback, whist, euchre, backgammon and hunting." The Civil War emptied the resort. On October 7, 1862, the USS Cimarron fired shells over the destitute, largely deserted town after sighting Confederate cavalry, until Mary Boyd pleaded with Union commander Maxwell Woodhull to spare the town, assuring him the horsemen were not residents. He complied. Confederate spy Lola Sánchez, whose father had been falsely accused of spying and imprisoned by Union soldiers, overheard officers quartered in her family's house planning a raid and warned the Confederates. Capt. John Jackson Dickison's men surprised and captured the Union troops at what became known as the Battle of Horse Landing. After the war the visitors came back, to hotels like the 250-guest Larkin House and to steamers bound up the Ocklawaha River for Silver Springs.

## Rebuilt in Brick

By the 1880s competing railroads met here, and Palatka became an important junction. Then, on November 7, 1884, a devastating fire swept the town. Tourists arriving that season found most accommodations gone and rode the trains farther south, the start of a slow drift of tourism elsewhere, while Jacksonville took the lead in trade and shipping. Palatka rebuilt its downtown in fireproof brick, and much of the original brick paving survives in its gridded historic districts. Industry took up some of the slack. In the early 1890s the Wilson brothers of Michigan bought a local sawmill and grew it into the Wilson Cypress Company, at its peak the second-largest cypress mill in the world, employing, by some accounts, up to sixteen hundred people before it ceased milling in 1944. The Great Freeze of 1894 and 1895 devastated the citrus groves for years. For the mill's young workers, James Ross Mellon, son of Pittsburgh's Judge Thomas Mellon and a regular winter visitor, funded the Larimer library, named for his wife Rachel Larimer and hailed as a "poor man's university."

## The River City Now

Palatka, home to 10,446 people in 2020, is still oriented toward its riverfront. The city is working to redevelop that waterfront and its downtown, where architect Henry John Klutho's 1916 James Hotel shows off the Prairie School style. Ravine Gardens State Park and the Florida Azalea and Blue Crab festivals draw visitors, and the National Register lists include the Central Academy, described as Florida's first accredited African-American high school. Unlike much of Florida, Palatka leans on manufacturing, anchored by a Georgia-Pacific paper mill that employed nearly 900 people in 2022 and was described as the largest private employer in Putnam County. Putnam County has also turned toward ecotourism, expanding nature trails and improving and mapping kayak routes, while portions of the Florida Trail link local paths to parks across the region.

## Flight Context

Palatka sits at 29.648°N, 81.651°W on the west bank of the St. Johns River, where the St. Johns River narrows and turns shallower upstream. From 2,000–3,000 feet, look for the downtown street grid pressed against the riverfront, and the Memorial Bridge carrying US 17 and SR 100 across the river to East Palatka. Palatka Municipal Airport - Lt. Kay Larkin Field (FAA identifier 28J; no ICAO code) lies about 2 nm northwest of town. The rainy season runs June through late September with frequent afternoon storms; October through May offers drier, clearer flying.
