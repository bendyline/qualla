# Fig Springs mission site

Beside a small spring run feeding the Ichetucknee River lie the traces of a 17th-century Spanish mission, most likely San Martín de Timucua, and the graves of the Timucua people who lived, worshipped and died there.

**Location:** 29.9674, -82.7761

**Categories:** Archaeological sites in Florida, Spanish missions in Florida, Timucua, Ichetucknee Springs State Park, 1608 establishments in the Spanish Empire

## Introduction

In 1597, the brother of the chief of Timucua made the journey to St. Augustine and asked the Spanish for a missionary. They sent him home with two iron axes and a hoe, tools for building a church and a house for a priest. That September, Friar Baltazar López came to stay for three months. The place he presumably went, an important Northern Utina town called Ayacuto, lies today beside Fig Springs, whose short spring run feeds the Ichetucknee River in North Florida. Archaeologists believe it is the site of the Mission San Martín de Timucua.

## The Friar Who Arrived on May Day

Ayacuto was one of five major towns in the Province of Timucua at the start of the seventeenth century, a territory stretching across north Florida above the Santa Fe River, from the St. Johns in the east to the Aucilla in the west. Spanish Florida had too few missionaries to replace Fray López for a decade. In 1607 Fray Martin Prieto began visiting the province, and on May 1, 1608, he arrived at its chief town, which became known as San Martín. No one knows whether the chapel and house built after 1597 were still standing when he came, or whether he raised new ones. The mission lasted at least until the Timucua Rebellion of 1656. After that, San Martín de Timucua disappears from Spanish records; Florida's Division of Historical Resources says the site was briefly resettled in 1657 and abandoned for good in the 1660s.

## Clues in the Spring Run

The first hint surfaced in the water. In 1949, archaeologist John Mann Goggin found an apparent refuse heap holding Spanish ceramics in the tributary that links Fig Springs to the Ichetucknee, about a mile downstream from the river's head springs. In 1986, a state project searching for places visited by Hernando de Soto's expedition dug test excavations and found burials, Spanish artifacts and a clay floor, the kind often associated with a mission church. Two years later, researchers drilled 1,341 auger holes at 10-meter intervals across 30 acres. The pattern that emerged suggested a church, a missionary's residence, a cemetery, a plaza and a native village, with Spanish objects clustered to the north and Native American ones to the south. People had lived here long before the friars came. Charcoal from the site's South End dates as far back as the tenth century, and pottery found there helped archaeologists define the Suwannee Valley culture, whose signature ware is now called "Fig Springs Roughened."

## A Church Open to the Air

The excavations of 1988 and 1989 uncovered what may have been the mission's first church, and it looked little like a European cathedral. It was an open-air building with a plank wall at its east end and its other sides open, a roof supported on posts about 10 centimeters square covering an area roughly 10.5 by 8 meters. Builders had cleared the ground to bare earth, laid a clean sand subfloor about 20 centimeters thick, and topped it with packed clay that rose in steps from west to east. At some point the church burned, and its remains were covered with clean sand. What came next is still debated. Later fieldwork suggested a larger church may have replaced it just to the north, and archaeologists have proposed several possible histories for the site's tangle of postholes, from one large church to two small ones.

## The People of San Martín

North of that first church lay a crowded cemetery, used for many years; later estimates put it at 400 to 500 graves. In 1990 and 1991, excavators uncovered the remains of 23 people: five women, nine men, four adults whose sex could not be determined, and five children, from an infant to a youth of 13 to 15. Eight had died between the ages of 25 and 30. They were laid on their backs, aligned with the church, their hands clasped beneath their chins or their arms folded across their chests, without coffins or shrouds. Only three were buried with objects: an older woman with a chert knife, a young man with small blue beads near his feet, and another man with an iron nail. In one grave, a young woman lay with a toddler against her and a small child beside her. Group burials like this may reflect one of the epidemics that periodically swept Florida's missions.

## What the Bones Remember

The skeletons record hard lives. Twelve of the 23 showed bone reactions to injury or chronic irritation, seven had spongy skull bone that can signal malnutrition, and six had osteoarthritis. Of the nineteen people whose teeth survived well, fifteen had defects in their enamel that formed between the ages of two and five, which researchers read as evidence of nutritional stress around weaning, a burden that appears to have grown over time. Yet their cavity rate was low compared with other maize-farming Native communities of the mission era. Today the site lies within Ichetucknee Springs State Park, a quiet place where a vanished town and its people are remembered through the smallest traces they left behind.

## Flight Context

The Fig Springs mission site lies within Ichetucknee Springs State Park in Columbia County, Florida, at approximately 29.9674°N, 82.7761°W, beside a short spring run near the head of the Ichetucknee River. The river's narrow, clear, tree-lined channel, running south toward its confluence with the Santa Fe River, is the key visual reference; the site itself is hidden under forest canopy and shows nothing from the air. A viewing altitude of 1,500–2,500 feet AGL keeps the river corridor in view. Lake City Gateway Airport (KLCQ) is about 16 nm to the northeast, and Gainesville Regional Airport (KGNV) is about 31 nm to the east-southeast. Summer afternoons bring frequent thunderstorms; mornings and the cooler months are usually clearest.
