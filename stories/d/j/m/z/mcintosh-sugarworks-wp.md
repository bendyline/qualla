# McIntosh Sugarworks

Long mistaken for a Spanish mission, the tabby ruins near St. Marys, Georgia, are the remains of an 1820s plantation sugarhouse built by John Houstoun McIntosh and worked by enslaved people.

**Location:** 30.7933, -81.5789

**Categories:** National Register of Historic Places in Camden County, Georgia, Tabby buildings, Ruins on the National Register of Historic Places, Sugar refineries in the United States, Plantations in Georgia (U.S. state), Slavery in the United States

## Introduction

For many years, people believed the standing tabby walls outside St. Marys, Georgia, were all that remained of an old Spanish mission. The truth is less romantic and more revealing. These are the ruins of the McIntosh Sugarworks, built in the late 1820s by the planter John Houstoun McIntosh, and they stand today as a significant example of tabby concrete architecture and a look at the industrial side of southeastern plantation agriculture. The site is also known as the Tabby Ruins. It sits six miles north of St. Marys on Charlie Smith Sr. Highway, roughly across the road from the entrance to Naval Submarine Base Kings Bay.

## Walls Made of Oyster Shell

Tabby is a type of concrete made by burning oyster shells to produce lime, then mixing that lime with water, sand, ash and broken shells. Early Spanish settlers in Florida built with it, and later so did British colonists along the coasts of Georgia and South Carolina.

The walls that still stand outline a rectangular building with three rooms set in a row and two porches off the central room. Each room handled a separate step of production, making the sugarhouse a streamlined operation. The two end rooms had wooden floors raised off the ground and supported by the tabby walls. The middle room was different: it had a floor of packed earth, because it held the fires.

## A Planter's New Canaan

John Houstoun McIntosh began planting in Camden County after the War of 1812, when he settled permanently at Mariana Plantation on the St. Marys River. In 1819 he bought two smaller plantations and renamed his combined holdings New Canaan. In the Georgia lowcountry, large-scale planting then centered on rice, and sugar called for a different growing routine but not a different kind of plantation, so for McIntosh it added another cash crop without adding much cost.

In 1825 Thomas Spalding recommended a sugar mill design to him, and by 1829, according to John Hamilton Couper, the McIntosh mill was already in production. Similar tabby sugarhouses were built elsewhere in the region, including one at Elizafield Plantation in Glynn County, near Brunswick.

## Heat, Cane and Forced Labor

Growing cane added little cost for McIntosh. Processing it was another matter, as the sheer size of the sugarhouse shows; building it alone took a large amount of materials and labor. The work of the harvest fell on enslaved people. They cut the cane, stripped its leaves and loaded it onto flat carts that hauled it to the mills. Thomas Spalding estimated that one enslaved laborer could take the cane from two acres in two months, a planter's arithmetic that measured human beings by their output.

Inside the middle room, the extracted juice was boiled in a series of kettles, each one condensing it further, until it became a clarified syrup. In the last room, the syrup was poured into containers to crystallize into granular sugar, and the molasses was drained away. Nothing went to waste: sugar and molasses were both sold, and the bagasse, the crushed cane fiber left after the juice was extracted, could be dried to fuel the boiling fires or used as animal feed.

## Bollingbrook and After

McIntosh died in 1836, and in 1840 New Canaan was sold to Caroline Hallowes. The Hallowes family renamed the plantation Bollingbrook and lived there until after the Civil War. During the war, Colonel Hallowes kept planting cane and making sugar, and he also turned the tabby sugarhouse into a starch factory, producing arrowroot starch in large quantities.

The walls outlasted the plantation, and for many years the mission story clung to them. On April 2, 1992, the sugarworks was added to the National Register of Historic Places, recognized for what it actually was: an industrial relic of plantation agriculture on the Georgia coast.

## Flight Context

The McIntosh Sugarworks ruins sit near 30.793°N, 81.579°W in Camden County, Georgia, six miles north of St. Marys, just across Charlie Smith Sr. Highway from the entrance to Naval Submarine Base Kings Bay. The small ruins are hard to spot from the air; at 1,500–2,500 feet use the base entrance and the highway as references. Nearby airports: Fernandina Beach Municipal Airport (KFHB) about 14 miles south-southeast and Jacksonville International Airport (KJAX) about 22 miles south-southwest. Check current charts for restricted airspace over Kings Bay and stay well clear of the base. Coastal fog and summer thunderstorms can reduce visibility.
