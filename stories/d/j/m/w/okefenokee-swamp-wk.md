# Okefenokee Swamp

Four hundred thirty-eight thousand acres of blackwater swamp where the peat islands literally tremble underfoot - the Hitchiti word for it means just that - and where, in 2025, a six-year campaign finally bought out the titanium mine that would have drained it.

**Location:** 30.7363, -82.1221

## Introduction

Step from a boardwalk onto one of the floating peat islands and the ground gives. Not in the way wet earth gives. The whole island flexes, the trees on it shift their leaves, the small things living on it adjust their footing. This is what the Hitchiti meant when they called the place oki-fanoki, often rendered into English as land of trembling earth. Closer to the literal sense is bubbling water, because what makes the peat tremble is methane from decomposing vegetation working its way up from below. Either translation fits. Four hundred thirty-eight thousand acres of blackwater wetland sit astride the Georgia-Florida line, the largest swamp of its kind in North America, the slow-moving headwaters of the Suwannee River, and one of the wildest places left in the eastern United States. Roads end at its edges. The interior moves on its own schedule.

## Water the Color of Strong Tea

The blackwater is not dirty. It is clean - cleaner than most river water on its way to the Gulf of Mexico. The color comes from tannins leached by cypress, gum, and the slow rot of peat, the same chemistry that gives strong tea its red-brown sheen. Stick a hand in and you can read your watch through the water. The swamp sits in a shallow basin that was the floor of the Atlantic during higher sea levels, now drained slowly toward the Suwannee, which heads here and runs southwest about 250 miles to the Gulf. A smaller share drains east through the St. Marys River. The peat beneath the water is thick - up to fifteen feet in places - and in dry years it cracks and burns. Drought, fire, regrowth: this is the rhythm. The 2007 Bugaboo Scrub Fire burned for months and sent smoke as far as the Carolinas. The swamp regrew.

## Trembling Earth

The most surprising thing about Okefenokee is not the alligators or the cypress or the black water, though all of those are remarkable. It is the ground. Peat mats break free from the bottom and float, sometimes hosting a full stand of trees, sometimes only sedges and pitcher plants. Walk on one of these floating islands and the surface moves under you - a slow undulation that feels more like the deck of a ship than land. The Hitchiti, and after them the Muscogee Creek, named the place for this peculiar quality. Settlers borrowed the name without quite understanding it. The Chesser family, who homesteaded a slightly more stable island on the eastern side in the late nineteenth century, lived for generations on ground that, technically, was always considering drifting away. Their cabin still stands, on what is still, technically, a floating island.

## Alligators, Cranes, and Pitcher Plants

The wildlife inventory reads like a checklist of southeastern wetland icons. Alligators, thousands of them, lounging on hummocks and patrolling the canals at dawn. Sandhill cranes nesting in the wet prairies, their bugling carrying for miles across open water. Florida black bears denning in the higher pine islands. River otters, gar, bowfin, ibis, anhinga, swallow-tailed kite. The nutrient-poor blackwater starves most plants but suits the carnivores: pitcher plants, sundews, bladderwort, and butterworts trap and digest insects to make up the difference. The federal refuge counts thirty-nine fish species, sixty-four reptile species, two hundred-plus birds. The wilderness designation covers most of the interior. Nearly four hundred thousand visitors come each year, almost all of them staying close to the three official entrances, which is exactly the way the swamp prefers it.

## The Mine That Almost Was

On the eastern rim of the basin rises Trail Ridge, a low sand ridge that acts as a dam holding the swamp in. Twin Pines Minerals, an Alabama-based company, proposed digging a titanium mine into that ridge - extracting heavy minerals for white pigment in paint and toothpaste. Hydrologists warned that mining the ridge could lower the water table inside the refuge and bleed the swamp dry, gradually, irreversibly. For six years, a coalition of Georgians, scientists, the Muscogee Nation, recreational paddlers, and the Southern Environmental Law Center fought the proposal through permits and courts. In June 2025, The Conservation Fund announced it had bought Twin Pines' eight thousand acres and the underlying mineral rights for sixty million dollars. The mine is finished before it began. The water table is, for now, safe. Roughly twice that acreage along the ridge remains unprotected, and the campaign now turns toward those parcels.

## Three Entrances

The refuge offers three ways in, and each shows a different swamp. The East Entrance, southwest of Folkston, Georgia, opens onto wet prairies and the Chesser Homestead with its observation tower - the panoramic view that puts the scale of the place in perspective. The West Entrance at Stephen C. Foster State Park, east of Fargo, plunges paddlers directly into the cypress cathedral, where the canopy closes overhead and the water turns mirror-still between the trunks. The North Entrance at Okefenokee Swamp Park, near Waycross, is the most curated, with boardwalks and exhibits. Multi-day canoe routes thread the interior, with shelters every fifteen or twenty miles - the only legal way to spend a night inside the wilderness. The refuge was nominated in 2023 as a UNESCO World Heritage Site, which would make it Georgia's first. The trembling earth is being slowly, formally, taken seriously.

## Flight Context

Located at 30.74 N, 82.12 W on the Georgia-Florida border. From altitude the Okefenokee is unmistakable - a vast dark expanse, roughly 38 miles long and 25 wide, distinct against the surrounding pine forest and farm fields. Wet prairies show as lighter open patches inside the darker cypress canopy. The Suwannee Canal is a visible straight line cutting in from the east entrance. The St. Marys River traces the swamp's southern boundary, an excellent navigation reference. Waycross-Ware County (KAYS) is approximately 20 nm north; Jacksonville International (KJAX) is around 60 nm southeast; small fields at Folkston serve the east entrance. Best viewing at three to five thousand feet. Morning is calmest; afternoon brings summer thunderstorms. Trail Ridge, the low sand ridge along the eastern edge, is the unobvious feature worth identifying - that is the wall that keeps the swamp wet.
