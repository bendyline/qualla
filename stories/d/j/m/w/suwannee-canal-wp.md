# Suwannee Canal

Dug in the 1890s to drain Georgia's Okefenokee Swamp, the failed Suwannee Canal became one of the main waterways that carry visitors into it.

**Location:** 30.7376, -82.1416

**Categories:** Canals in Georgia (U.S. state), Okefenokee Swamp, Logging history

## Introduction

The ditch was meant to kill the swamp. Instead it became a road into it. In the 1890s, a company of investors set out to drain large portions of Georgia's Okefenokee Swamp by digging a canal toward the St. Marys River, and they failed. What they left behind, the Suwannee Canal, survives as a principal waterway into the Okefenokee, and thousands of visitors travel it every year. Even the name wandered: records also spell it "Suwanee Canel," since the single-n spelling of Suwannee was once more common than the double-n form that is standard today.

## The Ridge That Would Not Yield

In 1889, the Georgia General Assembly authorized the governor to sell the land containing the Okefenokee Swamp to the highest bidder. The winning offer came from a group of capitalists and former Confederate officers, who paid 26.5 cents per acre. Their plan was to drain the swamp through a ditch running out to the St. Marys River.

Work began on September 20, 1891. The company's president, Captain Henry Jackson, and his crews spent three years pushing the Suwannee Canal 11.5 miles into the swamp. It was slow, frustrating labor. Poor engineering design and bad weather caused the sides of the ditch to collapse, and a stretch known as Trail Ridge proved harder still.

The company first put leased convict labor on the job, prisoners hired out to do the grueling digging. It brought in large steam shovels. Finally it recruited gold miners from north Georgia, who attacked the ground with large water hoses. None of it worked. No one could cut the ditch deep enough through Trail Ridge to drain the swamp, and in October 1894 the drainage effort was abandoned.

## Cypress on the Water

Alongside the digging, the company turned to timber. In 1894 it built a large sawmill at Camp Cornelia and laid a railroad from Folkston, Georgia, to the mill. It was one of the first cypress companies in the nation to use industrial logging equipment, and a steamboat towed rafts of cypress logs along the canal to the mill. The operation turned out more than 7 million feet of lumber and dredged almost 20 miles of canals.

The hope was that lumber sales would raise enough money to continue the drainage project. They never did. The market was flooded with lumber, prices were too low, and the high cost of freighting wood that could not be sold drained the company's assets. It borrowed money until its funds were exhausted.

## A Family Fortune Sinks

On December 13, 1895, Captain Jackson died following an operation for appendicitis. That same month his father, General Henry R. Jackson, was elected president and lent the company more money, keeping it alive until 1897, when he stopped lending. The company went into receivership in June 1897. The receiver, Joel Hurt, tried to run the business himself and found it as unprofitable as ever, and economic recessions pushed the company into bankruptcy.

In 1899 the property went to members of the Jackson family as the "Jackson Trust," which in 1901 sold it to Charles Hebard, a prominent Philadelphia lumberman, and the Hebard Lumber Company of Lowndes County, Georgia. The real harvest came later. After a railroad was built into the swamp's west edge, logging began in 1909, and by the time it ceased in 1927, more than 400 million board feet of timber (sources give roughly 425 to 431 million), much of it old-growth cypress, had been taken out of the Okefenokee.

## Flight Context

The canal lies near 30.738°N, 82.142°W, on the east side of the Okefenokee Swamp in southeast Georgia, southwest of Folkston. From 2,000–3,000 feet, look for the straight line of the canal cutting west into the swamp. Nearest fields: Davis Field at Folkston (FAA identifier 3J6, no ICAO code) about 8 miles east-northeast and Hilliard Airpark (FAA 01J) about 14 miles east-southeast; Jacksonville International Airport (KJAX) lies about 32 miles to the east-southeast. Summer thunderstorms can reduce visibility; fly high to avoid disturbing wildlife.
