# SS Alcoa Puritan (1941)

Shelled and torpedoed by a U-boat in the Gulf of Mexico in May 1942, the unarmed freighter Alcoa Puritan lost no one, and six decades later her name was found still legible on her stern nearly two kilometers down.

**Location:** 28.5833, -88.3667

**Categories:** World War II merchant ships of the United States, Ships sunk by German submarines in World War II, Shipwrecks in the Gulf of Mexico, Maritime incidents in May 1942, Shipwrecks on the National Register of Historic Places

## Introduction

The submarine's captain was sorry. That, at least, is what he shouted across the water on the afternoon of May 6, 1942, as his U-boat drew near the lifeboats of the freighter it had just sent to the bottom of the Gulf of Mexico. The 34-year-old commander told the survivors of the SS Alcoa Puritan that he hoped they would "make it in all right." Then he waved, followed his crew down a hatch, submerged and was gone. Behind that strange courtesy lay nearly half an hour of shellfire, a ship turning helpless circles, and a warning her previous captain had given that shoreside management had not heeded.

## Alone and Unarmed

The Alcoa Puritan carried freight and passengers between U.S. and Caribbean ports for the Alcoa Steamship Company, typically with 10 officers, 33 crew and room for 8 to 10 passengers. Around May 1, 1942, she sailed from Port of Spain, Trinidad, for Mobile, Alabama, loaded with bauxite, traveling alone and without guns. Her former master, Axel B. Axelsen, had just left the ship after unsuccessfully urging shoreside management to arm her, and Captain Yngvar A. Krantz was newly in command. Among the passengers were survivors of the Standard Oil tanker T.C. McCobb, which had already been torpedoed. They were sailing into the height of the German submarine campaign. Postwar records show that merchant shipping losses in American and Caribbean waters were heavy that spring.

## Half an Hour of Shellfire

Just before noon on May 6, one of the McCobb survivors spotted a torpedo wake passing astern. The general alarm sounded, and moments later a submarine surfaced about two nautical miles away and fired a warning shell over the ship. Krantz ordered full speed and a zig-zag course, hoping to outrun the attacker. The first few shells missed. Then the gunners found their range, and over roughly half an hour about 70 rounds struck the Puritan, laying open her superstructure, riddling the funnel, shattering every window and instrument face, setting fires inside and finally wrecking the steering gear at the stern. At about 12:30, with the ship circling out of control, Krantz stopped the engines and ordered everyone off. Once all were in the water, a second torpedo hit the engine room on the port side, and the Puritan listed heavily and sank. Within an hour a Navy patrol plane, called in by the radio operator during the shelling, found the lifeboats, and at about 4:05 p.m. the Coast Guard cutter Boutwell rescued every passenger and crew member, some of them badly injured.

## A Name Still Legible in the Deep

Postwar research identified the attacker as U-507 under Harro Schacht, who was still in command when his submarine was bombed and sunk off Brazil by U.S. Navy aircraft on January 13, 1943. Six decades after the sinking, contractors running deep-tow surveys for Shell came across a large wreck in the Mississippi Canyon area, roughly 45 to 50 nautical miles southeast of the mouth of the Mississippi River, and marine archaeologists identified it as the Puritan. Investigations by C&C Technologies began in 2002, and the ROV vessel HOS Dominator found the hull mostly intact at 1,965 meters, its superstructure only moderately damaged. Video shows the words "ALCOA PURITAN, NEW YORK" still on her stern. The ship became part of a study of World War II wrecks in the Gulf and their role as artificial reefs in deep water, and it was judged eligible for the National Register of Historic Places.

## Flight Context

The wreck lies near 28.583 N, 88.367 W in the open Gulf of Mexico, in the Mississippi Canyon area roughly 45 to 50 nautical miles southeast of the Mississippi River's mouth, beneath nearly 2,000 meters of water. Nothing is visible on the surface; the nearest references are the tip of the Mississippi's Birdfoot Delta to the northwest and any offshore platforms in view. Land airports are all about 100 nm or more away, including Gulf Shores International Airport (KJKA, formerly Jack Edwards National) at Gulf Shores about 109 nm north-northeast, and Gulfport-Biloxi International (KGPT) about 115 nm north-northwest. This is an overwater flight far beyond gliding distance of shore, so plan fuel, flotation and weather carefully; summer thunderstorms and hurricanes (June to November) build quickly over the Gulf.
