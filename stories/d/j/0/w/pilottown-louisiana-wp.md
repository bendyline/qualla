# Pilottown, Louisiana

Built on piers near the mouth of the Mississippi after a hurricane erased its predecessor, Pilottown has lost its families, its school and its post office, but the river pilots who guide ships to New Orleans still work from there.

**Location:** 29.1817, -89.2575

**Categories:** Ghost towns in Plaquemines Parish, Louisiana, Road-inaccessible communities of Louisiana, Unincorporated communities in Plaquemines Parish, Louisiana, Maritime pilots, Mississippi River

## Introduction

Pilottown still has its own ZIP code, 70081. What it no longer has is a post office. The U.S. Postal Service closed it after failing to find a postmaster willing to live in the village, a fair measure of how remote this place is. Built on piers in swampy ground on the east bank of the lower Mississippi, a few miles above the Head of Passes and about ten miles south of Venice, Louisiana, Pilottown has no permanent residents at all. Yet ships bound for New Orleans still depend on it, because this is where the river pilots take over.

## The Seamark

Pilots worked the mouth of the Mississippi long before Pilottown existed. In 1699, the first French settlers built a crude fort and dwellings near the river's mouth and called the place La Balize, meaning "seamark." In 1721 they raised a 62-foot wooden pyramid to guide ships through the shifting channels of the delta, and river pilots made their homes there. The settlement endured for more than 160 years. At its peak, probably around the 1860s, La Balize had some 800 people. Then came the hurricane of September 14-15, 1860, which blew its buildings down and swept the area with a storm surge. La Balize was finally abandoned, and the main shipping route shifted to the deeper Southwest Pass. The pilots moved upriver, above the Head of Passes where the Mississippi splits into its branches, and built a new settlement. They named it Pilottown.

## A Relay of Pilots

The lower river is difficult water. Shifting passages and sandbars, tides and a powerful downstream current make the journey difficult, so every oceangoing ship entering the Mississippi River system must take a pilot aboard, even though the captain remains responsible for the vessel. The work is handed off in stages. Pilots from the Associated Branch Pilots guide ships between the Gulf of Mexico and Pilottown. At Pilottown, members of the Crescent River Port Pilots' Association take over for the run to New Orleans, and pilots of the New Orleans-Baton Rouge Steamship Pilots Association handle ships continuing to Baton Rouge. The Crescent pilots use Pilottown as a temporary home while on duty, and the settlement also serves as a base for oil exploration, with large oil tanks standing among its few buildings.

## Life a Few Feet Above the River

Pilottown sits only a few feet above river level, so a raised concrete walkway runs the length of the island, linking the pilots' housing, a weather station and the tanks and giving people solid footing when the water rises. The only ways in are by boat or helicopter. In the 19th and early 20th centuries, fishermen, pilots and their families lived here year-round. Over time most moved to larger communities upriver, and the one-room school closed in the 1970s. The last few residents did not return after Hurricane Katrina in 2005, which struck southern Louisiana near Pilottown and heavily damaged nearly every structure. The storm pushed the Associated Branch Pilots' headquarters, which had stood in Pilottown for more than a century, backward off its foundation, and that association moved ten miles upriver to Venice. The Crescent River Port Pilots stayed, because their route begins here. They still keep their headquarters in Pilottown, even though no pilot lives there full-time.

## Flight Context

Pilottown lies at 29.1817°N, 89.2575°W on the east bank of the Mississippi, a few miles above Head of Passes and about 10 miles south of Venice, Louisiana, deep in the Mississippi River Delta. From 1,500-2,500 feet AGL, follow the river south from Venice and look for a short line of buildings, oil tanks and a raised walkway on a narrow strip of land just above the point where the river splits into its passes. There is no airport nearby; access is by boat or helicopter. The closest fields with codes are New Orleans NAS JRB/Alvin Callender Field (KNBG, military), about 56 nm northwest, and Louis Armstrong New Orleans International (KMSY), about 71 nm northwest. Expect over-water and marsh terrain with few emergency landing options, plus summer thunderstorms and tropical weather off the Gulf.
