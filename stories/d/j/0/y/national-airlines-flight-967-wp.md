# National Airlines Flight 967

In the small hours of November 16, 1959, a DC-7B bound for New Orleans vanished from radar over the Gulf of Mexico with 42 people aboard, leaving behind a ticket in another man's name and a mystery no investigator ever closed.

**Location:** 29.2167, -88.6667

**Categories:** Aviation accidents and incidents in the United States in 1959, Airliner accidents and incidents in the United States, Unsolved airliner bombings in the United States, Accidents and incidents involving the Douglas DC-7, Gulf of Mexico

## Introduction

"Smooth flight." At 12:44 a.m. on November 16, 1959, Captain Frank E. Todd of Miami radioed those words from the cockpit of National Airlines Flight 967, somewhere over the dark Gulf of Mexico between Tampa and New Orleans. Ceiling and visibility were unlimited, he reported, though a low solid undercast lay to the west-northwest. Seven minutes later the Douglas DC-7B slipped off a military radar scope in Louisiana. All 42 people aboard were presumed killed, and more than six decades on, nobody can say with certainty what brought them down.

## One Airplane, Three Airlines

Flight 967 was a journey stitched together by three carriers. The airplane had left Miami at 10:22 the previous evening, touched down in Tampa at 11:00, and lifted off again for New Orleans at 11:22. National Airlines flew those first two legs. At New Orleans, Delta Air Lines was scheduled to take over for the hop to Dallas, where American Airlines would fly the very same aircraft nonstop to Los Angeles. The DC-7B in service that night was, in fact, a Delta airplane. Interchange flights like this one, offering through-plane service across the country without a change of aircraft, were fairly common in the late 1950s. They faded only after the Civil Aeronautics Board began awarding more direct routes, beginning with the Southern Transcontinental Service Case, decided in 1961. For the people who settled into their seats in Tampa just before midnight, this was simply the late flight west.

## Seven Minutes Over Dark Water

Todd's final message reached his company's radio in New Orleans. Controllers at the New Orleans Air Route Traffic Control Center, housed in the terminal building at New Orleans Lakefront Airport, cleared him to descend, relaying the instruction through the FAA radio station at Pensacola, a normal step before the approach to Moisant International Airport. The Civil Aeronautics Board's report puts the clearance at 6,000 feet, at the pilot's discretion, and Todd said he would stay at his cruising altitude a little longer. The weather waiting at Moisant was thick: the last observation relayed to the flight, at midnight local time, gave a 700-foot overcast, two and a half miles of visibility, very light drizzle and fog, and the Board concluded that low stratus and fog would have put the airport below landing limits by the time the flight arrived. At 12:46, radar operators at a military station in Houma, Louisiana, picked up the flight. It held a track of 296 degrees magnetic for a few minutes, then turned right to a heading of roughly 010. At 12:51 it disappeared from the scope. When company radio began calling the flight at 1:06, nothing came back, and FAA facilities, New Orleans Approach Control and air traffic control fared no better.

## What the Gulf Gave Back

Search aircraft found floating debris and bodies the next morning, some eight miles southeast of the last radar return, which lay about 30 miles east of Pilottown, Louisiana. Searchers retrieved nine bodies and part of a tenth. The main wreckage has never been found, despite months of sonar searches and dives by the Navy and others. Those who saw the scene could not agree on what it meant. Lieutenant James L. Sigman, executive officer of the Coast Guard station at New Orleans, said the airliner might have exploded when it struck the water. The victims' clothing had been stripped away and they bore severe burns, he said, and because only one life preserver turned up among the debris, the passengers apparently had no warning to put on lifebelts. Sigman noted that the wreckage lay within a comparatively small area of two to three miles, which to him meant any explosion came after impact. Two Air Force fliers who spent four hours over the scene read the same waters differently: to them, the wreckage looked scattered enough to suggest a blast in the air. The Civil Aeronautics Board's report later said that the nine bodies showed impact injuries and no exposure to fire or smoke before death, and that the burn marks on bodies and debris came from a brief flash fire on the surface at impact.

## The Name on the Ticket

The day after the crash, a Pennsylvania paper, the Clearfield Progress, put the question plainly: "Was there an explosion aboard the National Airlines DC-7B liner that crashed in the Gulf of Mexico Monday with 42 persons aboard?" The airline's vice president of operations, I. W. Dymond, told the New York Times that the weather "was anticipated, but it was not a factor." Suspicion soon settled on a passenger flying under someone else's name. William Taylor had boarded with a ticket issued to Robert Vernon Spears, a convicted criminal then working as a naturopath. A hypothesis took shape: Spears, a longtime criminal associate of Taylor's, had tricked him into boarding with a piece of luggage containing a bomb, expecting authorities to count Spears among the dead so that his wife could collect on his life insurance. The paper trail told the story: Taylor had bought a $37,500 flight insurance policy in his own name from a coin-operated machine at Tampa's airport minutes before departure, naming his son as beneficiary, while National's records showed no ticket in his name and his body was not among those recovered.

## A Case Without a Cause

Spears disappeared after the crash. In January 1960 he was arrested in Phoenix, Arizona, with Taylor's car, and he was convicted of interstate transportation of a stolen car, Taylor's, receiving the maximum sentence of five years. For lack of evidence, he was never charged in the alleged bombing, and the theory that he was behind it was never proven. He died of coronary thrombosis in Dallas on May 2, 1969. The Civil Aeronautics Board, predecessor of today's NTSB, could not determine a probable cause, because there was simply too little to examine; it added that, with the FBI's help, it had investigated Spears's activities and found no relationship to the accident. The theories remain theories. What is certain is smaller and heavier: a late flight bound for New Orleans carried 42 people out over the Gulf, and none of them came home.

## Flight Context

Article coordinates: 29.217°N, 88.667°W, open water in the northern Gulf of Mexico about 30 miles east of Pilottown, Louisiana, where radar observed the aircraft's descent; the Civil Aeronautics Board plotted the debris and bodies about 8 nautical miles to the southeast, near 29.12°N, 88.55°W. There are no surface landmarks. Flying around 6,000 feet over the water echoes the altitude Flight 967 was cleared to descend to on its way into New Orleans. Nearby airports for retracing the route: Louis Armstrong New Orleans International (KMSY, the former Moisant International), New Orleans Lakefront (KNEW), and Pensacola International (KPNS). The night of the crash brought low stratus, fog and very light drizzle at New Orleans (a 700-foot overcast in the last observation relayed to the flight), so be ready for instrument conditions near the coast.
