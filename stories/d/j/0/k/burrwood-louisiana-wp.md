# Burrwood, Louisiana

Near the very tip of the Mississippi River delta, Burrwood was an Army Corps of Engineers outpost, a World War II naval base, and a hurricane warning station home to as many as 1,000 people, and it has since been all but swallowed by coastal erosion.

**Location:** 28.9681, -89.3775

**Categories:** Geography of the New Orleans metropolitan area, Ghost towns in Plaquemines Parish, Louisiana

## Introduction

At one time about a thousand people lived at Burrwood. There were cottages in a row, barracks, a two-room schoolhouse, and a lookout tower watching the mouth of the Mississippi. Today it is uninhabited, and only a ship pilots' station still stands in the marsh. Burrwood sat near the far southern end of the Mississippi River delta in Plaquemines Parish, Louisiana, and most of the ground it stood on is now fully or partially underwater, taken by coastal erosion. To find it, you have to know where the town used to be.

## Keeping the Channel Open

Burrwood began as a working outpost. The U.S. Army Corps of Engineers established and maintained it on Southwest Pass of the Mississippi River as a base for dredging, jetty work, and the other activities needed to keep that channel navigable. A photograph from 1938 shows a neat row of cottages, and an aerial photograph from 1940 captures the settlement, a small community built around the job of keeping the river's mouth open to shipping.

## Sentinel at the River's Mouth

World War II gave Burrwood a sharper purpose. The United States built a naval base there, known as Section Burrwood Naval Base, to guard against enemy ships and submarines in the Gulf of Mexico and to monitor traffic entering the river's mouth. Construction began in 1941, and the base was placed in commission in December 1941, commanded by N.J. Ashley. The Corps of Engineers raised a lookout tower about 120 feet high, and ships bound from New Orleans and Baton Rouge were inspected here. In 1942 the Army reportedly positioned mobile howitzers on both sides of the river, with a range of about nine miles, and set up a second watchtower on South Pass near Port Eads. Burrwood could support civilian pilot boats, tugs, and dredges alongside patrol boats, submarine chasers, PT boats, and ships as large as destroyers.

## Weather Watch and a Two-Room School

From 1942 to 1965, Burrwood also hosted a United States Weather Bureau office, which took upper-air observations and, from 1956, ran weather radar, an important post at the edge of the Gulf. Life here had the shape of a small company town. A row of officers' houses sheltered families, a barracks complex housed the unmarried men, and a two-room schoolhouse taught both the children of the station's staff and the children of local fishermen who lived nearby.

## Returned to the Water

Burrwood is now a ghost town, and an unusual one. Most ghost towns leave behind ruins to wander. Here, the land itself has largely gone, fully or partially submerged as coastal erosion eats away at the delta. The community that once kept the river open to the world has been reclaimed by the water around it.

## Flight Context

The former site of Burrwood lies at 28.9681°N, 89.3775°W on Southwest Pass near the southern tip of the Mississippi River delta in Plaquemines Parish, Louisiana. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL, follow Southwest Pass south toward the Gulf of Mexico; expect a landscape of open water and low, eroding land. This is a remote area with no public airport nearby. South Lafourche Leonard Miller Jr. Airport (GAO) at Galliano lies about 54 nm west-northwest, New Orleans Lakefront Airport (KNEW) about 73 nm north-northwest, and Louis Armstrong New Orleans International Airport (KMSY) about 77 nm northwest. Plan fuel carefully, expect over-water and marsh flying, and watch for Gulf weather and helicopter traffic serving the region.
