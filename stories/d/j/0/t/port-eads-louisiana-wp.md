# Port Eads, Louisiana

At the very end of the Mississippi's South Pass, a port named for the engineer who made the river dig its own channel became a haven for offshore anglers, until Hurricane Katrina left little but its lighthouse.

**Location:** 29.0156, -89.1711

**Categories:** Unincorporated communities in Plaquemines Parish, Louisiana, Road-inaccessible communities of Louisiana, Populated coastal places in Louisiana, Historic civil engineering landmarks, Mississippi River

## Introduction

James Buchanan Eads made the federal government an unusual offer. He would build jetties at the mouth of the Mississippi first and charge later, and only if the river cut a channel 30 feet deep and held it for 20 years would the government pay him $8 million. Eads had already finished the Eads Bridge at St. Louis in 1874, the first bridge of significant size built primarily of steel. At the South Pass, where the river finally meets the Gulf of Mexico, his jetties worked, and the remote outpost at the tip of that pass still carries his name: Port Eads.

## Teaching a River to Dig

The problem was mud. Along the 100-plus miles between the Port of New Orleans and the Gulf, the river's outlets kept silting up, stranding ships or leaving parts of the river unnavigable for periods of time. Starting in 1875, Eads built a wooden jetty system of pilings and willow mattresses that narrowed the main outlet. Squeezed between the walls, the river sped up and scoured its own channel deeper, and when the jetties were completed in 1879 they held the 30-foot channel his contract required. Large steamers now had safe, year-round access to the river. The effect on commerce was dramatic: in the twenty years after the jetties were completed, trade at New Orleans doubled. The port at South Pass was named in Eads's honor, and in 1982 the American Society of Civil Engineers designated his South Pass jetties a National Historic Civil Engineering Landmark.

## Five Miles From the Deep

At the Head of Passes, the Mississippi splits into three main fingers, and Port Eads sits at the southern end of the center branch. There are no roads; the only ways in are by boat or helicopter, and offshore fishermen typically set out from Venice, Louisiana, 20.3 miles to the north. A lighthouse guides oceangoing ships, an iron-skeleton tower first lit on August 25, 1881, and still active. What drew people here was the water just offshore. Port Eads is the closest port in the entire Gulf of Mexico to the continental shelf's 100-fathom curve, only five miles away, and offshore fishermen came from around the country for its docks, fuel, bunk rooms, weigh station and small restaurant. Each June it hosted the New Orleans Invitational Billfish Tournament. Boats using Port Eads are reported to have landed two thousand-pound Atlantic blue marlin, two of the three such "granders" on record in the Gulf.

## Waiting After Katrina

Hurricane Katrina devastated the port completely. Very little remained except the lighthouse and a few fishing camps that are no longer in service. The Federal Emergency Management Agency first set aside $400,000 to rebuild the Port Eads Marina. Billy Nungesser, who took office as Plaquemines Parish president in 2007, considered that insufficient and traveled to Washington, D.C., himself to appeal. In early 2009, FEMA authorized up to $12 million for the project, thirty times the original figure. A rebuilt marina with 60 slips, a restaurant and bunkhouses did reopen in May 2014, almost nine years after the storm, but it closed indefinitely by 2019 as silt filled South Pass and larger boats could no longer reach it. As of March 2025 the facility was reported vacant, leaving the old lighthouse standing watch over Eads's channel at the edge of the Gulf.

## Flight Context

Port Eads lies at 29.0156°N, 89.1711°W at the southern tip of South Pass, the center of the three main branches of the Mississippi River below Head of Passes, about 20 miles south of Venice, Louisiana. From 1,500-2,500 feet AGL, follow the pass's narrow line of land and jetties out into the Gulf to the lighthouse at its end; the open Gulf and the drop-off to deep water lie just beyond. There is no airport nearby, and the area is reached by boat or helicopter. The closest fields with codes are South Lafourche Leonard Miller Jr. Airport (KGAO) at Galliano, about 63 nm west-northwest, and New Orleans NAS JRB/Alvin Callender Field (KNBG, military), about 66 nm northwest; Louis Armstrong New Orleans International (KMSY) is about 82 nm northwest. Over-water flight here calls for careful fuel planning, and summer thunderstorms and tropical systems can build quickly over the Gulf.
