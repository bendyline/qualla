# South Pass Light

After three wooden towers were battered, rotted or washed away, an iron skeleton lighthouse at the mouth of the Mississippi's South Pass rose in 1881, and in 2005 it was the only structure at Port Eads to survive Hurricane Katrina.

**Location:** 29.0153, -89.1669

**Categories:** Lighthouses in Louisiana, Lighthouses completed in 1881, Mississippi River Delta, Plaquemines Parish, Louisiana, Hurricane Katrina

## Introduction

When Hurricane Katrina swept over the mouth of the Mississippi in August 2005, it took every structure at Port Eads but one. The survivor was an iron skeleton more than 100 feet tall, eight inclined pillars laced with a web of smaller braces, standing on Gordon's Island at South Pass. That design was no accident. It was built to resist the wind from major storms while letting the wind pass straight through. The South Pass Light earned its toughness the hard way, after half a century in which its wooden predecessors were knocked about by river debris, rotted, or were simply swept away.

## A Foundation That Floated

On March 3, 1831, Congress appropriated $40,000 to build lighthouses at South Pass and Southwest Pass, the two principal entrances to the Mississippi from the Gulf of Mexico. Winslow Lewis, the government's favored lighthouse contractor, won a $19,150 contract covering the stations at both passes. His South Pass station was a wooden tower rising from the top of a wooden keeper's dwelling, lit by his patented lamps and reflectors, seven on either side of a revolving chandelier. He placed it on Gordon's Island, named for the New Orleans customs collector Martin Gordon. Lewis had planned to drive piles into the marsh, but Gordon persuaded him to set the station on a floating grid of cross-hatched, squared timbers instead, a choice that would prove fatal on the shifting silt below. Still, on May 15, 1832, the first keeper, Henry Heistand, lit the lamps.

## Three Towers in Seventeen Years

Trouble came quickly. In 1839, logs floating down the river knocked the keeper's dwelling off its pilings, and in 1841 a storm destroyed the whole station. A replacement wooden tower went up on the opposite bank in 1842, but within five years it had rotted beyond repair. The third tower, finished in 1848, was a wooden octagon about fifty-four feet tall, topped with a Cape Cod-style round-domed lantern. Its base disappeared into the middle of a steep-roofed house whose porch rested on six wooden columns, recalling the French-Colonial plantation houses of south Louisiana. It served well. In 1851 Lieutenant David Dixon Porter reported that it and the Southwest Pass Light could be seen twelve miles away. When the Civil War began in 1861, Confederates put out its beacon, as they did at most southern lighthouses, and Union forces relit it the next year with a revolving third-order Fresnel lens, soon swapped for a fourth-order lens.

## Eads's Jetties and an Iron Giant

By the late 1860s the tower was failing, and the Lighthouse Board pressed Congress for a first-class seacoast light, since this beacon was often the first one seen by ships arriving from Europe and the West Indies. Then, in 1875, James Buchanan Eads began building wooden jetties that deepened the river's mouth and kept the shipping channel from silting up. Harper's Weekly ran large engravings of the work, trade at the Port of New Orleans doubled in the twenty years after the jetties were finished, and the little settlement around the lighthouse was named Port Eads in his honor. In 1879 Congress appropriated about $50,000 for a new tower, built from materials originally meant for the Trinity Shoal Light before a lightship was stationed there instead. First lit on August 25, 1881, the iron tower stood 100 feet southeast of the old one and carried a first-order Fresnel lens. In 1900 it was painted white with a black lantern to set it apart from its sister at Southwest Pass.

## Still Burning at Land's End

Inside the iron frame, an elevated cylindrical building is pierced by a central iron tube enclosing the spiral staircase to the lantern, which was built large enough to hold the great first-order lens. That lens was later replaced by a DCB-224 beacon (sources date the change from about 1945 to 1951), and a portion of the lens is displayed at the Capitol Park Museum in Baton Rouge. The station lost its resident keepers in 1971 when it was automated. After Katrina, the federal government appropriated some $12 million to rebuild and enlarge the marina at Port Eads, which reopened in 2014 with docking, fuel, bunk rooms and a restaurant but closed indefinitely by 2019 as silt filled South Pass; as of March 2025 the facility was reported vacant. A string of front range lights once stood nearby, from a beacon on four wooden piles in 1886 to a skeletal iron tower in 1947, but the jetty they stood on has since disappeared into the sand, and a small light on pilings now marks the spot. The light station, established in 1831, is still active.

## Flight Context

The lighthouse stands at 29.015 N, 89.167 W on Gordon's Island at Port Eads, where South Pass, one of the Mississippi's primary entrances from the Gulf of Mexico, meets open water at the tip of the Birdfoot Delta in Plaquemines Parish, Louisiana. From 1,000-2,500 feet the iron-skeleton tower, the marina and the jetty-lined pass are the key references, with Southwest Pass off to the west. There is no nearby airport: the closest public-use fields are South Lafourche Leonard Miller Jr. Airport (KGAO) at Galliano, about 63 nm west-northwest, and New Orleans Lakefront (KNEW), about 76 nm northwest, so plan fuel carefully over water and marsh. Oil-field heliports around Venice mean frequent low-level helicopter traffic. Watch for coastal fog, summer thunderstorms and the June-November hurricane season.
