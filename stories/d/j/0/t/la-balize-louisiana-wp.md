# La Balize, Louisiana

For well over a century, river pilots and their families clung to a stilted village at the mouth of the Mississippi, rebuilding after hurricane after hurricane until the storms of 1860 finally drove them upriver.

**Location:** 29.1224, -89.1073

**Categories:** Ghost towns in Plaquemines Parish, Louisiana, Louisiana (New France), Colonial forts in Louisiana, Mississippi River, Hurricanes

## Introduction

"I never beheld a scene so utterly desolate as this entrance of the Mississippi," wrote Fanny Trollope, recalling her arrival at Christmas 1827, in a book published in 1832. She described pelicans standing on long masses of mud, a pilot coming out to guide her ship over the bar long before land was visible, and the lone mast of a wrecked vessel rising from the eddying water. Then, after miles more of mud and enormous bulrushes, she saw a cluster of huts called the Balize, "by far the most miserable station that I ever saw made the dwelling of man." Yet many families of pilots and fishermen lived there, and ships bound for New Orleans depended on them.

## A Seamark in the Mud

The name La Balize means "seamark." When Robert de La Salle claimed the land for the French crown in 1682, he recognized the importance of this spot near the river's mouth, just above two major forks where passage could be controlled. A map from about 1720 shows the isle and fort of La Balize among the Mississippi's branching mouths, and by 1721 the French had raised a wooden pyramid 62 feet high as the seamark itself. Engineers soon began fighting the delta. In 1726 French engineers dragged an iron harrow through the sandbanks to ease ships across the bar. The low-lying site was always vulnerable to hurricanes, but the French, and later the Spanish, needed to control the river's mouth and give pilots a place to meet incoming ships, so they always rebuilt.

## The Pilots' Village

La Balize was home chiefly to fishermen, river pilots and their families. The pilots were indispensable. Ships bound to and from New Orleans had to cross a bar of shifting currents, mud and sandbars, and the main ship passage itself changed four times before 1888. In 1750 it ran through the Northeast Pass, then in turn through the Southeast Pass, Southwest Pass and South Pass; today it is Southwest Pass again. After the Louisiana Purchase, Americans sometimes called the village Pilotsville, and steam tugboats in the 19th century gave the pilots far more power to maneuver oceangoing ships. The French traveler Élisée Reclus, later a renowned geographer, described the village in 1853 as plank shacks built as light as possible and perched on high stilts over soaked ground, sometimes moored like ships against storm waves. His view of the roughly four hundred Americans living there was jaundiced: he accused them of hoping to rob passing ships.

## The Storms That Kept Coming

The settlement's record reads like a list of disasters. A hurricane in 1740 destroyed La Balize, and the village was rebuilt on San Carlos, a newly emerged island. Another storm destroyed it in October 1778, and again it was rebuilt. By 1853 the village had moved to the Southwest Pass, following the ships. Then came 1860. On August 11, the season's first hurricane uprooted trees and flooded the area under as much as 10 feet of water. On September 14 and 15, a second storm struck the river's mouth with tides six feet above the high-water mark and destroyed La Balize outright. A third hurricane in early October caused widespread damage as far inland as Baton Rouge. The pilots and their families gave up and rebuilt upriver. When a hurricane struck on September 13, 1865, it erased the last traces of the abandoned village.

## Pilottown and a Rusted Tomb

The new settlement rose several miles upriver on the east bank of the Mississippi, just above the Head of the Passes, and took the name Pilottown. At its 19th-century peak it may have had about 800 residents, and a school operated there into the 20th century. Today pilots usually stay only temporarily, for their work shifts. Of La Balize itself almost nothing survives; by the early 20th century, only a rusted iron tomb marked the site. The village lives on in a name, though: the river's active delta lobe is called the Balize Delta after the settlement, or the Birdfoot Delta for its shape.

## Flight Context

The coordinates, 29.122 N, 89.107 W, fall in the far reaches of the Mississippi's Birdfoot Delta in Plaquemines Parish, Louisiana, where the river splits into its passes before reaching the Gulf of Mexico. From 1,500-3,000 feet the delta's long fingers of marsh and the Head of the Passes make striking references; nothing of the village remains to be seen. There is no public airport nearby. The closest public-use airports are South Lafourche Leonard Miller Jr. Airport (KGAO) at Galliano, about 63 nm west-northwest, and New Orleans Lakefront (KNEW), about 73 nm northwest. Many oil-field heliports cluster around Venice, about 16 nm northwest, so expect low-level helicopter traffic. Coastal haze, summer thunderstorms and hurricane season (June to November) all affect flying here.
