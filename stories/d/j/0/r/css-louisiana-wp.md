# CSS Louisiana

Rushed into service before she could steer or even steam against the current, the Confederate ironclad Louisiana fought Farragut's fleet while tied to a riverbank, then was burned and blown up by her own crew.

**Location:** 29.3633, -89.4614

**Categories:** Ironclad warships of the Confederate States Navy, Louisiana in the American Civil War, American Civil War shipwrecks in the Mississippi River, Naval magazine explosions, Ships built in New Orleans

## Introduction

CSS Louisiana went into her only battle tied to a riverbank. She had two paddlewheels, two screw propellers and an iron-armored casemate running almost her full length, and she had been built to help keep the Union Navy out of the lower Mississippi. Yet when the moment came in April 1862, her engines could barely push her against the current, her steering engines had never been installed, and workmen with their tools still crowded her gun deck. The Confederate ironclad had to be towed downriver to Forts Jackson and St. Philip, about 75 miles below New Orleans, and moored there like a floating battery.

## Iron in Short Supply

E.C. Murray laid her down in mid-October 1861 at a new shipyard just north of New Orleans. The design was unusual: two paddlewheels mounted one behind the other in a center well, plus two screws meant not for propulsion but to help her rudders steer through the Mississippi's confined waters and unpredictable currents. Her engines came from the steamer Ingomar, and transferring them took two months. Her casemate was sheathed in two courses of T-rail iron, and iron was exactly what the Confederacy lacked. The Union blockade and the Army's demands on overstrained Southern railroads made it hard to get, and the blockade also cut off the light oak the builders wanted from Florida. A strike cost about a week. The local militia kept calling the workers away for drills and even parades, and the yard competed for skilled hands with Nelson and Asa Tift, who were building the ironclad Mississippi in the adjacent yard. Louisiana was not launched until February 6, 1862.

## Towed Into Battle

By then Flag Officer David Farragut's West Gulf Blockading Squadron had moved into the lower river. On April 18, Commander David Dixon Porter's mortar boats began bombarding the forts. The forts' commander, Brigadier General Johnson K. Duncan, and his superior, Major General Mansfield Lovell, pleaded with the naval commander, William C. Whittle, to send Louisiana down even though she was unfinished and still in her builders' hands. Whittle gave way and commissioned her on April 20 under Commander Charles F. McIntosh. Even at dangerously high boiler pressure she could barely make headway, so she was towed down with workmen aboard and tied to the left bank a short distance above Fort St. Philip. Duncan wanted her below the forts, but Whittle refused to expose her unarmored deck to plunging mortar fire. Many gun carriages sat too high or too low and had to be modified, the gun crews had no room to practice, and soldiers from the forts filled out her incomplete crew.

## The Night the Fleet Ran Past

After nearly a week of bombardment, Farragut judged the mortars ineffective and, on the night of April 24, ran his fleet past the forts. Moored against the bank, Louisiana could use neither her stern guns nor her port broadside. How much she contributed to the firefight is uncertain. General Duncan said she may have fired as few as 12 shots, while Union testimony indicates she exchanged fire with at least one attacking ship, putting three shots all the way through it while the return broadside bounced harmlessly off her armor. That armor did its job. Only three men aboard Louisiana were killed or mortally wounded, all of them in exposed positions, and one of them was her captain, Commander McIntosh.

## Fire, Then Thunder

With Farragut's ships upriver and out of range, Louisiana's fate was bound to the forts. On the night of April 27-28, the enlisted men in Fort Jackson mutinied and forced the surrender of both forts to Porter. No one had consulted the naval officers during the negotiations, so they did not consider themselves bound by the truce, and they resolved that their ship would not fall into Union hands. While terms were still being discussed, they set her afire and went ashore. The flames burned through the lines holding her to the bank, and she drifted downstream. Nearly abreast of Fort St. Philip, the fire reached her magazine, and the blast killed a soldier in the fort.

## A Wreck Beneath the Current

Afterward, Commander John K. Mitchell, Whittle's second in command, listed her flaws, perhaps to answer charges that navy inaction had let Farragut through. The rear paddlewheel churned uselessly in the wash of the forward one, and that wash made an eddy at the rudders that made steering impossible. Her gun ports were too small to elevate or traverse the guns, limiting them to 40 degrees of arc, and her gun deck was uninhabitable in summer, especially with the boilers in use. The wreck still lies on the bottom of the Mississippi, where NUMA located it magnetically in November 1981.

## Flight Context

The coordinates, 29.363 N, 89.461 W, place the site on the Mississippi River in lower Plaquemines Parish, Louisiana, close to Fort St. Philip on the east bank and across from the star-shaped Fort Jackson on the west bank near Triumph and Buras. From 1,500-2,500 feet the river's great bends, the two forts and the narrow ribbon of levee-protected land between marsh and open water make clear navigation references; nothing of the wreck is visible. The nearest public airports are distant: South Lafourche Leonard Miller Jr. Airport (KGAO) at Galliano is about 42 nm west, New Orleans Lakefront (KNEW) about 50 nm northwest, and Louis Armstrong New Orleans International (KMSY) about 56 nm northwest. Expect Gulf humidity, low coastal haze and summer thunderstorms.
