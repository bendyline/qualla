# Rene Beauregard House

The white-columned house on the Chalmette Battlefield looks like a witness to the Battle of New Orleans, but it was built nearly two decades later as a country retreat and passed through a colorful chain of owners before the National Park Service took it on.

**Location:** 29.9406, -89.9942

**Categories:** Buildings and structures in St. Bernard Parish, Louisiana, Greek Revival architecture in Louisiana, French colonial architecture in the United States, Houses completed in 1832, Chalmette National Battlefield

## Introduction

It looks like it ought to have seen the battle. Eight tall columns line each long side of the Malus-Beauregard House, and its hipped slate roof overlooks the field at Chalmette where the Battle of New Orleans was fought in 1815. But the house arrived later, built in 1832 and 1833 as a country home, and it has no connection to the fighting at all. Its real story is a quieter one about a string of wealthy owners, a sensible design for the south Louisiana climate, and the long struggle to keep an old house standing on sinking ground beside the Mississippi.

## A House for a Widow

In 1817, two years after the death of Lino de Chalmet, his 22-arpent property was sold to Hilaire and Louis St. Amand, wealthy brothers who were free men of color. They returned the land to sugarcane, building production facilities and quarters for the people they enslaved, and kept it intact until 1832, when debts forced them to subdivide it. The upper six arpents went to Alexandre Baron, as Charles F. Zimpel's map of 1834 shows, and nothing indicates a house stood there when he bought it. Around 1833 Baron built a French Creole-style residence for his mother-in-law, Madeleine Pannetier, the widow of Guillaume Malus. After his own home in the French Quarter was seized, Baron and his wife moved in with her, a practical arrangement since he was often away as a mariner and merchant sailing between New Orleans and Mexico. Widow Malus lived there until her death on September 16, 1835.

## Bueno Retiro

The house passed through a succession of owners who used it as a country retreat. In 1856 Caroline Fabre, widow of Michel Bernard Cantrelle, bought it and remodeled it in the Greek Revival style, adding the colonnades of eight columns across each long side and Greek Revival mantels and trim throughout. A later owner, the Spaniard José Antonio Fernández y Lineros, named the property "Bueno Retiro," added a two-story brick wing, stuccoed the exterior, and in 1874 advertised it as a "mansion containing all the modern improvements." In 1880 it passed to René Toutant Beauregard, eldest son of Confederate General P. G. T. Beauregard, whose name the house long carried. Early sale documents boasted of "a splendid orchard, containing every variety of rare fruit trees and vegetables, a beautiful flower garden, containing the choicest plants to be found." In 1904 Beauregard sold the land to the New Orleans Terminal Company.

## Shade, Galleries and a Hidden Arch

The design is a rather late version of the Louisiana plantation house, and its plan is extremely simple. The main body is only one room deep, with four rooms to a floor, each opening onto galleries and connected by doorways rather than an interior hall, a plan well suited to south Louisiana's climate. A broad hipped roof shades the house and its two-story galleries, creating covered outdoor living space. The original brick walls were painted red and "pencilled," with a white stripe painted along each mortar joint, a common practice in the 1830s; the 1850s remodeling brought full-height columns. One observer described the main body as a sort of apricot color, with white columns and green blinds. The cleverest detail hides in the attic, where two chimneys are joined by an arch and rise through the roof as one, just below the ridge.

## Rot, Rescue and Rising Water

By 1937 the house was in poor shape. The north wing had collapsed and the entire first floor had rotted out, though the walls, roof and columns of the main house were still sound. The National Park Service acquired it in 1949 and restored it in the late 1950s, and the house became part of the Chalmette National Battlefield, later serving as its visitor center; one account also reports a restoration costing $247,000, mostly donated by local St. Bernard Parish families, in 1965. Then came Hurricane Katrina in 2005. Overtopping and breaches along the Mississippi River-Gulf Outlet and the Industrial Canal flooded nearly all of St. Bernard Parish, most of it under 5 to 15 feet of water, though the nearby Mississippi River levee held. The flood ruined the park's visitor center and substantially damaged the house, the Chalmette Monument and the national cemetery. The house has been closed since because of its condition, and the land beneath it keeps sinking; in spring 2026 the park stabilized its river-side facade and is planning further repairs.

## Flight Context

The house stands at 29.941 N, 89.994 W on the Chalmette Battlefield in St. Bernard Parish, about 5 miles east of downtown New Orleans, near the Mississippi River levee. From 1,500-2,500 feet, look for the open battlefield between St. Bernard Highway and the river, with the tall Chalmette Monument and the Chalmette National Cemetery nearby and the house near the levee end of the field. New Orleans Lakefront Airport (KNEW) is about 6 nm north-northwest, Louis Armstrong New Orleans International (KMSY) about 14 nm west-northwest, and Naval Air Station Joint Reserve Base New Orleans (KNBG), a military field, about 7 nm south-southwest across the river. Respect New Orleans Class B airspace. Summer thunderstorms, humid haze and hurricane season (June to November) shape flying conditions here.
