# Fazendeville, Louisiana

Founded by freed people after the Civil War, the Black village of Fazendeville thrived for nearly a century beside the Chalmette battlefield until it was expropriated and razed to expand the park for a battle anniversary.

**Location:** 29.9414, -89.9914

**Categories:** Ghost towns in St. Bernard Parish, Louisiana, Forcibly depopulated communities in the United States, African-American history of Louisiana, Chalmette National Battlefield, Populated places disestablished in 1964

## Introduction

Walk the Chalmette Battlefield today, where the Battle of New Orleans is commemorated, and you might notice a slight depression in the grass. According to the National Park Service, it is the only visible trace of the street that once ran through Fazendeville. For nearly a hundred years, that street was the spine of a close-knit Black village its residents simply called "The Village," with churches, stores, a school, brass bands and baseball behind the houses. In the 1960s, the federal government razed it to enlarge the battlefield park.

## Thirty-Three Lots on One Street

In 1854 the land belonged to the estate of Jean Pierre Fazende, a "free man of color," and passed to his son of the same name. At the end of the Civil War, the younger Fazende divided the farmland into lots and sold them to people who had just been freed from slavery, and by 1867 a Black community had taken root. The Battle Ground Baptist Church was founded on April 16, 1868. According to historian Ron Chapman, a one-street community of 33 lots grew up along Fazendeville Road, which ran from St. Bernard Highway to the old River Road at the foot of the Mississippi levee. The houses, several of them narrow shotgun homes with rooms lined up front to back, stood on the east side of the road. Behind them lay a pasture that served as a baseball field, and a large pecan grove grew nearby.

## A Village That Looked After Itself

Fazendeville built its own institutions. In 1881 residents formed the Progressive Mutual Aid and Benevolent Association to pay for medical care, relief and burials. On New Year's Day 1890 they marked Lincoln's Emancipation Proclamation with speeches and a concert by the Chalmette Brass Band, part of a lively band scene that area newspapers covered often. Villagers raised money for sidewalks, planned a school in the basement of their Progressive Hall in 1902, and threw a festival for their school with baseball, a greasy pig contest, dancing and foot races. In 1919 the church hosted a Grand Celebration to welcome home the men who had served in World War I. Hardship tested them too. On January 9, 1927, a fire destroyed the church, eight houses and seven automobiles, leaving about sixty people homeless with no insurance. The congregation laid the cornerstone of a new church the following year.

## Pressure From Every Side

Outsiders kept eyeing the land. In 1903 the St. Louis-San Francisco Railway tried to buy the riverfront and proposed moving residents to the much smaller settlement of Versailles, but many refused to sell. By 1931, options had been taken on village properties as a first step toward a national park at the battlefield, and in 1939 Congress passed a bill authorizing the government to acquire Fazendeville, which President Franklin D. Roosevelt vetoed. That July, the local St. Bernard Voice wrote that the villagers "must bear segregation in mind" when choosing any new site, since any new site would need the approval of adjoining property holders. Another fire in 1949 left six families homeless. In 1950 the parish closed its schools for Black children except one, busing Fazendeville's students to a consolidated school in Violet. Still, the village endured, and in 1953 newspapers across the country reported on a mass baptism its church held in the Mississippi.

## Razed for an Anniversary

The end came through a celebration. In 1961 Congressman F. Edward Hébert introduced a bill to create a commission for the 150th anniversary of the Battle of New Orleans and to add about seven acres along Fazendeville Lane to the park, land owned, as newspapers put it, "principally" by Black families. President John F. Kennedy signed it in 1962. In 1964 the federal government took the land by eminent domain, forcing dozens of families to leave. Critics charged then, and still do, that the purpose was as much to remove one of the parish's largest concentrations of Black residents as to expand the park. Radio producer Eve Abrams reported that residents received about $4,000 per home when a new house in St. Bernard cost around $19,000; other accounts put the payment near $6,000 against a $16,000 new home. Fazendeville Road closed on November 25, 1964, and demolition was complete by 1966. Many families rebuilt in the Lower Ninth Ward, founding a new Battleground Baptist Church, only to see that area severely damaged by Hurricane Betsy in 1965 and even more so by Katrina in 2005.

## Irises in the Grass

Memory has been slowly returning. Anthropologist Joyce Marie Jackson recorded oral histories with former residents and their descendants for a National Park Service study, completed in 2003 and titled Life in the Village, and later titled a journal article "Declaration of Taking Twice." In the spring of 2020 or 2021 (accounts differ), rangers and iris conservationists noticed Louisiana irises and African crinum lilies blooming on the battlefield and concluded that people must have planted them, probably residents while the village still thrived; one volunteer suggested the lilies may have descended from plants brought by enslaved people. Loyola University researchers built a 3D model of Fazendeville from old photographs and maps. On April 19, 2021, former residents and descendants gathered to dedicate a state historical marker at the battlefield entrance, and in 2022 and 2023 a public television documentary, Battlegrounds: The Lost Community of Fazendeville, and a photography exhibit at New Orleans' Ogden Museum of Southern Art carried the village's story to new audiences.

## Flight Context

The village site lies at 29.941 N, 89.991 W on the Chalmette Battlefield in St. Bernard Parish, on the east bank of the Mississippi just downriver from New Orleans. From 1,500-2,500 feet look for the open battlefield lawn stretching from St. Bernard Highway to the river levee, with the tall Chalmette Monument obelisk and the Chalmette National Cemetery next to it; the Lower Ninth Ward, where many villagers resettled, lies just upriver. New Orleans Lakefront Airport (KNEW) is about 6 nm north-northwest and Louis Armstrong New Orleans International (KMSY) about 14 nm west-northwest; Naval Air Station Joint Reserve Base New Orleans (KNBG) is about 7 nm south-southwest across the river but is military. Stay clear of New Orleans Class B airspace unless cleared. Expect summer thunderstorms, humid haze and the June-November hurricane season.
