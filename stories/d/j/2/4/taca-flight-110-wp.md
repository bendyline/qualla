# TACA Flight 110

In 1988 a brand-new Boeing 737 lost both engines in a thunderstorm over New Orleans, and its crew glided it onto a narrow grass levee at NASA's Michoud Assembly Facility with everyone aboard surviving.

**Location:** 30.0119, -89.9196

**Categories:** Airliner accidents and incidents in Louisiana, Airliner accidents and incidents caused by weather, Aviation accidents and incidents in the United States in 1988, Accidents and incidents involving the Boeing 737 Classic, 1980s in New Orleans

## Introduction

At 16,500 feet over southeastern Louisiana, both engines of a brand-new Boeing 737-300 went quiet. It was May 24, 1988, and TACA Flight 110, inbound from San Salvador by way of Belize City with 45 people aboard, had just flown into the heart of a thunderstorm on its descent toward New Orleans International Airport. Rain and hail had drowned both engines. What happened over the next few minutes ended not in the marsh or a canal but on a strip of grass beside NASA's Michoud Assembly Facility, with no one hurt beyond a few minor injuries.

## Three Pilots in the Cockpit

The crew was highly experienced. Captain Carlos Dárdano was only 29 but had already logged 13,410 flight hours, almost 11,000 of them as pilot in command. Earlier in his career he had lost an eye to crossfire on a short flight to El Salvador, where civil war was raging at the time. First Officer Dionisio Lopez, 48, had more than 12,000 hours. And riding along was an instructor pilot, Captain Arturo Soley, there to monitor how the new 737 performed. For most of the trip there was little to watch. The jet took off from Belize City's Philip S. W. Goldson International Airport and crossed the Gulf of Mexico toward the Louisiana coast without incident, beginning its descent from 35,000 feet as it neared its destination.

## Threading the Storm

Ahead, the weather radar painted green and yellow areas of light to moderate rain, with isolated red cells of heavy precipitation on either side of the planned path. The pilots aimed for the gap between two of them. Entering cloud at about 30,000 feet, they switched on continuous ignition and engine anti-ice as precautions. It wasn't enough. Despite the route between the red cells, the 737 flew into an intense thunderstorm of torrential rain, hail and turbulence, and with the thrust levers at flight idle for the approach, both CFM56 engines flamed out. The jet lost all generated electrical power. The crew started the auxiliary power unit passing 10,500 feet, restoring electricity and hydraulics. Windmill restarts failed; a standard restart with the starters relit both engines, but neither would climb above idle, and advancing the throttles only overheated them. To avoid an engine fire, the pilots shut both down. Lopez radioed a Mayday, and New Orleans controllers offered vectors to the closer airport at Lakefront, but it was too far.

## A Levee Instead of a Canal

Breaking out beneath the lower clouds, the pilots scanned swampy wetland with no runway in reach. They chose to ditch in the Gulf Intracoastal Waterway with flaps and gear retracted. Dárdano lined up on the canal in an industrial area east of the airport and stretched the glide as far as he dared without stalling, while Lopez worked through the ditching checklist and configured the jet for a water landing. Then Lopez spotted something better: a grass levee to the right of the canal. Dárdano agreed. Sideslipping to bleed off speed while losing just enough height to reach the narrow strip, he set the 737 down on the levee on the grounds of the Michoud Assembly Facility, near where the Intracoastal Waterway meets the Mississippi River Gulf Outlet. All 45 aboard survived, and the airplane itself came through intact, with only minor hail damage.

## Takeoff From Saturn Boulevard

The rescue of the airplane was nearly as remarkable as the landing. After an engine replacement right there on site, the 737 took off from Saturn Boulevard, a road at Michoud that had once been an aircraft runway, and flew to Moisant Field for further work. It went back into service and later flew for Southwest Airlines as N697SW until December 2, 2016, when it was retired to storage at Pinal Airpark. The investigation left a lasting mark on jet engines. The NTSB found that water ingestion had caused both flameouts during the low-power descent, even though the engines met FAA water-ingestion standards. CFM56 engines were redesigned in response: the spinner (nose cone) was reshaped to guide hail outward, the splitter was cut back so rain and hail are flung into the bypass flow, and more variable bleed valve doors were added to extract ingested water at low power.

## Flight Context

The landing site lies at about 30.0119°N, 89.9196°W on the grounds of NASA's Michoud Assembly Facility in eastern New Orleans, beside the Gulf Intracoastal Waterway near its junction with the Mississippi River Gulf Outlet. From 1,500-2,500 feet AGL, look for the vast Michoud industrial complex with the waterway and its levees along its edge, and the flat wetlands stretching east. Nearby airports: Lakefront Airport (KNEW), about 6 nm west-northwest, the field controllers offered the crippled jet; New Orleans NAS JRB/Alvin Callender Field (KNBG, military), about 13 nm south-southwest; and Louis Armstrong New Orleans International (KMSY), the flight's destination, about 18 nm west. Thunderstorms with torrential rain and hail, the conditions that flamed out Flight 110's engines, can build quickly over this coast, so give convective cells a wide berth.
