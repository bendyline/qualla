# IHNC Lake Borgne Surge Barrier

Designed and built at the same time on an urgent deadline, this wall of steel, concrete and floodgates east of New Orleans first faced a storm on the seventh anniversary of Hurricane Katrina.

**Location:** 30.0056, -89.9014

**Categories:** Flood barriers, Flood control in the New Orleans metropolitan area, Intracoastal Waterway, Dams in Louisiana, Engineering

## Introduction

Seven years to the day after Hurricane Katrina, on August 29, 2012, another storm was pounding New Orleans, and something new stood in its way. Hurricane Isaac was the first occasion on which the Inner Harbor Navigation Canal Lake Borgne Surge Barrier was put to use, with the Corps closing its gates the morning before. The barrier crosses the Gulf Intracoastal Waterway and the Mississippi River Gulf Outlet near where the two channels meet, east of the city, and it has one job: to stop storm surge rolling in from Lake Borgne and the Gulf of Mexico before it can reach the neighborhoods behind it.

## A Line Drawn in the Water

Congress authorized the barrier in 2006, the year after Katrina. It was designed to reduce the risk of storm damage to some of the region's most vulnerable areas: New Orleans East, metro New Orleans, the 9th Ward and St. Bernard Parish. The wall runs generally north to south, from a point just east of the Michoud Canal on the north bank of the Intracoastal Waterway to just south of the existing Bayou Bienvenue flood control structure. It does not work alone. Another navigation gate, the Seabrook Floodgate, was built where the Inner Harbor Navigation Canal meets Lake Pontchartrain, so that a surge cannot enter the canal from the lake side either.

## Designing While Building

Time was the enemy. In April 2008, the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers awarded the construction contract to Shaw Environmental & Infrastructure, making the barrier the largest design-build civil works project in Corps history. Designing and constructing a civil works project simultaneously is highly unusual, but the compressed schedule demanded it: the goal was a 100-year level of risk reduction by 2011. The Corps' New Orleans District Commander signed off on the environmental review of alternative alignments and designs in October 2008, and a Notice to Proceed followed. Test pile driving began with a groundbreaking ceremony that December. A Traylor-Massman-Weeks joint venture, working as Shaw's subcontractor, started the flood wall on May 9, 2009, and on October 21, 2009, the last of the barrier's 1,271 main piles went into the ground.

## Steel at an Angle

Much of the barrier is hidden below the waterline. Behind its concrete plumb piles, steel piles are driven at a slant, at an angle of two horizontal to three vertical. Each steel pile is 288 feet long and goes in as two pieces: a lower 158-foot section is driven first, then an upper 130-foot section is fitted on top and welded in place. A precast concrete pile cap then joins the steel batter piles and concrete plumb piles into one structure. The flood wall itself includes the closure across the Mississippi River Gulf Outlet, more than a mile long, with concrete T-walls anchoring the north and south ends on land. COWI Marine North America, formerly Ben C. Gerwick, Inc., designed the main barrier, the sector gate's foundation and the bypass swing gate, while INCA, now Tetra Tech, designed the sector gate itself and the Bayou Bienvenue structures.

## Gates for Barges and Shrimp Boats

A wall across working waterways needs doors. On the Intracoastal Waterway, a 150-foot-wide steel sector gate, 42 feet tall, closes with two gate leaves, and beside it a 150-foot-wide bypass gate closes with a concrete barge swing gate, comparable to a caisson. In the Bayou Bienvenue channel, a 56-foot-wide vertical lift gate serves recreational craft and shrimpers. The builders also looked to the surrounding wetlands: organic material dredged from the waterways during construction was deposited in nearby habitats to enhance the marsh. By June 2013 all major construction was complete. Isaac had already tested the barrier the summer before, and the same gates that let barges and fishing boats pass can be worked to hold back the sea.

## Flight Context

The barrier lies at approximately 30.01 N, 89.90 W in the marshes on the eastern edge of New Orleans, where the Gulf Intracoastal Waterway and the Mississippi River Gulf Outlet converge. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL it appears as a long concrete line running north to south across open water and marsh, with the large sector gate on the Intracoastal Waterway and the smaller Bayou Bienvenue lift gate standing out; Lake Borgne opens to the east. Lakefront Airport (KNEW) is about 7 nm west-northwest, New Orleans NAS JRB/Alvin Callender Field (KNBG), a military field, about 13 nm south-southwest, and Louis Armstrong New Orleans International (KMSY) about 19 nm west under the New Orleans Class B airspace. Expect summer thunderstorms and haze; avoid the area when tropical systems threaten.
