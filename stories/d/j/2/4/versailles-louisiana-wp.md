# Versailles, Louisiana

A planter's dream of a Versailles on the Mississippi that would outshine New Orleans became a small St. Bernard Parish community, remembered for a battlefield, a ruined mansion and an avenue of live oaks.

**Location:** 29.9483, -89.9608

**Categories:** Unincorporated communities in St. Bernard Parish, Louisiana, Unincorporated communities in the New Orleans metropolitan area, Battle of New Orleans, Plantations in Louisiana, War of 1812

## Introduction

Pierre Denis de La Ronde did not plan small. In 1805 he and fellow investors made plans to build a town on the Mississippi below New Orleans, named it Versailles after the French royal city, and proposed cutting a barge canal through about a dozen miles of swamp to Lake Pontchartrain, where a second town, Paris, would rise. He imagined his Versailles overtaking New Orleans. It never did. Today the community, about 3.5 miles below the New Orleans city limit in St. Bernard Parish, is counted as part of Chalmette for postal purposes, though locals still use the old name.

## A Councilman for Life

De La Ronde, born in 1762, became one of Louisiana's wealthiest plantation owners. By local tradition he bought the riverfront property in 1783 and, to mark his 21st birthday, had enslaved people plant an avenue of oak trees leading from the river landing toward the house, though a state historical marker says he acquired the land in 1799 and believes the oaks date from after the 1815 battle. Either way, their labor outlasted nearly everything else he built. In 1802, while Louisiana was under Spanish rule, he joined the Cabildo, the colony's governing council, succeeding his late brother-in-law Andrés Almonaster y Rojas as Regidor Perpetuo, or councilman for life. Such Spanish offices were a form of property, purchased from the Crown and passed down like land. The family web ran wide. His niece, Micaela Almonester, Baroness de Pontalba, would later build the Pontalba Buildings in New Orleans in the 1840s and complete the Hôtel de Pontalba in Paris in 1855.

## Paris Road

Political unrest, culminating in the War of 1812, stalled the grand plan. Versailles stayed a small town through the rest of the 19th century, and no navigable canal would link river and lake until the Industrial Canal was built in New Orleans in the 20th century. De La Ronde's path through the swamp fared better than his canal. It grew into a major artery, and Paris Road, still carrying the name of the lakeside town that was never built, remains the farthest-downriver route connecting the Mississippi to the lake in Greater New Orleans. The community itself stayed modest. In 1903, when the St. Louis-San Francisco Railway sought land in St. Bernard Parish for an expansion, a newspaper described Versailles as merely a row of very small properties along a road running from the river to the railroad track. The railroad proposed moving residents of nearby Fazendeville, a historically Black community, there. Some agreed, but many refused the railway's offers.

## Night Battle Among the Oaks

On December 23, 1814, General Andrew Jackson learned of the British advance and the position of their encampment from Colonel de La Ronde and his son-in-law, Major Gabriel Villeré, son of Jacques Villeré, the future first Creole governor of Louisiana. That night the fighting began on the La Ronde plantation itself. The Night Attack effectively launched the Battle of New Orleans, which was fought both there and on the neighboring Chalmette plantation belonging to de La Ronde's half-brother, Ignace Martin de Lino de Chalmette. De La Ronde commanded the Louisiana militia's Third Regiment. His mansion paid the price: the British looted and heavily damaged it, then took it over as a field hospital. Tradition holds that General Edward Pakenham, mortally wounded in the January 8 assault, died among de La Ronde's oaks, and a historical marker says his body was brought to the house; some still call the avenue Pakenham's Oaks, though the name is often called a mislabel.

## Parnassus in Ruins

The mansion was considered the most stylish plantation home in that part of Louisiana, and many writers have called it "Versailles." There is no evidence it was ever known by that name in its time. The family called it Parnassus, a home "where lavish hospitality was dispensed." The house burned about 70 years after the battle, though most of its walls stood until the hurricane of 1915. A few ruins remain visible along Highway 46 in St. Bernard Parish. So does the allée of Southern live oaks, largely intact and properly known as the La Ronde Oaks, though often mislabeled Versailles Oaks. More than two centuries after enslaved workers planted them, they still line the path from the river landing to a house that is no longer there.

## Flight Context

Versailles lies at about 29.9483°N, 89.9608°W on the east bank of the Mississippi River in St. Bernard Parish, about 3.5 miles below the New Orleans city limit, between Chalmette and Meraux. From 1,500-2,500 feet AGL, follow the river downstream from New Orleans and look for the line of live oaks running inland from the riverbank along Highway 46, near the Chalmette battlefield; Paris Road runs north from the area toward Lake Pontchartrain. Nearby airports: Lakefront Airport (KNEW), about 7 nm north-northwest; New Orleans NAS JRB/Alvin Callender Field (KNBG, military), about 8 nm south-southwest; and Louis Armstrong New Orleans International (KMSY), about 16 nm west. Expect humid Gulf Coast conditions with fast-building summer thunderstorms, and busy New Orleans airspace.
