# Battle of Lake Borgne

On December 14, 1814, nearly a thousand British sailors and marines rowed for about a day and a half to overwhelm five American gunboats on Lake Borgne, clearing the way for the invasion of New Orleans.

**Location:** 30.1644, -89.4372

**Categories:** Naval battles of the War of 1812, Battles in Louisiana, Battles of the War of 1812 in the Gulf theater, 1814 in Louisiana, History of Louisiana

## Introduction

For about thirty-six hours, the British pulled at their oars. Some 980 sailors and Royal Marines, packed into 42 armed rowboats and three unarmed gigs, made their way toward Lake Borgne in December 1814, hunting a handful of American gunboats that stood between a British army and New Orleans. When they finally closed in on the morning of December 14, they were rowing against a strong current into round shot and grapeshot. The fight that followed lasted about two hours. Its outcome let the British land their troops unhindered nine days later.

## A Warning From Pensacola

In August 1814, Vice Admiral Alexander Cochrane convinced the Admiralty that a campaign against New Orleans would weaken American resolve against Canada and hasten the end of the War of 1812. By winter, an expeditionary force of about 8,000 troops under General Edward Pakenham had reached the Gulf Coast, aiming to seize the entrance to the Mississippi and challenge the legality of the Louisiana Purchase. The Americans got a warning. An anonymous letter from Pensacola, dated December 5 and addressed to Commodore Daniel Patterson, reached him on December 7. Patterson sent Lieutenant Thomas ap Catesby Jones with a small flotilla to watch the British ships near Cat Island. Jones had five Jeffersonian gunboats, the schooner USS Sea Horse and the sloop USS Alligator as a tender. His flagship, Gunboat No. 156, carried one long 24-pounder, four 12-pounder carronades and four swivel guns.

## Caught by the Current

Jones moored at Bay St. Louis on December 10. Two days later his squadron reached Cat Island, found the British far too strong, and turned back toward the Rigolets, where Patterson wanted them to fall back and make a stand. The water would not cooperate. Against a strong current, the gunboats made it only as far as the channel between the mainland and Malheureux Island by December 13. That afternoon the Sea Horse was sent to remove or destroy a stores dump at Bay St. Louis; facing superior British forces, she was scuttled, and an explosion at 7:30 that evening marked her end. Meanwhile Cochrane had put every rowboat in the British fleet under Commander Nicholas Lockyer of HMS Sophie, and on the night of December 12 they had set out after Jones. The Americans, somewhere between 182 and 225 men depending on whose count is trusted, formed a line across the channel and waited.

## Boarding Gunboat No. 156

The British caught the Alligator first, capturing her at 9:30 a.m. on December 14. The main action began at 10:39. Two of the five gunboats, Jones's No. 156 and No. 163, had drifted out of line. At 11:50, Lockyer personally boarded No. 156, and both he and Jones were severely wounded. The hand-to-hand fighting was brief, and all five gunboats were taken. The British lost 17 killed and 77 wounded; Jones claimed far heavier losses, more than 300 casualties and at least two boats sunk. On the American side, 41 men were killed or wounded, and the surviving crews were taken prisoner. The Royal Navy put all five gunboats into its own service under new names, among them Firebrand, Destruction and Harlequin, and Lockyer was promoted to post captain in March 1815.

## Panic and Misdirection

The loss opened Lake Borgne as the British landing zone for the advance on New Orleans. When the city learned of the defeat, panic gripped some inhabitants, and Andrew Jackson, after a public address on December 15 meant to calm the city, declared martial law on December 16. Without the gunboats, Jackson had no way to watch the British, and he did not send scouts to make up for it. Yet the defeat carried one unintended benefit. Questioned in captivity, the gunboat crews managed to mislead the British about how many men Jackson had. At the end of January 1815 the prisoners were shipped to the Caribbean aboard HMS Ramillies, and after news of the peace treaty's ratification arrived in February, a ship was sent to Jamaica to bring them home.

## A Boat Action Remembered

The fighting left its mark on the coastline. Although Jones never reached the fort at Petit Coquilles, work began in 1819 on Fort Pike to replace it, the first of the Louisiana forts built under the postwar Third System. Nineteenth-century writers did not call the clash a battle; an 1840s painting by Hornbrook used the word "action," and only 20th-century sources settled on the Battle of Lake Borgne. The British remembered it in their own way. When the Admiralty created the Naval General Service Medal in 1847, it issued a clasp marked "14 Dec. Boat Service 1814." It became the largest boat action for which the medal was granted, and 205 surviving veterans claimed the clasp.

## Flight Context

The engagement site lies at about 30.1644°N, 89.4372°W in the waters near Malheureux Island, south of the modern settlement of Ansley, Mississippi. From 1,500-2,500 feet AGL, look for low marsh islands and open water, with the Bay St. Louis shoreline to the north. Nearby airports: Stennis International Airport (KHSA) at Bay St. Louis, about 12 nm north; Slidell Airport (KASD), about 23 nm west-northwest; and Gulfport-Biloxi International (KGPT), about 24 nm northeast. Low, featureless marsh and water make visual navigation tricky in haze, and Gulf thunderstorms build quickly in summer.
