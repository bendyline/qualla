# Caernarvon, Louisiana

In 1927 New Orleans bankers had the levee at this St. Bernard Parish community dynamited to protect their city, flooding its downriver neighbors unnecessarily; six decades later the same spot became a gate that lets the Mississippi rebuild the marsh.

**Location:** 29.8639, -89.9064

**Categories:** Unincorporated communities in St. Bernard Parish, Louisiana, Louisiana populated places on the Mississippi River, Great Mississippi Flood of 1927, Coastal restoration, Unincorporated communities in the New Orleans metropolitan area

## Introduction

The levee at Caernarvon was destroyed to save a city that, as it turned out, did not need saving. In the spring of 1927, with the Mississippi in flood, a group of influential New Orleans bankers met to discuss how to guarantee their city's safety. Their answer landed on this stretch of riverbank in St. Bernard Parish, a historic unincorporated community now often considered part of Poydras. The people downriver paid for that decision. Six decades later, engineers came back to the same spot with a gentler idea: letting the river through on purpose.

## A Welsh Name in St. Bernard Parish

Caernarvon takes its name from a plantation that once stood here, and the plantation's name is widely believed to come from the similarly named town and castle in Wales. That was a familiar habit along the Mississippi. Names of antebellum plantations in the American South often reflected European roots and aspirations of grandeur, as upriver examples such as Nottoway, near White Castle, Louisiana, and Sans Souci, near Osceola, Arkansas, show. Today its residents are zoned to St. Bernard Parish Public Schools, like their neighbors across the parish. The community's name, though, is widely known for what happened here in 1927.

## Tons of Dynamite

Blasting at the Caernarvon levee began on April 29, 1927, and accounts put the dynamite used at 30 to 39 tons. The breach released 250,000 cubic feet of water per second, and it was meant to spare New Orleans serious damage by sending the flood elsewhere. Elsewhere meant the parishes downriver. The water flooded much of the less densely populated St. Bernard Parish and all of Plaquemines Parish's east bank, causing major destruction. It was also unnecessary. Several major levee breaks well upstream of New Orleans, including one the day after the demolition, released huge amounts of floodwater and reduced what reached the city. Residents were promised full compensation, but many received little or nothing, and the New Orleans businessmen did not compensate the people of the downriver parishes for their losses.

## Spillways and Second Thoughts

The flood forced a rethinking of how to protect New Orleans. Farther downriver, the Bohemia Spillway, started in 1924 and completed in 1926 just before the flood, had been one of the earliest attempts to shield the city. The Bonnet Carré Spillway was built in direct response to the 1927 disaster. Since the beginning of the 1990s, a newer generation of diversion projects has followed, planned not only for flood control but for marshland rehabilitation. The same thinking reaches well beyond St. Bernard Parish. Bayou Lafourche, dammed off from the Mississippi at Donaldsonville around 1904, lost so much fresh water that salt water intruded and affected drinking water, and since a 1950 pump station engineers have pumped, studied and dredged to bring the river's water back into it.

## Letting the River Back In

Fittingly, one of them rose at the very site of the destroyed levee. The Caernarvon Freshwater Diversion, about 15 miles south of New Orleans, was completed in 1991 at a cost of about $26 million. Five 15-foot gates allow up to 8,000 cubic feet per second of river water to flow by gravity into a conveyance canal, a controlled trickle beside the torrent of 1927. The canal runs to the Big Mar pond and drains into Bayou Mandeville, Lake Lery and the Delacroix Canal, which flows on into Oak River, and the flow has created a new landform, the Caernarvon Delta. Its success led to other structures, including the $106 million Davis Pond Freshwater Diversion, begun in the late 1990s and completed in 2002, which carries sediments and nutrients into the Barataria Bay estuary to benefit hundreds of thousands of acres of marshes and bays.

## Flight Context

Caernarvon lies at approximately 29.86 N, 89.91 W on the east bank of the Mississippi River in St. Bernard Parish, downriver from New Orleans. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL, look for the diversion structure cut through the east-bank levee and the conveyance canal leading east into the marsh toward Big Mar and the fan of the Caernarvon Delta; the contrast between the river, the levee and the open wetlands is striking. New Orleans NAS JRB/Alvin Callender Field (KNBG), a military field, is about 7 nm west-southwest, so observe its airspace. Lakefront Airport (KNEW) is about 12.5 nm north-northwest and Louis Armstrong New Orleans International (KMSY) about 20 nm west-northwest under the New Orleans Class B. Expect summer thunderstorms, haze and humidity, and morning fog over the marsh in the cooler months.
