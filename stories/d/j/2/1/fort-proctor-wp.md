# Fort Proctor

Begun in 1856 to guard a railroad port and the water approaches to New Orleans, the brick tower known as Beauregard's Castle was never finished, never garrisoned, and now stands alone in the shallow waters of Lake Borgne.

**Location:** 29.8673, -89.6783

**Categories:** Buildings and structures in St. Bernard Parish, Louisiana, Forts on the National Register of Historic Places in Louisiana, National Register of Historic Places in St. Bernard Parish, Louisiana

## Introduction

Look out from Shell Beach across Lake Borgne and you can make out a brick ruin standing square in the water, with nothing around it but the lake. Locals call it Beauregard's Castle. It was meant to defend New Orleans, and it never fired a shot, never held a garrison, and was never even finished. Fort Proctor has been losing a slow siege to wind and water since before the Civil War.

## Lessons from 1814

The fort's reason for being lay in a painful memory. In 1814, a British fleet advanced up Lake Borgne and defeated a small flotilla of U.S. Navy gunboats in the Battle of Lake Borgne, opening the way for the British Army to attack New Orleans. The lake was a back door to the city, and in the 1850s the government set out to close it. Fort Proctor was planned as part of the fortifications protecting the water routes toward New Orleans, on the shore of Lake Borgne just north of the mouth of Bayou Yscloskey. It also guarded something newer: an adjacent railroad port called Proctorville, the terminus of the Mexican Gulf Railroad.

## Beauregard's Castle

Officially the structure was known as the Tower at Proctor's Landing, but it took its popular names, Fort Beauregard and Beauregard's Castle, from P.G.T. Beauregard, who supervised its construction within the fortification program of Army Chief Engineer Joseph G. Totten. The design called for a square, castle-like, three-story brick tower inside a square double moat, with a water battery outside. Eight guns were to be mounted on top, while the lower floors were pierced only with loopholes for muskets. The fort was unusual for two innovations: comfortable living quarters, including bathrooms, and the extensive use of structural iron. Construction began in March 1856 but stopped in September 1858 for lack of funds.

## A Fort That Never Was

Hurricane damage in 1859 or 1860 (accounts differ) ended the work for good, and the third story was never built. Then came the Civil War. Hurricane delays and the war together meant the fort was never garrisoned or armed, although Confederate troops posted at Proctorville in 1861, and Union troops after 1862, may have used the site as a lookout. By the end of the war, advances in artillery had made its design obsolete anyway. A caretaker looked after the fort until 1872. By 1915 the exterior battery had washed away entirely, and the Army disposed of the fort and its 100-acre reservation in 1919, selling it off in 1922. During World War II, in 1944, the Army placed an anti-aircraft battery in the vicinity.

## Surrounded by the Lake

In the 1940s and 1950s, before Lake Borgne engulfed it, the ruin was, by local accounts, a popular gathering place for teenagers looking for somewhere nobody would supervise them. That ended in the 1960s, when construction of the Mississippi River-Gulf Outlet Canal cut off all land access to the site. Fort Proctor was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1978. Today it is completely surrounded by water about a foot deep and can be reached only by boat. Before Hurricane Katrina there remained one small piece of dry land inside the walls; the 2005 storm also damaged the outer wall of the moat. The castle that was built to hold back invaders is quietly giving way to the lake instead.

## Flight Context

Fort Proctor stands at 29.8673°N, 89.6783°W in the shallow water along the shore of Lake Borgne, just north of the mouth of Bayou Yscloskey, and it can be seen in the distance from Shell Beach in St. Bernard Parish, Louisiana. From 1,000 to 2,000 feet AGL, look for the square brick ruin isolated in the water just off the shoreline. New Orleans Lakefront Airport (KNEW) lies about 21 nm west-northwest and Louis Armstrong New Orleans International Airport (KMSY) about 31 nm west-northwest. New Orleans NAS JRB (KNBG) is about 19 nm west, so check military airspace status. Clear days give the best contrast between the brick and the water.
