# Pass Christian, Mississippi

Named by French explorers for a channel full of oysters and built up as a resort for wealthy New Orleanians, Pass Christian has twice been nearly erased by the most intense hurricanes to strike the United States.

**Location:** 30.3244, -89.2472

**Categories:** Cities in Harrison County, Mississippi, Populated coastal places in Mississippi, Gulfport–Biloxi metropolitan area, Hurricane Katrina, Hurricane Camille

## Introduction

The bullets bounced off the glass. On August 29, 2005, thirteen members of the Pass Christian police department had retreated from their unsafe station to the town library, only to have a floating police car smash through its south doors and let Hurricane Katrina's surge pour in. Trapped in a concrete box filling with water, they tried to shoot out the north windows, but the laminated glass stopped their .45-caliber rounds. The only way out was with the current. Police Chief John Dubuisson worked his way through the water to the doors and forced them open. Everyone inside got out and rode out the rest of the storm on the roof.

## The Channel of Oysters

People lived along this shore for many thousands of years before Europeans arrived. A 1768 English map shows a large mound near today's Market Street, though many of the region's mounds have since been lost to artifact hunters, farmers, developers and floods, and near White Harbor Road, where a village once stood whose inhabitants locals called the "Pitcher Point Indians," the beach has yielded many arrowheads and pottery sherds. French-Canadian explorers arrived in 1699, soon after founding their first colony at Biloxi. Sounding the channel off the peninsula that June, they named it Passe aux Huîtres for its many oysters. The town's name came from a nearby deepwater pass, itself named for Nicholas Christian L'Adnier, who lived on Cat Island from 1746. By 1781 the whole peninsula belonged to Julia de la Brosse, the Widow Asmard, and when she died in 1799 she deeded 800 arpents, all of what is now downtown, to Charles Asmar, a free person of color.

## Mansions Along the Sound

Chartered as a town in 1848, Pass Christian became a famous resort before the Civil War, a favorite summer retreat for wealthy New Orleanians who built a row of mansions along the shore. The Southern Yacht Club, founded here in 1849, was the first yacht club in the South and the second in the United States, before moving to New Orleans in 1857. In the club's founding year, 1849, the Mississippi Sound off the town hosted what is described as the Gulf Coast's first regatta; accounts of the fleet range from twelve to twenty-two boats. The town drew a future president, too: Woodrow Wilson, then president-elect, spent part of the winter of 1912-13 at Beaulieu, a house built in 1854. In the 1920s, New Orleans residents Rudolph and Lynne Watkins Hecht built Middlegate Japanese Gardens at their summer home to preserve memories of their travels in Japan; Hurricane Camille closed the gardens to visitors in 1969, and Katrina devastated them in 2005. Scenic Drive, with its historic mansions, became one of the country's notable historic districts.

## Camille's Warning

The town sits on a peninsula with water on three sides: the Gulf to the south, the Bay of St. Louis to the west and a long bayou to the north. Pass Christian has stood in the path of two of the most intense hurricanes ever to strike the United States, and each nearly destroyed it. The first was Hurricane Camille. On August 17, 1969, with the storm about 200 miles southeast of New Orleans, a hurricane warning went up for the entire Mississippi Coast and evacuation was advised. At the Richelieu Apartments, some occupants chose to ride out the storm. Camille destroyed the building and killed eight people there. It also wrecked Beaulieu, the house where Wilson had wintered, which was demolished afterward. Navy Seabees came to help the town rebuild, the first time they would answer that call in Pass Christian.

## The Night the Gulf Came Ashore

The second time was worse. Katrina's storm surge at Pass Christian was measured at 27.8 feet, among the highest ever recorded in the United States, and it leveled the town up to half a mile inland. Of roughly 8,000 homes, all but 500 were damaged or destroyed. Even Scenic Drive, running along the crest of a small bluff, could not save the mansions; many were destroyed outright, including Union Quarters, a restored 1855 Greek Revival house where, according to its historical marker, Union officers had once been quartered. The bridge over the Bay of St. Louis was torn apart, and a ferry service filled the gap until a new bridge partly opened in May 2007. Seabees returned, clearing about three miles of railroad track for a temporary sewer line and building temporary housing for 1,000 people left homeless. As late as early 2007, many residents were still living in FEMA trailers.

## Still Named for Oysters

The town rebuilt, and its public library, destroyed by the storm, was among the buildings replaced. The 2020 census counted 5,686 residents in a city that is nearly half water by area. The water itself has struggled. The Deepwater Horizon oil spill in 2010, toxic sediments stirred up by Katrina and record freshwater releases through Louisiana's Bonnet Carré Spillway badly disrupted regional seafood production, and field crews reported oyster mortality of 50 to 65 percent in some offshore beds and as high as 90 to 95 percent in others. For a place first named for its oysters, that loss carries a particular weight. The novelist Jesmyn Ward, winner of the 2011 National Book Award for Fiction, grew up in nearby DeLisle.

## Flight Context

Pass Christian lies at 30.3244°N, 89.2472°W on a peninsula on the Mississippi Sound, with the Gulf to the south, the Bay of St. Louis to the west and a long bayou to the north. From 1,500-2,500 feet AGL, follow U.S. Route 90 and the beachfront west from Gulfport; look for the harbor with its concrete breakwater and the Bay of St. Louis bridge at the town's western edge. Nearby airports: Diamondhead Airport (FAA identifier 66Y, no ICAO code), about 8 nm west-northwest; Gulfport-Biloxi International (KGPT), about 10 nm east-northeast; and Stennis International Airport (KHSA) near Bay St. Louis, about 11 nm west-northwest. Expect humid Gulf Coast weather with summer thunderstorms and hurricane season from summer into fall.
