# St. Augustine Seminary (Bay St. Louis)

Driven from Greenville partly by Klan harassment, a Divine Word seminary on the Mississippi Gulf Coast became the first intended to train African Americans for the priesthood and formed many of the nation's first Black priests and bishops.

**Location:** 30.3172, -89.3311

**Categories:** African-American history of Mississippi, African-American Catholic schools, Defunct Catholic seminaries in the United States, Divine Word Missionaries Order, Bay St. Louis, Mississippi, National Register of Historic Places in Mississippi

## Introduction

The Ku Klux Klan helped decide where this seminary would stand. In 1923 the Divine Word Fathers moved their school for Black candidates to the priesthood from Greenville, Mississippi, to Bay St. Louis on the Gulf Coast, to be closer to a large city and to avoid harassment from the Klan. As St. Augustine Seminary, it was the first seminary intended to educate African Americans for the Catholic priesthood. In an era of Jim Crow and widespread opposition to the very idea of Black priests, it went on to educate and ordain many of the first Black priests in the United States.

## A Door Held Shut

The need was recognized long before anyone met it. Various American bishops saw the need for ministry to African Americans, especially after the Civil War, but racism ran deep, even among the bishops themselves. Before the late 19th century, the only Black priests ordained were the Healy brothers, all of whom passed for white during their ministries. When the Society of the Divine Word arrived in the Deep South in 1905, African-American men were barred from all but two seminaries in the United States, both run by the Josephites, and only a few had been ordained, almost all of them overseas. The Josephites would soon shut down Black vocations in their own society after early struggles involving their first Black members. Most U.S. seminaries stayed closed to Black applicants through the mid-20th century.

## The Divine Word Comes South

The Society of the Divine Word was a missionary congregation founded in Steyl, in the Netherlands, in 1875 by Father Arnold Janssen. Its American story began modestly in 1895, when Brother Wendelin Meyer arrived to sell the order's publications. Others followed, and by 1897 the society had established itself in Techny, Illinois. By 1905 it had reached the Deep South, working among African Americans. By 1920 the Divine Word Fathers had gathered the support and money to open a seminary of their own at their Sacred Heart Catholic Church in Greenville. Its roots were in Sacred Heart College, founded as a parochial school in 1913 before becoming a minor seminary. Father Matthew Christman started the seminary, and three years later it separated from the high school and made its move to the coast.

## Four Priests in 1934

Some of the first students at the seminary were among the few Black men in the United States with a path to the Catholic priesthood. Its first ordination class, on May 23, 1934, produced four Black priests: Anthony Bourges, Maurice Rousseve, Vincent Smith and Francis Wade. Over the following decades St. Augustine educated many of the nation's African-American priests, because most other seminaries remained closed to them. Its influence reached the highest ranks of the American church. Several of the first openly Black Catholic bishops in the country came from the Divine Word ranks and studied here, as did Joseph O. Bowers, who was consecrated bishop of Accra, Ghana, at Bay St. Louis in 1953. Harold R. Perry, a St. Augustine graduate, became the first African-American Catholic bishop of the 20th century when he was consecrated in 1966. Other alumni include Dominic Carmon, Leonard Olivier, Joseph Abel Francis, Raymond Caesar, J. Terry Steib and Curtis J. Guillory.

## Grottoes and a Quieter Mission

St. Augustine remained an all-Black seminary for a number of years before integrating in the mid-20th century. As other seminaries, including the Josephites' institutions, began accepting Black men, its unique role faded. It closed for higher studies in 1967, and its high school seminary closed in 1982; the site served as the society's novitiate until 1990. American Divine Word seminarians now study at Divine Word College in Epworth, Iowa, and the Bay St. Louis property serves as a retreat center for the society's southern province and as headquarters of In a Word, the province's periodical on African-American Catholic news. On the grounds stands a Marian grotto dedicated to Our Lady of Lourdes, open to the public by appointment. The Divine Word Chapel, an Agony Grotto and a Sacred Heart Shrine are contributing properties in the Old Bay St. Louis Historic District, listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 2010.

## Flight Context

St. Augustine Seminary lies at approximately 30.32 N, 89.33 W in Bay St. Louis, Mississippi, on the Gulf Coast beside the Bay of St. Louis. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL, the shoreline, the bay and the bridge across it are the obvious landmarks, with the old town of Bay St. Louis on the bay's western side. Stennis International Airport (KHSA) is about 7 nm west-northwest and Gulfport-Biloxi International (KGPT) about 14.5 nm east-northeast; Diamondhead Airport (FAA identifier 66Y; no ICAO code) is about 4 nm northwest. Keesler Air Force Base (KBIX) lies farther east at Biloxi, so check military airspace along the coast. Summer brings afternoon thunderstorms and humid haze; the coast is exposed to tropical weather from June through November.
