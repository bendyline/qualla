# Gulfside United Methodist Assembly

Founded in 1923 by one of the first two African American bishops of the Methodist Episcopal Church, Gulfside gave Black Southerners a rare beach, a school and a meeting ground on the Mississippi coast, until hurricanes and history nearly erased it.

**Location:** 30.2663, -89.3930

**Categories:** African-American history of Mississippi, Methodism in Mississippi, Buildings and structures in Hancock County, Mississippi, Campgrounds in the United States, Civil rights movement

## Introduction

Before Gulfside, there were no accommodations of any kind for people of color along the entire Gulf Coast. There was no beach where a Black family could swim. So in the 1920s, visitors to this Methodist retreat in Waveland, Mississippi, would step off the train and walk three miles through the woods, because the dirt road flooded into mud whenever it rained. At the end of the walk stood an old mansion hidden behind oak trees, and beyond it the water. For decades, Gulfside offered African Americans something segregation denied them almost everywhere else in the South: a place to rest, learn and gather.

## An Idea Born in Ohio

In the early 20th century, the Methodist Episcopal Church, South, was strictly segregated along racial lines, and most hotels, restaurants, beaches and public facilities were for whites only. Robert E. Jones, who in 1920 became one of the first two African American bishops of the Methodist Episcopal Church, was speaking at Lakeside Assembly, a white Methodist campground in Ohio, when he conceived of a similar place where African Americans could gather in a relaxed atmosphere. His district covered West Texas, Louisiana and Mississippi, and he chose Waveland, a resort town centrally located on the Gulf Coast and 55 miles from his office in New Orleans. With help from churches and individuals, Jones raised about $4,000, bought 300 acres and leased another 316 from the state. The Gulfside Chautauqua and camp meeting ground became a reality on April 16, 1923, and it was incorporated on January 24, 1924, by some of the most prominent Black leaders in Methodism.

## The House Behind the Oaks

Only one building stood on all 616 acres: the Jackson House, a grand old mansion that had once housed family members of President Andrew Jackson. Raised 8 to 10 feet off the ground and screened from the beach by large oaks, it had big rooms full of antique furniture and a wraparound porch wide enough for meetings. Bishop Jones and a crew of 14 preachers and local craft workers made it livable, enclosing the space under the porch for a kitchen, dining room and sweet shop, and building a screened pavilion with a dirt floor. From April through August, Gulfside filled with YMCA gatherings, summer schools for rural pastors and aspiring ministers, Boys' Camp, Girl Reserves, 4-H, Scouting and church picnics. The one year-round program was the Poor Boys' School, which helped students with little schooling develop salable skills; they worked from 9 until noon, then studied regular textbooks and crafts in class until 4. Several went on to enter the ministry.

## Pennies, Fire and Concrete

Jones's tenure spanned the Great Depression, and foreclosure threatened more than once. Gulfside always made its payments, collecting pennies, courting philanthropists and selling lots from the land it owned. It also endured the hostility of the era, including several cross burnings. One winter morning the Jackson House mysteriously caught fire (accounts date the fire anywhere from 1935 to the 1940s), and the 1947 Fort Lauderdale Hurricane finished it off along with other buildings. After Bishop William A. C. Hughes led from 1940 to 1944, Bishop Robert N. Brooks, known as "Mr. Methodist" for his command of doctrine, formed a board of trustees and urged supporters to give dollars instead of pennies. Over eight years, Brooks Chapel, the Gulfside Inn, Harry Hoosier Auditorium and a bishop's house rose in reinforced concrete block, made on site one block at a time by a single machine to better withstand fire and hurricanes. Gulfside also became a place where Black and white people could come together with much less questioning from local authorities than elsewhere in the South.

## Where the Movement Met

Gulfside's quiet grounds sheltered history. Constance Baker Motley made it her base of operations during the court sessions to integrate the University of Mississippi Law School, and Thurgood Marshall came to work with other NAACP lawyers. Leontyne Price sang there, because it was one of the few places in the South where she could perform opera for her own people. Civil rights activist Hollis Watkins recalled that "there were only three places where Blacks could meet in Mississippi during the movement—Tougaloo College, Rust College, and Gulfside." Integration, ironically, weakened it. When The United Methodist Church formed in 1968, the all-Black Central Jurisdiction was dissolved and Black members joined the formerly white conferences, and Gulfside was no longer the essential meeting place it had been.

## Camille, Katrina and a Small Burial Ground

Hurricane Camille in 1969 destroyed 26 buildings, and with Black Methodists now welcome at other conference centers, there was talk of selling the property and dividing the proceeds among the 12 Black Methodist colleges. Bishop Mack B. Stokes put a stop to that, and Bishop Ernest T. Dixon and laymen including Wayne Calbert and Henry Harper worked to revitalize Gulfside, adding adult cottages known as Dixon Village. Then, in August 2005, Hurricane Katrina destroyed it completely. All that remained was a historic marker and a small burial ground where Bishop Jones, his wife Elizabeth, and Bishop Brooks lie. Even so, by April 2006 Gulfside was serving its neighbors again as a work center for a relief group helping rebuild the coast. Both the United Methodist Church and the State of Mississippi recognize it as a historic site. An open-air chapel near the graves, completed in 2016, was the first piece of rebuilding, and as of 2025 the buildings were still gone, with today's Gulfside ministry running a community garden and youth mentoring.

## Flight Context

Gulfside sits at 30.266 N, 89.393 W on the Mississippi Sound shoreline in Waveland, Hancock County, Mississippi, between Bay St. Louis to the northeast and the Louisiana state line to the west. From 1,500-2,500 feet follow the long, straight beach road along the Sound; the Bay of St. Louis and its bridge lie a few miles northeast. Stennis International Airport (KHSA) is about 7 nm north-northwest, Gulfport-Biloxi International (KGPT) about 19 nm east-northeast, and Slidell Airport (KASD) about 23 nm west-northwest. Expect sea breezes, summer afternoon thunderstorms and the June-November hurricane season along this coast.
