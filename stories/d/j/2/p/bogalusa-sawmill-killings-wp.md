# Bogalusa sawmill killings

In November 1919, in a Louisiana company town built around one of the world's great sawmills, white union men who stood up for a Black union leader were killed in an attack meant to keep Black and white workers from organizing together.

**Location:** 30.7806, -89.8639

**Categories:** 1919 labor disputes and strikes, African-American history of Louisiana, History of African-American civil rights, Labor-related violence in the United States, Massacres of African Americans

## Introduction

In the fall of 1919, in a Louisiana company town built around what was reported to be, at its peak, the largest sawmill in the world, a group of white union men did something the mill's owners were determined to stop: they stood up for a Black union leader. On November 22, that stand ended in gunfire. Four white unionists were killed in racially motivated attacks orchestrated by a white paramilitary group, the Self-Preservation and Loyalty League. The League had the support of the owners of the Great Southern Lumber Company, and its goal was to keep Bogalusa's workers from unionizing and its Black and white labor organizations from joining forces.

## Longleaf Pine and Thirty Cents an Hour

The Goodyear family chartered the Great Southern Lumber Company in 1902 to harvest the longleaf pine forests of southeastern Louisiana and southwestern Mississippi. Bogalusa was developed as its company town, and the sawmill there began operating in 1908. The company prospered, especially during World War I, but its workers earned only 30 cents an hour. At its peak, the Great Southern mill was reported to be the largest sawmill in the world. In a company town, the gap between that success and what workers took home would set the stage for conflict.

## A Town Already on Edge

Racial tensions in Bogalusa were running high before any strike began. On August 31, 1919, Lucius McCarty, a Black veteran, was lynched after being accused of assaulting a white woman. He was killed brutally, and his death deepened the racial hostility in town. That September, labor organizer Lem Williams was working to unite the town's unions into a Central Trades Assembly and sought formal recognition from the sawmill. The company answered by firing many union organizers and barring union members from buying goods at local stores. When the workers struck, the company brought in Black strikebreakers from New Orleans, sharpening tensions further. Demands for better wages were also met with the arrests of Black men for minor offenses, after which they were forced to labor at the mill.

## November 22, 1919

The confrontation came when white union members defended Sol Dacus, the head of the Black union. The Great Southern Lumber Company had its own private force of deputies and special officers to deploy against unrest. A riot signal sounded, and the deputies tried to arrest J. P. Bouchillon and Stanley O'Rourke, two union men carrying shotguns. Accounts conflict over who fired first. When the shooting stopped, Lem Williams, Bouchillon and a carpenter named Thomas Gaines had been shot dead, and O'Rourke, wounded in the fight, is counted as the fourth victim. Sol Dacus escaped and later fled to New Orleans. Federal troops were sent to Bogalusa to restore order.

## Part of the Red Summer

The killings were a severe setback for efforts to unionize the timber industry, and they belong to a wider national reckoning. They were part of the Red Summer of 1919, a period of civil unrest marked by attacks on Black communities and racial oppression in cities and counties across the United States. What happened at Bogalusa, sometimes called the "Bloody Bogalusa Massacre," highlights the dangers Black labor organizers faced and the broader struggle for civil rights in the United States. It also preserves the names of union men who died after choosing to stand with their Black fellow workers across the color line.

## Flight Context

Bogalusa lies at approximately 30.78 N, 89.86 W in southeastern Louisiana, close to the Mississippi state line. From 2,500 to 3,500 feet AGL the town's grid and industrial areas stand out against the surrounding forest and farmland. George R. Carr Memorial Air Field (KBXA) is about 2 nm north of town.
