# Fort Kaskaskia State Historic Site

Above the Mississippi near Chester, Illinois, an eroded French redoubt and a cemetery of reburied villagers keep the memory of Kaskaskia, Illinois' first capital, which the river began swallowing in 1881.

**Location:** 37.9688, -89.9106

**Categories:** Illinois State Historic Sites, American Revolutionary War sites, Pre-statehood history of Illinois, French colonial history, Randolph County, Illinois

## Introduction

The dead of Kaskaskia had to be moved uphill. After a flood in 1881 sent the Mississippi River pouring into the channel of the smaller Kaskaskia River, the great river began swallowing the village that had stood beside it for more than 170 years, and even its cemeteries were in danger. In an emergency operation, according to one account some 3,000 boxes of remains, a few holding entire families, were moved and reburied on top of the bluff to the east, where French settlers had once raised an earthen redoubt; the General Assembly authorized the move in 1891, and the Garrison Hill Cemetery monument went up in 1892. That blufftop near Chester, Illinois, is now Fort Kaskaskia State Historic Site, a park of more than 275 acres the state agreed to maintain forever as a memorial to a town the river largely erased.

## A Mission on the Oxbow

Kaskaskia began in 1703 as a Jesuit missionary post at the mouth of the Kaskaskia River, on lands that members of the Illinois Confederation hunted and farmed. Settlers from the Quebec and Louisiana regions soon began to trickle in, drawn to the rich alluvial farmland of the central Mississippi Valley. They built their village and fields around the mission, on a half-circle of bottomland cradled by the Kaskaskia River and an oxbow of the Mississippi. Some French settlers and soldiers married Illini women and raised children with them, with the blessing of the mission priests. Yet as the settlement grew through the eighteenth century, the land's original people may have come to realize there might not be enough room for everyone.

## Not Quite a Fort

Around 1759 the French settlers raised Fort Kaskaskia atop the bluff that looked down on their village. Strictly speaking it was never a fort but an earthen redoubt, the kind of defensive work frontier settlers across woodland North America often built in times of threat. Its French career was short. In 1763 France ceded the eastern Illinois Country, including Kaskaskia and the abandoned fort, to Great Britain. The British did not use the redoubt and left Kaskaskia almost defenseless, and the town carried on as a French-speaking village on the Mississippi frontier. Today the remains of the eighteenth-century earthwork can only be vaguely traced in the ground.

## Independence Day on the Frontier

The American Revolution reached this quiet bluff in 1778. Early that year George Rogers Clark, eager to defend western Virginia and the Kentucky country from attacks by Native Americans allied with the British, led a tiny force down the Ohio River, hoping to link up with the French-speaking settlements of the Mississippi Valley. His men marched overland from Fort Massac, near present-day Metropolis, Illinois, avoided being sighted by the British or their Native allies, and arrived at Kaskaskia on July 4, 1778. Most of the townspeople welcomed them. When a British force at Vincennes, Indiana, posed a threat, Clark used Kaskaskia as his jumping-off place and captured Vincennes in early 1779. The Americans controlled Kaskaskia and its redoubt for the rest of the war, and the 1783 Treaty of Paris gave them legal control of the territory.

## A Capital at the Water's Edge

Under American rule the large French-speaking population slowly became Americanized, yet the village's importance grew. In 1803 Lewis and Clark stopped here and picked up eleven additional men for their 1804-1806 expedition to the Pacific Ocean. Kaskaskia was the only capital of Illinois Territory, from 1809 to 1818, and after Illinois entered the Union on December 3, 1818, it served briefly as the new state's first capital, until 1820. Pierre Menard, a wealthy fur trader who lived across the Kaskaskia River at the foot of the bluff, was elected the state's first lieutenant governor.

## When the River Changed Course

Through the nineteenth century, and especially after the Civil War, the Mississippi cut through many of its old oxbows and shortened its channel. During the 1881 flood its moving water found the smaller, parallel bed of the Kaskaskia, which lay a few feet lower, and the whole river shifted into it, cutting across the head of a former oxbow. A village built on the waterfront of a modest river suddenly faced the full Mississippi. Reburying its dead on the bluff gave the old redoubt a second life, and the prominent Kaskaskia cemetery there still recalls the flood. In 1974 the site became a contributing property of the French Colonial Historic District, alongside Fort de Chartres, the Creole House, the Pierre Menard House and the Kolmer Site, a former Native American village. Today it is a campground and picnic area where visitors look out over the river that took most of the town below. A tiny village of Kaskaskia, home to 21 people in the 2020 census, survives on the river's far bank.

## Flight Context

Fort Kaskaskia State Historic Site sits at 37.9688°N, 89.9106°W on a blufftop on the Illinois side of the Mississippi River, northwest of Chester. From 2,000-3,000 feet AGL, follow the Mississippi and watch for the bluff line rising above the river on the Illinois bank. Chester and its Mississippi River bridge lie a few miles downstream. Nearby airports: Perryville Regional Airport in Perryville, Missouri (FAA identifier PCD), about 6 nm south-southeast across the river; Sparta Community Airport-Hunter Field (KSAR), about 15 nm northeast; and Southern Illinois Airport at Murphysboro (KMDH), about 33 nm east-southeast. River fog is common in the valley on cool mornings.
