# 1947 Centralia mine disaster

On March 25, 1947, coal dust that inspectors had warned about exploded in the Centralia No. 5 mine in southern Illinois, killing 111 men and echoing through Woody Guthrie's songs and the state's politics.

**Location:** 38.4963, -89.1445

**Categories:** Coal mining disasters in Illinois, 1947 mining disasters, Labor movement in Illinois, Washington County, Illinois, Disasters

## Introduction

Survivors remembered the dust. When the electric power in the Centralia No. 5 coal mine stopped on March 25, 1947, a strong blow of air and dust rushed through the workings for about five minutes, and one miner said it looked like black powder. There were 142 men in the mine at Wamac, on the edge of Centralia, Illinois. Only 31 of them came out alive. The US Department of Labor lists the explosion, which killed 111 men, as the second-worst American coal mining disaster since 1940.

## Warnings on File

The mine had received numerous warnings about its conditions before the blast. The danger was coal dust, which can turn a small spark into an explosion. After the disaster, United Mine Workers president John L. Lewis testified before Congress that Secretary of the Interior Julius Krug had ignored inspectors' warnings that coal dust had been accumulating. An Illinois government report, as later summarized by historians, turned the spotlight on the state's own regulators. It blamed the explosion on a buildup of coal powder and, although it could not identify what set it off, found that the buildup was due to officials following a "weak, ineffectual, and indifferent policy toward enforcement of state mining laws." The federal Mine Safety and Health Administration later reported the cause as an underburdened or blown-out shot, a blasting charge that ignited the dust instead of breaking coal.

## Five Minutes of Dust

Survivors' testimony is the clearest record of the moment. First the electric power died. Then came the rush of dust, lasting about five minutes by their accounts. A mine employee said he could smell powder smoke, though he did not enter the mine until after the explosion. Below ground, men died in two ways. Sixty-five were killed by burns and other injuries from the blast itself, and forty-five were killed by afterdamp, the poisonous gases an explosion leaves behind in a mine's tunnels. Rescuers brought eight men out alive, but one of them later died from the effects of the afterdamp he had breathed. That made 111 dead.

## One Hundred Eleven Names

The published list of the dead runs alphabetically from Joe Altadonna to its last name, and reading it slowly turns a number back into people. Surnames repeat down the page. Four Gutzlers are there: Adolph, Fred, John H. and John W. So are Martin Freeman Sr. and Martin Freeman Jr., Otto and Raymond Buehne, and Joe and Dude Vancil. There are two Lenzinis, two Piazzis and two Spinners, along with Clifford and Frank Copple. Between them stand the names of men like Roy Alvarez, Odia Lee Francis, H. W. Niepoetter, Alfred Pollacci and Emmett Uhls, each one a worker who went underground that day and did not come home.

## New Made Graves

The disaster became part of American song. Woody Guthrie wrote and recorded The Dying Miner about Centralia, along with two more songs: Waiting at the Gate, told from the perspective of a miner's son, and Talking Centralia, also known as Talking Miner. Guthrie's recording of The Dying Miner is available on the Smithsonian Folkways collection Struggle, first issued as an LP in 1976 and reissued on CD in 1990. Songwriter Bucky Halker later rearranged it for Welcome to Labor Land, his 2002 album of labor songs from Illinois, and recorded New Made Graves of Centralia for his 2000 album Don't Want Your Millions, basing it on an original recording in the Country Music Hall of Fame whose author and performer are unknown. The disaster also shaped Illinois politics. In November 1948 Governor Dwight H. Green, seeking a third term, lost to Adlai Stevenson in an upset, a defeat many attribute in part to the deaths at Centralia No. 5.

## Flight Context

The Centralia No. 5 mine site is at 38.4963°N, 89.1445°W, at Wamac on the southern edge of Centralia in south-central Illinois. From 2,000-3,000 feet AGL, the Centralia street grid and surrounding flat farmland dominate the view; the disaster is remembered more in the town than in any large surface feature. Centralia Municipal Airport (KENL) is only about 3 nm east-northeast, Salem-Leckrone Airport (KSLO) is about 12 nm northeast, and MidAmerica St. Louis Airport (KBLV) near Belleville is about 33 nm west. Visibility is usually good over the flat prairie, though summer haze and spring thunderstorms are common.
