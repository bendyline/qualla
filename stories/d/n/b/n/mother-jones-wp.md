# Mother Jones

Widowed by yellow fever and burned out by the Chicago Fire, Mary Harris Jones became "the most dangerous woman in America" to mine owners, and Illinois miners raised eighty tons of granite over her grave at Mount Olive.

**Location:** 39.0807, -89.7333

**Categories:** American trade unionists, Labor history, Child labor reform, Irish emigrants to the United States, Burials in Illinois, Macoupin County, Illinois

## Introduction

On October 11, 1936, an estimated 50,000 people came to the Union Miners Cemetery in Mount Olive, Illinois, to see a gravestone. Coal miners had paid for it, saving more than $16,000 to buy eighty tons of Minnesota pink granite: a twenty-foot shaft with a bas-relief portrait at its center, flanked by bronze statues of two miners. The portrait was of Mary Harris Jones, known since 1897 as Mother Jones, a former schoolteacher and dressmaker whom a prosecutor once called the most dangerous woman in America. She lies here among miners killed in strike violence, men she called "her boys."

## Famine, Fever and Fire

Mary G. Harris was born on the north side of Cork, Ireland, the daughter of Catholic tenant farmers, and baptized on August 1, 1837; her exact birth date is uncertain. The Great Famine drove her family, like more than a million others, to North America, where they faced discrimination as Irish Catholic immigrants. She trained at the tuition-free Toronto Normal School and, without graduating, took a teaching post at a convent in Monroe, Michigan, in 1859. In 1861 she married George E. Jones, a member and organizer of the National Union of Iron Moulders. Six years later a yellow fever epidemic in Memphis killed him and all four of their children, three girls and a boy, all under five. She returned to Chicago and opened a dressmaking business, sewing for the city's upper class, until the Great Chicago Fire of 1871 destroyed her shop. From those losses came a new life organizing for the Knights of Labor and the United Mine Workers of America.

## Brooms, Tin Pans and Mill Children

A charismatic speaker who liked props and dramatic stunts, she had a gift for drawing whole families into a strike. When UMWA president John Mitchell brought her to northeastern Pennsylvania to encourage unity among striking workers, she organized their wives into a group that wielded brooms, beat on tin pans and shouted "Join the union!" Children weighed on her most. The 1900 census had revealed that one sixth of American children under sixteen were employed, and she saw children at union headquarters missing fingers. When newspapermen said they could not publish the facts because mill owners held stock in their papers, she replied, "Well, I've got stock in these little children and I'll arrange a little publicity." In 1903 she led the March of the Mill Children, a 125-mile trek from Kensington, Philadelphia, to President Theodore Roosevelt's summer home at Oyster Bay, New York, with rallies each night in a new town. Roosevelt refused to meet the marchers, but child labor moved to the forefront of the public agenda.

## Twenty Thousand Men Walk Out

In 1902, at her trial in West Virginia for ignoring an injunction against meetings by striking miners, district attorney Reese Blizzard gave her the title that stuck. "There sits the most dangerous woman in America," he announced. "She comes into a state where peace and prosperity reign... crooks her finger, [and] twenty thousand contented men lay down their tools and walk out." A decade later she returned for the Paint Creek-Cabin Creek strike, speaking amid a shooting war between union miners and the mine owners' private army. Arrested on February 13, 1913, and court-martialed on charges including conspiracy to commit murder, she refused to recognize the court and was sentenced to twenty years. Under house arrest she caught a dangerous case of pneumonia. She was freed after 85 days, just as Indiana Senator John W. Kern launched a Senate investigation into conditions in the local mines. Months later she was organizing in Colorado's coalfield war, and after the Ludlow Massacre her meeting with John D. Rockefeller Jr. helped bring about his 1915 visit to the mines and long-sought reforms.

## Her Boys at Mount Olive

She organized for the UMW into the 1920s, and in 1925 Charles H. Kerr & Co. published The Autobiography of Mother Jones. On May 1, 1930, at the Maryland farm of her friends Walter and Lillie May Burgess, she celebrated a self-proclaimed 100th birthday and made a statement for a newsreel. She died there that November 30. Her grave in Mount Olive lies beside miners who died in the 1898 Battle of Virden. In 1932 Illinois miners broke away from the United Mine Workers to form the Progressive Miners of America, founded that September at nearby Gillespie and renamed the Progressive Mine Workers of America in 1938, and Mount Olive's miners were among its supporters. Convinced they had acted in her spirit, they resolved to give her a proper headstone, and Mount Olive still observes Miners' Day each October; the date of the 1936 dedication, October 11, is also remembered there as Mother Jones's Day.

## Fight Like Hell for the Living

She was never easy to categorize. She broke with many women activists over suffrage, declaring that "you don't need the vote to raise hell!" Her record had shadows too: in 1921, trying to turn back armed miners marching on Logan County, she claimed to hold a telegram from President Warren Harding, refused to show it, and was suspected of having invented it. Her words outlasted the controversy. "Pray for the dead and fight like hell for the living" is still invoked by union supporters. The National Women's Hall of Fame inducted her in 1984 and the National Mining Hall of Fame in 2019. During the 1989-90 Pittston Coal strike, miners' wives and daughters called themselves the Daughters of Mother Jones, and in 2012 her native Cork unveiled a plaque marking the 175th anniversary of her birth.

## Flight Context

Mother Jones's grave and monument stand in the Union Miners Cemetery at Mount Olive, Illinois, at 39.0807°N, 89.7333°W, in farm country beside Interstate 55 between Springfield and St. Louis. From 2,000-3,000 feet AGL, Mount Olive's small street grid and the interstate corridor are the best references. Litchfield Municipal Airport (FAA identifier 3LF) is about 6 nm north-northeast, St. Louis Regional Airport at Alton (KALN) is about 19 nm southwest, St. Louis Lambert International (KSTL) is about 36 nm southwest, and Abraham Lincoln Capital Airport at Springfield (KSPI) is about 46 nm north. Expect summer haze and convective storms over the flat prairie.
