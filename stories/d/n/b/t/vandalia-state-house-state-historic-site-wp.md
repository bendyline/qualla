# Vandalia State House State Historic Site

Built in about four months in 1836 and abandoned as a capitol within three years, Vandalia's plain brick State House is the oldest surviving capitol building in Illinois.

**Location:** 38.9611, -89.0939

**Categories:** Former state capitols in the United States, Illinois State Historic Sites, Abraham Lincoln, Federal architecture in Illinois, National Register of Historic Places in Illinois

## Introduction

It took about four months in 1836 for crews and companies from around the region to raise a new brick statehouse in Vandalia, Illinois. When Abraham Lincoln and his fellow legislators came back that fall, the building was waiting for them: unpainted, topped with a gable roof and a cupola, finished at a cost of $16,000. By February 1837, those same legislators had voted to move the capital to Springfield. The Vandalia State House served as the seat of Illinois government only from 1836 to 1839, and yet it is the oldest Illinois capitol building to survive.

## Capitals That Did Not Last

Illinois had trouble holding on to its seats of government. Admitted to the Union in 1818, the state quickly abandoned its first governmental center at Kaskaskia in 1820 because of environmental threats. At the new capital of Vandalia, a second statehouse went up in lumber, and it burned down in 1823, after only three years. A third was hastily built in 1824.

That third building saw the beginning of Abraham Lincoln's political career, as a member of the Illinois General Assembly in 1834. Elected from Sangamon County, closer to the geographic center of the state, Lincoln led a central Illinois caucus that called for the government to move to the growing town of Springfield. The first three capitol buildings have all since disappeared, which is why the fourth, built in a hurry and used for so short a time, carries so much of the state's early story.

## Cracked Walls and a Sinking Floor

By the beginning of 1836, the third capitol was so structurally unsafe that assemblies refused to convene in it. Some Vandalia townsmen wrote to Governor Joseph Duncan about the problem, and he told them to find out whether the building could be repaired. A closer look at the cracked walls and sinking floor settled the matter: it would be torn down and replaced.

The replacement was built quickly, with companies and laborers drawn from across the region. It was a plain brick building in the Federal style, left unpainted, with a gable roof and a cupola. It was also intentionally designed with larger quarters for state offices, which were deemed more important than the question of moving the capital. That question, as it turned out, was about to be answered.

## Three Years at the Center

When Lincoln and his colleagues returned to Vandalia in the fall of 1836, the new "State House" stood ready. They did not stay long. In February 1837, the legislature voted to move the governmental center to the more populous town of Springfield, the outcome Lincoln's caucus had pushed for. The fifth capitol, now the Old State Capitol State Historic Site in Springfield, was finished in 1840 and served until 1876, when it was replaced by the sixth and current Illinois State Capitol.

So the building in Vandalia had just three years, 1836 to 1839, as the heart of Illinois government. It is a short tenure for a structure that would go on to outlast every capitol that came before it.

## Courthouse, Memorial, Museum

The state gave the building to Fayette County, which used it as its courthouse from 1839 until 1933. In the late 1850s, porticoes were added, turning the Federal-style building into a Greek Revival structure. The state had bought the old statehouse and square back in 1918, according to the city's history, though county offices stayed until 1933. That year the building became the Vandalia State House State Memorial, and an extensive program of interior refitting and reconstruction began. Illinois has operated it as a monument to the state's pioneer years ever since.

In 1985 it became a State Historic Site within the new Illinois Historic Preservation Agency, whose successor, the Illinois Historic Preservation Division of the Department of Natural Resources, maintains the old statehouse and interprets it to its brief years as the fourth capitol. Guided tours are offered, and visitors can also explore the furnished rooms on their own; the site has been open on reduced hours, 10 a.m. to 2 p.m. Tuesday through Friday, because of a staffing shortage, so check before you go. The building sits on the historic National Road, where the Madonna of the Trail statue on the southwest corner of the square marks Vandalia as the road's western end, and it was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1974.

## Flight Context

The Vandalia State House stands at approximately 38.96°N, 89.09°W in Vandalia, Illinois, on the historic National Road. It is a small brick building with a cupola and porticoes, so a low pass at 1,500–2,500 feet AGL over the town gives the best chance of picking it out. Vandalia Municipal Airport (KVLA) is about 4 nm away, making this an easy stop. Greenville Airport (KGRE) is about 15 nm to the west-southwest and Salem–Leckrone Airport (KSLO) about 20 nm to the south-southeast. Best in clear weather.
