# Air Illinois Flight 710

On a rainy October night in 1983, an Air Illinois airliner flew on toward Carbondale on a draining battery after both generators went offline, and crashed near Pinckneyville, killing all ten aboard.

**Location:** 38.1500, -89.3167

**Categories:** Aviation accidents and incidents in the United States in 1983, Airliner accidents and incidents in Illinois, Accidents and incidents involving the Hawker Siddeley HS 748, Airliner accidents and incidents caused by electrical failure, Disasters

## Introduction

About a minute and a half after taking off from Springfield, Illinois, the crew of Air Illinois Flight 710 told air traffic control they had a "slight electrical problem" and would keep controllers "advised." It was just after 8:20 on the night of October 11, 1983, and the Hawker Siddeley HS 748 still had the leg south to Carbondale ahead of it. About 35 minutes after takeoff, with its instruments and radios dead, the airliner came down in a hilly pasture near Pinckneyville. All ten people aboard were killed.

## Forty-Five Minutes Behind

Flight 710 was a scheduled service from Chicago to Carbondale with a stop in Springfield. The first leg was uneventful, but by the time the airplane touched down in Springfield it was running 45 minutes late. When the electrical trouble appeared after the 20:20 departure, the first officer set about isolating the faulty generator. The National Transportation Safety Board concluded that he mistakenly isolated the right generator instead of the left; the right generator had a history of maintenance issues. About four minutes after takeoff he told the captain that the left generator was "totally dead" and that the right one was putting out voltage but he could not get a load on it. With direct-current power lost from both generators, the airliner was flying on its battery.

## A Battery Running Down

Springfield, the departure airport, was still nearby, while Carbondale lay much farther away. The captain chose to press on. Within about thirteen minutes of takeoff the crew had switched off the beacons, the navigation lights and the excess cabin lights, but they did not further reduce the electrical load on the battery, and it was eventually depleted. The flight instruments failed, and so did the communication and navigation radios. At 20:52 the captain decided to descend to 2,400 feet. From there the airplane sank slowly toward a hilly pasture and banked right while the first officer used a flashlight to try to work out where they were. Why it came down where it did is not established: the crew may have been attempting a forced landing, or, as one account has it, a disoriented pilot may have mistaken the strip lighting at a mine for the approach lights at Carbondale's airport.

## Two Circles in the Rain

On the ground, John Fisher and his wife, Arilla, heard the airplane circle over their property a couple of times. It had been raining hard. "The aircraft circled two times," Arilla Fisher said. "It made a lot more noise the second time. I ran to the back porch. I saw one flash and heard a lot of noise. [John and I] could smell gas and fuel." Local accounts described the flash as a brief fire from spewing fuel; the NTSB reported no post-crash fire. Those who died, seven passengers and three crew members, were on board an airplane the NTSB found destroyed by impact forces, its fuselage disintegrated along a wreckage path about half a mile long.

## What the Investigators Found

The NTSB placed the probable cause on "the captain's decision to continue the flight toward the more distant destination airport" after both generators stopped supplying power, rather than returning to the nearby departure airport. That decision, the board found, was adversely affected by "self-imposed psychological factors" that led him to misjudge the battery's endurance and the risks of continuing. Interviews with other Air Illinois pilots led the investigation to describe the captain as an average pilot who ran "a one man operation," did not welcome suggestions from his first officers and was in a hurry to make schedules; he had been on duty about nine and a half hours when the emergency began. Those pilots also said he would overspeed the airplane on descent to save time and have the overspeed warning disabled. Yet the board did not stop at the cockpit. It found that the airline's failure to provide, and the FAA's failure to assure, an adequate recurrent training program left both pilots unable to cope promptly and correctly with the electrical failure. The crash was later the subject of "Pitch Black," an episode of the Canadian documentary series Mayday.

## Flight Context

This story's map point, 38.150°N, 89.317°W, is the crash site, near Pinckneyville in Perry County. Flying the route today at 3,000-4,000 feet AGL, the flat farmland of central Illinois gives way to hillier pasture and mine workings toward Pinckneyville. Abraham Lincoln Capital Airport at Springfield (KSPI), the flight's last departure point, is about 103 nm north of the crash site. Near the crash site, Pinckneyville-Du Quoin Airport (FAA identifier PJY) is about 10 nm south, and Southern Illinois Airport (KMDH), which serves Carbondale, is about 22 nm south. Night flying here is very dark away from the towns; check forecasts for autumn rain and low ceilings.
