# Brooks Catsup Bottle water tower

A 1949 water tower in Collinsville, Illinois, built to look like a bottle of Brooks catsup, that the city once declined to take on and volunteers saved with $80,000 and two years of work.

**Location:** 38.6628, -89.9822

## Introduction

Fill the tank with catsup instead of water, and it could theoretically stock 640,000 bottles. On the south side of Collinsville, Illinois, a water tower dressed as a giant bottle of Brooks catsup stands 70 feet tall atop a 100-foot stand, with room for 100,000 gallons inside. Trademarked as "The World's Largest Catsup Bottle," it has been a landmark since its construction in 1949, and it is listed on the National Register of Historic Places as a prime example of mid-20th-century novelty architecture. Yet in 1993, when the tower was offered to the city, Collinsville said no.

## A Company President's Idea

The tower was built to do an ordinary job. In 1949 the W.E. Caldwell Company of Louisville, Kentucky, constructed it to supply water to the nearby Brooks catsup plant, owned by the G.S. Suppiger Company. What made it extraordinary was a suggestion from the top. Gerhart S. Suppiger, the company's president, is credited with the idea that the water tower be designed to resemble one of the firm's own catsup bottles. The result turned a piece of industrial plumbing into an advertisement, a bottle 70 feet tall perched 100 feet in the air, some 170 feet from the ground to the top. Anyone passing by could see exactly what was being made nearby.

## The City Said No, the Volunteers Said Yes

By the early 1990s the bottle was aging. In 1993, Curtice-Burns, Inc., then owner of the Brooks plant, prepared to sell the property and offered the water tower to Collinsville. The city declined to buy it, citing the high projected cost of repairs and restoration. That might have been the end of the story. Instead, volunteers took up the cause. Over the course of two years they raised $80,000 to "repair, strip, and paint" the structure, and the project was completed in June 1995. Their effort was vindicated in August 2002, when the tower was added to the National Register of Historic Places. The landmark owes its survival less to any institution than to volunteers who refused to let it go. In 2015 a local contractor bought the bottle and the adjacent warehouse, promising to preserve it.

## Catsup in the Spotlight

Saved and repainted, the bottle took on a second life as a star. Its official website doubles as a fan club, and the site says it has drawn visitors from all over the country for more than 75 years. In 2004 it appeared in A Program About Unusual Buildings & Other Roadside Stuff, a PBS documentary by Rick Sebak of WQED that celebrated exactly this sort of architectural whimsy. It is even mentioned in The Twilight Saga: Eclipse as a place Bella should visit. Today the tower is most notable as a regional landmark and a roadside attraction, the kind of structure that makes travelers pull over simply to confirm that yes, it really is a catsup bottle the size of a building.
