# Millennium Manor

Convinced that Armageddon would come in 1969, an aging mason and his wife spent nearly a decade building a marble house in Alcoa, Tennessee, meant to stand for a thousand years beyond it.

**Location:** 35.7800, -83.9650

**Categories:** Houses in Blount County, Tennessee, Roadside attractions in Tennessee, Alcoa, Tennessee, National Register of Historic Places in Blount County, Tennessee, Folk architecture

## Introduction

William Andrew Nicholson believed the world would end in 1969, and he meant for his house to outlast it by a thousand years. So in June 1937, at the age of 61, he began to build. He and his wife, Fair, had come to Alcoa, Tennessee, from Pickens County, Georgia, where William had worked as a mason and carpenter. That year he took a job at the Alcoa plant as a replacement for striking workers, and across the street from the plant, while still working full time, he started raising a house of stone. The couple finished it in December 1946. They called it Millennium Manor, and it is still standing.

## A Thousand-Year Promise

The Nicholsons did not belong to a church in Tennessee, but they were deeply religious people who believed in a literal interpretation of Revelation 20:6: "Blessed and holy is he that hath part in the first resurrection. Over such the second death has no power, but they shall be priests of God and Christ, and shall reign with him a thousand years." Their house was meant to match that promise, built to survive Armageddon and the thousand years beyond it. For a model, the Nicholsons looked to Roman architecture, whose structures can still be seen standing after more than 2,000 years. They did all the work themselves, without the aid of machines, a remarkable feat for two people who were both thin and small of stature.

## Arches All the Way Down

The pale stone is Tennessee Pink Marble, found in nearby Friendsville; the state's National Register summary also lists hand-carved granite. Its secret is the arch and keystone, visible over the doors and windows and repeated throughout the entire house, including the floor and roof. The Nicholsons first built a wooden form shaped like the inside of the building and laid a rubber tarp over it. They stacked marble on top and set a keystone, and at that point, the form could have been pulled away and the roof would have stood on its own without any mortar. Cement was then poured over the stone and sifted down to harden against the tarp. Wrinkles from that rubber sheet can still be seen on the inside ceilings. Because the mortar only filled gaps and bore no load, it should in theory be possible to remove every bit of cement and have the building keep standing.

## Walls Built to Last

The numbers speak to the couple's intent. More than 4,000 bags of cement went into the house, and in places agricultural lime replaced sand to limit water seepage. The thinnest inside wall is 19 inches thick and the thinnest outside wall 25 inches. The roof is more than three feet thick and supposedly weighs 423 tons on its own, while the floor exceeds four feet. Every wall bears load. Inside are 14 rooms and a two-car underground garage, about 3,000 square feet under roof, along with seven chimney flues upstairs and a well six stories deep and five feet across. Even the street tested Nicholson's resolve. When Wright Road was widened in November 1943, he was forced to tear down the entire wall facing it, and he promptly rebuilt it six feet farther back. Until the house was finished, the couple lived in a small white kit home beside it, which was later moved to the back lot.

## Faith and Doubt

Fair Nicholson did not live to see 1969. She died of cancer in 1950, at 72, after more than 50 years of marriage. William spoke of the loss with painful honesty: "It was hard to be parted from her after so many years. My wife believed in me but her faith in eternal life was weak. She tried to believe, but she had her doubts. There came times when she talked of dying." Fifteen years later, at 88 and nearly blind and deaf, William died. The day before he went to the hospital, he had told the pastor of the church next door that "if God doesn't intervene soon, I will die." He was buried beside Fair in Clarks Grove Cemetery, four years before the Armageddon he had built for.

## The Castle Endures

The Nicholsons left ten children, but none came to live in the stone house after William died, and vandals soon stole or broke everything inside. Later owners found other uses for it: a meeting place for the Odd Fellows, a rental property, even a haunted house for the Jaycees. In 1995 it sold at auction for $39,000 to a new owner who took on the restoration work himself, an ongoing project. Millennium Manor, also called the Castle, was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 2020. It is a private residence, though its owners, Dean and Karen Fontaine, have offered guided tours, mainly on summer weekends or by appointment, and have held an open house on Memorial Day.

## Flight Context

Millennium Manor stands at about 35.78°N, 83.965°W in Alcoa, Tennessee, across the street from the Alcoa aluminum plant, which is the landmark to look for; the house itself is small and hard to pick out from the air. The site lies just southeast of McGhee Tyson Airport (KTYS), about 2 nm away, so it lies within the busy controlled airspace around KTYS; coordinate with approach control and expect to view it only in passing. Other nearby fields: Knoxville Downtown Island Airport (KDKX), about 12 nm north-northeast. The Great Smoky Mountains rise to the southeast, and valley haze is common on summer afternoons.
