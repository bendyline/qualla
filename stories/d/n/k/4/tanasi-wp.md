# Tanasi

The Overhill Cherokee town of Tanasi gave its name to a river and then to a state, and now lies beneath Tellico Reservoir, marked by a monument on the shore above it.

**Location:** 35.5486, -84.1324

**Categories:** Cherokee towns in Tennessee, Overhill Cherokee, Archaeological sites on the National Register of Historic Places in Tennessee, Pre-statehood history of Tennessee, Populated places inundated by the Tennessee Valley Authority

## Introduction

Every time someone says "Tennessee," they are repeating the name of a Cherokee town that now lies underwater. Tanasi, also rendered Tanase, Tenasi, Tenassee, Tunissee and other variations, stood on the west bank of the Little Tennessee River in what is now Monroe County, Tennessee. From as early as 1721 until 1730 it served as the de facto capital of the Overhill Cherokee. Early Euro-American explorers and traders named the river after the town, replacing what was probably its Cherokee name, Callamaco, and the name kept traveling until it covered a state. What the word meant is a mystery; the ethnologist James Mooney reported that its meaning is unknown.

## A Terrace Beneath the Hills

Tanasi sat on a relatively flat terrace 27 miles upstream from the river's mouth, opposite a sharp curve called Bacon's Bend. The river ran along one side of the town, and on the other rose a line of high, steep hills, part of the Appalachian Ridge-and-Valley Province, which Henry Timberlake noted on his map of 1762. A few miles to the south, the Appalachian Range proper begins. Immediately north, across an unnamed stream, lay the town of Chota, which developed later than Tanasi and would eventually eclipse it. English colonists called these communities the Overhill Towns, because reaching them meant crossing over the Appalachian Mountains from the east.

## A Colonel Named Chicken

Tanasi enters the written record in the early 18th century, when the fur trade between the English and the Cherokee had grown large enough that the colonists wanted to regulate it. Among the first traders to live here was Eleazar Wiggan, whom the Cherokee nicknamed "Old Rabbit." He was operating out of Tanasi as early as 1711. In 1725 South Carolina sent Colonel George Chicken to Tanasi to seek Cherokee help in the colony's struggles with the Creek. Chicken wrote that the town's leader, known as the Tanasi Warrior or Head Warrior of Tanasi, ruled the Overhill, Middle and Valley towns alike, a claim that sits uneasily with how decentralized Cherokee society was and how far apart, across rugged mountains, those town groups lay. Chicken did record a speech in which the Tanasi Warrior pledged support for the English, followed by a pipe-smoking session in which the warrior described recent Creek hostilities. Two years later, Colonel John Herbert came on a similar mission and met the "King and Long Warriour" of Tanasi at the Cherokee townhouse.

## The Crown of Possum Hair

In 1730 Sir Alexander Cuming, a Scottish adventurer who claimed to be an emissary of King George II, arrived at Tanasi and secured the Tanasi Warrior's allegiance to England. He wanted proof to carry home: the Crown of Tanasi, an esteemed ceremonial headdress described as resembling a wig of dyed possum hair. To get it, Cuming turned to Moytoy, chief of Great Tellico, and used his flamboyance to persuade the Cherokee to crown Moytoy "Emperor of the Cherokee." Moytoy obtained the headdress in return. Cuming then departed for England with seven Cherokee men, among them the future leader Attakullakulla and two Tanasi warriors, Clogoittah and Oukanaekah (and, by one account, the trader Wiggan), to be presented to the king. The crowning moved the center of Cherokee power to Great Tellico, about 16 miles southwest. After Moytoy died in 1741, Old Hop of Chota began consolidating power, and Chota soon overshadowed its older neighbor. When Timberlake wrote in 1765, he counted 12 dwellings and 21 warriors at Tanasi, with Old Hop as chief of both towns. Still, in 1775 the Tanasi Warrior was among the chiefs who signed a treaty with the Watauga Association, colonists seeking their own jurisdiction west of the mountains, in what became Tennessee.

## Two Towns or One?

Tanasi's bond with Chota has long puzzled historians and archaeologists. Eighteenth-century traders and diplomats clearly described two separate towns. The maps drawn by John Herbert in 1727 and George Hunter in 1730 show Tanasi but not Chota, which does not appear in the record until around 1745. In 1746 the English agent George Pawley met Cherokee leaders at the Chota townhouse, a sign that Chota had taken the lead. The missionary William Richardson, visiting in the late 1750s, described the towns as separated by a "small river." Yet in the ground the difference nearly vanishes. Excavators found virtually no distinction in the cultural materials and features on either side of the creek, and some have speculated that Tanasi and Chota were really a single community with two districts and two governments.

## Under the Reservoir

Archaeologists came repeatedly, from a Smithsonian mound survey in the late 1880s to a 1939 dig led by Thomas Lewis and Madeline Kneburg. When a dam project threatened to drown the site, University of Tennessee teams excavated from 1969 to 1974, with two seasons, in 1970 and 1972, focused on Tanasi itself. The 1972 work alone uncovered 191 features, 22 burials, 1,005 postmolds and six structures. Across the combined site, diggers found two townhouses. The older and smaller one, just over 50 feet across, may have been the Tanasi townhouse that emissaries reported in the 1720s, or an earlier version of Chota's. Tellico Dam was completed in 1979, and the reservoir covered both towns. In 1986 the remains of most of the people buried at Tanasi and Chota were reinterred in a burial mound at the Sequoyah Birthplace Museum near Vonore, while one identified warrior, Oconostota, was reburied at Chota with a marker. In the 1980s the Tennessee Valley Authority also placed a monument on the shoreline above Tanasi, southeast of Vonore, honoring the town that named a state.

## Flight Context

The monument above the submerged site of Tanasi stands near 35.5486°N, 84.1324°W on the shore of Tellico Reservoir, southeast of Vonore, Tennessee. From the air, follow the Little Tennessee arm of Tellico Reservoir upstream and look for the sharp river curve at Bacon's Bend, with steep ridges rising behind the site and the Appalachian front a few miles to the south. A viewing altitude of 3,500-4,500 feet MSL gives a good view of the lake and ridges. Nearest airports: Monroe County Airport in Madisonville (KMNV), about 12 nm west; McGhee Tyson Airport in Knoxville/Maryville (KTYS), about 17 nm north-northeast; and Western Carolina Regional Airport in Andrews (KRHP), about 25 nm southeast across the mountains. Lake haze and morning valley fog are common.
