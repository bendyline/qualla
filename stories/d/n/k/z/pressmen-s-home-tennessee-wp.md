# Pressmen's Home, Tennessee

For more than half a century a printing union ran its headquarters from a self-sufficient town in a remote Tennessee valley, complete with a trade school, sanatorium, hotel, power plant and a chapel with windows attributed to Tiffany, much of it now lost to decay and fire.

## Introduction

In a valley north of Rogersville, Tennessee, the liveliest thing on the property in recent years has been a golf course and country club that, by one account, sometimes runs a restaurant. It is a quiet use for a place that once had its own power plant, hotel, sanatorium, post office and trade school. For more than half a century, from 1911 to 1967, this was the headquarters of the International Printing Pressmen and Assistants' Union of North America, a working town built by and for a labor union, in the Poor Valley area of Hawkins County. Today it is a ghost town that never quite emptied, and the whole complex is listed on the National Register of Historic Places as a historic district.

## A Hometown Boy's Idea

Pressmen's Home was the brainchild of George L. Berry, who was born nearby in Hawkins County. After he became president of the Pressmen's Union, he convinced its leaders to buy the Hale Springs Resort, a mineral springs retreat whose sulphur-rich water was believed to have medicinal qualities. The resort buildings became the core of the new headquarters, and a Home Building, under construction when the union arrived in 1911, took in the visitors who used to come for the springs. As the union grew, so did its town. Larger and more elaborate facilities went up, and Pressmen's Home became a self-sufficient community with a hydroelectric plant, telecommunication utilities, a retirement home and farms. It was reportedly generating its own electricity several years before the Tennessee Valley Authority did the same for the rest of Hawkins County.

## Sandstone, Tiffany Glass and a Printing Press

The most striking building was the Memorial Chapel, built in 1926 as a non-denominational church honoring union members who died in World War I; its dedication was later widened to everyone who served in the United States and Canadian military after that war. Architect John Sheridan designed it in the Italianate style from native sandstone, with stained glass windows attributed to Louis Comfort Tiffany and a fresco on the ceiling. When it was built, it was thought to be the only church owned by a labor union. In the garden outside stood the printing press on which the union's logo was based. That same year a four-story hotel rose to house members and their families who came to train, its facade cut from a sandstone quarry on the property. Guests ate home-cooked meals drawn largely from the site's own dairy farm, chickens, vegetable garden and hog lot.

## Ink and Illness

The union built more than offices here. In 1916 it opened a tuberculosis sanatorium, at a time when exposure to printer's ink was thought to be a cause of the disease. The hospital was fully staffed and well equipped for its era, and members who fell ill could receive care at no charge. Many who died of tuberculosis are buried in the cemetery at Pressmen's Home. The sanatorium closed in 1961 and was demolished the following year. Training was the other great mission. By the late 1940s a new Trade School held more than $500,000 worth of presses and equipment, lent by manufacturers, for teaching letterpress, gravure and offset printing, ink mixing, platemaking, color separation and bindery work. Trainees were supposed to have five years in the union, but many people from Hawkins County were allowed to train there free, with no experience at all.

## After the Union Left

By the mid-1960s, pressure from competing unions lobbying the federal government convinced the pressmen's leaders that a rural East Tennessee address was working against them. The union announced in 1967 that it was moving its headquarters, and lack of funding, along with a merger with other printing unions, closed Pressmen's Home as a retirement facility in 1969. Plans to revive the place came and went: a tourist resort, a retirement community, even a state penitentiary. In the 1970s an investment group partly redeveloped the site as a resort called Camelot and sold tracts for vacation homes, but buyers found some of their lots on slopes unsuitable for building, lawsuits followed, and the developer went bankrupt. Since then, buildings have fallen into disrepair, been demolished or burned, some by accident and some by arson. The hotel was destroyed by arson in October 1994. The chapel and George Berry's mausoleum were still standing in the most recent accounts, though Berry's remains were later moved to a cemetery in Rogersville.

## Flight Context

Pressmen's Home lies in the Poor Valley area of Hawkins County, Tennessee, north of Rogersville; the recorded coordinates, 36.45°N, 83.0542°W, are approximate. The old golf course is among the easiest features to spot, with the sandstone chapel and scattered older buildings nearby. Rogersville's historic downtown lies to the southeast. A viewing altitude of about 3,000-3,500 feet MSL keeps you comfortably above the valley floor and the surrounding ridges. Hawkins County Airport northeast of Rogersville (KRVN) is about 8 nm east, and Tri-Cities Regional Airport (KTRI) at Blountville is about 31 nm east. Valley fog is common on calm mornings in this ridge-and-valley country, and ridgelines can produce turbulence on windy days.
