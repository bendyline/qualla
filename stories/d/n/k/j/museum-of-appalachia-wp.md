# Museum of Appalachia

What began with one man's purchases at a 1962 farm auction grew into the Museum of Appalachia, a roughly 65-acre village of rescued log buildings and thousands of relics near Norris, Tennessee, each one kept with its story.

**Location:** 36.1815, -84.0687

**Categories:** Living museums in Tennessee, Appalachian culture in Tennessee, Folk art museums and galleries in Tennessee, Log cabins in the United States, Museums established in 1969

## Introduction

At the auction of an old local farm in 1962, John Rice Irwin noticed something that troubled him: many of the buyers would be removing the items forever from their historical context. Irwin, an East Tennessee educator and businessman, decided the true value of such things lay in the story of how they were used. So he bought some himself, including a horse-shoeing box that had been fished out of the Clinch River after the Big Barren Creek Flood of 1916. Those purchases became the seed of the Museum of Appalachia, a living history museum near Norris, Tennessee, about 20 miles north of Knoxville.

## The Weekend Collector

Irwin had deep roots here. He descended from the region's early settlers, and his ancestor James Rice built the Rice Gristmill that now stands at Norris Dam State Park. Soon after the auction he acquired his first log structure, the General Bunch House, and moved it from the county's remote New River section. He spent his weekends seeking out isolated communities across the region in search of pioneer relics, which he displayed in his yard and garage. In 1969 he officially opened the Museum of Appalachia and began charging a small admission fee. His guiding philosophy was to preserve not only buildings and artifacts but also each object's individual history: who owned it, when and how it was made or acquired, and how it was used. Those oral histories and recollections are kept in the museum's archives, along with thousands of photographs.

## A Pioneer Farm, Reassembled

From a single log building, the museum grew over four decades into a collection of more than 30 historic structures gathered onto about 65 acres of pasture and fields. The grounds display most types of buildings found on a typical pioneer Appalachian farm: cabins and barns, including a rare Appalachian cantilever barn, smokehouses, corn cribs, animal pens, mills, an underground dairy and cellar, and a loom house. Blacksmith shops, a working sawmill, a rural schoolhouse, a log church, a broom and rope shop and a leather shop are all fully equipped and occasionally used for demonstrations. One cabin carries a literary footnote: the museum calls it the Mark Twain Family Cabin, believed to be associated with the author's father, John Clemens. The museum, a Smithsonian Affiliate, also keeps one of the nation's largest folk art collections, hosts traditional Appalachian music, and draws hundreds of regional craftspeople for annual demonstrations.

## Five Thousand Four Hundred Shingles

The Arnwine Cabin, listed on the National Register of Historic Places, shows how much work a rescue can take. Built of hewn poplar logs sometime between 1795 and 1820, it originally stood along the Clinch River near the Liberty Hill community in Grainger County, and its first known occupant was John Wesley Arnwine. When the Tennessee Valley Authority acquired its 20-acre tract in the early 1930s for Norris Lake, the cabin was moved a short distance. Two of Arnwine's daughters, Polly Anne and Eliza Jane, lived in it their entire lives, Polly Anne until 1923 and Eliza Jane until 1936. After that it served for storage and fell into ruin. Irwin acquired it in 1964. The roof could not be saved, so he and two associates split 5,400 shingles from a single red oak tree. For the missing floor he found a puncheon floor of the same era at a smokehouse near Sneedville, and the stone chimney came from a ruined house of the period in the nearby Laurel Grove community, finished with a stick-and-mud section.

## Every Tool Has a Name

The two-story Display Barn holds one of the nation's largest collections of pioneer frontier relics. Among the thousands of objects are a fully stocked general store and a rural post office from Arthur, Tennessee, near Harrogate. There is an axe that may have been used at colonial Fort Loudoun, and there is the shoeing box that started it all, still telling its story of the 1916 flood. Photographs and short descriptions accompany the collection, naming each item's original owner and how it was used. The museum has also served as a stage. WBIR-TV taped several episodes of its documentary program The Heartland Series here, and the final show was recorded at the museum on August 8, 2009, before an estimated 10,000 people, one of the largest crowds in the museum's history.

## Flight Context

The Museum of Appalachia is at about 36.18°N, 84.07°W near Norris, Tennessee, roughly 20 miles north of Knoxville in the ridge-and-valley country around the town of Norris. Look for the open pastures and clustered log buildings on the museum's roughly 65 acres. A viewing altitude of 3,000-4,000 feet MSL works well. Nearest airports: Colonel Tommy C. Stiner Airfield (Campbell County Airport) in Jacksboro (KJAU), about 10 nm north-northwest; Knoxville Downtown Island Airport (KDKX), about 16 nm southeast; and McGhee Tyson Airport (KTYS), about 23 nm south. Valley fog is common on autumn mornings, so late morning often gives the clearest view.
