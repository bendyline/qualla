# Coal Creek War

In 1891 and 1892, coal miners in Tennessee's Coal Creek valley took up arms against the state's convict-leasing system, freeing hundreds of leased prisoners, burning stockades and besieging a militia fort — and helped bring the practice to an end.

**Location:** 36.2064, -84.1222

**Categories:** Coal Wars, Labor disputes in Tennessee, Penal labor in the United States, Mining in Tennessee, Anderson County, Tennessee

## Introduction

"Here, as elsewhere," the labor leader Eugene Debs said in 1899, "the convicts, themselves brutally treated, were used as a means of dragging the whole mine-working class down to their crime-cursed condition." He was talking about what he called the "Tennessee tragedy," still fresh in public memory. In 1891, mine owners in the Coal Creek watershed of Anderson County began replacing their paid miners with convicts leased from the state prison system. The miners answered with rifles and fire. Over the next two years they freed hundreds of prisoners and burned the stockades that held them, in what one historian calls "one of the most dramatic and significant episodes in all American labor history."

## Prisoners for Rent

Convict leasing grew out of the years after the Civil War. The "Black Codes," modeled on the South's old slave codes, used broad vagrancy laws to target freed African Americans for mass arrest, and after their enactment in 1865 the share of African Americans in Tennessee's prison population jumped, by one account, from 52 to 75 percent. From 1866 the state leased its prisoners to companies willing to pay for their housing in exchange for their labor, and in 1871 it leased them to the Tennessee Coal, Iron, and Railway Company, which subleased most of them to smaller mines.

Post-war railroads had opened the coalfields, and the Coal Creek valley became one of Tennessee's most lucrative mining regions. The town of Coal Creek (renamed Lake City in 1936 and Rocky Top in 2014) grew to about 3,000 people by the end of the 1870s. The companies profited, while miners struggled and began organizing in the 1880s. Owners answered every union threat with the same warning: organize, and we will bring in convicts.

## The Night at Briceville

In 1890, Tennessee elected several members of the labor-friendly Farmers' Alliance, among them Governor John P. Buchanan, and the Coal Creek miners pressed their demands: pay in cash instead of company scrip, and their own checkweighmen to weigh the coal that determined their earnings. State law already barred scrip pay and company-hired checkweighmen, and most owners agreed. The Tennessee Coal Mining Company at Briceville refused. It shut down on April 1, 1891, and when its miners rejected an iron-clad contract, it reopened on July 5 with leased convicts, tearing down miners' houses to build a stockade for them.

On the night of July 14, with rumors that more convicts would arrive the next day, about 300 armed miners surrounded the Briceville stockade. The guards surrendered without a fight. The miners marched the convicts to Coal Creek, put them on a train, and sent them to Knoxville.

## Stockades in Flames

The miners telegraphed Governor Buchanan, saying they were defending their property and wages. On July 16 he arrived with three militia companies and led the convicts back to Briceville. The legislature debated for three weeks and adjourned on September 21, having done little beyond making it a felony to interfere with the leasing system and authorizing the governor to do whatever was necessary to protect it.

On October 31, miners burned the Briceville stockade and seized the one at Coal Creek. More than 300 convicts were freed and given fresh food and civilian clothes by the insurgents, who urged them not to commit further crimes. Two days later another band burned the stockade at Oliver Springs, freeing 153 more. Under a truce, convicts returned to Coal Creek and Oliver Springs, though not Briceville. To guard them, the state sent 84 militiamen under J. Keller Anderson, who built Fort Anderson on what came to be called Militia Hill, overlooking Coal Creek, and armed it with a Gatling gun.

## Siege on Militia Hill

Resentment burned on through 1892. On August 17, miners led by John Hatmaker attacked the stockade at Oliver Springs; beaten back at first, they returned in greater numbers until the guards surrendered, and the convicts were sent by train to Nashville. The next day the miners captured Anderson at Coal Creek and demanded that his second-in-command, Lieutenant Perry Fyffe, surrender the fort. Fyffe refused. The miners charged, killing two militiamen, but could not take the position.

Buchanan sent 583 militiamen under General Samuel T. Carnes and ordered county sheriffs to raise posses, an order most ignored or carried out half-heartedly. A group of Knoxville volunteers marching to relieve the fort was ambushed as it descended Walden Ridge, and press reports said two of them were killed. Carnes arrived on August 19, restored order and won Anderson's release, then swept the region from Coal Creek to Jellico, arresting hundreds of miners and largely ending the war.

## What the Miners Won

The miners lost the battles, but the conflict turned convict leasing into a public controversy. The national attention helped bring down Governor Buchanan, and when the lease contracts expired in 1896, Tennessee refused to renew them, making it one of the first states in the South to end the practice.

The war lived on in music. The banjo tune "Coal Creek March," recorded by Kentucky banjoist Pete Steele for the Library of Congress in 1938, is still popular among old-time musicians, and the Grand Ole Opry pioneer Uncle Dave Macon based his song "Buddy Won't You Roll Down the Line" on the conflict. Behind the tunes lie two sets of people squeezed by the same system: miners fighting to protect their wages, and the leased prisoners themselves, drawn from a prison system that laws like the Black Codes had filled increasingly with African Americans.

## Flight Context

The coordinates, 36.2064°N, 84.1222°W, mark the former town of Coal Creek (renamed Lake City in 1936 and Rocky Top in 2014) in northern Anderson County, Tennessee, where Coal Creek cuts through Walden Ridge at the edge of the Cumberland Plateau, north of Clinton. From the air, the water gap in the long ridge is the key landmark; the mining valley ran between Coal Creek and Briceville. Terrain rises sharply west of the gap, so plan a 3,000 ft AGL pass or higher. Nearby airports: Colonel Tommy C. Stiner Airfield (Campbell County Airport), Jacksboro (KJAU), about 8 nm north-northwest; Knoxville Downtown Island Airport (KDKX), about 19 nm southeast; and McGhee Tyson Airport, Knoxville (KTYS), about 25 nm south-southeast. Morning fog often fills the valleys, and summer afternoons bring thunderstorms along the plateau edge.
