# Clinton, Tennessee

A Clinch River county seat first named for Aaron Burr, Clinton lent its name to the Manhattan Project and, in 1956, became the place where twelve Black students desegregated a state-supported public high school, widely described as the first in the South.

**Location:** 36.1048, -84.1285

**Categories:** Cities in Anderson County, Tennessee, County seats in Tennessee, School desegregation in the United States, Manhattan Project, Cities in Knoxville metropolitan area

## Introduction

For its first eight years, this Clinch River town was called Burrville, in honor of Aaron Burr, Thomas Jefferson's first-term vice president. Then Burr's name became a liability. In 1809, with the disgrace of the Burr–Hamilton duel that killed Alexander Hamilton still attached to him, the Tennessee legislature renamed the town Clinton. The county seat of Anderson County, home to 10,056 people at the 2020 census, has a habit of turning up in national history: its name went onto a secret wartime project, and in 1956 twelve Black students at its high school drew the nation's attention to the fight over desegregation.

## From Burrville to Clinton

People lived along the Clinch long before the town. Excavations in the 1930s and 1950s, ahead of the construction of Norris Dam and Melton Hill Dam, found Woodland and Mississippian period sites, including two substantial Woodland villages at Bull Bluff and Freels Bend, about 20 miles downstream. By the mid-18th century the land was part of a vast territory claimed by the Cherokee. The 1791 Treaty of Holston was meant to keep Euro-American settlers out, but settlers kept coming from Virginia and North Carolina, and their push onto Cherokee land brought skirmishes that eased after the Treaty of Tellico in 1798.

Burrville was founded in 1801 as the seat of the new Anderson County. When it was renamed on November 8, 1809, the new name most likely honored George Clinton, one of Burr's New York political rivals and the sitting vice president (some accounts instead credit his nephew DeWitt Clinton). Clinton had succeeded Burr in 1805 and went on to serve under James Madison, becoming the first vice president to serve under two presidents and the first to die in office.

## Coal, Socks, Pearls and Uranium

Coal changed the town. With the industry booming nearby, Clinton grew from about 325 people in 1874 to 1,198 in 1890, and in the 1890s a Southern Railway depot went up. The Southern Hotel housed rail passengers until it burned in 1908, along with most of Depot Street.

Clinton's first large factory, Magnet Knitting Mills, began making men's socks in 1906 with fewer than 60 workers. By 1916 it employed more than 500 and was planning to make women's silk hosiery. The river had its own industry: the Clinch at Clinton was known for freshwater pearls. That ended when the Tennessee Valley Authority's Melton Hill Dam raised the river, and the warmer water disrupted the mussels' habitat.

The strangest chapter came during World War II, when the Manhattan Project site in Tennessee was officially named the Clinton Engineer Works, after the town. Also known by the name of its largest township, Oak Ridge, it began about three miles southwest of Clinton and stretched some ten miles toward Kingston, covering roughly 58,900 acres. The works produced the enriched uranium used in the 1945 bombing of Hiroshima, as well as the first examples of plutonium made in a reactor.

## Twelve Students

After the U.S. Supreme Court's decision in Brown v. Board of Education, a court order required Clinton High School to desegregate. Until then, Black teenagers in Clinton had been expected to travel to Knoxville for high school; the town's segregation-era Green McAdoo School served only Black elementary-age children. On August 27, 1956, twelve African American students, remembered as the Clinton 12, attended classes at Clinton High School for the first time. They are widely described as the first African Americans to desegregate a state-supported public school in the South.

The first day passed without incident. Within days, segregationists including John Kasper and Asa Carter were rallying the town's white citizens. Riots broke out in early September, and Governor Frank G. Clement stationed National Guard units in Clinton throughout September. Sporadic violence and threats continued for two more years, culminating in the bombing of Clinton High School on October 5, 1958. With an influx of outside aid, the school was quickly rebuilt. Today the old Green McAdoo School houses a museum dedicated to the crisis, the Green McAdoo Cultural Center.

## Back to the River

Clinton is turning toward the water again. After the state began building a new bridge to carry US 25W across the Clinch, city officials and developers met in 2018 to plan a redevelopment of downtown and the waterfront, centered on the 7-acre site of the former Magnet Mills, with 1,400 feet of river frontage. Proposals included a riverfront esplanade and multi-story buildings with homes above shops and restaurants.

South of downtown, Aspire, a 370-acre park proposed by the local Hollingsworth Foundation on land it owns along with the TVA, opened in October 2024 after five years of construction. It has 22 miles of hiking and biking trails, a mountain bike circuit, meadows and shade gardens, a military monument, a boat launch, and a restaurant called The Pearl.

## Flight Context

Clinton sits at 36.1048°N, 84.1285°W on the Clinch River in Anderson County, Tennessee, about 15 nm northwest of Knoxville. From the air, look for the town where the river, part of Melton Hill Lake here, bends sharply around Lost Ridge; Interstate 75 passes northeast of downtown, and Walden Ridge, the eastern escarpment of the Cumberland Plateau, rises to the northwest. The long, narrow ridges of the Appalachian Ridge and Valley run southwest to northeast around the town. A 2,500–3,500 ft AGL pass shows the river, the downtown and the Aspire park lands to the south. Oak Ridge National Laboratory and the other Department of Energy facilities lie a few miles to the southwest; check charts and NOTAMs for restrictions there. Nearby airports: Knoxville Downtown Island Airport (KDKX), about 15 nm southeast; McGhee Tyson Airport, Knoxville (KTYS), about 19 nm south-southeast; and Colonel Tommy C. Stiner Airfield (Campbell County Airport), Jacksboro (KJAU), about 14 nm north.
