# Norris Dam

The Tennessee Valley Authority's first major project, Norris Dam tamed the Clinch River and drew admirers from around the world, while displacing thousands of families from the valleys beneath its lake.

**Location:** 36.2242, -84.0914

**Categories:** Tennessee Valley Authority dams, New Deal in Tennessee, Hydroelectric power plants in Tennessee, Historic Civil Engineering Landmarks, Dams in Tennessee

## Introduction

Jean-Paul Sartre came to see it. So did Le Corbusier, Jesse Owens, Ernie Pyle, Jawaharlal Nehru, and both President Franklin D. Roosevelt and Eleanor Roosevelt, on separate visits. In 1941 the Museum of Modern Art in New York mounted a photography exhibit about it. The object of all this attention was a concrete wall across the Clinch River in the hills north of Knoxville, Tennessee. Norris Dam was the first major project of the Tennessee Valley Authority, the agency created in 1933 to bring economic development to the region and to control the flooding that had long plagued the Tennessee Valley. It was named for Senator George Norris of Nebraska, a longtime champion of government-owned utilities and of TVA in particular.

## Two Decades of Argument

The site was spotted early. As far back as 1911, the place then called the Cove Creek site was identified as a prime location for a sizable dam. Government and private interests alike believed that a dam in the upper Tennessee Valley, working with dams at Muscle Shoals, Alabama, could bring badly needed flood control to East Tennessee and keep the Tennessee River navigable year-round. In the early 1920s the Tennessee Electric Power Company, the Knoxville Power & Light Company and Union Carbide all applied for permits to build at Cove Creek, but every application was withdrawn or rejected. Senator Norris was part of the opposition. He argued that a private owner would care almost entirely about power rather than flood control and coordination with other projects, and he proposed a network of dams to regulate the valley's outflow toward the lower Mississippi. Through the late 1920s the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers proposed dams here too, only to see them rejected by Congress or vetoed by President Calvin Coolidge.

## Learning on the Job

When TVA took up the project, it renamed Cove Creek for Senator Norris. The young agency had no engineering or dam-building experience, so it leaned heavily on the Army Corps' original design and drew ample advice from the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation. Construction began on October 1, 1933, and the Civilian Conservation Corps pitched in, building recreational facilities and helping to remove structures from the land that would flood. The gates closed on March 4, 1936, and the first generator went online that July 28. The result is a straight concrete gravity dam 1,860 feet long and 265 feet high, built for $36 million, with two generating units whose capacity is quoted at between 126 and 132 megawatts, depending on the rating used. Norris Freeway, a section of U.S. 441 widened in the 1930s to help with construction, still runs across its top. Though it was the first dam TVA built, it is not the oldest the agency owns; TVA later bought the Tennessee Electric Power Company's assets, including some older dams.

## The Valleys Beneath

Progress came at a steep human price. Building the dam and reservoir required buying more than 152,000 acres of land. Some 2,841 families were relocated, along with 5,226 graves. The community of Loyston, about 20 miles upstream, disappeared entirely beneath the water, as did the town of Big Barren and the settlement of Baker's Forge. Roughly a third of Caryville, at the head of the Cove Creek embayment, was flooded, and a number of its buildings had to be moved. Union County suffered most, losing Loyston and other small communities of its Big Valley region. Families there had been promised electricity by the project's end, but for many it did not arrive for a decade or more. For some of the displaced, the loss of the only way of life they had known was more than they could bear. A local history records at least one farmer who took his own life after selling his land.

## A Lake of Many Arms

The Clinch River flows southwest for 300 miles from its headwaters in Virginia through the rugged hills of northeastern Tennessee to the Tennessee River near Kingston. Norris Dam stands just over 79 miles above the Clinch's mouth, immediately below its confluence with Cove Creek. Behind it, Norris Lake reaches 73 miles up the Clinch and across the lower 56 miles of the Powell River, spreading into five counties. With 33,840 acres of water and 809 miles of shoreline, it is the largest reservoir on any tributary of the Tennessee River. Small supplemental dams at Caryville and Big Ridge keep Cove Lake and Big Ridge Lake full year-round for recreation. Much of the lower lake is ringed by conservation land, including Norris Dam State Park, the Cove Creek Wildlife Management Area and the Chuck Swan State Forest. In 2016 the dam and its infrastructure were listed on the National Register of Historic Places.

## Flight Context

Norris Dam spans the Clinch River at about 36.224°N, 84.091°W, on the line between Anderson and Campbell counties roughly 20 miles north of Knoxville, Tennessee. The long, straight concrete dam with U.S. 441 running across its crest is easy to spot, with the many-armed Norris Lake spreading behind it to the northeast and Melton Hill Lake beginning below it. A viewing altitude of about 3,500-4,500 feet MSL gives a good view of both the dam and the reservoir. Nearest airports: Colonel Tommy C. Stiner Airfield in Jacksboro (KJAU), about 8 nm north-northwest; Knoxville Downtown Island Airport (KDKX), about 19 nm southeast; and McGhee Tyson Airport (KTYS), about 25 nm south. Morning fog can linger over the lake and river valleys.
