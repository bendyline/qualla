# Museum of Salt and Pepper Shakers

A Belgian archaeologist's collection of more than 20,000 pairs of salt and pepper shakers, displayed in Gatlinburg as a record of how everyday design, and everyday life, have changed.

**Location:** 35.7160, -83.5025

**Categories:** Museums in Sevier County, Tennessee, History museums in Tennessee, Great Smoky Mountains, Gatlinburg, Tennessee, 2002 establishments in Tennessee, Museums established in 2002

## Introduction

"All my effort is to show the 20s, the 40s, the 60s," Andrea Ludden once explained, "how civilization can change, you see it through the salt and pepper shakers." It is an archaeologist's way of looking at a kitchen table. Ludden, a Belgian-born archaeologist who died in 2015, turned that habit of mind on the humblest pair of containers in the house, and the result, which her children now run, fills a museum in Gatlinburg, Tennessee: more than 20,000 pairs of salt and pepper shakers from all over the world.

## It Began with a Pepper Mill

The collection started small. In 1984 Ludden began collecting pepper mills, and before long salt and pepper shakers had taken over as the focus. Her stated goal was to show the creativity of all the artists who had turned an everyday item into a myriad of shapes and materials. In 2002 she opened the museum in Cosby, Tennessee, before it settled in its present home in Gatlinburg. Pepper mills still have their place, too: the museum includes more than 1,500 of them, a nod to where the collecting began. Eight years after the first museum opened, in May 2010, Ludden added a sister: the Museo de Saleros y Pimenteros in El Castell de Guadalest, in Alicante, Spain, which displays another 20,000 pairs. For its opening she was interviewed on the Valencian TV station Canal Nou.

## Cows, Cats and Delft

Walking the galleries feels a little like browsing the cupboards of a thousand different households. Display cases sort the shakers by theme: Christmas, Plastic, Americana, Amish, Wood, Vegetables, Transportation, Chefs, Foods, Drinks, Chickens, Cows, Cats and Dogs, Wild Animals, Marine, Characters, Souvenirs, Asian, Glass, Metals and Delft. Within those groups they are arranged by material and color, and when a particular set was made in more than one color, the museum shows every version. The effect is part art gallery, part catalog of taste across the decades. What surprises visitors and reviewers alike is that, once the variety has been revealed, they can never take a shaker for granted again.

## Before the Shaker

The museum also answers a question most diners never think to ask: why shakers at all? A section devoted to the Morton Salt Company explains how the shaker came to be, thanks to Morton's invention that kept salt from clumping together. Before that, salt was kept in salt cellars, and examples of those are in the collection too. It is the educational half of a place that sets out to entertain and teach at once. And in a touch that suits a mountain town full of travelers, the museum is dog-friendly. The place has a way of getting noticed. The museum appeared in 2006 on the Food Network's Unwrapped with Marc Summers, was mentioned in a skit on Late Night With Jimmy Fallon on July 18, 2009, and turned up in Smithsonian Magazine online in 2012 and The Guardian in 2015. The two museums are regulars on lists of the strangest and most unusual museums, in outlets from Travel and Leisure and CNN Travel to El Mundo and Vanity Fair.

## Flight Context

The museum is in Gatlinburg, Tennessee, at 35.7160°N, 83.5025°W, in the valley at the northern edge of the Great Smoky Mountains. The best visual marker in town is the 407-foot Gatlinburg Space Needle, about 1 nm west-southwest of the museum, and the Ober Mountain aerial tramway climbs from downtown toward the ski area about 3 miles to the west. With the Great Smoky Mountains rising just to the south, plan a viewing altitude of at least 4,000-4,500 feet MSL and stay alert to the rising ridges to the south. Gatlinburg-Pigeon Forge Airport (KGKT) is about 9 nm north in Sevierville, and McGhee Tyson Airport (KTYS) in Knoxville/Maryville is about 25 nm west-northwest. Haze and low cloud frequently settle over the Smokies, and winds off the ridges can bring turbulence at low altitude.
