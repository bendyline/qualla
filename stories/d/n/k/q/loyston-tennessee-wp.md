# Loyston, Tennessee

A small, relatively prosperous crossroads town in Tennessee's Big Valley that the Tennessee Valley Authority surveyed, photographed and then drowned beneath the reservoir behind Norris Dam in 1936.

**Location:** 36.2654, -83.9499

**Categories:** Geography of Union County, Tennessee, Tennessee Valley Authority, Former populated places in Tennessee, Submerged places in the United States, Destroyed populated places, Populated places inundated by the Tennessee Valley Authority

## Introduction

One Loyston family needed twenty-five trips. Agents from the Tennessee Valley Authority and the University of Tennessee's agricultural extension service would load them up and drive them out to look at a farm for sale somewhere in the region, and the family would decline, and the car would go out again. It took twenty-five of those drives before they found a place that satisfied them. The land they were leaving was better than most in the Norris Basin, by TVA's own surveys, and it was about to disappear under the waters of the Clinch River, backed up behind the new Norris Dam.

## Loy's Crossroads

The town began with a foundry. John Loy built it in the early 19th century, and the community that grew up around it went by "Loy" or "Loy's Crossroads." The crossroads was a real one: State Highway 61 met a major local road here, near where Mill Creek empties into the Clinch at river mile 98. Mill Creek had carved a broad valley called Big Valley, with Lone Mountain on its south side and Big Ridge on its east, and settlers had been drawn to this country for a long time. In the 1780s the German immigrant Henry Sharp built a frontier fort called Sharp's Station on the slopes of Big Ridge, overlooking the Clinch east of Loyston, in an area long hunters had scouted a decade before. When a post office opened in 1866 it took the name "Loy's Cross Roads", and in 1894 the name was shortened to Loyston.

## A Town Worth Counting

By the early 1930s Loyston was home to about 70 people and served as a trading center for the farmers around it. It had a post office, two general stores, a filling station, a cafe, a mill and a barbershop, and a Delco Light system supplied electricity to its businesses and a few of its houses. Knoxville was a 30-mile drive down Highway 61. The TVA studied the place closely before flooding it, and its surveys found that while most people in the Norris Basin lived in relative poverty, Loyston's residents fared better because of their better farmland. The Stooksbury general store took in $50,000 a year in sales and cleared a $7,000 profit. Homes ranged from primitive two-room log cabins to eight-room frame houses, most of them heated by fireplaces and some by coal-burning stoves. Children attended Loyston Elementary School or the nearby one-room Oakdale School. Sharp's descendants were still prominent in the community, and the Sharp's Station Methodist Episcopal Church stood just outside town.

## Photographed, Then Flooded

The TVA did not only measure Loyston. It recorded it. Along with extensive sociological surveys of the residents, the community was documented by the photographer Lewis Hine, so the town's last years survive in pictures. Then came the dam. Loyston's people were forced out by eminent domain, and as Norris Dam was completed in 1936, the waters of the Clinch River rose over the crossroads. Many of the displaced families tried to hold their community together by founding New Loyston in the hills a few miles to the south. A significant number moved to Knoxville, and others scattered to nearby Anderson County or to Blount and Loudon counties south of the city. Even the park that now borders the lake has a Loyston connection: Civilian Conservation Corps Company 4495, originally based in Loyston, began work on Big Ridge State Park in October 1934.

## Loyston Sea

The name did not drown with the town. Where the Clinch River and Mill Creek now meet, a mile-wide stretch of the reservoir is known as Loyston Sea. An inlet runs south from it for just over a mile to a knob called Loyston Overlook, and a recreation area along the Mill Creek embayment carries the town's name as well. Almost nothing physical is left. In 2008 the Museum of Appalachia accepted the donation of a children's playhouse that originally stood in Loyston and was said to be the last intact building from the community. It is a small thing to stand for a whole town: a child's house that outlasted everything around it.

## Flight Context

The site of Loyston lies beneath Norris Lake at 36.2654°N, 83.9499°W in Union County, Tennessee; in its day the town was a 30-mile drive from Knoxville. Look for Loyston Sea, the mile-wide stretch of water where the Mill Creek arm meets the main Clinch River channel, with the long crest of Big Ridge and the shoreline of Big Ridge State Park just to the south and east. A narrow inlet running south from the open water toward Loyston Overlook helps confirm the spot. A viewing altitude of about 2,500-3,000 feet MSL shows the drowned valley and the ridges that frame it. Colonel Tommy C. Stiner Airfield at Jacksboro (KJAU) is about 11 nm west-northwest, Knoxville Downtown Island Airport (KDKX) about 19 nm south-southeast, and McGhee Tyson Airport (KTYS) about 27 nm south. Morning fog can linger over the lake and in the valleys of this Ridge-and-Valley country.
