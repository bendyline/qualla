# University of Tennessee Anthropological Research Facility

Behind a razor-wire fence in Knoxville, the University of Tennessee's Body Farm studies how human bodies decompose, knowledge that has helped investigators pin down times of death in real criminal cases.

**Location:** 35.9403, -83.9423

**Categories:** University of Tennessee, Forensic anthropology, Forensic entomology, Biological anthropology organizations, Knoxville, Tennessee

## Introduction

It started with a cow. In 1966, while teaching at the University of Kansas, anthropologist William M. Bass was asked whether it was possible to tell how long ago a decomposed cow had died. He concluded that more research was needed to answer it, and suggested a way to get there: let a dead cow decompose in a field and watch what happened. The idea stayed with him. Years later, at the University of Tennessee, it became the Anthropological Research Facility, better known as the Body Farm, the first facility created for the study of how human remains decompose. It sits a few miles from downtown Knoxville, behind the University of Tennessee Medical Center.

## Two and a Half Acres Behind the Fence

The facility is a wooded plot of roughly 2.5 acres (published accounts range from two to three), surrounded by razor wire. Inside, human bodies are placed in different settings and left to decompose, exposed in a variety of ways so researchers can learn how decomposition changes under varying conditions. Some placements re-create the scenes investigators actually encounter, such as a car accident that goes unseen for days, or a murder victim buried in a shallow grave. The work has grown considerably. The number of exposed bodies rose from about 20 in 2003 to around 150 in 2007. The facility is part of the Forensic Anthropology Center, which Bass established in 1987, and its research has driven many advances in determining the postmortem interval, the time that has passed since death.

## Subject 1-81

By the early 1980s, Bass had headed the University of Tennessee's anthropology department for a little over a decade and had been appointed Tennessee's first state forensic anthropologist, the official called on to determine cause and time of death. During the 1970s, donated and unclaimed bodies had been macerated at an abandoned farm the university owned. In the fall of 1980, Bass and a small group of students began building a dedicated facility, and on May 15, 1981, it received its first donated research subject, a 73-year-old man who had died of emphysema and heart disease. For confidentiality he was labeled "1-81," the number of the body followed by the year it arrived, a convention Bass kept for donors while reversing it for his real-world cases. That first subject yielded information on the four stages of human decomposition. In the facility's first year, graduate student Bill Rodriguez observed the bodies every day, noting which insects were present, how the bodies changed and when, work that became his doctoral thesis.

## What the Flies Knew

Bass and his students did not keep their knowledge behind the fence. Law enforcement agencies brought them real cases. In one, case "91-23," a car had been set on fire near the Tennessee border with a body inside. Bone examinations and other forensic work allowed Bass's team to identify the victim, but the timing of the death remained a mystery. The answer came from flies. Two graduate students collected maggots and puparia at the scene and brought them back to the Body Farm, where testing showed that the puparia appeared to have been charred by the same fire that burned the car. In other words, the man had been dead in his car for almost an entire blowfly life cycle, roughly two weeks, before the fire was set. That gave investigators a far more accurate time of death and helped point them in the right direction.

## Counting Degree Days

The science has served the defense as well as the prosecution. Alan Gell had been convicted in North Carolina in 1998 of murdering Allen Ray Jenkins, who was found dead in his home in Aulander in April 1995. When Gell was granted an appeal, his attorney, Mary Pollard, needed to establish when Jenkins had actually died. She turned to Murray Marks, a forensic anthropologist and decomposition specialist who worked at the Body Farm under Bass. Marks used a measure called degree days, which accounts for daily temperatures, together with crime scene photographs of maggots, to work out a range of dates in which the death could have occurred. At his 2004 retrial, a jury acquitted Gell of the killing.

## A Final Gift

Everything at the Body Farm depends on people willing to give their bodies to research, and donation comes with firm guidelines. Donors complete a donation packet in advance, with consent forms and a biological questionnaire. After its time at the facility, a donated body is accessioned into the university's Donated Skeletal Collection, which supports research and training. The program does not accept a donor who had any infection at the time of death or a communicable disease such as HIV, hepatitis A, B or C, tuberculosis, or an antibiotic-resistant infection such as MRSA.

## Flight Context

The Anthropological Research Facility is at about 35.940°N, 83.942°W, a few miles from downtown Knoxville, Tennessee, behind the University of Tennessee Medical Center. The facility itself is a small fenced wooded plot and is not a sightseeing target; the medical center complex is the landmark. The site lies close to Knoxville Downtown Island Airport and within the busy airspace around McGhee Tyson Airport, so a transit altitude of about 3,000-3,500 feet MSL with appropriate ATC coordination is sensible. Nearest airports: Knoxville Downtown Island Airport (KDKX), about 4 nm east-northeast, and McGhee Tyson Airport (KTYS), about 8 nm south-southwest. Haze is common over the Tennessee Valley in summer.
