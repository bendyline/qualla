# Women's Basketball Hall of Fame

Crowned by the world's largest basketball, Knoxville's Women's Basketball Hall of Fame keeps the stories of barnstorming Red Heads, the nearly unbeatable Edmonton Grads, flying Texas champions and the Olympians who followed them.

**Location:** 35.9631, -83.9108

**Categories:** Basketball museums and halls of fame, Women's basketball in the United States, Women's halls of fame, Museums in Knoxville, Tennessee, Sports in Knoxville, Tennessee

## Introduction

A basketball reported to be 30 feet tall and 10 tons sits on the north rotunda of a building in Knoxville, Tennessee. The hall bills it as the world's largest, and it marks the only facility of its kind dedicated to every level of women's basketball. The Women's Basketball Hall of Fame opened here in 1999, in a city known for its large women's basketball following and home to the University of Tennessee's Lady Vols, once coached by Pat Summitt, a member of the hall's first class of inductees. Inside are stories far older than the building: barnstorming exhibition teams, a Canadian club that almost never lost, and a Texas college team that flew to its games.

## Honoring the Past, Promoting the Future

Inductees are chosen as coaches, veteran coaches, players, international players, veteran players, contributors and officials, and after the Class of 2026 was inducted on June 27, 2026, the hall's website counts 214 Hall of Famers. Their achievements fill the Hall of Honor. At the entrance, the Pat Summitt Rotunda remembers the founding board member and Class of 1999 inductee, opening onto a courtyard shaped like a basketball and paved with personalized, engraved bricks.

Visitors can play, too. Three courts in the north rotunda represent the hall's mission of "honoring the past, celebrating the present, and promoting the future" of the game, with a timed dribbling course, a passing area, and a photo spot for posing as players from different eras. At the entrance stands the Berenson, a 17-foot bronze statue by sculptor Elizabeth MacQueen, named for Senda Berenson, the physical education teacher credited with adapting basketball for women in 1892; some sources still call it the Eastman Statue. Each year's inductees receive a smaller copy of it.

## Thirty-Eight Women from Edmonton

Among the game's trailblazers, few can match the Edmonton Commercial Graduates. The club began in 1915, when players from the McDougall Commercial Girls High School team in Edmonton, Canada, graduated and persuaded their coach, John Percy Page, to keep them together. Membership was exclusive: only 38 women were ever official members of the Grads, beginning with first captain Winnie Martin (Tait), who played from 1915 to 1924.

Over 25 years the Grads played in Canada, the United States and Europe. Official records credit them with 522 games and a 502–20 record, though one biographer's research puts the total nearer 412 games, with the same 20 losses. Page coached them to Canadian championships from 1922 until their last in May 1940, 18 by the Hall's count, and at the Olympic Games of 1924, 1928, 1932 and 1936 they won four unofficial Olympic titles. Short of money, members often chipped in to fund their national play. Their last game, on June 5, 1940, was against a Chicago team at Edmonton Arena; the Hall records it as a 62–52 win. Dr. James A. Naismith admired not only their record but their "unbroken record of good sportsmanship."

## Red Heads on the Road

For 50 years, from 1936 to 1986, the All American Red Heads crisscrossed the continent, billed as the longest-running women's professional team. C. M. Olson, who had coached and owned a men's exhibition team called Olson's Terrible Swedes, founded the Red Heads in Cassville, Missouri, with his wife, Doyle, and the women who worked in her beauty salons. In the mid-1950s (1954 by the Hall's account, 1955 by another) coach Orwell Moore and his wife, Lorene "Butch" Moore, bought the team and moved it to Caraway, Arkansas. Lorene played more than a decade, in over 2,000 games, and scored 35,426 points.

The Red Heads, who regularly took on men's teams, grew so popular that between 1964 and 1971 as many as three squads may have been touring at once. They played in all 50 states, Mexico, Canada and the Philippines and appeared in Life, Look and Sports Illustrated before Moore disbanded them in 1986. The players have held reunions in the years since.

## Flying Queens and Mighty Macs

In 1948 Claude Hutcherson, a Wayland Baptist graduate who owned an air service, flew the school's women's team to games in Mexico. By 1950 he was sponsoring them outright, and the Hutcherson Flying Queens traveled by private plane, played on athletic scholarships and warmed up with showy drills learned from the Harlem Globetrotters. From November 1953 to March 1958 they won 131 straight games, collecting four consecutive AAU national championships, before losing 46–42 to Nashville Business College.

In the 1970s the college game changed. Immaculata's Mighty Macs won the first three national championships of the Association for Intercollegiate Athletics for Women, from 1972 to 1974, and were among the first women's teams on national television and at Madison Square Garden; their story became the 2011 film The Mighty Macs. Delta State ended their reign with a 28–0 season in 1975 and won three straight titles, going 93–4 with a then-record 51 consecutive wins. Those teams produced two Hall of Famers, Lusia Harris Stewart and Margaret Wade, whose name is carried by the Wade Trophy, often called the Heisman of women's basketball.

## Silver, Then Gold

The 1976 U.S. Olympic team won silver, the country's first Olympic medal in women's basketball, and it produced 11 Hall of Fame inductees, among them Ann Meyers Drysdale, Nancy Lieberman, Lusia Harris Stewart and Pat Summitt. Twenty years later, the 1996 team reclaimed Olympic gold in Atlanta and finished 60–0 overall, counting a 52–0 pre-Olympic run that began after training opened on October 2, 1995. More popular than any women's basketball team before it, the squad is credited with drawing a record 202,556 fans during the Games, an average of 25,320 a game, and the Hall counts ten inductees among its players and coaches, among them Lisa Leslie, Sheryl Swoopes, Dawn Staley and Teresa Edwards.

## Flight Context

The Hall of Fame is at 35.9631°N, 83.9108°W in Knoxville, Tennessee, close to downtown and the Tennessee River. From the air, look for the giant basketball on the building's north rotunda. Knoxville Downtown Island Airport (KDKX) lies only about 2 nm east, so expect pattern traffic nearby; McGhee Tyson Airport (KTYS), about 10 nm south-southwest, is Knoxville's main airport, and the downtown area lies within or close to its Class C airspace, so check the sectional and contact approach before overflying. A 1,500–2,500 ft AGL pass works well where airspace allows. Expect summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms; fall and spring usually bring the clearest days.
