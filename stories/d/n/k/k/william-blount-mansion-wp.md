# William Blount Mansion

The clapboard home of territorial governor William Blount served as the de facto capitol of the Southwest Territory, was reputedly where much of Tennessee's first constitution was drafted, and was nearly torn down for a parking lot.

**Location:** 35.9611, -83.9152

**Categories:** National Historic Landmarks in Tennessee, Historic house museums in Tennessee, Houses in Knoxville, Tennessee, Daughters of the American Revolution museums, Southwest Territory

## Introduction

Tennessee's state librarian and archivist, John Trotwood Moore, once called it "the most important historical spot in Tennessee." Yet by 1925 the house had deteriorated, and a local developer, B. H. Sprankle, intended to demolish it for a parking lot serving the new Andrew Johnson Hotel, then under construction in downtown Knoxville. The building in his way was the Blount Mansion, home of William Blount, the only territorial governor of the Southwest Territory, a signer of the U.S. Constitution, and later a U.S. Senator from Tennessee. From this house, and from the small office behind it, Blount had governed a frontier territory. It took five years, a publicity campaign and a new association to save it.

## A Capital in the Making

Knoxville was brand new when Blount arrived. After he selected White's Fort as the site of the territorial capital, James White, who owned the fort and the land around it, and his son-in-law Charles McClung laid out a grid of 64 half-acre lots and in 1791 held a lottery for buyers. Construction of Blount's house began around March 1792, when he relocated to Knoxville; while it was going up, the family may have lived in a cabin on Barbara Hill, the rise University of Tennessee students now call "The Hill." Once finished, the mansion served as the de facto capitol of the Southwest Territory. Blount lived there with his family and with enslaved African Americans, people held in bondage in the household of a man now counted among the nation's Founding Fathers. The museum notes that documentation is fragmentary, but a 1797 deed transfer lists twenty-six African Americans enslaved to Blount and his family.

## Where Tennessee Was Written

The most consequential room on the property is not in the house at all. Blount's office, often called the Governor's Office, was built along with the mansion and stands behind its west wing as a separate one-story building. From it he governed the territory and ran his business affairs. It is a modest frame-and-clapboard structure with a brick chimney on its south side, a pair of sash windows with louvered blinds along its east and west walls, wide-board paneling and a carved mantel. In 1796, as the territory prepared to become a state, much of the Tennessee Constitution is said to have been drafted here, in Governor Blount's office. Blount presided over that year's constitutional convention.

## Clapboard Among the Cabins

Most homes in Tennessee at the time were log cabins, which made Blount's house stand out. It is a wood-frame building sheathed in clapboard, with a two-story central block and one-story wings to the east and west. The frame was probably cut from local timber, but the finished woodwork, paneling, flooring and weatherboarding were shipped from North Carolina. The main block holds a parlor, a dining room and a hallway with a quarter-turn staircase downstairs, with two bedrooms above. The house grew in stages. The central block is the oldest part. The west wing, which holds a single bedroom, came next, and archaeologists suspect it began as an outbuilding, possibly servants' quarters, that was moved and attached to the house. The east wing, with its drawing room and a basement reached by stairs and a trap door, came last, perhaps as late as 1820, two decades after Blount's death in 1800.

## The Kitchen That Came Home

Even the kitchen has a story of loss and recovery. It began as a detached building behind the east wing. At some unknown date it was taken apart, and its materials were reused to build a kitchen attached to the rear of the house. Then, in 1958, archaeologists found the foundation of the original kitchen. The attached kitchen was dismantled in turn, and its materials were used to rebuild the detached kitchen on its first foundation. Today it echoes the design of Blount's office, standing once more behind the east wing where it began.

## Saved From the Pavement

The fight against the parking lot began quickly. The Blount Mansion Association was chartered in 1926, and Mary Boyce Temple, the Bonny Kate Chapter of the Daughters of the American Revolution and the East Tennessee Historical Society led a massive publicity campaign. By 1930 the association had raised enough to buy the house. It has kept the mansion as a museum ever since, making numerous renovations to return the house and grounds to their late 18th-century appearance. On January 12, 1965, Blount Mansion was designated a National Historic Landmark.

## Flight Context

Blount Mansion stands at 200 West Hill Avenue in downtown Knoxville, Tennessee, at about 35.961°N, 83.915°W. The house itself is small; use downtown Knoxville's buildings as the reference. Knoxville Downtown Island Airport (KDKX) is only about 2 nm east, and McGhee Tyson Airport (KTYS) is about 10 nm south-southwest, so the site lies within busy terminal airspace; a transit altitude of about 3,000-3,500 feet MSL with ATC coordination is appropriate.
