# Kituwa

Plowed for corn for generations after the Cherokee were forced out, the ancient mound and town site of Kituwa beside the Tuckasegee River has been reclaimed by the Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians, who hold it sacred as their people's original town.

**Location:** 35.4389, -83.4011

**Categories:** Cherokee towns in North Carolina, South Appalachian Mississippian culture, Archaeological sites on the National Register of Historic Places in North Carolina, Religious places of the Indigenous peoples of North America, Communities of the Great Smoky Mountains

## Introduction

In 1996 the Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians bought 309 acres beside the Tuckasegee River in the Great Smoky Mountains of North Carolina. By the end of its years in private hands, the mound stood within a property called Ferguson's Field. For the Cherokee, the place was something else: Kituwa, also spelled Kituwah or Keetoowah, the town they claim as their people's original home and one of the "seven mother towns" of their traditional homeland in the American Southeast. At its center rises a low earthwork platform mound, built by about 1000 CE. After the Cherokee were forced from these valleys, farmers plowed it again and again for corn. Now the people who hold it sacred have chosen to leave it undeveloped.

## A Mound at the Center

Kituwa lies along the Tuckasegee River, near its confluence with the Oconaluftee, on the site of Woodland period villages. The platform mound was raised during the era of the South Appalachian Mississippian culture, a regional variation of the Mississippian cultures whose chiefdoms were linked by trade networks reaching from the Gulf Coast to the Great Lakes. In North Carolina, unlike some Midwestern centers with whole complexes of mounds, most major towns had a single platform mound, with smaller villages growing up around the mother towns. Archaeologists believe the people of the smaller chiefdoms were eventually absorbed by the developing Catawba and Cherokee peoples. On top of the mound, the historic Cherokee built a townhouse, or council house, where the community gathered and reached decisions by consensus, and they rebuilt it repeatedly over decades. At the time of European contact, they regularly burned the vegetation on the mound for agricultural use, and perhaps also as a ritual to keep the mound visible and free of trees.

## The People of the Mother Town

The people of Kituwa, the Ani-kitu-hwagi, influenced the Cherokee towns along the Tuckasegee and Oconaluftee rivers, known as the Out Towns, and the Middle Towns along the upper Little Tennessee and its tributaries. The whole region's people came to be called Kituwah after their mother town. Because they were seen as guardians of the Cherokee's northern border against raids by Iroquois nations from New York and by various Algonquian peoples, the name Kituwah gradually became synonymous with Cherokee itself. It has traveled far. Since the mid-19th century, "Keetoowah" has been linked with Cherokee people who supported a kind of religious nationalism and adhered to pre-contact communal ways, and descendants of Cherokee who migrated west in the 1810s and 1820s later formed the United Keetoowah Band, a federally recognized tribe based in Oklahoma.

## Lost and Plowed

By 1819 the Cherokee had lost control of the Kituwa area, forced into treaties and land cessions to the United States. In the late 1830s the federal government forcibly removed most Cherokee in the Southeast, and they walked overland on the Trail of Tears to Indian Territory. European Americans took over the land and repeatedly plowed the mound and village area to grow corn. The mound is still visible today, but it is much shorter than it stood in Cherokee times. Some Cherokee remained in North Carolina, and their descendants organized and won federal recognition in 1868 as the Eastern Band of Cherokee, based in Western North Carolina and the only federally recognized tribe in the state. In 1973 Kituwa was added to the National Register of Historic Places for its historic and archaeological significance.

## Coming Home

After the Eastern Band's 1996 purchase, an archaeological survey in 1997 turned up thousands of artifacts, evidence of thousands of years of human habitation, including an early 18th-century Cherokee town that covered 65 acres. At first some tribal members wanted to develop the property for community uses. Later, non-invasive surveys with a gradiometer revealed 15 burials, and because it was Cherokee custom to bury people in the village where they lived, there may be as many as 1,000. The tribe decided to leave the sacred site undeveloped. That resolve was tested when Duke Energy announced plans for a substation, or tie station, near the mound, part of a transmission upgrade one local paper estimated at $79 million, a dispute that came to a head in 2010. Both Swain County and the Eastern Cherokee opposed it. On February 4, 2010, the Tribal Council resolved that it was the tribe's "solemn responsibility and moral duty to care for and protect all of Kituwah from further desecration and degradation by human agency." Swain County called for a 90-day moratorium in March, Duke agreed to halt the construction threatening Kituwa for 90 days while a complaint was heard, and in July the county required Duke to consult local stakeholders on its projects. Duke later agreed to move the tie station to another site.

## Carrying the Fire

Cherokee oral traditions say all Cherokee settled at Kituwa after migrating from the Great Lakes region as early as 4,000 years ago. Cultural and archaeological evidence supports the account of a migration, though scholars have not agreed on when the people reached the Southeast. One ancient dance may have been practiced here. During the Green Corn Ceremony, dancers shout ye-lu-le, "to the center," and move toward the fire at the middle of the circle, a dance that symbolizes the sharing out of the sacred fire given to the people in their ancient legends. In traditional Cherokee society, home fires in outlying communities were put out before the ceremonies. Coals of the new fire were carried to all the Cherokee to kindle each town's ceremonial fire before any new corn could be eaten, and the home fires were relit from them.

## Flight Context

Kituwa lies near 35.4389°N, 83.4011°W along the Tuckasegee River, about three miles east of Bryson City in Swain County, North Carolina. From the air, follow the Tuckasegee valley and look for open, flat bottomland along the river; the mound itself is low and hard to distinguish, and the site is sacred ground, so observe it from a respectful altitude. Surrounding ridges of the Great Smoky Mountains rise steeply, so a viewing altitude of 5,500-6,500 feet MSL is sensible. Nearest airports: Jackson County Airport near Sylva (FAA identifier 24A, no ICAO code listed), about 12 nm southeast; Macon County Airport in Franklin (FAA identifier 1A5, no ICAO code listed), about 13 nm south; Western Carolina Regional Airport in Andrews (KRHP), about 27 nm west-southwest; and Asheville Regional Airport (KAVL), about 42 nm east. Expect mountain weather, with valley fog common in the mornings.
