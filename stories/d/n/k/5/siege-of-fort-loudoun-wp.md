# Siege of Fort Loudoun

In 1760 Cherokee warriors besieged the British fort that had been built on their land as a symbol of alliance, starving its garrison into a surrender that ended in bloodshed.

**Location:** 35.7825, -84.2597

**Categories:** Battles of the French and Indian War, Battles involving the Cherokee, Colonial American and Indian wars, Battles in Tennessee, Conflicts in 1760

## Introduction

In the summer of 1760, the only people who could pass freely in and out of Fort Loudoun were women. Several soldiers of the besieged garrison were married to Cherokee women, and the warriors surrounding the fort let those wives come and go rather than start feuds with their families. So the women smuggled in food, though never enough to save anyone. The siege of Fort Loudoun was fought from March to August 1760 beside the Little Tennessee River, in what is now Tennessee, during the Anglo-Cherokee War. Its roots lay in an alliance that, for a time, had served both sides.

## A Fort Built on a Promise

During the French and Indian War, the British courted the Cherokee as allies against the French. The Cherokee supplied warriors; in return the British promised supplies and protection for the warriors' homelands. Fort Loudoun, begun in 1756 by South Carolina provincial troops and British regulars, was part of that bargain: a trading post for manufactured goods, guns and powder, and a refuge for Cherokee women and children while the men were away fighting.

Named for John Campbell, 4th Earl of Loudoun, the British commander in chief in North America, it stood on high ground on the south side of the Little Tennessee, about five miles below the Cherokee capital of Chota. German engineer William Gerard de Brahm designed its diamond of palisaded earthen walls, with a gun-mounted bastion at each corner. By the summer of 1757 some 210 soldiers garrisoned it, their families had moved in, and a small community took shape nearly 200 miles from the nearest white settlements.

## How the Alliance Came Apart

The partnership frayed quickly. The Cherokee felt their service in the 1758 British expedition against Fort Duquesne went unappreciated. On the Virginia frontier, some Cherokee warriors killed at least one settler and tried to pass off the scalps as enemies' for a reward. The British disarmed them and sent them home, and some Cherokee who had played no part, bitter at the treatment, raided settlements on the way back. Virginia settlers then killed, scalped and mutilated about 20 Cherokee and collected bounties on them. Retaliation followed retaliation.

In 1759 South Carolina governor William Henry Lyttelton declared war and cut off gunpowder the Cherokee needed for their winter hunts. When a peace delegation of moderate chiefs came to negotiate, the chiefs were taken hostage and confined at Fort Prince George in a room big enough for only six people. In February 1760 the Cherokee attacked that fort to free the hostages still held there, killing its commander. The garrison under his successor then killed the hostages, whether in revenge or, by another account, in a struggle after they were ordered into irons.

## Hunger Behind the Palisades

At Fort Loudoun, the warriors under Ostenaco fired on the walls for a few days, then stopped to save ammunition and settled into starving the garrison out. Inside, Captain Paul Demeré commanded. He had replaced his brother Raymond, the fort's first commander, who had been friendly with the Cherokee, and Paul's overbearing nature had only worsened relations. In January he had reported that his supplies could last just four months.

With messengers killed or captured and no colonist willing to try, Demeré turned to an enslaved African American man named Abram, promising him freedom if he could carry dispatches through the Cherokee lines and across the mountains to South Carolina. Abram made the harrowing passage several times, and South Carolina's Commons House freed him in 1761.

Relief never came. A British army of roughly 1,200 to 1,600 men under Archibald Montgomerie burned Cherokee Lower Towns including Keowee, but on June 27 it was stopped at the battle of Echoee and forced to withdraw. By August the garrison was eating its horses.

## Surrender on the Little Tennessee

On August 6 the officers agreed to seek terms. The Cherokee refused to deal with Demeré, but Captain John Stuart, well liked among them, reached an agreement with the chiefs at Chota: the garrison could keep its arms and enough ammunition to reach the colony, leaving the rest to the Cherokee. The soldiers marched out on August 9. Inside, the Cherokee discovered ten bags of powder and ball buried and the cannon and small arms thrown into the river, a breach that led some to hold a secret council.

The next morning, the Cherokee escort drifted away and perhaps 700 warriors attacked. About two dozen soldiers or more, including every officer except Stuart, and three women were killed, and 120 or more were taken prisoner. By most accounts Demeré was tortured and beaten until he died. Stuart survived because a Cherokee woman, likely his wife Susannah, ran among the soldiers under fire to shield him.

## Burned Towns and a Journal

The heaviest price for the Cherokee came the next year. Jeffrey Amherst, the British commander in North America, sent a force counted at roughly 2,000 to 2,800 men under James Grant. On June 10, 1761, about 1,000 Cherokee warriors met Grant's force near Echoee and fought until their ammunition ran out. Grant's troops then burned fifteen Middle Towns and their crops; one account says they were ordered to kill any Cherokee they captured. Some 4,000 to 5,000 people from the Middle Towns were left homeless and starving, and in August 1761 the Cherokee sued for peace.

That November, peace was also concluded with the Overhill towns, and Lieutenant Henry Timberlake was sent among the villages to consolidate it. His journal and map later proved invaluable to the explorers and settlers of Tennessee. John Stuart became Royal Superintendent of Indian Affairs for the Southern Districts in 1762.

## Flight Context

The story's reference point is 35.7825°N, 84.2597°W, near Tellico Dam and Lenoir City, Tennessee, where the Little Tennessee River joins the Tennessee. The fort itself stood roughly 11–12 nm to the south-southeast, near Vonore, at about 35.596°N, 84.204°W; the site was raised and the fort reconstructed as a state park on the shore of Tellico Lake, which flooded Chota and other Overhill Cherokee town sites.
