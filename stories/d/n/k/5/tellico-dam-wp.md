# Tellico Dam

The Tennessee Valley Authority's last dam pitted a small fish, a valley of farmers and the sacred towns of the Overhill Cherokee against a planned city that never came — and ended up before the U.S. Supreme Court and a president's desk.

**Location:** 35.7779, -84.2598

**Categories:** Tennessee Valley Authority dams, Dams on the Little Tennessee River, United States environmental case law, Cherokee history, Controversies in the United States

## Introduction

On August 12, 1973, a group of students led by University of Tennessee biology professor David Etnier slipped into the Little Tennessee River to look for endangered species. Construction of Tellico Dam was already underway. At the Coytee Springs shoal, Etnier spotted several small fish he later said he "knew nobody had ever seen before." They were snail darters, and within five years that discovery would carry the Tennessee Valley Authority's last dam to the Supreme Court of the United States, where a 6–3 majority ruled that finishing it would be illegal. The dam was finished anyway. How that happened is a story about fish, farms, sacred ground and politics.

## A City on Paper

The TVA had eyed a dam on the Little Tennessee as early as 1936, when it studied sites for its plan to develop the Tennessee River, but an early version was canceled on October 20, 1942, for lack of federal funding during World War II. When the idea returned, it had an unusual purpose. Unlike the agency's earlier dams, built for hydroelectric power and flood control, Tellico was meant primarily as an economic development and tourism project.

Its centerpiece was Timberlake, a city to be built from scratch for a projected 42,000 people in a rural region struggling economically. The plan was named for Henry Timberlake, who had explored the Cherokee villages that once occupied the valley. Congress lent support and the aerospace manufacturer Boeing invested, but a 1974 state bill to incorporate the area failed, Boeing judged the project economically unfeasible and withdrew in 1975, and the city never materialized. Critics branded the dam pork barrel.

## The Valley Pushes Back

TVA officials expected little more than token opposition when the Tellico Project was revealed to the public as early as 1960. They misjudged the valley. In 1963 landowners and businesspeople formed the Fort Loudoun Association to fight it. On September 22, 1964, some 400 residents packed a public forum at Greenback High School, and more than 90 percent reported strong opposition; the mood turned hostile. A month later a larger group, the Association for the Preservation of the Little Tennessee River, took shape.

Many owners saw the "taking lines" TVA personnel provided, marking how much private land would be acquired, as the agency overreaching its authority, and historians of the project suggest that most TVA staff did not understand the complexity of the communities they were intruding into. Construction began on March 7, 1967. In the end 330 farms along the river were lost, much of the land farmed by the same families for generations, and TVA spent $25.5 million on land acquisition.

## A Fish Before the Justices

Etnier's snail darter changed the fight. Environmental groups sued, and in the 1978 case Tennessee Valley Authority v. Hill, the Supreme Court affirmed the injunction halting the dam. Citing the explicit wording of the Endangered Species Act, the Court wrote that "it is clear that TVA's proposed operation of the dam will have precisely the opposite effect, namely the eradication of an endangered species." The litigation delayed completion by more than two years. Efforts to move the fish did not always go well: the New York Times reported the death of nearly 100 snail darters during an October 1977 translocation.

Congress found another route, attaching a rider exempting Tellico from the act to an unrelated public works appropriations bill. President Jimmy Carter had publicly opposed completing the dam, but on September 25, 1979, he signed the bill. Administration officials speculated that a veto would have invited legislative retaliation against his Panama Canal treaty plans and his proposed federal education department.

## What the Water Covered

Three Cherokee individuals and two Cherokee bands and organizations sued in 1979 to stop the flooding of their sacred homeland, in Sequoyah v. Tennessee Valley Authority. They lost. On November 29, 1979, the gates closed and Tellico Reservoir began to fill.

Archaeologists carried out surveys and salvage excavations in some areas, but the water rose over the sites of Tanasi, the origin of Tennessee's name, and Chota, Toqua, Tomotley, Citico, Mialoquo and Tuskegee. Some had been occupied by ancestors of the Cherokee for up to 1,000 years, their centers marked by earthen platform mounds built by people of the South Appalachian Mississippian culture, where the Cherokee later raised their councilhouses. Older sites reaching back to the Archaic period went under too, including Icehouse Bottom, along with the town of Morganton and its port. The site of colonial Fort Loudoun was raised and the fort reconstructed as a state park. Many displaced landowners could not qualify to bid on their own former land.

## Golf Courses and a Survivor

Most of the land around the new lakeshore, originally seized through eminent domain, was sold to private developers for retirement-oriented golf resort communities. Tellico Village opened in March 1986, Rarity Bay followed in 1995, and in 2002 the TVA board approved selling land on the eastern shore for a $750 million golf-course community.

The snail darter outlasted its river. Remnant populations were moved to other streams, mainly the Hiwassee River, where they were established by 1982; the Holston, French Broad and Nolichucky rivers also became habitats. The species was reclassified from endangered to threatened in July 1984 and removed from the federal list entirely on November 4, 2022.

As of 2022, Tellico was still the last dam built by the TVA. By the 1970s, the era of American dam-building had effectively ended, and the Tellico case stands as a marker of how attitudes changed.

## Flight Context

Tellico Dam is at 35.7779°N, 84.2598°W near Lenoir City, Tennessee, close to where the Little Tennessee River meets the Tennessee. From the air, look for the concrete dam and long earthen embankment at the northern end of Tellico Lake, with Fort Loudoun Dam and Lake nearby. The reservoir winds south and southeast toward the reconstructed Fort Loudoun near Vonore, about 11–12 nm away, and over the drowned Overhill Cherokee town sites. A 2,500–3,500 ft AGL cruise down the lake shows the golf-course communities on its shores. Nearby airports: McGhee Tyson Airport, Knoxville (KTYS), about 13 nm east (mind its Class C airspace and approach traffic), and Monroe County Airport, Madisonville (KMNV), about 15 nm south-southwest. Morning fog is common over the reservoirs in cool seasons.
