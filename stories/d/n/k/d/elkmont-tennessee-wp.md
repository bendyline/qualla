# Elkmont, Tennessee

A Smoky Mountains valley that was a pioneer settlement, a logging company town and a retreat for Knoxville's elite — and where the idea for Great Smoky Mountains National Park first took hold.

**Location:** 35.6537, -83.5804

**Categories:** Communities of the Great Smoky Mountains, Ghost towns in Tennessee, Historic districts on the National Register of Historic Places in Tennessee, National Register of Historic Places in Great Smoky Mountains National Park, Logging in Tennessee

## Introduction

When two justices of the U.S. Supreme Court came up the trail to Lem Ownby's cabin, he would not let them in. Their host, the prominent Knoxville lawyer Foster Arnett, wanted to introduce Harry Blackmun and Potter Stewart to a real mountain man. He knocked and announced his distinguished guests, and Ownby simply replied that they were not welcome in the cabin. The justices loved it, because here was someone who would tell them no, and the story reportedly made the rounds among the justices for years. Ownby had been born near Jakes Creek in 1889, in a valley called Elkmont that would be a pioneer settlement, a logging town and a resort before becoming part of a national park.

## Little River Country

Elkmont occupies a narrow but relatively flat valley in Sevier County, where Jakes Creek meets the Little River. Steep ridges close it in: Meigs Mountain to the west, Sugarland Mountain to the east, Cove Mountain to the north, and Blanket Mountain to the south, with the slopes of Kuwohi, the peak formerly called Clingmans Dome, and Mount Collins beyond. The Little River begins as a trickle on Kuwohi about five miles above Elkmont and gathers strength from Fish Camp Prong, Rough Creek and Meigs Post Prong before it reaches the valley.

The first settlers here called their community simply Little River. They grew corn and apples, kept bees for honey, and built gristmills along Jakes Creek. Two structures survive from that era: the Levi Trentham cabin of about 1830, among Elkmont's oldest, and the Avent cabin of about 1850, built by the Ownby family and later used by Nashville artist Mayna Avent as her studio. In 1908 Lem Ownby and his father built the cabin where he would live for the rest of his life.

## Shay Engines on the River

Industry arrived by water first. In the 1880s Knoxville businessman John L. English built splash dams along the Little River, opening their floodgates so the rushing torrent carried logs downstream. His venture had folded by 1900, possibly because of a disastrous flood in 1899.

Then came Colonel Wilson B. Townsend, a Pennsylvania entrepreneur who bought 86,000 acres along the river around 1900 and incorporated the Little River Lumber Company in 1901. Instead of trusting volatile mountain streams, he built a logging railroad from his band saw mill in Tuckaleechee Cove, where a town would later bear his name, all the way to Three Forks. Ten Shay engines hauled log-filled flatcars down the valley. In 1908 the company established Elkmont as its base, and as cutting moved higher, workers' shanties were loaded onto flatcars and set onto new foundations by crane. By the time the company stopped in 1939, it had produced 750 million board feet of lumber.

## Society Hill and Millionaires' Row

Even as the saws ran, Elkmont was becoming a getaway. In 1910 the company began selling plots to hunting and fishing enthusiasts from Knoxville, who formed the Appalachian Club just south of the logging town. Membership proved remarkably difficult to obtain, so several rejected Knoxvillians bought the Wonderland Park Hotel, built in 1912 on a hill overlooking the valley, and in 1919 formed a rival, the Wonderland Club.

Over the next two decades both became elite retreats for East Tennessee's wealthy. The Appalachian Club section divided into Daisy Town, Society Hill along Jakes Creek, and Millionaires' Row along the Little River. Most cottages went up between 1910 and 1930, typically balloon-framed with board-and-batten siding. Some, including the Addicks and Mayo cabins, are believed to be remodeled lumber company shanties. A children's playhouse carried the name "Adamless Eden."

## Birthplace of a Park

In 1920, after a visit to Yellowstone, Willis P. Davis and his wife Anne, who owned a summer cottage at Elkmont, began suggesting a national park in the Smokies. Another cottage owner, David C. Chapman, took the initiative, and in 1925 he hosted a group of Tennessee legislators at Elkmont to promote the idea. The following year Townsend made the initial sale of 76,000 acres to the new Great Smoky Mountains Park Commission.

Elkmont also bred fierce resistance. Jim Wright, an attorney for the lumber company, rallied attorneys, businessmen and mountaineers to push for a national forest instead. Largely because of him, the first land-purchase bill exempted Elkmont from eminent domain, and cottage owners won the right to sell at half price in exchange for lifetime leases. Lem Ownby, who died in 1984, was the last of the park's lifetime lessees outside Cades Cove.

## Chimneys in the Forest

Most Appalachian Club members later converted their lifetime leases to 20-year terms, renewed in 1952 and 1972. Under pressure from environmental groups, especially the Sierra Club, the 1992 renewal did not happen, and the hotel and nearly all the cottages reverted to the National Park Service; the last leases ran until 2001. The park's 1982 plan called for removing every structure, but in 1994 the Wonderland Hotel and several cottages were listed on the National Register of Historic Places, and debate followed.

The hotel collapsed in 2005, and its surviving annex burned on April 19, 2016. In 2009 the park agreed to preserve 19 of the 74 remaining structures, the Appalachian Clubhouse, rebuilt in 1934, and 18 others; the other 55 were to be documented and removed, with the demolition of 29 of them scheduled for completion in 2017. Many of their chimneys still stand among the trees, near a campground built in the 1960s on the site of the old logging town.

## Flight Context

Elkmont lies at 35.6537°N, 83.5804°W in the upper Little River valley of Great Smoky Mountains National Park, Sevier County, Tennessee. From the air, look for the campground clearing where Jakes Creek meets the Little River, walled in by Sugarland Mountain to the east and Meigs Mountain to the west; Kuwohi (6,643 ft), the highest point in Tennessee, rises to the south. Terrain rises steeply on every side, so plan to stay at least 2,000 ft above the highest nearby ridges and consult the sectional for the national park's requested minimum overflight altitude. Nearby airports: Gatlinburg-Pigeon Forge Airport, Sevierville (KGKT), about 12.5 nm north-northeast, and McGhee Tyson Airport, Knoxville (KTYS), about 22 nm west-northwest. The Smokies earn their name: haze, low clouds and fast-building afternoon storms are common, and ceilings can obscure the high ridges when valleys are clear.
