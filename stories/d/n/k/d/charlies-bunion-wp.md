# Charlies Bunion

A rare bare-rock summit on the crest of the Great Smoky Mountains, Charlies Bunion was stripped by a 1925 fire and a 1929 cloudburst, then named, by most accounts, for a mountain guide's swollen foot.

**Location:** 35.6389, -83.3722

**Categories:** Mountains of Great Smoky Mountains National Park, Mountains on the Appalachian Trail, Protected areas of Sevier County, Tennessee, Protected areas of Swain County, North Carolina, Mountains of Swain County, North Carolina, Mountains of Sevier County, Tennessee

## Introduction

A few days after a cloudburst tore across the Great Smoky Mountains in 1929, Horace Kephart led a party of mountaineers up to the crest to inspect the damage. On the newly barren western flank of the Sawteeth, one of the group, a mountain guide from Oconaluftee named Charlie Conner, took off his shoe and revealed a badly swollen foot. Kephart is said to have looked at it and promised, "I'm going to get this put on a Government map for you." Whether or not it happened quite that way, the name made it onto the map. Today that knob of bare rock on the state line between Tennessee and North Carolina is known to Appalachian Trail hikers as Charlies Bunion.

## Two Tellings of a Name

Like many good mountain stories, this one comes in more than one version. In his book The Names and Lore of the Great Smoky Mountains, Paul M. Fink gives a slightly different account. As he tells it, someone in the party looked at the bare rock pinnacle the storm had left behind and, knowing about Conner's foot trouble, remarked, "Well that sticks out like Charlie's bunion," and Kephart settled on the name. Even the bunion itself is disputed: in a 1948 newspaper interview, Conner said he had no true bunion, only ingrown toenails he jokingly called one. Either way, the joke stuck. The United States Geological Survey adopted "Charlie's Bunion" shortly afterward. Kephart received a tribute of his own: the mountain rising just to the west of the Bunion was named for him.

## Fire, Then Flood

The rock was not always bare. Like most of the central crest of the Smokies, Charlies Bunion was fairly heavily forested until the 20th century. Large-scale logging in the Oconaluftee valley changed that, leaving piles of dry, dead brushwood scattered along the streambeds above Smokemont. In 1925 a massive forest fire fed on that debris, raced up Kephart Prong, and spread across the Sawteeth and over into Tennessee. The heat was so intense that it scorched every plant in its path and left the soil sterile. Four years later, in 1929, a cloudburst dumped torrential rain on the mountains and washed the scalded soil off the western flank of the Sawteeth. What remained was the stark, rocky face that makes the Bunion one of the few bare-rock summits in the Smokies.

## More Than Half a Billion Years in the Making

The stone revealed by the flood is ancient. Charlies Bunion forms the western wing of the Sawteeth, a series of narrow, jagged cliffs linking the high peaks of the Central Smokies with those of the Eastern Smokies. Beneath them lies the Anakeesta Formation, a Precambrian metamorphic rock of the Ocoee Supergroup made mostly of slate. It formed from ancient ocean sediments (the Park Service dates the Smokies' Proterozoic sedimentary rocks to roughly 800 to 545 million years ago) and was thrust upward during the Appalachian orogeny, when the North American and African plates collided between about 310 and 245 million years ago. The mountain shows two very different faces. Its steep northern side, drained by the headwaters of the Middle Fork of the Little Pigeon River, drops 2,000 feet in just over a mile. The gentler southern slope feeds the Oconaluftee River through Kephart Prong and Bradley Fork.

## Seven Miles a Day

Before the 1900s, few people other than the occasional surveyor or naturalist ventured onto the Sawteeth. Kephart described this part of the Smokies as a 40-mile stretch of wilderness "so rough that you could not make seven miles a day in it to save your life." The main route over the crest was the old Indian Gap Trail, which crossed at Indian Gap, about two miles west of Newfound Gap, on the way toward Kuwohi; the highway over Newfound Gap, now U.S. Route 441, replaced it in 1932. There was another way across, though. Travelers could climb Kephart Prong to Dry Sluice Gap, on the eastern flank of Charlies Bunion, and descend into the Porters Creek valley. Around 1800, the Whaley brothers, William and Middleton, used that route on their way from South Carolina to what is now Greenbrier.

## Four Miles From Newfound Gap

Access became far easier in 1935, when the Civilian Conservation Corps built a leg of the Appalachian Trail across the northern face of Charlies Bunion for Great Smoky Mountains National Park, which had officially opened the year before. The trail crosses the mountain's northern slope about four miles east of Newfound Gap, close enough for day hikers and rock climbers to reach with ease. The summit stands about 5,500 feet above sea level (maps and guides give figures from 5,528 to 5,565), and from it, and from the characteristic boulder-like protrusion just below, the views sweep across Tennessee and along the main ridge of the Smokies, taking in Mount Le Conte and Mount Kephart.

## Flight Context

Charlies Bunion stands at about 35.639 N, 83.372 W on the crest of the Great Smoky Mountains, on the Tennessee-North Carolina line about four miles east of Newfound Gap, at an elevation of about 5,500 feet. Look for the bare rock face and protrusion on the steep northern side of the ridge, with Mount Le Conte and Mount Kephart nearby. Terrain here is high and unforgiving, so keep at least 2,000 feet above the ridgeline, around 7,500 feet MSL or higher, and respect the national park's quiet. Gatlinburg-Pigeon Forge Airport (KGKT) is about 15 nm north-northwest, and Jackson County Airport (FAA code 24A) near Sylva, North Carolina, about 21 nm south-southeast. Expect haze, and be ready for clouds, strong winds and turbulence to build quickly over the crest.
