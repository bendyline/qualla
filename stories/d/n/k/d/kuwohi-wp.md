# Kuwohi

The highest summit in the Great Smoky Mountains, long called Clingmans Dome, has taken back its Cherokee name, Kuwohi, the 'mulberry place' where Cherokee tradition placed the council house of the White Bear.

**Location:** 35.5628, -83.4986

**Categories:** Great Smoky Mountains National Park, Mountains on the Appalachian Trail, Highest points of U.S. states, Cherokee culture, Mountains of Tennessee, Mountains of North Carolina

## Introduction

Somewhere between this summit and the headwaters of the Oconaluftee River, the Cherokee believed, lay the enchanted lake of Ataga'hi, the "Gall Place," whose waters could heal wounded bears. The mountain itself, according to a Cherokee myth recorded by the ethnologist James Mooney in the late 19th century, was home to the White Bear, great chief of all bears, and held one of the bears' council houses. The Cherokee call it Kuwahi or Kuwohi, "mulberry place." For 165 years maps called it Clingmans Dome. At 6,643 feet it is the highest mountain in Great Smoky Mountains National Park, the highest point in Tennessee and the highest point along the entire Appalachian Trail, which runs roughly 2,200 miles, and since September 2024 it once again officially carries its Cherokee name.

## A Place for Prayer

To the Cherokee, this summit was more than a landmark. When members of the Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians pressed to restore its name, their tribal resolution described Kuwahi as "the highest point in our area" and a place of significance "as it was visited by medicine people who prayed and sought guidance from the Creator regarding important matters facing our people, and then returned to our towns to give guidance and advice." American settlers arriving from elsewhere saw it differently and called it Smoky Dome. In 1789, when the North Carolina legislature ceded what is now Tennessee to the federal government, the act fixed part of the boundary along the crest of the "Great Iron or Smoky Mountains," a line that would have included this peak.

## An Argument Over Altitude

The name Clingmans Dome came out of a quarrel between two men over which mountain in the region stood tallest. Thomas L. Clingman, who explored, measured and promoted the area extensively in the 1850s, insisted that Smoky Dome was the highest. Elisha Mitchell, a professor at the University of North Carolina, argued for a peak called Black Dome. The geographer Arnold Guyot settled it, finding Black Dome about 40 feet higher. Yet it was Clingman's name that went on the map: because of the argument, Guyot renamed Smoky Dome for him in 1859. Clingman would later become a Confederate general in the Civil War. Black Dome, for its part, became Mount Mitchell.

## The Name Comes Home

In June 2022, Lavita Hill and Mary "Missy" Crowe, both members of the Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians, drafted legislation for the tribe to support renaming the mountain Kuwahi. The Tribal Council passed it during its regular session on July 14, 2022. The change was presented as a chance "to talk about the language" and to remind people that "this was Cherokee homeland." On August 2, 2022, the Buncombe County Board of Commissioners voted unanimously in support, after consulting with the Eastern Band, and the National Park Service "strongly supported" the change. The formal process ran through the United States Board on Geographic Names. The application was approved, and the change to Kuwohi, effective immediately, was announced on September 18, 2024.

## A Ramp Into the Sky

Kuwohi is the most accessible mountaintop in the national park. The seven-mile Clingmans Dome Road climbs toward the summit and is open seasonally, from April 1 through November 30. From there a short, steep trail, with a small visitor information center and park store staffed by the Great Smoky Mountains Association, passes through spruce-fir forest scarred by blowdowns and dead Fraser firs. That die-off was caused by a non-native insect, the balsam woolly adelgid. At the top waits the observation tower, completed on October 23, 1959. A spiral ramp 375 feet long rises at a 12 percent grade to a circular platform 28 feet across, where cantilevered signs point out peaks, ridges and distant cities. On hazy days the view reaches about 20 miles; on very clear days, about 100.

## Where Trails Meet

The Appalachian Trail crosses the summit just north of the tower, and in winter its 7.5-mile leg from Newfound Gap is the only way up. The nearest trail shelters are Double Spring Gap, 2.6 miles west, and Mount Collins, four miles east. Kuwohi is also the upper end of the Forney Ridge Trail to Andrews Bald and the Forney Creek Trail down toward Fontana Lake, and it marks the western end of the Mountains-to-Sea Trail, which runs all the way to North Carolina's Outer Banks. The summit has seen tragedy, too. In the early morning hours of June 12, 1946, a Boeing B-29 Superfortress crashed near the top, killing all twelve people aboard. Beneath it all lies ancient rock of the Copperhill Formation, part of the Ocoee Supergroup, sediments laid down some 560 million years ago in deep rift basins as the supercontinent Rodinia pulled apart.

## Flight Context

Kuwohi's summit is at 35.5628°N, 83.4986°W, 6,643 feet, on the Tennessee-North Carolina line along the crest of the Great Smoky Mountains. The concrete observation tower with its spiral ramp and the Clingmans Dome Road parking area below it are the key visual cues; Newfound Gap lies along the crest to the northeast. Because the summit is the highest terrain in the park, maintain at least 8,500-9,500 feet MSL for a comfortable view and check the terrain around you. Nearest airports: Gatlinburg-Pigeon Forge Airport (KGKT), about 18 nm north; Jackson County Airport near Sylva (FAA identifier 24A, no ICAO code listed), about 20 nm southeast; and McGhee Tyson Airport in Knoxville/Maryville (KTYS), about 28 nm northwest. Low clouds often obscure the peaks, and visibility ranges from about 20 miles in haze to about 100 miles on the clearest days; expect fast-changing mountain weather and turbulence near the ridgeline.
