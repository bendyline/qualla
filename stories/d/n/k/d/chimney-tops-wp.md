# Chimney Tops

A twin-pinnacled knob of bare slate in the heart of the Smokies, which the Cherokee saw as a forked antler and Sugarlands settlers saw as a pair of chimneys, and which fire has since closed to climbers.

**Location:** 35.6293, -83.4763

**Categories:** Mountains of Great Smoky Mountains National Park, Mountains of Tennessee, Natural history of the Great Smoky Mountains, Protected areas of Sevier County, Tennessee, Religious places of the Indigenous peoples of North America, Cherokee Nation (1794–1907), Mountains of Sevier County, Tennessee, Sacred mountains of the United States

## Introduction

The Cherokee looked at this mountain and saw a deer. They called it Duniskwalgunyi, "forked antler", for the two points of rock that split its crown. The families who later farmed the Sugarlands valley below looked at the same pair of points and saw something closer to home: chimneys. Local legend even held that the top of the mountain was black with soot. Both names describe the same odd silhouette, a double-capstone knob standing 4,724 feet above sea level on the eastern slope of the Sugarland Mountain massif, in the north-central Great Smoky Mountains of Tennessee. In a range known for rounded, forested summits, Chimney Tops is one of the few peaks whose top is bare rock.

## Slate Laid Bare

Most Smoky Mountain summits hide under trees. Here the rock lies open to the weather: the Anakeesta Formation, mostly slate, phyllite and metasiltstone, metamorphic rock formed when the North American and African plates collided during the mountain-building that raised the Appalachians. The setting adds to the drama. Mount Le Conte rises to the east and Mount Mingus to the southeast, and Sugarland Mountain itself runs along the west. So although the view from the summit takes in the full circle, Chimney Tops is practically walled in on three sides. From the top, climbers once looked east to Le Conte and Mount Kephart, west to Sugarland Mountain, and north down into the Sugarlands valley.

## The Gap of the Forked Antler

The mountain's Cherokee name reaches into one of the tribe's legends. In "Aganunitsi and the Uktena", a captured medicine man named Aganunitsi wins his freedom by promising to find the Uktena, a giant reptile, and take the amulet from its forehead. His quest carries him through the remote gaps and peaks of the Smokies. He goes to Duniskwalgunyi, the Gap of the Forked Antler, and on to the lake of Atagahi, and at each place he finds monstrous reptiles waiting. In the story the high country itself is dangerous and alive, and Chimney Tops is one of its landmarks, a stop on a hero's perilous search.

## An Old Road Beneath

Long before hikers came for the view, people passed along the mountain's base. The Road Prong Trail, which follows the stream of the same name at the foot of Chimney Tops, is one of the oldest trails in the Smokies. In the 18th and 19th centuries this ancient path was commonly called the Indian Gap Trail. The Oconaluftee Turnpike, authorized in 1831 and completed in 1839, ran between Indian Gap and the Oconaluftee valley, and during the Civil War Col. William Holland Thomas, a Confederate officer who served as the Eastern Cherokee's legal agent, and his legion improved the road, which ran parallel to the modern trail. On the northern side lay the Sugarlands, home to a small Appalachian community before the national park was created. Back then the valley had not yet grown back into forest, and Chimney Tops could be seen clearly from most of it, which is probably how the settlers came to give the peak its name.

## After the Fire

For years the standard way up was the Chimney Tops Trail, which starts about 6.9 miles southeast of the Sugarlands Visitor Center on Newfound Gap Road. A longer route follows the Appalachian Trail west from Newfound Gap to the Road Prong Trail, which links down to the Chimney Tops Trail. It covers twice the distance. Then came 2016. A blaze known as the Chimney Tops 2 Fire was first reported on November 23 of that year, and it became part of the 2016 Great Smoky Mountains wildfires, which forced at least 14,000 residents and visitors to evacuate, damaged or destroyed more than 2,000 buildings and, after merging with other fires, caused 14 deaths in the Gatlinburg area. Many trails in the park closed. The Chimney Tops Trail reopened in October 2017 with a new observation area that looks out at both bare points of the summit. The final stretch to the pinnacles has stayed closed since the fire. The fire's extreme heat accelerated the weathering of the exposed rock and raised the risk of mass wasting, so the soot of the old legend has become, in a way, part of the mountain's real history.

## Flight Context

Chimney Tops stands at 35.6293°N, 83.4763°W, a 4,724-foot double knob on the eastern slope of Sugarland Mountain in Great Smoky Mountains National Park, Tennessee. Look for the two bare rock points of the summit against the surrounding forest, with the bulk of Mount Le Conte to the east and Mount Mingus to the southeast; the Chimney Tops trailhead sits on Newfound Gap Road about 6.9 miles southeast of the Sugarlands Visitor Center. The surrounding ridges rise higher than the summit, so plan on about 7,500 feet MSL or higher for a safe view and keep clear of the terrain. Nearest airports: Gatlinburg-Pigeon Forge Airport (KGKT), about 14 nm north; Jackson County Airport near Sylva (FAA identifier 24A), about 23 nm southeast; and McGhee Tyson Airport in Knoxville/Maryville (KTYS), about 27 nm west-northwest. Mountain weather here changes quickly, low cloud often hides the peaks, and turbulence is common near the ridgelines.
