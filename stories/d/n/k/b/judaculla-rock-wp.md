# Judaculla Rock

A soapstone boulder beside Caney Fork Creek in western North Carolina is reported to carry more petroglyphs than any other known boulder east of the Mississippi, and the Cherokee tie it to Judaculla, the Master-of-Game of their oral history.

**Location:** 35.3010, -83.1099

**Categories:** Petroglyphs in North Carolina, Precontact history of the Cherokee, Archaeological sites on the National Register of Historic Places in North Carolina, Rock formations of the United States, Jackson County, North Carolina

## Introduction

A minimum count of the carvings on Judaculla Rock begins with 1,458 cup marks. Then come 47 curvilinear designs, ten stick-like figures, three concentric rings, three deer tracks, three claw-like imprints, a cross within a circle and a single winged shape, among others, roughly 1,548 motifs in all, pecked and incised into one slanting boulder of soapstone. The carvings crowd so densely across the upper two-thirds of the stone that it is often hard to tell one from another. No other known boulder east of the Mississippi River is reported to carry as many petroglyphs. It sits in a quiet corner of Jackson County, North Carolina, about 60 meters from Caney Fork Creek, and for the Cherokee it has never been just a rock.

## Quarry Before Canvas

Long before anyone carved pictures here, people came to Judaculla Rock for the stone itself. The boulder still bears the scars of bowl extraction. Three depressions show where stems were removed to make pipes, and three more are hollow scallops. A few smaller soapstone outcrops just upslope show definite marks of the same ancient quarrying. Archaeologists found that cultivation had badly disturbed the soils immediately around the boulder, but auger samples higher on the slope suggest intact layers survive, holding soapstone and stone fragments from bowl-making that probably date to the Late Archaic period, roughly 2000 to 1000 BCE. The carvings came afterward. Some petroglyphs sit inside the hollow scallops, which suggests they were made after quarrying at the site had stopped, a sequence also seen at Brinkley Rock in western North Carolina and at Track Rock Gap and Sprayberry Rock in northern Georgia.

## The Master-of-Game

The rock takes its name from Judaculla, also known as Tuli-cula, Juthcullah or Tsul 'Kalu, the Master-of-Game of Cherokee oral history. Cherokee accounts link him to the surrounding landscape, its landforms, rivers and towns. The boulder lies on an old trail that ran between the former Cherokee townhouse at Cullowhee, whose name means "Juthcullah's Place," and Judaculla's reputed townhouse inside Tannasee Bald, also called Tsunegûñyĭ. Most Cherokee people were forcibly removed to Indian Territory in 1838, but the tribes in Oklahoma and the one in North Carolina all continue to teach their oral history. The Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians, based today in Swain and Jackson counties, and the two Cherokee tribes in Oklahoma still regard this boulder as spiritually significant.

## A Map in Stone

What were the carvings for? Petroglyph boulders like this one are probably stylized picture maps of the terrain where they are found. Judaculla Rock appears to represent the whole landscape the Cherokee inhabited. Its position, between the Cullowhee townhouse and Judaculla's home in Tannasee Bald, echoes Cherokee stories told about other petroglyph boulders in the mountains and foothills of North Carolina and northern Georgia. Those in-between locations connect two halves of the Cherokee year: summer farming in the river floodplains, and fall and winter hunting in the uplands. The scale of Judaculla's record stands out even among such sites. Its roughly 1,548 motifs are about 3.7 times the 421 counted at Track Rock Gap, a substantial petroglyph complex in far northern Georgia, and further study is likely to reveal more about the region's history and prehistory.

## From Cornfield to Commons

The ground around the boulder was once cultivated as a cornfield. Today it rests in a man-made, bowl-shaped depression on a 0.85-acre property owned by Jackson County, surrounded by mowed grass and bordered on the west by a thicket of rivercane. Its protection came through an unusually broad partnership: the Parker family, who formerly owned the land, the North Carolina Rock Art Project, the Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians, Western Carolina University, the North Carolina Department of Cultural Resources and the Caney Fork Community Council. Together they built a semicircular, elevated viewing platform with interpretive signs, an effort held up as a textbook example of how rock art can be preserved and shared with the public for generations. In 2013 Judaculla Rock and its site were placed on the National Register of Historic Places.

## Flight Context

Judaculla Rock is at 35.301°N, 83.110°W in the Caney Fork valley of Jackson County, North Carolina, about 60 meters east of Caney Fork Creek, a branch of the Tuckasegee River. The boulder is far too small to see from altitude; use the Caney Fork and Tuckasegee River valleys as references. Surrounding ridges rise steeply, so a viewing altitude of 5,500-6,500 feet MSL is sensible. Nearest airports: Jackson County Airport near Sylva (FAA identifier 24A, no ICAO code listed), about 5 nm west; Macon County Airport in Franklin (FAA identifier 1A5, no ICAO code listed), about 16 nm west-southwest; and Asheville Regional Airport (KAVL), about 29 nm east-northeast. Mountain valleys here often fill with morning fog, and afternoon thunderstorms are common in summer.
