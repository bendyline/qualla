# New Market train wreck

On September 24, 1904, a misread order sent two Southern Railway passenger trains toward each other on a single track near New Market, Tennessee, and their collision killed at least 56 people.

**Location:** 36.0793, -83.6298

**Categories:** Railway accidents in 1904, Railway accidents and incidents in Tennessee, Accidents and incidents involving Southern Railway (U.S.), 1904 disasters in the United States, New Market, Tennessee

## Introduction

The telegraph message that reached Strawberry Plains, Tennessee, on the morning of September 24, 1904, could not have been clearer: "Number 15 has run the switch and is on the main line!" The depot staff understood exactly what it meant. The eastbound Carolina Special, carrying about 210 passengers, had just pulled out of the station, heading straight toward a westbound local on the same single track. They waved their arms and threw stones at the departing train, but no one aboard noticed as it gathered speed. Soon afterward, on a grade near New Market, the two Southern Railway trains met head-on. At least 56 passengers and crew were killed and more than 100 injured.

## An Order Misread

The line between Bristol and Knoxville was a single track, so trains traveling in opposite directions had to take turns. The westbound No. 15, a local pulled by 4-4-0 locomotive No. 1838, normally stepped aside on a siding at Hodges' Switch to let the eastbound train pass. On this day, when it stopped at Morristown, its engineer was handed special orders to pull into a siding at New Market instead. Both the conductor and the engineer signed to confirm they had read the order. The conductor later told a reporter that he had misread it. At New Market, the local should have pulled onto the side track and stopped a few hundred yards along it. It did not. The No. 15, with three cars and some 140 passengers, rolled on down the main line.

## No Way to Warn Them

The eastbound train was the No. 12 Carolina Special, running from Chattanooga to Salisbury, North Carolina, behind 4-6-0 locomotive No. 1051. It was a long train of nine cars: two mail cars, three wooden passenger coaches and four steel Pullman cars. Many of its passengers were on their way home from the Louisiana Purchase Exposition, the World's Fair in St. Louis. By the time the New Market warning reached Strawberry Plains, the Special was already leaving. One last chance remained. A telegraph was sent to Hodges' Switch, the usual passing point, in the hope of warning the trains. But no one was on duty at Hodges' Switch, and the message was never received.

## New Market Hill

The trains met on New Market Hill at 10:18 a.m. The Special had built up speed on the upgrade to about 60 miles an hour. The local, running downhill and trying to make up lost time, had reached about 70. When the crews finally saw each other, they threw on the emergency brakes, but the trains struck at a combined speed of more than 100 miles an hour, although one contemporary source put it at 70. The crash could be heard 15 miles away. Both engineers were killed. The local's locomotive and coal tender were hurled into the air, flipped upside down, and carried over the Special's engine, tender and baggage cars before landing squarely on the wooden passenger coaches. The wooden coaches were also struck from behind by the weight of the steel Pullmans. Within seven seconds, the wooden cars were, in one description, "crushed like eggshells." The sturdy Pullmans came through relatively undamaged. For many of the victims, death came quickly.

## Counting the Lost

When word reached Knoxville, a relief train was organized to carry doctors and medical supplies to the site and bring the injured back to Knoxville General Hospital. Reporters rode along, and many photographs were taken of the wreckage, where splintered timber, iron and steel lay piled across the rails among the dead. The toll is uncertain. One count puts it at 56 killed and 106 injured; the Knoxville News Sentinel's later retrospective gives 64 dead and 141 injured, and some estimates suggested the true death toll may have exceeded 100. The inquiry that followed could not determine why the No. 15 had failed to pull onto the side track at New Market. Its engineer and fireman, the two people who might have explained it, both died in the collision. One account suggests the engineer may have been asleep.

## The Fate of Two Engines

The locomotives had very different endings. No. 1838 had been built by the Schenectady Locomotive Works in 1887 as Virginia Midland Railway No. 692, renumbered 831 when the Southern took over that railroad in 1894, and renumbered again as 1838 in 1903. It was destroyed in the wreck, and its remains were collected and sold for scrap. No. 1051, a Baldwin Locomotive Works ten-wheeler built new for the Southern in 1897, was repaired after the wreck and returned to service. It kept working for the Southern Railway for more than a quarter century, until it was scrapped at Princeton, Indiana, on June 5, 1930.

## Flight Context

The wreck site on New Market Hill is near 36.079°N, 83.630°W, close to the town of New Market in Jefferson County, Tennessee, between Knoxville and Morristown. Follow the rail line through the valley near New Market; there is no large monument visible from the air, so the town and the railroad are the references. A viewing altitude of about 3,000-4,000 feet MSL suits the rolling ridge-and-valley terrain here. Nearest airports: Moore-Murrell Airport (KMOR) at Morristown, about 14 nm east-northeast; Knoxville Downtown Island Airport (KDKX), about 14 nm southwest; and Gatlinburg-Pigeon Forge Airport (KGKT), about 14 nm south-southeast. Morning fog is common in the valleys of East Tennessee.
