# Joyce Kilmer Memorial Forest

Spared by the loggers and dedicated in 1936 to the poet of "Trees," this 3,800-acre old-growth forest in the North Carolina mountains holds yellow-poplars more than 20 feet around and trees over 400 years old.

**Location:** 35.3736, -83.9756

**Categories:** Old-growth forests, Wilderness areas of North Carolina, Protected areas of Graham County, North Carolina, Protected areas established in 1936, Nantahala National Forest

## Introduction

The oldest yellow-poplars here measure more than 20 feet around and rise 100 feet overhead, and some trees in this forest have been growing for more than 400 years. Stand among them and the scale rearranges your sense of time. Joyce Kilmer Memorial Forest, 3,800 acres in Graham County, North Carolina, is one of the largest contiguous tracts of old-growth forest in the eastern United States. It became a memorial because a group of veterans wanted to honor a poet killed in World War I with the one monument that suited him best: the trees themselves.

## A Living Memorial

Joyce Kilmer was a poet and journalist who was killed during World War I. His 1913 poem "Trees," which opens "I think that I shall never see / A poem lovely as a tree," had become a popular favorite, twelve rhyming lines arguing that nothing made by human hands can match the beauty of nature. In 1934 the Bozeman Bulger Post of the Veterans of Foreign Wars in New York petitioned "that the government of the United States examine its millions of forested acres and set aside a fitting area of trees to stand for all time as a living memorial" to him. The Forest Service considered forests throughout the country before settling on an uncut tract of 3,800 acres along Little Santeetlah Creek. It was dedicated as the Joyce Kilmer Memorial Forest on July 30, 1936.

## Saved by a Dam and a Depression

That the forest was still uncut owed something to luck. Beginning in 1915, the Babcock Lumber Company of Pittsburgh ran a standard-gauge railroad through the area and logged out roughly two-thirds of the Slickrock Creek watershed. Then construction of Calderwood Dam threatened to flood the lower part of the railroad, and during the Great Depression the price of lumber fell, which also encouraged preservation of the remaining trees. The tract along Little Santeetlah Creek was never cut. In 1975 the memorial forest was joined with a much larger tract of the Nantahala National Forest to become part of the Joyce Kilmer-Slickrock Wilderness, and the U.S. Forest Service administers it today.

## Among the Cove Hardwoods

What survived is a rare example of old-growth cove hardwood forest, a diverse type found only in the Appalachian Mountains. Yellow-poplar, oak, basswood, beech and sycamore dominate the canopy. Visitors experience it on an easy two-mile trail laid out as a figure eight, and at the juncture of the two loops a memorial plaque recalls the poet whose name the forest carries. The walk is gentle, but the scale is not. Under trees that were already centuries old when Kilmer wrote his poem, the poem's argument feels less like sentiment and more like a plain description of the view.

## Ghosts of Chestnut and Hemlock

Even a protected forest changes. The American chestnut, once the dominant tree here, is gone, killed by the chestnut blight accidentally introduced from Asia in the early 20th century. The last of the Kilmer chestnuts had probably died by the late 1930s, but their wood resists rot so well that remnants of the massive logs and stumps can still be seen. A more recent loss has been the giant eastern hemlocks, killed by an infestation of an exotic insect, the hemlock woolly adelgid. Worried that falling limbs or trees could injure visitors, Forest Service managers decided to bring down dead trees near the memorial trail in a way they believed would mimic natural windthrow. In November 2010 they used explosives. The lower loop became much lighter and drier, changing the environment and creating a public relations challenge for the agency.

## Flight Context

Joyce Kilmer Memorial Forest lies at about 35.374 N, 83.976 W along Little Santeetlah Creek in Graham County, North Carolina, within the Joyce Kilmer-Slickrock Wilderness of the Nantahala National Forest. From the air it is a sea of unbroken mountain forest, so navigate by coordinates and stay well above the terrain at 3,000 feet AGL or more; wilderness areas deserve a respectful altitude to protect their quiet. Western Carolina Regional Airport (KRHP) in Andrews is about 12 nm south-southeast, and McGhee Tyson Airport (KTYS) near Knoxville, Tennessee, about 26 nm north. Mountain wave and turbulence are possible on windy days, and fog and low cloud often fill the valleys in the morning.
