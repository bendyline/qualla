# Hazel Creek (Great Smoky Mountains)

A roadless valley on the North Shore of Fontana Lake holds the traces of a century of Cherokee trails, pioneer farms, copper claims and a booming logging town, all emptied when the TVA's lake rose in 1944.

**Location:** 35.4434, -83.7574

**Categories:** Communities of the Great Smoky Mountains, Rivers of Swain County, North Carolina, Appalachian culture in North Carolina, Great Smoky Mountains National Park, Ghost towns in North Carolina, Logging history

## Introduction

Sometime around 1888, probably, a herd of cattle froze to death in a side valley off Hazel Creek, and the bones they left behind gave the place its name: Bone Valley. Names like that still cling to this stream in the southwestern Great Smoky Mountains - Proctor, Medlin, Possum Hollow - long after the people who used them moved away. Hazel Creek runs some 16 to 18 miles from near the summit of Silers Bald down to Fontana Lake, and between 1830 and 1944 its bottomlands held farms, mills and logging towns. No public road reaches it now. The valley is a backcountry campsite and historical area in Great Smoky Mountains National Park, and the way in is by boat or on foot.

## Over Ekaneetlee Gap

The Cherokee were the valley's first known permanent inhabitants, though Hazel Creek was far more thinly settled than the Overhill towns farther down the Little Tennessee. A small Cherokee village stood where Sugar Fork meets the creek, and when settlers arrived, Cherokee families were living in Possum Hollow. A well-worn Cherokee trail climbed Shehan Branch, crossed the Pinnacle and rose to Ekaneetlee Gap on the crest before dropping into Cades Cove in Tennessee. In 1829, Moses and Patience Rustin Proctor came the other way. After a brief stay in Cades Cove, they crossed at Ekaneetlee Gap and built a cabin on a hill in Possum Hollow, and they stayed on good terms with the Cherokee living farther up the hollow. Samuel and Elizabeth Cable settled downstream in 1835. In the 1850s Josiah and Sarah Bradshaw built the valley's first gristmill, and around then the creek took its name, probably from the hazelnut bushes along its banks.

## Cousins on Opposite Sides

Only a handful of families lived here by the 1860s, yet the Civil War cut deep. Like most of the North Carolina side of the Smokies, Hazel Creek's small population supported the Confederacy, which set it against its cousins over the mountain in Cades Cove, who were staunchly pro-Union. The old Cherokee route became an escape line, as Union supporters fleeing Confederate North Carolina often slipped through Hazel Creek and over Ekaneetlee Gap. Bushwhackers and vigilantes on both sides of the crest raided one another, usually for livestock and crops. Moses and Patience Proctor lost two sons fighting for the Confederacy, and a third barely made it home. After the war, Confederate veterans largely settled Bone Valley, and six of them are buried in the Bone Valley Cemetery on the hill above what is now Campsite 83.

## Copper, Lawsuits and a Librarian

In 1883, Jacob Fonslow Hall, visiting his brother, found a copper vein on Little Fork Ridge. Word reached New York entrepreneur W. S. Adams, who in 1889 set up a mining camp and widened the trail to the mouth of Hazel Creek into a wagon road. Then, in 1901, a New Orleans land speculator named George Westfeldt sued, claiming he owned the land. The case shut the mine down and ran for 27 years; Adams was declared the rightful owner, but both men were dead by then. In 1904, Horace Kephart, a librarian from St. Louis, moved into a cabin at the idle mining camp. Medlin, he wrote, "comprised two little stores built of rough planks and bearing no signs, a corn mill, and four dwellings." His 1913 book Our Southern Highlanders became the first widely read study of Southern Appalachian culture, though former resident Duane Oliver recalled that its stereotyped portrayal angered many people on the creek.

## Cove After Cove

Real industry arrived with the W. M. Ritter Lumber Company. Ritter persuaded Southern Railway to extend its line from Bushnell to the mouth of Hazel Creek, built a hub there that bore the company's name, and laid a logging railroad 16 miles up the valley, with passenger service to Proctor and Medlin. A large sawmill rose just east of Proctor, with a planing mill for furniture and drying kilns for flooring, and by 1920 Proctor had over 1,000 people. Crews cut the ridges and coves on the south side of the creek nearly to its source, with a high camp at Buckeye Gap on the crest, then worked back down the north bank, stripping bare cove after cove. By 1928, when Ritter ceased operations, it had cut 201 million board feet of lumber from the watershed. The company brought jobs and cash, but its destructive practices left long-term damage to the forest and the creek.

## The Water Closes the Road

Fontana Dam ended settled life on the creek. The Aluminum Company of America had bought 15,000 acres for a power dam, and when World War II sent aluminum demand soaring, the Tennessee Valley Authority took over and finished it in 1944. That year TVA bought out the North Shore settlements, including Hazel Creek, at prices most residents found absurdly low, and those who refused were forced out through condemnation suits. The last families left in November 1944, before the rising lake drowned the only major road out, and the land passed to the national park. A promised North Shore road stalled after 7.2 miles, earning the local nickname "the Road to Nowhere," and in 2010 Swain County accepted a $52 million settlement in its place. The walls of the Ritter sawmill still stand, and Hall Cabin, the log cabin of Jesse "Crate" Hall at the end of the Bone Valley Trail, a roughly 9-mile hike from the ferry landing, is billed as the most remote historic structure in the Smokies; sources date it anywhere from the 1880s to 1910.

## Flight Context

Hazel Creek empties into Fontana Lake at about 35.44°N, 83.76°W, in Swain County, North Carolina, on the roadless North Shore of the lake. Terrain rises steeply to the north: Welch Ridge and Jenkins Trail Ridge, which bound the watershed, run above 5,000 feet for long stretches, and the creek heads near Silers Bald on the main Smokies crest, so plan on 7,500 feet MSL or higher. Follow Fontana Lake eastward from Fontana Dam and Fontana Village (a few miles west of the creek mouth across the lake) and look for the Hazel Creek embayment on the north shore. Nearby airports: Western Carolina Regional Airport at Andrews (FAA identifier RHP, GPS code KRHP, about 16 nm), Macon County Airport at Franklin (FAA identifier 1A5, about 21 nm), McGhee Tyson Airport at Knoxville (KTYS, about 25 nm) and Gatlinburg-Pigeon Forge Airport (KGKT, about 27 nm). Mountain weather here changes quickly; low cloud and haze often hang in the valleys, so wait for a clear day.
