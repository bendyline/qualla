# Fontana Dam

Rushed into being to power wartime aluminum production, Fontana Dam in the North Carolina mountains is the tallest dam in the eastern United States, with the Appalachian Trail running across its crest and a drowned town beneath its lake.

**Location:** 35.4522, -83.8047

**Categories:** Tennessee Valley Authority dams, Dams on the Little Tennessee River, Hydroelectric power plants in North Carolina, Appalachian Trail, Great Smoky Mountains

## Introduction

Hikers on the Appalachian Trail do something unusual when they reach the southwestern edge of the Great Smoky Mountains: they walk across the top of the tallest dam in the eastern United States. Fontana Dam stands 480 feet high and 2,365 feet long on the Little Tennessee River in Swain and Graham counties, North Carolina. When it was built, only three dams in the world were taller. Behind it spreads Fontana Lake, roughly 10,000 acres of water with about 240 miles of shoreline, and beneath that water lies the town the dam was named for. Fontana was once a lumber and copper-mining hub at the mouth of Eagle Creek. Today its name belongs to the dam, and the town itself lies under the lake.

## Aluminum Dreams

Long before the federal government arrived, an aluminum company had its eye on this valley. Around 1910 the Aluminum Company of America, Alcoa, began investigating the Little Tennessee in search of water power for the massive amounts of electricity needed at its aluminum production operations in nearby Alcoa, Tennessee. In 1913 it purchased the Tallassee Power Company, now known as Tapoco, and by 1930 the two had completed Cheoah and Calderwood dams on the Little Tennessee and Santeetlah Dam on the Cheoah River. By the mid-1930s Alcoa had assessed the Fontana site and bought the first 15,000 acres needed to build there. Then, in 1935, the Tennessee Valley Authority stepped in. Concerned about the river's effect on flood control across the greater Tennessee Valley, TVA began negotiating to take over the project, and Alcoa, for its part, preferred that TVA build it.

## Built for the War

Wanting to build a dam and paying for one were different matters. TVA could not secure the federal funding it needed until war changed the equation, as emergency wartime programs called for a drastic increase in aluminum production. On August 14, 1941, TVA and Alcoa signed the Fontana Agreement. TVA gained possession of Fontana and control over the releases and output of Tapoco's dams on the Little Tennessee, and in return Alcoa was guaranteed to be the primary beneficiary of the dams' electricity for at least twenty years. Congress authorized funding on December 17, 1941, and construction began on New Year's Day, 1942. A temporary village went up just south of the site to house the people building the dam, and it later developed into the small resort of Fontana Village. The power also reached beyond the war effort, bringing electricity to what had been a rural area.

## A Lake Among the Smokies

Fontana sits 61 miles above the mouth of the Little Tennessee, at a spot where the westward-flowing river bends briefly to the south. It is a remote place. The nearest cities, Maryville, Tennessee, Bryson City and Robbinsville, are each about 30 miles away by road, and North Carolina Highway 28 provides the only major access. The Great Smoky Mountains rise to the north, and the Yellow Creek Mountains, mostly protected within the Nantahala National Forest, rise to the south. The lake fills 29 miles of the Little Tennessee, the lower 11 miles of the Tuckasegee River and roughly the lower five miles of the Nantahala River, reaching into the Nantahala Gorge. Along its north shore, rapid mountain streams tumble in, some dropping several thousand feet from the crest of the Smokies in just a few miles. Eagle Creek and Hazel Creek form substantial embayments just upstream of the dam. Downstream, Fontana is the first of five dams on the river, followed by Cheoah, Calderwood, Chilhowee and Tellico; three of them, Cheoah, Chilhowee and Tellico, carry the names of historic Cherokee towns.

## The Road to Nowhere

The lake swallowed more than a town. It also flooded most of North Carolina Highway 288, the road between Deals Gap and Bryson City. In the 1940s the National Park Service, having taken possession of the north shore tracts, agreed with Swain County to build a replacement road. By 1972 only 7.2 miles were finished. Funding was scarce, and people had grown more aware of what such construction could do to the land. Environmental concerns halted the work entirely in the 1970s, and locals nicknamed the unfinished stretch "The Road to Nowhere." In 2000, U.S. Senator Jesse Helms of North Carolina secured funding, and the Park Service carried out its first environmental impact study of the project. Published in 2007, it predicted "major, adverse, long-term impacts to topography, geology, and soils," though road supporters argued those concerns were exaggerated. That same year Swain County accepted a $52 million cash settlement in place of the road. The final installment, $35.2 million, arrived in June 2018. Under a 2010 agreement the money sits in an account with the state treasury, and the county can spend only the interest it earns.

## A Dam That Grows

Fontana is still a working giant. Its three generators have a combined capacity of roughly 240 to 300 megawatts, depending on how it is measured, it can hold 513,965 acre-feet of floodwater, and the lake's surface rises and falls by 57 feet over the course of a year. The dam also has an odd habit: it grows. To stop that growth, new slot cuts are made in the structure every four to five years, the last recorded in 2016. In 2017 the dam and its associated infrastructure were listed on the National Register of Historic Places.

## Flight Context

Fontana Dam is at 35.4522°N, 83.8047°W on the Little Tennessee River in western North Carolina, along the southwestern boundary of Great Smoky Mountains National Park. The massive concrete wall and the long, branching arms of Fontana Lake are unmistakable from the air; Fontana Village lies just south of the dam, and NC Highway 28 crosses the river on a bridge downstream. Terrain rises steeply on all sides, with the Smokies crest to the north, so plan a viewing altitude of at least 6,500-7,500 feet MSL and keep ridge clearance in mind. Nearest airports: Western Carolina Regional Airport in Andrews (KRHP), about 16 nm south; McGhee Tyson Airport in Knoxville/Maryville (KTYS), about 23 nm north-northwest; and Asheville Regional Airport (KAVL), about 62 nm east. Mountain weather here changes quickly, and valley fog and low clouds are common in the mornings.
