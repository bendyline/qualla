# Y-12 National Security Complex

Built in secret in the Tennessee hills with coils wound from Treasury silver, Y-12 enriched the uranium for the Hiroshima bomb and still makes the uranium parts of America's nuclear arsenal.

**Location:** 35.9883, -84.2547

**Categories:** Manhattan Project sites, Nuclear weapons infrastructure of the United States, United States Department of Energy facilities, Oak Ridge, Tennessee, Military history of Tennessee

## Introduction

Colonel Kenneth D. Nichols needed silver, a great deal of it, and he asked the Under Secretary of the Treasury, Daniel W. Bell, for thousands of tons. Bell's stunned reply, as Nichols recalled it, amounted to a lesson in units: at the Treasury, silver is counted in troy ounces, not tons. Wartime copper was scarce, and the massive electromagnetic coils of a secret plant in Oak Ridge, Tennessee, had to be wound from something. So the Manhattan Engineer District borrowed roughly 400 million troy ounces of silver from the West Point Depository. The plant was code-named Y-12. It separated the uranium for the bomb dropped on Hiroshima, and it is still at work today as the Y-12 National Security Complex.

## Borrowed Silver, Separated Atoms

Construction began in February 1943 at the Clinton Engineer Works, with Stone & Webster in charge, and the plant started operating that November. Its task was to pull uranium-235 out of natural uranium, which is 99.3 percent uranium-238, using calutrons to perform electromagnetic isotope separation. The silver was closely guarded. Special guards and accountants were assigned to it, and in the final accounting less than 0.036 percent of the borrowed silver, about 155,000 troy ounces, had been lost to the process; the rest went back to the Treasury. In the push of early summer 1945, other Oak Ridge plants helped. K-25, a gaseous diffusion plant that only began operating that March, and the S-50 Thermal Diffusion Plant fed slightly enriched uranium into Y-12's Beta Calutrons. The uranium-235 that Y-12 separated went into Little Boy, the bomb dropped on Hiroshima, Japan, on August 6, 1945.

## The Calutron Girls

Tennessee Eastman, hired by the Army Corps of Engineers, ran the plant from 1943 to May 1947 and brought scientists over from Kingsport, Tennessee. Scientists from the University of California, Berkeley, first ran the electromagnetic units to work out the bugs, then handed them to trained Tennessee Eastman operators, young women with only a high school education. When Nichols compared the production figures, he told physicist Ernest Lawrence that these operators, whom he called "hillbilly" girls in the language of the day, were outproducing Lawrence's doctorate-holding scientists. The two agreed to a production race, and Lawrence lost, a morale boost for the Tennessee Eastman workforce. The women had been "trained like soldiers not to reason why," while "the scientists could not refrain from time-consuming investigation of the cause of even minor fluctuations of the dials." They came to be known as the Calutron Girls.

## A Drum in Building 9212

On June 16, 1958, under Union Carbide's management, a solution of highly enriched uranium was mistakenly diverted into a steel drum in the C-1 Wing of Building 9212. It set off a fission reaction that lasted 15 to 20 minutes. Eight workers, Bill Wilburn, O. C. Collins, Travis Rogers, R. D. Jones, Howard Wagner, T. W. Stinnett, Paul McCurry and Bill Clark, were hospitalized with moderate to severe radiation sickness or exposure. All eventually returned to work. In 1960 they sued the Atomic Energy Commission, and the suit was settled out of court; Wilburn, who had received the highest dose, was awarded $18,000. Later, under a federal compensation program for energy workers, the eight received further payments, and Y-12's own history of the accident says all eight suffered long-term health effects, including numerous forms of cancer. Later emergencies followed: a chemical explosion that injured several workers on December 8, 1999, and a fire in a uranium processing area of the same building on February 22, 2023, contained later that day.

## Cold War Workshop

After the war, Y-12 became a manufacturing plant for nuclear weapons components. Union Carbide took over in 1947 and stayed until 1984, followed by Martin Marietta, later Lockheed Martin, in November 2000 by BWXT Y-12, and today by Consolidated Nuclear Security. Today it is a facility of the Department of Energy's National Nuclear Security Administration, responsible for producing and maintaining all the uranium parts and "secondary" mechanisms for every weapon in the U.S. nuclear arsenal. It also stores nuclear material securely, for the United States and other governments; early efforts focused on material from the former Soviet Union, and later work included recovering highly enriched uranium from Chile. The environmental legacy is real. Y-12 was listed as an EPA Superfund site for groundwater and soil contamination, and federal recovery funding has paid to demolish and decontaminate aging buildings.

## Prayers Past the Fence

Since 1988 the Oak Ridge Environmental Peace Alliance has organized non-violent direct action protests at Y-12, aiming to close the weapons plant. The most dramatic came before dawn on July 28, 2012. Sister Megan Rice, an 82-year-old Catholic sister of the Society of the Holy Child Jesus, and two military veterans, all Plowshares activists, got past fences and disabled security sensors to reach the Highly Enriched Uranium Materials Facility. They spray-painted peace messages on its exterior, prayed and sang, and spent several hours inside the complex before guards stopped them. The Department of Energy's inspector general found the plant's defenses insufficient and its security response marked by "troubling displays of ineptitude." The three were convicted of sabotage in May 2013, but on May 8, 2015, a federal appeals court overturned the sabotage convictions, finding they lacked the necessary intent, and the three were released on May 16, 2015, ahead of resentencing on the lesser charge of injuring government property. In her testimony, Rice had said, "I regret I didn't do this 70 years ago."

## Flight Context

The Y-12 National Security Complex is at about 35.988°N, 84.255°W in Oak Ridge, Tennessee, in the ridge-and-valley country west of Knoxville. It is an active nuclear weapons facility: check current charts, NOTAMs and any temporary flight restrictions before flying in the area, keep a respectful distance and altitude, and do not loiter. A transit altitude of 4,500-5,500 feet MSL gives a view of the Oak Ridge valleys. Nearest airports: McGhee Tyson Airport in Knoxville/Maryville (KTYS), about 17 nm southeast, and Knoxville Downtown Island Airport (KDKX), about 19 nm east. Morning haze and valley fog are common in the Tennessee Valley.
