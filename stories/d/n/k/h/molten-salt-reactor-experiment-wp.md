# Molten-Salt Reactor Experiment

At Oak Ridge in the 1960s, a small reactor ran on liquid fuel — melted fluoride salts laced with uranium — became the first in the world to operate on uranium-233, and left behind a cleanup problem no one had anticipated.

**Location:** 35.9218, -84.3067

**Categories:** Molten salt reactors, Nuclear reactors, Oak Ridge National Laboratory, Science experiments, 1965 in science

## Introduction

Most reactors hold their fuel in solid form. In the Molten-Salt Reactor Experiment, the fuel flowed. Uranium was dissolved in a mixture of melted fluoride salts that circulated through the core, out to a heat exchanger and back again, and the whole charge could be drained into holding tanks when the reactor shut down. Built at Oak Ridge National Laboratory in Tennessee, the MSRE went critical in 1965 and ran until December 1969. In that time it met every one of its objectives, became the first reactor in the world to run on uranium-233, and set up a problem that would take decades to clean up.

## Salt Instead of Rods

The idea grew out of earlier molten-salt research for the Aircraft Reactor Experiment, and by 1960 the chemistry was better understood. Fluoride salts are strongly ionic, and when melted they stay stable at high temperatures, low pressures and intense radiation. Low pressure meant the reactor vessel did not need to be as robust, which improved reliability, and the reactive fluorine trapped most of the fission byproducts. It even appeared that the fluid salt would allow fuel and wastes to be separated chemically on site.

The fuel salt was a blend of lithium, beryllium, zirconium and uranium fluorides. Its circulating pump did double duty: in the pump bowl, about 50 gallons a minute of fuel was sprayed into a gas space so that xenon and krypton could escape. Stripping out xenon-135, the most significant neutron poison, made the reactor safer and easier to restart than a conventional solid-fuel design.

## A New Alloy, Built at Home

Every metal part that touched the salt was made of Hastelloy-N, a low-chromium nickel-molybdenum alloy that proved compatible with the fluoride salts. Almost 200,000 pounds of it were produced commercially in various shapes. When requests for bids went out to several companies in the nuclear fabrication industry, all of them declined to offer lump-sum bids because they lacked experience with the new alloy, so every major component was fabricated in Atomic Energy Commission-owned shops at Oak Ridge and at Paducah, Kentucky.

Construction began in 1962 inside the old Aircraft Reactor Experiment building, and the salt systems were installed by mid-1964. The design was full of inventions: freeze valves, air-cooled pipe sections where salt could be frozen solid or thawed to stop and start flow; flexible control rods working at 1,200°F; and tools for cutting and brazing pipe by remote control, because the fuel system sat in sealed cells laid out to be maintained with long-handled tools through openings in the shielding.

## Six Thousand Full-Power Hours

Nuclear testing began in June 1965, when enriched uranium-235 was added to the carrier salt to make the reactor critical. The road to full power was bumpy. Oil that leaked into the fuel pump was polymerized by radioactive offgas and plugged filters and valves, delaying the climb. Maximum power, capped at 7.4 megawatts thermal by the heat-rejection system, was reached in May 1966, and after two months at high power the reactor sat idle for three months because a main cooling blower failed.

By the end of that year the startup problems were mostly behind it. Over the next 15 months the reactor was critical 80 percent of the time, with runs of one, three and six months uninterrupted by a fuel drain, and in 1967 the staff marked 6,000 full-power hours. By March 1968 the original objectives had been accomplished.

## Uranium-233 and a Crack in the Metal

Rather than stop, the program changed fuel. Enough uranium-233 had become available to try it, so the uranium was stripped out of the salts on site by fluorination and recovered as uranium hexafluoride. Atomic Energy Commission Chairman Glenn Seaborg took the controls for the 1968 startup, and in October 1968 the MSRE became the world's first reactor to operate on uranium-233.

Not every finding was welcome. Every metal surface exposed to the fuel salt showed intergranular cracking, caused by tellurium, a fission product created in the fuel. The cracks grew fast enough to threaten the planned 30-year life of a follow-on thorium breeder reactor. Adding small amounts of niobium to the alloy reduced the cracking in the short term, but longer studies were still needed. The experience also flagged tritium control and keeping the salt liquid in every corner of the system as problems a commercial molten-salt reactor would have to solve.

## The Salt That Would Not Rest

After shutdown, the frozen salt was thought to be in safe long-term storage. It was not. At low temperatures, radiation can free fluorine from the salt, so the salt was reheated to about 150°C every year until 1989. From the mid-1980s came concern that radioactivity was migrating through the system, and 125 employees working above the reactor were ordered evacuated. Sampling in 1994 found uranium concentrated enough to pose a potential criticality accident and roughly one atmosphere of fluorine gas above the solid salt.

The cleanup was described as "the most technically challenging" activity assigned to contractor Bechtel Jacobs under its environmental management contract with the Department of Energy. In 2003 it was expected to cost $130 million. Uranium removal was completed in March 2008, but the fission products stayed in the tanks. As of 2019, the MSRE sat intact in a monitored, shut-down state known as SAFSTOR.

## Flight Context

The MSRE building stands at 35.9218°N, 84.3067°W in the Melton Valley area of Oak Ridge National Laboratory, Oak Ridge, Tennessee, among the long parallel ridges west of Knoxville. It is inside a Department of Energy reservation: do not circle or loiter, and check current charts and NOTAMs for restricted areas or flight restrictions before planning a route nearby. A sightseeing pass is best made from a respectful distance at 3,000 ft AGL or higher, using the Clinch River as a reference. Nearby airports: McGhee Tyson Airport, Knoxville (KTYS), about 17 nm east-southeast, and Knoxville Downtown Island Airport (KDKX), about 21 nm east. Summer haze and morning valley fog are common.
