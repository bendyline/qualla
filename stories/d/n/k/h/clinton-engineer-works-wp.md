# Clinton Engineer Works

On condemned East Tennessee farmland, the Manhattan Project's Site X built three giant uranium and plutonium plants and a fenced, segregated secret city, Oak Ridge, whose uranium fueled the bomb dropped on Hiroshima.

**Location:** 36.0133, -84.2625

**Categories:** Manhattan Project sites, Oak Ridge, Tennessee, Military history of Tennessee, History of the Manhattan Project, World War II

## Introduction

The engineer Benjamin Bederson took in the distillation equipment and its enormous appetite for energy and drew his own conclusion. "I thought they were making sour mash to drop on the Germans, get them all drunk," he later recalled. They were not. In the ridges about 18 miles west of Knoxville, the Clinton Engineer Works was enriching uranium, and its product went into the bomb dropped on Hiroshima in 1945. Its X-10 reactor also made the first reactor-produced plutonium. Around the plants the Army built a secret town, Oak Ridge, that held about 75,000 people at its peak in 1945.

## Site X

The Manhattan Project needed a place remote enough for secrecy yet reachable by road and rail, with a mild climate for year-round building, ridges to contain accidental explosions, and huge supplies of power and water; the plants were expected to need 150,000 kW of electricity and 370,000 gallons of water a minute. The Tennessee Valley Authority assured planners it could supply the power, and land between the county seats of Kingston and Clinton emerged as the most promising site. In September 1942, within days of being put in charge of the Manhattan Project, Leslie Groves took a train to Knoxville, toured the land and judged it "an even better choice than I had anticipated." It became the Clinton Engineer Works in January 1943, codenamed Site X. The town took the name Oak Ridge from employee suggestions, a choice approved because "its rural connotation held outside curiosity to a minimum."

## A Notice Nailed to a Tree

More than 1,000 families lived on the land, on farms and in the hamlets of Elza, Robertsville and Scarboro. Most learned of the takeover when a government representative arrived; some came home from work to find an eviction notice nailed to the door or to a tree in the yard. Most got six weeks to leave, some just two. For some it was the third time the government had moved them, after the Great Smoky Mountains National Park in the 1920s and the TVA's Norris Dam in the 1930s. Unlike the TVA, the Army had no funds to help families relocate, and with tires and moving vehicles scarce, some left possessions behind. More than 200 landowners organized to challenge the valuations and a House subcommittee held hearings in Clinton and Kingston, but no additional compensation came. Governor Prentice Cooper called the project "an experiment in socialism" and tore up the Army's exclusion proclamation rather than read it.

## The Calutron Girls

Three great plants rose in the valleys. At X-10, a block of graphite weighing about 1,500 tons went critical on 4 November 1943, the world's second artificial nuclear reactor and the first built for continuous operation. At K-25, a four-story, U-shaped gaseous diffusion plant stretched half a mile. At Y-12, Ernest Lawrence's calutrons used magnets so powerful they dragged 14-ton vacuum tanks out of alignment. Once Berkeley scientists had worked out the bugs, the machines passed to Tennessee Eastman operators, young women with only high school educations, labeled "hillbilly" girls in the condescending language of the day. Kenneth Nichols compared the numbers and told Lawrence that these Calutron Girls were outproducing his doctorate-level scientists. They agreed to a production race, and Lawrence lost. The women had been "trained like soldiers not to reason why," while the scientists "could not refrain from time-consuming investigation of the cause of even minor fluctuations of the dials."

## Behind the Fence

Oak Ridge spread across the gentle slopes of Black Oak Ridge, and by May 1945 some 82,000 people worked at the Clinton Engineer Works. Everyone signed a security declaration and wore a color-coded badge limiting where they could go; mail was censored and lie detectors were used. Girl Scouts were registered by first names only to protect their fathers' identities. Even so, the atomic spies George Koval and David Greenglass got in and passed secrets to the Soviet Union. The town had 36 bowling alleys, a 9,400-volume library and well-funded schools, but it was built segregated, and Black residents could live only in one-room "hutments" in Gamble Valley. On 10 April 1945, Ebb Cade, a Black cement mixer hospitalized with fractures from a car accident, was injected with plutonium without his consent so its effects on the human body could be studied. Bone samples were taken, and fifteen of his teeth were extracted and tested for plutonium; a federal advisory committee later could not determine whether the extractions were medically motivated or done for sampling.

## The Gates Come Down

The final wartime shipment of uranium-235 left the Clinton Engineer Works on 25 July 1945. Oak Ridge uranium went into Little Boy, the bomb dropped on Hiroshima on 6 August, which killed an estimated 90,000 to 166,000 people there. Oak Ridge greeted the news with wild celebration. Then the secret city began to change. In 1946 tenants were allowed to paint their houses something other than wartime olive drab. The Atomic Energy Commission took control in 1947, and the reactor site became Oak Ridge National Laboratory in 1948. After national attention fell on the conditions Black residents endured, the village of Scarboro was built for them on the site of the Gamble Valley trailer camp. On 19 March 1949, though a straw poll reportedly showed residents opposed ten to one, guards took down the barriers as Vice President Alben W. Barkley looked on, and Oak Ridge opened to the public.

## Flight Context

The former Clinton Engineer Works is centered on Oak Ridge, Tennessee, at about 36.01°N, 84.26°W, roughly 18 miles west of Knoxville, with the town of Clinton about 8 miles to the north. From 3,000-4,000 feet AGL, look for the long parallel ridges and valleys that separated the plants and the town of Oak Ridge on the slopes of Black Oak Ridge. Parts of the site, including Y-12 and Oak Ridge National Laboratory, remain in federal use; check current charts and NOTAMs for restrictions and avoid loitering over them. The nearest airports with ICAO codes are McGhee Tyson Airport (KTYS, about 18 nm) and Knoxville Downtown Island Airport (KDKX, about 19 nm). Choose a clear day for the best view of the ridge-and-valley country.
