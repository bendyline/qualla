# Ghost Town Village

Perched on Buck Mountain above Maggie Valley, the Wild West park once billed as North Carolina's mile-high theme park drew hundreds of thousands a year, then slid into breakdowns, landslides, bankruptcy and a long, lawsuit-bound silence.

**Location:** 35.5288, -83.1024

**Categories:** Amusement parks in North Carolina, Western (genre) theme parks, Abandoned amusement parks, Buildings and structures in Haywood County, North Carolina, Amusement parks opened in 1961

## Introduction

Ghost Town in the Sky called itself "North Carolina's mile-high theme park." The billing was generous. Its highest point sits at about 4,650 feet, some 600 feet short of a mile, but the ride up made the case anyway: from a parking area beside Jonathan Creek in Maggie Valley, visitors climbed 1,250 feet by chairlift or incline railway to a Wild West town strung along a ridge of Buck Mountain, where a gunfight broke out in the street every hour. For four decades it ranked among Western North Carolina's most popular attractions. Today it stands abandoned, its future unresolved after years of lawsuits, on ground that has a habit of giving way.

## Uncle Dan Carpenter's Sheep

The park was the brainchild of R. B. Coburn, a native of Covington, Virginia, who had moved to Maggie Valley. He first planned to build between Waynesville and Clyde, but Alaska Presley, who would one day own the place, suggested the mountaintop. Local investors bought debenture bonds starting in 1959, and the child of one investor supplied the name. In 1960 the land was bought from a local landowner known as Uncle Dan Carpenter, and with it came a story. Carpenter had reportedly lost sheep in a large cavern on top of Buck Mountain, and Coburn, imagining cavern tours, asked to be shown the spot. When they reached the place where Carpenter had heard his sheep crying, the ground had closed up and no entrance could be found. The land between the Indian Village and the frontier town has kept collapsing ever since. Coburn filled and paved it, yet a minor landslide struck in 2007, and in 2010 a major landslide brought down the retaining wall. Some dismiss the tale as folklore; others blame caverns slowly swallowing the mountain after rain.

## The Longest Chairlift in the State

Ghost Town opened in the spring of 1961 (May 1 by some accounts) and quickly became one of the region's most popular tourist attractions. A double incline railway hauled visitors up the mountain on a journey of more than 3,300 feet, and the park soon added a two-seat chairlift that was, at the time, the longest in North Carolina and the second longest in the United States, able to carry 1,200 people an hour. Crowds followed. Attendance reportedly averaged about 500,000 a year (other accounts say more than 400,000) and at one point reached 620,000, making the park a major economic driver for Maggie Valley. In 1973 it was sold to National Services in a stock swap, and one source says the small park suffered under an owner for whom it was never a major concern.

## High Noon, Every Hour

The heart of the park was a recreated Old West town with two saloons, a schoolhouse, a bank, a jail and a church. Visitors lined the board sidewalks for the hourly street gunfight. Can-can dancers performed in the Silver Dollar Saloon, while country and bluegrass bands played the Red Dog Saloon all day. Mining Town offered gold and silver panning, and Mountain Town told stories of Smoky Mountain life. The Indian Village staged shows that included a deer hunt and a raid on a frontier settlement, the kind of Hollywood-Western scene that mid-century parks presented as entertainment. One performer, Robert Bradley, whose grandfather Jim Jumper was Eastern Band Cherokee, took a fall so spectacular in a 1961 gunfight that Coburn declared, "Don't let him go back to the bottom of the mountain again, he stays here, he's my gunfighter." Up top, the Red Devil coaster, later renamed Cliffhanger, sent riders through a loop on track that extends over the edge of the mountain, and the Gunslinger swung them over the mountainside.

## Stranded

On July 16, 2002, the chairlift stopped and left passengers hanging for more than two hours. With attendance already falling because of the park's poor condition, Ghost Town closed a few days later and went up for sale. It sat unmaintained for four years. Ghost Town Partners bought it in August 2006 and reopened it on May 25, 2007, after spending $38 million on renovations, but the rides kept failing. Cliffhanger reopened on June 30, 2009, and closed less than two days later after a hairline crack was found. By 2010 the park was closed again, the landslide had buried its main access road, and its largest creditors were moving to foreclose on debts of $9 million and $5 million. In 2011 the bankruptcy administrator recommended dismissing the Chapter 11 case, clearing the way for foreclosure.

## Alaska Presley's Mountain

In February 2012, Alaska Presley, a Maggie Valley businesswoman involved with the park since its beginning, bought it out of foreclosure at public auction, reportedly for $2.5 million. That June the A-frame entrance and chairlift reopened for the first time in four years. She faced the damage left by vandalism and theft, condemned wells and ride-inspection trouble, dreamed of a Holy Land replica called Resurrection Mountain, and managed a late opening in 2014, but in 2016 the park was offered for sale again. Investors later drew up plans for a $200 million revival, delayed by the pandemic and local worries about tourism. Presley died on April 4, 2022, at the age of 98, and lawsuits followed. A business court ruling let development continue, but managing partner Frankie Wood died in 2024, further complicating matters. Up on Buck Mountain, the hourly gunfights have long since fallen silent.

## Flight Context

Ghost Town sits on a ridge of Buck Mountain, an extension of the Cataloochee Divide, directly above Maggie Valley, North Carolina, at about 35.53°N, 83.10°W. The park's buildings stand at roughly 4,400-4,650 feet above sea level, some 1,250 feet above the valley floor along U.S. Highway 19 and Jonathan Creek, with the high ridges of Great Smoky Mountains National Park rising beyond. Stay at 7,000 feet MSL or higher and look for the cleared ridgetop and the chairlift line climbing from the valley. Nearby airports: Jackson County Airport at Sylva (FAA identifier 24A, no ICAO code, about 14 nm), Macon County Airport at Franklin (FAA identifier 1A5, about 24 nm), Asheville Regional Airport (KAVL, about 28 nm) and Gatlinburg-Pigeon Forge Airport (KGKT, about 29 nm). Mountain weather can close in quickly; fly it on a clear day.
