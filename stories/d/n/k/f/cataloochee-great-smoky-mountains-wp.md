# Cataloochee (Great Smoky Mountains)

Once a Cherokee hunting ground and later a stubborn mountain community of apple growers, herders and schoolhouse arsonists, Cataloochee is now an empty, elk-grazed valley of preserved homes inside Great Smoky Mountains National Park.

**Location:** 35.6397, -83.0819

**Categories:** Communities of the Great Smoky Mountains, Great Smoky Mountains National Park, Appalachian culture in North Carolina, Geography of Haywood County, North Carolina, Cherokee history

## Introduction

In 1906, three men from Cataloochee went to Waynesville to ask for a bigger school and were turned away, told their valley did not pay enough taxes. On the way home, Hiram and George Caldwell and Steve Woody decided to burn the old schoolhouse down. They carried out the furniture, set the building ablaze, moved classes into the old Caldwell cabin, and petitioned again: their school, they reported, had burned. North Carolina's mandatory attendance laws left officials no choice. The Beech Grove School that resulted still stands along Palmer Creek, in a valley of the Great Smoky Mountains that most of its families had left by 1943.

## Gadalutsi

The name comes from the Cherokee Gadalutsi, "fringe standing erect," probably for the tall rows of trees along the surrounding ridges. Cataloochee is really three narrow parallel valleys - Little Cataloochee, Big Cataloochee and Caldwell Fork - walled in by ridges that rise above 5,000 feet, with 6,155-foot Big Cataloochee Mountain to the southwest. The Cherokee used it mainly as a hunting ground. Their Cataloochee Trail, linking the Middle Settlements with the Overhill towns, had been worn a foot deep in places by the time Europeans arrived, and Bishop Francis Asbury followed it across the mountains in 1810. The Cherokee gave up their claim in the 1791 Treaty of Holston but kept hunting and fishing here through the 19th century. Hattie Caldwell Davis, a descendant of the first settlers, recalled that her ancestors spoke "fluent Cherokee," and her great-grandfather Levi is believed to have aided Cherokees hiding in the forest during the Trail of Tears.

## A Fresh Start in the Balds

Herders came first, grazing livestock on the grassy balds; temporary camps were in place by 1814, when Henry Colwell made the first land purchase. In 1834 his son James moved the family in, and the spelling eventually became Caldwell. The Bennetts came with them, and in 1838 George Palmer followed. Family tradition says he had lost a fortune drinking and gambling in Waynesville and wanted a fresh start. Life revolved around animals. Sheep and cattle summered on the balds, hogs foraged in the forest, and each year residents drove livestock and turkeys to market in Waynesville or as far as Charleston, South Carolina. Furs bought powder, lead, salt, coffee, cloth and indigo, and Palmer later persuaded the state to put a bounty on the wolves killing valley stock. By 1860 Cataloochee had 160 people and township status, and the Cataloochee Turnpike, completed in the early 1860s along the old Cherokee trail, became the first wagon road in the Smokies.

## Bonaparte's Retreat

Unlike much of southern Appalachia, Cataloochee sided largely with the Confederacy, and the war brought extraordinary hardship. Most able-bodied men left, fields lay fallow, and raiders from both armies looted the valley. Union raiders under Colonel George W. Kirk, the 3rd North Carolina Mounted Infantry, found a makeshift hospital residents had set up for returning veterans and killed or wounded 15 patients recovering there. Confederate raiders, meanwhile, hunted the deserters and Union sympathizers hiding in its remote coves. In an incident that became legend, local tradition holds that Captain Albert Teague captured George and Henry Grooms and Mitchell Caldwell, marched them to a remote point on Sterling Ridge, and ordered Henry Grooms to play his fiddle. He chose "Bonaparte's Retreat." When the tune ended, all three men were executed. Peace brought little relief at first, since many returning men were too weak to plant and the Confederate money families had saved was worthless.

## Apples, Moonshine and the Boogerman

The valley grew to 764 people by 1900. When railroads reached western North Carolina in the 1870s, many residents had never seen a train, so Hiram Caldwell and Steve Woody went to Old Fort to look; neighbors refused to believe them when they reported they could not outrun it on horseback. Apples, suited to the cool valley, became the main cash crop, and around 1910 Will Messer, who became Cataloochee's wealthiest man, built a large communal applehouse. By 1920 there were two post offices, Ola and Nellie, named for daughters of Messer and of George Palmer. Some experts estimate that 95 percent of households made their own whiskey, mostly for personal use, though during Prohibition some poorer families sold it. On Caldwell Fork lived Robert Palmer, who, legend has it, told his teacher he wanted to grow up to be "the Boogerman." He refused every lumber company's offer, and some of the valley's tallest trees still stand along the Boogerman Trail.

## The News at Palmer's Chapel

In 1928, the Reverend Pat Davis told the people gathered at Palmer's Chapel that their valley lay inside the proposed national park and that they would be forced to sell. Hattie Caldwell Davis remembered women crying and men threatening to dynamite the roads and shoot anyone who tried to enter. Lifetime leases eased the opposition, and most families took what was offered, skeptical that lawyers would gain them anything, but restrictions on farming and logging drove most people out by 1943. Their buildings now belong to the park: the 1869 Palmer House with its self-guided museum, Palmer Chapel from 1898, and the Caldwell House and barn. In February 2001, 25 elk from Land Between the Lakes were released here, returning an animal lost to overhunting and habitat loss, and herds are now a common sight in spring and fall. As recently as 2007, former residents and their descendants still gathered for a reunion every August.

## Flight Context

Cataloochee lies at about 35.64°N, 83.08°W in Haywood County, North Carolina, in the northeastern corner of Great Smoky Mountains National Park. The three parallel valleys are hemmed in by Sterling Ridge and the Cataloochee Divide, both above 5,000 feet for long stretches, with Big Cataloochee Mountain reaching 6,155 feet to the southwest, so stay at 8,000 feet MSL or higher. Look for the cleared bottomland of Big Cataloochee along Cataloochee Creek, reached by the winding Cove Creek Road from Interstate 40. Nearby airports: Jackson County Airport at Sylva (FAA identifier 24A, no ICAO code, about 20 nm), Gatlinburg-Pigeon Forge Airport (KGKT, about 25 nm) and Asheville Regional Airport (KAVL, about 29 nm). Mountain weather changes fast here; plan for clear, stable conditions.
