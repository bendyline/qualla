# Grouseland

William Henry Harrison's red brick mansion in Vincennes was a statement of power on the frontier, the place where land treaties were negotiated and where Tecumseh confronted the territorial governor on the grounds.

**Location:** 38.6855, -87.5260

**Categories:** William Henry Harrison, National Historic Landmarks in Indiana, Historic house museums in Indiana, Native American history of Indiana, Vincennes, Indiana, Federal architecture

## Introduction

In the early 1800s, Vincennes was a frontier town with an American and French population estimated at only 700, and the Indiana Territory's other residents lived in log cabins. The territory's young governor built something else entirely: a two-story red brick mansion in the Federal style, decorated with items imported from Europe, at an estimated cost of $20,000. William Henry Harrison reportedly named it Grouseland for the abundance of grouse in the area. Under its roof he met Native leaders to negotiate the cession of their lands, and on its grounds he came face to face with Tecumseh.

## A Governor Only Twenty-Seven

In 1800, President John Adams appointed twenty-seven-year-old William Henry Harrison as the first governor of the Indiana Territory. Soon after arriving in Vincennes in 1801, Harrison bought 300 acres next to the town and began planning a home. Once he was sure of a second term, construction started, and the house went up between 1802 and 1804.

The architect, William Lindsay, designed it in the style of the aristocratic Virginia homes where Harrison had spent his boyhood. Harrison came from that world: he was the youngest son of Benjamin Harrison V, a signer of the Declaration of Independence. At least one biographer surmises that the governor built so elaborate a home in the wilderness partly to command the respect due his office despite his youth. Whatever the motive, Grouseland announced his place in American aristocracy to everyone who saw it.

## Home and Headquarters

Grouseland was a family house first. William Henry and Anna Tuthill Symmes Harrison lived there with their children until 1812, and at least three of the couple's ten children were born under its roof. One of them, John Scott Harrison, became the father of Benjamin Harrison, the 23rd president of the United States.

The house was also the focal point of the territory's social and official life. Visiting legislators were frequently entertained, and many stayed the night. In 1812 the Harrisons left for North Bend, Ohio. Harrison went on to serve in the U.S. Army during the War of 1812, then in the U.S. House of Representatives, the Ohio Senate and the U.S. Senate, before his election in 1840 as the ninth president of the United States.

## Councils on the Grounds

Grouseland's most consequential rooms were the ones where Harrison met Native American leaders, among them Little Turtle and Buckongahelas, to negotiate land cessions. Between 1803 and 1809, Harrison concluded eleven treaties with Native leaders that transferred the southern third of present-day Indiana and most of Illinois, millions of acres, to the United States for future settlement.

Not every leader accepted this. The Shawnee leader Tecumseh confronted Harrison at Grouseland twice, in August 1810 and again in July 1811. In 1810 Tecumseh arrived with between 100 and 400 warriors, as accounts differ, and met the governor in the grounds beside the house; one account has the council moved to a walnut grove about a hundred yards away after Tecumseh refused the chairs set out by the portico. The talks stretched over several days, one account says eight, and ended without a treaty. The two sides settled their differences in war instead. Harrison defeated Tecumseh's brother Tenskwatawa, known as the Prophet, at the Battle of Tippecanoe in 1811, and defeated Tecumseh himself at the Battle of the Thames in 1813.

## What the House Holds Now

Today Grouseland occupies half a city block about 100 yards from the Wabash River, a remnant of Harrison's original 300-acre estate. It is a National Historic Landmark, valued for both its Federal-style architecture and its role in American history. The house has a high roof with dormer windows and an attic, and it rests on a basement with a limestone foundation. A covered passage joins the main house to a rear dependency. On the first floor, the parlor, which served as the council chamber, lies to the left of the entry hall and the dining room to the right, with a library at the back. Upstairs are six bedrooms.

Among the objects on display is the Grouseland Rifle, an early 19th-century firearm that has been designated the state firearm of Indiana. The main campus of Vincennes University sits right next door.

## Flight Context

Grouseland stands at approximately 38.69°N, 87.53°W in Vincennes, Indiana, about 100 yards from the Wabash River and next to the main campus of Vincennes University. Follow the Wabash at 1,500–2,500 feet AGL; the river and the university campus are the best references for finding the house. The former O'Neal Airport (FAA identifier OEA), about 1 nm to the west-northwest across the river in Illinois, was abandoned in 2009 and is no longer an airfield. Lawrenceville-Vincennes International Airport (KLWV), about 6 nm to the northwest, is the nearest active field. Best in clear weather.
