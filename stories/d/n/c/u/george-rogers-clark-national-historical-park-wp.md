# George Rogers Clark National Historical Park

On the Wabash at Vincennes, a granite rotunda dedicated by Franklin Roosevelt marks the lost site of Fort Sackville, where George Rogers Clark's winter march ended in a 1779 victory that helped win the Old Northwest.

**Location:** 38.6792, -87.5356

**Categories:** American Revolutionary War sites, National historical parks of the United States, National Park Service areas in Indiana, Monuments and memorials in Indiana, Knox County, Indiana

## Introduction

Sixteen fluted granite columns ring the memorial on the banks of the Wabash River at Vincennes, Indiana, holding up a saucer dome of glass, and visitors climb thirty granite steps to reach it. Beneath all that classical order lies an unfinished basement with exposed concrete walls and a dirt floor. Somewhere in the ground of this park, though no archaeological evidence has shown exactly where, stood Fort Sackville. Its capture on February 25, 1779, by Lieutenant Colonel George Rogers Clark remains one of the most memorable feats of the American Revolution.

## A March Through Winter

Clark, the older brother of William Clark, was sent by Virginia to protect its interests in the Old Northwest. His 1778-1779 Illinois Campaign included the founding of Louisville, Kentucky, and the capture of British forts in the lower Ohio and Mississippi valleys. Forces under his command had taken Fort Sackville months earlier, but word came that British troops under Lieutenant Governor Henry Hamilton had retaken it. Clark answered with a desperate mid-winter march from Kaskaskia on the Mississippi, and on February 25, 1779, he captured both the fort and Hamilton. It was the finest moment of his career. The victory extended American land claims in the Ohio Valley and led the new United States to claim, in the 1783 Treaty of Paris, the lands that would become Ohio, Illinois, Indiana, Michigan and Wisconsin.

## Remembering a Lost Fort

As Vincennes grew through the 1800s, the town overran the fort's site and its boundaries were lost. In 1905 the Daughters of the American Revolution placed a stone marker where they believed it had stood, and in 1929 residents made a major effort to mark the 150th anniversary of Clark's campaign. President Calvin Coolidge established a sesquicentennial commission in 1928 that planned a classical memorial, and in the 1930s it was built with federal, Indiana and local money, a combined total reported at roughly $2 million to $2.5 million. New York architect Frederic Charles Hirons designed it, and President Franklin D. Roosevelt dedicated it on June 14, 1936. In 1976 the National Park Service called it the "last major Classical style memorial" built in the United States, though it has company from the same era, including John Russell Pope's Jefferson Memorial in Washington, D.C., completed between 1939 and 1943.

## Granite, Glass and Murals

The memorial is a circular granite building wrapped in a peripteral colonnade of Greek Doric columns, set on a stylobate beneath its glass dome. Inside, a statue of Clark stands among a series of seven murals by Ezra Winter, and the finished rooms have plastered walls, marble wainscoting and terrazzo floors. Bronze doors carry Native American detail, and a display of flags is part of the interior too. Sculptor Joseph Kiselewski made a bas-relief for the park between 1930 and 1934 that, according to the National Park Service, depicts a young Clark receiving his orders from Patrick Henry to attack the British outposts on the western frontier.

## Allies on the Riverbank

Clark did not win alone, and the park also honors Father Pierre Gibault and Francis Vigo, who sided with him against the British. John Angel's granite statue of Vigo, the Italian-American merchant who assisted Clark, has overlooked the Wabash since 1934, and on the adjacent grounds of the Basilica of St. Francis Xavier a 1934 bronze by Albin Polasek honors Gibault. Raoul Josset designed the Lincoln Memorial Bridge across the Wabash to complement the memorial, with a monument by Nellie Walker on the Illinois side celebrating Abraham Lincoln's migration. Even the concrete floodwall that protects the memorial and Vincennes from Wabash floods is designed in a complementary classical style. The grounds also hold a memorial to Knox County's World War I soldiers, a marker where Clark's headquarters probably stood during the siege, and the original DAR marker, moved several times over the years.

## Mending the Monument

Indiana transferred the site to the National Park Service in 1966, under an Act of July 23 that year, and a visitor center on South 2nd Street, within the Vincennes Historic District, now presents programs and displays. No structures from the Revolution survive. The memorial itself has needed care, since its terrace had leaked from the 1930s, a problem first identified in a 1939 structural inspection. On August 9, 2008, the memorial closed for about 14 months for a three-million-dollar renovation, during which about 1,000 large granite paving stones were removed, numbered and stored while new waterproofing went down. It reopened in late September 2009 and was rededicated on October 3. In 2017 the park was honored on Indiana's America the Beautiful quarter.

## Flight Context

George Rogers Clark National Historical Park sits at 38.6792°N, 87.5356°W on the east bank of the Wabash River in Vincennes, Indiana, beside the Lincoln Memorial Bridge to Illinois. From 1,500-2,500 feet AGL, the round granite memorial with its pale colonnade and glass dome is easy to pick out at the riverside, next to the Basilica of St. Francis Xavier. The former O'Neal Airport (FAA identifier OEA), about 1 nm northwest across the river, was abandoned in 2009 and is no longer an airfield; Lawrenceville-Vincennes International Airport (KLWV) is the nearest active field, about 6 nm north-northwest, and Evansville Regional Airport (KEVV) is about 39 nm south. River fog can form along the Wabash on cool mornings.
