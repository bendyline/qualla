# Willard Library

Built by a philanthropist and Underground Railroad agent, Evansville's Victorian Gothic Willard Library promised free use to "all persons" and, legend says, is still haunted by its founder's daughter, who lost a suit over his will.

**Location:** 37.9783, -87.5742

**Categories:** Libraries on the National Register of Historic Places in Indiana, Gothic Revival architecture in Indiana, Genealogy, Reportedly haunted locations in Indiana, Evansville, Indiana

## Introduction

Look closely at the capitals atop the arcade of Evansville's Willard Library and you may notice that they were never finished. They were apparently only roughed out for carving, and the carving was never completed, a small gap on a building that otherwise wears its Victorian Gothic finery with confidence: a tower, steeply pitched roofs with ornamental gables, white stone set against brick, and terra cotta owls, symbols of wisdom, in roundels on the side gables. Formally opened in 1885, the library came with a stubborn founder, a daring idea of who counted as the public and, if the stories are true, a ghost.

## The Pioneer of Public Charity

Willard Carpenter, remembered as Evansville's "pioneer of public charity," built and endowed the library himself. In 1876 he established a trust, writing that he meant "to establish and endow a public library, to be located in a public park, on land owned by me," in the hope that it would improve "the moral and intellectual culture of the inhabitants of Evansville" and spread "a taste for the fine arts." The name came from his years in Troy, New York, where he became enamored with the Emma Willard School, a female seminary founded by Emma Willard. He resolved to found an educational institution and call it Willard. The plan began as a college before it was refocused on a library.

## Free to All Persons

Carpenter, a fervent abolitionist, acted as an agent for the Underground Railroad before the Civil War, according to Indiana Landmarks, and his concern for the rights of African Americans shaped the library. He required that it "be maintained for the free use of all persons who may desire to consult it," a definition of "public" that was a daring social experiment in the latter half of the nineteenth century. The principle has held. Through every period of its operation, Willard has remained open and free to residents of Vanderburgh County and neighboring counties. It is a private donation library, incorporated in 1881 to carry out the terms of Carpenter's trust and governed by a self-perpetuating seven-member board, yet it also receives public support through a tax levy that must be approved by the Evansville Vanderburgh Public Library.

## A Foundation Left Waiting

Carpenter and his wife deeded the land and an endowment in 1876, and ground was broken in May 1877 at First Avenue and Pennsylvania Street, in Carpenter's field, a place said to have hosted circuses. A weak real estate market led the trustees to halt work after the stonework was finished that September, and the foundation lay unfinished for five years. When building resumed in 1882, Carpenter devoted the rest of his life to supervising it, though he died in November 1883, before it was finished. He worked with the architects, hired the workmen himself and saw to it that only the best materials were used. James and Merritt Reid, who took over from the original architects, are credited with the superstructure, designed by James W. Reid, and inside, the doorways and grand staircase make an imposing Gothic entrance finished in select quarter-grain oak. The formal opening came on March 28, 1885, and in 1972 the library was listed on the National Register of Historic Places.

## Midnight Among the Microfilm

Much of Willard's reputation now lives upstairs. The genealogy and local history collections on the second floor, opened in August 1976, include Vanderburgh County newspapers from 1821 to the present, county records and Catholic diocese records on microfilm, church, cemetery and funeral home records, family histories in everything from books to loose papers, and a DAR book collection. Genealogists come from all over to trace their families, and its week-long "Midnight Madness" genealogy conference has kept the library open late for research (the 2023 edition, billed "(Almost) Midnight Madness," ran to 10 p.m. Monday through Thursday). The Tri-State Genealogical Society, formed in partnership with the Special Collections department, publishes a packet for its members every September, December, March and June.

## The Grey Lady

Some visitors come looking for a different kind of history. The library is purported to be haunted by a figure known as the Grey Lady, thought to be Louise Carpenter, Willard's daughter. Carpenter gave the library away, and in the 1890s Louise sued the library board, arguing that her father had not been of sound mind when he wrote his last will and testament. She lost, and she is said to haunt the building in revenge. The first reported sighting came in 1937, when a custodian encountered her in the basement and quit shortly afterward. Others who claim to have met her describe the scent of perfume and strange noises. The library began putting up webcams in 1999, and its online "Grey Lady Ghost Cam" lets anyone keep watch.

## Flight Context

Willard Library stands at 37.9783°N, 87.5742°W in Evansville, Indiana, a city wrapped around a broad bend of the Ohio River. From 1,500-2,500 feet AGL, look for the Gothic tower and steep roofs set in a green park block amid the city grid. Evansville Regional Airport (KEVV) is only about 4 nm north-northeast, so expect to be inside its controlled airspace; Skylane Airport (FAA identifier 3EV) is about 2 nm north-northwest, and Henderson City-County Airport across the river in Kentucky (FAA identifier EHR) is about 12 nm south-southwest. River haze is common on summer mornings.
