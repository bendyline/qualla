# St. Anthony's Hospital fire

A fire that swept through an outdated Effingham, Illinois, hospital on an April night in 1949 killed at least 74 people and pushed hospitals across the United States toward the fire protections now taken for granted.

**Location:** 39.1256, -88.5507

**Categories:** Hospital fires, Fires in Illinois, Disasters in Illinois, Effingham, Illinois, Fire safety

## Introduction

At about 11:30 on the night of April 4, 1949, Sister Mary Edmunda Hiersig was going off duty at St. Anthony's Hospital in Effingham, Illinois, when she smelled smoke on the first floor. She reported it to the switchboard operator, Sister Anastasia Groesch. What followed was a catastrophe: before it was over, at least 74 people had died, and some accounts put the toll at 75 to 77. The disaster became a lesson that hospitals across the country would study, and the reasons it spread so fast can be found in the building itself.

## Tinder in the Walls

St. Anthony's was run by the Sisters of St. Francis, who lived in a convent next door. It had about 100 beds and was built mainly of wood and brick, with parts of the building dating back to 1876. By 1949 the facility was completely outdated.

Its corridors and staircases were open, so nothing stood in the way of smoke and flame moving from floor to floor. Many walls and ceilings were covered with oilcloth fabrics and combustible soundproofing tiles. There were no sprinklers, no fire detection system and no alarm system. On the night of the fire, 116 patients and ten staff members were inside.

## Ten Minutes

Sister Groesch's first call went not to the fire department but to Frank Ries, the hospital's engineer, who came over and tried to fight the fire by spraying an extinguisher down the laundry chute on each floor. He would be among those who died.

Fed by the open construction and the combustible materials, it spread rapidly through the building. Within ten minutes of the fire being detected and the fire department arriving, it had already burned through the roof. The Effingham Volunteer Fire Department, twenty-six men with three pumpers, was badly unprepared for a fire spreading this fast. The small department had no ladder trucks or other equipment to reach patients trapped on the second and third floors.

## The People Upstairs

Many patients on the first floor were able to walk out or climb through low windows. Upstairs, the fire trapped many more. Among them were around a dozen newborn infants in the nursery and the nurse who stayed behind with them.

Some people on the second and third floors jumped to escape the flames, and many of them suffered injuries that lasted the rest of their lives. Doctors and nurses went back into the burning building again and again to carry others out, until the fire or their own injuries stopped them from returning.

A historian for the Effingham County Museum has said that about 88 percent of the victims were women and girls or children under the age of twelve. Many were patients, people who had come to St. Anthony's to be cared for.

## What Effingham Built Next

The community set out to rebuild almost at once. A letter campaign sent about 293,500 letters that referred to the death of a nurse, Fern Riley. Within two weeks it had raised $300,000, and by the end of the summer the total reached $563,000 toward a new hospital.

The cause of the fire was never determined. Investigators, however, quickly identified the many safety deficiencies at St. Anthony's, and Governor Adlai Stevenson ordered an evaluation of every hospital in Illinois to find and correct fire hazards. The impact reached far beyond the state. Hospitals across the United States made many of the fire protection improvements that are standard today, and St. Anthony's is still cited as a prime example of the hazards hospitals can face.

## Flight Context

St. Anthony's Hospital stood at approximately 39.13°N, 88.55°W in Effingham, Illinois. This is a memorial site rather than a visual landmark; a pass over the city at 2,000–3,000 feet AGL is a way to remember the 74 or more people who died here. Effingham County Memorial Airport (FAA identifier 1H2, no ICAO code listed) is about 3.4 nm away. Coles County Memorial Airport at Mattoon (KMTO) is about 25 nm to the north-northeast, and Vandalia Municipal (KVLA) about 30 nm to the west-southwest. Best in clear weather.
