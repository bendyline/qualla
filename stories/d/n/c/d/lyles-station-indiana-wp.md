# Lyles Station, Indiana

Founded by free Black farmers in the river bottoms of southwestern Indiana, Lyles Station survived the 1913 flood to become, by 2008, the only early Black rural settlement still remaining in the state, with its soil now on display in the Smithsonian.

**Location:** 38.3703, -87.6592

**Categories:** Populated places established by African Americans, Unincorporated communities in Gibson County, Indiana, African American history, Communities of Southwestern Indiana, 1849 establishments in Indiana

## Introduction

Soil from a farm in Gibson County, Indiana, is on display in the Smithsonian's National Museum of African American History and Culture on the National Mall. It comes from the Greer family farm, worked by the Greers for more than 150 years, and it shares the museum's "Power of Place" exhibition, which opened in 2016, with a horse-drawn plow once used by Joshua Lyles, clothing, a quilt and hundreds of other items from the area. Together they tell the story of Lyles Station, one of Indiana's early Black rural settlements and, as of 2008, the only one remaining.

## Ten Free Households

The first settlers arrived in the 1830s. Among them was Joshua Lyles, a free African American who came to Indiana from Tennessee with his family around 1837. He and his wife, Carparta, known as Clara, had six of their thirteen children in Tennessee before the move. The Lyles family bought land near the confluence of the White, Patoka and Wabash rivers, and in the township's 1840 census Joshua and his father, John, appear as two of ten free Black men heading households. The other eight were Nelson Bass, Joel Stewart, John A. Morland, Robert Cole, Banister Chaves, Thomas McDaniel, Isaac Williams and Duke Anderson. A 1902 newspaper article may have started the later story that Joshua Lyles had been freed from slavery.

## Butter, Honey and Indian Corn

By 1850 Lyles appears to have been a successful farmer. That year's agricultural census recorded him owning 320 acres, 60 of them improved, on a farm valued at $500. The year before, he had kept 4 horses, 10 cows and 50 hogs, and his land had produced 150 pounds of butter, 10 pounds of maple sugar, 60 pounds of honey and 500 bushels of Indian corn. He kept farming and expanding until his holdings reached 1,200 acres, and by the 1860s three more of his siblings had joined the community, which dates from 1849. Joshua Lyles died in 1885. The following year the settlement was formally named Lyles Station in his honor.

## The Depot Years

The railroad turned the farms into a town. A depot on the Louisville-Saint Louis line, a division of the Southern Railway, brought freight, passenger and mail service and helped the settlement grow. Between 1880 and 1912 Lyles Station reached its peak, with the depot, a post office, a lumber mill, two general stores, two churches and a school. By the turn of the twentieth century it had fifty-five homes and more than 800 residents. By 1900 it stretched from the Patoka River on the north to Indiana State Route 64 on the south, and from old U.S. 41 on the east to the Illinois line on the west. Two other Black settlements grew nearby, Roundtree to the north and Sand Hill two miles south. Its churches were a Hardshell Baptist congregation and Wayman Chapel African Methodist Episcopal Church, which remains today, though its sanctuary has been closed because of a sinking foundation while the congregation works to restore it.

## After the Water

The rivers that fed the farms also made the place especially vulnerable to flooding. The Great Flood of 1913 destroyed much of the town, and the farming community never fully recovered. Most residents eventually left for economic reasons, seeking higher-paying jobs and more education in larger cities, and passenger service at the depot ended as the population fell. Those who stayed still built for their children. When the older schools at Lyles Station, Sand Hill and Sugar Bluff closed in a statewide trend toward consolidation, Lyles Consolidated School opened in 1919. It was integrated until 1922, when it became an all-Black public school and white students went to Baldwin Heights School. It was integrated again in the 1950s and closed in 1958 as enrollment declined. By 1997 about fifteen families remained, nearly all of them descended from the original settlers.

## From the Bottomlands to the White House

People with roots in Lyles Station traveled far. Alonzo Fields became the first African American chief butler of the White House, working there 21 years under Presidents Hoover, Roosevelt, Truman and Eisenhower. His brother George C. Fields was Franklin D. Roosevelt's valet for four years, until a 1942 inspection tour with the President at the Great Lakes Naval Training Station inspired him to join the Navy. Aaron R. Fisher, a World War I veteran, received the Distinguished Service Cross. Matthias Nolcox was the first principal of Crispus Attucks High School in Indianapolis, and the Reverend Lyman Parks became the first African American mayor of Grand Rapids, Michigan. Vertus Hardiman's story is harder: subjected at age five to a U.S. government human radiation experiment that left him with a painful skull deformity, he became the subject of the documentary Hole in the Head: A Life Revealed. Back home, the restored Lyles Consolidated School now serves as a living history museum and community center.

## Flight Context

Lyles Station lies at 38.3703°N, 87.6592°W in Patoka Township, about 4.5 miles west of Princeton, Indiana, in flat bottomland near where the White and Patoka rivers approach the Wabash and the Illinois state line. From 2,000-3,000 feet AGL, look for the rivers' meanders and the patchwork of large farm fields, with Princeton's town grid to the east. Mount Carmel Municipal Airport across the Wabash in Illinois (FAA identifier AJG) is about 15 nm north-northwest, and Evansville Regional Airport (KEVV) is about 21 nm south-southeast. The low-lying land here is prone to flooding, and river fog can linger in the mornings.
