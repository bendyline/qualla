# Air Indiana Flight 216

On a December night in 1977, a DC-3 carrying the University of Evansville basketball team crashed moments after takeoff, a loss the university still marks with memorials on campus and at its arena.

**Location:** 38.0366, -87.5188

**Categories:** Aviation accidents and incidents in the United States, Accidents involving sports teams, Evansville Purple Aces, Evansville, Indiana, 1977 disasters, Memorials

## Introduction

On the campus of the University of Evansville stands a memorial known as the Weeping Basketball. Its stone slabs carry the names of a team that never finished its season. On the evening of December 13, 1977, the Purple Aces boarded a Douglas DC-3, registration N51071, at Evansville Regional Airport, bound for Nashville and a game against Middle Tennessee in Murfreesboro. At 7:22 p.m. Central time, shortly after takeoff, the aircraft lost control and crashed. All 29 people on board died.

## Two Problems at Once

The National Transportation Safety Board traced the crash to two failures that compounded each other. The external control locks on the right aileron and the rudder had not been removed before takeoff, and the passenger baggage had not been loaded as the load manifest specified: nearly all of it went into the aft baggage compartment. That shifted the plane's center of gravity toward the rear, though the board found the airplane within its weight limit and the center of gravity probably still forward of the rearmost allowable limit.

The NTSB reasoned that the rearward center of gravity made the DC-3 lift off early and pitch up, and that the locked controls kept the pilot from recognizing and countering that pitch-up until the airplane had entered a nose-high, high-drag regime from which he could not recover; it noted that DC-3s had taken off successfully with loading even farther aft. Together, the two problems left no margin at all, on a flight that crashed less than a minute and a half after takeoff.

## Who Was Aboard, and Who Was Not

Head coach Bobby Watson was the only coach on the flight; his assistant coaches were away on scouting assignments. Athletic director Jim Byers had planned to board but stayed behind to interview a candidate for the university's baseball program. Those small twists of schedule decided who lived.

Neighbors and rescuers found four people still breathing. Three died at the scene, and the fourth died hours after the accident.

One member of the team was not on the plane at all. David Furr, an 18-year-old freshman, was out for the season with an ankle injury and was not on the plane that day. For a brief time, he was the last of the 1977 Purple Aces.

## Two Weeks Later

The grief in Evansville had barely begun when it deepened. Two weeks after the crash, David Furr and his younger brother, Byron, were killed in a car accident near Newton, Illinois. With his death, the entire 1977 Evansville team was gone.

After consideration, the rest of the season was cancelled. It had been meant as a new beginning: 1977–78 was the Purple Aces' first season in NCAA Division I, after years of success in Division II, and Watson, previously an assistant at Oral Roberts, had been announced as head coach only that March. The team had played four games and won one, against Pittsburgh on December 6, a week before the crash. What had been a basketball team, with a schedule, a coach and a group of young men traveling to play a game, had become a list of names and a community in mourning.

## Out of the Agony

The Weeping Basketball memorial bears the names of the players who were killed, Furr among them. The final name on the monument is Charles Goad of the Goad Equipment Company, who had been invited on the flight by his friend Bob Hudson.

Also carved into the stone is a line from the eulogy that university president Wallace Graves delivered at a memorial service: "Out of the agony of this hour we will rise." The line reads as a promise as much as a comfort. The Purple Aces play on, and a second memorial stands at the Ford Center, the arena where they now play their home games.

## Flight Context

The crash site lies at approximately 38.04°N, 87.52°W, immediately beside Evansville Regional Airport (KEVV) on the north side of Evansville, Indiana. This is a place of loss rather than sightseeing; if you depart or arrive at KEVV, it is a moment to remember the 29 people who died here. The airport reference point is about 0.5 nm from the site. Owensboro-Daviess County Regional (KOWB) is about 24 nm to the southeast, and the flight's intended destination, Nashville International (KBNA), is about 120 nm to the south-southeast.
