# Sugar Grove Station

Born as a Cold War plan to catch Soviet radar signals bouncing off the Moon, Sugar Grove Station in West Virginia's Radio Quiet Zone became a National Security Agency listening post, while its old Navy base went up for auction.

**Location:** 38.5150, -79.2842

**Categories:** National Security Agency facilities, UKUSA listening stations, Earth stations in the United States, Communications and electronic installations of the United States Navy, National Radio Quiet Zone

## Introduction

In the early 1960s, the Naval Research Laboratory set out to build something enormous in the mountains of Pendleton County, West Virginia: a fully steerable radio telescope 600 feet across. It was meant to gather intelligence on Soviet radar and radio signals reflected off the Moon, and to collect radio astronomy data from deep space. The giant dish was never finished. The project was halted in 1962, but the site near the village of Sugar Grove kept its purpose as a place for listening, and it went on to become one of the quieter secrets of the Appalachians.

## From Telescope to Antenna Farm

General Dynamics was the primary contractor for the abandoned telescope, with the Philadelphia Gear Corporation developing its gear drives. After the project stopped, the Navy developed the site as a radio receiving station instead. Naval Radio Station Sugar Grove was activated on May 10, 1969, and on November 8 of that year two Wullenweber AN/FRD-10 Circularly Disposed Antenna Arrays were completed. Many more antennas, dishes and domes followed. Among the most notable dishes are a 60-foot antenna, the oldest on site, a 105-foot dish with a special waveguide receiver, and a 150-foot dish, the largest at Sugar Grove.

## Part of a Global Ear

Sugar Grove is widely reported to have become part of ECHELON, the communications network said to be operated by the United States and its allies to intercept and process electronic telecommunications, alongside sites such as Waihopai Valley in New Zealand, Menwith Hill in the United Kingdom and Yakima, Washington. Its reach has been described in the press. A 2005 New York Times article by journalist James Bamford is cited as saying the station intercepts all international communications entering the eastern United States. The Navy Information Operations Command was long involved at the site, but in April 2013 the Chief of Naval Operations ordered its support base closed by September 30, 2015, citing the National Security Agency's decision to relocate the command's mission. The antenna base to the south, with its NSA listening station, was not closed or auctioned, and news reports say the NSA continued to operate it.

## Where Radios Go Quiet

Sugar Grove sits inside the National Radio Quiet Zone, about 13,000 square miles of West Virginia and Virginia, plus a small part of Maryland, set aside in 1958 to protect the sensitive receivers here and at the National Radio Astronomy Observatory at Green Bank, about 30 miles away. The zone is centered between the two sites, and the rules are strictest within 10 miles of each, where most omnidirectional and high-power transmissions are prohibited. Most broadcast transmitters in the central zone must run at reduced power with directional antennas, which is why cable and satellite are essential for decent television across much of the region. A Quiet Zone administrator at Green Bank oversees the zone and handles coordination for both sites' unclassified assignments.

## A Navy Base for Sale

With the Navy gone, its base at Sugar Grove went up for auction. An online sale that concluded on July 25, 2016, produced a winning bid of $11.2 million from an anonymous buyer, but the purchase was never completed, and the General Services Administration reopened the sale in September as a sealed-bid auction. On December 1, 2016, the high bid, about $4 million, came from Robert Pike, working with Alabama-based Mellivora Partners on a healthcare campus for active-duty military personnel and West Virginia residents, and the sale closed on January 31, 2017. That plan did not go forward, according to the West Virginia Encyclopedia. In March 2018 a Tennessee company announced a 95-bed treatment center on the site, and the encyclopedia says Legacy by Gersh leased the base in January 2022 for a residential community and training center for people on the autism spectrum; what operates there today is unconfirmed. The antenna base to the south was never sold.

## Flight Context

Sugar Grove Station lies at 38.515°N, 79.2842°W near the village of Sugar Grove in Pendleton County, West Virginia, in mountainous country near the Virginia line. The antenna site is reported to be a National Security Agency facility and sits inside the National Radio Quiet Zone, so check the sectional chart and current NOTAMs for any airspace restrictions, avoid circling or loitering, and keep radio use to what is operationally necessary. From a respectful altitude of about 6,000–7,000 ft MSL, the site's dishes and antenna arrays are the key visual cue. Shenandoah Valley Regional Airport (KSHD) at Weyers Cave, Virginia, is about 24 nm southeast, Bridgewater Air Park (FAA identifier VBW, no ICAO code listed; privately owned, open to the public) is about 18 nm east-southeast, and Elkins-Randolph County Airport-Jennings Randolph Field (KEKN) is about 35 nm northwest. Mountain weather can close in quickly, and low cloud can hide the ridges.
