# South Union, Kentucky

A Logan County crossroads that was home to a Shaker village for more than a century, where in 1868 the Shakers paid a heavy price for hiring and defending freedmen.

**Location:** 36.8764, -86.6564

**Categories:** Unincorporated communities in Logan County, Kentucky, Unincorporated communities in Kentucky

## Introduction

"We treat them like men and pay them as such." A Shaker at South Union, Kentucky, wrote those words to a friend in New York, trying to explain why his community had become a target. In late August 1868, a white mob had burned the cabins of freedmen who worked in the Shakers' mills, and when the Shakers offered a reward for the culprits, the attackers came back and burned the mills themselves. The Shaker believed he knew what "their offenses" were: they had "hired the negroes and pay them for their work." That sentence, written in the language of its time, sums up a moment when a small religious community in rural Kentucky refused to look away.

## A Village of Believers

A large share of South Union's original inhabitants were Shakers, and their village here lasted more than a century, from its founding in 1807 until 1922. In that time they built on an astonishing scale for a rural settlement. The Shakers constructed 200 buildings and worked 6,000 acres of farmland. Their workshops and fields turned out garden seed, fruit preserves, brooms, baskets, rugs, linen, hats, bonnets and silk, some of it for use within the community and some for sale to the outside world. That trade connected the village to the wider world. South Union was not simply a retreat from society but a working village, organized, productive and largely able to supply its own needs.

## The Summer of Fire

After the Civil War, the Shakers hired about a dozen freedmen to work in their woolen and flour mills and allowed them to build cabins on the property. It was a dangerous decision in Logan County at the time, where other white residents were attacking Black people and trying to drive them out of the county altogether. In late August 1868, a white mob burned some of the freedmen's cabins, destroying homes these workers had built for themselves. The Shakers responded by offering a reward for the capture of those responsible. The mob answered by returning and setting fire to the Shakers' mills. The buildings were uninsured, and the community's losses came to nearly $70,000. The freedmen had lost far more than money, and the record does not tell us where they went next.

## The House That Holds the Memory

The Shaker village is gone, but a key piece of it remains. The South Union Shaker Center House and Preservatory was built in 1824 as a Centre House, a dwelling of roughly 40 rooms for the Centre Family, and today it is part of South Union Shaker Village, a museum and historic site also known as the Shaker Museum at South Union. Its rooms are filled with original artifacts that show the Shakers' craftsmanship and their distinctive way of life. The site keeps nine original Shaker buildings on nearly 500 acres, is open Tuesday through Saturday, and the Centre House has been on the National Register of Historic Places since 1974. Standing inside it, the scale of the Shaker enterprise becomes easier to picture: 200 buildings once stood in this village, and this one still stands to tell their story.

## A Crossroads Between Two Towns

Modern South Union is an unincorporated community in the easternmost part of Logan County, roughly halfway between Russellville and Bowling Green. It lies along Kentucky Route 73, just off U.S. Route 68 and Kentucky Route 80, a short way west of the Warren County line, and the boundary with Simpson County runs less than a mile to the south. Alongside the museum, the community hosts something far more ordinary: the county's only waste transfer station. It is an unglamorous neighbor for a place with such a history, but it is also a reminder that South Union is still a working corner of the county, as it was when the Shakers ran their mills here.
