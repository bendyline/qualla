# Bill Monroe Farm

The Ohio County, Kentucky, farm where the father of bluegrass was born, its 1920 homeplace built around the hearth of the log cabin that burned in 1916.

**Location:** 37.4328, -86.7647

**Categories:** National Register of Historic Places in Ohio County, Kentucky, Buildings and structures completed in 1920, Farms on the National Register of Historic Places in Kentucky, 1920 establishments in Kentucky, Bluegrass music, Bill Monroe

## Introduction

The oldest part of the house is the hearth. When the saddlebag log cabin on this Ohio County farm burned in 1916, a new house went up on the same site in 1920, and it incorporated the cabin's original chimney and hearth. That cabin had been the birthplace of Bill Monroe and many of his siblings. Monroe grew up to create an entire genre of American music, bluegrass, and the Bill Monroe Farm near Rosine, Kentucky, a property of roughly 1,000 acres, has been listed on the National Register of Historic Places since 2003. That hearth is where the story began.

## The Youngest of Eight

Bill Monroe was born on September 13, 1911, on his family's farm in Rosine, the youngest of eight children of James Buchanan "Buck" Monroe and Malissa (Vandiver) Monroe. Being the youngest shaped his music in a very practical way. His older brothers already had the fiddle and the guitar, so Bill was left with the less desirable mandolin. He made it the voice of a new sound. Another early influence was close to home: his disabled uncle, Pendleton Vandiver, a fiddler whom young Bill often accompanied when Vandiver played at dances. Those dances were an early schooling in the music he would later reshape into something new.

## A Genre Named for a Band

Few musicians can say an entire style of music carries the name of their band. Monroe could. His group, the Blue Grass Boys, named themselves for the bluegrass of Monroe's home state of Kentucky, and the genre took its name from theirs. Among his compositions is "Blue Moon of Kentucky," which Elvis Presley later recorded, and in his later years Monroe recorded the instrumental "Jerusalem Ridge." He died on September 9, 1996, in Springfield, Tennessee, four days short of his 85th birthday. The music he built from his childhood on this farm had, by then, spread far beyond Ohio County.

## The Homeplace and Its Neighbors

The main house, known as the Bill Monroe Homeplace, is a modest building of about 1,000 square feet, built in 1920 on the site of the burned log cabin. It sits on a property in Rosine about 2 miles west of the junction of U.S. Route 62 and Kentucky Route 1544. The farm holds other structures too, though they were counted as non-contributing when the property was listed on the National Register. The Charlie Monroe House, built in 1945 or 1946, was described by Paul McCoy as having been built by Charlie Monroe with wooden clapboards and Permastone; it has a garage, two porches and three brick chimneys. Two festival stages and a sorghum mill round out the property, signs of a place where music and farm work share the same ground.

## Saving the Homeplace

Fame brought an unwelcome kind of attention. In the years after Monroe's death, vandals took pieces of wood from the main house to carry away as souvenirs, adding to the damage that weather had already done. The homeplace needed rescuing, and in 2001 the Bill Monroe Bluegrass Music Foundation, which had bought the property, began restoring it with the help of Vie Hood, a restoration expert from Tennessee "whose restoration credits include the Tennessee State Capitol Building, Davy Crockett's home, and the Hermitage the home of Andrew Jackson." Two years later, in 2003, the farm joined the National Register of Historic Places. Today the modest house, its old hearth still in place, stands as a quiet counterpoint to the music that began here.
