# Great Onyx Cave

A disputed discovery, a landmark lawsuit and a twenty-year holdout shaped Great Onyx Cave, a Flint Ridge cavern that now sits inside Mammoth Cave National Park.

**Location:** 37.2186, -86.0789

**Categories:** Caves of Kentucky, Mammoth Cave National Park, Show caves in the United States, Property law, Edmonson County, Kentucky

## Introduction

When Great Onyx Cave was opened to paying visitors, rocks and sand were piled against its walls to make trails. Those piles may have sealed off passages that could connect to Mammoth Cave. Legend has it the blockage was no accident but the revenge of Edmund Turner, the explorer credited with finding the cave in 1915, who never profited from his discovery. Whether or not the story is true, it fits a cave whose history is as tangled as its passages: a bargain gone sour, a lawsuit that legal scholars still argue over, and a holdout that kept the cave private for twenty years after the national park around it was established.

## A Share for a Secret

According to one story, Turner's cave explorations had convinced him that a cave lay beneath land on Flint Ridge owned by L. P. Edwards. He made Edwards a proposition: in return for a share in the cave's ownership, he would show Edwards where to dig. Edwards agreed.

The cave they opened was named Great Onyx Cave for its cave onyx formations. Turner kept exploring underground while Edwards rushed to turn the find into a business. Then, not long afterward, Turner died, probably of pneumonia. With the discoverer gone, Edwards claimed that he had found the cave himself. Turner's name survived anyway, attached to the discovery and to the legend of what he may have done to the trails.

## Who Owns the Dark?

Edwards soon had another fight on his hands. A neighboring landowner named Lee alleged that the cave ran underneath his property, and the case, Edwards v. Lee's Administrator, reached Kentucky's Court of Appeals, which ruled in 1936. Lee's side won. In the usual telling, the court invoked the old principle that a landowner's rights extend up to the heavens and down to the depths, and it made Edwards hand over profits earned from tours on Lee's property; summaries differ on whether that meant all of those profits or a share matching the part of the cave beneath Lee's land.

The ruling has been controversial ever since. Critics argue that under the usual measure of damages for trespass, Lee should have been owed only a reasonable rental value for the use of his land, not every dollar Edwards made there. Unjust enrichment theorists at Oxford University read it differently, as a case in which Edwards was enriched at the expense of Lee's property rights, and they cite it in support of their analysis of other cases in the field.

## An Island in the Park

In the 1930s, as the federal government bought up property to create Mammoth Cave National Park, the owners of Great Onyx Cave refused to sell. When the park was established in 1941, the cave remained a privately held island within its borders, a commercial attraction surrounded by public land.

The standoff lasted two decades, and for all that time the national park had, within its own borders, a cave it did not own. Only in January 1961 was the cave finally sold to the National Park Service and made part of the park. Today the Park Service offers a tour of Great Onyx Cave, and the formations that gave it its name belong to everyone.

## Passages That Never Meet

Great Onyx Cave is unusual in one striking way: it has not yet been connected to nearby Mammoth Cave. That is not for lack of neighbors. Passages in the Flint Ridge Cave System actually run beneath surveyed passages in Great Onyx Cave, close on the map and yet separate in the rock.

When the cave was commercialized, workers piled rocks and sand against the walls to make trails, and those piles may have blocked the very openings that would have linked the caves. It is easy to see why the legend took hold. A man who shared his secret, lost his claim and died before he could profit might well have wanted the last word, and in the dark beneath Flint Ridge, the story says, he may have had it.

## Flight Context

Great Onyx Cave lies at approximately 37.22°N, 86.08°W on Flint Ridge within Mammoth Cave National Park, Kentucky. The cave itself is underground, so from the air you are looking at the park landscape above it; 2,500–3,500 feet AGL gives a good sense of the ridge country.
