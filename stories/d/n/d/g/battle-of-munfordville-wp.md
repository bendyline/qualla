# Battle of Munfordville

In September 1862 a Union colonel held the Green River railroad bridge at Munfordville against one Confederate assault, then walked through enemy lines, counted the guns and surrendered more than 4,000 men.

**Location:** 37.2622, -85.8901

**Categories:** Battles of the American Civil War in Kentucky, Confederate Heartland Offensive, American Civil War on the National Register of Historic Places, Hart County, Kentucky, 1862 in Kentucky

## Introduction

Late on September 16, 1862, Union Colonel John T. Wilder walked under a flag of truce into the Confederate lines around Munfordville, Kentucky, to see what he was up against. His escort, Confederate Major General Simon B. Buckner, showed him the army's strength so that he would understand resistance was futile: by the usual account 45 cannon and more than 25,000 infantry, though a contemporary newspaper report counted 72 guns and the American Battlefield Trust puts the Confederate force engaged at about 16,000. Wilder had already beaten off one attack at this crossing of the Green River. Faced with those odds, he agreed to surrender, and the Battle of Munfordville, also called the Battle of Green River, ended with more than 4,000 Union soldiers in Confederate hands.

## A Bridge Worth Fighting For

Munfordville mattered because of a bridge. The town was a station on the Louisville & Nashville Railroad, which crossed the Green River here on an 1,800-foot bridge designed by Albert Fink and built in 1859. On August 26, 1862, Confederate General Braxton Bragg's army left Chattanooga, Tennessee, and marched north through Sparta, Tennessee, and Glasgow, Kentucky, with Major General Don Carlos Buell's Union army in pursuit. By mid-September Bragg was approaching the crossing, held by Wilder's garrison of three regiments behind extensive fortifications that included Fort Craig, a star-shaped wood and earthen fort built by the Union.

## An Attack Without Orders

The first Confederates to arrive had not been sent. Brigadier General James R. Chalmers marched on Munfordville from Cave City without orders, trusting an erroneous cavalry report that the garrison had "not more than 1,800 men, entirely raw troops," cut off from all communication and reinforcement. Arriving on September 14, he demanded surrender, was refused, and launched frontal assaults that the defenders repulsed. His brigade suffered 288 casualties before falling back to Cave City. Among the wounded was Colonel Robert A. Smith, the Edinburgh-born commander of the 10th Mississippi Infantry, who died several days later. Bragg was angry at the "unauthorized and injudicious" attack but determined to force the town's surrender anyway, believing that leaving Munfordville intact would "throw a gloom upon the whole army."

## New Uniforms for the Defeated

Bragg's army made a forced march of 25 to 35 miles on the night of September 15-16. Late on the 16th, realizing that Buell's forces were near and not wishing to kill or injure innocent civilians, the Confederates sent another demand for surrender, and Wilder made his walk through their lines. The formal ceremony took place the next day, when the paroled Union soldiers marched out of Munfordville in new uniforms. The sight stayed with Confederate soldier W. L. Trask. The federals, he said, "were well clothed, looked fat and sleek and clean and neat and were in strange contrast to our own hungry, ragged and dirty looking rebels."

## Three Days Lost

On paper it was a clear Confederate victory. Union casualties totaled 4,148, most of them captured, against 714 for the Confederates, and stores of supplies fell into Southern hands. The win let the Confederates temporarily strengthen their hold on the region and impair Union supply lines. Yet it did little for them beyond slowing them down. Author Kenneth W. Noe judged that unless Bragg intended to fight it out along the Green River, "an idea that flickered only briefly under duress, Munfordville was a three-day distraction the Confederate cause could ill afford." The episode showed how little overall vision Bragg brought to the campaign, reacting from one event to the next.

## A Brother's Obelisk

The entire battlefield is listed on the National Register of Historic Places, including Fink's Green River bridge, Fort Craig and a small cemetery at its northern edge, and the American Battlefield Trust and its partners had preserved about 135 acres by mid-2023. Near the river stands the Colonel Robert A. Smith Monument, a white limestone obelisk about 25 feet tall (one marker says 21). Smith's brother James came from Scotland, searched for the place his brother died, bought the land and spent about $4,000 on the monument, built of limestone quarried in Bowling Green. A second memorial to Smith stands in Dean Cemetery in Edinburgh. In nearby Horse Cave, the Unknown Confederate Soldier Monument marks the grave of a Louisiana soldier accidentally killed while clearing timber for the Confederate advance.

## Flight Context

The Munfordville battlefield centers on 37.2622°N, 85.8901°W, where the Louisville & Nashville rail line crosses the Green River at Munfordville, Kentucky. From 2,000-2,500 feet AGL, follow the Green River's winding channel to the railroad bridge; Interstate 65 and U.S. 31W run nearby. Glasgow Municipal Airport (KGLW) is about 14 nm south-southwest, Addington Field/Elizabethtown Regional Airport (KEKX) is about 25 nm north, and Bowling Green-Warren County Regional Airport (KBWG) is about 31 nm southwest. River fog can fill the Green River valley on cool mornings.
