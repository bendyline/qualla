# Lost River Cave

Ice Age hunters, gristmills, Civil War soldiers and a 1930s nightclub have all found shelter at the mouth of Lost River Cave in Bowling Green, where an underground river once fooled Ripley's Believe It or Not.

**Location:** 36.9528, -86.4717

**Categories:** Caves of Kentucky, Show caves in the United States, Tourist attractions in Bowling Green, Kentucky, Nature centers in Kentucky, Western Kentucky University

## Introduction

In 1847 Thomas Kite set a Bengal light on a small board, lit it and pushed it into the current of a river flowing into a cave three miles from Bowling Green, Kentucky. It floated away, he wrote in his journal, "illuminating the extensive cavern, with the intense brilliancy of its light," until a bend in the cave hid it from the watchers at the mouth. The cave was Lost River Cave, a system reported at seven miles, with what is billed as one of the largest natural entrances in the eastern United States, and over some ten thousand years it has sheltered hunters, powered mills, collected soldiers' signatures and kept a nightclub cool.

## Winter Camp at the Blue Hole

Archaeologists at Western Kentucky University have traced human presence here to the Paleo-Indians at the end of the last ice age, about 10,000 years ago, when Kentucky was cooler and wetter and evergreen forest covered the land. Those nomadic hunters used Clovis spear points to bring down animals such as the giant ground sloth, the woolly mammoth and the mastodon. WKU anthropologist Jack Schock believes groups camped at Lost River in winter, sheltered by the cave and drinking clean water from the blue hole. Schock also found here one of the only Dalton points discovered in place in southeastern Kentucky, a spear point about 8,000 years old that makes the site important for studying the Archaic period. Native groups occupied the area until around 1,000 B.C.

## The Shortest, Deepest River

The river has a talent for fooling people. It surfaces in three short sections before vanishing into the cave, and the most striking of them is the blue hole. Ripley's Believe It or Not once listed Lost River as the "shortest, deepest river in the world," because plumb bobs suggested the blue hole was 437 feet deep while the river ran only 400 feet. In fact the blue hole is only about 15 feet deep. It links to a further underground river, which swept the plumb bobs away and produced the false readings. Kite recorded the setting in 1847: a large oval sink with a river twenty or thirty yards wide flowing a hundred feet below, rising "at once full grown" and entering a cavern 150 feet wide and at least forty or fifty feet high beneath a solid arch of rock.

## Mills Beneath the Arch

That arch drew industry. Some undocumented reports say a corn gristmill and sawmill were built here in 1792, the year Bowling Green was founded, though other accounts date the first mill much later. By 1825 James Skiles owned the mill. When Kite visited, he heard "the clattering of the cog wheels" of a grist mill under the arch, and that same year a Mr. Shanks ran a mill built directly in the stream beneath the rock, adding a wool carding machine. W. F. Smith, who bought the cave in 1917, said Shank's Mill was the only underground water mill in the country. In the mid-1870s John L. Row built a stone dam, drilled through 42 feet of bedrock in the arched ceiling and raised a three-story mill above the cave, which he ran as a distillery and flour mill until 1889. The last mill, owned by Robert Crump, burned in 1915, ending more than a century of industry. Row's hole in the ceiling is still pointed out today.

## Names on the Ceiling

The Civil War brought both armies. Confederate forces occupied Bowling Green from September 18, 1861, to February 14, 1862, and then Union troops took over and used the area as a camp; during the period of Union control, nearly 40,000 soldiers camped in the vicinity of the cave. Explorers later found where soldiers had written their names, ranks and companies on its ceilings and walls, and archaeologists have recovered Union bullets inside. Legends cling to the place as well. Confederate raider John Hunt Morgan supposedly hid in the cave, a story with little credibility given the Union camp, and local lore held that Jesse James hid in its depths after robbing the Southern Deposit Bank in Russellville.

## Dancing in the Cool

In 1933 or 1934 (sources differ), around the repeal of Prohibition, local businessman Jimmy Stewart opened the Cavern Nite Club in the mouth of the cave, with a stage, bar and dance floor. Its natural chill drew crowds when air conditioning was rare, and in 1939 Billboard named it the only air-conditioned nightclub in the U.S. The water wheel generated its electricity. Dinah Shore, Francis Craig and the NBC Orchestra performed here, and the Dixie Highway, now U.S. 31W, brought the tourists. FM radio, television, home air conditioning and the new I-65 eventually drew them away, and the club closed in the early 1960s. By the 1980s the site had become a dump. The Friends of Lost River formed in 1990, and the nonprofit now runs the 72-acre property, which reopened to the public in 1997. Visitors walk past the blue hole before boarding a boat into the cave, and a ballroom now stands where the nightclub once did.

## Flight Context

Lost River Cave lies at 36.9528°N, 86.4717°W on the south side of Bowling Green, Kentucky, beside U.S. 31W, the old Dixie Highway. From 1,500-2,500 feet AGL, look for a wooded valley and sinkhole pocketed into the city's southern edge; I-65 runs to the east. Bowling Green-Warren County Regional Airport (KBWG) is only about 3 nm east-northeast, so coordinate with its traffic, and Nashville International (KBNA) is about 51 nm south-southwest. Summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are common.
