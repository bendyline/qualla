# National Corvette Museum

Built next door to the only factory that makes Corvettes, Bowling Green's National Corvette Museum became world news in 2014 when the Kentucky karst opened beneath its Skydome and swallowed eight cars.

**Location:** 37.0042, -86.3750

**Categories:** Automobile museums, Chevrolet Corvette, Museums in Bowling Green, Kentucky, Sinkholes, Karst topography

## Introduction

At about 5:44 on the morning of February 12, 2014, part of the floor in the Skydome of the National Corvette Museum in Bowling Green, Kentucky, gave way. Eight Corvettes, some rare and some one of a kind, dropped into a sinkhole along with their display stands, rails, slabs of concrete floor and dirt. The museum had spent twenty years celebrating America's sports car. Now the ground beneath it had swallowed a piece of the collection whole.

## Next Door to the Factory

The Chevrolet Corvette has been in production since 1953, and for a museum devoted to it, Bowling Green is an obvious address. The museum sits just off Interstate 65 at Exit 28, near General Motors' Bowling Green Assembly Plant, the only place where Corvettes are manufactured. It was built in 1994 and opened to the public in September of that year.

The museum also sponsors the Corvette Hall of Fame, honoring people who have been involved with the car and made significant contributions in their fields. Between two and six people are inducted each year, in three categories: enthusiasts, GM and Chevrolet, and racing.

## The Ground Beneath

Kentucky is one of many states known for karst topography, the landscape that forms when rock such as limestone dissolves. Under the museum, the limestone in the ground had dissolved, opening pockets beneath the surface. For a time the soil above them held. Eventually the weight of the building proved too much, and the top layer collapsed into the void.

The cars that fell were rare, and some were one of a kind. Together they carried an estimated value of a million dollars, and some suffered serious damage in the fall. The remaining 20 cars in the Skydome were moved out immediately, before the ground could claim any more of them.

## Digging Out

Between March 3 and March 6, 2014, crews recovered five of the eight Corvettes from the sinkhole. Before work began on the last three buried cars, the spire area of the Skydome was reinforced. Multiple gravity tests were run to make sure no other sinkhole was present or forming, and when the results came back clear, construction could begin. As an added precaution, micropiles, systems of steel rods, were inserted into the ground before the concrete floor was poured again.

Remarkably, the museum reopened the day after the sinkhole appeared, with the Skydome itself blocked off. Two years later, it turned the disaster into an exhibit that tells the story of what happened that morning and why, and even lets visitors try to recover the cars virtually.

## Still Growing

The collection keeps growing. The museum was recently given a 1967 Corvette Sting Ray once owned by astronaut Neil Armstrong, the first man on the Moon. In October 2025 it broke ground on a 66,000-square-foot collections and preservation facility, with the collection due to move in during fall 2026 and public access, including behind-the-scenes tours, planned for early 2027.

For a building that once watched part of its floor disappear, it is a confident plan. The limestone under Bowling Green has not changed, but beneath the rebuilt floor, steel micropiles now help support the building. Corvettes are still built at the assembly plant nearby, and the story of the morning the ground opened has become part of what visitors come to see.
