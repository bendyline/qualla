# United States Bullion Depository

Shipped in by mail on guarded trains in 1937, the gold at Fort Knox has shared its Kentucky vault with the Constitution, a Hungarian crown and an opium stockpile, behind a door that sits on a 100-hour time lock.

**Location:** 37.8832, -85.9652

**Categories:** Gold in the United States, United States Mint, Fort Knox, Art Deco architecture in Kentucky, Government buildings on the National Register of Historic Places in Kentucky

## Introduction

The gold at Fort Knox came by mail. When the Treasury began filling its new depository in Kentucky in 1937, the Post Office Department oversaw the move and billed the Treasury at the fourth-class postage rate, charged on the weight of the crates and gold, with insurance fees added. Today that fortified granite building beside the Army post of Fort Knox, formally the United States Bullion Depository, holds about 147.3 million troy ounces of gold, a little over half of all the gold the federal government owns, and its name has become shorthand for security itself.

## Beyond the Mountains

The idea was to put the nation's gold where an invader could not easily reach it. In June 1935 the Treasury announced that it would quickly build a depository on the grounds of Fort Knox to hold gold then kept at the New York City Assay Office and the Philadelphia Mint, part of a policy of moving reserves away from coastal cities. Planners cited the site's military advantages. An army attacking from the Eastern Seaboard would have to fight through the Appalachian Mountains, the location was isolated from railways and highways, and even flying there across the mountains was considered dangerous for a pilot unfamiliar with the territory. The Army's only completely mechanized cavalry unit was stationed at the adjacent fort. The Treasury built the depository in 1936 on land transferred to it from the military.

## Thirty-Nine Trains

The first wave of gold moved semi-weekly between January 11 and June 17, 1937. Postal trucks with municipal police escorts carried it from New York and Philadelphia to armored train cars, where postal workers rode with soldiers, Secret Service agents and mint guards, and decoy trains were employed. In Kentucky, soldiers armed with armor-piercing bullets and machine guns guarded the transfer onto Army trucks, which combat cars of the 1st U.S. Cavalry Regiment escorted to the vault. That wave took 39 trains of 215 cars. A second wave, begun in July 1940 and announced complete on March 1, 1941, took 45 trains of 337 cars. The timing was striking. By 1940 the United States held a majority of the world's monetary gold.

## The Constitution Goes to Kentucky

Gold was not the only treasure. Librarian of Congress Archibald MacLeish worried about the safety of the library's irreplaceable holdings from the time he took office in 1939, and when it became clear Congress would not fund a separate facility, he asked the Treasury for space at Fort Knox. He was offered ten cubic feet, later raised to 60.3. On December 26, 1941, four crates went by train to the depository holding the signed originals of the Constitution, the Declaration of Independence and the Articles of Confederation, Lincoln's Second Inaugural Address and drafts of the Gettysburg Address, a Gutenberg Bible, and Lincoln Cathedral's exemplified copy of Magna Carta, on loan for the 1939 New York World's Fair. The vault gained an air conditioning unit and calcium chloride dryers. The Declaration and Constitution briefly left in April 1943 for the opening of the Jefferson Memorial, and everything returned to the Library of Congress on October 1, 1944.

## A Crown and a Stockpile

After World War II the vault held the Crown of St. Stephen and other Hungarian crown jewels, including a gold scepter and orb, which members of the Royal Hungarian Crown Guard had given to U.S. military authorities for fear they would fall into Soviet hands. They went home to Hungary in 1978. Beginning in 1955, the Defense Logistics Agency stored opium and morphine here and at the West Point Mint, a stockpile that grew to 68,269 pounds, enough to meet the legal painkiller needs of the entire country for a year if supplies were cut off. Morphine is no longer stored at the depository. Today it is known to hold ten 1933 Double Eagle gold coins, a 1974-D aluminum cent, and gold Sacagawea dollars that flew aboard Space Shuttle Columbia on STS-93 in 1999 (reports differ on how many).

## As Safe as Fort Knox

The building, 105 by 121 feet and 42 feet high, used 16,500 cubic feet of granite and 4,200 cubic yards of concrete. Below it lies the vault, made of steel plates, I-beams and cylinders encased in concrete, behind a torch-and-drill resistant door 21 inches thick and weighing 20 tons. The door is set on a 100-hour time lock, and depository staff must each dial separate combinations to open it. Rings of razor wire, night vision cameras and microphones watch the grounds, and visitors are not allowed inside. Outside visits to the vault are very rare, and three are commonly cited, the latest in 2017: President Franklin Roosevelt's in 1943, a 1974 visit by members of Congress and the news media, led by Mint Director Mary Brooks in answer to a conspiracy theory that the vaults were empty, and a 2017 visit by Senator Mitch McConnell with Treasury Secretary Steven Mnuchin. Ian Fleming's 1959 novel sent Goldfinger after the gold, and "as safe as Fort Knox" has long since become a cliché.

## Flight Context

The Bullion Depository sits at 37.8832°N, 85.9652°W at Bullion Boulevard and Gold Vault Road, beside the Fort Knox Army post south-southwest of Louisville, Kentucky. The low, square granite building is visible from the air, but Fort Knox is an active military installation: check current charts and NOTAMs for restricted airspace over its ranges, and do not loiter over the depository. Godman Army Airfield (KFTK) is only about 1.5 nm north-northwest, Addington Field/Elizabethtown Regional Airport (KEKX) is about 12 nm south, and Louisville Muhammad Ali International Airport (KSDF) is about 20 nm north-northeast, so expect Louisville's airspace to the north. Ohio River haze is common in summer.
