# Bell Witch Cave

A 490-foot karst cave on former Bell family land in Adams, Tennessee, where legend says a famous spirit took refuge, even though the cave played no part in the haunting's original telling.

**Location:** 36.5914, -87.0556

**Categories:** Caves of Tennessee, Show caves in the United States, Limestone caves, Landforms of Robertson County, Tennessee, American witchcraft, National Register of Historic Places in Tennessee, Tourist attractions in Robertson County, Tennessee, Reportedly haunted locations in Tennessee, National Register of Historic Places in Robertson County, Tennessee, Folklore of Tennessee

## Introduction

A boy was stuck fast in a hole deep inside a Tennessee cave, and his friends, young Betsy Bell among them, could not free him. Then, the story goes, a voice cried out, "I'll get him out!" Invisible hands grasped his feet and pulled him clear, and the unseen rescuer followed up with a stern lecture on reckless cave exploring. It is one of the more endearing episodes in the lore of the Bell Witch, and it belongs to the Bell Witch Cave in Adams, Tennessee. It may also be one of the least historical, which only makes the cave's story more interesting.

## The Trouble at the Bell Farm

The legend behind the cave comes from Southern folklore. It centers on the 19th-century Bell family of northwest Robertson County, Tennessee, and on John Bell Sr., a farmer who lived along the Red River. From 1817 to 1821, the family was allegedly tormented by an entity that came to be called Kate, after its voice once claimed to be "Old Kate Batts' witch." The legend holds that the spirit poisoned John Bell, and he died on December 20, 1820. In 1894, Martin V. Ingram published An Authenticated History of the Famous Bell Witch, and critics have challenged it hard. Science writer Brian Dunning concluded that Ingram falsified key claims, and Joe Nickell has pointed to anachronism that undercuts its credibility.

## Where the Witch Went

The cave itself is a karst cave, roughly 490 feet long, on property that the Bell family once owned, near where the Bell Farm stood. Its connection to the legend rests on a belief that many people still hold: that when the witch finally departed the Bell family, she fled to the sanctuary of this cave. The rescue story fits that picture neatly. Betsy Bell and her friends venture underground, one of the boys wedges himself into a hole, and the entity, still invisible, saves him before scolding the whole group for their recklessness. It is less a tale of terror than of a strangely protective ghost, a spirit with opinions about safety underground.

## A Legend Written Later

The trouble is chronology. Although the cave lies close to the Bell family homestead, it played no role in the original Bell Witch stories; the alleged cave adventures of the Bell children were created and written many years after the events they describe. The cave was not a fundamental element of the original recorded legend, but in the 20th century it became a source of continuing interest. The cave is a vivid example of legend in motion: a real place near a real family's land gradually drawn into a story that keeps growing. The cave is privately owned, and its 2026 tour season runs from May into November, with the cave closed from December through April, so visitors can come to stand in the dark and decide for themselves.
