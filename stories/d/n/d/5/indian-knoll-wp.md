# Indian Knoll

A shell midden beside Kentucky's Green River holds the story of more than a thousand Archaic people who lived here some five thousand years ago, buried their dogs with care, and lived by rules that surprised archaeologists.

**Location:** 37.2633, -86.9731

**Categories:** Archaeological sites in Kentucky, Archaic period in North America, Shell middens, National Historic Landmarks in Kentucky, Native American history of Kentucky, Green River (Kentucky)

## Introduction

More than five thousand years ago, beside the Green River in what is now Ohio County, Kentucky, people buried their dogs with the same care they gave to one another. Twenty-one dog burials have been found at Indian Knoll, and in at least nine of them a dog lies alongside a person. The site is a shell midden, a great deposit of shell that built up over thousands of years, and it held the remains of more than a thousand people. Research on what they left behind showed that the people of Indian Knoll were strikingly unlike the inhabitants of most Archaic sites.

## Two Digs and a Flood

Indian Knoll was first excavated in 1915 by C. B. Moore and a crew of eight men. The second, larger effort came after the flood of 1937, when Webb and his team of University of Kentucky archaeologists began a second excavation as part of the Works Progress Administration's economic recovery programs. They found 880 more burials.

The site was never fully excavated. Webb blamed "difficulties arising from a shortage in the Works Progress Administration labor quota of the county," though little of the area was left unexplored. Between them, Moore and Webb reported 1,178 burials. Later researchers, working from the skeletal fragments, estimated at least 1,234 individuals. Carbon-dated material from the site averages about 5,300 years old, and radiocarbon dates on the shell deposit itself run from about 5,600 to 4,500 years ago. Every one of those individuals was a person laid to rest by people who knew them.

## A River That Provided

The area was most heavily occupied from approximately 3000 to 2000 BC, when the climate and vegetation were nearing modern conditions. The floodplain offered a stable environment. The people who lived here were complex hunter-gatherers who drew on the rich resources of the deciduous forest and the floodplain, hunting animals from the water and the land and gathering a steady crop of hickory nuts, acorns, roots and seeds. Later domestication of squash in the Green River region shows a trend toward agriculture, though that has not been confirmed at Indian Knoll itself.

Their tools survive too. Forty-three atlatl weights, also called bannerstones, used with spear-throwers, were found with burials. The excavations also turned up 792 pottery shards, mostly in the top foot and a half of the mound and closely related to the pottery of the later Mississippian culture.

## Not Everyone Was Equal

Archaic peoples were typically egalitarian. Indian Knoll complicates that picture. Grave goods were found in 187 burials, and the burials suggest that the community was divided into two social groups regardless of age or sex, with social class the most likely explanation.

The graves also record hardship. Researchers Johnston and Snow concluded that the community had a high infant mortality rate, with most of those deaths among babies under a year old and many among children under four. Those small graves are a reminder of how fragile life was here.

The dogs tell a more complicated story. Their graves were given the same attention as human graves, and researcher Cheryl Claassen suggests that these dogs were not only beloved companions but also carried symbolic and ritual significance. The dogs buried with people appear to have been killed when their owner died, and Claassen found possible evidence of a violent death in at least six of the ten.

## Conflict by the Rules

Life at Indian Knoll was not always peaceful. Some individuals show fatal injuries, including one scalping, a slit throat and a crushed skull. Analysis by Mensforth found many skeletons with multiple puncture marks and fractured or missing bones, evidence of warfare and trophy taking. It is estimated that twelve incomplete skeletons may have provided as many as 34 human bone trophies to their opponents.

Yet most of the reported injuries came from blunt force trauma and were usually not fatal. That pattern suggests organized war parties operating under well-defined rules meant to limit the death toll, rather than sporadic feuds.

Indian Knoll was designated a National Historic Landmark in 1964 and listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1966. Today the site lies within 290 acres of private agricultural fields, quiet ground holding a record of lives lived five thousand years ago.

## Flight Context

Indian Knoll lies at approximately 37.26°N, 86.97°W near the Green River in Ohio County, Kentucky. The site lies within private agricultural fields, so it is one to appreciate from the air; follow the Green River at 2,000–3,000 feet AGL and use the river bottomland as your reference. The nearest fields are Muhlenberg County Airport at Greenville (FAA identifier M21, no ICAO code listed) about 9 nm away and Ohio County Airport at Hartford (FAA identifier JQD, no ICAO code listed) about 13 nm away. Owensboro-Daviess County Regional (KOWB) is about 30 nm to the north-northwest and Bowling Green-Warren County Regional (KBWG) about 32 nm to the southeast. Best in clear weather.
