# Northwest Airlines Flight 710

The 1960 in-flight breakup of a nearly new Lockheed Electra over Perry County, Indiana, which took 63 lives and helped expose a hidden flaw in a new generation of airliner.

**Location:** 37.9110, -86.6330

**Categories:** Aviation accidents and incidents in the United States in 1960, Airliner accidents and incidents in Indiana, Airliner accidents and incidents caused by in-flight structural failure, Accidents and incidents involving the Lockheed L-188 Electra, Monuments and memorials in Indiana, Buildings and structures in Perry County, Indiana, 1960 in Indiana, Northwest Airlines accidents and incidents, March 1960 in the United States, Perry County, Indiana

## Introduction

In farm country east of Cannelton, Indiana, a granite shaft rises nine feet from an eight-ton memorial, topped by a "flame of everlasting life." Panels around it carry 63 names. The inscription explains why it stands here: it is "dedicated to the memory of 63 persons who died in an airline accident at this location, March 17, 1960," and it "was erected by public subscription in the hope that such tragedies will be eliminated." That hope was not an empty one. The loss of Northwest Airlines Flight 710 helped force investigators to find, and fix, a deadly flaw hidden inside one of the newest airliners in American skies.

## An Afternoon Flight South

Flight 710 was a regularly scheduled service from Minneapolis to Miami, with a stop in Chicago. On March 17, 1960, it left Wold-Chamberlain Airport in Minneapolis at 12:51 p.m. Central Standard Time. From Chicago's Midway Airport it took off again at 2:38 p.m., carrying 57 passengers and a crew of six, bound for Florida. The aircraft was a Lockheed L-188 Electra, only six months old. Nothing about the afternoon suggested danger. The plane climbed to 18,000 feet and settled into level flight over southern Indiana, cruising at about 400 miles per hour on a journey that should have ended in Miami.

## Over Perry County

At about 3:20 to 3:25 p.m., the Electra unexpectedly encountered clear-air turbulence at 18,000 feet. The aircraft's right wing separated from the fuselage, and a portion of the left wing broke away as well. Without its wings, the airliner fell almost straight down and struck farmland about six miles east of Cannelton (accounts range from six to eight miles), a Perry County town on the Ohio River. The ground was frozen and covered in snow, and the impact left a crater roughly 15 to 25 feet deep and 30 to 50 feet wide, depending on the account. Pieces of the wings came down as far as 4 miles away. All 63 people on board were killed. For Cannelton and the surrounding communities, the disaster was not a distant headline but something that had fallen onto their own fields.

## A Pattern Emerges

The breakup closely resembled another. In September 1959, Braniff International Airways Flight 542, also flown with an almost-new Electra, had come apart near Buffalo, Texas, killing all 34 people aboard; in that case the left wing had landed about a mile from the rest of the aircraft. Investigators had not been able to explain it. Two nearly identical failures could not be written off. The Federal Aviation Agency placed flight restrictions on the Electra until a cause could be found, and ordered Lockheed to reevaluate the aircraft's structural integrity and demonstrate its airworthiness. More than 250 engineers and technicians took part in the investigation that followed. They discovered that if an Electra had damage to the mounting structures of one of its outboard engines, flying at high speed or through turbulence could trigger a destructive phenomenon called whirl mode wing flutter, leading to wing failure.

## The Fix

The Civil Aeronautics Board released its final report on Flight 710 on April 28, 1961. It concluded that the probable cause was the failure of the right wing due to flutter, driven by oscillations of the outboard engine assemblies combined with reduced stiffness in the aircraft's wings. After the cause of the wing failures was discovered, Lockheed launched a program to design structural changes that would prevent whirl mode flutter, and to apply those modifications retroactively to every Electra already in service. The changes worked. Modifications to the final aircraft were completed on July 5, 1961, a little under sixteen months after the crash in Perry County. The Electra's problem had been solved, at a cost measured in 97 lives across two accidents.

## Remembering the Sixty-Three

The community that received the wreckage also took on the work of remembrance. The week after the accident, Northwest Airlines purchased a ten-lot section of Greenwood Cemetery in Tell City to inter the victims' remains, and in September 1960 the company arranged for a 15-foot granite monument there, bearing the names of the victims and the words "In Memoriam - March 17, 1960." A year after the crash, local residents led by the Cannelton Kiwanis Club and a local newspaper editor raised money for a second memorial at the crash site itself: the eight-ton granite monument with its flame and its panels of names. It remains a place of quiet reflection in open country, paid for, as its inscription says, by public subscription.
