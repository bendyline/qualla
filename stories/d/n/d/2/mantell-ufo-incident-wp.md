# Mantell UFO incident

In January 1948 a decorated Kentucky Air National Guard pilot climbed after an unidentified object in the winter sky, blacked out for lack of oxygen and crashed near Franklin, launching one of the most publicized early UFO stories.

**Location:** 36.6678, -86.5578

**Categories:** UFO sightings, Aviation accidents and incidents in Kentucky, Cold War, North American P-51 Mustang, Simpson County, Kentucky

## Introduction

At 1:15 on the afternoon of January 7, 1948, the Kentucky Highway Patrol called Godman Army Airfield at Fort Knox with an odd report: people near Maysville were seeing an unusual object in the sky that nobody could identify. Around 1:35 a second call brought details. The object was circular and moving west at "a pretty good clip." By about 1:45 the tower operators at Godman could see it for themselves, and they later said they had determined it was neither an aircraft nor a weather balloon. Before the afternoon was over, a 25-year-old pilot would be dead, and the country would have one of its most publicized early UFO reports.

## The Climb

A flight of four P-51 Mustangs from the Kentucky Air National Guard's 165th Fighter Squadron was sent after the object, led by Captain Thomas F. Mantell. He took his fighter into a steep climb. His wingmen, lacking sufficient oxygen equipment, did not follow him up, and they tried to reach him by radio to ask that he level off.

Mantell kept climbing. Investigators concluded that at around 25,000 feet he lost consciousness from lack of oxygen, and his Mustang began spiraling back toward the ground. At 3:50 p.m., the tower at Godman received word that his plane had crashed on a farm south of Franklin, Kentucky, near the Tennessee line.

## A Veteran of D-Day

Mantell was no novice. Born on June 30, 1922, he graduated from Male High School in Louisville and joined the United States Army Air Corps, the forerunner of the Air Force, on June 16, 1942. He finished flight school on June 30, 1943, his 21st birthday.

In World War II he flew C-47 Skytrain transports with the 96th Troop Carrier Squadron of the 440th Troop Carrier Group, the unit that air-dropped the 101st Airborne Division into Normandy on June 6, 1944. He received the Distinguished Flying Cross for heroism while piloting a C-47 named Vulture's Delight and towing a glider under heavy anti-aircraft fire. He had come through the skies over Normandy. He died in the sky over his home state less than four years later.

## Rumors Take Flight

Newspapers across the nation reported the crash, and it drew significant press attention. Sensational rumors spread alongside the coverage. UFO historian Curtis Peebles collected some of them: that the flying saucer was a Soviet missile; that it was an alien spacecraft that shot Mantell down when he got too close; that his body was found riddled with bullets; that the body was missing; that the plane had completely disintegrated in the air; that the wreckage was radioactive.

Investigators found no evidence for most of these rumors, and the Air Force rejected the radioactivity claim, though accident accounts do say the Mustang broke up during its dive. Together the rumors show how quickly a pilot's death could be turned into a legend, and they help explain why the case became one of the most publicized UFO reports of its era.

## What Was Really Up There

The first explanation pointed to the planet Venus, which had been in the same part of the sky. Astronomer J. Allen Hynek, then a consultant to the Air Force's Project Sign, reached that conclusion in 1948, but he later retracted it, concluding that Venus was not bright enough to have been seen by Mantell and the other witnesses, and that considerable haze would have obscured it further.

The better answer is probably a balloon. Project Blue Book, the Air Force UFO study that began in 1952, concluded that the object was most likely a Skyhook balloon, part of a top-secret project that Mantell would not have known about. These massive balloons rose to tens of thousands of feet, and this one was likely launched from Clinton County Air Force Base. A witness in Madisonville, Kentucky, identified the object as a balloon through a telescope, and Vanderbilt University astronomer Carl K. Seyfert, watching through binoculars as it drifted south of Nashville, described "a pear-shaped balloon with cables and a basket attached." Skyhook balloons were behind many UFO reports in the 1940s and 1950s.

## Flight Context

The crash site lies at approximately 36.67°N, 86.56°W, on farmland south of Franklin, Kentucky, a few miles from the Tennessee line. It is a story best remembered at altitude: Mantell's death is a classic lesson in hypoxia and in why supplemental oxygen matters on any high climb. A pass at 2,500–3,500 feet AGL over the fields south of Franklin marks the area. Bowling Green-Warren County Regional (KBWG) is about 19 nm to the north-northeast, and Nashville International (KBNA) about 33 nm to the south. Portland Municipal Airport in Tennessee (FAA identifier 1M5, no ICAO code listed) is about 6 nm to the southeast. Godman Army Airfield at Fort Knox (KFTK), where the tower watched the object, is about 80 nm to the north-northeast and is a military field.
