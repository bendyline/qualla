# Lynching of Ell Persons

In May 1917 a Memphis newspaper announced the time and place of a lynching, about 5,000 people came to watch Ell Persons burned alive without a trial, and the city's Black leaders answered by building one of the first NAACP chapters in the South.

**Location:** 35.1600, -89.8810

**Categories:** History of Memphis, Tennessee, African-American history in Memphis, Tennessee, Lynching deaths in Tennessee, 1917 in Tennessee, Civil rights

## Introduction

On the morning of 22 May 1917, readers of The Commercial Appeal in Memphis did not have to guess where the crowd would gather. The newspaper printed it: the lynching would take place between 9:00 and 9:30 a.m., near the bridge at the Wolf River, out along Macon Road. By nine o'clock the road was packed with automobiles. About 5,000 people came to watch Ell Persons, a woodcutter of about fifty, burned alive without ever standing trial. The historian Kenneth T. Jackson would later call it one of the most vicious lynchings in American history. What followed in Memphis, though, was not silence.

## A Girl Who Never Came Home

The story began with a real loss. Antoinette Rappel, fifteen years old, was a student at Treadwell School in Memphis. On the morning of 30 April 1917 she left for school and did not return. A newspaper briefly reported that she had run off to join the war, a story her mother, Mrs Wood, reportedly believed. Then Antoinette was found dead in woods near Macon Road, with evidence she had been raped; she had been decapitated with an axe. The press described her in terms meant to reach every household, as a girl who "must have reminded many readers of their own daughters, nieces, or cousins." Her killing was a genuine horror, and her family's grief was real. The woods where she was found lay half a mile from the home of Ell Persons.

## Evidence Read in a Dead Girl's Eyes

Police arrested several Black men before bringing in Persons. For 24 hours officers subjected him to brutal treatment, with Sheriff Mike Tate, a deputy and two city detectives taking turns firing questions at him, after which police said he had confessed. Eager to prove his guilt, Tate had Antoinette's body exhumed so her pupils could be photographed, on the belief, drawn from the ideas of the French biometrics researcher Alphonse Bertillon, that a dead person's eyes preserved the last image they saw. Eye specialists told the authorities this was impossible. The authorities said they saw Persons there anyway. He was taken by train to the Tennessee State Prison in Nashville to await trial, and his guards met angry mobs at nearly every city along the way.

## The Train Back to Memphis

On 19 May, Tate ordered Persons returned for a trial set for 25 May. Two days earlier, county criminal court judges had tried and failed to persuade Governor Thomas Clarke Rye to send men to protect him. The press had been predicting that unofficial action would be taken. A mob had already stormed Memphis police headquarters looking for him, and when that failed they began searching trains bound for the city. On 21 May they took him from one. The capture was planned and, reportedly, anticipated by the authorities; researchers Margaret Vandiver and Michel Coconis found no evidence that officials tried to get him back or stop what came next. Among the planners was David J. Mays, a future attorney and Pulitzer Prize winner, who "howled with excitement" at the news.

## A Crowd in Its Best Clothes

One newspaper likened the scene to a holiday. Many people had stayed overnight. Women wore their best clothes, parents excused children from school, and one teacher counted fifty boys absent. Two trucks of drinks sold out quickly. Antoinette's mother addressed the crowd, asking that Persons suffer "as my little girl suffered, only 10 times worse." Then Persons was chained down, doused with gasoline and set alight while spectators snatched pieces of his clothing and the rope that bound him. His body was decapitated and dismembered, and his remains were scattered across Beale Street, the heart of Black Memphis, where his head was thrown from a car at a group of African Americans. Photographs of it sold as postcards for months. The Commercial Appeal's editorial called the lynching "orderly."

## What Memphis Built in Answer

No one was ever charged. James Weldon Johnson, field secretary of the NAACP, investigated soon afterward and said there was no evidence Persons was guilty. Rabbi William Fineshriber of nearby Temple Israel rallied his congregation to condemn the killing and helped a group of clergymen publish a statement. The deeper answer came from Black Memphians themselves. Robert Church Jr., Bert M. Roddy and other Black businessmen founded a Memphis chapter of the NAACP, one of the first in the South; it counted 53 members on 11 June 1917 and by 1919 was the largest in the South. Lynchings in Memphis are said to have ceased after this one. On the centennial in 2017, Overton High School students who had formed Students Uniting Memphis unveiled a marker on Summer Avenue near the Wolf River, at a prayer service attended by relatives of both Ell Persons and Antoinette Rappel.

## Flight Context

The lynching site lies at approximately 35.16°N, 89.88°W on the northeastern side of Memphis, where the Wolf River winds past Summer Avenue near the old Macon Road crossing; the site is listed on the National Register of Historic Places, and two markers stand nearby, one on Summer Avenue near the river and one at Summer Avenue and Bartlett Road. From 2,500 to 3,500 feet, follow the tree-lined Wolf River corridor as it cuts through the grid of eastern Memphis; the Mississippi River and downtown lie to the west. Beale Street, where Persons' remains were scattered, is in downtown Memphis near the riverfront. Nearby airports include Frederick W. Smith International Airport (KMEM), formerly Memphis International and the city's main airport, to the southwest, which sits under busy Class B airspace; General DeWitt Spain Airport (KM01) just north of downtown; and Charles W. Baker Airport (K2M8) in Millington to the north. Summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are common over the Mississippi Valley.
