# Discovery Park of America

Built on a former soybean field in Union City, Tennessee, Discovery Park of America grew from a volunteer-run county museum into a 50-acre showcase of dinosaurs, earthquakes, ironclads and log cabins that was meant to stop traffic on the highway.

**Location:** 36.4383, -89.0697

**Categories:** Museums in Obion County, Tennessee, Science museums in Tennessee, Natural history museums in Tennessee, History museums in Tennessee, Union City, Tennessee, Museums established in 2013

## Introduction

Robert Kirkland's instructions to his architects fit on a road sign: "design a building that will stop traffic at 70mph." The result rises from what was once a soybean field on the northwest edge of Union City, Tennessee, a museum and 50-acre heritage park that opened in 2013 after nearly ten years of design and construction and roughly $80 million. Inside are dinosaurs, a woolly mammoth, a vampire killing kit and a 48-foot metal giant you can slide through. Its roots, though, lie in something far smaller: a county museum kept alive by volunteers for four decades.

## The Secret Meetings at the Library

The Obion County Museum was founded in 1970 to house the historical collections of the McNatt and Harmon families, and for 40 years it was run entirely by volunteers. In 2005 Kirkland, a local businessman who had cofounded the Kirkland's chain of home decor stores, approached its board about building something much larger. He and his wife, Jenny D. Kirkland, had sold their stake in the parent company to focus on local philanthropy. Kirkland met secretly at the Obion County Library with businessman Jack Hudgens and museum board members Larry Mink and Polly Brasher to form a Vision Team. The secrecy was deliberate: if the idea proved unworkable, the public would never have been disappointed. Once it moved forward, the team insisted the community help build it, forming eighteen committees made up mostly of local volunteers.

## An Architect Lost, a Trade Show Rescue

Ground was broken on July 1, 2008, with Canadian architect Douglas Cardinal designing both the building and its exhibits and completion expected in 2010. A year later, on July 8, 2009, Kirkland terminated Cardinal and construction halted. Rescue came from a chance conversation. Months earlier, Polly Brasher, by then the park's founding director, had met Kurt Cederquest of Maltbie Exhibit Fabricators at a trade show in Philadelphia. Hearing of the dismissal, Cederquest persuaded Verner Johnson Museum Architects of Boston and Thinc Design of New York to join him in a pitch, and the three firms won the contract in January 2010. Lou Sirianni designed the main building and Tom Henness the galleries. Beneath it all sit 2,000 tons of steel and 1,056 geopiers, making the building resistant to the occasional small earthquakes this region still feels.

## A Cabinet of Wonders

The three-floor Discovery Center holds nine permanent galleries. In Dinosaur Hall stand six full-size dinosaur skeleton casts, among them Apatosaurus and Tyrannosaurus rex, alongside mosasaurs and a megalodon jaw. A 12,000-year-old woolly mammoth skeleton stands in the Native American Gallery, which displays 4,812 of the museum's 8,400 Native American artifacts along a timeline from the Paleoindian to the Historic Period, billed as one of the largest exhibits of its kind in the eastern United States. Upstairs sits a 640-pound fragment of the Nantan meteorite. The Enlightenment Gallery plays the role of a cabinet of curiosities, with a suit of armor, a replica Rosetta Stone and that vampire killing kit, many pieces from Kirkland's own collection. Car lovers have found a 1929 Dual-Cowl Lincoln Sport Phaeton described by the museum as the most pristine of its kind known to exist, plus Dale Earnhardt Jr.'s 2007 NASCAR Chevrolet.

## The Lake the Earthquakes Made

Local ground has its own drama, and the Regional History Gallery tells it. An Earthquake Simulator room explains how nearby Reelfoot Lake formed during the New Madrid earthquakes of 1811 and 1812, and hundreds of artifacts trace the lake's story. Snakes, turtles, salamanders and frogs live in the gallery, and a 20,000-gallon aquarium holds fish native to the region. Even the café carries the theme: Sabin's Cafe honors Verne and Nonie Sabin, whose photographs of Reelfoot Lake brought it to national attention in the 1920s. For the widest view, climb Cooper Tower, reached through Dinosaur Hall. From its 120-foot observation deck you look down on the heritage park and Union City, and on a clear day the horizon stretches about 12 miles.

## Fifty Acres of Yesterday

Outside, landscape architects from Memphis shaped two small lakes joined by a moat and a terraced series of weirs, surrounded by gardens and a hedge maze. On the north side, eleven 19th-century log cabins form The Settlement, filled with artifacts of 19th-century chores such as weaving, cooking and making soap and candles. Mill Ridge adds an 1890s one-room schoolhouse, a blacksmith shop and a grist mill with a waterwheel. One building arrived by road: the chapel, built in 1897 as New Chapel Methodist Church in nearby Elbridge, was moved to the park on a tractor trailer. Near the Depot stands a 1913 Swedish State Railways steam engine, No. 1149, and STEM Landing displays a Grumman F-11 Tiger and a retired Titan I ICBM that NASA donated. The site was chosen partly for the planned Interstate 69 corridor nearby, whose Union City bypass opened in early 2024, putting day trips within easy reach of Memphis, St. Louis and Evansville.

## Flight Context

Discovery Park of America sits at about 36.44°N, 89.07°W on the northwest edge of Union City in northwestern Tennessee, just off Everett Boulevard near US 51 and the Future I-69 corridor. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet, look for the large Discovery Center and its 120-foot Cooper Tower beside two small lakes, with the log cabins, mill and chapel of the heritage park spread around them. Reelfoot Lake lies roughly 15 to 20 miles to the west, and the Kentucky state line a few miles to the north. The closest field is Everett-Stewart Regional Airport (KUCY), about six miles southeast of the park. Flat Mississippi Valley farmland gives good visibility on clear days, though summer haze and spring thunderstorms are common.
