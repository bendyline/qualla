# Billy Tripp

In Brownsville, Tennessee, outsider artist and writer Billy Tripp has spent decades welding The Mindfield, a 127-foot metal sculpture of life, death and memory that he intends to keep building for as long as he can.

**Location:** 35.5930, -89.2666

**Categories:** Outsider art, Sculpture, Artists, Brownsville, Tennessee, Roadside attractions

## Introduction

When Billy Tripp's father died in 2002, Tripp dealt with his grief through a water tower he had hauled from a defunct factory in Kentucky. He reassembled it almost single-handedly, named it "Deena," and made it part of The Mindfield, the enormous metal sculpture he had been building in Brownsville, Tennessee, since 1989. The Mindfield now stands about 127 feet high and stretches the length of a football field. It is billed as the largest sculpture in Tennessee, and its maker has no plans to stop.

## The Minister's Son Learns to Weld

William Blevins Tripp was born in 1955 in Jackson, Tennessee. His father, the Reverend Charles Tripp, was a Methodist minister and the owner of Tripp Country Hams, a nationally recognized store, and the two were close. Billy spent six weeks in welding trade school, just long enough for the mandatory safety training, before teaching himself, and he took art classes at Jackson State Community College and the University of Memphis before leaving to pursue writing, painting, sculpture and poetry. He is often described as an outsider artist, and he works across forms: poet, writer, painter, welder and sculptor.

## Summoned to City Hall

In 1989 Tripp began his lifelong project. The Mindfield drew attention almost immediately. The city ordered him to stop building and summoned him to city hall to explain what, exactly, he was putting up. Local papers including The States Graphic and The Jackson Sun interviewed him, and a Nashville television station covered the story. He kept going. While the structure rose, he was also writing a novel and keeping a journal. The novel became The Mindfield Years Vol. 1: The Sycamore Trees, a stream-of-consciousness autobiography published in 1996, and the journal later became The Mindfield Years Vol. 3: The Mindfield Notes. Characters and events from The Sycamore Trees reappear in the sculpture itself, binding the book and the sculpture together.

## A Cemetery of Memory

Also known as The Mindfield Cemetery, the sculpture is filled with symbols reflecting on life and death and memorializing events from Tripp's own life. Both the water tower and the tallest point in The Mindfield, which he calls his father's grave marker, are dedicated to his parents. Other pieces honor his wife and his favorite authors, and some reflect on beliefs he once held. The work has grown steadily for more than three decades, and Tripp intends to keep expanding it until he is no longer physically able. The ending is already planned: the city of Brownsville has given him permission to be interred within his sculpture. The Mindfield has been documented by institutions including the Smithsonian Institution and SPACES Archives, and the Kohler Foundation is to preserve it after his death.

## A Life Written in Volumes

The Mindfield is one record of Tripp's life; his books are another. His writing is gathered under the title The Mindfield Years. After The Sycamore Trees came the multi-part Mindfield Notes, drawn from the notebooks he kept while building, and a fourth volume whose title, My Year of Deena, Goodbye to Dad [And to Mom], ties it to the water tower and to his parents. His first diaries, published as a pre-volume to the series, carry the subtitle Requiem for a Young Man. His blend of sculpture and self-portrait has drawn scholarly attention abroad as well: Marc Décimo devoted a chapter, "Les autofictions de Billy Tripp," to him in his French-language book Les Jardins de l'art brut, published in Dijon in 2017.

## Flight Context

The Mindfield stands in Brownsville, Haywood County, Tennessee, at about 35.593N, 89.267W. At 127 feet it rises above most of its surroundings in this largely flat part of West Tennessee; from 1,500-2,000 feet, look for its tall metal structures and the "Deena" water tower in town. Covington Municipal Airport (FAA identifier M04) is about 16 nm west, and Jackson Regional Airport (KMKL, formerly McKellar-Sipes Regional) is about 17 nm east. Summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are common.
