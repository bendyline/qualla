# Battle of Island Number Ten

In the spring of 1862 a Mississippi sandbar at the tight New Madrid Bend held back the Union advance for weeks, until a gunboat slipped past its batteries in a thunderstorm and the Confederate garrison found itself trapped.

**Location:** 36.4588, -89.4708

**Categories:** Battles of the western theater of the American Civil War, Naval battles of the American Civil War, Sieges of the American Civil War, Lake County, Tennessee, New Madrid County, Missouri, 1862 in the American Civil War

## Introduction

Island Number Ten got its name by simple counting: it was once the tenth island in the Mississippi River below the mouth of the Ohio. It was never much of an island, just an enlarged sandbar roughly a mile long and standing about ten feet above low water, the kind of thing the river builds and erases on its own schedule. Yet for nearly six weeks in the spring of 1862 this scrap of sand at the New Madrid Bend, on the border of Missouri and Tennessee, stood between the Union and the lower Mississippi.

## A River That Doubles Back

What made the island formidable was the river around it. Here the Mississippi swings clockwise through 180 degrees and then immediately counterclockwise, so that it ends up flowing almost parallel to its original course, shifted about eight miles west. The double bend is so tight that its two limits are only nine miles apart by air but twelve by water. Island Number Ten sat at the southern tip of the first turn, where boats coming downstream had to approach bow-on and slow down to make the curve. The defenders had one weakness. Behind them lay lakes, sloughs and swamps, the largest being Reelfoot Lake, and the only dry route in or out was a single road on the natural levee to Tiptonville, Tennessee. Cut that road, and the garrison would be isolated.

## Guns at the Bend

Confederate engineer Captain Asa B. Gray began laying out batteries in August 1861, but the work was starved of workers and equipment while attention went to Columbus, Kentucky. That changed in February 1862, when Forts Henry and Donelson fell and General P. G. T. Beauregard ordered Columbus abandoned and its garrison moved to the island. Under Brigadier General John P. McCown the bend became a gauntlet. By mid-March there were 24 guns in five batteries on the shore above the island, 19 more in five batteries on the island itself, and a floating battery called New Orleans, carrying nine guns, moored at its western end. Two forts guarded New Madrid, Missouri, and Flag Officer George N. Hollins kept six unarmored gunboats on the river below.

## A Winter March and a Canal

Union Major General John Pope ignored the custom of waiting out winter. He marched his Army of the Mississippi, then about 10,000 men, across Missouri, corduroying muddy roads as he went, and reached New Madrid on March 3. Siege guns arrived on the 12th and opened fire the next day. That night, under cover of a heavy rainstorm, McCown abandoned the town and its forts, leaving spiked guns behind. Flag Officer Andrew Hull Foote's gunboats and fourteen mortar rafts, each carrying a single 13-inch mortar, arrived upstream on March 15, but the bombardment did little harm. Foote, in pain and on crutches from a wound received at Fort Donelson, argued that his boats were not invincible and refused to risk them. So Pope's men cut a canal across the neck of land east of New Madrid; too shallow for gunboats, it still let transports through.

## Carondelet in the Thunderstorm

At a council of war on March 29, Commander Henry Walke volunteered to take USS Carondelet past the batteries. His crew wrapped her in rope, chain and whatever loose material lay at hand, lashed a barge loaded with coal and hay to her side, and diverted her steam exhaust away from the smokestacks to muffle the noise. Raiders from the flotilla and the 42nd Illinois Infantry spiked the guns of Battery No. 1 on April 1, and the next day Union fire cut the floating battery loose and sent it drifting downstream. On the moonless night of April 4, with a thunderstorm rolling in, Carondelet slipped downriver undetected until soot in her stacks, no longer dampened by steam, caught fire and blazed up. The batteries opened on her, but their fire was inaccurate, and she came through unscathed. A second gunboat followed two nights later.

## The Footrace to Tiptonville

Covered by the two gunboats, Pope's army crossed the river on April 7 at Watson's Landing without opposition. The new Confederate commander, Brigadier General William W. Mackall, started his mainland troops toward Tiptonville, and what might have been a battle became a footrace. Pope's men got there first, and Mackall surrendered; the island's garrison gave up separately to Foote. Pope reported nearly 7,000 prisoners, almost certainly a great exaggeration: incomplete Confederate records suggest no more than 5,350 men were present. More than a thousand captives, about 1,400 by one count, were sent north to Camp Randall in Madison, Wisconsin, where roughly 140 died that spring amid unsanitary conditions. Because April 7 was also the second day of Shiloh, the victory faded from headlines. Yet it is usually described as the first time the Confederate Army lost a position on the Mississippi River in battle, and Carondelet's run showed that steam-powered ships could simply sail past fixed defenses.

## Flight Context

The battle site is centered near 36.46°N, 89.47°W on the Mississippi River's New Madrid Bend, where the river's tight double loop wraps around the Kentucky Bend exclave; the island itself has largely eroded away and is no landmark today, so navigate by the unmistakable S-shaped meanders. From 4,000 to 6,000 feet the whole bend fits in one view: New Madrid, Missouri, sits at the northern apex of the second loop, Tiptonville, Tennessee, lies to the south, and the broad, shallow expanse of Reelfoot Lake spreads to the southeast. Nearby fields include Reelfoot Lake Airport (K0M2) at Tiptonville and County Memorial Airport (KEIW) at New Madrid. Expect river haze on summer mornings and fast-building thunderstorms in the Mississippi Valley during spring.
