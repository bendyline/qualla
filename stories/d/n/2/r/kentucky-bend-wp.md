# Kentucky Bend

A loop of the Mississippi and a surveyor's estimate left a sliver of Kentucky stranded between Tennessee and Missouri, reachable by road only through another state.

**Location:** 36.5350, -89.5104

**Categories:** Border irregularities of the United States, Subnational exclaves in the United States, Mississippi River, Geography of Kentucky, Borders of Tennessee

## Introduction

To drive into this piece of Kentucky, you have to go through Tennessee first. Tennessee State Route 22 is the only highway into the Kentucky Bend, the area's mailing address is Tiptonville, Tennessee, and the town of New Madrid, Missouri, sits directly across the river to the north with no road or ferry linking the two. Wrapped inside a tight oxbow loop of the Mississippi River and encircled by Tennessee and Missouri, this exclave of Fulton County is the extreme southwestern corner of Kentucky. Locals and mapmakers have called it many things over the years: the New Madrid Bend, Madrid Bend, Bessie Bend, even Bubbleland. Its existence as part of Kentucky at all comes down to one old uncertainty about where the river actually ran.

## A Line Drawn Before Anyone Knew the River

The boundary that created this oddity is older than both of the states it divides. It traces back to the Royal Colonial Boundary of 1665, drawn to sort out the overlapping inland claims of the Colony of Virginia and the Province of Carolina. When Kentucky separated from Virginia and Tennessee from North Carolina, the line came with them. The trouble lay at its western end. Surveyors marking the Kentucky–Tennessee boundary had only estimated where their line would meet the Mississippi. Later, more detailed surveys showed that the line actually crossed the river where it swings through north–south bends, cutting a peninsula in two. Because the Mississippi forms both Kentucky's western border and Missouri's eastern one, the northern portion of that peninsula became a detached notch of Kentucky, while the Tennessee side stayed joined to its state by land. Tennessee did not accept this quietly. It claimed the Bend as part of Obion County until at least 1848, and the two states kept disagreeing until the Cox-Peebles survey of 1858-59 brought a compromise.

## The Year the River Ran Backward

In 1812 this stretch of the Mississippi was thrown into chaos. The New Madrid earthquakes of 1811 and 1812, some of the most powerful ever felt in the United States, disrupted the river so badly that it was reported to flow backward. One of the Bend's names, the New Madrid Bend, still ties it to the town across the water whose name those earthquakes carry. Half a century later the river brought a different kind of upheaval. From February 28 to April 8, 1862, the waters from Island Number Ten, just to the east, around to New Madrid became the site of the Civil War's Battle of Island Number Ten. The peninsula that looks so quiet from above has spent much of its recorded history in the path of forces far larger than itself.

## Cotton Fields and a Feud Twain Remembered

The river's floodplain gave the Bend highly productive soil, and it was developed as a major cotton-producing area. The 1870 census recorded more than 300 residents. Writing in The West Tennessee Farm, a 1979 volume edited by Marvin Downing, Norman L. Parks reported a population of 303 in 1880, 18 of them African American, though other sources put the 1880 peak at 332. By 1900, large numbers of Black laborers were working in the Bend to plant and harvest the cotton. Mark Twain found material here as well. In Life on the Mississippi, he described the six-decade-long feud between the Darnell and Watson families, along with other elements of life in the bend, fixing this isolated loop of farmland in American literature.

## Nine People and a Notch on the Map

Today the Bend is nearly empty. At the 2020 census, the Kentucky Bend Census County Division counted just nine people. The area covers 69.6 square kilometers, of which about 45 are land; more than a third is water, most of it the Mississippi itself. Kentucky's southern boundary line crosses the peninsula at the riverbank, and the continuation of Tennessee State Route 22 into the Bend was once signed as Kentucky State Route 313. New Madrid may look close enough to shout to, but anyone needing to cross the river must head for the Dorena–Hickman Ferry, which shuts down in high or low water and for dredging, or the Caruthersville Bridge in nearby Dyer County. For those nine residents, being Kentuckians means a long way around.

## Heat, Floodwater and the Occasional Freeze

Like the nearby Missouri Bootheel and West Tennessee, the Bend has a humid subtropical climate with hot, humid summers and chilly though not severe winters. The records below are as a weather-data service reports them for the Bend, so they may reflect a nearby station rather than the exclave itself. Winter can swing from mild and wet when Gulf of Mexico air takes hold to cold, dry and windy under northerly winds, as in the famously cold January of 1977, which also brought 13.1 inches of snow, the most ever recorded in a single month. On average, 82 nights a year fall to freezing or below, while nearly three days top 100°F; the hottest reading was 107°F on July 1, 1952. Rain comes heavily and year-round, fed by moist Gulf air and the occasional remnant of a hurricane drifting up the Mississippi Valley. On September 23, 2006, 11.38 inches fell in a single day. In October 1964, by contrast, not a drop fell at all.

## Flight Context

The Kentucky Bend lies at approximately 36.535°N, 89.510°W, inside a tight horseshoe loop of the Mississippi River where Kentucky, Tennessee and Missouri meet. From 3,000–5,000 feet AGL the oxbow loop is an unmistakable reference: the river wraps almost completely around the peninsula, with the town of New Madrid, Missouri, on the bank directly to the north. The only road in, Tennessee State Route 22, enters from the Tennessee side. Nearby airports: County Memorial Airport, New Madrid, MO (KEIW), about 5 miles away; Reelfoot Lake Airport, Tiptonville, TN (FAA identifier 0M2, no ICAO code), about 10 miles; and Sikeston Memorial Municipal Airport (KSIK), about 25 miles. Expect summer haze and humidity, and heavy rainfall possible in any season.
