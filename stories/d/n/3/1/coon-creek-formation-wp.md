# Coon Creek Formation

In the sandy marl of western Tennessee and northeast Mississippi, a Late Cretaceous seafloor some 70 to 76 million years old gives up shells so well preserved that many are still made of their original material.

**Location:** 35.3336, -88.4305

**Categories:** Geologic formations of Mississippi, Cretaceous Tennessee, Upper Cretaceous Series of North America, Natural history of Tennessee, Fossil sites, Paleontology

## Introduction

Most fossils are stone copies, the original shell or bone long since dissolved and replaced by minerals. Many shells of the Coon Creek Formation are the real thing. Sealed in clay for some 70 to 76 million years, depending on the dating, the snails, clams and crabs of this Late Cretaceous seafloor were largely spared by groundwater, so they survive in a largely original state. The formation runs through western Tennessee and extreme northeast Mississippi and takes its name from Coon Creek in McNairy County, Tennessee, where the sandy marl is so thick with fossils that collectors often find them lying on top of one another.

## A Sea Where Memphis Stands

The story begins with a trough. The Mississippi Embayment, which runs roughly north to south from central Mississippi to southern Illinois, formed, in one widely discussed hypothesis, after the North American continent, driven by tectonic forces, passed over a magma hotspot. The region had already been weakened by rifts and faults during the breakup of the supercontinent Rodinia, hundreds of millions of years earlier. On that account, pressure from the hotspot pushed the land up nearly 2 kilometers in an arch about 300 kilometers wide, and erosion carried nearly all of that uplift away. Once the continent moved past the hotspot, the cooling land sank into a deep trough, and seawater poured in. Where Memphis now stands, the water may have been 1,000 feet deep. In the Late Cretaceous, western Tennessee, eastern Arkansas, western Kentucky and southeast Missouri all lay beneath this arm of the Gulf of Mexico. Coon Creek formed in its shallows; a 2023 U.S. Geological Survey study puts the water depth there at roughly 115 to 200 feet (35 to 60 meters), deeper than older estimates of under 100 feet.

## Driftwood at the Bottom of the Food Chain

Inland to the east lay a marshy lowland bordering the limestone bluffs of the Western Highland Rim, a heavily forested country home to duckbill and theropod dinosaurs. Sluggish rivers washed tons of driftwood into the bay every year, along with the occasional dinosaur carcass, and that wood fed an entire ecosystem. Bacteria and other microscopic scavengers ate the decaying wood. Plankton ate the bacteria, clams filtered the plankton, snails ate the clams, and crabs and fish ate the snails, while mosasaurs and cephalopods hunted the fish and crabs. The climate was semi-tropical, like present-day southern Florida, with warm water of normal salinity kept well oxygenated by the waves. Scallops, whelks, lobsters, nautilus and sharks shared the bay with less familiar creatures: highly ornamented cephalopods, marine crocodiles, sea turtles, plesiosaurs and fanged fishes. Burrowing clams and shrimps churned the bottom so thoroughly that the deposit shows no distinct layering, and periodic hurricanes may have dumped heavy loads of river sediment that buried the living seafloor.

## Clay That Kept Its Secrets

Most organisms never become fossils at all. Unless they are buried quickly, their bodies are eaten or weathered away, and even buried shells can later be dissolved by groundwater. At Coon Creek, the clay in the sediment sealed the fragile remains off from water, and the hard parts of clams, snails, crabs and shrimps came through intact. Sometimes vertebrate bones turn up, along with the cartilaginous vertebrae of sharks, fish scales and plant leaves. That combination has made Coon Creek a recognized Konservat-Lagerstätte, a site of exceptional preservation, and it has been described as one of the country's top twelve fossil sites. The reasons are easy to list: hundreds of species (the University of Tennessee at Martin counts well over 400, and some tallies run above 600), one of the highest fossil densities in eastern North America, and sediment so unconsolidated that the fossils are easy to collect and prepare. Among the celebrities are the spiral gastropod Turritella and the bivalve Pterotrigonia thoracica, the state fossil of Tennessee.

## Monsters and Missing Dinosaurs

The top predators here were mosasaurs, and the remains of at least two have been found. They were not dinosaurs but large, carnivorous aquatic lizards that could reach 45 feet in length. Pterosaur fossils, mainly azhdarchids such as Arambourgiania, have also come out of the formation. True dinosaurs remain elusive. As of 2016, the only known dinosaurs from this region were indeterminate hadrosaur remains, although dinosaur bones and teeth have turned up in marine deposits in Mississippi, and it is possible that some will one day surface at Coon Creek. Pinning down the age of it all falls to index fossils, species that lived across wide areas for geologically short spans. The cephalopod Jeletzkytes nodosus appears in rocks a little younger than 70.6 million years old in the western United States, while other Coon Creek index fossils date a little older, and that overlap long pointed to deposition between 70 and 71 million years ago. A 2023 U.S. Geological Survey study of microfossils instead dates the Coon Creek type locality to the late Campanian, about 76.8 to 76.0 million years ago, so published ages range from roughly 70 to 76 million years. The unit itself is alternately treated as its own formation or as a member of the wider Ripley Formation, in which case it goes by the Coon Creek Member or Coon Creek Tongue.

## Flight Context

Listed coordinates: 35.334 N, 88.431 W, the Coon Creek Science Center near Adamsville in McNairy County, Tennessee, the namesake locality of the Coon Creek Formation, which also crops out in northeast Mississippi. Fossil beds are not visible from the air; enjoy the rolling, wooded country of the old embayment margin at 3,000-4,500 ft. Nearby airports: Robert Sibley Airport at Selmer (FAA identifier SZY) is about 8.5 nm south-southwest, Savannah-Hardin County Airport (FAA identifier SNH) about 14 nm southeast, and Roscoe Turner Airport at Corinth, Mississippi (KCRX) about 26 nm south-southwest. Expect summer haze and pop-up afternoon thunderstorms.
