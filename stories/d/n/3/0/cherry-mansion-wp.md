# Cherry Mansion

On a bluff above the Tennessee River in Savannah, the white Georgian house where Ulysses S. Grant was sitting down to breakfast when the Battle of Shiloh began became a place where two Union generals came to die.

**Location:** 35.2256, -88.2567

**Categories:** Houses on the National Register of Historic Places in Tennessee, Houses in Hardin County, Tennessee, Houses completed in 1830, Historic American Buildings Survey in Tennessee, 1830 establishments in Tennessee, National Register of Historic Places in Hardin County, Tennessee

## Introduction

Breakfast was on the table when the guns started. According to Cherry family accounts, General Ulysses S. Grant and his staff had just sat down to eat on the morning of April 6, 1862, when they heard distant cannon fire. The Battle of Shiloh had begun, about nine miles up the Tennessee River by water. The officers went down to the landing below the house, boarded a steamboat and headed for the fighting, leaving their meals uneaten. The house they left behind, a white Georgian mansion on a bluff above the river's east bank in Savannah, Tennessee, is Cherry Mansion.

## Headquarters on the Bluff

For about seven weeks in the spring of 1862, from March 13 to April 29, this private home served as headquarters for a succession of Union generals: Grant, C. F. Smith, Don Carlos Buell and W. H. L. Wallace. Those dates bracket the Battle of Shiloh, so the house saw the army both before the fighting and after it. The landing below the bluff tied the headquarters to the river, and it was from there that Grant and his staff set off by steamboat on the morning the battle began. The house's role changed once the guns fell silent. Two of the generals who had made their headquarters here would never leave it.

## Two Generals Who Stayed

Generals Smith and Wallace both died in the house in the aftermath of Shiloh. Smith never reached the battle. Before the battle he suffered a serious leg injury while jumping into a rowboat, and the wound kept him out of the fight. Infection and then dysentery followed, and he died at Savannah on April 25, 1862. Wallace's story is harder still. A new division commander, he held his ground for six hours beside the Hornet's Nest before his division was surrounded. He was wounded during the withdrawal and was later found barely alive on the battlefield by his own troops. By a cruel coincidence, his wife had arrived that very morning for a surprise visit. She helped tend him on the way back to Grant's headquarters here, and he died three days later in her arms. His last words were "We meet in heaven."

## Older Than the House

Long before any general arrived, people lived on this bluff. The house sits atop the site of a palisaded prehistoric settlement whose first mounds, built by Native peoples, date back some 2,000 years; the site was occupied again centuries later. Later construction largely obliterated those mounds. The mansion itself was built by David Robinson, one of Hardin County's early settlers, who owned extensive acreage on both sides of the Tennessee River. Most accounts date the house to about 1830, though an analysis for the Historic American Buildings Survey suggests it was not finished until around 1849, and it later passed to the Cherry family, whose name it carries and who kept it until 1935.

## Porticoes Lost and Found

Cherry Mansion faces west toward the river, with a two-level porch across that front and a series of terraces stepping down the slope to the water. The house has been reshaped more than once. In the late 19th century the Cherry family replaced some of the original porticoes with woodwork in the Victorian style. The next owner, Bob Guinn, set out in 1935 and 1936 to put them back as they had been, relying on the memories of living members of the Cherry family. That work was part of a larger restoration and remodeling designed by Memphis architect Hubert T. McGee, which added a new kitchen and bathrooms, replaced the weatherboard on the east wall with brick and gave more emphasis to the west front. The house was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1977. It remains in private hands and is best seen from the sidewalk, where interpretive markers stand; Hardin County tourism says touring inside is not allowed, though the lawn opens to ticket holders for an annual Generals Breakfast near the Shiloh anniversary.

## Flight Context

Cherry Mansion is at 35.2256°N, 88.2567°W on a bluff on the east bank of the Tennessee River at Savannah, Tennessee. From 1,500–2,500 feet AGL, follow the river to Savannah and look for a white house facing west over the water, with terraces running down to the bank. The Shiloh battlefield lies about 7 miles to the southwest, roughly 9 by river. Savannah-Hardin County Airport (FAA SNH, GPS KSNH) is about 3.9 nm south-southeast, and Robert Sibley Airport at Selmer (FAA SZY) is about 12 nm west. The nearest larger fields are Jackson Regional (KMKL, formerly McKellar-Sipes Regional), about 39 nm northwest, and Northwest Alabama Regional (KMSL) at Muscle Shoals, about 43 nm southeast. River fog can linger on cool mornings; summer brings haze and afternoon storms.
