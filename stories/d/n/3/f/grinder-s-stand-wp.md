# Grinder's Stand

At a two-cabin inn on the Natchez Trace in 1809, Meriwether Lewis died of gunshot wounds in circumstances still disputed, with Thomas Jefferson believing it was suicide and Lewis's family suspecting murder.

**Location:** 35.5094, -87.4575

**Categories:** Civilian Conservation Corps in Tennessee, Buildings and structures in Lewis County, Tennessee, Natchez Trace

## Introduction

More than two centuries later, nobody can say for certain what happened here. In the predawn dark of October 11, 1809, at a rough log inn on the Natchez Trace, Meriwether Lewis was fatally shot. His friend Thomas Jefferson suspected the explorer had taken his own life. Lewis's family suspected he had been murdered. The inn was Grinder's Stand, and a replica cabin now stands at Meriwether Lewis Park on the Natchez Trace Parkway in Lewis County, Tennessee, east of Hohenwald, southwest of Columbia and south of Nashville.

## Indian Line Stand

The stand is believed to have opened in 1807, and it was first known as Indian Line Stand. The name made sense. The Chickasaw had ceded this land only in 1805, and according to tradition, Native American lands came within a few feet of the cabin. The proprietors were Robert Evans Griner and Priscilla Knight Griner, so by rights the place should be called Griner's Stand. Soon after it opened, though, travelers began calling it Grinder's Stand, and the extra letter stuck. It has carried the mistaken name ever since. A stand was a frontier inn, and this one was simple. Two rough log cabins met at a right angle, with an open dogtrot passage between them. Both rooms had doors facing the Natchez Trace, and one also had a door toward the detached kitchen behind. A barn and stable stood somewhere on the property, though no one has determined exactly where. The stand's proper business was feeding and lodging travelers on the Trace. Tradition holds that Robert Griner also sold whiskey to his Native American neighbors across the nearby line.

## A Governor on the Road

Lewis came to this remote stand as a public figure. He had co-led the Lewis and Clark Expedition of 1803 to 1806, and in 1807 Jefferson appointed him governor of the Louisiana Territory, where he settled in St. Louis. By 1809 he was locked in a payment dispute with the War Department over drafts he had drawn as governor, and on September 3, 1809, Lewis set out for Washington, D.C., to resolve the matter. His route brought him down the Trace to the Griners' stand. In the early morning hours of October 11, Priscilla Griner heard gunshots. Lewis was found badly wounded, and he died shortly after sunrise.

## An Argument That Will Not Rest

What happened in that cabin has been argued over ever since. Jefferson, Lewis's friend and colleague, suspected suicide. Lewis's family suspected homicide. Lewis was buried near the stand, and in 1848 the State of Tennessee erected a monument over his grave. Lemuel Kirby, a stonemason from Columbia, chose the design of a broken column, commonly used at the time to symbolize a life cut short. The question never went away. From 1993 to 2010, relatives of Lewis sought to have his body exhumed for forensic analysis. The Department of the Interior approved an exhumation in 2008, then rescinded that decision in 2010 and declared it final. Lewis remains where he was buried, and the mystery remains with him. Only remains of the original stand survive. In the 1930s the Civilian Conservation Corps built a replica cabin less than 20 feet from the remains of the original. No reliable description of the original could be found, so it is an approximation, but it gives visitors a sense of the modest place where Lewis spent his last night. The site is now part of Meriwether Lewis Park on the Natchez Trace Parkway, in the county named for him.

## Flight Context

Grinder's Stand and Meriwether Lewis Park are at 35.5094°N, 87.4575°W on the Natchez Trace Parkway in Lewis County, Tennessee, east of Hohenwald. The parkway is the best visual guide: follow its corridor at around 2,000 feet AGL to the park. Paul Bridges Field at Hohenwald (FAA 0M3) is about 7 nm west-northwest, and Maury County Regional Airport (KMRC) near Columbia and Mount Pleasant is about 14 nm east. Centerville Municipal (KGHM) is roughly 20 nm north. Nashville International (KBNA) lies about 53 nm northeast. Watch for morning valley fog in cooler months, and for haze and afternoon storms in summer.
