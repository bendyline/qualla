# 1963 Camden PA-24 crash

On a gusty, overcast March evening in 1963, a small Piper Comanche carrying Patsy Cline, Hawkshaw Hawkins and Cowboy Copas home to Nashville went down in the woods west of Camden, Tennessee, after its pilot flew into weather he was not trained to fly through.

**Categories:** Aviation accidents and incidents in Tennessee, Aviation accidents and incidents in the United States in 1963, Aviation accidents and incidents caused by pilot error, Benton County, Tennessee, Country music

## Introduction

At Dyersburg, Tennessee, just after five in the afternoon on March 5, 1963, Randy Hughes asked for a weather briefing. It was not encouraging. Conditions were marginal for visual flight, and Nashville, his destination, was reporting weather below minimums. Hughes said he would fly east toward the Tennessee River and navigate to Nashville from there, and he talked about how well he knew the terrain. Behind him in the small Piper Comanche sat three of country music's stars: Patsy Cline, Hawkshaw Hawkins and Cowboy Copas. They were trying to get home.

## A Benefit in Kansas City

The trip began with a benefit show. On March 3, Cline performed at the Soldiers and Sailors Memorial Hall in Kansas City, Kansas, for the family of disc jockey "Cactus" Jack Call. She was 30 years old, born Virginia Patterson Hensley, "Walkin' After Midnight" had been her first major hit, "I Fall to Pieces" her first to top the country chart, and "Crazy" her biggest pop hit. Traveling with her were Harold Franklin "Hawkshaw" Hawkins and Lloyd Estel "Cowboy" Copas. At the controls was Ramsey Dorris "Randy" Hughes, who was both Cline's manager and Copas's son-in-law. Around 2 p.m. on Tuesday, March 5, they left Fairfax Municipal Airport in Kansas City, bound for Nashville, 411 nautical miles to the southeast.

## A Pilot Short on Hours

Hughes owned the Comanche, registration N7000P, and was its only pilot. He had taken possession of it in 1962, less than a year earlier. He held a valid private pilot certificate for single-engine land airplanes but was not rated to fly by instruments, and his logbook showed just 160.2 hours in total, about 44 of them in the Comanche. The flight was planned under visual flight rules, which depend on the pilot keeping sight of the ground. They stopped to refuel at Rogers, Arkansas, and were back in the air 15 minutes later, then landed at Dyersburg at 5:05 p.m. At 6:07 p.m. Hughes took off again. The reported weather at that time was cloudy, with a ceiling of 500 feet, five miles of visibility, a temperature of 43°F and a gusty, turbulent east wind of 20 miles per hour. No one heard from the aircraft again.

## Twenty-Two Minutes

Investigators believe Hughes flew into deteriorating weather and lost sight of the ground. Without that visual reference, spatial disorientation set in, the condition in which a pilot's senses can insist the airplane is level when it is not. The Comanche slid into what pilots call a graveyard spiral, a diving right-hand turn with its nose about 25 degrees down. When the airplane broke out beneath the clouds, Hughes tried to arrest the descent, pulling the nose up and applying full power, but it was too late. Investigators later found evidence the propeller was at maximum speed at impact. At 6:29 p.m., twenty-two minutes after takeoff, the aircraft crashed into a wooded, swampy area a mile north of U.S. Route 70 and five miles west of Camden. All four people aboard were killed.

## Searching Through the Night

Witnesses alerted the Tennessee Highway Patrol, and officers made a preliminary search around 7 p.m. without success. By 11:30 p.m. an organized party of Highway Patrol, Civil Defense and local officers was combing the area, and they searched through the night. At 6:10 a.m. on March 6, the wreckage was found, scattered across an area 166 feet long and 130 feet wide. The FAA concluded that a pilot without an instrument rating had attempted visual flight in adverse weather, leading to disorientation and loss of control. Today a memorial marks the crash site off Mt. Carmel Road near Camden. Ten years later, in 1973, Patsy Cline was inducted into the Country Music Hall of Fame.

## Flight Context

The crash site lies near 36.06°N, 88.16°W, in wooded bottomland roughly a mile north of U.S. Route 70 and a few miles west of Camden, Tennessee, where a memorial stands off Mt. Carmel Road. From 2,500 to 3,500 feet, follow US 70 west from Camden; the broad Tennessee River, the landmark Hughes intended to navigate by, lies a few miles east of town. Benton County Airport (0M4) at Camden is only about 4 miles to the south-southeast. Dyersburg Regional Airport (KDYR), the flight's last departure point, is about 70 miles west, and Nashville, the intended destination, is roughly 80 miles east, where Nashville International Airport (KBNA) serves the city today. This is a fitting place to remember the hazard of VFR flight into instrument conditions: low ceilings, gusty winds and winter fronts can close in quickly over the river valley.
