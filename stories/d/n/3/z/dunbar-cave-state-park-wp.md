# Dunbar Cave State Park

In Clarksville, Tennessee, a cave that sheltered Paleo-Indian hunters, supplied saltpeter for gunpowder and echoed with big-band swing also hides more than 30 Mississippian drawings, including a supernatural warrior on its walls.

**Location:** 36.5503, -87.3059

**Categories:** State parks of Tennessee, Caves of Tennessee, Show caves in the United States, Mississippian culture, Petroglyphs in Tennessee, Geography of Clarksville, Tennessee

## Introduction

Big-band swing once drifted from the mouth of a Tennessee cave. Dunbar Cave, on the edge of Clarksville, was the site of musical festivities and entertainment shows that hosted bands like Benny Goodman's and Tommy Dorsey's. The dances are long gone. What remains is a 110-acre park, a man-made lake called Swan Lake, a poured-concrete entrance with three distinct arches, and a cave that stretches some 9.85 miles into the earth, holding a much older story than any dance band could tell.

## Ten Thousand Years of Visitors

People have been coming to this cave for a very long time. Archaeological finds show it was used in Paleo-Indian times, roughly 10,000 to 8,000 B.C., and again through the Archaic period and the Woodland era, which lasted until about A.D. 800. During the Mississippian era, Native Americans used it for ceremonial purposes. Then came claims on paper. A man named Thomas Dunbar claimed the cave in 1784, and it still bears the Dunbar name, though by 1792 the U.S. government had awarded the land to Robert Nelsen. Later the cave was put to work: during the Mexican–American War, it was mined for saltpeter to make gunpowder.

## Swing Music at the Cave Mouth

By 1931 the site had hosted years of dances, concerts and fairs and badly needed repair. When the state finished a new road in front of the old hotel, a couple of local businessmen saw their chance. They added a concrete swimming pool, a bathhouse and tennis courts, restored and enlarged the hotel, and dammed the existing lake to 20 acres. The cave itself became a stage. On April 26, 1948, Roy Acuff bought Dunbar Cave for $150,000 and added a golf course beside the lake. Popularity faded, though, and in 1950 the hotel burned and was never rebuilt. In 1973, under Governor Winfield Dunn, the State of Tennessee bought the cave from Mrs. McKay King and made it a State Natural Area.

## A Warrior on the Wall

For all the crowds that passed through, the cave kept its most remarkable secret until 2005. That January, park specialist Amy Wallace, geologist and author Larry E. Matthews, local historian Billy Frank Morrison and history professor Joe Douglas found Native American pictographs and petroglyphs in the cave. There were more than 30 drawings and etchings in all, dated to the Mississippian era with the help of torches and other artifacts found nearby. Some are religious symbols. One depicts a Mississippian supernatural warrior. The State of Tennessee announced the discovery on July 29, 2006, during the second annual Dunbar Cave Day, and a cave best known for swing bands turned out to hold art made centuries before any of them.

## Quiet for the Bats

Today, bats set the calendar. Dunbar Cave has a small bat population, and it has been closed from September through April so the bats can hibernate undisturbed. In March 2010, researchers from Austin Peay State University, assessing species diversity and roosting patterns, found a bat infected with white-nose syndrome. On March 24 the state closed the cave to all visitors and discontinued tours. The disease had been discovered in New York only in 2006 and had since spread to Ontario, New Jersey, New Hampshire and Tennessee, killing more than a million bats. The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service called for a moratorium on caving in affected areas and urged cavers to decontaminate clothing and gear after each use. Guided tours returned in May 2015 and now run seasonally, from May through September. Then, in February 2025, the highest flooding ever recorded inside the cave lifted the visitor boardwalks, drowned several bats and, park staff believe, submerged the cave art for the first time. State archaeologists later reported no signs of damage to the art, but the cave stayed closed for that tour season. A March 2026 travel guide said it was expected to reopen in 2026, so check with the park before planning a visit.

## Flight Context

Dunbar Cave State Park lies at about 36.55°N, 87.31°W on the northeastern side of Clarksville, Tennessee, in Montgomery County. From 2,500 to 3,000 feet, look for Swan Lake and the wooded ground around the cave entrance amid Clarksville's suburban neighborhoods; the Cumberland River curves through the city to the southwest. The nearest public-use airport is Clarksville–Montgomery County Regional Airport (KCKV), about eight miles to the northwest. Campbell Army Airfield (KHOP) at Fort Campbell lies farther northwest, so check for military airspace activity before flying that direction. Summer brings haze and afternoon thunderstorms, and river-valley fog is common on calm mornings.
