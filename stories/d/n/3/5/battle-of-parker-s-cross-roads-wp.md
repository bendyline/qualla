# Battle of Parker's Cross Roads

On the last day of 1862, Nathan Bedford Forrest's Confederate cavalry was nearly trapped between two Union brigades at a West Tennessee crossroads, and fought his way out on a blunt order: "Charge 'em both ways."

**Location:** 35.7930, -88.3930

**Categories:** Battles of the western theater of the American Civil War, Battles of the American Civil War in Tennessee, Forrest's Expedition into West Tennessee, Henderson County, Tennessee, 1862 in Tennessee

## Introduction

Interstate 40 now runs straight through the fight. Halfway between Memphis and Nashville, where the highway crosses Tennessee State Route 22, travelers pass over fields where, on December 31, 1862, Confederate Brigadier General Nathan Bedford Forrest came close to being caught between two Union brigades. The place was called Parker's Cross Roads then; the town spells it Parker's Crossroads today. The battle lasted a single day, and its most vivid moment is a four-word order Forrest gave when the trap began to close.

## Racing for the River

Forrest's expedition into West Tennessee was nearing its end, and his route home lay across the Tennessee River. Union Brigadier General Jeremiah C. Sullivan meant to cut him off, sending the brigades of Colonels Cyrus L. Dunham and John W. Fuller to block the way. Their march routes brought Dunham's column and Forrest's command into contact right at the crossroads. Skirmishing began about nine in the morning, with Forrest posted along a wooded ridge northwest of the intersection, and his artillery quickly gained the upper hand. Dunham pulled his brigade back half a mile and redeployed to face north. His men turned back frontal feints until Forrest's troopers, some mounted and some fighting on foot, struck both of his flanks and his rear.

## Charge 'Em Both Ways

During a lull, Forrest sent Dunham a demand for unconditional surrender. Dunham refused and braced for the next assault. Then the day turned. Fuller's Union brigade arrived from the north and hit the Confederates from behind, a surprise made possible because the security detachments Forrest had posted failed to warn of Fuller's approach. Caught between two forces, Forrest's order was blunt: "Charge 'em both ways." His men briefly reversed front and drove Fuller back, then rushed past Dunham's demoralized brigade and withdrew south toward Lexington, Tennessee. Forrest got his command across the Tennessee River. Both sides would claim the battle as a victory, though it was the Confederates who left the field.

## Holding Ground Against the Highway

The interstate reached the crossroads in late 1963 and was completed between Memphis and Nashville in 1966, and the town beside the exit has grown considerably since. The battlefield lies right at the interchange, so developers and investors keep eyeing it, and preservationists have treated the work as urgent. More than 369 acres have been saved so far by the American Battlefield Trust and its members, working with partners that include the local Parker's Crossroads Battlefield Association. In 2011 the Trust launched a campaign for what it called the "keystone tract," a 52-acre parcel billed as one of the last unpreserved pieces of the old battlefield and the ground where most of Forrest's artillery stood. Many preservationists believe that losing it to commercial development would take away the ability to interpret the battle in any meaningful way. Since 2019 the battlefield has been an affiliated area of Shiloh National Military Park.

## Flight Context

Parker's Crossroads battlefield lies at about 35.79°N, 88.39°W in Henderson County, Tennessee, where Interstate 40 running east–west meets Tennessee State Route 22 running north–south, roughly midway between Memphis and Nashville. From 2,500 to 3,500 feet, the interchange is the obvious reference; the preserved fields and woodlots spread around it, and the Tennessee River lies to the east. Lexington, where Forrest withdrew after the battle, is to the south along Route 22. Nearby fields include Beech River Regional Airport (KPVE) to the southeast at Darden, Carroll County Airport (KHZD) to the north near Huntingdon, and Jackson Regional Airport (KMKL, formerly McKellar-Sipes Regional) at Jackson to the southwest. Expect summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms over the rolling West Tennessee farmland.
