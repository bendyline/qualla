# Battle of New Hope Church

On May 25, 1864, Sherman's attempt to slip around the Confederate army toward Atlanta ran headlong into entrenched defenders in the Georgia woods, and Union soldiers came away calling the place the "Hell Hole."

**Location:** 33.9610, -84.7875

**Categories:** Atlanta campaign, Battles of the American Civil War in Georgia (U.S. state), Battles of the western theater of the American Civil War, 1864 in Georgia (U.S. state), Paulding County, Georgia

## Introduction

On the afternoon of May 25, 1864, Confederate General Joseph E. Johnston arrived at John Bell Hood's headquarters near New Hope Church, Georgia, and asked one of Hood's division commanders, Alexander P. Stewart, whether he needed reinforcements. Three Union divisions, some 16,000 men, would soon come at his 4,000 entrenched Confederates through dense woods. Stewart's answer, as it is usually quoted, was brief: "My own troops will hold the position." They did. Over the next three hours, in a fight that ended under a thunderstorm, Union soldiers learned why they would come to call this ground the "Hell Hole."

## Leaving the Railroad Behind

Major General William T. Sherman had opened his Atlanta Campaign on May 7, and for most of that month his maneuvers worked. At Rocky Face Ridge and again after Resaca he turned Johnston's flank, and each time the Confederate Army of Tennessee fell back. By late May, Johnston had posted his army at Allatoona Pass, where the Western and Atlantic Railroad ran through a gorge, in an extremely strong defensive position. Rather than attack it, Sherman planned to swing west through Dallas toward Marietta and force Johnston back behind the Chattahoochee River. It meant leaving his railroad supply line, so his army loaded 20 days of supplies into its wagons, sent its wounded and unfit men to the rear, and began moving on May 23. Sherman believed the way was clear.

## Dust Clouds from Elsberry Mountain

Johnston read the move in time. Alerted by his cavalry, he concluded that Sherman was trying to turn his left flank and shifted his army rapidly to the southwest. On the morning of May 25, Hood's corps settled in near New Hope Church, northeast of Dallas, and his soldiers immediately dug rifle pits and piled up breastworks of logs and rocks. Confederate lookouts on Elsberry Mountain watched dust clouds rising from the approaching Union columns. Leading the way was Major General Joseph Hooker's XX Corps. At Pumpkinvine Creek his escort drove off Confederates trying to burn the bridge, and Brigadier General John W. Geary's division pushed on toward New Hope Church against stiff resistance from two Alabama regiments and a unit of sharpshooters. Prisoners told Geary's men that Hood's corps lay directly ahead, news that startled both Hooker and his army commander, George H. Thomas. Still convinced he faced only minor forces, Sherman ordered an attack.

## Three Hours in the Hell Hole

Soon after 5 p.m., Hooker sent his divisions forward, each formed in columns of brigades, one line behind another, through thick woods and underbrush. At the bottom of a slope they met a storm of rifle and artillery fire from Stewart's brigades and 16 cannons behind earth and log fortifications. The attackers threw themselves to the ground behind whatever cover they could find and fired back. The only target they could clearly see was a Confederate battery. Colonel James S. Robinson's brigade fired off all 60 of its rounds per man before being relieved. In the last hour a thunderstorm added its own noise and rain to the fighting. Hooker reported 1,665 men killed and wounded; Stewart reported 300 to 400 casualties. As the Federals slowly withdrew, the Confederates cheered.

## Blame, Panic and the Next Move

Sherman was badly disappointed. He unfairly blamed Hooker, a man he hated, for waiting for reinforcements instead of pushing Geary's division forward at once, believing the delay had given the Confederates time to entrench. More troubling was the growing realization that he faced not just Hood's corps but Johnston's entire army. The sound of the fighting reportedly carried as far as Atlanta and caused a minor panic among civilians. Through the night the Union army dug in, and skirmishing continued throughout May 26 as McPherson's wing occupied Dallas and both armies' lines stretched thin, with gaps between their wings. Blocked on the Confederate left, Sherman tried the right, leading to the Battle of Pickett's Mill on May 27.

## Five Acres of Remembrance

Today much of the New Hope Church battlefield is privately owned, lying around the intersection of Bobo Road and Highway 381, the Dallas Acworth Highway, in Dallas. The American Battlefield Trust and its members have saved more than five acres of the ground where thousands of men on both sides fought through woods and rain. It is a small piece of land for so much loss, but it keeps the memory of the Hell Hole anchored to the place itself.

## Flight Context

The New Hope Church battlefield lies at approximately 33.961°N, 84.788°W, northeast of Dallas in Paulding County, Georgia, roughly 27 miles northwest of downtown Atlanta. From 3,000–4,500 feet MSL, look for the Dallas Acworth Highway (Highway 381) and its crossing with Bobo Road amid suburban development and remaining woodland. Nearby airports: Silver Comet Field at Paulding Northwest Atlanta Airport (KPUJ), about 9 miles; Cobb County International Airport–McCollum Field (KRYY), about 12 miles; and Cartersville Airport (KVPC), about 12 miles. Dobbins Air Reserve Base (KMGE) at Marietta is about 16 miles east, so check charts carefully for Atlanta-area airspace. Expect summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms.
