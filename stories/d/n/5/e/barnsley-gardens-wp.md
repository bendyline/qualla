# Barnsley Gardens

Near Adairsville, Georgia, the roofless ruins of Godfrey Barnsley's Italianate manor, begun for a wife who did not live to see it, stand amid boxwood gardens planted in the 1840s and rescued from a century of overgrowth.

**Location:** 34.2985, -84.9876

**Categories:** Houses completed in the 19th century, Houses in Bartow County, Georgia, Resorts in Georgia (U.S. state), Ruins in the United States, Tourist attractions in Bartow County, Georgia, 1840s establishments in Georgia (U.S. state)

## Introduction

Godfrey Barnsley started the house for his wife, Julia, and she never lived in it. Before the manor on his north Georgia estate was finished, Julia fell ill and died, and the grieving Barnsley stopped the work. Later he said he felt her presence at the site, urging him to finish the house for himself and their children. He did. The Italianate manor he completed in the late 1840s now stands in ruins near Adairsville, its broken walls framing what is described as one of the few surviving antebellum gardens in the southern United States.

## Woodlands

Barnsley was an English-born cotton merchant who had begun his career in Liverpool. In Georgia he acquired some 10,000 acres from the state (accounts of the acreage vary), land the state had in turn taken from the Cherokee Nation, and the land was divided into 160-acre plots. He called his estate Woodlands; only later would it become known as Barnsley Gardens. Around the house meant for Julia he planted rare trees and shrubs brought from as far afield as Norway, and in the early 1840s he set out the boxwood hedges that would outlast almost everything else he built. The manor itself took the form of an Italian villa, and it rose slowly, interrupted by grief, on the hillside above a spring.

## Tower, Marble and an Emperor's Table

The finished mansion was divided into three sections. The two-story center was topped by a tower, and marble steps led up to the main entrance. Spacious drawing rooms and libraries flanked the hall. In the right wing were a banquet hall, billiard and smoking rooms, and, in the cellar below, the kitchen and storerooms. The dining table, a mahogany piece large enough to seat 40, and its sideboard were said to have been made for Pedro I of Brazil, and a gilt clock in the library was said to have once belonged to Marie Antoinette. The left wing housed temporary quarters for the workers building the house. Upstairs were sixteen bedrooms, and a terrace ran along the rear. At the foot of the slope stood an ivy-covered springhouse. Writer Lucian Lamar Knight, visiting around 1913, called it "one of the prettiest spots in which the imagination could possibly revel."

## A Warning and a Grave

The Civil War reached Woodlands in the spring of 1864. On May 18 of that year, Colonel Richard G. Earle was killed while warning Barnsley of the Union approach. He was buried over the brow of a hill behind the mansion, where Knight found the grave on his visit; it was later moved into the gardens among the ruins. Barnsley lost his fortune during the war. He later moved to New Orleans and died in 1873. His descendants stayed on at Woodlands, keeping the house he had finished after Julia's death.

## The Roof Comes Off

In 1906 a tornado blew the roof off the main house. Barnsley's granddaughter, Adelaide Saylor, was living there with her family at the time, and they moved into the kitchen wing. The main house was never restored. Between the lost roof and later vandalism, it eventually fell to ruin. Outside, the gardens went wild. The boxwood hedges from the 1840s survived, but over the decades they grew up into a thicket of small trees and vines, burying the design Barnsley had laid out.

## Untangling the Boxwoods

In 1988 Prince Hubertus Fugger bought the estate and began a major project to stabilize the ruins and rescue the gardens. The overgrown boxwoods were cut back carefully over a number of years, and as they came down they revealed the interweaving paths and flower beds of the original parterre garden. What emerged is now described as one of the few surviving antebellum gardens in the southern United States. The grounds today are home to Barnsley Resort, and the restored beds lie beneath the roofless shell of Barnsley's manor, a house finished out of devotion and undone by a storm.
