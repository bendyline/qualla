# Hales Bar Dam

Built across the Tennessee River Gorge on soft bedrock between 1905 and 1913, Hales Bar Dam pioneered new construction methods, leaked for its entire working life, and was finally replaced, leaving behind a powerhouse that now hosts events, tours and a distillery.

**Location:** 35.0467, -85.5394

**Categories:** Dams on the Tennessee River, Tennessee Valley Authority dams, Industrial buildings and structures on the National Register of Historic Places in Tennessee, Buildings and structures in Marion County, Tennessee, Dams completed in 1913

## Introduction

In the late 1950s, boils began rising in the water below Hales Bar Dam. The upwellings were a warning. When investigators looked into them, they found the dam leaking again, this time at an alarming 2,000 cubic feet per second. Dye tests in 1960 suggested that many of the leakage channels had joined together, raising the possibility that the dam could one day fail. It was the latest chapter in a problem that had shadowed Hales Bar on the Tennessee River in Marion County, Tennessee, almost from the moment it was finished.

## Taming the Gorge

For generations, the Tennessee River Gorge was one of the great obstacles to navigation in the upper Tennessee Valley, a particularly unpredictable and dangerous stretch full of hazards. Nineteenth-century canal projects had some minor success at other shoals downstream, but the gorge remained largely untamed. In 1898 Chattanooga business interests formed the Tennessee River Improvement Association to push for year-round navigation to their city. Around 1900, Major Dan C. Kingman of the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers drafted a design for a dam that would flood the gorge and remove the swift current and hazards that had long blocked large-scale navigation. In 1904 his friend Josephus Conn Guild offered to build it with private money in exchange for the rights to its hydroelectric power. Congress passed the enabling legislation on April 27, 1904, and with backing from Chattanooga entrepreneur Charles E. James and New York financier Anthony Brady, Guild formed the Chattanooga and Tennessee River Power Company.

## Eight Years on Soft Rock

Work began on October 17, 1905. Two self-contained communities, Guild, now Haletown, and Ladds, sprang up to house the thousands of construction workers the job required. The dam was supposed to be finished in 1909, but the soft bedrock beneath it stalled progress again and again; by 1910 only the lock and powerhouse were complete. Engineers began to make progress only after turning to pressure grouting and concrete caissons, described as the first use of either in a major dam project. Hales Bar was completed on November 11, 1913, at a cost of about $10 million against an estimate of $3 million. It stood 2,315 feet long and, by most accounts, 113 feet high, making it one of the first major multipurpose dams in the United States and one of the first major dams built across a navigable channel. Its lock, 60 by 260 feet, opened on November 1, 1913; its lift, usually given as 41 feet (one Army Corps of Engineers account says 39), was described as the highest in the world at the time.

## A Dam That Would Not Stop Leaking

Leaks appeared almost as soon as the concrete was in place. In 1919 engineers pumped hot asphalt into the foundation, which helped only for a while; by 1931 a study found the dam leaking at 1,000 cubic feet per second. On August 15, 1939, the Tennessee Valley Authority, created under President Franklin D. Roosevelt to manage flood control and hydropower in the valley, took control of Hales Bar after a lengthy court battle that went all the way to the U.S. Supreme Court. TVA's extensive foundation repairs halted the leaking by 1943. In 1949 the agency raised the dam's generating capacity to 99,700 kilowatts and fitted its spillway with radial gates, extending the reservoir's navigation channel to the base of Chickamauga Dam. Then, in the late 1950s, the boils appeared, and the leaks were back at twice their earlier rate.

## Replaced Downstream

By the 1960s, river traffic on the Tennessee had grown since the end of World War II, and TVA was enlarging its locks to keep up. A 1963 study found that expanding the Hales Bar lock would be extremely expensive. Weighed against the endless cost of fighting leaks, the answer was to start over. TVA authorized Nickajack Dam in January 1963, six miles downstream, and completed it in December 1967. Hales Bar shut down that same month, and by September 1968 the dam had been dismantled so it would not threaten navigation on the new Nickajack Lake. Part of it lived on: two of its generators and pieces of its switchyard were installed at the new dam.

## Whiskey in the Powerhouse

The dam is gone, but its powerhouse still stands beside the river. It was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 2008, and it has been described as a private event space, with tours by reservation. A marina has grown up beside the site, and the old building is reported to house Dam Whiskey and Moonshine Distillery, which lists a Hales Bar location open Friday through Sunday. In its day, the reservoir behind Hales Bar reached up through the gorge all the way to Chattanooga, with 162 miles of shoreline. Today the water lapping at the old powerhouse belongs to Nickajack Lake, the reservoir of the dam built to replace it, and a site that once turned the river into electricity now makes whiskey.

## Flight Context

The Hales Bar powerhouse stands at 35.0467°N, 85.5394°W on the Tennessee River in Marion County, Tennessee, near the southwest end of the Tennessee River Gorge, at river mile 431. From 3,000 to 4,000 feet MSL, follow the river west from Chattanooga as it winds through the gorge; the site lies near the gorge's southwest end, about six miles upstream of Nickajack Dam, and a marina beside the powerhouse helps mark it. Terrain rises steeply on both sides of the gorge, so keep adequate clearance. Marion County Airport-Brown Field (KAPT) near Jasper is only about 2.5 nm to the west-northwest, and Chattanooga Metropolitan Airport-Lovell Field (KCHA) is about 16 nm to the east. Morning river fog is common in the gorge.
