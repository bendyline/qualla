# Nickajack Cave

Half drowned by a TVA lake since 1967, Tennessee's Nickajack Cave has been a Cherokee landmark, a Confederate gunpowder mine, a tourist attraction with a publicity hoax, a turning point for Johnny Cash and, today, a refuge for an estimated 100,000 or more endangered gray bats.

**Location:** 34.9898, -85.6105

**Categories:** Protected areas of Marion County, Tennessee, Caves of Tennessee, Nature reserves in Tennessee, Landforms of Marion County, Tennessee

## Introduction

The mouth of Nickajack Cave is 140 feet wide, and it once stood 50 feet high. Since 1967, when the Tennessee Valley Authority's Nickajack Dam backed up the lake that now bears the same name, 25 to 30 feet of water have filled the entrance, leaving an opening only 20 to 25 feet tall above the surface. At dusk from late April to early October, visitors at an overlook above the entrance watch the cave's current residents pour out to feed: a colony of endangered gray bats estimated at more than 100,000. They are the latest tenants of a cave that has served, in turn, as a Cherokee landmark, a gunpowder mine, a tourist show and the scene of one of Johnny Cash's darkest moments.

## Named for a Cherokee Town

The cave took its name from Nickajack, a town of the Chickamauga Cherokee that stood between the cave's mouth and the Tennessee River. In 1794 the Nickajack Expedition attacked the town and destroyed it. The cave outlived the community that named it. Beginning in 1800, while the land was still owned by the Cherokee, James Ore mined the cave for saltpeter with their permission, and the digging continued through the War of 1812. A stream ran through the cave and, before the flooding, fed a small lake in front of the entrance.

## Gunpowder for the Confederacy

Saltpeter was the main ingredient of gunpowder, and when the Civil War began, that made Nickajack valuable. Robert Cravens, a Chattanooga businessman, was operating it along with his own Lookout Mountain Cave, but soon after the war started the Confederate government took over the Nickajack operation. Under the Confederate Nitre and Mining Bureau it is described as one of the largest saltpeter caves the Bureau operated during the war, and a highly strategic site. The February 6, 1864, issue of Harper's Weekly carried a drawing of the cave's entrance, with saltpeter mining and refining equipment outside. The Confederate operation did not last. Sometime in late 1863 or very early 1864, Federal troops occupied the area and mining stopped, and the loss of Nickajack is described as a serious blow to the Confederacy.

## Steamboats and a Staged Disappearance

Tourism came early. A newspaper clipping from July 2, 1872, describes excursions from Chattanooga aboard the steamer R. J. Jackson, with guides taking visitors into the cave by boat and a train from Shellmound, directly in front of the entrance, carrying them home. By 1927 the cave was being shown by Lawrence S. Ashley, who supposedly vanished inside on August 15 of that year. Chattanooga's newspapers and The New York Times covered the disappearance. A week later Ashley reappeared, claiming he had dug his way out through a new entrance eight miles away. The whole episode was a hoax to draw tourists. In the 1940s, Leo Lambert, the developer of Chattanooga's Ruby Falls, ran the cave, and a brochure from the period called it "Nickajack LaCaverns." The commercial operation closed sometime in the late 1940s. Afterward, anyone wanting to see the interior had to wade about a quarter mile down a waist-deep stream. At the rear, a cave-in blocked what were rumored to be miles of additional passages.

## 1967: A Turning Point and a Flood

By his own account, Johnny Cash came to Nickajack Cave around 1967 in despair, intending to end his life inside it. Instead, he said, he had a spiritual experience in the cave that helped turn him away from drug abuse. Country singer Gary Allan later recorded a song about it, "Nickajack Cave (Johnny Cash's Redemption)," on his 2005 album Tough All Over. For Cash, the dark passages marked a turning point rather than an ending. In 1967, the water came. Nickajack Lake partially flooded the cave, and at least two species found nowhere else were wiped out: the pseudoscorpion Microcreagris nickajackensis and the ground beetle Pseudanophthalmus nickajackensis. Today the cave is a wildlife refuge operated by the Tennessee Wildlife Resources Agency, and it protects a species that could not afford a similar loss. Each spring, pregnant female gray bats arrive at this maternity roost to give birth and raise their young, and water levels have posed a danger to the colony. From the overlook above the mouth, reached by a boardwalk from the Maple View Day Use Area and open April through October, the evening flight is the cave's great show now, no guides or boats required.

## Flight Context

Nickajack Cave lies at 34.9898°N, 85.6105°W on the shore of Nickajack Lake in Marion County, Tennessee, west of Chattanooga. The cave mouth is at the waterline, so from the air use the lake itself as your reference; a viewing altitude of 3,000 to 4,000 feet MSL keeps a comfortable margin over nearby high ground. Marion County Airport-Brown Field (KAPT) at Jasper is the closest field, about 4.4 nm north-northeast. Stevenson Airport in Alabama (FAA identifier 7A6) lies about 11 nm west-southwest, and Chattanooga Metropolitan Airport (KCHA) about 20 nm east. Dusk, when the bats emerge, is the most dramatic time to be nearby, but it is also when daylight fades, so plan accordingly.
