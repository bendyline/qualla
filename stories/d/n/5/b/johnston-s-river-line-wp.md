# Johnston's River Line

Built in two weeks in 1864 largely by enslaved laborers, the Confederate River Line above the Chattahoochee was so strong that Sherman's engineer called it the strongest he had seen, and Sherman simply went around it; its earthen 'Shoupades' survive in the suburbs of Mableton and Smyrna.

**Location:** 33.7919, -84.5230

**Categories:** Archaeological sites on the National Register of Historic Places in Georgia (U.S. state), Buildings and structures in Mableton, Georgia, Cobb County, Georgia, History of Mableton, Georgia, National Register of Historic Places in Cobb County, Georgia

## Introduction

Sherman's chief engineer was blunt about it. Captain Orlando M. Poe reported that the Confederate works along the Chattahoochee River were "by far the strongest" he had yet encountered and that it would cost many lives to take them by assault. So the Union army never tried. Johnston's River Line, a chain of earth-and-log forts on a ridge above the river in what are now Mableton, Smyrna and Vinings, Georgia, saw no significant battle. Its strength was the whole story, and its remains are still there, tucked into the suburbs northwest of Atlanta.

## A Last Line Before Atlanta

From the start of the Atlanta Campaign in May 1864, Union General William T. Sherman had outmaneuvered Confederate General Joseph E. Johnston's Army of Tennessee across northwest Georgia, forcing it back to Kennesaw Mountain by late June. Johnston saw the Chattahoochee as the largest natural barrier left between his army and Atlanta. He had already sent a hundred men to guard the Western & Atlantic Railroad bridge near the community of Bolton, and he feared losing thousands of men if Sherman caught his army in the middle of a river crossing. This, he knew, would likely be where Atlanta made its last stand. On June 18, 1864, his artillery commander, Brig. General Francis A. Shoup, proposed a solution: a defensive line along a ridge on the northwest bank, so strong that a fraction of the army could hold it while the rest guarded the river.

## A Saw Blade of Logs and Earth

Shoup's design was distinctive. Thirty-six arrowhead-shaped infantry forts were spaced 60 to 175 yards apart and linked by trenches, which were broken every 30 to 75 yards by artillery redans holding two guns each. Each fort rose on an earthen base with log walls 14 to 20 feet high, depending on the terrain. Inside, an earthen platform stopped short of the outer wall so riflemen could fire over the top, and a sally port opened at the rear. Seen from above, the line looked like a saw blade, its trenches angling back to each redan and forward to the next fort. Up to eighty men could hold each fort. Soldiers below would load rifles and hand them up to the men on the platforms, whose interlocking fire would funnel attackers into the angles where the artillery waited.

## Two Weeks of Forced Labor

Construction began on June 19, 1864, and took about two weeks. Some of the work was done by recovering soldiers from nearby hospitals, but much of it fell to roughly a thousand enslaved people compelled into service, by one historian's estimate. The earthworks that survive today were raised in large part by people who were forced to build them. When Confederate troops filed into the line on the evening of July 4, opinions were divided. Lt. General John B. Hood and others doubted the unusual design and began modifying it toward conventional works. Maj. General Patrick Cleburne, however, judged the works excellent, and Maj. General Gustavus W. Smith predicted the design would make Shoup famous. It was Smith who named the forts "Shoupades."

## Sherman Goes Around

Sherman studied the fortifications on July 5 and recognized their strength, and Poe's report confirmed it. Rather than attack, Sherman sent divisions north to find crossings beyond the Confederate right flank. By July 8 and 9, Union forces under Brig. General Kenner Garrard and Maj. General John M. Schofield had secured crossings near Sope Creek and Roswell. With the river turned, Johnston abandoned the River Line on the evening of July 9, a retreat that led to his dismissal by Jefferson Davis. The line had done exactly what it was built to do, deterring a direct assault, but it could not stop Sherman from crossing the river elsewhere. It has often been compared to the French Maginot Line built before World War II.

## Shoupades in the Suburbs

Today the remains of the River Line stretch for six to seven miles along the northwest side of the Chattahoochee, from just north of where Nickajack Creek joins the river in Mableton to north of South Atlanta Road in Smyrna and Vinings. They are part of the River Line Historic Area and the Chattahoochee River Line Battlefield, and the site was added to the National Register of Historic Places on July 5, 1973. Of the original 36 Shoupades, about nine remain, and many of the surviving earthworks sit on private property. Some can be visited at Shoupade Park and River Line Park, both off Oakdale Road, and at Cobb County's Discovery Park at the River Line, which opened in 2021 near Nickajack Creek and the river. Just north of River Line Park once stood the Hooper-Turner House, an 1850s antebellum home associated with the Turner family, who operated the Mayson-Turner Ferry across the river; the city of Smyrna, which owned it, demolished the house in February 2015. About a mile south of the house site, the Turner-Sewell Cemetery holds John Turner, his wife Jane and members of the Sewell family.
