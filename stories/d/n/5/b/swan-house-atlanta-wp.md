# Swan House (Atlanta)

Philip Shutze's 1928 hilltop mansion for Edward and Emily Inman blends Italian and English classicism, and after decades as a family home it became a museum and a favorite film set, from The Hunger Games to The Amazing Race.

**Location:** 33.8403, -84.3881

## Introduction

Emily Inman cared so much about the grand staircase in her Buckhead mansion that she asked her family not to use it, to protect it. Family members, living in the house with her after her husband's death, were sent up and down the servants' staircase at the back instead. It is a telling detail about the Swan House, a residence designed with such care that its owner treated parts of it like a museum long before it became one. Today the 1928 mansion belongs to the Atlanta History Center, and its classical rooms have played a presidential palace on screen.

## Heirs to a Cotton Fortune

Edward and Emily Inman were affluent heirs to a cotton fortune, and the house was designed specifically for them. Construction began in 1925 in Buckhead, a residential suburb of Atlanta, and the Inmans moved in when the mansion was completed in March 1928. It was only the centerpiece of a 28-acre estate that also held a garage, servants' quarters and a barn. The name came from a swan motif that Philip Shutze worked throughout the interior, one of Emily Inman's favorite things about the house. The nickname stuck, and the Edward Inman House became, to nearly everyone, the Swan House.

## An Atlanta Architect Schooled in Rome

Shutze is widely regarded as "Atlanta's best known architect," a classical designer prominent across America and known for his skill with detailing and proportion. He studied at the Georgia School of Technology, at Columbia University and at the American Academy in Rome, where he saw the Italian Renaissance up close. His career began as a draftsman for the Atlanta firm Hentz, Reid and Adler, and that grounding in the Beaux-Arts tradition shaped everything he built. He traveled often, studying how climate and surroundings shaped architecture, and he paid close attention to texture, color and setting. At the Swan House, all of that training came together in a single commission, one he designed down to the formal gardens outside.

## Two Faces, One Villa

The house is often described as eclectic, and it earns the label by facing two directions at once. The symmetrical western facade and its garden cascade are distinctly Italian Renaissance, while the east front and portico are Anglo-Palladian, the Italian-derived style of seventeenth-century English country houses. Broken pediments and Roman Doric columns on the portico were inspired by Duncombe Park in Yorkshire, and sculptures of summer and autumn by the entrance add what has been called "the Italian villa feeling." Behind the house, a staircase descends the hill beside a dramatic water cascade modeled on the Palazzo Corsini in Rome, which Shutze had studied at the Academy. Stone obelisks and fountains, terraced lawns, stone walls and a formal motor court surround the house, set against small, private boxwood gardens. Inside, Shutze worked with Emily Inman to make room for her antiques collection, carving details in high relief so they would cast shadows through the rooms.

## From Family Home to Museum

Edward Inman died three years after moving in. Emily asked her children and grandchildren to come live with her, and she remained in the house until her death. In 1966 the Atlanta Historical Society bought the Swan House, hoping both to preserve it and to make it the society's new headquarters, and the purchase drew wide interest and participation from the community. The mansion soon proved too small for offices, so the society built a new headquarters on the estate grounds and turned the house itself into a museum. It has been open for tours since 1967. When the society consolidated its properties into the Atlanta History Center in 1993, the Swan House came along. Restored inside and out to its original state, it offers a glimpse of Atlanta society in the 1920s, and it is listed on the National Register of Historic Places.

## A Mansion That Looks Like Forever

Filmmakers keep coming back. The house appears early in the 1980 movie Little Darlings, and it served as the finish line of the 19th season of The Amazing Race. The Atlanta History Center says it hosted location filming for three Hunger Games films, and for Catching Fire in 2013 and Mockingjay, Part 2 in 2015 it stood in for President Snow's mansion. The filmmakers chose it for its classical style and antique details, wanting a location that "looked like it had been there forever." Today the History Center runs a daily Behind the Scenes tour that tells the stories of that filming.

## Flight Context

The Swan House sits on its hilltop at 33.8403°N, 84.3881°W in Buckhead, about 5 nm north of downtown Atlanta, on the grounds of the Atlanta History Center. From 2,500 to 3,500 feet MSL the downtown skyline to the south is the easiest reference; the house itself is small from altitude, so a street map helps. Nearby airports: DeKalb-Peachtree Airport (KPDK), about 4.8 nm east-northeast; Fulton County Airport-Brown Field (KFTY), about 7.6 nm west-southwest; and Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International (KATL), about 12 nm south. Clear mornings usually give the best view.
