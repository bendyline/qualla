# Atlanta Zero Mile Post

A 42-inch stone railroad marker engraved with a double zero marked the spot where the Western and Atlantic Railroad began and a settlement called Terminus grew into Atlanta, until a bridge project and a preservation debate sent it to a museum.

**Location:** 33.7523, -84.3886

**Categories:** History of Atlanta, Monuments and memorials in Georgia (U.S. state), National Register of Historic Places in Fulton County, Georgia, Buildings and structures completed in 1842, Former National Register of Historic Places in Georgia

## Introduction

Atlanta began at zero. The city grew up around the southern end of a state railroad, a settlement so defined by the tracks that it was first called simply Terminus, meaning "end of the line." For more than a century and a half, a squat stone post engraved "W&A RR OO" marked the exact spot where the Western and Atlantic Railroad began its run to Chattanooga. The Atlanta Zero Mile Post has since moved to a museum, and a replica now stands in its place, but it remains the closest thing Atlanta has to a birth certificate carved in stone.

## The End of the Line

In 1836 the Georgia General Assembly voted to build the Western & Atlantic Railroad of the State of Georgia, a line that would run 138 miles from what became Atlanta to Chattanooga, Tennessee. The railroad was founded on December 21, 1836, and the area at its southern end developed into the settlement called Terminus. In 1843 Terminus was incorporated as the city of Marthasville, and two years later an act of the General Assembly renamed it Atlanta. In 1842 the line's southern terminus was moved to a spot immediately beside what is now the southern entrance to Underground Atlanta, and around 1850 the railroad began placing mile posts along its 138-mile line, including the zero post, which stayed there as the city rose around it.

## Reading the Stone

Mile markers were usually placed along rail lines at every mile to tell train crews where they were on a route. This one is rectangular, about a foot wide on each side, with a pyramidal crown and about 42 inches showing above ground. One face reads "W&A RR OO," the W&A for the Western & Atlantic and the double zero for the start of the line. The opposite face reads "W&A RR 138," and 138 miles is the length of the railroad to Chattanooga. More than half of the post sat hidden underground. Fully exposed, it stands 7 feet 5 inches tall and weighs about 800 pounds.

## Locked Beneath the Viaduct

Over time the city literally built over its origin. The post ended up inside a disused building in Downtown Atlanta, within the Underground Atlanta Historic District, under the Central Avenue viaduct between Alabama and Wall streets. The Georgia Historical Commission recognized it with a historical marker in 1958, and it joined the National Register of Historic Places in 1977. In the 1980s it moved indoors when a passenger depot was built around it for the New Georgia Railroad, a tourist operation. When that railroad shut down in the mid-1990s, the depot was locked behind a fence, and anyone who wanted to see the birthplace of Atlanta had to make an appointment with the Georgia Building Authority.

## A Move and an Argument

In October 2018 the Zero Mile Post left its home of nearly 170 years. It was moved to the Atlanta History Center, and the depot building was demolished to make way for reconstruction of the Central Avenue and Courtland Street bridges overhead. After the bridge work was finished, a replica post was placed at the exact location of the original, paired with an interpretive marker from the Georgia Historical Society. The post was removed from the National Register in 2019. The move stirred controversy. Some argued that the marker lost much of its meaning once it was taken from the spot it was made to mark. The history center countered that the original is now well preserved and that a replica is better suited to standing outdoors.

## Beside the Texas

Today the post is displayed in the exhibition Locomotion: Railroads and the Making of Atlanta, alongside the restored locomotive Texas, one of the two remaining Western & Atlantic locomotives, which would have passed the mile post many times in service. The Texas has its own story. Built in October 1856 by Danforth, Cooke and Company, it played a starring role in the Great Locomotive Chase of April 1862, after Union raiders stole the locomotive General at Big Shanty, now Kennesaw. Steaming in reverse, the Texas chased the General for more than 51 miles until the raiders abandoned it. Now the locomotive and the stone that marked the start of its line share a gallery.

## Flight Context

The original site of the Zero Mile Post, now marked by a replica, is at 33.7523°N, 84.3886°W in Downtown Atlanta, beneath the Central Avenue viaduct near Underground Atlanta. The marker itself is invisible from the air, so use the downtown skyline as your reference from about 3,000 feet AGL, subject to air traffic control. Downtown lies within the busy airspace around Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International (KATL), about 7 nm south-southwest, so plan with current charts and expect ATC coordination. Fulton County Executive Airport-Charlie Brown Field (KFTY) is about 7 nm west, and DeKalb-Peachtree (KPDK) is about 9 nm north-northeast. Summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are common over Atlanta.
