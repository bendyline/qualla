# Rhodes Hall

A furniture magnate's granite castle on Peachtree Street, Rhodes Hall has been a family home, the Georgia State Archives, a Halloween haunted house and the headquarters of the Georgia Trust for Historic Preservation.

## Introduction

Amos Giles Rhodes came home from Europe in the late 1890s with castles on his mind. The Atlanta furniture magnate, proprietor of Rhodes Furniture, had admired the fortresses of the Rhineland, and he decided to build one of his own on Peachtree Street, the city's main street. The result, finished in 1904, is a granite house that still looks faintly surprised to find itself in a modern city. It was known as Le Rêve, "The Dream." Most people simply call it Rhodes Hall.

## A Castle by the Wagonload

Architect Willis F. Denny gave Rhodes his castle, blending medieval Romanesque, Victorian and Arts and Crafts design with the practical adaptations an early 20th-century family needed. The walls are Stone Mountain granite, hauled in 500-pound blocks by wagon from Stone Mountain, east of Atlanta. Inside, the mahogany came from the West Indies. Construction took two years and cost Rhodes $50,000, and the finished Romanesque Revival house covers some 9,000 square feet. It remains one of the finest intact expressions of medievalism and late Victorian design in Atlanta.

## Three Hundred Light Bulbs

For all its medieval airs, Le Rêve was a thoroughly modern machine. The house was wired for electricity from the start, and more than 300 light bulbs lit it from top to bottom. Most rooms had electric call buttons, and the house even had a security system. Rhodes Hall is a prime example of the fascination new technology held for Atlantans at the turn of the century, when a house full of glowing bulbs was itself a kind of spectacle.

## The Windows Above the Stair

The grandest feature inside is a series of stained and painted glass windows rising above a carved mahogany staircase. The three panels trace the rise and fall of the Confederacy, from Fort Sumter to Appomattox, and include medallion portraits of more than a dozen Confederates. The craftsmanship is striking. So is the company it keeps. Among the men honored in glass are Robert Toombs, an ardent opponent of Reconstruction; Nathan Bedford Forrest, a Grand Wizard of the Ku Klux Klan; and John B. Gordon, described as the head of the Klan in Georgia. Climbing those stairs today means holding two truths at once: the glass is a masterpiece, and the gallery it frames includes men tied to the Ku Klux Klan.

## Archives, Ghosts and a Homecoming

After Rhodes and his wife died, their children deeded the house to the state of Georgia on one condition: it had to be used for "historic purposes." State historian Ruth Blair moved the Georgia Department of Archives and History into the mansion and lived there herself. From 1930 to 1965 the castle held the Georgia State Archives, and even after the collection moved to a more modern building, Rhodes Hall carried on as a branch. The staircase and windows went with the archives to the new building. In 1983 the Georgia Trust for Historic Preservation took over the house on a long-term lease from the state and began a long restoration. Before the work was done, for roughly a decade in the 1980s and 1990s, the old castle put its looks to use every Halloween as the backdrop for a popular haunted house attraction. The stairs and windows finally came home and were reinstalled in 1990.

## Holding Its Ground on Peachtree

Commerce arrived early. In 1937 the Rhodes Center, which the Georgia Trust calls Atlanta's first strip shopping center, went up on the north, west and south sides of the house; only the south building survives, vacant and named to the Trust's Places in Peril list in 2019. Today Rhodes Hall is hemmed in by commercial buildings and heavy traffic, and it has come through intact thanks to careful preservation and restoration. The Georgia Trust keeps its headquarters on the upper floors, and the house is listed on the National Register of Historic Places. Visitors can join guided tours on select Saturdays, and the rooms Rhodes built for his family now host weddings, receptions and parties.
