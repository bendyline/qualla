# Techwood Homes

Dedicated by Franklin D. Roosevelt in 1935, Atlanta's Techwood Homes was an early showcase of American public housing, built for white families only on the site of an integrated shantytown, and torn down before the 1996 Olympics.

## Introduction

When Franklin D. Roosevelt dedicated Techwood Homes on November 29, 1935, the new apartments came with a bathtub and an electric range in every unit, a central laundry, a kindergarten and a library. What the ceremony did not advertise was who had lived on the land before, or who would be allowed to live there now. The project that rose beside Georgia Tech was one of the country's early experiments in public housing. It was also reserved for white families, and it was built by clearing a neighborhood where Black and white Atlantans had lived side by side.

## Before the Bulldozers: Tanyard Bottom

The neighborhood went by two names, Tanyard Bottom and Tech Flats. It was a shantytown adjacent to downtown, home to 1,611 families, most of them poor. About 28 percent were African American, and the Flats were integrated in practice. To make way for Techwood, the Flats were demolished. The Public Works Administration replaced them with 604 units for white families only, and the income requirements were out of reach for many of the people who had lived there. Whatever the new buildings offered, they were closed to the Black families of the Flats, and many of their white neighbors could not afford them either.

## Architects of a New Idea

Charles Forrest Palmer, a real estate developer who had become an expert on public housing, organized the project, and he went on to head both the newly created Atlanta Housing Authority and the Chamber of Commerce. The buildings were designed by Flippen David Burge, a Georgia Tech alumnus with the firm Burge and Stevens, later Stevens & Wilkinson, and the grounds were landscaped by Edith Henderson. The name came from Techwood Drive, itself named for the university next door, and the connection ran deeper than a street sign: the project included McDaniel Dormitory, a 300-student residence for Georgia Tech commonly called the Techwood Dorm. Roosevelt's dedication came first, and the complex was completed on August 15, 1936, with garages for 189 of its units.

## A Color Line Drawn in Brick

Techwood was not alone. Next door, Clark Howell Homes opened in 1940 or 1941 (sources differ) with 630 units, in a less institutional style credited to architect A. Ten Eyck Brown, with landscaping again by Edith Henderson and her partner Grace Campbell. Clark Howell was also reserved for white families, and it remained so until 1968. Its all-Black counterpart was University Homes, near the Atlanta University Center, where the first families moved in during 1937. Public housing in Atlanta had arrived, and it arrived segregated.

## Blight, Then the Olympics

Run by the Atlanta Housing Authority, Techwood aged badly. Through the 1970s, 1980s and 1990s the area became synonymous with urban blight in Atlanta. Then came the 1996 Summer Olympics, and in advance of the Games nearly all of Techwood Homes was torn down, with only a few historic buildings left standing. Techwood and Clark Howell together became Centennial Place, a mixed-income development whose first phase opened in 1996, just before the Centennial Olympics that gave it its name. The former residents were relocated and given Section 8 vouchers to help with rent. Centennial Place had far fewer subsidized units than Techwood, and only 78 of the original residents were able to move back. For the second time in roughly sixty years, the people who lived on this ground were moved off it to make room for something new.

## Flight Context

The former Techwood Homes site lies at 33.7678°N, 84.3917°W, just north of downtown Atlanta near the Georgia Tech campus; the complex was bounded by North Avenue, Parker, William and Lovejoy streets. From 2,500 to 3,500 feet MSL the downtown skyline just to the south is the obvious reference, while Centennial Place's low-rise buildings are hard to separate from the surrounding city without a street map. Nearby airports: Fulton County Airport-Brown Field (KFTY), about 6.5 nm west; DeKalb-Peachtree Airport (KPDK), about 8 nm northeast; and Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International (KATL), about 8 nm south-southwest. Clear mornings usually give the best view of the city.
