# Sweetwater Creek State Park

Beside a creek west of Atlanta, the roofless brick walls of the New Manchester mill recall the Cherokee land lotteries, a Union raid in 1864 and the mill women and children who were arrested, accused of treason and sent north as refugees.

**Location:** 33.7538, -84.6390

**Categories:** Protected areas of Douglas County, Georgia, State parks of Georgia (U.S. state), Ruins in the United States

## Introduction

On July 9, 1864, on orders that traced back to William Tecumseh Sherman, Union cavalry set fire to a five-story brick textile mill on Sweetwater Creek. The factory had already been shut down a week earlier, when soldiers arrived with orders to close it and arrest everyone who worked there. The workers they arrested were mostly women and children, and accounts describe them as charged with treason or held as political prisoners. Today the mill's brick walls stand roofless in the woods of Sweetwater Creek State Park, a 2,549-acre preserve in east Douglas County, about 15 miles from downtown Atlanta. The ruins are the reason the park exists.

## Land Drawn by Lottery

This was Cherokee country. According to legend, the name "Sweetwater" refers to a Cherokee chief, Ama-Kanasta. In 1819, hungry for land, the state of Georgia asked the federal government to remove the Cherokee, and was refused. Georgia pressed on anyway. In 1830 the legislature declared its control over all Cherokee territory within the state, and in 1831 surveyors began mapping the land for a lottery. In 1832, despite a U.S. Supreme Court ruling in Worcester v. Georgia that the state had no authority over the Cherokee Nation, the land was handed out by lottery. In 1838 federal troops began forcing the Cherokee out of Georgia and neighboring states, and some 16,000 people were made to march west to Indian Territory, in present-day Oklahoma, on the Trail of Tears; thousands died along the way. The paper trail of one parcel shows how fast the land changed hands. In the Gold Lottery of 1832, Philip J. Crask won 40-acre Lot 929 for an $18 grant fee. It sold at auction in 1837 for $12.50, and in 1845 for $500 to a former governor, Charles J. McDonald, and Colonel James Rogers.

## A Mill Named for Manchester

McDonald and Rogers wanted the creek's power. In 1846, using bricks made by enslaved laborers, they began building a water-powered mill on the bank, and on December 21, 1849, the five-story factory went into operation as the Sweetwater Manufacturing Company, producing cotton yarn and fabric. In 1858 McDonald renamed it the New Manchester Manufacturing Company, after the English city at the heart of the British textile trade, and a mill town called New Manchester grew up around it. By 1860 the factory was working through 700 pounds of cotton a day, turning it into 120 bunches of yarn and 500 yards of the cloth known as osnaburg.

## Treason at the Loom

In the summer of 1864, during the Atlanta campaign, General Joseph E. Johnston pulled the Confederate Army back across the Chattahoochee River and left the factory exposed. On July 2, Union cavalry under Colonel Silas Adams and Major Haviland Thompkins arrived with orders to close the mill and arrest its employees. Most of the workers were women and children, since able-bodied men had been called up to fight for the Confederacy, though a few dozen men were also taken. A week later Thompkins burned the mill and the general store. The captured workers were taken to Marietta and joined with prisoners from other mills; estimates range from about 150 to 200 women and children from New Manchester to a combined group of 600. The women were reportedly charged with treason and held for a week at the Georgia Military Institute, where some Union soldiers allegedly assaulted their captives. Then they were put on trains, many bound for Louisville, Kentucky, others for Indiana. Some died along the way, and many were left among strangers in southern Indiana with no family or means of support. After the war a significant number returned to the Sweetwater area or elsewhere in Georgia, though the fate of many is unknown.

## Ruins and Rapids

All that remains of New Manchester are the brick walls and the millrace that once led water to the factory's wheel. Protecting them, and the unspoiled land around them, is why the park was founded. The Georgia Conservancy, an environmental group that was itself formed at a meeting at Sweetwater Creek in 1967, did much of the work, and Sweetwater became an official state park in 1972. About 15 miles of trails now wind through the woods, and the mill's interior is open only on guided hikes and special events, though visitors can hike to the outside of the ruins on their own. Visitors fish the creek and a 215-acre lake, rent canoes in the warmer months, picnic, camp or sleep in one of ten yurts. The park also runs ranger-led programs, including Inside the Ruins tours and evening hikes, and it has hosted Full Moon Hikes and a New Manchester Days festival in the fall to remember the vanished town.

## Old Stone, Green Roofs

The rocks at the surface here were laid down at least 450 million years ago as shales, sandstones and greywackes, in an environment much like the sea off today's Georgia coast. Basaltic lava later covered them, and heat and pressure remade the whole stack into mica schists, quartzites, metagraywackes and amphibolites before uplift and erosion brought rocks once miles deep to the surface. The modern threats are closer at hand. Rainwater carries urban runoff into the streams, and in 1998 American Rivers named the Chattahoochee River one of the nation's most endangered rivers. Conservation shaped the park's visitor center, built into a hillside in 2006 with planted green roofs, solar panels, rainwater collection and a composting toilet. It earned LEED Platinum certification, and in 2007 it was reportedly among only twenty platinum-rated buildings in the world and the first in the Southeast. Inside are Native American artifacts, Civil War-era remnants, and mounted animals and birds.

## Flight Context

Sweetwater Creek State Park is centered near 33.7538°N, 84.6390°W in east Douglas County, about 12 nm (roughly 15 miles) west of downtown Atlanta. From 2,500 to 3,500 feet MSL, look for the park's 215-acre lake and the wooded creek corridor; the mill ruins themselves are hidden under the trees. Fulton County Airport-Brown Field (KFTY) is about 6 nm east-northeast, and Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International (KATL) about 13 nm east-southeast. The downtown Atlanta skyline is visible to the east on clear days, and morning air is usually the clearest.
