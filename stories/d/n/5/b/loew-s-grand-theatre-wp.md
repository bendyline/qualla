# Loew's Grand Theatre

Built in 1893 as DeGive's Grand Opera House, Atlanta's Loew's Grand hosted the 1939 premiere of Gone with the Wind, which its Black stars were barred from attending, before a 1978 fire ended its run and its bricks and chandelier scattered across the city.

## Introduction

On December 15, 1939, an estimated 300,000 people lined the streets of Atlanta for seven miles. Georgia's governor, Eurith D. Rivers, had declared the day a state holiday, and nearly all the stars of Gone with the Wind had come to town for the film's premiere at Loew's Grand Theatre, at the corner of Peachtree and Forsyth streets. It was the most famous night in the theater's history. It was also a night that shut out some of the film's own cast: Hattie McDaniel and the other Black actors were kept away by Georgia's Jim Crow laws.

## Premiere Night

Most of the film's stars were there. Leslie Howard was the notable exception, having returned to England after the outbreak of World War II, while Victor Fleming, who had fallen out with Selznick, declined to attend any of the premieres. Hattie McDaniel's absence was of another kind entirely. She and the film's other Black cast members were prevented from attending because Georgia's Jim Crow laws kept them from sitting with their white colleagues. By the account usually told, when Clark Gable learned McDaniel had been barred, he threatened to boycott the event, and McDaniel persuaded him to go anyway. The glittering premiere that Atlanta celebrated with a holiday was one that some of the film's own actors were not allowed to see.

## From Opera House to the Home of MGM

The building began life long before the movies, as DeGive's Grand Opera House. Laurent DeGive, an entrepreneur who also served as Belgian consul, built it in 1893, and for years it hosted concerts and touring opera productions. DeGive had his name carved prominently above the entrance, which helps explain a lasting confusion: many people mix the Grand up with DeGive's first opera house, which opened in 1870 four blocks to the south at the corner of Marietta and Forsyth streets. In 1927 the Loews organization bought the Grand and had architect Thomas W. Lamb renovate it into a movie theater. The single-screen theater held 2,088 seats and focused on films from Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer, the Loews-owned studio, even hanging a sign beneath its marquee that proclaimed it "The Home of Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer Pictures." The 1948 Supreme Court case United States v. Paramount Pictures forced studios to divest their theater chains, but the relationship endured. Many later MGM films still had their first Atlanta showings at the Grand, among them Singin' in the Rain, the 1959 Ben-Hur and Doctor Zhivago.

## Fire, Bricks and a Chandelier

On January 30, 1978, a fire badly damaged the theater. The land beneath it was valuable, but the building's historic status had prevented it from being torn down, and that combination led many to suspect the fire was arson. The suspicion has never been proven. What remained of the Grand was demolished that same year, and the Georgia-Pacific Tower now rises on the site of that 1939 premiere. The Grand did not entirely vanish. Bricks salvaged from the building were used to build Houston's, a popular Atlanta restaurant, whose Peachtree Road location at 2166 Peachtree, a few miles north, has displayed a plaque in its lobby remembering the theater. One of the theater's chandeliers is reported to have found an even more fitting second life, hanging at the center of the Tabernacle, a former church turned concert venue, lighting up crowds gathered for a show.
