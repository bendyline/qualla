# Atlanta Beltline

A ring of old railroad corridors around the core of Atlanta, reimagined in a 1999 student thesis, is becoming a 22-mile loop of trails, parks, public art and planned transit that has remade neighborhoods and raised hard questions about who benefits.

## Introduction

In 1999 a Georgia Tech student named Ryan Gravel wrote a master's thesis about a ring of railroad tracks around Atlanta. The next year, working at an architecture firm, he and two colleagues, Mark Arnold and Sarah Edgens, boiled it down, folded in earlier ideas for trails and parks, and mailed copies to two dozen influential Atlantans. That mailing helped launch the Atlanta Beltline, a planned 22-mile multi-use loop on former rail corridors that encircles the city's core, linking 45 neighborhoods and aiming to reconnect communities that infrastructure had long divided and marginalized.

## A Belt for a Railroad City

The loop exists because Atlanta was a railroad town. After the Civil War, Atlanta industrialized, unlike most of the South, and new factory jobs drew workers and swelled the population. Demand on the rails grew so heavy that a "belt" of railway was proposed to bypass the congested downtown. It took roughly 30 years and four separate lines, each owned by a different company: the Atlanta and Richmond Air-Line in the 1870s, the Seaboard Air Line in 1892, the Atlanta & West Point Belt Line in 1899, and a Louisville & Nashville belt line operating by 1902. An 1888 U.S. Geological Survey map of Atlanta already labeled one segment the "Belt Line R.R." By 2014, three of the four lines had fallen out of use. The Seaboard line still carries CSX trains, so the northwest trails must follow a new route.

## From Thesis to Movement

Gravel was not the first to imagine a new life for the corridor. The Georgia Rails-to-Trails Conservancy proposed a ring of trails and parks in 1991, and city planner Alycen Whiddon promoted a similar plan in 1993. Gravel's thesis focused on rail transit. City Council member Cathy Woolard became an early supporter, and she, Gravel, Arnold and Edgens spent months pitching the idea to neighborhood groups and business and civic leaders, forming the nonprofit Friends of the Belt Line. Woolard, by then council president, persuaded Mayor Shirley Franklin to back it. Studies, notably the Trust for Public Land's "Beltline Emerald Necklace" report by Alexander Garvin, argued that trails, greenways, affordable housing and zoning should come before rail. The City Council adopted a Beltline Redevelopment Plan reflecting those priorities in 2005, and Atlanta Beltline, Inc. formed in 2006.

## Warehouses, Murals and Moving Feet

The first trail, the 2.4-mile West End Trail, opened in 2008; the first section of the Northside Trail came in 2010 and the Westside Trail in 2017. The most famous stretch is the Eastside Trail, running from Piedmont Park south to Inman Park and the Old Fourth Ward past the city's greatest concentration of industrial buildings reborn as homes, offices, shops and restaurants. Ponce City Market and Krog Street Market are the best-known examples. Since 2010, Atlanta Beltline Art has staged what is billed as the city's largest temporary public art exhibition, with hundreds of artists and performers along 9 miles of the corridor, while unofficial murals and sculptures appear on their own. As of June 2026, nearly 17 miles (16.7) of continuous mainline trail were open, along with about 10 miles of connector trails, and a $30 million gift from the James M. Cox Foundation in 2022 helped put the remaining philanthropic funding in place to complete the full 22-mile loop by 2030.

## An Emerald Necklace

Parks are strung along the loop like beads. Garvin's report imagined 13 "Beltline Jewels," and the Trust for Public Land acquired 33 properties toward the plan's goal of 1,300 acres of green space, enough by the plan's estimate to increase Atlanta's green space by nearly 40 percent. Historic Fourth Ward Park covers 17 acres near Ponce City Market. D. H. Stanton Park near Peoplestown is billed as Atlanta's first energy-cost-neutral park. The most dramatic is Shirley Clarke Franklin Park, about 280 acres on the site of the former Bellwood Quarry (the original 2005 plan called for 351), larger than Piedmont Park, where the old quarry now holds a reservoir of more than two billion gallons of backup drinking water for the city. Stitching it all together is not simple. The route once had five major gaps where rights-of-way did not connect, among them two active rail yards and an I-20 crossing; the two on the Westside have been resolved, and by an October 2026 report the Beltline had closed its final land purchase, completing the land needed for the full trail.

## Who the Loop Is For

Success brought a reckoning. As interest in land near the trail surged, developers bought property in previously low-income neighborhoods and turned it into luxury housing, and home values within half a mile of the Beltline rose between 17.9 and 26.6 percent from 2011 to 2015. A few protests over gentrification followed. The project's stated goals include 50,000 permanent jobs, 1,300 acres of green space and $10 billion in private redevelopment around the loop. Rail, the heart of Gravel's original thesis, has moved slowest. A 2012 sales-tax referendum that would have funded streetcars failed, but in 2016 city voters passed the More MARTA tax, providing $1.3 billion for transit expansion that includes Beltline rail lines. In March 2025, Mayor Andre Dickens withdrew his support for starting that rail on the popular Eastside Trail, favoring a Southside start, and in September 2026 the City Council unanimously asked the administration, Atlanta Beltline Inc. and MARTA for a plan and timeline for the 22 miles of rail by January 2027, a reminder that the loop's final shape is still being argued over.

## Flight Context

This story's coordinates, about 33.76°N, 84.42°W, fall on the Westside of the Beltline loop, which circles central Atlanta roughly 3 miles on either side of the city's elongated central business district. From the air, trace the curving green corridor through the urban grid: Piedmont Park and the Eastside Trail to the northeast, the Bellwood Quarry site of Shirley Clarke Franklin Park on the west side, and the downtown towers at the center. Atlanta sits beneath busy Class B airspace around Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International Airport (KATL), about 8 miles south, so plan carefully with ATC; Fulton County Executive Airport-Charlie Brown Field (KFTY) lies about 6 miles west and DeKalb-Peachtree Airport (KPDK) about 11 miles northeast. Summer haze and pop-up afternoon thunderstorms are common.
