# Paschal's

Opened in 1947 as a sandwich shop with no kitchen, Paschal's grew into a soul food restaurant, jazz lounge and motel that served as the unofficial headquarters of the Civil Rights Movement in Atlanta.

## Introduction

For its first years, the kitchen at Paschal's was a taxi ride away. When brothers Robert and James Paschal opened their sandwich shop at 837 West Hunter Street in Atlanta in 1947, the little luncheonette had no room to cook, and the brothers did not own a car. So Robert prepared the food at home and sent it over by cab. The menu held only sandwiches and soda, and the house specialty was a 52-cent fried chicken sandwich made from Robert's own recipe. From that improbable start grew a restaurant, a jazz lounge, a motel and an airport food business. Along the way, the dining room became the unofficial headquarters of the Civil Rights Movement.

## Two Brothers from Thomson

Robert, born in 1909, and James, born in 1920, came to Atlanta from Thomson, Georgia. They set up shop in the city's Black business district, close to downtown, and the gamble paid off quickly. Within a year they had acquired the adjoining property, bringing seating up to 75. By the late 1950s even that was too small, and in 1959 they moved across the street into a larger building at what is today 830 Martin Luther King Jr. Drive, the new name West Hunter Street eventually took. Ebony magazine described the place in 1966 as a "large attractive brick building with soda fountain and booths in front and a large dining room in the rear." Robert ran the kitchen as head chef and recipe developer, and the restaurant became known for soul food: fried chicken, collard greens, cornbread, sweet potato pie and peach cobbler. The brothers would work side by side for more than 50 years.

## Tables Without a Color Line

What happened in the dining room mattered as much as what came out of the kitchen. Paschal's was known as a place where Black and white customers were both welcome, and gay patrons too. It was one of the first restaurants to seat Black and white diners at the same tables, in an era when segregated seating was the norm. On paper the business should not have worked that way. Paschal's held "colored only" business and liquor licenses, but the brothers openly disregarded the law and served everyone who came through the door. It was a quiet kind of defiance, repeated every day over plates of chicken and greens.

## Strategy Over Supper

During the 1960s, Paschal's became the movement's unofficial headquarters. Martin Luther King Jr. and other leaders met there often for strategy sessions, planning protest marches, sit-ins and voter registration drives. The roll of regulars reads like a history of the era: Andrew Young, John Lewis, Julian Bond, Fannie Lou Hamer, Stokely Carmichael, Ralph Abernathy, the Reverends Joseph Lowery and Jesse Jackson, and Maynard Jackson. According to Lewis, activists came to Paschal's to decompress after "arrests, death threats and beatings." The brothers kept the restaurant open all night as a safe haven for activists coming back from jail and as a place where their families could find them. They posted bond for activists and fed them for free. The tables drew national politicians as well, with strategy meetings involving Ted Kennedy, Hubert Humphrey, Richard Nixon and Bill Clinton.

## A Jazz Mecca and Room 101

In 1960 the brothers opened the La Carousel Lounge next door. The 200-seat club drew Aretha Franklin, Dizzy Gillespie, Ramsey Lewis and Joe Williams, and was considered Atlanta's "jazz mecca" through the 1960s and early 1970s. It was Atlanta's only formal nightclub open to Black patrons during segregation, and like the restaurant it drew a largely interracial crowd; Muhammad Ali and Jayne Mansfield were among its celebrity guests. In 1967 the Paschals added a six-story motel of roughly 120 rooms, completing their vision of "food, drink, merriment, entertainment, and a place to rest up" within one complex. The $2 million motel, described as the first Black-owned hotel in Atlanta, had banquet space for 350, another 160-seat dining room and a swimming pool. Room 101 was permanently set aside for King. Robert F. Kennedy kept an office there during his 1968 presidential campaign, and Jesse Jackson began planning his 1984 run at the motel.

## Saved by an Outcry

The restaurant, lounge and motel closed in 1996, and Clark Atlanta University bought the property for $3 million, turning the motel into a student dormitory and conference center called The Paschal Center. Robert's recipes came with the sale, and his fried chicken stayed on the menu, until the university closed the restaurant in 2003 and planned to tear it down. Public outcry led to a $100,000 congressional grant to save the historic building, and the original buildings were not demolished, though the site has been named to the Atlanta Preservation Center's list of Most Endangered Historic Places. By then the Paschal name had already traveled far beyond Hunter Street. In 1980, by the company's account (the Georgia Encyclopedia says 1978), a joint venture won a 15-year contract to run food service at Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International Airport, and by 2008 Paschal's and its partners were feeding travelers at 18 U.S. airports. Robert died in 1997. In 2002, James and Herman J. Russell opened a $6 million Paschal's on Northside Drive in Castleberry Hill, with an event space modeled on La Carousel, and that Northside Drive restaurant is still open and family-run, with a counter at the airport as well. James led the company until his death in 2008.
