# Cotton States and International Exposition

Atlanta's 1895 world's fair drew nearly 800,000 visitors, premiered Sousa's 'King Cotton' march, staged Booker T. Washington's 'Atlanta Compromise' speech and left behind the grounds that became Piedmont Park.

**Location:** 33.7848, -84.3751

## Introduction

The Cotton States and International Exposition opened with the flip of a switch. On September 18, 1895, President Grover Cleveland presided over the opening from his house in Massachusetts by throwing an electric switch, and in Atlanta a world's fair came to life. For the next three and a half months, until December 31, the fairgrounds drew nearly 800,000 visitors from the United States and thirteen countries. The fair was built to sell the South: to foster trade between the Southern states and South American nations, and to show the region's products and facilities to the rest of the nation and to Europe.

## Race at the Fair

The most lasting moment of the exposition came on its first day. On September 18, Booker T. Washington delivered an address on race relations that became known as the "Atlanta Compromise" speech. It laid the foundation for what was called the Atlanta Compromise, an understanding between African American leaders and Southern white leaders under which Black Southerners would accept white political rule while whites guaranteed them basic education and due process of law. The speech has been recognized as one of the most important, influential and controversial in American history. The fair also had a Negro Building, whose chief was Irvine Garland Penn. December 26, 1895, was designated "Negro Day" at the exposition, in the language of the era. Among those who came was Harriet Powers, the famed African American quilter, who met with Penn during her visit. Her presence, and Penn's, are a reminder that Black Southerners were not only the subject of the fair's debates but participants in it.

## Gold Medals and King Cotton

The exhibits ranged widely: minerals and forestry, agriculture, food, machinery and appliances, horticulture, manufactures, electricity, fine arts, painting and sculpture, liberal arts, education and literature. The Award Committee examined about 6,000 exhibits and handed out 1,573 medals, 634 of them gold, 444 silver and 495 bronze. An electric railway was built for the exposition by the Dixie Intramural Railway Company, founded and led by Col. Ira Yale Sage. In the New York City exhibit, the National League of Mineral Painters, including Adelaide Alsop Robineau and Mary Chase Perry, showed decorative objects and artwork. The fair even had its own soundtrack. John Philip Sousa composed his famous march "King Cotton" for the exposition, and it was named the fair's official march.

## The Woman's Building

Elise Mercur, described as Pittsburgh's first female architect, designed the Palladian-style Woman's Building, which showcased the accomplishments of women across the South and the country in education, health care and the fine and decorative arts. Women from Georgia curated it, and women around the country supplied its contents. Rooms were assigned to different states, among them the Baltimore Room, the Lucy Cobb Room and the Mary Ball Washington Tea Room. The Colonial Room displayed Dolley Madison's spectacles, a gun carried at the Battle of Concord and brass medallions that had belonged to George Washington, and was said to represent "the growing bond of cooperation between the North and South." Most striking were working prototypes of a hospital room, a nursery, a kindergarten classroom and a model library stocked with publications by women authors from every state, each one representing a setting where women played important roles outside the home.

## From Fairgrounds to Piedmont Park

The exposition succeeded in showcasing Atlanta as a business center and drawing investment to the city. After it closed, the exhibition buildings were demolished, and in 1904 the City of Atlanta bought the grounds, which are now Piedmont Park; in 1976 the city leased the park's high ground to the Atlanta Botanical Garden. Joseph Forsyth Johnson had designed the fair's grounds, including the pond expanded into today's Lake Clara Meer, and the Olmsted Brothers' later plan shaped the park. The stone balustrades scattered around the park are remnants of the enormous main building, which they once helped approach with steps. Anyone who passes them today is walking through the ghost of a world's fair.
