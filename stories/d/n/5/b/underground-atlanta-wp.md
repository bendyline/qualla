# Underground Atlanta

When Atlanta raised its streets over the railroad in the late 1920s, it buried a Reconstruction-era downtown beneath the viaducts, a hidden district that has since been a speakeasy haunt, a nightlife mecca, a festival mall and a perennial comeback project.

## Introduction

In the late 1920s, downtown Atlanta rose a story and a half. The newly completed "twin viaducts" lifted Pryor, Central, Wall and Alabama streets above the busy railroad tracks, and the old street-level storefronts beside them became, in effect, basements hidden beneath the new roadway. Atlanta had, in effect, buried a piece of itself. Nearly a century later, that hidden layer in the Five Points neighborhood is Underground Atlanta, a district that has been abandoned and revived again and again.

## A Boomtown on the Rails

The buildings date from Atlanta's post-Civil War boom between 1866 and 1871, when the city's population doubled from 11,000 to 22,000. In 1869 the Georgia Railroad Freight Depot was completed to replace the one destroyed by Sherman's troops in 1864; it still stands beside Underground Atlanta on Central Avenue, often described as the oldest building downtown, though fire took its upper floors in 1935. Around the tracks grew a bustling district of hotels, banks, law offices and saloons. An electric streetcar arrived in 1889, and by 1900 the depot handled 100 trains a day, with direct service to New York City, Cincinnati, Chattanooga, Knoxville, Augusta, Macon and Columbus. So much traffic crowded the railroad gulch that by 1910 several iron bridges carried people and vehicles over it, and in 1909 architect Haralson Bleckley proposed covering the tracks entirely with plazas and boulevards.

## Speakeasies Below the Streets

Bleckley's grand plan never happened, but the late-1920s viaducts accomplished something similar. Because the raising of the streets came during Prohibition, and because the new "basements" were relatively hidden from the city above, some became speakeasies and juke joints where music and illegal drinking were common. The area's reputation as an entertainment district was already established; one of the first mentions of it comes in the opening lines of Bessie Smith's 1927 song "Preachin' the Blues." For the next forty years, as Atlanta kept growing at street level, the roughly 12-acre underground area was effectively abandoned and forgotten, though it was frequented by people experiencing homelessness.

## The City Beneath the City

In the late 1960s, private developers set out to restore and reopen "the city beneath the city." Underground Atlanta, Inc. was incorporated on May 2, 1967, construction began in November 1968, and the district opened in 1969, its original facades largely intact along Alabama and Pryor streets and Kenny's Alley. In 1970 the R&B shouter Piano Red even recorded a tribute called "Underground Atlanta." The heyday lasted only about half a decade. Georgia lowered its drinking age from 21 to 18 in 1972, neighboring DeKalb County relaxed its alcohol rules in the early 1970s, and new bars elsewhere drew customers away. Dress codes were dropped, fights broke out and crime made the area feel dangerous. Construction of MARTA's East Line, begun in 1975, tore out about a third of the Underground blocks. Underground Atlanta closed, the 26 businesses that remained struggled to stay open, and by February 1982 the place was abandoned again. Dante Stephensen, proprietor of Dante's Down the Hatch, was the last to leave.

## Festival Mall and Olympic Nights

Newly elected mayor Andrew Young vowed in 1982 to reopen Underground as part of reviving downtown. After the Georgia Supreme Court ruled that the plan's bond financing first needed approval from Fulton County voters, the project stalled, but it finally reopened on June 15, 1989, in heavy thunderstorms, as more of a modern shopping mall than a nightlife district, with 225,000 square feet of retail across about 12 acres. The American Institute of Architects honored it for excellence in urban design in 1990, though critics felt the sanitized district had lost its original charm; Jeff Kunerth of the Orlando Sentinel called it "a new heart [of Atlanta] with the soul of Disney World's Main Street." The World of Coca-Cola opened next door in August 1990, and that September crowds gathered in Kenny's Alley to watch Atlanta win the 1996 Summer Olympics. Hard times followed: damage and a curfew after the Rodney King verdict unrest on April 30, 1992, an evacuation after the 1996 Olympic bombing when an abandoned bag was found, and a $6.5 million loss that year. A 2004 ordinance allowing a 4 a.m. last call could not bring back steady crowds.

## Waiting for the Next Revival

In December 2014, Mayor Kasim Reed announced that the city would sell Underground Atlanta to developer WRS Inc., which planned a $150 million to $200 million mixed-use project with apartments above ground. The sale closed in March 2017, and the lower level shut to the public that August, but by January 2020 the renovation showed few visible signs of change. On November 28, 2020, the property was sold again, to Norcross investor Shaneel Lalani, whose firm was then reported as Billionaires Funding Group and is now described as Lalani Ventures, and whose advisers recommended housing, expanded retail and dining, and reopened outdoor spaces. By 2021 planners had been hired to draw up a master plan, and several new leases were announced in 2022. Nightlife has since crept back: a nightclub called MJQ Concourse arrived in early 2025 in the space once held by Dante's Down the Hatch, and a restaurant and lounge called Utopia anchors Upper Alabama Street. In July 2026 the owner said the property was about 65 percent leased, with a food hall under construction and a proposed housing tower still dependent on public funding. It remains a work in progress, one more chapter in the long story of a neighborhood Atlanta keeps rediscovering beneath its own feet.

## Flight Context

Underground Atlanta lies at approximately 33.753°N, 84.390°W, in the Five Points neighborhood of downtown Atlanta, beside the Five Points MARTA station and the railroad gulch that runs roughly northwest to southeast through the city center. From 3,000–5,000 feet MSL, the district is hidden among the downtown towers; use the skyline and the rail corridor as references. Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International Airport (KATL) is about 8 miles away and downtown lies beneath its Class B airspace. Fulton County Airport–Brown Field (KFTY) and DeKalb-Peachtree Airport (KPDK) are the nearest general aviation fields. Coordinate with ATC and expect heavy traffic, summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms.
