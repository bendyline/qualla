# Atlanta: The City Too Busy to Hate

The Phoenix of the South that burned to the ground in 1864 and rose to become the headquarters of Coca-Cola and CNN, the birthplace of Martin Luther King Jr., and the busiest airport on Earth.

**Location:** 33.7500, -84.3911

**Categories:** georgia, atlanta, city, civil-rights, history

## Introduction

General Sherman burned Atlanta to the ground on November 15, 1864, watching as 4,500 buildings went up in flames - everything except churches and hospitals. What he destroyed was a railroad hub; what rose from the ashes became something else entirely. Atlanta became the city that would rather tear down its past than preserve it, always building, always reinventing. The city that gave the world Coca-Cola in 1886 and Martin Luther King Jr. in 1929 and CNN in 1980 now processes more airline passengers than any other airport on Earth. The unofficial motto emerged during the civil rights era: 'The City Too Busy to Hate.' It was both aspiration and excuse, but it captured something true about a place that has always valued commerce over conflict.

## Rising from Sherman's Ashes

Sherman's March to the Sea began in Atlanta and left the city in ruins. Only 400 buildings survived. But the railroads that made Atlanta worth burning also made it worth rebuilding. By 1868, the city was Georgia's capital; by 1900, it had grown tenfold. Atlanta's relationship with its past has always been complicated - the city demolished more antebellum structures than Sherman ever torched, replacing history with parking lots and glass towers. Underground Atlanta, the original street level buried when viaducts were built over the railroad gulch, is one of the few places where the 19th century survives. The Cyclorama, the massive 1886 painting depicting the Battle of Atlanta, captures a moment the city has largely chosen to forget.

## The King Center and Sweet Auburn

Martin Luther King Jr. was born on January 15, 1929, at 501 Auburn Avenue, in a neighborhood so prosperous that African Americans called it 'Sweet Auburn.' The Victorian house still stands, now part of a National Historic Site that includes Ebenezer Baptist Church where King was baptized, where he co-pastored with his father, and where his funeral was held in 1968. The King Center, founded by Coretta Scott King, contains his tomb inscribed with the words 'Free at last.' The neighborhood around Auburn Avenue tells the other half of King's story - the segregation that created Sweet Auburn's Black-owned businesses, and the integration that, ironically, dispersed the community they served.

## Coca-Cola and Corporate Atlanta

John Pemberton invented Coca-Cola in Atlanta in 1886, selling it as a patent medicine for five cents a glass. The company never left. Today, Coca-Cola's headquarters anchors a cluster of corporate giants that have made Atlanta the capital of the New South: Delta Air Lines, UPS, Home Depot, CNN. The World of Coca-Cola museum celebrates the drink's history; visitors can sample over 100 beverages from around the world. The company's influence pervades the city - the Coca-Cola logo appears everywhere, and the corporation's philanthropic arm has shaped Atlanta's cultural institutions. When people say Atlanta is a business city, this is what they mean.

## Three Skylines and Sprawl

Atlanta has three distinct skylines: Downtown with the hotels and convention centers, Midtown with the cultural institutions and residential towers, Buckhead with the luxury shopping and office parks. Between them lies more sprawl than almost any American city - Atlanta metropolitan area covers 8,376 square miles, larger than Massachusetts. The interstates that enabled this sprawl now choke on traffic; Atlanta consistently ranks among America's worst commutes. MARTA, the rapid transit system, helps but doesn't solve. The Beltline, a 22-mile loop of parks and trails along abandoned railroad corridors, represents Atlanta's attempt to stitch together neighborhoods that cars pulled apart.

## Flying Through Atlanta

Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International Airport (ATL) has been the world's busiest airport since 1998, processing over 93 million passengers annually. Delta's hub operations mean connections through Atlanta dominate domestic travel. The airport's domestic terminal spans 130 gates; the international terminal adds 12 more. The Plane Train automated people mover carries passengers between concourses. For visitors arriving by air, the MARTA rail connects directly to downtown in 20 minutes. Atlanta itself sits at 1,050 feet elevation in the Piedmont region. From above, the city appears as sprawling development radiating from three downtown clusters - what seems like endless suburbs surrounding islands of density.
