# The Varsity (restaurant)

Founded in 1928 as a college hangout called The Yellow Jacket, Atlanta's Varsity grew into a two-block drive-in that bills itself as the world's largest, feeding game-day crowds of more than 30,000 beside the Downtown Connector.

## Introduction

"Have your money out and your food on your mind, and I'll getcha to the game on time!" For decades, that was the sound of a Georgia Tech football Saturday at The Varsity. The man calling it out was Erby Walker, who started at the restaurant at 15 sweeping floors, was nearly fired on his first day, and stayed for 45 years. His job, as the kickoff crowds poured in, was to keep the line moving. It was no small task at a Midtown Atlanta institution that has served hot dogs by the mile and, on football game days, more than 30,000 people.

## From Yellow Jacket to Varsity

Frank Gordy of Thomaston, Georgia, opened the place in 1928 at the corner of Luckie Street and Hemphill Avenue and called it The Yellow Jacket. A Reinhardt University graduate, Gordy had briefly studied industrial management at Tech himself. Popular legend says he dropped out; historian Janice McDonald says he actually transferred to Oglethorpe College. Either way, the business outgrew its corner. Gordy moved it to 61 North Avenue, on the northwest corner of Spring Street, and there renamed it The Varsity, a name meant to travel to other college campuses. The building kept growing to handle the crowds until it covered two city blocks, with room for up to 800 diners, and in the drive-in era carhops began serving customers right at their cars.

## Hot Dogs by the Mile

The numbers read like a tall tale, but they come from the restaurant and the press. In 1996 The Varsity said it served more than 5,000 fried fruit pies, two miles of hot dogs, 300 gallons of chili, 2,500 pounds of potatoes and 2,000 pounds of onions every day, and it described itself as the largest seller of Coca-Cola in the world. Two years later The Baltimore Sun reported that the kitchen sold over 12,000 hot dogs a day and could send more than 1,000 a minute down a conveyor belt. In 2018 Garden & Gun reported the restaurant could turn out up to 2,400 fried pies an hour. According to the Atlanta History Center, football game days draw more than 30,000 people, and Supercross Saturday and July Saturdays bring extra crowds of their own.

## Carhops, Comedians and Presidents

Some of The Varsity's staff became legends in their own right. In the 1940s a young carhop named Nipsey Russell dressed flamboyantly and seasoned his order-taking with jokes and songs, earning extra tips on his way to a career as a comedian. Erby Walker's speed on the service line made him one of its best-known employees; he retired in 2003, came back three weeks later, and that same year entered the Atlanta Convention and Visitors Bureau Hospitality Hall of Fame. He worked there until his death in 2008. Several sitting presidents, among them Jimmy Carter, George H. W. Bush, Bill Clinton and Barack Obama, are said to have stopped in during their terms. Mad magazine artist Jack Davis drew advertising for the restaurant, Rick Sebak featured it in the PBS documentary A Hot Dog Program, and it turned up in the film We Are Marshall. Dick Parker's 2003 history of the restaurant carries a fitting title: What'll Ya Have.

## A Freeway at the Door

The Varsity's neighborhood changed dramatically when the Georgia Department of Transportation built the Downtown Connector, now I-75/85. The freeway took out several blocks of Williams Street and much of the restaurant's western parking lot, forcing the company to build a parking garage as a replacement. Today the restaurant sits right beside the Connector's interchange with North Avenue, while Georgia Tech lies on the far side of the freeway, linked by the North Avenue bridge. Car-side service, a Varsity ritual since the drive-in era, was suspended in 2020 as the pandemic cut business, and the restaurant's own Atlanta page now lists curb service as closed; a drive-thru lane opened that September. The chain did grow, spreading across Georgia. Seven other branches now operate, in Bethlehem (which the chain calls its Winder location), Cartersville, Dawsonville, Kennesaw, Norcross, Rome and Watkinsville.

## Openings, Closings and a Campus Home

Not every outpost survived. The first Athens Varsity opened in 1932 at East Broad Street and College Avenue and closed around the late 1970s; a later Athens location on West Broad Street closed in June 2021. Alpharetta's branch closed in 2016 after becoming unprofitable. The Varsity Jr. in northeast Atlanta, the only other location with curbside service, shut in August 2010 after more than 40 years when the city rejected plans to replace its building, and those plans went to Dawsonville instead. The Gordy story also loops back to Reinhardt University in Waleska, where Frank met his wife, Evelyn, in 1924. The couple's custom-designed home was later moved there from Atlanta. Part of the family's land became the Mountain View campus of Chattahoochee Technical College, served by a road called Gordy Parkway.

## Flight Context

The Varsity sits at 33.7716°N, 84.3893°W in Midtown Atlanta, beside the North Avenue interchange on the I-75/I-85 Downtown Connector. From 2,500 to 3,500 feet, follow the Connector north from the downtown towers to the North Avenue crossing; the Georgia Tech campus spreads west of the freeway and the restaurant and its parking garage sit on the east side. The area lies beneath Atlanta's Class B airspace. Nearest airports: Fulton County Airport-Brown Field (KFTY), about 7 nm west; DeKalb-Peachtree Airport (KPDK), about 8 nm northeast; and Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International (KATL), about 8 nm south. Summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are common.
