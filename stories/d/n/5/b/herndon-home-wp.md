# Herndon Home

In Atlanta's Vine City stands the columned 1910 mansion of Alonzo Herndon, born into slavery and later the city's first Black millionaire, designed by his wife Adrienne, an actress and professor, and built almost entirely by African American hands.

## Introduction

Adrienne Herndon barely lived to see the house she designed. An actress and professor, she planned a Classical Revival mansion on University Place in Atlanta, working with a team of Black craftsmen, and she died in April 1910, around the time it was completed. The house became the home of her husband, Alonzo Franklin Herndon, a man born into slavery who rose to become Atlanta's first Black millionaire. Today the Herndon Home at 587 University Place NW is a historic house museum and a National Historic Landmark, a monument to what one family built.

## Seven Years Old and Free

Alonzo Herndon was born into slavery in 1858 in Walton County, Georgia. He was seven when emancipation came in 1865, and his family went to work as sharecroppers near Social Circle, about 40 miles east of Atlanta. Herndon found his way out through barbering. He opened his first barbershop in Jonesboro, Georgia, and went on to own three barbershops in Atlanta. The grandest stood at 66 Peachtree Street, a luxurious establishment whose customers included presidents, judges, businessmen and lawyers. Success did not protect him from the city's violence. During the Atlanta race massacre of 1906, white mobs damaged one of his large barbershops that served white customers, and Black barbers across the street were killed.

## Atlanta's First Black Millionaire

Herndon's greatest venture began in 1905, when he took over a failing mutual aid association and incorporated it as the Atlanta Mutual Insurance Association, becoming its founder and president. The company grew, and in 1922 it was reorganized as the Atlanta Life Insurance Company. Its success made Herndon the first Black millionaire in Atlanta, a genuine rags-to-riches story. His business legacy is written into the Sweet Auburn district as well. The Herndon Building was one of three contributing properties in the Sweet Auburn Historic District associated with his life, along with the Atlanta Life Insurance Company Building and the Rucker Building.

## The Actress Who Drew the Plans

Adrienne McNeil married Alonzo Herndon in 1893. In the fall of 1895 she joined the faculty of Atlanta University, described as its first African American female faculty member, serving as the first director of dramatics and teacher of elocution. On stage she performed as Anne Du Bignon, and in 1904 she made her professional debut in a one-woman show of Antony and Cleopatra. She was also the architect of the family home. Working closely with Black craftsmen, she shaped a Beaux Arts-influenced design of fifteen rooms, including a personalized mural of Alonzo's life, and the house was built almost entirely with African American labor. She died of Addison's disease in April 1910; sources give the date as April 6 or April 10. Accounts differ on whether she lived a few weeks in the finished house or died just before it was completed.

## Columns on University Place

The mansion stands in Atlanta's Vine City neighborhood, on the north side of University Place NW, next to the campus of Morris Brown College, formerly the campus of Atlanta University. It is a two-story rectangular brick building with porches projecting from its sides and a massive neoclassical entrance portico held up by large paired columns. The doorway is framed by wide sidelights and crowned with a semi-oval transom, and the windows display etching and tracery. A full entablature and modillioned cornice cap the walls, and the flat, balustraded roof is usable as an open terrace. Inside, the rooms are fitted with original period wood, stone and plaster, and murals in the main living rooms depict scenes from the Herndon family's history.

## A Landmark in Vine City

Herndon later married Jessie Gillespie of Chicago, and he lived until 1927. His and Adrienne's only son was Norris B. Herndon, and the Alonzo F. and Norris B. Herndon Foundation, named for father and son, supports entrepreneurship. The city of Atlanta declared the house a landmark building exterior in 1989, and in 2000 it became a National Historic Landmark. That same year the flat roof terrace was reported to be in poor condition, and in 2026 the Georgia Trust awarded the Herndon Foundation a $20,000 grant for urgently needed roof and drainage repairs, a reminder that preserving a house like this takes continuing care.
