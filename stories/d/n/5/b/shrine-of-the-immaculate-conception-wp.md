# Shrine of the Immaculate Conception

Atlanta's oldest Catholic church was founded by Irish railroad workers, saved from Sherman's torches by a stubborn priest, gutted by fire in 1982, and reborn as a downtown parish for the homeless, people with AIDS and LGBT Catholics.

**Location:** 33.7503, -84.3894

**Categories:** Roman Catholic church buildings in Atlanta, Gothic Revival church buildings in Georgia (U.S. state), Churches on the National Register of Historic Places in Georgia (U.S. state), Archdiocese of Atlanta, LGBTQ culture in Atlanta

## Introduction

When General William Tecumseh Sherman ordered Atlanta's civilians to leave the captured city in 1864, Father Thomas O'Reilly stayed. He was the only Catholic chaplain in the area, and he kept ministering to the wounded, including many of Sherman's own soldiers. So when the order came in November to burn the city's useful infrastructure before the March to the Sea, the Irish priest had some leverage. His church, the Immaculate Conception, survived. The Gothic Revival building that replaced it in 1873 is often called the oldest church in Atlanta and is among the city's oldest standing buildings.

## Mass in the Railroad Workers' Homes

Atlanta was founded in 1837 as Terminus, the end of the Western and Atlantic Railroad, and many of the people who built and ran the railroad were immigrants, mostly Irish Catholics. By 1850 about a tenth of the town's roughly 2,500 residents had been born abroad. Beginning around 1845, priests traveled from Augusta and Macon to say Mass in the workers' houses; the oldest surviving parish record is a baptism on August 9, 1846. As the Great Famine drove more Irish across the ocean, the congregation grew, and in 1848 the parish bought a lot one block from City Hall for $300. The wooden church it built there had a white-painted exterior, a small porch with stairs on either side, and a copy of Murillo's Immaculate Conception hanging behind the altar. In 1849 it was formally dedicated as the Church of the Immaculate Conception.

## Leverage Against the Torch

Thomas O'Reilly, a 30-year-old priest from County Cavan, Ireland, became pastor in 1861, the year the Civil War began. The church became a military hospital, and O'Reilly ministered to soldiers from both armies, receiving a commission as a Confederate chaplain in 1864. After Sherman's army captured Atlanta on September 2, 1864, O'Reilly defied the evacuation order. Much of that army was made up of Irish Catholic immigrants, and when Sherman ordered the burning, O'Reilly persuaded one of Sherman's officers, Henry Warner Slocum, to spare his church, hinting at a rebellion among the Catholic troops if it were destroyed. Sherman agreed and posted guards. Thanks to O'Reilly, the church, several nearby churches, City Hall and a number of homes downtown were among the few buildings left standing downtown. In 1945 the Atlanta Historical Society placed a memorial to him on the City Hall grounds.

## A Brick Church for a Growing City

The wooden church survived, but shelling had battered it and its floors were stained with blood from its days as a hospital. The congregation kept growing, reaching 900 members by 1871; among O'Reilly's altar boys was J. J. Haverty, who would go on to found the Havertys furniture company. In 1869 the parish hired William H. Parkins, the first architect to practice in Atlanta, to design a brick church in the Gothic Revival style. The poet-priest Abram Joseph Ryan preached when the cornerstone was laid on September 1, 1869. O'Reilly did not live to see it finished. He died in 1872, and his funeral was held in the old wooden church. The new building, which cost between $75,000 and $80,000, was dedicated on December 10, 1873, and O'Reilly was later laid to rest in a crypt beneath its main altar.

## Fire and Rebirth

As Atlanta spread outward in the 20th century, downtown's residential population declined, and by World War II the parish had only about 100 families. Some church leaders favored selling the property, but Bishop Gerald O'Hara chose to restore it instead, and in 1954 it was rededicated as a shrine. The Franciscans took charge in 1958, and the church joined the National Register of Historic Places in 1976. Then, on August 6, 1982, faulty wiring started a fire that destroyed almost the entire structure, leaving only the exterior brick walls standing. The main altar was lost, but the two side altars survived, as did a Pietà carved in Italy. During a restoration of about two years that cost roughly $4 million, the congregation worshipped at nearby Central Presbyterian Church. The shrine was rededicated on May 25, 1984.

## A Church for the City's Margins

In 1988 the archdiocese sent Father John Adamski, who had ministered to people with AIDS in New York City. He opened the crypt for guided tours to attract visitors and began weekly dinners for people with AIDS, which drew about 100 to 120 people by the early 1990s. By 1992 the shrine had started a ministry for LGBT people. The decision divided opinion in the church, but LGBT Catholics came. Adamski also worked with Central Presbyterian to open a nightly emergency shelter, and in 1992 the soup kitchen was feeding about 500 people a week. By the mid-2000s the parish counted about 325 families, and roughly a third of its congregants were LGBT. The church built by Irish immigrants by then had a congregation split roughly evenly between white and Black members, the latter mostly African Americans and Haitian Americans. Inside, beneath 12 chandeliers and some 50 stained glass windows, O'Reilly still rests under the altar, beside Thomas Cleary, one of his successors.

## Flight Context

The Shrine of the Immaculate Conception stands at about 33.75°N, 84.39°W at Martin Luther King Jr. Drive and Central Avenue in downtown Atlanta, sharing its block with Central Presbyterian Church and surrounded by city and state government buildings. From the air it is small among the downtown towers; look for its two unequal front towers and slate roof amid the government buildings, from 3,000 feet or higher. Atlanta sits beneath busy Class B airspace around Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International Airport (KATL), about 8 miles to the south, so coordinate with ATC; Fulton County Airport-Brown Field (KFTY) lies about 8 miles to the west-northwest and DeKalb-Peachtree Airport (KPDK) about 10 miles to the northeast. Summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are common.
