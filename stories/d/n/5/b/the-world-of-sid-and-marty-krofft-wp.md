# The World of Sid and Marty Krofft

For six months in 1976, an entirely indoor amusement park built on the Krofft brothers' television worlds filled the Omni International complex in downtown Atlanta, and its cavernous empty halls later became the home of CNN for decades.

## Introduction

The first ride came before any of the rides. Visitors to The World of Sid and Marty Krofft began by stepping onto a multi-story escalator that carried them to the top of the park, a machine listed by Guinness World Records as the longest freestanding escalator in the world, supported only at its ends. From there they worked their way down through six levels of carousels, dark rides and live shows, all of it indoors in downtown Atlanta's Omni International complex. The park was built on the television worlds of the sibling producers Sid and Marty Krofft. It opened in May 1976, and it lasted six months.

## Four Tickets, Six Floors

Each visitor received a ticket booklet with four tickets, one for each of the park's themed areas. Most people started at the top and descended. Tranquility Terrace filled the top three of the six levels. Below it came Uptown, then Lidsville, and finally Living Island Adventure on the bottom level. The layout turned gravity into a guide, and the park's floors were largely open to the Omni's main atrium. Down on the Omni's ground level was a skating rink. In keeping with the colorful, creative spirit of its namesakes, the park packed its floors with elaborate attractions.

## A Crystal Carousel and a Human Pinball

The attractions were pure Krofft. A large carousel was adorned with mythological creatures made of crystal. In a giant pinball-machine dark ride, riders sat inside big ball-shaped pods and ricocheted through the "machine." Just below the reception level, a stilt-walking master of ceremonies presided over a carnival floor with three circus trailers, each home to a costumed performer doing a two- to three-minute show to another performer's voice. The middle trailer held Betty Broadbent, a purple belly-dancing rhino voiced by Ruth Buzzi. Farther down came a caricature artist, a slackrope walker, and 33 RPM, a trio of locking-style street dancers from South Central Los Angeles, who also shared a song-and-dance show with Patty Maloney. Little people performed throughout the park, among them Debbie Dixon, who later appeared in Star Wars, and John and Greg Rice of Lake Worth, Florida, listed by Guinness as the World's Shortest Living Twins until John's death in 2005. A glass-blowing shop run by Lee Mabes shared a level with a gift shop and food emporium, and on the forest-themed lower level a theater staged a live-action Krofft character show.

## Six Months of Fanfare

The park opened with great fanfare and plenty of hoopla in the national press, and it appeared prominently in the host segments of the first season of the Saturday morning Krofft Supershow. Many of the performers came from Los Angeles for the run. None of it filled the turnstiles. The park never drew more than 300,000 visitors, and it closed in early November 1976, after six months. The Kroffts partly blamed its location: downtown Atlanta was then experiencing urban decay, sat close to major housing projects, and was generally not considered a safe place for a family outing. Others said the park was simply overpriced, especially next to the much larger Six Flags Over Georgia. Dana White, a professor of urban studies at Emory University, put it bluntly: "People were thinking of it as a full-day, family experience but it only took a couple hours. It wasn't worth the money or the drive."

## From Lidsville to the Newsroom

After the closing, the park's space sat largely vacant. A dinner theater ran briefly on one of the lower levels before it, too, shut down, and the Omni complex went through renovation after renovation while struggling to attract tenants into the mid-1980s. Then Ted Turner went looking for a new home for CNN, which had outgrown its sparse headquarters in Atlanta's old Progressive Club building. He found the old Krofft section of the Omni largely unchanged, its enormous rooms and hallways ideal for television studios and newsrooms. CNN moved in in 1987, and Turner soon bought the whole complex and renamed it CNN Center. The famous escalator survived for decades and became part of the CNN studio tour, which carried visitors through spaces where Krofft characters once performed until tours ended in 2020. CNN moved its Atlanta operations back to its Midtown campus on Techwood Drive in 2023 and early 2024, and the escalator was reportedly removed in February 2025, though its steel frame remains. The complex, which has included a hotel since it opened in 1976 (now the Omni Atlanta Hotel at Centennial Park, renamed after CNN left), stands beside State Farm Arena and, since its owner CP Group announced a rebrand in April 2024, goes by the name The Center.
