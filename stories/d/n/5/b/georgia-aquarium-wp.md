# Georgia Aquarium

Built largely with a Home Depot co-founder's fortune, the Georgia Aquarium opened in 2005 as the world's largest aquarium and was designed around a 6.3-million-gallon indoor ocean built for whale sharks.

## Introduction

Walk into the 100-foot acrylic tunnel that runs through the Ocean Voyager exhibit and the ocean closes over your head. Above and around you move several thousand fish in 6.3 million gallons of water, in an exhibit made to showcase whale sharks and manta rays, the animals this entire building was designed around. It is an unlikely scene for downtown Atlanta, far from any coast, but that is the point of the Georgia Aquarium. When it opened in 2005, it was the largest aquarium in the world.

## A Hardware Fortune Goes to Sea

The money came largely from one source. A $250 million donation from the foundation of Bernard Marcus, a local businessman and co-founder of The Home Depot, provided the bulk of the funding to build and stock the aquarium, and a further $40 million in corporate contributions allowed it to open debt-free. Jeff Swanagan, hired in 2002 as the aquarium's first employee, served as founding president and executive director until 2008 and is largely credited with creating it, from the design of the structure to the procurement of its animals. After 27 months of construction, it opened with 60 animal habitats. Although its admission charges are among the highest in the country, attendance far exceeded expectations: a million visitors in the first 100 days and 10 million by June 2009. Aquariums in Singapore and China surpassed it in size in 2012, but it is still billed as the largest in the United States.

## Gentle Giants on an Indoor Reef

Ocean Voyager is the aquarium's heart. Built by The Home Depot, the exhibit measures 284 by 126 feet, runs 20 to 30 feet deep, and recreates the life of the Mesoamerican Barrier Reef System, making it, by the aquarium's own billing, the largest indoor aquatic habitat in the world. Besides the tunnel, it offers one of the world's largest viewing windows. In 2007 two whale sharks arrived, named Taroko, after Taiwan's Taroko Gorge National Park, and Yushan, after Taiwan's Jade Mountain, just before a ban on capturing the species took effect. Taroko was euthanized in August 2025 because of declining health, leaving Yushan as the aquarium's only whale shark at that time. The manta rays have their own story. Georgia Aquarium is one of only a handful of sites in the world to have displayed manta rays, and its first, Nandi, had been accidentally caught in the nets that protect South Africa's coast from sharks. She joined Ocean Voyager in 2008 as the first manta ray on display in the country.

## The Hard Questions of the Beluga Pool

Not every chapter has been triumphant. The aquarium has been home to as many as five beluga whales at once, beginning with two males acquired from an amusement park in Mexico. Marcus hailed their rescue from an inadequate environment as the kind of conservation work the aquarium should pursue. Breeding proved heartbreaking. The beluga Maris gave birth to an underweight calf in 2012 that died within a week; her second calf, born on Mother's Day 2015, lived less than a month, and Maris herself died that October, reigniting debate over whether captive beluga breeding was humane or successful. In 2015 the aquarium sought to import 18 belugas captured in Russia. Federal regulators denied the permit, the aquarium sued, and a federal court let the denial stand. As of 2016, the aquarium stated that it would not take whales or dolphins caught in the wild. In August 2026 it received two more male belugas, Balor and Zephyr, born in human care at the shuttered Marineland of Canada, bringing its pod to six.

## Coral from Fiji, Turtles from a Hurricane

Bernard Marcus, who died in November 2024, said that the aquarium's conservation mission was as important as its role as an attraction. Even before opening, it worked with Georgia Tech, Georgia State University and the University of Georgia on endangered-species research and education. Coral for its exhibits is grown through a collaboration between Georgia Tech and the University of the South Pacific, by suspending blocks of pumice over a reef near the Fijian village of Tagaqe for eight months so that seaweeds and reef invertebrates can colonize them. In 2019 the aquarium began housing coral species threatened by a bleaching disease in the Florida Keys, and that same year it sheltered sea turtles from the Georgia Sea Turtle Center, evacuated ahead of Hurricane Dorian. Its researchers study whale sharks off the Yucatán Peninsula, belugas in Alaska, penguins in South Africa and dolphins in Florida's Indian River Lagoon.

## Sharks, Touch Pools and New Ways to See

The aquarium keeps growing. Its largest expansion, a $100 million, 45,000-square-foot addition, opened on October 23, 2020, as Sharks! Predators of the Deep, a 1.2-million-gallon saltwater gallery holding great hammerheads, tiger sharks, silvertips and more, complete with a dive cage that lets visitors join them. Dolphin Coast houses a pod of 13 bottlenose dolphins in 1.8 million gallons, and its controversial show was reworked in 2016 to be more educational. Explorers Cove opened in August 2024 with touch pools of cownose rays and lake sturgeons. In 2025 the aquarium became the first in the country to offer a free app using filters and AI tools to help visually impaired visitors see animals, signs and menus more clearly.

## Flight Context

Georgia Aquarium sits at approximately 33.763°N, 84.395°W, in downtown Atlanta, Georgia. From 3,000–5,000 feet MSL, look for the downtown skyline; the aquarium itself is hard to single out among the surrounding buildings. This is busy airspace: Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International Airport (KATL) lies about 9 miles away, and downtown sits beneath its Class B airspace. Fulton County Executive Airport–Charlie Brown Field (KFTY) is about 7 miles away and DeKalb-Peachtree Airport (KPDK) about 9 miles. Coordinate with ATC and expect heavy traffic, summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms.
