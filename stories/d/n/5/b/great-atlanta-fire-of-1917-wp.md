# Great Atlanta fire of 1917

On a windy May afternoon in 1917, a fire that started in a pile of mattresses while Atlanta's firefighters were stretched thin across the city burned roughly 1,900 buildings, displaced about 10,000 people and reshaped the Old Fourth Ward.

**Location:** 33.7506, -84.3772

**Categories:** Atlanta metropolitan area disasters, History of Atlanta, Urban fires in the United States, Fires in Atlanta, 1917 fires in the United States, 1917 in Georgia (U.S. state), 20th century in Atlanta

## Introduction

At 12:46 on the afternoon of May 21, 1917, a call came in from a small warehouse just north of Decatur Street in Atlanta's Old Fourth Ward. The crew sent to check on it found a stack of mattresses burning, but they had brought no firefighting equipment with them. On any other day the fire would probably have been put out right there. That day, though, the city's fire department was already spread across several other blazes, and by the time reinforcements arrived, the flames were leaping north on a brisk wind. Before the night was over, the Great Atlanta Fire had burned 300 acres of the city.

## Four Calls in an Hour

It was a clear, warm, sunny day with a brisk breeze from the south, the kind of weather that turns a spark into a disaster. The mattress fire was not the day's first emergency but the fourth call in about an hour. At 11:39 a small fire broke out at the Candler Warehouse. Four minutes later came a fire seven blocks north that destroyed three houses. At 12:15, south of the Georgia Railroad, another fire destroyed ten homes before crews brought it under control. So when the call from Decatur Street arrived, Atlanta's firefighters were scattered across the city. How the big fire actually started remains unclear, but hot temperatures and strong winds did the rest.

## North Through Sweet Auburn

The fire ran quickly up to Edgewood Avenue and from there through the main residential areas of Sweet Auburn, sparing little. The blocks between Decatur and Edgewood were crowded with shanties and lean-tos, the homes of people with the least, and they burned fast, feeding the fire until it was strong enough to drive north on its own. It carved a corridor between Jackson, now Parkway and Charles Allen, and Boulevard, bulging outward at Highland and just south of Ponce de Leon Avenue. At Houston Street, Boulevard still held the fire on its eastern side, just sparing John Wesley Dobbs' block. At Highland it turned both east and west, racing through many fine homes.

## Dynamite on Boulevard

Around four in the afternoon, firefighters began to slow the fire by deliberately destroying homes in its path with dynamite, along Pine, Boulevard and finally Ponce de Leon. It was not enough to stop it outright. By nightfall the fire had crossed Ponce de Leon and, though weakened, pushed north through the newly built neighborhood along St. Charles, Vedado Way and Greenwood Avenue. It finally stopped at 10 p.m., more than a mile north of where it began. Help had come from far beyond Atlanta, with fire trucks sent from nine Georgia towns, including Macon and Augusta, and from Chattanooga and Knoxville in Tennessee. In all, 22 million gallons of water were pumped against the flames. The toll was staggering: roughly 1,900 buildings destroyed (counts range from 1,900 to 1,983) across more than 300 acres and, by various counts, 50 to 73 city blocks, more than 10,000 people displaced, and damage estimated at $5 million. Fires smoldered for the rest of the week.

## The End of Wood Shingles

Atlanta took one clear lesson from the ruins. About 85 percent of the destroyed buildings had wood shingle roofs, so the city quickly passed an ordinance banning wood shingle roofs, and by one account every older building had replaced them by 1931. The scale of the loss put the 1917 fire in grim company. In the city's history, only the fire of 1864 during Sherman's campaign is said to have done more extensive damage.

## A Neighborhood Remade

Rebuilding came slowly and unevenly, and large swaths of burned land stayed open for years. Commercial strips rose quickly along Edgewood and Auburn avenues, where busy streetcar routes 17 and 3 ran. Along Boulevard up to Ponce de Leon, where large estates with spacious front yards had stood, builders put up dozens of two- and three-story apartment buildings pressed right up to the sidewalk. Low-income housing developments filled the destroyed southern section and the areas south of North Avenue, and some 50 acres around Boulevard and Highland eventually became the campus of Atlanta Medical Center, which closed in 2022 and whose campus has since been largely demolished for redevelopment. Other land stayed open, including what is now the King Memorial and Bedford-Pine Park, today's Central Park. Only north of Ponce de Leon, where single-family homes were quickly rebuilt, did the old pattern return. Everywhere else, the fire changed the character of this part of Atlanta for good.

## Flight Context

The fire began near 33.7506°N, 84.3772°W, just north of Decatur Street in Atlanta's Old Fourth Ward, and burned more than a mile north toward and across Ponce de Leon Avenue, east of downtown. From about 3,000 feet AGL, subject to air traffic control, trace the Boulevard corridor north from the downtown edge toward Ponce de Leon; Central Park and the Martin Luther King Jr. historic area are open spaces within the old burn zone. The area lies beneath the busy airspace around Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International (KATL), about 7 nm south-southwest, so plan with current charts and expect ATC coordination.
