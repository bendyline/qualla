# Sweet Auburn

Born in the aftermath of Atlanta's 1906 racial violence, Auburn Avenue grew into one of the country's great African-American business districts, the birthplace of Martin Luther King Jr., and a neighborhood that survived a freeway cut through its heart.

**Location:** 33.7547, -84.3814

**Categories:** Sweet Auburn, African-American history in Atlanta, National Historic Landmarks in Atlanta, Historic districts on the National Register of Historic Places in Georgia (U.S. state), Festivals in Atlanta, Old Fourth Ward

## Introduction

"Sweet Auburn! loveliest village of the plain." Oliver Goldsmith opened his 1770 poem The Deserted Village with those words, and John Wesley Dobbs, an Atlanta civil rights leader, borrowed them for a street. Auburn Avenue, running east of downtown, held one of the largest concentrations of African-American businesses in the United States. Dobbs called it "the richest Negro street in the world," using the language of his era, and in 1956 Fortune magazine repeated the phrase to a national audience. The name Sweet Auburn stuck. So did the street's reputation as the place where black Atlanta built banks, newspapers, churches and nightclubs of its own.

## A Refuge Forged by Violence

The first settlement here grew on land once occupied by Union troops and was known for many years as Shermantown. It developed quickly thanks to the nearby Georgia Railroad. Yet Auburn Avenue's rise as the city's black business district was to a great extent a consequence of terror. Before 1906, black-owned businesses operated largely downtown, in a district integrated as far as ownership was concerned. Competition between working-class whites and blacks for jobs and housing bred fear, and newspapers fanned it with hearsay about alleged sexual assaults on white women by black men. The result was the 1906 Atlanta race massacre, long called a riot, which left at least 27 people dead, 25 of them black, and more than 70 injured. Afterward, black entrepreneurs moved their businesses to relative safety, some to the area around the Atlanta University Center west of downtown, others east to Auburn Avenue in the Fourth Ward.

## Insurance, Ink and Enterprise

Some of the street's builders had known slavery firsthand. Henry A. Rucker, a formerly enslaved man who became a businessman and politician, put up the Rucker Building in the early 1900s, one of Atlanta's first black-owned office buildings. Alonzo Herndon, a formerly enslaved man from Walton County, founded Atlanta Life Insurance in 1905, and it grew into one of the nation's leading black-owned insurance companies, with its headquarters buildings along the avenue. In 1928 the Atlanta World began publishing here as a weekly; by the early 1930s it had become the Atlanta Daily World, often described as the first successful black daily newspaper in the country. When the Atlanta Police Department integrated in 1948, it hired eight black officers and assigned every one of them to Auburn Avenue. Together with the city's black colleges, the street anchored a prosperous black middle and upper class that rose despite enormous social and legal obstacles.

## Pulpits, Peacocks and a Birth Home

Churches anchored the avenue's heritage. Big Bethel AME dates to 1847 and Ebenezer Baptist to 1886, and First Congregational also helped build the street's heritage. Just off the avenue, Our Lady of Lourdes, founded in 1912, became the city's first Black Catholic parish. Nights belonged to places like the Royal Peacock, which opened in 1938 as the Top Hat Club and brought B.B. King, the Four Tops, the Tams and Atlanta's own Gladys Knight to its stage. The Odd Fellows Building and Auditorium went up in 1912 and 1913, and the Sweet Auburn Curb Market opened in 1924. On January 15, 1929, Martin Luther King Jr. was born in the neighborhood, in a house built in 1895. That birth home is now part of the Martin Luther King Jr. National Historical Park.

## Split, Endangered, Rebuilt

In 1976 a 19-acre National Historic Landmark District was designated here, recognized for its history and development as a segregated community under Jim Crow law, and the district joined the National Register of Historic Places the same year. Recognition did not stop decline. Like many inner-city neighborhoods, Sweet Auburn suffered disinvestment, crime, homelessness and abandonment, all compounded by the Downtown Connector freeway, which split it in two. In 1992 the National Trust for Historic Preservation named it one of America's 11 Most Endangered Historic Places, and the Georgia Trust later put it on its 2006 Places in Peril list. The Historic District Development Corporation set out to reverse the slide, starting with the houses around Dr. King's birth home and working outward. Some losses were permanent: the 1920s Herndon Building was demolished in 2008 after damage in that year's Atlanta tornado. In 2014 the Atlanta Streetcar began looping west along Auburn and east along Edgewood, linking the King park to downtown.

## Stages and Painted Walls

The avenue still knows how to throw a party. Sweet Auburn Springfest took over the district again in May 2026 for its 40th year, and a Sweet Auburn Music Fest fills the street each fall with live music and food. The street's festival tradition reaches back to 1984, when civil rights leader Hosea Williams founded the Sweet Auburn Heritage Festival to celebrate and revitalize the district; its stage is said to have given early exposure to up-and-coming artists, among them Usher, Outkast, India.Arie and Raven-Symoné. Along Edgewood Avenue, murals by international artists such as Sten Lex and local painters including Dr. Dax and Chris Veal have made the corridor a hotspot for street art in Atlanta. The Auburn Avenue Research Library on African American Culture and History and the APEX Museum, founded in 1978, are dedicated to preserving and highlighting that history.

## Flight Context

Sweet Auburn centers on 33.7547°N, 84.3814°W, immediately east of downtown Atlanta. From 2,500 to 3,500 feet, follow the I-75/I-85 Downtown Connector where it runs north-south along the east side of the downtown towers; the freeway cuts straight through the district, and Auburn Avenue continues east toward Boulevard, with Freedom Parkway curving along the neighborhood's northern edge. The area sits beneath Atlanta's Class B airspace. Nearest airports: Fulton County Airport-Brown Field (KFTY), about 7 nm west; Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International (KATL), about 7.5 nm south-southwest; and DeKalb-Peachtree Airport (KPDK), about 8 nm north-northeast. Summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are common; cool, dry mornings offer the clearest views.
