# Center for Puppetry Arts

Opened in 1978 by a frog with a pair of scissors, Atlanta's Center for Puppetry Arts grew from a touring marionette troupe into what it bills as the largest puppetry organization in the United States, home to what it calls the world's largest collection of Jim Henson artifacts.

## Introduction

On September 23, 1978, the guest of honor at a ribbon-cutting in Midtown Atlanta was made of felt. Kermit the Frog, with his creator Jim Henson at his side, cut the ceremonial ribbon on a converted elementary school, and the Center for Puppetry Arts opened its doors to the public. Nearly half a century later, it is billed as the largest organization in the United States dedicated to puppetry and is one of the few puppet museums anywhere in the world. Its work stands on three legs, performance, education and museum, and all of them trace back to one touring puppeteer's conviction that an old art form deserved a permanent address.

## A Marionette Troupe Looks for Home

Vincent Anthony learned his craft on the road. A young puppeteer from Florida, he toured with Nicolo Marionettes, a company based in New York City, under the tutelage of Nicholas Coppola. By 1966 he wanted something smaller: a community where he could be an active partner and make a difference. He and Mitchell Edmonds, a colleague from his Nicolo days, moved to Atlanta and founded their own company, the Vagabond Marionettes. The troupe toured the Southeast and presented several seasons at Atlanta's Memorial Arts Center, renamed the Woodruff Arts Center in 1982. Anthony's vision, though, reached past any single tour. He wanted a center that would promote puppetry and become a vital part of the city. In 1978 he found its home in the former Spring Street Elementary School. That first season set a pattern the center still follows, with an exhibition of puppets, shows for adults and families, and community workshops that continue to this day.

## Where the Muppets Retire

Kermit's ribbon-cutting turned out to be a prologue. On July 25, 2007, the center announced a new Jim Henson Wing, planned to house anywhere from 500 to 700 retired Muppets drawn from Fraggle Rock, The Muppet Show and Sesame Street, along with films, sketches and other materials from the Jim Henson Company archives. The wing, part of the center's new building, finally opened on November 14, 2015. Today the Worlds of Puppetry Museum holds what the center describes as the world's largest collection of Jim Henson artifacts, with items rotating in and out of view. Familiar faces look out from the collection: Kermit, Miss Piggy, Rowlf the Dog and Ernie, with the Swedish Chef, the Pigs in Space, and Dr. Teeth and the Electric Mayhem also counted among its holdings. For anyone who grew up with these characters on television, meeting them face to face in a Midtown gallery is a strange and oddly moving encounter.

## Skeksis, Servos and a Lion King Mask

Henson's creations share the building with puppets from many periods and many countries. The museum ranks as one of the largest collections of global puppetry artifacts in the hemisphere, and it treats exhibition as essential to understanding performance rather than as a sideline to it. The range is startling. A Skeksis from the film The Dark Crystal shares the collection with Wayland Flowers' Madame and The Little Players. Two of the mask prototypes Julie Taymor created for the Broadway smash The Lion King are here, and so are Tom Servo and Crow T. Robot from Mystery Science Theater 3000. Behind the galleries sits a library devoted to puppetry art, a resource for anyone who wants to study the form rather than simply admire it.

## Curtain Up, Hands On

Every year the center stages its Family Series, a mix of adapted classics and new works in a variety of puppetry styles. Past productions have included The Shoemaker & The Elves reset in 1940s Manhattan, Jon Ludwig's Dinosaurs, and The Ghastly Dreadfuls' Compendium of Graveyard Tales and Other Curiosities, a Halloween variety show. During SummerFest, national touring troupes set up shop in Midtown. Many family shows come paired with a Create-A-Puppet workshop, so children leave having built a puppet connected to the story they just watched. Older audiences have the New Directions Series, teen and adult-oriented work by Atlanta artists and visiting companies, each show carrying a recommended age limit. A Film Series screens classic movies, hidden gems and contemporary productions tied to puppetry. Adults can study the craft through the Adult Education Series, and the Distance Learning program sends virtual field trips to classrooms across the country, with many programs now also offered online.

## Honors for a Puppet House

Recognition arrived from well beyond the puppet world. In 2000 the Ford Foundation named the center one of 28 arts organizations to receive grants from its $40 million New Directions/New Donors program, and the Kresge Foundation awarded it three separate grants for its capital campaigns. When Atlanta prepared for the 1996 Olympics, the center was the only theater group chosen to take part in all four years of the Games' arts festival program, a run after which, in the center's account, Newsweek called it "one of the most exciting companies in American theater." In 2008 its education department received the Microsoft Education Award as a Laureate of the Tech Museum Awards. Within its own field the center stands out further still, having received the UNIMA-USA Citation of Excellence, a top honor in American puppetry, on 13 separate occasions as of 2024.
