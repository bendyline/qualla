# Battle of Taliwa

Around 1755, near the Etowah River outside present-day Ball Ground, Georgia, a Cherokee force defeated the Muscogee Creek in a battle remembered through oral tradition, and a teenage Nanyehi, later known as Nancy Ward, earned the title Ghigau.

**Location:** 34.3386, -84.3775

**Categories:** Battles involving the Cherokee, Muscogee, Cherokee County, Georgia, Battles in Georgia (U.S. state), Conflicts in 1755, Native American history

## Introduction

Nanyehi was still a teenager when she followed her husband, the Cherokee warrior Kingfisher, into battle. As the fighting raged, tradition says, she chewed on bullets to make them more ragged. When Kingfisher was killed, she picked up arms herself, rallied the Cherokee and led a charge that sent the Muscogee Creek fighters into flight. Tradition places that moment around 1755 near the meeting of Long Swamp Creek and the Etowah River, about two and a half miles from present-day Ball Ground, Georgia. The fight became known as the Battle of Taliwa, and the young woman who helped turn it would later be known to history as Nancy Ward.

## Decades of Raids

The battle capped a long and bitter rivalry. The Creek held territory in Georgia and Alabama, while the Cherokee held lands in Tennessee and the Carolinas. The Yamasee War of 1715 to 1717, when several Native nations rose against colonial South Carolina, split both peoples internally and drove them further apart. The Cherokee were divided over the war, but in early 1716, after a Creek delegation was killed in the Cherokee town of Tugaloo, they sided with South Carolina against the Creek, though not all of the nation supported that choice. The killings deepened a conflict between the two nations. Even after South Carolina and the Creek made peace by 1717, raids and counter-raids between Creek and Cherokee continued for decades. A state historical marker describes Taliwa as the fiercest and most decisive battle of the long war of the 1740s and 1750s.

## Oconostota's Victory

According to the account that has come down to us, the great Cherokee war chief Oconostota led about 500 warriors against a larger Creek force. The defeat was so complete that the Creek withdrew south of the Chattahoochee River, leaving northern Georgia to the Cherokee. For the Muscogee Creek, it meant being pushed out of homelands in the region. For the Cherokee, it opened the way to settle land that the marker calls the heart of the "ill-fated Cherokee Nation." Behind that tidy summary lies a battle in which real people fought and died, among them the husband of the young woman who would become its most famous figure.

## The Beloved Woman

For her actions at Taliwa, Nanyehi received the title Ghigau, meaning "Beloved Woman" or "War Woman," one of the highest honors the Cherokee bestowed upon women. With it came a seat in council meetings and a voice in the nation's decisions, and she went on to become a Cherokee political leader. One biography says her reward also included a captive, given to her as an enslaved person. That detail is a sobering reminder that the honors of the day were bound up with the taking of human beings, and that this person, whose name the account does not record, was also part of Taliwa's story.

## A Story Told Sixty Years Later

How much of this is certain? Some scholars have pointed out that the battle's details all seem to come from oral tradition, and specifically from one man, James Wafford. He said he heard the story as a child from a trader named Brian Ward, who claimed to have witnessed the fighting as a young man and told his account in 1815, about 60 years after the event. The battle has also attracted more speculative theories. Some historians have proposed that the petroglyphs on Judaculla Rock, a carved soapstone rock near Caney Fork Creek in Jackson County, North Carolina, form a map of the battle. The Cherokee connect the rock to the legend of Tsu'kalu, or Judaculla, and estimates of the carvings' age vary widely: the North Carolina Department of Natural and Cultural Resources dates them from about the year 500 up to the 1700s, and some archaeologists place the earliest carving thousands of years back.

## The Land Changes Hands Again

The Cherokee victory at Taliwa did not secure the land forever. By the early 19th century the Cherokee Nation was losing territory to the United States through a series of negotiations. The area around Ball Ground saw few major encroachments by settlers until around 1820, but in 1838 the Trail of Tears began, and the Cherokee were forcibly removed from all of northern Georgia. A 1953 Georgia Historical Commission marker in Ball Ground calls the Cherokee Nation "ill-fated." Today the battle is commemorated by that roadside marker, a reminder of a place where one people displaced another, only to be driven out in turn.

## Flight Context

The story's coordinates, 34.3386°N, 84.3775°W, mark the town of Ball Ground in Cherokee County, Georgia, near the downtown railroad crossing and State Highway 372. The 1953 historical marker places the traditional battle site about 2.5 miles east, near the confluence of Long Swamp Creek and the Etowah River. From 2,500 to 3,500 feet, look for the small town grid of Ball Ground and the wooded Etowah River valley to its east, with hillier country rising toward the north Georgia mountains. Cherokee County Regional Airport (KCNI) is about 3 nm to the southwest, and Pickens County Airport (KJZP) near Jasper is about 8 nm to the north-northwest. Morning valley fog is common along the river in cooler months.
