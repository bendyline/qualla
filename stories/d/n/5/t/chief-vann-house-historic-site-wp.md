# Chief Vann House Historic Site

A mansion usually described as the first brick house in the Cherokee Nation hosted a U.S. president, rested on the labor of enslaved people, and was taken from its Cherokee owner by Georgia in 1834, just before the Trail of Tears.

**Location:** 34.7631, -84.8222

**Categories:** Cherokee Nation (1794–1907) buildings and structures, Historic house museums in Georgia (U.S. state), African-American history of Georgia (U.S. state), Federal architecture in Georgia (U.S. state), State parks of Georgia (U.S. state), Native American museums in Georgia (U.S. state)

## Introduction

In 1819, President James Monroe was traveling from Augusta to Nashville and meant to spend the night at the spartan Moravian mission at Spring Place, Georgia. Instead he went down the road to a two-story brick mansion he found more comfortable, and asked its owner for a bed. His host was a young Cherokee named Joseph Vann, known as Rich Joe, then in his early twenties, and the house was already called the "Showplace of the Cherokee Nation." Less than two decades later, the state of Georgia would take it from him.

## Red Clay Made Solid

Joseph's father, James Vann, built the house as he rose to become the wealthiest businessman and chief in the Cherokee Nation, and he wanted a home that reflected his status. It is usually described as the first brick residence in the Nation. Construction began around 1803 and was complete by about 1804, though a Moravian diary has Vann moving in only in March 1805, with help from the Moravians, who also educated local Cherokees, and from a man named Vogt, perhaps Vann's brother-in-law Charles Vogt. The bricks were made from red clay on the plantation itself, and the handwrought nails and hinges came from Vann's own blacksmith shop. Exterior walls are about eighteen inches of solid brick, interior walls about eight. The lead mason, Robert Henry Howell of Virginia, lies buried in the Moravian cemetery at Spring Place. Blending late Federal and early Georgian styles, the house rises two full stories with twelve-foot ceilings, topped by a half story.

## Four Colors in Every Room

Inside, the Vann House bursts with red, blue, green and yellow, with white used only as filler. One interpretation reads the colors as nature: red for Georgia clay, blue for sky, green for trees and grass, yellow for the harvest of wheat and corn. Another sees a standard Federal-style palette. What sets the house apart is distribution. Most Federal-period homes kept each color to a single room, but here the colors mingle in almost every room, on mantels, doorjambs and wainscoting that are all original to the house. The doors, known as Christian doors, carry details of a cross and an open Bible. On the third floor, once storage and later children's rooms, a larger room believed to belong to the boys could be closed off from a narrower girls' room for privacy.

## The People Who Built the Wealth

The showplace rested on forced labor. The 800-acre grounds held a blacksmith shop, six barns, five smokehouses, a trading post, a still, more than 1,000 peach trees and 147 apple trees, and 42 cabins for the enslaved people whose work sustained it all. Beneath the house, the basement had two rooms. One served as a wine cellar. The other was likely used to confine enslaved people Vann chose to punish, and he was known to be exceptionally cruel to the people he held in bondage. Those 42 cabins are a reminder of how much the Cherokee Nation's showplace depended on people held there against their will. James Vann himself lived in the house only a few years before he was murdered at Buffington's Tavern in 1809.

## A Staircase That Hangs in Air

The house passed to Rich Joe, his father's favorite child, neither the youngest nor the eldest. Between 1809 and 1818 he paid to have it decorated, and in 1818 a father and son, John and James McCartney, arrived to add all of the current woodwork, including Ionic columns. Their masterpiece is the staircase. Its upper landing sits over the first-floor hall with no visible supports, so it seems to hang in midair. The trick is balance: the opposite end of the stair is anchored roughly six inches into a solid brick wall far denser than the landing, which works like a set of scales that can never be tipped. It is one of the oldest examples of cantilevered construction in Georgia.

## Seized, Smoked Out and Restored

As the Trail of Tears approached, the family was caught between two claims. Colonel William Bishop and the Georgia Guard moved to take the house on the grounds that Rich Joe had hired a white man to run it, violating a new Georgia law against whites working for Cherokees without a permit, while Spencer Riley claimed he had won it in the Land Lottery of 1832. Bishop evicted Rich Joe, made the house his headquarters and later threw a smoldering log onto the cantilevered stairs to smoke Riley out, damaging the house. The house is also tied to the story of John Howard Payne, the playwright who wrote the words to "Home, Sweet Home": arrested with Chief John Ross in November 1835 and taken to Spring Place, he is said to have been jailed at the Vann House, though a local museum's archive disputes that, since Payne described his prison as a small log hut. By 1834 the Vann family had been forced out for good; Joseph Vann moved west in 1837, ahead of the main removal, to Webbers Falls in present-day Oklahoma, and never returned. He later won $19,605 in compensation. After seventeen owners and years of ruin, the state acquired the house in 1952, and a six-year restoration led by Dicksie Bradley Bandy began in 1958. Since 2002 the Robert E. Chambers Interpretive Center beside it has told the Cherokee story.

## Flight Context

The Chief Vann House lies at approximately 34.763°N, 84.822°W, at Spring Place on the outskirts of Chatsworth in Murray County, Georgia. From 3,000–4,500 feet MSL, look for the Cohutta Mountains rising about 10 miles to the east as the main visual reference; the house sits on high ground with a commanding view of the surrounding land. Dalton Municipal Airport (KDNN) is about 4 miles away, and Gilmer County Airport at Ellijay (FAA identifier 49A) is about 19 miles away. Mind the rising terrain toward the Cohuttas, and expect summer haze, afternoon thunderstorms and morning valley fog.
