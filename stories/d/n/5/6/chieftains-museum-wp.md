# Chieftains Museum

In Rome, Georgia, the white frame house that grew from Cherokee leader Major Ridge's log cabin tells the story of a man who signed away his people's Southeastern homeland, predicted it would cost him his life, and was right.

## Introduction

"I have signed my death warrant." Major Ridge reportedly said those words on December 29, 1835, as he finished making his mark on the Treaty of New Echota, the agreement that ceded the remainder of Cherokee lands in the Southeast to the United States. He was right. Less than four years later he was assassinated for his part in ceding the tribe's communal land, an act considered a capital crime. His home still stands on the Oostanaula River in what is now Rome, Georgia, a two-story white frame house built around a log house (dated to 1819 in some accounts). Today it is the Chieftains Museum, also known as the Major Ridge Home.

## A Title from Horseshoe Bend

Ridge earned the rank that became part of his name in 1814, when he led the Cherokee alongside General Andrew Jackson at the Battle of Horseshoe Bend during the Creek War. After the war he moved his family to the Cherokee town known as Head of Coosa, the site of present-day Rome, settling near the town of Chatuga around 1819. His first house here was small and built of hand-hewn logs in the dogtrot style. Over time he enclosed the dogtrot and added extensions on each side, transforming the cabin into a white wood-frame, two-story house. It became the "Big House" of his busy plantation.

## Cotton on the Oostanaula

The plantation began with 223 acres and grew to roughly 280 to 300 acres, and it ran down to the banks of the Oostanaula River, upstream of where it joins the Etowah to form the Coosa. Ridge used the river for travel and to carry his cotton to market, and he ran a profitable ferry that took wagons and their teams across it. With George Lavender he also founded a trading post. Ridge became a wealthy planter, and by one count about thirty enslaved African Americans labored on his plantation. Their work is part of this house's story, and any honest account of the Big House includes the people who were held there against their will.

## The Treaty Party

Removal was the question that divided the Cherokee in the 1830s. The chiefs had agreed they could not go to war against the United States over removal, but most Cherokee still opposed giving up the land. Ridge belonged to a minority, the Treaty Party, who believed relocation was inevitable and wanted to negotiate the best possible terms for their people. Their signatures on the Treaty of New Echota sealed the cession. Ridge, his family and many other Cherokee emigrated west in March 1837. Most of the tribe was later forcibly removed to what became the Cherokee Nation in Indian Territory. On June 22, 1839, Ridge was assassinated, and other members of the Treaty Party were killed as well, for having signed away land that belonged to the whole nation.

## A House on the Trail of Tears

The Major Ridge Home was saved for preservation when Celanese Fibers Corporation donated the property to Rome's Junior Service League in 1969, and in 1971 it opened as a historic house museum called Chieftains Museum. It was declared a National Historic Landmark in 1973. In 2002 the National Park Service designated it an official site on the Trail of Tears National Historic Trail, which had been established in 1987. The building was later renamed Chieftains Museum / Major Ridge Home in his honor, and its exhibits focus on Ridge and on 19th-century Cherokee life and culture. Part of the old plantation along the river is now part of Rome's Riverside Park. It is a place that asks visitors to hold complicated truths at once: a Cherokee leader who sought the best terms he could get for his people, who enslaved others, and who paid with his life for the treaty that gave up the Cherokee homeland in the Southeast.

## Flight Context

Chieftains Museum stands at 34.2771°N, 85.1702°W on the Oostanaula River in Rome, Georgia. From 1,500–2,500 feet AGL, find the confluence where the Oostanaula and Etowah meet to form the Coosa, then follow the Oostanaula upstream; the museum sits near its bank beside Riverside Park. Richard B. Russell Regional Airport (KRMG) is about 4.5 nm north, close enough that the river confluence is an easy visual reference on arrival or departure. Cobb County International Airport-McCollum Field (KRYY) is about 33 nm east-southeast, and Chattanooga's Lovell Field (KCHA) about 46 nm north. Summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are common in northwest Georgia.
