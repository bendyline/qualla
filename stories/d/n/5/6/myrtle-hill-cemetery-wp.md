# Myrtle Hill Cemetery

Terraced like a wedding cake above the meeting of Rome, Georgia's rivers, Myrtle Hill holds more than 20,000 graves, among them a First Lady, a football player whose mother saved the sport in Georgia, and America's Known Soldier.

**Location:** 34.2519, -85.1792

**Categories:** Rome, Georgia, Cemeteries on the National Register of Historic Places in Georgia (U.S. state), Protected areas of Floyd County, Georgia, Cemeteries established in the 1850s

## Introduction

A blindfolded sailor picked the name. When the United States set out to honor its World War I dead with both an Unknown Soldier and a Known Soldier, the choice for the second fell to chance, and it landed on Private Charles W. Graves of Rome, Georgia. His grave is the centerpiece of a brick plaza at Myrtle Hill Cemetery, where more than 20,000 people rest on a terraced hill above the place where the Etowah and Oostanaula rivers meet. The hill's stories run from a 1793 battle to a First Lady's funeral.

## Before the Graves

The hill saw bloodshed long before it held the dead. In October 1793, General John Sevier led 800 men in pursuit of Cherokees who had killed thirteen settlers at Cavett's Station, near Knoxville, the previous month. The pursuit ended in Georgia, at a Cherokee village he called Hightower, and on this hill Cherokee defenders took up a position to keep him from fording the rivers. By Sevier's own account, he moved to cross the Etowah about a mile south, drew the defenders out, then galloped back to cross here. When the Cherokee leader Kingfisher was killed, the remaining warriors fled, and Sevier burned the village. Cherokee bones and relics were later found in the hill's crevices. Within a few decades the Cherokee lost the land entirely: after gold was discovered at Dahlonega in 1829, the forced removal known as the Trail of Tears drove them from their homes, and their territory was allotted to white settlers. Rome was founded in 1834.

## A Wedding Cake Above the Rivers

Rome is a city of seven hills and three rivers, and because the Etowah, Oostanaula and Coosa flood, three of those hills became burial grounds. When the city's first cemetery, Oak Hill, was nearly full, Colonel Thomas A. Alexander and Daniel S. Printup chose this hill in 1850. Civil engineer Cunningham Pennington, one of its owners, laid it out as a picturesque 19th-century rural cemetery on six levels of steep terrain, with roads circling the slope and terraces stacked in a layered, "wedding cake" look. It opened in 1857 and took its name from the trailing myrtle, Vinca minor, sometimes called the "Flower of Death," that grew wild across it. It now covers 32 acres and is listed on the National Register of Historic Places.

## War Comes to the Hilltop

In 1863 Rome's citizens paid for three earthen forts, and Fort Stovall rose on the crest of Myrtle Hill to guard against Union raiders targeting the Noble Foundry across the Etowah. Terracing for burials has erased every visible trace of it. The war left a smaller mark too: the top of the monument on John Billups's grave, one of the first on the hill, is believed to have been shot off by a musket ball. Because Dr. Samuel H. Stout ordered permanently disabled soldiers of the Army of Tennessee sent to Rome, many are buried in the soldiers' section, which holds more than 377 Confederate and Union dead, 85 of them unknown. The cemetery also has a Freedman (African American) section, set aside when it was segregated. Tom McClintock, an African American man who worked as the cemetery's gravedigger for 42 years, was buried at Myrtle Hill in 1915.

## Buried Three Times

Private Graves, an infantryman in the American Expeditionary Force, was killed by German artillery shrapnel on the Hindenburg Line on October 5, 1918. His body did not come home until March 1922, aboard the troopship Cambria. After a blindfolded sailor drew his name, the government wanted him buried at Arlington, but his mother objected, and on April 6, 1922, she buried him near Antioch Church. After her death, with his brother's agreement, Romans moved him to Myrtle Hill in 1923, his third and final burial. On Armistice Day that year, Graves and the 33 other Floyd County men who died in the war were honored with three Maxim guns and 34 magnolia trees. Today the plaza around his grave holds more than 3,000 bricks engraved with the names of those who served.

## A First Lady Comes Home

Ellen Axson Wilson was born in Savannah but grew up in Rome, the daughter of a Presbyterian minister. She graduated from Rome Female College, met a young lawyer named Woodrow Wilson in 1883, studied art in New York after her father's death in 1884, and married Wilson in 1885. As First Lady she had a skylit studio installed in the White House in 1913 and kept painting. She died of Bright's disease on August 6, 1914, and a funeral train carried her back to Rome, where crowds lined Broad Street as her carriage passed. She lies beside her parents and her brother. Also buried here are Richard Vonalbade Gammon, a University of Georgia football player who died of injuries from the Georgia-Virginia game, and his mother, Rosalind, who is credited with saving football from being outlawed in Georgia.

## Flight Context

Myrtle Hill Cemetery rises at about 34.25°N, 85.18°W on the south side of downtown Rome, Georgia, across the South Broad Street bridge, right where the Etowah and Oostanaula rivers join to form the Coosa. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet, the river confluence is the key landmark; look for the steep, tree-dotted hill with its ring roads and stepped terraces just south of the junction, and a soldier statue on the crest. Richard B. Russell Regional Airport (KRMG) lies about seven miles north of downtown. The ridges of northwest Georgia can trap morning fog in the river valleys, and summer afternoons often bring thunderstorms.
