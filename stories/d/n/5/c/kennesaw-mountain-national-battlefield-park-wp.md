# Kennesaw Mountain National Battlefield Park

On the wooded slopes of a mountain whose Cherokee name is said to mean burial ground, a national battlefield park preserves the earthworks where Sherman's frontal assault broke against Johnston's lines in the summer of 1864.

**Location:** 33.9832, -84.5779

## Introduction

The name came first. "Kennesaw" is said to derive from the Cherokee "Gah-nee-sah," meaning "cemetery" or burial ground. In the summer of 1864, the name proved grimly apt for this mountain northwest of Atlanta. For two weeks, from June 18 to July 2, William Tecumseh Sherman's Union armies and Joseph E. Johnston's Confederates faced each other across these ridges, and on June 27 Sherman set aside the flanking maneuvers that had carried him this far into Georgia and ordered his men straight at the enemy's works. Today the roughly 2,900 acres of Kennesaw Mountain National Battlefield Park, between Marietta and Kennesaw, preserve the ground where that decision played out.

## A Mountain in the Way

The numbers favored the Union. Sherman brought 100,000 men, 254 cannons and 35,000 horses to the Atlanta campaign, while Johnston had only 50,000 men and 187 guns. Johnston's answer was fortification. Again and again he blocked Sherman's advance with fortified defensive lines, and at Kennesaw he did it once more, anchoring his position on the heights. Up to this point Sherman had worked around such obstacles. This time he chose to change his tactics and attack head-on. Much of the fighting that followed took place not on Kennesaw Mountain itself but on a spur of Little Kennesaw Mountain now known as Pigeon Hill, and on the ground to its south around Cheatham Hill.

## The Dead Angle

On June 27, Sherman launched a large frontal assault. Union troops under Major General John A. Logan went up against Pigeon Hill, while Major General George H. Thomas threw strong attacks at Cheatham Hill, the center of the Confederate line. Both were repulsed with heavy losses. Cheatham Hill is still commonly called the Dead Angle. Sherman's armies suffered about 3,000 casualties in the battle, against roughly 1,000 for Johnston. A few days later Sherman wrote to his wife that he was beginning "to regard the death and mangling of couple thousand men as a small affair, a kind of morning dash." The battle was deemed a Confederate victory. Yet a demonstration by Major General John M. Schofield had threatened Johnston's left flank, and the Confederate army once again withdrew toward Atlanta. Winning the fight did not stop the march.

## From War Department to Park Service

The effort to protect this ground began more than half a century after the guns fell silent. On February 8, 1917, Congress authorized the Kennesaw Mountain National Battlefield Site under the War Department, starting with a 60-acre tract at Cheatham Hill. On August 10, 1933, it was transferred to the Department of the Interior as a unit of the National Park System, and on June 26, 1935, Congress renamed it Kennesaw Mountain National Battlefield Park and authorized it to grow to take in Big and Little Kennesaw Mountains. Like every historic area administered by the National Park Service, it was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on October 15, 1966. The battlefield includes the site of some of the heaviest fighting of the entire Atlanta campaign, and it was set aside for public inspiration and for interpreting the events that happened here.

## Earthworks Under the Trees

The park protects three battlefield areas: the ground in front of the Visitor Center off Burnt Hickory Road, the major site at Cheatham Hill, and the Kolb farm. At the park's southern tip, Peter Valentine Kolb's farmhouse, where a smaller battle was fought, has been restored to its original condition. More than 25 miles of trails lead past historic earthworks and cannon emplacements, and monuments honor soldiers from three of the states whose men fought here, Illinois, Texas and Georgia. The Visitor Center holds a museum and a theater that screens films about the battle. Hikers climb from the Visitor Center to the 1,808-foot summit, a gain of about 664 feet, by roughly 1.1 miles of trail or 1.4 miles of road, and since January 2026 the summit road has been closed to private cars, leaving the trail, the park shuttle, bicycles and foot travel. Part of the park's natural area is old-growth forest, recognized by the Old-Growth Forest Network. As Atlanta's urban sprawl pushes outward, concerns have grown that overuse or misuse could threaten the preserved ground.

## Flight Context

Kennesaw Mountain National Battlefield Park is centered near 33.9832°N, 84.5779°W, between Marietta and Kennesaw northwest of Atlanta. The mountain's summit reaches 1,808 feet MSL, so plan to overfly at 3,500 feet MSL or higher; rising some 660 feet above the park's Visitor Center, the mountain makes a clear visual landmark. Cobb County International Airport-McCollum Field (KRYY) lies only about 2 nm north-northwest, so watch for traffic and keep the terrain in mind on approaches. Dobbins Air Reserve Base (KMGE), a military field, is about 5 nm southeast, and Fulton County Airport-Brown Field (KFTY) about 12.6 nm south-southeast. Clear mornings usually offer the best visibility.
