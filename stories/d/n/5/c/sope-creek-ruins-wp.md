# Sope Creek Ruins

Stone walls along a Cobb County creek mark the site of a paper mill that supplied Civil War Atlanta's newspapers, burned twice, rebuilt twice, and later picked up a Confederate-mint legend that the records do not support.

**Location:** 33.9406, -84.4378

**Categories:** Industrial ruins, Civil War history, National Register of Historic Places in Cobb County, Georgia, Industrial buildings and structures on the National Register of Historic Places in Georgia (U.S. state), Chattahoochee River National Recreation Area

## Introduction

"I have on hand a few bundles of paper, which I will ship you, and this will be the last for some time." In September 1863, a paper maker on Sope Creek wrote those words to an Atlanta newspaper that depended on him, and his letter still reads like a man at the end of his rope. His workers had been called away to guard the city. The one machine he could keep running needed eighteen-hour days. Today the mill he described survives only as stone ruins scattered along about a mile of creek in Cobb County, Georgia, where a succession of enterprises harnessed falling water from 1850 to 1940.

## Paper for a City at War

The Marietta Paper Mills was incorporated on December 19, 1859, possibly by Andrew Schofield Edmondston and Saxon A. Anderson. It was the Daily Intelligencer of Atlanta that printed Edmondston's letter, dated September 10, 1863, and in it he explained why the paper was running out. His hands had volunteered for the Home Guard and were now guarding stores and preparing the city's defenses. At the start of the war he had encouraged two of them to join Confederate service, and losing two or three more had left the mill so short that one machine ran only when the remaining workers put in eighteen-hour days. The government was urgent for paper, he wrote, yet it would not exempt his workers. Paper makers, he complained, "are not like Shoe-Makers" and "cannot be learned after the Conscript officer takes after a fellow."

## Fire, and Fire Again

Worse was coming. On July 5, 1864, Union soldiers burned the Marietta Paper Mill along with Denmead's Flour Mill, which Edward Denmead had built in 1855 on the west bank about 200 yards upstream of today's Paper Mill Road crossing. The flour mill apparently never came back. The paper mill did, rebuilt in the late 1860s (the National Park Service says it was running again by September 1868), and its paper soon reached Atlanta again: H.H. Cabaniss, business manager of the Atlanta New Era in 1867, later recalled buying his paper in rolls from the Marietta mills, which under Saxon A. Anderson's ownership supplied a great many of the area's daily newspapers. Then the mill burned again in 1870 and rose once more in 1871. It struggled after that second rebuilding and was sold at public sale in 1873, reorganized as the Marietta Paper Manufacturing Company. A wood pulp mill arrived in 1888 and a twine factory in 1889. In 1902, production at the site stopped. The creek had one more job to do: in 1922 a hydroelectric dam and power plant went up on its west side, 900 yards upstream.

## Walls in the Woods

When the Georgia Historical Commission submitted its report on the site in March 1973, it detailed the purpose of every surviving structure. The largest was the mill building, about a quarter mile downstream from Paper Mill Road on the east bank: five rooms, roughly 300 feet long. The pulp-grinding mill, just south of the road, stood two stories high and about 100 feet long. The commission found the oil room and office across a small creek and the foundations of an old bridge near the dam. It also described Denmead's flour mill, though evidence of it is hard to spot today because of construction. The 1922 dam was already inoperable. On April 27, 1973, the ruins joined the National Register of Historic Places, their address listed as restricted. Today they are preserved within the Sope Creek unit of the Chattahoochee River National Recreation Area.

## Who Built the Walls

In May 1932 the Atlanta Constitution ran a photograph of the ruins under a stark caption: "GREAT WALLS BUILT BY SLAVE LABOR." It is the only reference to slavery anywhere in the surviving material about the Sope Creek industrial area. The caption also implied, wrongly, that the mill had never recovered from the 1864 burning. A single line in a newspaper cannot settle who raised these walls, and the record offers nothing more. Who laid these stones, and under what conditions, remains a question the surviving documents cannot answer, and the people who did that heavy work are left unnamed.

## The Mint That Never Was

A more colorful story clings to Sope Creek: that the Confederacy printed its money here. The 1973 survey mentions no printing press, and neither do the articles written about the ruins. The only published reference to money-making appears in a New York Times account of the Atlanta Golf Classic, written by Lincoln A. Werden and printed on May 31, 1968. Describing the par-3 13th hole at Atlanta Country Club, which borders the creek, Werden noted a waterfall to the left and, off to the right of the green, "the site of a former Confederate mint." His only quoted source besides the golfers was the tournament director, and the club, formed in 1964, had opened only two years earlier. Research into the site has found no evidence of a mint, and the National Park Service calls claims that the mill printed Confederate money unsubstantiated. The mill made paper, including cartridge paper for Confederate guns.

## Flight Context

The Sope Creek ruins lie at 33.9406°N, 84.4378°W in eastern Cobb County, near the Chattahoochee River northwest of Atlanta. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet, trace the river's wooded corridor to the mouth of Sope Creek and look for the open fairways of Atlanta Country Club, which borders the creek; the ruins lie along the creek near the Paper Mill Road crossing and are likely to be hidden under tree canopy. Dobbins Air Reserve Base (KMGE) is only about 4 nm to the west-southwest, so respect its military airspace, and the area lies under Atlanta's Class B shelf. Civil airports nearby: DeKalb-Peachtree (KPDK), about 8 nm east-southeast, and Cobb County International-McCollum Field (KRYY), about 9 nm west-northwest. Leaf-off months give the best chance of glimpsing the stone walls.
