# Battle of Kolb's Farm

On June 22, 1864, near Marietta, Georgia, John Bell Hood hurled his Confederate corps at entrenched Union lines around a farmhouse on the Powder Springs road, a one-sided defeat that also deepened a bitter feud between Sherman and Joseph Hooker.

**Location:** 33.9400, -84.5800

**Categories:** Atlanta campaign, Battles of the American Civil War in Georgia (U.S. state), Battles of the western theater of the American Civil War, Cobb County, Georgia, 1864 in Georgia (U.S. state)

## Introduction

What did John Bell Hood think he saw on the afternoon of June 22, 1864? Nobody can say for certain. His daily report that day was unclear, his memoirs never mentioned the fight, and other Confederate sources fell silent. Apparently he believed the Union troops before him were strung out in marching columns, ripe for a powerful flank attack. Instead, a little after 5 p.m. on the road west of Marietta, Georgia, his corps charged into entrenched infantry and massed artillery near the farmhouse of the Kolb family. By the time the firing stopped, his corps had lost at least a thousand men in the fields and ravines.

## The Road to Kennesaw

The Atlanta campaign began on May 7, 1864, when William Tecumseh Sherman's roughly 100,000 men started south under orders from Ulysses S. Grant to break up Joseph E. Johnston's Confederate Army of Tennessee. For six weeks the pattern repeated: Sherman swung around a flank, and Johnston fell back, through Rocky Face Ridge, Resaca, Cassville, New Hope Church, Pickett's Mill and Dallas. At Pine Mountain on June 14, Confederate Lieutenant General Leonidas Polk was killed. By mid-June Johnston's army held Kennesaw Mountain, probably stronger than Rocky Face Ridge or Allatoona Pass, whose summit let lookouts see every Union movement by day. Heavy rain swelled the creeks, and sick soldiers on both sides were sent to the rear.

## Shifting South in the Mud

Rather than assault the mountain, Sherman kept extending his right wing south. On June 21, Major General Joseph Hooker's XX Corps pushed forward to occupy hills near Kolb's Farm, also known as Culp's Farm, while Major General John Schofield's XXIII Corps crossed Noses Creek on its right. Johnston saw his left being stretched almost to breaking. He pulled Hood's entire corps out of the right of his line and moved it to the left, filling the gap with Joseph Wheeler's cavalrymen, who dismounted to man the empty trenches. It was a gamble, and Johnston knew it, but the only alternative he saw was another retreat. By that evening Hood's men were camped on the Powder Springs road west of Marietta.

## Into the Open Fields

June 22 dawned clear, and Hooker advanced. When his skirmishers reported Confederates forming for an attack, he ordered his corps to entrench along a line running to Kolb's Farm on the Powder Springs-Marietta road, with Schofield's men fortifying south of the road. The 123rd New York and 14th Kentucky, sent ahead to scout, saw the enemy massing. When the assault came, a volley from the 14th Kentucky sent Alfred Cumming's brigade, made up largely of former Georgia militia, reeling; it rallied and was thrown back again. North of the road, Confederate brigades drove in the New Yorkers and emerged into cleared fields, where batteries on a hill held by John W. Geary's division tore into them. Some sought cover in a ravine that became a death trap under Union guns. Hindman's division followed and came apart under artillery fire, some brigades fleeing and others stalled at a boggy creek.

## More Slaughter Than Battle

Johnston admitted losing 1,000 men. Historian Albert E. Castel estimated that Hood's attack cost about 1,500 killed, wounded and missing, two-thirds of them from Carter L. Stevenson's division, against roughly 250 Union casualties, and he called it "more a one-sided slaughter than a battle." The victory soured into a quarrel. During the fighting Hooker twice asked for reinforcements, claiming the "whole rebel army" was in front of him, and his report to Sherman, delayed for hours, fretted about his right flank. Sherman, who had heard only some cannon fire, later wrote that he chided Hooker for claiming three corps faced him, though Castel judged Sherman's account the product of faulty memory and personal animosity. Hooker's standing with Sherman declined steadily from there. When James B. McPherson was killed in July, Sherman passed over the more senior Hooker and chose Oliver Otis Howard to command the Army of the Tennessee, and Hooker resigned.

## One Vast Fort

Kolb's Farm was a tactical Union victory, but Sherman's attempt to turn Johnston's left seemed blocked. Hood's men entrenched, Schofield reported his line stretched too thin, and Sherman telegraphed Chief of Staff Henry Halleck in frustration: "The whole country is one vast fort." Unwilling to let a stalemate develop, he ordered a frontal assault on June 27, the Battle of Kennesaw Mountain, and his attacks were repulsed with heavy casualties. Much of the Kolb's Farm battlefield has since been altered and fragmented, and residential subdivisions now cover the heights from which the Confederates began their attack. Some essential pieces survive inside Kennesaw Mountain National Battlefield Park: the Kolb farmhouse and family cemetery, Ward Creek, and the high ground where Union troops turned back the assault.

## Flight Context

The Kolb's Farm battlefield lies at about 33.94°N, 84.58°W along Powder Springs Road on the southwest side of Marietta, Georgia, roughly three miles south of Kennesaw Mountain. From 3,000 to 4,000 feet, the wooded ridge of Kennesaw Mountain to the north is the key landmark; the surviving farmhouse sits on protected park land surrounded by suburban neighborhoods. Dobbins Air Reserve Base (KMGE) is only about four miles to the east-southeast, and Cobb County International Airport-McCollum Field (KRYY) about five miles to the north-northwest; check charts carefully, as Dobbins' military airspace and the Atlanta Class B shelves overlie the area. Summer brings haze and afternoon thunderstorms to the Atlanta region.
