# Ocoee Whitewater Center

For the 1996 Atlanta Olympics, engineers squeezed a normally dry stretch of Tennessee's Upper Ocoee River into the only in-river canoe slalom course in Olympic history, a course that still roars to life on summer weekends for rafters willing to face Humongous.

**Location:** 35.0690, -84.4620

**Categories:** Venues of the 1996 Summer Olympics, Olympic canoeing venues, Artificial whitewater courses, Tourist attractions in Polk County, Tennessee, Canoeing and kayaking venues in the United States

## Introduction

Every hour the Upper Ocoee runs with water, the Tennessee Valley Authority gives up 35 megawatts of generation, worth about $2,100 at six cents a kilowatt-hour. Most of the year the river here is kept dry, its flow diverted through a tunnel to a powerhouse downstream. But on summer weekends the dam opens, 1,600 cubic feet of water per second pour into the old riverbed, and a 500-meter stretch near Ducktown, Tennessee, turns back into an Olympic canoe slalom course. It is the only in-river course ever used for Olympic slalom competition, built for the 1996 Summer Olympics in Atlanta and still run today by guided rafts and private boaters.

## Squeezing a River

Canoe slalom is a relatively recent Olympic sport, largely because most host cities lie far from usable whitewater. The first two Olympic venues, the Augsburg Eiskanal for Munich in 1972 and Segre Olympic Park for Barcelona in 1992, were canals built around dams on small mountain rivers. Atlanta went a different way, reshaping the then-dry streambed of the Upper Ocoee. Levees faced with natural rock narrowed a 1,640-foot stretch from 200 feet wide to 70, cutting it by about two-thirds to create the drops and eddies a slalom course needs. Even that narrowed channel is twice the width of most artificial courses, and to give the surface the right dynamics it needed 1,400 cubic feet per second, two or three times the flow of most artificial channels. Engineers first tested the changes on an outdoor one-to-ten-scale model near the base of Ocoee Dam No. 1. As the only in-river Olympic venue, it had the greatest water volume, the highest drop and the steepest slope.

## Through Humongous

The 1996 final used 25 gates, six red upstream gates and 19 green downstream gates, spread along a course twice as long as the one used at London's Lee Valley in 2012. Racers sprinted between widely spaced gates at the top, then threaded tight ones at the bottom. The rapids carry names worth savoring: Best Ledge, Smiley's, where a boulder splits the stream, Slam Dunk, the Conveyor Belt wave train, and Callihan Ledge. Last and steepest comes Humongous, a constricted rapid with a wave train on the left of a midstream boulder and two big drops on the right, Godzilla and Humongous Hole. Olympic gate 23 sat in the small eddy between them. To avoid getting stuck in the hole, paddlers had to catch the Godzilla wave to stop their momentum, then catch it again to surf across toward gate 24. Commercial rafts often take the right side today for the two-drop ride, and sometimes flip at the second drop.

## The Price of Water

Keeping this river wet is expensive. The Upper Ocoee is the 3.5-mile stretch between Dam No. 3 and its powerhouse, where lake water normally travels by tunnel and penstock at 1,050 cubic feet per second to generators 300 feet below the lake surface, producing 23 megawatts. Any water sent down the riverbed bypasses them. The Olympic release of 1,400 cubic feet per second cost 31 megawatts of generation, and today's 1,600-cubic-foot recreational releases sacrifice 35 megawatts, about $2,100 an hour at six cents a kilowatt-hour. Rafting revenue partly compensates TVA, but slalom athletes needed a cheaper place to train, and in 2006 the U.S. National Whitewater Center opened in Charlotte, North Carolina, its course fed by three half-megawatt pumps. The Ocoee course was also meant to host the 2001 ICF Canoe Slalom World Championships, which were cancelled in the wake of the September 11 attacks.

## A Dry River Comes Alive

Ocoee rafting owes its start to a repair job. From 1913, when Dam No. 2 and its wooden flume were completed, the 4.5-mile Middle Ocoee below it was waterless most of the time. In 1976 the flume was shut down for renovation, and water suddenly flowed through the long-dry riverbed. Canoeists, kayakers and commercial rafting outfits flocked to it, and by 1983, when the flume returned to service, they had enough clout to win regular summer recreational releases from TVA. Upper Ocoee releases followed with the Olympics. Today the Middle Ocoee has water roughly 106 days a year, while the Upper runs at midday on about 34 summer weekend days, when roughly two dozen commercial companies take more than 750 rafters a day through the Olympic course. Private boaters paddle free of charge whenever there is water.

## Trails, Fire and a Fresh Start

Because the river is dry most of the year, the center, now run by the U.S. Forest Service, doubles as a base for hiking, mountain biking, conferences and weddings, drawing about 300,000 visitors a year. The Tanasi Trail System offers 30 miles of mountain bike trails around it, Thunder Rock Campground sits a mile downstream at Powerhouse No. 3, and the Ocoee Scenic Byway, U.S. 64, whose westbound lanes began as Olympic parking and staging, provides access. Early on April 26, 2022, fire destroyed the visitor center's main building. The day-use area reopened on May 27, 2023, and the Forest Service began gathering public input on a master plan for the center's future. The Forest Service's visitor page says the trails and both parking lots are open, while the fenced area around the destroyed visitor center remains closed.

## Flight Context

The Ocoee Whitewater Center lies at 35.069°N, 84.462°W in Polk County, Tennessee, near Ducktown, along U.S. 64 in the Ocoee River valley. The valley is narrow and the surrounding terrain is mountainous, so overfly at 4,000 to 5,000 feet MSL and keep generous terrain clearance; follow U.S. 64 and the river upstream from the Ocoee dams to the Whitewater Center, with Dam No. 3 above it. On summer weekend release days the rapids show as bright white water. Nearest airport: Martin Campbell Field at Copperhill (FAA identifier 1A3, no ICAO code), about 6.5 nm to the southeast. Larger fields include Cleveland Regional Jetport (KRZR), about 19 nm west-northwest, and McMinn County Airport (KMMI) at Athens, about 20 nm north-northwest. Mountain fog and low cloud often fill the valley on cool mornings.
