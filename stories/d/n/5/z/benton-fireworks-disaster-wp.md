# Benton fireworks disaster

In 1983 an explosion leveled a barn on a Tennessee bait farm, killing eleven people and exposing what officials called the largest illegal fireworks factory ever discovered in the United States.

## Introduction

Webb's Bait Farm sold worms. Along Welcome Valley Road in rural Polk County, about two miles south of Benton, Tennessee, it had grown and sold fishing bait and tackle since 1978. But from December 1982, in an old metal dairy barn on the property, workers were mixing chemicals by hand into illegal M-80 and M-100 fireworks. Law enforcement and the public learned the factory existed only on the morning of May 27, 1983, when the barn exploded and eleven people died.

## A Factory in the Dairy Barn

The idea came from Howard Emmett Bramblett of Ocoee, who owned a fireworks store in Benton and knew the farm's owner, Dan Lee Webb. With Webb facing financial problems, Bramblett suggested the operation, reportedly taught Webb and his relative David Parks how to make the fireworks, and connected them with suppliers and distributors in several states. Authorities came to consider him the mastermind. John Franklin Miller, an Ohio fireworks manufacturer, was the main supplier of materials and found customers. Inside the 40-by-70-foot barn, chemicals were mixed on the first floor and fireworks assembled on the second. At its peak the factory employed 12 to 14 people, reportedly related to Webb by blood or marriage, who earned five dollars an hour. In roughly five months they turned out well over a million fireworks, with estimates ranging from about 1.5 million to more than 5 million, for buyers in twelve states, earning profits of roughly $1.25 million.

## Morning of May 27

At about 9:15 a.m., eleven workers were on duty when a cache of M-80 and M-100 explosives, flash powder and other chemicals in the barn detonated. The blast killed all eleven instantly and leveled the building. It was heard and felt in Cleveland, more than 20 miles away, and witnesses described a white mushroom cloud estimated at 600 to 800 feet tall. The shock wave leveled trees 100 yards out, and debris landed as far as 200 yards from the site. Over the following minutes, smaller blasts that witnesses compared to shotgun fire went off, believed to be cases of fireworks that had not detonated at first. One neighbor's windows shattered and sheetrock came off his ceiling. A cousin of the owner, mowing grass nearby, was thrown more than 70 yards and suffered burns to about 35 percent of his body; he was the one person injured.

## Eleven Lives

Many of the dead were family. They included Webb's mother, his brother and his uncle; a mother and her nineteen-year-old daughter; and David Parks and his wife. The oldest was 53. William M. Bass, a forensic anthropologist from the University of Tennessee, arrived with a Tennessee Emergency Management Agency crew to identify them, and his work is the subject of a chapter in Beyond the Body Farm, the 2007 book he wrote with Jon Jefferson. Investigators never settled on a single cause. Possibilities included sparking from an electrical wire, a light fixture or another appliance found at the site, or even the scraping of boots on the floor. Searching the property, authorities found 172 boxes of unexploded fireworks worth about $20,000.

## Twenty-One Convictions

Webb surrendered within two days of the explosion. Charged with eleven counts of involuntary manslaughter and with illegally manufacturing and possessing explosives, he was held on a $300,000 bond. In 1984 he received a ten-year federal sentence for manufacturing explosives without a license, then pleaded guilty to the manslaughter charges, receiving concurrent ten-year terms and a $10,000 fine. Bramblett was arrested two days after the blast in Chatsworth, Georgia, after cases of M-80s were found at two locations in Murray County. In 1984 he was sentenced to ten years over a similar explosion at Rowesville, South Carolina, on May 24, 1983, which killed two people. A two-year federal investigation followed. In August 1985, twenty people from nine states were indicted; in June 1986 a federal jury convicted Bramblett, Miller and two others of conspiracy, and the remaining sixteen pleaded guilty. With Webb, twenty-one people were convicted.

## Rafts Where the Barn Stood

The disaster drew national attention, and federal authorities called the blast the largest and deadliest explosion at an illegal fireworks plant in U.S. history, and officials called the factory the largest illegal fireworks factory ever discovered. It still appears in regional histories: Dewaine A. Speaks devoted a chapter to it in his 2019 book Historic Disasters of East Tennessee. The farm itself has changed completely. Deed records from 1990 show the land belonging to Big Frog Expeditions, a rafting company. Nothing in the quiet valley south of Benton announces that eleven people, many of them related to one another, died here at work on a May morning in 1983.
