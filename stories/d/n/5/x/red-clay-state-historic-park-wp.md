# Red Clay State Historic Park

Beside a sacred blue spring on the Tennessee–Georgia line, the Cherokee Nation held its last eastern councils and pleaded in vain to stay in its homeland before the forced removal known as the Trail of Tears.

**Location:** 34.9931, -84.9472

**Categories:** Trail of Tears, Cherokee Nation (1794–1907), 19th-century Cherokee history, State parks of Tennessee, Native American museums in Tennessee, National Register of Historic Places in Tennessee

## Introduction

From 1832 until the removal, an estimated four to five thousand Cherokees at a time gathered here in southern Tennessee for eleven general councils of their Nation, held beside a spring they considered sacred, and again and again they gave the same answer to demands that they surrender their homeland: no. Red Clay was the last capital of the Cherokee Nation in the eastern United States, the place where a people argued, petitioned and organized to remain on their ancestral land. Their pleas failed. Today the 263-acre Red Clay State Historic Park keeps the council grounds and the memory of what followed.

## A Capital Pushed to the Edge

The Cherokee homeland once spread across parts of present-day western North Carolina, western South Carolina, East Tennessee, northern Georgia and northern Alabama. In the late 18th and early 19th centuries, the Cherokee organized a national government modeled on the United States Constitution, and European Americans counted them among the "Five Civilized Tribes," as the language of the era put it. None of that protected them. After the Hiwassee Purchase of 1819, White settlers began moving rapidly into the area in anticipation of a forced removal. Between 1827 and 1831, Georgia passed laws forbidding the Cherokee to hold public meetings and nullifying Cherokee law within the state, and in 1830 Congress passed the Indian Removal Act. The Cherokee left their capital at New Echota, met briefly in Alabama in 1831, and in 1832 moved their seat of government to Red Clay, chosen for its central location and its abundant spring. By 1836 the site held 91 log buildings.

## Eleven Councils and a Refusal

Under Principal Chief John Ross, eleven general councils met at Red Clay, each drawing an estimated 4,000 to 5,000 Cherokees. From here the Nation sent delegations to Washington to argue its case before Congress and the President, and here it repeatedly rejected agreements to give up its lands east of the Mississippi and move west. Many Cherokees considered the Treaty of New Echota an act of treason: the council at Red Clay had rejected the proposed terms almost unanimously in 1835 and rejected the treaty again in early 1836. The refusal did not stop the government. Enforcement of the Indian Removal Act drove most of the Cherokee people from their homes on a forced migration to present-day Oklahoma, the Cherokee removal remembered as the Trail of Tears.

## From Farmland Back to Council Ground

After the removal, the council grounds became private land used mostly for farming. The state sold a large tract, including the future park, to Frank Kincannon and John D. Traynor in 1841, and a village called Red Clay grew up to the south, where Cohutta, Georgia, stands today. Only in the late 1950s did local historians begin working to preserve the site. One of them, James F. Corn, bought 150 acres on June 15, 1964, and the Cherokee-Red Clay Association formed six months later. The grounds joined the National Register of Historic Places in 1972. Excavations from 1973 to 1975 turned up a small number of artifacts but never located the council house. On May 8, 1976, members of the Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians joined state and local leaders to dedicate the park, and it opened to the public on September 28, 1979.

## A Flame That Is Never Allowed to Go Out

On April 6, 1984, the Eternal Flame of the Cherokee Nation was placed at Red Clay, at an event attended by both the Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians and the Cherokee Nation of Oklahoma. Kept permanently lit, it memorializes the Cherokees who suffered and died during the removal. In April 2009 a joint council convened here again to mark that gathering's 25th anniversary. Then, on August 28, 2015, all three federally recognized Cherokee tribes, the Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians, the Cherokee Nation and the United Keetoowah Band of Cherokee Indians, reconvened at Red Clay for the first time since the removal. The PBS series We Shall Remain filmed part of its "Trail of Tears" episode in the park in 2008, and in 2018 a sign was unveiled designating the park part of the Trail of Tears National Historic Trail.

## Blue Water on Red Ground

At the heart of it all is the Blue Hole Spring, also called the Council Spring. It rises from a bowl-like depression, takes its name from its deep blue color and flows at about 414,720 gallons a day into Coahulla Creek, a tributary of the Conasauga River. The land climbs from just over 800 feet at the council grounds to more than 1,050 feet atop a ridge, and the 1.7-mile Council of Trees Trail ascends the ridge in the park's eastern half to a stone overlook tower. The park shelters two endangered species, the swamp lousewort and the Conasauga blue burrower crayfish. In the James F. Corn Interpretive Center, stained glass windows depict the forced removal, and in the eastern part of the park lies an unmarked grave reportedly belonging to Sleeping Rabbit, a prominent Cherokee who fought in the War of 1812.

## Flight Context

Red Clay State Historic Park lies at approximately 34.993°N, 84.947°W, in southern Bradley County, Tennessee, right on the Tennessee–Georgia state line, with Cohutta, Georgia, forming its southern boundary. It sits in the Ridge and Valley province of the Appalachians; from 3,000–4,500 feet MSL, look for the Norfolk Southern mainline along the park's eastern edge and the long parallel ridges of the Ridge and Valley country. Collegedale Municipal Airport (KFGU) is about 5 statute miles away, Chattanooga Metropolitan Airport–Lovell Field (KCHA) about 15 statute miles, Cleveland Regional Jetport (KRZR) about 17 and Dalton Municipal Airport (KDNN) about 19. Expect morning valley fog, summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms.
