# Battle of Ringgold Gap

Two days after the Confederate rout at Missionary Ridge, Patrick Cleburne's single division held a narrow mountain pass at Ringgold, Georgia, for five hours so the defeated Army of Tennessee could escape south.

**Location:** 34.9112, -85.1015

**Categories:** Chattanooga campaign, Battles of the western theater of the American Civil War, Confederate victories of the American Civil War, Battles of the American Civil War in Georgia (U.S. state), Catoosa County, Georgia, Conflicts in 1863, 1863 in Georgia (U.S. state), November 1863

## Introduction

The Confederates on the ridges held their fire until the Union line was close enough that every shot would tell. It was the morning of November 27, 1863, in the narrow pass called Ringgold Gap, just outside Ringgold, Georgia, and Major General Patrick R. Cleburne had been given a job he doubted he could do. Two days earlier, the Army of Tennessee had been routed at Missionary Ridge. Its artillery and wagon trains were now moving south toward Dalton, and Cleburne's lone division was all that stood between them and the pursuing Union army. For five hours, it would be enough.

## A Rout and a Rear Guard

The disaster at Missionary Ridge on November 25 sent General Braxton Bragg's army retreating into northwest Georgia. To buy time for his guns and wagons to get through the gap, Bragg ordered Cleburne to hold it with about 4,200 men. Cleburne doubted a single division could defend the pass adequately, and Bragg refused to send more. Cleburne's men reached Ringgold around 10 p.m., crossed East Chickamauga Creek and camped in the gap. On the Union side, Ulysses S. Grant had ordered a pursuit on the morning of November 26, but confusion over the orders delayed the start. General Joseph Hooker, leading divisions drawn from four corps, found the bridges over Chickamauga Creek already burned. Cleburne arranged his defenses with care. Brigades under Mark Lowrey and Lucius Polk took position on Taylor's Ridge, Hiram Granbury's Texans on White Oak Mountain, and Texas cavalry under Major William Taylor on the flank, while two cannons under Richard Goldthwaite waited, disguised, at the mouth of the gap.

## Hold Your Fire

Around 8 a.m., Brigadier General Peter Osterhaus's division went out to scout and ran into Cleburne's lookouts, who raced back to warn him. When Hooker reached a lookout point near the depot, he saw only a thin line of infantry, and he decided to send his men into the gap without waiting for his artillery. Brigadier General Charles Woods's brigade went in first and was quickly driven back by Granbury's Texans. The Confederates waited until the Union line was close, within about 150 yards in some accounts and as near as fifty in others, before opening fire, and on Cleburne's order Goldthwaite's hidden twelve-pound Napoleons fired into the attackers. Woods tried again, sending the 13th Illinois to shelter in the buildings of Jobes Farm, but fire from Taylor's Ridge and the guns in the gap stopped his men once more. Osterhaus sent the 76th Ohio and 4th Iowa against Taylor's Ridge. The first Confederate volley disorganized them, and they could not advance for the rest of the battle.

## Five Hours on the Ridges

John Geary's division of the XII Corps arrived around 10:40 a.m. and pressed the attack again. One brigade under David Ireland struck the Confederate right while another regiment pushed into the gap, and both were halted by heavy fire from Taylor's Ridge and White Oak Mountain. On White Oak Mountain, the brigade of William Creighton and Orrin Crane was driven back by Polk's and Lowrey's regiments, and Creighton was killed trying to rally his men. Around noon a message from Bragg reached Cleburne: the army had made it safely through, and he could withdraw. Cleburne left skirmishers along his front to hide the move, pulled out of the gap around 2 p.m., and burned the bridge on its eastern side behind him. When Grant arrived near the gap and saw how scattered his army was, he decided to return to Chattanooga. No further Union pursuit was organized.

## Scars on the Depot

Cleburne's official report listed 20 men killed, 190 wounded and 11 missing, though other tallies run higher. Union losses are given as roughly 430 to 510 killed and wounded. Hooker was severely criticized for his handling of the battle, by Assistant Secretary of War Dana and by several of his own men, but Grant chose to keep him on, at least for the time being. Today a small roadside park about half a mile east of Ringgold commemorates the battle, with markers and a statue of Cleburne, dedicated in 2009, by sculptor Ron Tunison. A monument to Colonel David Ireland's New York brigade, which suffered heavy casualties here, stands at the end of Depot Street. The nearby Western and Atlantic depot still shows scars from the artillery fire it took that day. The Ringgold Gap Battlefield was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 2011.

## Flight Context

Ringgold Gap lies at 34.9112°N, 85.1015°W beside the town of Ringgold in Catoosa County, northwest Georgia, where a narrow pass cuts between Taylor's Ridge and White Oak Mountain. From 3,000 to 4,000 feet MSL the break in the ridgeline beside the town is the landmark to look for. Chattanooga Metropolitan Airport (KCHA) is about 9 nm northwest, and Collegedale Municipal Airport (KFGU) about 9 nm north-northeast; Dalton Municipal Airport (KDNN) lies about 16 nm southeast. Chattanooga, where the retreat began, lies to the northwest; clear mornings give the best visibility.
