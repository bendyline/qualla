# Chattanooga Choo Choo

Glenn Miller's 1941 swing hit put Chattanooga on the world's musical map, earned the first gold disc RCA ever presented, took liberties with the real train it celebrated, and decades later carried a West German rock star's plea across the Iron Curtain.

**Location:** 34.9614, -85.1533

**Categories:** Glenn Miller songs, 1941 songs, Grammy Hall of Fame Award recipients, Songs with lyrics by Mack Gordon, Train songs, Chattanooga, Tennessee

## Introduction

There was no Track 29. When the traveler in the song asks a shoeshine boy, "Pardon me, boy, is that the Chattanooga Choo Choo?" and hears "Yes, yes, Track 29!", the answer is pure invention: at the time, New York's Pennsylvania Station had only 21 tracks. That small fib hardly mattered. Written by Mack Gordon and composed by Harry Warren, the 1941 song became the first to be awarded a gold record, and it carried the name of a Tennessee river city far beyond the South.

## Artistic License on the Rails

Gordon and Warren allegedly wrote the song while riding the Southern Railway's Birmingham Special, one of three trains running from New York City through Chattanooga. The Tennessean continued on to Memphis and the Pelican to New Orleans, and the Southern ran all three in cooperation with the Norfolk and Western Railway and the Pennsylvania Railroad. The lyrics, though, wander from the timetable. "You leave the Pennsylvania station 'bout a quarter to four," goes one line, yet the Birmingham Special departed at 12:30 p.m. The famous promise of ham 'n' eggs in Carolina is another stretch, since none of the three trains passed through the Carolinas; they ran through western Virginia straight into East Tennessee. The mismatches suggest the writers took some artistic license, trading the timetable for rhythm.

## Trombones for a Whistle

Glenn Miller and His Orchestra recorded the song in Hollywood on May 7, 1941, for RCA's Bluebird label, and featured it in the film Sun Valley Serenade. Tex Beneke took the lead vocal, joined by Paula Kelly and the Modernaires, over an arrangement by Jerry Gray. The record opens with the band sounding like a train pulling out of the station, trumpets and trombones imitating the whistle, before the melody arrives and the story begins with a traveler and a shoeshine boy. "Can you afford to board the Chattanooga Choo Choo?" the passenger is asked. "I've got my fare, and just a trifle to spare." Decades later, in the early 1990s, a two-channel recording of part of the film soundtrack surfaced, allowing a true-stereo version of the movie performance to be reconstructed.

## Gold Without the Radio

The song's success came against the odds. Because of the ASCAP boycott, it could not be heard on network radio for much of 1941. It sold anyway. On February 10, 1942, it became the first record to be awarded a gold disc, a framed copy sprayed with gold paint that RCA Victor presented for sales of 1.2 million copies, years before the RIAA began formally certifying gold records in 1958. A transcription of that ceremony later appeared on RCA's "Legendary Performer" compilations in the 1970s. The single had a curious history of its own: its flip side, "I Know Why (And So Do You)," was originally the A-side. The composition was nominated for the 1941 Academy Award for Best Song, and in 1996 Miller's recording entered the Grammy Hall of Fame.

## Everyone Climbs Aboard

The song has carried a long line of passengers since. Cab Calloway covered it in 1941, and Carmen Miranda recorded it on July 25, 1942, and sang it in Springtime in the Rockies. Bill Haley & His Comets cut a version in 1954, and pianist Floyd Cramer followed in 1962. The Shadows put it on Dance with the Shadows, which reached number two on the UK album chart in 1964. Harpers Bizarre spent two weeks at No. 1 on the Easy Listening chart with it in 1968, and the disco group Tuxedo Junction took it into the American Top 40 in 1978. Ray Charles, John Denver, Harry Connick Jr. and even the Muppets are among the many others who recorded it. In Young Frankenstein, a 1974 exchange about directions to the Transylvania Station closely follows the song's lyrics.

## A Special Train to Pankow

The tune found its most surprising second life in Germany. In 1947 Bully Buhlan used it for "Zug nach Kötzschenbroda," a humorous account of a train ride out of postwar Berlin, with coal shortages, passengers riding on buffers, steps and roofs, and a night stop for delousing. In 1983 West German rocker Udo Lindenberg released "Sonderzug nach Pankow," which asked East German leader Erich Honecker directly for permission to play a concert in East Berlin's Palace of the Republic. Lindenberg did get an invitation to the Rock for Peace festival on October 25, 1983, on condition that he not perform the song, and in 1987 he and Honecker exchanged gifts: a leather jacket and a metal shawm. Back in Chattanooga, the name has stuck around. The University of Tennessee at Chattanooga once had a mascot, a mockingbird named Scrappy, dressed as a railroad engineer, and pilots approaching the city can tune a radio navigation aid called the Choo Choo DME.

## Flight Context

The story's coordinates, 34.9614°N, 85.1533°W, are those of the Choo Choo DME, a radio navigation aid southeast of Chattanooga, rather than a spot in the city itself; Chattanooga's Terminal Station, the old depot that is now the Chattanooga Choo Choo complex (its hotel was renamed The Hotel Chalet in 2023) and displays a steam locomotive and historic railcars, lies in the city to the west-northwest. From 3,000 to 4,000 feet MSL, the Tennessee River's bends through the city and the high profile of Lookout Mountain on its southwest side make Chattanooga easy to pick out. Chattanooga Metropolitan Airport-Lovell Field (KCHA) is about 5 nm north-northwest of the navaid, and Collegedale Municipal Airport (KFGU) is about 8 nm to the northeast. The Tennessee Valley often holds morning haze and fog, especially in summer and fall.
