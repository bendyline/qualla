# Fort Oglethorpe (prisoner-of-war camp)

From 1917 to 1920, a barbed-wire camp at Fort Oglethorpe in north Georgia held as many as roughly 4,000 German sailors, famous conductors, businessmen and suspected radicals, many of them detained on suspicion alone and left waiting long after the war had ended.

**Location:** 34.9497, -85.2530

**Categories:** World War I internment camps, Internment of German Americans, Prisoner-of-war camps in the United States, World War I sites in the United States, Military installations in Georgia (U.S. state)

## Introduction

"In the immediate future." For the men still held at Fort Oglethorpe after World War I ended, those four words became a torment. They wrote to senators, congressmen, judges and lawyers, and hundreds of times to the Department of Justice, asking to be released. The department invariably replied that it regretted exceedingly not being able to free them in the immediate future. "How we came to loathe that phrase," one internee later wrote. Between 1917 and 1920, this camp in Catoosa County, Georgia, held as many as roughly 4,000 people (accounts of the exact count differ), and for many of them the hardest part was not knowing when it would end.

## A Mile of Barbed Wire

The War Prison Camp at Fort Oglethorpe covered a huge, somewhat hilly plot of land roughly a mile square, surrounded by two barbed-wire fences about ten feet high. Beyond the wire stood tripod watchtowers, each fitted with a searchlight, a telephone and a machine gun. Inside, the camp split in two. Camp A, nicknamed the "millionaire's camp," housed wealthy prisoners in private rooms; they paid for their own food and kept cooks and servants recruited from the stewards and sailors of the German maritime fleet. They could also avoid required labor or hire other internees to do it. Camp B held the great majority of prisoners in some thirty barracks, dominated by an immense mess hall. Men filled their days with chess, pinochle, soccer, handball, reading, carpentry and walks, and with letters home that often smuggled in comments about camp conditions.

## Sailors, Suspects and Radicals

The population was a strange mix. Military prisoners included crews from German raiders such as the SS Prinz Eitel Friedrich, along with Imperial German Navy sailors and merchant seamen who had been in the United States when the war began. Civilian internees included businessmen denounced by their American commercial rivals, and people of German, Czech, Polish and other nationalities charged with offenses under the Espionage Act of 1917. Some were openly supportive of, or suspected of supporting, the German or Austro-Hungarian war effort; others were held merely on suspicion of making pro-Central Powers statements. A final group, nicknamed "trouble-makers," included far-left radicals and members of the Industrial Workers of the World. These groups often clashed over questions of loyalty, and in time the camp was divided, with the intellectual and cultural elite kept at Fort Oglethorpe and others sent to different prisons.

## The Conductor and the Anthem

Among the best-known internees was Dr. Karl Muck, conductor of the Boston Symphony Orchestra. A newspaper editor, John R. Rathom, falsely accused him of refusing a request to play "The Star-Spangled Banner" at an October 1917 concert. Muck had not known of the request, and he went on to end his concerts with the national anthem, but Theodore Roosevelt and many other Americans believed the charge and were furious. Muck was arrested and held at Fort Oglethorpe until he agreed to be deported in the summer of 1919. Dr. Ernst Kunwald, conductor of the Cincinnati Symphony Orchestra, was arrested as an "enemy alien" because he was a citizen of Austria-Hungary; after a year in the camp, he won his freedom only by agreeing to deportation to the new Austrian Republic. Professor Richard Benedict Goldschmidt, detained for his German citizenship, was still held after the Armistice (accounts differ on his exact release date), and Count Albrecht von Montgelas was also held here.

## Signatures, Rations and Silence

Neutral diplomats spoke for the prisoners: Switzerland's embassy for German interests, Sweden's for those of Austria-Hungary. Some internees performed hard labor on the roads and in the quarry and were ordered to sign papers saying they did so of their own free will. Many refused and were locked in a separate enclosure. Protests to the Swiss consul went nowhere, but Sweden's Count Rosen reversed the decision, and the men were returned to the main camp and put back on full rations. By one prisoner's account, treatment was otherwise generally fair, but heavy censorship of mail and, after the Armistice of November 11, 1918, "the unbearable uncertainty as to the duration of our detention" remained two major irritants. One account claimed, unofficially, that more or less 50 people died in the wake of influenza; the total number of dead is not known from the record. Most escapees were recaptured, although one man known as Henckel finally got away after several failed attempts.

## Prisoner No. 3598

Perhaps 400 of the remaining prisoners hoped to stay in the United States, and it was they who waged the letter-writing campaign that brought so much disappointment. Another internee, Erich Posselt, who described his captivity in a 1927 magazine article titled "Prisoner of War No. 3598," was questioned by a Justice Department representative who accused him of having traveled on British ships, including HMS Hampshire, on which Lord Kitchener died, and of thereby aiding the U-boat campaign. Posselt called the charges idiotic. He was finally released on parole on January 12, 1920. The Army post itself outlived the camp by decades. Its land was declared surplus in 1947, and the buildings that once framed a prison became the foundation of the present-day town of Fort Oglethorpe.

## Flight Context

The former camp site lies at 34.9497°N, 85.2530°W in the town of Fort Oglethorpe, Catoosa County, in northwest Georgia just south of Chattanooga. From 2,500 to 3,500 feet, look for the open fields and woods of Chickamauga and Chattanooga National Military Park, which fill the southern two-thirds of the town, with Lookout Mountain rising to the west. Chattanooga Metropolitan Airport-Lovell Field (KCHA) is about 6 nm to the north-northeast, so expect busy airspace and contact Chattanooga approach; Collegedale Municipal Airport (KFGU) is about 13 nm to the east-northeast. Morning haze and fog are common in the valleys around Chattanooga.
