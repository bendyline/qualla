# Manitou Cave

In the side of Lookout Mountain near Fort Payne, Manitou Cave holds writing in the Cherokee syllabary left by Sequoyah's son, the scars of Confederate saltpeter mining, nearly a century of tourism, and a snail found nowhere else on Earth.

**Location:** 34.4289, -85.7239

**Categories:** Caves of Alabama, Landforms of DeKalb County, Alabama, Tourist attractions in DeKalb County, Alabama, Properties on the Alabama Register of Landmarks and Heritage

## Introduction

On the walls of a cave in the side of Lookout Mountain, someone wrote in a script that had not existed a generation earlier. The writer was Sequoyah's son, and the characters were those of the Cherokee syllabary his father had created while living nearby. Using that new writing system, he recorded ceremonial events and other culturally significant information and history on the stone of Manitou Cave, near Fort Payne, Alabama. The inscriptions make the cave a record of Cherokee life written in the Cherokee language, in the place where the Cherokee lived.

## Willstown and the Syllabary

In the early 1800s, the Fort Payne area was a Cherokee settlement called Willstown. Sequoyah moved there after losing his first Alabama home in 1819, and it was during his time in Willstown that he worked on the syllabary. By 1821 he had created a set of 86 symbols, later streamlined to 85, each standing for a syllable of the Cherokee language. The Cherokee Nation officially adopted the writing system by 1825 (some accounts date its acceptance to 1821), and by 1830 as many as 90 percent of Cherokees were literate in their own language. The writing on the walls of Manitou Cave belongs to that remarkable moment, when Cherokee people were putting their own language into writing and using it to record their lives.

## Removal, Saltpeter and Shelter

That moment did not last. In the 1830s the Cherokee were forcibly removed from this area along the Trail of Tears, driven from their homes, and the cave was left empty for some time. Its next users came for what lay in the ground. During the Civil War, Manitou Cave was a source of saltpeter for the Confederate Army, mined by laborers to supply the essential ingredient of black powder. Later, during the Cold War, the cave was designated a fallout shelter, a refuge against a very different kind of war.

## Nearly a Century of Visitors

In 1888 the Fort Payne Coal and Iron Company opened Manitou Cave to the public, and it became a tourist destination. Management later passed to the family of Walter B. Raymond Sr., who ran it through the mid-1970s. On August 6, 1976, the cave was placed on the Alabama Register of Landmarks and Heritage, and in 1979 it closed as a tourist attraction. Its next chapter would not begin for more than three decades.

## A Snail Found Nowhere Else

That chapter began in 2015, when the cave was purchased and a nonprofit was founded to care for it. With help from the Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians, the organization installed conservation features, including an eco-friendly gate. During these efforts, the Manitou Cave snail was confirmed to still live in the cave, which is the only place in the world it is known to exist. Today Manitou Cave of AL, Inc. stewards the site with a mission "to respect and protect this historic, sacred site through conservation and education so that the cave, land, and water are preserved for visitors and wildlife as a place of peace." To protect both the fragile ecosystem and the cave as a living record of history, access is tightly restricted: the stewards currently describe the cave as temporarily closed, with no tours being offered at this time. In earlier years, access was limited to community tours a couple of times a year and to educational research.

## Flight Context

Manitou Cave is at 34.4289°N, 85.7239°W in the side of Lookout Mountain near Fort Payne, Alabama. The cave entrance is not visible from the air; instead, use the long ridge of Lookout Mountain and the town of Fort Payne as your references from 2,500–3,500 feet AGL. Isbell Field at Fort Payne (FAA 4A9) is only about 2.7 nm north. Little River Canyon National Preserve lies about 6 nm to the east on top of the mountain. Richard B. Russell Regional Airport (KRMG) at Rome, Georgia, is about 28 nm east, and Chattanooga's Lovell Field (KCHA) about 45 nm northeast. Expect mechanical turbulence along the ridge on windy days and summer thunderstorms over the high ground.
