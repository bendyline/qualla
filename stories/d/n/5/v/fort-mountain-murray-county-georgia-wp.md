# Fort Mountain (Murray County, Georgia)

Near the crest of a north Georgia mountain, a line of mortarless stone piles has drawn theories about Spanish conquistadors and moon-eyed people, though most archaeologists credit Native builders, often placing them in the Middle Woodland period.

**Location:** 34.7831, -84.7092

**Categories:** Mountains of Georgia (U.S. state), National Register of Historic Places in Georgia (U.S. state), Landforms of Murray County, Georgia, Chattahoochee-Oconee National Forest, National Register of Historic Places in Murray County, Georgia

## Introduction

Nobody laid the wall on Fort Mountain with mortar, and nobody can say for certain who laid it at all. Near the crest of this north Georgia mountain, just east of Chatsworth, a line of loose stone piles wanders along the contour of the slope for hundreds of feet. The ruin gave the mountain its name, and it has gathered explanations the way the forest gathers leaves: a Spanish conquistador, a vanished people with pale eyes, the slow grinding of geology, or Native builders of the Middle Woodland period, well over a thousand years ago.

## A Line of Stones on the Contour

The ruin sits inside Fort Mountain State Park. The mountain belongs to the Cohutta Mountains, a small range at the southern end of the Appalachians, and lies within the Chattahoochee National Forest. What survives is not a wall in the neat, mortared sense but a series of stone piles laid in a long, uneven line that follows the mountainside. Even its length is disputed. In an archaeological report, Philip E. Smith of the University of Georgia gave it as 928 feet, while the Georgia Department of Natural Resources puts it at 855 feet and a plaque on the mountain says 885. The state's description leans into the uncertainty, calling it "the mysterious 855-foot-long wall" and suggesting that early Native people built it as a defense against rival groups or for ancient ceremonies.

## De Soto's Fort That Wasn't

In the nineteenth century, some looked to a Spanish explorer. People speculated that Hernando de Soto had raised the stones around 1540 as a fort against the Creek people, and the idea gained an official-sounding voice in 1879, when George Little, Georgia's state geologist, described the state's mountains in The Cartersville Free Press. He called the ruins "fortifications erected by DeSoto and his followers as they journeyed to the Mississippi." The theory ran into a basic problem of time. As early as 1917, a historian pointed out that de Soto was in the area for less than two weeks, a brief stay that undercut the whole idea. The mountain kept its name, fort or no fort.

## Moon-Eyed Legends

Local storytelling has offered its own answers. Some local accounts credit the Cherokee with the construction. One persistent legend reaches further back, attributing the stone piles to a race of moon-eyed people said to have lived here before the Cherokee. Historians picked up such stories, and from there they drifted into park histories, tourism brochures and markers. Geology suggests a far less romantic possibility: stone formations can occur naturally, produced by the thrust that builds a ridge crest as a mountain forms. With loose rock, no mortar and no written record, it is easy to see why the question has never quite closed.

## The Woodland Builders

Published sources point to Native people far older than the Cherokee. In 1994 the Rome News Tribune ran an article by Stacy McCain reporting that anthropologists attribute the wall to people of the Middle Woodland era. Writing in 1997 about this wall and others like it, David Freedman observed that "most experts believe that such wall enclosures were built during the Middle Woodland period (100 BCE-500 CE) when agriculture and food storage methods were well established." That stability, he wrote, let people live a more settled existence and devote their energies to large-scale building projects "which perhaps would have taken generations to complete." Archaeologists have not settled the question, though: excavations on the mountain turned up no sign of a battle and nothing that identifies the builders. In 1977 the site was placed on the National Register of Historic Places for its archaeological potential, as a source of information about prehistoric eras.

## Tower, Lake and Switchbacks

The modern mountain has stonework of its own. A stone fire tower built by the Civilian Conservation Corps marks the summit, and the park opens it for self-guided visits on scheduled open-house days. A hiking path around the park leads to a scenic overlook of Cool Springs Valley. The park also offers a mountain lake, cabins, camping and public facilities. Getting there is part of the experience. Georgia State Route 52, a steep and curving scenic road, crosses the mountain east–west between Chatsworth and Ellijay, with views of the Cohutta Mountains and at least one overlook with parking. Southeast of Chatsworth, SR 282 also crosses the mountain, running together with SR 2 and US 76 from Ramhurst to East Ellijay. Both routes link Murray and Gilmer counties over a ridge that still keeps its secret.
