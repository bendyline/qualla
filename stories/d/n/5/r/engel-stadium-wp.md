# Engel Stadium

Built in 1930 by a showman known as "The Barnum of Baseball," Chattanooga's Engel Stadium saw, by the usual account, a teenage pitcher strike out Babe Ruth and Lou Gehrig, gave away a house during the Depression, stood in for Ebbets Field on film, and now faces demolition.

**Location:** 35.0454, -85.2863

**Categories:** Baseball venues in Tennessee, Minor league baseball venues, Negro league baseball venues, Sports venues in Chattanooga, Tennessee, Sports venues on the National Register of Historic Places in Tennessee

## Introduction

In 1931 the New York Yankees came to Chattanooga for an exhibition game, and the Lookouts sent a 17-year-old pitcher named Jackie Mitchell to the mound. By the usual account, she struck out both Lou Gehrig and Babe Ruth, though some writers question how much of the day was showmanship. It was exactly the kind of moment Joe Engel lived for, and it happened in the ballpark that carries his name. For nearly seven decades Engel Stadium, on East Third Street near the Fort Wood neighborhood, was home to the Chattanooga Lookouts, a 12,000-seat stage for some of the greatest players in the game and for some famously wild promotions.

## A Scout Buys a Ballclub

In 1929, Washington Senators owner Clark Griffith sent a young scout named Joe Engel south to find a home for the club's first minor league affiliate. Engel went to Atlanta first, cash in hand, to buy the Atlanta Crackers, but the Southern Association refused to approve the purchase, so he headed north to Chattanooga. There he bought the Lookouts from Sammy Strang and replaced their old ballpark, Andrews Field, with a state-of-the-art stadium that cost somewhere between $150,000 and $180,000, depending on the account. Andrews Field had been the team's home since 1910, on the same block, with home plate at the northwest corner and stands built against the upslope to Third Street. Engel moved home plate and the stands to the southwest corner. The new park hosted its first exhibition on March 23, 1930, and the regular season opened on April 15.

## Elephants, a House and a Hill

Engel's stunts earned him the nickname "The Barnum of Baseball." In 1936, in the depths of the Great Depression, a record crowd of nearly 25,000 squeezed into a park with 12,000 seats for a chance to win a house. On Opening Day in 1938 he staged a "Wild Elephant Hunt," a stunt that drew more than 14,000 fans. The park itself kept changing. In the 1935-36 off-season a large scoreboard went up in left-center and most of the old grandstand came down, opening a vast outfield whose deepest point, in center field, was posted at 471 feet. Harmon Killebrew is said to be the only man to hit a ball over it. The exposed slope along the left-field wall became a terrace spelling out "LOOKOUTS" in huge letters. Lights arrived that winter too, and at the first night game, on April 21, 1936, Knoxville spoiled the party, 3-2.

## Legends on the Field, Fans Kept Apart

The list of players who took this field reads like a who's who of the game: Babe Ruth, Lou Gehrig, Hank Aaron, Satchel Paige, Willie Mays, Harmon Killebrew, Ferguson Jenkins, Kiki Cuyler and Rogers Hornsby. The stadium also hosted Negro league baseball. Yet the ballpark reflected the segregated South it stood in. When the new stadium opened, the old grandstand was kept and remodeled into bleachers, used for knothole gangs of young fans and for segregated seating, a daily reminder of who could sit where in Jim Crow Chattanooga. The park had its own close calls, too. On the night of May 13, 1942, fire broke out near the left-field corner, destroying the remaining wooden bleachers and part of the fence and damaging some of the lights. Repairs were made, and the season went on.

## Peeling Paint and Sparkle Days

Hard years followed when the Lookouts had no major league affiliate from 1966 to 1975. Amateur games filled the field day and night all summer, but the stadium was not properly maintained. In 1972 Chattanooga News Free Press columnist Allan Morris wrote that the paint was peeling, the floor was filthy, the roof was falling down, and "it looks like a tornado hit the place." When Woody Reid bought the club in 1976 and landed an affiliation with the Oakland Athletics, he held "Sparkle Days," when fans volunteered to fix up the old park. A $2 million renovation in the winter of 1988 brought a new exterior, a two-story front office, a new press box and a restaurant overlooking the field, but also a new field that did not drain properly, prompting a half-million-dollar lawsuit by the Lookouts against the city and county. In 1999 the team moved to AT&T Field, and Tennessee Temple University played its home games at Engel.

## Ebbets Field in Chattanooga

The property passed to UT Chattanooga in the 2000s, and in April 2009 the Engel Foundation formed to restore and promote the old park, which joined the National Register of Historic Places in late 2009. A tornado damaged the stadium in April 2011. The next year, Hollywood arrived: Engel Stadium served as the set for 42, the film about Jackie Robinson, with much of the movie's baseball action shot here and the ballpark doubling for Brooklyn's Ebbets Field. It was a striking role for a park that had once seated its fans by race. Afterward, in July 2012, crews began removing the film's alterations to restore the park's historical look. In October 2025, UTC announced that the stadium was not salvageable and would be torn down to make way for a women's athletic complex; demolition was expected in 2026, UTC said no firm timeline had been set, and early concept plans included a possible museum space.

## Flight Context

Engel Stadium sits at 35.0454°N, 85.2863°W in Chattanooga, Tennessee, east of downtown beside Erlanger Hospital, the Fort Wood neighborhood and Norfolk Southern's DeButts rail yard. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet, the long rail yard is the easiest reference; the ballpark lies on its western side, between Third Street and Engel Drive. Chattanooga Metropolitan Airport-Lovell Field (KCHA) is only about 4 nm to the east, so expect busy controlled airspace and contact Chattanooga approach. Lookout Mountain rises to the southwest and Missionary Ridge to the east. Valley haze and morning fog are common, especially in late summer and fall.
