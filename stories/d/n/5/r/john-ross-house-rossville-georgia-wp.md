# John Ross House (Rossville, Georgia)

A two-story log house in Rossville, Georgia, was a family home of Cherokee principal chief John Ross, who by many accounts lived there in the 1830s after the state took his home, during the years he fought and failed to stop the removal of his people.

**Location:** 34.9811, -85.2848

## Introduction

Legend says a Scots immigrant named John McDonald built this log house near Missionary Ridge in 1797. The tree rings tell a different story. Dendrochronology dating suggests the house was built in 1816 or 1817, most likely by McDonald's grandson, John Ross. Either way, the modest two-story house in Rossville, Georgia, is bound up with the story of Cherokee removal. Accounts of when Ross lived here differ: the National Park Service says he lived in the cabin between 1808 and 1827, while other accounts place him here from 1830 to 1838, the years in which the long-serving leader of the Cherokee Nation fought to keep his people on their land.

## Cherokee Land, Cherokee Family

When the house went up, the entire area was Cherokee land. John McDonald had come to the region from Scotland and married a Cherokee woman. McDonald's grandson, John Ross, born in 1790, inherited the land where the house was built in 1809, from his uncle, William Shorey. Ross rose to lead his nation. He was a leading figure in the adoption of the Cherokee Constitution of 1827, and the following year he became chief of the Cherokee. He led the faction within the nation that opposed the taking of Cherokee lands by the state and federal governments, and that opposed Cherokee Removal.

## A Chief Without His Home

In 1830 the state of Georgia took over Cherokee lands, and Ross himself was dispossessed. His fine home near the Coosa River was taken, and by some accounts he moved into this log house, where he lived until 1838, though he also had a home across the state line in Tennessee, where Georgia's guard arrested him at Flint Springs in November 1835. He represented his nation in its legal efforts to keep its lands. Those efforts failed. Ross led the Cherokee for many years and opposed removal throughout, but he was unable to stop it. The house therefore marks a bitter irony: a leader stripped of his own home by the state, living in a family house on land that his people, too, were about to lose.

## Two Pens and a Breezeway

The house itself is plain and sturdy. It is a two-story log structure built on a dogtrot plan, with two log pens flanking an open first-floor breezeway, all beneath a low-pitched gable roof of wood shingles. The left pen, 16 feet square, holds a single room, as does the larger right pen, which measures 16 by 23 feet. A single-story log-rail porch runs across the front, and the logs are now chinked with modern cement. The house is one of the oldest in the area, and it survived the Civil War, when both sides used it as a hospital.

## Saving the Chief's House

By the 1950s the house had fallen into disrepair and was being hemmed in by commercial development. The Chief John Ross House Association formed to preserve it, and in the 1960s the house was moved a short distance, from a more central downtown location to its present parklike setting on a lane joining Andrew Street and East Lake Avenue. In 1973 it was declared a National Historic Landmark. The city around it carries the chief's name as well: Rossville, chartered in 1905, is named for John Ross, a lasting marker of the man the state once pushed from his home.
