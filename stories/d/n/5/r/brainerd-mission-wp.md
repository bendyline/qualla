# Brainerd Mission

From 1817 until Cherokee removal forced it to close in 1838, the Brainerd Mission on South Chickamauga Creek schooled more than 300 Cherokee children; today only its small cemetery remains in modern Chattanooga.

**Location:** 35.0101, -85.2130

**Categories:** 1817 establishments in Tennessee, Christian missions in North America, Cemeteries on the National Register of Historic Places in Tennessee, Protected areas of Hamilton County, Tennessee, Trail of Tears, National Register of Historic Places in Chattanooga, Tennessee, 1838 disestablishments in the United States, Cemeteries established in the 1810s

## Introduction

Only the graves are left. The schoolhouses, the mill, the farm fields and the dormitories where Cherokee boys and girls once lived and studied are gone, and the ground where the Brainerd Mission stood has been redeveloped, and the National Park Service says it is now mostly covered by the Brainerd Village shopping center in Chattanooga, Tennessee. What survives is a small cemetery of about 60 graves, one of the oldest in Hamilton County and listed on the National Register of Historic Places. For 21 years, from 1817 to 1838, this was one of the places where the Cherokee Nation's encounter with American missionaries played out, and where hundreds of Cherokee children learned to read and write English.

## A Trader's Tract on Chickamauga Creek

Cyrus Kingsbury founded the mission in 1817 for the American Board of Commissioners for Foreign Missions. The site was a tract on South Chickamauga Creek that had belonged to John McDonald, a former Scots trader and the grandfather of John Ross, who would become principal chief of the Cherokee Nation. At first it was called Chickamauga Mission. A few months later it was renamed for David Brainerd, an early New England missionary to Native Americans. When the missionaries arrived, the property held only a dilapidated gristmill and a few other buildings, but with help from Cherokee living nearby they built quickly: schoolhouses and separate dwellings for boys and girls, a sawmill, a blacksmith shop, a wash house, a smokehouse, a corn house, stables, a residence for the missionaries and a cemetery. By the end of the first year, about 40 acres had been cleared and fenced as a farm.

## Lessons, Teachers and a President

The mission's purpose was to give Cherokee children a basic education while instilling Christian religious values. In practice that meant learning to read and write English and to read the Bible in English. The missionaries also taught skills along gendered lines: spinning, knitting and sewing for the girls, and animal husbandry, farming and mechanical work for the boys. Kingsbury himself did not stay long. Before the end of 1818 the Board withdrew him and sent him to Mississippi to serve the Choctaw Nation, where he remained for the rest of his active career. Other prominent missionaries served at Brainerd over the years, among them Samuel Worcester, Ainsworth Blunt and Cephas Washburn. On May 27 and 28, 1819, President James Monroe visited the mission, accompanied by General Edmund Pendleton Gaines. It was the first presidential visit to the Hamilton County area, and Monroe left behind a donation of $1,000 for the mission's work.

## Students, a Treaty and 1838

Several people tied to Brainerd became notable figures. Elias Boudinot, who was schooled at the Moravian mission at Spring Place and at the Foreign Mission School in Connecticut, and whose children were later enrolled at Brainerd, edited the Cherokee Phoenix, the first newspaper published by a Native American nation. David Brown, a Cherokee clergyman, translated the New Testament from English into Cherokee using the Sequoyah syllabary. John Ridge, the son of Major Ridge and a cousin of Boudinot, is said to have studied here too. Boudinot, John Ridge and Major Ridge all signed the 1835 Treaty of New Echota, which most Cherokee, including Principal Chief John Ross, opposed. Three years later, in August 1838, the last church service was held at Brainerd, and the mission closed because of the Cherokee removal to Indian Territory. The people it had been built to serve were being forced from their homeland. Over its 21 years, Brainerd had served more than 300 Cherokee students, male and female. By its own stated goals, it was modestly successful at teaching English, giving many students a basic proficiency, and less successful at religious conversion, since only a few Cherokee joined the church. The cemetery that remains holds mostly missionary workers. The Daughters of the American Revolution have been integral to preserving it, and they continue to own and care for this last piece of the mission.

## Flight Context

The Brainerd Mission Cemetery lies at 35.0101°N, 85.2130°W in Chattanooga, Tennessee, near South Chickamauga Creek. The site is just 1.6 nm south-southwest of Chattanooga Metropolitan Airport (KCHA), so it is best seen on arrival to or departure from KCHA rather than as a separate sightseeing overflight. Collegedale Municipal Airport (KFGU) lies about 10 nm east-northeast. The small cemetery itself is hard to spot from the air among the surrounding commercial development.
