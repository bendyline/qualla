# The Read House Hotel

On a downtown Chattanooga corner where Jefferson Davis once argued for secession and Union soldiers raised their flag, the Read House has welcomed guests for more than 150 years, along with, if the stories are true, one ghost who never checked out.

**Location:** 35.0461, -85.3108

**Categories:** Hotel buildings on the National Register of Historic Places in Tennessee, National Register of Historic Places in Chattanooga, Tennessee, Historic hotels, Civil War history, Reportedly haunted locations

## Introduction

In January 1861, Jefferson Davis, newly resigned from the United States Senate and on his way home to Mississippi, rose in the dining room of Chattanooga's Crutchfield House and argued for secession. William Crutchfield, brother of the hotel's owner and a future congressman, answered with a fiery speech of his own. He denounced Davis as a "renegade and a traitor" and vowed that Tennessee would not be "hood winked, bamboozled and dragged into your Southern, codfish, aristocratic, tory blooded, South Carolina mobocracy." Accounts say a brawl or a duel was narrowly avoided, and the scene was widely reported as the country slid toward war. The hotel on that site today is the Read House.

## A Hotel at the Heart of a Railroad Town

The first hotel here, the Crutchfield House, opened in 1847 by most accounts, though one Tennessee Civil War listing gives 1856. It was built by Thomas Crutchfield Sr., a future mayor of Chattanooga, and its spot directly across from the Union Depot tied its fortunes to the railroads that were the town's main source of business. The steady flow of travelers kept it prosperous. After Crutchfield died in 1850, his son, Thomas Crutchfield Jr., another future mayor, took over. Then the war came to the front door. In 1862 the hotel served as headquarters for the local Confederate garrison, and that winter General Samuel Jones, commander of the Department of East Tennessee, turned it into a military hospital. When Union forces occupied Chattanooga on September 9, 1863, the 92nd Illinois Infantry planted its regimental colors atop the hotel to announce who held the town, and the building later sheltered Union soldiers wounded at Chickamauga.

## The Reads Take Over

The Crutchfield House eventually burned, and the family chose not to rebuild. Into the gap came John T. Read, a Civil War surgeon, and his wife, Caroline, who had already lost a hotel of their own to fire back home in McMinnville, Tennessee. They moved to Chattanooga in 1871 and bought the old Crutchfield site, and the Read House dates its founding to 1872. In 1879 Read sold the hotel to his 19-year-old son, Samuel R. Read, who expanded it to 202 rooms by 1902. The main building standing today dates to 1926. Designed in the Georgian style by the Chicago firm Holabird & Roche and built by the George A. Fuller Construction Company of Washington, D.C., it cost $2.7 million and is listed on the National Register of Historic Places. A 100-room rear wing followed in 1962, originally as a motel.

## Faded and Restored

Samuel R. Read died in 1942. By the 1980s the hotel had become a Best Western franchise, renamed the Best Western Choo-Choo Read House, and between 1980 and 2000 it passed through seven different owners as its luster slowly dimmed. An $11 million renovation in 2004 brought in Sheraton, and the Sheraton Read House reopened on November 18 of that year. The hotel dropped that affiliation in 2015 to become The Read House Historic Inn & Suites. Then, on August 30, 2016, Avocet Hospitality Group of Charleston, South Carolina, bought the property and spent roughly $27 million renovating it, adding dining outlets, redoing every guest room and moving the main entrance from Broad Street back to its original place on Martin Luther King Jr. Boulevard. It reopened in 2018 simply as The Read House, and in 2022 it celebrated its 150th anniversary. Over the decades its guests have reportedly included several U.S. presidents, from Ulysses S. Grant to Ronald Reagan.

## The Woman in Room 311

What the Read House is perhaps best known for, though, is Room 311. Many believe it is haunted by a woman named Annalisa Netherly, said to have been a sex worker in Chattanooga in the 1920s and 1930s who supposedly lived in the room for an extended time and died there. The details are hazy, and the legends disagree about how she died: some tell of a killing by a jealous lover or husband, others of heartbreak and suicide after a suitor turned to another woman. Whatever the truth, the stories remember her less as a person than as a ghost, and her real life is largely lost. Guests say her presence is hostile to men, especially smokers, while many others spend a quiet night. Hotel lore also holds that Al Capone once stayed in Room 311, and the custom iron bars said to have been added to its windows for him feature in the tale, which the hotel itself presents as folklore.

## Flight Context

The Read House stands at 35.0461°N, 85.3108°W in downtown Chattanooga, Tennessee, on Martin Luther King Jr. Boulevard at Broad Street. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet, the downtown grid south of the Tennessee River is the reference, with the river's bend to the north and Lookout Mountain rising to the southwest. Chattanooga Metropolitan Airport-Lovell Field (KCHA) is about 5 nm to the east, so downtown lies within busy controlled airspace; contact Chattanooga approach. Morning haze and river fog are common in the valley, especially in late summer and fall.
