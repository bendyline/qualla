# Moccasin Bend

Once dismissed by a park superintendent as having lost its historical significance, this Tennessee River peninsula in Chattanooga holds 12,000 years of human history and became a unit of the National Park Service as the Moccasin Bend National Archeological District.

**Location:** 35.0358, -85.3367

**Categories:** National Historic Landmarks in Tennessee, Archaeological sites on the National Register of Historic Places in Tennessee, Trail of Tears, Chickamauga and Chattanooga National Military Park, Geography of Chattanooga, Tennessee

## Introduction

When advocates pressed the National Park Service to protect the great loop of land across the Tennessee River from Lookout Mountain, the park superintendent of the day, John Cook, turned them away. Moccasin Bend, he argued, "has lost its historical significance and part of its natural beauty." History has not agreed with him. Within the peninsula, among its trails, golf course and hospital grounds, lie remnants of 12,000 years of continuous human habitation, and the Park Service now calls the Moccasin Bend Archeological District one of the "most unique units found in the entire National Park Service."

## A Family Home from 1335 B.C.

The oldest layers date to the Paleo-Indian period, from about 10500 to 8000 B.C. Much later, at the Vulcan Site, archeologists found a Late Archaic house dated to about 1335 B.C., considered one of the earliest family houses in the southeastern United States. During the Woodland period, from about 700 B.C. to A.D. 1000, bows and arrows became the main way to hunt and pottery spread widely among Native peoples; the Mallards Dozen Site preserves traces of Middle Woodland life, and on the southern tip of the Bend a complex of Late Woodland burial mounds still stands. In the Mississippian period, from A.D. 1000 to 1630, people lived here in large villages surrounded by corn fields. The primary site, Hampton Place, holds two Late Mississippian towns.

## Spanish Iron and the Long Removal

Spanish artifacts at Hampton Place suggest that those towns were perhaps visited by Spanish explorers, and Hernando de Soto's march through the area in 1540 marked the start of contact. Those early encounters were especially destructive for American Indian populations. French and British trading networks later brought firearms, reshaping the Native economy around hunting for hides, which European settlers prized. During this era, Cherokee people used Moccasin Bend only lightly. The Bend was also part of the Trail of Tears, the forced removal that drove American Indian nations from their southeastern homelands. For a place with twelve millennia of Native history, it is a painful chapter, and one the archeological district now helps keep in view.

## Ferrymen and Union Guns

The Civil War brought the Bend into the national story again. John Brown, who operated Brown's Ferry, the site of a Civil War battle, had his homestead here, and Brown's Ferry Federal Road still marks the peninsula, though few other traces of him remain. In 1863 the Union Army occupied a series of positions on the southern tip of Stringers Ridge, with Lookout Mountain, scene of its own Civil War battle, rising just across the river. The historic roads from that period are still usable today, and several of the sites can be seen from the ridge.

## A Hospital on the Mounds

For much of the twentieth century, few people recognized what the Bend held, and industrial development spread on and around important sites. In 1961 the Moccasin Bend Mental Health Institute opened after about three years of construction, most of its five buildings designed by Mario Bianculli, called "Chattanooga's First Modernist." Preliminary Park Service research suggests three of those buildings now appear unused. American Indian mounds lie on the hospital property, where security keeps public access minimal. The state has since dropped a plan to build a replacement hospital on the Bend: a September 2024 archaeological review found that 73 percent of its parcel is a multi-component archaeological site, and it is looking for a site elsewhere. A golf course arrived in the 1960s, taking in parts of the Vulcan Site, and before the Bend was protected, Hampton Place was so heavily looted and vandalized that little visible evidence of it remains.

## Decades of Persistence

The same year the hospital opened, the Moccasin Bend Association revived efforts to bring in the Park Service. The idea stalled for decades until the Friends of Moccasin Bend National Park formed in 1994, conducted an economic impact study concluding that a park would benefit Chattanooga, and built community support. In 2001 U.S. Representative Zach Wamp introduced HR 980; it passed in 2003, establishing the district as a unit of Chickamauga and Chattanooga National Military Park, and in 2004 state and local governments donated land to it. It is often described as the first and only National Archeological District in the Park Service. The result is a fragmented park, threaded around the hospital, golf course, radio towers, a firing range and a model airplane field. Acquiring more land has proved difficult, but an interpretive center has been planned on Hamm Road, and visitors can already walk the Blue Blazes Trail through the Vulcan and Mallards Dozen sites.

## Flight Context

Moccasin Bend lies at approximately 35.036°N, 85.337°W, in Chattanooga, Tennessee, where the Tennessee River swings in a dramatic loop directly beneath Lookout Mountain. From 3,000–4,500 feet MSL the peninsula is an unmistakable shape: a broad tongue of land wrapped by the river, with Lookout Mountain rising directly across the water. Chattanooga Metropolitan Airport–Lovell Field (KCHA) is about 6.5 nm to the east, and Marion County Airport–Brown Field (KAPT) at Jasper is about 12 nm to the west-northwest. Mind the terrain of Lookout Mountain and the surrounding ridges, and expect valley fog on cool mornings plus summer haze and thunderstorms.
