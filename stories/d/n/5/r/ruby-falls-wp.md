# Ruby Falls

While drilling into Lookout Mountain in 1928 to reopen a different cave, Leo Lambert found an unknown passage leading to an underground waterfall, which he named for his wife and turned into one of Chattanooga's best-known attractions.

**Location:** 35.0192, -85.3394

## Introduction

Leo Lambert was not looking for a waterfall. In 1928 the chemist and cave enthusiast was drilling into the side of Lookout Mountain in Chattanooga, Tennessee, trying to reach a cave whose natural entrance had been sealed years before. Partway down, his crew broke into a small passage only about 18 inches high and 4 feet wide. Lambert went in to explore, and at the far end he found something no one had ever seen: a waterfall pouring down a vertical shaft deep inside the mountain. Its height is given as 85 feet in a National Speleological Society guidebook and as 145 feet in popular tourism accounts. On his next trip into the cave he brought his wife, Ruby, and told her he would name the falls after her.

## Drilling for the Wrong Cave

The cave Lambert was after was Lookout Mountain Cave, whose natural entrance had been closed in 1905 during construction of a railway tunnel. In the 1920s Lambert formed a company to reopen it as a tourist attraction. Rather than use the old entrance, he planned to cut a new opening higher up the mountain and carry visitors down to the cave by elevator. His company bought land on the side of Lookout Mountain above the cave, and in 1928 began drilling through the limestone. The accidental discovery did not stop the work. After finding the new cave, the tunnelers kept going and reached Lookout Mountain Cave after approximately 200 feet of tunneling, at a point 1,120 feet below the mountain's summit.

## A Cave with No Door

The rock around the falls began forming about 300 to 360 million years ago, in the Carboniferous Period, when a shallow sea covered what is now eastern Tennessee and its sediments hardened into limestone. About 200 million years ago the region was uplifted, raising Lookout Mountain and nearby ridges. Slightly acidic groundwater then worked through cracks in the limestone, slowly dissolving the rock and widening narrow fractures into passages. Two of the caves that formed were Lookout Mountain Cave, which opened to the surface, and the higher Ruby Falls Cave, which did not. Because it had no known natural entrance, Ruby Falls Cave lacked the artifacts sometimes found in caves of the southeastern United States. It held other treasures instead: a variety of geological formations and curiosities not found in the cave below, and, at the end of its main passage, the waterfall.

## Lights, Elevator, Falls

Lambert opened both caves to the public. The Lookout Mountain Caverns opened on December 30, 1929, and the expanded Ruby Falls cave followed in June 1930. Electric lights made it one of the first commercial caves so equipped, and paved trails and an elevator carried visitors to the falls. The lower cave did not last as an attraction. Coal ash from nearby trains had coated its surfaces in thick black soot, and it was far less popular, so by 1935 it was blocked off. In 1954 a pathway was cut around the basin to give visitors a better view, which started a long-running tour quip about not drinking the water: it is said to be pure, but rich enough in magnesium from the mountain's rock to act as a natural laxative. Pumps reportedly circulate water to keep the falls flowing year-round. In 1975 a secondary exit to the base of the mountain was cut, in case the main elevator ever fails.

## See Ruby Falls

Getting people to come took marketing, and Ruby Falls had plenty. By the mid-20th century the slogan "See Ruby Falls" was painted on barn roofs throughout the South, familiar enough that Johnny Cash and Roy Orbison co-wrote a song called "See Ruby Fall" in the mid-to-late 1960s. Through the 1970s and 1980s, billboards reading "SEE RUBY FALLS" lined Interstate 75, starting hundreds of miles north and south of the falls. The attraction is often associated with Rock City, which sits atop the same mountain, and the falls can be visited only on a guided tour. Ruby Falls is listed on the National Register of Historic Places as part of a historic district, and it remains a staple of Chattanooga tourism, still welcoming visitors, with a new Junior Caver Tour added in 2026, nearly a century after a drill bit opened a passage into the dark.

## Flight Context

Ruby Falls is inside Lookout Mountain at about 35.0192°N, 85.3394°W in Chattanooga, Tennessee; the cave is invisible from the air, so look for the attraction on the side of the mountain. From 4,000 to 5,000 feet MSL, the mountain rising above the city makes the reference. The Lookout Mountain Incline Railway lies less than 1 nm to the south-southeast, and Rock City about 3 nm south on the mountaintop. Chattanooga Metropolitan Airport (KCHA) is about 6.7 nm east, and Marion County Airport-Brown Field (KAPT) about 12 nm west-northwest. Clear mornings usually give the best view.
