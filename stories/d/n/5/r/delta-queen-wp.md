# Delta Queen

Built from Scottish parts and assembled in California, the sternwheeler Delta Queen carried wartime wounded, crossed to the Mississippi, survived decades on congressional exemptions, and spent her last Tennessee years as a floating hotel on Chattanooga's North Shore.

**Location:** 35.0599, -85.3086

**Categories:** Paddle steamers of the United States, Steamboats of the Mississippi River, National Historic Landmarks in Tennessee, Ships built on the River Clyde, Passenger ships of the United States

## Introduction

The Delta Queen began life as a kit. Her hull, first two decks and steam engines were ordered in 1924 from the William Denny & Brothers shipyard at Dumbarton, Scotland, where the River Leven meets the Clyde. In 1926 the parts were shipped in pieces to Stockton, California, along with those of her sister, the Delta King, and the California Transportation Company assembled the two boats for service on the Sacramento River. More than eighty years later, this Scottish-Californian sternwheeler would become a Tennessee landmark, tied up on Chattanooga's North Shore, with a lounge named for the woman who saved her and, some say, a captain who never left.

## From the Delta to the War

Entering service in 1927, the Delta Queen and Delta King ran between San Francisco and Sacramento, with excursions to Stockton on the San Joaquin River. At the time, they were described as the most lavishly appointed and expensive sternwheel passenger boats ever commissioned. The Sacramento River delta gave the Queen her name. During World War II, the U.S. Navy requisitioned both boats for duty in San Francisco Bay. Painted battleship gray, the Delta Queen carried the wounded from ocean-going ships to hospitals around the bay. In 1946 Greene Line of Cincinnati bought her, and under pilot Frederick Way Jr. she was towed through the Panama Canal and up the Mississippi and Ohio rivers to Pittsburgh for refurbishment. In 1948 she began regular passenger service on the Ohio, Mississippi, Tennessee and Cumberland rivers, calling at Cincinnati, New Orleans, St. Paul, Chattanooga, Nashville and ports in between.

## Steam, Paddles and a Calliope

She is a substantial boat: 285 feet long and 58 feet wide, drawing 11.5 feet and weighing 1,650 tons, with room for 176 passengers. Cross-compound steam engines producing 2,000 indicated horsepower turn her stern-mounted paddlewheel, and the National Trust for Historic Preservation has called her "the last remaining authentic link to our country's 200-year tradition of passenger steamboat transportation." One of her most unusual features sits on the Texas deck just aft of the pilothouse: a steam calliope spanning roughly three octaves, used to play the boat in and out of her berth, and sometimes played by the master as a courtesy to other vessels. Three presidents, Herbert Hoover, Harry Truman and Jimmy Carter, sailed aboard. So did Mary Becker Greene, wife of Greene Line founder Gordon C. Greene and a riverboat captain in her own right, who died on April 22, 1949, reportedly aboard the boat. Guests and employees have since reported sounds and activity they attribute to her spirit, particularly around her former quarters.

## Saved by Act of Congress

In 1966 Congress passed a Safety at Sea law that threatened to put the Delta Queen out of business. Richard Simonton, who had bought a controlling interest in the struggling Greene Line in 1958, traveled to Washington with Bill Muster and Edwin "Jay" Quinby after consulting attorney William Kohler, and Quinby, as board chairman, asked the Senate for an exemption. It had to be renegotiated every two to four years. When Congressman Edward A. Garmatz, chairman of the House Committee on Merchant Marine and Fisheries, tried to block the 1970 exemption, public relations officer Betty Blake rose to become the boat's savior, and the boat's Betty Blake Lounge is named in her honor. Through the efforts of Blake and Muster, the Delta Queen joined the National Register of Historic Places in 1970 and became a National Historic Landmark in 1989. In all, Congress granted the exemption nine times over four decades.

## Golden Antlers on the Ohio

For years the Delta Queen raced the Belle of Louisville on the Ohio River during the Kentucky Derby Festival's Great Steamboat Race. The winner mounted a trophy of golden antlers on its pilothouse until the next race. The boat also raced at the Tall Stacks festivals held every three or four years in Cincinnati, her former home port. Her regular cruises stretched from New Orleans and Memphis to St. Louis, St. Paul, Cincinnati and Pittsburgh, and some probed the Arkansas, the Red, the Black Warrior and the Tennessee-Tombigbee Waterway. In the early 1970s, songwriter Leroy Drumm saw her running down the Tennessee River and was inspired to write "Delta Queen" with Pete Goble; Charlie Waller & The Country Gentlemen recorded it in 1974.

## A Hotel on the North Shore

On August 1, 2007, the operator announced that the Delta Queen would stop sailing after the 2008 season because her latest exemption was expiring. Supporters revived the "Save the Delta Queen" campaign, and in Scotland, Jackie Baillie, the Member of the Scottish Parliament for Dumbarton, backed by 15 other members, filed a motion calling for the boat's preservation. It was not enough, and she ended service in 2008. Leased to Chattanooga businessman Harry Phillips, she tied up at Coolidge Park Landing and opened as the Delta Queen Hotel on June 5, 2009, offering dining, period music and theater until closing in December 2014. The National Trust for Historic Preservation named her a "National Treasure" in 2013. The newly formed Delta Queen Steamboat Company bought her in early 2015, and on March 22 she left Chattanooga under tow, reaching Houma, Louisiana, on April 7 for restoration. In December 2018 Congress passed, and the President signed, a law restoring her exemption, but the money for her restoration never materialized. Hurricane Ida damaged her at Houma in 2021, and in June 2025 her owners announced she was for sale, with priority for buyers who would not dismantle her.

## Flight Context

These coordinates, 35.0599°N, 85.3086°W, mark the Delta Queen's former berth at Coolidge Park Landing on Chattanooga's North Shore, across the Tennessee River from downtown; the boat herself was towed to Houma, Louisiana, in 2015. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet, follow the Tennessee River through the city to the stretch between the downtown bridges, with Lookout Mountain rising to the southwest. Chattanooga Metropolitan Airport-Lovell Field (KCHA) is about 5.4 nm to the east-southeast, so expect busy airspace and coordinate with Chattanooga approach. The river valley often holds morning fog and haze, especially from late summer into fall.
