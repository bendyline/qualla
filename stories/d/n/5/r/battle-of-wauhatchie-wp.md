# Battle of Wauhatchie

In one of the Civil War's rare night battles, Confederate troops struck an isolated Union division in Lookout Valley in October 1863, trying and failing to cut the newly opened "Cracker Line" that would keep the besieged Union army in Chattanooga alive.

**Location:** 35.0097, -85.3744

**Categories:** Chattanooga campaign, Battles of the American Civil War in Tennessee, Battles of the western theater of the American Civil War, Battles commanded by Ulysses S. Grant, Conflicts in 1863, Hamilton County, Tennessee

## Introduction

After the shooting stopped, the joke in the Union camps was that the mules deserved a promotion. A rumor spread that animals stampeded by the fighting had convinced the Confederates they were under cavalry attack, and soldiers suggested the mules be "breveted as horses." The truth was smaller but still real: the stampede threw the Hampton Legion into disorder for only a short time, yet that lull let the 137th New York plug a gap in the Federal line. It was one odd moment in the Battle of Wauhatchie, fought in the dark of October 28 and 29, 1863, in Lookout Valley, and one of the few night battles of the Civil War.

## An Army Under Siege

The fight grew out of hunger. After their disastrous defeat at Chickamauga, Union forces under Maj. Gen. William Rosecrans had retreated into Chattanooga, and Gen. Braxton Bragg's Confederate Army of Tennessee besieged the city, threatening to starve it into surrender. Bragg's troops held Missionary Ridge and Lookout Mountain, heights with excellent views of the city, the river and the Union supply lines, and Confederate raiders struck every wagon heading toward town. Maj. Gen. Ulysses S. Grant replaced Rosecrans with Maj. Gen. George H. Thomas, and on reaching Chattanooga Grant made resupply his first priority. The answer was the "Cracker Line Operation," launched on October 26, 1863, to open a road from Brown's Ferry on the Tennessee River while a second force advanced up Lookout Valley.

## Pontoons Around Moccasin Bend

The plan's architect, Brig. Gen. William F. "Baldy" Smith, chief engineer of the Military Division of the Mississippi, was assigned to establish the Brown's Ferry bridgehead with two infantry brigades under Brig. Gens. John B. Turchin and William B. Hazen. At 3 a.m. on October 27, part of Hazen's brigade boarded pontoons and floated around Moccasin Bend to the ferry, while Turchin's men waited on the bend across the river. Hazen seized the bridgehead and laid a pontoon bridge so Turchin could cross. On the Confederate side, Col. William C. Oates of the 15th Alabama guarded the valley, but Law's three reserve regiments had been recalled to the east side of Lookout Mountain two days earlier, leaving him thinly supported. His scratch counterattack failed and he was wounded, and by the time Brig. Gen. Evander M. Law arrived with the reserves, the Federals were too numerous and too well entrenched to dislodge.

## Hooker in the Valley

Meanwhile Maj. Gen. Joseph Hooker marched three Union divisions from Bridgeport, following the railroad through Shellmound and the Running Water Creek gorge. On October 28, after a rapid march, his column entered Lookout Valley, astonishing Bragg and James Longstreet, who were conferring on Lookout Mountain. Longstreet, fixated on a possible Union attack farther southwest, had failed to scout Hooker's approach. As his force moved through the valley, Hooker left Brig. Gen. John W. Geary's division at Wauhatchie Station, a stop on the Nashville and Chattanooga Railroad, to guard the line of communications and the road to Kelley's Ferry. After detaching railroad guards, Geary had only about 1,500 men, and they were posted in isolation, while Howard's understrength XI Corps camped haphazardly at Brown's Ferry.

## Midnight at Wauhatchie Station

Bragg ordered Longstreet to drive off the new arrivals, and Longstreet, noting a wagon train halted near Wauhatchie, chose to crush Geary. Brig. Gen. Micah Jenkins's division would attack by night. Law, with his own and Brig. Gen. Jerome B. Robertson's brigades, would block reinforcements, while Col. John Bratton led Jenkins's 1,800-man South Carolina brigade against the station and Brig. Gen. Henry Benning's brigade stood in support. Scheduled for 10 p.m., the attack was delayed by confusion until midnight. Geary's officers had expected trouble and thrown out pickets, but the suddenness of the assault still surprised them. Enveloped from the north, the Union defenders bent into a V-shaped line facing north and east. Somewhere in that darkness Geary's son, Lt. Edward Geary of Knap's battery, was killed by a shot while sighting a cannon. His men held on even as their ammunition ran low.

## Confusion on the Hill, and the Cost

Hearing the gunfire, the XI Corps fell into ranks, and Hooker, bypassing Maj. Gen. Oliver O. Howard, ordered Maj. Gen. Carl Schurz to march to Geary's aid. Instead Brig. Gen. Adolph von Steinwehr's division reached the road first, and Col. Orland Smith's brigade came under fire from Law's men on a 200-foot hill commanding the route. Hooker mistakenly sent units from both XI Corps divisions against that hill, leaving no one to relieve Geary. In the dark, only Smith's 700 men actually engaged Law's 2,000, and several assaults were beaten back before Law, acting on erroneous reports, withdrew. At the station, Bratton sensed victory just as word came that Union reinforcements were closing on his rear, and he fell back to Lookout Mountain under Benning's cover. Union losses came to 78 killed, 327 wounded and 15 missing; the Confederates reported 34 killed, 305 wounded and 69 missing. The Cracker Line stayed open, and the besieged army could now receive food, ammunition and reinforcements, clearing the way for the Battles for Chattanooga that began on November 23.

## Flight Context

The battle site centers on 35.0097°N, 85.3744°W in Lookout Valley, Tennessee, west of Lookout Mountain and just south and west of Chattanooga. From 3,000 to 4,000 feet MSL, look for the long, steep flank of Lookout Mountain on the east side of the valley and, to the northeast, the tight loop of the Tennessee River around Moccasin Bend, where Hazen's pontoons floated to Brown's Ferry. Lookout Mountain rises well above the valley floor, so keep terrain clearance in mind. Chattanooga Metropolitan Airport-Lovell Field (KCHA) is about 8.5 nm to the east, beyond the mountain, and Marion County Airport-Brown Field (KAPT) near Jasper is about 11 nm to the west-northwest. Autumn mornings often bring valley fog.
