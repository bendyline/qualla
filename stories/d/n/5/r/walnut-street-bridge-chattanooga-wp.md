# Walnut Street Bridge (Chattanooga)

Built in 1890 from parts made by a Georgia jail works, Chattanooga's 2,376-foot Walnut Street Bridge carried trolleys, bore witness to two lynchings, fell into disrepair, and returned as one of the city's favorite places to walk.

## Introduction

Most of the iron in Chattanooga's Walnut Street Bridge came from a company that built jails. The Manly Jail Works of Dalton, Georgia, manufactured the bulk of its parts and shipped them to the site by rail, where the Smith Bridge Company of Toledo, Ohio, assembled the superstructure. When it opened in 1890, the 2,376-foot crossing tied downtown Chattanooga to North Chattanooga across the Tennessee River, and the Historic American Engineering Record later concluded that it "was apparently the first non-military highway bridge across the Tennessee River." No cars cross it now. It belongs to walkers and runners.

## Iron Humps Over the Tennessee

Seen from the riverbank, the main spans rise and fall in a line of iron humps. They are pin-connected Parker through trusses, meaning the roadway runs between the trusses rather than on top of them, and their top chords are built in five sections, giving them a profile close to the Camelback truss design. A plaque on the bridge names Edwin Thacher as chief engineer. The Smith Bridge Company, a prolific builder of the late nineteenth century, put the ironwork together, while the piers beneath were the work of a local firm, Neeley, Smith, and Company of Chattanooga. The result drew on firms from three states. Engineering historians value the bridge as an extremely long and old example of its type, and at nearly half a mile it remains a striking survivor of the era of pinned iron.

## The Stone Contractor's Window

The stonework went to William Andrew Slayton (1854–1935) of Ann Arbor, Michigan. Slayton lived in a stone house at 533 Barton Avenue, a place later occupants knew for years as the home of the "Little Art Shop." Whether he built the house himself is unknown, but from its window he could watch his piers take shape, much as Washington Roebling watched over the Brooklyn Bridge. The project left traces all over the neighborhood. Many of the low stone walls in North Chattanooga are built from stones judged too small for the piers. Old subdivision plats suggest that Slayton developed parts of the area to make it easier to haul stone in from quarries in northeastern Alabama, and his name survives on Slayton Street and Slayton Avenue, near the public library on Broad Street.

## Two Shores, One Crossing

Chattanoogans once called it simply the "county bridge." It joined a predominantly white city on the south side of the river with Hill City on the North Shore, home to a large Black workforce and a separate town until Chattanooga annexed it in 1912. Trolleys of the Chattanooga Traction Company crossed it on their way to Signal Mountain. The bridge also carries a darker memory. Two Black men were lynched from its spans: Alfred Blount on February 14, 1893, and Ed Johnson on March 19, 1906, each accused of attacking a white woman. Johnson's killing led to United States v. Shipp, which stands as the only criminal trial in the history of the U.S. Supreme Court. Any honest walk across the bridge includes these two men.

## From Asphalt to Planks

In 1978 the bridge closed to motor vehicles, and for nearly a decade it sat unused and falling apart. A community group, Chattanooga Venture, started the Walnut Street Bridge fund to collect money the city could put toward restoration. Repairs and structural modifications turned the old highway bridge into a pedestrian walkway, and on February 23, 1990, a century after its completion, it was added to the National Register of Historic Places. Money left over from the campaign paid for further improvements, including replacements for original plaques that had been damaged, vandalized or stolen. The work did not stop there. Between December 2009 and May 2010, the deteriorating asphalt surface came up and wood planking went down, under a $1.3 million contract the City Council awarded to Tower Construction. A far bigger overhaul followed: the bridge closed in March 2025 for a renovation the city put at $47 million, which replaced or braced more than 200,000 pounds of steel, laid new wood decking, rewired the span and repainted it, and it reopened to walkers on October 3, 2026.

## A Stage Above the River

Chattanooga treats the bridge as a stage. Wine over Water, a wine-tasting event, began on the span in 1994, though its 2026 edition was held at nearby Renaissance Park. For years the Riverbend Festival brought country and rock bands to town every June, and on its last night the bridge turned into a launch platform. Fireworks were set off from the middle of the span and fell toward the water in a bright waterfall, while boaters were kept several hundred feet away; the festival has been on hiatus since 2024. Runners know the planks too. The Seven Bridges Marathon normally includes the bridge in its course, and when Chattanooga hosted Ironman races in 2014 and 2015, athletes crossed it during the running leg, with the river stretching away in both directions below them.
