# Washington, Kentucky

Reputedly the second-largest town in what would become Kentucky, tiny Old Washington gave the country two opposing Civil War generals, a freedom-seeker whose case helped make Canada a haven, and the slave auction Harriet Beecher Stowe never forgot.

**Location:** 38.6159, -83.8085

## Introduction

In 1790, by local accounts, only one town in the future state of Kentucky was bigger than Washington. Lexington had more people; nowhere else did. The census that year counted about 460 residents here (sources give 462 or 482), 21 of them enslaved, in a frontier town barely four years old. Today Washington is a neighborhood of Maysville, sometimes called Old Washington, and it has grown little since the 1840s. That is its gift. Log cabins still stand along streets where a future president recruited soldiers, a Confederate general was born, and a young teacher from Cincinnati watched human beings sold outside the courthouse.

## A Town Named for the General

Two Virginians founded the settlement in 1786: Arthur Fox, a Revolutionary War soldier, and William Wood, a Baptist preacher. They bought the land from Simon Kenton, the explorer and settler who lived close by, and Daniel Boone served among the first trustees. Since Kentucky was not yet a separate state, the town began life as Washington, Virginia, and it is believed to be the first settlement named for George Washington. Many early settlers were veterans of the Revolution, and 17 are known to be buried here. Captain Thomas Marshall, brother of the future Chief Justice John Marshall, settled in town, and his parents joined him at the Marshall Home, still standing on Green Street. In 1789 Washington received the region's first post office, which at first served the whole Northwest Territory across the Ohio River.

## The Boy in the Postmaster's House

Washington's third postmaster, William Murphy, built a large house on Old Main Street that still stands. From about 1815 until the mid-1820s, a boy named Thornton Blackburn lived there, enslaved in Murphy's household. His life later took him to Hardinsburg and then Louisville, and in 1831 he and his wife, Lucie, escaped to Michigan. In 1833 they were arrested in Detroit as fugitives. Lucie escaped by swapping clothes with a visitor, and when Thornton was about to be returned to Kentucky, Detroit's Black community rose up in protest; he was freed in the riot that followed, and both reached Canada. Efforts to return them as criminals followed, but the Lieutenant-Governor of Upper Canada (now Ontario), Sir John Colborne, refused, reasoning that a person could not steal himself. The case helped confirm Canada as a safe terminus of the Underground Railroad. In Toronto, Blackburn launched the city's first taxi service in 1837: a red and yellow horse-drawn cab named The City.

## An Auction Outside the Courthouse

In 1833 Harriet Beecher, then teaching at the Western Female Institute in Cincinnati, came to Washington to visit a student, Elizabeth Key. In front of the old courthouse, the museum says, she saw enslaved people sold at auction. That scene, together with her other encounters with slavery, led the woman the world would later know by her married name, Harriet Beecher Stowe, to write Uncle Tom's Cabin, and the novel contains a number of references to Washington. Its character Topsy is thought to be modeled on Jane Anderson, a woman who lived here first in slavery and then in freedom. The Key House on Main Street, where the young teacher stayed, is now the Harriet Beecher Stowe Slavery to Freedom Museum.

## Two Generals, One House

Albert Sidney Johnston, probably Washington's most famous native, was born here in 1803, the son of a physician. He went to West Point, rose to senior brigadier general in the Army of the Republic of Texas and served as the Texan Secretary of War, then returned to the U.S. Army. In 1861 he resigned to join the Confederacy, commanding its Western Department, and he died at the Battle of Shiloh on April 6, 1862. His house is now a museum. William "Bull" Nelson, son of another Washington doctor, is said to have lived in that same house and attended the same Washington Presbyterian church. Trained as a naval officer, Nelson sided with the Union, and at Shiloh his fresh troops reached the Union line late on the day Johnston fell. Within months Nelson was dead too, shot by a fellow Union general, Jefferson C. Davis, in Louisville.

## From a Small Town to the Wider World

Washington made national news in 1830, when President Andrew Jackson vetoed federal investment in the Maysville-Washington-Lexington Turnpike, the so-called Maysville Road Veto, seeing it as an intrastate road benefiting the state of his rival Henry Clay. Its people traveled far anyway. Charles William Forman, born in Washington, left for India as a Presbyterian missionary in 1847, with the backing of the Washington Presbyterian Church, and founded a school in Lahore that grew into Forman Christian College. Lorrin Andrews is said to have taught school here, and he married a local woman, Mary Ann Wilson, in 1827, before helping to run Lahainaluna Seminary in Hawaii. Zachary Taylor briefly recruited soldiers in town. Maysville, on the river, took over as county seat in 1848 and annexed Washington in 1990. Today several small museums, among them the Harriet Beecher Stowe museum and Mefford's Fort, a log house tied to a family that came down the Ohio in 1787, help keep the old town, whose historic district was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1970.

## Flight Context

Old Washington lies at 38.6159 N, 83.8085 W, a few miles southwest of downtown Maysville and the Ohio River in Mason County, Kentucky. From 2,500-3,500 ft AGL, use the Ohio River at Maysville as your reference, then look inland for the small grid of the historic town surrounded by rolling farmland. Nearest airport: Fleming-Mason Airport near Flemingsburg (FAA identifier FGX, GPS code KFGX), about 10 km southeast. Larger fields: Cincinnati Municipal Airport-Lunken Field (KLUK) about 76 km northwest, Cincinnati/Northern Kentucky International (KCVG) about 89 km west-northwest, and Blue Grass Airport in Lexington (KLEX) about 95 km southwest. River valley fog is common on calm mornings along the Ohio.
