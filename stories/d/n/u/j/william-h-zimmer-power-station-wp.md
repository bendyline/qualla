# William H. Zimmer Power Station

Once estimated to be 97 to 99 percent finished as a nuclear plant, Zimmer on the Ohio River became what is billed as the world's first nuclear-to-coal conversion, ran for three decades on coal, and shut down in 2022.

**Location:** 38.8664, -84.2281

**Categories:** Former coal-fired power stations in Ohio, Cancelled nuclear power stations in the United States, Buildings and structures in Clermont County, Ohio, Energy infrastructure completed in 1991, Vistra Corp

## Introduction

At one point the William H. Zimmer Power Station was estimated to be 97 percent complete as a nuclear plant, and other accounts put it at 99. It never became one. Poor construction and failures of quality assurance left the plant on the Ohio River near Moscow, Ohio, stranded just short of the finish line, and its owners made a choice that was reportedly unprecedented. They converted it to burn coal. When the 1,351-megawatt station finally came online in 1991, it was billed as the world's first nuclear-to-coal power plant.

## Two Reactors on the Drawing Board

The plan was announced in 1969 by a consortium of Cincinnati Gas and Electric, a forerunner of Duke Energy, together with Columbus & Southern Ohio Electric and Dayton Power & Light. For a reported $395 million to $420 million, depending on the account, they would build two 840-megawatt nuclear units, due to be finished in 1975 and 1976. General Electric boiling water reactors were chosen. Work did not begin until 1972, because the Atomic Energy Commission was demanding stricter standards before approving nuclear plants. In 1974 the second unit was upgraded to 1,170 megawatts, but it was never built, a casualty of regulatory issues and costs.

## Welds, Time Cards and Investigators

Trouble surfaced in 1978, when a worker charged that a welding contractor had done defective work below nuclear safety standards, and an investigation began. In 1980 the Chicago Sun-Times reported accusations that Cincinnati Gas and Electric had covered up falsified time cards and the installation of defective piping in one of the plant's safety systems. The Nuclear Regulatory Commission fined the utility $200,000 in 1981 for a faulty quality assurance program. The Henry J. Kaiser Company was the constructor, but the utility handled its own procurement, equipment contracts and quality assurance requirements. The FBI opened an investigation into the plant's safety records and the harassment of inspectors. Retired Navy Admiral Joe Williams Jr. was brought in to get the plant online, and Bechtel was hired to nuclear-qualify it. Bechtel's estimate was sobering: more than $1.5 billion on top of the $1.6 billion already spent. The reckoning reached the courts as well: in November 1987 a lawsuit was settled with Sargent & Lundy agreeing to pay the utilities $27.4 million, and the utilities agreeing to pay Sargent & Lundy $12.7 million.

## Rebuilt Around Fire

Conversion to coal began in March 1987. As crews reworked the site, Little Indian Creek was routed through the plant to protect its fish, and several environmental features were added to keep the creek in its natural state. Mussels along the riverbed were relocated upstream. After $3.3 billion in total expenditures, the plant was completed in 1991. Its heart was now a Babcock & Wilcox supercritical boiler running at about 3,845 psi and roughly 1,010 degrees Fahrenheit, feeding a modified Westinghouse turbine, while a turbine designed by ABB is reported to have driven the feedwater pump with 67,000 horsepower. A 573-foot smokestack carried away the flue gas, and gypsum from the scrubbers was sold to make drywall. One legacy of the nuclear design is said never to have gone away. The cooling tower had reportedly been sized for the original plant, which created efficiency problems on very hot summer days.

## The Last Shift

Ownership shifted in the plant's final decade. Duke Energy agreed in 2014 to sell its stake to Dynegy, a deal completed in 2015, American Electric Power traded its 25 percent share to Dynegy in 2017, and Dayton Power & Light's 28 percent followed, giving Dynegy sole ownership. Vistra took over through its 2018 merger with Dynegy. In early 2020 Vistra agreed to a federal consent decree, proposed by the Justice Department and the Environmental Protection Agency, resolving claims that the plant had exceeded its sulfur dioxide and opacity limits over more than a decade, some of it under earlier owners. Vistra agreed to a $600,000 civil penalty, $45,000 of energy-efficient lighting at a nearby school and the replacement of three older school buses with cleaner-burning ones. That September Vistra said Zimmer would retire by the end of 2027. Then, in July 2021, after failing to secure capacity revenue in the grid operator PJM's May auction, it moved the date up by five years. Zimmer closed on May 31, 2022.

## Flight Context

The Zimmer station stands at 38.8664 N, 84.2281 W on the Ohio side of the Ohio River near Moscow, in Clermont County, about 35 km southeast of downtown Cincinnati. From 3,000-4,000 ft AGL the large cooling tower and 573-foot smokestack beside the river make it an easy visual checkpoint (the plant closed in 2022 and Vistra said in 2023 that it was not being demolished, but check current imagery before relying on them); note the stack height when flying low along the river corridor. Nearby airports: Clermont County Airport near Batavia (FAA identifier I69), about 24 km north; Cincinnati Municipal Airport-Lunken Field (KLUK), about 31 km north-northwest; Brown County Airport near Georgetown (FAA identifier GEO, GPS code KGEO), about 30 km east; and Cincinnati/Northern Kentucky International (KCVG), about 43 km west-northwest. Morning river fog is common in cooler months.
