# Chateau Laroche

On the banks of the Little Miami River north of Loveland, Ohio, a Boy Scout leader and World War I veteran spent more than fifty years building a medieval-style castle from river stones and milk-carton bricks, then left it to the troop of 'knights' who still keep it.

## Introduction

By the castle's own telling, Harry Delos Andrews was declared dead in 1918. He had been serving as a medic in World War I when he contracted spinal meningitis, and by the time the error was corrected, six months later, his fiancée had married another man. Andrews stayed on in Europe, visiting castles. Back in Ohio he eventually began building one of his own on the banks of the Little Miami River, north of Loveland. Château Laroche, better known as the Loveland Castle, took him more than fifty years, and it is now a museum run by the scout troop he left it to.

## Knights Without a Castle

The castle, its keepers like to say, was almost an afterthought. In the early 1920s Andrews led a Sunday School and Boy Scout troop of a dozen or so boys who loved to camp. A Cincinnati newspaper had been handing out plots of land on the Little Miami as a reward for subscribing, and the parents of one of Andrews's pupils had some of those plots, which they sold to Andrews and his boys. The riverbank became their regular campsite, but gear left there kept going missing or coming back damaged. By 1928 Andrews's knights numbered more than 100 and a problem: real knights, they decided, needed a castle. Andrews struck a bargain. If they helped him fetch stone from the river, he would build them a real castle in the style of the 10th century.

## Stone by Stone, Carton by Carton

Accounts differ on when construction began: one dates it to 1927, while the castle's own history says Andrews bought the property in 1929 and began building on June 5 of that year. Either way, he kept at it for more than half a century. At first the stones came from the nearby Little Miami. When that supply was exhausted, Andrews molded his own bricks, pouring cement into quart milk cartons. He had strong views about why the Middle Ages appealed to him: he objected to modern warfare, with its killing at a distance, and preferred the idea of sword-to-sword combat. He also built the road down to the castle, which buses still cannot manage, and grew vegetables year-round in hotbeds of his own design, warmed by railroad lanterns. Somewhere along the way he hid a secret room in the garden, its entrance one of the arches in the arch wall. Nobody found it until it collapsed from neglect.

## The Knights Inherit

Andrews died in 1981 and bequeathed the castle to the Knights of the Golden Trail. The troop has since upgraded and renovated it extensively and has largely completed what he started. The East tower now shows a short video about his quest to finish his dream. Upstairs, the chapel walls are set with stones Andrews brought home from his travels around the world, along with others sent to him from abroad by friends and followers. The castle is full scale except for its ballroom, a one-fifth replica of the ballroom Andrews used as a field hospital during the war. Later work has expanded the outside gardens and added a greenhouse. Visitors tour on their own, though the knight on duty may offer a little history. The order still describes the castle as its world headquarters, and says its members take only one set of vows: the Ten Commandments.

## The Rock Castle's Ghosts

The name Château Laroche means "Rock Castle" in French, a fitting title for a building that began with boys fetching stones from the river. The knights' creed, published by the castle, praises the medieval knight's "zeal for honor, valor, and manly purity" and argues that present-day decadence calls for the same, declaring that the ancient order of knights has been reactivated to save society. Over the years the castle has collected stories of hauntings, and they often come from the castle's own volunteer knights. Whether or not anyone believes them, the tales suit the place. This is, after all, a castle built by a man once declared dead, its chapel set with stones from his travels, guarding a garden room that stayed secret until its walls fell in.
