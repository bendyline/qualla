# Voice of America Bethany Relay Station

Built on Ohio farmland in 1943-44, the Bethany Relay Station beamed Voice of America news into wartime Europe with some of the world's most powerful shortwave transmitters, and its control room now anchors a museum.

**Location:** 39.3550, -84.3567

**Categories:** Voice of America, International broadcasting, Telecommunications museums in the United States, National Register of Historic Places in Butler County, Ohio, 1944 establishments in Ohio

## Introduction

"Those Zinzinnati Liars." That, reportedly, is what Adolf Hitler called the broadcasters beaming news into his Reich from a stretch of Ohio farmland about 25 miles north of Cincinnati. The Voice of America Bethany Relay Station was once one of the world's most powerful shortwave radio stations, built in wartime haste to carry fact-based news to people whose own governments punished them for listening. Today its antenna towers are gone, but the building and its original control room survive as a museum.

## Why a Farm in Ohio

President Franklin D. Roosevelt saw an urgent need for more American shortwave capacity, and he created the Office of War Information to counter the radio programs of the Axis governments. Roosevelt and the OWI rejected propaganda in favor of fact-based news. Location mattered. Existing transmitters in Massachusetts, on Long Island and in New Jersey were vulnerable to German submarines or invading forces, so the government looked inland. In 1943 it bought about 624 acres in Butler County from five owners, among them Hazel Beckley, who sold 170 acres, and Suzie Steinman, who sold 142. The site was chosen for its elevation and shallow bedrock, about a mile west of the Crosley company's tower for radio station WLW in Mason. It took its name from the community of Bethany, about two miles to the north.

## Six Transmitters, Two Hundred Thousand Watts Each

Crosley Broadcasting Corporation was no stranger to the work. Since 1940 it had operated WLWO, "WLW Overseas," for the government, pushing programs toward South America and Europe with specially designed, top-secret reentrant rhombic antennas. Bethany demanded far more. Under the supervision of R.J. Rockwell, Crosley built six 200,000-watt transmitters, taking its engineers into new technological territory. Construction began in the summer of 1943, and the station was dedicated on September 23, 1944, although a contemporary NBC trade magazine says its first broadcast had gone out on July 1. By the end of 1944 all six transmitters were running on six international frequencies. Because shortwaves bounce off the ionosphere, broadcast times and frequencies shifted through the day to catch the best conditions, and operators could move to a new frequency in ten minutes.

## Getting Through the Jamming

Power alone was not enough. Under the Axis regimes, people caught listening to foreign broadcasts were imprisoned or killed, and authorities jammed signals whenever they could. Bethany's answer was agility. The same program went out on two frequencies at once, and the station's rapid frequency changes helped it slip past jammers, earning it a reputation for reliable, clear transmissions. That strength showed at the war's end in Europe. Bethany rebroadcast Winston Churchill's Victory in Europe speech of May 8, 1945, over several days, reaching Germany at a time when, reportedly, the BBC and AFN lacked the power to do so.

## Cold War and Silence

President Harry S. Truman abolished the Office of War Information in 1945, and the station passed through the hands of the State Department and the U.S. Information Agency over the following decades. Crosley kept running it for the government until November 1963, when the Voice of America assumed direct control. Technology eventually overtook the shortwave giant as transmissions shifted to satellites. Bethany went off the air on November 14, 1994, and the antenna towers came down over the winter of 1997-1998.

## The Control Room Lives On

Through the Federal Lands to Parks Program, the land was divided. West Chester Township took the station building and 22 acres for historic preservation, and after several earlier attempts, citizens and preservationists formed a nonprofit in 2009 to turn it into a museum. Visitors now see the original control room, exhibits on how the station worked, and displays on the Crosley brothers' innovations in radio and television, along with a Radio Discovery Lab on the science of radio. The West Chester Amateur Radio Association, WC8VOA, keeps an extensive installation in the building. Elsewhere on the old station grounds are Voice of America Park, the Miami University Voice of America Learning Center, which opened in January 2009, and a shopping center called the Voice of America Centre. In 2022 and 2023 the park hosted a large music festival, "Voices of America," partly to honor the station's history.
