# Kings Island

Born when floodwaters drove Cincinnati's Coney Island inland, Kings Island grew into a 364-acre park of record-setting wooden coasters, a one-third-scale Eiffel Tower and a few spectacular failures.

**Location:** 39.3413, -84.2725

**Categories:** Amusement parks in Ohio, Amusement parks opened in 1972, Six Flags amusement parks, Tourist attractions in Warren County, Ohio, Mason, Ohio

## Introduction

Kings Island exists because of a river. In 1964, the Ohio River flooded Coney Island, a popular amusement park 10 miles east of downtown Cincinnati, and submerged the grounds under more than 14 feet of water. Gary Wachs, son of Coney Island president Ralph Wachs, decided it was time to move the park to stay competitive. The result, built by Taft Broadcasting and opened in 1972, was a new park 24 miles northeast of the city in what is now Mason, Ohio. Today Kings Island covers 364 acres, with more than a hundred attractions, fifteen roller coasters and a 33-acre water park called Soak City.

## Carried Up From the River

The move was meant to relocate and expand Coney Island, and some of the old park came along. The Bavarian Beetle, a small steel coaster, was brought over from Coney and running on opening day in 1972, and the Grand Carousel, a classic carousel built in 1926, still turns on International Street after its years at Coney. That street was the park's front door from the start. Bruce Bushman, a former Walt Disney Imagineering layout artist, designed it with architecture evoking Italy, Spain, Germany and Switzerland. Its landmarks include the park's signature Eiffel Tower, a one-third-scale replica with a view over the entire park, and the Royal Fountain, which can hurl 10,000 gallons of water into the air every minute. Elsewhere, the Oktoberfest area, with its timber-framed buildings, celebrates Cincinnati's German heritage.

## The Racer, the Bradys and a Man on a Wire

Kings Island made noise quickly. The Racer, a wooden coaster by designer John C. Allen that opened with the park, is often credited with reviving worldwide interest in roller coasters during the 1970s, and American Coaster Enthusiasts later named it a Roller Coaster Landmark. The most expensive ride on opening day was Enchanted Voyage, a $2-million dark ride populated by animatronic Hanna-Barbera characters. Television came calling too. The park was promoted in The Partridge Family (an episode filmed in 1972 and aired in January 1973) and The Brady Bunch (aired November 1973), the latter produced by Paramount Television, a large Taft shareholder. Then, in 1974, 69-year-old Karl Wallenda stepped onto a wire 60 feet above the ground and walked 1,800 feet, setting what was then a world skywalk distance record.

## Measured in Feet of Track

Records became a habit. The Beast, which opened in 1979, is still the longest wooden roller coaster in the world, and it consistently ranks among the top ten wooden coasters in Amusement Today's annual Golden Ticket Awards. Screamin' Demon, running from 1977 to 1987, was one of the first looping coasters in the United States to run both forward and backward, and King Cobra, from 1984 to 2001, was billed as the world's first stand-up coaster designed as one from the start. Banshee arrived in 2014 as the world's longest inverted coaster. In 2017, the 109-foot Mystic Timbers added 3,265 feet of track and brought the park's wooden coaster total to 18,804 feet, the most of any amusement park in the world. The largest single investment in park history, at the time, came in 2020: Orion, a $30-million giga coaster with a 300-foot drop and a top speed of 91 mph.

## The Rides That Didn't Last

Not every experiment worked. In the 1980s the park partnered with Arrow Development, a ride company well known for its work at Disneyland and Disney World, on a new coaster concept. The Bat opened in 1981 as the first modern-day suspended roller coaster, but design flaws and constant maintenance led to frequent closures over its short, three-year life. Son of Beast told a harder story. When it opened in 2000, it was the tallest and fastest wooden roller coaster in the world and the first of its kind with a vertical loop. Structural problems and several rider injuries followed, and the ride closed permanently in 2009 before being demolished in 2012. One early venture survives only in the park's history books: today's Action Zone began in 1974 as Lion Country Safari, a 100-acre section where a monorail carried guests on a safari-style tour through an animal preserve.

## Changing Hands, Changing Characters

Ownership changed almost as often as the skyline. Taft sold most of its theme park division in 1984 for $167.5 million to Kings Entertainment Company, formed by Taft's own executives. In 1987 Kings Island was sold to American Financial Corporation, led by Carl Lindner, and in 1992 Paramount bought it as part of a larger $400 million transaction, bringing movie themes and Nickelodeon characters. Cedar Fair took over in 2006, dropped the Paramount branding after a single season despite holding a ten-year license and, from 2010, filled the children's area with Peanuts characters as Planet Snoopy. Today the park belongs to Six Flags. By one estimate it drew 3.18 million guests in 2021, third in attendance among North America's seasonal amusement parks, and its children's area won Amusement Today's "Best Kids' Area" award eighteen years running, from 2001 through 2018. Each season stretches from spring through Labor Day, followed by Halloween Haunt and Winterfest.

## Flight Context

Kings Island lies at 39.3413°N, 84.2725°W in Mason, Ohio, about 24 miles northeast of downtown Cincinnati. From 3,000–4,000 ft MSL the park is easy to identify by its one-third-scale Eiffel Tower replica at the head of International Street, the tall steel profile of the Orion giga coaster, and the long wooden coaster layouts spread across the grounds. Warren County Airport/John Lane Field (FAA identifier I68) at Lebanon is about 7 nm north, Butler County Regional Airport (KHAO) is about 12 nm west, and Cincinnati Municipal Lunken Field (KLUK) is about 16 nm south-southwest. Cincinnati/Northern Kentucky International Airport (KCVG) lies about 25 nm southwest, so check airspace boundaries around the Cincinnati area before planning a low pass. Summer haze can reduce visibility over the Ohio Valley, so plan for clearer air after a frontal passage.
