# Son of Beast

Kings Island's Son of Beast opened in 2000 as the tallest and fastest wooden roller coaster on Earth, spent its short life in repairs and controversy, and was sold off as souvenirs piece by piece in 2012.

**Location:** 39.3461, -84.2647

**Categories:** Former roller coasters in Ohio, Roller coasters operated by Cedar Fair, Buildings and structures demolished in 2012, Demolished buildings and structures in Ohio, Kings Island

## Introduction

In the autumn of 2012, Kings Island sold Son of Beast by the piece. A plaque bearing a section of its track went for $99.99. A chunk of the wooden structure cost $49.99, and a single structural bolt could be had for $24.99. By then the roller coaster had been standing idle for three years, and the demolition crews were already at work. It was a strange end for a ride that, only twelve years earlier, had opened in Mason, Ohio, as the tallest and fastest wooden roller coaster in the world, marks that no wooden coaster has since topped.

## Something in the Crate

The idea took shape in 1997, when the Roller Coaster Corporation of America discussed building the world's first wooden hypercoaster with Kings Island. On May 11, 1999, the park staged a public reveal near a large covered crate placed along a footpath, and when the ride opened, its entrance sign echoed that moment: a wooden crate wrapped in chains, rope and metal straps, its front ripped open to show the name inside. The coaster was marketed as a sequel to The Beast, the park's signature wooden coaster built in 1979. Officially it was designed by the Roller Coaster Company of Ohio, an affiliate and shell company of RCCA, with Wooden Structures Inc. of Georgia as primary structural engineer and lumber from Universal Forest Products of Hamilton, Ohio.

## Two Hundred Eighteen Feet of Timber

Son of Beast opened on April 28, 2000. Its 218-foot lift hill made it the first wooden hypercoaster, the class reserved for rides of 200 feet or more, and its trains hit a record 78 miles per hour. From the crest, riders turned left and plunged 214 feet, followed by a 70-degree banked turn to the right, then dropped another 164 feet into a left-hand double helix. It was also the first wooden coaster of the modern era with a vertical loop. A circuit lasted about two minutes and twenty seconds, and the whole ride sprawled across 12 acres. When it debuted, it was reportedly the only wooden coaster nationally ranked in the top ten for track height, track length, drop height and speed, and only its predecessor, The Beast, was longer.

## Trouble Before the First Ride

Problems began before opening day. On January 11, 2000, a portion of the ride was damaged, at first attributed to a strong gust of wind; it was later revealed that the second hill, held up by a temporary ribbon support system and built only a day earlier, had collapsed by itself. Rain halted construction and pushed back the planned April 14 opening. In February the Occupational Safety and Health Administration fined Paramount $110,000 after finding 18 safety violations, 11 of them deemed serious. The day after the ride opened, officials discovered defects in a 15-foot section of track, and Son of Beast closed for three weeks of repairs. Design corrections continued through its first year, and the dispute reached the courts: in 2005 a federal court ordered the designer's insurer, Admiral Insurance, to pay Kings Island $20 million, a ruling overturned on appeal in 2008.

## A Dip in the Rose Bowl

On the evening of July 9, 2006, a structural failure in the section known as the Rose Bowl formed a slight dip in the track, and trains hit it with a pothole effect. Twenty-seven riders were sent to the hospital, many with head and neck injuries; all but two were released the same day. Inspectors traced the cause to cracked or split wood along the track. Afterward, Kings Island bought two lighter Gerstlauer trains from the demolished Hurricane: Category 5 coaster at the Myrtle Beach Pavilion, carrying 24 riders each instead of the original 36, to reduce the load on the wooden structure. To help those lighter trains finish the circuit, the famous loop was removed in January 2007 and recycled for scrap.

## Closed, Then Gone

On June 16, 2009, a woman reported that she had suffered a burst blood vessel in her brain while riding Son of Beast on May 31, an injury that put her in intensive care. Kings Island closed the ride voluntarily that day, and it never reopened. By then an estimated 7 million guests had ridden it, and in March 2010 general manager Greg Scheid said the park had spent nearly $30 million on the coaster. In July 2012 Kings Island announced its removal, and on November 20 the lift hill, one of the last parts standing, came down. The steel coaster Banshee opened on the site in April 2014, near a mock graveyard holding a tombstone for Son of Beast. Its old station lived on as the Wolf Pack haunted house until 2019.

## Flight Context

Son of Beast stood at 39.3461 N, 84.2647 W inside Kings Island amusement park in Mason, Ohio, northeast of Cincinnati. From 2,500-3,500 ft AGL the park and its coaster structures are easy to spot; Banshee now stands on part of the former Son of Beast site, and the old station platform was still standing in mid-2026, when the City of Mason approved a permit to demolish it. Nearby airports: Warren County Airport/John Lane Field near Lebanon (FAA identifier I68, no ICAO code listed), about 13 km north; Butler County Regional Airport-Hogan Field (KHAO), about 22 km west; Middletown Regional Airport (KMWO), about 23 km north-northwest; Cincinnati Municipal Airport-Lunken Field (KLUK), about 30 km south-southwest; and Cincinnati/Northern Kentucky International (KCVG), about 48 km southwest. Summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are common in the Ohio Valley.
