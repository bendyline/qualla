# WLW

From a diamond-shaped tower in Mason, Ohio, Powel Crosley's WLW once broadcast at 500,000 watts, the most powerful signal ever used for regular American radio, until Washington decided that no station should be that loud.

**Location:** 39.3531, -84.3251

**Categories:** Radio stations in Cincinnati, Clear-channel radio stations, Radio stations established in 1922, Peabody Award winners, Mason, Ohio, Broadcast towers

## Introduction

On May 2, 1934, President Franklin D. Roosevelt pressed a golden telegraph key, the same one Woodrow Wilson had used to open the Panama Canal, and officially launched the 500,000-watt signal of a transmitter in Mason, Ohio. WLW, the Cincinnati station of industrialist Powel Crosley Jr., had become the first station in the world to broadcast at that strength. Neighbors reported their lights flickering in time with the broadcast, and rival stations as far away as Toronto complained. For five years WLW used the highest power ever employed in the United States for public, domestic radio, and the diamond-shaped tower that carried it still stands.

## The Henry Ford of Radio

It began with a shopping trip. On February 22, 1921, Crosley took his son to a Cincinnati store to look at radio receivers and was shocked by the price. He built his own set from parts and realized that mass production could make radios far cheaper. Through the 1920s his company was a leading maker of inexpensive sets, and Crosley was known as "the Henry Ford of radio." He experimented with transmitting too, most accounts say by playing a record of "Song of India" over and over on a 20-watt set in an upstairs billiard room and asking local amateurs to call if they heard it. His company received the randomly assigned call letters WLW on March 2, 1922, and the station made its debut broadcast at 7:15 p.m. on March 23. The publicity promised the concert would be heard from Canada to the Florida Keys.

## Always More Power

Crosley was a fanatic for the new technology, and he kept pushing the signal higher. WLW climbed to 500 watts in 1922 and 1,000 watts in 1924. In May 1925, from a remote site at Harrison, Ohio, it became one of the first two stations in the nation to transmit with the full 5,000 watts, alongside WSAI, whose transmitter stood at Mason. Crosley bought WSAI in 1928 and moved WLW's transmitter to the Mason site, where on October 29, 1928, WLW joined the select group of American stations running 50,000 watts. By then it had settled on 700 kHz, the frequency it has used ever since, and in November 1928 regulators made it a clear channel, assigning WLW exclusive use of the frequency in the United States and Canada. Crosley's advertising soon called it "The Nation's Station."

## Half a Million Watts

Fifty kilowatts reached from New York to Florida, and Crosley still was not satisfied. He spent some $500,000 on a 500-kilowatt amplifier, its buildings and a new antenna at Mason, with RCA overseeing construction of what was billed as "the largest broadcasting transmitter in the world." The machine drew about 750 kilowatts of input power, plus another 400 kilowatts for the modulator, and needed two dedicated 33-kilovolt substation lines and a large cooling pond with spray fountains. It worked, and it caused trouble. Complaints of interference arrived from across the United States and Canada, most notably from CFRB in Toronto, and in December 1934 WLW had to cut back at night until it built two shorter towers to steer its signal away from Canada. Arcing often occurred near the site. Stories spread of people hearing WLW through their mattress springs, though those reports have been assessed as possible urban legends.

## Turned Down by the Senate

The giant signal worried more than competitors. In June 1938, the U.S. Senate adopted a resolution sponsored by Burton K. Wheeler of Montana stating that no station should operate above 50 kilowatts. Wheeler, an avid anti-monopolist, wanted to protect smaller stations, and he feared that control of powerful airwaves could help a dictator rise, as Mussolini, Hitler and Stalin had each used radio. The resolution was not binding, but it shaped the outcome. In early 1939 the FCC kept clear channel stations at 50,000 watts and ended WLW's temporary authority, and on March 1, 1939, WLW returned to 50,000 watts. The great amplifier kept working at night as experimental station W8XO until the end of 1942, and by the end of World War II it could produce a million watts. Security tightened after a suspicious fire in 1940, with a 75-foot guardhouse and 12 guards patrolling around the clock.

## The Diamond on the Seal

The tower is still the landmark. Blaw-Knox designed and erected WLW's diamond-shaped antenna in 1934, the second of its type after WSM's in Nashville, and it is one of only a handful still operating in the United States. Built at 831 feet, it was later shortened to roughly 740 feet (sources give 736 to 747) to reduce nighttime fading, and today it appears on the official seal of the city of Mason. The voices it carried became part of American culture. Doris Day and Rosemary Clooney worked at WLW, as did Rod Serling, who joined as a staff writer in 1950, after graduating from Antioch College, and wrote for its radio and television shows before creating The Twilight Zone. The Boone County Jamboree showcased country performers including Grandpa Jones, Merle Travis and The Delmore Brothers. Since 1969 WLW has been the flagship station of the Cincinnati Reds, and even at 50,000 watts its signal can still span remarkable distances: in 1985 an overnight host took a call from a listener in Hawaii.

## Flight Context

WLW's transmitter site is at 39.3531°N, 84.3251°W in Mason, Ohio, about 2.8 nm west of Kings Island. The diamond-shaped Blaw-Knox tower stands roughly 740 feet tall (sources give 736 to 747), so treat it as a serious obstacle and stay at or above about 3,500 ft MSL, which still gives a clear view of its distinctive tapered silhouette. Warren County Airport/John Lane Field (FAA identifier I68) at Lebanon is about 7 nm northeast, Butler County Regional Airport (KHAO) is about 9 nm west, and Cincinnati Municipal Lunken Field (KLUK) is about 16 nm south. Cincinnati/Northern Kentucky International Airport (KCVG) lies about 24 nm southwest, so check Cincinnati-area airspace before descending. The tower is easiest to spot in clear air with the sun behind you.
