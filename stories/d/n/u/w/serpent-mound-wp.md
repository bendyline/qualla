# Serpent Mound

The largest serpent effigy in the world stretches 1,348 feet across an ancient meteorite crater in rural Ohio, its origins still debated after more than a century of archaeological investigation.

**Location:** 39.0264, -83.4308

**Categories:** archaeological-site, native-american-history, national-historic-landmark, ohio, effigy-mound

## Introduction

Somewhere between 2,300 and 900 years ago, someone decided to build a quarter-mile-long snake on a hilltop in southern Ohio. No one buried their dead inside it. No artifacts were placed within its coils. The Great Serpent Mound simply exists -- 1,348 feet of undulating earthwork, four feet high, winding along a bluff above Ohio Brush Creek in Adams County. Its tail spirals into a triple coil. Its open jaws appear to close around an oval shape that has been variously interpreted as an egg, the sun, or the body of a frog. It is the largest serpent effigy mound in the world, and after more than 170 years of study, archaeologists still argue over who built it and why.

## Written in Yellowish Clay

The mound is built from yellowish clay and ash, reinforced with a layer of rocks and soil on its outer surface. It conforms precisely to the natural contours of the bluff, curving back and forth for more than eight hundred feet, with seven distinct coils running through its body before the tail winds into its final triple spiral. The structure contains no burials, no tools, no offerings -- just shaped earth in the form of a serpent. This absence of artifacts is part of what makes dating and attributing Serpent Mound so difficult. Ephraim G. Squier and Edwin Hamilton Davis published the first surveys of the mound in 1848 in their landmark volume, Ancient Monuments of the Mississippi Valley, commissioned by the Smithsonian Institution. The U.S. Department of the Interior designated it a National Historic Landmark in 1966.

## The Argument Over Time

The builders of Serpent Mound remain the subject of a scholarly debate that has swung back and forth for decades. The mound was initially attributed to the Adena culture, which flourished in the Ohio Valley between roughly 800 BCE and 1 CE. But radiocarbon dating of charcoal samples in the 1990s yielded a date around 1070 CE, pointing instead to the Fort Ancient culture. In 2014, researchers from the Ancient Earthworks Project published new radiocarbon evidence placing construction around 300 BCE, back in the Adena period, with a renovation around 1100 CE by Fort Ancient peoples. Monaghan and Hermann refined these dates in 2019, concluding the mound was originally built 2,100 to 2,300 years ago and rebuilt about 900 years later. But archaeologist Brad Lepper has pushed back, noting that the dated material was organic sediment rather than charcoal, and that the Adena are not known to have built effigy mounds or used serpent symbolism, while the Fort Ancient culture built Ohio's Alligator Effigy Mound and frequently depicted serpents in their art.

## Crater, Comet, and Supernova

The mound sits atop an ancient astrobleme -- a meteorite impact crater where strata have been uplifted several hundred feet at the center. In 2003, geologists from Ohio State University and the University of Glasgow confirmed the impact origin of the structure, dating it to the Permian Period, roughly 248 to 286 million years ago. The crater's surface expression has been entirely erased by erosion, but the disrupted geology remains detectable underground. Some archaeologists have connected the mound's construction to celestial events: the supernova that created the Crab Nebula in 1054 CE would have been visible in daylight for two weeks, and Halley's Comet appeared in 1066 CE. In 1987, Clark and Marjorie Hardman published their finding that the serpent's head is aligned to the summer solstice sunset. A structure built on an impact crater, possibly inspired by a supernova, aligned to the solstice -- Serpent Mound occupies a rare intersection of geological violence and human intentionality.

## Rescued from the Plow

Frederic Ward Putnam of Harvard's Peabody Museum first visited the Ohio mounds in 1885 and was horrified to find plowing and development destroying them. In 1886, with funding from a group of Boston philanthropists including Mary Lee Ware, Putnam purchased 60 acres at the Serpent Mound site. He spent four years excavating the serpent and two nearby conical mounds, recovering pipes, gorgets, earspools, and projectile points from both Hopewell and Adena cultural periods. After publishing his findings, he restored the mounds to their original contours. In 1900, the land was transferred to the Ohio State Archaeological and Historical Society. National Geographic later listed Serpent Mound as a Great Wonder of the Ancient World. Today the Ohio History Connection manages the site, where a museum and walking path guide visitors around the effigy.

## Flight Context

Serpent Mound is located at 39.026N, 83.431W on a bluff above Ohio Brush Creek in Adams County, Ohio. The nearest airport is Alexander Salamon Airport (KAMT) in West Union, about 4 nautical miles away. Cincinnati Municipal Lunken Airport (KLUK) is roughly 60 nautical miles to the southwest. From the air, the serpent's sinuous shape is best appreciated at 1,000-2,000 feet AGL. Look for the cleared hilltop site along the creek bend in heavily forested terrain. The mound is 1,348 feet long but only four feet high, so lower altitudes and good lighting conditions are essential for visibility.
