# Cane Ridge Meeting House

A 1791 log meetinghouse in Bourbon County, Kentucky, where a vast 1801 camp meeting helped ignite the Second Great Awakening and a new American Christian movement was born.

**Location:** 38.2126, -84.1192

## Introduction

In August 1801, the crowds kept coming until the log church could not begin to hold them. Preachers climbed onto stumps, wagons and fallen trees and preached at the same time in different corners of the camp, and at least one speaking platform was built outside the meeting house to reach the overflow. By most estimates, between 10,000 and 20,000 people came to Cane Ridge, Kentucky, that week. The camp meeting became the largest and most famous of the Second Great Awakening, and the log building at its center still stands today, wrapped inside a stone shell near Paris, Kentucky.

## A Log Church on Cane Ridge

The Cane Ridge Meeting House was built in 1791 on Cane Ridge, near Paris in Bourbon County. It is one of the oldest church buildings in Kentucky, and it has been called the largest one-room log structure. A Presbyterian congregation met here, and at the turn of the nineteenth century its minister was Barton W. Stone. Every year the church held a Communion service, and in 1801 it decided to invite other local Presbyterian and Methodist churches to join in. The format came from old Scottish traditions, the "Holy Fairs" or communion seasons, with a large gathering of ministers, sermons, tables and successive servings of the sacrament. The response far outstripped anything the building could hold.

## A Week That Shook the Frontier

The meeting ran from August 6 to August 12 or 13, 1801. It opened with preaching on a Friday evening that carried on through Saturday, and Communion began on Sunday. As many as eighteen Presbyterian ministers took part, along with Methodist preachers; some Baptist ministers may have exhorted as well. Estimates of attendance range from 10,000 to 20,000, and some run as high as 30,000. The Encyclopedia of the Stone-Campbell Movement judges it unlikely that more than 10,000 were present at any one moment, but suggests 20,000 may have come over the course of the week, nearly ten percent of Kentucky's recorded population in 1800. Somewhere between 800 and 1,100 received Communion. The effect spread far beyond Bourbon County. Cane Ridge launched a multitude of smaller camp meetings on the frontier, and those in turn drew hundreds of thousands of men and women into a deeply personal experience of salvation.

## Simply "Christians"

Cane Ridge was the birthplace of something more lasting than a revival. In 1804, according to the site's museum, a small group of Presbyterian ministers from Kentucky and Ohio wrote and signed a document here called "The Last Will and Testament of the Springfield Presbytery." The document gave birth to a movement seeking unity among Christians along non-sectarian lines, and its members would call themselves simply "Christians." Three major branches of what became the Stone-Campbell movement, which Stone's followers later joined with Alexander Campbell's in 1832, trace their origins in part to this spot: the Christian Church (Disciples of Christ), the Churches of Christ, and the independent Christian Churches. The museum notes that the movement is often described as the first to arise on American soil. For a one-room log church on a Kentucky ridge, it is a remarkable legacy.

## A Shrine Inside a Shrine

The log church was restored in the 1930s, and a stone building was constructed around it between 1954 and 1957, so today the old church stands inside it like a relic in a reliquary. Step through the stone memorial building and you find the interior of the 1791 meeting house, where Stone preached and from which the frontier crowds once overflowed into the camp outside. The building is not only a museum piece. Cane Ridge describes itself as a living church, where visiting congregations and groups come to worship, and the site's museum tells the story of the revival and the movement that grew from it. The museum is open from April 1 to October 31 (Sundays from 1 p.m.), and in winter the meeting house is open by appointment when a curator is available.

## Flight Context

The Cane Ridge Meeting House sits at 38.213°N, 84.119°W in the rolling farmland of Bourbon County, Kentucky, east of Paris. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL, look for the small stone memorial building among fields and tree lines. Mount Sterling-Montgomery County Airport (KIOB) is about 11 nm southeast, and Blue Grass Airport in Lexington (KLEX) is about 25 nm west-southwest, so expect some traffic in the Lexington area. Cincinnati/Northern Kentucky International (KCVG) is about 56 nm north-northwest. The gently rolling terrain here makes for easy flying, though summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are common.
