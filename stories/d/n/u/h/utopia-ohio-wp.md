# Utopia, Ohio

On the north bank of the Ohio River, Utopia was founded by followers of Charles Fourier, struck by a deadly flood in 1847, and reborn as an anarchist colony that traded in hours of labor instead of dollars.

**Location:** 38.7761, -84.0572

**Categories:** Utopian communities in the United States, Anarchist intentional communities, Ohio populated places on the Ohio River, Unincorporated communities in Clermont County, Ohio, Populated places established in 1844

## Introduction

Utopia came by its name honestly. The people who founded it in 1844, on the north bank of the Ohio River in Clermont County, Ohio, were trying to build one. They were followers of Charles Fourier, whose writings inspired readers to create their own ideal societies, and they had already watched an earlier attempt, the Clermont Phalanx, fail. The dream they planted here would be tried three times over by three very different groups, and one of those attempts would end in the icy water of a December flood.

## A House for Twenty-Five Dollars

The Fourierists believed the world would enter a 35,000-year period of peace, and that the path to enlightenment ran through communal living. To fill their town they offered families a simple bargain: for a fee of $25 a year, each household received a wooden house on a small parcel of land. Several families came. The harmony did not last. Within three years the community had broken up, and the land the sect owned was sold to John O. Wattles, the leader of another group, a band of spiritualists with their own vision for the riverbank.

## The Hall That Went to the River

Wattles and his followers made a fateful decision. Ignoring the warnings of local people, they took apart the community's brick dining hall, which also served as the town hall, and rebuilt it brick by brick at the very edge of the Ohio River. The move was finished in December 1847. Only days later came one of the biggest floods of the 19th century. On the night of December 13, the Ohio burst its banks and climbed dangerously close to the hall. As water filled their houses, people sought shelter inside, and that evening they were holding a party there. Then the flooding river washed out the building's south wall. The hall collapsed and the current swept many of them into the dark water. Some of those carried away survived. Most did not, drowning or succumbing to hypothermia in the icy river. Seventeen people died in all, in a settlement reported to number only 32.

## Money Measured in Hours

In 1847 Utopia was reorganized once more, this time as an individualist anarchist colony by Josiah Warren and his associates. Nothing here was held in common. Land belonged to individuals, and lots were bought and sold at cost. Admission required a personal invitation from the first settlers, because Warren held that the most valuable liberty was "the liberty to choose our associates at all times." Labor itself became the measure of value: goods and services changed hands through labor notes, and the town had two "time stores." By the mid-1850s Utopia counted about forty buildings, roughly half of them industrial. Warren left after a year to lecture and help start other colonies, most notably Modern Times, but he returned now and then. After a visit in the winter of 1855-1856 he remarked that the "little germ of Equitable society" had given him higher hopes than he had dared to entertain.

## Smith's Landing

The experiment faded slowly. The Civil War, rising prices on surrounding land that made expansion difficult, and the rule that newcomers had to be invited are said to have brought the project to an end in 1876. Even so, several of the original occupants remained as late as 1875, when some local business was still conducted in labor notes. By then the place was known as Smith's Landing. Today Utopia is an unincorporated community in far southern Franklin Township, strung along U.S. Route 52, where an Ohio Historical Marker stands beside the highway. It has been called a ghost town, though people still live there.

## Flight Context

Utopia sits at 38.7761 N, 84.0572 W on the northern bank of the Ohio River in the southeast corner of Clermont County, Ohio, along U.S. Route 52. From 2,000-3,000 ft AGL follow the Ohio River upstream (east) from Cincinnati; the community is a thin line of buildings between the highway and the river, with the Kentucky shore across the water. Nearby airports: Brown County Airport near Georgetown (FAA identifier GEO, GPS code KGEO), about 19 km northeast; Clermont County Airport near Batavia (FAA identifier I69), about 36 km north-northwest; Cincinnati Municipal Airport-Lunken Field (KLUK), about 48 km northwest; and Cincinnati/Northern Kentucky International (KCVG), about 61 km west-northwest. River fog can form in the valley on calm, cool mornings.
