# Chillicothe, Ohio

Built where Hopewell mound-builders once traded across a continent and named for a Shawnee word meaning "principal town," Chillicothe served twice as Ohio's capital and sheltered freedom seekers on the road north.

**Location:** 39.3364, -82.9839

**Categories:** Cities in Ohio, County seats in Ohio, Former state capitals in the United States, Cities in Ross County, Ohio, Underground Railroad locations, Hopewell tradition

## Introduction

The name traveled before the town did. Chillicothe comes from the Shawnee Chalakatha, meaning "principal town," and the Shawnee applied it to the main settlement wherever that division of their people lived. Seven places in Ohio have carried it, from present-day Piqua to a site near Xenia to a spot on Paint Creek. The one that kept the name sits along the Scioto River 45 miles south of Columbus, a city of 22,059 people at the 2020 census and the seat of Ross County. It was also the first capital of the state of Ohio, and the third.

## Earthworks Older Than Memory

Long before the Shawnee, this valley was the heart of the Hopewell tradition, which flourished from around 200 BC until 500 AD. Hopewell trade routes reached as far as the Rocky Mountains, and the people raised earthen mounds for ceremony and burial throughout the Scioto and Ohio river valleys. Their work still shapes the north end of the city, where Hopewell Culture National Historical Park, run by the National Park Service, protects a large concentration of Native American earthworks. Later peoples, including the Shawnee, lived here through the time of European contact. In 1758, Shawnees who had abandoned their village of Lower Shawneetown founded a settlement on this site, joined by a group from Logstown. The Shawnee leader Tecumseh was likely born in the area. That world was broken by the Treaty of Greenville in 1795, in which the defeated Native nations ceded most of what is now Ohio.

## A Capital in the Wilderness

Settlers from Virginia and Kentucky followed the Ohio River west looking for land, and in 1796 a party led by surveyor Nathaniel Massie founded Chillicothe on his land grant. It rose fast. Ross County was organized in 1798 with Chillicothe as its seat, and in 1800 the town became capital of what remained of the Northwest Territory after Indiana Territory split away. In 1802, as Ohio moved toward statehood, Chillicothe hosted the Ohio Constitutional Convention. It served as the state capital from statehood in 1803 until 1810, and again from 1812 to 1816. Three of its leading figures are remembered as members of the Chillicothe Junto: Massie, who became the first speaker of the Ohio Senate, and Edward Tiffin and Thomas Worthington, who both went on to serve as governor.

## On the Road to Freedom

Ohio was a free state, and among Chillicothe's early migrants were free Black people who came seeking a place with fewer restrictions than the slave states. They built a vibrant community and helped people fleeing slavery. Freedom seekers crossed the Ohio River and then followed the Scioto north to put more distance between themselves and the slave hunters, and as tensions rose before the Civil War, Chillicothe's free Black community kept up stations and aid along the Underground Railroad, with help from white abolitionists. Chillicothe's list of notable names reflects that history. Joseph Carter Corbin worked as a conductor on the Underground Railroad. Lewis Woodson was an educator, minister and abolitionist. David Leroy Nickens, who had been enslaved and gained his freedom, is described as the first African-American licensed minister in Ohio.

## Twenty-One Thousand Soldiers and a Portrait

Chillicothe has a knack for spectacle. In 1918, at Camp Sherman, 21,000 soldiers stood in formation to create an image of President Woodrow Wilson. The Majestic Theatre traces its roots to the Masonic Hall of 1853, a two-story brick building that doubled as lodge room, dance hall and theater. Enlarged in 1876 as the Masonic Opera House, it was renamed the Majestic by the Myers Brothers, who bought it in 1914 and installed a movie screen, and its stage has hosted Laurel and Hardy, Milton Berle, Sophie Tucker and Bob Hope. Since 1973 the drama Tecumseh! has been staged in town, and its 2026 season runs June 4 to September 6. Since 1984 the Feast of the Flowering Moon has brought Native American music, dancing and traders to Yoctangee Park each May, drawing crowds that Ohio Magazine puts at more than 35,000.

## A Hub in the Foothills

Chillicothe lies between the Scioto River and Paint Creek, near where they meet, on the Western Allegheny Plateau. Residents describe the surrounding farm country as the foothills of the Appalachians. As the only city in Ross County, it serves as the area's economic hub, with hospitals, prisons and a branch campus of Ohio University among its largest employers. Its industrial era took a blow in 2025, when the Pixelle paper mill, known earlier under the names of past owners Glatfelter and Mead, announced in April that it would close; production ended on August 10, 2025, leaving nearly 800 workers without jobs. A 5-mile paved path runs along the floodwall that protects the city from the Scioto, and it connects to the Tri-County Triangle Trail, which follows a former railroad line through Frankfort toward Washington Court House.

## Flight Context

Chillicothe sits at 39.3364°N, 82.9839°W on the Scioto River, about 45 miles south of Columbus, where the river meets Paint Creek among low, rolling hills. From 3,000–4,000 ft MSL the Scioto and the floodwall along its bank are easy to follow, and the Hopewell Culture National Historical Park earthworks lie on the city's north side. Ross County Airport (KRZT) is about 6.5 nm north-northwest, Pickaway County Memorial Airport (KCYO) at Circleville is about 11 nm north, and Pike County Airport (KEOP) at Waverly is about 10 nm south. Rickenbacker International Airport (KLCK) near Columbus is about 29 nm north. Morning river fog can linger in the Scioto valley.
