# Indian Head Rock

An eight-ton boulder carved with a human face served for generations as the Ohio River's gauge and Portsmouth's Sunday curiosity, then vanished under the water for nearly ninety years, and when divers finally raised it, Ohio and Kentucky fought over who owned it.

**Location:** 38.7253, -82.9931

**Categories:** Ohio River, Petroglyphs in Kentucky, Sandstone formations of the United States, Interstate conflicts in the United States, History of Ohio, History of Kentucky

## Introduction

Most boulders never get a resolution passed about them. This one got two, from two different states, and each demanded something different. The Indian Head Rock is an eight-ton sandstone boulder with a human face carved on its underside, and for most of the twentieth century it lay out of sight at the bottom of the Ohio River, just off the Kentucky shore opposite Portsmouth, Ohio. When local divers pulled it from the water in September 2007, residents of both states celebrated. Within months the celebration had turned into a criminal indictment, dueling legislatures and a question nobody could quite answer: whose rock was it?

## Smooth as a Bowling Ball

The rock began as part of a landslide, one of the angular boulders that tumbled from the Kentucky hillside facing Portsmouth. Generations of river current wore its edges away. "It is now smooth as a bowling ball, made so by the motion of the water for generations," a newspaper wrote in 1908, adding that river histories mentioned the rock as early as 1811. The face gave it a name, first "the Indian Head" in nineteenth-century papers and later "Indian Rock." Who carved it remains an open question. Some have theorized it was a Native American petroglyph, others a boatman's river gauge. A 1908 account credited John Book of Portsmouth, remembered as "a very adventurous boy," who was reputed to have carved it in the 1830s and who later served as a captain in the Civil War and was killed in battle.

## Measuring the River by a Face

Whatever its origin, the face became a ruler. A local resident kept a log of the river stage against the carving, beginning on November 10, 1839, when the mouth stood "10 1/4 inches out of the water." On September 27, 1851, the eyes could be seen, the lowest reading since 1839. When the water dropped, the town went out to visit. In October 1891, skiffs gathered around the rock "in fleets," especially on Sundays. The fall of 1908 brought unusually low water, and the Portsmouth Daily Times counted more than 1,000 visitors on one Sunday and 1,500 on another, when "every gasoline launch on the river front was in commission." Visitors had been adding their own marks for years. One family story holds that in the 1890s, Judge James Bannon learned of three fresh sets of initials and concluded they belonged to his sons Henry, Arthur and Jim.

## Drowned by a Dam

The rock was a treasure to some and a hazard to others. In 1908, Portsmouth historian Henry A. Lorberg proposed hauling it out with a government snag boat and displaying it in York Park, overlooking the river, with an iron railing and its history painted on the face. The newspaper that reported his idea added a practical note: the boulder was "an undoubted menace to navigation," and in the steamboat days "the bottom of many a boat and barge was torn out by it." Lorberg's plan never happened. Instead, engineering settled the matter. Between 1875 and 1929 the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers built a series of Ohio River dams, and when Lock and Dam No. 31 was completed downstream of Portsmouth in 1917, the river rose for good. Damage to the dam in October 1920 briefly exposed the rock, and hundreds of Portsmouth residents went to see it. After that it vanished, and it was not seen again until 2007.

## Two States, One Boulder

In September 2007, a group of divers led by amateur historian Steve Shaffer of Ironton, Ohio, raised the rock, and Portsmouth officials planned to display it. Kentucky objected. The rock had rested on the Kentucky side of the river, and the University of Kentucky had registered it in 1986 as a protected archaeological object. In June 2008 a Greenup County grand jury indicted Shaffer under Kentucky's Antiquity Act, a felony carrying one to five years in prison. The Kentucky House adopted a resolution condemning the removal and urging the rock's return to the river bottom, echoing an earlier call from the Kentucky Native American Heritage Council. Ohio answered with its own resolution, backed by sixty-six co-sponsors, declaring the rock inextricably linked to Portsmouth. The case collapsed in 2009, when the prosecutor said he could not prove this was the same registered rock. "The official Indian Head Rock cannot be in several places at once," his motion read.

## Garage Limbo

A settlement in 2010 sent the boulder to Kentucky, and it arrived on November 4 of that year. It could not go back into the river, because the original site "had been compromised." So it went into a county highway maintenance garage in Greenup, where a reporter in 2015 found it "surrounded by worn-out tractor tires and crusted with four years worth of cobwebs and grit." Filmmaker Steven Middleton, a Morehead State University instructor, saw the rock in a tiny county storage shed and decided to make a film about it. His documentary, Between the Rock and the Commonwealth, has aired on public television in Kentucky and Ohio since 2017. Local officials proposed a gazebo in South Shore, Kentucky, as its permanent home, and the rock reportedly went on public display in a park there in September 2020, under a small roof and behind chain-link fencing, though some concerns remain that it should be protected in a climate-controlled building.

## Flight Context

The rock's original riverbed site is at 38.7253°N, 82.9931°W on the Ohio River, just off the Kentucky shore opposite downtown Portsmouth, Ohio; the rock was reported in 2020 to be on display in a park at South Shore, Kentucky, on the south bank. From 2,500–3,500 ft MSL, follow the Ohio River: Portsmouth spreads along the north bank, with steep Kentucky hillsides rising across the water. Greater Portsmouth Regional Airport (KPMH) is about 10 nm northeast, Ashland Regional Airport (KDWU) near Ashland, Kentucky, is about 16 nm southeast, and Tri-State Airport/Milton J. Ferguson Field (KHTS) at Huntington, West Virginia, is about 30 nm southeast. Watch for valley fog over the river on calm mornings.
