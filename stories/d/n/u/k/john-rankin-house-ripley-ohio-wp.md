# John Rankin House (Ripley, Ohio)

From a small brick house on the hill above Ripley, Presbyterian minister John Rankin and his family watched the Kentucky shore and are estimated to have helped more than 2,000 people escape slavery, one of whose crossings became the story of Eliza in Uncle Tom's Cabin.

## Introduction

Who abolished slavery? When Henry Ward Beecher was asked that question after the Civil War, he gave a startlingly local answer: "Reverend John Rankin and his sons did." The Rankins lived in a modest brick house on a hill above Ripley, Ohio, overlooking the Ohio River and, beyond it, Kentucky, a slave state. Built around 1828, the house became one of the original stops on the Underground Railroad, and Rankin is estimated to have helped more than 2,000 people secure their freedom, often at great personal risk. One of their stories, carried beyond Ripley, became part of Uncle Tom's Cabin.

## The Preacher Who Rowed Across

Rankin was born in Dandridge, Tennessee, in 1793, to a mother who was an unyielding opponent of slavery. As a young minister he spoke against slavery from the pulpit, and when his church elders suggested he might leave Tennessee if he meant to keep doing so, he left in 1817. He spent four years preaching in Carlisle, Kentucky, where he started a school for enslaved people until club-carrying mobs drove it from building to building and the students stopped coming. On the night of December 31, 1821, into New Year's Day, he rowed his family across the icy river to Ripley. Their first home stood just yards from the water on Front Street, and slave owners and hunters came to the door at all hours demanding information. Rankin decided his family needed a safer perch.

## A Light on Liberty Hill

He found it on the hill. From the house's prominent position the family could see the village, the river and the Kentucky shore. Folklore holds that a lantern or candle burned in the front window to guide people across the Ohio, though a formerly enslaved person's account recalls a light raised on a pole, which would have been easier to see from the river. Rankin also built a staircase up the hillside so that fugitives could climb to safety on their way north. The house they reached was not large: a brick structure of one and a half stories, three bays wide, with a center hall, four rooms on the ground floor and two small bedrooms in the attic. Rankin raised a big family there, nine children when they moved up the hill, of an eventual thirteen. "I have had under my roof as many as twelve fugitive slaves at a time," he wrote, "all of whom made good their way to Victoria's dominions," meaning Canada.

## The Woman on the Ice

In 1838 the Rankins sheltered a woman who had escaped by crossing the frozen Ohio River with her child in her arms. Later, visiting one of his sons at Lane Seminary in Cincinnati, Rankin told the story to Professor Calvin Stowe. Stowe's wife, Harriet Beecher Stowe, heard the account too, and she later drew on that account for the character Eliza in Uncle Tom's Cabin. Through the novel, the image of a mother crossing the ice with her child traveled far beyond Ohio. Behind the fiction stood a real woman and child, and a hilltop household that took them in. That fall, Ulysses S. Grant was a student at Rankin's Presbyterian academy for boys in Ripley; the next year he entered the U.S. Military Academy.

## Letters That Traveled

Rankin's influence reached well past his hill. Early in his Ripley years he learned that his brother Thomas, a Virginia merchant, had bought slaves, and he answered with a series of letters that the local newspaper published. Collected in 1826 as Letters on Slavery, they were among the first clearly argued anti-slavery writings printed west of the Appalachians. Thomas moved to Ohio in 1827 and freed the people he had enslaved. In 1832 William Lloyd Garrison reprinted the letters in The Liberator and later called Rankin his "anti-slavery father." Fame brought danger. The house was often watched by slave catchers, a bounty of as much as $3,000 is reported to have been placed on Rankin's life, and in 1841 he and his sons fought off attackers who came in the night to burn the house and barn. After the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850, he declared that "disobedience to the enactment is obedience to God."

## A House Kept Open

The Rankins sold the house after the Civil War, and the State of Ohio bought it in 1938 as a historic site. It still sits on about 20 acres, reached by a drive and visitors center off Rankin Hill Road. Inside, the original wide floorboards and carved fireplace surrounds survive, and a portico on square posts shelters the center door. The Ohio History Connection operates it for tours, and it was designated a National Historic Landmark in 1997. Rankin died in 1886; six years later a monument called "Freedom's Heroes" was dedicated to him and his wife, Jean Lowry Rankin, in Ripley's Maplewood Cemetery. Down on the waterfront, the John P. Parker House recalls another conductor who worked the same stretch of river.
