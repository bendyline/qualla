# Battle of Augusta (1862)

On September 27, 1862, about 150 hometown Union Home Guards fought Basil Duke's Confederate cavalry street by street in Augusta, Kentucky, and though the town burned, the fight kept Duke from crossing the Ohio River.

**Location:** 38.7703, -84.0076

**Categories:** Battles of the American Civil War in Kentucky, Confederate Heartland Offensive, Bracken County, Kentucky, 1862 in Kentucky, September 1862

## Introduction

A single row of hay was all that shielded the boiler of the Union gunboat Belfast as it sat on the Ohio River off Augusta, Kentucky, on September 27, 1862. On the heights above town sat two small Confederate mountain howitzers, nicknamed the "bull pups." What followed was a short, savage fight between Confederate cavalry and about 150 local men of the Bracken County Home Guard, a battle the Confederates won, only to abandon the very plan that had brought them there.

## The State Both Sides Needed

Kentucky sat between the Confederacy and the Union states of the Midwest, and its rivers, above all the Ohio, made it prize territory. "I think to lose Kentucky is nearly the same as to lose the whole game," Kentucky-born Abraham Lincoln wrote privately in September 1861. The state's declared neutrality collapsed that same month, when Confederate troops occupied Columbus and Union Brig. Gen. Ulysses S. Grant seized Paducah within days. In the late summer of 1862 the Confederates invaded in force. Maj. Gen. Edmund Kirby Smith captured Richmond and Lexington and threatened Cincinnati, while Bragg's Army of Mississippi marched north to join him, part of what has been called the largest simultaneous Confederate offensive of the war.

## Riding for the Ford

In northern Kentucky, Col. Basil W. Duke, brother-in-law of the raider John Hunt Morgan, led part of the Second Kentucky Cavalry on reconnaissance and small raids alongside Henry Heth's infantry. After a brush at Snow's Pond on September 25, Duke heard that a Union force was forming at Augusta, where Dr. Joshua Taylor Bradford, home after falling ill following Shiloh, was raising a new Union Kentucky infantry regiment. Augusta offered Duke something more: a ford about a mile below town. Crossing there would let him threaten Cincinnati and perhaps pull Federal troops out of northern Kentucky to defend it. He left Falmouth on September 26 with about 450 troopers and his two six-pounder howitzers.

## Gunboats and Bull Pups

Around noon, Duke's column came in from Brooksville and took the heights overlooking Augusta. Two Union gunboats waited on the river, the Florence Miller and the Belfast. The Belfast opened with three to five rounds from its twelve-pounder howitzer, hitting a Confederate gun crew, and the bull pups answered, scoring hits on both boats. Duke then sent his advance guard and Company A down to a sandbar east of town. With enemy riflemen closing in and artillery on the bluffs, the lightly protected boats steamed upriver and out of the fight. Accounts differ on which boat took the worst of it, but one of them was later found to have more than a hundred holes from Confederate small-arms fire.

## Fire in the Brick Houses

Duke expected an easy victory and sent two more companies into town. The Home Guard, ordinary citizens of Augusta, had taken positions in brick buildings, many on the second floors, and they fired down into the streets, cutting down Confederate officers. Confederates galloping in from the sandbar were hit by their own artillery on the hills, apparently mistaken for Union troops. The fighting turned hand-to-hand. Some defenders raised white flags while others kept shooting, and Duke ordered a bull pup into town to fire at point-blank range into the buildings. That shelling, together with fires some Confederates set deliberately, turned houses into traps, and some Home Guard defenders burned to death inside them. Twenty buildings were destroyed. The fight lasted perhaps sixty to ninety minutes, and ten Confederate junior officers were killed or mortally wounded.

## The Crossing That Never Came

Duke held the town, but the cost was too high to go on. With so many casualties and his artillery ammunition dangerously low, he abandoned the plan to cross the Ohio and fell back to Brooksville. He later called that night "the gloomiest and saddest that any man among us had ever known." The next morning, while Duke was paroling prisoners, another Federal force attacked, a small skirmish with only one casualty. Duke withdrew to Falmouth with about eighty prisoners, Bradford among them, and they too were later paroled. A week later, on October 4, the invasion's leaders were in Frankfort for the inauguration of a Confederate governor, a ceremony interrupted by the sound of approaching Union cannon.

## Flight Context

Augusta lies at 38.7703 N, 84.0077 W on the Kentucky bank of the Ohio River in Bracken County, upriver from Cincinnati. From 2,000-3,000 ft AGL the compact riverfront town is easy to pick out, with the heights where Duke placed his howitzers rising behind it; the community of Utopia, Ohio, sits across the river and slightly downstream. Nearby airports: Brown County Airport near Georgetown, Ohio (FAA identifier GEO, GPS code KGEO), about 17 km northeast; Fleming-Mason Airport near Flemingsburg (FAA identifier FGX, GPS code KFGX), about 34 km southeast; Cincinnati Municipal Airport-Lunken Field (KLUK), about 51 km northwest; and Cincinnati/Northern Kentucky International (KCVG), about 65 km west-northwest. Expect river fog on calm mornings.
