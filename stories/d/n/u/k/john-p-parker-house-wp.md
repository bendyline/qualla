# John P. Parker House

On the Ohio River waterfront in Ripley, a plain brick house is all that remains of the foundry complex of John P. Parker, a formerly enslaved iron moulder who bought his own freedom, patented his inventions, and crossed into Kentucky again and again to guide people out of slavery.

## Introduction

John Parker was eight years old when he was forced to walk from Norfolk, Virginia, to Richmond, where he was sold at the slave market. Decades later he lived beside the most consequential border in antebellum America: the Ohio River, with slaveholding Kentucky on one bank and the abolitionist town of Ripley, Ohio, on the other. His two-story brick house at 300 North Front Street still stands on the Ripley waterfront, a little north of the town center. It was Parker's home from 1853 until his death in 1900, the base from which a foundryman, inventor and Underground Railroad conductor helped hundreds of people escape slavery. Today it is a museum and a National Historic Landmark.

## Letters and Iron

Parker was born into slavery around 1827, the son of an enslaved mother and a white father. The man who bought him in Richmond was a physician from Mobile, Alabama, and in that household the doctor's family taught the boy to read and write, even though the law forbade educating enslaved people. He was apprenticed in a foundry, a trade that would shape the rest of his life. Freedom did not come easily. Parker tried to escape to New Orleans by riverboat, then persuaded a widow among the doctor's patients to purchase him. She let him hire out his labor, and in 1845 he bought his freedom from her for $1,800, money earned in two of Mobile's iron foundries and at whatever odd jobs he could find. He went north, first to Jeffersonville, Indiana, and then to Cincinnati, where in 1848 he married Miranda Boulden, who had been born free in that city.

## Crossing Into Kentucky

Ripley was a hotbed of abolitionist sentiment, and its position made it a well-known destination for people fleeing across the river from Kentucky. Parker did not simply wait for them to arrive. He crossed into Kentucky regularly, found people trying to escape, and brought them into Ripley's network of Underground Railroad supporters. Every trip put his own hard-won freedom at risk, all the more after the federal Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 raised the penalties for such work, and slaveholders offered a $1,000 bounty for him. He kept going for nearly fifteen years. During the Civil War he recruited a few hundred enslaved people for the Union Army. Oddly, the house itself seems to have sheltered few fugitives. Its isolated riverside setting, apart from Ripley's other homes, suited slave catchers as well as it suited the people they hunted, so Parker apparently rarely hid anyone under his own roof.

## The Works on Front Street

By trade Parker was an iron moulder, and the house was only one piece of a manufacturing complex built around 1853 that also held a machine shop, a blacksmith shop and a foundry. In 1865 he and a partner bought a foundry business they named the Ripley Foundry and Machine Company, which Parker managed; it built engines, reapers, mowers and sugar mills. He was an inventor as well. Parker patented a tobacco press and a harrow, or soil pulverizer, in the 1880s (some accounts date the pulverizer patent as late as 1890), the pulverizer an idea he had first worked out as a young man in Mobile. That made him one of the few Black Americans to patent an invention before 1900. In 1889 a fire damaged or destroyed most of the Front Street works, and Parker did not rebuild them. The brick house survived.

## A Voice Recovered

John and Miranda Parker made sure their children were educated, and several went on to college. Their daughter Hortense was among the first African-American graduates of Mount Holyoke College. Parker's own account of his life nearly disappeared. In the 1880s he sat for interviews with the journalist Frank Moody Gregg, who was researching the resistance movement, but Gregg never published the manuscript. Generations later, the historian Stuart Seely Sprague found Gregg's manuscript and notes in the Duke University archives and edited them for publication, taking care to keep Parker's own language. The memoir appeared in 1996 as His Promised Land: The Autobiography of John P. Parker, Former Slave and Conductor on the Underground Railroad, finally letting the man who had guided so many people toward freedom speak for himself.

## Brick Under Stepped Gables

After Parker died in 1900, his wife sold the property, and for many years it supported a coal shipping operation. In the 1990s a local nonprofit bought the house and rehabilitated it as a museum; the John P. Parker Historical Society, formed in 1996, restored it and runs exhibits and educational programs about Parker's life and the abolitionist movement. The building is modest: two stories of brick under a side-gabled roof with stepped gables, three bays wide, with plain sash windows beneath stone lintels and a lower wood-frame ell stretching to the rear. It was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1980 and designated a National Historic Landmark in 1997. Up the hill stands the John Rankin House, another major Underground Railroad stop, a reminder of how much of that network ran through one small river town.

## Flight Context

The John P. Parker House sits at 38.7500°N, 83.8492°W on the Ohio River waterfront in Ripley, Ohio, a little north of the town center, with Kentucky directly across the water. The house is small, so the river and the town grid are the useful references; from about 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL the waterfront streets of Ripley are easy to pick out along the Ohio bank. The John Rankin House, the town's other Underground Railroad landmark, stands on the hill about 0.4 miles to the east-northeast.
