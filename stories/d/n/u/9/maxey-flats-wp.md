# Maxey Flats

Kentucky courted the atomic age and never got a reactor; what it got instead was Maxey Flats, a plateau of leaking radioactive-waste trenches that must now be watched over in perpetuity.

**Location:** 38.2583, -83.5694

**Categories:** Superfund sites, Radioactive waste, Environmental history, Nuclear history, Kentucky

## Introduction

Kentucky was first in line for the atomic age. In 1962 it became the first of the old Atomic Energy Commission's "Agreement States," and in retrospect it seems that a whole series of new state agencies was created in hopes of landing a nuclear power plant. The plant never came. What Kentucky got instead lies on a plateau in Fleming County, about 9 miles northwest of Morehead: the Maxey Flats low-level radioactive waste disposal site, a Superfund site that will have to be monitored and maintained in perpetuity.

## Courting the Atom

The groundwork was laid quickly. As part of a program to encourage the nuclear industry, the Kentucky General Assembly created a Division of Nuclear Information. In 1960 lawmakers gave the governor power to reach an agreement with the federal government transferring regulatory authority over atomic energy to the state, and that same year Governor Bert T. Combs charged the Cabinet of Health with regulating and licensing radioactive materials. The Division of Nuclear Information gave way to a Division of Atomic Development, then to the Kentucky Atomic Energy Authority, which eventually became the Kentucky Science and Technology Commission. In 1962 the Nuclear Engineering Company, Inc., known as NECO, bought 252 acres on Maxey Flats and applied for a license to bury radioactive waste there. The license was granted in January 1963. Locals call the area "Maxey Flat"; the waste site took the name "Maxey Flats."

## The Bathtub Effect

From 1963 to 1977, Maxey Flats served as a dump site for low-level nuclear waste. Waste went into trenches, and when a trench reached capacity it was capped with shale and clay. Over time, those caps collapsed and the trenches filled with water, a problem that came to be called the "bathtub effect." The water became radioactive and had to be disposed of. An evaporator was used to get rid of the accumulated radioactive water as steam, leaving the contaminated concentrates behind in tanks. It generally ran 24 hours a day, from 1973 until April 1986, nearly nine years after the site had stopped accepting waste. By the end it had processed approximately six million gallons of liquid, though a state official has put the total removed from the trenches at about 13 million gallons. The trenches were not the only burials. In "Hot Wells," typically 10 to 15 feet deep, built of concrete, coated steel pipe or tile and capped with slabs of concrete, the operators stored special nuclear material, meaning plutonium and enriched uranium. Approximately 950 pounds of it is buried at Maxey Flats.

## What Would Not Stay Buried

Investigations by the Environmental Protection Agency, among others, found that plutonium stored at the site had migrated beyond the trenches, and the site stopped taking waste in 1977. Five years later, on September 27, 1982, the Kentucky New Era reported that Maxey Flats had been closed because of "radioactive leakage." No immediate health hazard was reported, but "the potential [was] still there." State Natural Resources Coordinator Jackie Swigart confirmed that radioactive material had escaped the burial trenches and been detected beyond the borders of the 250-acre site. The land itself may have helped it travel, since the geology of the Maxey Flats area may contribute to the spread of radioactive materials underground, beyond the site's boundaries. In 1986 the EPA designated Maxey Flats a Superfund site. The agency identified 832 parties as potentially responsible for the contamination.

## A Lid Meant to Last Forever

A study from 1987 to 1991 weighed how best to clean the site up, and extensive remediation followed. The work included an interim cap built around a 45-mil scrim-reinforced geomembrane liner to keep water from seeping in and, once the trenches had settled, a permanent earthen cap that was substantially complete in November 2016. The site is now managed by the Commonwealth of Kentucky's Energy and Environment Cabinet. It is considered non-reclaimable, and it will have to be monitored and maintained in perpetuity. A place built to welcome the nuclear future now asks only to be remembered by the people charged with guarding it.

## Flight Context

Maxey Flats disposal site: 38.2583°N, 83.5694°W, on a plateau in Fleming County, northeastern Kentucky, about 9 miles northwest of Morehead. A viewing altitude of about 3,000-3,500 ft MSL shows the plateau top where the covered trench area lies. Nearest airports: Morehead-Rowan County Clyde A. Thomas Regional (KSYM), about 3 nm south; Fleming-Mason Airport at Flemingsburg (KFGX), about 19 nm north-northwest; for a larger field, Blue Grass Airport at Lexington (KLEX) is about 51 nm west-southwest. The site is state-managed land, not a visitor attraction; view it only from a normal, legal overflight altitude.
