# Abbey of Our Lady of Gethsemani

Founded by 43 French monks who came up the Mississippi in 1848, Kentucky's Abbey of Gethsemani is the oldest operating Trappist monastery in the United States and the resting place of Thomas Merton.

**Location:** 37.6642, -85.5294

**Categories:** Trappist monasteries in the United States, Basilica churches in Kentucky, Christian monasteries established in the 19th century, Nelson County, Kentucky, Thomas Merton

## Introduction

Among the rows of uniform crosses in the cemetery at Gethsemani, one grave is often arrayed with mementos left by visitors. Its marker, in the same basic style as all the others, reads "Fr. Louis Merton, Died Dec. 10, 1968." Thomas Merton, the Trappist monk, author and social activist, lived at the Abbey of Our Lady of Gethsemani near Bardstown, Kentucky, from 1941 until his death, and he is buried as the monks here are, in his monastic habit and without a casket. The abbey around him was already nearly a century old when he arrived.

## Forty-Four Set Out

Trappists had tried Kentucky once before. In 1805 French monks from the Abbey of La Valsainte in Switzerland came to the Bardstown area at the invitation of Stephen Badin, the first Catholic priest ordained in the United States, but they left in 1809 after a season of bad flooding. In 1847 Dom Maxime, abbot of Melleray in France, sent two monks to look again, and Bishop Benedict Joseph Flaget guided them to a tract in Nelson County, owned by the Sisters of Loretto and called Gethsemani, where the earlier community had lived. On October 26, 1848, 44 monks left Melleray under Eutropius Proust. One died on the voyage. The other 43 landed at New Orleans on December 11, rode the steamboat Martha Washington up the Mississippi, and reached Gethsemani on December 21, in time to celebrate Christmas Mass a few days later.

## Proto-Abbey of the New World

Life at first was exceedingly harsh. Proust came down with pneumonia soon after arriving and recovered only after receiving the Last Rites. By 1849 the monks had torn down the old cabins and built a second chapel. Bishops in other states invited the order to move, but Proust refused them all, determined to establish one monastery before expanding. In 1851 Gethsemani was declared the "Proto-Abbey of the New World," with Proust its first abbot. It is considered the motherhouse of all Trappist and Trappistine monasteries in the United States, and it is the oldest still operating. Construction of a three-story monastery modeled on Melleray began in October 1852 to plans by architect William Keely, with brick walls made on site, a foundation of Indiana limestone and a Neo-Gothic abbatial church crowned by a large steeple. Many local people helped the monks build it, some of them enslaved people whose labor was not theirs to offer; Proust promised the locals free schooling for their children in return. The church was consecrated on November 15, 1866.

## Soldiers at the Gate

Proust resigned in 1859 and returned to France, leaving 65 monks. His successor, Benedict Berger, was installed as abbot just after the Civil War began in April 1861. Berger kept his monks on an austere footing, and many locals viewed them as a strange and unhappy lot, yet he was remembered for his kindness to soldiers of both sides, offering nourishment, horses or distilled spirits. He sympathized with the Union, and his monks enjoyed the protection of Union troops in the area. The decades that followed were lean. By 1878 the abbey had roughly as many monks as at its founding, none of them American, and it leased land to local farmers to avoid closing. A fire destroyed its grain mill in 1884. The next year brought its first lifelong American monk, a former cowboy from Texas.

## Writers in the Enclosure

In the twentieth century Gethsemani became known far beyond Kentucky through its writers. Thomas Merton wrote on the spirituality of the Cistercians, while fellow monk Raymond Flanagan aimed his books at a more popular audience, and both were widely published and translated. The Nicaraguan poet Ernesto Cardenal entered the abbey under Merton's supervision. The monastery later opened itself to another kind of conversation. In July 1996 it hosted the first Gethsemani Encounter, a dialogue between Catholic and Buddhist monastics on prayer, meditation, work and monastic life, attended by the 14th Dalai Lama, who called such discussions important in paving the way for future dialogue. A second encounter followed in April 2002.

## Fruitcake, Fudge and Silence

The monks still follow the Rule of Saint Benedict, a contemplative life of prayer and work on a working farm of 2,000 acres. They support themselves through Gethsemani Farms, selling handmade fruitcake and bourbon fudge on site and by mail order. The community numbers roughly 40 monks by recent accounts (the abbey publishes no current count), and becoming one of them takes years: six months as a postulant, two as a novice and three as a junior professed monk before solemn vows. Silence is still encouraged. A 30-room retreat house welcomes both men and women, speaking is allowed only in designated areas, and guests may wander the trails, woodlands and fields. On weekdays Mass follows Lauds, which the abbey's site lists at 5:45 a.m., and from the cemetery on its buttressed embankment, the crosses look out over the valley and woods below.

## Flight Context

The Abbey of Our Lady of Gethsemani sits at 37.6642°N, 85.5294°W in the rolling countryside of Nelson County, Kentucky, south of Bardstown. From 2,000-2,500 feet AGL, look for the monastery complex and church steeple set among fields and woodland on the abbey's 2,000-acre farm. Samuels Field at Bardstown (KBRY) is about 9 nm north, Lebanon-Springfield Airport (FAA identifier 6I2) is about 14 nm east, Addington Field/Elizabethtown Regional Airport (KEKX) is about 19 nm west, and Louisville Muhammad Ali International (KSDF) is about 32 nm north-northwest. Please keep noise to a minimum over this contemplative community; morning valley fog is common in spring and autumn.
