# Camp Nelson National Cemetery

Above the Kentucky River south of Lexington, a Civil War camp's graveyard became a national cemetery where 837 headstones mark the graves of African American soldiers of the United States Colored Troops.

**Location:** 37.7860, -84.6012

**Categories:** United States national cemeteries, American Civil War cemeteries, African-American history of the United States military, Military history of African Americans in the American Civil War, Kentucky in the American Civil War

## Introduction

Hundreds of headstones at Camp Nelson carry four letters above the name: USCT, for United States Colored Troops. Of the 2,452 burials here that date to the Civil War, 837 are known to be the graves of African American soldiers. The cemetery grew out of a Union camp in southern Jessamine County, Kentucky, a camp that became the state's largest recruiting ground for Black soldiers and, for their families, a place of both refuge and cruelty. The rows of stones remember men who put on the Union blue before the law had freed their own wives and children.

## A Depot Named for a Fallen General

Camp Nelson was established in 1863 as a supply depot for Union advances into Tennessee. It took its name from Major General William "Bull" Nelson, commander of the Army of Kentucky, who had been murdered by a fellow officer in 1862. A busy army camp meant a busy hospital, and the first graveyard lay near it; 379 people were buried there between June 1863 and July 1865. A second plot, designated "Graveyard No. 2," was later chosen, and it remains the core of the national cemetery today. The federal government designated the ground a National Cemetery in 1866, while many of the war's dead still lay in battlefield graves across the state.

## Soldiers, and the Families Who Followed

More than 23,000 of the roughly 200,000 men of the U.S. Colored Troops were recruited and trained in Kentucky, making it one of the largest contributors of any state, and Camp Nelson was its largest recruiting center, with more than 10,000 men serving in regiments organized here. Many were enslaved men; in May 1864, for example, a group of nearly 250, most of them enslaved, marched from Boyle County to enlist here. Their wives and children followed them to the camp, and the army did not always protect them. Beginning on November 22 or 23, 1864, Brig. Gen. Speed S. Fry, the camp commandant, ordered soldiers to force more than 400 women and children onto wagons, at times under threat of being shot, and drive them out of the camp. Their huts were destroyed behind them. It was intensely cold, and at least 102 of the 250 who made their way back to the camp died in the following weeks and months; the fate of about 150 others is unknown. Fry's order was quickly rescinded, the army soon authorized a Home for Refugees at the camp, and on March 3, 1865, Congress passed an act freeing the wives and children of U.S. Colored Troops.

## Gathering the Dead

After the war, Camp Nelson became a place to bring the fallen home. A stone wall was built around the cemetery in 1867 and 1868. In June and July of 1868, the remains of 2,023 Union soldiers were recovered from battlefield graves in Frankfort, Richmond, Perryville, London and Covington, carried to Camp Nelson and reinterred with honor. The work was not flawless. In the 1990s, genealogical evidence reached the cemetery director suggesting that two soldiers recovered from Covington had been Confederates, mistakenly assumed to be Union men. Exhuming 130-year-old graves was judged impractical, so their headstones were simply replaced with Confederate stones. Among the other graves is that of William M. Harris, a private in Company D of the 7th U.S. Cavalry, awarded the Medal of Honor for his actions at the Battle of the Little Big Horn.

## A Superintendent's House and a Riderless Horse

In the 1870s a two-story brick house with an open entry porch rose near the stone wall as a home for the cemetery's superintendent, designed in the Second Empire style by Quartermaster General Montgomery C. Meigs. Since 1995 it has served as the main office and visitor center. The older part of the cemetery covers about 30 acres and holds more than 12,000 interments, 21 more acres were bought in 2010 for future graves, and it joined the National Register of Historic Places in 1998. Each Memorial Day, the ceremony gathers at the flagpole near the front gates; in past years it has featured a keynote speaker, a local high school band, a bugler sounding Taps, and a salute fired from a replica 19th-century cannon by Civil War re-enactors. Honor guards in period uniform have included members of the re-enacted 54th Massachusetts Infantry, the all-Black regiment later depicted in the 1989 film Glory. The ceremony has at times included a horse-drawn caisson bearing an empty, flag-draped coffin, followed by a soldier leading a riderless horse. The site of the camp itself was proclaimed Camp Nelson National Monument on October 26, 2018.

## Flight Context

Camp Nelson National Cemetery lies at approximately 37.786°N, 84.601°W in southern Jessamine County, Kentucky, about 18 miles south of Lexington along U.S. Route 27, just north of where the highway bridges the Kentucky River. From 1,500–2,500 ft AGL, follow U.S. 27 south from Nicholasville toward the river; the walled cemetery with its regular rows of headstones sits beside the highway before the bridge. Blue Grass Airport in Lexington (KLEX) is about 15 nm to the north. Central Kentucky weather is generally flyable, but expect summer haze and convective storms, and watch for fog in the Kentucky River valley on cool mornings.
