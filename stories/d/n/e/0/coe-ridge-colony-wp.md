# Coe Ridge Colony

Founded in 1866 by a formerly enslaved couple on Cumberland County ridge land, variously reported as 300 to 1,500 acres, Coe Ridge became a rare multiracial refuge whose story survives almost entirely through spoken memory.

**Location:** 36.6780, -85.4680

**Categories:** African-American history of Kentucky, History of slavery in Kentucky, Cumberland County, Kentucky, Oral history, Former settlements

## Introduction

"Pig oooey, pig oooey." It sounded like a hog call. On Coe Ridge, in Cumberland County, Kentucky, it was a warning that the law was on its way. The call belonged to the colony's last decades, when its timber and chestnut income had disappeared and moonshining and bootlegging took their place, helping to bring the community to an end. But the story of Coe Ridge begins in 1866, with a formerly enslaved couple buying land from the man who had once claimed to own them, and building something almost nobody else in their world had managed: a place where people of different races lived side by side.

## Three Hundred Acres for Six Hundred Dollars

The Emancipation Proclamation of January 1863 declared enslaved people free in the areas in rebellion, but it did not reach Union-held Kentucky, where slavery ended only with the ratification of the Thirteenth Amendment in December 1865. Freedom did not come with a way to make a living. Many freed people sharecropped for their former enslavers, others left to found their own settlements, and some bought land from the plantations where they had been forced to labor.

Ezekiel Coe, who sometimes went by Zeke, had been enslaved by the Coe family. He was of white, African American and Native American ancestry. He married Patsy Ann, a formerly enslaved woman from the same plantation, and after freedom came the couple reclaimed their children, ten by the most common count. In the best-known account, Jesse Coe sold Ezekiel and Patsy 300 acres of ridge land for six hundred dollars, though the Kentucky Historical Society's marker puts the colony at about 1,500 acres, and in 1866 sixteen people began turning that wilderness into a working community.

## A Refuge on the Ridge

Coe Ridge became a refuge for African Americans, Native Americans and disenfranchised white women seeking shelter from the persecutions of society, and above all for freed people who needed a place beyond the reach of their oppressors. In neighboring areas the colony was known as a gathering place for "misfits" and "outcasts," outsiders' labels that did not stop residents from building a thriving community.

The ridge was isolated physically, socially and culturally. For nearly a century the Cumberland River was the main way in and out. Its racial mix, found almost nowhere else at the time, gave it a distinct culture of its own. Timber and extensive chestnut orchards supplied food as well as extra income.

## Trouble From Over the Hill

Racial tension was a constant problem, and it grew worst during the colony's middle years, from 1885 to 1910. Residents placed the blame for the feuds on the neighboring white communities, and the oral histories suggest that in most instances that blame was warranted. Young white men from nearby would get drunk and ride their horses through the colony, tearing down fences and damaging property.

In 1888, a massive fight broke out between members of the colony and their white neighbors. After the death of a prominent white man, threats from neighboring communities grew so severe that residents protected themselves by leaving their homes in the middle of the night. Then nature dealt another blow: a chestnut blight in the early 1900s dried up the orchards that had fed the colony and helped pay its way.

## Remembered Out Loud

As its legitimate resources dried up, the colony turned to moonshining and bootlegging, and those trades became major factors in its decline. By the 1930s, the physical and cultural isolation had nearly disappeared as neighboring communities interacted more with the ridge. By 1958 Coe Ridge was desolate, completely abandoned. Only three houses were reported still standing there, and today little remains but a cemetery with more than a hundred unmarked graves.

No newspapers or court records chronicled daily life here, and nearly all the residents were unable to read or write, so no diaries survive. The history of Coe Ridge was carried by the spoken word, with help from neighbors who had known the colony. The scholar William Lynwood Montell preserved those memories through interviews in The Saga of Coe Ridge: A Study in Oral History, published in 1970. In an era of lynching, the Ku Klux Klan and legalized segregation, Coe Ridge showed that people of different races could build a life together.

## Flight Context

Coe Ridge lies at approximately 36.68°N, 85.47°W in Cumberland County, Kentucky, in hill country near the Cumberland River, which for nearly a century was the colony's main connection to the outside world. Treat it as a place to view from the air; 2,500–3,500 feet AGL gives a sense of the ridges and the river valley. Tompkinsville-Monroe County Airport (FAA identifier TZV, ICAO KTZV) is about 9 nm to the west-northwest, and Livingston Municipal in Tennessee (FAA identifier 8A3) about 18 nm to the south-southeast. Glasgow Municipal (KGLW) is about 32 nm to the northwest. Best in clear weather.
