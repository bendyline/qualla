# Ephraim McDowell House

In this Danville, Kentucky, house on Christmas morning 1809, Dr. Ephraim McDowell removed an ovarian tumor of about 22 pounds without anesthesia, and his patient, Jane Todd Crawford, lived another 32 years.

**Location:** 37.6450, -84.7711

**Categories:** National Historic Landmarks in Kentucky, Historic house museums in Kentucky, Medical museums in the United States, History of medicine, Houses completed in 1795

## Introduction

On Christmas morning in 1809, in his own house in Danville, Kentucky, Dr. Ephraim McDowell removed an ovarian tumor weighing about 22 pounds (accounts range from 20 to 22.5) from Jane Todd Crawford. He worked without anesthetic and without antisepsis. Crawford had ridden some 60 miles on horseback from Green County to reach him. She recovered without complications, went home 25 days later and lived another 32 years. It is usually described as the first successful removal of an ovarian tumor in the world, and the house where it happened, once nearly lost to neglect, is now a National Historic Landmark.

## A Frontier Surgeon with an Edinburgh Education

Ephraim McDowell was born on November 11, 1771, in Rockbridge County, Virginia, and moved with his family to Danville, Kentucky, in 1784. He studied medicine under Dr. Alexander Humphreys in Staunton, Virginia, then crossed the Atlantic to attend medical lectures at the University of Edinburgh in Scotland from 1793 to 1794, studying privately with John Bell. In 1795 he came home, settled in Danville and began practicing as a surgeon. His patients included a teenage James K. Polk, the future president, for whom McDowell removed a urinary stone and repaired a hernia. McDowell died on June 25, 1830, and he has since been called 'the father of ovariotomy' and a founding father of abdominal surgery.

## Christmas Morning, 1809

On December 13, 1809, McDowell diagnosed an ovarian tumor in Jane Todd Crawford of Green County, Kentucky. Nothing like the operation she needed had ever been done successfully. She agreed to try, and made the long ride to Danville. On Christmas morning, in McDowell's house, she underwent the surgery fully conscious. Her endurance was as remarkable as his skill: she faced an untested operation with nothing to dull the pain, and survived it. Her recovery was uncomplicated. Twenty-five days later she returned home, and she lived for 32 more years. In 1965 the house was declared a National Historic Landmark for its role as the site of the world's first ovariotomy.

## A House Built in Three Stages

The house grew over nearly three decades. A brick ell was built between 1792 and 1795, before McDowell owned it. He bought the property in late 1802, added the clapboard front portion in 1803 and 1804, and in 1820 built the small brick office to the left of the back porch, remodeling the house at the same time. After McDowell's death in 1830 the house was sold. For a short time it was the home of a president of Centre College. Then the neighborhood around it declined into slum and tenement property, and the house itself deteriorated badly.

## Rescued from the Ash Heap

Dr. August Schachner of Louisville led the effort to save it. When he visited in 1921, he found that the house had 'continued its downward course until it has reached a point where it now seems almost beyond redemption.' McDowell's original office had become a shoeshine stand, and the upstairs room that had served as the operating room was being used as a dump for ashes from the grates. In 1935 the Kentucky Medical Association bought the house and deeded it to the state, which had it restored by the Works Progress Administration. It was dedicated on May 20, 1939, and in 1948 the state returned it to the medical association. The Kentucky Pharmaceutical Society later restored the Apothecary Shop with help from the Eli Lilly Foundation; furnished by Pfizer Laboratories, it was dedicated on August 14, 1959.
