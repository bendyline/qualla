# Battle of Perryville

In the drought-parched Chaplin Hills of Kentucky, a fight that began over a few algae-covered pools of water became the battle that decided which side would hold the state.

**Location:** 37.6752, -84.9711

**Categories:** American Civil War, Battles of the American Civil War in Kentucky, Confederate Heartland Offensive, Boyle County, Kentucky, Battlefields

## Introduction

The first shots at Perryville were fired over pond scum. Early on October 8, 1862, men of the 10th Indiana edged toward the dry bed of Doctor's Creek, where a few algae-covered pools still held water, and ran into the forward soldiers of the 7th Arkansas. A stagnant high-pressure ridge had baked the South all summer, shrinking most of the creeks west of town to isolated puddles. By dark, the Battle of Perryville, also known as the Battle of Chaplin Hills, had become the culmination of the Confederate invasion of Kentucky, a clash sometimes called simply the Battle for Kentucky.

## Nearly to Lose the Whole Game

In September 1861, Kentucky-born Abraham Lincoln wrote in a private letter, "I think to lose Kentucky is nearly to lose the whole game." The border state sat between North and South and controlled key rivers, particularly the Ohio.

In 1862 the Confederacy came for it in earnest. Maj. Gen. Edmund Kirby Smith pushed for an invasion, hoping for supplies, recruits and Kentucky itself, and Gen. Braxton Bragg moved 30,000 infantrymen on a tortuous rail journey from Tupelo, Mississippi, through Mobile and Montgomery to Chattanooga to join the effort. Their partnership frayed almost at once, as Kirby Smith broke their plan to strike out on his own. On October 4, Bragg was in Frankfort for the inauguration of a Confederate governor, Richard Hawes, a ceremony disrupted by cannon fire from an approaching Union division. Organizers canceled the inaugural ball.

## Six Roads and a Dry Creek

Maj. Gen. William J. Hardee chose Perryville for practical reasons. The village of roughly 300 residents had roads running to nearby towns in six directions, it blocked the Union path to the Confederate supply depot at Bryantsville, and it offered a chance at water. Bragg's Army of Mississippi counted about 16,800 men. Maj. Gen. Don Carlos Buell was coming with some 55,000, many of them so green that his second-in-command, George H. Thomas, described them as "in every way unfit for active operations against a disciplined foe."

Buell, thrown from his horse and unable to ride, set up headquarters at the Dorsey house, about three miles west of town. Then the landscape played a trick on him: because of an unusual acoustic shadow, few sounds of battle reached his headquarters only two miles from the fighting. When artillery sounded around 2 p.m., Buell was at dinner with his III Corps commander, Charles Gilbert; assuming it was Union gunners practicing, they sent word to Phil Sheridan not to waste gunpowder.

## Fire on the Open Knob

The main blow fell on the Union left. Around 2 p.m., Brig. Gen. Daniel Donelson's brigade climbed the bluffs above the Chaplin River as Maj. Gen. Benjamin Cheatham shouted, "Give 'em hell, boys!" In one of the war's enduring legends, Gen. Leonidas Polk, who was also an Episcopal bishop, seconded him: "Give it to 'em boys; give 'em what General Cheatham says!" Instead of an open flank, the brigade struck the center of the Union position. The 16th Tennessee lost 219 of its 370 men.

Brig. Gen. George Maney's brigade came next, slipping through the woods toward Parsons' eight guns on the Open Knob, crewed by inexperienced soldiers. Union division commander James Jackson was killed, and William Terrill sent the raw 123rd Illinois, 770 men, on a bayonet charge down the hill into 1,800 veterans. The knob fell, and Lt. Charles Parsons had to be dragged from his guns by his retreating men. Beyond it, the 21st Wisconsin, formed less than a month earlier, waited in corn standing 10 to 12 feet tall. Some of its soldiers had never fired their weapons.

## High Water on a Stone Wall

A 1,400-musket volley shattered the Wisconsin regiment. The Confederate tide then broke against Col. John Starkweather's brigade, posted with artillery behind a stone wall at the top of a steep slope. The Confederates attacked three times and failed. Historian Kenneth W. Noe calls that final repulse the "high-water mark of the Confederacy in the western theater, no less important than the Angle at Gettysburg."

Elsewhere the fighting turned cruel. Confederate shells set fire to Squire Henry Bottom's log barn, where wounded Union soldiers had taken refuge, and many burned to death. Near the Dixville Crossroads, Polk rode forward to investigate what he believed was friendly fire and found himself among the 22nd Indiana. He bluffed his way out by pretending to be a Union officer, then ordered a volley that killed Col. Squire Keith and left the regiment with 65 percent casualties, the highest of any Federal regiment at Perryville. The night before, Jackson, Terrill and Col. George Webster had idly discussed the odds of all three being killed and dismissed them as negligible. The battle took all three.

## Rain After the Retreat

Casualties reached about one-fifth of those involved: roughly 4,200 to 4,300 Union and about 3,400 Confederate, including some 850 to 900 Union and 510 to 530 Confederate killed, since sources differ. Bragg had arguably won a tactical victory, pushing his opponent back more than a mile, but once he learned how much of Buell's army stood nearby, he ordered a withdrawal after midnight and left 900 wounded men behind. He retreated through the Cumberland Gap, and the Union held Kentucky for the rest of the war. Two days after the battle, a cold front broke the drought with rain and cool air.

Today, Perryville Battlefield State Historic Site preserves part of the ground, and the downtown historic district includes the Elmwood mansion, pressed into use as a field hospital. The American Battlefield Trust and its partners had saved about 1,202 acres of the battlefield by late 2021.

## Flight Context

The battlefield lies at about 37.675 N, 84.971 W in the rolling Chaplin Hills just west and northwest of the small town of Perryville in Boyle County, Kentucky. From 2,500 to 3,500 feet AGL, look for the town's road crossroads and the winding bend of the Chaplin River; the open fields and wooded ridgelines of the state historic site sit on the high ground west of the river. Stuart Powell Field in Danville (KDVK) is about 11 nm to the east-southeast, Lebanon-Springfield Airport (FAA 6I2) about 13 nm to the west-southwest, and Blue Grass Airport in Lexington (KLEX) roughly 28 nm to the northeast. The terrain is gentle farmland with no significant obstacles, though low morning haze and fog can settle in the river valleys in autumn.
