# Constitution Square Historic Site

On a three-acre square in Danville, Kentucky, ten conventions argued a frontier district toward statehood, a debating club kept a weary Army paymaster awake past midnight, and the first post office west of the Alleghenies still stands.

**Location:** 37.6453, -84.7708

**Categories:** Kentucky State Historic Sites, Open-air museums in Kentucky, National Register of Historic Places in Danville, Kentucky, Early American history, Museums in Boyle County, Kentucky

## Introduction

In the years before Kentucky became a state, a U.S. Army paymaster named Major Beatty tried to get a night's sleep at Grayson's Tavern in Danville and failed. He and his companions, he grumbled in his journal, were "very much disturbed by a Political Club which met in the next house where we slept and kept us awake till 12 or 1 o'clock." The late-night talkers belonged to a debating society whose members would go on to fill Kentucky's governorship, its courts and seats in Congress. The ground where they argued is now Constitution Square Historic Site, a three-acre park and open-air museum that remembers how a district of Virginia talked itself into becoming a state.

## A Courthouse on the Frontier

The story starts with Fort Harrod, built in 1774 by pioneers led by James Harrod and remembered as the first permanent settlement in Kentucky. Three of those settlers, Thomas Harrod, John Crow and James Brown, soon claimed land around present-day Danville, and the town square was laid out near the eastern end of the plot rather than at its center, as was usual at the time. When the District of Kentucky and its Supreme Court were created in 1783, Harrodsburg served as the first seat of justice, but its facilities fell short. The court moved to Crow's Station and ordered a courthouse built there, and it was completed on the square in March 1785. Between 1785 and 1792, that courthouse housed ten constitutional conventions, the long negotiation that finally separated Kentucky from Virginia. A log jail stood nearby, its walls specified as hewn logs at least nine inches thick. So did a meetinghouse where the first two Presbyterian ministers in Kentucky were ordained on November 10, 1785.

## Minutes in a Desk Drawer

The Danville Political Club existed only from 1786 to 1790, and it often gathered at Grayson's Tavern, built by Benjamin Grayson around 1785. Its roster reads like a founding generation: James Brown, Kentucky's first Secretary of State and later a U.S. Senator from Louisiana; Christopher Greenup, the state's third governor; judges Harry Innes, later a federal judge, and Samuel McDowell, one of Kentucky's first district court judges; U.S. Senator John Brown; and Thomas Todd, who rose to the Supreme Court of the United States. For generations, almost no one knew the club had existed. Then Thomas Speed II found the extensive minutes his grandfather, the club's secretary, had kept and tucked away in a desk drawer. The younger Speed published them with his own commentary, observing that "full and complete biographies of some of the members would present a history of Kentucky from the beginning of its settlement past the first quarter of the ensuing century." Beatty's irritated journal entry is one of the few other records that the club ever met.

## Seminary, Hospital, Shrine

Statehood in 1792 dissolved the district court. In 1817 the town's trustees sold half of the square to raise money for a school, Danville Academy, which was apparently never established; its funds went to Centre College. A brick courthouse became the main building of Danville Theological Seminary, which moved to the square in 1854, and during the Civil War it served as a hospital for wounded Union and Confederate soldiers after the Battle of Perryville. The seminary closed in 1878, and the building was later razed. The square became part of Kentucky's state park system in 1937, and when it was dedicated in 1942 it bore the name John G. Weisiger Memorial State Park, honoring the brother of Emma Weisiger, who donated the land. The Danville Chamber of Commerce and the local American Legion chapter built replicas of the jail, courthouse and meetinghouse, and the replica courthouse now holds personal items belonging to Isaac Shelby, Kentucky's first governor. One building here is no replica. The post office, built sometime before 1792, was the first U.S. post office west of the Allegheny Mountains; Thomas Barbee was commissioned as its first postmaster on August 20, 1792. It was moved to the square from Walnut Street in 1950.

## The March to Camp Nelson

Not every story told on the square concerns statesmen. A Kentucky Historical Society marker placed in February 2013 remembers the African Americans who enlisted in the Union Army during the Civil War. On May 23, 1864, a group of nearly 250 men, most of them enslaved, marched from Boyle County to Camp Nelson in Jessamine County, where Union Colonel Andrew Clark at first refused them because no policy allowed enlisting enslaved men; they were ultimately accepted, and the episode led to a change in Union policy. Most of them were still held in slavery when they chose to walk out of Danville and put on a Union uniform, and the marker makes sure that choice is part of the square's record. That same presence is honored each year at the Soul of Second Street, a celebration of the African American community around the park.

## Brass Bands and Stump Speeches

The square has never really stopped hosting public argument and public joy. Since 1987, when the Danville Advocate-Messenger sponsored the first Rally on the Square, candidates for statewide office have come here every four years in April to make their case ahead of the May primary. Since 1990, the Great American Brass Band Festival has come to town each June, with the square serving as one of its outdoor stages. The site passed from the state to Boyle County on March 6, 2012, and took its present name. A reconstructed Grayson's Tavern now stands in the park, and on a festival evening it seems a fitting neighbor: this is still a place where people stay up late talking.

## Flight Context

Constitution Square sits in downtown Danville, Kentucky, at approximately 37.645°N, 84.771°W, in central Kentucky's Boyle County, south-southwest of Lexington. The three-acre park is small from the air; orbit at 1,500–2,500 ft AGL over downtown Danville and look for a green block of lawn and small historic buildings in the street grid.
