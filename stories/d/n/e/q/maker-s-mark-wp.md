# Maker's Mark

In the small town of Loretto, Kentucky, a bourbon chosen by baking bread and named, labeled and wax-sealed by Margie Samuels grew into one of the most recognizable bottles in American whisky.

**Location:** 37.6478, -85.3489

**Categories:** Bourbon whiskey, Distilleries, National Historic Landmarks in Kentucky, Cuisine of Kentucky, Kentucky Bourbon Trail

## Introduction

The recipe was settled in an oven, not a still. While Bill Samuels Sr. was planning a new bourbon, the story goes, he worked up seven candidate mash bills and had no time to distill and age every one of them. So he baked instead, turning each grain recipe into a loaf of bread, and the loaf judged best was the one made without rye. That wheated bourbon still comes out of a distillery in Loretto, Kentucky, bottled at 90 proof in squarish bottles whose necks drip with red wax.

## A $35,000 Gamble in Loretto

On October 1, 1953, T. William "Bill" Samuels Sr. bought the Burks' Distillery in Loretto for $35,000. Production began in 1954, and the first run was bottled in 1958. It was his wife, Marjorie "Margie" Samuels, who gave the whiskey its identity: she named it, drew its label, and thought up the wax dipping that makes the bottle recognizable across a crowded bar. Accounts date its arrival on the market to 1958 or 1959. The old distillery buildings carried their own weight of history. They were listed on the National Register of Historic Places on December 31, 1974, and on January 16, 1980, they were designated a National Historic Landmark under the name "Burks' Distillery" — described as the first distillery in America recognized while its landmark buildings were still in active production. Today the grounds are open for tours and form a stop on both the American Whiskey Trail and the Kentucky Bourbon Trail.

## Wheat Where the Rye Should Be

Most bourbons lean on rye for spice. Maker's Mark leaves it out entirely, using a mash bill of 70 percent corn, 16 percent red winter wheat and 14 percent malted barley. Samuels did not arrive at that formula alone. He reportedly received considerable help and recipes from Pappy Van Winkle, owner of the Stitzel-Weller distillery, whose own wheated bourbons included Old Fitzgerald and W. L. Weller. The whisky ages for around six years and is bottled when the company's tasters agree it is ready. Along the way, barrels are rotated from the upper floors of the aging warehouses to the lower ones, a practice few distillers bother with. The upper floors swing through the widest temperature changes of the year, so moving the barrels evens out the aging and keeps the bourbon consistent from barrel to barrel. Even the spelling is a small declaration: along with George Dickel and Old Forester, Maker's Mark is one of a handful of American-made whiskies to use the Scottish "whisky" rather than the usual American "whiskey."

## Eight Days in February

Maker's Mark was sold to Hiram Walker & Sons in 1981 and, after several changes of corporate ownership, ended up with Beam. On February 9, 2013, the company emailed its customers with a plan to lower the whiskey from 90 proof to 84, citing supply issues; the change would have stretched inventory by about six percent. The distillery's own tasting panel of employees reported no difference in flavor, and industry analysts agreed the change would be subtle, since most drinkers mix their bourbon or pour it over ice. Customers did not see it that way. The backlash was strong enough that on February 17 the company announced it had reconsidered and would keep bottling at the original strength, although some overseas markets, Australia among them, continue to receive the whiskey at 40 percent. In January 2014 Beam Inc. announced its sale to Suntory, and executives promised that the product's taste would not change. The parent company became Suntory Global Spirits in May 2024.

## Red Wax, Green Wax

The core bottle has grown a family. Maker's 46 is finished with seared French oak staves slipped into the charred white oak barrel late in its aging, and is bottled at 47 percent alcohol. A mint julep version, marked by green wax on the neck, appears seasonally in limited amounts. A Cask Strength bourbon, released in 2014 and at first sold mainly through the distillery gift shop, is bottled at barrel proof, between about 107 and 114 proof (roughly 53.5 to 57 percent) depending on the batch; it reached global travel retail in July 2016. The wax has also become a canvas for horse racing and college sports. Keeneland bottles, begun in 1997, bore a white label with a dark green horse and green wax, and later editions honored Derby winners including Secretariat, Seattle Slew and American Pharoah. University of Kentucky bottles have run since 1993, with signing parties held at Keeneland, where fans can have a bottle signed by the honoree and a member of the Samuels family. Critics have been kind, too: the flagship bourbon took a gold medal at the 2010 San Francisco World Spirits Competition, and Jane MacQuitty of The Times of London praised "the softness and smoothness of its rich oak, vanilla and raisiny-like flavours."

## Flight Context

The Maker's Mark distillery sits at approximately 37.648°N, 85.349°W, just outside the small town of Loretto in Marion County, central Kentucky, in rolling farm country southeast of Bardstown. From 2,000–3,000 ft AGL, look for a compact campus of distillery buildings and long aging warehouses set among fields and woodlots. Nearest airports: Lebanon-Springfield Airport (FAA identifier 6I2; no ICAO code), about 10 km to the east; Samuels Field in Bardstown (KBRY), about 23 km to the northwest; and Louisville Muhammad Ali International (KSDF), about 67 km to the north-northwest. Summer haze is common over the Kentucky countryside, so the site is easiest to pick out on clear spring and autumn days.
