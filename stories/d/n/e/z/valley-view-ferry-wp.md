# Valley View Ferry

Chartered in 1785, seven years before Kentucky became a state, this free Kentucky River crossing once carried Daniel Boone, Henry Clay and Ulysses S. Grant and still carries hundreds of cars a day.

## Introduction

The Valley View Ferry is older than Kentucky. Its story begins in 1780, when John Craig acquired the land, and its franchise dates to 1785, seven years before Kentucky's admission to the Union in 1792; it has been billed as the commonwealth's oldest continually operating business, and a 2025 public-radio report called it possibly the oldest continuously operating ferry in the U.S. Its purpose is as simple as it was then: getting travelers across the Kentucky River. On Kentucky Route 169, in rural central Kentucky, the ferry links auto traffic between the county seats of Richmond in Madison County and Nicholasville in Jessamine County, and on toward Lexington. Daniel Boone was once a passenger. These days, hundreds of cars a day make the crossing, and none of them pays a fare.

## A Perpetual Franchise

The ferry's story begins with a soldier's reward. John Craig, a Virginia veteran of the Revolutionary War, acquired land in the area in 1780 through a military warrant. Five years later, in 1785, the Virginia General Assembly granted Craig "a perpetual and irrevocable" franchise to operate a ferry here. The language was generous, and it proved durable. Over the decades the ferry carried a remarkable list of passengers, among them Daniel Boone, Henry Clay, James Mason and Ulysses S. Grant. Each of them made this same crossing of the Kentucky River, watching the far bank draw closer, as travelers still do more than two centuries later.

## Seven Families, Then the Public

For more than 200 years the ferry was a private business, passing through the hands of seven successive families. That long run ended in 1991, when the Lexington-Fayette Urban County Government and Madison and Jessamine counties bought it jointly for $60,000. Today the ferry is free, funded by the Kentucky Transportation Cabinet together with the fiscal courts of Madison and Jessamine counties and the Lexington-Fayette Urban County Government. A 2025 news report put its traffic at roughly 14,000 to 17,000 drivers a month, proof that a ferry chartered in the 18th century still has real work to do.

## Tugs, Barges and a Movie Cameo

The boats have changed even if the crossing has not. A tug named the John Craig, honoring the franchise's first owner, dates to 1996. The vessel it replaced was heavily damaged after it sank under the weight of a heavy snowfall, and further damaged by the salvage effort that followed. Another vessel, the John Craig II, cost $700,000 to build, paid for with $600,000 in federal grants and $100,000 in local funding. In January 2023 it was hauled out for major rework and repairs. "The internals and mechanics are all new," one of its captains said afterward. "The paint job is fresh and the people are waiting." By June 2023, talk had turned to a new barge. Late in 2024 the ferry was out of service for about three months for repairs and a Coast Guard hull inspection, during which crews replaced about 75 percent of the port side of its barge hull. The ferry has even had its moment on screen: it appears in background scenes of The Flim-Flam Man, Irvin Kershner's 1967 film.

## Flight Context

Coordinates: 37.847N, 84.436W, on the Kentucky River where Kentucky Route 169 crosses between Jessamine and Madison counties, southeast of Lexington. The ferry itself is small, so look for the road descending to the riverbank on both sides and the landings facing each other across the water; 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL gives a good view of the river and the crossing. Nearby airports: Blue Grass Airport at Lexington (KLEX), about 14 nm northwest, with scheduled service; and Central Kentucky Regional at Richmond (FAA: RGA), about 14 nm south-southeast. River valleys here can hold morning fog, so plan for later in the day.
