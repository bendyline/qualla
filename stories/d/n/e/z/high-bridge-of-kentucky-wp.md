# High Bridge of Kentucky

A railroad bridge about 300 feet above the Kentucky River Palisades, begun by John Roebling, finished as America's first major cantilever bridge, and rebuilt around itself while the trains kept running.

**Location:** 37.8168, -84.7200

**Categories:** Bridges completed in 1877, Buildings and structures in Jessamine County, Kentucky, Buildings and structures in Mercer County, Kentucky, Historic American Engineering Record in Kentucky, Historic Civil Engineering Landmarks, Railroad bridges in Kentucky, Bridges over the Kentucky River, 1877 establishments in Kentucky, Norfolk Southern Railway bridges, Continuous truss bridges in the United States

## Introduction

For eighteen years, a pair of stone towers stood above the Kentucky River waiting for a bridge. John A. Roebling, the engineer who would later design the Brooklyn Bridge, had been hired in 1851 to span the gorge with a railroad suspension bridge. By 1855 the railroad company had run out of money, leaving only the towers and anchorages behind. When work finally resumed, the bridge that rose here was something new: recognized as the first major cantilever bridge built in the United States, riding 275 feet above the river when it opened, and about 300 feet after its 1911 rebuild, between Jessamine and Mercer counties. It still carries trains today.

## Roebling's Unfinished Towers

The first attempt belonged to the Lexington & Danville Railroad. With Julius Adams as chief engineer, the company retained Roebling in 1851 to build a railroad suspension bridge across the Kentucky River for a line linking Lexington and Danville, at a crossing west of the confluence of the Dix and Kentucky rivers. It was an ambitious plan for a deep gorge in the Kentucky River Palisades, and it stalled. In 1855 the company ran out of money, having completed only the stone towers and anchorages. The towers were left standing over the river, a monument to a bridge that did not yet exist.

## A Cantilever First

Rescue came from another railroad. In 1873 the Cincinnati Southern Railway revived the project, following a proposal by C. Shaler Smith for an innovative cantilever design that used Roebling's stone towers as anchors. The bridge was built as a three-span continuous under-deck truss and opened in 1877. It stood 275 feet tall and ran 1,125 feet long, making it the tallest bridge above a navigable waterway in North America and, at its completion, the tallest railroad bridge in the world; it held the North American height record until 1888. Its construction consumed 3,654,280 pounds of iron at a total cost of $404,373.31. The formal dedication in 1879 drew President Rutherford B. Hayes and Gen. William Tecumseh Sherman to the bridge.

## A Painter's River

The bridge and its setting attracted more than engineers. Around 1908, the High Bridge area was a favorite destination for Kentucky artist Paul Sawyier. He made many friends among the people who lived nearby, and the landscape here became the subject of many of his best-known paintings. The river gave him something more personal as well. It was during her own travels to the Kentucky River area that he met Mary Thomas Bull, known as Mayme, the love of his life. In 1908 she became the subject of one of his paintings, Portrait of Mayme Bull in River Landscape, which sets her within a river landscape.

## Rebuilt Without Stopping

Years of heavy railroad use took their toll, and in 1911 engineer Gustav Lindenthal rebuilt the bridge in a remarkable way. Rather than tear it down, he reinforced the foundations and constructed the new bridge around the original structure. The goal was to keep the trains running: during construction the track deck was raised by roughly 30 feet, to about 300 feet above the river, and a temporary trestle was built so traffic could keep flowing. In 1929 a second set of tracks was added to handle growing traffic, and Roebling's old stone towers were removed at some point in these rebuilds (accounts date it to 1911 or 1929). Today Norfolk Southern Railway runs trains across the bridge between Lexington and Danville, and the structure is a National Historic Civil Engineering Landmark. In 2005, state and county officials reopened a nearby park, closed since the mid-1960s, with a restored open-air dance pavilion first used in the 19th century, a playground, a picnic area and a viewing platform that looks out over the bridge and river from the top of the palisades.

## Flight Context

Coordinates: 37.817N, 84.720W, where the railroad crosses the Kentucky River between Jessamine and Mercer counties. The bridge deck sits about 300 feet above the water (275 feet before the 1911 rebuild) in the steep-walled Kentucky River Palisades, and the confluence of the Dix and Kentucky rivers lies upstream of the bridge, a good visual marker. A viewing altitude of 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL lets you look down into the gorge and see the truss span clearly; keep in mind the ground falls away sharply at the river. Nearby airports: Blue Grass Airport at Lexington (KLEX), about 14 nm north-northeast, with scheduled service; Stuart Powell Field at Danville (FAA: DVK), about 15 nm south; and Central Kentucky Regional at Richmond (FAA: RGA), about 22 nm east-southeast. Morning fog often settles in river gorges, so midday light is usually best.
