# Massacre at Ywahoo Falls

A story of Cherokee women and children killed at Yahoo Falls in 1810 has circulated in southeast Kentucky for decades, but no contemporary record supports it, and federal officials removed an unofficial monument to it.

**Location:** 36.7690, -84.5106

**Categories:** History of Kentucky, Native American history of Kentucky, 19th-century Cherokee history, Daniel Boone National Forest, Legends

## Introduction

On August 12, 2006, persons unknown set up a monument at Yahoo Falls in southeast Kentucky, on land now part of the Big South Fork National River and Recreation Area and once part of the Daniel Boone National Forest. It commemorated a massacre said to have happened at Yahoo Falls on August 10, 1810, when a number of Cherokee women and children were purportedly killed by European Americans. A little over a year later, in late September 2007, federal officials removed it. A spokesperson explained that it was illegal to put up a monument on federal land without permission, and added a second reason: officials questioned whether the massacre had taken place at all. No documentary or other evidence supports the account.

## The Story as It Is Told

According to the tale, the Reverend Gideon Blackburn proposed to open a school on Cherokee land some 125 miles away, so that the women and children of the Cumberland River valley could receive what the story calls a 'white-man's education'; the story places the school in the Sequatchie Valley. On the appointed day, everyone seeking the school's protection was to gather at Yahoo Falls at the full moon. They were to be led by 'Cornblossom,' described as a daughter of the war chief Doublehead. Instead, the story says, soldiers sent by John Sevier of Tennessee fell on the group and massacred them. Tradition also connects a Cherokee chief called Arun or Aaron Brock Redbird to the events in some way.

## Looking for the Record

Those who have searched for evidence have come up empty. Journalists have found no contemporary records documenting the massacre, and none that mention a 'Princess Cornblossom' - a title the Cherokee did not use. Even Chief Redbird's existence is uncertain. The authors of the book Rock Art of Kentucky found no mention of him in several 19th-century Kentucky histories and wrote to the Kentucky Historical Society, which replied in 1969 that its own marker describes him as a legendary Cherokee. 'There is much legend in the area,' the society wrote, 'but very little of any specific nature and no reliable dates are available.'

## How a Story Travels

The account traces to an unpublished manuscript by Dan Troxell, titled 'The Great Cherokee Children Massacre at Ywahoo Falls,' a copy of which is held by the Kentucky Historical Society. Troxell said the account came from Cherokee oral history. From there it moved into print. Robert F. Collins's A History of the Daniel Boone National Forest 1770-1970, published by the Forest Service in 1975, referred to the massacre, and Collins noted that he based his Yahoo Falls section on Troxell's unpublished manuscript. The 1999 guidebook Hiking the Big South Fork mentioned the purported event too, citing Troxell as its source. The unofficial 2006 monument stood beside the grave of Jacob Troxell, who died on October 10, 1810. Today the falls lie quietly within Big South Fork National River and Recreation Area. The story endures in print and in local tradition, but it remains what the evidence allows it to be: an alleged event, told and retold, that no contemporary document confirms.

## Flight Context

Yahoo Falls lies at approximately 36.7690 N, 84.5106 W, in the Big South Fork National River and Recreation Area of southeast Kentucky (land once part of the Daniel Boone National Forest), in rugged, forested terrain about 10 nm north of the Tennessee line. From 2,500-3,500 ft MSL the forest canopy dominates the view; the falls themselves are small and hard to pick out from the air. Nearest airports: McCreary County Airport, Pine Knot (FAA identifier 18I; no ICAO code listed), about 7 nm southeast; Lake Cumberland Regional Airport, Somerset (KSME), about 18 nm north-northwest; and London-Corbin Airport/Magee Field (KLOZ), about 28 nm northeast. Terrain rises and falls sharply here, so keep a safe margin above the ridges; morning valley fog is common.
