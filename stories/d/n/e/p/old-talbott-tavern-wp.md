# Old Talbott Tavern

Traditionally dated to 1779, before Bardstown itself was settled, the Old Talbott Tavern has hosted frontiersmen, future presidents, an exiled French prince and, if the stories are true, a few guests who never left.

**Location:** 37.8089, -85.4675

**Categories:** Historic taverns, Bardstown, Kentucky, Reportedly haunted locations in Kentucky, Bed and breakfasts in Kentucky, National Register of Historic Places in Kentucky

## Introduction

According to tradition, the Old Talbott Tavern in Bardstown, Kentucky, has never closed since it opened in 1779 (other accounts say the 1780s). According to rumor, some of its guests have never checked out. Each of its rooms is named after a famous or infamous visitor, several of them said to haunt it, and the most famous ghost of all is the outlaw Jesse James. Set the legends aside, and the stone building across from the Nelson County Courthouse still has a claim rarer than any ghost story: it is likely the oldest surviving building in the state.

## Older Than the Town

By the traditional dating the tavern was built in 1779, a year before settlement began at Salem, the town later renamed Bardstown. An old map of Bardstown shows the lot was originally bought by a man named Hynes, and the tavern was first known as the Hynes Hotel. It stood near the end of the stagecoach road that once led east to Philadelphia and Virginia, hence its reputation as the "oldest western stagecoach stop" still in operation.

The original tavern is the present eastern section, with stone walls two feet thick, heavy ceiling timbers and two separate fireplaces for cooking. Upstairs were just two guest rooms, one for men and one for women, since individual rooms for guests did not become widespread in the United States until the early 19th century. The brick western section was added a century later.

## A Frontier Guest Book

The tavern's guest list reads like a history lesson. George Rogers Clark used the tavern as a resource base near the end of the American Revolutionary War, and Daniel Boone stayed here. On October 17, 1797, the exiled Louis-Philippe of France, later king, stayed at the tavern, and a member of his entourage painted murals on the walls that were rediscovered in the 20th century.

In the 19th century came three future presidents: Andrew Jackson, William Henry Harrison and Abraham Lincoln. Lincoln's parents stayed here too, when a court ruling went against them, a setback that led the family to move to Indiana when Abraham was only seven. Henry Clay, steamboat inventor John Fitch, naturalist John James Audubon and songwriter Stephen Foster all visited as well. As late as 1926, Queen Marie of Romania stopped in for lunch.

## Bullet Holes and Sorrow

Not every visitor was so refined. Jesse James is said to have put the bullet holes in the murals, shooting drunkenly at the birds painted in a tree. Legend holds that he still haunts the inn, alongside a mysterious woman who is said to continue her own haunting of the hotel.

Some of the building's history is simply sad. George Talbott bought the tavern in 1886, and, by one account, six of his children died there within two years. The grief behind that sentence is easy to pass over in a place that trades on ghost stories, but it belonged to a real family.

The spooky reputation has served the tavern well. A writer for Travel and Leisure magazine praised its "slightly spooky charm," it has been featured on Food Network and Travel Channel, and it was once ranked the 13th most haunted inn in the United States.

## Fire and the Tradition

On March 7, 1998, fire struck the tavern, severely damaging the roof and the second floor. The Louis-Philippe murals were damaged too, and they have not been restored. The repairs that followed were described as "generic," and the tavern reopened on November 8, 1999. Since it had to reopen, the tradition that it has never closed is best taken as exactly that: a tradition.

Today the Old Talbott Tavern is both a restaurant and a bed and breakfast with six named guest rooms by its own count, next door to the historic Nelson County Jail and within the Bardstown Historic District. It was placed on the National Register of Historic Places on October 30, 1973. Travelers still come for a meal and a bed, as they did when the stagecoaches rolled in from the east.
