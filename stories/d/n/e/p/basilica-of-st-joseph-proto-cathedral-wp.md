# Basilica of St. Joseph Proto-Cathedral

Bardstown's St. Joseph was the first Catholic cathedral west of the Allegheny Mountains, a frontier church filled with gifts from a pope and European kings, and the scene of a daring 1952 art theft.

**Location:** 37.8108, -85.4722

**Categories:** Catholic cathedrals, Basilica churches in Kentucky, Bardstown, Kentucky, Art theft, African-American history of Kentucky, National Register of Historic Places in Kentucky

## Introduction

In the middle of the night on November 12, 1952, thieves entered St. Joseph's church in Bardstown, Kentucky, and cut nine paintings out of their frames. Some of the church's paintings had come to this small Kentucky town more than a century earlier as gifts from a pope and European kings. It took the FBI until April 1953 to find the stolen canvases, four rolled up in the trunk of a car in Chicago and five in the hands of a New York buyer, and years more before they hung in the church again. The theft is only one chapter in the story of a building that was the first Catholic cathedral west of the Allegheny Mountains.

## A Cathedral for the Frontier

On April 8, 1808, the Holy See divided the original Diocese of Baltimore, creating new dioceses in Boston, New York, Philadelphia and Bardstown, while Baltimore itself became an archdiocese. Bardstown was the first inland diocese in the United States, and its first bishop was Benedict Joseph Flaget.

The cathedral's cornerstone was laid on July 16, 1816. The architect and builder was John Rogers of Baltimore, and the materials came from the immediate area. By 1819 the building was far enough along for Mass to be celebrated inside, and the interior was fully complete by 1823. Far from the eastern seaboard, Bardstown now had a true cathedral, built from local materials and led by its first bishop.

## Gifts, Thieves and the FBI

Many of the paintings and interior decorations were donated by Pope Leo XII, King Louis-Philippe of France and others. The French king's gift included paintings by Murillo and Van Dyck. King Francis I of the Two Sicilies sent The Martyrdom of St. Bartholomew by Mattia Preti.

The nine works stolen in 1952 were The Flaying of St. Bartholomew, The Coronation, The Descent of the Holy Ghost, St. Anne, The Blessed Virgin and four Stations of the Cross. The FBI recovered all of them in April 1953, and the paintings were in good condition. Twelve men were arrested in connection with the robbery, but only one was ever sentenced, and his conviction was later overturned for lack of evidence. The paintings came home in 1957 and were rehung in the church in 1958.

## The First Cathedral

In 1841 the seat of the diocese moved to Louisville, and St. Louis Church there, now the Cathedral of the Assumption, became the new cathedral. St. Joseph's became a proto-cathedral, the original cathedral of a see that has since moved. The Diocese of Louisville was elevated to an archdiocese in 1937.

The old cathedral has collected honors since. It was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on January 9, 1974, and in 1976 the listing grew to include the neighboring Spalding Hall and Flaget Hall, originally St. Joseph College. Over time, the location of the cornerstone was forgotten, until workers uncovered it during a restoration in 1980. In 1995 the Holy See honored the proto-cathedral by naming retired Bishop Charles G. Maloney the Titular Bishop of Bardstown. Then, on July 18, 2001, Pope John Paul II elevated the church to a minor basilica, and an umbraculum was installed at the altar.

## A Son of the Parish

One of the most famous people connected with this church is Daniel Rudd. He was born into slavery on August 7, 1854, on Anatok Plantation in Bardstown, the son of enslaved parents Robert and Elizabeth Rudd. He and all eleven of his siblings were baptized in the Catholic Church.

Rudd grew into a journalist who believed the press could advance the cause of Black Americans and persuade Catholic, business and civic leaders. In 1885 he started the Ohio Tribune, later renamed the American Catholic Tribune, which became the first Black-owned and -operated national Catholic newspaper. After moving it to Cincinnati, he published articles speaking out against segregation and discrimination. His philosophy fit in one line: "The Catholic Church alone can break the color line. Our people should help her to do it." A child enslaved in Bardstown had become a national voice for civil rights within his church.
