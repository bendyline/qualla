# Bardstown, Kentucky

One of Kentucky's oldest towns, Bardstown was once the Catholic seat of the whole trans-Appalachian frontier and now calls itself the Bourbon Capital of the World, with a tavern, a proto-cathedral and My Old Kentucky Home among its landmarks.

**Location:** 37.8153, -85.4628

**Categories:** Cities in Kentucky, County seats in Kentucky, Bourbon whiskey, Catholic history in the United States, Nelson County, Kentucky

## Introduction

On April 8, 1808, a small Kentucky town became the Catholic seat of the entire trans-Appalachian frontier. The newly created Diocese of Bardstown was carved from the Diocese of Baltimore to serve every Catholic between the Appalachians and the Mississippi River, making Bardstown the first center of Catholicism west of the mountains. The bishop's seat moved to Louisville in 1841, but the Basilica of Saint Joseph Proto-Cathedral still stands in town. Today Bardstown, the Nelson County seat with 13,567 residents in 2020, is better known for another kind of spirit: the phrase "Bourbon Capital of the World" is trademarked for it alone, first registered in 2009 by the Kentucky Bourbon Festival's organizers and now listed under the City of Bardstown.

## The Bard Brothers' Town

First settled in 1780, Bardstown is among the oldest cities in Kentucky. It takes its name from the pioneering Bard brothers. David Bard obtained a 1,000-acre land grant in 1785 in what was then Jefferson County, Virginia, and William Bard surveyed and platted the town. It was named county seat of the newly created Nelson County, Virginia, now Kentucky, in 1784, formally established in 1788 and incorporated by the state assembly in 1838. The Old Talbott Tavern dates to those earliest years and has been called the "oldest western stagecoach stop" still in operation. Frontiersman Daniel Boone and a future president, Abraham Lincoln, were among its patrons. Bullet holes in an upstairs wall are reputed to have been fired by Jesse James, and rumor has long held that the tavern is haunted.

## Cathedral on the Frontier

Bardstown's Catholic roots run deep. Until 1808, a single all-encompassing Diocese of Baltimore, established in 1789, had served the whole country. The new diocese created here reflected the westward migration of Americans over the mountains after the Revolutionary War, and for more than three decades this town was its center, until the seat of the diocese was transferred to Louisville in 1841. The Basilica of Saint Joseph Proto-Cathedral remains the town's great church. Two Catholic schools, St. Joseph School and Bethlehem High School, have been operating since 1819 and are now under the Archdiocese of Louisville; Bethlehem remains the city's Roman Catholic parochial high school.

## Bourbon Capital

Bourbon whiskey production is a major industry here. Bourbon makers with Bardstown operations include Heaven Hill (headquartered here, with its Bernheim distillery in Louisville), Barton 1792, Willett and Lux Row Distillers, and the Oscar Getz Museum of Bourbon History (formerly the Museum of Whiskey History) celebrates the town's long history in the trade. Each year the regional craft is celebrated at the Kentucky Bourbon Festival, billed as the state's official bourbon festival since 2000. The town also serves as a gateway to the Kentucky Bourbon Trail, one of the attractions Travel + Leisure cited in early 2023 when it ranked Bardstown eighth among the 20 most beautiful small towns in America, along with its Federal and Georgian architecture and cobblestone paths.

## My Old Kentucky Home

In Bardstown, Judge John Rowan and his wife, Ann Lytle Rowan, commissioned a large mansion called Federal Hill, now the heart of My Old Kentucky Home State Park. There The Stephen Foster Story, an outdoor musical about the composer of "My Old Kentucky Home," has run since 1959 and is billed as Kentucky's official outdoor musical. Another house, Wickland, has been home to three governors, two of Kentucky and one of Louisiana. Charles A. Wickliffe, the 14th governor of Kentucky and a U.S. postmaster general, built it; his son Robert C. Wickliffe, later governor of Louisiana, and his grandson J. C. W. Beckham, Kentucky's 35th governor, were both born there. In Courthouse Square a memorial to steamboat inventor John Fitch includes a replica of his first steamboat, and the town's Civil War Museum is billed as the fourth-largest in the country.

## Harder Histories

Not every Bardstown landmark was built on freedom. Anatok, a now-demolished slave plantation built by Charles Haydon, was the birthplace of Daniel Arthur Rudd, who became an early civil rights activist and a Black Catholic journalist. Other notable people with ties to the town include William Pope Duval, first civilian governor of the Florida Territory; Ephraim H. Foster, a U.S. senator from Tennessee; Lucy Ann Kidd-Key, president of North Texas Female College; journalist Marie Mattingly Meloney; and retired Lieutenant General Hal Moore, co-author of We Were Soldiers Once... And Young. The Bardstown Historic District is listed on the National Register of Historic Places, and the city's streets, still anchored by U.S. routes 31E, 62 and 150, keep more than two centuries of those stories close together.

## Flight Context

Bardstown lies at 37.8153°N, 85.4628°W in north-central Nelson County, Kentucky, where U.S. routes 31E, 62 and 150 meet; the Bluegrass Parkway passes just to the south and southeast. From 2,500-3,500 feet AGL, look for the compact historic grid around the courthouse square and the distillery buildings around town. Samuels Field (KBRY) is only about 2 nm west, Lebanon-Springfield Airport (FAA identifier 6I2) is about 15 nm southeast, and Louisville Muhammad Ali International (KSDF) is about 25 nm north-northwest, so remain mindful of Louisville's airspace. Summers are hot and humid, with haze and afternoon thunderstorms.
