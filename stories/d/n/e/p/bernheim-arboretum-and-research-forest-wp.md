# Bernheim Arboretum and Research Forest

Bought for a dollar an acre after miners stripped it for iron ore, Isaac Bernheim's forest of more than 16,000 acres south of Louisville became Kentucky's official arboretum, home to a celebrated holly collection, a living-roofed visitor center and returning beavers.

**Location:** 37.9170, -85.6675

**Categories:** Arboreta in Kentucky, Botanical gardens in Kentucky, Nature centers in Kentucky, Research forests, Bullitt County, Kentucky

## Introduction

Isaac Wolfe Bernheim paid a dollar an acre. The land he bought in 1928 in Clermont, Kentucky, about 25 miles south of Louisville, came cheap because most of it had been stripped for mining iron ore. What he made of it is now Bernheim Forest and Arboretum, an arboretum, forest and nature preserve of more than 16,000 acres given in trust to the people of Kentucky and described as the largest privately owned natural area in the state. Bernheim, his wife, daughter and son-in-law are buried in the forest he gave away.

## A Whiskey Fortune Turned to Trees

Bernheim was a German immigrant whose whiskey distillery business established the I.W. Harper brand. He founded the forest in 1929, and in 1931 the Frederick Law Olmsted landscape architecture firm began designing it. Following the firm's recommendations, roads, paths and natural areas were laid out, trees were planted, and old farmland was restored to meadow, lawn and forest, with lakes and ponds added for "an enlivening effect." The arboretum plan was adopted in 1935, and the forest opened to the public in 1950. At the Bernheim gravesite, a bronze by George Grey Barnard shows a woman with uplifted arms, flanked by granite pylons representing the Jewish and Christian religions. Its plaque asks that "tolerance and fairness guide our relationship with our fellow men."

## A Letter for the Future

On August 30, 1939, Bernheim set out his hopes in a letter to the trustees of his foundation. There would be "no discussion of religion or politics, no trading or trafficking," and, in the language of his day, "no distinction will be shown between rich or poor, white or colored." He imagined an art gallery of bronze busts of honored Kentuckians, a museum holding every classified wild animal of the hemisphere, and a tall flagpole for patriotic gatherings of children. Above all he extended an invitation to everyone to "re-create their lives in the enjoyment of nature" in a park he hoped "will be kept for ever free." Admission is still a suggested contribution of $15 per car for non-members, and members enter free. He knew his plans might change, writing that "nothing is static in this world" and that "the grave has no voice," and indeed the wildlife museum he envisioned once existed but has since been rearranged into an administrative and education center.

## Seven Hundred Hollies

The 600-acre arboretum holds more than 1,900 labeled species and cultivars of trees, shrubs and other plants, and in 1994 the legislature designated it Kentucky's official arboretum. Its pride is the Buddy Hubbuch Holly Collection, among the best in North America, named for Clarence E. "Buddy" Hubbuch Jr., Bernheim's first horticulture director, who oversaw its planting over his 33-year tenure from 1962 to 1995. It contains more than 700 specimens representing over 350 species and cultivars from across the Northern Hemisphere, among them 176 cultivars of American holly. Maples, crab apples, conifers, oaks, buckeyes, ginkgoes and dogwoods fill other collections. On Lake Nevin, the six-sided redwood Garden Pavilion, designed in 1961, sits at the center of gardens laid out after the cross-section of a clematis stem.

## Towers, Trails and Giants

More than 40 miles of hiking trails wind through the property, most of them through the natural area's beech-maple forest, including the 13.3-mile Millennium Trail, described as the longest and most challenging. The Canopy Tree Walk extends off a cliff to place visitors above the treetops, and a short loop leads to a 48-foot fire lookout built in 1929, staffed until 1980 and listed by the National Historical Lookout Register in 1998. The 2005 visitor center, designed by William McDonough + Partners as a "building like a tree," is built of recycled materials under a living roof and earned LEED Platinum certification; its parking lot was placed so mushrooms could absorb contaminants from cars before they reached nearby streams. In 2019 the Danish artist Thomas Dambo added three "forest giants" made only of recycled wood, and they have drawn many new visitors.

## The Beavers Come Home

Restoration here is not only for show. In a $500,000 project funded mostly by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, University of Louisville engineers of the Stream Institute designed the reshaping of a 4,000-foot corridor of Wilson Creek, returning a meandering course to a channel once straightened for farming. Native trees and wildflowers were planted, and fish populations increased. By about 2011 beavers, which the forest had begun encouraging in 2008, had returned, and their dams have removed sediment and cleared the water, while their ponds have drawn birds, amphibians, reptiles, raccoons and river otters. In the Great Meadow, native long grass supports a quail program meant to repopulate the region. On land that was once stripped for ore, the forest Bernheim imagined keeps growing back.

## Flight Context

Bernheim Forest and Arboretum centers on 37.9170°N, 85.6675°W at Clermont in Bullitt County, Kentucky, about 25 miles south of Louisville. From 2,500-3,500 feet AGL, look for a large unbroken block of forest with open meadows and the arboretum's lakes. Samuels Field at Bardstown (KBRY) is about 10 nm southeast, Godman Army Airfield at Fort Knox (KFTK) is about 14 nm west, and Louisville Muhammad Ali International (KSDF) is about 16 nm north-northwest, so stay alert for Louisville's airspace and Fort Knox restricted areas. The forest hosts its ColorFest each fall; summer brings haze and thunderstorms.
