# Pleasant Hill, Kentucky

In Kentucky's Bluegrass, a celibate Shaker community built stone dwellings, forty miles of walls and one of the state's first water systems, then faded away, leaving a village that now bills itself as home to the country's largest private collection of original 19th-century buildings.

## Introduction

The Meeting House floor at Pleasant Hill was built to take a pounding. Shaker worship involved dancing and movement, and the wooden boards were made to withstand the dancing of several hundred brethren and sisters. Years after the community dissolved, the building became an automotive garage, and the same floor proved strong enough to carry cars driven onto its surface. It is a fitting afterlife for a place whose builders are often mistaken for technophobes. Shaker Village of Pleasant Hill, known locally as Shakertown, sits about 25 miles southwest of Lexington in Kentucky's Bluegrass region, a National Historic Landmark District where a religious community lived, farmed and invented from 1805 to 1910.

## A Thousand Miles on Foot

The Shakers came to Kentucky chasing a religious fire. The Second Great Awakening had filled the Ohio Valley with camp meetings, where preachers held forth for hours and music and dancing lifted crowds that sometimes numbered in the thousands. Lucy Wright, head of the Shakers' parent Ministry at New Lebanon, New York, decided to send missionaries west. On January 1, 1805, John Meacham, Benjamin Seth Youngs and Issachar Bates set out, traveling more than a thousand miles, mostly on foot, alongside the pioneers pouring west through the Cumberland Gap and along the Ohio River.

By August they had gathered new followers of Mother Ann Lee, the English founder whose sect taught that God was both male and female. Believers did not marry. Instead they lived communally as a family of brothers and sisters, and couples who joined lived apart, while young children and foundlings were raised in a nursery. On reaching maturity, those children could decide for themselves whether to stay.

## A Covenant on Elisha Thomas's Farm

Many of the early converts had been stirred by the fervent Cane Ridge Revival. In December 1806, forty-four converts of legal age signed a covenant of mutual support and common ownership of property. They began living together on the 140-acre farm of Elisha Thomas, and more converts and land followed quickly, until the community held more than 4,000 acres. By 1812 three communal families, East, Center and West, had formed, with a fourth, North, serving as a "gathering family" for newcomers still deciding whether to commit. On June 2, 1814, 128 Believers bound themselves in a more formal covenant modeled on the Ministry's own village back at New Lebanon.

## Brooms, Preserves and Forty Miles of Stone

The Shakers started out poor, but they were skilled farmers, and even skeptics could see them prosper. In 1852 a visitor reported that every article of Shaker produce sold for a third more than other farmers received. Their flat brooms outsold "ordinary" ones, they turned fruit into more than ten tons of preserves in a single year, and they sold garden seeds. By 1816 they were carrying their goods down the rivers to markets as far away as New Orleans.

The land itself shows their ambition. A visitor in 1857 learned they had paid one hired man for twelve years to do nothing but build stone walls, and he had finished forty miles of them at about $1,000 a mile. In the 1850s they kept about 500 head of cattle, bred imported cows for better milk, and raised Saxony sheep whose wool the sisters spun and wove. One observer, dubious about their worship, still called them a "trafficking, humane, honest and thrifty people."

## Horsepower in the Wash House

Visitors who see the plain clothing and handmade furniture often assume the Shakers, like the Amish, rejected new technology. The opposite was true. By 1825, Pleasant Hill kitchens had pumps, at a time when many farmwives still carried water from a creek. In the early 1830s the community built a water tower on high ground, where a horse-drawn pump lifted water that pipes then carried to kitchens, cellars and wash houses. It is believed to have been the first such system in the state. Horses also powered the washing machines the members built to ease the heavy work of laundry, and their mill had an elevator to raise grain to the upper floor.

Music mattered as much as machinery. Men and women both wrote the community's songs, hymns and anthems, and one of the best known, "Gentle Words," was written by Polly M. Rupe in the 1860s.

## Listening to That Silence

After the Civil War, few new converts came. Orphans the Shakers took in rarely stayed past their indentures, and so-called "Winter Shakers," impoverished locals who feigned interest during the cold months, seldom earned their keep. A village that once had almost five hundred members had fewer than half that by 1875, despite reported new arrivals from Sweden. In 1900 only 34 remained, and the community dissolved in 1910. The last Believer, Mary Settles, lived until 1923, long enough to see women's suffrage, and she noted that Shaker sisters had always had equal rights within their society.

The Trappist monk Thomas Merton, from the nearby Abbey of Gethsemani, first visited in 1959 and wrote of "the empty fields, the big trees—how I would love to explore those houses and listen to that silence." Two years later, in 1961, Lexington-area citizens led by Joseph Graves and Earl D. Wallace founded a nonprofit to restore the village. Today its 34 original buildings and some 3,000 acres of farmland and preserve make up what the village calls "the country's largest private collection of original 19th century buildings" and the largest National Historic Landmark in Kentucky.

## Flight Context

Shaker Village of Pleasant Hill sits at about 37.818 N, 84.740 W in Mercer County, Kentucky, roughly 25 miles southwest of Lexington. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL, look for a tidy cluster of large stone and brick buildings along a road surrounded by open pasture and stone-walled fields, with the deep, wooded gorge of the Kentucky River nearby. Blue Grass Airport in Lexington (KLEX) is about 15 nm to the north-northeast, Stuart Powell Field in Danville (KDVK) about 14 nm to the south, and Capital City Airport in Frankfort (KFFT) about 23 nm to the north-northwest. The rolling Bluegrass terrain is low and forgiving, but watch for morning fog pooling in the river valley.
