# Burning Springs, West Virginia

The West Virginia hollow where natural gas seeping from the ground has burned continuously since before European settlement - and where the first oil well in the region was drilled in 1860, five months after Drake's famous well.

**Location:** 39.0100, -81.3000

**Categories:** west-virginia, oil-history, civil-war, ghost-town, natural-phenomenon

## Introduction

Long before European settlers arrived, the Shawnee knew about the hollow where fire came from the water. Natural gas, seeping through springs and creeks, could be ignited and would burn indefinitely. They called it 'burning springs.' White settlers arrived in the 1770s and found the phenomenon equally mysterious - water that burned, rocks that oozed flammable fluid. The hollow became a curiosity, then an industry. In 1860, five months after Edwin Drake's famous well in Pennsylvania, drillers struck oil here. Burning Springs briefly became one of America's first oil boomtowns, producing 300 barrels a day before Confederate raiders destroyed it during the Civil War. The oil industry moved on. But the springs still burn - natural gas still seeps from the rocks - and the hollow remains a strange, forgotten footnote to America's petroleum history.

## The Phenomenon

Natural gas seeps to the surface through cracks and faults in the rock throughout the Appalachian region. In Burning Springs hollow, the concentration was unusual - enough gas to sustain visible flames when ignited. Early visitors described flames dancing on the surface of springs and creeks, burning without apparent fuel. The effect was and is real: light the seeping gas and it burns. It's not unique to this hollow, but the intensity was notable enough to give the place its name. Settlers considered it supernatural; geologists later understood it as methane rising through porous rock.

## The Oil Rush

Edwin Drake's successful oil well in Titusville, Pennsylvania, in August 1859 launched America's oil rush. Five months later, in January 1860, drillers struck oil at Burning Springs. At 300 barrels per day, the Rathbone well was one of the most productive early wells anywhere. Speculators flooded in. Within months, Burning Springs had thousands of residents, hotels, saloons, and all the chaos of a boomtown. Derricks covered the hillsides. Oil barrels floated down the Little Kanawha River to market. For a brief moment, Burning Springs rivaled the Pennsylvania oil fields in importance.

## The Destruction

The Civil War reached Burning Springs on May 9, 1863. Confederate cavalry under General William E. Jones, on a raid through West Virginia, recognized the oil field's strategic importance - the Union army ran on whale oil and petroleum. Jones's men destroyed everything: oil derricks, storage tanks, boats loaded with barrels, and the town itself. They set fire to 150,000 barrels of oil. The conflagration burned for days; oil ran blazing into the river. The raid destroyed the industry at Burning Springs. After the war, drilling resumed elsewhere. Burning Springs never recovered.

## The Aftermath

Today, Burning Springs is a quiet hollow in Wirt County, West Virginia, population minimal. The oil boom left almost no trace - some old well sites, some foundations, local memories. The hollow returned to forest. But the springs still seep gas. Local tradition holds that if you know where to look, you can still light the water on fire. The natural phenomenon that drew attention for centuries continues; only the human industry is gone. The site is remote, unpromoted, and largely forgotten. Pennsylvania claims the oil industry's birthplace; West Virginia's brief starring role is a historical footnote.

## Visiting Burning Springs

Burning Springs is located in Wirt County, West Virginia, about 20 miles east of Parkersburg along the Little Kanawha River. There are no formal attractions or interpretive sites - the area is rural and sparsely populated. The approximate location of the original wells can be found along Burning Springs Run. A state historical marker on Route 14 commemorates the oil field. The Little Kanawha River, down which oil barrels once floated, is visible. The towns of Elizabeth and Burning Springs are nearby, offering minimal services. Yeager Airport in Charleston is 80 miles southeast; Parkersburg's Mid-Ohio Valley Regional Airport is closer but has limited service.
