# 2020 Williamsburg Massacre

On a December afternoon in 2020, five West Virginia children between the ages of one and seven were killed in their own home - a tragedy that exposed years of warnings ignored.

**Location:** 37.9925, -80.5093

**Categories:** true crime, west virginia, child welfare, appalachia, tragedy

## Introduction

Five children climbed off a school bus on a December afternoon in 2020 and walked into a house that should have been a refuge. By evening, all five were dead. The youngest was one year old. The oldest had just turned seven. In the rural quiet of Greenbrier County, where Williamsburg sits among the folded ridges of southeastern West Virginia, what happened on December 8 became the worst mass shooting in the state's history - and a case study in how a system designed to protect children can fail them entirely.

## The Children

Before they were a statistic, they were five small lives. Three were the biological children of 25-year-old Oreanna Myers. Two were her stepchildren. They lived in a two-story house on a road that winds through the kind of West Virginia hills where neighbors are minutes apart by car and miles apart by foot. On that Tuesday afternoon, Myers was seen picking two of them up from a bus stop - an ordinary moment, the last ordinary moment, in lives that ended hours later inside a burning house. Investigators would not finish identifying all the children's bodies until December 12, four days after the fire. Their names are recorded in court documents and obituaries, not in the language of true-crime headlines.

## The Warnings

The most haunting detail came later, in a hearing room. In August 2020, four months before the killings, a dentist examined one of the children and noticed something wrong. The child was visibly afraid of his father. There was a large bruise. The dentist also reported seeing the father verbally abusing the child outside. A call went in to Child Protective Services on August 10. The operator ended the call. The reporter was told there would be no investigation. The biological mother of two of the children would later sue the West Virginia Department of Health and Human Resources, alleging that multiple abuse reports had been filed and ignored. The lawsuit became part of a broader reckoning: in 2023, the West Virginia legislature voted to dissolve the DHHR and split it into three agencies, an admission that the system had grown too unwieldy to do the job it existed for.

## Greenbrier County

Williamsburg is an unincorporated community in Greenbrier County, the kind of place where the population is measured by post office boxes rather than census tracts. The Greenbrier River curves nearby. The Monongahela National Forest spreads to the north. Most days, the loudest sound is wind in the hardwoods or a distant tractor. The county seat at Lewisburg is famous for its historic district and the annual State Fair of West Virginia. Williamsburg itself rarely makes the news. When it did - in headlines that crawled across screens nationwide on December 9, 2020 - the dissonance was almost cruel. A massacre is not what places like this are for.

## What Comes After

There is no satisfying ending to a story like this. Oreanna Myers left a note that referenced demons taking over her. She set the house on fire and walked outside to take her own life. The investigation closed the way such investigations close: with a determination of what happened, but no real explanation for why. What the case left behind was a question that West Virginia is still trying to answer. How many warnings does a child get? When abuse is reported, who actually listens? The lawsuit, the legislative committee hearings, the dissolution of the DHHR - these were the institutional aftershocks. The personal ones belong to the families who buried five children just before Christmas.

## Remembering

Stories like this resist easy closure, and they should. The temptation, in writing about a place where something terrible happened, is to leave the terrible thing as the only thing. But Williamsburg is also a school bus stop on a winter morning, and a dentist who saw a bruise and tried to do something about it, and neighbors who searched the woods when the smoke rose. The five children who died had favorite toys, favorite snacks, opinions about what they wanted for dinner. They were one, three, four, six, and seven years old. Naming the small facts of their lives is the closest thing to a memorial a place like this gets.
