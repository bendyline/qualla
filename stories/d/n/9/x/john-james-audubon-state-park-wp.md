# John James Audubon State Park

In the Wolf Hills of Henderson, Kentucky, where John James Audubon spent a frontier decade sketching birds, a New Deal park and museum now guard what is billed as one of the world's largest collections of his original art.

**Location:** 37.8822, -87.5481

**Categories:** State parks of Kentucky, Biographical museums in Kentucky, Art museums and galleries in Kentucky, Nature centers in Kentucky, Civilian Conservation Corps, Henderson County, Kentucky

## Introduction

Even the museum makes room for birds. When the Audubon Museum went up in the Wolf Hills of Henderson, Kentucky, its builders gave it a French-style design with small niches in its tower for nesting birds, a fitting courtesy in a park named for John James Audubon. The ornithologist, naturalist and painter lived in Henderson from 1810 to 1819, when the town was a frontier village just south of the Ohio River. Today the park that bears his name covers roughly 700 acres of hilly forest along U.S. Route 41, and its museum is billed as holding one of the world's largest collections of his original art.

## More Starving Artist Than Success

Audubon's Henderson years were no commercial triumph. He ran small businesses in the town with mixed success, and during these years he also bought and sold enslaved people to support his ventures, a part of his legacy that the National Audubon Society acknowledges. His real passion lay in the surrounding forests, where he explored, sketched and painted wildlife, above all birds. The museum does not hide the hardship. Its four exhibit halls trace his life, including the Henderson decade, and the personal artifacts and memorabilia on display portray an often difficult existence, more starving artist than artistic success. Among them is the silver service he sent from England to his devoted wife, Lucy, to replace the set lost during bankruptcy. It shares the halls with journals written in his own hand, his personal seal, the American Bald Eagle oil painting and a four-volume edition of The Birds of America.

## A Librarian Makes the Case

The park began with research. In 1934 the Wolf Hills area was chosen for a new state park, meeting the 300-acre minimum needed for development, and Henderson librarian Susan Towles supplied the documentation that tied the land to Audubon. That September a Civilian Conservation Corps base, Camp Cromwell, was set up off US 41, named for Emma Guy Cromwell, director of the Kentucky State Parks. About 200 men of CCC Company 1540 came from their West Virginia base, joined by others including several Henderson natives. Over the next four years they drained swamps, built two lakes and developed trails and roads. With help from the Works Progress Administration, the shelters, the Tea House and the museum were built from local stone and millwork, for a total of about $500,000. Progress was not smooth, slowed by funding gaps and arguments over the park's focus, and Henderson attorney and businessman Gibney Oscar Letcher helped guide the project among federal agencies, state officials, contractors and Audubon's descendants.

## A Great-Grandson's Widow Sends Her Treasures

The museum's heart came from one family collection. Alice Tyler, widow of Audubon's great-grandson Leonard Sanford Tyler, had been in contact with Susan Towles during the park's development, and she wished her large personal collection of Audubon material to be placed on loan in the new museum. She shipped it to Henderson in the spring of 1938, and the museum opened on July 16 that year, the same year Wilderness Lake was completed. The Tea House, today the park office, was finished in June 1940 and served three meals a day until December 1941. The cottages and a recreational lake followed in spring 1941, finishing the New Deal work. After Pearl Harbor, Camp Cromwell became a Naval Cadet Aviation Training School supervised by Evansville College, now the University of Evansville. Decades later, in 1994, donations to the Friends of Audubon, together with contributions from the Preston Foundation and Kentucky State Parks, allowed the Tyler collection to be purchased.

## Old-Growth and Pedal Boats

The postwar years brought steady additions. From 1948 to 1955 a restoration program added new museum lighting, a picnic area, a bathhouse and beach, and a lake dam, and during the 1960s another $500,000 went into a campground and a nine-hole golf course as the park grew to nearly 700 acres. In 1990 the General Assembly allocated $2.5 million to renovate the museum and add a 9,500-square-foot nature center. The largest renovation in the museum's history began in spring 1992, and the expanded building reopened in December 1993. The park was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1988. Much of it is now a state nature preserve, including 338 acres of old-growth trees and trails, and the Office of Kentucky Nature Preserves added a 650-acre addition in 2016. Visitors walk miles of forest trails (counts range from 6.5 to more than 12), rent pedal boats on a 28-acre fishing lake and join programs led by a full-time naturalist and museum educator, doing the kind of close looking Audubon once did in the forests around Henderson.

## Flight Context

John James Audubon State Park lies at 37.8822°N, 87.5481°W on U.S. Route 41 at the northern edge of Henderson, Kentucky, just south of the Ohio River. From 2,000-3,000 feet AGL the park reads as a block of dark, hilly forest with small lakes, set between Henderson's streets and the broad bend of the Ohio. The US 41 bridges over the river and the city of Evansville, Indiana, on the far bank make easy references. Evansville Regional Airport (KEVV) is about 9 nm north; Henderson City-County Airport (FAA identifier EHR) is about 8 nm southwest. River valley haze and morning fog along the Ohio are common in warm months.
