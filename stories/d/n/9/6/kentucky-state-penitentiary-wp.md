# Kentucky State Penitentiary

Built by Italian stonemasons from Cumberland River limestone, Eddyville's "Castle on the Cumberland" is Kentucky's oldest prison and still holds the commonwealth's death row.

**Location:** 37.0481, -88.0764

**Categories:** Prisons in Kentucky, Capital punishment in Kentucky, Prisons on the National Register of Historic Places, Lyon County, Kentucky, History

## Introduction

In 1910 Kentucky's lawmakers decided that the state's electric chair belonged inside a penitentiary, with a death house built around it. The old prison at Frankfort wanted no part of it. It had no room, and its officials did not want the curiosity seekers such a machine would draw. So in September of that year the chair went west instead, to a limestone fortress on a hill above the Cumberland River at Eddyville, a building still known as the Castle on the Cumberland. More than a century later it remains Kentucky's oldest prison facility, a maximum-security institution with supermax units that held roughly 850 men at the Department of Corrections' latest count, and it still houses the commonwealth's male death row and its execution chamber.

## Slime-Covered Walls

The castle exists because the old system was killing people. An 1875 study of the Kentucky State Prison found that 20 percent of its inmates had pneumonia and 75 percent had scurvy, in a place described as one of "slime covered walls, open sewage, and graveyard coughs." About seventy of its thousand prisoners died that year. Kentucky officials had rarely focused on prison life until Governor Luke P. Blackburn, elected in 1879, persuaded the legislature to approve a new prison. In 1880 three commissioners, Judge Richard H. Stanton, Hylan Benton Lyon and William Morgan Beckner, were appointed to find it a location. On April 28, 1884, an act passed both houses providing for a branch penitentiary at Eddyville. Lyon, a former Confederate brigadier general and an Eddyville native, was the moving force behind that choice.

## A Castle Raised From the River

Construction began in 1884 on a hill overlooking the Cumberland. The builders used massive limestone blocks quarried at a site down the river, and Italian stonemasons were recruited to raise the original buildings, which resemble medieval castles. The legislature appropriated $484,143.93 for the work and spent $470,753.41 of it. The finished prison could hold 1,000 men, and by one account it officially opened on December 24, 1890 (the Department of Corrections dates the prison to 1889), to ease overcrowding at the Frankfort Penitentiary. The need was real. Around the same years, the General Assembly let some inmates work outside prison walls, sometimes unsupervised, and around half of the fifty working outside in the first few years escaped. Abuse by guards and overseers reached its worst because no one was watching how prisoners were handled. When the law was abolished in the early 1890s and all inmates were kept inside, the new penitentiary filled almost at once.

## Out of the Stripes

The new century brought change in small, visible ways. A 1909 law ended the requirement that inmates wear stripes. In their place came baggy denim pants and jackets, cloth hats and cotton shirts, with numbers stenciled on the back. Older punishments lingered: the ball and chain was used on prison offenders until 1940. The 1910 reform bill separated convicts by age, sending those under 30 to a reformatory and keeping older men in a penitentiary, and Frankfort would soon be called the Reformatory. In the 1940s Eddyville began removing its convicts under eighteen, most of them to that institution. Through all of it, the prison's chronic weakness was its corps of correctional officers, always few in number and poorly paid. Kentucky's first penitentiary was made uninhabitable by a flood in 1937, and Eddyville, the second, is today the commonwealth's oldest prison facility.

## Extension Cords and Midnight Verdicts

At about 2 a.m. on June 17, 1988, eight convicts, three of them men on death row for murder, escaped while sneaking around fires set by other inmates. They had sawed through cell bars, walked through the cell-house doors and climbed roughly thirty feet to a window using an electrical extension cord. Nine years later, on July 1, 1997, Kentucky carried out its first execution in thirty-five years. Harold McQueen, 44, had been convicted in 1981 of murdering Rebecca O'Hearn, a convenience store clerk, during a robbery that netted him $1,500. He was electrocuted and pronounced dead at 12:07 a.m., while, by one account, more than a hundred death penalty opponents and twenty-five supporters of capital punishment protested outside. The most recent execution there came on November 21, 2008, when Marco Allen Chapman, convicted of murdering two northern Kentucky children, Chelbi and Cody Marksberry, died by lethal injection. No execution has followed, and court challenges over the state's execution protocol have kept executions on hold. In January 2014 inmate James Kenneth Embry Jr. died of starvation and dehydration after a hunger strike, and after inquiries by the Associated Press the state's corrections commissioner asked the attorney general's office to review his death.

## Khaki, Canary and Scarlet

Inside, the color of a man's uniform shows his custody level. There are five: khaki for general population, kelly green for protective custody, canary yellow for administrative segregation, scarlet red for death row and dark green for minimum security. Most men do not come straight from a courtroom. They are sent here because of violent or disruptive behavior at less secure facilities elsewhere in the commonwealth. As of 2015 the prison had roughly 350 staff (the Department of Corrections lists 348 today) and an annual operating budget of about $20 million. Outside the walls, a small minimum-security unit sends inmates to work assignments across the grounds, and in their spare time they may fish in Lake Barkley. The penitentiary was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1981 as part of the Old Eddyville Historic District. In 2016 author Steve E. Asher gathered accounts of paranormal activity there in Hauntings of the Kentucky State Penitentiary, and on July 1, 2018, DeEdra Hart became the first woman to serve as its warden; the Department of Corrections now lists Laura Plappert, appointed in 2024, as warden.

## Flight Context

Kentucky State Penitentiary sits at 37.0481°N, 88.0764°W on Lake Barkley, the impounded Cumberland River, about 3 miles from downtown Eddyville. A pass at 2,500-3,500 feet AGL shows the pale limestone buildings on their hill above the water. Lake Barkley and, a few miles to the west across Land Between the Lakes, Kentucky Lake make unmistakable references. Nearby fields: Kentucky Dam State Park Airport near Calvert City (FAA identifier M34, about 11 nm west-southwest) and Princeton-Caldwell County Airport (FAA identifier 2M0, about 11 nm east-northeast). Airports with ICAO codes include Murray-Calloway County Airport (KCEY), about 27 nm southwest, and Barkley Regional Airport at Paducah (KPAH), about 33 nm west. Summer haze and river fog over the lakes can reduce visibility in the early morning.
