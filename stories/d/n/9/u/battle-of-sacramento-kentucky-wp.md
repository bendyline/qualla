# Battle of Sacramento (Kentucky)

On a cold, rainy December day in 1861, Nathan Bedford Forrest fought what became known as "Forrest's First Fight," routing a larger Union force outside a small western Kentucky town.

**Location:** 37.4194, -87.2617

**Categories:** American Civil War, Battles of the American Civil War in Kentucky, Nathan Bedford Forrest, National Register of Historic Places in McLean County, Kentucky, Cavalry battles

## Introduction

The tip came from a sympathizer named Mollie Morehead. On December 28, 1861, she told Lieutenant Colonel Nathan Bedford Forrest that Union troops were watering their horses near Sacramento, a small town in McLean County, Kentucky. The weather was cold and a light rain was falling, but Forrest had already received scouting reports that morning of an enemy force moving along the banks of the Green River, and he was keen to attack. By the end of the day, a few hundred Confederate horsemen had routed a Union force of some 400 to 500, and the fight had a name that would stick: Forrest's First Fight.

## A State Pulled Two Ways

Kentucky in late 1861 belonged fully to neither side. Both the Union Army and the Confederates recruited and drilled within its borders, and its people's loyalties were divided. In September, Confederate Major General Leonidas Polk began taking Kentucky villages with the blessing of Jefferson Davis, though other Confederate leaders, reportedly including Simon Bolivar Buckner and Leroy Walker, objected. Union Brigadier General Ulysses S. Grant approached by way of the Ohio River and established himself at Paducah.

Forrest's battalion arrived from Memphis that October. Its eight companies were armed with shotguns and drilled for close combat. On the Union side, Major Eli H. Murray commanded 400 to 500 men (sources differ) who had crossed the Green River and stopped to water their horses south of Sacramento. Forrest had between 200 and 300 cavalry. He was outnumbered, and he chose to attack anyway.

## Blow the Charge, Isham

Forrest split his force. Some of his men dismounted and advanced from concealed positions, while mounted detachments swung wide to get around the Union troops. The Federals hesitated at first, then returned fire. Forrest had fired the opening shot himself.

When the moment came he called to his bugler, "Blow the charge, Isham," and rode forward at the head of his men. The outflanking cavalry closed on the Union soldiers as they fell back, and the fight broke into hand-to-hand combat. Murray's command was encircled and decisively routed.

Among the Confederate riders that day was a private named Adam Rankin Johnson. Seven months later, across the Ohio River in Indiana, he would capture the town of Newburgh using fake cannons made from a stovepipe, a charred log and wagon wheels, and earn the nickname "Stovepipe" for the rest of his life. He was later appointed a brigadier general, though the Confederate Congress never confirmed the appointment.

## Two Sets of Numbers

How many men fell at Sacramento depends on whose account you read. The Confederates reported two of their own killed and three wounded. Union sources put their losses at ten killed and thirteen captured, while Confederate accounts claimed close to a hundred Union dead and thirty-five prisoners. The two sides' accounts are far apart, and behind every one of those numbers were soldiers, some of whom never went home.

Forrest's superior, Brigadier General Charles Clark, praised his "skill, energy and courage." Clark wrote that officers and men assured him Forrest "was conspicuous for the most daring courage; always in advance of his command," and that at one point he had fought hand to hand with four Union soldiers, killing three and taking the fourth prisoner.

## The Rest of the Record

Sacramento is described as the first battle in which Forrest commanded troops in the field, though he had already skirmished with Union gunboats that November, and it previewed the methods he would become known for: dividing his force, concealment, outflanking and encirclement, and leading cavalry charges in person. It was also the opening chapter in a career that cannot honestly be told through battlefield courage alone.

Before the war, Forrest was directly involved in the interstate slave trade, including operating a slave jail in Memphis. In April 1864, at the Battle of Fort Pillow, Confederate forces under his command killed a large number of Union troops after the fort had effectively ceased resistance; most of them were Black soldiers. After the war he became the first Grand Wizard of the Reconstruction-era Ku Klux Klan.

The Sacramento battlefield was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1998, and the town and its residents stage an annual re-enactment of the fight each May; the 28th was held in May 2026.

## Flight Context

Sacramento, Kentucky, lies at approximately 37.42°N, 87.26°W in McLean County, in the Green River country of western Kentucky. A pass at 2,000–3,000 feet AGL gives a good view of the town and the surrounding countryside where the fight took place. Owensboro-Daviess County Regional (KOWB) is about 20 nm to the north-northeast. Closer general-aviation fields are Muhlenberg County Airport at Greenville (FAA identifier M21, no ICAO code listed) about 13 nm and Ohio County Airport at Hartford (FAA identifier JQD, no ICAO code listed) about 20 nm. Best in clear weather.
