# Slack Farm

In 1987 looters tore open hundreds of graves at a centuries-old Caborn-Welborn village above the Ohio River, and the outcry helped change how Kentucky protects the dead.

**Location:** 37.7697, -88.0078

**Categories:** Archaeological sites in Kentucky, Mississippian culture, Caborn-Welborn culture, Native American history of Kentucky, Archaeological theft

## Introduction

In 1987, ten men from surrounding states paid the tenant farmers of a field near Uniontown, Kentucky, $10,000, brought in equipment, and spent two months digging into a terrace above the Ohio River. They were looters, and what they broke open were graves: at least 650 of them by one count, the resting places of a community that had lived here from about 1400 into the 1600s. When they finished, human remains lay scattered across the ground. Slack Farm was one of the major villages of the Caborn-Welborn people, yet it had drawn little professional attention before the looters came. After 1987, the rest of the world learned its name too.

## A Town Above the River

The people who lived here belonged to the Caborn-Welborn variant of the Mississippian culture, and they chose their ground with care. The village sat on a projecting terrace and an adjacent levee about 300 meters from the Ohio River, close to where the Wabash flows in. On the bluff above stood a mound, recorded by archaeologists as site 15 UN 70. Below it, according to the Kentucky Archaeological Survey, at least seven residential areas spread across about 75 acres, six of them set around a plaza or series of courtyards and the seventh across a ravine, downstream from the others.

Families lived in rectangular houses of wattle and daub, their walls set in trenches and their floors sunk into shallow basins. Many had prepared clay hearths. Beside most homes were pits for storing maize and other dried foods, large enough, by one estimate, to hold a year's grain for seven to twelve people. This was a settled town, organized around shared space and built to last through the seasons.

## Rows Someone Remembered

Each section of the village kept its own cemetery. The dead were usually laid out at full length, often in parallel rows, and the graves did not cut into one another, a detail suggesting that each burial was marked in some way so the living knew where their people lay. Mourners placed small, personal things with the dead, usually near the upper body: disc pipes, shell beads and ear plugs, little pottery jars. In the village's later years, new objects joined them. Glass beads and copper or brass bracelets and tubes, European trade goods, went into the ground beside the old familiar ones.

Infants and children are under-represented among the graves the looters opened, so archaeologists speculate that the seven cemeteries may have held a thousand people or more. Every one of them was somebody's parent, sibling or child, buried by a community that expected them to rest undisturbed.

## What the Law Could Not Reach

It was the people of Uniontown who brought the digging to an end. Local complaints led to the arrest of the looters, but the law of the day offered little to charge them with: the misdemeanor of desecrating a venerable object. Even that was difficult to prosecute in the late 1980s, before the federal Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act and related state legislation made it clearer that such digging was illegal.

The damage drew attention far beyond Kentucky. National Geographic magazine wrote about it, and Slack Farm became a focus of widespread outcry against the illicit removal of antiquities. The looting contributed to the passage of stricter Kentucky laws protecting burials, sacred grounds, and Indigenous and archaeological sites. The charge the looters faced is now a felony, in part because of the controversy over what happened here. The original charges brought no punishment: according to the Trafficking Culture research project, four of the men lived in other states and could not be extradited, and the case was dismissed in March 1990 for lack of prosecution.

## Returned to the Earth

The hundreds of broken bones did not stay scattered. After archaeologists and hundreds of volunteers documented the damage, Native American groups claimed the remains, and most were reburied at the site, returning the dead to the ground they had been torn from. The act of care did not end with the burial. As of May 2007, twenty years after the looting, Native American groups were still meeting in the area to commemorate the site and to mourn the damage done to it.

Slack Farm's importance was never in anything a passerby could see. It lies in what the soil still holds, in the seven cemeteries of a river town occupied for well over two centuries, and in the gatherings that continued long after the looters were gone.
