# Newburgh Raid

In July 1862 some thirty to thirty-five Confederate partisans captured Newburgh, Indiana, without firing a shot, bluffing the town with 'cannons' made from a stovepipe, a charred log and wagon wheels.

**Location:** 37.9467, -87.4036

**Categories:** American Civil War, Cavalry raids of the American Civil War, Military operations of the American Civil War in Indiana, Warrick County, Indiana, Ohio River

## Introduction

The artillery that captured Newburgh, Indiana, was built from a stovepipe, a charred log, and the axles and wheels of a broken wagon. On July 18, 1862, Confederate partisan Adam Rankin Johnson set two of these "Quaker guns" on hills within view of the town, crossed the Ohio River with some 30 to 35 men recruited around Henderson, Kentucky, and convinced Newburgh's defenders they were surrounded by cannon. Not a shot was fired. Newburgh is often described as the first town in a Northern state to be captured during the Civil War, and its captor carried a nickname for the rest of his life: Adam "Stovepipe" Johnson.

## A Scout With a Secret Message

Johnson was no stranger to the war. He had served as a scout for Nathan Bedford Forrest, just missed the Battle of Shiloh in April 1862, and rejoined Forrest at the Confederate base in Corinth, Mississippi. Forrest then sent him to Henderson, Kentucky, to deliver a secret message to D. R. Burbank, a former employer of Johnson's.

In Henderson, Johnson pulled together a small band by combining three-man guards for General John C. Breckinridge with recruits from Kentucky: thirty-five men by his later count, thirty-two by others. They camped at the Soaper Farm before the raid. Johnson could not find enough firearms for everyone, though every man got a horse. He gave each of them the chance to stay behind. To a man, they chose to ride with him.

## An Unguarded Warehouse

Across the river, Newburgh was badly prepared. Its company of the Indiana Legion, the state militia, was commanded by a man named Union Bethell, who had enjoyed only limited success raising and training it. He stored the unit's weapons in his own unguarded riverfront tobacco warehouse, which also held 75 loose sabers and 130 pistol-and-holster sets.

Johnson and two subordinates, Felix Akin and Frank A. Owen, crossed in one boat while the rest of the force came over on a flatboat. At the Exchange Hotel, then serving as a hospital, Johnson confronted the Union soldiers inside and warned that he would "shell this town to the ground" if they resisted. The raiders took supplies and ammunition, paroled their prisoners, and returned to Kentucky soil.

About ten miles away in Evansville, five companies of the Indiana Legion were being raised, but none could reach Newburgh before the Confederates withdrew. By chance rather than by any Confederate action, the telegraph line to Evansville was not working, and word of the raid took hours longer to arrive.

## Indiana Sounds the Alarm

The total surprise and bloodless success of the raid shocked Hoosier leaders. Lieutenant Colonel John Watson Foster, on leave from the 25th Indiana Infantry, took command in Evansville, called for volunteers including convalescent Union soldiers, and assembled a small riverboat flotilla. It sailed up the Ohio to the mouth of the Green River and skirmished with a small number of Confederate guerrillas.

Governor Oliver P. Morton issued repeated calls for volunteers, first in Indianapolis and then in Evansville. Within three days, state and federal officers had sent roughly a thousand regulars and volunteers to the scene. They occupied Henderson and probed its countryside, and one probe, led by Captain Bethell, recovered part of the stolen arms at a nearby farm. The raid's longer shadow mattered more: it is said to have convinced the federal government to give Indiana a permanent force of regular Union Army soldiers, and it gave Union recruiting in the state a significant boost.

## Soldier or Brigand?

What Johnson actually was on July 18, 1862, depends on who is telling the story. In a book he wrote later in life, he described his men in the language of the Confederacy's 1862 Partisan Ranger Act, as an irregular force acting under the authority of Forrest and Breckinridge. Yet his own account suggests that at the time he held no formal appointment as an officer, wore no uniform, and led a hastily assembled body of civilians, more guerrillas than soldiers. Union authorities saw him as little more than a brigand and later rejected the paroles he had issued to his roughly eighty prisoners.

Johnson came back to Indiana a year later as a brigade commander in Morgan's Raid of 1863. His memoir, full of enthusiasm, Southern chivalry and name-dropping but often sparse on corroboration, has led many commentators to fit the raid into the Confederate movements in Kentucky that summer. Reenactors of Cobb's Key Battery have at times commemorated the event at Henderson's Sunset Park.

## Flight Context

Newburgh sits at approximately 37.95°N, 87.40°W on the Indiana bank of the Ohio River, just upstream (east) of Evansville. Follow the Ohio at 2,000–3,000 feet AGL to take in the riverfront town and the Kentucky side opposite, from where Johnson's fake cannons were meant to be seen. Evansville Regional (KEVV) is about 8 nm to the northwest and Owensboro-Daviess County Regional (KOWB) about 17 nm to the southeast. Boonville Airport (FAA identifier I91) is about 7 nm to the northeast. Best in clear weather.
