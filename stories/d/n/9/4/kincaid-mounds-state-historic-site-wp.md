# Kincaid Mounds State Historic Site

On the Ohio River at the southern tip of Illinois, a Mississippian town of platform mounds around a great plaza flourished from about 1050 to 1400 CE and later became a proving ground for modern archaeology.

**Location:** 37.0806, -88.4917

**Categories:** Middle Mississippian culture, Mounds in Illinois, National Historic Landmarks in Illinois, Illinois State Historic Sites, Archaeological sites on the National Register of Historic Places in Illinois

## Introduction

Sometime between 250 BCE and 1 CE, someone living beside the Ohio River in what is now southern Illinois dug a pit and deliberately buried a small domestic dog. Archaeologists found it in 2003, in excavations ahead of the construction of an informational kiosk and viewing platform at Kincaid Mounds, and it remains a rare find for the area. Yet that dog belongs to an early chapter here. More than a thousand years later, on the same stretch of river bottom, Mississippian people built a town of platform mounds around a vast plaza, and it flourished from about 1050 to 1400 CE.

## Layers Before the Mounds

University of Chicago excavators in the 1930s traced human presence around Kincaid back thousands of years into the Archaic Period, which ran from 8000 to 2000 BCE. They called this earliest layer the Faulkner Component, a culture without pottery but otherwise much like the Early Woodland cultures that followed. Occupation grew more intensive in the Early and Middle Woodland periods, as people settled into semi-permanent houses, a partly agricultural life and limestone-tempered ceramics. Archaeologists call this long occupation the Baumer phase, a local version of the Crab Orchard culture. Its potters had their own habits: rather than decorate with the Havana Hopewell motifs other Crab Orchard peoples used, they marked their pots with cord and fabric and favored limestone and grog in their clay. The buried dog comes from this era.

## A Plaza Among Giants

The Mississippian town was built on a grand scale. At its heart lay a large central plaza, made by filling and leveling the ground, surrounded by the major mounds. One of them is almost 500 feet long. None of Kincaid's earthworks rivals Monks Mound at Cahokia, but the largest is said to rank 12th in size among all known Mississippian mounds, and by the number of mounds built, the site ranks fifth among Mississippian sites (the site's own history counts 11 major mounds). The other platform mounds rise from 8 to 30 feet. Remains of large buildings on top of the main mounds suggest temples or council houses. The site straddles modern Massac and Pope counties; the Illinois Historic Preservation Agency owns and operates an area in Massac County that includes several of the mounds, while the Pope County portion is privately owned. The site's own page says the Kincaid Mounds Support Organization, a local nonprofit, manages the state property under contract, and that public access is currently limited to an observation and interpretation platform beside Kincaid Mounds Road. Kincaid was declared a National Historic Landmark on July 19, 1964.

## Coal, Fluorite and Painted Pots

Kincaid's people carved figurines from coal and fluorite, with imagery showing connections to the Southeastern Ceremonial Complex. They traded for chert from Missouri, Tennessee and other parts of Illinois, and also used Mill Creek chert from quarries close by. Their potters produced shell-tempered wares, including pieces painted with a negative-resist technique. Archaeologists group Kincaid with Wickliffe, Tolu and Angel Mounds as the "Kincaid focus" of the lower Ohio valley in Illinois, Kentucky and Indiana, because the four towns share pottery styles and site plans, with types such as Angel Negative Painted, Kincaid Negative Painted and Matthews Incised. The parallels between Kincaid and Angel are so striking, in layout, artifacts and geography, that some experts hypothesize the same society built and lived in both.

## Empty Centuries, Then Shovels

Mississippian life at Kincaid appears to have ended by 1400 to 1450 CE. No occupation by historic Native American tribes is documented, and the town may have been abandoned as timber and game ran out. It stood empty for centuries before American settlers arrived. In the 1930s and 1940s the University of Chicago excavated here and identified nine mounds on the Massac County portion, and the site has since played a central role in the development of modern archaeological techniques. The ground was not finished giving up surprises. Since 2003, Southern Illinois University teams using new technology have returned to re-excavate hills once thought to be possible mounds, and they identified a tenth: a small mound, later covered by a midden, on the southeastern corner of the plaza near the county line. The Chicago crew had dug around it but left it off their list because they could not be sure what it was.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 37.0806°N, 88.4917°W, on the Illinois bank of the Ohio River in Massac and Pope counties, at the southern tip of Illinois upstream of Paducah, Kentucky. The mounds are modest from the air, so look for open ground on the river bottomland rather than dramatic relief; the broad Ohio is the key landmark. A pass at about 2,000-2,500 feet MSL gives the best chance of picking out the plaza and mounds in low-angle light. Nearby airports include Kentucky Dam State Park Airport (FAA identifier M34, no ICAO code) about 10 nm east-southeast, Barkley Regional at Paducah (KPAH) about 14 nm west, and Metropolis Municipal (FAA M30) about 14 nm west-northwest. River fog is common on cool mornings; summers bring haze and thunderstorms.
