# Cairo, Illinois

Walled in by levees at the meeting of the Ohio and Mississippi, Cairo rose as a steamboat port and Union fortress, endured lynching and a long civil rights struggle, and has lost about nine in ten of its people since its peak.

**Location:** 37.0131, -89.1803

## Introduction

Huck and Jim never made it to Cairo. In Mark Twain's novel, the town at the southern tip of Illinois was their goal, the place where they would turn up the Ohio River toward freedom for Jim. They drifted past it by mistake and ended up in the slave state of Arkansas. The real Cairo sits exactly where Twain put it, on a low spit of land at the confluence of the Ohio and Mississippi, two of the greatest rivers in North America. It is the lowest place in Illinois and the only city in the state surrounded by levees, a town that lives behind walls and has spent two centuries negotiating with the water.

## A Port Named for the Nile

Named for Egypt's capital, Cairo began on paper. Its first municipal charter and the Bank of Cairo were issued in 1818, with no settlers and no depositors. A second attempt, by the Cairo City and Canal Company in 1836-37, built a large levee around the site and stuck. When Charles Dickens visited in 1842 he was unimpressed, seeing a disease-ridden backwater, and he made it the model for the town of Eden in Martin Chuzzlewit. Cairo grew anyway. Steamboats ran south to New Orleans, Congress designated Cairo a port of delivery in 1854, the Illinois Central Railroad arrived in 1855, and by 1860 more than 2,000 people lived here. Later, ferries carried nearly 250,000 railroad cars across the river in as little as six months, and wealthy merchants built mansions such as the Riverlore, raised by Capt. William P. Halliday in 1865, and Magnolia Manor, whose double brick walls hold ten-inch air spaces meant to keep out the city's famous damp.

## Grant's River Base

Geography made Cairo a prize in the Civil War. On September 6, 1861, Flag Officer Andrew Hull Foote made it the naval station for the Mississippi River Squadron, and because the city had no land available for a base, the navy yard's repair machinery floated on wharf-boats, old steamers, tugs, flatboats and rafts. Brigadier General Ulysses S. Grant had arrived two days earlier, on September 4, to set up his headquarters in the city, where Fort Defiance, already garrisoned by about 3,000 Union troops since June, guarded the confluence. From here he launched and supplied his campaigns south, and Cairo remained an important Union supply base and training center for the rest of the war. A later war brought a gentler kind of visitor: from 1943 to 1945, wartime travel restrictions sent the St. Louis Cardinals to Cairo for spring training.

## An Island by Choice

Levees and floodwalls enclose the whole city. Planners once meant to raise the streets to the height of the levees, and some buildings, including the old custom house, were designed for that higher grade before the cost of fill killed the plan. In 1914, Stupp Brothers of St. Louis built the "Big Subway Gate" to seal the northern levee across U.S. Highway 51: 80 tons, 60 feet wide, 24 feet high and five feet thick. Close it, and Cairo becomes an island. After the Great Mississippi Flood of 1927, the levees were reinforced and the Corps of Engineers created the Birds Point-New Madrid Floodway. The system was tested in 1937, when the Ohio crested at a record 59.5 feet. The Corps closed the gate and, for the first time, blew a breach in the Birds Point levee to ease pressure on Cairo's wall. Afterward the concrete wall was raised to guard against water up to 64 feet.

## The Long Struggle for Justice

In 1900 about 5,000 of Cairo's nearly 13,000 residents were Black, an unusually large share for a northern town, yet they faced discrimination in jobs and housing. On the night of November 11, 1909, mobs lynched two men in public: William James, a Black resident accused of the rape and murder of a young white woman, Anna Pelley, and Henry Salzner, a white man accused of murdering his wife; neither was given a trial. Journalist Ida B. Wells was sent from Chicago to investigate, and Governor Deneen used the state's 1905 anti-lynching law to remove the sheriff who had failed to protect them. In July 1967, Robert Hunt, a 19-year-old Black soldier home on leave, was found hanged in the police station; police said he had hanged himself, while many Black residents accused the police of murder. Rioting and protests followed over the next three days. More than 600 white residents formed a patrol known as the "White Hats," empowered by the sheriff's department, and in 1969 the Cairo United Front led protests against police brutality and job discrimination and began a boycott of white-owned businesses, with Saturday pickets for nearly three years (one account puts the boycott at a decade).

## What the Rivers Left Behind

As river shipping, the railroads and the ferries moved on, jobs went with them. The Interstate 57 bridge, opened in 1978, bypassed the town to the north; the hospital closed in December 1986, and Amtrak's City of New Orleans stopped calling in 1987. Cairo's population, which peaked at 15,203 in 1920, fell in every census from 1950 to 2020, when it stood at 1,733, about 89 percent below its peak. By 2024 it was estimated at roughly 1,500, and much of the city stood empty. Still, residents and the region are restoring landmarks and planning heritage tourism around the rivers, and the old grandeur survives in places: the Queen Anne-style A. B. Safford Memorial Library, dedicated in 1884 with stained glass and ornate woodwork, and Magnolia Manor, a Victorian house museum where, in late 2025, supporters were still raising money to repair the roof.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 37.0131°N, 89.1803°W, in Cairo at the southern tip of Illinois. The confluence of the Ohio and Mississippi rivers is unmistakable from the air: a narrow wedge of land ringed by levees, with the Ohio to the east and the Mississippi to the west, and bridges leading to Kentucky and Missouri. A cruise around 2,500-3,500 feet MSL gives a clear view of the point, the levee ring and the town grid. Cairo Regional Airport (KCIR) is about 4 nm away; Barkley Regional at Paducah (KPAH) is about 20 nm east and Cape Girardeau Regional (KCGI) about 23 nm northwest. Summers are hot and humid with afternoon storms, and river fog is common in cool months.
