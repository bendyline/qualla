# Wickliffe Mounds

A Mississippian town on a bluff near the meeting of the Ohio and Mississippi rivers, Wickliffe Mounds spent decades as a roadside attraction called the "Ancient Buried City" before becoming a place of research, respect and reburial.

**Location:** 36.9710, -89.0929

**Categories:** Middle Mississippian culture, Mounds in Kentucky, Archaeological museums in Kentucky, Kentucky State Historic Sites, Archaeological sites on the National Register of Historic Places in Kentucky

## Introduction

In the 1930s, a dollar bought a one-hour guided tour of the "Ancient Buried City." Fain W. King, a Paducah lumberman, amateur archaeologist and artifact collector, and his wife, Blanche Busey King, had opened a Mississippian town site to paying visitors, promoting it with sensational and misleading advertising. The place they sold as a spectacle had once been something far more important. Wickliffe Mounds, on a bluff above the Ohio River just outside Wickliffe in Ballard County, Kentucky, about three miles from where the Ohio meets the Mississippi, was the ceremonial and administrative center of an important chiefdom.

## A Town Above Two Rivers

People lived here from about 1100 (some accounts say 1000) to 1350 CE. At its peak the town's population probably reached into the hundreds. Two large platform mounds dominated it, with at least eight smaller mounds scattered around a central plaza. Maize was the staple crop, and because it could be stored, it supported a denser population and a more stratified society, ruled, as in most Mississippian chiefdoms, by a hereditary chief. Wickliffe's people traded with societies as far away as North Carolina, Wisconsin and the Gulf of Mexico, and archaeologists link the site with others along the Ohio River in Illinois and Kentucky as part of the Angel phase of Mississippian culture. The town was not abandoned all at once. By around 1300, when Wickliffe began to empty, its population had been slowly relocating to the Twin Mounds Site, several miles to the northeast near the confluence of the two great rivers.

## Layers of Daily Life

Mound A, the Ceremonial Mound, is the largest here, and it held the ceremonial structures of what would have been the community's political and religious center. Excavations in 1932 and again in 1984–85 revealed six phases of development. Mound B tells a humbler story. It was residential, built up over 200 years, and until 2016 visitors could look into its layers: bands of charred material from cooking fires, and the postholes that once held the poles for wattle-and-daub walls. The potters of Wickliffe left their own signature. Painted and incised sherds are rare at related sites, under one percent of the pottery near Kincaid, but at Wickliffe they make up about three or four percent, in styles such as Angel Negative Painted, Kincaid Negative Painted and Matthews Incised, all of it shell-tempered.

## The Ancient Buried City

Amateur and semi-professional digging began around 1913 and went on sporadically for decades. In 1932 Fain King, a regent of the Alabama Museum of Natural History, privately paid for the museum's archaeology staff to excavate the centers of three mounds, including Mound C, the community's cemetery. Alabama State Geologist Walter B. Jones directed the work, with David L. DeJarnette as crew chief, and the first published accounts called it a scientific and educational enterprise that let the public examine a page of unwritten history. What followed was more controversial. The Kings altered the site to make it more visually appealing and offered dubious, exaggerated interpretations, putting them squarely at odds with professional archaeologists who wanted it left undisturbed. In 1946 they deeded the site to Western Baptist Hospital in Paducah in return for a monthly stipend, and the hospital ran it as a tourist business until 1983, the year Blanche King died.

## Letting the Dead Rest

For years the Cemetery Building covered the community's burial ground. The burials date from the 13th century, and they include many infants as well as people who lived with arthritis, tuberculosis and various injuries. They were residents of this town, not exhibits, and many Native American traditions forbid displaying the dead. The original remains were reinterred, and artificial skeletons were placed to show how the burials had been laid out. In 1983 the hospital donated the site to Murray State University for research and student training; it joined the National Register of Historic Places in 1984, and in 2004 it became Kentucky's 11th State Historic Site under the state parks. Today all the excavations have been filled, and a museum run by Kentucky State Parks displays the site's pottery and artifacts alongside a mural of the village as it looked from above. The site keeps seasonal hours, so check before visiting.

## Flight Context

Wickliffe Mounds lies at 36.9710°N, 89.0929°W on a bluff above the Ohio River just outside Wickliffe, Kentucky, about three miles from the Ohio's confluence with the Mississippi. From 2,000–3,000 feet AGL the meeting of the two great rivers at Cairo, Illinois, makes an unmistakable landmark just to the northwest; follow the Kentucky bank of the Ohio to the small town of Wickliffe and the bluff beside it. Cairo Regional Airport (KCIR) is about 8 nm northwest, and Barkley Regional Airport at Paducah (KPAH) about 16 nm east-northeast. River fog can fill the bottomlands on still, cool mornings, and summer haze softens the far banks.
