# Battle of Cape Girardeau

On April 26, 1863, a bluff, two disobeyed orders and a ring of Union forts turned a Confederate cavalry raid into an artillery duel on the western edge of Cape Girardeau, and ended Marmaduke's second Missouri raid.

**Location:** 37.3132, -89.5514

**Categories:** Battles of the American Civil War in Missouri, Battles of the Trans-Mississippi theater of the American Civil War, Cape Girardeau, Missouri, 1863 in Missouri, Union victories of the American Civil War

## Introduction

The letter arrived on April 25, 1863, demanding the surrender of Cape Girardeau and an answer within 30 minutes. It was signed by Confederate Major General Sterling Price, and that was the trick. Price was not there. The letter came from Colonel George W. Carter, whose Confederate cavalry had chased a Union general across southeast Missouri, and he apparently borrowed Price's name in the hope that McNeil would fear Price himself was nearby. Brigadier General John McNeil, sitting behind a ring of forts on the Mississippi bluffs, was not frightened. He refused, and the next morning the shooting began.

## A Raid for Horses and Guns

Brigadier General John S. Marmaduke launched his second raid into Missouri from northeast Arkansas on April 18, 1863. He needed supplies badly. Several hundred of his men had neither weapons nor horses, and Marmaduke feared that if he left them behind they might desert, while if he brought them along they might be armed and mounted with whatever the raid captured. His division of about 5,000 men moved in two columns. One was led by Carter. The other belonged to Colonel Joseph O. Shelby and included Shelby's famous "Iron Brigade," and the division carried between eight and ten pieces of artillery. The plan was for Carter to capture McNeil's garrison at Bloomfield, while Marmaduke and Shelby waited at Fredericktown to catch him if he fled north toward the Union headquarters at Pilot Knob.

## Two Disobeyed Orders

Nothing went to plan. Carter's column struggled across the Mingo swamps and did not reach Bloomfield until April 23, only to find that McNeil had left the town in ruins two days earlier. McNeil had learned where Marmaduke was waiting. Disobeying his orders to retreat to Pilot Knob, he turned northeast instead and reached heavily fortified Cape Girardeau on the evening of April 24. Carter, who had been told not to pursue McNeil in any direction but toward Pilot Knob, disobeyed his own orders and followed him anyway, arriving within four miles of Cape Girardeau at midday on April 25. After his borrowed-name surrender demand failed, Carter sent word to Marmaduke, who hurried toward the city with Shelby's column to back him up.

## Four Forts on the Bluffs

McNeil had reason for confidence. In 1861 the Union Army had begun four forts around the city, named simply A, B, C and D, and General Ulysses S. Grant visited to inspect the work. Fort A stood on a bluff at the north edge of town to stop Confederate gunboats, and Fort D, on a river bluff to the south, was the largest of the four garrisons, though it never saw real combat. Fort B crowned a hill now occupied by Southeast Missouri State University, guarding the Perryville and Jackson roads, while Fort C watched the roads from Bloomfield, Gordonville and Commerce. On the night of April 25, McNeil sent the city's women and children upriver by steamboat. Two gunboats and a steamer arrived with more troops, and trusting the gunboats to hold the river, McNeil shifted cannons from Forts A and D to Forts B and C on the western side. In all his forces came to about 4,000 men, with regiments from Iowa, Wisconsin, Nebraska and Illinois, though some may have arrived after the fighting ended.

## Four Hours of Cannon

On April 26, Marmaduke's full division formed a line along the western edge of the city, from near St. Mary's Cemetery in the north to the Gordonville Road in the south, centered on the Jackson Road. Burbridge's brigade held the center, Shelby's the left and Carter's the right. Cavalry from both sides charged and failed. Shelby's horsemen drove back the Federal riders, but the Confederates in turn ran into heavy fire from field artillery and the guns of Forts B and C. Most of the battle was an artillery exchange between the forts and Shelby's brigade. After four to five hours, sometime after 2 p.m., Marmaduke ordered his men to withdraw. They fell back to Jackson and then all the way to Arkansas, pursued but never caught, and the second Missouri raid was over.

## Not Quite a Battle

Nobody ever produced reliable casualty figures. Confirmed dead numbered no more than ten on either side, though some reports claimed nearly a hundred killed and more than three hundred wounded. Neither side won a clear victory that day, but by forcing the Confederates back to Arkansas, the fight became a strategic Union win. Carter was likely demoted from commanding a column to commanding a brigade, possibly as punishment for disobeying orders and starting a needless fight. Historian Henry Phillips judged that the engagement was "not of sufficient magnitude to be termed a battle," yet "all of the potentials were present for a sanguinary battle," and that it stopped short because each commander had his own reasons for not forcing the issue. Of the four forts, only Fort D remains in Cape Girardeau today.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 37.3132°N, 89.5514°W, in Cape Girardeau, Missouri, on the west bank of the Mississippi River. The Union forts stood on the bluffs and hills around the old town; look for the Southeast Missouri State University campus on the hill that held Fort B, and the river bluffs to the north and south where Forts A and D guarded the Mississippi. The Confederate line ran along the western edge of the 1863 city. A cruise around 2,500-3,500 feet MSL gives a good view of the river, the bluffs and the town.
