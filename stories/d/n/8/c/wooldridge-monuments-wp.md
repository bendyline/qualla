# Wooldridge Monuments

In a Mayfield, Kentucky, cemetery, a lifelong bachelor surrounded his grave with eighteen statues of his family, his horse and his hounds, a crowd known as 'The Strange Procession Which Never Moves.'

**Location:** 36.7497, -88.6358

**Categories:** Monuments and memorials on the National Register of Historic Places in Kentucky, Limestone statues, Marble sculptures in Kentucky, 1892 sculptures, Cemeteries

## Introduction

They have been called 'The Strange Procession Which Never Moves.' In Maplewood Cemetery in Mayfield, Kentucky, a crowd of stone figures stands in ranks facing east: a mother, sisters, brothers and nieces, a man on horseback, and hounds chasing a fox and a deer. All of them were made for one man, Colonel Henry G. Wooldridge, who spent the last years of his life gathering his family around him in stone. Among all these likenesses, he is the only person actually buried there.

## The Last of the Family

Wooldridge never married. In 1892 his last surviving sister died, and the lifelong bachelor was left with no immediate family at all. That loss prompted him to buy a lot at Maplewood Cemetery, and from 1892 until his own death on May 30, 1899, he had monuments raised there to the family members and loved ones of his life. The result fills a plot about 17 by 33 feet. Wooldridge, a Freemason, lived long enough to see his project become a curiosity: the monuments were already drawing attention in his lifetime, featured in the November 7, 1897, issue of the Republic.

## Who Walks in the Procession

All eighteen monuments face east, like most of the gravestones in Maplewood Cemetery. At the center stands the most striking figure, a life-size, six-foot marble statue of Wooldridge himself, made in Italy. The rest are figures five feet tall on three-foot bases, carved from limestone (some sources say sandstone). The largest shows Wooldridge again, this time astride his horse, Fop. The women are his mother, Keziah; his sisters Minerva, Narcissa and Susan; and his nieces Maud and Minnie (some accounts call them great-nieces). The men are his brothers Alfred, John, Josiah and W.H. Behind them run a fox and a deer, pursued by two hounds that represent his dogs Towhead and Bob. One figure is missing entirely. There is no statue of Wooldridge's father.

## Tales Told in Stone

A place this unusual attracts stories. One holds that the statue of Minnie actually depicts a childhood sweetheart whose early death in a riding accident kept Wooldridge from ever marrying; family records say Minnie was a niece. Another claims he was such a miser that money was buried with him in his tomb. Beneath the legends lies a simpler story: a man who outlived his family and chose to commemorate them in stone, down to his horse and his dogs. In September 1984 the monuments reached a national audience when they were featured on the television show Ripley's Believe It or Not.

## Broken by Ice, Made Whole Again

On January 27, 2009, an ice storm brought a large tree crashing down in the cemetery, and it broke the heads off several of the statues. Only the figures of Wooldridge's sisters and a dog were left standing. City and county officials debated how to repair one of Mayfield's few tourist attractions. Over the summer of 2010, Monument Conservation Collaborative, a Connecticut firm that restores historic monuments, repaired every statue on site. On October 21, 2010, after more than a year of work, the monuments were officially rededicated with all the figures back in place. To reach them today, visitors pass through another National Register landmark, Mayfield's Confederate Memorial Gates.

## Flight Context

The Wooldridge Monuments are in Maplewood Cemetery at approximately 36.7497 N, 88.6358 W, in Mayfield, the county seat of Graves County in far-western Kentucky. The statues are too small to pick out from the air, so use the town of Mayfield and its surrounding farmland as the reference, from about 1,500-2,500 ft AGL. Mayfield Graves County Airport (FAA identifier M25; no ICAO code listed) is only about 3 nm away. Murray-Calloway County Airport (KCEY, also known as Kyle-Oakley Field) lies about 14 nm east-southeast and Barkley Regional Airport, Paducah (KPAH), about 20 nm north-northwest. Winter brings the risk of ice storms like the one that damaged the monuments in 2009; summer brings haze and afternoon thunderstorms.
