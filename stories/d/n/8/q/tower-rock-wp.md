# Tower Rock

A rocky island in the Mississippi that Jacques Marquette was warned about in 1673, Tower Rock has hosted missionaries' crosses, river pirates and rivermen's toasts, and in the driest years you can walk to it.

**Location:** 37.6317, -89.5151

**Categories:** Islands of the Mississippi River, River islands of Missouri, Natural features on the National Register of Historic Places, Landforms of Perry County, Missouri, Protected areas of Missouri

## Introduction

In 1673 the French missionary Jacques Marquette was warned about a place ahead of him on the Mississippi. The Native people his party met, whom he called 'savages' in the language of his time, hoped to turn the travelers from their undertaking. They believed a manitou lived there, which Marquette rendered as 'a demon,' one that devoured travelers. What Marquette described was a small cove ringed by rocks twenty feet high, where the whole current of the river rushed in, collided with the waters behind it and was forced through a narrow channel beside an island. The clash of waters, he wrote, made 'a great din, which inspires terror' in the people who had warned him. That island is generally identified as Tower Rock.

## The Island in the Current

Tower Rock, also called Grand Tower, is a rock formation and landmark island in the Mississippi River, in Brazeau Township of Perry County, Missouri. It stands near the town of Wittenberg, Missouri, across the river from Grand Tower, Illinois. Marquette's account is regarded as the earliest European mention of the island, and his description of the churning water around it helps explain why this stretch of river was feared. For the people who warned him, the danger had a spiritual dimension, a presence in the water itself. Marquette, for his part, set down the physical scene in careful detail: the cove, the rocks, the island checking the current and the narrow channel through which the river forced its way. The ridge directly across the river from the island bears a fittingly ominous name, Devil's Backbone.

## A Cross, a Pirate Den and a Toast

In 1698 three French-Canadian seminarian priests, Fathers Montigny, Davion and St. Cosme, planted a cross on the rock. According to local history, its first European residents were far less pious. A band of river pirates, driven off Spanish soil west of the Mississippi, settled on the island and used it as an ambush spot. In 1803, the same local history says, United States Army soldiers, most likely sent from the post upriver at Fort Kaskaskia, broke up the outlaw haven. Meriwether Lewis wrote about the rock in his 1803 journal. Rivermen who had never passed it before, he noted, were expected to furnish spirits to drink or be ducked, a custom he compared to the one sailors observe when crossing the equator. For German Lutherans who came to America seeking a place to practice their faith freely, the rock is said to have meant something else again. They landed near here, and local tradition holds that they came to regard the island as their Plymouth Rock.

## Walking Out to the Rock

Tower Rock is normally reachable only by water. Yet the river does not always cooperate. The Missouri Department of Conservation says it is possible to walk out to the rock when the Mississippi River's Chester gauge reads below 1.5 feet. In 2003 Mike Keeley, then manager of the 32-acre Tower Rock site for the Missouri Department of Conservation, said there is usually a time every year when low water lets people walk out to it, but a department biologist said in 2023 that before October 2022 it had probably been reachable on foot only once or twice in a decade. In October 2022, during a severe drought, the river dropped so far that it was reported 'you can walk over to Tower Rock and not get your feet wet or muddy.' The rock that once terrified travelers, sheltered pirates and inspired toasts has been listed on the National Register of Historic Places since 1970, a small island with an outsized share of the river's history.

## Flight Context

Tower Rock rises from the Mississippi River at approximately 37.6317 N, 89.5151 W, near the Missouri shore by Wittenberg and across the river from the town of Grand Tower, Illinois. From 1,500-2,500 ft AGL, follow the Mississippi and look for the small rock island near the Missouri bank, with the Devil's Backbone ridge opposite it on the Illinois side; in very low water it may be joined to the shore. Nearest airports: Southern Illinois Airport, Carbondale/Murphysboro (KMDH), about 15 nm northeast; Perryville Regional Airport, Missouri (KPCD), about 22 nm northwest; and Cape Girardeau Regional Airport (KCGI), about 25 nm south. River fog is common on cool mornings, and summer brings haze and afternoon thunderstorms.
