# Fort Massac

On the Ohio River in southern Illinois, a site fortified by the French in 1757 and rebuilt by the young United States passed through war, conspiracy and earthquake before becoming Illinois's first state park.

**Location:** 37.1439, -88.6872

**Categories:** Forts in Illinois, French forts in the United States, State parks of Illinois, Ohio River, Colonial forts in Illinois

## Introduction

Early 18th-century maps mark something curious on this stretch of the Ohio River: an 'Ancien Fort,' an old fort, already standing before the French built theirs. The Spanish explorer Hernando de Soto and his soldiers may have raised a fort nearby as early as 1540. Whatever came first, the riverbank in what is now Massac County, Illinois, kept drawing soldiers for generations. French troops fortified it, the Chickasaw destroyed it, American soldiers rebuilt it, and Aaron Burr held talks there a year before the conspiracy that bears his name began to unfold. Today Fort Massac is a state park, where a replica fort has been built for visitors, though the state says that replica remains closed pending structural rehabilitation.

## The Fort of the Ascension

The French built the fort in 1757, in the middle of the French and Indian War, and first called it Fort de L'Ascension. Two years later, in 1759, they renamed it to honor Claude Louis d'Espinchal, Marquis de Massiac, the French naval minister, whose title came from Massiac, a town in the Cantal department of France. The name stuck longer than the French did. When the war ended, the French gave up the post, and sometime after 1763 the Chickasaw destroyed it. For a time, the fort that had carried the name of a minister in faraway France was nothing but a memory on the riverbank.

## Long Knives and Whispered Plans

The site returned to history during the American Revolution. In 1778 Colonel George Rogers Clark led his regiment of 'Long Knives' into Illinois near the old fort, at Massac Creek. Sixteen years later, during the Northwest Indian War, the fort was rebuilt in 1794. It soon saw famous visitors. In the fall of 1803 the Lewis and Clark Expedition stopped at Fort Massac on its way west and recruited two volunteers there. In 1805 General James Wilkinson and Aaron Burr held discussions there. How that meeting related to the Burr Conspiracy, which unfolded in 1806 and 1807, remains unclear. Still, as an important river fort in what was then the western United States, Fort Massac was tied to several events connected with the plot. Then nature intervened. The New Madrid earthquakes of 1811 and 1812 damaged the fort, and although it was repaired, it was decommissioned in 1814, ending the site's military history.

## Illinois's First State Park

In 1908 Fort Massac became the first state park in Illinois, and in 1971 its site was listed on the National Register of Historic Places. The fort itself has been rebuilt more than once for visitors. In the 1970s a partial reconstruction of the 1794 U.S. Army fort went up, but it was torn down in 2002 and replaced with a smaller, more detailed version showing the fort as it appeared in 1802. The Illinois Department of Natural Resources' park page currently says the 1802 replica remains closed pending structural rehabilitation (the notice carries no date), so check the park's page before planning a visit. Each fall, reenactors gather for the Fort Massac Encampment, which brings 18th-century life back to the riverbank. Just north of the reconstruction, a visitor center houses a museum with Native American artifacts, mannequins in period clothing and exhibits that trace the fort's long and tangled history.

## Flight Context

Fort Massac State Park lies at approximately 37.1439 N, 88.6872 W, on the Illinois bank of the Ohio River in Massac County, across the river from the Paducah, Kentucky, area. From 2,000-3,000 ft AGL, follow the wide Ohio and look for the reconstructed fort and park grounds on the north bank (the replica fort itself is closed to visitors for now); the city of Paducah lies a few miles to the southeast. Nearest airports: Metropolis Municipal Airport (FAA identifier M30; no ICAO code listed), about 4 nm northwest, and Barkley Regional Airport, Paducah (KPAH), about 6 nm southwest across the river. Expect river fog on cool mornings and afternoon thunderstorms in summer.
