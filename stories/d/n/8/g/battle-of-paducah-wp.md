# Battle of Paducah

In March 1864 Nathan Bedford Forrest's cavalry raided Paducah on the Ohio River, and an assault he never ordered on the town's Union fort cost his men dearly for little gain.

**Location:** 37.0931, -88.6058

**Categories:** Battles of the American Civil War in Kentucky, Forrest's Expedition into West Tennessee and Kentucky, Raids of the American Civil War, Paducah, Kentucky, 1864 in Kentucky

## Introduction

'If I have to storm your works, you may expect no quarter.' Nathan Bedford Forrest sent that warning to the Union garrison at Paducah, Kentucky, on March 25, 1864, hoping to frighten it into surrender. It was a bluff. Colonel Stephen G. Hicks, whose roughly 650 men had pulled back into a fort at the west end of town, knew the position could not be easily taken, and he refused. The fighting that followed came from an attack Forrest never ordered, and it is that assault, costly and fruitless, that history remembers as the Battle of Paducah.

## A Raid into Union Country

Union troops had occupied much of Tennessee since 1862, but that did not stop Confederate cavalry from riding through it. In March 1864 Forrest set out from Columbus, Mississippi, with just under 3,000 men, bound for West Tennessee and Kentucky. His goals were practical: recruit soldiers, re-equip his men with captured supplies and disrupt Union operations. On March 25 he reached Paducah, on the Ohio River, and quickly occupied the town. Hicks withdrew his garrison into Fort Anderson. Two Union gunboats on the Ohio, the USS Peosta and the USS Paw Paw, supported the fort, and Hicks began shelling the surrounding area with his artillery.

## An Assault Nobody Ordered

With the Union soldiers holed up in their fort, Forrest's troopers went to work on what they had come for. They loaded every useful Union Army supply they could find into wagons, destroyed the rest and rounded up all the army horses and mules in reach. But some of Forrest's Kentucky soldiers decided to attack Fort Anderson on their own, much to his irritation. They were led by Colonel A. P. Thompson, a native of Paducah, who was now leading an unauthorized charge against a fort in his own hometown. The assault was repulsed, and it cost the Confederates heavy and needless casualties.

## Hidden Horses and a Hard Reckoning

Accounts of the casualties differ. Union losses are commonly given as 14 killed and 46 wounded, with about 40 more captured, while estimates of Confederate losses range from about 50 to as many as 300, most of them in the attack on the fort. The raid is usually counted as a Confederate victory, since Forrest left with captured supplies and horses. In practical terms it achieved little beyond destroying Union stores and capturing much-needed cavalry mounts. The fort never fell, and control of the region did not change. What the raid did accomplish was a warning. It put the Union Army on notice that Forrest and other Confederate raiders could still strike deep into Union-held territory, even in the war's fourth spring. There was one loose end. Many newspapers reporting on the raid claimed that Forrest had missed more than a hundred fine horses the Union troops had hidden from him. Forrest, whose command depended on good mounts, did not let that stand. In mid-April he sent Colonel Abraham Buford back to Paducah, and Buford captured the horses.

## Flight Context

The Paducah battle site lies at approximately 37.0931 N, 88.6058 W, in the city of Paducah near the confluence of the Tennessee and Ohio Rivers; Fort Anderson stood at the west end of the Civil War-era town. From 2,000-3,000 ft AGL, the broad Ohio River and the city waterfront make unmistakable landmarks, with Illinois on the far bank. Nearest airports: Barkley Regional Airport, Paducah (KPAH), about 8 nm west, and Metropolis Municipal Airport (FAA identifier M30; no ICAO code listed), about 9 nm northwest across the river. River fog is common on cool mornings, and summer brings haze and afternoon thunderstorms.
