# Paducah Gaseous Diffusion Plant

For six decades a vast plant west of Paducah, Kentucky, enriched uranium for weapons and then power reactors, leaving behind contaminated groundwater, hard-fought lawsuits and, now, plans to enrich again.

**Location:** 37.1139, -88.8103

**Categories:** Isotope separation facilities in the United States, United States Department of Energy facilities, Superfund sites in Kentucky, Buildings and structures in McCracken County, Kentucky, Nuclear history of the United States

## Introduction

The machinery at the Paducah Gaseous Diffusion Plant did one patient thing on an enormous scale. It pushed uranium hexafluoride gas through stage after stage, 1,812 of them, to separate the lighter, fissile uranium-235 from the heavier uranium-238. The four process buildings that housed the work cover 74 acres, and at their peak they demanded 3,040 megawatts of electricity, supplied by the Tennessee Valley Authority. From 1952 to 2013 this plant in McCracken County, Kentucky, produced enriched uranium, first for military reactors and nuclear weapons and later as fuel for commercial nuclear power plants.

## A Cold War Factory

In 1950 the government chose the former Kentucky Ordnance Works site from a list of eight candidates. F.H. McGraw of Hartford, Connecticut, built the plant, Union Carbide ran it, and it opened in 1952 as a government-owned, contractor-operated facility. Paducah produced low-enriched uranium that was further refined at the Portsmouth plant in Ohio and the K-25 plant at Oak Ridge, Tennessee. From the 1960s onward it was dedicated to enriching uranium for nuclear power plants. Martin Marietta Energy Systems took over the operating contract in 1984, when the plant's capacity stood at 11.3 million separative work units a year, and Lockheed Martin later ran it. The Department of Energy says commercial enrichment then continued under a lease to the company USEC from 1993 to 2013. From 2001 to 2010 it was the only operating uranium enrichment facility in the United States. Enrichment ended on May 31, 2013, and the site returned to the Department of Energy.

## What Seeped Into the Ground

Running the plant took about 1,200 people, represented by the Oil, Chemical and Atomic Workers International Union. Over the years, its operations also contaminated the site. The chief concern is trichloroethylene, or TCE, a degreaser commonly used at the plant, which leaked into groundwater both on and off the property. That groundwater also carries trace amounts of technetium-99, a radioactive fission product, and polychlorinated biphenyls are among the site's other contaminants, while process equipment is contaminated with uranium. In 1988, TCE and traces of technetium-99 turned up in the drinking-water wells of homes near the plant. To protect their health, the Department of Energy began supplying the affected residents with city water at no cost, and it continues to do so.

## Claims in Court

Some of the plant's history was written in courtrooms. In the 1980s the family of a former employee, Joe Harding, sued over medical conditions they believed he developed from working at the plant; his widow, Clara Harding, eventually settled for $12,000. A 1999 class action on behalf of workers who believed radiation exposure had caused them significant medical expenses was dismissed in 2003, after a judge ruled the plant was covered by the Price-Anderson Act. A separate 1998 suit by employees accused Lockheed Martin of falsifying contamination reports, and the federal government partially joined it in 2003. In 2016 the company agreed to pay the government $5 million to settle allegations that it had violated the Resource Conservation and Recovery Act and knowingly submitted false claims for payment. Of that sum, $920,000 went to the whistleblowers.

## Unbuilding, and Building Again

Since enrichment stopped, contract workers, around 1,400 by one undated count, have kept the site running, deactivating buildings and cleaning up. Pumping and treating groundwater, along with electrical resistance heating, has significantly reduced TCE concentrations. Of the site's more than 500 facilities, a February 2026 trade-press report counted more than 180 demolished, and the Department of Energy says 33 inactive facilities came down in 2025 alone. In 2019 legislation allocated $314 million to the cleanup. In 2020 crews began safely removing one of the largest known refrigeration systems in the world, which held 3,856 tonnes of R-114, an ozone-depleting CFC with a global warming potential equivalent to 38.56 million tonnes of carbon dioxide; the Department of Energy says about 2.5 million pounds of refrigerant still remains on site, and crews finished downsizing nearly 500 process converters in the C-333 building in early 2026. Then the story turned. In August 2025 the enrichment company General Matter signed a lease with the Department of Energy for the site, with plans for a $1.5 billion updated facility, and in January 2026 it received a $900 million DOE contract to produce high-assay low-enriched uranium for the next generation of American reactors.

## Flight Context

The Paducah Gaseous Diffusion Plant lies at 37.1139°N, 88.8103°W in western McCracken County, Kentucky, west of Paducah. From 3,000 feet AGL or higher, its enormous process buildings, 74 acres under roof, are hard to miss; this is a Department of Energy site, so stay well clear, do not circle it, and check for any temporary flight restrictions before flying. Barkley Regional Airport (KPAH) is only about 4 nm south-southeast, so check that airport's airspace requirements and watch for traffic in its pattern; Metropolis Municipal Airport in Illinois (FAA identifier M30, no ICAO code listed) is about 5 nm northeast across the river. Summer haze and river fog on cool mornings can reduce visibility.
