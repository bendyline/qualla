# Columbus-Belmont State Park

On a Kentucky bluff above the Mississippi, a Confederate general tried to chain the river shut; the park at Columbus keeps the links, the anchor and the story of a cannon that killed its own crew.

**Location:** 36.7656, -89.1069

**Categories:** Battlefields of the western theater of the American Civil War, Kentucky in the American Civil War, State parks of Kentucky, Protected areas on the Mississippi River, Civilian Conservation Corps in Kentucky

## Introduction

In 1861 a Confederate general tried to lock the Mississippi River. From the bluff at Columbus, Kentucky, Leonidas Polk stretched a massive chain across the water to Belmont, Missouri, to stop Union gunboats and supply vessels from passing. Lengths of it, each link eleven inches long, are still here. At Columbus-Belmont State Park in Hickman County, the chain rests on a stone monument, a relic of the fortress Polk proudly called the "Gibraltar of the West."

## Holding the High Ground

Both North and South saw this stretch of river as strategically vital to controlling the Mississippi. Polk began fortifying the area on September 3, 1861, building on a bluff along the "cutside" of the river, where the current bites into the bank. The fort was christened Fort De Russey, after an engineer supervising its construction, though Polk preferred his grander name. Armed with roughly 140 cannons (accounts give 140 to 143) and the great chain, Columbus became the northernmost Confederate base on the Mississippi, shielding Memphis, Vicksburg and other critical Confederate-held territory downstream. It also sat at the northern terminus of the Mobile and Ohio Railroad, tying it to Confederate supply lines. Estimated to have been more than a mile long before flooding and erosion destroyed part of it, and held by an anchor that weighed between four and six tons, the chain was meant to close the river to Union traffic bound to and from the western theaters of the war.

## Grant's First Fight

On November 7, 1861, the war came to the opposite bank. At the Battle of Belmont, Brigadier General Ulysses S. Grant faced his first combat test of the Civil War. His Union troops destroyed the Confederate encampment at Belmont and inflicted considerable casualties, but they were driven back in disorder, and some histories describe the outcome as inconclusive. From the bluff at Columbus, Confederate guns joined in, among them the "Lady Polk," an experimental cannon named for the general's wife. Ten feet long and weighing 15,000 pounds, it hurled 128-pound conical projectiles at Grant's men, with a range of up to three miles.

## The Gun That Turned on Its Crew

The Lady Polk's power was also its flaw. Repeated firing during the battle heated and expanded the metal barrel, deforming it. Four days later, on November 11, soldiers fired a round that had been loaded during the battle but never discharged, and the projectile could not escape the barrel. The cannon burst into three pieces, killing Confederate soldiers (the park's account says 18, while a medical history of Confederate generals says seven) and knocking Polk, who was present, unconscious. A Federal newspaper soon mocked that "a person would be likely to consider himself as safe on one end [of the cannon] as the other." The men it killed were Polk's own soldiers, lost not to the enemy but to their own gun. The remains of the gun are now one of the park's attractions.

## What the River Took and Gave Back

The Mississippi eventually did what no army managed at Columbus. During heavy flooding in the 1920s, the river ate into the bluff, carrying away many of the earthen fortifications, buildings and artillery pieces. But when the water receded in 1925, it exposed the giant chain, and the people of Columbus decided to save it for future generations. Kentucky bought the land in 1934, and that same year, in the depths of the Great Depression, the Civilian Conservation Corps built the stone monument that holds the chain. One antebellum building survived it all: a farmhouse that served as a Confederate hospital early in the war. Restored, it now serves as the park's museum, with exhibits on the area's Civil War history and its natural and cultural past.

## Flight Context

Columbus-Belmont State Park sits at 36.7656°N, 89.1069°W on a high bluff on the Kentucky (east) bank of the Mississippi River at Columbus, in Hickman County, with Belmont, Missouri, on the low floodplain opposite. From 2,000–3,000 feet AGL, look for the bluff on the Kentucky side with the town of Columbus behind it; the broad Mississippi makes navigation easy, and Cairo, Illinois, where the Ohio joins the Mississippi, lies upriver to the north. Clinton-Hickman County Airport (FAA identifier 0KY7, no ICAO code listed; private use, permission required before landing) is about 9 nm southeast, Cairo Regional Airport (KCIR) about 19 nm north, and Barkley Regional Airport at Paducah (KPAH) about 24 nm northeast. River fog is common on cool mornings, and haze can blur the far bank in summer.
