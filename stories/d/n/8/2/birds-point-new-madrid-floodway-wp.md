# Birds Point-New Madrid Floodway

A 130,000-acre stretch of Missouri bottomland below the Ohio-Mississippi confluence, set aside in 1928 as the place the river would be sent when Cairo, Illinois, was in danger.

**Location:** 36.7000, -89.3000

**Categories:** Buildings and structures in Mississippi County, Missouri, United States Army Corps of Engineers, Buildings and structures in Missouri, Mississippi River, Flood control infrastructure in the United States

## Introduction

Late on the evening of May 2, 2011, the first of three planned detonations tore open a Mississippi River levee in southeast Missouri. Upstream, the gauge at Cairo, Illinois, had climbed above 61 feet and was forecast to crest near 63, with both the Ohio and the Mississippi in major flood. Water poured west into the Birds Point-New Madrid Floodway, a broad sweep of bottomland on the Missouri bank just below the place where the two great rivers meet. Congress had authorized it back in 1928 for exactly this moment: a designated place to send the river so that the towns upstream, Cairo above all, would stay dry.

## Born of the 1927 Deluge

The idea grew out of catastrophe. After the Great Mississippi Flood of 1927, engineering policy on the river shifted. Levees would no longer simply be raised high enough to hold the greatest recorded flood; the system would also include floodways, relief channels that could take pressure off the main river when it rose too far. In June 1927, President Calvin Coolidge instructed the Mississippi River Commission and the Army Corps of Engineers to devise a plan to protect the Mississippi's alluvial valley. The Commission proposed four floodways below the mouth of the Arkansas River and, above it, stronger, higher levees set back from the channel. Maj. Gen. Edgar Jadwin, the Chief of Engineers, rejected that plan as too costly and submitted his own. The Flood Control Act of 1928 adopted Jadwin's approach for this reach, authorizing the Corps to build the Birds Point-New Madrid Floodway in Missouri alongside the Morganza and Bonnet Carre spillways in Louisiana. The Flood Control Act of 1965 later modified the project.

## A Levee Designed to Break

Jadwin's design was blunt. A setback levee would run between 3 and 10 miles back from the existing mainline levee, and eleven miles of that mainline levee would be lowered by 3 feet 6 inches to form a fuse plug, a section built to give way first. Under the original plan, when the Cairo gage reached 55 feet, the river would spill over the low section and crevasse into the floodway. Today the floodway spans 3 to 15 miles in width, enclosed by a 56-mile frontline levee and a 36-mile setback levee around roughly 130,000 acres, with a 1,500-foot gap at New Madrid serving as a drainage outlet. Unlike the Louisiana spillways, it has no floodgates. It opens only when the levee is overtopped or destroyed, and during the project design flood it is meant to divert 550,000 cubic feet of water per second, lowering the river at Cairo by 7 feet. The call to operate it belongs to the president of the Mississippi River Commission, after consulting the Chief of Engineers.

## Dynamite, Then Liquid Explosives

The floodway was first put to work in January 1937. The river overtopped the fuse-plug levee but did not erode it enough to open the channel, so crews finished the job with dynamite. The levee was rebuilt afterward, and in 1983 it was upgraded to a fuse-plug design threaded with 11,000 feet of pipe that could be filled with liquid explosives and detonated on command. That system waited twenty-eight years. When it finally came into play in 2011, most of Cairo's roughly 2,800 residents had already evacuated in the face of the rising water, and the state of Missouri had gone to court to stop the operation. The lawsuit failed, and the blasts went ahead. The Corps later announced that it would rebuild the blasted levee to its original height, a $45 million job.

## The Cost of Being the Relief Valve

Pinhook paid the price. The small village of Pinhook, Missouri, settled by African American farmers and home to about 30 people in 2011, lay inside the floodway, and one former resident said afterward that "no one asked us." Most of its residents lost their homes and possessions, were unable to return, and the village was abandoned. That loss sharpens a tension written into the project from the start. The 1928 act included a provision to compensate landowners: Coolidge authorized a one-time indemnity paid to owners for the right to flood their land, along with the purchase of land beside the upper fuse plug, and the fuse plug was not to be built until at least half of the flowage rights had been secured. Even so, residents and landowners have fought the floodway in court since its inception. A related effort, the St. John's Bayou-New Madrid project authorized in 1986, set out to close the New Madrid gap and build two pumping stations. Environmental groups sued in 2004, and in 2007 a federal judge ordered $7 million of completed work undone, citing violations of the Clean Water Act.

## Flight Context

Reference point: 36.7N, 89.3W, inside the floodway on the Missouri (west) bank of the Mississippi. The floodway runs south from Birds Point, just below the Ohio-Mississippi confluence opposite Cairo, Illinois, to New Madrid, Missouri. From 5,000 to 8,000 feet you can trace the frontline levee along the river on the east side and the setback levee 3 to 15 miles inland on the west, with the flat, farmed floodway between them. The confluence makes an unmistakable northern marker. Nearby airports: Mississippi County Airport at Charleston (FAA: CHQ), about 9 nm north-northwest; Sikeston Memorial Municipal (KSIK), about 17 nm northwest; County Memorial Airport at New Madrid (FAA: EIW), about 17 nm southwest; Cairo Regional (KCIR), about 22 nm north; and the scheduled-service fields Barkley Regional at Paducah (KPAH), about 33 nm northeast, and Cape Girardeau Regional (KCGI), about 34 nm north-northwest. The terrain is low and flat, so the levees stand out best in low-angle morning or evening light.
