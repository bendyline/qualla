# Pierre Menard House

The French colonial home of Illinois's first lieutenant governor is tied to both the story of a fur trader turned statesman and that of the enslaved people held on his land.

**Location:** 37.9647, -89.9099

**Categories:** National Historic Landmarks in Illinois, Historic house museums in Illinois, French colonial architecture in the United States, Illinois State Historic Sites, History of slavery

## Introduction

Pierre Menard is still remembered for his good nature and his generosity toward the poor. He rose from clerk to become the first lieutenant governor of Illinois, a statue of him once stood on the grounds of the State Capitol in Springfield, and his home in Ellis Grove, with its beautiful gallery porch, is preserved as a historic site. The same property holds a harder story. In 1810, Menard held seven enslaved people; by 1830 the count was 22 by one account, and Illinois did not abolish slavery until its constitution of 1848.

## From Montreal to Kaskaskia

Menard was born near Montreal, Quebec, on October 7, 1766, the third of ten children. At about the age of 15 he left home with two of his brothers, hoping to make his fortune trading furs in what was then called the Illinois Country. He went to work as a clerk for Francois Vigo of Vincennes, Indiana, and in March 1790 moved to Kaskaskia. By the time he was thirty, he was a successful businessman. Politics followed. Menard served as a delegate to the Indiana Territorial Legislature, as a regimental major and as presiding officer of the Illinois Territorial Legislature, and he was one of the select few chosen to help draft Illinois's first constitution. From 1818 to 1822 he served as the new state's first lieutenant governor.

## A Creole Home with a Gallery

According to records and family tradition, Menard bought the land in 1802, and construction of the house began later that year (some accounts date the house to as late as about 1815). It is an example of Southern French Colonial architecture, sometimes called 'Creole,' and among its most striking features is the gallery, or porch. The two-story house is furnished with early 19th-century pieces. On the main floor are an entry hall, a parlor, a master bedroom, a dining room, two more bedrooms, a maid's room and a nursery. Behind the house stands a period stone kitchen, and the grounds include a privy, a reconstructed smokehouse and springhouse, and a historic herb and vegetable garden. Because of seasonal staffing, the state asks visitors to contact the Fort Kaskaskia site office at 618-859-3741 before a visit to confirm availability or arrange a tour.

## The People Held Here

The furnished rooms tell only part of the house's history. Menard held people in slavery on this property, and their number grew from seven in 1810 to 22 in 1830 by one account (another source lists 18 in 1830). They labored here without freedom or pay, and Illinois did not abolish slavery until its constitution of 1848. The quarters where they lived no longer stand. In 1999 archaeologists carried out an investigation to determine where the slave quarters had stood, an effort to recover at least the outline of lives that the grand gallery and period furniture do not show.

## A Statue Comes Down

Menard's reputation took a public turn in 2020. On August 19 of that year, the Capitol's governing board voted to remove his statue from the grounds of the State Capitol in Springfield, at the request of the Speaker of the Illinois House, and the statue came down in late September. Speaker Michael Madigan explained the request in plain terms: 'Memorializing people and a time that allowed slavery and fostered bigotry and oppression has no place in the Illinois House, where the work of all Illinoisans is conducted.' The house in Ellis Grove remains, and with it both halves of Menard's legacy - the generous frontier statesman and the enslaver - on the same patch of ground.

## Flight Context

The Pierre Menard House stands at approximately 37.9647 N, 89.9099 W, near Ellis Grove in Randolph County, Illinois, close to the Mississippi River and the site of historic Kaskaskia. From 1,500-2,500 ft AGL, follow the Mississippi and its broad floodplain to the Kaskaskia area to find the site. Nearest airports: Perryville Regional Airport, Missouri (KPCD), about 6 nm south-southeast, and Sparta Community Airport - Hunter Field (KSAR), about 15 nm northeast. River fog is common on cool mornings; summer brings haze and afternoon thunderstorms.
