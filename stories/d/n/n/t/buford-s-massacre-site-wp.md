# Buford's Massacre Site

Two monuments near Lancaster, South Carolina, mark the grave of Continental soldiers killed at the Battle of Waxhaws in 1780, a lopsided defeat that Patriots called a massacre and turned into a rallying cry.

**Location:** 34.7406, -80.6264

## Introduction

The first monument could not withstand its admirers. Erected on June 2, 1860, the ten-foot white marker stood over the grave of American soldiers killed in a Revolutionary War battle near Lancaster, South Carolina. Over the years, souvenir hunters chipped away at it, piece by piece, until the inscription was hard to read. On May 1, 1955, the Waxhaw Chapter of the Daughters of the American Revolution and the Lancaster County Historical Commission put up a new monument bearing the same inscription. Today the two stand together at Buford's Massacre Site, also called Buford's Battleground, a place whose very name carries an argument that began in 1780.

## The Road North

In the spring of 1780, Colonel Abraham Buford was leading a force of Continental Army soldiers, mostly Virginians, north through the Waxhaws region after the British captured Charleston. Behind him came a provincial unit, the British Legion, under Lieutenant Colonel Banastre Tarleton, whose troops were largely Loyalists. Buford's column was moving along the Rocky River Road. On May 29, 1780, a few miles south of the North Carolina line, Tarleton caught up. He sent an offer of surrender terms, exaggerating the size of his force. Buford refused, and Tarleton's cavalry charged.

## What Happened in the Field

Exactly what followed has been argued over ever since, because the primary accounts disagree. As the cavalry broke Buford's line, an unknown number of his men tried to surrender. At about the same moment, Tarleton's horse was shot and fell, pinning him beneath it. Some of the Loyalist troops, angered by what they took as treachery, kept attacking and killed Patriot soldiers until order was restored. Of the roughly 420 men serving under Buford, 113 were killed, 150 were wounded and 53 were captured, while 104 escaped. The British lost five men killed and 12 wounded. Behind those numbers were soldiers far from their Virginia homes, many of them new recruits with little experience of battle.

## Tarleton's Quarter

Patriots called the lopsided defeat a massacre, and the name stuck: locals still call the engagement Buford's Massacre, though it is also known as the Battle of Waxhaws. The battle became the subject of an intense Patriot propaganda campaign, used to spur recruiting and stir resentment against the British. Patriots coined the phrase "Tarleton's quarter" for giving no quarter in battle, even though, by some accounts, Tarleton had not ordered his men to attack soldiers who were surrendering and instead lost control of them. Historians still debate exactly what happened. In later fighting in the Carolinas, it became rare for either side to take many prisoners. The memory runs through the local landscape. The community around the battlefield is now called Buford, and Buford High School, about a mile north of the battleground, is named for the colonel. The site itself was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1990.

## Flight Context

Buford's Massacre Site is at 34.7406°N, 80.6264°W in Lancaster County, South Carolina, a few miles south of the North Carolina state line. The site sits on SC Highway 522, about a quarter mile south of SC Highway 9, in the small community of Buford; SC 522 is Rocky River Road, the old route along which Buford's column marched. The monuments are small, so use the highway junction and Buford High School, about a mile north of the site, as references. A viewing altitude of about 2,000-2,500 feet MSL works well here. Lancaster County Airport-McWhirter Field (KLKR) is about 11 nm west, and Charlotte Douglas International Airport (KCLT) is about 32 nm north-northwest; check the sectional for Charlotte's airspace. Summer afternoons bring haze and pop-up thunderstorms.
