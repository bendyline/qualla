# Brewer Gold Mine

A South Carolina gold mine worked since 1828 suffered a 1990 cyanide spill that reportedly killed fish for up to 50 miles downstream, and the cleanup has outlasted the company that caused it.

**Location:** 34.6358, -80.4156

**Categories:** Superfund sites in South Carolina, History of mining in the United States, Gold mines, Environmental disasters, Chesterfield County, South Carolina

## Introduction

In October 1990, heavy rain fell on the hills near Jefferson, South Carolina, and a dam at the Brewer Gold Mine failed. More than 10 million gallons of cyanide solution rushed into Little Fork Creek. Fish reportedly died along as much as 50 miles of water, all the way down into the Lynches River. The spill marked a turning point in a story that had begun more than a century and a half earlier, when gold first drew people to this ground. It also started a slow, costly, unfinished effort to keep what the mine left behind out of the water.

## Gold Fever in Chesterfield County

South Carolina's gold came to light slowly. Small, independent discoveries in the early 1800s gave way to serious mining in the early 1820s, and development at Brewer began in 1828. Alongside the Haile Gold Mine, Brewer grew into a powerhouse of the state's industry. By the early 1830s, these mines employed hundreds of miners and were drawing investment from outside the state. Steam power and industrial practice transformed the work through the 1830s and 1840s. Operators adopted more advanced ways to recover and process gold, and many of those methods relied on dangerous chemicals like mercury. Companies also began hiring miners who had immigrated from Cornwall, or who descended from Cornish immigrants.

## Labor That Was Never Paid

Not everyone who worked Brewer's ground was there by choice. Like many major mining companies of its era, Brewer relied on enslaved laborers. They mined ore, and they did the unglamorous work that kept a mine alive: cutting timber and growing the food that fed the miners. Their labor is part of every ounce taken from this ground in those decades. The outside investment, the steam engines and the imported expertise all rested in part on work taken without pay or consent. Any account of Brewer's early prosperity has to begin with the people who were forced to produce it.

## Chasing Low-Grade Rock

Mining at Brewer came and went for generations. Then, in the 1980s, the Brewer Gold Mining Company returned to deposits that had once been dismissed as too poor to be worth the trouble, using the cyanide heap-leach method to pull gold from low-grade ore. From the late 1980s to 1995 came what is described as the largest and most recent period of mining at the 1,000-acre site, as the company dug more than 12 million tons of ore and waste rock from three open pits. The October 1990 dam failure changed everything. South Carolina's Department of Health and Environmental Control ordered the mine closed and reclaimed, and the company built a water treatment plant to handle contaminated discharge, running it under a state permit. Then a second problem surfaced. Acid rock drainage began seeping out near Little Fork Creek, and it would not go away.

## Walking Away

In 1999, the Brewer Gold Company and its parent, Costain Holdings, abandoned the site before the cleanup was finished, leaving untreated acid drainage behind. The Environmental Protection Agency stepped in to keep the water treatment running. It proposed the mine for the National Priorities List on September 23, 2004, and the site was formally added on April 27, 2005, making it a federal Superfund site. Costain dissolved the company through forfeiture at the end of 2007, despite court demands from state regulators and the EPA. The agency carried on caring for land it had no long-term legal right to. The work since has been methodical: a site-wide investigation completed in 2010, a feasibility study in 2013, a 2014 decision to capture and treat contaminated groundwater before it reaches surface water, and a 2016 design for a new treatment plant, all checked by recurring five-year reviews.

## The Prospectors Return

Gold still has a pull here. In 2019, a South Carolina circuit court approved an exploratory receiver for the property, opening the door to a new owner who could work the land while waste treatment continued. In January 2020, Carolina Rush won the right to explore and buy the former mine, with an option originally running through 2030. Its president called early results promising, with 96 percent of samples showing detectable traces of gold. By early 2024, more than 11,000 meters had been drilled using diamond core, sonic and rotary air blast rigs. The company also paid $150,000 into escrow over 19 capped geophysical survey boreholes, money it can recover once those holes are shown to be properly abandoned. In November 2025 an earn-in agreement between Carolina Rush and OceanaGold became active, OceanaGold funded a three-hole deep-drilling program in 2026, and in September 2026 the company announced that OceanaGold had ended the agreement and that Carolina Rush retains 100 percent of its interest in the project. Nearly two centuries after the first shafts, the ground near Jefferson is still being tested for what it holds.

## Flight Context

The Brewer Gold Mine sits at approximately 34.64°N, 80.42°W, near the town of Jefferson in Chesterfield County, South Carolina. The former 1,000-acre site, with its three open pits and water-treatment works, is best viewed from 2,500 to 4,000 feet AGL. Little Fork Creek drains the site toward the Lynches River, the waterway where the 1990 fish kill extended nearly 50 miles downstream. Pageland Airport (FAA: PYG, KPYG) lies about 7 nm northeast; Bermuda High Gliderport (FAA: SC79, private use) lies about 2 nm southwest, so watch for glider traffic. For larger fields, Charlotte Douglas International (KCLT) is about 43 nm northwest and Florence Regional (KFLO) about 44 nm southeast. Summer haze can reduce visibility.
