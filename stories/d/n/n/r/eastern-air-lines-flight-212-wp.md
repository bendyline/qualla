# Eastern Air Lines Flight 212

On a foggy September morning in 1974, a DC-9 bound for Charlotte descended too low while its crew talked of other things, killing 72 of the 82 people aboard and helping push the FAA toward its sterile cockpit rule.

**Location:** 35.1539, -80.9261

**Categories:** Aviation accidents and incidents in the United States in 1974, Airliner accidents and incidents in North Carolina, Eastern Air Lines accidents and incidents, Airliner accidents and incidents caused by pilot error, History of Charlotte, North Carolina

## Introduction

Ground fog lay over Charlotte on the morning of September 11, 1974. The Automated Terminal Information Service warned of a sky partially obscured, broken clouds and visibility of about 1.5 nautical miles. Eastern Air Lines Flight 212 had left Charleston, South Carolina, at 7:00 a.m. local time on what should have been a hop of less than an hour, the first leg of a trip to Chicago O'Hare. Takeoff and cruise were uneventful. The danger lay in the last few miles, in the gray air short of Charlotte Douglas International Airport, where a McDonnell Douglas DC-9 would come down among the trees with 82 people aboard.

## Who Was Aboard

The aircraft was a five-year-old DC-9-31, registered N8984E, delivered new to Eastern on January 30, 1969, and flown for 16,860.6 hours since. Its passengers included several well-known South Carolinians. Rear Admiral Charles Cummings, commandant of the Sixth Naval District in North Charleston, was aboard. So was James William Colbert Jr., vice president for academic affairs at the Medical University of South Carolina, traveling with two of his sons. They were the father and two of the brothers of Stephen Colbert, who would go on to become a well-known television personality. Local television anchorman Wayne Seal, who had earlier worked in Columbia, was on the flight too. Of the 82 people on board, 72 were killed. Ten survived.

## A Descent Without Callouts

First Officer Daniels was flying, and Captain Reeves was the pilot not flying. The planned approach to Charlotte's Runway 36 from the southeast was demanding: a double bend while descending, then a minimum of 1,800 feet until the final approach fix, known as Ross Intersection. Only after Ross could the crew continue down to the minimum descent altitude of 1,120 feet, and they could land only if they had the runway in sight. Instead, the cockpit filled with talk that had nothing to do with the approach. The two pilots became engrossed in conversation ranging from politics to used cars, and they tried to identify a tall tower, one they associated with the Carowinds amusement park. That tower, rising to 1,314 feet above sea level, or 340 feet above the ground, may have further distracted and confused them. None of the required altitude callouts were made. The DC-9's right wingtip struck a tree, then its left wing sheared through a cluster of pines, and the aircraft broke into three sections.

## What the Investigators Found

The National Transportation Safety Board placed the cause squarely in the cockpit. Its official probable cause cited "the flightcrew's lack of altitude awareness at critical points during the approach due to poor cockpit discipline." The missing callouts, the investigators noted, compounded the crew's nearly total lack of altitude awareness. The lesson took years to become regulation. More than six years after the crash, the Federal Aviation Administration published its sterile cockpit rule in 1981, barring nonessential conversation and activity during critical phases of flight such as takeoff and landing. Airline crews still fly under that rule, and the lesson behind it was learned, in part, in the pines south of Charlotte.

## Remembering, Fifty Years On

Grief moved into the courts. Several families sued Eastern Air Lines for compensation of up to $22 million, and a jury eventually awarded $5 million. The airline appealed, calling the verdict "excessive," and ultimately settled the lawsuits out of court for an undisclosed sum. Memory took longer to find a permanent home. For the 50th anniversary, The Charlotte Observer produced a five-part documentary series, 9/11/74: The Untold Story of Charlotte's Deadliest Plane Crash. Then, on September 11, 2025, exactly 51 years after the crash, an official memorial monument was erected in the Airport Overlook area of Charlotte Douglas International Airport, beside the airfield the flight never reached. Relatives of those who died had been working to see the crash remembered in Charlotte.

## Flight Context

The crash-site coordinates, approximately 35.1539 N, 80.9261 W, lie about 3.7 nm south-southeast of Charlotte Douglas International Airport (KCLT), beneath the southern approach to the airport's north-facing runways. This is busy Class B airspace: stay in contact with Charlotte Approach and expect to be vectored. From 3,000-4,000 ft MSL the airport's long parallel runways make an unmistakable reference. Nearby alternates: Rock Hill-York County Airport (KUZA; about 12 nm south-southwest) and Concord-Padgett Regional Airport (KJQF; about 18 nm northeast). Low morning fog, the condition on the day of this accident, can still form in the Piedmont on calm mornings.
