# Congaree National Park

The only national park in South Carolina, a 26,692-acre floodplain forest with some of the tallest trees in the eastern United States, saved from chainsaws by a grassroots campaign in 1976.

**Location:** 33.7833, -80.7833

**Categories:** national-park, south-carolina, old-growth-forest, wetlands, biosphere-reserve

## Introduction

The trees here are taller than they should be. A bald cypress along Cedar Creek reaches over 167 feet. A loblolly pine pushes 169. A sweetgum stretches 157. By eastern American standards these are giants - the tallest forest canopy east of the Mississippi - and they exist because almost no one ever cut them down. Around 1905, the Santee River Cypress Lumber Company tried. They acquired most of the land and started felling cypress, but the perpetually wet floodplain held the cut logs too green to float, and operations folded within a decade. The trees the loggers had killed lay in the water, slowly returning to soil. The ones still standing kept growing. Seventy years later, a grassroots campaign saved them again.

## Smallpox, Maroons, and Cypress

The Congaree people gave their name to the river and the floodplain. Around 1700, a smallpox epidemic introduced by European settlers decimated the tribe. The land passed by royal grant, then by state grant. Attempts to farm the floodplain continued through 1860 but the spring and fall flooding defeated them - which is precisely what kept the soil rich and the trees thriving. In the bottomland forest during slavery, freedom seekers built hidden Maroon settlements - communities of self-emancipated people who used the swamp's impenetrable thickness as defense. The historical record is fragmentary, as such records tend to be, but the swamp held people who refused to be held.

## 1976: The Grass Roots

In 1969, with timber prices high, private landowners began considering renewed logging of the old growth that had survived the first attempt. A coalition formed in response - the Sierra Club joined local citizens in what became one of the more effective grassroots campaigns in the conservation movement. Congress acted. On October 18, 1976, the area became Congaree Swamp National Monument. In 1983 it was designated an International Biosphere Reserve, in 2001 a Globally Important Bird Area, and on November 10, 2003, the 57th National Park in the United States. The word "swamp" was dropped from the name, perhaps to better describe what the park actually is - not a swamp but a bottomland hardwood forest, periodically flooded, never standing water.

## Hurricane Hugo

On September 22, 1989, Hurricane Hugo made landfall in South Carolina as a Category 4 storm and ripped through the park. Several National Champion trees - individuals certified by American Forests as the largest of their species - came down. The destruction looked, at first, like a catastrophe. But the long lens of forest ecology saw something different. Snapped canopies let sunlight reach the floor for the first time in decades, triggering new growth. Standing dead trees became homes for woodpeckers, bats, fungi, and insects. Fallen trunks gave shelter to amphibians and small mammals. Hugo was a brutal pruning, not a wound. The forest is bigger now than it was before.

## The Boardwalk and Beyond

Most visitors never leave the boardwalk. The 2.4-mile elevated loop from the Harry Hampton Visitor Center is wheelchair accessible and offers the easiest introduction to a place that can otherwise feel impenetrable. You see pileated woodpeckers, hear barred owls, watch the Spanish moss drip in still air. The cypress knees crowd around the boardwalk pilings like silent watchers. Deeper in, the marked trails get muddier and the wildlife list expands: white-tailed deer, coyotes, wild turkeys, ospreys near the river, and an alligator population at the northern edge of its range. The Cedar Creek Canoe Trail and the longer Congaree River Blue Trail (a 50-mile paddling route) take visitors farther in. Ranger-guided wilderness canoe tours run in spring and fall.

## Stay Safe, Look Up

Eastern cottonmouths, copperheads, canebrake rattlers, and the rare eastern coralsnake all live here. So do white-tailed deer ticks, dog ticks, mosquitos at industrial scale, and feral hogs whose mothers are not in any mood to discuss things. The trees, however, are what people come for. Ask a ranger to point you toward the champions - they will if you sound serious. The walk to the tallest of them takes hours through standing water and uneven ground, and the forest goes silent enough that you can hear leaves shifting overhead 150 feet up. Backcountry camping is free with a permit from the visitor center, daily 9 AM to 5 PM.

## Flight Context

Centered around 33.78 degrees N, 80.78 degrees W, about 20 miles southeast of downtown Columbia, South Carolina. The park covers 26,692 acres of bottomland hardwood floodplain along the Congaree River. From the air the park reads as an irregular dark-green oval among the lighter pine plantations and farmland that surround it - the dense canopy and dark river water are distinctive. Best viewed from 3,000-5,000 feet AGL; the tall canopy creates a visible texture difference from surrounding forest. Nearest airports: Columbia Metropolitan (KCAE) 13 nautical miles northwest, Jim Hamilton-L.B. Owens Field (KCUB) 11 nautical miles north, McEntire JNGB (KMMT) 5 nautical miles north (note Class D airspace and military traffic). Watch for low-altitude wildlife viewing flights; please respect the park's natural quiet. Best conditions are clear mornings; afternoon thunderstorms common in summer.
