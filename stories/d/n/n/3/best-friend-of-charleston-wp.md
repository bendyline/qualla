# Best Friend of Charleston

Widely considered the first locomotive built entirely in the United States for revenue service, the Best Friend of Charleston carried passengers at up to 25 miles per hour - until a silenced safety valve made it the site of the nation's first locomotive boiler explosion.

**Location:** 33.9986, -81.0481

**Categories:** Individual locomotives of the United States, Early steam locomotives, Railway boiler explosions, History of rail transportation in the United States, Railroad museums in South Carolina

## Introduction

Somebody got tired of the whistle. That, in the account most often repeated, is how the first locomotive boiler explosion in the United States began: a fireman, weary of the noise from the steam pressure release valve, tied it down so it would stop. The locomotive he tended was already famous. Called the Best Friend of Charleston, it is widely considered the first locomotive built entirely within the United States for revenue service. For six months it had carried passengers faster than almost anything else in their world. Then, on June 17, 1831, its boiler burst.

## Built in New York, Delivered by Boat

The engine came from the West Point Foundry of New York, which built it in 1830 for the South Carolina Canal and Rail Road Company. Getting it south was a project in itself. The locomotive was taken apart for shipment and carried by boat to Charleston, South Carolina, arriving in October 1830. Somewhere along the way it picked up an unofficial name that stuck far better than most official ones: The Best Friend of Charleston. It arrived as a kit of parts, a machine that had to be put back together before it could prove anything at all.

## Christmas on the Wings of Wind

Its first run came on Christmas Day, 1830. After that the Best Friend went into regular passenger service on a six-mile demonstration route in Charleston, and riders found the experience startling. The locomotive was considered one of the fastest ways to travel at the time, carrying passengers, in one account's phrase, "on the wings of wind at the speed of fifteen to twenty-five miles per hour." Only an experienced horse and rider could go faster. It is easy to smile at those numbers now, but in a world where a good horse set the speed limit, a machine that came close to one was something near a marvel. For half a year, the Best Friend lived up to the promise in its name.

## The Silenced Valve

The trouble lay in a small, noisy safety device. The fireman, the story goes, had grown tired of hearing the release valve whistle and fastened it shut for good. Another version has him laying a stout piece of lumber across the safety valve and sitting on it. He may also have been deliberately pushing the boiler past its limits so the engine could handle hard work, such as climbing a gradient on setting off - a common practice in those years, and a common cause of burst boilers until tamper-proof safety valves came along. Whatever the exact method, the result was the same. With the valve blocked, pressure inside the boiler climbed past what it could hold, and it exploded. The blast was said to have hurled metal fragments over a wide area, and it killed the fireman. Accounts differ about the engineer, Nicholas Darrell: some say he was uninjured, while others describe the crew as badly hurt.

## Rising Again, More Than Once

The Best Friend did not simply vanish. Its salvageable parts were used to build a new locomotive, aptly called the Phoenix, which seems to have run up to the time of the American Civil War. A century later, the original was reborn in another form: a replica built in 1928 to commemorate the centenary of the South Carolina Canal and Rail Road. That replica was widely exhibited, spending time on public display at Norfolk Southern's headquarters at 1200 Peachtree Street in midtown Atlanta before it was trucked home to Charleston in October 2013. In May 2014, the Best Friend of Charleston Museum opened at 23 Ann Street in the Charleston Historic District, behind the Charleston Visitor Center and near the Charleston Museum. Another full-size replica is on exhibit at the South Carolina State Museum in Columbia, so the little engine now greets visitors in two cities at once.
