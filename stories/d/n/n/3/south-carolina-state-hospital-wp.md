# South Carolina State Hospital

One of America's first public mental hospitals, Columbia's Bull Street asylum was designed by the architect of the Washington Monument, briefly held Union prisoners late in the Civil War, and after nearly two centuries of care and neglect is being reborn as a new neighborhood.

**Location:** 34.0144, -81.0333

## Introduction

As Union forces closed in on Columbia in February 1865 and the grounds were evacuated, a captured Union soldier named S. H. M. Byers climbed into the attic of the state's asylum and hid. He was one of the prisoners from Camp Sorghum who had been moved onto the hospital's property, which served briefly as a prisoner-of-war camp late in the war. It was an odd chapter for an institution founded to heal. The South Carolina State Hospital, opened as the South Carolina Lunatic Asylum in the plain language of its day, was one of the first public mental hospitals in the United States, and for about 170 years its campus on Bull Street held the state's hopes, and failures, in caring for people with mental illness.

## Built for Safety

State legislation authorized the asylum in 1821, making it, in the usual account, the second such state hospital authorized in the country, after Virginia's, though some accounts rank it third. Its first building was designed by Robert Mills, the architect best known for the Washington Monument, and it was built between 1822 and 1827 with the latest innovations in fire resistance and patient security. Today the Mills Building is a National Historic Landmark. At first only paying patients were admitted, and the costs of patients without means were billed to the governments of the regions they came from. The asylum was also shaped by the racial order of its time. Admission was mostly limited to white patients, though some African Americans, including enslaved people, were admitted before 1848, when their admission was formally authorized.

## A Poem in a Wooden Leg

Byers left more than a hiding place behind. While a prisoner in Columbia he learned of Sherman's march from a newspaper smuggled in with bread, and he wrote a poem about it that a fellow prisoner, W. O. Rockwell, set to music. The song was smuggled out of the prison in the wooden leg of Lieutenant Daniel W. Tower and became an immediate hit in the North. After Union forces captured Columbia on February 17, 1865, Byers handed Sherman a slip of paper and asked him to read it at his leisure. The general was moved by the poem, took Byers onto his staff, and the two formed a lifelong friendship. It remains one of the stranger footnotes in the hospital's long history.

## Crowded Wards

After the war the hospital grew, expanding the Mills Building and adding new ones across the campus. It opened a nursing school in 1892 that ran until 1950, and in 1896 it became the South Carolina State Hospital for the Insane. By 1900 it held more than 1,000 patients. By 1910 the campus was at capacity and, like many similar institutions nationwide, it was underfunded and understaffed, and its patients were not well cared for. A second campus was then opened north of Columbia for African American patients, first called Palmetto State Hospital and later Crafts-Farrow Hospital. Problems with staffing, funding and patient conditions persisted through the twentieth century. The state began shifting mental health care into community settings, and legal action in the 1980s over patient care and funding pushed a more focused effort to reduce the hospital's population. In 1996 the two campuses were consolidated into 410 beds, and the State Hospital closed in the late 1990s. Historic Columbia's records date the end of patient care on Bull Street to 1989 and its official abandonment to 1996.

## Bull Street Reborn

For years afterward, the old buildings held offices and storage for the state Department of Mental Health, and the Mills Building housed the Department of Health and Environmental Control. In October 2014 the department sold the first parcels to private owners, and by January 2021 the entire property, long known simply as "Bull Street," had passed into private hands. The proceeds must be used to benefit the agency's patients, and by August 2020 the state's Mental Health Commission had authorized spending part of them on additional community housing. The campus is becoming the BullStreet District, a 181-acre mixed-use redevelopment. On September 12, 2020, fire destroyed part of the central Babcock Building, but developers pressed ahead, and leasing of the first apartments in the building began in 2022.
