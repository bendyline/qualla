# Mann-Simons Cottage

Bought in 1843 for Celia Mann, a Charleston-born midwife who had been enslaved, this Columbia lot stayed in one Black family for 127 years and was saved from demolition by a community campaign led by her great-great-granddaughter.

**Location:** 34.0117, -81.0344

**Categories:** African-American museums in South Carolina, Historic house museums in South Carolina, Houses on the National Register of Historic Places in South Carolina, Houses in Columbia, South Carolina, African-American history of South Carolina

## Introduction

Celia Mann signed her will with an "X." Dated August 25, 1867, it left a house and lot in Columbia, South Carolina, to her daughter Agnes, and two weeks later, on September 8, Celia died. She had been born into slavery in Charleston in 1799. By the end of her life she was a midwife and a prominent figure in Columbia's free Black community, and the lot she left would stay in her family for well over a century. The house that stands there today, the Mann-Simons Cottage at the corner of Richland and Marion streets, is a museum of that community's economic and cultural life through the Antebellum and Reconstruction eras.

## Bought by a Boatman

Celia's husband, Ben DeLane, bought his own freedom with wages earned as a boatman in the river trade, and Historic Columbia says he later bought Celia's freedom too. In 1843 he purchased a lot measuring 208 by 104 feet at Lumber and Marion streets from James S. Guinard, for Celia. The records of those years carry the marks of slavery's law. The 1840 census lists DeLane as the owner of three enslaved people, most likely Celia, her daughter Agnes and his own daughter Mary. Records do not say when Celia gained her freedom, only that she was certainly free by 1850, when the census lists her. By then the family belonged to a small community: free Black households made up 6 percent of Columbia's population that year. After the Civil War, Calvary Baptist Church was organized in Celia's house, the first of several Black Baptist congregations to meet there.

## Agnes and the Musician

Agnes Jackson was born in South Carolina in 1831. The document recording the 1843 sale calls her "the daughter of the said Celia," a phrase suggesting that DeLane was not her biological father. Her first child, Hattie, was born in 1853, and sometime before 1858 she married William "Bill" Simons, a free man of color and a popular musician. After Celia's death the Simonses transformed the property. Sometime between about 1872 and 1883 they replaced the old hall-and-parlor house with the 1½-story "cottage style" home that survives, with a raised porch, four Tuscan columns carrying the gabled roof, three unevenly spaced dormers and a raised basement. They added other buildings too, including one that served as a lunch counter from 1891 to 1904 and later as a grocery.

## A Landlord on Marion Street

When Agnes died in 1907, the property passed to her son Charles, a deacon in the Baptist church, a Freemason and a prominent man in Columbia's Black community. In 1912 he bought adjoining lots on Marion Street and built three two-story American foursquare houses to rent. The west side of Marion Street had been a historically Black neighborhood, but after 1913 Simons rented only to white tenants, most of them immigrants from Eastern and Southern Europe, reflecting larger changes in the city. Those rentals gave him a middle-class life that remained out of reach for most Black South Carolinians of his time. His widow, Amanda Green Simons, a dressmaker respected in her own right, inherited the property in 1933 and sold the rental houses, shrinking the holdings back to their original size. When she died in 1960, the house passed to her niece and adopted daughter, Bernice Conners.

## A Family Home Reclaimed

Conners was the last of the family to live in the house. In 1970 the Columbia Housing Authority acquired the property and announced plans to demolish it for a low-income senior apartment complex. Robbie Atkinson, Celia Mann's great-great-granddaughter, led a grassroots campaign to stop it, supported substantially by the Black community, and in 1973 the house was listed on the National Register of Historic Places. The Richland County Historic Preservation Society bought it and opened it as a house museum five years later. In 1994 the City of Columbia purchased the site and placed it in the care of the Historic Columbia Foundation. Archaeological work from 2006 to 2012 reshaped what the site could say about the family, and from 2014 to 2016 community concerns and oversight guided a new interpretation. Black organizations such as the Wisteria Garden Club and Calvary Baptist Church, the congregation born in Celia's house, remain involved in its interpretation and stewardship.
