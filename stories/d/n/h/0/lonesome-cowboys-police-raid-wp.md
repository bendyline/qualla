# Lonesome Cowboys police raid

Six weeks after Stonewall, Atlanta police raided a screening of Andy Warhol's Lonesome Cowboys at a small Ansley Mall cinema, and the anger it stirred helped launch the Georgia Gay Liberation Front and, in time, Atlanta Pride.

**Location:** 33.7988, -84.3715

**Categories:** History of LGBTQ civil rights in the United States, 1969 in LGBTQ history, History of Atlanta, 1960s in Atlanta, 1969 in American cinema

## Introduction

"The Stonewall of the South that History Forgot." That is how a 2019 Smithsonian article described what happened on the night of August 5, 1969, at the Ansley Mall Mini-Cinema in Atlanta. The theater, in Ansley Park near the gay neighborhood of Midtown, was known for showing independent films, and that night it was screening Andy Warhol's Lonesome Cowboys. About fifteen minutes into the movie, the Atlanta Police Department raided it. The raid targeted members of the city's LGBT community, and its aftermath set off a push for gay liberation in Atlanta that still echoes every year in one of the largest Pride celebrations in the country.

## A Film Under Watch

Lonesome Cowboys was a 1968 underground film that satirized westerns. Historians Harry M. Benshoff and Sean Griffin wrote that the movie, featuring "sexy cowboys and a cross-dressing sheriff," "explore[d] and exploit[ed] homosocial-homosexual boundaries," and it depicted gay sex. The LGBT-interest magazine The Advocate reviewed it positively, but many critics attacked it for its homosexual content. The film had drawn official suspicion from the start. The Federal Bureau of Investigation monitored both its production and its San Francisco premiere, treating it as a possible national security risk, and in London an entire audience was arrested during a screening.

## Fifteen Minutes In

Ten police officers carried out the raid in Atlanta, and three of them blocked the exits so that no one in the audience could leave. Officers seized the film's release print and arrested the theater's manager. Almost all of the 70 to 100 people in the audience (accounts differ) were photographed by police, and many were harassed and eventually arrested, on charges that included public indecency and drug possession. It came just six weeks after the Stonewall riots in New York City, the uprising credited with opening a more radical era in the movement for LGBT rights. In Atlanta, that new era had not yet arrived.

## The Spark

The answer came from within the community. After the raid, members of Atlanta's LGBT community gathered for a meeting at the New Morning Café near Emory University, and there they founded the Georgia Gay Liberation Front. On the first anniversary of Stonewall, in 1970, activists handed out literature about the new group at the Piedmont Park Arts Festival. In 1971, when the city refused to grant a parade permit, about 125 marchers took to the sidewalks near Peachtree Street and Piedmont Park anyway. By 1972, permits in hand, they held an official protest march and Atlanta's first official Pride. Atlanta Pride, which grew into one of the largest pride parades in the United States, began in part as a response to that August night. "I truly believe the Lonesome Cowboys raid was the spark that ignited the Atlanta homosexual population," Abby Drue, an activist who was present at the raid, told Smithsonian in 2019. The magazine compared the night to other turning points in gay liberation, including the Dewey's sit-ins in Philadelphia and the UpStairs Lounge arson attack in New Orleans.

## Flight Context

The raid took place at 33.7988°N, 84.3715°W, at Ansley Mall on the edge of Atlanta's Ansley Park neighborhood, just north of Midtown. Piedmont Park, where activists handed out their first Gay Liberation Front literature in 1970, lies just to the south, its green expanse an easy landmark from 3,000-4,000 feet MSL, with the Midtown towers rising just beyond it. The area sits beneath Atlanta's Class B airspace, so sightseeing requires ATC coordination. DeKalb-Peachtree Airport (KPDK) is about 6 nm to the northeast, Fulton County Airport-Brown Field (KFTY) about 8 nm to the west, and Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International (KATL) about 10 nm to the south-southwest. Expect summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms.
