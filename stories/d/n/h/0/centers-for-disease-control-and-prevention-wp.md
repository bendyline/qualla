# Centers for Disease Control and Prevention

Born in 1946 to fight malaria-carrying mosquitoes in the American South, the CDC grew from a $10 land deal on Atlanta's Clifton Road into the nation's public health agency, with triumphs, dark chapters and hard tests along the way.

**Location:** 33.7994, -84.3283

**Categories:** Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, Public health, Medical research institutes in the United States, Organizations based in Atlanta, Government agencies established in 1946

## Introduction

The land cost ten dollars. In 1947, employees of a young federal agency in Atlanta pooled their money to make a token payment to Emory University for 15 acres on Clifton Road. The real benefactor was Robert W. Woodruff, chairman of the Coca-Cola Company, who had a long-standing interest in malaria because it had been a problem in areas where he went hunting. That modest plot is still the headquarters of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, the national public health agency of the United States, whose work now reaches from American communities to outbreak zones around the world.

## War on the Mosquito

The agency began on July 1, 1946, as the Communicable Disease Center, successor to the World War II program called Malaria Control in War Areas. Atlanta was chosen because malaria was endemic in the Southern United States, and the first offices occupied the sixth floor of the Volunteer Building on Peachtree Street.

The early CDC looked less like a laboratory than a pest-control operation. Its budget was about $1 million, and 59 percent of its personnel worked on mosquito abatement and habitat control. Among its 369 employees, the main jobs were entomology and engineering, and in 1946 there were only seven medical officers on duty. In its first years, crews sprayed more than six and a half million homes, mostly with DDT. Under Joseph Walter Mountin, the agency pushed to take on other communicable diseases as well, and its mandate grew: sexually transmitted diseases in 1957, tuberculosis control in 1960 and an immunization program in 1963.

## Clifton Road

Although the Clifton Road land was donated in 1947, the campus did not open until 1960. Now called the Roybal Campus, after the late Representative Edward R. Roybal, it is the largest of the agency's three major Atlanta-area campuses. Its Building 18, opened in 2005, contains the premier biosafety level 4 laboratory in the United States, and the CDC holds one of only two official repositories of smallpox in the world; the other is in Russia.

The name kept evolving too. It became the National Communicable Disease Center in 1967 and the Center for Disease Control in 1970. In 1992 Congress added "and Prevention," but directed that the familiar initials CDC be kept because of their name recognition. The headquarters sat in unincorporated DeKalb County until January 1, 2018, when the city of Atlanta annexed it as part of its largest annexation in 65 years.

## Disease Detectives and a Dark Inheritance

Among the agency's best-known programs is the Epidemic Intelligence Service, whose "boots-on-the-ground disease detectives" investigate outbreaks at home and abroad on short assignments called "Epi-Aids." During the 2014 Ebola outbreak in West Africa, two infected American aid workers were flown home for treatment at Emory University Hospital, whose specially built isolation unit for highly infectious diseases was set up in collaboration with the CDC.

The agency's history also carries one of the gravest wrongs in American medicine. The CDC inherited from its predecessor, the U.S. Public Health Service, the Tuskegee syphilis study, which ran from 1932 to 1972. For forty years, a group of Black men, nearly 400 of whom had syphilis, were studied while their disease was left untreated. None of them had given informed consent to serve as research subjects.

## Ready for Anything, Even Zombies

Sometimes the agency's challenge is simply getting people to listen. In 2011, as his team planned its annual hurricane campaign, communications official David Daigle mused, "we say pretty much the same things every year, in the same way, and I just wonder how many people are paying attention." A social media colleague mentioned that zombies had come up often on Twitter while she was posting about radiation after the Fukushima nuclear disaster.

On May 16, 2011, the CDC's blog told the public how to prepare for a zombie invasion. It never claimed such a thing was possible; the point was to reach a new audience before hurricane season and urge readiness for earthquakes, tornadoes and floods. "The whole idea was, if you're prepared for a zombie apocalypse, you're prepared for pretty much anything," Daigle said. A "CDC Zombie Task Force" judged a YouTube video contest, and the agency released a zombie-themed graphic novella.

## A Trust Tested

The COVID-19 pandemic brought the agency its hardest scrutiny. Its first coronavirus test malfunctioned in February 2020, and it took about three weeks to sort out the failed kits. In 2022, director Rochelle Walensky acknowledged "some pretty dramatic, pretty public mistakes," adding that "in our big moment, our performance did not reliably meet expectations." Polls tracked a steep fall in public trust.

The years that followed brought further upheaval, including nearly 1,300 probationary employees forced out in February 2025 and repeated leadership changes, which public health experts warned undermined the agency's capacity. On August 8, 2025, a gunman fired nearly 500 rounds at the Roybal Campus, about 200 of which struck buildings there, including at least four CDC buildings. DeKalb County police officer David Rose, 33, was fatally wounded as he arrived at the scene. His death is now part of the story of Clifton Road, too.

## Flight Context

The CDC's Roybal Campus sits at about 33.799 N, 84.328 W on Clifton Road in northeast Atlanta, beside Emory University. From 2,500 to 3,500 feet AGL, look for the cluster of modern office and laboratory buildings alongside the Emory University campus, with the downtown and Midtown Atlanta skylines to the southwest. DeKalb-Peachtree Airport (KPDK) is about 5 nm to the north-northeast, Fulton County Airport-Brown Field (KFTY) about 10 nm to the west, and Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International Airport (KATL) about 11 nm to the south-southwest. This is busy, complex airspace; plan routes carefully and expect summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms.
