# Piedmont Park

Atlanta's great park began as a gentleman's farm and a horse track, hosted two grand expositions, including the 1895 world's fair, and Booker T. Washington's most famous speech, and became the city's most visited green space.

**Location:** 33.7861, -84.3733

**Categories:** Parks in Atlanta, National Register of Historic Places in Atlanta, World's fair sites in the United States, Urban forests in the United States, Sports history

## Introduction

The great exposition halls of Piedmont Park were built to be taken apart. For the Cotton States and International Exposition of 1895, Atlanta put up temporary exposition halls, then tore most of them down after the fair and sold the salvaged materials. What survives of the enormous main building is a scattering of stone balustrades among the lawns and trees. Around them lies Piedmont Park, a roughly 200-acre urban forest and park about two miles northeast of downtown, between Midtown and Virginia Highland, where Atlanta has raced horses, hosted fairs, started college rivalries and gathered by the tens of thousands for more than a century.

## A Farm Becomes a Fairground

The land first belonged to Dr. Benjamin Walker, who used it as an out-of-town gentleman's farm. Atlanta was booming around him, growing from 9,554 residents in 1860 to 65,533 by 1890. In 1887 Walker sold the property to the Gentlemen's Driving Club, later the Piedmont Driving Club, which wanted "to form exclusive club and racing ground for horse enthusiasts." The club agreed to let the Piedmont Exposition Company, headed by Charles A. Collier, use the grounds around the track for fairs.

Joseph Forsyth Johnson designed the park for the Piedmont Exposition, which opened in October 1887. Its main building stretched 570 feet long and two stories high. Twenty thousand visitors came on the first day, when Samuel J. Randall spoke on the resurgence of the post-Civil War South, and more than 50,000 turned out to hear President Cleveland.

## Where the Rivalries Began

Several important college rivalries were forged on this ground. Five years after the first exposition, it hosted the start of what has been called the "Deep South's Oldest Rivalry," between the University of Georgia and Auburn, then the Agricultural and Mechanical College of Alabama. Two former Johns Hopkins classmates, Dr. Charles Herty of Georgia and Dr. George Petrie of Auburn, arranged the game. Auburn won 10–0. The baseball rivalry between Georgia and Georgia Tech also took shape here.

From 1902 through 1906, the Atlanta Crackers, the city's minor-league baseball club, played on the park's fields before moving to a stadium on Ponce de Leon Avenue.

## Cotton States, 1895

The Cotton States and International Exposition ran from September 18 to December 31, 1895, drawing visitors from across the United States and 13 other countries. More than $2 million went into transforming the park. The federal government spent $250,000 on its own building, and states and nations such as Argentina built pavilions. A small pond was enlarged into the 11.5-acre Lake Clara Meer, and the fair's tropical gardens later became the Atlanta Botanical Garden. John Philip Sousa wrote his "King Cotton" march for the expo and conducted it himself.

The fair's most lasting moment was a speech. Booker T. Washington delivered his Atlanta Exposition Speech here, an address widely regarded as one of the most significant in American history. Washington urged progress for Black Americans but not integration, a position that drew criticism from W. E. B. Du Bois and other Black leaders.

## Paths, Monuments and a Playscape

In the early 20th century, the sons of Central Park designer Frederick Law Olmsted drew up a new plan. Submitted in 1910, it called for a five-mile scenic path and driveways linking Piedmont Park to the streets of Ansley Park, and the joined landscapes became known as the Twin Parks. In 1911, the first bathhouse opened and the Peace Monument, a Confederate monument, was dedicated near the 14th Street gate. Tennis courts rose in 1913 where the exposition's Manufacturers and Liberal Arts Building had stood, and demand was so great that eight more followed the next year.

Later decades added a bandstand in 1915 and a picnic shelter built by the WPA in 1936. In 1976 came the Playscapes, a modernist playground of swings, slides and a climbing dome in bright colors, designed by sculptor Isamu Noguchi with support from the High Museum and the National Endowment for the Arts.

## The Lawn Comes Back

By the late 1980s the park was deteriorating rapidly. The Piedmont Park Conservancy, founded in 1989, took on improvements and most daily maintenance and security, and it has since invested more than $110 million in capital improvements, and today the park draws more than six million visitors a year, making it Atlanta's most visited green space. A 53-acre expansion broke ground in 2008, and in April 2011 its first phase opened the park's northern third, with the Six Springs Wetlands, an interactive fountain and a much larger dog park. Dirt trails now follow Clear Creek and the Beltline Eastside Trail north toward Ansley Mall.

The lawns have also become one of the city's great stages. The Dave Matthews Band and the Allman Brothers Band played a benefit here in 2007, Paul McCartney performed for the Conservancy on August 15, 2009, and the Eagles followed in 2010. Music Midtown returned in 2011 with Coldplay and The Black Keys.

## Flight Context

Piedmont Park lies at about 33.786 N, 84.373 W, roughly two miles northeast of downtown Atlanta between Midtown and Virginia Highland. From 2,500 to 3,500 feet AGL, look for the large green expanse and Lake Clara Meer just east of the Midtown high-rises. DeKalb-Peachtree Airport (KPDK) is about 6.5 nm to the northeast, Fulton County Airport-Brown Field (KFTY) about 7.5 nm to the west, and Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International Airport (KATL) about 9.5 nm to the south-southwest. Atlanta's airspace is busy and complex, so plan routes carefully, and expect summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms.
