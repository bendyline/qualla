# Kelly Barnes Dam

A patched-together earthen dam above Toccoa Falls, Georgia, gave way in the early hours of November 6, 1977, and the flood that followed killed 39 people.

**Location:** 34.6006, -83.3631

**Categories:** Disasters in Georgia (U.S. state), Failed dams in the United States, 1977 disasters in the United States, Toccoa Falls College, Dams in Georgia (U.S. state)

## Introduction

Seven inches of rain fell around Toccoa, Georgia, between November 2 and November 5, 1977, and half of it came in the six hours before midnight on the last day. Upstream of Toccoa Falls, an old earthen dam was holding back a lake the storm had swollen far past its normal size. At 1:30 in the morning on November 6, a 200-foot stretch of the Kelly Barnes Dam gave way. The water that surged down Toccoa Creek killed 39 people and caused $2.8 million in damage. The dam was never rebuilt. Downstream, beside the falls on the campus of Toccoa Falls College, a memorial now honors the dead.

## Light for a Small Town

The story began with electricity, not recreation. In 1899 E. P. Simpson built a rock crib dam on Toccoa Creek to form a reservoir for a small hydroelectric plant, which started running that same year and produced 200 kilowatts for the town of Toccoa. The plant survives as a historical site on the college campus, the Old Toccoa Falls Power Plant. In 1933 it passed to the Toccoa Falls Institute, which wanted a steadier source of power. Between 1939 and 1940 the institute built an earthen embankment dam directly over the original rock crib dam, and after World War II the dam was raised again, creating Barnes Lake, a reservoir of 40 acres. It kept the institute supplied with power until 1957, when generation stopped. From then on, the lake was used only for recreation.

## A Dam Made of Layers

Decades of additions left an unusual structure. By the end, the dam stood 38 feet high, 400 feet long and 20 feet wide at its crest, built mostly of residual soils and silt and resting on a foundation of silt and stable biotite gneiss. It sat about 2,000 feet upstream from Toccoa Falls. There were no gates to manage floods. Two uncontrolled earthen spillways did that work: a main spillway 380 feet long on the left side, and a low point on the right, away from the dam, that could carry overflow when the reservoir ran high. Buried inside the embankment were two masonry structures. One helped support a pipe used as a low-level spillway, and the other held the penstock that once fed the power plant. Neither was in use by the time of the flood.

## The Night the Lake Let Go

Under normal conditions, Barnes Lake held about 505,730 cubic meters of water, or roughly 17.9 million cubic feet. By the early hours of November 6 the rain had pushed that to an estimated 27.4 million cubic feet. When the embankment failed across 200 feet of its length, the flood reached a peak discharge of 24,000 cubic feet per second. It tore through everything in its path. Along with the 39 lives lost, among them Toccoa Falls College students later mourned at a memorial service, the water destroyed nine houses, 18 mobile homes, two college buildings and many vehicles, and damaged four more houses and five more college buildings. Two bridges on Toccoa Falls Drive and a culvert at County Farm Road were swept away entirely. The pipe carrying Toccoa's water supply was damaged, and the city's water stayed contaminated for several days.

## No Blueprints, Only Memories

Georgia's governor, George Busbee, called for an immediate investigation, and a Federal Investigative Board of the United States Geological Survey took it on. Its report, released on December 21, 1977, named no single cause. The investigators had a fundamental problem: no engineering plans for the dam existed, and they had to reconstruct its building history from witnesses, photographs and newspaper articles. They did identify several possible or probable causes. A failure of the dam's slope may have weakened the structure under the heavy rain, and a collapse of the low-level spillway could have made matters worse. One piece of evidence looked like a warning in hindsight: a 1973 photograph showing that a slide 12 feet by 30 feet had already occurred on the dam's downstream face. The overall verdict was blunt. The dam was in generally poor condition, and its design, or lack of one, was a contributing factor.

## Flight Context

The former dam site lies at 34.6006°N, 83.3631°W on Toccoa Creek, just outside the city of Toccoa in Stephens County, Georgia, and about 2,000 feet upstream of Toccoa Falls and the Toccoa Falls College campus, where the memorial stands. The dam was never rebuilt, so look for the creek valley and the college grounds rather than a lake. A viewing altitude of about 2,500-3,500 feet MSL suits this small, wooded site. Nearest airports: Toccoa Airport - R.G. LeTourneau Field (KTOC), about 3 nm east; Habersham County Airport near Cornelia (FAA identifier AJR, no ICAO code assigned), about 11 nm southwest; and Anderson Regional Airport (KAND), about 33 nm east.
