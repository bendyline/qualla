# Camp Toccoa

In the foothills of northeastern Georgia, a hurriedly built camp beneath Currahee Mountain turned World War II volunteers into paratroopers, including the Easy Company men later made famous by Band of Brothers.

**Location:** 34.5504, -83.3973

**Categories:** Former installations of the United States Army, Military history, World War II, Landmarks in Georgia (U.S. state), Buildings and structures in Stephens County, Georgia

## Introduction

The last thing a nervous recruit needed on the road into camp was a casket factory. Yet that was the route: down Route 13, past the Toccoa Casket Company, and through the gate of a post named Camp Toombs. Colonel Robert Sink, commander of the 506th Parachute Infantry Regiment, is credited with getting the place renamed Camp Toccoa, and by the most familiar account he feared that sequence would breed superstition among the young men arriving. Under that name, about five miles west-southwest of Toccoa in the hills of northeastern Georgia, the camp turned volunteers into paratroopers during World War II. Among them were the men of the 506th's Company E, the "Easy Company" that HBO's 2001 miniseries Band of Brothers would carry into living rooms around the world.

## A Camp Built for Someone Else

Camp Toccoa was not born as a paratrooper school. It was conceived in 1938, and the Georgia National Guard and the Works Projects Administration began construction on January 17, 1940. When it was dedicated that December 14, it carried the name of Robert Toombs, a Confederate general of the Civil War. Then the wider world intervened. During the national emergency that preceded American entry into World War II, the War Department took over the site, on the understanding that Georgia would get it back once the crisis passed. The Army moved in faster than the builders could keep up. Permanent barracks became available only after the first trainees had already arrived, and the post lacked even a rifle range. To practice their marksmanship, recruits were marched 30 miles to Clemson Agricultural College, a military school in South Carolina, and fired on the college's range.

## Three Miles Up, Three Miles Down

Above the camp, at roughly 1,150 feet, stood the mountain that would define it. Currahee climbs to 1,735 feet, and every paratrooper trainee was required to run it, not once but regularly. Band of Brothers compressed the ordeal into a single shouted refrain: "Three miles up, three miles down!" The men of the 506th took the mountain's name for their own and call themselves Currahees, an anglicized form of the Cherokee word gurahiyi, which may mean "standing alone." The regiment made the connection permanent. Currahee Mountain appears on the 506th Parachute Infantry Regiment's distinctive unit insignia, in recognition of how much the slope did to shape the men who wore it.

## Wheaties with Legs

Jump training began close by, at the Toccoa municipal airport. After a training accident, though, the field's runway was judged too short for safe takeoffs and landings by C-39 and C-47 transports, and all further jump training moved to Fort Benning, Georgia. Toccoa kept the part of the job that happened on foot. The roster of units that passed through reads like an order of battle for America's new airborne arm: the 501st, 506th, 507th, 511th and 517th Parachute Infantry Regiments, attached between them to the 11th, 13th, 17th, 82nd and 101st Airborne Divisions, along with the 457th Parachute Field Artillery Battalion. Not everyone who trained here jumped. The 295th and 296th Ordnance Heavy Maintenance Companies completed basic training at the camp between July 21 and November 24, 1943. That same year Bob Hope visited the camp and is said to have given the recruits a verdict they could live with: "You guys are so rugged, you look like Wheaties with legs."

## After the Soldiers Left

Once Japan was defeated, the War Department returned Camp Toccoa to state control in 1946, as had been intended all along. Its next occupants came under very different circumstances. In the late 1940s the site became a satellite camp of Georgia State Prison, housing mainly young offenders, but repeated escapes led the state to move the unit in the 1950s to a new facility at Alto, Georgia. A manufacturing company eventually took over part of the grounds. Time has been hard on the rest. Of all the wartime structures, just one original building remains standing. The foundation that now cares for the site calls it the camp's Regimental Headquarters, while other accounts describe the lone survivor as the training camp's PX, or post exchange.

## The Trail Named for Col. Bob

The mountain has outlasted the barracks, and so has the route up it. The road the trainees ran is now the Colonel Robert F. Sink memorial trail, following Currahee Mountain Road from the site of the old camp to the summit. At its start, a commemorative plaque from the Five-O-Sinks, the 506th Parachute Infantry Regiment Association, dedicates the trail to "Col. Bob" Sink, the commander credited with giving the camp the name it carries today. Each fall the trail hosts the Annual Currahee Challenge, a three- and six-mile race on the mountain. In 2012 a not-for-profit foundation, Camp Toccoa at Currahee, was formed to celebrate the lives and contributions of the Airborne paratroopers who trained here, and it set out a plan to restore the facilities at the camp site. The foundation has since renovated the surviving building and built an entrance archway, a guard shack, a pavilion and barracks, and the site is open to visitors free of charge on several days a week.

## Flight Context

Camp Toccoa's site lies at 34.5504°N, 83.3973°W, about 5 miles west-southwest of Toccoa, Georgia, at roughly 1,150 feet elevation. The key visual reference is Currahee Mountain (1,735 ft), the lone peak directly above the site; Currahee Mountain Road climbs from the camp site to the summit. A viewing altitude of 3,000-4,000 feet MSL keeps you well clear of the summit while framing the mountain and the old camp grounds together. Nearest airports: Toccoa Airport - R.G. LeTourneau Field (KTOC), about 6 nm east-northeast; Habersham County Airport near Cornelia (FAA identifier AJR, no ICAO code assigned), about 8 nm west-southwest; Anderson Regional Airport (KAND), about 34 nm east in South Carolina; and Athens Ben Epps Airport (KAHN), about 36 nm south. Haze and low cloud can settle over the northeast Georgia foothills, so clear days give the best view of the mountain.
