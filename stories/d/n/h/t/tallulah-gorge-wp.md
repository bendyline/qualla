# Tallulah Gorge

A nearly 1,000-foot-deep canyon in northeastern Georgia, Tallulah Gorge drew Sunday crowds and tightrope walkers until a 1913 dam quieted its falls to power Atlanta's streetcars.

**Location:** 34.7250, -83.3703

**Categories:** Seven Natural Wonders of Georgia (U.S. state), Canyons and gorges of Georgia (U.S. state), Waterfalls of Georgia (U.S. state), Protected areas of Rabun County, Georgia, Protected areas of Habersham County, Georgia

## Introduction

The falls could once be heard for miles. Visitors came by train from Atlanta to Tallulah Gorge, a canyon almost 1,000 feet deep and about two miles long, where the Tallulah River roared over its waterfalls. Then, in 1913, a hydroelectric dam was finished just upstream, built so that Atlanta's city streetcars could run, and the roar was quieted. Tourism dwindled along with it. More than a century later, the gorge is protected by Georgia's Tallulah Gorge State Park and counted among the "Seven Natural Wonders of Georgia," and on scheduled days each year water is released from the dam and the river rises again.

## Stone Folded Twice

The gorge is the Tallulah River's work, carved as the water cut down through a rock formation called the Tallulah Dome. The dome itself is much older than the canyon. It formed through the double folding of the Earth's crust during the assembly of the supercontinent Pangaea, sometime between about 500 and 250 million years ago, and it is made mostly of quartzite along with schist. Those steep walls do more than impress. Because the cliffs create sharp differences in sunlight, shade and moisture, several distinct ecosystems exist in and around the gorge. Among the plants that grow here is the persistent trillium, an endangered species that grows in this river basin and in only a few other parts of the Georgia and South Carolina area.

## Sunday Crowds and High Wires

Tallulah Gorge had been a tourist attraction since the early 19th century, but the railroad changed its scale. In 1882 a railroad line, later reorganized as the Tallulah Falls Railway, reached the falls and opened the area to visitors from Atlanta and elsewhere in Georgia, and the gorge became North Georgia's leading attraction. Resort hotels and bars sprang up to serve the trade, and on any given Sunday as many as 2,000 people might arrive. The canyon also invited spectacle. On July 24, 1886, a tightrope walker known as Professor Leon crossed the gorge at its highest point, drawing crowds to the resort town. Eighty-four years later, on July 18, 1970, the 65-year-old Karl Wallenda stepped onto a high wire above the same chasm and walked across it.

## The Fight to Save the Roar

In the 1910s, Georgia Railway and Power, the company now known as Georgia Power, began damming the river. Progress had a price for the people who lived here. The company bought the town of Burton, relocated its residents, cleared the land and flooded it, and by 1919 it lay beneath Lake Burton. Many nearby residents opposed the dams. Among them was Helen Dortch Longstreet, widow of the Confederate general James Longstreet, who in 1911 led a campaign to have the state protect Tallulah Gorge. The Georgia Assembly could not raise the $1 million needed to buy it, and the campaign failed, yet her effort stands among the first recorded conservation movements in Georgia. The dam that rose above the gorge created Tallulah Falls Lake. It still diverts most of the falls' water through a 6,666-foot tunnel to a 72-megawatt power station downstream, 608 feet below the lake.

## Release Days

A state park was eventually created by Georgia governor Zell Miller in cooperation with Georgia Power, the same company whose dam had quieted the falls. The water, too, returns on schedule. On scheduled days each year, water is released from the dam, the river rises considerably, and kayakers and whitewater rafters crowd in for some of the gorge's most popular days. Filmmakers have found the place as well. Parts of the 1972 film Deliverance were shot in the gorge, and the opening credits of the 1976 film Grizzly were filmed while flying through it, with several key shots taken in one of the gift shops on the rim. Portions of Avengers: Infinity War, released in 2018, were filmed in the state park.

## A Name Nobody Can Translate

Georgians have long assumed Tallulah is a Cherokee word, a natural guess given the prominence of Cherokee history in the state's far north. The truth is less certain. The word does not clearly match Cherokee or nearby Muscogean languages such as Creek. In his book Georgia Place-names, Kenneth K. Krakow cites an authority on the Cherokee language who traced the name to the Native American words talulu or taruri but concluded that both forms are untranslatable. The name traveled anyway. Krakow notes that the actress Tallulah Bankhead was named for her grandmother, who had been named after Tallulah Falls.

## Flight Context

Tallulah Gorge is centered near 34.725°N, 83.370°W, beside the town of Tallulah Falls in northeastern Georgia. From the air, look for Tallulah Falls Lake and its dam at the head of the gorge, then the narrow, steep-walled canyon, about two miles long and almost 1,000 feet deep, running below it. A viewing altitude of about 3,500-4,500 feet MSL gives a clear look into the gorge while staying well above the surrounding ridges. Nearest airports: Toccoa Airport - R.G. LeTourneau Field (KTOC), about 9 nm south-southeast, and Habersham County Airport near Cornelia (FAA identifier AJR, no ICAO code assigned), about 16 nm southwest. Morning fog often fills Appalachian valleys, so midday light is usually best for seeing to the canyon floor.
