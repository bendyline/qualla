# Georgia Nuclear Aircraft Laboratory

Deep in Georgia's Dawson Forest, Lockheed once raised a bare reactor out of its pit to irradiate aircraft parts, trees and wildlife; today flooded tunnels and concrete foundations are nearly all that remain.

**Location:** 34.3512, -84.1440

**Categories:** Buildings and structures in Dawson County, Georgia, Military installations in Georgia (U.S. state), Energy infrastructure in Georgia (U.S. state), Research installations of the United States Air Force, Military installations closed in 1971

## Introduction

The reactor did not stay underground. At a Cold War test site in the Dawson Forest of north Georgia, it sat in a shielded pit and could be raised vertically out of it and into the open air, where it bathed whatever had been placed in front of it in radiation. Irradiated materials moved by special rail car to a hot cell, where workers examined them remotely. This was the Georgia Nuclear Aircraft Laboratory, also known as Air Force Plant 67, and its purpose was as audacious as its machinery: to find out whether an airplane could fly on nuclear power.

## Lockheed in the Woods

The facility, officially AFP No. 67, was a United States Air Force test site near Dawsonville, and it housed Lockheed's laboratory for investigating the feasibility of nuclear aircraft. The work reached well beyond the airplane itself. Researchers irradiated military equipment to see how it would hold up, and they irradiated the surrounding forest itself to study the effects of nuclear war, including its effects on wildlife. James Mahaffey, who researched the site for his book Atomic Adventures (Pegasus Books, 2017), describes a lab that tested components for nuclear-powered aircraft and studied how radiation affects equipment, the environment and animals.

## An Airport That Never Was

The laboratory closed in 1971. The land then found an unlikely buyer: the city of Atlanta acquired it as a possible site for a second airport. The plan went nowhere, because the area's topography proved ill-suited to an airport, and the city's name has stayed attached to the land, which is known as the Dawson Forest City of Atlanta Tract. Much about what happened here while the reactor was running is still hard to pin down, since documents explaining the site's work are reported to remain classified. What survives in public view is fragmentary: historical film footage of the facility, sometimes labeled Georgia Nuclear Laboratories, and accounts like Mahaffey's that piece together the experiments from the outside.

## What the Forest Kept

Almost everything above ground is gone. The concrete foundations that once held the buildings and reactors are the main survivors, along with portions of the walls of the hot-cell facility, which have been visible from the one trail that runs beside the site, where a sign recounts its history. Below ground, the entrance to the underground portion of the facility can still be reached, but the space beyond is entirely flooded except for a few feet at the top level. Fencing remains around the site for safety reasons, and anyone caught inside it is trespassing. For a place built to test the destructive power of radiation, it is now remarkably quiet, a scatter of concrete in the woods that gives little hint of the machine that once rose from the ground here.

## Flight Context

The former Georgia Nuclear Aircraft Laboratory lies at about 34.3512 N, 84.1440 W in the Dawson Forest near Dawsonville, Georgia. From the air there is little to identify beyond forest cover, since only foundations and wall fragments remain; fly at 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL and navigate by coordinates rather than expecting a visible landmark. Nearby public-use airports include Cherokee County Regional Airport (KCNI) about 14 nm west, Lumpkin County-Wimpys Airport (FAA code 9A0) about 15 nm north-northeast, Lee Gilmer Memorial Airport (KGVL) in Gainesville about 16 nm east-southeast, and Pickens County Airport (KJZP) about 17 nm west-northwest. North Georgia's foothills can hold morning fog and summer haze.
