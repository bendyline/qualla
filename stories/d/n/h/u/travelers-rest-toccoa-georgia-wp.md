# Travelers Rest (Toccoa, Georgia)

A rambling wood-frame inn on the old Unicoi Turnpike near Toccoa, Travelers Rest served as tavern, trading post, post office and plantation headquarters, and survives as a National Historic Landmark.

**Location:** 34.6092, -83.2394

**Categories:** National Historic Landmarks in Georgia (U.S. state), State parks of Georgia (U.S. state), Historic house museums in Georgia (U.S. state), Museums in Stephens County, Georgia, Houses in Stephens County, Georgia

## Introduction

At Travelers Rest, the ledger and the guest book were the same thing. The account books kept by the Jarrett family doubled as hotel registers, so the names of paying guests sit alongside the business of a sprawling plantation. One of those names belongs to George William Featherstonhaugh, an English scientist and author who passed through this long, two-story inn near the Tugaloo River. The building still stands about six miles east of Toccoa, Georgia, on Riverdale Road just north of U.S. Route 123. It was designated a National Historic Landmark on January 29, 1964, both as a well-preserved 19th-century tavern and for its role in the settlement of northeastern Georgia by European Americans.

## Cherokee Ground

The inn's story begins on Cherokee ground. Travelers Rest was built on historic Cherokee land, close to the former Cherokee town of Tugaloo, a site now under the waters of Lake Hartwell. In 1785 the state granted the land to Major Jesse Walton in lieu of payment for his service in the Revolutionary War. Walton, a veteran and political leader, did not hold it long. In 1789 he was killed near here by Native fighters who were resisting the encroachment of European Americans onto their lands, though accounts differ on which nation they belonged to.

## An Inn on the Turnpike

The Walton family sold the land to James Rutherford Wyly, who built the main part of the house in the 1810s and 1820s and opened it as an inn for travelers on the newly constructed Unicoi Turnpike, which Georgia's state parks agency describes as a busy highway over the Appalachian Mountains. In the 1830s the property passed to Devereaux Jarrett, a man the same agency calls the "richest man in the Tugaloo Valley." He made the house the headquarters of a plantation of some 14,000 acres, developed through the labor of enslaved people who cultivated cotton as a commodity crop. Jarrett enlarged the original building and kept it open to the public. As the population grew and through traffic increased, the house served at once as inn, trading post and post office, with the post office on the second floor.

## Six Chimneys and a Drawer for Valuables

The main building is large and rambling, a two-story wood-frame structure more than 90 feet long, built from wide pine planking and topped by six chimneys. One fireplace has a mantel fitted with drawers for storing valuables, a small detail that says a good deal about life in a busy roadside inn. An inside staircase leads to the second floor. The layout separated family life from business: the innkeeper's family lived at the rear of the house, with the public rooms, the dining room and parlor areas, in between. Three generations of Jarretts lived here, according to Georgia's state parks agency, until the state bought the remaining few acres of the once-vast plantation for $8,000 in 1955.

## What Visitors Find Today

Travelers Rest is now a state historic site, and the house is open for self-guided tours on weekends. Inside are many original artifacts and furnishings, some of them crafted by Caleb Shaw, a renowned cabinetmaker from Massachusetts. Around the main building stand reconstructions of typical 19th-century outbuildings. Among them are slave quarters, a reminder of the enslaved people whose forced labor built the plantation that surrounded this inn.

## Flight Context

Travelers Rest lies at 34.6092°N, 83.2394°W, about 6 miles east of Toccoa, Georgia, near the Tugaloo River and just north of U.S. Route 123. The Tugaloo River and nearby Lake Hartwell are the best visual references; the historic site itself is a small cluster of buildings, so a low viewing altitude of about 2,000-3,000 feet MSL works best. Nearest airports: Toccoa Airport - R.G. LeTourneau Field (KTOC), about 3 nm west-southwest; Anderson Regional Airport (KAND), about 27 nm east; and Athens Ben Epps Airport (KAHN), about 40 nm south. Clear, calm days give the best view of the river valley.
