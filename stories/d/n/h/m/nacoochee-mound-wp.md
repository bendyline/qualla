# Nacoochee Mound

Beside the young Chattahoochee River near Helen, Georgia, a Mississippian platform mound that later sprouted a Victorian gazebo carries a history reaching back more than 1,500 years, and an excavation here uncovered the burials of 75 people who lived in this valley.

**Location:** 34.6834, -83.7090

**Categories:** Mounds in Georgia (U.S. state), South Appalachian Mississippian culture, Swift Creek culture, Geography of White County, Georgia

## Introduction

The gazebo came late. In 1890, by most accounts, Captain Nichols, the owner of this land in the Nacoochee Valley, removed the top two feet of an ancient earthen mound and built a gazebo on its new summit, and the little structure became something of a local landmark. But people had been living beside this bend of the Chattahoochee River in northeast Georgia for well over a thousand years before anyone thought to add a nineteenth-century flourish. The Nacoochee Mound's story has been told, and retold, in several different ways.

## Two Peoples, One Valley

The earliest known residents came early. Woodland culture people occupied the site as early as 100 to 500 CE. Much later, between about 1350 and 1600 CE, people of the South Appalachian Mississippian culture, a regional form of the wider Mississippian tradition, settled here more intensively. They built a characteristic platform mound here, and traces of their villages have been found both east and west of it. Excavation later showed that the mound had been raised in two separate intervals of construction.

## Seventy-Five Lives

In 1915, George Gustav Heye sponsored an excavation here, now considered one of the earliest scientific archaeological digs in Georgia. It uncovered the burials of 75 people: 56 adults, seven adolescents and four children, along with eight whose remains were too degraded for their ages to be determined. Behind each number was a person. Artifacts found with them helped date the site. Some of the later burials held glass beads and sheet brass ornaments, signs that they were placed in the mound during the seventeenth century, when the region's peoples had come into contact with Europeans.

## Legend, Evidence and a Rebuilt Mound

Stories about the mound have not always matched the evidence. Heye argued that the historic Cherokee had inhabited the site, which lay within their homelands, and a 1734 land grant between Great Britain and the Cherokee lists Nacoochee as a town of Cherokee territory, without saying exactly where it stood. A bronze state historical marker at the site, dated 1955, goes further, stating that according to legend this is the "ancient Cherokee town of Gauxule, visited by Hernando de Soto in 1540." Such claims grew out of speculation about De Soto's path, and later twentieth-century scholars concluded that the early translations of his chronicles were not accurate. They have since pieced together his route from topographic and geographic data as well as the chronicles, though parts of it remain disputed. The original mound was excavated, though accounts differ on how completely. Afterward, former Georgia governor Lamartine Griffin Hardman had a reconstruction of it built on his estate in the Nacoochee Valley, two miles south of Helen, and it still stands. The mound sits in the Nacoochee Valley, which was listed on the National Register of Historic Places as the Nacoochee Valley Historic District on May 22, 1980.

## Flight Context

The mound lies at 34.6834°N, 83.7090°W on the banks of the upper Chattahoochee River in White County, near the junction of Georgia State Routes 17 and 75, about two miles south of the town of Helen. Forested ridges rise around the valley, so fly no lower than 3,500-4,500 feet MSL and keep well clear of rising terrain to the north. Habersham County Airport near Cornelia (KAJR) is about 13 nm to the southeast, Toccoa's R.G. LeTourneau Field (KTOC) about 21 nm east-southeast, and Lee Gilmer Memorial Airport (KGVL) in Gainesville about 25 nm south-southwest. Mountain valleys here often hold morning fog, and summer afternoons can build thunderstorms over the ridges.
