# Crawford Long

Born in Danielsville, Georgia, country doctor Crawford Long used a towel and sulfuric ether to perform a painless operation in 1842 — years before the famous Boston demonstration — and spent the rest of his life seeking credit that came only after his death.

**Location:** 34.1261, -83.2244

**Categories:** History of anesthesia, Physicians from Georgia (U.S. state), 19th-century American surgeons, People from Madison County, Georgia, University of Georgia alumni

## Introduction

The equipment was almost nothing: a towel and some sulfuric ether. On March 30, 1842, in the small Georgia town of Jefferson, a 26-year-old country doctor named Crawford Williamson Long soaked the towel, had his patient breathe from it, and removed a tumor from the neck of James M. Venable. Four and a half years later, a Boston dentist would demonstrate ether anesthesia before a medical audience at Massachusetts General Hospital. Long, born in Danielsville in 1815 and still remembered there by his childhood home, would spend much of the rest of his life trying to prove he had done it first.

## A Senator's Son from Madison County

Long was born on November 1, 1815, in Danielsville, Madison County, to James and Elizabeth Long. His father was a state senator, a merchant and a planter, and he named the boy after his close friend and colleague, the Georgia statesman William H. Crawford.

The son moved fast. By the age of fourteen he had graduated from the local academy and applied to the University of Georgia in Athens, where he joined the Demosthenian Literary Society and shared a room with Alexander Stephens. He received his A.M. degree in 1835, spent a year at Transylvania College, and then transferred to the University of Pennsylvania in Philadelphia, earning his M.D. in 1839. After an 18-month internship in New York he came home, and in 1841 he took over a rural medical practice in Jefferson, in Jackson County.

## A Towel and a Bottle of Ether

In Jefferson, Long began experimenting with ether. Among his subjects were three enslaved people, who were in no position to refuse, and on them he observed that ether produced the same physiological effects Humphry Davy had described for nitrous oxide in 1800. Then came Venable's operation, performed with nothing more elaborate than a towel held to the patient's face.

Long kept going. He removed a second tumor from Venable and used ether for amputations and in childbirth, bringing the technique into his obstetrics practice. By family tradition, in 1845 he had his own wife, Caroline, inhale it while she was giving birth, which would make him the first obstetric anesthetist. Before 1846 he had given ether to seven patients, none of whom felt pain during their operations. What he did not do quickly was publish. His results finally appeared in 1849 in The Southern Medical and Surgical Journal, and an original copy is held today by the U.S. National Library of Medicine.

## The Race He Didn't Know He Was Running

On October 16, 1846, unaware of anything happening in Jackson County, William T. G. Morton administered ether anesthesia before a medical audience at Massachusetts General Hospital in Boston. Long learned of it from the December 1846 issue of the Medical Examiner.

The question of priority shadowed him for decades. In 1854 he asked U.S. Senator William Crosby Dawson to bring his claim to the attention of Congress, and despite extensive petitioning and documented proof, he never received full credit in his lifetime. He also had a theory about how the idea might have traveled north. "A Dentist and a surgeon from Boston, Mass. were in Jefferson Jackson County in 1842, 3 or 4 and remained for several weeks," he wrote in one letter. "I have always thought it probable, that the Dentist was Morton or Wells, & that a knowledge of my use of ether in surgical operations was obtained at that time." He wrote that he had never been able to learn the dentist's name.

## Broad Street, a Big Family and a Famous Cousin

Long married Caroline Swain in 1842. They had twelve children, seven of whom survived to adulthood. The family moved to Atlanta in 1850 and then, in 1851, to Athens to be closer to friends and family, where Long and his brother Robert opened a private practice and pharmacy on Broad Street, just across from the campus. He was also a cousin of the Western legend Doc Holliday, and may have operated on Doc's cleft lip.

Long died on June 16, 1878. At his funeral, Chancellor Andrew Lipscomb described a man who "assumed nothing, and was thoroughly truthful in looks, tone, manner and action." He went on: "Modest even to the verge of timidity, he could be stern and bold, and utterly forgetful of self, if responsibility had to be met, or danger confronted."

## Credit, Arriving Late

Wider recognition began only after Marion Sims, a New York surgeon, published the first major article on Long's contribution. In 1879, a year after his death, the National Eclectic Medical Association declared him the official discoverer of anesthesia, and today most scholars agree that he performed the first successful surgery using it.

The honors kept accumulating. Jefferson unveiled a monument to him on April 21, 1910. Georgia created Long County by a constitutional amendment ratified on November 2, 1920. Emory's Crawford W. Long Hospital in downtown Atlanta bore his name for 78 years, from 1931 until it became Emory University Hospital Midtown in 2009. A 1940 postage stamp joined the Famous American Series, and the Crawford W. Long Museum in Jefferson has been open since 1957. His statue stands in the crypt of the U.S. Capitol as one of Georgia's two in the National Statuary Hall Collection; the other honors his old roommate, Alexander Stephens. Every March 30, Doctors' Day marks his contribution. Back in Danielsville, his childhood home on Crawford W. Long Street joined the National Register of Historic Places on December 6, 1977.

## Flight Context

Jefferson, where the 1842 operation took place, lies about 17 nm to the west. A cruise-level view works, but 2,000–3,000 ft AGL is best for picking out the town itself.
