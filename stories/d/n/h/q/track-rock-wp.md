# Track Rock

High in Georgia's Chattahoochee National Forest, six soapstone boulders carry hundreds of ancient carvings of tracks, crosses and circles that the Cherokee called the printed place and that no one has ever fully explained.

**Location:** 34.8821, -83.8779

**Categories:** Petroglyphs in Georgia (U.S. state), Archaeological sites in Georgia (U.S. state), Chattahoochee-Oconee National Forest, Protected areas of Union County, Georgia

## Introduction

No one knows what the carvings mean. Across six table-sized soapstone boulders in a mountain gap in north Georgia, hundreds of symbols have been carved or pecked into the stone: animal and bird tracks, human footprints, crosses, circles and other geometric shapes. Archaeologists have suggested dates ranging from the Archaic Period, between 8000 and 1000 BC, to the Cherokee, who lived here until the nineteenth century. The Cherokee had names for the place, Datsu'nalasgun'ylu, "where there are tracks," and Degayelun'ha, "the printed or branded place." Track Rock keeps its secret still.

## Stories in Stone

The Cherokee told several stories to explain the marks, and the ethnographer James Mooney, who gathered Cherokee stories, recorded one of the favorites about Track Rock Gap. In one telling, hunters paused in the gap and amused themselves by carving the figures. In another, the marks were made during a great hunt, when animals were driven through the gap. And in a third, the tracks were left as the animals climbed out of a great canoe after a flood had almost destroyed the world, while the earth and rocks were still soft enough to take their prints. Each offers a different way of reading the same stones.

## Travelers' Accounts

The earliest written account of the rocks dates to 1834, by Dr. Matthew Stephenson. A generation later, in 1867, the conservationist John Muir passed through the area and met a mountaineer who described the site to him in his own vivid words: "It is called Track Gap... from the great number of tracks in the rocks – bird tracks, bar tracks, hoss tracks, men tracks, all in the solid rock as if it had been mud." By the time a Georgia Historical Marker was placed here in the 1990s, it could call the site one of the best-known of Georgia's petroglyph, or marked stone, sites.

## Protecting the Printed Place

Fame has not always been kind to Track Rock. Relic hunters have removed parts of the carvings, and diagrams of the boulders mark the missing sections with dashed lines. The rocks now lie within the 52-acre Track Rock Gap Archaeological Area in the Blue Ridge Ranger District of the Chattahoochee-Oconee National Forest, where the Forest Service works with tribal partners to protect the site. The site does not appear among Union County's listings on the National Register of Historic Places. Then, in March 2021, an archaeologist found that vandals had scratched five of the boulders and painted two, damage that may date to 2020, and the Forest Service promised a full investigation. A gravel parking lot and the Arkaquah Trail lead to the gap, and Explore Georgia lists the site as open daily, so the boulders, carved by people whose purpose may never be fully known, depend on visitors to leave them unharmed.

## Flight Context

Track Rock Gap lies at 34.8821°N, 83.8779°W in Union County in the mountains of north Georgia. The best visual reference is Brasstown Bald, at 4,784 feet the highest point in Georgia, which rises about 3.3 nm to the east-southeast; the gap sits in the ridges to its west. Terrain here is high and steep, so stay at or above 6,500-7,000 feet MSL and keep clear of the ridgelines. Blairsville Airport (KDZJ) is only about 6 nm to the west-southwest, and Western Carolina Regional Airport in Andrews, North Carolina (KRHP) is about 19 nm to the north. Lee Gilmer Memorial Airport (KGVL) in Gainesville is about 37 nm south. Mountain fog often fills the valleys on cool mornings, and summer afternoons build thunderstorms over the high peaks.
