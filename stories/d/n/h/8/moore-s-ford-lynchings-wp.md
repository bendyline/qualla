# Moore's Ford lynchings

On July 25, 1946, a mob of unmasked white men murdered two young Black couples, Roger and Dorothy Malcom and George and Mae Murray Dorsey, near Moore's Ford Bridge in Georgia, a crime that stirred a nation but was never prosecuted.

**Location:** 33.8542, -83.5758

**Categories:** Lynching deaths in Georgia (U.S. state), Civil rights movement, African-American history, 1946 in Georgia (U.S. state), Walton County, Georgia

## Introduction

Dorothy Malcom was killed on her twentieth birthday. On July 25, 1946, she, her husband Roger Malcom, and George and Mae Murray Dorsey were murdered by a mob of unmasked white men on a dirt road near Moore's Ford Bridge, which spans the Apalachee River between Monroe and Watkinsville, about 60 miles east of Atlanta. The killings of the two married couples, sometimes called the 1946 Georgia lynching, drew national outrage, protests in Washington and New York and a large FBI investigation under federal civil rights law. Dozens of witnesses testified, and the governor said mob members were known by name. No one was ever charged with their murders.

## Home From the War

George Dorsey had served nearly five years in the Pacific War and had been back in the United States less than nine months. After World War II, African American veterans across the South resented being treated as second-class citizens and pressed for civil rights, including the right to vote, while many white supremacists sought to reassert dominance. Lynchings of Black people rose.

In April 1946, the U.S. Supreme Court declined to hear an appeal in a case that had struck down Georgia's white primary, opening the way for some African Americans to vote in that year's Democratic primaries. In Georgia, some Black citizens prepared to vote in the July primary against the resistance of most whites, and that change is believed to have contributed to the lynchings. Former three-term governor Eugene Talmadge was fighting a hard campaign for the Democratic nomination, and he held a rally of about 600 people in Monroe.

## The Road Near the River

Roger Malcom had been jailed after allegedly stabbing Barnette Hester, a white man. On July 25, Loy Harrison, the farmer who employed the two couples, posted Malcom's $600 bail and drove the four of them back toward his farm. At 5:30 that afternoon, near Moore's Ford Bridge, a gang of 15 to 20 armed white men blocked the road and forced him to stop.

The mob first seized the two men. When one of the women recognized one of the attackers, the mob took both women as well. The four were tied beside one another at a large oak tree, and the mob fired three volleys at point-blank range. The coroner estimated that 60 shots were fired. Harrison watched. He later gave contradictory accounts, at one point saying he had been "directed" to use a less traveled road by someone whose name he claimed not to remember, and police suspected he may have been involved in planning the killings.

## A Nation Takes Notice

The killings made national news. Large protests and marches took place in New York City and Washington, D.C., where demonstrators marched outside the White House demanding an end to lynching. President Harry S. Truman directed the Justice Department to investigate under federal civil rights law, and in December 1946 he created the President's Committee on Civil Rights. His administration's anti-lynching legislation was blocked by the Southern Democratic bloc in the Senate. Along with outrage over the 1946 race riot in Columbia, Tennessee, the Moore's Ford lynchings built greater support among the white public for the growing Civil Rights Movement.

A federal grand jury of 23, including two African Americans, heard nearly three weeks of testimony. Governor Ellis Arnall claimed that 15 to 20 mob members were known by name. Dorothy's mother, Moena Williams, told the jurors her daughter had died on her twentieth birthday. The grand jury was nonetheless "unable to establish the identity of any persons guilty."

## The Cost of Speaking

Testifying carried its own danger. On January 1, 1947, brothers James and Tom Verner confronted Lamar Howard, a young African American who had testified before the secret grand jury, at the municipal ice house where he worked. "What did you tell 'em down at Athens?" James asked. They beat him for 10 or 15 minutes while he offered no resistance, fearing he would be killed. James Verner acknowledged beating Howard until his fists were bloody, and witnesses confirmed it. The jury deliberated for nearly two hours and returned a verdict of not guilty.

Others carried what they knew in silence. In the early 1990s Clinton Adams told investigators that he had witnessed the murders as a ten-year-old and had feared for his life for 45 years, though author Laura Wexler, who wrote a book about the case, said his story had holes.

## Remembering Their Names

Recognition came slowly. A community memorial service honored the Malcoms and Dorseys in 1998, and in 1999 a state historical marker became Georgia's first official recognition of a lynching. That same year, a military memorial service honored George Dorsey, the veteran, on the anniversary of his death. Since 2005, activists have staged a re-enactment of the lynchings at Moore's Ford each July.

Governor Roy Barnes reopened the investigation in 2001, and the FBI rejoined the case. FBI files that the Associated Press obtained and reported on in 2007 show that agents investigated suspicions that Talmadge had sanctioned the killings to win rural white votes, including an unverified witness's claim that he offered immunity to anyone who would take care of a Black man, a claim that did not name Malcom. The files reach no conclusion, and Talmadge was apparently never interviewed before his death. Talmadge won the governorship that year but died before his inauguration. By early 2018, the FBI and the Georgia Bureau of Investigation had closed their cases, still unable to prosecute anyone. According to the Equal Justice Initiative, Georgia has 589 documented lynchings, second only to Mississippi.

## Flight Context

The Moore's Ford site lies at about 33.854 N, 83.576 W along the Apalachee River on the line between Walton and Oconee counties, between Monroe and Watkinsville and about 60 miles east of Atlanta. This is a place of remembrance; if flying over, a respectful 2,500 to 3,500 feet AGL keeps noise low over the rural farmland and wooded river bottoms below. Monroe-Walton County Airport (FAA D73) is about 7 nm to the southwest, Barrow County Airport in Winder (KWDR) about 9 nm to the north-northwest, and Athens-Ben Epps Airport (KAHN) about 14 nm to the east-northeast. Expect summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms typical of the Georgia Piedmont.
