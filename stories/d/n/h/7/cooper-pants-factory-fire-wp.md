# Cooper Pants Factory fire

On April 6, 1936, an F4 tornado tore through downtown Gainesville, Georgia, collapsing part of the Cooper Pants Factory and setting off a fire that killed about seventy workers trapped on its second floor.

**Location:** 34.2971, -83.8279

**Categories:** Fires in Georgia (U.S. state), April 1936 in the United States

## Introduction

By half past eight on the morning of April 6, 1936, the workday had begun at the Cooper Pants Factory, a two-story brick building at the corner of Maple and West Broad streets in the heart of Gainesville, Georgia. Around that time, a violent tornado reached the town. The factory partly collapsed, a fire broke out in the wreckage, and the only stairway down from the second floor was blocked by flames. About seventy people died there, in what is recorded as the worst tornado death toll in a single building in U.S. history.

## Two Funnels Over Gainesville

The storm had already brought catastrophe elsewhere. It was part of an outbreak of tornadoes that swept across the Southeast on April 5 and 6, and the same storm system had produced a deadly tornado at Tupelo, Mississippi, before moving through Alabama overnight. It reached Gainesville at around 8:30 a.m. local time. According to Ted Fujita, the Gainesville storm was a double tornado: one funnel came in along the Atlanta highway and another along the Dawsonville highway, and the two merged on Grove Street. Together they destroyed everything throughout the downtown area, piling wreckage as high as 10 feet in places. The tornado was later rated F4 on the Fujita scale.

## Fire on the Second Floor

The Cooper Pants Factory had stood in Gainesville since around 1893, producing clothing for the Cooper Pants company. When the tornado struck, the building partially collapsed, the electricity failed, and fire broke out. A report in the Times Leader described the building blowing up like an explosion, with fire sweeping the wreckage. For the people upstairs, the worst had only begun. The single stairwell that connected the second floor to the first was blocked by flames, and more than half of the factory's roughly 125 employees were trapped. Accounts of how many managed to escape before the fire engulfed the building vary, and so do estimates of the toll, but about seventy of the workers died. Each of them had simply come to work that morning, and their families were left to grieve in a town that had itself been torn apart.

## Hearings and an "Act of God"

In the months that followed, the people affected brought seventy-eight hearings before the Georgia Industrial Board and the Georgia Workmen's Compensation Board. The Cooper Pants company defended itself by calling the tornado an "act of God." The claimants saw it differently. They argued that the factory's machinery was too heavy for the building and that its safety equipment was inadequate. Almost a year after the disaster, in March 1937, the Georgia Industrial Relations Department awarded a total of $45,158 to the claimants. Fourteen people received nothing, because they could not show a relationship to the victims. The sums could not undo the loss, but the hearings put on record the claim that the building and its safety equipment had failed the people inside.

## A Record No Town Wants

The wider toll was staggering. More than two hundred people died in the Gainesville tornado, one of the deadliest in U.S. history, and three days after the tornado, on April 9, President Franklin D. Roosevelt spoke from a train platform in Gainesville. Yet within that larger tragedy, the factory fire stands apart. No single building struck by a tornado in the United States has recorded a higher death toll. Ninety years on, the Cooper Pants Factory is remembered less as a place of work than as a warning about what happens when a storm, a collapsing building and a single blocked stairway meet.

## Flight Context

The site of the Cooper Pants Factory lies at about 34.2971 N, 83.8279 W in downtown Gainesville, Georgia, near the corner of Maple and West Broad streets, where a 2014 Georgia Historical Society marker stands. Nothing of the factory marks the spot from the air, so treat this as an overflight of the downtown grid rather than a search for a structure; 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL gives a good view. Lee Gilmer Memorial Airport (KGVL) is only about 1.5 nm south, so remain alert to traffic in its pattern and coordinate with the airport as needed. Jackson County Airport (KJCA) is about 15 nm east-southeast, and Gwinnett County Briscoe Field (KLZU) about 20 nm south-southwest. Check forecasts carefully for thunderstorms, especially in spring and summer.
