# Amicalola Falls State Park

Named from a Cherokee word for "tumbling waters," Amicalola Falls drops a reported 729 feet in the north Georgia mountains and serves as the gateway to Springer Mountain, the southern end of the Appalachian Trail.

**Location:** 34.5654, -84.2427

**Categories:** Protected areas of Dawson County, Georgia, State parks of Georgia (U.S. state), State parks of the Appalachians, Seven Natural Wonders of Georgia (U.S. state)

## Introduction

William Williamson could not make it to the top. Traveling through the north Georgia mountains in 1832, the year of the Sixth Georgia Land Lottery, he came upon a waterfall and tried to climb beside it, but, as he wrote, "the ascent was so great that I was completely exhausted by the time I reached half way." Still, from where he stopped he had "a perfect view of the entire Fall." What he found was, in his words, "a Water Fall perhaps the greatest in the World the most majestic Scene that I have ever witnessed or heard of." His was the first written account of Amicalola Falls. Today the cascade the park measures at 729 feet, the highest waterfall in Georgia, is the centerpiece of an 829-acre state park between Ellijay and Dahlonega, and one of the Seven Natural Wonders of Georgia.

## Tumbling Waters

The name comes from a Cherokee word meaning "tumbling waters," and Williamson recorded a version of it himself, writing that the stream was called "Um-ma-eolola" from the fall. His measurements were less reliable than his ear. He guessed the drop could not be less than six hundred yards and the mountain at least three-quarters of a mile high. Modern figures are more modest but still impressive. The falls are usually given as 729 feet, an elevation change the park has long measured from the top of the falls to its visitor center. Topographic measurements put the vertical drop closer to 580 feet, and an analysis by the World Waterfall Database suggests the main plunge is 429 feet, followed by a long, gently sloping run in which the water drops another 279 feet. Either way, the water does exactly what the old name promises, spilling and sliding down the mountainside.

## The People Who Named It

Little was known by European Americans about the falls before the 19th century. The land belonged to the Cherokee, an Iroquoian-speaking people believed to have migrated in ancient times from the Great Lakes region, who controlled a large territory that included today's park. The Treaty of New Echota, signed in 1835 by United States officials and a minority Cherokee faction, forced the nation to remove west of the Mississippi River to Indian Territory. The Cherokee called that mass removal and overland journey the Trail of Tears, and many of the thousands who made it died along the way. Not everyone left. Settlers in the area knew a Cherokee woman who refused to go when her people were removed, and who lived here until the 1850s.

## Too Rugged to Farm

After the land lottery, an unknown settler was granted the land around the falls. He decided not to live on it, because the terrain proved too rugged. The State of Georgia bought the falls in 1940, and the park opened to the public that year. What changed its fortunes was a decision about the Appalachian Trail. In 1958, the Georgia Appalachian Trail Club moved the trail's southern terminus from Mount Oglethorpe to nearby Springer Mountain, and an 8.5-mile trail was blazed from the base of Amicalola Falls to the top of Springer so that hikers could reach the start of the trail from a major highway. The waterfall had become a doorway.

## Where the Long Walk Begins

Today the park is a threshold for hikers setting out on the Appalachian Trail. Its lodge serves as a starting point for them, and a shelter is set aside for long-distance hikers. Four groups of trails wind through the park, including the route to the top of the waterfall, with stairs and an observation bridge that spans the falls. There are also cabins, a restaurant and a campground. The park checks in guests for the Len Foote Hike Inn, an eco-friendly backcountry inn about 5 miles away and 4.4 miles from Springer Mountain. The park remains a Georgia state park, managed by the North Georgia Mountains Authority together with Coral Hospitality, a hotel and resort management company that operates the lodge under contract.

## Flight Context

Amicalola Falls State Park lies at about 34.5654 N, 84.2427 W in the mountains of Dawson County, Georgia, between Ellijay and Dahlonega. The falls can be hard to pick out in mountainous terrain, so fly at 2,500 to 3,500 feet AGL, keeping well above the ridgelines, and look for the white line of falling water on the mountainside. Lumpkin County-Wimpys Airport (FAA code 9A0) near Dahlonega is about 11 nm east, Pickens County Airport (KJZP) in Jasper about 13 nm west-southwest, and Gilmer County Airport (FAA code 49A) near Ellijay about 14 nm west-northwest. Mountain terrain produces turbulence on windy days, and valleys can hold morning fog and low cloud.
