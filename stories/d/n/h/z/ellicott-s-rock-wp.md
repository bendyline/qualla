# Ellicott's Rock

Two inscribed stones in the Chattooga River mark where Georgia, North Carolina and South Carolina meet, and the more famous of them usually carries the wrong name.

**Location:** 35.0008, -83.1085

**Categories:** National Register of Historic Places in Oconee County, South Carolina, Geography of Oconee County, South Carolina, Borders of U.S. states, Surveying, History of Georgia (U.S. state)

## Introduction

Two rocks sit along the Chattooga River where three states meet, and the one most people call Ellicott's Rock is not Ellicott's at all. The surveyor Andrew Ellicott cut his mark in 1811, engraving a large rock beside the Chattooga River with the letters "N-G," for North Carolina and Georgia. Two years later, commissioners from North Carolina and South Carolina chose a different large rock on the riverbank and carved "Lat 35 AD 1813 NC + S.C." That second stone, properly called Commissioners Rock, is the one commonly accepted as the tripoint of South Carolina, North Carolina and Georgia. Neither of them, as it turns out, sits exactly where the 35th parallel runs.

## A Border Worth Fighting Over

The survey grew out of a quarrel. The dispute between Georgia and North Carolina over their shared boundary even produced a brief armed conflict, known as the Walton War. A survey in 1807 followed, but Georgia refused to accept it. The state then hired Ellicott to run a new one, and his work confirmed the earlier line. The starting point had deep roots. The 1787 Beaufort Convention between Georgia and South Carolina set their line along the most northern branch of the Savannah River, and its commissioners treated the Tugaloo-Chattooga branch as that stream, which is how the Chattooga came to mark the corner. The line between Georgia and North Carolina was fixed at 35 degrees north latitude.

## Few Feet or Five Hundred

How far apart are the two rocks? The printed accounts disagree. One version holds that Ellicott's original rock lay 500 feet upstream of the commissioners' stone. The other puts them much closer. Trail writer De Hart, in his South Carolina trails guide, called them a "few feet apart," and in his North Carolina guide he placed Commissioners Rock "ten feet downstream." The confusion has kept the names tangled. The 1813 stone is often mistakenly called Ellicott Rock, which is why the clarifying name Commissioners Rock is used for it. The site was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1973, and it lies within the Ellicott Rock Wilderness, which Congress designated in 1975 under the Eastern Wilderness Act and enlarged in 1984; it now covers 8,274 acres.

## The Line That Missed

For all the care in the carving, neither rock is actually on the 35th parallel. That line runs about 230 feet south of Ellicott Rock, according to U.S. Geological Survey coordinates. Farther west the error grows. A survey run west from the rock in 1819, built on the mathematician James Camak's faulty calculations, strayed south of the parallel, and rather than correct it the surveyors ran a straight line to join an earlier one, leaving a jog in the state line known as Montgomery Corner. Because Georgia did not appeal in a reasonable time, a legal principle known as acquiescence is held to apply, and the offset boundary stands.

## Three Forests, One River

The rocks now sit in deep country. The Ellicott Rock Wilderness spans three national forests at once: the Sumter in South Carolina, the Nantahala in North Carolina and the Chattahoochee in Georgia. Through the heart of it runs the Chattooga, a Wild and Scenic River, dropping a few hundred feet as it crosses the wilderness. A two-century-old argument about lines on a map is written, quite literally, in stone beside its current.

## Flight Context

Ellicott's Rock and Commissioners Rock sit on the Chattooga River near 35.0008°N, 83.1085°W, at the tripoint of Georgia, North Carolina and South Carolina inside the Ellicott Rock Wilderness. The rocks themselves are far too small to see from the air; look instead for the Chattooga River valley winding through unbroken national forest. A viewing altitude of about 4,500-5,500 feet MSL keeps comfortable clearance over the surrounding ridges. Nearest airports: Oconee County Regional Airport near Clemson (KCEU), about 23 nm south-southeast; Macon County Airport in Franklin, North Carolina (FAA identifier 1A5, no ICAO code assigned), about 20 nm northwest; Anderson Regional Airport (KAND), about 36 nm southeast; and Asheville Regional Airport (KAVL), about 38 nm northeast. Mountain valleys here often hold morning fog, and summer afternoons bring thunderstorms.
