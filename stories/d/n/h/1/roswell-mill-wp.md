# Roswell Mill

Built by enslaved laborers beside Vickery Creek in the 1830s, the Roswell Mill grew into north Georgia's largest cotton mill until July 1864, when Union forces seized it and sent some 400 workers, most of them women and children, north on charges of treason.

## Introduction

In the summer of 1864, a French flag flew over a cotton mill in north Georgia. The owners of the Roswell Mill had planted it there, apparently in a ploy to safeguard their property, and put a French millhand in charge. The ploy failed. What followed at this cluster of mills on Vickery Creek became a largely forgotten episode of the Civil War, when hundreds of mill workers, most of them women and children, were charged with treason and shipped north by train.

## Built by Other Hands

The first mill was founded by Roswell King, a wealthy Connecticut businessman who had earlier settled in Darien, a small town on Georgia's Atlantic coast. His strict recordkeeping made him well suited to factory management. Construction began in 1836, and the Georgia General Assembly incorporated the mill in 1839. King's sons Barrington and Ralph moved to the area to help run the young business. King was an enslaver, and many of the people he held in bondage built both his home and the original mill, though the number of people his family enslaved decreased once the mill was running. Life inside was hard. In 1847 and 1848, an outbreak of mumps and measles left "over half the workers stricken" and three enslaved people dead, likely because workers lived in close quarters in dark, cramped conditions.

## Power From the Creek

Vickery Creek supplied the power, and nearby plantations supplied the raw cotton. The first building rose four stories, 88 feet long and 48 feet wide, and was later expanded to 140 by 53 feet. A second mill followed in 1853, and before the war the complex had grown to six structures, producing wool and flour as well as cotton textiles. At its height the group was described as "the largest cotton mill in north Georgia." In 1864, General William T. Sherman's war arrived at its door.

## Charged With Treason

On July 5, 1864, a Union general under Sherman's command seized the mill. Confederate forces had already burned the bridge over Vickery Creek before the Federals could reach it. Two days later, on July 7, Sherman wrote that he was repeating his orders to "arrest all people, male and female, connected with those factories, no matter what the clamor," and the workers were to be charged with treason and sent north. The order fell on the people who actually ran the looms. Most of the mill's adult men were away fighting for the Confederacy, so the roughly 400 workers taken to Marietta and put on trains north were mostly women and children. All were charged with treason. There is little evidence that more than a few ever came back to Georgia.

## Ruins and a Broken Column

After the war, one of the cotton mills and the woolen factory were rebuilt, and another cotton mill was added in 1882. The Roswell Manufacturing Company kept changing through Reconstruction and into the twentieth century. In 1897 the mills began using steam power, and by about 1920 they ran 120 looms and 12,000 spindles. Southern Mills bought the company in 1947, and textile production finally ended in 1975. Today the ruins are part of the Chattahoochee River National Recreation Area, popular for its nature trails, running paths and history. Near the mill stands a sculpture of a crumbling column, unveiled in 2000 as interest grew in a deportation that had been largely forgotten. Its inscription honors "the four hundred women, children, and men mill workers of Roswell who were charged with treason and deported by train to the north."

## Flight Context

The mill ruins lie at 34.0134°N, 84.3589°W along Vickery Creek in Roswell, just north of the Chattahoochee River, whose wooded corridor through Atlanta's northern suburbs is the best visual guide from 3,000-4,000 feet MSL. DeKalb-Peachtree Airport (KPDK) is about 9 nm to the south-southeast, and Cobb County International Airport-McCollum Field (KRYY) about 12 nm to the west. Dobbins Air Reserve Base (KMGE) in Marietta, about 10 nm to the southwest, is a military field, and much of the area lies under or near Atlanta's Class B airspace, so check airspace and coordinate with ATC. Summer brings haze and afternoon thunderstorms.
