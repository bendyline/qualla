# Bulloch Hall

In the dining room of this 1839 Greek Revival house in Roswell, Georgia, Mittie Bulloch married Theodore Roosevelt Sr. — and the house holds both a president's family story and the names of the enslaved people who built and served it.

**Location:** 34.0151, -84.3678

**Categories:** Antebellum architecture, Historic house museums in Georgia (U.S. state), Plantation houses in Georgia (U.S. state), Roswell Historic District (Roswell, Georgia), Houses on the National Register of Historic Places in Georgia (U.S. state)

## Introduction

On December 22, 1853, guests traveled for miles to crowd into a dining room in Roswell, Georgia. The bride was Martha Bulloch, known to everyone as Mittie. The groom was a New Yorker called Thee — Theodore Roosevelt Sr. — and one of the children they would raise became the 26th president of the United States. Bulloch Hall, the white-columned Greek Revival house where they married, still stands north of Atlanta, listed on the National Register of Historic Places both on its own and as part of the Roswell Historic District. Its story is a family romance. It is also the story of the people who were forced to build and serve it.

## A Coastal Planter Moves Inland

The house began with a friendship. Major James Stephens Bulloch, a prominent planter from the Georgia coast, came to the new settlement at Roswell at the invitation of his friend Roswell King. He chose a ten-acre plot and engaged a skilled builder, Willis Ball, to design and raise an elegant Greek Revival home. The mansion itself was built by enslaved people working alongside trained laborers, and in 1839 the Bullochs moved in.

It was a blended household. After the death of his first wife, Hester "Hettie" Elliott, mother of his son James D. Bulloch, the major married Martha "Patsy" Stewart Elliott — the widow of his first wife's father. Together they had four more children: Anna, Martha, Irvine, and Charles, who died young.

## The Names Behind the Columns

Bulloch soon held land for cotton, and the wealth behind those columns came from enslaved African Americans who worked his fields. Counts vary: the 1850 slave schedules are cited as showing his widow Martha, by then widowed a second time, holding 31 people in bondage (another account of the census says 19), and a surviving list associated with the house names 33 enslaved people. Most labored in cotton and other crops. Some worked inside the house, cooking and keeping it running for the family.

A few of their names survive. Rose was the cook, Charlotte the housekeeper, and Grace the nursemaid; William, who was coachman and butler, and Luke also worked in the house. They are remembered as "Maum" Rose, "Maum" Charlotte and "Maum" Grace, and as "Daddy" William and "Daddy" Luke — forms of address from a slaveholding household. Anyone admiring the proportions of Willis Ball's design is standing in rooms these men and women built, cleaned and cooked for, and had no freedom to leave.

## Letters Between Roswell and New York

Mittie was born in 1835 in Hartford, Connecticut, but she grew up at Bulloch Hall. When she was fifteen, a nineteen-year-old Theodore Roosevelt came to Roswell with his friend Hilborne West, who was to marry Mittie's oldest half-sister, Susan Elliott. Exactly when the pair first met is unclear. What is known is that in January 1853 Mittie went to Philadelphia to stay with Susan, met Thee again, and fell in love. They planned the wedding in letters traded between Roswell and New York.

The ceremony in the Bulloch Hall dining room was a gala affair. Seventy years later, Mittie's friend and bridesmaid, Mrs. William Baker, recalled it in an interview with Margaret "Peggy" Mitchell — the Margaret Mitchell of Gone with the Wind fame — published in the Atlanta Journal on June 10, 1923.

The newlyweds settled in New York, living with Thee's parents until their wedding gift, a house at 28 East 20th Street, was built. Their children were Anna, Theodore, Elliott and Corinne. Elliott's daughter Eleanor would become First Lady of the United States.

## A President Comes to His Mother's House

The family's Georgia roots ran deeper than one wedding. Through Mittie, the four Roosevelt children descended from Archibald Bulloch, the first governor of Georgia. Yet President Theodore Roosevelt was slow to visit. He had invited Booker T. Washington to dine at the White House, and the invitation infuriated the white South. Roosevelt waited a few years for the episode to blow over, and only while touring the South in 1905 did he see his mother's girlhood home for the first time. He was long thought to be the first sitting president to visit the South since the Civil War. That claim is wrong: William McKinley had already been there, celebrating victory in the Spanish-American War.

On October 20, 1905, Roosevelt and his wife Edith arrived in Roswell, and he spoke at Bulloch Hall. Eleanor Roosevelt returned to Roswell and the house several times.

## Other Lives of a Georgia House

Bulloch Hall's columns have found their way into fiction. The house is said to have supplied several scenes for Monte Hellman's Cockfighter, the Warren Oates film released in 1974, and it served as the model for the cover illustration of William Harben's novel Mam' Linda.

Today it is a historic house museum, one of several historically significant buildings in Roswell. The dining room where Mittie and Thee exchanged vows is still there. So is the fuller history the house carries: the descendants of Georgia's first governor, a president's long-delayed homecoming, and the enslaved men and women whose labor built and sustained all of it.
