# Double-barreled cannon

Designed in 1862 to fire two chained cannonballs that would scythe through enemy ranks, Athens, Georgia's double-barreled cannon failed spectacularly in testing and now sits on the city hall lawn as one of the town's best-loved curiosities.

**Location:** 33.9600, -83.3764

**Categories:** Individual cannons, Field artillery of the Confederate States of America, Multiple-barrel firearms, Trial and research firearms, Tourist attractions in Athens, Georgia

## Introduction

The most famous weapon in Athens, Georgia, was dreamed up by a dentist and never fired a shot in battle. John Gilleland, a local dentist, builder and mechanic, set out to build a weapon for the Confederacy: a single cannon with two barrels, firing two cannonballs linked by a chain that would sweep across a battlefield and "mow down the enemy somewhat as a scythe cuts wheat." What his invention actually mowed down was a cornfield, a stand of saplings and, according to accounts of its tests, a cow. Today it sits on the lawn of Athens City Hall, pointing north.

## An Old Idea

Gilleland's idea was far older than the Civil War. In 1642, the Florentine gun maker Antonio Petrini cast a cannon meant to fire two balls linked by a chain from side-by-side barrels, and a treatise describing his weapon still survives in the Royal Armories at the Tower of London. The concept had a fatal weakness. Petrini's chained shot was meant to scythe down enemy soldiers like standing wheat, but for it to work, the powder behind each ball had to ignite at exactly the same instant, and that rarely happened. Gunmakers kept experimenting with multiple barrels. During the English Civil War, the Cavaliers used a double-barreled gun called Elizabeth-Henry, named after Charles I's youngest children, its barrels wrapped in leather to keep off rust. The French army fielded a three-barreled cannon in the War of the Spanish Succession, and sixteenth-century Poland used cannons with as many as seven barrels. None of them, though, tried to fire a chained pair of balls at once.

## Three Hundred Fifty Dollars

In 1862, Gilleland raised $350 from a group of Confederate citizens in Athens to build his chain-shot gun. It was cast in one piece, with side-by-side bores each a little over 3 inches across. The barrels splayed outward by 3 degrees, so the two shots would diverge and stretch the chain taut between them. On paper, it was a terrifying idea. In practice, it depended on the same perfect simultaneous ignition that had defeated Petrini more than two centuries earlier.

## The Day of the Test

On April 22, 1862, the gun was aimed at a target of two upright poles in a field along the Newton's Bridge road, north of downtown Athens. Uneven burning of the powder and flaws in the casting sent the linked balls spinning off-center. Witnesses said the first shot "plowed up about an acre of ground, tore up a cornfield, mowed down saplings, and then the chain broke, the two balls going in different directions." The second shot sent the chain across the horizon into a thicket of young pines, which, another witness reported, "looked as if a narrow cyclone or a giant mowing machine had passed through." Over the course of the tests, the gun also knocked down a chimney and killed a cow. None of those casualties were anywhere near the intended target.

## Defiance on the Lawn

The invention was a failure, and the cannon never saw battle. Yet it survived to become something its maker never intended: a local landmark and public curiosity. It now rests on the front lawn of Athens City Hall as a contributing property of the Downtown Athens Historic District, listed on the National Register of Historic Places, and it ranks among the most popular and well-known attractions in the city. It still points north, a gesture of defiance toward the side it was built to fight. In 2012, a slightly modernized version was built for the television series American Guns, and with a more modern ignition system than anything available in the Civil War, its crew managed some success in firing both barrels properly.
