# Linnentown

In the 1960s, urban renewal erased Linnentown, a 22-acre Black neighborhood in Athens, Georgia, to make way for University of Georgia dormitories; decades later, its former residents pushed the city to remember, apologize and make redress.

**Location:** 33.9502, -83.3827

**Categories:** African-American history of Georgia (U.S. state), University of Georgia campus, Neighborhoods in Athens, Georgia

## Introduction

"We were happy children," Hattie Thomas Whitehead has said of growing up in Linnentown. "We had family Easter egg hunts. We had community baseball games. We played all over the community. Until urban renewal hit." Today, three University of Georgia dormitories, Russell Hall, Creswell Hall and Brumby Hall, stand where her neighborhood used to be. Students come and go on land that, until the 1960s, was a 22-acre Black community in Athens, Georgia, home to about fifty families who were forced out through eminent domain. For decades almost nothing marked what had been lost. Then its former residents began telling the story themselves.

## Off Baxter Street

Linnentown lay off Baxter and Church streets, and most of its families owned their homes. Whitehead, born in 1948, belonged to the fourth generation of families there. News reporting drawing on county documents describes a neighborhood of plumbers, electricians, construction workers and beauticians. It was also once home to Jeruel Academy, a school for Black children, now remembered by a historical marker. The community was destroyed in the 1960s by an urban renewal project undertaken by the University of Georgia and the city government of Athens, part of a federal program that stemmed from the Housing Act of 1949. The urban renewal contract spoke of clearing out "the total slum area which now exists off Baxter Street," words that described a place its residents knew as home.

## What Was Taken

The families were forced out to make room for student housing. According to county documents cited in news coverage, some residents received as little as $1,450 for their properties. The losses did not stop at the sale price. Jerry Shannon, an associate professor of geography at UGA, has estimated that undervaluing the properties at the time of sale, combined with pushing residents into areas "of the city where property values have not climbed as quickly," cost the families more than $5 million in generational wealth. It went the way of the houses themselves, and of the play area, the Easter egg hunts and the ball games that Whitehead remembers.

## The File Called R-50

The story resurfaced almost by accident. In 2018, while researching wage suppression by universities, Joseph Carter came across records of "Urban Renewal Project GA. R-50," the project that had covered Linnentown's homes. He met two former residents, Geneva Johnson and then Hattie Thomas Whitehead, and in September 2019 they and three other former residents founded the Linnentown Project. Its members drafted a resolution calling for reparations, both financial redress and a memorial on the site. Apart from the Jeruel Academy marker, nothing on the ground recalled the community that had lived there.

## An Apology, and a Disagreement

In February 2021, Athens Mayor Kelly Girtz apologized for the role the city had played in destroying Linnentown. "I think it's important to recognize that apology is a strength," he said at a proclamation ceremony. On February 16 of that year, the Athens-Clarke County Commission unanimously approved a resolution supporting recognition and redress for Linnentown, its descendants and the county's Black communities harmed by urban renewal. The commitments included the Athens Justice and Memory Project, which the county describes as its first resident-led effort to address urban renewal's history and impact, and a memory wall. The University of Georgia took a different position, issuing a statement that it "respectfully disagree[s]" with the "conclusions" of the Linnentown Project. The question of how, and where, Linnentown will be remembered is still being worked out, by the people who remember it best.

## Flight Context

The former Linnentown site lies at about 33.950 N, 83.383 W on the University of Georgia campus in Athens, Georgia, where Russell, Creswell and Brumby residence halls now stand, roughly half a nautical mile south-southwest of downtown. Nothing of the neighborhood is visible today; from 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL look for the residence halls on the campus close to downtown Athens. Athens-Ben Epps Airport (KAHN) is about 2.8 nm east, so remain alert for traffic in its pattern. Barrow County Airport (KWDR) is about 14 nm west, and Jackson County Airport (KJCA) about 16 nm north-northwest. Expect summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms.
