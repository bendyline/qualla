# Lake Lanier

North Georgia's vast reservoir supplies water and recreation to metro Atlanta, but its waters cover old farms and the site of Oscarville, a small community at the center of the 1912 expulsion of Black residents from Forsyth County, and they have fueled decades of fights among three states.

**Location:** 34.2400, -83.9500

**Categories:** Lakes of Georgia (U.S. state), Reservoirs, ACF River Basin, African-American history, Olympic canoeing venues

## Introduction

In late 2007, as Georgia suffered one of the worst droughts in its recorded history, Lake Lanier sank to a record low and began giving up its secrets. Parts of the lake bottom not seen since the 1950s reappeared: remnants of a road and the foundations of homes abandoned when the valley was flooded. Newer debris surfaced too, from trash and boat batteries to sunken boats, and law enforcement recovered several automobiles, some of them stolen, along with discarded firearms. For a few months, the reservoir that waters metro Atlanta showed what lies beneath its familiar blue surface, and much of that history is harder to look at than old roadbeds.

## Before the Water

Before the 1950s, the land at the bottom of Lake Lanier was forest and farmland. Building the reservoir displaced more than 250 families and 15 businesses, and 20 cemeteries were relocated along with their remains. A stretch of Georgia Highway 53 was abandoned, and Gainesville's Looper Speedway was condemned.

Part of the lake also covers the site of Oscarville, a small Forsyth County community at the center of a racial expulsion. In 1912, after the alleged assault of two white women and the lynching of a Black man named Rob Edwards, who was accused of involvement, white mobs terrorized Black families across Forsyth County with arson, intimidation and the destruction of property. Within weeks, most of the county's Black residents, more than 1,000 people, had been forced to flee, leaving behind homes and land that later passed into white ownership. Decades afterward, the government bought what remained of the town, and the water rose over it.

## Buford Dam Rises

Congress authorized the project in 1946, and ground was broken for Buford Dam on the Chattahoochee River on March 1, 1950. Atlanta mayor William B. Hartsfield went back to Washington in 1955 to lobby for $11 million more, stressing his growing city's need for water, and Congress approved the funds. On February 1, 1956, the dam's sluice gates closed and the lake, also fed by the Chestatee River, began to fill. Buford Dam was dedicated on October 9, 1957, and the lake reached full pool on August 1, 1958, about a year late because of droughts.

It proved its worth almost immediately. That fall, two solid months of drought would have left the Chattahoochee and its tributaries nearly dry without the reservoir. Operated by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers for flood control and water supply, the dam has an installed capacity of about 126 megawatts, and since its construction only a handful of major floods have struck the river downstream.

## Three States, One Lake

Georgia, Alabama and Florida all have rights to the water, because the lake feeds rivers running through all three. The Corps must also release enough to support endangered species downstream, even as Atlanta's rapid suburban growth has driven up water use for lawns and gardens. Since the 1990s, the parties have been fighting over it.

The fight sharpened in 2006, when the Corps revealed that a miscalibrated lake gauge had read nearly two feet too high, and nearly 22 billion gallons of excess water had been released. Governor Sonny Perdue called it a "manmade drought," and outdoor watering was eventually banned. In July 2009 a federal judge ruled that Congress had never authorized Lake Lanier as a water supply for metro Atlanta, but the Eleventh Circuit reversed that decision in 2011. Florida then took Georgia to the U.S. Supreme Court, which unanimously dismissed its complaint on April 1, 2021, finding that Florida had not proved serious injury.

## Stumps Below the Surface

The valley did not go under cleanly. Before the lake finished filling, construction crews felled the treetops but left tall stumps standing, and in some areas they remain not far beneath the surface. The water spreads through Hall, Forsyth, Dawson, Gwinnett and Lumpkin counties in numerous small arms and fingers, and its shoreline runs to about 690 miles.

That shoreline now holds dozens of Corps, state, county and city parks, many of them with swim beaches, plus dozens of beaches on islands. Lake Lanier Islands, a resort complex that opened in 1974, stages a winter holiday light display called Magical Nights of Lights. The lake has served as an Olympic canoeing and rowing venue and has hosted the 2003 ICF Canoe Sprint World Championships and the 2018 ICF Dragon Boat World Championships. Local folklore, meanwhile, holds that the lake is haunted, stories connected to the cemeteries affected by its creation.

## A Poet's Name

The lake is officially Lake Sidney Lanier, named for the 19th-century poet whose "Song of the Chattahoochee" is written about the river the dam now holds back. Lanier also served in the Confederate Army, and that service has made the name the subject of public debate and renaming efforts. In 2021 Congress directed a commission to remove names tied to the Confederate Army from Department of Defense property, which includes the Army Corps of Engineers that manages the lake. In March 2023, the Lake Lanier Association said there was no good enough reason to change the name, and no timeline for a decision had been set.

The name is only one of the lake's layers. Its surface is a playground for boaters and swimmers from across the Atlanta region, while beneath it lie the traces of farms, highways, relocated graves and Oscarville, in a county whose Black residents were driven out long before the water came.

## Flight Context

Lake Lanier's center lies at about 34.24 N, 83.95 W in north Georgia, northeast of metro Atlanta. From 3,000 to 4,500 feet AGL, the reservoir's long, branching arms and coves are unmistakable; look for Buford Dam on the Chattahoochee, which holds the water back, and for the Chestatee River arm that also feeds the lake. Lee Gilmer Memorial Airport in Gainesville (KGVL) is about 6 nm to the east-northeast, and Gwinnett County-Briscoe Field in Lawrenceville (KLZU) about 16 nm to the south. Summer brings haze, heavy boat traffic below and afternoon thunderstorms, and in drought years low water can expose shallow ground and the stumps left standing beneath the surface.
