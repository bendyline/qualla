# John C. Campbell Folk School

Founded in 1925 on a Danish model in the mountains of far western North Carolina, the John C. Campbell Folk School has taught crafts, music and dance without grades for a century.

**Location:** 35.0361, -83.9650

**Categories:** Folk schools, Appalachian crafts, Education in North Carolina, Historic districts on the National Register of Historic Places in North Carolina, Blacksmith shops

## Introduction

As late as 1934, Brasstown kept its own time. Cherokee County ran on Central time and Clay County on Eastern, and the little community sat along the line between them. Because of frequent gatherings at the John C. Campbell Folk School, Brasstown's clocks were set halfway between: when it was 1 p.m. in Murphy and 2 p.m. in Hayesville, it was 1:30 in Brasstown. The school at the heart of that compromise, founded in 1925, is one of the oldest and largest folk schools in the United States, a place where adults come to learn blacksmithing, weaving, music and dance with no grades and no competition. Its motto is "I sing behind the plow."

## A Wagon Through Appalachia

The school's namesake never saw it. John C. Campbell, born in Indiana in 1867 and raised in Wisconsin, studied education and theology in New England and became an educator and reformer known for his survey of social conditions in southern Appalachia. At the turn of the century, the region was widely viewed as in need of educational and social missions, and in 1908 and 1909, newly married to Olive Dame of Massachusetts, Campbell set out to see it for himself.

The couple outfitted a wagon as a traveling home and studied mountain life from Georgia to West Virginia. John interviewed farmers about their agricultural practices, while Olive collected Appalachian ballads and studied the handicrafts of the mountain people. Both hoped education could improve life in the mountains, and both wanted to preserve and share with the world the crafts, techniques and tools that people there used every day. John died in 1919.

## A Lesson From Denmark

The Campbells had talked of bringing a Danish idea to the rural South. In Denmark the folkehøjskole, or folk high school, had long been a force in rural life, and they saw such a school as an alternative to the colleges that drew young people away from the family farm. Before founding it, Olive and her colleague Marguerite Butler spent eighteen months traveling through Denmark, Norway, Sweden and Finland, visiting schools along the way.

Several locations were considered. On an exploratory trip, Butler described the idea to Fred O. Scroggs, Brasstown's storekeeper, and said she would return in a few weeks to see whether residents were interested. When she came back, it was to a meeting of more than 200 people at the local church, where people from across far western North Carolina pledged labor, land and building materials. The school opened in 1925, dedicated to John's memory. As in Denmark, no grades were given; students and teachers formed a community that helped one another learn.

## Courthouse Bricks and a Carved Mule

The first building went up in 1927: the Community Room, whose chimney was built with bricks salvaged from the Cherokee County Courthouse after it burned in 1926. The four-story Keith House was added the next year. Early students, aged 17 to 35, stayed a year or two, and the campus grew to include dormitories, a dairy barn, poultry houses and even a savings and loan association. Classes ranged from gymnastics and public health to literature and folk dancing, with an emphasis on agriculture and "the doing of life's work."

The Brasstown Carvers formed at the school in 1929, and in the 1930s a wood-carved mule from the Folk School stood on President Franklin D. Roosevelt's desk in the Oval Office. The school's creamery could churn 6,000 pounds of butter a week, a third of it sold to businesses in Atlanta, and it produced ice cream and whole milk as well.

## Forge, Carol and Dance

In 1973 the school ended its farming operation and turned the farm buildings into craft studios. When the Francis Whitaker Blacksmith Shop opened in 1980, it was the largest teaching blacksmith shop in the United States. Traditions piled up: a dramatic reading of A Christmas Carol every December since 1949, the Fireside Sale since 1967, and Winter Dance Week between Christmas and New Year's Day since 1979. Composer and singer John Jacob Niles first performed "I Wonder as I Wander" at the school, and singer and Appalachian dulcimer player Jean Ritchie is among its notable alumni and educators.

A great deal of the school's modern growth came under Jan Davidson, the longest-serving director, from 1992 to 2017. The school moved to a year-round schedule, annual enrollment rose from 2,500 to 6,000 students, and yearly classes grew from 150 to more than 850. Seven new studios went up, for blacksmithing, cooking, book and paper arts, painting, woodturning, spinning and music. In 2003 the school launched its Junior Appalachian Musician program, teaching local students to play folk music.

## A Century Behind the Plow

Today the 300-acre campus, listed on the National Register of Historic Places as a historic district in 1983, welcomes more than 6,000 adult students and 100,000 visitors a year. Classes run from basketry, broom making and bookbinding to woodturning, storytelling and kaleidoscopes, and the school holds more than 300 performance events a year, including a concert series and community dances. National Geographic named it one of the "100 Best Vacations to Enrich Your Life" in 2007. The school began its 100th anniversary year in 2025 by restoring and reopening its log cabin museum, two cabins built in the 1850s and 1890s and moved to campus in 1926, and launching new campus walking tours.

## Flight Context

The Folk School lies at about 35.036 N, 83.965 W in Brasstown, on the line between Cherokee and Clay counties in the mountains of far western North Carolina, between the towns of Murphy and Hayesville. From 3,000 to 4,000 feet AGL, look for a cluster of farm-style buildings, barns and open fields tucked into a valley among forested ridges. Western Carolina Regional Airport in Andrews (KRHP) is about 11 nm to the north-northeast, and Blairsville Airport (KDZJ) in Georgia about 11 nm to the south. Surrounding ridges rise well above the valley floor, so maintain generous terrain clearance, and expect morning valley fog and rapidly building summer thunderstorms.
