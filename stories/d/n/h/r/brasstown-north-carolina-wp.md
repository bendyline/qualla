# Brasstown, North Carolina

Clay County's oldest continuous settlement grew up on an old Cherokee trade route, became home to the John C. Campbell Folk School, and for nearly three decades rang in the New Year by lowering an opossum at midnight.

**Location:** 35.0394, -83.9569

**Categories:** Unincorporated communities in Clay County, North Carolina, Unincorporated communities in Cherokee County, North Carolina, North Carolina placenames of Native American origin, Appalachian folk culture, Festivals in North Carolina

## Introduction

For nearly three decades, the New Year arrived in Brasstown, North Carolina, with an opossum. At midnight, outside a convenience store called Clay's Corner, the animal was lowered to the ground while neighbors watched and sometimes set off fireworks, and afterward it was released. The ritual made this unincorporated mountain community, the self-proclaimed "possum capital of the world," briefly famous. But Brasstown was a crossroads long before it was a headline. It is the oldest continuous settlement in Clay County, gathered where Brasstown Creek meets the Hiwassee River, with the waterways marking the line between Clay County and neighboring Cherokee County, which holds roughly a third of the town.

## A Toll Road Through Fresh Green Country

The name itself is a mistranslation: settlers confused the Cherokee Itse'yĭ, "Place of Fresh Green," with Ûňtsaiyĭ, meaning brass, and applied "Brasstown" to several towns across the Cherokee region, this one among them.

The community grew on the Native American route known as the Unicoi Turnpike. When the path became a toll road in 1813, a store and an inn went up that same year in what would become Brasstown. The inn was run by David Thompson, reputedly the first white settler, and stood near today's community center. Joshua Harshaw's smokehouse, likely built in the 1840s, is the oldest surviving building in western Clay County and may be the oldest structure in the county still standing where it was built. Little Brasstown Baptist, now the largest church, was founded in 1850, and a post office opened in 1871, closed, and was replaced in 1889.

## Folk Arts, Butter and Ice Cream

Schooling began in the mid-1880s in a log house at Green Cove Church, on the site of today's Hickory Stand Church, and in 1926 the five-classroom Ogden School consolidated the small schools of the Warne and Brasstown area. Construction of US 64 through Hayesville, Warne and Brasstown started in 1921.

The institution that shaped Brasstown most arrived in 1925. Fred O. Scroggs donated land to preserve the folk teachings of mountain culture, and the John C. Campbell Folk School, founded by folklorist Olive Dame Campbell, took root. It even ran the local creamery during the 1930s, turning out butter, then ice cream, and by 1937 whole milk. Listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1983, the school is today often described as the oldest and largest folk school in the United States, said to host more than 6,000 adult students and 100,000 visitors a year. Its Fall Festival, begun in 1974, draws thousands, and Little Brasstown Creek Park on its campus offers the Rivercane Walk and exhibits of Cherokee history and artwork.

## Midnight at Clay's Corner

The Possum Drop began in 1990, organized by Clay and Judy Logan at Clay's Corner, a spot that started out as the Caldwell family's produce stand in the 1940s. That first year drew twenty people to a covered-dish supper with jam music, and the "possum" was ceramic, lowered in a fish bowl. The next year Logan used a real opossum, trapped for the occasion.

The joke kept growing. In 1992 Brasstown's "honorary mayor," Mercer Scroggs, launched a tongue-in-cheek run for the presidency on the Possum Party ticket, chosen, the joke went, because it was "middle-of-the-road." The New York Times covered the drop in 2003, and hours before the December 31 event that year, People for the Ethical Treatment of Animals called Logan and threatened to sue if a live animal was used. Logan used roadkill instead. The live opossum returned the following year, and by 2010 the crowd had grown to 2,000. The drop was featured on CBS Sunday Morning, and PETA threatened another suit and urged the North Carolina Wildlife Resources Commission to intervene; the commission issued Logan a permit anyway.

## The Last Drop

When Clay and Judy Logan retired, the Possum Drop moved to nearby Andrews for the 2018-2019 New Year's Eve. There, one of the opossums was injured and used with a broken leg that was later amputated. After lawsuits by PETA and appeals to state officials from concerned citizens, Andrews opted not to continue, and the event has not been held since.

The tradition lingers in gentler form. Many locals now ring in the New Year with dance, music and food at the Folk School, and every December the "Possum Drop Song," inspired by the event, is performed in the Brasstown Follies during the school's Winter Dance Week.

## A Small Place With a Big Calendar

Brasstown keeps itself busy. The 6,000-square-foot community center, opened in 1998, has a commercial kitchen, a gym that seats 850, a playground and a quarter-mile walking track, and it doubles as the voting site. The volunteer fire department, founded in 1976, moved into a new headquarters on Old Highway 64 West in 2023. A Hollywood-style sign went up on a hillside by 2008, and since 2009 Clay County's two-day Punkin Chunkin Festival has launched pumpkins by catapult on Settawig Road.

The valley has its hazards, too. Two small tornadoes touched the area during the 1974 Super Outbreak, and a recent Tennessee Valley Authority map reportedly shows that about 25 structures in downtown Brasstown would flood if Chatuge Dam ever failed.

## Flight Context

Brasstown sits at 35.0394°N, 83.9569°W in far western North Carolina, along Brasstown Creek, which separates Clay and Cherokee counties. From the air, trace US 64 through the valley floor; Chatuge Lake and the TVA's Chatuge Dam lie to the east near Hayesville. Brasstown Bald (4,784 ft), Georgia's highest summit, rises about 12 nm to the southeast, and ridges surround the valley on all sides, so plan at least 2,500–3,000 ft AGL. Nearby airports: Western Carolina Regional Airport at Andrews (KRHP), about 10 nm north-northeast, and Blairsville Airport, Georgia (KDZJ), about 11 nm south. Valley fog is common on calm mornings, and summer afternoons bring mountain thunderstorms.
