# Scottsboro Boys Museum and Cultural Center

In a former Black church near the railroad tracks in Scottsboro, Alabama, a museum founded by a woman who first read about the case as a teenager tells the story of nine young Black men falsely accused in 1931.

**Location:** 34.6739, -86.0413

## Introduction

In 1977, when she was 17, Sheila Washington found a paperback book hidden under her parents' bed. It was about the Scottsboro Boys, and her stepfather forbade her from reading it, saying it was too traumatic for a young girl. Decades later, Washington, who died in January 2021, founded the Scottsboro Boys Museum and Cultural Center at 428 West Willow Street in Scottsboro, Alabama, the town whose name became attached to the case of nine young Black men falsely accused in 1931 of raping two white women aboard a freight train.

## A Freight Train to Paint Rock

On March 25, 1931, nine Black teenagers were riding a freight train on the Southern Railway line between Chattanooga and Memphis. They ranged in age from 13 to 19. The train was stopped at Paint Rock, Alabama, and the young men were arrested and accused of rape. At their first trials in Scottsboro, all but 14-year-old Roy Wright were convicted and sentenced to death. The case became a years-long legal struggle. In Powell v. Alabama in 1932, the U.S. Supreme Court reversed the convictions, holding that due process guarantees the effective assistance of counsel at a criminal trial. In Norris v. Alabama in 1935, it reversed convictions again because Black citizens had been excluded from the jury pool. One of the two accusers eventually testified that no rape had occurred. Justice came slowly: not until November 21, 2013, did Alabama's parole board grant posthumous pardons to the three Scottsboro Boys who had never been pardoned or had their convictions overturned.

## A Walking Trail and an Idea

Until the museum opened, the only acknowledgment of the case in town was a historical marker at the courthouse, placed there in 2003. Washington first raised the idea of a Scottsboro Boys museum in 2000, during a public discussion among local officials about creating a historic walking trail in the area. It took another decade to become real. The Scottsboro Boys Museum opened on February 1, 2010, and it is now part of the U.S. Civil Rights Trail.

## A Church by the Tracks

The museum occupies the former Joyce Chapel United Methodist Church, described as the oldest surviving African American church in Jackson County. Its congregation dissolved in January 2009 and leased the building to the museum. When the United Methodist Church put the building up for sale, the museum raised $75,000 to buy it, with half coming from the Jackson County Commission and half from a foundation with ties to the family of Samuel Leibowitz, the New York defense attorney who represented the Scottsboro Boys at their retrials. The setting carries its own weight. The church stands near the railroad tracks that carried the very train on which the young men were riding. The building was renovated starting in 2020 and reopened to the public in 2022.

## What the Rooms Hold

Inside, the collection brings the case down to the scale of objects and paper. There is a metal table said to have been used by the Scottsboro Boys in their cell at the Jackson County Jail, and a chair used by a member of the jury during the trial. There are stamps sold by the International Labor Defense to raise money for the defense, photographs of the trial and original newspaper articles about the case. The museum also holds scrapbooks compiled by local residents, most of them never examined by historians before. Together they let visitors see the case not as a chapter in a textbook but as something that happened here, in this town, to nine young men whose lives it upended.
