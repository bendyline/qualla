# Browns Ferry Nuclear Plant

On the north shore of Alabama's Wheeler Lake, TVA's first nuclear plant survived a 1975 fire started by a candle, a 22-year restart saga and a tornado blackout to become one of the most powerful nuclear stations in the United States.

**Location:** 34.7039, -87.1186

**Categories:** Nuclear power plants in Alabama, Tennessee Valley Authority, Nuclear power stations using boiling water reactors, Nuclear accidents and incidents in the United States, Buildings and structures in Limestone County, Alabama

## Introduction

The workers were only trying to find a draft. In 1975, at the Browns Ferry Nuclear Plant on the Tennessee River in northern Alabama, crews resealing a cable passage through a wall checked the airflow the way a homeowner might hunt for a leaky window: with a candle flame. The temporary seal was highly combustible, and it caught fire. What followed damaged the controls for two reactors, led the Nuclear Regulatory Commission to add significant new fire-protection standards, and became a lasting chapter in the life of the Tennessee Valley Authority's first nuclear plant.

## Fire Behind the Wall

The fire began in the cable spreading room, where bundles of cables passed through a wall into the Unit 1 reactor building. That wall was designed to keep slightly higher air pressure on the cable-room side, which meant a steady draft flowed through any gap. The original seal had been breached to add cables, and workers were testing a temporary seal before installing the permanent material. They fought the flames at the point of ignition, apparently without realizing the draft was carrying the fire to the far side of the wall. By the time anyone noticed, cables that controlled Units 1 and 2 were badly damaged. Unit 1 was shut down for a year. The NRC responded with major new fire-protection standards, and the event proved pivotal for firestopping, not only in nuclear plants but in commercial and industrial construction too.

## Named for a River Crossing

The plant takes its name from a ferry that operated at the site until the middle of the 20th century. It was TVA's first nuclear plant, approved on June 17, 1966, with construction starting that September. Three General Electric boiling water reactors followed, with Unit 1 entering commercial operation in 1974, Unit 2 in 1975 and Unit 3 in 1977. Wheeler Lake provides the main cooling, while seven mechanical-draft "helper" cooling towers assist when limits on water temperature apply, and a 600-foot concrete chimney vents gases. With a generating capacity of roughly 3.6 to 3.9 gigawatts, depending on how it is measured, Browns Ferry is TVA's largest generating asset and is generally ranked the third-most-powerful nuclear plant in the country, behind Vogtle in Georgia and Palo Verde in Arizona.

## Twenty-Two Years to Restart

On March 19, 1985, after extremely negative assessments from the NRC, TVA shut down all three units indefinitely to bring them back into regulatory compliance. For one reactor, the pause would last 22 years. Unit 2 returned on May 24, 1991, and Unit 3 on November 1, 1995. Unit 1, the reactor damaged in the 1975 fire, stayed dark until TVA began a $1.8 billion restoration effort in 2002. Even the restart had drama: two days after the reactor reached criticality in May 2007, a hydraulic control pipe in the turbine hall burst and spilled about 600 gallons of non-radioactive fluid. Unit 1 finally began supplying the grid on June 2, 2007. Meanwhile the cooling towers had their own troubles, with fires in 1986 and 1996; in the first, sparks from fans ignited redwood slats that had dried out during nearly two months of inactivity.

## Tornadoes, Heat Waves and Weapons Uranium

Weather has repeatedly tested the plant. During an August 2007 drought, Unit 2 shut down for a day because the Tennessee River had grown too warm to use for cooling. At 5:01 p.m. on April 27, 2011, a tornado near the plant knocked out external power and all three reactors scrammed. Control rods and cooling systems worked as designed, diesel generators took over, and there was no physical damage or release of radiation, though grid damage left Browns Ferry unable to deliver power while blackouts spread across the Southeast. Some of the plant's fuel has an unusual past: beginning in 2005, Unit 2 was loaded with blended low-enriched uranium recovered from weapons programs, material that would otherwise have been waste. A $475 million uprate project, finished in 2019, raised each unit's output, and during the January 2024 snowstorms the plant ran at full capacity while serving 2 million customers across the Tennessee Valley. In December 2025 the Nuclear Regulatory Commission renewed the operating licenses of all three units for another 20 years, carrying operation into the mid-2050s.

## Flight Context

Browns Ferry sits at about 34.70°N, 87.12°W on the north shore of Wheeler Lake, the dammed Tennessee River, between Athens and Decatur, Alabama. The large reactor complex, the 600-foot concrete stack and the mechanical-draft cooling towers make it easy to identify from 5,000 feet or higher. Pilots are advised to avoid loitering or circling over power plants, so view it from a respectful distance while following the broad lake east toward Decatur and Huntsville, about 28 miles away. Nearby airports include Pryor Field Regional Airport (KDCU) at Decatur, Huntsville International Airport (KHSV) to the east and Northwest Alabama Regional Airport (KMSL) at Muscle Shoals to the west. Summer brings haze and pop-up thunderstorms, and spring in the Tennessee Valley can produce severe weather, as the 2011 tornado showed.
