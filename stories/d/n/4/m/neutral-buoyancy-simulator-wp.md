# Neutral Buoyancy Simulator

At NASA's Marshall Space Flight Center in Huntsville, a water tank born from a pit once used to shape metal with explosives let engineers and astronauts rehearse spacewalks for Skylab, the Space Shuttle, Hubble and the space station.

**Location:** 34.6530, -86.6774

**Categories:** Marshall Space Flight Center, Neutral buoyancy pools, National Historic Landmarks in Alabama, Buildings and structures in Huntsville, Alabama, Space exploration

## Introduction

The first tank was small, just eight feet across and eight feet deep, and it had been built for something else entirely: forming metal parts with explosives. In 1965, armed with a couple of NASA scuba suits, two men at NASA's Marshall Space Flight Center in Huntsville, Alabama, put it to a new use: finding out whether water could teach them how humans would work in space. From that improvised pool grew the Neutral Buoyancy Simulator, where from 1968 until 1997 engineers and astronauts rehearsed the spacewalks that built and repaired some of NASA's most famous spacecraft.

## Twenty-Five Seconds Is Not Enough

NASA had long simulated weightlessness in aircraft. In 1959, Project Mercury astronauts trained in a C-131 Samaritan dubbed the "Vomit Comet," but an airplane can offer only 25 seconds of weightlessness at a time, and a spacewalk might last hours. Water offered another way. A body that neither sinks nor floats can mimic weightlessness, although water adds drag, so tasks are done slowly, and astronauts are not weightless inside their suits, which makes precise suit sizing critical. In the early to mid-1960s, NASA was already testing the idea with Environmental Research Associates of Baltimore, first in a pool near Langley Research Center and then at McDonogh School, where Scott Carpenter became the first astronaut to take part in a suit. After difficult spacewalks through Gemini 11 in 1966, the Gemini 12 crew went there to train.

## An Idea from a Film Reel

At Marshall, Charlie Cooper was reviewing film of the Gemini 4 spacewalk when he theorized that neutral buoyancy could help plan spacewalks. Marshall's designers needed that insight: the Apollo Applications Program would require astronauts to convert a mostly empty S-IVB rocket stage into a space station. Cooper and Charles D. Stocks took a couple of NASA scuba suits into the old explosive-forming pool, and in November 1965 they tested tasks that were early steps toward what became Skylab. A second, larger tank followed, and Marshall's Manufacturing Engineering lab developed a constant-pressure valve that made it possible to hold neutral buoyancy at any depth in a pressure suit. On September 6, 1966, astronaut Alan Bean tried it. His glove leaked, his Navy Mark IV suit tore under the arm, and divers had to help him out, yet he carried on in scuba gear and came away an enthusiastic advocate.

## Rehearsing a Rescue

The third and largest tank, the Neutral Buoyancy Simulator itself, was completed in 1968. Its temperature-controlled water was continuously recirculated and filtered, and built-in systems handled underwater audio and video, suit environmental control and emergency rescue, providing life support for up to four pressure-suited subjects at once. Johnson Space Center had a smaller pool, a 25-foot Water Immersion Facility, from early 1967 and opened its larger Weightless Environment Training Facility in November 1980, but Marshall's big tank was where large hardware could be rehearsed. Its most dramatic early use came with Skylab. A NASA photograph from 1972 shows astronauts practicing the rigging of protective shielding for Skylab here, and before Skylab 2 astronauts Conrad and Kerwin carried out their repairs to the station in orbit, they simulated the work in this tank.

## Hubble, Solar Max and a Station in Pieces

After Skylab the simulator became a design laboratory as much as a training pool. The crew of Space Shuttle mission STS-41-C trained here for the 1984 capture, repair and release of the Solar Max satellite, practicing with an underwater Manned Maneuvering Unit powered by trolling motors. Techniques that Jerry Ross and Sherwood "Woody" Spring used to assemble structures in orbit on STS-61B in 1985 were developed here too. Engineers shaped Hubble Space Telescope servicing concepts in the tank, eventually building a full-scale underwater Hubble mockup for crew training, and the designs that evolved into the International Space Station were tested here regularly, with Boeing, the European Space Agency and Japan's NASDA among the users. Students from MIT and the University of Maryland studied in the tank, and its recompression chamber occasionally treated people in hyperbaric distress.

## Draining the Tank

The simulator was declared a National Historic Landmark in 1985, and it stayed busy with Hubble and space station work to the end. Its operating costs per test were significantly lower than those of Johnson Space Center's Neutral Buoyancy Laboratory, but NASA felt it could not fund two similar facilities, and Johnson's larger new tank near Clear Lake, Texas, was closer to the astronauts. Marshall's tank was decommissioned in 1997. Fittingly for a tank that had hosted university students for years, its last test was run by the University of Maryland's Space Sciences Laboratory. Demolition of the building began in mid-December 2025, and NASA archived architectural documentation and photographs of the tank with the Library of Congress's Historic American Engineering Record beforehand.

## Flight Context

The simulator's site is at about 34.65°N, 86.68°W inside NASA's Marshall Space Flight Center on Redstone Arsenal, southwest of downtown Huntsville, Alabama; demolition of the building began in December 2025, so look instead for the broad campus of Marshall's test stands and laboratories north of the Tennessee River. Redstone Arsenal is covered by restricted military airspace, so plan to view the area from outside it or with proper clearance, at 3,000 feet or higher. Redstone Army Airfield (KHUA) lies just to the north on the arsenal, and Huntsville International Airport (KHSV) is about six miles to the west. The Tennessee Valley often brings summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms.
