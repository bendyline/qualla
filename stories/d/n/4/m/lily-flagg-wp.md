# Lily Flagg

In 1892 a Huntsville Jersey cow named Lily Flagg made more butter than any other Jersey in the world, and her owner reputedly celebrated by painting his house butter yellow and lighting the party with some of the South's first electric lights.

**Location:** 34.6549, -86.5663

**Categories:** Individual cows, Individual cattle in the United States, History of Huntsville, Alabama, Agriculture

## Introduction

In the summer of 1892, General Samuel H. Moore reputedly painted his Huntsville home the color of butter. He built a 50-foot dancing platform, drew guests, by one account, from as far away as Baltimore, and arranged for some of the first electric lights in the Southeast so the party could run through the night. The reason for it all was a cow. Signal's Lily Flagg, a Jersey owned by Moore and W. E. Matthews, had just been crowned the top Jersey butter producer in the world, and Huntsville has never quite stopped celebrating her.

## A Year Measured in Butter

Lily Flagg was bred in Kentucky, and by the standards of her day she was no aristocrat. An Australian newspaper, the Sydney Mail, noted that despite the famous name she carried, she was only one-twelfth Signal blood and belonged to none of the fashionable Jersey families. She was not, the paper admitted, perhaps a model cow in appearance, but she had good form and a strong constitution. In her record year, at eight years old and weighing about 950 pounds, she produced 1,047 pounds and three-quarters of an ounce of butter. She had already dropped eight calves, including a pair of twins, and two of her daughters had been tested as strong producers themselves, which the paper took as proof that she could pass her gifts to her offspring.

## Chasing the Champion Cup

Her record capped a long contest. In 1816 a Massachusetts cow owned by Caleb Oakes had made a celebrated 484 pounds of butter in about seven months. Later the Jersey Eurotas set the Jersey world agog with 778 pounds in a year. Mary Ann of St. Lambert topped her, and a challenge cup was offered to any Jersey that could do better. Landseer's Fancy claimed it, Eurotissima took it away, and Bisson's Belle seized it with 1,028 pounds. Then, in 1892, Lily Flagg led them all. Her reign had one asterisk. For sheer butter in a year, a Holstein-Friesian named Pauline Paul had reportedly made more, over 1,153 pounds. Among Jerseys, though, the cup was Lily's.

## Electric Lights for a Cow

Huntsville threw two parties. The first was a formal reception at the Monte Sano Hotel on Wednesday, June 29, where the citizens of Huntsville honored Moore, Matthews and the men who had tested the cow, Captain Milton Humes and L. C. Goddell. Three weeks later, on July 21, 1892, the celebration came down the mountain. Moore, so taken with the record that one reception was not enough, threw an elaborate party of his own at his home, said to be freshly painted butter yellow. The dancing platform and the electric lights made it a renowned affair, one that drew notice as far away as Baltimore.

## Her Ladyship at the World's Fair

Fame brought demand for her calves and for public appearances, and in 1893, after an injury delayed her trip until sometime after mid-June, Lily Flagg went to the World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago. The Chicago Times found her lying on a pile of straw, chewing her cud "just as calmly as if she were not the champion Jersey cow on the earth," while cattlemen spoke about her in respectful voices. When a visitor from Missouri wanted her prodded up so he could see her legs, the superintendent gave him a withering look. The fair's dairy cows lived like royalty. Milkers, some in creased trousers and diamonds, reportedly tipped their caps and tapped a resting cow gently until she chose to rise, beneath signs reading "Do not disturb the cows when lying down." Shortly before September 23, 1893, Lily Flagg was sold to C. I. Hood of Lowell, Massachusetts, for a reported $1,250, joining his herd of prize-winning Jerseys.

## A Neighborhood Named for a Cow

She left Alabama, but her name stayed. Her barn had stood on Dairy Lane in northeast Huntsville, yet she is best remembered in Lily Flagg Village, south of the city, a cotton community that had its own Lily Flagg Gin and won a $2,000 state prize for its one-variety cotton program in 1948. When the Space Race brought a population boom in the 1950s and 1960s, Huntsville annexed the village. The cow's name survived the rockets. A road, a neighborhood, an apartment complex and many businesses carry it, the local swim team competes as the Lily Flagg Cows, a Huntsville brewery makes Lily Flagg Milk Stout, and a local history podcast launched in 2022 is named for her. The date of her death is not known.

## Flight Context

The Lily Flagg area lies near 34.65°N, 86.57°W in south Huntsville, Alabama, between the Tennessee River to the south and the city center to the north. From 3,000 to 4,000 feet, look for the dense suburban grid of south Huntsville with Monte Sano, the mountain where the Monte Sano Hotel reception was held, rising to the northeast. Huntsville International Airport (KHSV) lies about 12 miles to the west, and Redstone Army Airfield (KHUA) sits on Redstone Arsenal to the west, where restricted military airspace requires careful planning. Moontown Airport (3M5, FAA identifier) serves general aviation northeast of the city. Summer afternoons in the Tennessee Valley often bring haze and thunderstorms.
