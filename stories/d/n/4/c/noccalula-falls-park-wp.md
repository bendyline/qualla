# Noccalula Falls Park

A 90-foot waterfall in the middle of Gadsden, Alabama, carries a Cherokee-derived name, a disputed legend, a surveyor's 1859 signature and a cave that once held a dance hall.

**Location:** 34.0415, -86.0213

**Categories:** Waterfalls of Alabama, Parks in Alabama, Botanical gardens in Alabama, Gadsden, Alabama, Properties on the Alabama Register of Landmarks and Heritage

## Introduction

The name probably comes from a Cherokee phrase, ama uqwalelvyi, which translates roughly as "place where water thunders." Stand at the rim of Noccalula Falls in Gadsden, Alabama, and the description needs no translation. The 90-foot waterfall is the centerpiece of a 250-acre public park where trails wind through Black Creek Gorge past caves, a Native American fort, an abandoned dam, a pioneer homestead and carvings left from the Civil War era. Few city parks hold this much history within earshot of a waterfall.

## Signatures Behind the Curtain

People have been leaving their mark behind the falls for a long time. In 1859 a surveyor named Faxon surveyed Noccalula Falls and carved his name and the date into the rock wall behind the waterfall. Later in the nineteenth century, the Gadsden Land and Improvement Company turned a cave behind the falling water into a tavern and dance hall. When the operators tried to enlarge the flat floor inside with dynamite, the effort ended in a cave-in. It is hard to imagine a stranger setting for an evening out than a room hidden behind a waterfall.

## A Mayor's Dream, Delayed

Most of today's park sits on land once owned by R. A. Mitchell, a former mayor of Gadsden, who bought 169 acres in 1909 intending it to become a city park. The dream outlived him. His daughter, Sadie Mitchell Elmore, inherited the property and offered it to the city for $50,000 in 1940. Voters approved the purchase in an election, yet the city did not complete the deal until 1946, by which time the price had risen to $70,000. Improvements began in 1950, and more acreage followed in 1959. On May 12, 1976, the park was listed on the Alabama Register of Landmarks and Heritage. The family name endures across the street, where R. A. Mitchell Elementary School sits across the street from the falls.

## The Legend in Bronze

Above the falls stands a nine-foot-tall bronze statue of a young Cherokee woman named Noccalula. According to local legends, she plunged to her death after her father ordered her to marry a man she did not love. It is a haunting tale, but its history is uncertain. No peer-reviewed historical journal has evaluated the legend, and at least one independent researcher has published evidence suggesting it is apocryphal. The statue, in other words, may honor a story rather than a person.

## Azaleas, Trains and Kayaks

Beyond the gorge, the park is built for families. The Noccalula Falls Botanical Gardens display more than 25,000 azaleas, and visitors can ride a replica of the 1863 C. P. Huntington train, cross the Gilliland-Reese Covered Bridge, built in 1899, or visit a petting zoo and mini-golf course. In 2017 an MSN.com survey of all 50 states rated the park's campground the best campsite in Alabama. That campground, which dated to the 1960s, was closed for more than a year for a rebuild reported at $12 million and reopened in March 2025. The falls have drawn bolder visitors too. In late November 2011, with the water unusually high and the plunge pool deep enough, three expert kayakers ran the drop, and others have done so since. Police met those first paddlers and let them go uncharged, but local law enforcement has since reportedly cited paddlers, since the state does not consider this stretch a state-owned water bottom.

## Flight Context

Noccalula Falls Park lies at approximately 34.041°N, 86.021°W, in Gadsden, Alabama. From 2,500–4,000 feet MSL, look for the wooded line of Black Creek Gorge and the park's open grounds; the falls themselves are small from the air, so the gorge is the better visual cue. Northeast Alabama Regional Airport (KGAD) is about 6 miles away, and Albertville Regional Airport–Thomas J. Brumlik Field (KBFZ) is about 19 miles away. Expect summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms, and mind the rising terrain of the surrounding ridges.
