# Fern Cave National Wildlife Refuge

Beneath a 199-acre refuge on an Alabama mountainside lies a 15-mile cave maze that shelters more than a million endangered gray bats, and almost no one is allowed in.

**Location:** 34.6490, -86.2979

**Categories:** National Wildlife Refuges in Alabama, Caves of Alabama, Protected areas of Jackson County, Alabama, Landforms of Jackson County, Alabama

## Introduction

Most wildlife refuges want visitors. Fern Cave National Wildlife Refuge has no roads, no hiking trails, no developed facilities and not even a public easement across the private land that surrounds it. Entering requires special permits plus permission from several landowners, and trespassing into the cave can bring federal charges. The reason lies underground. Beneath these 199 acres on the flank of Nat Mountain in Jackson County, Alabama, lives the largest known wintering colony of federally endangered gray bats in the United States.

## Ferns at the Edge of the Dark

The refuge takes its name from its cave, and the cave takes its name from a plant. In the entrance sinkhole, early explorers found an abundance of American hart's-tongue ferns, Asplenium scolopendrium var. americanum, a variety listed as federally threatened. Their green fronds gave an underground world an aboveground name. The ferns have not fared well since. Illegal plant collectors have drastically reduced the population at the cave since 1985, The fern also grows in a small pit in Morgan County, and that site has been closed off for similar reasons.

## A Maze Stacked on Itself

Fern Cave has been described as a "vertical and horizontal maze." More than a dozen vertical levels of intertwined canyons are linked by pits and domes, adding up to over 15 miles of known passage and a total vertical extent of 450 feet. Five entrances open into the system. Four lie on refuge land, all closed to visitation, and the fifth belongs to the Southeastern Cave Conservancy. For now, the entire cave is closed to the public to protect the endangered gray and Indiana bats that use it, notably as a hibernaculum, where they pass the winter, and, by one account, as a maternity roost, where young are raised.

## A Million and a Half Wings

Federal officials say more than a million gray bats use the cave, the largest wintering colony of the species in the country. Such a concentration raises the stakes, and U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service biologists confirmed in 2013 that the fungus causing white-nose syndrome is present inside the cave, although mass die-offs of gray bats have not been documented. The bats share the darkness with other specialists: southern cavefish, blackbarred crayfish, spotted-tail salamanders and northern slimy salamanders. Across the refuge as a whole, approximately 200 species of animals make use of the land, from banded sculpin, bluegill and yellow bullhead catfish in the water to white-tailed deer, wild turkeys, squirrels, opossums and raccoons in the woods above.

## A Mountainside Held in Trust

Most of the refuge sits on the western side of Nat Mountain, between Scottsboro and Huntsville, near the town of Paint Rock. The Paint Rock River, a tributary of the Tennessee River, borders its northwestern side. From the relatively flat valley floor, the land climbs to more than 1,500 feet at the mountaintop. The refuge is administered by the Wheeler National Wildlife Refuge in Decatur, and it was never meant for recreation. It exists so that the bats and ferns can go on living undisturbed, which is why the best way for most people to appreciate Fern Cave is from a distance.

## Flight Context

Fern Cave National Wildlife Refuge lies at approximately 34.649°N, 86.298°W, on the western side of Nat Mountain in Jackson County, Alabama, near Paint Rock and between Huntsville and Scottsboro. From 3,500–5,000 feet MSL, look for the Paint Rock River winding along the valley floor at the refuge's northwestern edge, with the forested mountain rising more than 1,500 feet above it. The refuge is undeveloped, with no roads or trails to spot. Nearby airports: Moontown Airport, Huntsville (FAA identifier 3M5), about 11 miles; Scottsboro Municipal Airport–Word Field (FAA identifier 4A6), about 17 miles; and Huntsville International Airport (KHSV), about 27 miles. Expect summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms, and mind the ridgelines in low visibility.
