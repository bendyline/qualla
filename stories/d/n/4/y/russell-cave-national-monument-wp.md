# Russell Cave National Monument

In the hills of northeastern Alabama, a sinkhole opened the huge mouth of Russell Cave, and for thousands of years people wintered beneath it, leaving behind two tons of tools, hearths and burials that trace the prehistory of the Southeast.

**Location:** 34.9766, -85.8143

**Categories:** National Park Service national monuments in Alabama, Archaeological sites in Alabama, Caves of Alabama, Protected areas of Jackson County, Alabama, Limestone caves

## Introduction

Somewhere between 9,000 and 12,000 years ago, the roof of a cavern gave way beneath a hillside near Doran's Cove in what is now northeastern Alabama. The collapse left a sinkhole and exposed an exceptionally large cave entrance. In time, people moved in. For thousands of years, beginning by around 6500 BCE and possibly far earlier, families sheltered beneath that wide entrance. The layers of what they left behind, from campfire charcoal to fishhooks carved from bone, make Russell Cave one of the richest archaeological records in the Southeast.

## A Sea, a Stream and a Rockfall

The rock here began forming more than 300 million years ago at the bottom of a shallow, tropical inland sea, when continental drift had placed this part of Alabama very close to the Equator. Skeletons and shells slowly became limestone, and rainwater, made mildly acidic by carbon dioxide, dissolved a portion of it into a cave. For a long time the cave was uninhabitable, because a large stream filled the entire cavern. Then a rockfall from the ceiling pushed the stream to one side and exposed part of the floor. The first occupants lived on that uneven surface of rock slabs. Over the centuries their debris, along with material falling from the ceiling, raised the floor another 7 to 8 feet, and up to 30 feet near the upper entrance. A spring still flows into the cave and runs underground for 1.5 miles before joining Widow's Creek and, eventually, the Tennessee River.

## Winters in the Shelter

The cave seems to have served mainly as a seasonal winter shelter. Its earliest people relied on the surrounding forest for plant foods, game, fish, stone and firewood, and they left pits for storing nuts and seeds along with mortars, pestles and nut stones for cracking shells and grinding. They were efficient hunters. Their chief weapon was a short, stone-tipped spear hurled with an atlatl, or throwing stick, and they chipped points, knives and scrapers from chert found in the limestone near the cave. Hides were softened and made into clothing. Bone became awls, needles and small fishhooks. Baskets and wooden tools were probably made too, but the soil did not preserve them, and archaeologists found no ornaments at all in these earliest layers.

## Pottery, Arrows and a Hunting Camp

Around 1000 BCE, the tools in the cave changed dramatically. Pottery appeared for the first time, smaller points show that the bow and arrow had replaced the atlatl, bone tools grew more refined, and other implements offer the first evidence of gardening at the site. Bone and shell ornaments finally appeared. Even the pottery keeps time: early pieces carry fabric impressions, later ones the marks of carved wooden paddles. As the Woodland period went on, hunting took over, and the cave became essentially a hunting camp. After about 500 CE people used it less and less. Small hunting parties of the Mississippian culture, who came from permanent farming villages, stopped here occasionally, and the last occupants left around 1000 CE. Centuries later the Cherokee lived in this part of the Tennessee Valley but made little use of the cave; the only modern artifact found was a metal fishhook.

## Two Tons of Evidence

The first excavation reached six feet down, and in all around two tons of artifacts have come out of the site: charcoal from fires, animal bones, spear and arrow points, pottery sherds, and the remains of several adults and children who were buried here, ranging in age from infants to 50 years. Studied together, the finds show a camp with few domestic tools relative to weapons, the signature of seasonal hunters rather than permanent residents. After excavation and reporting in 1956, the National Geographic Society bought the land and donated 310 acres to the American people. Russell Cave became a national monument on May 11, 1961, during the presidency of John F. Kennedy. Its visitor center, dedicated in 1967, honors Gilbert Hovey Grosvenor, editor of National Geographic Magazine from 1903 to 1954.

## The Shelter Today

An elevated wooden boardwalk leads about 300 yards from the visitor center to the cave shelter, where a diorama shows the people who lived there, although the Park Service says touring inside the cave itself is not offered or permitted. The visitor center currently shows temporary exhibits, with permanent ones planned for 2026. The cave itself has a mapped length of about 11.6 miles, the third-longest mapped cave in Alabama, but recreational caving is no longer allowed because of the discovery of several rare species inside, including a scorpion known nowhere else in the world. Brown bats and northern long-eared bats roost within, and sculpin swim in the cave stream. Outside, a paved nature trail and a dirt backcountry trail wind through forest with views of Montague Mountain, and birders come for summer and scarlet tanagers and yellow-billed cuckoos. The monument has traditionally hosted a Native American Festival on the first weekend in May, with storytelling, dancing, flute music and demonstrations of flintknapping and atlatl throwing.

## Flight Context

Russell Cave National Monument lies at about 34.98°N, 85.81°W in Doran's Cove, northeastern Alabama, a few miles northwest of Bridgeport and just south of the Tennessee state line. From 3,000 to 4,000 feet, look for a forested cove tucked beneath Montague Mountain, with the Tennessee River running southwest past Bridgeport and Stevenson to the east and south. Nearby fields include Stevenson Airport (7A6, FAA identifier) about six miles away, Marion County Airport-Brown Field (KAPT) at Jasper, Tennessee, and Chattanooga Metropolitan Airport (KCHA) about 35 miles to the east. Morning fog often fills the valleys, and summer thunderstorms build quickly over the ridges.
