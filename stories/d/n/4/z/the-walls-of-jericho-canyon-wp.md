# The Walls of Jericho (canyon)

On the Tennessee–Alabama line, Turkey Creek has carved a limestone amphitheater with 200-foot walls that a nineteenth-century preacher named for the city of the Bible.

**Location:** 34.9900, -86.1030

**Categories:** Canyons and gorges of the United States, Protected areas of Franklin County, Tennessee, Landforms of Franklin County, Tennessee, Protected areas of Tennessee

## Introduction

Sometime in the late 1800s, a traveling preacher found his way to a hidden limestone bowl on the Tennessee–Alabama border and performed baptisms there. He gave the place a name from the Bible, and it stuck. Today the Walls of Jericho are the heart of a 750-acre natural area in southern Franklin County, Tennessee, about twelve miles south of Winchester, at the end of a strenuous trail.

## An Amphitheater Carved by a Creek

The Walls form a great bowl-shaped amphitheater, enclosed by sheer rock walls some 200 feet high. Turkey Creek drains through the bowl and has been an active force in shaping it, working through the limestone over time. The rock itself seems alive with water. Embedded in the limestone are holes the size of bowling balls, and from them water drips and spouts, turning the walls into a natural fountain. The feature sits right along the southern edge of the natural area, where its boundary follows the Alabama–Tennessee state line, so the canyon sits right on the border between the two states.

## Crockett's Country, Carter's Land

Legend claims that Davy Crockett explored this area in the late 1700s, when his family owned land nearby. Much later, in the 1940s, the Texas oil magnate Harry Lee Carter acquired some 60,000 acres across Franklin County, Tennessee, and Jackson County, Alabama, a territory in the heart of the Southern Cumberlands that became known as the Carter Lands. The Walls of Jericho were part of that holding. For years the Tennessee property stayed open for recreation under the management of the Tennessee Wildlife Resources Agency, until 1977, when the Walls were closed to the public. Protection came slowly. The Nature Conservancy in Tennessee and Alabama carried out a lengthy effort to acquire the land, and in 2006 the State of Tennessee designated the natural area it now owns.

## A Rare Plant and a River Downstream

The Walls are only part of what makes the place worth protecting. The surrounding forest of maples, oaks, hickories, tulip trees, American beech and eastern red cedar hides bluffs, large rock outcroppings, caves and sinkholes. Turkey Creek, which bisects the natural area from north to south, supports the limerock arrowwood, a state-endangered plant; this is one of only three known occurrences in Tennessee. Other rare species live in the watershed, and protecting the creek also helps protect the Upper Paint Rock watershed downstream, where numerous rare mussel and fish species occur in the Paint Rock River.

## Seven Miles Across the Line

The natural area lies within the 8,943-acre Bear Hollow Mountain Wildlife Management Area, which adjoins Alabama's Skyline Wildlife Management Area; together the public lands on both sides of the line total 21,453 acres. The Walls of Jericho have become a popular hiking and camping destination. Strenuous trails lead into the canyon from trailheads in both states; the hike from the Tennessee side is roughly seven miles round trip, mostly downhill going in and uphill coming out, and a primitive campsite at the bottom of the canyon lets hikers spend the night inside the amphitheater.

## Flight Context

The Walls of Jericho lie at approximately 34.990°N, 86.103°W, right on the Tennessee–Alabama state line in southern Franklin County, Tennessee, about twelve miles south of Winchester. From 4,000–5,500 feet MSL, look for the deep, forested Turkey Creek drainage cutting south through the plateau country; the amphitheater itself is tucked under tree cover and is hard to pick out. Nearby airports: Winchester Municipal Airport (KBGF), about 13 miles; Franklin County Airport at Sewanee (KUOS), about 19 miles; and Stevenson Airport, Alabama (FAA identifier 7A6), about 18 miles. Expect summer haze, afternoon thunderstorms and valley fog on cool mornings, and keep clear of the ridges in low visibility.
