# Ave Maria Grotto

In an old quarry at St. Bernard Abbey in Cullman, Alabama, a Benedictine monk who spent his days shoveling coal built 125 miniature churches, shrines and landmarks from stone, concrete and donated odds and ends, most of them copied from photographs of places he never saw.

## Introduction

Brother Joseph Zoettl saw only a handful of the buildings he spent his life recreating, about six by the grotto's own count. The rest he built from photographs and printed descriptions, in the hours when he was not shoveling coal into the furnaces of the St. Bernard Abbey power house. St. Peter's Basilica, the abbey of Monte Cassino, the Sanctuary of Our Lady of Lourdes, the Alamo, the Leaning Tower of Pisa: all of them stand in miniature on a wooded hillside in Cullman, Alabama. The result is Ave Maria Grotto, a landscaped, four-acre park in an old quarry, holding 125 small reproductions that people have been coming to see since 1934.

## The Monk in the Power House

Joseph Zoettl was born in 1878 in Landshut, Bavaria, and came to America to pursue monastic life. He arrived at St. Bernard Abbey in 1892, shortly after the monastery's founding in 1891, and there he spent his life as a Benedictine monk, in prayer and in labor at the abbey power house. The miniatures filled the rest of his time. He built his first replicas in about 1912, though one account says 1918, and his last, the Lourdes Basilica, in 1958, when he was 80 years old. Among the grotto's more unexpected pieces is a model of the St. Bernard Abbey power station itself, the very place where the monk worked shoveling coal. Brother Joseph died in 1961 and is buried in the abbey cemetery.

## Broken Plates and Marbles

Look closely at the models and you see what they are made of. Brother Joseph worked in stone and concrete, but he also used unwanted donated materials: broken plates, costume jewelry, ceramic tile, beads, marbles and seashells. The detail is remarkable, yet the proportions wander. Towers and buttresses are often too large or too small, a quirk the grotto's own literature freely acknowledges. That imperfection is part of the charm. These are not architectural models built from blueprints but a devoted craftsman's interpretations of pictures, assembled from whatever the world sent his way.

## Jerusalem in Miniature

The park fills a former stone quarry that the abbey once used, on the grounds of St. Bernard Abbey. Its display is strung along a forested trail that winds down a steep hillside past clusters of tiny buildings set into the slope. Roman Catholic cathedrals and monasteries dominate, but half of the hillside is given to buildings and scenes from the Holy Land, including views of ancient Jerusalem, which earned the grotto its nickname, "Jerusalem in Miniature." At the heart of it all is the Ave Maria Grotto proper, an artificial cave with a ceiling encrusted in imitation stalactites, sheltering statues of the Virgin Mary and assorted monks and nuns. The grotto was added to the Alabama Register of Landmarks and Heritage on February 24, 1976, and to the National Register of Historic Places on January 19, 1984.

## Castles, Fairies and Babel

Not everything here is sacred. Brother Joseph also built secular buildings and the occasional pagan temple. Spanish missions and German castles share the hillside with South African shrines and Hansel and Gretel's Temple of the Fairies. Near the end of the path rises an imagined Tower of Babel, recalling humanity's vainglorious attempt to build a tower to the heavens. Close by stands a gentler counterpart, the Tower of Thanks, in which Brother Joseph expressed his gratitude for the support he received over the years he spent building his sculptures. The grotto has even inspired fiction: a fictionalized version serves as the backdrop for Jacob Appel's love story Winter Honeymoon.
