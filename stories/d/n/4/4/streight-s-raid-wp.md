# Streight's Raid

In the spring of 1863 a Union column of about 1,700 men marched across northern Alabama to cut a Confederate railroad, only to surrender to a pursuing force less than a third its size.

**Location:** 34.2129, -86.9584

**Categories:** Cavalry raids of the American Civil War, Battles of the western theater of the American Civil War, Military operations of the American Civil War in Alabama, 1863 in Alabama, Nathan Bedford Forrest

## Introduction

On May 3, 1863, a few miles east of Cedar Bluff, Alabama, Union Colonel Abel D. Streight watched Confederate troops file past in front of him, then back again, then past once more. By the time he agreed to surrender his roughly 1,700 men, he believed he was facing a far larger army. He was not. Brigadier General Nathan Bedford Forrest had about 500. When Streight learned of the deception, he reputedly demanded his weapons back so the two sides could fight properly, a request Forrest cheerfully declined. So ended Streight's Raid, a two-week dash across northern Alabama that had been poorly supplied and poorly planned from the start.

## By River, by Foot, by Hope

The goal was ambitious. Streight was to break parts of the Western and Atlantic Railroad, which was supplying the Confederate Army of Tennessee to the north. His force drew on four infantry regiments, the 80th Illinois, the 51st and 73rd Indiana and the 3rd Ohio, along with two companies of the 1st Middle Tennessee Cavalry. They left Nashville by river in early April, marched overland from Palmyra by way of Yellow Creek to Fort Henry, then took to the river again as far as Eastport, Mississippi. From Bear Creek onward, on April 22, they went overland the rest of the way, most of the infantry riding mules, many of them unbroken or worn out, pushing through Tuscumbia, Mount Hope and Moulton. The expedition was coordinated with a more famous operation farther west, where Colonel Benjamin Grierson's raid was driving south through Mississippi from LaGrange, Tennessee, toward Baton Rouge, partly as a feint to confuse scattered Confederate forces.

## Four Days on the Run

Forrest caught up on April 30. At Day's Gap on Sand Mountain, in Cullman County, Streight's men sprang a surprise attack from a concealed hillside position and forced Forrest's men to abandon their artillery. The victory bought only time. Skirmishes followed that same day at Crooked Creek and Hog Mountain, then on May 1 at Blountsville and at the East Branch of the Black Warrior River. Forrest had the advantage of knowing the home territory, and he enjoyed the sympathy and aid of the local population, who hindered Streight throughout his march. On May 2 the column fought again at the crossing of Black Creek near Gadsden, and while crossing Will's Creek its ammunition was damaged. Skirmishing carried on through May 2 and 3 near Blount's plantation, about 15 miles from Gadsden in Cherokee County.

## A Girl at Black Creek

The most famous of those local helpers was sixteen-year-old Emma Sansom. Streight had burned the Black Creek bridge to slow his pursuers, and when Forrest arrived, Emma offered to show him a place where she had seen cows cross the creek. She rode behind him on his horse to the spot, and Forrest's men crossed where the cattle waded, continuing the chase that ended the next day. The Gadsden chapter of the United Daughters of the Confederacy unveiled a monument to her in 1907. In 2020, during racial justice protests, the statue became contested: the Southern Poverty Law Center counted it among the many monuments built in the Jim Crow era to support white supremacy through the Lost Cause movement, and a group of Sansom's own descendants signed a letter supporting its removal. The city council voted against moving it, and, by recent accounts, it remains on Broad Street downtown.

## The Price of a Bluff

On May 3, Streight's column passed through Centre and reached Cedar Bluff. Forrest's final stroke required no charge. He simply paraded his small force back and forth in front of the Union commander until Streight was convinced he was outnumbered. The raid's toll was stark: by one count, 12 Union soldiers killed, 69 wounded and 1,466 captured, for total losses of 1,547. The captured men were taken east to Richmond, Virginia, the Confederate capital, as prisoners of war. A march meant to cut the lifeline of a Confederate army instead delivered most of its men into captivity, short of the railroad it had set out to destroy.

## Flight Context

The article's coordinates, approximately 34.213°N, 86.958°W, fall in Cullman County, Alabama, about seven miles south-southeast of the Day's Gap battle site on Sand Mountain, where Forrest caught up with the raiders on April 30, 1863. The surrender took place far to the east, a few miles east of Cedar Bluff in Cherokee County. From 4,500–6,500 feet MSL you can follow the general line of the raid eastward across the ridges toward Gadsden. Nearby airports: Cullman Regional Airport–Folsom Field (KCMD), about 7 miles away; Northeast Alabama Regional Airport (KGAD) at Gadsden, near the Black Creek crossing; and Centre-Piedmont-Cherokee County Regional Airport (KPYP) near Centre, close to the end of the raid. Expect summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms over the ridges.
