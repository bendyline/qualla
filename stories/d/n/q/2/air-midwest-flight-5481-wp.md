# Air Midwest Flight 5481

In 2003 a commuter flight leaving Charlotte stalled and crashed into a hangar, killing all 21 aboard, after a botched maintenance adjustment met an overload hidden by outdated passenger-weight estimates.

**Location:** 35.2069, -80.9464

**Categories:** Aviation accidents and incidents in the United States in 2003, Airliner accidents and incidents in North Carolina, Airliner accidents and incidents caused by maintenance errors, Charlotte Douglas International Airport, History of Charlotte, North Carolina

## Introduction

At 8:46 on the morning of January 8, 2003, the tower at Charlotte Douglas International Airport cleared a Beechcraft 1900D commuter plane for takeoff from Runway 18R. Air Midwest Flight 5481, flying as US Airways Express, was bound for Greenville–Spartanburg International Airport in Greer, South Carolina, a short hop across the state line. The aircraft was airborne for about 35 seconds. It stalled, fell and struck an aircraft hangar on the airport grounds, killing all 21 people aboard. What doomed it was not a single failure but two hidden problems that met on the same morning, neither of which would have been enough alone.

## Twenty-One Aboard

Nineteen passengers and two pilots were on the flight. Twelve of the passengers were American, as were both crew members; the others came from the Bahamas, India, Canada and Portugal. In command was Captain Catherine "Katie" Leslie, 25, the youngest captain flying for the airline at the time. She had logged 1,865 hours of company flying, 1,100 of them in command of a Beechcraft 1900D. Beside her was First Officer Jonathan Gibbs, 27, with 706 hours in the type. Both were based in Charlotte. The aircraft itself, registration N233YV, had been delivered new to Air Midwest in 1996 and had flown 15,003 hours. That morning, ramp agents loaded 23 checked bags into it, two of them unusually heavy.

## Thirty-Five Seconds

The trouble began the instant the wheels left the runway. The nose pitched up fast, reaching 20 degrees by the time the plane was just 90 feet above the ground. Both pilots pushed forward on the control column, forcefully, and the airplane would not answer. The nose kept rising until it reached 54 degrees. The stall-warning horn sounded and the crew declared an emergency. After climbing to about 1,150 feet, the Beechcraft stalled and came down into the hangar. A US Airways mechanic on the ground was treated for smoke inhalation. Of the 21 people on the plane, no one survived.

## A Cable Set Wrong

The first problem had been built into the airplane two nights earlier. At Air Midwest's maintenance station at Tri-State Airport in Huntington, West Virginia, a contract mechanic adjusted the elevator control cables, which govern the aircraft's pitch. Investigators found he was performing the task for the first time, under on-the-job training. The turnbuckles that set tension on the cables were adjusted incorrectly, leaving the elevator without enough travel, so the pilots would not have enough pitch control. A control-travel check after the adjustment would have caught the error, but the procedure did not require one and none was made. The inspector who was training the mechanic was also the person assigned to inspect the work independently, a conflict of interest the investigators noted.

## The Weight of Assumptions

The second problem was hidden in arithmetic. Before departure, the pilots added up the plane's take-off weight and found it within limits. But their figures relied on FAA-approved average passenger weights that were simply wrong. The NTSB found the real average passenger weighed more than 20 pounds more than the estimate. Using baggage recovered from the site and passenger weights drawn from next-of-kin and the medical examiner, investigators concluded that the aircraft was 580 pounds over its maximum take-off weight, with its center of gravity 5 percent behind the allowable limit. Neither the tail-heavy load nor the misrigged cables would have caused the loss of control on its own, which is why the plane had flown safely out of Huntington. Together they left the pilots with no way to hold the nose down. The FAA's estimated weight values had not been revised since 1936. In 2003 the agency raised its assumed passenger weight for commuter aircraft like this one by about 10 pounds and the standard checked-bag weight from 25 to 30 pounds.

## What Remains

Air Midwest ceased operations in 2008. A memorial to the 21 victims stands outside Charlotte. The crash was later examined in the documentary series Mayday, in a season-five episode titled "Dead Weight," a blunt title for a crash in which a maintenance shortcut and a set of trusted but outdated numbers combined, quietly, into catastrophe.

## Flight Context

The crash site lies at approximately 35.21°N, 80.95°W, at the northwest corner of the US Airways hangar on the grounds of Charlotte Douglas International Airport (KCLT). Flight 5481 departed Runway 18R. Any overflight here means operating inside Charlotte's Class B airspace, so contact approach and expect vectors; the hangar is best seen from the terminal side or on approach rather than from a dedicated sightseeing pass. The flight's intended destination was Greenville-Spartanburg International (KGSP), about 65 nm to the west-southwest. Nearby alternatives for light aircraft include Gastonia Municipal (FAA: AKH, KAKH), about 10 nm west, and Rock Hill-York County (KUZA), about 14 nm south-southwest. Winter mornings can bring low clouds and fog to the Piedmont.
