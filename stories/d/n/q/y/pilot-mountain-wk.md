# Pilot Mountain

A lone quartzite knob in the North Carolina Piedmont that has told travelers they were nearly home for centuries, and is now a state park where the summit belongs to the birds.

**Location:** 36.3401, -80.4742

## Introduction

Thirty-seven days into their journey from Pennsylvania in 1753, a party of Moravian settlers caught sight of a solitary knob of rock standing up out of the rolling North Carolina Piedmont. Their diary records the moment: they "saw the Pilot Mountain, and rejoiced to think that we would soon see the boundary of Carolina, and set foot in our own dear land." That has always been this mountain's job. It is not a peak you conquer so much as a sign that you are nearly there, and it has served travelers as a navigation aid since time immemorial.

## The Great Guide

Long before the Moravians, the people of this corner of the state had their own name for it. According to NCpedia, the Saponi and Tutelo who lived in the area called the peak Jomeokee, the "great guide" or "pilot." The Great Wagon Road, which carried settlers into the Piedmont from the Northeast, skirted its base, and the English name says the same thing. What makes the mountain such a good marker is its silhouette. A curved depression dips between the ridge slope, the Little Pinnacle and the round knob of the Big Pinnacle, giving the whole thing a saddle shape that reads clearly from miles away. It can even be seen from milepost 189.1 of the Blue Ridge Parkway, across the line in Virginia. Today the peak gives its name to a nearby town, and U.S. Route 52 threads past it on the way north from Winston-Salem, about 20 miles to the southeast.

## The Core That Would Not Wear Away

Pilot Mountain is a monadnock, a lone survivor left standing after the land around it eroded away. It rises to about 2,420 feet and is a remnant of the ancient Sauratown Mountains. Its story begins as beach sand on Gondwana. During the Alleghanian orogeny, that sand was forced beneath Laurentia, and the pressure turned it into quartzite that is 98 percent pure. As the surrounding mountain wore down over the ages, the hard quartzite core held its ground, and that stubborn remnant is the Big Pinnacle, the dome known locally as "The Knob." Its bare rock walls are high and colorful, its rounded top is covered in vegetation, and it stands roughly 1,400 feet above the surrounding terrain. The Little Pinnacle is made of different stuff entirely: biotite gneiss layered with schist. The federal government recognized the mountain as a National Natural Landmark in 1974.

## A Summit Left to the Birds

Here is the twist for anyone who likes to stand on top of things: nobody stands on top of this one. Climbers are not permitted to summit the Big Pinnacle, because doing so would disturb the plants and the nesting birds that live up there. The rock work happens lower down instead, on a long cliff along the loop of the Ledge Spring Trail, and climbers must register with the park before they climb. Hikers get their own version of the view. A paved road climbs to the visitor center and campground, then on to a parking lot on the ridge, where trails lead out to the Little Pinnacle Overlook and other viewing points. The Jomeokee Trail circles the base of the Big Pinnacle, close enough to crane your neck at its walls. For those who prefer to earn the view, the Grindstone Trail, which the park rates strenuous, climbs 3.5 miles one way from across the road from the visitor center to the summit picnic area.

## From Ridge to River

In 1968, primarily through the efforts of local citizens, Pilot Mountain became North Carolina's fourteenth state park. The park does not stop at the mountain. A corridor of land runs down to the Yadkin River, where a separate river section is set aside for fishing and paddling, and anglers need a state fishing license. On the other side, the Mountains-to-Sea and Sauratown trails link the park to Hanging Rock State Park. The park keeps things simple. There are no restaurants or food stands, so visitors pack their own meals to picnic areas, some open to the sky and some under shelter roofs. Camping and trailer parking are allowed in designated areas on the lower slopes from March 15 to November 30, though the two paddle-in campsites on the river stay open year-round. The mountain has had its hard seasons too: in November 2021 a wildfire of more than 1,000 acres swept the park, and smoke wrapped the Knob that generations of travelers had steered by.

## Flight Context

The coordinates mark the summit area, near 36.340°N, 80.474°W, where the mountain tops out at about 2,420 feet. The Big Pinnacle's rock walls and the saddle between it and the Little Pinnacle are the unmistakable marker; look for U.S. Route 52 running past the mountain toward Winston-Salem to the southeast, the Yadkin River to the south, and the higher Sauratown ridges and Hanging Rock to the east. Plan to stay at least 1,000 feet above the roughly 2,420-foot summit; 4,500 to 6,500 feet MSL gives a good profile view of the Knob. Distances from the summit: Mount Airy/Surry County Airport (KMWK) is about 8 nm NNW, Smith Reynolds Airport in Winston-Salem (KINT) about 17 nm SE, Elkin Municipal (KZEF) about 16 nm WSW, and Piedmont Triad International (KGSO) about 30 nm ESE. Haze can soften the silhouette from a distance, so pick a clear day.
