# Shell Service Station (Winston-Salem, North Carolina)

A 1930 filling station built in the shape of a giant scallop shell, the last survivor of a Winston-Salem distributor's fleet of shell-shaped stations, restored by Preservation North Carolina in the 1990s.

**Location:** 36.0678, -80.2142

## Introduction

How do you tell drivers which brand of gasoline you sell before they can read the sign? In 1930 a Winston-Salem oil distributor settled on the most literal answer possible. At the corner of Sprague and Peachtree Streets it put up a filling station shaped like a giant scallop shell, painted yellow-orange, so that the building itself became the Shell logo. Nearly a century later, that station is still there, the last of its kind in town.

## Advertising You Could Drive Into

The idea came from the Quality Oil Company, which had become Winston-Salem's new local Shell distributor in the 1920s and wanted to build brand awareness in the market. Its owners decided to draw customers with a series of shell-shaped service stations. According to the National Register nomination, the first station, and perhaps the others, was built by the contractor Frank L. Blum and Company (another account credits R.H. Burton and his son, Ralph), shaping the curving form from wire covered in concrete stucco. About eight such stations were built around Winston-Salem, including one in nearby Kernersville. The single-story station at Sprague and Peachtree is the only one that remains. It is an example of what is known as representational or novelty architecture, and it speaks to the literalism that ran through some advertising in the 1920s and 1930s, when a building could be its own billboard.

## Peeling Back the Paint

By the time preservationists took an interest, the shell had faded. In the 1990s Preservation North Carolina, an organization dedicated to saving historic sites, spent a year and $50,000 bringing the station back to its original condition. Workers stripped away layers of faded yellow paint to reveal the shell's original yellow-orange color. They repaired the original front door and fixed a crack that had been sealed with nothing more than black tar. They also rebuilt the wooden, trellised shelter where cars had once been washed and serviced in the shade. Quality Oil Company, the business that had commissioned the station in the first place, donated restored gas pumps and replica lamp posts to finish the job.

## A Shell in the Neighborhood

The station was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on May 13, 1976, and it sits within the Waughtown-Belview Historic District, a largely residential section of Winston-Salem with more than a thousand contributing buildings dating from about 1834 to 1955. Among the district's Greek Revival, Colonial Revival, Queen Anne and Craftsman houses, the shell stands out like a seaside souvenir left on a city street corner. It no longer sells gasoline. After its restoration it served as a regional office for Preservation North Carolina until 2011, and today it is privately owned, a roadside attraction whose exterior can be viewed at any time. It keeps company, in spirit, with other roadside oddities of the era, such as the airplane-shaped service station in Powell, Tennessee, and the Teapot Dome station in Zillah, Washington.

## Flight Context

The Shell Service Station is at 36.0678°N, 80.2142°W at the corner of Sprague and Peachtree Streets in the Waughtown-Belview neighborhood of southeast Winston-Salem, North Carolina. The building is small, so orient first on downtown Winston-Salem, marked by the 314-foot Reynolds Building about 2.4 nm to the north-northwest, with Old Salem's historic district about 1.8 nm northwest. A viewing altitude of about 2,500-3,000 feet MSL gives a good look at the neighborhood. Smith Reynolds Airport (KINT) is only about 4 nm north, and Piedmont Triad International Airport (KGSO) is about 14 nm east, so check the sectional for controlled airspace and talk to the appropriate controllers. Summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are common.
