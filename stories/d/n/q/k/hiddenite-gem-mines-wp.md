# Hiddenite Gem Mines

In the North Carolina Piedmont town that shares its name with a rare green gem, farmers ploughed up emeralds, a museum lost a record crystal to thieves, and miners have pulled some of North America's largest emeralds from the ground.

**Location:** 35.9137, -81.0830

**Categories:** Gemstone mines, Mines in North Carolina, Tourist attractions in Alexander County, North Carolina, Emeralds, Geology

## Introduction

Somewhere, if it still exists, is a 1,276-carat emerald crystal from Hiddenite, North Carolina. When it was found in 1882, it was reportedly the largest emerald crystal then known from North America. It ended up at the American Museum of Natural History in New York, and in 1950 it was reportedly stolen. It has never been recovered. The lost crystal is only one chapter in the story of the Hiddenite Gem Mines, a cluster of workings in Alexander County where the ground has given up emeralds, hiddenite (a gem first found here), and treasure enough to keep people digging.

## A Gem With a Man's Name

Emeralds turned up here first, and by accident: they were found during ploughing, one account says as early as 1874. In 1879 the first specimens of a different stone came to light nearby. Local collector J. A. D. Stephenson brought them to William Earl Hidden, a geologist who had come to North Carolina looking for platinum for Thomas Edison. Hidden sent samples to Dr. J. Lawrence Smith in Louisville, Kentucky, who determined that the green crystals were a variety of the mineral spodumene. The variety was given a name that echoed Hidden's: hiddenite. The town where it was found now shares that name. Stephenson lives on in the record, too: the late 19th-century collector was instrumental both in the discovery of the first North Carolina emerald and in the first discovery of hiddenite anywhere in the world.

## Three Mines, Many Names

Three larger mines are known collectively as the Hiddenite Gem Mines. The Emerald Hollow Mine is a public gem mine, where visitors can search for stones themselves and bring them to an on-site lapidary shop to be cleaned, finished and even made into jewelry. The North American Emerald Mine, known as NAEM and formerly as the Rist Mine, is operated privately by North American Emerald Mines Inc., founded by James K. Hill; there, large, modern professional equipment has been used for the excavating. The third, the Adams mine, has gone by a string of earlier names - the Warren mine, the Emerald & Hiddenite mine, the Turner mine and the Hiddenite mine - a list that hints at how many hands have worked this ground.

## Bigger, and Bigger Again

The region's record book keeps getting rewritten. In 1969 an emerald of 1,438 carats was uncovered, reported as the largest yet found in North America at the time. It is said to have been named the Stephenson Emerald in honor of John A. D. Stephenson, the 19th-century collector. In 1971, approximately 9,400 carats of emeralds reportedly came out of the area, and some of those stones are said to be displayed at the Smithsonian. The gem hiddenite had its own banner moment in 1980, when more than 1,500 hiddenite crystals were reportedly found in a large underground vein pocket. Then came the NAEM finds. In 2003, Jamie Hill uncovered an emerald crystal of about 1,869 carats there (some sources say 1,861.9), reported to be worth over one million dollars. In 2006, the mine reportedly unearthed another large emerald, said to be ten inches long. All told, more than 30,000 carats of emeralds are reported to have come from NAEM, with an estimated value of $9 million.

## Digging in the Piedmont

Hiddenite lies in the Piedmont of North Carolina, in Alexander County, and gem mining is its calling card. The contrast between the two kinds of operation tells the story of the place. At the private NAEM, heavy equipment has worked the ground in search of the next record-breaker. At Emerald Hollow, members of the public can try their own luck and have their finds cleaned and finished in the lapidary shop. The same soil that gave up emeralds to a farmer's plough, by one account in 1874, has been turned over by professionals and hopeful visitors alike, each hoping to hold something no one has seen before.

## Flight Context

The Hiddenite mining area centers on approximately 35.9137 N, 81.0830 W in Alexander County, North Carolina, northwest of Statesville in the rolling Piedmont foothills. From 3,000-3,500 ft MSL the small town of Hiddenite and the scattered clearings of the mine sites lie among farmland and woods. Nearby airports: Statesville Regional Airport (KSVH; about 11 nm southeast) and Hickory Regional Airport (KHKY; about 18 nm southwest). Concord-Padgett Regional Airport (KJQF) is about 37 nm south-southeast. Expect summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms; morning valley fog is possible in cooler months.
