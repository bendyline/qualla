# Fort Dobbs (North Carolina)

A log blockhouse near present-day Statesville guarded North Carolina's colonial frontier, saw one night battle in the Anglo-Cherokee War, vanished, and was rebuilt in 2019 from archaeology and old reports.

**Location:** 35.8217, -80.8950

**Categories:** French and Indian War forts, Forts in North Carolina, Anglo-Cherokee War, Military and war museums in North Carolina, Museums in Iredell County, North Carolina, Forts on the National Register of Historic Places in North Carolina

## Introduction

On the night of February 27, 1760, between eight and nine o'clock, the dogs at Fort Dobbs began making an uncommon noise near a spring the garrison sometimes used. Captain Hugh Waddell suspected a trap meant to draw his small force out, so he took only ten men, Captain Bailie among them. They had not gone 300 yards when, by Waddell's count, at least 60 or 70 Cherokee warriors attacked. It was the only battle this lonely log fort near present-day Statesville ever saw, and it came at the violent peak of a war built on years of broken trust.

## A Blockhouse on the Edge

Settlers had poured into North Carolina's backcountry along the Great Wagon Road from Pennsylvania, Scots-Irish, English and German settlers among them. In 1747, only about 100 men of militia age lived west of present-day Hillsborough; by 1754, six western counties held around 22,000 of the colony's 65,000 people. In 1755, Royal Governor Arthur Dobbs ordered a fortified log structure to protect Rowan County settlers from French-allied Native American attacks. He also had his own stake in the frontier, owning more than 200,000 acres on the Rocky River. The legislature set aside £1,000, a bargain beside the £60,000 reportedly spent on New York's Fort Stanwix. Waddell oversaw construction, and by late 1756 inspectors described an oblong blockhouse 53 feet by 40 and about 24 feet high. The state historic sites program calls it North Carolina's only permanent provincial frontier fort.

## A War of Broken Faith

The Cherokee had fought alongside the British at Fort Duquesne, only to feel their service went unappreciated, in some accounts treated by General Forbes as enlisted men rather than allies. Worse followed. Virginia frontiersmen killed and scalped as many as 40 Cherokee, and some later claimed bounties by passing the scalps off as Shawnee. Settlers pressed past treaty lines onto vital hunting grounds. In one account from North Carolina's Brushy Mountains, a hunter named Hamilton and a friend invited two Cherokee hunters to share their camp, then killed them with axes while they slept. Seeking retaliation, Cherokee warriors struck settlements on the Yadkin and Catawba in the spring of 1759, against the wishes of leaders like Attakullakulla, killing as many as 40 men, women and children, some living within 10 miles of the fort. Then South Carolina's governor took Cherokee peace delegations hostage and marched them to Fort Prince George. Soldiers at the fort abused local Cherokee women, in some accounts raping them at Estatoe, and the reprisals killed South Carolina settlers. After a Cherokee attack on the fort left an officer dead, the garrison killed the remaining Cherokee captives in their cells. In January and February 1760 alone, by one count nearly 100 settlers were killed, and the edge of settlement in both Carolinas was pushed back more than 100 miles.

## Fire and Retribution

The fight outside Fort Dobbs that February night ended in a victory for the Provincials. Waddell reported that the attackers "could not have had less than 10 or 12 killed or wounded," while he lost one boy killed near the fort and had two men wounded, one of them scalped. Around the same time, Cherokee war parties struck Fort Loudoun, Fort Prince George and Ninety-Six. The British answered with fire. In June 1760, a force under Archibald Montgomerie burned 10 Cherokee towns before being defeated at Echoee. The next year, James Grant's army burned some 15 to 18 towns, destroying the homes, fields and food stores of approximately 5,000 Cherokee, and forcing their leaders to sue for peace.

## Rot, Rumor and a Stone Marker

With the war over, North Carolina refused to pay for a garrison. A former lieutenant, Walter Lindsay, kept watch over the fort and its arms, but in 1764 legislators recommended removing the supplies, and by 1766 the fort was described as rotting and collapsing. It slipped from the landscape. In 1847, locals went digging for a rumored cannon; traces of their hole turned up in the 21st century. The Daughters of the American Revolution received the believed site in 1909, raised a stone marker, and later built a two-room cabin from logs donated from houses on a nearby farm. The state bought the land in the 1970s, and rain pushed back the planned July 4, 1976 opening to that September. Excavations begun in 1967 confirmed the fort's site by 1968. That first year, archaeologist Stanley South laid a transparency of an 18th-century survey over a modern aerial photograph and found the old surveyed lines still visible in the terrain.

## The Fort Walks Back

In 2006, researcher Lawrence Babits proposed a reconstruction. He argued that Dobbs likely drew on Scottish barracks he knew, such as Bernera and Ruthven, and that the "opposite angles" in the 1756 description were flankers, square wooden structures at the corners that let defenders shoot into the flank of any attackers. The Friends of Fort Dobbs raised the money, and from 2016 to 2019 the blockhouse rose again. Nearly 2,000 people came to the grand opening on September 21, 2019. Inside, which visitors see on a guided tour, it is furnished as if the soldiers just stepped out. A new visitor center opened at the site in 2026, and Fort Dobbs is the only state historic site in North Carolina tied to the French and Indian War. Attendance has been reported at about 20,000 visitors a year.

## Flight Context

Fort Dobbs State Historic Site lies at approximately 35.82°N, 80.90°W, just north of Statesville in Iredell County, North Carolina. The reconstructed log blockhouse is best picked out from 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL; use the town of Statesville as a reference point. Statesville Regional Airport (KSVH) is about 4 nm southwest, making this an easy local sightseeing hop. Hickory Regional (KHKY) is about 25 nm west, Concord-Padgett Regional (KJQF) about 28 nm south, and Charlotte Douglas International (KCLT) about 37 nm south. Piedmont haze is common on summer afternoons.
