# Railway accident on the Bostian Bridge

In the early hours of August 27, 1891, a late-running passenger train plunged from the Bostian Bridge west of Statesville, killing at least 22 people - and on the same date 119 years later, a real train killed a man who had come to the bridge hoping to hear the ghost of the first.

## Introduction

August 27 is a heavy date on the Bostian Bridge. On that day in 1891, a passenger train fell from this stone and brick span over Third Creek, just west of Statesville, North Carolina, and 23 people died, by the most commonly cited count. On the same date in 2010, a group of people stood on the bridge in the dark, hoping to hear echoes of that disaster, and a train that was entirely real bore down on them. Between those two nights runs a story of a disputed cause, of a small town caring for its injured in private homes, and of a legend that drew people onto a working railroad bridge.

## Thirty-Four Minutes Behind

Train No. 9 of the Richmond & Danville Railroad pulled out of Statesville at around 2:30 a.m. on August 27, 1891, behind Steam Locomotive No. 166. It was a five-car train: a baggage car, a first- and second-class car, a Pullman sleeper, and a railroad superintendent's private car. It was also running late, by one account 34 minutes. To make up time, it was probably traveling at 35 to 40 miles per hour as it left town. Less than five minutes after departing Statesville, the train reached the Bostian Bridge, a 60-foot-high, five-bay natural stone and brick structure spanning Third Creek. There it derailed and crashed from the bridge. A coroner's jury four days later blamed unknown people who had removed spikes from the rails, though others blamed the track's neglected condition and the train's speed. One detail conveys the violence of the fall: the sleeping car struck the ground 153 feet from the point where it had left the span.

## A Town Without a Hospital

In the darkness, several of the less seriously injured survivors ran back toward Statesville to raise the alarm. Rescue workers made their way out to the wreck and carried the injured into town, but Statesville had no hospital. The injured were instead taken in and cared for in private homes, which turned ordinary households into sickrooms overnight. The dead were brought to a tobacco warehouse, where they could be identified. Twenty-three people were killed by the most commonly cited count, though some sources say 22. Each of them had boarded a train that should have arrived merely late. For Statesville, a town that had to become its own hospital that night, the disaster was not something read about in a newspaper but carried through its own front doors.

## The Crash That Wasn't There

Fifty years later, on the morning of August 27, 1941, a woman sat in a car with a flat tire near the bridge, waiting for help. She later described hearing and seeing a train crash off the bridge, its old-fashioned wooden passenger cars shattering, and she said she could hear the screaming and moaning of the wounded. Her husband and the owner of a nearby shop went to look. They found no wreck in Third Creek. Whatever she experienced, her account became part of the bridge's reputation, and it helped turn a real disaster into a ghost story - one that, decades later, would draw people to the bridge in the middle of the night.

## The Train That Was Real

Just before 3:00 a.m. on August 27, 2010, the 119th anniversary of the accident, somewhere between ten and twelve amateur ghost observers were on the bridge illegally, hoping to hear the sounds of 1891 and perhaps see something. Instead, a Norfolk Southern train came toward them, three locomotives and one car. Officials said the group at first did not run, because they took it for the ghost train; then they ran back toward Statesville, trying to cover about 150 feet to safety. Christopher Kaiser, 29, of Charlotte, did not make it. He was struck and killed by the train. A woman he had been trying to bring to safety fell 30 to 40 feet from the bridge and was so badly injured that she was treated at Carolinas Medical Center in Charlotte. The bridge's hardest lesson is also its simplest: on a working railroad, the trains are never ghosts.

## Flight Context

Statesville Regional Airport (KSVH) lies only about 1.6 nm to the west, so the bridge sits almost beneath the airport's traffic area - keep a sharp lookout and monitor local traffic.
