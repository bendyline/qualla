# Key Underwood Coon Dog Memorial Graveyard

In the woods of rural Colbert County, Alabama, more than 350 coonhounds lie beneath marble and tin headstones in a graveyard that began in 1937 when one hunter buried his dog, Troop, at their old hunting camp.

**Location:** 34.6300, -87.9667

**Categories:** Animal cemeteries, Cemeteries in Alabama, Dog monuments, Hunting with hounds, Roadside attractions

## Introduction

"He wasn't the best, but he was the best I ever owned." The epitaph belongs to a dog, one of more than 350 buried in a graveyard in rural Colbert County, Alabama, not far from the town of Cherokee. The Key Underwood Coon Dog Memorial Graveyard admits no cats, no lap dogs and no ordinary family pets. It is reserved for coon dogs, and the people who run it take the distinction very seriously.

## Fifteen Years With Troop

It began with one hunter and one hound. On September 4, 1937, Key Underwood buried his coon dog, Troop, at an old hunting camp, the place where the two had hunted raccoons together for 15 years. Underwood meant only to lay Troop to rest somewhere that mattered to them both; the memorial that grew around that grave was a serendipitous afterthought. Nearly half a century later, in a 1984 interview with columnist Rheta Grimsley Johnson, he explained that he had simply wanted to do "something special for a special coon dog." In the decades since, other hunters have brought their own hounds here. Today a statue of two coonhounds treeing a raccoon marks the entrance, and the Tennessee Valley Coon Hunters Association maintains the grounds, which draw nearly 7,000 visitors a year. The State of Alabama officially recognizes it as a historic cemetery.

## No Poodles Need Apply

Underwood left no doubt about who belonged. "It would reveal that you must not know much about coon hunters and their dogs," he said, "if you think we would contaminate this burial place with poodles and lap dogs." Three requirements govern admission. The owner must verify that the dog was a purebred coonhound, a witness must declare that the animal was a coon dog, and a member of the local coonhunters' organization must be allowed to view the remains. The rules are less about breed than about quarry. "A dog can't run no deer, possum — nothing like that," said O'Neal Bolton, a former caretaker. "He's got to be a straight coon dog, and he's got to be full hound." Redbones, black and tans, blueticks, Plotts and Treeing Walkers qualify, and non-AKC Southern hunting hounds such as Black Mouth Curs, Catahoulas and Mountain Curs may be admitted too, but only with at least three witnesses who have seen the dog track and tree coons single-handedly. Even so, the standard can seem to depend on who is telling the tale, and when.

## Marble, Tin and Bullet Holes

Walking among the graves, you see every kind of devotion. Some markers are homemade from metal or wood; others are engraved marble stones that would not look out of place in a human cemetery. Hunter's Famous Amos, Ralston Purina's 1984 Dog of the Year, rests here among hundreds of less famous hounds. As the cemetery's 75th anniversary approached, visitors began leaving coins and replica service medals on the graves. The entrance is festooned with warning signs, some pocked with bullet holes, advising that the neighborhood keeps watch, forbidding fires and stressing that only coon dogs buried with permission are allowed. The sport itself has changed around the graveyard. People once hunted raccoons for their hides, or ate them during the Depression; "these days hunting's about competition," and "we don't shoot [raccoons] anymore." Grounds caretaker Lee Hatton describes the ideal dog as "honest, meaning it won't run deer or rabbits; and it'll stay put, meaning it'll stay no matter rain, a storm, or another dog aggravating it."

## Bluegrass and a Liar's Contest

Every Labor Day, the Tennessee Valley Coon Hunters Association throws a gathering at the cemetery in tribute to its founding. There is bluegrass music, dancing, barbecue and a liar's contest, which seems fitting for a place where the admission standard depends partly on a good story. Admission is free, though donations help pay for upkeep; in 2014, eight Redbone Coonhound puppies were sold at the gathering to raise money for the grounds. Local politicians often turn up. The graveyard has even reached Hollywood. The 2002 movie Sweet Home Alabama featured a recreated version of it, with the filmmakers taking artistic license and moving it to south Alabama, and its characters go searching for the graves of dogs named Bear and Bryant, an homage to the football coach Bear Bryant.
