# Second Battle of Corinth

On October 3-4, 1862, Union troops held the vital rail junction of Corinth, Mississippi, against Earl Van Dorn's assaults, then let the beaten Confederate army slip away.

**Location:** 34.9710, -88.5217

**Categories:** Battles of the American Civil War in Mississippi, Battles of the western theater of the American Civil War, Union victories of the American Civil War, Corinth, Mississippi, October 1862

## Introduction

At 1 p.m. on October 4, 1862, with the Confederate assault on Corinth, Mississippi, broken, Major General William S. Rosecrans rode along his lines. He was not launching a pursuit. He was denying, in person, a rumor that he had been killed. At Battery Robinett he dismounted, bared his head and reportedly told his soldiers, "I stand in the presence of brave men, and I take my hat off to you." It was a gracious gesture, made at the very hour when a pursuit of the retreating enemy would have been most effective.

## Where the Railroads Met

Corinth mattered because of its tracks. Here the Memphis and Charleston Railroad crossed the Mobile and Ohio, making the town a critical rail junction for supply lines and communications across the Western Theater. Rosecrans held it with roughly 23,000 Union troops. Coming at him was Major General Earl Van Dorn with about 22,000 men, his combined army including Sterling Price's Army of the West, with divisions under Mansfield Lovell, Louis Hébert and Dabney H. Maury. The weather was brutal for fighting. On October 3 the temperature reached 94 degrees Fahrenheit, water was scarce, and many men came close to fainting from exertion. That morning, three Union divisions advanced into old Confederate rifle pits north and northwest of town, and Van Dorn tried to envelop them from both sides.

## Falling Back to the Redoubts

Around 1:30 p.m. on October 3, a gap opened between the Union divisions of Davies and McKean, and the whole Union line fell back to within half a mile of the redoubts ringing Corinth. The day took a heavy toll on Union commanders. General Pleasant Hackleman was killed, and General Oglesby was seriously wounded, shot through the lungs. By nightfall, Rosecrans's army had withdrawn inside its fortifications. The Confederates had pushed the Union back, but the hardest ground still lay ahead of them: the ring of redoubts and batteries around the town. For the exhausted, thirsty men on both sides, the night offered little rest before the guns opened again.

## The Ditch at Battery Robinett

At 4:30 a.m. on October 4, Confederate artillery opened the second day. The infantry assault was supposed to follow, but at 7 a.m. Hébert reported himself ill, and Martin E. Green took over his command; Van Dorn would later blame the defeat on Hébert's failure to open the attack on time. When Green's brigades finally went in, they captured Battery Powell, only to be driven out by a Union counterattack. The fiercest fighting raged at Battery Robinett, a redan protected by a five-foot ditch. Colonel William P. Rogers of the 2nd Texas and Colonel John Daly of the 18th Arkansas were both killed in the failed attack. Phifer's brigade briefly broke into the town itself before Union reserves routed it. By 1 p.m. the battle was effectively over. The cost was staggering, though tallies differ: one commonly cited count gives 2,520 Union casualties, including 355 killed, and 4,233 Confederate, including 473 killed and 1,763 captured or missing, while the American Battlefield Trust's estimate is 2,359 Union and 4,838 Confederate.

## Two or Three Hours

Grant had ordered Rosecrans to pursue Van Dorn without delay, yet the Union march did not begin until the morning of October 5. Rosecrans explained that his troops needed rest and that the thicketed country made progress difficult by day and impossible by night. Grant was disgusted: "Two or three hours of pursuit on the day of battle, without anything except what the men carried on their persons, would have been worth more than any pursuit commenced the next day could have possibly been." Historians still disagree about Rosecrans in the battle. His biographer William Lamers quoted a soldier who called him the "Central leading and victorious spirit." Peter Cozzens, author of a book-length study of Iuka and Corinth, saw the opposite: a general who had lost control of his infamous temper and "cursed as cowards everyone who pushed past him until he, too lost hope." The Northern press made Rosecrans a hero, and he was soon given command of the army that would become the Army of the Cumberland.

## Blame and the Long Road South

Badly mauled, Van Dorn's army nonetheless escaped completely, evading Union troops Grant sent against it at Hatchie's Bridge on October 5 and marching to Holly Springs. Across the South there were widespread outcries of indignation over the senseless casualties at Corinth. Van Dorn was replaced by Lieutenant General John C. Pemberton immediately after the battle, and he requested a court of inquiry to answer charges that he had been drunk on duty and had neglected his wounded on the retreat. The court cleared him of all blame by unanimous decision. That December, leading cavalry, he would strike back at the Union supply depot in Holly Springs. Today the American Battlefield Trust and its members have saved more than 820 acres of the Corinth battlefield.

## Flight Context

Coordinates: 34.971 N, 88.5217 W, just north of downtown Corinth in Alcorn County, northeastern Mississippi, near the Tennessee line. The railroad crossing in the center of town makes a good reference; the fighting of October 3 ranged across the ground north and northwest of the town center. Fly at 2,000-3,000 ft AGL for a view of the town and its approaches.
