# Shiloh Indian Mounds Site

On a bluff above the Tennessee River, inside the Shiloh military park, the mounds and house rings of a palisaded Mississippian town abandoned centuries ago survive almost untouched.

**Location:** 35.1415, -88.3208

**Categories:** Mississippian culture, Archaeological sites, Native American history, National Historic Landmarks, Hardin County, Tennessee

## Introduction

In 1899, Cornelius Cadle, chairman of the Shiloh Park Commission, had a trench cut into a conical burial mound on a bluff above the Tennessee River. Among the things it turned up was a large stone pipe carved in the shape of a kneeling man. The figure is made of Missouri flint clay, in the same style as statuettes from the Cahokia site in Illinois, and it has become the most famous artifact of the Shiloh Indian Mounds Site, a town that was already centuries abandoned when the land around it became a military park.

## A Town Above the River

People began living on this bluff around 1000 CE, first a Late Woodland community and later people of the Early Mississippian culture, a regional variation known as South Appalachian Mississippian. The town sat on a high bluff between two ravines, overlooking the Tennessee River at the edge of the Shiloh Plateau. A wooden palisade encircled the town. Inside stood roughly 100 wattle-and-daub houses, over three dozen house mounds and seven or eight larger mounds, depending on who counts. The southernmost, oval with a rounded top, served as a burial place for leaders and other important people. Sources differ on when the town was abandoned: the National Park Service says its people moved away around 1200 or 1300 CE, while other accounts put it at around 1350 or even 1450.

## Maize, Deer and Hickory Nuts

The people of Shiloh were intensely involved in growing maize, along with other crops native to the Americas: squash, sunflowers, goosefoot, marshelder and maygrass. They also gathered wild foods such as acorns and hickory nuts. Hunting mattered too, for whitetail deer, squirrel, rabbit, turkey and raccoon, and the river offered fish. Their houses were built with walls of vertical posts interlaced with branches and coated with a thick layer of clay, a building method that left traces archaeologists can still read today.

## A Chiefdom With Distant Ties

Shiloh was the center of a chiefdom that included six smaller towns, each with one or two mounds, among them the Swallow Bluff Island Mounds site, and isolated farmsteads scattered on higher ground in the river valley. Downstream on the eastern bank, where Savannah, Tennessee, stands today, was another palisaded town with multiple mounds, though it is still unclear whether the two were occupied at the same time. Other neighbors lived all along the Tennessee and Tombigbee rivers, in what are now Alabama, Mississippi and western Tennessee. The presence of prestige goods from Cahokia leads archaeologists to think Shiloh's people were more closely tied politically to that distant center than to chiefdoms in Middle Tennessee.

## Saved by a Park Boundary

Because the site has lain within the boundaries of Shiloh National Military Park for so long, it was never disturbed by modern farming. That accident of history preserved something rare. The remains of the original wattle-and-daub houses are still visible as low rings or mounds, one of the few places in the eastern United States where such remains can still be seen. A survey in the winter of 1933-34 found many house mounds 10 to 20 feet across, many holding the remains of those clay-coated houses, and uncovered the line of the encircling palisade. The site was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1979 and declared a National Historic Landmark in 1989. The Tennessee River Museum in nearby Savannah features the kneeling-man pipe, though the museum's own listings describe its display piece as a replica.

## Flight Context

The mounds sit at about 35.141N, 88.321W on a bluff on the west bank of the Tennessee River inside Shiloh National Military Park, Hardin County, Tennessee. From 1,500-2,500 feet, follow the river upstream, south-southwest from Savannah; the park's open fields and woods border the water, and the mound group occupies the bluff edge above the river. Savannah-Hardin County Airport (FAA identifier SNH) is about 5 nm east-northeast, Robert Sibley Airport at Selmer (FAA identifier SZY) about 10 nm west-northwest, and Roscoe Turner Airport at Corinth, Mississippi (KCRX) about 19 nm southwest. River fog can linger on cool mornings.
