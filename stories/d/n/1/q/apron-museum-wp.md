# Apron Museum

In the small town of Iuka, Mississippi, a museum billed as the only one in the United States devoted to aprons displays thousands of them, from 1950s hostess frills to feed-sack workwear, along with the handwritten stories their donors sent in.

**Location:** 34.8139, -88.1931

**Categories:** Museums in Tishomingo County, Mississippi, Fashion museums in the United States, Museums established in 2006, 2006 establishments in Mississippi, Aprons, Iuka, Mississippi

## Introduction

Carolyn Terry went to estate sales and auctions looking for books. She kept coming home with aprons. The fabrics tempted her first, and soon she was buying them by the bundle, then studying them, then collecting their stories. In 2006 the Iuka native turned that fascination into the Apron Museum, billed as the only museum in the United States dedicated to aprons and the stories they tell. Today thousands of them fill its displays in this small northeast Mississippi town, organized state by state, with a few from Canada and Australia for good measure. Each one, it turns out, has something to say about the person who tied it on.

## More Than a Kitchen Garment

An apron can look like the humblest thing in a closet, but the museum makes the case that it rarely was. Aprons have been important garments for ladies, blacksmiths, butchers, carpenters, printers and shopkeepers, and above all for homemakers. They also carried quiet signals about class. Anyone could tie on a plain apron, but wealthy people could afford more expensive fabric, more elaborate embroidery and more intricate details. That makes the collection a record of social status as well as of work, with aprons functioning much like designer purses and shoes do today. Walking the displays, visitors can read the difference between an apron made for hard work and one made to be admired, and see how much could be said with a strip of cloth and a pair of ties.

## The Fifties, in Gingham and Rickrack

The bulk of the collection comes from the 1950s, a decade when an apron could be as much about style as about work. Women donned stylish, elaborate hostess aprons for bridge and garden parties, while men wore chef-style aprons for backyard barbecuing. The materials reveal both thrift and flair. There are aprons of checkered cotton gingham and aprons made from handkerchiefs, and there are aprons stitched from recycled feed and flour sacks, then dressed up with appliquéd bows, rickrack, ruffles and oversized pockets. Those feed-sack aprons tell a story of households that wasted nothing and still wanted something pretty. Seen together, the collection turns a familiar domestic object into a portrait of mid-century American life, its parties and its practicality side by side.

## Letters in the Pockets

What sets the Apron Museum apart is its attention to the people behind the fabric. Many of the aprons donated to the museum arrive with a handwritten letter recounting the apron's history, so the garment comes with its own biography. Visitors add to the archive too. Apron stories shared by people who come through the doors are preserved as part of the curated exhibit, so the collection keeps growing in memory as well as in cloth. The result is part fashion museum and part family album, built one donation at a time. The museum's singular focus has earned it wider notice as well: it has been featured in Ripley's Believe It or Not!, a fitting honor for a collection that began with a book hunter who could not resist the fabric.

## Flight Context

The Apron Museum is in Iuka, Mississippi, at 34.8139°N, 88.1931°W, near the town center in Tishomingo County in the state's far northeastern corner. From 1,500–2,500 feet, the museum is too small to pick out, so orient on Iuka's compact downtown grid. Segars Field in Iuka (FAA identifier 15M) is only about 3 nm south-southeast, making this an easy short hop. Other nearby fields include Roscoe Turner Airport at Corinth (KCRX) about 21 nm west-northwest and Tishomingo County Airport at Belmont (FAA identifier 01M) about 19 nm south. Expect summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms typical of north Mississippi.
