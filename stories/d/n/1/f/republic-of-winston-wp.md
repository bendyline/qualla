# Republic of Winston

In the hill country of north Alabama, a county of poor farmers refused to fight for the Confederacy, sent men to the Union cavalry, and gave birth to the enduring legend of the Free State of Winston.

**Location:** 34.1492, -87.3747

**Categories:** American Civil War, Southern Unionists, Winston County, Alabama, Secession, Alabama history

## Introduction

In front of the Winston County courthouse in Double Springs, Alabama, stands a statue of a young soldier split down the middle: half of his uniform is Union, half Confederate. It is called Dual Destiny, and it commemorates the county's divided loyalties. During the Civil War, disaffection with the Confederacy ran strong across this corner of north Alabama. In Winston County it turned violent, and it left behind a legend that the county had seceded from Alabama itself.

## Thin Soil, Few Slaveholders

Winston County sits in the hilly terrain of North Alabama, where the shallow soil was highly unsuitable for plantation agriculture. The 1860 census counted 3,450 white residents and 122 enslaved people. Most residents were poor farmers. When Alabama held its secession convention, the county sent Christopher Sheats, a 21-year-old schoolteacher and ardent Unionist elected by an overwhelming majority. The ordinance of secession passed 61 to 39, and Sheats refused to sign it even then. His stand eventually got him expelled from the state legislature and imprisoned for a time on charges of treason.

## A Joke at Looney's Tavern

County residents gathered at Looney's Tavern, north of present-day Addison. One early local historian dated the meeting to July 4, 1861, though other sources suggest it came later, perhaps as late as early 1862. The resolutions passed there declared that the people of Winston County had no desire to take part in the war and intended to support neither side. One went further: if a state could secede from the Union, then a county could secede from the state. Richard Payne, a pro-Confederate in the crowd, laughed with delight. "Winston County secedes!" he shouted. "Hoorah for the 'Free State of Winston'!" The name stuck, and from his remark grew the legend of the Republic of Winston.

## Deserters, Cavalry and Blood

Neutrality proved impossible. Many residents refused induction into the Confederate Army, and some talked openly of raising troops for the Union. State authorities answered with conscription and loyalty oaths, which only made things worse. After the Union Army invaded northern Alabama in April 1862, many Winston County men, along with others from the similarly Unionist Nickajack area near the Tennessee border, enlisted in the 1st Alabama Cavalry Regiment of the U.S. Army, organized late that year and commanded by George E. Spencer of New York. The regiment would play an important part in the war, mostly outside Alabama. Meanwhile, by one estimate, between 8,000 and 10,000 Confederate deserters found shelter in the county's hills. The war at home was ugly. Unionists and Confederates alike committed robbery, vandalism and even murder, and probate judge Tom Pink Curtis was killed by a band of Confederate horsemen.

## A County That Remembers Both Sides

The divisions outlasted the fighting. After the war, the county's Unionism made the Republican Party the dominant force in local politics. The legend of the Free State endures in the landscape. Between Haleyville and Double Springs, a decaying barn has been reported carrying the phrase "Free State of Winston" painted on its sign, and a passenger riverboat named the Free State Lady has cruised the waters of Smith Lake. And in Double Springs the Dual Destiny soldier keeps his two uniforms, a reminder that the people of this county were never of one mind about the war that tore through their hills.

## Flight Context

The heart of the Free State of Winston lies around 34.15N, 87.37W near Double Springs, the Winston County seat, in the wooded hills of north Alabama. From 2,500-3,500 feet, the long, branching arms of Smith Lake nearby are the most distinctive landmark in otherwise heavily wooded country. Nearby fields include Posey Field at Haleyville (FAA identifier 1M4, no ICAO code) about 14 nm northwest, Addison Municipal (FAA identifier 2A8) about 12 nm east-northeast, and Walker County Airport-Bevill Field at Jasper (FAA identifier JFX) about 15 nm south-southeast. Hilly terrain and morning fog in the valleys call for caution.
