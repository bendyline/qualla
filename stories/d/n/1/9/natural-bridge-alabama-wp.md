# Natural Bridge, Alabama

One of Alabama's smallest incorporated towns sits beside one of its biggest natural wonders, a sandstone and iron-ore arch described as the longest natural bridge east of the Rockies.

**Location:** 34.0917, -87.6045

**Categories:** Towns in Alabama, Natural arches, Winston County, Alabama, Geology, Parks

## Introduction

In 2000, just 28 people lived in Natural Bridge, Alabama, making it one of the least populated incorporated towns in the state. The rock arch that shares its name is far more imposing. Natural Bridge, the arch, is billed as the longest natural bridge east of the Rocky Mountains, with a span of more than 127 feet, and it is said to date back about 200 million years, long before anyone thought to draw town limits around it.

## Two Hundred Million Years of Patience

The arch stands near the town, at the southwest edge of Winston County. It is made of sandstone and iron ore, and it formed as the New River wore away weaker sandstone while the stronger stone that now makes up the spans held firm. The result is a formation about 60 feet high and 148 feet long, with a span of more than 127 feet. Native people, among them Muscogee (Creek) people by one account, lived in this area and probably used the cave-like enclosure beneath the bridge for shelter.

## Ferns, Hemlocks and a Stone Profile

The arch sits inside Natural Bridge Park, which the Legg family opened to the public in 1954 and which remains privately owned and open to visitors; admission, hours and ownership details vary by source, so check ahead. A two-mile nature trail leads beneath the arch and past other rock formations. The park is home to 27 varieties of fern and a stand of eastern hemlock. Along the way is one of the park's curiosities: a rock outcropping just over 15 feet high that resembles a human head in right profile, often likened to the portrait on the old U.S. "Indian head" nickel, a name from another era.

## Larissa, Lodi, Natural Bridge

The settlement took a while to find its name. It was originally called Larissa, then Lodi, before it settled on the name of the sandstone span to the west. During the Civil War, the country around the bridge served as a meeting point for Alabama Unionists avoiding the Confederate draft. Industry came decades later: the North Alabama Railroad came through in 1895, and the town flourished on coal mining and timber harvesting. By 1914 the community, near where U.S. Route 278, Alabama Highway 13 and Alabama State Route 5 now meet, had grown enough to incorporate, which it did on September 3 of that year.

## The Town That Went Dormant

Then the Great Depression closed most of the coal-mining and timber businesses, and people began moving away. By the 1930s the population had dwindled so far that the town's charter went dormant, and for roughly six decades it had no active charter. On July 24, 1997, Natural Bridge reincorporated. The 2000 census counted 28 people in 11 households on 0.4 square miles of land, which made it one of the smallest incorporated towns in Alabama. By 2010 Natural Bridge had grown to 37 residents. Later counts vary by source, from 32 in the 2020 census as Wikipedia reports it to an estimate of just 8 cited by the Encyclopedia of Alabama.

## Flight Context

Natural Bridge lies at about 34.09N, 87.60W in the forested hills of northwest Alabama, at the junction of U.S. 278 and Alabama Highways 13 and 5. From 1,500-2,500 feet, follow U.S. 278 and look for the highway junction; the arch itself is likely to be hard to pick out from the air. Marion County-Rankin Fite Airport (KHAB) at Hamilton is about 20 nm west; Posey Field at Haleyville (FAA identifier 1M4, no ICAO code) is about 11 nm north, and Walker County Airport-Bevill Field at Jasper (KJFX) about 18 nm southeast. Morning valley fog is possible in cooler months.
