# Ivy Green

At Ivy Green in Tuscumbia, Alabama, the 1820 house where Helen Keller was born, a well pump in the yard marks the spot where a deaf-blind child first understood that the letters spelled into her palm meant water.

**Location:** 34.7394, -87.7067

## Introduction

Keller remembered the water first. "I knew then that w-a-t-e-r meant the wonderful cool something that was flowing over my hand," she wrote years later, and the pump where that happened still stands in the yard at Ivy Green. This modest property in Tuscumbia, Alabama, built in 1820, was the birthplace and childhood home of Helen Keller, who grew up deaf-blind and became a well-known author and public speaker. At the well between the main house and a small cottage, she first broke through to her teacher and companion, Anne Sullivan. Today Ivy Green is a National Historic Landmark and a museum devoted to her life.

## Clapboard on North Commons

Ivy Green sits at 300 North Commons Street West, on the north side of the street at Keller Lane, in a residential neighborhood just north of downtown Tuscumbia. There is nothing grand about it, and that is part of its power. The main house is a frame building with a gabled roof and clapboard walls. Its five-bay front is lined with sash windows arranged around an entrance near the center, which sits beneath a gabled portico and is framed by sidelights and a transom. Three features define the property: this main house, the birthplace cottage nearby, and between them the well and its pump. Walk from one building to the other and you cross the ground where the most important lesson of Keller's childhood took place.

## An Office Turned Bridal Suite

The birthplace cottage did not start out as a home. It was built as a plantation office, a small working building beside the main house. Later it was refurbished as a bridal suite for the second wife of Arthur Henley Keller, and in 1880 she gave birth to their daughter Helen there. At 19 months old, the girl contracted an illness that doctors described as "an acute congestion of the stomach and the brain." She recovered, but it left her permanently blind and deaf. In her autobiography she recalled that time as being "at sea in a dense fog." She was not entirely cut off. Keller could communicate somewhat with Martha Washington, the daughter of the family cook, two years older than Helen, who understood the girl's signs.

## My Soul's Birthday

Anne Sullivan arrived at the Keller house on March 5, 1887, a date Keller would always remember as "my soul's birthday." The next month came the breakthrough. As cool water ran over one of Helen's hands, Sullivan spelled into the palm of the other, and the child realized that the motions stood for the idea of "water." That moment at the pump opened everything else. In 1904, at the age of 24, she graduated from Radcliffe as a member of Phi Beta Kappa, becoming the first deafblind person to earn a Bachelor of Arts degree. Her autobiography, The Story of My Life, published in 1903, made her education and her life with Sullivan widely known. It all traces back to a well in a Tuscumbia yard.

## Summers of The Miracle Worker

Ivy Green became a museum dedicated to Helen Keller in 1954. A fire in 1972 caused minor damage, and in its wake the Keller family donated additional materials to the collection. The house also has a second life every summer. For more than 60 years, the Helen Keller Foundation has staged outdoor performances here of William Gibson's play The Miracle Worker, which tells the story of Keller and Sullivan. The season usually runs from early June through mid-July, and the shows draw especially large audiences during the Helen Keller Festival, held in Tuscumbia every June. Watching the play on the grounds where Keller and Sullivan actually lived, a few steps from the real pump, gives the familiar story an unusual immediacy.
