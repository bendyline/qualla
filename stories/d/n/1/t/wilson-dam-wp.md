# Wilson Dam

Begun to power World War I munitions plants, coveted by Henry Ford, and finally handed to the Tennessee Valley Authority as its first dam, Wilson Dam tamed the treacherous Muscle Shoals of the Tennessee River.

**Location:** 34.8000, -87.6255

**Categories:** Dams, Tennessee Valley Authority, Hydroelectric power, National Historic Landmarks, Tennessee River, Muscle Shoals, Alabama

## Introduction

In 1921 Henry Ford offered $5 million for a 99-year lease on two federal nitrate plants and an unfinished dam in northern Alabama. He promised to complete it, build another upstream, and raise a planned industrial city around it that he would make the "Detroit of the South." Senator George Norris stopped him. Instead of becoming Ford's, Wilson Dam became the first dam of the Tennessee Valley Authority.

## Taming the Shoals

The trouble was Muscle Shoals, a stretch of the Tennessee River full of dangerous shallows and turbulent currents that hampered commerce and navigation. As steamboat traffic and plantation agriculture spread through the Tennessee Valley in the late nineteenth century, many attempts were made to get around the shoals, including a canal, and most largely failed. Federal engineers eventually settled on Muscle Shoals for a dam after determining that it had the greatest hydroelectric potential east of the Rocky Mountains. Today Wilson Dam stands at river mile 259.4, spanning the river roughly north to south between Florence and Muscle Shoals.

## Built for a War It Missed

The immediate reason was war. The National Defense Act of 1916 required two nitrate plants, powered by an adjacent hydroelectric plant, to give the country a domestic supply of nitrates for munitions. The Army Corps of Engineers began building the dam in 1918 under the supervision of Hugh L. Cooper, and at the time of its construction it was described as the largest hydroelectric installation in the world. It was not ready in time. The fighting ended before the dam was finished, and it played no part in the war's outcome. Completed in 1924 at a cost of almost $47 million, with its first generating unit entering service in September 1925, it was named for President Woodrow Wilson.

## Ford's Detroit of the South

With the war over and the dam unfinished, Henry Ford made his move. His 1921 offer would have given him the dam on a long lease, with a promise to complete it and to build a new city in a Muscle Shoals that did not yet exist. Senator George Norris believed the dam would do more good in public hands. He wanted it used as part of a large public works and development program across the whole Tennessee Valley. Norris blocked the Ford proposal in the Senate, and in 1924 Ford withdrew his bid, citing the delays. In the end the dam stayed in public hands, becoming the first to come under the administration of the Tennessee Valley Authority, which even kept its first headquarters at Wilson Dam before gradually moving to Knoxville, Tennessee.

## Power to the Valley

Under TVA, Wilson Dam brought electricity to thousands of people in Lauderdale and Colbert counties as part of the authority's campaign of rural electrification. One economist wrote that TVA gave residents "universally high standards of living, new jobs, leisure, freedom and an end to drudgery, congestion, noise, smoke and filth." The dam was even the subject of a U.S. Supreme Court case, Ashwander v. Tennessee Valley Authority. Through the 1930s, the nitrate plants it had been built to power made fertilizer. Then, during the Second World War, Wilson Dam finally served its original purpose, as the area's nitrate and phosphorus plants produced munitions for the war effort.

## Lifting Barges Nearly a Hundred Feet

The dam is 137 feet high and 4,541 feet long, with a net dependable capacity of 663 megawatts. Its main lock, built in 1959 and 110 feet wide and 600 feet long, has a maximum lift of 100 feet by TVA's count (some sources give 94 feet), making it the highest single-lift lock east of the Rocky Mountains. When it opened it was the largest single-chamber lock in the world. Behind the dam, Wilson Lake spreads across 15,500 acres with 166 miles of shoreline, and the surrounding reservation offers miles of hiking trails and a campground. On November 13, 1966, Wilson Dam was declared a National Historic Landmark for its role as the first dam under TVA's administration. It is one of nine TVA dams on the Tennessee River.

## Flight Context

Wilson Dam spans the Tennessee River at about 34.800N, 87.626W between Florence to the north and Muscle Shoals to the south in northwest Alabama. From 2,000-3,000 feet the long dam across the river and the broad sweep of Wilson Lake upstream to the east make an unmistakable landmark, with the Shoals cities spread around it. Northwest Alabama Regional Airport (KMSL) is only about 3.4 nm south, so the dam sits close to its traffic pattern. Courtland Airport (FAA identifier 9A4) is about 16 nm east-southeast. Haze and river fog are common in warm months.
