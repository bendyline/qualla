# Sipsey Wilderness

Alabama's largest and most visited wilderness, a waterfall-laced maze of sandstone canyons in Bankhead National Forest, exists because a Birmingham activist refused to accept that wilderness belonged only to the West.

**Location:** 34.3251, -87.4514

**Categories:** Wilderness areas of Alabama, Protected areas of Lawrence County, Alabama, Protected areas of Winston County, Alabama, Waterfalls, Old-growth forests

## Introduction

Its nickname promises a thousand waterfalls. The Sipsey Wilderness actually holds dozens, and that is plenty. On the low plateau of Brindlee Mountain in northwestern Alabama, creeks and rivers have cut the land into a rough maze of gorges, and wherever water spills over the layered limestone and sandstone, it falls. This is the "Land of 1000 Waterfalls," roughly 25,000 acres of Bankhead National Forest wrapped around the Sipsey Fork of the Black Warrior River, and it is the largest and most frequently visited wilderness area in Alabama.

## Wilderness Is Not Just for the West

When Congress passed the Wilderness Act in 1964, many people believed it should apply only to the western United States, and the Forest Service resisted designating logged-over eastern forests, which made it very hard to create new wilderness east of the Mississippi River. Mary Ivy Burks of Birmingham set out to change that. She worked to establish a Sipsey Wilderness in the Bankhead National Forest and stood at the forefront of what became known as the Eastern Wilderness Movement; securing Sipsey was her crowning achievement. Alabama became the agent of change, as an unlikely alliance of environmentalists, bird watchers and others pushed to amend the law. A bill sponsored by Senator John Sparkman and his Alabama colleague James Allen became the Eastern Wilderness Areas Act, signed on January 3, 1975, and Sipsey was designated with an original 12,000 acres or so. Dozens of wilderness areas across the country have been designated since, thanks to those changes.

## Second Growth, Old Giants

Sipsey helped show that a smaller tract of restored land in the East could be as much a wilderness as a larger expanse of untouched land in the West. Much of this forest was once logged, and new growth has since taken hold across those cutover slopes. In the 1980s, John Randolph and Mike Leonard led a second campaign that expanded the wilderness in 1988 to its present size. A few pockets escaped the saws entirely. The most significant, about 260 acres along Bee Branch Gorge and Buck Rough Canyon, still shelter old Eastern hemlock, American beech, sweet birch, white oak and tulip poplar. The whole wilderness lies within the Appalachian mixed mesophytic forests ecoregion.

## Saltpeter and Storms

Not far from the Bee Branches, a small cave hides behind a waterfall. It is a source of saltpeter, a major ingredient in gunpowder, and during the Civil War it mattered enough that a small skirmish was fought nearby at Hubbard's cotton mill. Nature has written its own violent chapters, too. The 2011 Super Outbreak of tornadoes badly damaged much of the northern Sipsey, and with the trail system too battered to repair all at once, crews focused on the most popular routes while several damaged trails were considered abandoned indefinitely. Beginning in late 2013, some clearing and rerouting of the rest began, though hikers have reported difficulty following some of the less-used paths. Today official trails thread the wilderness, from the 1.8-mile Gum Pond Trail to the 7.3-mile Mitchell Ridge Trail, and the Sipsey River Picnic Area, where Cranal Road crosses the river, serves as a parking area and starting point for hikes.

## Flight Context

Coordinates: 34.3251 N, 87.4514 W, over the Sipsey Wilderness in Bankhead National Forest, Lawrence and Winston counties, northwestern Alabama. From the air it is an unbroken forest canopy creased by deep, twisting stream gorges; follow the Sipsey Fork, which drains south toward Lewis Smith Lake. Wilderness areas carry a request that aircraft stay at least 2,000 ft above the surface; plan on 2,500-3,500 ft AGL. Nearby airports: Posey Field in Haleyville (FAA identifier 1M4; no ICAO code in the OurAirports listing) about 8 nm west-southwest; Bill Pugh Field in Russellville (FAA identifier M22; no ICAO code listed) about 15 nm northwest; Northwest Alabama Regional in Muscle Shoals (KMSL) about 26 nm north-northwest; Huntsville International (KHSV) about 38 nm northeast. Spring and summer bring thunderstorms.
