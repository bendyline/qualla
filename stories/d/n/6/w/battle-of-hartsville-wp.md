# Battle of Hartsville

On a snowy December morning in 1862, John Hunt Morgan's cavalry crossed the Cumberland River in darkness, surprised a larger Union brigade at Hartsville and marched away with more than 1,800 prisoners.

**Location:** 36.3777, -86.1743

**Categories:** Battles of the American Civil War in Tennessee, Stones River Campaign, Confederate victories of the American Civil War, Trousdale County, Tennessee, 1862 in Tennessee

## Introduction

Nobody agrees on how they got past the pickets. One account says John Hunt Morgan's Confederates wore blue uniforms; another says they came in civilian clothes, posing as refugees. Either way, at 6:45 on the morning of December 7, 1862, the Union camp guarding the Cumberland River crossing at Hartsville, Tennessee, woke to artillery fire and charging infantry. By 8:30 it was over. In less than two hours, a smaller Confederate force had surrounded a Union brigade, forced its surrender and set off with more than 1,800 prisoners and a wagon train heavy with captured supplies.

## A Crossing on the Cumberland

The raid was an opening move in the Stones River Campaign. In the weeks after Union Major General William S. Rosecrans took over the Army of the Cumberland at Nashville, he was preparing to march southeast toward Murfreesboro, where Confederate General Braxton Bragg's Army of Tennessee had retreated after its defeat at Perryville. Bragg sent Colonel John Hunt Morgan north with his cavalry to strike Rosecrans's lines of communication and keep him from foraging for supplies north of Nashville. Hartsville, a crossing point on the Cumberland River about 40 miles upstream from Nashville, was an inviting target. Guarding it was the 39th Brigade of the XIV Corps under Colonel Absalom B. Moore: the 106th and 108th Ohio Infantry, the 104th Illinois Infantry and the 2nd Indiana Cavalry.

## Two Hours in the Snow

Under cover of darkness, Morgan crossed the river in the early hours of December 7 with about 1,300 men, most of them Kentuckians. The brigade waiting on the other side outnumbered them by roughly a thousand. Another, much larger Union force was camped nine miles away at Castalian Springs - close enough to hear the guns, too far away to join the fight. The attack came all at once: artillery and infantry opened together while cavalry struck the Union flanks and rear. After an hour of fighting, one of Moore's units broke and ran, spreading confusion and forcing the line back. By 8:30 the Confederates had the Union soldiers surrounded, and Moore surrendered.

## What the 75th Indiana Found

Hearing cannon in the distance, the 75th Indiana marched from its camp at Castalian Springs, advancing hesitantly because it may have run into Morgan's rear guard. By the time it reached Hartsville, the fight was over. Wounded men of both sides lay in the snow, and accounts of the toll differ: a Tennessee battlefield summary gives Union losses of roughly 260 killed and wounded, plus 1,834 captured, and Confederate losses of about 140, while Basil W. Duke, one of Morgan's officers, later put the Union killed and wounded above four hundred. Duke also recalled how brutal the night crossing of the icy Cumberland had been: only fifteen of his men, he wrote, were 'frozen so stiff that they had to be left.' Many of the raiders, especially in Cluke's and Chenault's regiments, had nothing on their feet but old rags, and boots and shoes were among the most valuable things they captured. They also filled several wagons with supplies before hurrying away.

## The March South

Duke wrote that the Confederates knew the strength of the Union forces nearby and even where they were deployed, so Morgan posted Quirk's Scouts and Cluke's regiment to slow any pursuit. The raiders quickened the pace, and some of the captured Union soldiers were forced to wade the Cumberland or one of the smaller creeks on the march south to Lebanon. The victory earned Morgan a promotion. General Joseph E. Johnston, in overall command of Confederate forces in the Western Theater, called it a 'brilliant feat' and recommended that Morgan be made a brigadier general at once. Jefferson Davis happened to be nearby at Murfreesboro and promoted Morgan in person when he arrived.

## Prelude to a Larger Battle

Hartsville was a small fight with outsized echoes. It foreshadowed the Confederate cavalry raids that followed that winter: Nathan Bedford Forrest's into West Tennessee and Morgan's own into Kentucky, both running from December 1862 into January 1863. Rosecrans reached Murfreesboro on December 29, setting the stage for the Battle of Stones River a few days later. Today the Hartsville Battlefield is listed on the National Register of Historic Places, a quiet stretch of the Cumberland valley where, one winter morning, surprise did the work of numbers.

## Flight Context

The Hartsville battlefield lies at approximately 36.3777 N, 86.1743 W, near the town of Hartsville on the Cumberland River, about 40 miles upstream from Nashville. From 2,000-3,000 ft AGL, follow the broad bends of the Cumberland east of Nashville; the town of Hartsville sits near the river north of Lebanon, with Castalian Springs, where the idle Union force camped, some nine miles away. Nearest airports: Lafayette Municipal Airport (FAA identifier 3M7; no ICAO code listed), about 10 nm northeast; Music City Executive Airport, Gallatin (KXNX), about 11 nm west; and Lebanon Municipal Airport (FAA identifier M54; no ICAO code listed), about 13 nm south-southwest. Nashville International Airport (KBNA) is about 29 nm west-southwest. River valley fog is common on cool mornings.
