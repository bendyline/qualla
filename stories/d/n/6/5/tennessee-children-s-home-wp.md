# Tennessee Children's Home

Founded in 1909 for three orphaned children, this churches of Christ home moved in 1935 to a former military academy campus in Spring Hill, Tennessee, left it for a newer campus in 2021, and is reported to have cared for more than 21,000 children.

**Location:** 35.7477, -86.9312

**Categories:** Residential buildings in Tennessee, Orphanages in Tennessee, Organizations established in 1909

## Introduction

It began with three children. In 1909, the Scotten children of Columbia, Tennessee, were orphaned, and the Tennessee Orphan Home was founded to care for them. More than a century later, the organization that grew from that moment, now called the Tennessee Children's Home and based in Spring Hill, is reported to have cared for over 21,000 children. The children who come here have changed, and so has the way the home looks after them, but the purpose is recognizably the one the three Scotten children set in motion.

## A Military Academy Becomes a Home

The home is affiliated with the churches of Christ, and for its first quarter-century it remained in Columbia. In 1934 the Church of Christ Tennessee Orphan Home bought the campus of the former Branham and Hughes Military Academy in Spring Hill, and the following year the orphanage moved there. A campus designed for cadets became a campus for children without families of their own to rely on. Like many orphanages of its era, it was organized as an institution, with a central dining hall, a central laundry and dormitory living. There was even a small farming operation, meant to keep the home as self-sufficient as possible. The home later outgrew that campus: it sold the Main Street property in 2019 and moved to a new campus in western Spring Hill in the fall of 2021.

## Dining Halls and Dormitories

The approach to care in those decades was straightforward. The home provided for children's basic physical needs and offered Christian instruction, while the children received their main academic education in the public schools of Maury County, run by the county Board of Education. That arrangement lasted until relatively recently. Life followed the rhythms of the institution: meals in the dining hall, laundry done centrally, and nights in shared dormitories. That was the model the home would eventually set aside.

## Eight Children to a House

The 1980s brought steady growth, with improvements in programs and services and more children served as the decade went on. In late 1982 the Tennessee Orphan Home became the Tennessee Children's Home, and the institutional approach gave way to family-oriented group homes. Each house was led by a married couple, both of whom had to be active members of a churches of Christ congregation. Dormitories were remodeled into single-family homes holding no more than eight children each. Central dining ended; children ate family meals in their own houses. Each household handled most of its own daily life, including home devotionals, church attendance, housekeeping, laundry, cooking and cleanup, in an effort to provide a homelike, non-institutional setting.

## Who Comes Here Now

The home's reach widened through mergers with other churches of Christ-related ministries: West Tennessee Children's Home in 1988, then Happy Hills Youth Ranch near Ashland City and East Tennessee Christian Services in Knoxville in 2000 and 2001. The children changed too. Today the vast majority are not orphans in the original sense of having lost both birth parents. Many come from troubled family situations, including young people who have experienced abuse or neglect, and their needs may be emotional, social, behavioral, educational or psychological. The home's services are designed to be flexible, treating each child as an individual. Today the Spring Hill campus, the home's largest and the base for its statewide administration, serves 32 at-risk boys aged 13 to 18 in a residential setting, according to the home, while its campuses in Henderson, Clarksville and Knoxville serve other groups, including single parents and their children. One note of clarity matters here: the home has no connection to the similarly named Tennessee Children's Home Society, the organization run by Georgia Tann that was involved in a mid-twentieth-century baby-selling adoption scandal.
