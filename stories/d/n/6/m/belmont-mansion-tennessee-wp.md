# Belmont Mansion (Tennessee)

Adelicia Acklen's Italianate summer palace in Nashville, with its zoo, bowling alley and rooftop observatory, was built on a fortune extracted from enslaved labor and now anchors a university campus.

**Location:** 36.1358, -86.7947

**Categories:** Houses completed in 1853, Plantation houses in Tennessee, Historic house museums in Tennessee, Tennessee in the American Civil War, Belmont University, History of slavery

## Introduction

Alligators from Louisiana once lived on a hilltop outside Nashville, sharing a private zoo with bears, monkeys, peacocks, singing birds and a white owl. They belonged to Belle Monte - Belmont - the summer estate that Joseph and Adelicia Acklen created on 180 acres of what was then open country beyond the city. The Italian villa at its center, completed by 1853, became one of the most elaborate homes of the antebellum South. It was also built with the profits of slavery. The money came from Louisiana cotton plantations worked by enslaved people, inherited from a husband who had made his name in the slave trade.

## An Inheritance Rewritten

Before she was Mrs. Acklen, Adelicia Hayes was married to Isaac Franklin, co-founder of Franklin & Armfield, which became one of the largest slave-trading firms in the United States. When he died in 1846, his estate plan provided for her only until she remarried. After that, his adjoining Louisiana cotton plantations, including her home on Angola Plantation, were eventually to fund a school at the couple's Tennessee summer place, Fairvue Plantation in Gallatin. In 1849 she married Joseph Alexander Smith Acklen, and she managed to get the inheritance changed. She kept all of the Louisiana plantations and gave up only Fairvue. Angola and her six other plantations there totaled about 8,600 acres, and the Acklens lived much of each year on them. After she sold those lands in 1880, the Louisiana State Penitentiary was formed on Franklin's former plantations, which the state bought in 1901.

## A Villa Built to Dazzle

At Belmont the Acklens built and furnished a house of 36 rooms and 19,000 square feet, set at the top of the hill. Covered balconies trimmed in cast iron wrapped the exterior, shading the windows from the Tennessee sun. Crowning the roof, a ten-foot octagonal cupola vented summer heat and doubled as an 'astronomical observatory' for gazing at the stars, the estate and downtown Nashville. Beside the house stood a T-shaped building whose south wing held guest rooms and a bowling alley, while the north wing was an art gallery under a corrugated glass roof. Gardens, conservatories, an aviary and a lake filled the grounds. The conservatories sheltered tropical fruit and flowers, camellia japonica, jasmine, lilies and cacti, and a deer park rounded out the zoo.

## Cotton, Grief and an Army on the Lawn

Joseph and Adelicia had six children together, but their twins died of scarlet fever at the age of two in 1855. In 1863 Joseph died in Louisiana, where he had gone to supervise the plantations during the Civil War. Adelicia then struck a remarkable bargain: she secretly negotiated agreements with both Union and Confederate authorities that allowed 2,800 bales of her cotton to be shipped to Liverpool, where, by differing accounts, they sold for between $758,000 and $960,000. In December 1864, as the armies gathered for the Battle of Nashville, Union General Thomas J. Wood camped 13,000 troops on the grounds for two weeks. The grounds suffered some damage, but the mansion and its contents came through the war unscathed. Emancipation freed the enslaved plantation workers whose forced labor had produced her income, yet the Liverpool money left her wealthy for the rest of her life.

## Marble from Rome

Right after the war, Adelicia and her four surviving children traveled to Europe to collect the cotton money. There she kept building her art collection, acquiring five major marble statues by American sculptors working in Rome, among them Randolph Rogers, William Henry Rinehart, Joseph Mozier and Chauncey Ives. Four of those pieces remain in the mansion today. In France she was presented at the court of Emperor Louis Napoleon and Empress Eugénie. Back in Nashville, the house itself glittered: gilt-framed mirrors hang above marble mantels, reflecting gasoliers and richly furnished parlors, and much of the original Venetian glass still fills the windows, doors and transoms. Architectural historians consider the Grand Salon the most elaborate domestic interior built in antebellum Tennessee. Notable visitors are said to have included Mrs. James K. Polk, the evangelist Dwight L. Moody and the scientist Thomas Huxley.

## From Summer Palace to Campus

In January 1887, months before her death, Belmont and the surrounding land were sold to a land development company; one account names the buyer as Lewis T. Baxter and the price as around $54,000. Adapted for a new life, it opened in 1890 as a women's academy and junior college, merged with Ward's Seminary in 1913 to become Ward-Belmont, and in 1951 passed to the Tennessee Baptist Convention, which developed the four-year, coeducational Belmont College. That college grew into Belmont University, which separated from the convention in 2007. The mansion, listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1971, is now operated as a museum by the Belmont Mansion Association, a nonprofit formed in 1972; accounts differ on whether the university alone or the association and the university together own it. Five cast iron gazebos still stand in the gardens, and the estate's 105-foot water tower serves the university as its bell tower.

## Flight Context

Belmont Mansion sits at approximately 36.1358 N, 86.7947 W, on the Belmont University campus about 2 nm south-southwest of downtown Nashville. From 1,500-2,000 ft AGL, look for the hilltop villa with its rooftop cupola and the tall former water tower, now the university bell tower, rising beside it; Music Row lies just to the north and the downtown skyline to the northeast. The site lies close to Nashville International's controlled airspace, so coordinate with approach control. Nearest airports: Nashville International Airport (KBNA), about 6 nm east, and John C. Tune Airport (KJWN), about 5 nm northwest. Summer brings haze and afternoon thunderstorms; autumn and spring usually offer the clearest views.
