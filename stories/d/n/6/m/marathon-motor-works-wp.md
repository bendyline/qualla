# Marathon Motor Works

From 1910 to 1914, a converted cotton mill in Nashville built the Marathon automobile, reportedly turning out 200 cars a month at its peak before alleged financial improprieties sent the company into receivership.

## Introduction

From 1910 to 1914, Nashville built its own automobile. The Marathon came out of a converted cotton mill complex, an open roadster or touring car with a 35-horsepower, four-cylinder engine and a price of about $1,500. By 1912 the factory was reportedly producing 200 cars a month. Two years later the company was finished, brought down not by a lack of buyers but by allegations of financial wrongdoing, and the old factory it left behind has found a very different life.

## An Engine Maker Tries Cars

The story starts in Jackson, Tennessee, with Southern Engine and Boiler Works, a company founded in 1889 to build industrial engines and boilers. In 1906 one of its engineers, William Henry Collier, developed a gasoline engine and a prototype automobile. Southern Engine built a few cars to Collier's design, then decided to go bigger. By 1910 it had established a factory in Nashville, adapting a cotton mill complex into what became Marathon Motor Works, a brass-era automaker with ambitions well beyond a few prototypes.

## Roadsters and Touring Cars

The first lineup, first sold as the Southern around 1909, was simple: a roadster and a touring car, both powered by 35-horsepower four-cylinder engines. Both had open bodies, with tops offered as an option, and each sold for about $1,500. The company promoted its cars with brochures and magazine advertisements, and the Marathon found buyers. By 1912 the Nashville plant was reportedly turning out 200 cars every month, and even that was not enough: demand outran what the factory could supply.

## Trouble in the Boardroom

Strong sales did not save the company. Financial improprieties were alleged, and receivership soon followed. Nashville's car-building years ended in 1914. The Herff-Brooks Corporation then bought the Marathon machinery and moved production to Richmond, Indiana. The disputes left a long shadow. In 2008 Nashville author Chester Campbell published a mystery novel, The Marathon Murders, whose murder is fictional but whose motive draws on the documented disagreements and possible dishonesty among the company's board and executives.

## Marathon Village

The factory buildings survived the company. Today the preserved complex, known as Marathon Village, holds a museum and shops. It also housed Antique Archaeology, the business at the center of the television series American Pickers, which closed in April 2025. Music has moved in as well. Marathon Music Works, a mid-sized music venue and event space in Marathon Village, got its name through a local community process in which Nashville residents submitted ideas, and the winning choice pays tribute to the building's first life as Marathon Motor Works.
