# Sulphur Dell

In a spring-fed hollow below the Tennessee State Capitol, Nashville played professional baseball for nearly eighty years on a lopsided field with a hillside outfield, a 262-foot right-field fence and a smell that earned it the nickname "the Dump."

**Location:** 36.1728, -86.7856

**Categories:** Defunct baseball venues in the United States, Nashville Vols, Jewel Box parks, Baseball venues in Nashville, Tennessee, History of Nashville, Tennessee

## Introduction

On the night of July 6, 1894, fifty-four large electric lights glowed around a Nashville ballpark and a baseball coated in phosphorus arced through the dark. The Nashville Tigers and New Orleans Pelicans were staging what is described as the city's first night game, an exhibition after an afternoon doubleheader, at a time when playing under lights was considered a gimmick; the first major league night game was still more than forty years away. An estimated 4,000 fans watched fireworks, a slack-wire act and throwing contests, then saw base runners lead fielders on chases into the unlit corners of the outfield and climb a light pole. The ballpark was the place later called Sulphur Dell.

## A Spring in the Bottomland

Long before baseball, the hollow just north of today's Tennessee State Capitol drew people to its water. Archaeologists believe a Native American settlement stood here in the early Mississippian period, likely including a workshop where water from an underground sulphur spring was boiled down to collect salt. Early settlers knew the spot as French Lick Springs, a low bottomland, or dell, used for trading and watering. As Sulphur Spring Bottom it became a favorite place for picnics, and by the late 1830s entrepreneurs had fenced off the springs and charged admission to visitors who came to bathe in and drink the reputedly medicinal waters. Amateur teams were playing baseball in the bottom as early as 1870.

## Holding Back the Spring

Nashville's first professional team, the Nashville Americans, was established on October 6, 1884, and a month later its directors signed a five-year lease on the grounds. The site needed serious work. Builders raised a brick-and-cement dyke between Cherry and Summer Streets to hold back the spring water and put up a wooden grandstand, bringing in extra men to race the spring exhibition schedule. On April 10, 1885, nearly 4,000 people crowded into the unfinished park to watch the National League's Chicago White Stockings beat the locals 4-2; construction was not complete until May 24, more than a month into the season. The owners fought a running battle with freeloaders, adding boards to the Jackson Street fence and eventually building a second fence inside the first. By 1894 the park held about 1,000 spectators, roughly half of them in opera chairs, some in private boxes near the front.

## The Vols Turn Around

In 1901 the new Southern Association's Nashville club, later known as the Vols, moved in, and it became the ballpark's longest tenant, playing there until 1963. In 1908 the Nashville Tennessean sportswriter Grantland Rice gave the place the name that stuck: Sulphur Dell. The original grandstand had one serious flaw. Home plate faced southwest toward the Capitol, so batters squinted into the afternoon sun. After the 1926 season the whole ballpark was torn down and rebuilt in concrete and steel, with home plate turned to face northeast along Fourth Avenue North. Sulphur Dell hosted the Southern Association All-Star Game six times, and on each occasion the league's all-stars took on the Vols themselves.

## Uphill to Right Field

What made Sulphur Dell famous was its eccentric geometry. The right-field wall stood just 262 feet from home plate, and a steep terrace ran along the entire outfield wall, most dramatically in right and center, so outfielders chasing a long fly ball had to run uphill. Even with a tall screen atop part of the 16-foot wooden fence, pitchers frequently watched home runs disappear over the short fences. The stands crowded the field: first base was only 42 feet from the seats, close enough that Casey Stengel joked he could bunt a home run down the line, and third base was closer still at 26 feet. The neighborhood was no postcard. The park sat among the city's garbage dump, stockyards and warehouses, and the odor drifting in from the dump earned it another nickname, "the Dump."

## Where Home Plate Was

The Vols folded after the 1963 season. Amateurs played at the Dell in 1964, it served as a speedway for three weeks in 1965, and it became a tow-in lot for Metro Nashville before demolition on April 16, 1969. For decades the site held parking lots for state employees. Then baseball came back. First Tennessee Park, now First Horizon Park, opened on the old grounds on April 17, 2015, and the Nashville Sounds christened it by beating the Colorado Springs Sky Sox 3-2 in 10 innings. The ballpark, reported at about $47 million to build when it opened (a 2017 audit put the whole project, with site work, near $91 million), holds about 10,000 people with berm seating and remembers its predecessor: light stanchions echo Sulphur Dell's old lights, a tin sign on the back of the batter's eye reads "Site of Sulphur Dell, Baseball's Most Historic Park, 1870-1963," and a marker on the concourse shows where the Dell's home plate once sat.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 36.1728°N, 86.7856°W, in downtown Nashville just north of the Tennessee State Capitol. Today the site is First Horizon Park, home of the Triple-A Nashville Sounds; look for the ballpark's green diamond between the Capitol hill to the south and the Cumberland River to the east. A pass at about 2,500-3,000 feet MSL, offset from the downtown towers, gives a clear view. The field sits under busy Nashville airspace: John C. Tune (KJWN) is about 5 nm west and Nashville International (KBNA) about 6 nm east-southeast, so coordinate with Nashville approach. Summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are common.
