# Tennessee State Capitol

Crowning the highest point in downtown Nashville, Tennessee's domeless Greek Revival capitol holds its architect in its walls, a president's tomb on its grounds and a long history of labor, memory and protest in its stones.

**Location:** 36.1658, -86.7842

**Categories:** State capitols in the United States, Greek Revival architecture in Tennessee, National Historic Landmarks in Tennessee, Historic Civil Engineering Landmarks, Government of Tennessee

## Introduction

William Strickland never saw his masterpiece finished, but he never left it either. The Philadelphia architect died in 1854, five years before the Tennessee State Capitol was completed, and he was entombed in its walls. His son, Francis Strickland, saw the work through. The building they left behind still crowns Capitol Hill, the highest point in downtown Nashville, a limestone Greek temple that serves as Tennessee's seat of government, home to the House of Representatives, the Senate and the governor's office.

## A Temple for a Permanent Capital

On October 7, 1843, the Tennessee General Assembly declared Nashville the state's permanent capital, and planning for a statehouse began soon after. The site chosen was a prominent hill known first as Cedar Knob and later as Campbell's Hill, after Judge G. W. Campbell, who owned it. It had once held the Holy Rosary Cathedral, the first Roman Catholic church in Nashville. The city bought the property for $30,000 and conveyed it to the state for a single dollar. Strickland modeled the building on a Greek Ionic temple. The north and south porticoes each carry eight Ionic columns, the east and west six, and above the roof rises a round tower of eight Corinthian columns modeled on the Choragic Monument of Lysicrates in Athens. Unlike most statehouses, it has no dome; it is one of only about a dozen state capitols without one.

## Stone, Iron and the Men Who Dug

The cornerstone was laid on July 4, 1845. Both the interior and exterior were built of limestone from a quarry about a mile away, and some interior columns were cut from single pieces of stone, so heavy that massive wooden derricks were needed to hoist them into place. To protect the building from fire, the roof trusses were made of wrought iron instead of wood. That combination of extensive stonework and early structural iron later earned it designation as a National Historic Civil Engineering Landmark. The work drew on commercial, convict and enslaved labor. Beginning in 1846, fifteen enslaved Black men, loaned to the state by Nashville stonemason A. G. Payne, spent nearly a year carving the building's future cellar into the hill and carting away the debris, and Payne was paid $18 a month for their labor. It has been called the most significant project on which Tennessee's state government rented enslaved labor.

## A President in the Garden

Completed in 1859 at a cost of nearly $900,000, the capitol was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1970 and named a National Historic Landmark in 1971. Its grounds have become a gallery of memory. The tomb of James K. Polk, the 11th president of the United States, lies here, along with a statue of Andrew Jackson, the Sgt. Alvin C. York Memorial by Felix de Weldon, the Tennessee Holocaust Commission Memorial, the Sam Davis Memorial and a Memorial to Africans during the Middle Passage. Near the northern belvedere, the Charles Warterfield Reliquary gathers broken limestone columns and fragments saved from the building during its mid-1950s restoration. The capitol has weathered storms too: a tornado damaged its roof in March 1855, before it was finished, and the 1933 Nashville tornado passed over or very near it, shaking glass from its windows. Strickland's hand also shows nearby, in St. Mary's Cathedral at the foot of the hill and the Downtown Presbyterian Church a few blocks away.

## Monuments Brought Down

Some memorials on this hill have not survived changing judgments. State senator Douglas Henry sponsored a bust of Confederate general Nathan Bedford Forrest, which was dedicated in 1978 and displayed inside the capitol. Its presence was controversial for decades. On March 9, 2021, the Tennessee Historical Commission voted 25 to 1 to move it to a museum, and on July 23, 2021, the bust was removed, arriving at the Tennessee State Museum three days later. A year earlier, on May 30, 2020, protesters responding to the killing of George Floyd by Minneapolis police toppled the memorial to Senator Edward Ward Carmack near the south entrance. Demonstrators unofficially claimed the area where it had stood as "Ida B. Wells Plaza," honoring the anti-lynching journalist against whom Carmack, a newspaper editor before he was a senator, had demanded retaliation in 1892.

## Flight Context

The Tennessee State Capitol sits at approximately 36.166°N, 86.784°W, atop Capitol Hill, the highest point in downtown Nashville. From 2,500–4,000 feet MSL, look for the temple-shaped limestone building with its tall round tower, with the large Bicentennial Capitol Mall State Park to the north and Legislative Plaza to the south. Nashville International Airport (KBNA) is about 6.5 miles away, and downtown lies within its Class C airspace, so contact approach control. John C. Tune Airport (KJWN) is about 6 miles away. Expect spring and summer thunderstorms and haze.
