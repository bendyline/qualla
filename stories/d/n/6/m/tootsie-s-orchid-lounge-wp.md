# Tootsie's Orchid Lounge

An accidental coat of purple paint named the Nashville honky-tonk behind the Ryman where Willie Nelson, Patsy Cline and a generation of country musicians drank, swapped songs and found their footing.

**Location:** 36.1607, -86.7783

**Categories:** Honky-tonks, Music of Nashville, Tennessee, American country music, Tourist attractions in Nashville, Tennessee, 1960 establishments in Tennessee

## Introduction

Hattie Louise 'Tootsie' Bess did not choose the color. She bought a Nashville bar called Mom's in 1960, and a painter she hired mistakenly finished its exterior in purple. She leaned into it, renaming the place Tootsie's Orchid Lounge, and the building is painted the same color to this day. The address mattered as much as the paint. Tootsie's sits behind the Ryman Auditorium, for years the home of the Grand Ole Opry, with only an alley between its rear entrance and the Ryman. That short walk helped make a small honky-tonk a favored spot for some of the biggest names in country music.

## Across the Alley from the Opry

The early regulars read like a hall of fame roll call: Willie Nelson, Patsy Cline, Mel Tillis, Kris Kristofferson, Waylon Jennings and Roger Miller, along with many other country musicians. Nelson's first professional songwriting job, a $50-a-week deal at Pamper Music, came through his friend Hank Cochran, whom he drank with at Tootsie's. A 1995 documentary, Tootsie's Orchid Lounge: Where the Music Began, describes those early days, when some of the biggest names in the business would stop in to meet friends, have a drink and swap a few songs. Nelson offers his insight in the film, which also draws on archival footage of Patsy Cline, the songwriter Harlan Howard and Roger Miller, and includes performances by Dolly Parton, Aaron Neville, Marijohn Wilkin, Trisha Yearwood, Marty Stuart, Tanya Tucker, Ray Price and Bobby Bare. It was re-released in 2005.

## An Orchid for the Journey

Tootsie Bess died in 1978, and Nashville turned out for her. Tom T. Hall, Roy Acuff and Faron Young were among the luminaries at her funeral. She was buried in an orchid gown, in an orchid-colored casket, with an orchid placed in the casket so that she could take her favorite flower to heaven. Connie Smith, the Opry star who by then was emphasizing Southern gospel music, sang some of Tootsie's favorite hymns. For a woman whose bar had become famous by accident of a paintbrush, it was a fitting farewell in the color that made her name.

## Holding On Through the Lean Years

In 1974 the Opry left the Ryman for the newly built Grand Ole Opry House several miles east of downtown. Tootsie's survived the move. For years it was surrounded by adult entertainment businesses and pawn shops, yet it remained a stronghold of traditional 1950s- and 1960s-style country performance and a gathering place for songwriters and others in the trade. It lasted long enough to see the neighborhood reborn. Beginning in the 1990s, the music and tourist scene along Broadway revived, and the Ryman, after an extensive renovation, began hosting some Opry shows again. New customers followed. Those who know to ask can still order the 'Tootsies Tail' lobster plate, a favorite of Loretta Lynn, though it is no longer on the regular menu.

## Three Stages, Every Night

On November 7, 2010, Tootsie's marked its 50th anniversary with a show at the Ryman Auditorium, where Kris Kristofferson, Terri Clark, Little Jimmy Dickens, Mel Tillis, Jamey Johnson and Joanna Smith performed. Two of those names, Kristofferson and Tillis, had been among the bar's earliest regulars, half a century before. Today Tootsie's runs three stages that host live local talent every night. The performers play current country hits by artists such as Jason Aldean and Taylor Swift alongside original songs, keeping alive the tradition of local musicians trying out their material a few steps from the Ryman's back door.
