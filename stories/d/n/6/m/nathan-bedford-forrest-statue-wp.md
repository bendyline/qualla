# Nathan Bedford Forrest Statue

For 23 years a crudely made, 25-foot Confederate statue loomed over Interstate 65 in Nashville, drawing national ridicule, repeated vandalism and lasting anger until the heirs to its site took it down in 2021.

**Location:** 36.0617, -86.7714

**Categories:** Removed Confederate States of America monuments and memorials, Confederate States of America monuments and memorials in Tennessee, Equestrian statues in Tennessee, Vandalized works of art in Tennessee, Nathan Bedford Forrest

## Introduction

The sculptor started with materials used to make bathtubs, and a butcher knife. That was how the statue's owner, Nashville businessman Bill Dorris, described the working method of his friend Jack Kershaw, an amateur sculptor, to NPR in 2011. The result stood 25 feet tall beside Interstate 65 from 1998 to 2021: Confederate Lieutenant General Nathan Bedford Forrest on a rearing horse, rendered in molded polyurethane (some accounts say fiberglass) with gold leaf and silver leaf. Critics said its distorted face bore little resemblance to Forrest, and for more than two decades it drew national mockery for its crude craftsmanship and lasting controversy over what it celebrated.

## A Monument on a Narrow Strip

The statue went up in 1998 on 3.5 acres of private land owned by Dorris in Nashville's Crieve Hall area, a long, narrow property at 701D Hogan Road squeezed between Interstate 65 and a set of railway tracks. Thirteen flagpoles surrounded it, flying the Confederate battle flag and other Confederate and state flags, along with two cellphone transmission towers. A local camp of the Sons of Confederate Veterans called it one of its most ambitious projects. Kershaw said he pictured Forrest "crying 'Follow me!'" and posed him with a pistol in his left hand, pointed behind him, and a sword held high in his right. Dorris summed up his friend frankly: "As an artist, mediocre." The work was controversial even before it was installed, and it only grew more so.

## The Man It Honored

Forrest was no neutral subject. Before the Civil War he grew wealthy as a horse and cattle trader, real estate broker and cotton plantation owner, and he was directly involved in the interstate slave trade. In April 1864, at the Battle of Fort Pillow, Confederate forces under his command killed a large number of Union troops, many of them after the fort had effectively stopped resisting; far more of the Black soldiers than of the white were killed rather than taken prisoner, and the event is widely called a massacre. After the war he joined the Ku Klux Klan and is generally said to have been elected its first Grand Wizard in 1867, though historians disagree about how much direct control he held. The statue's makers did little to soften that legacy. Kershaw once declared, "Somebody needs to say a good word for slavery," and Dorris called slavery a form of "social security" for African Americans. When the statue was unveiled, newspaper coverage showed that support for it and dismay at it generally followed racial lines.

## Ridiculed From Coast to Coast

Much of the national criticism took aim at its craftsmanship as well as its message. Slate called it the "Confederacy's Dumbest Monument," and Atlas Obscura described it as a Confederate statue "that accurately reflects the ugliness of its subject." Canada's National Post called it a "towering eyesore." John Oliver said it looked "like if a nickel did cocaine," and after the 2017 Unite the Right rally in Charlottesville, Stephen Colbert mocked it on air, prancing across the screen in imitation of the statue. Locals tried to hide it. In July 2015 the Metropolitan Council of Nashville and Davidson County asked to plant screening in front of it, but the Tennessee Department of Transportation said no, and in 2017 the mayor of nearby Oak Hill urged the governor to obscure it with landscaping. Others struck at it directly, splashing it with pink paint in December 2017 and spray-painting the word "Monster" on its side in October 2020.

## Taken Down by Its Heirs

In the end, the statue fell to the terms of its owner's will. Dorris left the statue and its site to the Battle of Nashville Trust, and on December 7, 2021, the Trust removed it. In a joint statement with the executor of the Dorris estate, it gave four reasons, each setting aside the debate over Forrest himself: he was not present at the Battle of Nashville; the statue was ugly and a blight on the city; it was vandalized, in disrepair and dangerous; and its divisive presence distracted from the Trust's mission of protecting the battlefield's surviving sites. The Trust added that even Forrest himself would think it ugly. The statue was damaged as it came down, then disassembled and moved from the site, and the Trust said it would not be remounted or displayed.

## New Flags Beside the Interstate

The flags outlasted the statue, and a dispute between two nonprofit organizations followed over which ones should fly. The Trust sold the land itself in 2023, but the Sons of Confederate Veterans controls the flag poles and flags under a 99-year deed. In 2024 a trustee concluded that a multitude of Confederate battle flags failed to represent both sides of the 1864 Battle of Nashville, which the Trust describes as one of the most decisive of the Civil War, ending the Confederacy's western campaign. The 13 Confederate flags came down that year. In their place, according to the latest reports, fly the U.S. flag and the current state flags of the Union and Confederate states whose soldiers fought in that battle, a quieter display on the same narrow strip of land where drivers once passed one of the most mocked monuments in the country.

## Flight Context

The former statue site lies at approximately 36.062°N, 86.771°W, in the Crieve Hall area of south Nashville, on a narrow strip of land between Interstate 65 and a railway line near Hogan Road. The statue is gone, so use Interstate 65 and the parallel railroad as your visual references from 2,500–3,500 feet MSL; the flag display is small and hard to pick out. Nashville International Airport (KBNA) is about 7 miles away, and the area lies within its Class C airspace, so contact approach control. John C. Tune Airport (KJWN) is about 10 miles away and Smyrna Airport (KMQY) about 14 miles. Expect spring and summer thunderstorms and haze.
