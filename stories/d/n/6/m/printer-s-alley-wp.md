# Printer's Alley

Named for the printers and newspapers that once filled it, this downtown Nashville alley was also a haven for saloons, speakeasies and burlesque clubs, and it remains a nightclub district today.

**Location:** 36.1640, -86.7784

## Introduction

In 1915, one alley in downtown Nashville held two large newspapers, ten print shops and thirteen publishers. Depending on the decade, it also held hotels, gambling halls, saloons, brothels, speakeasies and burlesque clubs. Printer's Alley took its name from the first crowd. Its reputation came from the second, and more than a century later, restaurants and nightclubs still keep the alley busy after dark.

## Ink and Newsprint

The alley runs through the block between Union and Church Streets and 3rd and 4th Avenues North. The land came to the city in the 1780s as a gift from George Michael Deadrick, a Virginia businessman. Starting around 1830 it became the center of printing in Nashville, and by 1915 it was home to The Tennessean and the Nashville Banner along with its many print shops and publishers. The printers held on for a long time. The last of them, the Ambrose Printing Company, did not leave until 1977, well after the alley's other identity had taken over.

## The Men's Quarter

Printing was never the whole story. The alley was also lined with hotels, gambling halls, restaurants, saloons and brothels, and by the late nineteenth century it was known as the Men's Quarter. When statewide Prohibition took effect in Tennessee in 1909, many of those establishments simply became speakeasies, and some restaurants in the alley kept serving liquor, often claiming that customers had brought it themselves. Tennessee repealed Prohibition in 1937, but alcohol still could not be sold by the glass. The alley's owners found a workaround. They created member's bars, where customers brought their own liquor and bartenders mixed it for them on the spot.

## Burlesque, Dinner and a Band

In the 1940s the alley found a new formula. Nightclub operators, several of whom the alley's history remembers as gangsters, learned to blend fine dining with burlesque dancing and live music. Clubs such as The Captain's Table, The Brass Rail Stables, The Embers, The Black Poodle Lounge, the Voo Doo Lounge and The Rainbow Room drew crowds, and the stages drew talent. Performers in the alley included Chet Atkins, Floyd Cramer, Boots Randolph, Dottie West, Mel Tillis, Jeannie Seely, Denise Darcel, the Supremes and Hank Williams. The nightclub district born in that decade is the one that survives in the alley today.

## The Last Link

By the late 1980s, David "Skull" Schulman, owner of the Rainbow Room, was the last remaining direct link to the alley's historic nightlife. In 1998 he was killed by two robbers shortly before his club was due to open, and some believe his ghost still haunts the venue. Recognition of the alley's history had already come. In 1982 the National Park Service listed Printer's Alley as a historic district on the National Register of Historic Places. Today it is home to numerous restaurants and nightclubs, a narrow strip of downtown where the printers have long gone but the nightlife they lived beside carries on.
