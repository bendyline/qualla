# Fort Nashborough

A two-acre log stockade on the Cumberland River bluffs, reached by an overland trek in a brutal winter and a harrowing river voyage, became the seed of Nashville on land the Cherokee fought to keep.

**Location:** 36.1642, -86.7744

## Introduction

When the boats finally reached the mouth of the river in the spring of 1780, the travelers were not sure it was the right one. The stream looked far smaller than the Cumberland they had imagined. But they had heard of no other river entering the Ohio between the Tennessee and the Cumberland, so they turned upstream and gambled. As the channel widened, they knew they had guessed right. At the end of that river waited a new log stockade on the bluffs at French Lick, the settlement that would grow into Nashville.

## A Land Already Claimed

The country these settlers entered was not empty. In March 1775, a month before the American Revolution broke out, the speculator Richard Henderson signed a purchase agreement with the Cherokee for lands that included the region above the Cumberland River. Most Cherokee towns hoped to stay neutral in the colonists' war with Britain, but Chief Dragging Canoe saw it as a chance to resist the continual encroachment of frontiersmen on traditional Cherokee territory. American retaliatory raids forced his people south and west, where they settled along Chickamauga Creek near present-day Chattanooga and became known as the Chickamauga Cherokee. Dragging Canoe had warned the settlers that encroachment would bring "dark and bloody ground." The warning would shape everything that followed at French Lick.

## Christmas on the Frozen Cumberland

Apart from a short-lived trading post set up by fur trader Martin Chartier in 1689, no one had tried to settle French Lick permanently. In early 1779 John Buchanan Sr. and his party built a fortified station there. That February, James Robertson, a former Regulator and founding leader of the Watauga settlement, scouted the area with nine men, negotiated a 3,000-acre grant from Henderson at the steep price of 26 pounds of silver per hundred acres, and left three men behind to plant corn. On November 1, 1779, Robertson led some 200 settlers overland from Fort Patrick Henry at present-day Kingsport. His eleven-year-old son Jonathan drove the sheep. They arrived on Christmas Day, delayed by a winter described as the coldest any of them had ever known.

## The Voyage of the Adventure

The families traveling by water under John Donelson fared worse. Setting out from Fort Patrick Henry on December 22, 1779, they were stopped after just three miles by ice and could not move again until February. At the Chickamauga towns on the Tennessee River, near present-day Chattanooga, they were attacked, and a war party captured one trailing boat with 28 people aboard, smallpox among them. On March 20 the weary, hungry survivors were camped where Paducah, Kentucky, now stands, facing the swollen Ohio in boats built only to float downstream. Some gave up, drifting on to Natchez or settling in Illinois. The rest pushed 15 miles up the Ohio to that uncertain river mouth. To fight the current, Donelson rigged his boat, the Adventure, with a small sail made from a sheet, a man stationed at each lower corner ready to loosen it if a gust grew too strong.

## Twenty Cabins on the Bluff

Reunited, the two parties cleared the land and named their settlement for General Francis Nash, who had won acclaim fighting in the Revolution. The stockade, known variously as Fort Nashborough, Fort Bluff and Bluff Station, was square, covered two acres and held 20 log cabins. It was a shelter for settlers, not a military garrison. Robertson drew up the Cumberland Compact in 1780, and the settlers built nearby way-stations, among them Freeland's, Mansker's and Buchanan's, many still remembered as neighborhoods or towns around Nashville. Life was hard. Corn was the staple, corn whiskey the cure-all, and during that first winter Henderson arranged to ship corn from Kentucky at $200 a bushel. Guns, axes, seeds and hoes were a family's most treasured possessions. On January 11, 1781, Robertson's son Felix became the first white child born in the settlement; he grew up to become one of the most influential physicians of his era.

## Dogs at the Gate

Peace rarely came to the Cumberland. The Chickamauga Cherokee and their Muscogee allies, resenting past concessions, broken treaties and the loss of their hunting grounds, attacked the settlements for fourteen years. On April 2, 1781, in what became known as the Battle of the Bluffs, Cherokee warriors lured most of the fort's men outside and cut them off from the entrance. The settlers escaped back inside, helped by the fort's dogs, which the women turned loose, while the Cherokee took their horses. The fort was abandoned in 1794, but the settlement endured, incorporated as the town of Nashville in 1784, chartered as a city in 1806 and made the permanent capital of Tennessee in 1843. On July 13, 2017, the renovated Fort Nashborough History Center opened on First Avenue North, with a plaza dedicated to Native American history.

## Flight Context

Fort Nashborough lies at approximately 36.164°N, 86.774°W, beside the Cumberland River in downtown Nashville, Tennessee, along First Avenue North. From 2,500–4,000 feet MSL, follow the Cumberland as it curves past the downtown skyline; the history center stands along First Avenue North near the original fort site. Nashville International Airport (KBNA) is about 6 miles away, and downtown lies within its Class C airspace, so contact approach control. John C. Tune Airport (KJWN) is also about 6 miles away. Expect spring and summer thunderstorms and haze.
