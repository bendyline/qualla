# Fort Negley

The largest inland stone fort of the Civil War was raised on a Nashville hilltop by thousands of Black laborers, some of whom lie in unmarked graves beside it.

**Location:** 36.1451, -86.7747

**Categories:** Forts in Tennessee, History of Nashville, Tennessee, American Civil War museums in Tennessee, African American history, Works Progress Administration in Tennessee

## Introduction

In January 2018, a plan to remake the land beside Fort Negley collapsed over what lay beneath it. Archaeologists had determined that undisturbed ground at the edge of the old Greer Stadium property held the unmarked graves of enslaved people who had been forced to build the fort, and the developers walked away. The discovery pulled a hilltop's oldest story back into the light. Fort Negley, just south of downtown Nashville, was the largest inland stone fort built during the Civil War. The Union army planned it. Black laborers, many of them enslaved or conscripted, did most of the work of raising it.

## A Star of Limestone

Nashville fell almost without a fight. After Confederate forces were routed from Forts Henry and Donelson, on the Tennessee and Cumberland Rivers, in February 1862, their commanders decided that further defense of the city was pointless and abandoned it. Union troops moved in almost at once and began fortifying their prize. The largest of their works rose on St. Cloud Hill, south of the city: a star-shaped fort of limestone blocks, its construction overseen by Captain James St. Clair Morton. By one published tally it took 62,500 cubic feet of stone and 18,000 cubic feet of earth, and it cost $130,000. The army named it for Union General James S. Negley.

## The People Who Built It

The fort was built largely by Black Nashvillians and newcomers to the city. Local enslaved people, women among them, were put to work on it. So were people who had fled slavery and flocked to Nashville after the Union took the city, on the understanding that working for the Union would end their enslavement. Free Black residents were forcibly conscripted for the job. Records show that 2,768 Black workers were officially enrolled in the fort's construction. Some of them never left the hill. Their unmarked graves, identified by archaeologists beside the fort, turned a stadium redevelopment into a question of memory, and Nashville's mayor at the time said she wanted the site to honor the history of those who died building the fort. Today Fort Negley is listed as a Site of Memory in UNESCO's Slave Route Project.

## A Fortress That Waited

For all its size, Fort Negley never played a leading military role. When the Battle of Nashville finally came in December 1864, most of the fighting took place on heights even farther south of the city. Shortly after the war the fort was abandoned and fell into ruin, though its outline could be traced for many years among the encroaching trees. The hill then took on a darker use. During Reconstruction, the ruins served as a meeting place for the Ku Klux Klan, as former Klansman Marcus B. Toney later confirmed. Walls raised largely by Black hands became a gathering spot for a secret society formed in the war's aftermath, a bitter irony in the fort's history.

## Swallowed by the Forest

In the 1930s the Works Progress Administration made a major project of restoring Fort Negley, and the rebuilt fort opened to the public in 1938. The park plans were never completed, though, and as the United States entered World War II there was no money, manpower or interest left to keep it going as a historic site. After the war it languished again, attracting vandalism and minor crime until entry was prohibited and the hilltop reverted to forest. Around it, the grounds became a municipal park with baseball and softball fields, then Herschel Greer Stadium for minor league baseball, while the Cumberland Science Museum - today's Adventure Science Center - rose on the northwestern slope. Most people who came for a game or an exhibit had little idea what lay on the wooded summit, only that they were not allowed to go there.

## Ruins Brought Back to Light

After years of negotiation, preservationists secured funding in the early 2000s, and in December 2004, on the 140th anniversary of the Battle of Nashville, the fort reopened to the public for the first time in decades. The goal was not to rebuild it to its Civil War condition but to stabilize the ruins and make them visible, removing many of the largest trees and moving some blocks back to their original positions. What visitors see now blends original fortification with WPA restoration. The city spent an additional $1 million on a visitor center, which opened in December 2007. The later plan to redevelop the neighboring Greer Stadium property drew criticism before the graves ended it, and in 2022 a master plan was completed to restore the site as a park and interpret its complex history. The city scheduled the first construction phase of that plan to begin in February 2026.

## Flight Context

Fort Negley crowns St. Cloud Hill at approximately 36.1451 N, 86.7747 W, about 1 nm south-southeast of downtown Nashville. From 1,500-2,000 ft AGL, look for a wooded hilltop just south of the downtown skyline, with the Adventure Science Center on its northwestern slope and the former Greer Stadium grounds beside it; the star-shaped outline of the stone walls shows best when the trees are bare. The fort lies close to Nashville International's controlled airspace, so coordinate with approach control. Nearest airports: Nashville International Airport (KBNA), about 5 nm east-southeast, and John C. Tune Airport (KJWN), about 6 nm west-northwest. Expect haze and pop-up thunderstorms on summer afternoons.
