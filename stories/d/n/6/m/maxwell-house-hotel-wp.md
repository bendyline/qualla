# Maxwell House Hotel

Nashville's grand Maxwell House Hotel hosted seven presidents, lent its name to a famous coffee and harbored a dark chapter as an early meeting place of the Ku Klux Klan before burning on Christmas night 1961.

**Location:** 36.1638, -86.7793

**Categories:** Hotels in Nashville, Tennessee, Burned hotels in the United States, Demolished buildings and structures in Tennessee, Ku Klux Klan in Tennessee, Defunct hotels in Tennessee

## Introduction

The story goes that President Theodore Roosevelt tasted a cup of coffee and declared it 'good to the last drop.' The line became the coffee's advertising slogan, but General Foods, which once owned the Maxwell House brand, eventually acknowledged that Roosevelt had nothing to do with it; the line was written by Clifford Spiller, a former General Foods president. The hotel behind the name, however, was entirely real. For nearly a century the Maxwell House stood at the corner of Fourth Avenue North and Church Street in downtown Nashville, hosting presidents and celebrities - and, by long-told tradition, in its earliest years an early national meeting of the Ku Klux Klan.

## Overton's Folly

As Nashville grew, local businessmen decided the city needed a major hotel. Colonel John Overton Jr. took on the project, hired the architect Isaiah Rogers and named the building for his wife, Harriet (Maxwell) Overton. Not everyone was impressed; locals called the site 'Overton's Folly.' Then the Civil War stopped construction. Nashville fell to the Union Army in 1862 and stayed occupied until the war ended, and the soldiers turned the unfinished hotel into a barracks, a prison and a hospital. Those years left behind a ghost story. The hotel was said to be haunted by a Southern belle and two brothers posted there as guards: according to the tale, one brother killed the other and the young woman in a jealous rage, then died himself when a staircase collapsed as he moved the bodies.

## What Happened in Room 10

Even before it was finished, parts of the building were in use. By the account long repeated in Nashville, in the fall of 1866 the former Confederate general Nathan Bedford Forrest was inducted into the Ku Klux Klan, then a newly formed secret vigilante group, in Room 10 of the Maxwell House, with the politician John W. Morton performing the ceremony; he was later made Grand Wizard of what members called the Invisible Empire. Historians caution that this story rests on late, thinly documented recollections, and they disagree about how early and how directly Forrest led the Klan. Chapters spread across the South, and the Klan's April 1867 convention in Nashville is traditionally said to have met at the hotel. What those chapters did is not in doubt. During Reconstruction the Klan systematically used violence against Black people and their white allies, burning homes and killing Black Southerners to intimidate them. The Maxwell House gave that movement a meeting room.

## The Grandest Address in Town

Once complete, the Maxwell House made its statement from the street. Eight Corinthian columns flanked the front entrance on Fourth Avenue, in the 'Men's Quarter,' while women entered separately on Church Street. The lobby gleamed with mahogany cabinetry, brass fixtures, gilded mirrors and chandeliers. There were ladies' and men's parlors, billiard rooms, barrooms and shaving 'saloons,' and a grand staircase rose to the large ball or dining room. The hotel was at its height from the 1890s into the early 20th century, famous for a Christmas dinner that featured calf's head, black bear, opossum and other unusual delicacies. Its guest list read like a national who's who: Jane Addams, Sarah Bernhardt, William Jennings Bryan, Enrico Caruso, 'Buffalo Bill' Cody, Thomas Edison, Henry Ford, Annie Oakley and O. Henry among them.

## Seven Presidents and a Cup of Coffee

Seven presidents stayed under its roof: Andrew Johnson, Rutherford B. Hayes, Grover Cleveland, Theodore Roosevelt, William McKinley, William Howard Taft and Woodrow Wilson. The hotel also served a coffee that took its name, and that brand long outlived the building. Roosevelt's supposed verdict on a cup of it became the Maxwell House advertising slogan, even though the words were written by a company executive rather than spoken by a president. The coffee is still sold today, owned by Kraft Heinz in North America and by JDE Peet's, now part of Keurig Dr Pepper, in the rest of the world. The name on the coffee is now the hotel's most lasting trace.

## Christmas Night, 1961

The Maxwell House spent its final years as a residential hotel. On Christmas night in 1961, fire destroyed it - a grim coda for a building once famous for its Christmas feasts. Construction had begun before the Civil War, and the structure had survived years of military occupation, only to be lost to flames nearly a century later. The corner did not stay empty. The ServiceSource Tower was later built on the site at 201 Fourth Avenue North, and in 1979 a new Millennium Maxwell House Hotel opened elsewhere in Nashville, named in memory of the old one. Nothing of Overton's Folly remains to see, only its name and the complicated history attached to it.

## Flight Context

The Maxwell House site lies at approximately 36.1638 N, 86.7793 W, at Fourth Avenue North and Church Street in the heart of downtown Nashville; the hotel itself is gone, so use the downtown skyline and the Cumberland River just to the east as references. From 1,500-2,500 ft AGL the whole downtown grid, including the Tennessee State Capitol on its hill a few blocks northwest, is easy to pick out. The area sits beneath Nashville International's controlled airspace, so coordinate with approach control. Nearest airports: Nashville International Airport (KBNA), about 5 nm east-southeast, and John C. Tune Airport (KJWN), about 5 nm west-northwest. Summer afternoons bring haze and thunderstorms.
