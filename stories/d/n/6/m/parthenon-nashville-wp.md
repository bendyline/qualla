# Parthenon (Nashville)

Built of plaster for an 1897 exposition and never meant to last, Nashville's full-scale Parthenon was rebuilt in concrete and now shelters a gilded, 42-foot Athena.

**Location:** 36.1496, -86.8135

**Categories:** Museums in Nashville, Tennessee, Replica buildings, Greek Revival architecture in Tennessee, Tennessee Centennial and International Exposition, Art museums and galleries in Tennessee

## Introduction

Nashville's Parthenon was built to be torn down. Raised in plaster, wood and brick as the centerpiece of the 1897 Tennessee Centennial Exposition, it was meant to be a temporary backdrop for a celebration. But demolition would have been expensive, and residents and visitors alike had grown fond of the building, so it was left standing after the fair closed. More than a century later, this full-scale replica of the ancient temple in Athens still rises over Centennial Park - now in concrete - and inside it a gilded Athena stands 42 feet tall.

## The Athens of the South

The idea came from Major Eugene Castner Lewis, director of the Centennial Exposition and chief civil engineer for the Nashville, Chattanooga and St. Louis Railroad. At his suggestion, a reproduction of the Parthenon would anchor Tennessee's centennial, and Nashville's reputation as the 'Athens of the South' helped make the choice. The architect William Crawford Smith designed it, and in 1895 George Julian Zolnay was employed to make models for its ornamentation. Several exposition buildings borrowed from ancient originals, but the Parthenon was the only exact reproduction. It was also the only one the city preserved. The Knights of Pythias Pavilion survived in a different way: it was bought and moved to nearby Franklin, Tennessee.

## Chariots, Birds and Fire

Before it was rebuilt, the plaster temple starred in some extravagant theater. The 1913 production, The Fire Regained, was a mythological drama written by Sidney Mttron Hirsch and loaded with the kind of spectacle audiences of the era loved. The 1914 follow-up, called The Mystery at Thanatos in some accounts, had a similarly mythological plot but was shorter, and accounts differ on how well it was received; a copy of its script is on file at the Nashville Public Library. Both shows reportedly staged chariot races, large dance numbers, thousands of live birds and set pieces that shot flames, all against the columns of the Parthenon. The tradition of classical theater never quite left. In summer, local companies still perform Greek plays such as Euripides' Medea and Sophocles' Antigone on the building's steps, usually for free.

## Rebuilt in Concrete

Plaster could not survive Tennessee weather for long. Within about 20 years the landmark had deteriorated, and in 1920 work began to rebuild it on the same foundations, this time in concrete. The local architect Russell Hart was hired for the reconstruction. The exterior was completed in 1925 and the interior in 1931, turning a fair pavilion into a permanent monument. Time kept working on it, and in 2001 the exterior received a much-needed cleaning and restoration. Inside, the building became an art museum. Its permanent collection on the lower level is built around 63 paintings by 19th- and 20th-century American artists, donated by James M. Cowan between 1927 and 1929.

## Athena Comes Home

The great statue that once stood in the Athenian Parthenon was lost long ago. In 1982 the sculptor Alan LeQuire was commissioned to recreate it, as a reconstruction built to scholarly standards, and he unveiled the statue in 1990, with the gilding and painting completed in 2002. His Athena Parthenos wears a cuirass and helmet, carries a shield on her left arm and holds in her right palm a figure of Nike, the goddess of victory, that is itself six feet high. She stands 42 feet tall and, since the 2002 gilding, is covered in more than eight pounds of gold leaf. In the Treasury Room, the west room of the main hall, plaster replicas cast directly from the original sculptures of the Athenian pediments, dating to 438 BC, let visitors study the temple's sculpture up close. Performances have even been staged at the goddess's feet, including Mary Zimmerman's Metamorphoses.

## A Temple in the Movies

A Greek temple in the middle of Tennessee has proved irresistible to storytellers. In the 2010 film Percy Jackson & the Olympians: The Lightning Thief, the Parthenon served as the backdrop for a battle against the Hydra, and the PBS series Greeks: Crucible of Civilization used it in 2000. Casiotone for the Painfully Alone named a song 'Nashville Parthenon' on the album Etiquette and followed it in 2011 with 'Goodbye Parthenon.' The building appears in Heather Ross Miller's poem 'Ganymede' and figures in the climax of Craig McDonald's Hector Lassiter novel Three Chords and the Truth. Not bad for a building that was supposed to come down when the fair ended.

## Flight Context

The Parthenon stands in Centennial Park at approximately 36.1496 N, 86.8135 W, about 1.7 nm west-southwest of downtown Nashville. From 1,500-2,500 ft AGL, look for the pale, columned temple set in the open parkland of Centennial Park; the downtown skyline to the east is a good orientation reference. The site lies near Nashville International's controlled airspace, so coordinate with approach control. Nearest airports: John C. Tune Airport (KJWN), about 4 nm west-northwest, and Nashville International Airport (KBNA), about 7 nm east-southeast. Clear autumn and spring days give the best light on the columns; summer brings haze and afternoon storms.
