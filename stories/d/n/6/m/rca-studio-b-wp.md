# RCA Studio B

Sketched on a dinner napkin and built of pastel cinderblock, RCA Studio B became a birthplace of the Nashville Sound, where by one count more than 47,000 songs were recorded, more than 240 of them by Elvis Presley.

**Location:** 36.1499, -86.7928

**Categories:** Recording studios in Tennessee, RCA Records, Culture of Nashville, Tennessee, Country music, Country Music Hall of Fame and Museum

## Introduction

The building began as a sketch on a dinner napkin. RCA Victor's chief engineer and recording manager drew the plans, as Chet Atkins told it, a local businessman named Dan Maddox offered to construct it as an investment, and four months later, in November 1957, a pastel cinderblock studio stood finished at 1611 Hawkins Street in Nashville. It cost about $40,000, and it looked like the modest building it was. Yet in the two decades it operated, RCA Studio B is credited with producing 60 percent of the hits on Billboard's country chart, and by one count more than 47,000 songs were recorded inside its walls - more than 240 of them by Elvis Presley.

## Before the Cinderblock

For years Steve Sholes and Chet Atkins had recorded RCA Victor's Nashville projects with portable equipment in whatever studios they could find. In 1954 they set up the company's first Nashville recording facility inside the Methodist Television Radio & Film Commission building at 1525 McGavock Street. It was there, in January 1956, that they produced a session in which Elvis Presley recorded 'Heartbreak Hotel,' the song that became his first gold record and the biggest-selling single of 1956. That building no longer exists; it was demolished in 2006 to make way for a parking lot. RCA wanted a studio of its own, and the napkin sketch gave it one, first called simply the RCA Victor Studio. When the company built the larger Studio A in an adjacent building in 1965, the original became Studio B.

## A Small Room with a Big Piano

Business offices filled the single-story front of the building, and the studio lay behind them. The recording room had a 13-foot ceiling, and in one corner sat a grand piano that had come from NBC's Tonight Starring Steve Allen. The control room was tiny, only 12 feet deep. It held an RCA radio-station tube console with 12 microphone inputs and four outputs, which fed an Ampex two-track tape deck, while an echo chamber occupied the second story. Hawkins Street was later renamed Roy Acuff Place, and the neighborhood around the studio became Nashville's Music Row.

## Porter's Pyramids

In March 1959 Bill Porter replaced Bob Ferris as the studio's chief engineer, and by June he had mixed a number one hit, 'The Three Bells' by the Browns. Porter still thought the room sounded wrong. Resonant room modes gave it an uneven frequency response, so he took about $60 from petty cash, bought fiberglass acoustic ceiling panels, cut them into triangles and hung them at varying heights - the 'Porter Pyramids.' Then, through careful experiment, he found the spots where the resonances were weakest and marked them with X's on the floor. Lead singers, backup vocalists and acoustic guitarists stood at microphones placed directly over those marks. After the changes, Don Gibson recorded his album Girls, Guitars and Gibson here, and Porter later recalled people telling him, 'God, what a different sound!' He also favored the studio's EMT 140 plate reverb over its echo chamber, keeping the plates chilled in the air-conditioned room to brighten their sound.

## Home of the Nashville Sound

Chet Atkins and his production team, including Steve Sholes, Bob Ferguson and Bill Porter, made records here in what came to be called the Nashville Sound, a polished style built on background vocals and strings. Studio B, Bradley Studios and later RCA Studio A were the essential places where that style developed. The Nashville Sound revived country music's popularity and helped establish the city as an international recording center. The roster of artists who worked here includes Eddy Arnold, Jim Reeves, Roy Orbison, Hank Snow, Porter Wagoner, Dottie West, Connie Smith, Charley Pride, Boots Randolph and Willie Nelson. Dolly Parton made a memorable entrance. In her 1994 memoir, My Life and Other Unfinished Business, she recounted rushing to her first Studio B session in October 1967, shortly after signing with RCA Victor, and driving her car into the side wall of the building. She noted that the spot was still visible.

## The Tape Stops, the Tours Begin

Studio B closed in 1977. That same year it was made available to the Country Music Hall of Fame for tours, and in 1992 Dan Maddox, the businessman who had built it, donated it to the Hall of Fame; in 2002, the museum says, the Mike Curb Family Foundation bought the studio and leased it back for $1 a year. From 2001 to 2011 the Hall shared its operation with Belmont University's Mike Curb College of Entertainment and Music Business, whose students learned the basics of analog recording in the room where the Nashville Sound took shape. In 2012 the National Park Service listed RCA Studio B on the National Register of Historic Places, and the Country Music Hall of Fame and Museum took over sole operation, offering daily scheduled tours.

## Flight Context

RCA Studio B sits at approximately 36.1499 N, 86.7928 W, on Music Row about 1 nm southwest of downtown Nashville. The low building itself is hard to spot from the air; use the downtown skyline to the northeast and the Belmont University campus just to the south as references, from about 1,500-2,000 ft AGL.
