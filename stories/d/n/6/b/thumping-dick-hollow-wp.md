# Thumping Dick Hollow

A small forested cove in Sewanee, Tennessee, with caves, sinkholes, old-growth forest and a name that has puzzled and delighted visitors since at least 1858.

**Location:** 35.2278, -85.9554

**Categories:** Caves of Tennessee, Sewanee: The University of the South, Landforms of Franklin County, Tennessee, Valleys of Tennessee, National Natural Landmarks in Tennessee

## Introduction

Nobody is entirely sure who Dick was, or exactly what thumped. The name Thumping Dick Hollow was already in use by 1858, before the University of the South was built on the land above it, and it has been amusing visitors ever since. One of them, the New England author and Yale professor Walter Prichard Eaton, came here in spring and wrote about it, celebrating the Southern gift for colorful place names. Behind the name lies a small cove of old-growth forest, sinkholes and caves.

## What Went Thump

Local tradition offers more than one answer. The most popular version tells of a resident named Dick who built a water-powered grain mill in the nineteenth century. When the mill was running, it made a thumping sound that carried through the surrounding forest, and the hollow took its name from Dick's noisy machine. Another version keeps the thump but changes the machine, crediting a water-powered sump pump or a sawmill instead. Walter Prichard Eaton, in his 1922 essay, traced the name to a man named Dick whose log-and-seesaw corn pounder thumped day and night. None of these stories can be pinned down with certainty, which suits a place whose name has always been more memorable than its explanation. The hollow also goes by a slightly more sober alternative, Thumping Dick Cove, and the National Park Service knows it simply as Dick Cove.

## Caves Below the Road

The cove sits in Franklin County, in southern Tennessee, within the domain of the University of the South and the town of Sewanee. It lies off the old stagecoach route of Breakfield Road, roughly 700 feet below the road, and the ground is riddled with sinkholes and caves. Hikers who follow the path beyond gate 7 on Breakfield Road walk about 0.8 miles to a stream crossing, then pick up a faint path to reach the mouth of the Columned Entrance Cave. Farther along, around the cliff to the right, is the second cave, Solomon's Temple, with a waterfall and a collapsed sinkhole below it.

## Old Trees, Protected Ground

The hollow is also known for its old-growth forest, and in 1973 the National Park Service designated Dick Cove a National Natural Landmark. Today the hollow forms one segment of the Sewanee Perimeter Trail. The descent of some 700 feet from Breakfield Road trades the roads above for sinkholes, cave mouths, running water and trees that have been left to grow old. Getting there still means following the line of an old stagecoach road, then a faint path on the far side of a stream, which keeps the hollow feeling far more remote than it is.

## A Critic Comes Calling

Walter Prichard Eaton was an early twentieth-century writer from New England, a drama critic and a professor at Yale. In his 1922 collection of essays, Penguin, Persons, and Peppermints, he described a spring visit to the hollow, and used the occasion to celebrate the Southern American talent for giving places colorful names. More than a century later, the name has lost none of its power to make a reader stop and say it twice.
