# Sewanee: The University of the South

Founded on a Cumberland Plateau mountaintop as a university for the antebellum South, Sewanee has spent nearly 170 years building Gothic stone, a literary legacy and a football legend, and is now reckoning openly with the slavery and Confederate veneration in its foundations.

## Introduction

In 1863, Union soldiers blew apart a six-ton block of marble on top of the Cumberland Plateau. Bishop Leonidas Polk had consecrated it on October 10, 1860, as the cornerstone of a new university for the South, and when it shattered, many of the soldiers carried off pieces as keepsakes. A few eventually came back, and a large fragment now sits in a wall of All Saints' Chapel. That broken stone suits the University of the South, familiarly called Sewanee: a Gothic campus on a 13,000-acre mountain domain whose founding, and whose reckoning with that founding, are both built into its walls.

## Up Monteagle Mountain

The idea began in the 1830s with Bishop James Otey of Tennessee, who wanted an Episcopal seminary in the Deep South. After the Mexican War the church grew rapidly in the region, and Otey saw a need for a school "to train natives, for natives." South of the Mason-Dixon Line, only the Virginia Theological Seminary existed. The mountain site was chosen chiefly for its access to the rail hub of Chattanooga and an existing spur up the slope, the "Mountain Goat," which began service in the 1850s and was abandoned in 1985. From Sewanee, Polk remarked, one could reach any point in the South within thirty-six to forty-eight hours. On July 4, 1857, near Chattanooga, delegates from ten Southern dioceses, Texas to the Carolinas, named the first trustees of their college, with Polk among the leaders of the effort. The goal was a Southern university free of Northern influences. It would, Otey said, "materially aid the South to resist and repel a fanatical domination which seeks to rule over us."

## The Ledger Beneath the Domain

Most of the land was donated by the Sewanee Mining Company, on condition that a university "be put in operation within ten years." The company's early profits came from the forced labor of leased convicts, most of them African American. The largest single donor to the founding was John Armfield, co-founder of Franklin & Armfield, described as "the largest slave trading firm" in the United States. Founders Polk, Otey and Bishop Stephen Elliott Jr. later became prominent in the Confederacy; Polk died in action during the Atlanta campaign, and former Confederate generals Edmund Kirby Smith, Josiah Gorgas and Francis A. Shoup led the school's postwar revival. Even the university mace, an unsolicited gift dedicated to early Ku Klux Klan leader Nathan Bedford Forrest, who had no connection to Sewanee, became a flashpoint. In September 2020 the governing board acknowledged for the first time that the university "was long entangled with, and played a role in, slavery, racial segregation, and white supremacy," and said it "categorically rejects its past veneration of the Confederacy and of the 'Lost Cause.'" The Roberson Project on Slavery, Race, and Reconciliation, begun in 2017, is guiding what comes next.

## Nine Students and a Century-Long Chapel

War halted construction, and building resumed in 1866. The first convocation, on September 18, 1868, gathered nine students and four faculty members. Presiding was Charles Todd Quintard, second Bishop of Tennessee and the man called the "Chaplain of the Confederacy." In 1867, the year of the first Lambeth Conference, he was in England and secured financial support from Church of England clergy and laity for rebuilding, earning the title of Sewanee's "Re-Founder." The campus that grew from those beginnings overlooks the Tennessee Valley, its buildings mostly Gothic and faced with local stone. Ralph Adams Cram designed All Saints' Chapel and work began in 1904, but the Panic of 1907 left the university without funds to finish. It was completed in 1959 to a design by Vice-Chancellor Edward McCrady, and its final window was installed in 2004, nearly a century after construction began. Members of the Order of Gown may still teach or attend class in academic gowns, one of the last vestiges of that English practice in North America.

## Ink on the Mountain

The Sewanee Review, founded in 1892, is billed as the oldest continuously published literary magazine in the United States, and its success helped launch the Sewanee Writers' Conference, held each summer. Allen Tate, Andrew Lytle, Caroline Gordon, Walker Percy, Shelby Foote and Robert Lowell all lived in Sewanee or its surroundings, some briefly and some for good. The most surprising bequest came in 1983, when the Pulitzer Prize-winning playwright Tennessee Williams left his literary rights to the university. The royalties helped build the Tennessee Williams Center, now home to the Department of Theatre and Dance, and fund fellowships that bring well-known figures in the arts to campus. The motto comes from the opening of Psalm 133: "Behold how good and how pleasant it is for brethren to dwell together in unity."

## Iron Men, Then Shutouts

Sewanee's most improbable legend belongs to its 1899 football team. Coached by Billy Suter and captained by Henry Seibels, the "Iron Men" went 12-0, and on a single road trip they won five games in six days, every one a shutout, over Texas, Texas A&M, Tulane, LSU and Ole Miss. In 2012, in a College Football Hall of Fame fan-vote bracket of historic teams, the 1899 Iron Men beat the 1961 Alabama Crimson Tide. The glory faded fast. Sewanee was a charter member of the Southeastern Conference in 1932, but its program had already declined steeply, and in eight SEC seasons it never won a conference football game. The Tigers were shut out 26 times in 37 SEC games and outscored 1,163 to 84. Today they play in the Southern Athletic Association, of which Sewanee is a charter member.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 35.2031°N, 85.9197°W, on the flat top of the southern Cumberland Plateau just west of Monteagle. Look for a large wooded tract around a cluster of stone Gothic buildings; Interstate 24 crosses the plateau a few miles to the east, and the escarpment drops toward the Tennessee Valley and Winchester to the south and west. A cruise around 4,000-4,500 feet MSL keeps you clear of the plateau and gives a good view of the campus and the valley edge. Franklin County Airport (KUOS) sits about 1 nm from the campus; Winchester Municipal (KBGF) is about 7 nm and Chattanooga Metropolitan / Lovell Field (KCHA) about 37 nm east-southeast. Morning fog and low cloud often cap the plateau while the valleys below are clear.
