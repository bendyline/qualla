# Highlander Research and Education Center

On a Cumberland Plateau hillside near Monteagle, a Depression-era folk school trained labor organizers, hosted Rosa Parks and Martin Luther King Jr., helped carry "We Shall Overcome" into the civil rights movement, and was seized by the state of Tennessee for its trouble.

**Location:** 35.2551, -85.8087

**Categories:** History of civil rights in the United States, History of labor relations in the United States, Education in Tennessee, Labor schools, Monteagle, Tennessee, Educational institutions established in 1932

## Introduction

Striking tobacco workers sang it on the picket line during the 1945–1946 Charleston Cigar Factory strike. Zilphia Horton, the music director of a small school on the Cumberland Plateau, adapted the gospel song from their singing, and folksinger Pete Seeger published it in the People's Songs bulletin. Later it was revived at the school by Guy Carawan, who had succeeded her as music director in 1959, and Carawan taught it to the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee at their first convening at Shaw University. The song was "We Shall Overcome." The school was the Highlander Folk School, founded in the community of Summerfield in Grundy County, between Monteagle and Tracy City, and its work would draw both history-making students and relentless attacks.

## A Danish Idea in the Tennessee Hills

Highlander opened in 1932, in the depths of the Great Depression, on land donated for the purpose by educator Lilian Wyckoff Johnson. Its founders were activist Myles Horton, educator Don West, and Methodist minister James A. Dombrowski. Across the country, workers who tried to form unions met major resistance from employers, especially in the South. Horton had studied the rural adult education schools that Danish Lutheran Bishop N. F. S. Grundtvig inspired in the 19th century, and he wanted something similar here: in the founders' words, "an educational center in the South for the training of rural and industrial leaders, and for the conservation and enrichment of the indigenous cultural values of the mountains." Through the 1930s and 1940s the school's main work was labor education and the training of union organizers. The writer Myra Page taught here in the 1930s, and in 1937 the school appeared in the short film People of the Cumberland. Horton led Highlander for four decades and retired as its director in 1973, according to his obituary in The Seattle Times.

## Children of Summerfield

Not every program was aimed at adults. From 1938 to 1953 the school ran the Highlander Nursery School, offering free early learning to the white working-class children of Summerfield. It was a cooperative, dependent on the material support and goodwill of local residents, and it did quiet diplomatic work: by caring for their children, Highlander built relationships with mountain families who might otherwise have opposed its growing commitment to civil rights. The nursery is a small detail in a large history, but it shows how the school operated. Its founders believed communities should learn from their own experience, and they began by earning a place in the community around them.

## The First Integrated Meal

In the 1950s Highlander turned its energy toward desegregation. Working with Esau Jenkins of Johns Island, it developed a literacy program for Black citizens who were being kept off the voter rolls by literacy requirements. The Citizenship Education Schools that grew from it, coordinated by Septima Clark with help from Bernice Robinson, spread across the South and helped thousands of Black citizens register to vote. The school trained Rosa Parks before her role in the Montgomery bus boycott, and hosted Martin Luther King Jr., Ralph Abernathy, James Bevel, Bernard Lafayette, John Lewis, and Julian Bond. Lewis later recalled that he had his first meal in an integrated setting here: "I was a young adult, but I had never eaten a meal in the company of Black and white diners." Parks, he recalled, watched Septima Clark teaching side by side with Horton. "She had never seen an integrated team of equals working together, and it inspired her." Parks attended her Highlander workshop in the summer of 1955, several months before she kept her seat on a Montgomery bus on December 1.

## A Charter Revoked

The backlash was relentless. Southern newspapers accused Highlander of creating racial strife, and in 1957 the Georgia Commission on Education published a pamphlet branding it "Highlander Folk School: Communist Training School, Monteagle, Tennessee." As the state of Tennessee threatened to close the school, the citizenship program was handed to the Southern Christian Leadership Conference, led by King. After a long court battle that began with a police raid on the school in July 1959, Tennessee revoked Highlander's charter and confiscated and auctioned its land and property, in 1961 by most accounts. According to Septima Clark's autobiography, the stated grounds were that the school had engaged in commercial activities in violation of its charter. Because Highlander had been chartered as a non-profit without stockholders or owners, no one could make a legal claim on what was taken. The staff reincorporated as the Highlander Research and Education Center, moved to Knoxville, and in 1971 settled in New Market, Tennessee, its longest continuous home.

## What Remains on the Mountain

The work outlived the land seizure. In the 1960s and 1970s Highlander turned to worker health and safety in the Appalachian coalfields, helped start the Southern Appalachian Leadership Training program, and coordinated a survey of land ownership across Appalachia. Later decades brought environmental justice, globalization, LGBT rights, youth organizing, and work with immigrant and refugee leaders. Attacks continued too. On March 29, 2019, a fire destroyed the building that housed executive offices at the New Market center, along with the documents, speeches, and memorabilia kept there; no one was inside, and a white-power symbol, reported to be the Iron Guard symbol, was found spray-painted in the parking lot. In April 2026 a man pleaded guilty in federal court to setting the fire, admitting it was motivated by white-supremacist ideology, and sentencing was scheduled for September. Much of the older history survives elsewhere, in archives at the Wisconsin Historical Society and at the University of North Carolina's Wilson Library. Back on the plateau, the old library is one of the few original buildings left. It was named in 2014 to the Tennessee Preservation Trust's list of the ten most endangered historic sites in the state, the Trust bought the site that year, and it has since restored the building, though who should own and tell its story has been contested.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 35.2551°N, 85.8087°W mark the original Highlander Folk School site at Summerfield, on the southern Cumberland Plateau between Monteagle (to the west) and Tracy City (to the east). The present-day Highlander center is in New Market, East Tennessee, not at this point. Interstate 24 climbs the plateau at Monteagle and is the easiest line to follow; the wooded campus of the University of the South at Sewanee lies just to the west. A cruise of about 4,000-4,500 feet MSL gives a good view of the flat-topped plateau and its escarpment edges. Nearest fields: Franklin County Airport at Sewanee (KUOS) about 5 nm, Winchester Municipal (KBGF) about 14 nm, and Chattanooga Metropolitan / Lovell Field (KCHA) about 33 nm southeast. Plateau fog and low cloud are common on cool mornings; allow for rising terrain along the escarpment.
