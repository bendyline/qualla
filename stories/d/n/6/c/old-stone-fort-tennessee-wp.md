# Old Stone Fort (Tennessee)

Long credited to buccaneers or Vikings, the stone-and-earth walls on a river peninsula in Manchester, Tennessee, were raised over centuries by Middle Woodland Native Americans, most likely as a ceremonial enclosure rather than a fort.

**Location:** 35.4828, -86.1078

**Categories:** Archaeological sites on the National Register of Historic Places in Tennessee, State parks of Tennessee, Hopewellian peoples, Archaeological parks, Native American history, Protected areas of Coffee County, Tennessee

## Introduction

In 1823 a Jackson, Tennessee, newspaper called the Pioneer offered its readers a confident explanation for the mysterious walls on a peninsula in Coffee County. Buccaneers from Seville, it argued, had been shipwrecked off Florida, forced inland, and built the Old Stone Fort. Later generations, stirred by discoveries such as L'Anse aux Meadows, credited the Vikings. Both were wrong. The walls were the work of Native Americans of the Middle Woodland period, raised gradually over several centuries, and today the structure is considered the most complex hilltop enclosure found in the South. It was probably never a fort at all.

## Digging for the Builders

Serious study began in 1876, when Joseph Jones investigated the site for the Smithsonian Institution and uncovered several prehistoric artifacts. In 1928 Tennessee State Archaeologist P.E. Cox dug trenches and analyzed the walls' composition. The real answers came in 1966, when the University of Tennessee Department of Anthropology cut a series of trenches and test pits. Team member Charles Faulkner identified the builders as Middle Woodland people on three grounds. Radiocarbon dates from charcoal inside the walls fell roughly between 30 and 430 AD, and later summaries extend the building sequence to about AD 550. The enclosure's composition, setting and layout matched other Middle Woodland works, such as those of the Hopewell people of Ohio and the Pinson Mound builders of West Tennessee. And between 1966 and 1971, five Middle Woodland settlements turned up within 20 miles, including a substantial habitation area three miles downstream. The work, researchers believe, was begun by the local McFarland culture in the first century AD and completed by the Owl Hollow culture.

## Not a Fort, but a Sacred Place

For decades almost everyone assumed the walls were built for defense. Faulkner's evidence pointed another way. Defensive works usually go up quickly in response to a threat, while this enclosure grew over several centuries. Its walls are not high enough to stop an attacker, and excavators found no trace of a palisade. Even the "moat" along the southwestern base of the peninsula, long taken as a human-made ditch, may be a natural dry riverbed. The more likely purpose was religious or ceremonial. What looks like a stronghold was probably a sacred space, shaped on this hilltop over several centuries by two successive local cultures.

## Trails, Mills and Gunpowder

When long hunters and traders arrived in the mid-18th century, well-worn trails crossed the Cumberland Plateau, linking what is now Middle Tennessee with Georgia and northern Alabama, and one passed close to the Old Stone Fort along the route of today's U.S. 41. The Nickajack Expedition under Major James Ore, late in the Cherokee-American wars, is believed to have camped inside the walls on its way to the Chickamauga towns. The rivers brought industry. In 1823 Samuel Murray built a rope factory on the Little Duck River; it burned in 1847 and was followed in 1852 by W.S. Whitman's paper mill downstream. In 1862 Whitman added a powder factory to supply the Confederacy, which Union troops destroyed the next year. From 1879 the Stone Fort Paper Company's mill near Big Falls on the Duck River supplied newspapers across the Southeast, including the Nashville Banner and the Atlanta Constitution, into the early 20th century.

## Guarded by a Family, Opened to All

The land eventually passed to the Chumbley family, who had ties to Stone Fort Paper and turned down many private offers in order to protect the ancient walls. In April 1966 the State of Tennessee bought 466 acres of their estate (some accounts say 400), which became the core of Old Stone Fort State Archaeological Park, and the site joined the National Register of Historic Places in 1973. The foundations of the old paper mill still cling to the bluffs above Big Falls, reached by the Old Stone Fort Loop Trail. Near the park entrance, a museum built by Tennessee State Parks explains the theories about the builders, the excavations and the culture of the people who made the enclosure, and an observation deck on its roof looks out over the surrounding rivers toward Blue Hole Falls.

## Flight Context

Old Stone Fort State Archaeological Park lies at 35.4828°N, 86.1078°W on the western side of Manchester, Coffee County, Tennessee, on a river peninsula along the Duck and Little Duck rivers. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet, look for the wooded peninsula framed by the two rivers, with Manchester's streets and U.S. 41 close by and the Bonnaroo festival grounds a few miles to the east. Arnold Air Force Base (KAYX), a military airfield, is about 5.5 nm to the south, so respect its airspace, and Tullahoma Regional Airport-William Northern Field (KTHA) is about 9 nm to the southwest. Morning fog often lingers along the rivers; summer brings haze and pop-up thunderstorms.
