# Bonnaroo

Named for Creole slang meaning a really good time, Bonnaroo turned a 700-acre farm in Manchester, Tennessee, into one of America's defining music festivals, drawing up to 100,000 people a year, and weathering pandemic, hurricane rains and storms along the way.

## Introduction

The name comes from New Orleans. When the founders went looking for a title for their new festival, they borrowed it from Dr. John's 1974 album Desitively Bonnaroo, choosing "bonnaroo," Creole slang for a really good time, both for its meaning and to honor New Orleans music. Since 2002 that promise has played out most Junes on a 700-acre farm in Manchester, Tennessee, now called Great Stage Park, where tens of thousands of people camp for four days of indie rock, hip hop, bluegrass, jazz, country, reggae, electronic music and nearly everything in between.

## A Festival Born of a Closed Park

Bonnaroo began with a loss. For years the Hot Summer Nights rock festival had been a mainstay of Knoxville's World's Fair Park, but in 1999 it shut down for good after construction by city officials. "The closing of the World's Fair Park for concerts precipitated getting creative," recalled Ashley Capps, co-founder of the promoter AC Entertainment, and the question became how to stay in the summer outdoor concert business at all. The answer grew in a field. That same year, the future Bonnaroo site hosted the Itchycoo Park Festival, named for the Small Faces song and considered Bonnaroo's spiritual predecessor. In 2002 Capps and Superfly Presents, led by co-founders Jonathan Mayers, Kerry Black, Rick Farman and Richard Goodstone, launched Bonnaroo with manager Coran Capshaw and agent Chip Hooper. About 70,000 people came the first year.

## What, Which, That and This

The festival's geography has a playful vocabulary. The main venue area is Centeroo, the campgrounds are Outeroo, and the stage names sound like a riddle: the What Stage, where headliners play to as many as 80,000 fans, the Which Stage, That Tent, This Tent and The Other Stage, which began as The Other Tent and grew into a full stage as electronic dance music took off. Since 2018 the Where in the Woods stage has offered late-night electronic sets out among the campsites. Music runs from Wednesday evening to Sunday night, usually beginning around the second Thursday in June. Between sets there are parades, dance shows, yoga, sustainability lessons, a 5K run, vendor markets, a water slide and even a marriage barn. The festival once also ran a dedicated tent for comedy and cinema, removed in 2008.

## Nights People Still Talk About

Some Bonnaroo sets became part of music history. In 2009 the Beastie Boys played their final show here. On June 9, 2012, R&B singer D'Angelo returned to an American stage for the first time in more than 12 years with a surprise appearance at the annual Superjam, backed by Questlove, James Poyser and Pino Palladino of the Soulquarians, with guest guitarist Jesse Johnson, and playing a set made up almost entirely of covers. That same year attendance hit its recorded peak of 100,000. Critics kept pace with the crowds. Rolling Stone named Bonnaroo one of the "50 Moments That Changed Rock & Roll" in 2003 and called it "the ultimate over-the-top summer festival" in 2008, while The New York Times wrote in 2012 that "Bonnaroo has revolutionized the modern rock festival." With its camping, sense of community and big farm setting, it has been called the Glastonbury Festival of the United States.

## Rain, Money and a Year of Rest

The festival matters well beyond its gates. In 2023 Bonnaroo contributed an estimated $339.8 million to the regional economy, including more than $5.1 million in tax revenue, and supported 4,163 full-time job equivalents, while its Bonnaroo Works Fund, created in 2009, gives grants to local nonprofits and schools. It has pushed recycling too: in 2004 organizers said they avoided collecting 120 tons of trash by encouraging fans to recycle, and in 2011 A Greener Festival recognized its efforts. Ownership shifted in 2019, when Live Nation bought out Superfly's share. Then the weather and the world intervened. The COVID-19 pandemic cancelled the 2020 edition, torrential rain tied to the remnants of Hurricane Ida flooded the grounds and cancelled 2021 days before it was to begin, and in 2025 a forecast of severe weather and steady rain ended the festival the day after its Thursday opening. Co-founder Jonathan Mayers of Superfly died in June 2025, at 51. The 2026 festival, held June 11-14 and again marked by heavy rain, reportedly drew about 45,000 people, and that August organizers announced that Bonnaroo would take "a much-needed year off" in 2027.

## Flight Context

Great Stage Park, the Bonnaroo site, lies at 35.4778°N, 86.0506°W in Manchester, Coffee County, Tennessee. From 2,500 to 3,500 feet, look for the broad open farm fields and camping grounds on the edge of Manchester beside Interstate 24; in festival week the field fills with tents, vehicles and stage structures. Arnold Air Force Base (KAYX), a military airfield, is only about 5 nm to the south-southwest, so respect its airspace, and festival weekends may bring temporary flight restrictions, so check NOTAMs. Tullahoma Regional Airport-William Northern Field (KTHA) is about 11 nm to the southwest. June brings heat, haze and pop-up thunderstorms to Middle Tennessee.
