# Arnold Engineering Development Complex

Inspired by a giant wind tunnel found in Austria, the Air Force built in Tennessee one of the world's largest complexes for simulating flight, from sea level to 300 miles up and up to Mach 14.

**Location:** 35.3790, -86.0500

**Categories:** Centers of the United States Air Force, Aeronautics organizations, Military units and formations established in 1950, Superfund sites in Tennessee, Buildings and structures in Coffee County, Tennessee

## Introduction

In 1945, Frank Wattendorf visited a huge wind tunnel at Ötztal, Austria, and found a machine bigger and more powerful than any tunnel in the United States. That visit set an idea in motion. Theodore von Kármán, advising General Henry "Hap" Arnold, called for a new American center for the study and development of jet propulsion, supersonic aircraft and ballistic missiles, one he hoped "would be greater than any then known." The result rose on the site of a former Army camp in Tennessee, and it bears Arnold's name: the Arnold Engineering Development Complex.

## From a Scientist's Report to an Act of Congress

Von Kármán's proposal found a champion in Stuart Symington, who in 1947 became the first secretary of the newly independent Air Force. As von Kármán later recalled, Symington rounded up enough support in Congress to obtain about $100 million, and a committee traveled the country seeking a site. One requirement was a direct source of stored hydraulic power, as planned at Ötztal, to run the multi-thousand-horsepower motors the big tunnels would need. The 81st Congress authorized the Air Engineering Development Center through Public Law 415, approved on October 27, 1949. On March 7, 1950, it was renamed the Arnold Engineering Development Center, effective that February, by a general order signed by Air Force Chief of Staff General Hoyt S. Vandenberg. It became a "Complex" rather than a "Center" on July 6, 2012.

## From Sea Level to Three Hundred Miles Up

What happens here is the art of pretending to fly. AEDC's facilities can simulate flight conditions from sea level to 300 miles of altitude and from subsonic speeds up to Mach 14. By the complex's published figures, it operates more than 68 test facilities, among them aerodynamic and propulsion wind tunnels, rocket and turbine engine test cells, environmental and space chambers, arc heaters, ballistic ranges, sled tracks and centrifuges. Twenty-seven of its test units have had capabilities unmatched elsewhere in the United States, and 14 have been unique in the world. Older figures put the replacement value of its facilities at more than $7.8 billion, and a 2015 Air Force release said more than $11.6 billion; either way it ranks among the most advanced and largest complexes of flight simulation test facilities anywhere. Its purpose is to put aircraft, missiles and space systems through the conditions they will face on real missions, helping engineers improve designs and establish performance before production.

## Built on an Old Army Camp

The headquarters sits at Arnold Air Force Base, near Manchester and Tullahoma, Tennessee, on much of the site of the former Camp Forrest, a U.S. Army base that also served as a prisoner-of-war camp during World War II. The installation was dedicated in Arnold's honor on June 25, 1951. Unusually for a military facility, most of the workforce are contract personnel, with only a small contingent of active-duty members. Next door stands the University of Tennessee Space Institute. The complex's reach extends beyond Tennessee, with units at sites including NASA's Ames Research Center in California, the Hypervelocity Wind Tunnel 9 in Maryland and, after a reorganization that took effect on December 1, 2016, the McKinley Climatic Laboratory at Eglin Air Force Base in Florida. Its customers include the Air Force, Navy, Army, Missile Defense Agency and NASA.

## Engines of the Local Economy

AEDC has contributed to the development of practically every one of the nation's top-priority aerospace programs, from spacelift to aircraft, missiles and satellites. That work also reaches into the surrounding towns. An Air Force release put its economic impact on the local area at $609.3 million for fiscal year 2015, and the Air Force later reported an impact above $1 billion for the base in fiscal year 2025, counting the payroll, the secondary jobs created as that payroll was spent locally, and spending on supplies, utilities, fuel and services. Beyond testing, AEDC runs a research and technology program to develop new testing techniques and instrumentation, and it works continually to maintain and modernize its existing facilities so they keep pace with rapidly advancing aircraft, missile and space systems.

## The Cost Left in the Ground

The land carries a less celebrated legacy. Several areas of the facility are contaminated with polychlorinated biphenyls, volatile organic compounds and spills of jet and rocket fuel, chlorofluorocarbon solvents, nitric acid and other materials. PCBs from the site have been detected in local creeks, in the water, in sediment and in fish. The site was proposed for the Superfund National Priorities List in August 1994, but the EPA removed it from consideration for the list in March 2012, and it has never been added. As of 2010 the Environmental Protection Agency believed that human exposure to the contaminants and the migration of contaminated groundwater were under control, but the pollution remains part of the complex's legacy alongside its engineering achievements.

## Flight Context

The Arnold Engineering Development Complex lies at approximately 35.379°N, 86.050°W, at Arnold Air Force Base between Manchester and Tullahoma in Middle Tennessee. From 4,000–6,000 feet MSL, the Arnold AFB airfield (KAYX) lies about 2 miles from the complex's coordinates. This is a military installation, so check charts and NOTAMs for any restricted airspace and avoid low passes over the test areas. Civil airports nearby include Tullahoma Regional Airport–William Northern Field (KTHA), about 11 miles, and Winchester Municipal Airport (KBGF), about 14 miles. Expect summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms.
