# Carter House (Franklin, Tennessee)

The Carter family sheltered in their basement as the Battle of Franklin raged around their brick farmhouse in 1864, and one of their sons, returning home for the first time in three years, was carried back to the house mortally wounded.

## Introduction

Tod Carter had not been home in more than three years. When he finally returned to Franklin, Tennessee, it was as a soldier in the 20th Tennessee Infantry, a Confederate regiment, fighting in a battle that broke across his own family's farm. Federal troops had thrown up breastworks just south of the Carter house, and while the fighting raged, his family waited it out in the basement. The house on Columbia Avenue still stands, and so does the room where he died.

## A Farmhouse on the Pike

Fountain Branch Carter finished building the house in 1830. It was a Federal-style brick farmhouse, surrounded by the working buildings a farm family needed: a farm office, a smokehouse and a kitchen. In the 1850s Carter added a cotton gin to the property. For more than three decades it was simply a family farm in Franklin, at what is now 1140 Columbia Avenue. Nothing about its brick walls or outbuildings hinted that the Carter name would become bound up with a Civil War battle, or that a cotton gin would turn into one of that battle's most remembered landmarks.

## Breastworks in the Yard

On November 30, 1864, the Second Battle of Franklin arrived at the Carters' door. Federal breastworks ran just south of the home, stretching both east and west of the Columbia Pike, which placed the farm on the battle line. The cotton gin Fountain Carter had built in the 1850s became a much-remembered landmark of the fighting. Inside, the family took refuge in the basement, waiting for the battle to end while it was fought over the ground around them. The house's story is not only one of combat but of a family sheltering beneath its floor while the fighting went on outside.

## The Middle Son

Tod Carter was the middle son of Fountain Branch Carter. He was mortally wounded in the battle. The next morning he was found still alive and brought to the house, where his distraught family gathered around him. He died of his wounds on December 2, 1864, and was buried in Rest Haven Cemetery, just north of town. His homecoming, after more than three years away, had lasted only as long as his dying. Today the room where Tod Carter died is one of the highlights of the guided tour, and visitors can stand in the room where his family kept watch over him.

## Saved From Demolition

In the twentieth century the house faced a threat of its own. In 1951 the State of Tennessee bought it to save it from demolition, and it opened to the public in 1953; it is now one of the Tennessee Historical Commission's State Historic Sites. It is managed by the Battle of Franklin Trust, a nonprofit that also oversees nearby Carnton Plantation. The Carter House is a contributing property and the centerpiece of the Franklin Battlefield, a National Historic Landmark district designated in 1960 that also takes in sites such as Fort Granger, with the Lotz House across the street and Carnton Plantation nearby. Guided tours of the house run daily, and the state broke ground in March 2025 on the Herbert Harper Visitor Center, a new visitor center and museum planned to open in 2026.
