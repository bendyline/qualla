# Rattle and Snap

Named, the story goes, for a game of chance that won the land, this 1845 Greek Revival mansion near Mount Pleasant, Tennessee, was built by enslaved labor for George Washington Polk and is now a National Historic Landmark.

## Introduction

The land, the story goes, was won on a gamble. According to a tale attached to this Maury County estate, the ground beneath it was won from the Governor of North Carolina in a game of chance called "Rattle and Snap," and the game gave the place its name. What rose on that ground in the mid-1840s was no game at all: a mansion counted among the grandest expressions of Greek Revival architecture in Tennessee, raised by enslaved people, reportedly spared by a Union officer, and designated a National Historic Landmark in 1971.

## A Polk House Built by Enslaved Hands

George Washington Polk built the house in 1845. He was one of the sons of Colonel William Polk, a North Carolina native and Revolutionary War officer who had been appointed surveyor-general of the Middle Tennessee district in the 1780s, and a relative of President James K. Polk. The plantation originally covered 5,648 acres, and the mansion at its heart, described as the largest and most extravagant in Maury County, was built with the forced labor of enslaved people. The gamble and the portrait make for good stories, but any honest account of Rattle and Snap has to begin with the people who built it as much as with the family whose name it carries.

## A Temple Front on the Highway

The mansion is a large two-story brick building under a shallow hip roof, laid out in an L-shape with a stepped two-story colonnade. Its showpiece is a pedimented Greek temple portico carried on four columns, and inside, rich woodwork and plasterwork in the Greek Revival style survive. The estate stands on the south side of Andrew Jackson Highway (Tennessee State Route 243), roughly midway between the centers of Mount Pleasant and Columbia, at the eastern edge of Mount Pleasant's city limits; its address is 1522 North Main Street. Although it is far smaller than the original plantation, the estate still covers more than 500 acres.

## The Ring in the Portrait

During the Civil War, Union soldiers looted or burned many plantations and mansions across the South. Rattle and Snap survived, and the explanation handed down is a curious one. The senior Union officer, a Freemason, is said to have noticed the Masonic ring on George Polk's finger in his formal portrait and refused to damage the home of a fellow Freemason. Whatever the truth of the story, the house survived the war. The Polk fortune did not. After the war the family went bankrupt and could not afford to keep the land or the mansion, and in 1867 they sold Rattle and Snap to Joseph Granberry. The Granberrys called it "Oakwood Hall" and lived there for more than fifty years, keeping the property until about 1920, and their name lives on in the house's other title, the Polk-Granberry House. In 1971 the mansion was designated a National Historic Landmark for its architecture and its association with the Polk family. It remains privately owned, but may be viewed by appointment.
