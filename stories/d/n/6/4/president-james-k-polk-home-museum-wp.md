# President James K. Polk Home & Museum

Built by his father in 1816, the brick house in Columbia, Tennessee, is the only surviving private residence of President James K. Polk, and it holds the belongings his widow guarded for four decades and her family carried here.

**Location:** 35.6149, -87.0373

**Categories:** James K. Polk, Presidential homes in the United States, National Historic Landmarks in Tennessee, Historic house museums in Tennessee, Houses in Columbia, Tennessee

## Introduction

Of all the places James K. Polk called home, only one private residence still stands, and it is the house his father built. Samuel Polk raised the two-story brick house in Columbia, Tennessee, in 1816, and his son lived in it on and off as a young man until 1824. From here the future 11th president left to read law in Nashville under Felix Grundy, and to Columbia he returned to open his own law practice. Today the house, just west of the town's commercial center at the corner of West 7th and South High streets, is the President James K. Polk Home & Museum, the president's primary historic site and a National Historic Landmark since 1961.

## Brick, Keystones and a Fanlight

The house is L-shaped, two stories tall, with a gabled roof. Its front, facing West 7th Street, is three bays wide, and the main entrance sits in the rightmost bay, recessed within a segmented arch. Sidelights flank the door, a semi-oval transom with delicate tracery crowns it, and the walls of the recess are paneled. The windows in the other bays wear brick lintels set with stone keystones. Inside, the rooms keep finishes from the period of construction, though they have been adapted for museum displays. Out back stands a reproduction of the kitchen outbuilding that would have served the household in Polk's day. None of the original outbuildings from Polk's time survive. The house is also listed on the National Register of Historic Places, and it is open daily, except on select holidays, for guided tours.

## What the Widow Kept

Many of the objects here came by way of Polk's widow. After the president's death in 1849, Sarah Polk lived on at Polk Place, their home in Nashville, for 42 years until her own death in 1891. Early in her widowhood she took in a great-niece, Sallie, who spent a large part of her life with Sarah in Nashville and was married in the parlor of Polk Place to a Nashville merchant, George Fall. Sarah left her the contents of Polk Place, along with the president's personal belongings. In the early 1900s Sallie opened her own Nashville home so the public could come and see them. With her daughter, Saidee Grant, she founded the James K. Polk Association in 1924, and in 1929 Mrs. Grant and the state of Tennessee bought the Columbia house and moved the inherited contents of Polk Place into it.

## Pieces of a Lost House

Some of Polk Place itself made the journey too. Along with the furnishings and belongings, an iron fountain, garden urns and a gate were preserved from the Nashville property and now stand at the museum in Columbia. The president himself rests elsewhere. His tomb first stood on the front lawn of Polk Place until 1893, when it was moved to the grounds of the Tennessee State Capitol in Nashville. Where the Polks should rest has also reached the statehouse. In 2018 a resolution to relocate James and Sarah Polk's remains to Columbia failed in the House on March 19, returned to the floor through the Calendar and Rules Committee, and passed on April 9 by a vote of 51 to 47. Governor Bill Haslam allowed it to take effect without his signature, sending it on to the Tennessee Historical Commission, the Capitol Grounds Commission and a chancery judge. The museum's website still describes both Polks as buried on the Capitol grounds in Nashville.

## Flight Context

The Polk Home stands at 35.6149°N, 87.0373°W in Columbia, Maury County, Tennessee, just west of the downtown commercial district. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet, look for the compact downtown grid of Columbia near the Duck River; the house is a small brick building on a street corner and is hard to pick out without a street map. Maury County Airport (KMRC) is about 8 nm to the west-southwest, and Ellington Airport (KLUG) at Lewisburg is about 13 nm to the east-southeast. Middle Tennessee summers bring haze and afternoon thunderstorms; fall and spring usually offer clearer air.
