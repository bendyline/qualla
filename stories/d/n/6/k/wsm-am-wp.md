# WSM (AM)

From a diamond-shaped 1932 tower beside Interstate 65 in Brentwood, WSM's 50,000-watt clear-channel signal carried the Grand Ole Opry and country music across North America.

**Location:** 35.9982, -86.7909

**Categories:** Grand Ole Opry, Clear-channel radio stations, Country radio stations in Tennessee, Radio stations in Nashville, Tennessee, Radio stations established in 1925

## Introduction

Drivers on Interstate 65 just south of Nashville pass a strange silhouette near the Concord Road exit in Brentwood: a diamond-shaped mast climbing 808 feet into the sky. Built by Blaw-Knox in 1932, it is one of the oldest operating broadcast towers in the United States, and it belongs to WSM, 650 on the AM dial. By day its signal reaches from Chattanooga to Evansville, Indiana. At night its clear-channel signal carries across much of North America. Through that tower, for generations, a nation of late-night listeners learned what Nashville sounded like.

## We Shield Millions

WSM began as a sales tool. The National Life and Accident Insurance Company founded it as a platform to sell insurance, and the call letters came straight from the company motto, "We Shield Millions." The station first signed on October 5, 1925, from the company's building at Seventh Avenue and Union Street in downtown Nashville. Its first program director was George D. Hay, a newspaper reporter from Memphis. Nicknamed "The Air Castle of the South," the station in 1932 boosted its power to 50,000 watts, a level it still broadcasts around the clock. For decades WSM, its television sister WSM-TV and the Grand Ole Opry were all owned by the same Nashville insurance company.

## From Grand Opera to Grand Ole Opry

Less than two months after sign-on, on November 28, 1925, WSM launched the WSM Barn Dance, with Uncle Jimmy Thompson as its first performer. The show got its lasting name on December 10, 1927, when Hay followed a network program from the NBC Red Network with a jab: "For the past hour we have been listening to music largely from Grand Opera, but from now on we will present 'The Grand Ole Opry.'" The program lived in the insurance building until 1934 and grew into the longest-running radio program in history. Country music became WSM's nighttime identity even when daytime hours went to middle-of-the-road music; not until 1980 did the station adopt its 24-hour country format. From 1947, Ernest Tubb hosted the Midnite Jamboree from his record shop after each Opry, a tradition that outlived him by decades, through the Broadway shop's 2022 closure and a change of owners, until the show was removed from WSM's schedule in 2026.

## The Diamond on Concord Road

When the Blaw-Knox tower went up in 1932 it stood 878 feet tall, then the tallest tower in the United States. It was too tall. Engineers discovered that the extra height was causing the signal to cancel itself out in the fringes of the station's reception area, so in 1939 the mast was shortened to 808 feet, close to what is now known to be the optimum height for a station of its class on that frequency. During World War II it was designated to transmit to submarines if ship-to-shore communications were lost. The shape has become a tribute in its own right: the diamond design was worked into the new Country Music Hall of Fame and Museum in 2001, and the tower was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on March 15, 2011. Still mounted atop the mast is the antenna of WSM's first FM station, launched in 1941 and reported to be the first commercial FM station to be fully licensed.

## Voices After Dark

The people behind the microphone became part of the legend. Ralph Emery held the overnight shift from the late 1950s until the early 1970s, and because of that time slot, listeners all over the United States heard him spin country records; alongside the Opry, it cemented WSM's central role in the music's history. Grant Turner, the "dean of the Opry announcers," had a nearly 50-year association with the station, and his early-morning show, Opryland USA, was so popular that its title became the name of the theme park built in 1972. Keith Bilbrey worked every time slot at WSM over a 35-year career. Some voices went on to fame elsewhere. Pat Sajak, later the host of Wheel of Fortune, was WSM's afternoon disc jockey in the mid-1970s, and Larry Munson, who called Nashville Vols and Vanderbilt games in the 1950s and 1960s, became renowned as the voice of Georgia Bulldogs football.

## Keeping the Format

Twice the station's future hung in the balance. In 2001, management sought to convert WSM to all-sports; when word leaked, longtime Opry personalities and country singers joined protests outside the studios, and the classic country format stayed. In 2003, Cumulus Media bought WSM's sister stations and intended to buy WSM as well, but owner Gaylord decided at the eleventh hour to keep it. Gaylord was renamed Ryman Hospitality Properties in 2012, and today the station is owned by Opry Entertainment Group, a majority-owned Ryman subsidiary. The May 2010 flood destroyed the station's administrative offices beside the Grand Ole Opry House, taking several priceless documents from its history. After decades in a studio inside the Gaylord Opryland Resort, WSM moved in 2024 to a showcase studio inside the former home of Roy Acuff, next to the Opry House. The Opry still goes out on 650.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 35.9982°N, 86.7909°W mark the WSM transmitter site in Brentwood, beside Interstate 65 near exit 71 (Concord Road / State Highway 253), south of downtown Nashville. The diamond-shaped Blaw-Knox mast rises 808 feet above the ground, so treat it as an obstruction: keep at least 2,000 feet above ground level when overflying, and a 2,500-3,500 foot MSL pass offset to one side gives a good view of the tower against the I-65 corridor. Nashville International (KBNA) is about 9 nm to the northeast, so expect to work Nashville approach; John C. Tune (KJWN) is about 12 nm north-northwest and Smyrna (KMQY) about 13 nm east. Summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are common in Middle Tennessee.
