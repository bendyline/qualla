# Castalian Springs Mound Site

A Mississippian town of platform mounds, once ringed by earthen walls, near Bledsoe's Lick, Tennessee, has yielded stone statues and engraved shell gorgets, including a Falcon dancer warrior that was meant to be worn sideways.

**Location:** 36.3986, -86.3135

**Categories:** Middle Mississippian culture, Mounds in Tennessee, Archaeological sites in Tennessee, Geography of Sumner County, Tennessee

## Introduction

Hold the gorget upright and you see a warrior with a ceremonial mace in his left hand and a severed head in his right, his eyes ringed by the forked markings of the Falcon dancer. But the holes drilled along its edge say otherwise. Hung from a cord as its maker intended, the engraved shell would have shown the figure lying on its side, and no one yet knows why. It came from Castalian Springs in Sumner County, Tennessee, where a Mississippian town, probably enclosed by a wooden palisade, stood near the mineral springs of Bledsoe's Lick. It was occupied from about 1100 to 1350 CE, with its main ceremonial use from about 1200.

## A Town Behind Earthen Walls

When Ralph E.W. Earl dug here in the early 1820s, the town's outline was still plain to see. He described the remains of a low earthen embankment enclosing about 16 acres, which archaeologists now think supported a wooden palisade, perhaps with raised earthen towers. Inside rose the principal mound, Mound 2, a compound structure: a large level-topped platform about 60 yards (roughly 55 meters) across and 13 to 15 feet high, with a smaller mound on top that Judge John Haywood, writing in 1823, put at about 18 feet above the ground. A plaza opened beside it, ringed by other platform mounds, among them Mound 1, a circular platform about 26 meters (85 feet) across. Outside the palisaded precinct, just north of Lick Creek, stood a stone mound roughly 60 feet in diameter. Altogether the town's central precinct covered about 40 acres, with about a dozen platform mounds, a plaza, dwellings and civic buildings.

## Plows, Pits and a Smithsonian Man

Much of what Earl saw is gone. In the two centuries since, Euro-American farmers plowed the ground, and today the main platform mound and a few raised impressions are all that remain visible of the embankment and its dozen or so platform mounds. The digging began early, too. The first of several Mississippian stone statues came out of the ground sometime before 1823, and others followed, including one believed to have come from the platform of the main mound and several from a village area. In the early 1890s, and again in 1916 and 1917, the amateur archaeologist William E. Myer, later a "special archeologist" with the Smithsonian, excavated parts of the site. He opened one of the principal mounds and a number of stone box graves, and recovered artifacts bearing the imagery of what archaeologists call the Southeastern Ceremonial Complex, among them many engraved shell gorgets that the Museum of the American Indian acquired in 1926.

## The Falcon Dancer, Turned Sideways

The most important of those gorgets is carved in the Eddyville or Braden style, a style believed to be associated with the Cahokia polity near Collinsville, Illinois. Its warrior wears the Forked Eye Surround, the bellows-shaped apron and the Bi-Lobed Arrow, motifs all linked to the ceremonial complex's Falcon dancer. The design is often shown upright, yet the suspension holes make clear he was meant to be seen sideways, a choice whose meaning remains unresolved. The same cache held two Cox style and two Nashville I style gorgets, and several of the site's gorgets are now in the collections of the National Museum of the American Indian. The ground has not finished giving up its art. In 2005 a crew replacing a waterline along State Route 25 turned up an intact Cox style gorget carved not from marine shell but from dark gray shale, one of very few Cox style designs known on any other material.

## A Tablet from Rocky Creek

One of the region's most famous engraved stones surfaced a short distance away. In 1878, on the banks of Rocky Creek in what is now Trousdale County, a tablet was found measuring 19 inches wide, 14 inches tall and an inch thick, both faces crowded with figures dressed in the regalia of the ceremonial complex. It carries the name of Gates P. Thruston, a Nashville lawyer turned avocational archaeologist who excavated many sites around Nashville and built an extensive collection of artifacts. Yet Thruston neither discovered the Thruston tablet nor ever owned it. He championed it instead, writing about it in 1890 while the stone was held by the Tennessee Historical Society. Today it belongs to the Tennessee State Museum in Nashville.

## Digging to Understand

Tennessee bought the site in 2005, and a different kind of excavation began. Middle Tennessee State University took up the work, and Dr. Kevin E. Smith ran an archaeological dig school in the village from 2005 through 2011. The Castalian Springs Archaeological Project, sponsored by MTSU's Department of Sociology and Anthropology, the Bledsoe's Lick Historical Association and the Tennessee Division of Archaeology, set out to understand when and how the mound center was used, how its mineral springs were used over time, to train students in professional field and laboratory methods, and to show the public why such research matters. The land itself holds more. This is karst country, pocked with small caves, and archaeologists have found stone box graves and mortuary caves scattered through the area. A few hundred yards west lies a little cave known locally as the "Cave of the Skulls," which Myer explored during one of his three excavations.

## Flight Context

The Castalian Springs Mound Site lies at 36.3986°N, 86.3135°W in Castalian Springs, Sumner County, Tennessee, immediately east of Bledsoe's Fort Historical Park and beside Tennessee State Route 25 (Hartsville Pike). Plowing has flattened most of the earthworks, so from the air the main platform mound is the feature to look for; a low pass at 1,500–2,000 feet AGL gives the best chance of reading its long east–west footprint. Old Hickory Lake, the Cumberland River impoundment, spreads about a mile to the south and makes the easiest reference, with Gallatin to the west and Hartsville to the east. Music City Executive Airport in Gallatin (KXNX) is about 5 nm west; Lebanon Municipal Airport (FAA identifier M54, no ICAO code) is about 12.5 nm south, and Nashville International (KBNA) is roughly 24 nm southwest. The site is state-owned and not open to the public without permission, so view it from the air only.
