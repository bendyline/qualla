# Bledsoe's Station

On a hill above an ancient buffalo lick in Sumner County, Isaac Bledsoe's frontier fort sheltered settlers, hosted a future king of France and cost the Bledsoe family dearly in the long war for the Cumberland.

**Location:** 36.3994, -86.3206

**Categories:** Forts in Tennessee, Pre-statehood history of Tennessee, Archaeological sites on the National Register of Historic Places in Tennessee, Protected areas of Sumner County, Tennessee, Parks in Tennessee

## Introduction

For the winter of 1778, Thomas "Bigfoot" Spencer made his home inside a tree. The longhunter sheltered in a hollowed-out sycamore just south of the mineral springs at Bledsoe's Lick, and he spent much of that year building cabins nearby, believing, mistakenly, that a cabin on a tract of land would give him a claim to it. Spencer's scheme failed, but his instinct about the place was sound. Within a few years Isaac Bledsoe raised a fort on the hill above those springs, and Bledsoe's Station became one of the frontier outposts through which the first major wave of Euro-American settlers poured into Middle Tennessee after the American Revolution.

## Where the Buffalo Came to Lick

Long before any fort, the springs drew animals. The natural mineral lick attracted buffalo and other large game, and the game drew hunters. Native Americans hunted around Bledsoe's Lick for thousands of years, and they camped here on and off through the Archaic and Woodland periods. During the Mississippian period a civic-ceremonial center of roughly 40 acres, with about a dozen platform mounds, grew up a few hundred yards from the springs, at what is now the Castalian Springs Mound Site. By the mid-18th century, the Cherokee, Chickasaw and Creek all claimed the area as a hunting ground. Then came the longhunters, named for expeditions that stretched on for months. Isaac Bledsoe traveled with Kasper Mansker's 1771 party, which gathered several thousand skins, and the two men led a third expedition in 1772. On that trip Bledsoe followed the ancient buffalo paths to the creek and salt lick that still carry his name.

## A Fort Between Two Creeks

The Bledsoe brothers were Virginians, born in the early 1730s, and both had served in the French and Indian War. Anthony, the elder, had commanded Fort Patrick Henry at Long Island of the Holston and won election to the Virginia House of Delegates; Isaac had joined William Christian's 1776 punitive expedition against the Cherokee. In 1779 North Carolina sent Anthony to lead a surveying party into Middle Tennessee, and Isaac followed in 1781. He probably began the station soon after arriving, though he did not move his family inside until 1783. The ground was well chosen: a blunt hill between Bledsoe Creek on the west and Bledsoe Lick Creek on the east, both running south toward the Cumberland River, with the spring flowing at the hill's foot a few hundred yards away. Anthony built a fort of his own at Greenfield, two and a half miles to the north.

## The Long War for the Cumberland

The Chickamauga Cherokee, at war with the United States for most of the years since 1776, opposed the land concessions that would let settlers stay in Middle Tennessee for good, and the Upper Cumberland became contested ground. Hugh Rogan was nearly killed in an ambush near present-day Cragfont in 1782. Spencer survived an attack on his hunting party at Drake's Creek in 1784, only to die in a later ambush near Crab Orchard. In 1786 Anthony Bledsoe wrote to North Carolina governor Richard Caswell that 14 settlers had been killed that year, seeking permission to strike back. A schoolmaster named George Hamilton was shot and badly wounded at the station in 1787. Anthony moved his family in from Greenfield for safety, yet in 1788, when attackers hidden outside fired on the fort, he ran out into an exposed passageway and was mortally wounded. Isaac was shot and killed in 1793 while walking through a field near the fort, and in 1794 two of Anthony's sons and a nephew died in ambushes. That year the Treaty of Tellico ended the Cherokee–American wars.

## A Future King on Avery's Trace

In 1788 the station gained a road. Avery's Trace, the first major route linking the Upper Cumberland with settlements to the east, ran from Fort Southwest Point to Nashville and passed a few hundred feet east of the fort. Travelers in the 1790s included the French botanist André Michaux and Louis-Philippe, Duke of Orléans, who would later become king of France. General James Winchester, later instrumental in the founding of Memphis, bought the station in 1797 and began building his home, Cragfont, about a mile west the following year; it was completed in 1802. Nearby, Wynnewood, built in 1828 by A. R. Wynne and partners as a stagecoach inn, is the largest extant log structure in Tennessee. The Winchester family also renamed Bledsoe's Lick "Castalian Springs," after the spring of Greek mythology near Delphi, and the place thrived on and off as a health resort until 1914.

## Trenches, a Cabin and the Cavern of the Skulls

Nothing of Bledsoe's walls stands today, but excavations have confirmed the site, and the fort's outline shows in old trenches on the hillside. Sumner County residents and Bledsoe descendants created Bledsoe's Fort Historical Park in 1989. Nathaniel Parker's late-18th-century log cabin was dismantled and moved here from a few miles north; Parker married Anthony's widow, Mary Ramsey Bledsoe, and commanded the fort at Greenfield. A few hundred yards southeast, beside a stretch of Avery's Trace, the Belote Cemetery holds early settlers beneath a 15-foot obelisk the Bledsoe family raised in 1908 to honor Isaac and Anthony. Near the old lick, Middle Tennessee State University built a lean-to modeled on 18th-century longhunter shelters. East of the fort, a cave with a mouth roughly five by ten feet, closed to the public, is called the Cavern of the Skulls; 19th-century explorers reported human skulls inside, possibly trophies kept by the Mississippian villagers nearby. And the hilltop has been used by radio-controlled airplane flyers.

## Flight Context

Bledsoe's Station sits at 36.3994°N, 86.3206°W on a hill in Castalian Springs, Tennessee, roughly halfway between Gallatin and Hartsville and about 35 miles northeast of Nashville. From 1,500–2,500 feet AGL, look for the broad arms of Old Hickory Lake (the Cumberland River impoundment) just over a mile to the south, with Bledsoe Creek and Bledsoe Lick Creek running down to it on either side of the park, and Tennessee State Route 25 (Hartsville Pike) along the park's southern edge. The Castalian Springs Mound Site and Wynnewood lie immediately east, Cragfont immediately west. The hilltop has been used by radio-controlled model aircraft flyers, so stay well above it. Nearest airport is Music City Executive Airport in Gallatin (KXNX), about 4.5 nm west-southwest; Lebanon Municipal Airport (FAA identifier M54, no ICAO code) is about 12.5 nm south, and Nashville International (KBNA) is about 24 nm southwest. Summer haze over the lake can soften visibility on humid afternoons.
