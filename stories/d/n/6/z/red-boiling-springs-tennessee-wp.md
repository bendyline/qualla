# Red Boiling Springs, Tennessee

A small Macon County town built on sulphur springs with names like Red, Black and "Double and Twist" once drew more than 14,000 summer visitors to its verandaed hotels, and still keeps its old spa hotels, its hand pumps and the memory of a 1969 flood.

**Location:** 36.5325, -85.8497

**Categories:** Cities in Macon County, Tennessee, Spa towns in the United States, Mineral springs, Floods in Tennessee, Historic hotels

## Introduction

Some of the springs in Red Boiling Springs were named for the color they turned a silver coin. Others earned their names more bluntly. The most heavily mineralized water in town, sold as the "only water of its kind in the United States," was called "Double and Twist," after what it did to the person who drank it. In a Tennessee hamlet of roughly 800 people in the 1930s, these waters once drew crowds numbering in the thousands, filling hotel after hotel with judges, musicians, politicians and a president, all come to drink and bathe in water whose sulphur gave it the scent of rotten eggs.

## Salt Lick Creek

Before there were hotels, there was salt. A salt lick about four miles northwest of today's town drew animals, American Indians and other people, and the area was first known as Salt Lick Creek. Daniel Boone came to hunt the animal trails, and he reportedly carved his name and the year 1775 into a beech tree in a nearby community. Land grants came in the mid-1780s, a Salt Lick Creek post office opened in 1829, and in 1847 it was renamed Red Boiling Springs. One early champion of the water was "Aunt" Sooky Goad, who said she had been cured of dropsy by drinking it and claimed to be the first to discover its benefits. She oversaw the making of Aunt Sooky's Salve, a widely distributed local product. A Nashville man's 1914 article credited her brother, Shepherd Kirby, with finding that the water eased eye irritation, but Goad's use of it seems to have come first.

## Five Waters, Many Cures

Almost uniquely, five different kinds of mineral water rise here, mineralized by contact with exposed black shale that dissolves iron sulfate into them. "Red" and "Black" were capped and piped to hand-pumped wells around town. Red was only somewhat sulfurous and tolerable to many; Black was so strong it was an acquired taste at best. "White" was used only for dyspepsia, and "Freestone" held none of the prized minerals but was by far the most pleasant to drink. Taking the waters meant more than drinking them. Bathhouses offered steam and tub baths following the hydrotherapy regimen John Harvey Kellogg had developed at his Battle Creek Sanitarium, and doctors on hand prescribed treatments by ailment. The waters were touted as cures for a long list of ailments, from rheumatism and diabetes to kidney stones and skin disease.

## Verandas and a Palace

A stagecoach line to the railroad at Gallatin opened in 1873, and by 1876 store owner James Bennett had bought the springs and built a hotel of log cabins around a frame dining hall. New York businessman James F. O. Shaughnesy developed a resort in the 1880s, and in 1906 a new company bought the original springs tract and by 1916 had replaced his hotel with a lavish 64-room building called the Palace. Four hotels in 1918 doubled to eight a decade later, and more followed, all built to a similar plan of two stories, elegant verandas and large dining halls. Guests included Secretary of State Cordell Hull, who sent a French tapestry for the Donoho Hotel's lobby, and President Woodrow Wilson. Evenings meant dancing to hotel orchestras, while string bands played the taverns on the edge of town. Even in the Depression the crowds came: the summer of 1936 brought more than 14,000 visitors.

## Six Hours of Rain

The boom faded. Americans lost faith in mineral cures, new highways carried travelers to state parks and the Smokies, hotels burned and were not rebuilt, and wartime gasoline rationing made the remote town harder still to reach. The community incorporated in 1953, and on June 23, 1969, disaster came. Rain began around 3:30 in the morning, and within hours the whole Salt Lick Valley was under water; a gauge in town recorded nearly 8 inches in five hours, and an unofficial estimate put the rainfall over the Salt Lick Creek watershed near 10 inches. Houses and cars floated through town, a Trailways bus was swept some 500 feet into a bridge, and 15 businesses and 35 homes were heavily damaged or destroyed. Two sisters, Renah Louise Bilbrey, 8, and Jennifer Rae Bilbrey, 2, were killed. Relatives and townspeople searched for them; the younger girl was found days later, four miles downstream. Two songs were recorded about the tragedy, and one drew wide airplay. Renah's class dedicated its yearbook to her.

## Pumps, Ghosts and Rubber Ducks

State and federal grants helped the town rebuild and paid for watershed dams, one of them named for the two girls, and in the early 2000s a park near their home was named in their memory. From the late 1970s the town marketed its history again on a small scale, adding covered bridges and parkland. The town, home to 1,205 people at the 2020 census, still has old hand pumps standing on private property, and some people still believe in the waters. The Armour's Hotel has kept offering steam treatments and mineral tub baths, and the Thomas House, once the Cloyd Hotel and twice rebuilt after fire, drew attention in the early 2010s on the SyFy series Ghost Hunters and is known for its ghost hunt weekends. Each June a Folk Medicine Festival fills the parks along Salt Lick Creek, and a Labor Day weekend rubber duck race down Salt Lick Creek has raised money for neighbors in need.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 36.5325°N, 85.8497°W, in the narrow Salt Lick Creek valley of northern Macon County, Tennessee, a few miles south of the Kentucky line. From the air the town is a small grid tucked into wooded hills and farmland; follow the creek valley to find it. A cruise around 3,000-3,500 feet MSL keeps you above the ridges with a good view of the valley. Nearby fields include Lafayette Municipal Airport (FAA identifier 3M7, no ICAO code) about 10 nm, Jackson County Airport at Gainesboro (FAA 1A7) about 13 nm, and Tompkinsville-Monroe County Airport, Kentucky (KTZV) about 15 nm; the nearest larger airport is Bowling Green-Warren County Regional (KBWG), about 38 nm. Valley fog is common on calm mornings, and heavy summer storms can raise the creek quickly.
