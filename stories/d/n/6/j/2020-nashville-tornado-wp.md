# 2020 Nashville tornado

In the early hours of March 3, 2020, an EF3 tornado carved a 60-mile path through Nashville and Wilson County, killing five people and causing more than $1 billion in damage.

**Location:** 36.1700, -86.9500

**Categories:** Tornadoes of 2020, Tornadoes in Tennessee, F3, EF3 and IF3 tornadoes, Nashville, Tennessee, March 2020 in the United States

## Introduction

The forecast for the night of March 2, 2020, sounded almost routine. The Storm Prediction Center, watching storms fade as they moved into drier air, noted the potential for "severe hail, strong surface gusts and perhaps potential for a tornado or two" in an area stretching from eastern Arkansas into West Tennessee and southwestern Kentucky. The threat to Middle Tennessee had not been forecast well in advance, and a localized tornado watch went up only at 11:20 p.m. Seventy-two minutes later, a tornado touched down west of Nashville. By the time it lifted, just over an hour later, it had traveled some 60 miles, killed five people and caused damage that would total more than $1 billion.

## Across the River, Again and Again

The tornado first touched down around 12:32 a.m. in western Davidson County, about seven miles northeast of Pegram. At first it only downed trees and destroyed a barn in Bells Bend, but it strengthened as it wove back and forth across the Cumberland River, crossing it repeatedly. At John C. Tune Airport it reached high-end EF2 strength, damaging 17 hangars and destroying more than 90 aircraft, from charter jets to a news helicopter belonging to CBS affiliate WTVF. It went on to cause considerable structural damage to the former Tennessee State Prison, crossed the Bordeaux neighborhood and struck the northern campus of Tennessee State University, where agricultural buildings were heavily damaged, before damaging numerous homes in North Nashville.

## Germantown and Five Points

Crossing Interstate 65, the tornado grew to nearly two-thirds of a mile wide and moved into Germantown, just north of the Tennessee State Capitol. Multi-story apartment buildings lost their roofs and upper walls, churches and homes were badly damaged, and the Tennessee Department of Human Services building was largely destroyed. Local stations were broadcasting power flashes and videos of the storm as it passed, and WTVF's own studio on James Robertson Parkway narrowly escaped a direct hit, just as it had when an F3 tornado followed a similar path through downtown on April 16, 1998. After crossing the Cumberland on the Jefferson Street Bridge, the tornado hit the Five Points area, damaging restaurants, bars, homes and the music venue Basement East. On McFerrin Avenue, flying debris struck and killed two pedestrians, Michael Dolfini, 36, and Albree Sexton, 33.

## The Worst Came Last

Rather than weakening, the tornado rapidly re-intensified as it entered Donelson, destroying much of Donelson Christian Academy and the Stanford Estates subdivision before raking Hermitage at high-end EF2 strength. It followed Interstate 40 into Wilson County and there reached its peak. In Mount Juliet it produced a broad swath of high-end EF3 damage, heavily damaging schools, including West Wilson Middle School and Stoner Creek Elementary, and leveling a few homes. Three people died in Mount Juliet: Brandy Barker, 38, who was working security at a warehouse on Athletes Way North, and James and Donna Eaton, 84 and 81, in their home on Catalpa Drive. Maintaining EF3 intensity along a six-mile swath, it pushed east of Mount Juliet before weakening and moving into Lebanon, where it damaged homes, businesses, two large cemeteries and the municipal airport, then continued east along the interstate beyond the city.

## What the Engineers Found

A team led by Haag Engineering surveyed the wreckage in detail. They found that hundreds of wood-framed homes had been destroyed, with the worst damage near Mount Juliet, where homes were deroofed and some collapsed inward. South of Donelson Christian Academy, the tornado disintegrated a manufactured home; its bare steel frame was found on the school's roof. On North 8th Street, three multi-story studio apartment buildings collapsed after improperly set nailed connections failed. The survey initially rated the tornado a low-end EF4, with winds of about 180 miles per hour, but lowered that after finding the structural deficiencies. The final rating was EF3, the first intense tornado to move through downtown Nashville since 1998.

## A Billion-Dollar Night

The Nashville tornado ranks among the costliest tornadoes in United States history, with damage estimated at about $1.5 billion in 2020 dollars. It was one of fifteen tornadoes in a wider outbreak across the South, and the same supercell went on to spawn the Cookeville EF4 tornado. For Nashville, it was a grim echo of April 1998, when another intense tornado had crossed the heart of the city along a similar path. In the aftermath, the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development made $10.8 million in federal funding available to victims, and Disaster Recovery Connection helped 518 people in Davidson County. Behind the numbers were whole neighborhoods left to rebuild, and the families of five people who did not survive the night.

## Flight Context

The article's coordinates, approximately 36.17°N, 86.95°W, mark the tornado's starting point in western Davidson County near Bells Bend on the Cumberland River, about 7 miles northeast of Pegram. From 3,000–5,000 feet MSL, the path can be followed eastward along the looping Cumberland River past John C. Tune Airport, through Germantown just north of the State Capitol and downtown Nashville, then east through Donelson and Hermitage and along Interstate 40 to Mount Juliet and Lebanon. John C. Tune Airport (KJWN) is about 4 miles from the starting point, Nashville International Airport (KBNA) about 15 miles, and Lebanon Municipal Airport (FAA identifier M54) sits near the end of the track.
