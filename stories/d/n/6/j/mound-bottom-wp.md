# Mound Bottom

Inside a horseshoe bend of the Harpeth River, a Mississippian town of earthen mounds and a seven-acre plaza flourished for more than three centuries before its people scattered into walled villages.

**Location:** 36.1398, -87.1014

**Categories:** Middle Mississippian culture, Archaeological sites on the National Register of Historic Places in Tennessee, Protected areas of Cheatham County, Tennessee, Native American history, Mounds

## Introduction

Where Mound Creek meets the Harpeth River, the river swings into a tight horseshoe bend, and inside that loop lies one of the great ancient towns of Middle Tennessee. Mound Bottom, in Cheatham County, was home to a Native American community between roughly AD 1000 and 1350, during what archaeologists call the Mississippian period. Its people raised earthen platform mounds and burial mounds around a central plaza of about seven acres, and around them spread hundreds of houses, cemeteries and farm fields. The crowds are long gone. What remains is the shape of their ambition, still written in earth on the river bottom.

## A Hill Made by Hand

The principal mound commands the plaza. When it was finished it stood at least 36 feet tall, flat on top, with a base measuring about 246 feet along each side - a squared hill raised by human hands. Its builders set it roughly 11 degrees east of north and gave it a ramp or staircase at the midpoint of its eastern face, so anyone climbing to the summit approached from the east. As many as 13 smaller mounds were arranged around the open plaza below. Early historical reports describe the whole complex as enclosed by an earthen wall topped with a palisade of upright logs. That palisade no longer exists. By the early 1920s, plowing had erased every sign of it, and a site that had outlasted centuries of abandonment lost part of its story to the modern farm.

## The Great Mound Group

Mound Bottom did not stand alone. About a mile and a half to the southwest lies the Pack Site, also called the Great Mound Division, and together the two have been called the Great Mound Group. Their mounds share structural similarities and their pottery sequences line up, so archaeologists believe the two were occupied at the same time. Older and non-scholarly writings once credited sites like these to vague 'Mound Builders' or a 'Stone Graves Race.' Archaeologists now attribute Mound Bottom, Pack and other late prehistoric mound centers around Nashville to a regional culture called the Middle Cumberland Mississippian. It was first defined in the 1970s through its distinctive burial offerings and stone box graves, then refined through the study of pottery, and a reassessment by Moore and Smith produced a revised chronology. The culture belonged to a far larger world: the Mississippian Ideological Interaction Sphere, once called the Southern Cult or Southeastern Ceremonial Complex, which connected peoples from the Great Lakes to the Gulf Coast and the Appalachians.

## When the Center Gave Way

By about AD 1300 to 1350, Mound Bottom and Pack appear to have been abandoned as major centers. Mound building stopped, and so did the repair of the old mounds, yet both places kept a role in the lives of the people around them: they continued to serve as burial grounds. The change coincides with a period of apparent political instability across the region. The centralized authority once concentrated at Mound Bottom and other large early sites gave way to fortified, autonomous village centers. It is a quieter ending than a battle or a fire. A society that had organized the labor to raise a 36-foot mound broke into smaller communities behind their own defenses, while the old ceremonial ground endured as a place to bury the dead.

## Spades in Sacred Ground

Modern investigation began in 1923, when William E. Myer, working with the Smithsonian, examined the site. At Pack he found evidence of a structure and a hearth atop one of the mounds; at Mound Bottom he documented traces of 10 ancient houses. Three years later Parmenio Edward Cox, Tennessee's first State Archaeologist, spent a month in the field here. He uncovered stone box burials and baked clay floors, excavated at least 70 graves and tested nine mounds. No report on that work was ever published. The University of Tennessee returned in 1936, 1937 and 1940 under the Works Progress Administration, and excavations directed by Charles Nash at both Mound Bottom and Pack examined several cemeteries and a burial mound. Every one of those graves held someone laid to rest by their own community, which makes the missing report more than an academic loss.

## Protected Ground

Since 2005 Mound Bottom has been part of Harpeth River State Park, a linear park that ties together archaeological, historical and natural areas along the lower Harpeth. The site itself is closed to the public; the state says access is prohibited without permission, though the park has offered guided tours and hikes. Overlooking it, a carved mace petroglyph at Mace Bluff preserves another trace of the people who lived along this river. For those who make the trip, scale is the revelation: a plaza broad enough to gather a town, and at its head the great mound, raised by a community whose work still shapes this river bottom a thousand years on.

## Flight Context

Mound Bottom lies at approximately 36.1398 N, 87.1014 W, in Cheatham County about 15 nm west of downtown Nashville. Look for the tight horseshoe bend of the Harpeth River where Mound Creek enters; the mound complex occupies the bottomland inside the loop, with the Pack Site about a mile and a half to the southwest. An altitude of 1,500-2,500 ft AGL gives the best view of the principal mound and plaza, and low-angle morning or evening light helps the earthworks stand out against the fields. Nearest airports: John C. Tune Airport, Nashville (KJWN), about 11 nm east; Dickson Municipal Airport (FAA identifier M02), about 16 nm west; and Nashville International Airport (KBNA), about 20 nm east. Expect summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms over the Nashville Basin.
