# Montgomery Bell Tunnel

By 1819 ironmaster Montgomery Bell had cut a tunnel of roughly 290 feet through a limestone ridge at the Narrows of the Harpeth River to power an iron works, creating what is believed to be the first full-size tunnel in the United States.

## Introduction

At the Narrows of the Harpeth in Cheatham County, Tennessee, the river wanders off on a long oxbow loop and then comes back to within a short distance of itself, separated only by a narrow neck of limestone. Montgomery Bell saw what that meant. Water on one side of the ridge sat 16 feet higher than water on the other, and if he could get the river through the rock rather than around it, he would have power for an iron works. By 1819 he had done it, cutting a tunnel of roughly 290 feet that is believed to be the first full-size tunnel built in the United States.

## An Ironmaster's Shortcut

Bell was an entrepreneur from Pennsylvania who had become involved in iron foundries in central Tennessee. He bought the land here in 1818, and he recognized the potential for using water power to produce wrought iron. The Harpeth River meanders repeatedly in this stretch, and at the Narrows two parts of the river lie close together after a lengthy loop. Bell directed the digging of a tunnel roughly north to south across that isthmus, letting the river take the short way through and harnessing the 16-foot drop in the river's height to generate power. He called the place "Pattison Forge" in honor of his mother's maiden name, which other accounts spell "Patterson," though today the tunnel carries only his own name. Bell's ironworks ran on the forced labor of the enslaved people he held, more than three hundred by the Tennessee Encyclopedia's count, and accounts credit enslaved workers with digging the tunnel.

## Cut Through Bare Rock

The tunnel is dug entirely through limestone, and neither the tunnel nor its portals were ever lined. In profile it is a rectangle topped by a segmental arch, generally about 8 feet high and 15 feet wide. The entrance portal is wider and taller, at 12 feet high and 35 feet across, while the exit portal measures 9.5 feet high and 16 feet wide. Erosion has caused some damage over the years, but the basic shape of the 1819 tunnel remains. Because it was never lined, a visitor at the mouth looks straight at the raw stone of the ridge, shaped by the tools that cut it and by two centuries of erosion.

## A First in American Engineering

The Montgomery Bell Tunnel is the first known example in the United States of a full-scale water diversion tunnel, and the first used to divert water for industrial purposes. It also appears to be the first full-scale tunnel of any kind in the country: it was finished before the Auburn Tunnel in Pennsylvania, completed in 1821, even though work on the Auburn Tunnel had started first. Recognition followed much later. The tunnel was designated a Historic Civil Engineering Landmark in 1981 and a National Historic Landmark in 1994. Of Bell's iron works, it is the only feature to survive. The dam, the head race, the foundry itself and the house Bell built nearby have all disappeared.

## Scouts, Floodwood and Fire

The land around the tunnel took on new lives in the twentieth century. During the 1930s it was leased to the Boy Scouts of America for a summer camp, and in 1978 it was deeded to the state. Today the tunnel lies within a unit of Harpeth River State Park, north of the town of Kingston Springs. Its most dramatic modern moment came late on the evening of September 2, 2011, when a fire was lit inside the tunnel. Driftwood left behind by the 2010 flood fed the flames, and the fire burned into the early hours of the morning before it was put out. Both the tunnel and the road passing over it were damaged. Both have since been stabilized.
