# Hot Springs, North Carolina

Home to the only natural hot spring in North Carolina, this small town on the French Broad River and the Appalachian Trail has been a Native American bathing place, a grand resort, a wartime internment camp and a hikers' haven.

**Location:** 35.8920, -82.8290

**Categories:** Towns in Madison County, North Carolina, Hot springs of North Carolina, Spa towns in the United States, Internment of German Americans, North Carolina populated places on the French Broad River

## Introduction

The water comes out of the ground at close to 110 degrees Fahrenheit. In the mountains of western North Carolina, where the French Broad River meets Spring Creek, that warmth is a rarity: this is the only natural hot spring in the state. Native Americans were the first to find and use its mineral waters, and by 1778, visitors were reportedly coming for their healing properties. The small town that grew up around the spring, Hot Springs, had just 520 residents at the 2020 census, but its history includes a 350-room hotel, sixteen marble pools and a camp full of wartime internees.

## Warm Springs

The place was first known as Warm Springs. Jasper Dagy is believed to have been its first owner, and in 1791 he sold it to William Neilson, who built an inn. Philip Hoodenpile built an estate, a ferry across the French Broad and a toll road called Jewel Hill Road. In 1828 the Buncombe Turnpike was built through town, making the area far more accessible.

By 1831 James Patton of Asheville had bought the springs, and by 1837 he had erected the Warm Springs Hotel, a 350-room establishment fronted by 13 tall columns representing the Thirteen Colonies. Its dining room could seat 600 people. The railroad reached the village in 1882, but two years later, in 1884, the great hotel burned.

## Marble Pools and Wartime Guests

Hot Springs came back grander. In 1886 the Mountain Park Hotel went up, and the discovery of a spring hotter than the old one prompted the town to change its name from Warm Springs to Hot Springs that same year. Sixteen marble pools were set among landscaped lawns with croquet and tennis courts, and the resort was known as one of the most lavish in the Southeast.

By May 1917, with the United States at war, the federal government had leased the hotel and its grounds as an internment camp. The men held there, more than 2,000 by one local account, were mostly crew from German and Austrian commercial ships caught by the outbreak of war in 1914. The government labeled them "enemy aliens": civilians, not soldiers. They built stick-frame houses and held Sunday concerts, and after a typhoid outbreak in August 1918 and the armistice that followed, they were sent on to Fort Oglethorpe, Georgia, or home.

The resort era ended in fire. The Mountain Park Hotel burned in 1920. Two more hotels were built afterward, neither as large or opulent, and both of them burned as well.

## Ballads, Hot Tubs and the Appalachian Trail

The mountains around Hot Springs held another kind of treasure. In 1916 the ballad collector Cecil Sharp gathered songs at Sunnybank, where a sign outside the inn now marks the spot. Sunnybank is listed on the National Register of Historic Places, as are the Dorland Memorial Presbyterian Church, the Henry Ottinger House and, since 2009, the Hot Springs Historic District.

Today the hot mineral springs are privately owned by a spa and lodging businesses, and the water is piped to outdoor tubs overlooking Spring Creek. The Appalachian Trail runs right along Bridge Street downtown before climbing the mountains on either side of the river, and markers set into the sidewalk guide hikers through town. Rafting and kayaking are popular on the French Broad, the nearby Pisgah National Forest offers hiking, mountain biking, backpacking and sightseeing, and the Lover's Leap Trail looks out over the town.

The river that shaped the town also tested it. On September 27, 2024, flooding caused by Hurricane Helene devastated Hot Springs and its historic district. The spa resort lost three-quarters of its mineral baths, and by early 2026 it had rebuilt only some of its tubs and lodging. It was one more blow to a town that has rebuilt, again and again, after disaster.

## Flight Context

Hot Springs lies at 35.892°N, 82.829°W in Madison County, North Carolina, at the confluence of the French Broad River and Spring Creek, near the Tennessee line. From the air, follow the French Broad's winding valley through steep, forested ridges to the small town on its banks. Terrain rises well above the valley on both sides, so stay at least 2,000 ft above the ridgelines and beware of downdrafts in the river valley. Nearby airports: Greeneville Municipal Airport, Tennessee (KGCY), about 18 nm north, and Asheville Regional Airport (KAVL), about 31 nm south-southeast. River fog is common on cool mornings, and summer afternoons bring mountain thunderstorms.
