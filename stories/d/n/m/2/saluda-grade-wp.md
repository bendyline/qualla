# Saluda Grade

Once the steepest standard-gauge mainline railway grade in the United States, the climb from Melrose to Saluda, North Carolina, was built to beat the Blue Ridge and is now owned by a conservancy planning to turn it into a trail.

**Location:** 35.2236, -82.3478

**Categories:** Railway inclines in the United States, Rail infrastructure in North Carolina, Southern Railway (U.S.), Norfolk Southern Railway, Rail trails in North Carolina

## Introduction

On Saluda Grade, the trains going uphill were allowed to go faster than the trains coming down. In the grade's later years, downhill trains had to approach the bottom of the mountain at no more than 8 miles per hour, and if a train came any faster, a switch sent it off the main line onto a runaway track. That rule tells you everything about this stretch of railroad in Polk County, North Carolina. Between Melrose and the town of Saluda, the line gained more than 600 feet in less than three miles, at an average grade of roughly 4.7 percent in most accounts and nearly 5 percent at its steepest. It was the steepest standard-gauge mainline railway grade in the United States.

## No Easy Way Up

The problem was the Blue Ridge front, where the rolling hills of the Piedmont end at the foot of the mountains. Captain Charles W. Pearson was assigned to find a route for the Asheville and Spartanburg Railroad to climb it, and despite numerous surveys, no route crossed the mountains at a tolerable grade. The best option followed the valley and gorge of the Pacolet River.

The line began climbing at the bottom of Melrose Mountain, where Tryon stands today, at an elevation of about 1,080 feet, and ran along the south side of the Pacolet valley. At Melrose the grade proper began, climbing to the town of Saluda and cresting in the center of town at about 2,100 feet. For most uphill trains, even that was too much in one piece. Crews had to double or triple the grade, splitting a train into sections, hauling each up to Saluda separately, and reassembling them there for the rest of the trip to Asheville.

## Mountains of Earth for Runaways

Gravity made the descent the dangerous direction. After accidents involving runaway downhill trains in the late 1880s, the Southern Railway, successor to the Asheville and Spartanburg, built two runaway safety spur tracks, covered with 60-foot piles of earth that could stop a train out of control.

The logic of those spurs was inverted from ordinary railroading. The junctions were staffed, and the switches were always set for the safety track. Only on hearing the whistle signal of a descending train would the signaller throw the switch to keep it on the main line. In later years, centralized traffic control signaling was installed, along with automated switches and timer circuits for the one remaining runaway track, at the bottom of the grade at Melrose. A train approaching at 8 miles per hour or less proved it was under control, and the switch lined up for the main line. Anything faster stayed aimed at the runaway track.

## Silence on the Mountain

In December 2001, Norfolk Southern suspended freight traffic between East Flat Rock, North Carolina, and Landrum, South Carolina, ending operations on the grade for economic reasons. In April 2003 the railroad cut the segment off from the rest of its system, burying the tracks under mounds of dirt and disconnecting the rails at mileposts 26 and 45. Crossing gates lost their arms and signals were covered. Crews kept removing fallen trees and spraying encroaching vegetation, but repeated washouts between Zirconia and the South Carolina line left the route impassable. Norfolk Southern said it did not intend to abandon the line, and talk of excursion trains or a trail went nowhere. In 2014 the railroad sold the W Line south of Asheville, between mileposts 1 and 26, to the shortline operator Watco, which runs it as the Blue Ridge Southern Railroad, but it kept the idle segment over the grade.

## From Rails to Trail

Change came in the 2020s. On July 20, 2022, the nonprofit Upstate Forever announced it wanted to turn portions of the grade into a rail trail, and in February 2023 Norfolk Southern agreed to sell 31 miles of the line to the Saluda Grade Trail Conservancy. By August 2023 a coalition of three nonprofits was leading the effort, with Conserving Carolina working in North Carolina and PAL: Play. Advocate. Live Well. and Upstate Forever in South Carolina. On October 3, 2023, the North Carolina General Assembly passed a law funding acquisition of the corridor from the state line to Zirconia and authorizing its addition to the State Trail System. The conservancy went under contract in the summer of 2024 and closed on the 31.5-mile corridor between Zirconia, North Carolina, and Inman, South Carolina, on June 17, 2025, for $28.5 million, mostly state funds. That handed what was once the steepest mainline grade in the country to people who plan to make it a trail. It is still in planning and design, years from completion, and Hurricane Helene's damage between Tryon and Saluda will add to the cost.

## Flight Context

The grade's reference point is 35.2236°N, 82.3478°W on the Blue Ridge escarpment in Polk County, North Carolina, between Melrose, near Tryon at about 1,080 ft, and the town of Saluda at about 2,100 ft. From the air, follow the Pacolet River gorge up from the Tryon area to Saluda; the abrupt rise of the Blue Ridge front above the Piedmont is unmistakable. Terrain climbs quickly to the north and west, so plan at least 2,500–3,000 ft AGL. Nearby airports: Hendersonville Airport (FAA identifier 0A7, no ICAO code), about 7 nm northwest; Asheville Regional Airport (KAVL), about 16 nm northwest; and Greenville-Spartanburg International Airport (KGSP), about 21 nm south-southeast, both with Class C airspace. Expect turbulence along the escarpment in westerly winds and low clouds banking against the ridge in moist southerly flow.
