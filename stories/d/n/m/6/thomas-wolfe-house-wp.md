# Thomas Wolfe House

The rambling Asheville boarding house where Thomas Wolfe grew up became "Dixieland" in Look Homeward, Angel, and survived an arsonist's fire to reopen as a memorial to the novelist.

**Location:** 35.5975, -82.5508

**Categories:** Thomas Wolfe, National Historic Landmarks in North Carolina, Houses on the National Register of Historic Places in North Carolina, Houses completed in 1883, Queen Anne architecture in North Carolina, Historic house museums in North Carolina, Museums in Asheville, North Carolina, Biographical museums in North Carolina, Literary museums in the United States, North Carolina State Historic Sites

## Introduction

Thomas Wolfe was not sentimental about the house. He called it a "big cheaply constructed frame house of eighteen or twenty drafty, high-ceilinged rooms." He also made it immortal. The boarding house his mother ran in downtown Asheville, North Carolina, became "Dixieland" in his first novel, Look Homeward, Angel, and the family and lodgers who filled it became the raw material of a classic of American literature. Today the house at 52 North Market Street is the Thomas Wolfe Memorial, a state historic site and a National Historic Landmark.

## The Old Kentucky Home

The house was built in 1883 as a two-story frame building with Queen Anne influences, originally just seven rooms with a front and rear porch. By 1906 it was a boarding house, named "Old Kentucky Home" by a previous owner, and that year Julia E. (Westall) Wolfe bought it. She soon moved in to run her business and took her youngest son, Tom, with her, while the rest of the Wolfe family stayed at their house on Woodfin Street. It made for an unusual boyhood: Tom grew up among boarders, in a home that was also his mother's business. In 1916 Julia enlarged and modernized the house, adding indoor plumbing and 11 more rooms, and the sprawling building eventually counted 29 rooms.

## Becoming Dixieland

Wolfe lived at the boarding house until he left for the University of North Carolina in 1916. When he came to write his first novel, he went straight back to it. Look Homeward, Angel, published in 1929, is autobiographical fiction: Asheville becomes Altamont, the Wolfes become the Gants, Wolfe himself becomes Eugene and his mother becomes Eliza, and the Old Kentucky Home becomes Dixieland. Into it he poured his experiences among his family, his friends and the boarders who came and went through his mother's house. The book has never gone out of print since it appeared. For readers who know it, walking through the real rooms is a strange doubling, the setting of a novel made solid in wood and plaster.

## Fire in the Dining Room

Wolfe died young, of tuberculosis, in 1938, and his mother lived until 1945. After her death the house became a memorial to him, and it opened to the public as a house museum on July 19, 1949. It was designated a National Historic Landmark in 1971, and the state of North Carolina took over the property in the mid-1970s. Then, in 1998, during Asheville's Bele Chere street festival, an arsonist set fire to the house. The blaze destroyed the dining room and 200 of the house's 800 original artifacts. The person responsible has reportedly never been identified. Restoration took about six years and cost $2.4 million, and the house reopened in 2004. It still stands in the Downtown Asheville Historic District, surrounded by the city Wolfe once disguised as Altamont.

## Flight Context

The Thomas Wolfe House is at 35.5975°N, 82.5508°W in downtown Asheville, North Carolina. The house itself is small from the air; orient on downtown Asheville, which sits near the confluence of the French Broad and Swannanoa rivers, and look for the historic district's grid of older buildings. A viewing altitude of about 4,000-4,500 feet MSL gives a clear look at downtown while keeping a safe margin over the city and the surrounding mountains.
