# Jonesborough, Tennessee

Tennessee's oldest town was a frontier capital, a young Andrew Jackson's lodging, the birthplace of a pioneering abolitionist newspaper and a brawling press, and is now home to the National Storytelling Festival.

**Location:** 36.2942, -82.4725

**Categories:** Towns in Washington County, Tennessee, County seats in Tennessee, State of Franklin, Storytelling, Populated places established in 1779, Abolitionism in the United States

## Introduction

Sometimes a story in Jonesborough has to wait for a train. On the first full weekend in October, big tents rise in parks around town for the National Storytelling Festival, and now and then a teller on stage pauses while a Norfolk Southern train rolls past. It is a fitting interruption. The town calls itself "Tennessee's oldest town," and it has been collecting stories since before there was a Tennessee - tales of a vanished state, a future president, a crusading editor and two feuding editors who once settled a quarrel with a cane and a pistol.

## Older Than the State

European Americans founded Jonesborough in 1779, seventeen years before Tennessee became a state, when this far northeastern corner was still under North Carolina's jurisdiction. They named it for Willie Jones, a North Carolina legislator who had backed the state's westward expansion across the Appalachians. For a time the town spelled itself "Jonesboro" before reclaiming its historic name. In 1784 it became the capital of the autonomous State of Franklin, ostensibly named for Benjamin Franklin. Congress never recognized Franklin, and North Carolina reclaimed it in late 1788, but the episode left Jonesborough with a frontier-capital pedigree. One of the oldest surviving buildings in town predates the town itself: the Christopher Taylor House, a log cabin built in 1777 about a mile outside the original limits.

## A Future President Waits

That cabin has a famous former lodger. In 1788, the future president Andrew Jackson spent several months practicing law in Jonesborough while he waited for a caravan heading to what is now Nashville, and he stayed in the cabin belonging to Major Christopher Taylor. In 1974 the cabin was taken down from its original site and rebuilt in a town park inside the historic district. Local legend holds that Jackson has never entirely left; his ghost is said to appear now and then near the old logs. The district around it, listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1969, is a walk through the town's layers: Sister's Row on West Main Street from 1821, a female academy from about 1834, churches from the 1840s, and houses that shift from Federal to Greek Revival to Italianate as the decades pass.

## Ink and a Cane

Jonesborough's printing presses had a conscience and a temper. East Tennessee was a region of small farms whose owners enslaved relatively few people compared with planters elsewhere in the state, and its Unionist leanings ran deep. The Tennessee Manumission Society was founded in 1815, and in March 1819 Elihu Embree, one of its members, began publishing the weekly Manumission Intelligencer in Jonesborough. In April 1820 he turned it into a monthly called The Emancipator, often described as the first newspaper in the United States devoted exclusively to abolishing slavery. Embree died that December. Two decades later the press turned violent. William G. "Parson" Brownlow published the Jonesborough Whig here, and in the spring of 1840 he brawled in the street with Landon Carter Haynes, a young lawyer who would soon edit the rival Tennessee Sentinel. Brownlow attacked Haynes with a cane, and the fight ended when Haynes drew a pistol and shot Brownlow in the leg. For years they savaged each other in print. Brownlow, a future governor, moved the Whig to Knoxville in 1849.

## Tents on Main Street

The town's modern fame began modestly. In 1973, Jimmy Neil Smith, a high school journalism teacher, heard Grand Ole Opry regular Jerry Clower spin a tale on the radio while riding with a carload of students, and he decided the region needed a storytelling festival. That October, the first National Storytelling Festival used hay bales and wagons for stages, and tellers and audience together numbered no more than 60. Today the International Storytelling Center hosts it, drawing people from around the world to hear tellers who have included Carmen Agra Deedy, Syd Lieberman and Kathryn Tucker Windham, and the festival inspired a storytelling graduate program at nearby East Tennessee State University. The tradition it celebrates is an Appalachian one, rooted in the hills that ring this town of 5,860 people at the 2020 census.

## Flight Context

Jonesborough lies at about 36.29°N, 82.47°W in Washington County, Tennessee, in the Ridge-and-Valley country between the Watauga and Nolichucky rivers; the main crest of the Appalachians rises to the southeast. From 3,000-4,000 feet AGL, look for the compact historic center at the junction of U.S. 11E/321 and State Route 81, with Johnson City sprawling to the northeast along the highway. Nearby airports: Johnson City Airport (FAA identifier 0A4, about 9 nm), Tri-Cities Airport at Blountville (KTRI, about 11 nm), William B. Greene Jr. Regional Airport in Elizabethton (formerly Elizabethton Municipal; FAA identifier 0A9, about 15 nm) and Greeneville Municipal Airport (KGCY, about 17 nm). Terrain rises sharply toward the Appalachian crest to the southeast, so keep that in mind on low-altitude sightseeing.
