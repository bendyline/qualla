# Penland School of Craft

What began in 1923 as a mountain teacher's plan to help local women earn money at the loom grew into Penland School of Craft, a Blue Ridge campus where artists come to work in fiber, clay, glass, iron and more.

## Introduction

In 1923 a teacher named Lucy Morgan spent three months at Berea College learning to weave. She taught at the Appalachian School in Penland, an isolated community in the Blue Ridge Mountains of Mitchell County, North Carolina, and she had a practical idea: if the women of the area could weave, they could earn an income without leaving their homes. From that modest scheme grew a school whose reputation would reach far beyond these mountains: the Penland School of Craft.

## The Penland Weavers

Back in Penland, Morgan started the Penland Weavers, also known as Penland Weavers and Potters, which worked as a cottage industry. The association gave local women instruction, looms and materials, then marketed the cloth they wove at home. Local volunteers put up a cabin for the work, and then a larger hall. For families in a remote mountain community, it was a way to earn cash at home. Morgan, who lived from 1889 to 1981, turned out to be a tireless promoter, and word of what was happening in Penland spread well beyond Mitchell County.

## One Week in 1929

In 1928 Morgan invited Edward F. Worst, a noted weaving expert and author of Foot Power Loom Weaving, to come and work with the weavers. When he returned in 1929, several students from out of state joined a group of local women for a one-week class, and with that the school was born. It was officially founded as the Penland School of Handicrafts. Worst added classes in basketry and pottery, and he began a tradition that continues today: workshops taught by visiting artists. Under Morgan's enthusiastic promotion, Penland School of Handicrafts built a national and even international reputation as a center for learning by doing.

## Iron, Glass and a Jolt

By the time Lucy Morgan retired in 1962, enrollments had fallen and Penland was drawing very few younger students. The jolt it needed came from its second director, the sculptor and design teacher Bill Brown. Over his twenty-one years in charge, Brown added new media such as iron and glass, introduced eight-week sessions called Concentrations, expanded the work-study scholarship program, and started the core fellowship and resident artist programs. The resident artist program, which gives artists one or three years of housing and studio space, has nurtured the careers of many significant craftspeople. It also helped create a lively community of working craft artists around the school, so that Penland became a place people stayed, not just one they visited.

## A Campus in the Hills

Today the school covers about 420 acres with 57 buildings, and more than 1,400 people come each year for instruction. Workshops run in spring, summer and fall, in weaving and dyeing, bead work, glassblowing, pottery, papermaking, metalworking and woodworking, as well as printmaking, painting and photography. Penland does not award degrees, though students can earn college credit through Western Carolina University. The historic core of the campus was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 2003 as the Penland School Historic District: turn-of-the-century frame farmhouses and outbuildings, Rustic Revival log buildings, and landmarks such as the Colonial Revival Lily Loom House, the Craft Cabin and Beacon Church. Penland's buildings were designed primarily by North Carolina architects. Each year around early March, a community open house (called Community Day in some listings) opens the studios to visitors, who can try a small project with the artists' help; check Penland's calendar for the date.

## Flight Context

Penland School of Craft is at 35.9446°N, 82.1155°W in the Blue Ridge Mountains of Mitchell County, North Carolina, just north of the small community of Penland, whose center is the Penland Road bridge over the North Toe River and the railroad line beside it. Spruce Pine lies about 3 miles to the east-southeast. Terrain here is high and rugged, so plan on at least 5,500-6,000 feet MSL for a safe view. Avery County Airport/Morrison Field near Spruce Pine (FAA identifier 7A8) is about 6 nm east, Asheville Regional Airport (KAVL) is about 37 nm southwest, and Tri-Cities Airport (KTRI) is about 35 nm north-northwest. Mountain weather changes quickly; watch for low cloud on the ridges and valley fog in the mornings.
