# Erwin, Tennessee

A Tennessee railroad town apparently named by a postal error, Erwin is remembered for the 1916 hanging of a circus elephant named Mary and for the 1918 lynching of Tom DeVert and the expulsion of its entire Black community.

**Location:** 36.1450, -82.4108

**Categories:** Towns in Unicoi County, Tennessee, County seats in Tennessee, Expulsions of African Americans, Racially motivated violence against African Americans in Tennessee, Sundown towns in Tennessee

## Introduction

Erwin, Tennessee, was supposed to be named Ervin. In 1879 the town was renamed to honor D.J.N. Ervin, who had donated land for the county seat, but when the post office made the change on December 5, 1879, the name was recorded as Erwin, apparently a postal error. Nobody ever corrected it. The mistake is the gentlest story this Unicoi County seat has to tell. For more than a century Erwin was a railroad town, and its rail yard became the stage for two events, two years apart: the hanging of an elephant in 1916, and the lynching of a Black man and the expulsion of every Black resident in 1918.

## Built by the Clinchfield

Railroads shaped Erwin's economy and identity from 1890 to 2015. The first attempt, the Charleston, Cincinnati and Chicago Railroad, known as the Triple C, ran trains through Erwin in 1890 but disbanded by the end of that year. Its successor, the Ohio River and Charleston Railway, bought the assets in 1893 and also failed.

Success came with the Carolina, Clinchfield and Ohio Railway, completed in 1909 from Dante, Virginia, to Spartanburg, South Carolina, with its headquarters in Erwin. The line reached Elkhorn City, Kentucky, in 1915 to connect with the Chesapeake and Ohio, and in 1924 it became the Clinchfield Railroad. Through later mergers into CSX Transportation, Erwin kept its rail yard, diesel shop and car repair facility until 2015. Another local industry reached kitchens across the country: from 1916 to 1957 the Southern Potteries plant on Ohio Avenue produced hand-painted Blue Ridge dishware, popular nationwide in the late 1930s and 1940s and still sought by collectors.

## Mary

In 1916 an elephant named Mary, part of the Sparks World Famous Shows traveling circus, killed her handler, Walter Eldridge, in nearby Kingsport. Eldridge was inexperienced, and he had allegedly struck Mary on the head with a hook. Rumors and sensationalist news stories spread word of a killer elephant, calls for her execution began, and some towns announced they would turn the circus away if it arrived with her. To appease the crowds, Mary's owner, Charlie Sparks, decided to hang her.

Erwin lay roughly 30 miles south-southwest of Kingsport as the crow flies, and as home to a major railway yard, it happened to have a 100-ton crane car capable of lifting a five-ton elephant. By most estimates more than 2,500 people gathered at the yard to watch Mary hoisted to her death. It remains the only known public execution of an elephant in Tennessee. Playwright George Brant later told the story in a one-act play, Elephant's Graveyard, first produced at the University of Texas at Austin in 2007. In the years since, the town has held Elephant Revival events, including auctions of artist-painted fiberglass elephants, that raise money for The Elephant Sanctuary in Hohenwald.

## May 1918

On May 19, 1918, a Black man named Tom DeVert was shot dead in Erwin, and the surviving accounts differ on how it began. One says four white residents of Erwin attacked him during a poker game, that he fled with them shooting after him, and that a white teenager named Georgia Lee Collins, who happened to be passing by, was struck by a bullet; DeVert was killed, and afterward he was accused of having assaulted her. Another, drawn from a 1960 history of the Unicoi County court, says he was accused of grabbing a 15-year-old white girl near the Nolichucky River and was shot in the head by a man playing poker nearby. DeVert never stood trial.

A crowd of white men dragged his body to the rail yard powerhouse. There they forced Erwin's Black residents to stand and watch as DeVert's body was burned on a pyre of railroad cross-ties. According to the Bristol Herald of May 21, 1918, men with pistols, shotguns and clubs stood before the lined-up crowd to keep anyone from running away, and one of them is reported to have turned to the people forced to watch and said: "Watch what we are going to do here. If any of you are left in town by tomorrow night, you will meet the same fate."

## One Hundred Thirty-One Neighbors

At the height of the violence, the mob's leaders planned to burn the homes of all of Erwin's Black residents. The local rail yard manager talked them into forcibly evicting them instead. Under that threat, by the count most often cited, 131 men, women and children abandoned their homes and belongings and left at once; other accounts put Erwin's Black population far lower. They had been Erwin's neighbors. In a single day, they were driven from their own town.

The Erwin Expulsion of 1918, as it came to be called, shaped the town's reputation for generations. Throughout the 20th century Erwin was considered a sundown town, and in 1979 the Johnson City Press-Chronicle wrote that it had become known as "the place where Blacks dare not go." The rail yard that built Erwin was the same ground where Mary died and where Tom DeVert's body was burned.

## Flight Context

Erwin sits at 36.145°N, 82.4108°W in the Nolichucky River valley of Unicoi County, Tennessee, about 10 nm south of Johnson City, with high ridges and the North Carolina line to the south and east. From the air, look for the former rail yard stretched along the valley floor beside the town. Mountains surround the valley, so plan at least 2,500–3,000 ft AGL and more over the ridges to the southeast. Nearby airports: Johnson City Airport (FAA identifier 0A4, no ICAO code), about 14 nm north-northeast; William B. Greene Jr. Regional Airport in Elizabethton (formerly Elizabethton Municipal; FAA identifier 0A9, no ICAO code), about 18 nm northeast; and Tri-Cities Airport (KTRI), about 20 nm north, a towered airport with controlled airspace. Valley fog is common on autumn and winter mornings, and summer afternoons bring mountain thunderstorms.
