# Piedmont Airlines Flight 22

On July 19, 1967, a Piedmont Airlines Boeing 727 climbing out of Asheville collided with a Cessna 310 over Hendersonville, killing all 82 people aboard the two aircraft, in the first major airline accident investigated by the new NTSB.

## Introduction

Just after noon on July 19, 1967, people in Hendersonville, North Carolina, heard what many of them took for a jet breaking the sound barrier. It was not a sonic boom. Over the town, a Piedmont Airlines Boeing 727, only minutes into its climb out of Asheville, had collided with a twin-engine Cessna 310. Both aircraft were destroyed, and no one aboard either one survived. The collision over Hendersonville would become a test case for a brand-new federal agency, and, four decades later, the subject of a second look.

## Three Minutes Out of Asheville

Flight 22 took off from Runway 16 at Asheville Regional Airport at 11:58 a.m., bound for Roanoke, Virginia, on what should have been a 35-minute flight under instrument flight rules. The Cessna, meanwhile, was inbound to Asheville, and it too was flying on instruments. Both were in radio contact with Asheville's controllers, but on different frequencies. The controller cleared the Cessna to descend and maintain 6,000 feet, then cleared the 727 to climb unrestricted to the VOR navigation beacon, and then cleared the Cessna for an approach to Runway 16. At 12:01, with the jet still climbing, the two aircraft collided at about 6,100 feet, the impact coming just behind the 727's cockpit. The airliner rolled onto its back and fell into an area known as Camp Pinewood.

## Eighty-Two Lives

All 79 passengers and crew aboard the 727 died, along with the three people in the Cessna: 82 lives lost within minutes of an ordinary departure. In the jet's cockpit were a captain, a first officer and a flight engineer, and behind them a cabin full of travelers bound for Roanoke. Among the passengers was John T. McNaughton, who had just resigned as Assistant Secretary of Defense for International Security Affairs and had been confirmed by the U.S. Senate as the next Secretary of the Navy. He was regarded as one of Secretary of Defense Robert McNamara's closest advisors. McNaughton was traveling with his family, and his wife and younger son died with him. For Hendersonville, the disaster was not a distant headline; the collision happened directly overhead.

## A New Board's First Case

The crash arrived at a moment of institutional change. The National Transportation Safety Board had just been formed within the new Department of Transportation, taking over accident investigation from the Civil Aeronautics Board, and Flight 22 became the first accident involving a major airline that the new NTSB investigated. Its report placed primary responsibility on the pilot of the Cessna. It also cited air traffic control procedures as a contributing factor, and it recommended a review of the minimum skill levels required of pilots flying under instrument rules. Nearly four decades later, that finding would be put to the test.

## Reopened, Thirty-Nine Years On

In 2006 the NTSB reopened the investigation to review possible irregularities raised by Paul Houle, a former military traffic-accident investigator and historian who had spent several years studying the crash and wrote a book about it. Houle argued that the original report left out the fact that the Cessna pilot had properly reported his heading, which should have alerted controllers to a conflict. He pointed to the cockpit voice recorder transcript, which he said showed the 727's crew dealing with a fire in a cockpit ashtray during the 35 seconds before the collision. He also noted that the lead investigator's brother had been a vice president and director of Piedmont, according to 1968 court testimony, and that the young NTSB was not then fully independent of the FAA. In February 2007 the board upheld its original findings. Chairman Mark Rosenker wrote to Houle that the board had voted 3 to 1 that his arguments were unsubstantiated. The collision was later mentioned in the television series Why Planes Crash, in an episode called "Collision Course."

## Flight Context

The crash site is at 35.3372°N, 82.4378°W on the edge of Hendersonville, North Carolina, south-southeast of Asheville. This is a place of remembrance: 82 people died here. Asheville Regional Airport (KAVL), where Flight 22 departed from Runway 16, is about 8 nm northwest, and Hendersonville Airport (FAA identifier 0A7) is less than 2 nm south, so expect local traffic in the pattern. A viewing altitude of 4,500-5,000 feet MSL keeps you clear of the surrounding terrain while giving a view of the town; check the sectional for Asheville's airspace before overflying. Expect mountain haze, and watch for afternoon thunderstorms in summer.
