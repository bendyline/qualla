# Tweetsie Railroad

A narrow-gauge steam engine that once hauled freight and passengers through the Blue Ridge, nearly sold to Gene Autry, came home in 1957 to anchor a Wild West theme park between Boone and Blowing Rock.

**Location:** 36.1709, -81.6490

**Categories:** Heritage railroads in North Carolina, Narrow-gauge railroads in North Carolina, Amusement parks in North Carolina, Buildings and structures in Watauga County, North Carolina, Amusement parks opened in 1957

## Introduction

The railroad was named for the sound it made. Area residents turned the initials of the East Tennessee and Western North Carolina Railroad - ET&WNC - into a spoken nickname, "Tweetsie," which also caught the "tweet" of its locomotive whistles echoing through the mountains. That railroad is long gone, but its name and one of its engines survive between Boone and Blowing Rock, North Carolina, at the heart of a family Wild West theme park where steam trains have circled a three-mile mountain loop since 1958.

## Number 12

The engine at the center of it all is Locomotive No. 12, built in 1917 by the Baldwin Locomotive Works, a coal-fired 4-6-0 made for track just three feet wide. From 1917 until it was retired in 1949 it carried passengers and freight for the ET&WNC, whose narrow-gauge line ran from Johnson City, Tennessee, to Boone, North Carolina, from 1919 until 1940, threading narrow-gauge rails into remote corners of the Blue Ridge. Today it is the only surviving narrow-gauge engine of that railroad. After its working life ended, railroad enthusiasts took it to Virginia to run on the Shenandoah Central, until the rains of Hurricane Hazel washed out that line in October 1954. No. 12 went up for sale again. Cowboy actor and singer Gene Autry signed an option to buy the locomotive and cars for $17,000, planning to ship them to California for use in motion pictures, but the cost of moving and restoring them made the plan impractical, and he let the option lapse.

## Bought for a Dollar

Grover Robbins, an entrepreneur from Blowing Rock, heard the engine was available and, by most accounts, took over Autry's claim on it for one dollar. In August 1955 he paid the Shenandoah Central operators the $17,000 purchase price, intending to bring the train back to its home mountains as an excursion railroad. He found a site midway between Boone and Blowing Rock and laid a mile of track. The Southern Railway shops in Hickory restored the locomotive and cars, and the Moss-McLeod Trucking Company of Charlotte hauled them up the Blue Ridge. On July 4, 1957, No. 12 made its first public trip over the new line. In 1958 the track was extended into a three-mile loop around the mountain, and Tweetsie's trains have run that circuit ever since. Grover's brothers, Harry and Spencer, joined in the operation, and the Robbins family still controls the park. In 1960 a second coal-fired engine arrived, No. 190, the "Yukon Queen," from Alaska's White Pass and Yukon Route.

## Can-Can and Ghost Trains

Around the railroad grew a full Wild West park. There is the Tweetsie Palace Saloon with Diamond Lil's Can-Can Revue, gold panning and gem mining, the Deer Park zoo, a chairlift, a drop tower, a spinning coaster and a teacup ride that turns only at Christmas. The season runs from April to October, and in the fall the trains become the "Ghost Train" for Halloween nights before the park reopens after Thanksgiving for Tweetsie Christmas. Railroad Heritage Weekend, held in August in past years, celebrates the narrow-gauge engines themselves. Behind the scenes, Tweetsie's own locomotive shop keeps its two engines running and repairs narrow-gauge steam locomotives for other parks, including Busch Gardens, Dollywood, Six Flags and Walt Disney World. In 2004 it bought the tooling and rights to service engines built by Crown Metal Products.

## A Family of Railroads

Tweetsie's success spread. In 1961 Grover and Harry Robbins built Rebel Railroad in the Smoky Mountains near Pigeon Forge, Tennessee, first themed to the Civil War centennial and later remade as the Wild West Goldrush Junction, much like its parent park. After passing through several owners it became Dollywood, where the railroad still runs as the Dollywood Express. Grover Robbins went on to develop the Land of Oz on Beech Mountain as well. Tweetsie itself nearly vanished in 2008, when the owners of leased land under part of the park wanted to sell it for development. A May 2008 proposal had Watauga County buying the land, for $3.15 million in that proposal and $3.5 million in some later accounts, and leasing it back to the park until it could buy the land back. On August 17, 2024, No. 12 left service for a major rebuild after mechanics found thin areas in its firebox; the park says the firebox must be completely rebuilt and, as of 2026, it has not announced a return date, the latest chapter for an engine now more than a century old.

## Flight Context

Tweetsie Railroad lies at about 36.17°N, 81.65°W on U.S. 321 between Boone and Blowing Rock in Watauga County, North Carolina, high in the Blue Ridge. From 5,500-6,500 feet MSL, follow U.S. 321 between the two towns and look for the park's buildings and the three-mile rail loop circling a mountain beside the highway. Nearby airports: Johnson County Airport at Mountain City, Tennessee (FAA identifier 6A4, about 17 nm), Ashe County Airport at Jefferson (FAA identifier GEV, GPS code KGEV, about 19 nm), Foothills Regional Airport at Morganton (KMRN, about 21 nm) and Wilkes County Airport at North Wilkesboro (KUKF, about 27 nm). Fly it in clear weather and keep terrain clearance in mind over the high ridges.
