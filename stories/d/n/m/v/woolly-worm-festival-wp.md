# Woolly Worm Festival

Every October since 1978, Banner Elk, North Carolina, has raced fuzzy caterpillars up strings to crown an official winter forecaster, drawing crowds in the thousands to the High Country.

**Location:** 36.1629, -81.8699

## Introduction

The racecourse is about three feet of nylon string, hung vertically from a racing board. The competitors are caterpillars. A couple of dozen of them start each heat, and as they inch upward, their owners are allowed, even encouraged, to holler and whistle them on. This is the Woolly Worm Festival in Banner Elk, North Carolina, held every October since 1978, where the prize for the fastest fuzzy climber is not just money and glory. The winning worm becomes the High Country's official forecaster for the winter ahead.

## Thirteen Stripes, Thirteen Weeks

The woolly worm, also called the woolly bear, is the caterpillar of the Isabella tiger moth, a member of a subfamily of moths with some 11,000 species around the world. Its body has thirteen segments, banded in black and brown, and festival organizers accept that those colors can be read as a forecast of how hard the coming winter will be. The math is tidy: thirteen segments for the thirteen weeks of winter. By the festival's reading, a black segment calls for snow and below-average temperatures, a brown one for average temperatures, and a flecked one for light snow and below-average conditions. Scientists say the stripes do not actually predict winter: the width of the bands reflects the caterpillar's species, age and feeding, and the best-known study behind the folklore, an eight-year survey by Dr. C. H. Curran in New York beginning in 1948, covered a small area and a short period and is not conclusive proof. Still, celebrants hold the woolly worm in high esteem thanks to its proclaimed 80 to 85 percent accuracy rate.

## Race Day

The racing is serious business, at least by caterpillar standards. A Master of Ceremonies presides over the festival and, with help from the festival mascot, Merriweather, makes sure the rules are rigorously kept, especially at the race. About 1,000 worms are entered, running in heat after heat over two days of racing. The winner of Saturday's grand final is named the official forecaster, and its owner collects a prize that the festival bills as a $1,000 bounty; Sunday's races carry a $500 prize and no forecasting role. Crowds are large for a small mountain town: attendance is variously estimated at 15,000 to more than 20,000 people, and the festival has been featured in the Farmers' Almanac and Kiwanis International magazine.

## Tough Little Forecaster

The real woolly worm has a remarkable winter of its own. Before settling in for the cold, it feeds on a variety of plants and then produces glycerol, a kind of antifreeze that keeps its most vital cells from freezing; it is reported to survive temperatures approaching minus 90 degrees Fahrenheit. It shelters from bitter weather under logs, boulders, boards, rocks and other dark places, and does not emerge from its frozen state until the weather warms in spring. For all the fun of the forecast, the festival also does quieter work. It is co-hosted by the Kiwanis Club of Banner Elk and the Avery County Chamber of Commerce, and its proceeds go back into the community, supporting schools and children's programs and promoting business and tourism in Avery County.

## Flight Context

Banner Elk sits at about 36.1629°N, 81.8699°W in the North Carolina High Country of Avery County, in a valley surrounded by high peaks: Beech Mountain (5,506 feet) rises just to the north-northwest, Sugar Mountain (5,236 feet) about 2.5 nm to the south, and Grandfather Mountain (5,946 feet) about 4 nm to the southeast. The Lees-McRae College campus in town is a useful visual marker. With that much high terrain close by, fly no lower than about 7,500-8,000 feet MSL. Elk River Airport (NC06), just west of town, is private-use; the nearest public options include Avery County Airport/Morrison Field (FAA identifier 7A8) about 14.5 nm south-southwest, Tri-Cities Airport (KTRI) about 32 nm northwest, and Hickory Regional Airport (KHKY) about 34 nm southeast. Mountain weather here changes quickly; expect gusty ridge-top winds and early cold snaps in October.
