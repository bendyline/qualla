# Linn Cove Viaduct

Built piece by piece from above so that heavy machinery never touched the slope, the Linn Cove Viaduct curls around Grandfather Mountain and closed one of the last gaps in the Blue Ridge Parkway.

**Location:** 36.0932, -81.8132

**Categories:** Bridges, Blue Ridge Parkway, Engineering, Landmarks in North Carolina, Scenic drives

## Introduction

The builders of the Linn Cove Viaduct worked under an unusual rule: stay off the mountain. Heavy construction equipment was kept off the ground, drilling rigs were lowered into place by helicopter, and the bridge grew outward from its own tip, one precast piece at a time. The result is a 1,243-foot ribbon of concrete that snakes around Grandfather Mountain in western North Carolina. Built from 1979 into the early 1980s at a cost of about $10 million and opened to traffic in 1987, it was one of the last major construction projects on the 469-mile Blue Ridge Parkway.

## Half a Century to Close the Gap

Construction of the Blue Ridge Parkway, which runs mostly along the spine of the Blue Ridge to link Shenandoah National Park with Great Smoky Mountains National Park, began in 1935. Grandfather Mountain posed a problem from the start. Towering almost 6,000 feet, it is among the most rugged peaks on the Blue Ridge, and given the effects a traditional cut-and-fill road would have on it, the initial plan called for a viaduct. Conservationists championed the idea. They hoped to preserve the area as a mountain park and recreation area, warned of irreparable damage from lumbering and other exploitation, and stressed the need to protect the view of the mountain as it then existed. By 1966 the parkway was essentially complete except for a 7.7-mile section circling Grandfather Mountain. Acquiring the needed property, despite the government's concerted efforts, eventually took 40 years. Not until 1987 was the whole parkway finished and opened for through traffic.

## Drill Rigs From the Sky

The viaduct was designed by Figg and Muller Engineers, Inc., and the care began before a single segment was cast. Preliminary geotechnical exploration started in 1975, and the crews doing it hiked to the site rather than cutting a road in. Their drilling equipment was lowered by helicopter to each bore-hole location. Construction began in 1979. The finished structure runs some 1,243 feet and is a little less than 40 feet wide, resting on just seven piers. What made the project noteworthy was not its size but its restraint. Keeping heavy equipment off the ground meant that the only work done on the slope itself was drilling the footings for those piers, and even the piers were built in a segmental manner, stacked up piece by piece.

## A Hundred and Fifty-Three Pieces

Starting from its south side, the builders assembled the bridge by connecting 153 segments, one at a time. Each segment, nominally 8.5 feet long and weighing 50 tons, was precast at a facility on the site and moved on a carriage out to a custom crane. That crane moved along the viaduct itself as the bridge grew, so the machinery that built the road rode on the road. The geometry was demanding. To follow the contours of the mountain, the viaduct had to take the shape of an "S-and-a-half," so all but one of the segments were slightly curved. Only a single piece in the entire span is straight. The curve is the whole point. The road bends with the mountain instead of forcing its way through it.

## Driving on Air

The viaduct has received numerous design awards, including the 1984 President's Design Award. The citation praised construction "by pre-cast, segmental concrete elements set from above on segmental piers" as "elegant, economical, and new," producing "an elegant curving ribbon that caresses the terrain without using it as a support." It went further, saying the bridge gives the motorist the sensation of driving on air "while the earth goes by." Those words describe exactly what the viaduct's early champions had asked for: a road that reaches around the mountain without carving into it, leaving the view they fought for intact. Travelers who want a closer look can park near the viaduct's south end, where the National Park Service's Linn Cove Visitor Center and its bridge museum are currently closed but the parking lot stays open for trail access. From there the Tanawha Trail, which runs from Beacon Heights to Julian Price Memorial Park, offers views of the viaduct and Grandfather Mountain, though visitors cannot walk on the viaduct itself. Few roads give you a reason to pull over and admire the road itself. This one does.

## Flight Context

Linn Cove Viaduct: 36.0932°N, 81.8132°W, wrapping the slope of Grandfather Mountain (almost 6,000 ft) on the Blue Ridge Parkway in Avery County, North Carolina. A viewing altitude of about 7,500-8,000 ft MSL keeps safe clearance over Grandfather Mountain while showing the viaduct's S-curve against the forest; follow the parkway's ribbon along the ridge to find it. Nearest airports: Avery County Morrison Field at Spruce Pine (FAA identifier 7A8, no ICAO code), about 13 nm southwest; Foothills Regional at Morganton (KMRN), about 19 nm south-southeast; Hickory Regional (KHKY), about 30 nm southeast. High ridges here can be cloud-covered and turbulent in strong winds, so plan for clear VFR conditions.
