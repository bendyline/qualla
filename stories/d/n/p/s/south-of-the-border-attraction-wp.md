# South of the Border (attraction)

A 1949 beer stand at the North Carolina line grew into a sprawling, deliberately campy I-95 stopover whose sombrero tower and mascot have drawn travelers and criticism for decades.

**Location:** 34.4978, -79.3097

**Categories:** Roadside attractions in South Carolina, Amusement parks in South Carolina, Interstate 95, Landmarks in South Carolina, Tourist attractions in Dillon County, South Carolina, Amusement parks opened in 1950

## Introduction

The countdown starts many miles out. Billboard after billboard ticks off the distance along the highway until, just past the North Carolina line, a tower of roughly 200 feet wearing a sombrero-shaped observation deck rises over Hamer, South Carolina. South of the Border takes its name from exactly where it sits, just south of the border between the Carolinas, and in the early days of motor travel it marked the halfway point between New York and Florida. The joke in the name also reaches for another border: the whole place is dressed in a faux-Mexican theme, loud, bright and intentionally campy.

## A Beer Stand at the Line

It began with thirst. In 1949, Alan Schafer opened South of the Border Depot, a beer stand, right next to Robeson County, North Carolina, which was at one time one of many dry counties in that state. A year later, in 1950, he developed South of the Border itself. Schafer kept finding ways to profit from the line on the map. In 1962 the business expanded into fireworks, possibly capitalizing on the fact that fireworks were illegal in North Carolina. Then came the real windfall. In 1964, the route for Interstate 95 was announced, and it would pass right by, leaving the attraction next to two exits and within view of the highway. By the mid-1960s the complex had added a barber shop, a drugstore, a variety store, a post office, an outdoor go-kart track and a 104-foot image of its mascot, Pedro.

## Hundreds of Acres of Spectacle

Today the compound sprawls across roughly 300 acres, by most accounts, where I-95 meets US 301 and US 501. There is a motel with a reported 300 rooms, a truck stop, restaurants, gas stations, souvenir and fireworks shops, a campground, a video arcade, and an amusement area and miniature golf course that a 2024 visitor from The Assembly found closed. The attraction bills Reptile Lagoon as the largest indoor reptile exhibit in the United States. The architecture leans into the spectacle, with a "Jetsons-esque starburst chandelier" in the lobby and a swimming pool, Pedro's Pleasure Dome, set inside what one description calls "a junkyard version" of a geodesic dome. A Washington Post review captured the light show: flashing signs "throw technicolor pink and green and blue onto every surface." Less expected is a serious motocross complex with four circuits, including Supercross and Arenacross training facilities reported to be open only to licensed professional riders.

## The Trouble with Pedro

In the beginning, Schafer's advertising used only sombreros and serapes. Then he hired two men he had met on a business trip to Mexico to work as bellboys, and people began calling them Pedro and Pancho. The mascot grew from that: a figure in a sombrero, poncho and large mustache, a caricature of a Mexican bandito. Minstrel shows were still popular in Dillon County in the 1940s and 1950s, around the time Pedro was created, and the writer P. Nicole King argues that the character reflects how people of that era exoticized Mexico. Pedro has been called culturally offensive and racist. Schafer dismissed criticism that Pedro was an unfair characterization of Mexicans, arguing that the design was a light-hearted joke, and he denied that his attraction was racist, citing his record of hiring African Americans, helping them vote and standing up to the Ku Klux Klan. Over the years, billboards that some found racist and offensive were toned down, though the tongue-in-cheek voice remained.

## A Cameo on Every Road Trip

The attraction has turned up again and again on screen. Storyteller Jean Shepherd opened his TV movie The Great American Fourth of July and Other Disasters with a trip here, stopping at a fireworks market called Fort Pedro. In the 1999 film Forces of Nature, Sandra Bullock and Ben Affleck's characters pull in as passengers on a tour bus. An episode of Eastbound & Down opens with a robbery that viewers assume is happening in Mexico, until the camera reveals the gift shop at South of the Border. Even Impractical Jokers: The Movie sends one of its stars here on horseback.

## Smaller, but Still Glowing

In 2025, the owners put roughly 30 acres of the property up for sale, including 20 acres of undeveloped land, a motel building, the former casino and convention center buildings and a former amusement park space. The rest of South of the Border is slated to keep operating as it is. Employees of every background are traditionally called "Pedro." In spirit it belongs with Wall Drug, a similar attraction off Interstate 90 in South Dakota: a roadside stop whose own advertising, repeated mile after mile, became part of the destination.

## Flight Context

South of the Border sits at approximately 34.50°N, 79.31°W, at the junction of Interstate 95 with US 301 and US 501 in Hamer, Dillon County, South Carolina, just south of the North Carolina state line. The sombrero-topped observation tower (roughly 200 feet, though sources vary) and the brightly colored complex beside the interstate stand out clearly from 1,500 to 3,000 feet AGL, especially at dusk when the signs are lit. Dillon County Airport (KDLC) is about 4 nm southwest, and Lumberton Regional Airport (KLBT) about 14 nm northeast. Florence Regional (KFLO) lies about 28 nm southwest. Follow I-95 as a navigation line; the attraction is hard to miss where it meets the state border.
