# Market House (Fayetteville, North Carolina)

Built on the ashes of North Carolina's old state house, Fayetteville's arcaded Market House served as town hall and marketplace, including as a place where enslaved people were traded, and remains a contested landmark.

## Introduction

Four streets come together in downtown Fayetteville, North Carolina, and where Green, Person, Gillespie and Hay meet, traffic has to go around a building. The Market House sits in the middle of the rotary, a two-story brick block with an open, arched ground floor and a clock tower on top. It has stood there since the early 1830s, and through the generations it has remained the center of the city. It is also a place with a hard history, one that still stirs strong feelings.

## Rising from the Fire of 1831

The ground under the Market House once held a state house. Fayetteville never became North Carolina's capital, but the town built a state house here in hopes of it, and the General Assembly met in Fayetteville in 1789, 1790 and 1793; the 1789 convention that ratified the Federal Constitution met here too. The old town hall stood on the same ground. In 1831 a devastating fire swept through Fayetteville. It destroyed the state house and town hall along with, by one account, some 600 houses and the Fayetteville Academy. Rebuilding the old state house made little sense, so the town built something it needed more: a market house, with covered space on the ground floor for commerce, and meeting rooms above where town and county business could be done. The city dates the building to 1832, though some listings give 1838, and it served as town hall and general market until the early 20th century.

## An Old-World Form

The design came from across the Atlantic. An open arcade below and a meeting hall above is a standard market house format that originated in the British Isles and also became common in New England. Fayetteville's version has a five-bay arcaded ground floor whose arches are symmetrical arrangements of round and lancet-pointed openings framed in stone. Parts of the arcade extend beyond the upper block as single-story wings topped with balustrades. Above the hip roof rises a tower holding a belfry and clock, crowned by a cupola and weathervane. The building resembles market houses in South Shields, Stockton-on-Tees and Abingdon-on-Thames in England, and bears a striking likeness to the one built in 1790 in Hillsborough, County Down, in Northern Ireland.

## What Was Sold Here

A market house is a place of trade, and in antebellum North Carolina trade included human beings. The Market House was widely used as a market for goods and livestock, and those sales sometimes included enslaved people, whom the law of the time classed as property. Because of its role as the town's general trading place, it is assumed that enslaved people were bought and sold there. A study by Duke University professor John Cavanagh found that sales near this location were spaced on average about two months apart, "if that frequently," and that in most instances very few enslaved people were involved in each transaction. Few, though, is not none. Each such sale tore a person away from the life and people they knew.

## Landmark and Lightning Rod

The Market House was declared a National Historic Landmark in 1973. In recent years the city has been repurposing it as a venue for historical information and multicultural exhibits, with a state-funded renovation that the city expected to finish in fall 2026. Its meaning, though, has never been settled. On May 30, 2020, during protests over the death of George Floyd, many protesters in Fayetteville gathered at the Market House, a building whose history includes the trading of enslaved people. That day it was set on fire and its windows were smashed. The building survived, still standing in its traffic circle as it has for nearly two centuries, a reminder both of the city's past as a meeting place of state government and a market town and of the enslaved people who were traded in that market.
