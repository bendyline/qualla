# Battle of Tearcoat Swamp

A midnight charge through the South Carolina lowcountry hands Francis Marion another bloodless victory and helps cement his legend as the Swamp Fox.

**Location:** 33.8030, -80.1250

**Categories:** American Revolutionary War, Battles, South Carolina, Clarendon County, Francis Marion, Southern Campaign, 1780

## Introduction

The Loyalists were playing cards by lamplight when Marion's scouts crept close enough to count them. It was after midnight on October 25, 1780, in the fork of the Black River, and Lieutenant Colonel Samuel Tynes had bivouacked his new recruits with Tearcoat Swamp at their backs - a position he thought made them invisible. Two boys, sent ahead to watch, slipped back to report that the camp was barely awake and the sentries lax. Francis Marion already had his plan. He had used it before.

## The Williamsburg Militia

By the autumn of 1780 the Continental Army had effectively abandoned South Carolina. After the catastrophe at Camden in August, regular American resistance in the state collapsed. Into the vacuum stepped Francis Marion, a lieutenant colonel in his late forties who had grown up in the Pee Dee country and knew its swamps the way other men knew their own kitchens. His Williamsburg Militia rarely numbered more than seventy. They were citizens, farmers, and enslaved men who fought because they chose to, with their own horses and their own guns, free to leave whenever they wished. They had no uniforms, little powder, and almost no support. What they had was the country itself, and a commander who understood how to use it.

## A Rumor and a Ride

Marion's offensive began with a letter. General Horatio Gates - the recently humiliated loser at Camden - wrote from North Carolina encouraging him to keep up the pressure. Marion moved to Brittons Neck and called for volunteers. The country was skeptical at first; only when he threatened to ride north for good did one hundred and fifty-two men commit. Across the river country, Major General Nisbet Balfour had ordered Tynes to raise and train a Loyalist militia in the High Hills. Tynes drew arms and supplies from Camden and made camp near Tearcoat Swamp, intending to build his force. Marion learned of it and decided to strike before Tynes could grow. He told no one - not even his own officers - and spread a rumor that he was riding for McCallum's Ferry. On the morning of October 25, his column quietly turned the other way.

## The Trident

They rode hard all day and forded the Black River well after dark. The scouts returned with the news Marion wanted: a few card players awake, the rest of the Loyalists asleep, the sentries casual. He waited until midnight, when the camp would be deepest in sleep, then divided his men into three groups. It was the same tactic that had worked at Black Mingo nearly four weeks earlier - a trident, hitting left, right, and center at the same instant. Marion took the center himself. At his pistol shot the three columns charged out of the swamp on horseback, firing and yelling. The Loyalists never had time to form. Six were killed, fourteen wounded, twenty-three captured, eighty muskets taken along with horses and food. Marion lost two horses. The battle was over in the time it took to load a pistol.

## The One Who Got Away

What Marion had not done was capture Tynes. The Loyalist commander escaped into Tearcoat Swamp itself - the same wetland he had relied on for protection - and disappeared into the dark. It was the second time Marion had won the field and lost the man at the top. He believed Loyalist leaders, more than their soldiers, were the spine of the British irregular war in the South; cut them down and the rest would dissolve. He later assigned Captain William Clay Snipes to hunt Tynes, and Snipes eventually ran him to ground. But for now, several of the twenty-three captured Loyalists were so impressed by Marion's men - their discipline, their nerve, the speed of the strike - that they switched sides. The Salem-area Loyalist movement, briefly real, was broken.

## The Swamp Country Today

Tearcoat Swamp still folds along the Black River in present-day Clarendon County, a tangle of cypress and tupelo and tea-colored water that looks much as it did in 1780. The fork where Tynes camped is still wild. Marion would become legend in his own lifetime - the Swamp Fox, said to vanish into terrain no British regular dared follow - and the small fights at Black Mingo, Tearcoat Swamp, and Halfway Swamp helped keep Patriot hope alive in a state the war had nearly emptied. Eventually Marion's men would join Nathanael Greene's army for the broader Southern Campaign. Many never wore a uniform. They went home when the war went home.

## Flight Context

Approximate battle site at 33.80N, 80.13W, in the fork of the Black River in modern Clarendon County, South Carolina. Cruise at 3,000-4,500 feet AGL for a sense of the swamp country Marion exploited; the dark winding ribbons of the Black River and its tributaries are obvious from altitude. Nearest airports: Sumter Airport (KSMS) 25 miles west, Manning Field (Santee Cooper Regional - KMNI) 18 miles southwest, and Florence Regional (KFLO) 35 miles northeast. The flat Atlantic coastal plain stretches in all directions; Lake Marion is visible to the south.
