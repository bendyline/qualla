# Galivants Ferry, South Carolina

A 1792 river crossing on the Little Pee Dee became a tobacco empire's headquarters and the stage for a political stump meeting whose tradition reaches back to 1876.

**Location:** 34.0564, -79.2464

**Categories:** Unincorporated communities in Horry County, South Carolina, Tobacco history, Political history of South Carolina, Historic districts on the National Register of Historic Places, Ferries

## Introduction

When South Carolina first wrote this crossing into law in 1792, the fees were set in British pounds sterling. The spot was called Elirsee's Landing then, and the right to run the ferry was vested with Richard Gallivant for an initial fourteen years. Three years later, a new act named the landing again and priced the crossing in U.S. dollars, a small ledger entry that captures a young country finding its footing. The ferryman's name stuck to the place. Today Galivants Ferry is an unincorporated community on the Little Pee Dee River, west of Aynor on U.S. Route 501, and its story runs from ferry tolls to tobacco barns to campaign speeches.

## The Road Through the Swamp

In the 1790s this corner of the state belonged to Kingston County, with its seat at Conwayboro. The road and ferry here were the main way into Kingston from Liberty County, today's Marion County, so both were kept up at public expense for the good of all citizens. In the 19th century, the first wood plank road was laid through the Pee Dee swamp to reach the ferry. The country beyond it stayed rough. Unlike the large plantation farms of Marion County, western Horry County changed little from its pioneer days, an agrarian economy scratched out of sandy, swampy earth. Rivers and swamps cut the area off so thoroughly that its people were sometimes said to live in the "Independent Republic of Horry." When the Little Pee Dee runs low, the remains of an old fertilizer barge still show just upstream of the present highway bridge, looking much like the ferry barges of the early 1800s.

## An Orphan's Fortune

Joseph William Holliday's life began badly. Orphaned young, he watched his guardian squander his share of a sizable inheritance, and he ended up working for uncaring relatives as an indentured servant. He educated himself and worked his way to prosperity anyway. In 1852 he came to Horry County, leasing 9,000 acres of pineland at Pot Bluff on the Waccamaw River for turpentine. The longleaf forests bled sap that became turpentine, pitch and tar, shipped down the Pee Dee to Georgetown to protect wooden hulls from marine worms. The Civil War destroyed that business by 1865, and Holliday moved to Galivants Ferry. In 1869 he opened a store on the riverbank that grew into western Horry County's main source of farm supplies. He is perhaps best remembered for introducing the flue-cured tobacco that the county would come to be known for.

## Tobacco, Scrip and Sharecroppers

His son George J. Holliday, a Harvard graduate and grandson of Senator R. G. W. Grissette, came home and expanded the business with stores in Aynor and Jordanville. When the boll weevil ravaged cotton across the state and region, George experimented with tobacco in 1900 and spread its cultivation through the area by the 1920s. Horry County came to be recognized as South Carolina's largest tobacco producer, and George Holliday was described as the largest tobacco grower in America. Much of that crop was raised by families living on the land and splitting the harvest with the owner, generally half and half. At its peak, the Holliday farms supported 1,200 to 1,500 people. During the Great Depression, when cash had all but vanished, Holliday issued his own scrip, which locals could exchange for goods at the Holliday stores.

## Speeches from the Stump

In 1876, General Wade Hampton, a Civil War veteran then running for governor, spoke at a political gathering in the Galivants Ferry district. It was the start of a tradition, though a Horry County Historical Commission marker dates the stump meetings themselves to 1880, when Joseph William Holliday began hosting Democratic candidates at his store, and says they have been held in spring every two years since. Campaign speeches here were known as "stump speakings," and through its early-20th-century heyday Galivants Ferry kept up the Old Democratic Primary campaign speeches. In 2004, Galivants Ferry marked 128 years as what it called the longest-running Democratic campaign speech site in America. The tradition has reached into national politics, too: ahead of the 2020 primaries, in September 2019, presidential hopefuls including Joe Biden and Pete Buttigieg spoke at the stump.

## What the Barns Remember

The Galivants Ferry Historic District joined the National Register of Historic Places in 2001. Its buildings read like an inventory of a farming world: a church, the owners' residences, a grist mill, a packhouse, storehouses, the dwellings where tenant and sharecropping families lived, and barns of several kinds, including the big red barn. The two-story white brick John Monroe Johnson Holliday House was completed in 1950, modeled on a house George Holliday built in the 1920s that burned in 1943. George's sons Joseph and John Monroe carried on the farming and the stores, and the Hollidays still own farmland across western Horry County and still host the stump at their store, with a fifth generation, by one count, carrying on, even as tobacco fades from the fields and the old world it built slips away.

## Flight Context

Galivants Ferry lies at approximately 34.06°N, 79.25°W, where U.S. Route 501 crosses the Little Pee Dee River west of Aynor in Horry County, South Carolina. The river's wooded swamp corridor is the key landmark; follow US 501 to the bridge and look for the barns and farm buildings of the historic district nearby. A viewing altitude of 1,500 to 3,000 feet AGL shows the crossing well. Marion County Airport (FAA: MAO, KMAO) is about 9 nm northwest and Conway-Horry County Airport (FAA: HYW, KHYW) about 15 nm southeast. Florence Regional (KFLO) is about 25 nm west, and Myrtle Beach International (KMYR) about 28 nm southeast. Expect summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms near the coast.
