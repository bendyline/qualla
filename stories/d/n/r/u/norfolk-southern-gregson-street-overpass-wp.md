# Norfolk Southern–Gregson Street Overpass

Durham's railroad bridge, with just 11 feet 8 inches of clearance until 2019, peeled the roofs off so many trucks that a neighbor's cameras turned it into an international internet star - the 'Can Opener.'

**Location:** 35.9991, -78.9101

**Categories:** Railroad bridges in North Carolina, Bridges with frequent bridge strikes, Viral videos, Transportation in Durham, North Carolina, Bridges completed in 1940

## Introduction

Some bridges are famous for their beauty. This one is famous for a number: 11 feet 8 inches. That was the clearance under the Norfolk Southern railroad bridge over South Gregson Street in downtown Durham, North Carolina, and for decades trucks, buses and RVs that were taller than that kept driving into it anyway. Roof fixtures were torn away. Sometimes whole truck roofs were sheared off. The span earned nicknames that said everything - the "Can Opener" and the "Gregson Street Guillotine" - and a man with a few video cameras turned it into an international internet star.

## A 1920s Design in a Truck-Sized World

The bridge was designed in the 1920s and, by most accounts, built in 1940 by the Southern Railway (the News & Observer dates its opening to the mid-1920s), carrying passenger and freight trains past industrial buildings that once housed tobacco and textile businesses. Today it also serves as the northbound access to the nearby Durham Amtrak station. Its clearance was standard for its day. Standards changed: since 1973, new bridges have had to offer at least 14 feet, but older bridges were never required to be rebuilt to match. Trouble arrived as trucks grew. By the 1960s, several low bridges in Durham were hampering local industry, and the Gregson Street crossing was singled out as "the granddaddy stopper-of-them-all." In 1968 alone it took at least seven truck strikes, causing $20,000 in damage. North Carolina tried to fix the problem but could not obtain federal funding. Since the railway could not afford to rebuild the bridge, the accepted remedy of the era was to dig the road deeper instead.

## The Can Opener Effect

Instead of being rebuilt, the bridge got armor. A heavy steel crash beam stands in front of the span, and it does exactly one job: it saves the bridge. It does nothing for the trucks. When an over-height vehicle hits it, the beam produces what has been described as a "can opener effect," peeling the top of the truck back from its frame like the lid of a sardine can. The beam itself has been struck so often that it has had to be replaced at least once. Geography adds to the trap. Peabody Street runs parallel to the tracks and meets Gregson just before the bridge. Large trucks are allowed on Gregson as long as they turn onto Peabody before the overpass, so over-height trucks have a perfectly legitimate reason to be heading toward a bridge they cannot clear.

## The Man With the Cameras

In April 2008, Jürgen Henn, who works in a nearby office, mounted video cameras to record the collisions from several angles. He has since captured more than 180 crashes and posted them on YouTube. The videos first caught the eye of a local TV station and then spread around the globe - to CBC Radio's As It Happens in Canada, to Stuff in New Zealand, to news.com.au in Australia, to newspapers in Israel, Peru and Denmark, to radio in Ireland and Italy, and to French television news. The bridge has also appeared in a video by YouTuber videogamedunkey and in the music video for M.J. Lenderman's song "Wristwatch." Amid all the wreckage, one statistic stood out: a March 2014 report counted only three injuries at the bridge, which made rebuilding it a low priority.

## A Red Light and Eight Inches

Durham kept trying. In May 2016 the yellow warning beacons were replaced with a traffic signal. When sensors detect an over-height vehicle, the light cycles to red and a sign on the mast arm flashes "OVERHEIGHT MUST TURN" in white. The system went live on May 12, 2016, and no crashes occurred until July 7. Then trucks kept hitting the bridge - perhaps because local buses fit underneath, perhaps because drivers assumed the warning was meant for someone else, and sometimes because an RV's air conditioner or vent was too small for the sensor to notice. In October 2019, the North Carolina Railroad Company finally raised the bridge by 8 inches, a $500,000 project that lifted the clearance to 12 feet 4 inches, the most possible without disturbing the grades of nearby crossings. Gregson Street was closed for about two weeks, from October 23 into early November, but the lift itself was planned as a single eight-hour job and was carried out at the end of October. Vehicles too tall for the new clearance still find it, though the state Department of Transportation told the News & Observer in 2024 that the bridge is hit "less than a half dozen a year," about half the rate of a decade earlier.

## Flight Context

The Gregson Street overpass is at approximately 35.9991 N, 78.9101 W on the western edge of downtown Durham, North Carolina, where the railroad crosses South Gregson Street near the Durham Amtrak station. From 2,000-3,000 ft MSL, follow the east-west rail corridor through downtown Durham; the bridge itself is small, so use the rail line and downtown's taller buildings as references. Raleigh-Durham International Airport (KRDU) lies about 9 nm to the southeast; check the sectional chart for its controlled airspace and expect to work with Raleigh-Durham Approach. Summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are common.
