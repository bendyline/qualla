# Order of Gimghoul

A UNC secret society founded in 1889 built a stone castle in Chapel Hill around the legend of Peter Dromgoole, a young man who vanished from Chapel Hill in 1833 - and whose supposed blood, the story goes, still stains a rock by the castle door.

**Location:** 35.9119, -79.0361

**Categories:** Collegiate secret societies, University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill student organizations, Student organizations established in 1889, Ghost stories, Castles

## Introduction

In front of a stone castle at the end of Gimghoul Road in Chapel Hill, North Carolina, sits a rock that is supposedly stained red forever. According to legend, it was used to hide the body of Peter Dromgoole, a young man who came to Chapel Hill to enter the University of North Carolina and disappeared in 1833, and his ghost still haunts the grounds. The castle belongs to the Order of Gimghoul, a collegiate secret society that built its identity around that legend. The truth about Dromgoole is probably far less bloody than the story. The society, and its castle, turned the story into something lasting anyway.

## The Legend of Miss Fanny

The tale that grew up around Dromgoole's disappearance centers on a Chapel Hill girl known only as "Miss Fanny." Supposedly, Dromgoole fought a duel to win her hand and was killed. From there, the retellings diverge. In one version, Miss Fanny visited his grave every night until she died of sorrow. In another, she held his head in her arms as he died. The reality appears to have been far more ordinary. Dromgoole is believed to have left for Europe after failing his entrance exams, or to have joined the Army, possibly under the name of his roommate, John Buxton Williams. The legend may have borrowed, at least in part, from a real duel involving Peter's uncle, George C. Dromgoole. Whatever its origins, the romance stuck, and the story of a vanished young man became a Chapel Hill ghost tale that has outlived everyone who knew him.

## Five Students and a Ghoul

In 1889, five UNC students - Robert Worth Bingham, Shepard Bryan, William W. Davies, Edward Wray Martin and Andrew Henry Patterson - founded the Order of Gimghoul. Tradition holds that the order embraced the "Dromgoole legend and the ideals of Arthurian knighthood, and chivalry." Its emblem leans into the spooky side of that inheritance: a ghoul with a wicked grin, perched on a column with a triple foundation. For all the mystery of the name, the order appears to be exactly what it seems. By all accounts it is social, and it is believed to have no clandestine agenda. Its archives are held at UNC's Wilson Library, though records less than fifty years old can be consulted only with the order's permission.

## A Castle Built on a Story

The society's headquarters is Hippol Castle, also called Gimghoul Castle, and even its origins carry a whiff of uncertainty. Sources disagree about exactly how old it is and who built it. By one account, it was finished in 1926, its elaborate stonework the handiwork of Waldensian stonemasons from Valdese, North Carolina, a town settled by Italian immigrants in the 1890s. All sources agree that construction cost more than $50,000. To pay for it, the order sold 35 acres of its property, land that later became the Gimghoul Neighborhood Historic District. The castle stands off-campus at the end of Gimghoul Road, not far from the Old Chapel Hill Cemetery near Carmichael Auditorium. According to legend, it sits on or near Dromgoole's grave.

## What the Castle Is Worth Now

Today the castle is a contributing building in the Chapel Hill Historic District. Real estate records show the 2.15-acre site owned by a non-profit corporation, the Order of the Gimghoul, with a taxable value of more than $1 million. It is quite a legacy for a young man who, in all likelihood, simply left town. Yet the legend endures because it offers something the dull truth cannot: a doomed duel, a grieving sweetheart and a stain that will not wash away. The rock still sits directly in front of the castle, and whether anyone believes the legend or not, everyone who hears it knows what that stain is supposed to mean.
