# Silent Sam

For 105 years a bronze Confederate soldier stood at the front door of UNC-Chapel Hill, until decades of protest over its white-supremacist origins ended with protesters pulling it down in 2018.

**Location:** 35.9138, -79.0522

**Categories:** Removed Confederate States of America monuments and memorials, University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, 1913 sculptures, Civil rights protests, Monument and memorial controversies in the United States

## Introduction

The bronze soldier carried a rifle but no cartridge box on his belt. Historian Adam Domby suggests the missing box came from the sculptor's unfamiliarity with 19th-century weaponry, not from any message of peace. "In fact, he was never meant to be silent," Domby says, "and those who organized, funded, and dedicated the monument would be horrified at the thought that future generations would see Sam as a pacifist." From 1913 until the night protesters pulled him down in 2018, he stood on McCorkle Place at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, a spot described as "the front door" of the university.

## A Monument Facing North

The North Carolina chapter of the United Daughters of the Confederacy made a Confederate monument at a Southern university its goal in 1907, and UNC approved the request the next year. Alumni, the UDC and the university spent until 1913 raising the $7,500 that Canadian sculptor John A. Wilson charged, a discount from his $10,000 asking price. The statue had been meant for 1911, the 50th anniversary of the war's start. Wilson used a young Boston man, Harold Langlois, as his model, and the soldier was set to face north, toward the Union. A plaque dedicated it to the university's sons who fought in the war, invoking their "great commander" and the line "duty is the sublimest word in the English language," from a letter attributed to Robert E. Lee that was exposed as a forgery in 1914. At the unveiling on June 2, 1913, before a reported crowd of 1,000, Governor Locke Craig, university president Francis Venable and UDC members praised the students who had left to fight for the Confederacy, and the band played "Dixie."

## The Speech in the Archives

Another speaker that day was industrialist and UNC trustee Julian Carr. Historian W. Fitzhugh Brundage describes Carr as having "unambiguously urged his audience to devote themselves to the maintenance of white supremacy with the same vigor that their Confederate ancestors had defended slavery." Carr praised the Confederate soldier as a defender of the "Anglo Saxon race," and he bragged of whipping a Black woman "until her skirts hung in shreds." Carr did not name her. Domby uncovered the speech in the university archives in 2009 and, as a UNC history graduate student, highlighted it in a 2011 letter to The Daily Tar Heel. It became a galvanizing force for those calling for the statue's removal. The name Silent Sam, first recorded in the student newspaper in 1954, had long since stuck.

## Half a Century of Protest

The monument drew debate from the 1960s on. Students argued over its meaning in the letters pages in 1965, the Black Student Movement demonstrated there in the early 1970s, and in 1997 a Martin Luther King Jr. Day march for the university's housekeepers ended at its base. In 2015, when a pro-Confederate rally came to defend the statue, it was met by three times as many counterdemonstrators, most of them students. One student handed out 158 copies of W. E. B. Du Bois's The Souls of Black Folk, 158 being, by that student's count, the years between the university's founding and its admission of Black students. The push for removal took off in earnest in August 2017, when about 800 people rallied on the first day of the school year. The university spent $390,000 on security for the statue in a single year. In April 2018, a history graduate student who had helped lead the protests defaced it with red ink and her own blood, describing her act as giving the statue the context it lacked.

## Nine-Twenty on a Monday Night

On August 20, 2018, the night before classes began, a rally in support of that student marched through Chapel Hill. Protesters carried signs reading "For a world without white supremacy" and a list of victims of racial violence. The first entry read "Unnamed Black woman beaten by Julian Carr." At 9:20 p.m., Silent Sam came down, and the crowd cheered. Reaction split sharply. University and system leaders called it unlawful and dangerous, and a statewide poll found 70 percent of likely voters disapproved. The Charlotte Observer's editorial board called it civil disobedience, and editorials in Winston-Salem and Fayetteville blamed years of official inaction. Former chancellor James Moeser admitted that he "gave a private cheer when our students had the courage to do what I didn't have the courage to do."

## An Empty Place on McCorkle

The statue went into storage at an undisclosed location, and the pedestal and plaques were removed in January 2019, with Chancellor Folt citing public safety. A trustee proposal to house the statue in a $5.3 million history center on campus was rejected by the system's Board of Governors. In November 2019, UNC gave the statue to the Sons of Confederate Veterans with a $2.5 million trust for its care, a deal struck without public notice that critics called a back-room arrangement. In February 2020, the judge who had approved it overturned the settlement, ruling that the group lacked standing, and later ordered the statue and the remaining $2.42 million returned to the university system. UNC System interim president Bill Roper said the statue "will not go back on campus."

## Flight Context

The former site of Silent Sam lies on McCorkle Place, in the oldest part of the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill campus beside Franklin Street, the town's main downtown street, at approximately 35.91°N, 79.05°W. The statue and pedestal are gone, so the spot is identified by the campus around it rather than a monument; the historic campus core along Franklin Street is the landmark from 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL. Raleigh-Durham International Airport (KRDU) is about 13 nm east, and Raleigh Executive Jetport at Sanford-Lee County Airport (FAA: TTA, KTTA) about 20 nm south. The former Horace Williams Airport in Chapel Hill is closed. Summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are common across the region.
