# Pauli Murray Family Home

The modest Durham house where civil rights pioneer Pauli Murray grew up - bought for $200 in 1898 - has been rescued from neglect and reborn as a center for history and social justice.

**Location:** 35.9928, -78.9158

**Categories:** African-American history of North Carolina, National Historic Landmarks in North Carolina, Houses in Durham, North Carolina, African-American historic house museums, Civil rights movement

## Introduction

The Pauli Murray Center for History and Social Justice is built around words, voices and the house itself - a modest home at 906 Carroll Street in Durham, North Carolina, where Murray was raised. The center's executive director has said it is not a house museum. It does not need to be. The story of the person who grew up here, a pioneering legal advocate for African-American civil rights who lived from 1910 to 1985, is large enough to fill any room.

## A $200 Parcel on Carroll Street

In 1898, Robert and Cornelia Fitzgerald, Murray's maternal grandparents, paid $200 for a one-acre parcel of land, larger then than it is today. The present house went up soon afterward. It has a side gable roof and German siding, with a gable rising over the front entrance and a porch to shelter it. Frankly, the house is not architecturally distinguished. Its importance lies in who lived there. After Murray's mother died, three-year-old Pauli came to live in this house and was raised by grandparents and aunts in a household that placed a strong emphasis on education. That emphasis would echo through a remarkable life.

## A Mind Ahead of Its Time

Murray went on to a long and varied career as a civil rights activist, legal theorist, writer and Episcopal priest. In the 1930s, Murray was at the forefront of attempts to gain admission to racially segregated institutions of higher learning, and experimented with acts of civil disobedience against segregation in the American South that would later become important elements of the successful civil rights movements of the 1950s and 1960s. Murray's legal treatises and court briefs shaped a generation of civil rights jurisprudence and legislation, and Murray wrote a seminal work describing in detail the segregationist laws of each state. The influence reached the Supreme Court: Ruth Bader Ginsburg, an ACLU lawyer before she became a justice, explicitly credited Murray as a contributor to some of her legal briefs advancing the rights of women.

## Raising the House

By the 2010s, the house was suffering from neglect, water damage and partial demolition. In 2015, help arrived from several directions. The National Trust for Historic Preservation named the property a national treasure, and in July the Trust gave the Pauli Murray Center a $4,500 grant to produce a historic structure report. Later that year, the information management company Iron Mountain provided further funding, and the center began stabilizing the foundation and repairing the exterior. By 2016 the work was well underway, funded by Iron Mountain and The Marion Stedman Covington Foundation. The house was jacked up six inches in the center, its foundation replaced, and new piers laid to replace ones damaged by runoff from the public cemetery nearby. Missing doors, windows and the front porch were replicated to match photographs taken between 1898 and 1906, and the exterior changed from white to a blue-gray shade matching the home's look in 1910. That same year, 2016, the property was designated a National Historic Landmark.

## Doors Open at Last

In 2017 the National Park Service awarded the center a $237,575 grant through its African American Civil Rights Grant Program, and the money went toward rehabilitating the interior. A 2017 Duke report said the house was expected to be open regularly to the public by 2020. The opening came later: the center held its grand opening on September 7, 2024, and describes itself as a place for exhibits, community dialogues, the arts, activism and workshops focused on education, history and social justice. It says it is open to the public on Wednesdays and Saturdays (check its online calendar for hours), with guided and self-guided tours, and its outdoor exhibits are free and open during daylight hours. The house where a child once learned to value education now teaches others what that child went on to accomplish.

## Flight Context

The house itself is small; from 2,000-3,000 ft MSL, use downtown Durham's buildings and the rail corridor to orient.
