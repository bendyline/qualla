# Duke Homestead and Tobacco Factory

The Durham farm where Washington Duke turned a raided farm's last bit of tobacco and a log barn into the beginnings of the American Tobacco Company, and where the people he enslaved are now part of the story.

**Location:** 36.0350, -78.9211

**Categories:** Museums in Durham, North Carolina, Historic house museums in North Carolina, American Tobacco Company, Open-air museums in North Carolina, National Historic Landmarks in North Carolina, Houses completed in 1852, Duke family residences, History of Durham, North Carolina, Buildings and structures in Durham, North Carolina, University museums in North Carolina

## Introduction

The first labels were written by hand. On a farm outside Durham, in a small log building, Washington Duke and his children packed smoking tobacco and tied on tags bearing the words "Pro Bono Publico," Latin for "for the public good." The business behind those tags grew far beyond the farm. From this modest homestead, the Duke family built the business that became the American Tobacco Company, for a time the largest tobacco company in the world, and a fortune that would give a university its name.

## A Farmer Near Durham Station

Washington Duke spent most of his life as a farmer. After the death of his first wife and his remarriage, he came in 1852 to this land near Durham Station and built the house that still stands here. By the time the Civil War broke out, the farm covered about 300 acres. It was only after his second wife died that he planted his first tobacco crop, and he soon decided to put his energy not into growing tobacco but into manufacturing it. The war interrupted everything. Duke was drafted into Confederate service, and Union soldiers raided the house. When he came home, little was left beyond a small amount of overlooked tobacco. There was one surprise waiting, though. Union troops had discovered the local Bright Leaf tobacco and liked it, and Duke saw in their appetite a market.

## Caroline and Jim

The homestead's history includes people who never chose to be here. At least two people were enslaved on the farm. In 1855 Duke paid $601 for Caroline, a girl the historic site believes was probably 11 or 12, and the purchase separated her from her mother, Gracy, and her sister. What became of her is uncertain, though when Duke died in 1905 he left property to his housekeeper, Caroline Barnes, whose mother was also named Gracy; the site considers it likely they were the same woman. Jim, born into slavery around 1830, was leased to Duke and other small farmers, and he grew and cured tobacco here. He resisted constantly, working slowly and running away at least three times. In June 1863 he fled with three others, trying to reach Union lines at New Bern, 140 miles away. He was back at the homestead by October. After the war he took the name James Cox, married a woman named Martha, and spent the rest of his life in the Durham area as a tobacco sharecropper.

## For the Public Good

With his children, Duke started processing smoking tobacco in a small log structure on the homestead. The product sold so well that within a few years the family was working out of its third and much larger factory. The barn-like buildings where the Dukes cured, processed and packed their tobacco still stand on the grounds. The very first building they worked in, a log barn, was lost to fire and rebuilt in 1931. In 1869 Duke's son Brodie opened a small smoking-tobacco factory in the town of Durham, close to its railroad and tobacco market, and in 1874 Washington Duke and his sons James and Ben moved the business into town as well. The family introduced cigarettes in 1881 to compete with loose-leaf tobacco, and a few years later began mass-producing them by machine.

## Empire, Breakup and a Museum

In 1890 the Duke business was incorporated as the American Tobacco Company, the largest tobacco company in the world until an antitrust suit broke it apart in 1911. Much of the family's wealth went into land and into fast-growing Southern industries such as electric power and textiles. Much went to philanthropy too, including Trinity College, which became Duke University. In the early 1930s Washington Duke's granddaughter, Mary Duke Biddle, bought the old farm and gave it to the university. The National Park Service designated the homestead a National Historic Landmark in 1966, and in 1974 it became a North Carolina State Historic Site. Today more than 40 acres of the original Duke lands are preserved. Guided tours of the homestead, for a small fee, take visitors through the restored 1852 house, with four furnished rooms, and the grounds hold the tobacco barns and factory buildings. In the visitor center, the Tobacco Museum traces the crop from farming to processing, and the long history that turned hand-labeled tobacco into an empire.

## Flight Context

Duke Homestead lies at 36.035°N, 78.921°W in Durham, North Carolina. From 2,500 to 3,500 feet AGL, look for the 40-plus acres of preserved Duke land, with the farmhouse and tobacco barns set among fields and trees. Raleigh-Durham International Airport (KRDU) is about 11 nm southeast, so expect airline traffic, check the sectional chart for the controlled airspace, and plan to work with Raleigh-Durham approach control. Raleigh Regional Airport at Person County (KTDF) is about 15 nm north-northwest, and Burlington-Alamance Regional (KBUY) about 27 nm west.
