# Morehead Planetarium and Science Center

Under a 68-foot dome at UNC Chapel Hill, astronauts from Mercury through Apollo-Soyuz learned to navigate by the stars - including eleven of the twelve men who walked on the Moon.

**Location:** 35.9140, -79.0502

**Categories:** Planetaria in the United States, Science museums in North Carolina, University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill landmarks, NASA Museum Alliance, Spaceflight history

## Introduction

The Gemini capsule was made of plywood, and it sat on a barber chair. At the Morehead Planetarium in Chapel Hill, North Carolina, technicians built that improvised mockup so it could tilt in pitch and yaw beneath an artificial sky, letting astronauts practice finding their way by the stars. Between 1960 and 1975, astronauts from the Mercury, Gemini, Apollo, Skylab and Apollo-Soyuz Test Project programs spent hours here in celestial navigation training. Eleven of the twelve astronauts who walked on the Moon trained under this dome, on the campus of the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill.

## A Projector From Sweden

Morehead Planetarium opened on May 10, 1949, after seventeen months of construction, and was dedicated in a ceremony the same day. It was the first planetarium in the South, the eighth built in the United States, and the first built on a university campus. The gift came from alumnus John Motley Morehead III, who put about $3 million into the facility. Eggers & Higgins, the firm associated with the Jefferson Memorial, designed it, and its cost reportedly made it the most expensive building in North Carolina at the time. Finding the heart of the machine took a trip abroad. Zeiss, the German maker of planetarium projectors, had lost most of its factories during World War II, and very few projectors were available. So Morehead traveled to Sweden, where he had once served as American minister, and bought a Zeiss Model II there. The planetarium's first show was titled Let There Be Light.

## Cookie Time

The astronaut sessions were intense, hours-long affairs, and the trainees fueled them with soft drinks, cookies and other snacks. Planetarium employees began calling the training sessions by a code name: "cookie time." Morehead technicians developed simplified replicas of flight modules and tools for the work, often from humble materials; one Apollo-era projector was assembled from old car wax cans. The planetarium's Gateway Gallery now tells the story of that training. Word of the visits sometimes leaked. Clothing designer Alexander Julian, a Chapel Hill native, recalls meeting Mercury astronauts during a visit to the planetarium when he was in junior high. For a college town, having the country's space pioneers drop in to study its star dome was a remarkable secret to keep - and not always a well-kept one.

## When the Instruments Failed

That training may, on occasion, have helped save astronauts' lives. Gordon Cooper drew on it after a power failure affected his navigational systems, and he went on to make the most accurate landing of Project Mercury. The crew of Apollo 12 called on it too. During ascent, their Saturn V rocket was struck by lightning twice, knocking spacecraft systems offline, and the astronauts had to configure their navigation systems using fixes taken manually. In both cases, skills practiced beneath a dome in North Carolina became something more than an exercise. When the electronics faltered, the sky was still there, and they knew how to read it.

## From Star Balls to Digital Domes

The theater, reseated in 2019, seats more than 200 people beneath a dome 68 feet across and 44 feet tall. A Carl Zeiss Model VI star projector arrived in 1969 and could display about 8,900 stars overhead; by April 2011 it remained in limited use, mostly for field trip groups, and it was removed that August. It had already been joined, in February 2010, by a fulldome digital video system - billed as the largest such installation in the southeastern United States - funded by GlaxoSmithKline, whose name the theater took. Until the late 1990s, the building also held one of the largest working Copernican orreries in the world. Exhibits have ranged from Zoom In: Science at the Extremes, opened in 2007, to The Ancient Carolinians, which interpreted 10,000-year-old artifacts from the Hardaway archaeological site before becoming Morehead's first traveling exhibit.

## Still Looking Up

Morehead remains a unit of the university, and a multi-year renovation that reopened the center in November 2020 added new galleries, a new entrance on McCorkle Place and a hands-on science space. Its reach extends well beyond Chapel Hill. The DESTINY Traveling Science Learning Program, founded by UNC in 2000, joined Morehead in 2006, and the PLANETS Portable Planetarium Program carries the dome experience to elementary schools too far away for a field trip. On the Science Stage, educator-led Science LIVE! shows use experiments and audience volunteers. At the east end of the building's top floor, the Morehead Observatory houses a 24-inch Perkin-Elmer reflecting telescope operated by UNC's Department of Physics and Astronomy. Narrow wavelength filters cut through Chapel Hill's city lights, allowing research in bright star spectroscopy and on the optical counterparts of gamma-ray bursts.

## Flight Context

Morehead Planetarium stands at approximately 35.9140 N, 79.0502 W on the campus of the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill. From 2,500-3,000 ft MSL, the planetarium's dome is a distinctive marker on the northern part of the wooded campus, close to Old East and the university's historic core. Raleigh-Durham International Airport (KRDU) lies about 13 nm to the east-southeast; check the sectional chart for its controlled airspace and expect to work with Raleigh-Durham Approach. Burlington-Alamance Regional Airport (KBUY) is about 22 nm west-northwest. Expect summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms.
