# Flagship Airlines Flight 3379

A short commuter hop from Greensboro ended in a Morrisville forest in December 1994, when a mistaken belief that an engine had failed led to a stall that killed fifteen of the twenty people aboard.

**Location:** 35.8347, -78.8669

**Categories:** 1994 in North Carolina, Accidents and incidents involving the British Aerospace Jetstream, Aviation accidents and incidents in the United States in 1994, Airliner accidents and incidents in North Carolina, Airliner accidents and incidents caused by pilot error, December 1994 in the United States

## Introduction

Greensboro to Raleigh is about 60 miles as the crow flies, the kind of flight that is over almost as soon as the seatbelt sign goes off. On the evening of December 13, 1994, twenty people boarded Flagship Airlines Flight 3379 for that short hop east. One of them was an 18-year-old college freshman on her way home for Christmas. The weather waiting at the other end was raw. Accounts of the evening describe fog, sleet and steady drizzle, with the temperature near 37°F. By the end of the evening, fifteen of the people aboard were dead, and the five who lived lay badly injured in a forest southwest of Raleigh–Durham International Airport.

## A Small Plane on a Short Route

Flight 3379 was a domestic scheduled passenger service flown under the American Eagle name. The aircraft was a British Aerospace Jetstream, a twin-turboprop commuter plane built in 1991 that had logged 6,577 flying hours. That night it carried 18 passengers and two crew members. As the Jetstream neared Raleigh–Durham, the captain came to believe that one of its engines had failed. He decided to abandon the approach and go around, climbing away to try again. There are set procedures for exactly that situation, a go-around with one engine out, and the captain did not follow them.

## The Go-Around

During the go-around the aircraft stalled. Without enough speed to keep flying, it came down into a forest southwest of the airport. Both crew members and 13 of the 18 passengers died. The five passengers who survived all suffered serious injuries. The freshman heading home for Christmas was one of them. Years later she remembered the scene in a few plain words: "It was cold. It was raining. I couldn't breathe." What made the loss harder to bear emerged in the months that followed: the engine failure the captain had reacted to had never happened.

## What the Investigators Found

On October 24, 1995, the National Transportation Safety Board released its report. The board concluded that the captain had incorrectly assumed that an engine had failed, and that he had then failed to follow approved procedures for an engine failure, a single-engine approach, a go-around and stall recovery. The report did not stop at the cockpit. Flagship Airlines management was faulted for failing to identify, document, monitor and remedy deficiencies in pilot performance and training. It is a finding that reaches beyond one pilot's decisions to the airline that trained and oversaw him. Local reporting at the memorial years later summed it up the same way: low visibility and an improperly trained pilot had contributed to the accident.

## A Place to Remember

In May 2016, families, friends and survivors gathered at Carpenter Park in Cary, then still unfinished, to dedicate a memorial to the passengers, crew, families and responders of Flight 3379. The memorial also honors American Eagle Flight 3378, the AVAir flight that crashed in Cary in 1988, killing all 12 people aboard. The survivor who had been heading home for Christmas in 1994 helped raise money for it, working with her parents and the nonprofit Family Assistance Foundation. For years, she said, people had told her that surviving meant she must have a purpose. At the dedication, as the names of the dead were read, she gave her answer: "My purpose is to honor and remember." The crash has also been retold for television audiences, in an episode of the Canadian documentary series Mayday titled "Turboprop Terror."

## Flight Context

The crash site is at 35.835°N, 78.867°W in Morrisville, North Carolina, about 4.7 nm southwest of Raleigh-Durham International Airport (KRDU), where the flight had been approaching to land. The memorial stands at Carpenter Park, on Morrisville-Carpenter Road in Cary. Because the site lies so close to Raleigh-Durham International, expect to work with Raleigh-Durham approach control, check the sectional chart for the controlled airspace over the area, and stay well clear of arrival and departure paths. Other nearby fields include Raleigh Executive Jetport at Sanford (KTTA), about 19 nm southwest, and Johnston Regional (KJNX), about 29 nm southeast. The flight's origin, Piedmont Triad International at Greensboro (KGSO), is about 54 nm to the west-northwest. December can bring fog, drizzle and near-freezing temperatures here, the same conditions reported on the night of the accident.
