# Wyatt Outlaw

Graham's first African-American town commissioner and constable was lynched in the courthouse square in 1870, and though Klansmen were indicted for the murder, no one was ever tried.

## Introduction

The paper has survived. Dated July 5, 1867, and signed by William Woods Holden, Wyatt Outlaw's commission into the Union League survives, and the Southern Historical Collection at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill holds a facsimile. No photograph of Outlaw himself is known to exist. What remains of him is mostly documents: a signature marked with an "X," a land deed for a church, the record of an office held, and the grand jury indictments that followed his murder in the courthouse square of Graham, North Carolina.

## A Woodshop on Main Street

Wyatt Outlaw was born around 1820. His mother was a Black woman named Jemima Phillips, and whether she was free or enslaved is not known. The 1870 census described him as "mulatto," the term of its day, and historians still disagree about whether he was born enslaved or free. They largely agree that by the time of the Civil War, he was free. Historian Carole Troxler believed he was most likely literate, though he chose to sign with an "X." By April 1866 he was living in Alamance County, where over the following year he stood as marriage bondsman for at least six men, some of them veterans of the U.S. Colored Troops. On Main Street in Graham, on property leased from the Republican clerk of court William Albright, he opened a woodshop. He built coffins and repaired wagons there, and he sold liquor too, which sometimes turned the shop into an unlicensed barroom that the local courts tried, without apparent success, to shut down.

## A Commissioner and a Constable

In October 1866, Outlaw traveled to Raleigh as one of two Alamance County delegates to a convention of the North Carolina Equal Rights League of Freedmen, which elected him to its five-member state board. He came home and organized a biracial chapter of the Loyal League, whose first work was building a church and a school in Graham. In September 1868 he was one of four trustees deeded land for the first Black church in Alamance County, later an African Methodist Episcopal congregation. Holden, building a statewide Republican organization ahead of his run for governor, commissioned Outlaw into the Union League, and after Holden took office in 1868 he appointed Outlaw to Graham's town commission. Outlaw later won reelection. He was the first African American to serve as a town commissioner and constable in Graham. In 1869, after an armed group called the White Brotherhood, a branch of the Ku Klux Klan, demonstrated in Graham and fired on public officials, the town created a patrol and a curfew. Outlaw became one of five armed constables.

## February 26, 1870

White residents angered at being policed by Black constables, according to one account, rode through Graham at night in Klan garb, and Outlaw and another constable fired on them without injuring anyone. Historian Otto Olsen noted that this story, carried in the Conservative press, was not believed in Alamance, even by the Klan's own leader in Graham. On the night of February 26, 1870, a party of men rode into town, dragged Outlaw from his home and hanged him from an elm tree in the courthouse square. The killers pinned a note to his body: "Beware you guilty both white and black." The next morning his body was carried into the courthouse and lay there through the day. That same night, Klansmen went to the home of another Black Republican, Henry Holt, and told his wife he should leave or share Outlaw's fate; Holt fled the county. Less than two weeks later, William Puryear, a Black man with a mental disability who said he had followed two of the killers home and could identify them, was found dead in a millpond.

## Indicted, Never Tried

Outlaw's murder, together with the assassination of white Republican State Senator John W. Stephens at the Caswell County Courthouse, pushed Governor Holden to declare Alamance and Caswell counties in a state of insurrection, setting off what became known as the Kirk-Holden War of 1870. Some Conservative newspapers called the lynching a Republican fabrication. Justice moved slowly. Superior Court Judge Albion Tourgée, holding court in Graham, pursued the killers, and an Alamance County grand jury brought indictments in the early 1870s: 18 men were charged with Outlaw's murder, and Wikipedia's account counts felony indictments against 63 Klansmen in all. Two of those arrested confessed; others fled. In 1873 the General Assembly passed an Amnesty Act that exonerated people responsible for the political violence of the preceding years, and the legislature repealed the law under which most of the indictments had been brought. No one was ever tried for the murder of Wyatt Outlaw. Tourgée later drew on the killing for his novel A Fool's Errand. Historian Carole Troxler observed that over the years Outlaw had been seen in many ways, as "a tragic victim" and "even a forgotten man." The documents he left behind say otherwise.
