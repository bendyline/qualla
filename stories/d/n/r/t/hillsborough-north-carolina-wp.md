# Hillsborough, North Carolina

On the Eno River, where the Great Indian Trading Path once crossed, Hillsborough has seen Occaneechi villages, the Regulator revolt, the road to the Civil War's largest surrender and one of NASCAR's first dirt tracks.

**Location:** 36.0706, -79.1042

**Categories:** Towns in Orange County, North Carolina, County seats in North Carolina, Regulator Movement, American Civil War sites, Historic districts in North Carolina, Populated places established in 1754

## Introduction

In 1781, Loyalist raiders under David Fanning swept into Hillsborough, seized the bell of the town clock and threw it into the Eno River. When the war ended, townspeople fished it out and repaired the clock. After years in a church steeple and a market-house tower, it moved into the cupola of the new courthouse in the 1840s, where it still stands. Dating to around the 1760s, it is said to be one of the oldest working tower clocks in the United States. It was long believed to be a gift from King George III, though no one now knows where it came from. That mix of survival, stubbornness and good stories runs through this Orange County seat of fewer than 10,000 people.

## Where the Trading Path Crossed

Native American groups had lived around Hillsborough for thousands of years before Spanish explorers arrived. The Great Indian Trading Path, used by generation after generation, crossed the Eno River here. At the time of European contact, Siouan-speaking peoples such as the Occaneechi and the Eno lived in the area, and the English explorer John Lawson recorded visiting "Occaneechi Town" when he passed through in 1701. The tribes suffered heavy losses to new diseases brought by Europeans and to conflicts with Native groups from the north, and most survivors were eventually pushed from their land by British and other European settlers. By 1712 the Occaneechi had left the Eno valley for Fort Christanna in Virginia, and the modern Occaneechi Band of the Saponi Nation, recognized by North Carolina in 2001, lives in Orange and Alamance counties today. English settlers built their town near the old Occaneechi village and its river fords, and in the 1980s archaeologists from UNC-Chapel Hill excavated a historic Occaneechi farming village nearby.

## Farmers Against the Courthouse

In the late 1760s, Hillsborough became the stronghold of the Regulator movement. Hard money was scarce, and inland farmers who could not pay their taxes watched their property seized. Some sheriffs kept tax money for themselves or charged twice for the same tax, and Governor William Tryon's lavish new mansion in New Bern deepened the anger. Underrepresented in the colonial legislature, the western farmers had little hope of redress, and eventually they took up arms, closed the Hillsborough court and dragged officials they considered corrupt through the streets. The town's ties to the Revolution run deep in other ways too. William Hooper, who signed the Declaration of Independence for North Carolina, was buried in the Presbyterian churchyard in 1790. His remains were later moved to Guilford Court House, but his original gravestone still stands in town.

## The Road to Bennett Place

From 1837 to 1857, Robert and Margaret Anna Burwell ran a girls' academy in their Churton Street home. Among the people held in slavery in the Burwell household was Elizabeth Keckley, who later became the dressmaker and confidante of Mary Todd Lincoln. When the Civil War came, Hillsborough residents were reluctant to support secession, but many local men fought for the Confederacy. In April 1865, General Joseph E. Johnston, with General Wade Hampton, used the Alexander Dickson home just outside town as a temporary headquarters, working from a small office building behind the house. After his March to the Sea, Union General William T. Sherman offered an armistice from Raleigh. The two generals traveled toward each other along the Hillsborough-Raleigh Road and met near present-day Durham at the Bennett family farmhouse. At their third meeting, on April 26, Johnston agreed to surrender 89,270 troops still active in the Carolinas, Georgia and Florida, the largest surrender of the war. The Dickson house was moved downtown in the early 1980s and now serves as the Hillsborough Visitors Center.

## A Town That Kept Its Buildings

More than 100 structures from the late 18th and 19th centuries survive here. The Greek Revival Old Orange County Courthouse, built between 1844 and 1846 by local builder John Berry, remains in use for court functions beneath the old clock. Ayr Mount, an 1815 Federal-era plantation house, is furnished with period antiques and fine art, and its mile-long Poet's Walk invites a slow stroll. Margaret Lane Cemetery, sometimes called the Old Slave Cemetery, is believed to have been bought as a burial ground in 1854 by Peter Brown Ruffin, a landowner west of town. Even the town's name has been restored. Shortened to "Hillsboro" in the 19th century, it went back to its original spelling after a vote in the late 1960s.

## Dirt Tracks and Duke Stone

Just outside town, Occoneechee Speedway, a mile-long dirt oval that Bill France Sr. built out of an old horse track, hosted the third race of NASCAR's inaugural 1949 season. Local clergy objected to Sunday racing, and the local governments eventually banned it; by most accounts France then took his dream of a superspeedway to Alabama, where Talladega opened in 1969. Today the old track is part of Eno River State Park, and a walking trail crosses the old track bed. Stone from the Duke Quarry near Hillsborough went into Duke Chapel and other buildings of Duke University's West Campus. And for its size, Hillsborough has been home to a high concentration of nationally known writers, including Lee Smith, Jill McCorkle, Allan Gurganus and Frances Mayes.

## Flight Context

Hillsborough lies at approximately 36.07°N, 79.10°W along the Eno River in Orange County, North Carolina. From 2,000 to 3,500 feet AGL, trace the Eno River to the compact historic downtown; Occoneechee Mountain State Natural Area lies close by. Raleigh Regional Airport at Person County (FAA: TDF, KTDF) is about 14 nm northeast, Burlington-Alamance Regional (FAA: BUY, KBUY) about 18 nm west, and Raleigh-Durham International (KRDU) about 19 nm southeast; stay clear of RDU's Class C airspace. Summer haze and afternoon storms are common across the Piedmont.
