# Palmer Memorial Institute

Founded in 1902 by Dr. Charlotte Hawkins Brown at Sedalia, near Greensboro, the Palmer Memorial Institute educated more than 1,000 African American students - and after it closed, its alumni fought to make it North Carolina's first state historic site honoring African American history.

**Location:** 36.0672, -79.6236

**Categories:** African-American history of North Carolina, North Carolina State Historic Sites, School buildings on the National Register of Historic Places in North Carolina, Historic districts on the National Register of Historic Places in North Carolina, 1902 establishments in North Carolina

## Introduction

After 1980, a new owner hoped to open a teacher's college on this campus in Sedalia, North Carolina, and gave up: the buildings were in poor condition and the money for repairs was not there. It was a hard fate for grounds that had once held a fully accredited, nationally recognized preparatory school. For nearly seventy years, the Palmer Memorial Institute, near Greensboro, had educated more than 1,000 African American students. Its rescue would begin with two of its own former students, and it would end with a first for North Carolina.

## A Chance Meeting, a New School

Charlotte Hawkins Brown was born in Henderson, North Carolina, on June 11, 1883, and was raised and educated in Cambridge, Massachusetts. There she met Alice Freeman Palmer, a former president of Wellesley College, by chance. Palmer was so struck by the young woman's dedication to her education that she paid for Brown's schooling. After one year of college, in 1901, Brown was hired to work at the Bethany Institute in Sedalia, a school run by the American Missionary Association. When Bethany closed a year later, Brown started a school of her own. It opened on October 10, 1902, and she named it for her benefactor: the Alice Freeman Palmer Memorial Institute.

## From Prep School to Empty Buildings

Palmer was a school for upper-class African Americans, and over the decades it earned full accreditation and a national reputation as a preparatory school. Brown's influence reached beyond the campus: in 1941 she published The Correct Thing To Do--To Say--To Wear. Wilhelmina Marguerita Crosson served as the school's president from 1952 to 1966, and Brown herself died on January 11, 1961. After the school closed, Bennett College purchased the campus. In 1980 it sold 40 acres of the main campus, including the major surviving buildings, to the American Muslim Mission, the community that followed Imam Warith Deen Mohammed. The new owners tried to establish a teacher's college there but abandoned the project because the campus was in such bad condition and the funds for repairs fell short.

## Two Alumnae Take Charge

In late 1982, two women began a campaign to win recognition for Dr. Brown's social and educational contributions, and specifically for the institute she built. One was Maria Cole, Dr. Brown's niece and the widow of singer Nat King Cole. The other was her friend Marie Gibbs of Greensboro. Both had studied at Palmer themselves. They sponsored meetings of Palmer alumni, enlisted support and met with North Carolina's Division of Archives and History to explore what might be done. Their work paid off quickly. With the help of North Carolina Senator Bill Martin, the 1983 General Assembly passed a special bill allowing Archives and History to plan a memorial to Dr. Brown. It would be the state's first African American state historic site. In November 1987 the memorial officially opened. The following year, 1988, the Palmer Memorial Institute Historic District was listed on the National Register of Historic Places, and in 1994 the Historic Sites Section completed exhaustive research on Brown and the institute and restored or stabilized several more structures.

## Canary Cottage and the Bell Tower

Today the restored campus buildings form the Charlotte Hawkins Brown Museum, part of the North Carolina Department of Natural and Cultural Resources. The museum connects Dr. Brown and her school to the larger story of African American women, education and social history, with an emphasis on what African American citizens contributed to education in North Carolina. Visitors begin at the Carrie M. Stone Teachers' Cottage, built in 1948, which serves as the visitor center and exhibit space on Dr. Brown, the institute and African American education in the state. From there, guided tours lead through Canary Cottage, Dr. Brown's own residence, furnished to reflect the 1930s and 1940s, when the school was at its peak. Dormitories, the dining hall, the bell tower, a teahouse and several teachers' cottages still stand across the grounds; the state site says they can be viewed on the campus but have no interior tours.

## Flight Context

The Palmer Memorial Institute campus at Sedalia lies at approximately 36.0672 N, 79.6236 W, east of Greensboro in Guilford County, North Carolina. From 2,500-3,500 ft MSL, look for the cluster of historic campus buildings among the fields and woods of the Piedmont between Greensboro and Burlington. Nearby airports: Burlington-Alamance Regional Airport (KBUY; about 7 nm east), Greensboro Executive Airport (FAA identifier 3A4, no ICAO code listed; about 8 nm south-southwest) and Piedmont Triad International Airport (KGSO; about 15 nm west, with scheduled service). Summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are common.
