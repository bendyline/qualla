# Stagville

The surviving core of one of the largest plantation complexes in the American South, where rare two-story slave quarters still stand at Horton Grove.

**Location:** 36.1169, -78.8378

**Categories:** African-American history in Durham, North Carolina, Archaeological sites in North Carolina, Archaeological sites on the National Register of Historic Places in North Carolina, North Carolina in the American Civil War, Houses on the National Register of Historic Places in North Carolina, Historic house museums in North Carolina, Plantation houses in North Carolina, Open-air museums in North Carolina, Museums in Durham County, North Carolina, Georgian architecture in North Carolina

## Introduction

The name came from a tavern. Thomas and Judith Stagg ran one on this spot in what was then eastern Orange County, and the place was probably already known as Stagville when the widow Stagg sold the land in 1787. What grew from that sale was something far larger: the heart of a Bennehan and Cameron plantation empire that would spread across four counties, cover roughly 30,000 acres, and hold hundreds of people in slavery. Today the state runs Historic Stagville on a small fraction of that land (71 core acres were donated in 1976, and the site's official page now lists 165 acres), and some of the most remarkable buildings there are not the owners' house but the homes of the people the families enslaved.

## The Merchant's Plantation

Richard Bennehan, a Virginia-born merchant, arrived in North Carolina in 1768 to manage a store on the Little River owned by a Scottish merchant, William Johnston. He began buying plantation land nearby in 1776, and in 1787 he bought the Stagg tract, where he built a new store and a dwelling. That house, the Bennehan House, dates to about 1787, with a large addition around 1799. By the end of the eighteenth century Bennehan was among the area's most successful planters, with 3,914 acres and 42 enslaved people of working age raising tobacco, grain and livestock. His store stood near the crossing of the road between Person and Wake counties and the Great Trading Path, and it prospered, serving for years as the Stagville post office. Bennehan moved in high circles too. He sat on the commission charged with building the State Capitol and served as a trustee of the University of North Carolina.

## An Empire Built on Bondage

In 1803 Bennehan's daughter Rebecca married Duncan Cameron, a lawyer and planter who became a leading figure in the state's courts, politics and banking. The couple settled at Fairntosh Plantation, a mile south. Rebecca's brother Thomas never married, and when he died in 1847 Stagville passed to the Camerons. Under Duncan and Rebecca's son Paul Cameron, it became part of one of the largest plantation complexes in the antebellum South, with lands in Granville, Orange, Person and Wake counties. The numbers are staggering. By one count, almost 900 enslaved African Americans lived across the holdings in 1860, and the state historic site puts the figure for the 1860s at more than 1,000. More than 100 of them lived at Stagville itself, most in the quarter called Horton Grove. The old Bennehan house, meanwhile, was demoted to an overseer's dwelling. The Camerons lived elsewhere, and the people who worked this ground lived here.

## The Houses at Horton Grove

The quarters at Horton Grove were built around 1850 and are two stories tall. They are well preserved, and the state describes their two-story, four-room design as rare; Wikipedia calls them the only two-story slave quarters remaining in North Carolina. Four of them survive, along with the excavated foundation of an enslaved family's house and a massive timber-framed barn from around 1860. Archaeologists working around the quarters have made significant finds that open a window onto the lives of the people who lived and worked here and across the Bennehan-Cameron holdings. The records name some of them. Virgil Bennehan was born enslaved at Stagville and freed in 1847. Mary Walker, enslaved by the Cameron family, escaped and fought for her own family's freedom. The Hargis siblings were forced from North Carolina to a Cameron cotton plantation in Alabama in 1844; after emancipation, they became landowners and helped found a free African American community there. The historic site describes what visitors find here as intertwined stories of bondage and wealth, injustice and resistance, slavery and freedom.

## After Freedom

Emancipation did not empty Stagville. Many formerly enslaved people and their descendants stayed on, working the land as sharecroppers. After Paul Cameron died in 1891, his son Bennehan Cameron kept the farm running, and it later passed to Bennehan's daughter Isabelle, who sold it in 1950. The Liggett & Myers Tobacco Company bought the property in 1954 and used it to raise field crops and livestock. In 1976, in cooperation with the Historic Preservation Society of Durham, the company donated 71 core acres to the state, including the Bennehan House, the Horton Grove houses and the great barn. The Bennehan House had been listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1973, and Horton Grove followed in 1978. The remaining farmland, together with the former Cameron plantations at Fairntosh and Snow Hill, later became the 5,200-acre Treyburn development. What the state kept is small by comparison, a fraction of the old holdings, but it is the part that bears witness.

## Flight Context

Historic Stagville lies at 36.117°N, 78.838°W in Durham County, North Carolina. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL, look for the preserved fields and woods of the state historic site, where the Bennehan House, the Horton Grove quarters and the great timber barn stand. Raleigh Regional Airport at Person County (KTDF) is about 12 nm northwest, and Raleigh-Durham International (KRDU) about 15 nm south; stay alert for traffic arriving and departing RDU. Triangle North Executive Airport (KLHZ) is about 25 nm east-southeast. The Piedmont's summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms make spring and autumn mornings the best time to fly over.
