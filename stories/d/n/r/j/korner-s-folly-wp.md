# Korner's Folly

A tobacco-ad muralist came home to Kernersville and built a brick house said to have begun as part stable, grew into a 22-room showroom of his design business, and hid a children's theater under its roof.

**Location:** 36.1153, -80.0789

**Categories:** Historic house museums, Victorian architecture, National Register of Historic Places, Theaters, North Carolina

## Introduction

Jule Gilmer Körner made his name painting bulls. He spearheaded one of the first national advertising campaigns, covering buildings and barns along the American East Coast with murals of the Bull Durham Smoking tobacco bull. Then, by the 1870s, he had moved back to his hometown of Kernersville, North Carolina, to start an interior decorating and design business and to build a house of his own. Completed in 1880, it is known as Körner's Folly, and the caption on one Library of Congress photograph sums up its reception: a mansion derided as excessive when its owner built it in this small town.

## A Carriageway Through the Parlor

The house is an eccentric brick dwelling of three and a half stories and some 6,000 square feet, capped with a shingled cross-gable roof. It measures 48 feet on each side, with four bays. Its first life was stranger still. The house is said to have served originally as a combination dwelling, stable and carriage house, with an open carriageway running straight through its center. Family changed that. After Körner married Polly Alice Masten and the couple had two children, Gilmer and Dore, he closed the carriageway and renovated the house into the floor plan it keeps today. A building that once let a carriage roll through the middle became a home arranged around the people living in it.

## A Showroom You Could Live In

Körner used the house to showcase his decorating business, and every room made the argument for him. The 22-room interior features unusual architecture and many examples of Victorian furniture and interior decoration. Visitors today can see original furnishings and artwork, 15 fireplaces, cast-plaster details, carved woodwork and elaborate hand-laid tiles. The whimsy did not stop at the back door. Also on the property stands a small brick outbuilding built as a small-scale version of the main house, and it served as the privy. Even the outhouse, in other words, was designed to match. For a man whose trade was persuading people how their rooms should look, the house worked as both home and portfolio.

## Cupids Park Under the Roof

The third floor holds the house's most charming surprise. Called "Cupids Park," it is a theater, and the museum calls it the first private little theater in America. Jule Körner converted the third-floor billiard room into the theater in 1897, for the "Juvenile Lyceum," a children's dramatics organization his wife Polly Alice had founded in the house the year before, which gave local children a stage of their own. That purpose survives. The theater is still used by local theatrical groups, and the museum hosts school and family programs, including puppet plays.

## From Folly to Landmark

The house once derided as excessive is now a protected piece of Kernersville's history. Körner's Folly was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1973, and it stands within the South Main Street Historic District. The Historic American Buildings Survey photographed it for the national record as well. The 22-room mansion operates as a historic house museum, owned and run by the Körner's Folly Foundation, a 501(c)(3) nonprofit organization, and it is open to the public for self-guided tours. The name stuck. So did the house, with its brick walls, its carved woodwork, its hand-laid tiles and its little theater still in place.
