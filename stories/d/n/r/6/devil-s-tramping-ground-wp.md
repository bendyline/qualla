# Devil's Tramping Ground

A clearing in the Chatham County woods where, legend says, the Devil paces a barren circle at night, and where the oldest written account says the grass grew thick.

**Location:** 35.5848, -79.4870

**Categories:** Geography of Chatham County, North Carolina, Landforms of North Carolina, Reportedly haunted locations in North Carolina, Campgrounds in North Carolina, Satan

## Introduction

Legend says nothing will grow here. The Devil paces this ring of bare earth on certain nights, the story goes, and nothing has grown inside it for a hundred years. Yet the oldest written description a skeptical investigator could find, a doctor's letter to a North Carolina newspaper in 1882, describes the inside of the circle as "covered with a thick growth of long wire-grass." Somewhere between that letter and today, the Devil's Tramping Ground in the woods near Bear Creek became the stuff of persistent local legend, and somewhere along the way the grass disappeared from the story.

## Where the Devil Walks

The site itself is a camping spot in a forest near the Harper's Crossroads area of Bear Creek, in Chatham County, on private land belonging to a family farm. The legends are well known in the surrounding communities. Objects left inside the ring overnight are said to disappear. In the stories, dogs yip and howl and refuse to go near it, and people who spend the night within its boundaries report strange happenings. It has been claimed that nothing has grown inside the 40-foot ring for a hundred years. Why would the Devil choose a clearing in rural North Carolina? John William Harden, a North Carolina newspaperman and author who later served as executive secretary to Governor R. Gregg Cherry, set down the local answer. Chatham natives, he wrote, say "the Devil goes there to walk in circles as he thinks up new means of causing trouble for humanity. There, sometimes during the dark of night, the Majesty of the Underworld of Evil silently tramps around that bare circle – thinking, plotting, and planning against good, in behalf of wrong."

## The Doctor's Letter

The skeptic Brian Dunning went looking for the earliest printed version of the tale, and the oldest he could find was a letter from an H.T. Ivy, MD, published in The Weekly Star on January 6, 1882. Ivy placed the spot about three miles from "the Randolph line" and said it had been known "to the oldest inhabitant and his grandparents." He described a circle about 25 feet across, "as perfect as though drawn with compasses," marked by a clean-cut path with another path running through its center. The surrounding country was red clay, rocky and nearly grassless. Inside the ring, though, the soil was sand mixed with clay, thick with wire-grass that never crossed the circling path. The locals, Ivy wrote, had "a superstitious dread of the place." He persuaded one of them to help him dig into it. About three feet down they had found nothing, and his helper, convinced the circle was supernatural, refused to dig any further.

## Testing the Dirt

In 1946 the Raleigh News and Observer reported soil tests on the site by the W. A. Bridges Laboratory and the North Carolina Department of Agriculture, which found the soil acidic and "sterile." Decades later, a retired local soil scientist compared samples from near the fire pit with soil from outside and found higher pH and more zinc, copper and sodium, but not enough to affect fertility. He still suspected something beyond camping was at work. Dunning, working from photos, videos and aerial imagery, saw something plainer. Grass and low weeds cover most of the clearing, which is shaped less like a circle than, in his words, "a snake that swallowed a football." The truly bare ground lies around a well-used fire pit heaped with ash, broken glass, beer cans and scraps of burned wood. Campfires are hot, he pointed out, and foot traffic around them is heavy; look at any campground and every fire pit is ringed with bare dirt.

## A Legend That Keeps Walking

None of that has slowed the story down. The Devil's Tramping Ground turns up regularly on lists of unusual place names, and the horror novelist Poppy Z. Brite mentions it in two books, Lost Souls and Drawing Blood. Both are set at least partly in Missing Mile, a fictional North Carolina town. The site even goes by more than one name; some call it the Devil's Stomping Ground. By Dunning's account the path from the road is a short walk through the pines, and the clearing it leads to is modest, a patch of thin grass and a scorched ring of dirt. That is the whole of it. It has been a camping spot all the same, and the well-used fire pit suggests that people come out to spend the night inside the Devil's circle. The land is private: in a PBS North Carolina segment, the owner, whose family farms cattle there, said the site is private property, that there is a fee to visit, that it is open daily, and that overnight stays require a reservation, and a 2022 news report found the spot behind a gate with game cameras in the woods. Anyone planning a visit should check the family's own website first.

## Flight Context

The Devil's Tramping Ground lies at 35.585°N, 79.487°W, in the wooded hills of southwestern Chatham County, North Carolina, near the Harper's Crossroads area of Bear Creek. The clearing itself is small and hidden under the pine canopy, so treat this as an area overflight rather than a target you will spot; 2,500 to 3,500 feet AGL gives a good look at the rolling forest and farmland of the area. Siler City Municipal Airport (KSCR) is about 7 nm north, Raleigh Executive Jetport at Sanford (KTTA) about 19 nm east, and Asheboro Regional (KHBI) about 20 nm west-northwest. For larger fields, Piedmont Triad International (KGSO) is about 38 nm northwest and Raleigh-Durham International (KRDU) about 38 nm east-northeast. Summer afternoons in the Piedmont often bring haze and pop-up thunderstorms, so morning flights are usually clearer.
