# North Carolina Zoo

Spread across hundreds of acres of Randolph County woodland, the state's own zoo is described as the world's largest natural habitat zoo, and its work reaches from red wolves at home to gorillas in Cameroon.

**Location:** 35.6295, -79.7645

**Categories:** Zoos in North Carolina, Zoos established in 1976, Tourist attractions in Randolph County, North Carolina, Wildlife conservation, Asheboro, North Carolina

## Introduction

The chimpanzee who became the first hominid in outer space spent his final years in the woods of Randolph County. Ham arrived at the North Carolina Zoo in 1980 from the National Zoo in Washington, D.C., and lived here until his death three years later. He came at a turning point. That same year, the zoo's first African habitats opened to the public, the start of what the zoo bills as the world's largest natural habitat zoo: some 500 developed acres near Asheboro, home to roughly 1,700 to 1,800 animals of more than 200 species.

## Two Tortoises and a Plan

The idea began with a study. In 1967, the legislature created a nine-member commission to ask whether North Carolina should have a state zoo, and it concluded that a zoo was both feasible and desirable. A zoological society formed the next year to raise money and support, and after a two-year search among six candidate sites, led by State Representative Archie McMillan of Wake County, the commission chose Randolph County. The 1,371-acre site was donated to the state. The first animals, two Galapagos tortoises, arrived in 1973, and an interim zoo opened in 1974. Ground was broken for the Africa region in the spring of 1976, and its first five habitats opened in the summer of 1980. North America followed, beginning with Sonoran Desert habitats in 1993 and finishing in 1996. Today it is one of only two state-supported zoos in the country, the other being the Minnesota Zoo.

## Room to Roam

From the start, the zoo's philosophy was to show animals among plants in settings resembling the wild, and to give them space. The Watani Grasslands habitat alone covers about 40 acres. African elephants roam its savanna, while southern white rhinoceroses, gazelles, waterbucks, oryxes, kudu and bongos share a neighboring range designed to look like one continuous expanse. The zoo's chimpanzee troop, reported to be one of the largest of any zoo in America, lives in the Kitera Forest habitat, and western lowland gorillas inhabit the Forest Glade. On the North American side, the Rocky Coast recreates the Pacific Northwest shoreline for polar bears, harbor seals, Arctic foxes and horned puffins, part of what is reported to be the country's largest collection of Alaskan seabirds. Alligators lurk in the Cypress Swamp, bison and elk graze the Prairie, and red wolves have a home of their own. About 5 miles of paths connect it all, with free trams and buses linking the main areas for tired legs.

## Under Glass

Some habitats needed walls. The glass-domed Desert habitat shelters Gila monsters, Mexican beaded lizards, desert tortoises and racing roadrunners, with sand cats in their own enclosure and a nocturnal room of vampire bats and sidewinders. For years, the R. J. Reynolds Forest Aviary recreated a hot, humid tropical forest, with more than two thousand plants and free-flying birds overhead. It was known for hatching firsts, including the first U.S. hatchings of the golden white-eye, the red-faced liocichla and the African pied barbet. That same humidity eventually wore down the building. The aviary closed in January 2022 as a precaution against avian influenza detected in wild birds and poultry in the state, and in April the zoo announced the closure was permanent because of structural deterioration.

## Conservation Without Borders

The zoo's work stretches well past Asheboro. With the World Wildlife Fund and Cameroon's government, it tracks elephants with satellite collars to help design reserves around their most crucial habitat, a project honored by the Association of Zoos and Aquariums in 2008. In Cameroon and Nigeria, it helps map the rare Cross River gorilla, a subspecies once thought extinct. In Uganda, it has supported conservation education around Kibale National Park for more than a decade. Closer to home, zoo staff surveyed hellbender salamanders in the mountains of western North Carolina after steep population declines, and in 2001 helped move a threatened stand of endangered Schweinitz's sunflower out of the path of a road project. Since 1995, the zoo has been part of the red wolf recovery program, and two gorillas born in August 2012, Bomassa and Apollo, came out of its breeding work.

## A Third Continent

Growth continues. The zoo acquired adjacent forest in 2010, bringing its land to more than 2,000 acres, and in 2023 it welcomed more than a million visitors for the first time. Paid for by a mix of state funds and private donations, work on a new Asia region broke ground on August 17, 2022, with plans for tigers, Komodo dragons, king cobras, otters and gibbons, among other animals. The zoo first said the expansion would open in 2026; in October 2026 the state announced that Asia will open to the public at noon on March 1, 2027. At the entrances, art sets the tone: "Sum of the Parts," a pile of metal cubes, mostly shiny for living species, with a few rusted ones memorializing the extinct.

## Flight Context

The North Carolina Zoo lies at approximately 35.63°N, 79.76°W, southeast of Asheboro in Randolph County, about 25 miles south of Greensboro. From 2,000 to 3,500 feet AGL, look for the open habitat yards among the trees and the parking lots at each end of the park. Asheboro Regional Airport (FAA: HBI, KHBI) is about 6.5 nm west, and Piedmont Triad International (KGSO) in Greensboro about 29 nm north. Keep a respectful altitude over the animal habitats. Summer afternoons bring haze and pop-up thunderstorms over the Piedmont.
