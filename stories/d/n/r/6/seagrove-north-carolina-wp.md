# Seagrove, North Carolina

A tiny Randolph County town misnamed by a sign painter sits at the heart of a pottery tradition older than the American Revolution, with dozens of potteries along the roads around it.

**Location:** 35.5422, -79.7781

**Categories:** Towns in Randolph County, North Carolina, Pottery, Folk art, Towns in North Carolina, Craft traditions

## Introduction

By local accounts, the town was supposed to be called Seagraves. When the railroad came through, the community voted unanimously to name the station after Edwin G. Seagraves, the railroad official who had routed the line their way. Then, as local sources tell it, the sign painter ran out of room, dropped the final "s," and misspelled the rest for good measure. Seagrove it became. Today this town of fewer than 300 people in Randolph County carries a name far larger than its size, because for potters and collectors, Seagrove means clay, kilns and a craft that has been worked here since before the American Revolution.

## Clay Older Than the Nation

Seagrove's pottery tradition reaches back into the 18th century, before the American Revolution. Many of the first potters were immigrants from England and Germany, with Scots-Irish settlers also named in some accounts, and they made functional, glazed earthenware for everyday use. The local clay was of high quality, and traders had good access to the area, so the pottery earned a reputation. Then the market turned. The Industrial Revolution and modern food preparation cut demand for everyday earthenware. For a time, whisky jugs brought in good money, until the beverage was outlawed. By the early 20th century the potteries were in decline. Yet some families never stopped. The Cole, Auman, Owen, Teague and Albright families are described as eighth- and ninth-generation potters, carrying skills handed down across more than two centuries.

## From Jugtown to Greenwich Village

Rescue came from an unlikely direction. In 1917, Jacques and Juliana Busbee of Raleigh set out to revive the craft. Over several decades they hired Seagrove potters J. H. Owen, Charlie Teague and Ben Owen to make signature wares under the name Jugtown Pottery, sold first at the Village Shop the Busbees opened in Greenwich Village in New York City, and later at the Jugtown shop in Seagrove itself. Jugtown Pottery, which dates its start to 1917 and built its workshop in 1921, is still in operation and joined the National Register of Historic Places in 1999. The "Original" Owens Pottery, said to date to 1895, is older still. Around 1920, a second market appeared as motorists heading to Pinehurst, Southern Pines or Florida stopped to buy inexpensive souvenirs, and the potters shifted from purely utilitarian pieces toward more decorative ware.

## Tolls, Tracks and Pinto Beans

Roads have always shaped Seagrove's fortunes. Construction of the Plank Road began around 1849 to 1850, a 129-mile route of wooden planks eight feet long that carried riders, wagons and stagecoaches. Tolls ran from half a cent per mile for a rider on horseback to four cents per mile for a six-horse team. Once the railroad arrived, toll revenue dropped, and by 1862 the state had authorized the company to abandon the road, though parts of NC 705 still follow its path. Trains served Seagrove until December 31, 1951, and the old depot was later adapted into a pottery museum. Beans mattered too. Luck's Incorporated, which began in 1947 as the Mountain View Canning Company and took the Luck's name in 1953, specialized in pinto beans and canned vegetables and employed many Seagrove families until ConAgra closed the plant in 2002. The old cannery was later given to the Town of Seagrove and has been renovated as a multi-use center, the Luck Comer Lail Center, which hosts local events.

## Along the Pottery Highway

When the interstate replaced the old road, pottery sales slumped again from the 1950s through the 1970s. Then interest in traditional pottery returned. In 1982, local potters founded the North Carolina Museum of Traditional Pottery and started the Seagrove Pottery Festival, held on the weekend before Thanksgiving. Each November the Celebration of Seagrove Potters fills the renovated old bean cannery; the 19th annual show runs November 20 to 22, 2026. The North Carolina Pottery Center opened on November 7, 1998, drawing visitors from across the continent and around the world. Today, "Seagrove" stretches beyond the town limits to take in dozens of potteries, some 80 potters and more than 50 shops by the local pottery community's count, in Seagrove and neighboring Robbins and Star, and in unincorporated communities with names like Erect, Happy Hollow, Westmoore and Whynot, strung along what is called the North Carolina Pottery Highway, NC 705. Thousands of visitors come every year, and the town's economy depends on them. The town claims one more distinction: the center of North Carolina's population, as of the 2020 census, lies roughly six miles to its east.

## Flight Context

Seagrove lies at approximately 35.54°N, 79.78°W in southern Randolph County, North Carolina. NC 705, the Pottery Highway, runs through town and makes a useful ground reference; the scattered potteries sit along it and nearby country roads. A viewing altitude of 1,500 to 3,000 feet AGL suits the small town and rural surroundings. Asheboro Regional Airport (FAA: HBI, KHBI) is about 9 nm north-northwest, and the North Carolina Zoo lies about 5 nm north. Piedmont Triad International (KGSO) in Greensboro is about 34 nm north-northwest. Summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are common in the Piedmont.
