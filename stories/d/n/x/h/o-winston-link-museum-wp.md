# O. Winston Link Museum

Housed in Roanoke's former Norfolk and Western passenger station, remodeled by Raymond Loewy in 1949, the O. Winston Link Museum shows the meticulously staged, flash-lit night photographs one New York photographer made of the last years of steam railroading.

**Location:** 37.2736, -79.9381

## Introduction

On the night of August 2, 1956, at a drive-in theater in Iaeger, West Virginia, forty-three flashbulbs fired at once. In the instant they burned, a Norfolk and Western steam train was caught passing the drive-in, and O. Winston Link had his picture: Hotshot Eastbound, one of his best-known images, though a composite: Link later added a separate photograph to the drive-in screen. Link spent the second half of the 1950s staging scenes like that one along the N&W as the railroad gave up steam. Since January 2004, his work has had a home in downtown Roanoke, Virginia, inside the railroad's own former passenger station.

## An Engineer With a Camera

Ogle Winston Link was born in Brooklyn in 1914 and trained as a civil engineer at the Polytechnic Institute of Brooklyn. A public relations firm offered him a job as a photographer before he had even graduated, and there he learned to make posed pictures look candid. By 1946 he ran his own commercial studio in New York City. In 1955 an industrial assignment took him to Staunton, Virginia, near the Norfolk and Western line, and a long love of railroads suddenly had a focus. The N&W was the last major railroad in the country still making the transition from steam to diesel, and it had refined its steam operations to the point of earning a reputation for "precision transportation." He had arrived just in time to record the end.

## Racing the Diesels

Link made his first night photograph of the railroad on January 21, 1955, in Waynesboro, Virginia. That May, the N&W announced its first conversion to diesel, and his project became a documentation of a vanishing era. Over roughly twenty trips he photographed locomotives, but also the people of the N&W at work and the trackside towns they served, along with the railroad's massive Roanoke Shops. The work was entirely self-financed, though railroad officials, from president Robert H. Smith down, encouraged and helped him. His last night photograph came in 1959 and his last of all in 1960, the year the N&W completed its switch to diesel. By then he had roughly 2,400 negatives, by one count. He also recorded the trains' sounds, releasing six records between 1957 and 1977 as Sounds of Steam Railroading, later added to the National Recording Registry.

## Making His Own Night

"I can't move the sun — and it's always in the wrong place — and I can't even move the tracks," Link explained, "so I had to create my own environment through lighting." Others had photographed locomotives at night before him, but his vision demanded new flash techniques for subjects that large. He and an assistant hauled equipment into position and wired the bulbs in series, so a single failure meant no picture at all, and with a moving train the right moment could only be guessed. He shot with a 4x5 view camera on black-and-white film and made silver gelatin prints. The results are carefully planned tableaux, often with people placed in the frame: children in Hawksbill Creek Swimming Hole beneath a passing train in Luray, Virginia, or Main Line on Main Street, made in Northfork, West Virginia.

## A Station Remade

The museum's building has its own design pedigree. The N&W passenger station was built in 1905 and renovated in 1949 by the industrial designer Raymond Loewy, and it is one of three contributing structures in the Norfolk and Western Railway Company Historic District, listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1999. Link himself was actively involved in planning a museum of his work when he died in January 2001, three years before it opened. Inside, hundreds of his prints hang alongside interactive displays with audio about the people and places he photographed, and some of the equipment he used to make his night images. His N&W caboose is part of the display. In 2010 the railroad's own No. 611, the last surviving class J steam locomotive, was parked out front for National Train Day, bringing a steam locomotive from the very era Link documented to the door of his gallery.

## Flight Context

The O. Winston Link Museum is at 37.2736°N, 79.9381°W in downtown Roanoke, Virginia, in the former Norfolk and Western passenger station beside the rail corridor. It sits roughly 0.1 miles from the Hotel Roanoke, whose large Tudor Revival wing and tower make the best visual reference. From about 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL, follow the rail line through downtown. Roanoke-Blacksburg Regional Airport (KROA) is only about 3.5 nm north-northwest, so expect airport traffic, check the sectional chart, and expect to work with approach control. Other nearby airports: Lynchburg Regional Airport (KLYH), about 35 nm east; Greenbrier Valley Airport (KLWB) at Lewisburg, West Virginia, about 41 nm north-northwest; and Danville Regional Airport (KDAN), about 51 nm southeast. Best viewed in clear daytime conditions.
