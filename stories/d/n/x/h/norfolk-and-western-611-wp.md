# Norfolk and Western 611

Built in Roanoke in 1950 as one of the last mainline passenger steam locomotives in the United States, the streamlined class J No. 611 survived the scrapyard, two retirements and a wreck to become the 'Spirit of Roanoke' and Virginia's official state steam locomotive.

## Introduction

The Norfolk and Western's class J locomotives were among the most powerful passenger steam engines of their type ever built, yet they rolled on Timken roller bearings on every axle, so smoothly that four people with a rope could move one. No. 611 is the only one left. Built in May 1950 at the railroad's East End Shops in Roanoke, Virginia, it was the twelfth of fourteen class Js and one of the last mainline passenger steam locomotives built in the United States. It has since been retired twice, saved from scrap, restored twice and named the Spirit of Roanoke, and it is still an operating locomotive.

## Built for the Blue Ridge

Before the United States entered World War II, N&W engineers designed the streamlined class J 4-8-4 to handle rising passenger traffic over the Blue Ridge Mountains and the steep grades of Virginia and West Virginia. The numbers were formidable: 70-inch driving wheels, 80,000 pounds of tractive effort and a boiler pressure of 300 psi. On flat track a J could pull a 15-car train at 110 mph. No. 611, completed at a cost of $251,544, entered service on May 29, 1950, pulling the Powhatan Arrow, the Pocahontas and the Cavalier on the 676.6-mile run between Norfolk and Cincinnati by way of Roanoke. On September 24, 1952, it helped haul Dwight D. Eisenhower's campaign train from Columbus, Ohio, to Kenova, West Virginia, about six weeks before he won the presidency.

## The Wreck That Saved It

On January 23, 1956, No. 611 derailed near Cedar, West Virginia, with the westbound Pocahontas. The engineer was killed and 60 passengers and crew were injured; it is described as the country's last major steam-powered revenue passenger train wreck. Most railroads scrapped engines after such accidents, but N&W president Robert H. Smith ordered 611 extensively repaired, and it was back in service the next month. That rebuild mattered later. When dieselization came and the class Js were retired, 611 sat at Bluefield in late 1958 awaiting the scrapper. W. Graham Claytor Jr., a Roanoke native, lobbied to keep one J in working order, and the railroad chose 611 because its rebuilt boiler was in good condition. On October 24, 1959, it pulled the Rail Museum Safari, the N&W's final steam-powered revenue passenger train, and in 1962 it was donated to the Roanoke City Council for display.

## Roanoke Born, Roanoke Bred

For nearly two decades 611 sat in a city museum park. Then in 1981 Robert Claytor, Graham's brother and the new N&W president, leased the engine and sent it to Irondale, Alabama, for a restoration costing about $600,000, as a gift for Roanoke's centennial. On August 14, 1982, it moved under its own power for the first time in nearly 23 years, and when it rolled into Roanoke that month, Claytor introduced it as "Roanoke born, Roanoke bred, and Roanoke proud." Under the new Norfolk Southern, 611 became the star of the steam excursion program, running as far south as Florida, as far north as New York and as far west as Illinois and Missouri. In 1984 the American Society of Mechanical Engineers named it a National Historic Mechanical Engineering Landmark. Rising liability insurance costs ended the program in 1994, and on December 7 its fire was dropped in Roanoke.

## A Rothko for a Railroad

The engine went back on display in 1995 at the Virginia Museum of Transportation, which had moved into the former N&W Roanoke freight station after the 1985 Election Day floods destroyed the old museum. In 2011 the city council gave it the nickname the Spirit of Roanoke, and in 2012 it gave the locomotive to the museum outright. A year later, the Fire Up 611! committee set out to run it again. The campaign drew nearly 3,000 donors from 18 countries, and when it fell short, Norfolk Southern contributed $1.5 million from the proceeds of auctioning a Mark Rothko painting. Volunteers and specialists restored 611 at the North Carolina Transportation Museum in Spencer, and on May 9, 2015, the Queen of Steam moved under its own power for the first time in nearly 21 years.

## Virginia's Own Steam

No. 611 returned to mainline excursions until Norfolk Southern's 21st Century Steam program ended in 2017. That same year the Virginia General Assembly made it the official state steam locomotive. It was added to the Virginia Landmarks Register in 2023 and listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 2024. Operated by the Virginia Museum of Transportation, it has since worked as a traveling exhibit and excursion engine, with visits to the North Carolina Transportation Museum and Pennsylvania's Strasburg Rail Road, and since 2023 it has pulled Virginia Scenic Railway excursions out of Staunton. The bullet-nosed engine built in Roanoke's shops is still, by any measure, the city's own.
