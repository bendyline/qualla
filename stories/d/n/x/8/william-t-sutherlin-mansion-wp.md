# William T. Sutherlin Mansion

Danville's Italian Villa-style Sutherlin Mansion sheltered Jefferson Davis during the Confederacy's final week, later served as a whites-only public library that Black students challenged with a 1960 sit-in, and is now the Danville Museum of Fine Arts and History.

## Introduction

When the Danville Public Library reopened in the fall of 1960, Black residents could finally walk through its doors. They found no chairs. After a spring sit-in by Black students, the city council had shut down the whites-only library rather than integrate it, and when it reopened it had no seats. The building was the William T. Sutherlin Mansion, a stuccoed Italian villa where, less than a century earlier, Jefferson Davis had taken refuge after fleeing Richmond in April 1865. Today it is the Danville Museum of Fine Arts and History, and its walls hold both stories.

## A Villa Built on Tobacco

William T. Sutherlin was a tobacco planter and industrialist, reportedly the first Virginian to apply steam power to hydraulic tobacco presses and the owner of what is described as the second-largest tobacco factory in the state. He founded the Bank of Danville and served as the city's mayor from 1855 to 1861. His fortune depended in part on enslaved labor: in 1850 he held in slavery twenty-five men and nine boys between 10 and 16 years old. Between 1857 and 1859 he built this house, a two-story, five-bay stuccoed mansion in the Italian Villa style. The house has a one-story wooden porch, and a heavy bracketed cornice wraps the shallow hipped roof, which is crowned by a square cupola trimmed with pilasters and its own bracketed cornice.

## A Week in April

Sutherlin was one of Danville's delegates to the Virginia Convention of 1861. He opposed secession on the first vote but changed his vote after Fort Sumter was fired upon. In poor health and unable to serve in the field, he became quartermaster at Danville, a primary Confederate supply depot and arsenal, rising to the rank of major, and he allowed his tobacco factory to be used as a prison for captured Union soldiers. On April 3, 1865, as Union troops closed in on Richmond, Confederate President Jefferson Davis and his cabinet fled by rail to Danville, and Sutherlin opened his home to them. Davis stayed until April 10. On April 4 he wrote his last official proclamation as president of the Confederacy here, and on April 10 the government received news of Lee's surrender at Appomattox. He then continued south toward Greensboro, North Carolina.

## A Library Without Chairs

The city bought the mansion from the Confederate Memorial Association in 1915, and from 1928 to 1972 it housed the Danville Public Library, which admitted only white patrons until 1960. On April 2, 1960, sixteen Black students from John M. Langston High School staged a sit-in at the library. The city council closed the library rather than integrate it. When it reopened in September, a federal court order meant Black residents had to be admitted, but nothing required the library to make them comfortable, and the tables and chairs had been removed so that no one could sit and stay. The missing chairs made the message plain. But the doors were open, because a group of students had forced the question. Danville's struggle over segregation was far from finished. Three years later, on June 10, 1963, police and deputized men attacked a prayer vigil for jailed civil rights demonstrators at the city jail, injuring dozens, on a day remembered as Bloody Monday.

## Galleries in the Old Rooms

The mansion was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1969. Since 1974 it has housed the Danville Museum of Fine Arts and History, which focuses on the art, history and culture of the Dan River region. Visitors can tour the historic mansion, see rotating art and history exhibitions, and visit four permanent exhibits, including one on Danville's role in the Civil War. The museum presents the house as the "Last Capitol of the Confederacy." Standing in its rooms now, visitors can weigh the whole of its history: the enslaved labor behind the fortune that built it, the fleeing government it sheltered, and the students who sat down in its library in 1960 to demand a place.

## Flight Context

The Sutherlin Mansion stands at 36.5808°N, 79.4003°W in Danville, Virginia. It is a single two-story building, so it is best identified at low altitude; from about 1,500 to 2,000 feet AGL, look for the stuccoed villa with its square rooftop cupola. Danville Regional Airport (KDAN) is only about 3 nm to the east, so expect airport traffic, check the sectional chart, and expect to work with approach control. Other nearby airports: Piedmont Triad International (KGSO) at Greensboro, North Carolina, about 39 nm southwest; Lake Country Regional Airport at Clarksville (FAA: W63), about 41 nm east; and Lynchburg Regional Airport (KLYH), about 46 nm north-northeast.
