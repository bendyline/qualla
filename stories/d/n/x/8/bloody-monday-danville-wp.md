# Bloody Monday (Danville)

On June 10, 1963, police and deputized men in Danville, Virginia, attacked a prayer vigil held for jailed civil rights demonstrators, and the courtroom crackdown that followed became, in one account, a model for white resistance to desegregation.

**Location:** 36.5866, -79.3922

## Introduction

On the evening of June 10, 1963, a crowd gathered at the city jail in Danville, Virginia, to pray. Earlier that day, civil rights demonstrators had been arrested and locked up there, and the vigil was held in their name. It did not last. Police and deputized men waded into the crowd with billy clubs and water hoses. A historical marker outside the Danville courthouse counts at least 47 people injured and 60 arrested, though a former Danville police officer recalls seeing far fewer people treated. The day became known as Bloody Monday, and what followed in Danville's courtroom drew criticism of its own, including from the U.S. Department of Justice.

## What Danville's Marchers Wanted

Locally, the protests were led by the Danville Christian Progressive Association, founded in 1960 as an affiliate of the Southern Christian Leadership Conference. The demonstrators were protesting segregation laws and racial inequality, and their goals were concrete: participation in municipal government and services, and the hiring of Black workers in the white-owned businesses downtown. Bloody Monday was one of several protests held in the city that month, and it drew experienced organizers from out of town, among them Ivanhoe Donaldson, Avon Rollins, Robert Zellner and Dorothy Miller Zellner of the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee. The Southern Christian Leadership Conference and SNCC both sent representatives to help.

## The Vigil at the Jail

The people who came to the jail that evening were there to pray for the demonstrators behind its walls. The response was force. City police, joined by deputized men, some of them city garbage collectors, attacked the vigil with clubs and turned hoses on the crowd. Encyclopedia Virginia counts forty-seven people injured at the vigil, and the courthouse marker says at least 47 injured and 60 arrested; a former Danville officer recalls seeing only about 13 people treated for minor injuries at Winslow Hospital, the town's Black hospital, though demonstrators recall far more serious injuries. Whatever the exact tally, people had been clubbed and hosed for gathering in public to pray. In 2019, Danville's police department publicly apologized for Bloody Monday.

## Judge Aiken's Courtroom

The courtroom offered little relief. Judge Archibald M. Aiken issued an injunction that sharply limited demonstrations, and on June 17 he began trying the arrested protesters. Movement leaders were indicted under an 1859 state law against "conspiring to incite the colored population of the State to acts of violence," a statute written before the Civil War. Encyclopedia Virginia says he excluded virtually the entire public, kept a large force of armed police present, required defendants to answer roll call every day, and banned any discussion of the injunction's constitutionality. Other accounts describe a refusal to give out bills of particulars or grant continuances, and guilty verdicts read from a pre-typed script after a five-minute proceeding for each defendant. By August, bail bonds had soared to an estimated $300,000 total. The United States Department of Justice filed a brief strongly criticizing his courtroom procedures. Appeals by local and state NAACP attorneys, working with national lawyers such as William Kunstler, never brought swift federal court relief.

## King Comes to Danville

A month after Bloody Monday, on July 11, 1963, Martin Luther King Jr. came to Danville to support the demonstrators. He spoke to them, but he chose not to lead a march, and he returned only briefly on July 19. The civil rights attorney William Kunstler was among the national lawyers who worked on the protesters' appeals, even as the legal pressure on local activists mounted. Encyclopedia Virginia says arrests, high bail, restrictive injunctions and ordinances had worn down and frustrated the Danville movement. Another account holds that the ban on demonstrating weakened the movement's unity while Danville's white power structure united around a legal strategy for suppressing dissent.

## The Danville Method

As the demonstrations petered out, that strategy acquired a name. By one account, the "Danville method," a legal strategy for suppressing dissent, became a model for white resistance to desegregation. The movement's gains in Danville came slowly. By the autumn of 1963, negotiations had produced the city's first Black police officer, but little progress had been made on the protesters' central demands. The legal fight dragged on for a decade, until a judge suspended the sentences of six demonstration leaders in 1973. Bloody Monday's story holds both the courage of the people at the vigil and a lesson in how a courtroom can be turned against a movement.
