# Roanoke Star

Built by downtown merchants in 1949 as a Christmas publicity stunt, the 88.5-foot neon star atop Mill Mountain became Roanoke's nightly signal of celebration and mourning, and the reason it calls itself the Star City of the South.

**Location:** 37.2508, -79.9325

**Categories:** National Register of Historic Places in Roanoke, Virginia, Buildings and structures in Roanoke, Virginia, Mountains of Roanoke County, Virginia, Blue Ridge Mountains, Neon signs, 1949 establishments in Virginia

## Introduction

For years, when someone died in a traffic accident in Roanoke, Virginia, the star on Mill Mountain turned red; Wikipedia dates the practice to 1957 through 1976, with the star red for two nights each time. Anyone looking up at the mountain could read the message. That is the peculiar power of the Roanoke Star, also called the Mill Mountain Star: 88.5 feet of steel and 2,000 feet of neon tubing, standing high above the city and visible for 60 miles from the air. It is considered the world's largest freestanding illuminated man-made star. It was built to drum up Christmas trade, and it ended up speaking for Roanoke in its best and worst moments.

## A Mountain Within the City Limits

Mill Mountain is a 1,703-foot peak that stands apart from the surrounding ranges and lies entirely inside Roanoke's city limits. People have played on it almost since the city began. A resort hotel and an observation tower opened at the top in 1892, an amusement park appeared at its base in 1903, and from 1910 visitors could pay a quarter to ride an incline railway straight to the summit. None of it lasted. By the mid-twentieth century those attractions had all closed or burned, and the city was arguing over how much the mountain should be developed at all. Into that debate stepped a group with a very practical goal.

## The Merchants' Big Idea

In 1949, the Roanoke Merchants Association decided that a giant illuminated star on Mill Mountain, overlooking downtown, could win the city national publicity and bring in trade. Downtown merchants, who stood to benefit most, were asked to contribute toward a $25,000 goal, and with help from the city and the association itself the final cost of $28,000 was raised. The Roy C. Kinsey Sign Company built the star, Roanoke Iron and Bridge Works designed the steel support tower, and Corning Glass Works made the 2,000 feet of neon tubing. The finished star stands on an eight-story steel structure weighing 60,000 pounds, anchored in a base made with 500,000 pounds of concrete. El Paso, Texas, has a larger man-made star, but it is not permanently mounted on a steel structure, which is why Roanoke's is considered the largest freestanding one.

## Thanksgiving Eve, 1949

Construction and weather delays threatened the schedule, but the star was ready on time. On Thanksgiving Eve, November 23, 1949, the mayor hit the power switch. It was a ceremonial dummy; someone from the Kinsey sign company threw the real switch from behind a circuit box. Former Congressman Clifton A. Woodrum compared the new landmark to the Star of Bethlehem, quoting the Book of Matthew: "When they saw the star they rejoiced with exceeding great joy." Roanoke-born actor John Payne took part in the ceremony, and, Wikipedia notes, he had his own tie to the city's landmarks, since as a child he had won the contest to name the Patrick Henry Hotel. The star was meant to shine only at Christmas, but Roanokers liked it so much that the merchants kept it lit year-round, paying the electric bill until the city took it over in 1955.

## A Star That Speaks in Color

Over the decades, the star learned to signal. According to Wikipedia, it stayed red for three nights after the assassination of John F. Kennedy and for five after the Space Shuttle Challenger disaster. During the 1973 energy crisis, the city left it dark. For the 1976 Bicentennial, its design became an outer red star around inner stars of white and blue. After the September 11 attacks, it shone red, white and blue for nearly six years, until April 22, 2007, when officials turned it all white "as a symbol of healing and hope" after the Virginia Tech shooting. The patriotic colors returned on May 24, 2007, and on September 12, 2011, the star went back to white. It has stayed white since, except on Memorial Day, Flag Day, Independence Day, September 11 and Veterans Day, when the red, white and blue return.

## The City's Front Porch Light

Not everyone loved it at first. Some residents thought the star tacky, and others believed the money could have been better spent. Today it is a fixture, the source of Roanoke's nickname "Star City of the South" and considered "the city's front porch light." The overlook in front of it offers sweeping views of the Roanoke Valley and is where locals bring visiting friends and family. The star, the overlook and the Mill Mountain Zoo can be reached from the city or from a spur of the Blue Ridge Parkway, and trails for hiking, mountain biking and horseback riding lace the mountain. Keeping the light on is getting harder. A 2021 city study, as Wikipedia reports it, found significant rust on the anchors that hold the neon tubing. In 2023 the city manager put the cost of improvements at around $2 million, said the city was not planning to replace the star, and said the coating on the steel is likely lead-based paint. In September 2025 crews repaired loose bulbs and broken neon parts, which are now hard to source, after the star had gone dark six times in 20 years.

## Flight Context

The Roanoke Star stands at 37.2508°N, 79.9325°W atop Mill Mountain, a 1,703-foot peak inside the Roanoke city limits overlooking downtown. At night the star is visible for about 60 miles from the air, making it an outstanding visual landmark; by day, look for the isolated summit rising above the city with the Roanoke Valley spread around it. Roanoke–Blacksburg Regional Airport (KROA) is only about 5 nm north-northwest, so the star sits near the airport's controlled airspace; check the sectional chart and expect to work with approach control on any sightseeing pass, typically around 3,000–3,500 ft MSL. A spur of the Blue Ridge Parkway climbs to the star and overlook. Summer haze can soften the view, while clear winter nights show the star at its best.
