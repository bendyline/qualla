# Virginia State Colony for Epileptics and Feebleminded

Opened near Lynchburg in 1910 to isolate people the state judged unfit to have children, the Virginia State Colony became a center of America's forced-sterilization era through the case of Carrie Buck.

**Location:** 37.4158, -79.1197

**Categories:** Eugenics in the United States, Psychiatric hospitals in Virginia, Buildings and structures in Amherst County, Virginia, Hospital buildings completed in 1910, Disability history

## Introduction

Carrie Buck was seventeen and poor, and had given birth to a daughter about ten weeks earlier, when she arrived at the Virginia State Colony for Epileptics and Feebleminded on June 4, 1924. By her account, she had become pregnant after being raped. Her mother was already a patient there. The colony's superintendent saw in her a way to showcase eugenic ideas about heredity and to support Virginia's sterilization law. Her name would soon be attached to a landmark case in the U.S. Supreme Court.

## Built to Keep People Apart

The colony was authorized by a 1906 bill written by Aubrey Strode, a eugenicist and social welfare advocate, working with fellow eugenicists Albert Priddy and Joseph DeJarnette. It opened in 1910 in Madison Heights, near Lynchburg, with a stated goal of isolating people with mental disabilities, and others with traits deemed unfit for reproduction, away from the rest of society. The first patients arrived in 1911, and by the end of that year more than 150 men with epilepsy lived there, a condition then treated as a source of social shame. The colony admitted white patients only. Black Virginians were excluded, and when the state created the Petersburg Colony in 1939 for Black patients labeled "insane" or "feebleminded," it was reportedly poorly funded and operated as a work camp for its 182 patients, people the state had classified as having "borderline intelligence."

## The Superintendent's Test Case

Albert Priddy, the colony's first superintendent, was a central figure in the push to legalize forced sterilization, and he began performing sterilizations in 1916, with accounts of how many by 1917 ranging from just over a dozen to about 80. After she arrived, he recommended Carrie Buck for sterilization as a test case to showcase eugenic ideas of heredity and to support Virginia's sterilization law. Her case went all the way to the Supreme Court. On May 2, 1927, in Buck v. Bell, the justices upheld the law by a vote of 8 to 1, and Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr. wrote: "Three generations of imbeciles are enough." By then Priddy had died, and Dr. John Bell had taken over as superintendent.

## After the Ruling

With the law upheld, sterilizations began at the colony itself. Carrie Buck was sterilized on October 19, 1927. By one count, Virginia performed 1,333 sterilizations in the six years after the decision, and the last reported sterilization at the colony reportedly took place in 1956. Under Bell, the institution roughly doubled in size and expanded its educational programs. Later evidence cast doubt on the premise used against her. Her daughter, Vivian, was placed on the honor roll in April 1931, and reporters and researchers who visited Buck later in life described her as a woman of normal intelligence. She died in 1983.

## Empty Buildings on the Hill

The institution remained central to the care of people with intellectual disabilities in Virginia. In 1983 it was renamed the Central Virginia Training Center, with a mission of "effective, compassionate and individually responsive services," and it offered assisted living and classes aimed at eventual placement in the community. An investigation into its quality of care and compliance with the Americans with Disabilities Act led to a settlement, and in 2012 Governor Bob McDonnell announced that the facility would close by 2020. By December 2018 only 65 residents remained. The last residents were relocated in 2020, and the center closed permanently that year. As of May 2026, about 95 buildings stood vacant on the roughly 350-acre campus, which the state still owns and has not sold; a 2022 master plan calls for a mixed-use neighborhood, and the state is working toward demolishing some of the buildings.
