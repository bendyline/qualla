# Battle of Lynchburg

In June 1864, Jubal Early rushed troops by rail to save Lynchburg's railroads, canal and hospitals, and may have bluffed David Hunter's larger Union army into a retreat that opened the road toward Washington.

**Location:** 37.3845, -79.1832

**Categories:** Battles of the American Civil War in Virginia, Valley campaigns of 1864, Battles of the eastern theater of the American Civil War, Confederate victories of the American Civil War, 1864 in Virginia

## Introduction

The locomotive shuttling in and out of Lynchburg in June 1864 may have been carrying nothing but noise. Confederate Lieutenant General Jubal Early, who had reached the city by train on June 17 with only part of his command, may have set in motion a series of deceptions meant to convince the Union army outside that far more reinforcements were arriving. A yard locomotive ran continuously in and out of the city on the South Side Railroad. The 51st Virginia Infantry marched and counter-marched all night. By dinnertime on June 18, the Union commander, David Hunter, had decided to retreat.

## A City Worth Fighting For

Lynchburg mattered to the Confederacy. It had three railroads, a navigable canal and military hospitals, and it was a distribution center for food and military supplies, including stored medical supplies. Food, livestock and manufactured goods flowed from the surrounding region. Lieutenant General Ulysses S. Grant understood the stakes. In a letter dated June 6, 1864, he told Major General David Hunter to attack the "Lynchburg branch of the Virginia Central" Railroad at Lynchburg and urged him to destroy the nearby James River and Kanawha Canal.

## From New Market to Piedmont

The Union's spring campaign in western Virginia had begun badly. On May 15, Confederates under Major General John C. Breckinridge defeated Sigel at the Battle of New Market. Further west, Crook won at Cloyd's Mountain on May 9 and burned the New River railroad bridge the next day, but Averell was turned back at Cove Mountain, and both fell back to Meadow Bluff. Hunter replaced Sigel on May 21. On June 5 he defeated Confederate forces about ten miles from Staunton at the Battle of Piedmont, where the Confederate commander, Brigadier General William E. "Grumble" Jones, was killed. Hunter entered Staunton unopposed the next day, and on June 8 Crook's Army of West Virginia, with Averell's cavalry, joined him; the newcomers received badly needed supplies such as boots and uniforms. His combined army numbered 16,643 men.

## Fire in Lexington

Hunter's route toward Lynchburg ran through Lexington, where his troops marched down Main Street and burned buildings including the Virginia Military Institute and the home of former Virginia governor John Letcher before leaving on June 14. The Confederates scrambled to respond. Breckinridge, injured when his horse fell on him and no longer able to ride, reached Charlottesville by rail on June 9 and gathered about 5,000 soldiers, with Brigadier General John McCausland's cavalry further west. On June 13, Robert E. Lee ordered Early to take his corps of 8,000 soldiers to the Shenandoah Valley. Early and Major General Stephen Dodson Ramseur's division reached Lynchburg by train around 1:00 p.m. on June 17.

## Two Days Outside the City

On June 17, Imboden's Confederates at first repelled Averell's attackers, until Crook came up quickly with three infantry brigades. On June 18, Early pushed forward against the cautious Union soldiers, opening with artillery on the middle of the Union line before Ramseur's division and Gordon's partial division attacked. The assault failed to defeat Hunter's army, but it succeeded in another way. Low on ammunition and supplies, deep in enemy territory and fearing a Confederate force larger than his own, Hunter ordered his wagon trains west. At daybreak on June 19, his absence was confirmed, and Early began a pursuit.

## The Road Left Open

More than 30,000 soldiers took part. The size of the Confederate force is unclear, though at least two sources put it at about 14,000. Estimates for the battle itself run to about 700 Union casualties and 200 Confederate. Hunter retreated west into West Virginia, leaving the Shenandoah Valley open to the Confederates all the way to Maryland. Early moved down the valley and threatened Washington, D.C., until his defeat at the Battle of Fort Stevens on July 11-12. In Lynchburg, the remnants of a roughly square earthen fort from the city's outer defenses survive as Fort Early; a building inside it, constructed in 1922, serves as the headquarters of the Fort Hill Woman's Club, which keeps battle exhibits there.
