# Stonewall Jackson House

The brick town house on East Washington Street in Lexington, Virginia, was the only home Thomas 'Stonewall' Jackson ever owned, where he lived as a VMI professor with his wife and the people he enslaved from 1858 until he left for the Civil War in 1861.

## Introduction

In April 1861, a physics professor at the Virginia Military Institute was ordered to Richmond with the institute's corps of cadets. He left behind a brick town house at 8 East Washington Street in Lexington, Virginia, the only house he would ever own. He never came back to it. The professor, Thomas J. Jackson, would become the Confederate general known as "Stonewall," and two years later he was dead. The house he left is now a museum, restored to its appearance when the Jacksons lived there, and its story includes the six people Jackson held in slavery.

## A Doctor's House on Washington Street

The house was built by Cornelius Dorman at the turn of the nineteenth century. In 1845 a physician, Dr. Archibald Graham, bought it and expanded it considerably, adding a stone section at the rear and remodeling the front and interior to suit his medical practice. The result is a two-story, four-bay brick dwelling with a large stone rear addition, a side-gabled roof and chimneys set inside its end walls. On November 4, 1858, Graham sold the house for $3,000 to Major Thomas Jackson, a professor at nearby VMI. Jackson moved in with his second wife, Mary Anna Morrison Jackson, whom he had married in 1857, and they lived there until the war began.

## Major Jackson, Professor

Jackson had come to Lexington in 1851 to take a newly created post at VMI as Professor of Natural and Experimental Philosophy, meaning physics, and Instructor of Artillery. He was not a popular teacher; his students nicknamed him "Tom Fool." His years in Lexington also held deep grief. His first wife, Elinor Junkin, daughter of the president of Washington College, died in 1854 after giving birth to a stillborn son, and in 1858 his first daughter with Mary Anna died before she was a month old. The house on Washington Street, bought later that year, was the only home Jackson ever owned, and he would live in it for less than three years.

## The People in the Household

The Jackson household was not only the Jacksons. In the late 1850s Jackson held six people in slavery. Harriet, called Hetty, and her teenage sons Cyrus and George came to him as part of Mary Anna's dowry. Albert worked as a waiter in a Lexington hotel and was rented out to VMI from 1858 to 1860; he was left off the inventory of Jackson's estate in June 1863, which suggests he had gained his freedom. Amy served as cook and housekeeper and died in the fall of 1861. Emma was a four-year-old orphan whom Jackson bought, and whom Mary Anna later described as slow to learn. Mary Anna later claimed that some of them had asked Jackson to buy them, claims considered dubious because she spent the decades after the war trying to distance him from slavery. Jackson did organize a Sunday school for Black residents at the Presbyterian Church in 1855 and encouraged literacy there against state law, yet he largely accepted slavery as divinely sanctioned. After the war began, Jackson advised Mary Anna to make provision for the household's servants and return to her family in North Carolina; Cyrus and George were hired out to a Rockbridge County farmer, and Hetty remained with Mary Anna as nurse to the Jacksons' daughter Julia. After emancipation, Hetty (Harriet), George and Emma settled in Lincoln County, North Carolina, and took the surname Jackson. Hetty lived until 1911 and George until 1920, and researchers found many of George's descendants still living in the area; what became of Cyrus is unknown.

## The Door He Didn't Return Through

Life in the house was already shadowed by the coming conflict. In November 1859, VMI cadets were sent to Charles Town at the governor's request for the hanging of the abolitionist John Brown, and Major Jackson commanded the artillery, two howitzers manned by twenty-one cadets. Then, when Virginia seceded in April 1861, the governor ordered Jackson to report with the VMI cadet corps to Richmond. What followed made his name, but he never saw his Lexington home again. He was shot at Chancellorsville in 1863 and died on May 10. His body lay in Richmond for public mourning and was then brought back to Lexington and buried in the town's Oak Grove Cemetery.

## Hospital, Then Museum

After the war the house went on to other uses. From 1907 until 1954 it housed the Stonewall Jackson Memorial Hospital, and in 1954 it was converted to a museum. In 1979 it was carefully restored to its appearance at the time of the Jacksons' occupancy. It was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1973. Today the house and its garden are owned and operated by the Virginia Military Institute as a historic house museum, now called the Jackson House Museum, which VMI lists as open daily and closed in January and February. Walking through its rooms, visitors see the domestic life of a professor on the eve of war, and a household that also included the six people Jackson held in slavery.
