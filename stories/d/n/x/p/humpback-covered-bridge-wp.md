# Humpback Covered Bridge

After floods and fatigue destroyed three earlier crossings of Dunlap Creek, builders in 1857 raised Virginia's oldest surviving covered bridge four feet higher in the middle, giving it a curved span that the state calls the only one of its kind left in the country.

**Location:** 37.8006, -80.0470

**Categories:** Covered bridges on the National Register of Historic Places in Virginia, National Historic Landmarks in Virginia, Wooden bridges in Virginia, Bridges completed in 1857, Tourist attractions in Alleghany County, Virginia

## Introduction

Three bridges failed at roughly this spot on Dunlap Creek before anyone thought to build one with a hump. The first, from the 1820s, was swept away by a flood in 1837. Its replacement lasted only until another flood on July 13, 1842. The third simply wore out and collapsed in 1856. So in 1857, the builders tried something different: they put a roof over the new bridge and raised its middle four feet higher than its ends. It worked. The Humpback Covered Bridge, just west of Covington in Virginia's Alleghany County, is now the oldest remaining covered bridge in the state.

## A Road Over the Mountains

All four bridges carried the James River and Kanawha Turnpike, a heavily used mountain road linking the Shenandoah Valley with the Alleghany Mountains and the lands farther west. None of the first three were arched or covered, and the 1857 builders hoped to change their luck by doing both. The arch was meant to lift the vulnerable midsection above future floods, and the roof would shield the deck from the moisture and sunlight that rotted open bridges. Paint was no answer, because horses and wagons would quickly wear away any paint of the era. The bridge is a single span of about 100 feet across Dunlap Creek, a tributary of the Jackson River, and its gentle rise is what gives it its name. Virginia's historic register calls it the nation's only surviving curved-span covered bridge.

## Why Bridges Wore Hats

Covered bridges first appeared in the 1810s, when builders realized a roof could protect the decking and support trusses beneath it. On an uncovered wooden bridge of the era, those parts lasted an average of about ten years. Covered bridges, it turned out, lasted about ten times as long. They picked up a gentler reputation too. In the more modest late nineteenth century, they were sometimes called "kissing bridges," because the privacy of the roof gave couples crossing by horse and buggy a moment to kiss unobserved. The Humpback Bridge carried traffic for 72 years, from 1857 until 1929, when a steel truss bridge was built for U.S. Route 60 immediately to the north and the old wooden span was left behind.

## Oak, Hickory and Locust Pins

Bridge builders used the strongest wood close at hand, and here that meant white oak and hickory. Most of the hand-hewn support timbers and decking laid down in 1857 are still in place, although the walls and roof have been replaced several times. The scale of the timber is striking. Deck planks of this kind were traditionally a foot or more wide and four to six inches thick, and most of the support beams are at least a foot thick. The sections of the supports are fastened with handmade pins of honey locust wood. The real distinction lies in the frame: a curved multiple kingpost truss. Virginia's historic register calls the Humpback the nation's only surviving curved-span covered bridge, an original design duplicated nowhere else.

## From Hay Barn to Landmark

After 1929 the bridge sat abandoned, and into the early 1950s a local farmer sometimes used it to store hay bales. Its rescue came from the community. The Covington Business and Professional Women's Club and the Covington Chamber of Commerce raised $5,000, and in 1953 the Virginia Highway Department matched it. Five acres around the bridge were bought, and a small wayside park opened in 1954. The bridge was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on October 1, 1969, and in 2012 it was designated a National Historic Landmark. Today the park, about 3 miles west of Covington off Rumsey Road and less than a mile from Interstate 64's Exit 10, has restrooms, picnic tables and access to the creek, along with a close look at timbers that carried turnpike traffic before the Civil War. Local tourism officials said in September 2026 that revitalization work was continuing at the wayside park through the fall, so check before you go.

## Flight Context

The Humpback Covered Bridge crosses Dunlap Creek at 37.8006°N, 80.047°W, about 3 miles west of Covington in Alleghany County, Virginia, beside U.S. Route 60 and less than a mile from Interstate 64. The bridge itself is only about 100 feet long and hard to see from altitude, so use Covington, the Jackson River and the I-64 corridor as your guides and look for the small wayside park beside the creek. The surrounding mountains are steep; fly at about 4,500–5,000 ft MSL. Ingalls Field (KHSP) at Hot Springs, perched at 3,792 ft elevation, is about 14 nm northeast, Greenbrier Valley Airport (KLWB) at Lewisburg, West Virginia, is about 17 nm west, and Roanoke–Blacksburg Regional Airport (KROA) is about 29 nm south. Expect valley fog on calm mornings.
