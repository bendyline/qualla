# Foamhenge

Built as an April Fool's Day stunt in 2004, Mark Cline's full-scale styrofoam Stonehenge stood near Natural Bridge, Virginia, for more than twelve years before it was dismantled, restored and realigned to the solstice at a farm near Centreville.

## Introduction

Stonehenge took something like 1,500 years to build. Foamhenge took somewhere between ten days and six weeks, depending on who is counting. Artist Mark Cline unveiled his full-scale styrofoam replica of the ancient monument near Natural Bridge, Virginia, on April 1, 2004, a date that told visitors everything about the spirit of the thing. It was meant as an April Fool's Day stunt to bring in tourists. Instead the joke stuck around for more than twelve years, became a fixture of American roadside culture, and, when its home changed hands, was carefully packed up and moved across the state rather than thrown away.

## Inspired by Insulation

The idea came to Cline, of Enchanted Castle Studios, in 1998, when he saw 16-foot-tall foam blocks at a local insulation manufacturer. He had the concept and the material, but he needed a site. He found one through an agreement with The Natural Bridge, which gave him land rent-free on the theory that each attraction would draw visitors to the other. Cline designed Foamhenge to match the original, with similarly sized pieces arranged in astronomically equivalent positions. The faux stones are made entirely of styrofoam, painted gray, and weigh about 420 pounds apiece. Each is stabilized by 2.5-inch pipe running from a concrete footing to the top of the stone.

## A Joke That Wouldn't Leave

Foamhenge was built as a whimsical, temporary folly, but its popularity gave it a kind of permanence. It became a must-see for fans of American roadside attractions, turning up again and again in articles and television programs about the country's quirkier landmarks. Temporary materials, though, have their limits. By 2015, after years of exposure to the weather, the foam pieces had deteriorated markedly; many had split apart and were being held together with temporary supports. Then the ground shifted beneath it in a different way. In 2016 the Natural Bridge became a state park, and Foamhenge had to close. On August 30, 2016, the monument was taken apart and put into storage.

## Realigned to the Sun

The story might have ended there, but Cline reportedly received more than fifty inquiries from across the United States. Cline chose Cox Farms, a family-owned farm near Centreville, Virginia, in the Washington, D.C., suburbs. The pieces were repaired and repainted, then rebuilt for permanent display. This time the project reportedly had an astronomer: Harold Geller, observatory director at nearby George Mason University, guided the placement so that the stones align with the summer solstice, just as Stonehenge's do. Foamhenge reopened on September 16, 2017, in time for the start of the farm's Fall Festival, and it remains there. It is open to Fall Festival visitors, and in spring and summer on Saturday afternoons by shuttle.

## The Empty Hillside

Near Natural Bridge, the spot where Foamhenge first stood now holds only the memory of it, and of the dozen-plus years when travelers stopped to walk among the foam stones. Cline's other creations kept the local tradition going; his Dinosaur Kingdom II stands less than a mile to the south. Foamhenge itself has gone on to a kind of national fame. In 2022 it was reportedly one of 49 popular roadside attractions in the board game Zillionaires: Road Trip USA, with a photograph of it on the box. Not bad for an April Fool's gag made of styrofoam, and a reminder that some jokes, done with enough care, become landmarks.
