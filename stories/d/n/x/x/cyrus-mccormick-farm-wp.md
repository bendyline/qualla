# Cyrus McCormick Farm

At Walnut Grove, a Shenandoah Valley farm where, by one account, forty-one people were held in slavery, Cyrus McCormick built the mechanical reaper in a blacksmith shop, a machine that raised a day's harvest from half an acre to twelve.

**Location:** 37.9339, -79.2178

**Categories:** National Historic Landmarks in Virginia, Farm museums in Virginia, Museums in Rockbridge County, Virginia, Farms on the National Register of Historic Places in Virginia, Slave cabins and quarters in the United States

## Introduction

Before the mechanical reaper, a farmer could harvest about half an acre of grain in a day. With the machine, that rose to around twelve acres, with far less manual labor. The reaper that made that leap possible was engineered in a blacksmith shop on Walnut Grove, the McCormick family farm near Steele's Tavern and Raphine in Virginia's Shenandoah Valley. The farm is known as the birthplace of the mechanical reaper, forerunner of the combine harvester, and it is now a museum run by Virginia Tech, preserving what Virginia Tech describes as a two-acre memorial plot of the family's old farm.

## Six Weeks at the Forge

Cyrus Hall McCormick reportedly designed, built and tested his reaper at Walnut Grove within six weeks. He did not start from nothing. His efforts built on some two decades of work by his father, Robert McCormick, on a similar device, reportedly with help from Cyrus's brother Leander, and Cyrus took up the project with the aid of Jo Anderson, an enslaved African-American man held by the family. McCormick held one of his first demonstrations of mechanical reaping at nearby Steeles Tavern in 1831, harvested his first crop with the machine, and received a patent on June 21, 1834. He was one of several designers producing working reapers in the 1830s, though he always claimed sole credit. He never stopped tinkering either, returning to the drawing board again and again and producing new models almost every decade.

## A Working Valley Farm

Robert McCormick, who was born at Walnut Grove in 1780 and took over the family estate in 1810, built the brick manor house in 1822. Eight of the farm's nine original buildings still stand, many of them renovated over the years, and the manor house sits at the center. Behind it stands the smokehouse, where meat was dried and smoked to last through the winter in the decades before refrigeration. The grist mill, built before 1800, ground wheat into flour. The carriage house sheltered wagons and carriages, and the blacksmith shop built and repaired every implement the family needed, and it was there that Cyrus engineered his reaper. There is also a housekeeper's quarters and a schoolroom, where the McCormicks kept a school for neighboring children. Only the ice house is gone, demolished in the 1960s.

## The People in the Quarters

Another of the surviving buildings tells a harder story. The slave quarters were home to people the McCormick family held in slavery, a number put at forty-one in one account. At least one of them, Jo Anderson, helped bring the reaper into being, even as Cyrus McCormick claimed the invention as his alone. The machine would later be celebrated because it spared farm families from toiling all day at harvest, yet it was developed on a farm where many people were held in slavery. Their quarters still stand among the farm's buildings, part of the story of Walnut Grove rather than a footnote to it.

## From the Valley to the Prairie

After his father died in 1846, Cyrus moved his base of operations to Chicago in 1847 with his brother Leander James McCormick, drawn by the vast stretches of fertile prairie soil in the Midwest. Another brother, William Sanderson McCormick, joined the business in 1849; the firm was known as Cyrus H. McCormick and Brothers before it became the McCormick Harvesting Machine Company. With the reaper, family farms could work far more land than had seemed possible for one person. In 1902 the company merged with its competitor, the Deering Harvester Company, and several smaller firms to form International Harvester.

## Walnut Grove Today

The farm stayed in the McCormick family until 1954, when it was donated to Virginia Polytechnic Institute as an agricultural center and farm memorial. It was declared a National Historic Landmark in 1964. Today the museum is operated by Virginia Tech's Shenandoah Valley Agricultural Research and Extension Center, which lists museum hours of 8 a.m. to noon, Monday through Friday, closes the historic buildings on state holidays and in bad weather, and keeps the grounds open from sunrise to sunset; admission has been listed as free. Visitors can step into the schoolroom, stocked with textbooks, toys and school supplies dating to the 1830s, and see the blacksmith shop where the reaper took shape. The farm lies less than five miles from the Raphine Road interchange on Interstate 81, about halfway between Lexington and Staunton.
