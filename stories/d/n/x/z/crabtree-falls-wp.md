# Crabtree Falls

In Virginia's George Washington National Forest, Crabtree Falls drops roughly 1,000 to 1,200 feet in a chain of five major cascades, one of the tallest sets of waterfalls east of the Mississippi, saved from resort development by a 1968 land deal.

**Location:** 37.8508, -79.0783

**Categories:** Landmarks in Virginia, Waterfalls of Virginia, Protected areas of Nelson County, Virginia, Landforms of Nelson County, Virginia

## Introduction

The number people usually quote for Crabtree Falls, including the Forest Service, is 1,200 feet. Some accounts say topographic maps put the total drop closer to 1,000. Either way, this chain of cascades in Nelson County, Virginia, ranks among the tallest sets of waterfalls in the United States east of the Mississippi River. Crabtree Creek pours down through five major cascades and a string of smaller ones before emptying into the Tye River below, all within the George Washington National Forest, just off Virginia State Route 56. That it is public land at all is thanks to one determined local man and a land deal completed in 1968.

## Five Big Steps Down

Crabtree Falls is not a single plunge but a staircase of water. Five major cascades do most of the work, the tallest of them dropping about 400 feet, and several smaller cascades fill in between. The whole series stretches across roughly 2,500 feet horizontally, which means the creek is falling steeply for nearly half a mile. Above the falls, the water gathers as Crabtree Creek; below, it drains into the Tye River. The falls are thought to take their name from William Crabtree, who settled in this part of Virginia in 1777, and his name has stayed with the creek and its cascades ever since.

## The Resort That Wasn't

In the late 1960s, plans began to turn the falls into a resort area. L.A. Snead, a Nelson County man who is said to have served as a U.S. Assistant Fuel Administrator during World War I and was known as an environmentalist, stepped in. He is credited with leading the negotiations to secure the land around the falls, and with a combination of personal and Congressional funds, the purchases were reportedly completed. On June 3, 1968, Snead is said to have transferred the deeds to the National Forest System. Instead of becoming a private resort, Crabtree Falls became part of the George Washington National Forest, open to anyone willing to make the walk.

## Stay on the Trail

The U.S. Forest Service maintains the Crabtree Falls Trail to the cascades and says hikers must stay on it, warning that leaving the trail has resulted in numerous fatalities because of extraordinarily slippery, algae-covered rocks. The rocks around the water have proved deadly. Since the Forest Service began tracking incidents in 1982, at least thirty people are known to have died here after slipping or leaving the trail to get closer to the falls, and a hiker died as recently as May 2025. Each of those deaths was a family's loss, and together they are the clearest possible advice for anyone who visits: enjoy the cascades from the path, and let the water keep its distance.

## Flight Context

From the air, follow the Tye River valley and look for the steep drainage of Crabtree Creek feeding into it. Keep a generous margin above the terrain, around 2,500 to 3,500 feet AGL.
