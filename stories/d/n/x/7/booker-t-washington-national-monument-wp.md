# Booker T. Washington National Monument

On a former tobacco farm in rural Franklin County, Virginia, the National Park Service preserves the place where Booker T. Washington was born into slavery in 1856, a site whose purchase was backed by a commemorative half dollar, the first U.S. coin to feature an African American.

## Introduction

"I was born a slave on a plantation in Franklin County, Virginia," Booker T. Washington wrote. "I am not quite sure of the exact place or exact date of my birth, but at any rate I suspect I must have been born somewhere and at some time." The dry humor hides a hard truth: he never knew the day, month or year of his birth. Evidence found after his death suggests April 5, 1856, and the place was the 207-acre tobacco farm of James Burroughs, near Hale's Ford. Today portions of that farm, near the community of Hardy, form the Booker T. Washington National Monument.

## The Kitchen Cabin

Booker's mother, Jane, was the Burroughs family's cook. Compared with their Franklin County neighbors, the Burroughses were an upper-middle-class family, measured by the land and the people they held in slavery. Tobacco was their cash crop, and they grew flax, potatoes and grains to feed the household. Jane, Booker and his two half-siblings lived in the farm's one-room kitchen cabin. He never knew his father, said to be a white man from a neighboring plantation who played no part in his life. As a small child Booker carried water to the men in the fields and took grain to the local mill. He also carried the Burroughs daughters' books to school, though the law barred him from an education of his own. Years later he wrote that he could not remember a single time his whole family sat down to the table together; food came to the children "a piece of bread here and a scrap of meat there."

## The Day of Freedom

Booker lived on the farm throughout the Civil War. In 1865, as the war closed and when he was nine, the people enslaved on the Burroughs farm were told they were free. Washington later described the scene: a stranger, probably a United States officer, read what he took to be the Emancipation Proclamation. "My mother, who was standing by my side, leaned over and kissed her children, while tears of joy ran down her cheeks," he wrote. It was the day she had long prayed for while fearing she would never live to see it. After emancipation, Jane took her children to West Virginia to join her husband, Washington Ferguson, who had escaped slavery during the war. There Booker began teaching himself to read and went to school for the first time. Asked for a surname at registration, he chose Washington.

## Up From the Farm

What followed took him far from Franklin County. Washington worked in West Virginia's salt furnaces and coal mines, then at 16 made his way, mostly on foot, to Hampton Institute, where he paid his way as a janitor. In 1881 he became the first leader of the new Tuskegee Institute in Alabama. His Atlanta Address of 1895 brought him national prominence, and in 1901 he published his autobiography, Up from Slavery, and dined with Theodore Roosevelt at the White House. His approach, emphasizing education and economic self-help rather than direct challenges to segregation, drew opposition from Black activists led by W. E. B. Du Bois, and his legacy remains debated. The farm tells the story of where that remarkable journey began.

## A Coin to Buy a Birthplace

Preserving the farm took an unusual kind of fundraising. Congress authorized a Booker T. Washington Memorial half dollar to raise money for the site's purchase and upkeep. It was the first U.S. coin to feature an African American, and it was struck from 1946 to 1951 at three different mints, in 18 varieties. More than 1.5 million were minted, and buyers were mostly established collectors. Even so, the sales fell short: the Booker T. Washington commission owed more than it held, the Commonwealth of Virginia stepped in, and the land ended up in federal hands. The site was designated a national monument on April 2, 1956.

## An 1850s Farm, Rebuilt

The monument evokes the 1850s tobacco farm of Washington's childhood and tells the story of his life and achievements alongside the realities of slavery and farming in that decade. It does so with buildings, gardens, crafts and animals. A reconstruction of the kitchen cabin where Booker was born and spent his first nine years stands with a smokehouse and an herb garden, and interpreters sometimes portray enslaved people inside by the fire. Draft horses work the fields, trails wind past the pastures, and the park's headquarters occupies the Booker T. Washington Elementary School. It is a quiet, rural place, which is part of its power: the ordinary setting of an extraordinary beginning.

## Flight Context

Booker T. Washington National Monument lies at 37.1233°N, 79.7658°W near the community of Hardy in rural Franklin County, Virginia, southeast of Roanoke. The site is a small farm landscape of fields, pasture and a few buildings, best seen from about 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL in clear weather. Nearby airports: Smith Mountain Lake Airport (FAA: W91) at Moneta, about 8 nm east; Roanoke-Blacksburg Regional Airport (KROA), about 16 nm northwest; Lynchburg Regional Airport (KLYH), about 30 nm east-northeast; and Danville Regional Airport (KDAN), about 39 nm south-southeast. Best viewed in clear daytime conditions.
