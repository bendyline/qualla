# Richard Mentor Johnson

Kentucky's Richard Mentor Johnson rode a disputed claim to have killed Tecumseh into the vice presidency, the only one chosen by the Senate, while his relationship with Julia Chinn, an enslaved woman he never freed, shadowed his career.

**Location:** 38.1978, -84.8672

**Categories:** Vice presidents of the United States, War of 1812, Kentucky history, History of slavery in Kentucky, Frankfort, Kentucky

## Introduction

"Rumpsey Dumpsey, Rumpsey Dumpsey, Colonel Johnson killed Tecumseh." In 1836, that jingle helped carry Richard Mentor Johnson toward the vice presidency of the United States. Whether he actually fired the shot that killed the Shawnee chief was argued over for the rest of his life. The rest of Johnson's story is stranger still: a war hero who proposed an expedition to the center of the Earth, a crusader against debtors' prisons who held people in slavery, and the only vice president elected by the Senate under the Twelfth Amendment. He is buried in Frankfort Cemetery, in Kentucky's capital.

## A Son of Bryan's Station

Johnson was born on October 17, 1780, at Beargrass, a Kentucky frontier settlement that is now Louisville. Around the time of his birth, the family moved to Bryan's Station, a fortified outpost near present-day Lexington. According to later reports, when Simon Girty's raid struck the station in August 1782 and water ran short, his mother, Jemima, led the women to a nearby spring while warriors hid in the woods, and the attackers let them return.

The family later settled at Great Crossing, in Scott County. Richard attended Transylvania University and joined the bar in 1802, often taking poor people's cases for free when they had merit. In 1804 he became the first native Kentuckian to serve in the state legislature, even though he was under the required age of twenty-four; he was so popular that no one objected. Two years later he became the first native Kentuckian elected to Congress.

## The Charge at the Thames

When war with Britain came in 1812, Johnson, a War Hawk ally of Henry Clay, raised mounted volunteers and became their colonel. His men reached besieged Fort Wayne in time to save it, then went out of their way to burn Potawatomi villages along the Elkhart River on the way home. The next summer he raided across the Northwest with a thousand horsemen, paying for gunsmiths and doctors himself.

On October 5, 1813, at the Battle of the Thames in Upper Canada, while his brother James led a battalion against the British regulars, Richard ordered a suicide squad of twenty men to charge with him and draw the fire of Tecumseh's warriors at the edge of a swamp. About fifteen of the twenty were killed or wounded. Johnson was wounded and left with a damaged left arm and hand. When the Native force broke and fled, Tecumseh was slain.

Johnson himself said only that he had killed "a tall, good-looking Indian." Another claimant appeared in 1816, but historian John Sugden judged that Johnson's "claim is surely the stronger."

## Julia Chinn

At home, Johnson broke the conventions of his class, though never the hold of slavery itself. Julia Chinn was enslaved on his plantation, a woman of mixed race classified in the language of the era as an "octoroon." Where other planters and leaders kept such relationships hidden, Johnson treated Chinn as his common-law wife and was heard to call her "my bride." She managed his estate, and he told his white employees that her authority had to be respected. Yet they could not marry because she was enslaved, and he never freed her. Because she was enslaved, she could not freely consent; the National Park Service describes the relationship, which began around 1815 when she was likely 14 to 16 years old, as non-consensual. Historian Christina Snyder argues that liberating her would have eroded the "ties that bound her to him."

They had two daughters, Adaline and Imogene, whom Johnson acknowledged, gave his surname and educated. It is believed the relationship cost him his Senate seat in 1828. Chinn died in the cholera epidemic of 1833. Johnson then began a relationship with another enslaved woman, and when she left him for another man, he had her picked up and sold at auction. At his death, a court found he "left no widow, children, father, or mother living," and Imogene, deemed illegitimate, could not inherit.

## Salaries, Sabbaths and a Hollow Earth

Johnson's legislative career ran from the admirable to the bizarre. For years he fought imprisonment for debt, first in Kentucky and then in Congress, where a federal law finally passed in 1832. In his 1829 Sunday mail report he argued that government was "a civil, and not a religious institution." He also oversaw the Choctaw Academy on his Scott County farm, a school for the European-American education of students from the Southeastern tribes; some colleagues considered his role there ethically questionable.

His 1816 bill replacing Congress's $6 per diem with a $1,500 salary provoked such a backlash that only 15 of the 81 House members who voted for it kept their seats. Johnson survived. Then, in 1823, encouraged by his friend John Cleves Symmes Jr., who believed the Earth was hollow, he proposed a government-funded expedition to its center. Congress turned it down; accounts of the tally differ, with one source putting the yeas at only twenty-five.

## One Vote Short

In 1836 Johnson ran for vice president alongside Martin Van Buren. His relationship with Chinn cost the Democrats votes in the South, and when Virginia's electors refused to back him, he fell one electoral vote short of a majority. The Senate settled the race in his favor. He served from 1837 to 1841 with little influence over Van Buren. Visiting a tavern Johnson was running, Amos Kendall reported finding him "happy in the inglorious pursuit of tavern keeping."

In 1840 the Democrats did not renominate him, leaving vice-presidential choices to the states. Johnson ended where he had begun, in the Kentucky House of Representatives. He won a seat there in 1850 but died of a stroke on November 19, soon after the session began. His brothers James and John Telemachus Johnson and his nephew Robert Ward Johnson all followed him into Congress.
