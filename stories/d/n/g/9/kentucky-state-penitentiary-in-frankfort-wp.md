# Kentucky State Penitentiary in Frankfort

Completed in 1800 and described as the first prison west of the Allegheny Mountains, Kentucky's Frankfort penitentiary was leased out for profit for decades, grew castle-like towers, became a reformatory, and was finally abandoned when the great flood of 1937 swept through its walls.

## Introduction

How do you evacuate a prison when the river has already come inside? In January 1937 the Ohio and Kentucky Rivers flooded, the Kentucky River cresting at close to 48 feet at Frankfort and submerging much of the city, including the grounds of the old state prison. National Guardsmen and state police ferried the prisoners by boat to dry land. About 800 men were housed in the Frankfort Armory while a camp of tents and barbed wire went up on a hill about a mile away. The flood ended a story that had begun 137 years earlier, when Kentucky was still virtually a wilderness.

## A Penitentiary House on the Frontier

In 1798 the Kentucky legislature appointed five commissioners, Harry Innes, Alexander S. Bullitt, Caleb Wallace, Isaac Shelby and John Coburn, to choose a site for a "penitentiary house," to be built of brick or stone, with cells, workshops and an outside wall "high enough and strong enough to keep the prisoners from getting away." They chose Frankfort. One of the commissioners gave an acre of land, and the legislature put up $500 to start. The prison was completed on June 22, 1800, and is described as the first built west of the Allegheny Mountains. Three months later John Turner of Madison County arrived, arrested for horse stealing, and in March 1804 a Lexington convict named Rachel Miller may have become its first woman prisoner. During the War of 1812 it held 30 British prisoners. Running a prison workshop was new territory: there was no other prison of its kind nearer than Virginia and Philadelphia, and no clear precedent for managing prison industry.

## Leased for Profit

When Joel Scott took charge in 1825, the 84 convicts he found were filthy and diseased, and he became known as the prison reformer of his day. That same year, though, the state turned the prison into a business partnership, lending the keeper $6,000 at 6 percent and letting him take half the net profit from goods the inmates made, provided the state received $1,000. By 1848 the keeper took a third of the profits and had to guarantee the state at least $5,000 a year. The system's darkest chapter came under Zebulon Ward, keeper from 1854 to 1859. Ward leased the prison at $6,000 a year and made $100,000 from it in four years. Calvin Fairbank, an abolitionist minister who spent some seventeen years in this prison, across two sentences, for helping enslaved people escape, described Ward as utterly without heart or conscience and charged that "to do this he literally killed 250 out of 375 prisoners." The lease system ended in 1880, when the state took back control.

## Towers Like Warwick Castle

Between 1835 and 1837 the prison gained a Gothic entrance at the intersection of High and Mero Streets, flanked by Norman-style towers said to copy Caesar's Tower and Guy's Tower at Warwick Castle in England. Behind them, life could be harsh and precarious. On August 30, 1844, fire consumed every wooden building inside the walls, along with shops, machinery and tools, though no convicts escaped and the prisoners were unharmed. The prison's rolls hold some startling entries. In 1893 Sam Dodd, eight years old, is recorded as the youngest prisoner sent to Frankfort, serving a one-year sentence for grand larceny. In 1929 Grace Browder, a member of a gang that had tried to hold up a West Louisville bank with a machine gun, was described as the first woman bank robber sent to the state reformatory, beginning a 20-year sentence.

## From Penitentiary to Reformatory

A 1910 reform law reshaped the prison, calling for one institution to be penal and the other to reform, and changing the parole law as well. Striped uniforms gave way to light blue clothing, and the Frankfort Penitentiary became the Kentucky State Reformatory (the Kentucky Historical Society dates the renaming to 1912), meant for men under 30, while the branch penitentiary at Eddyville became the Kentucky State Penitentiary, for men over 30. The same law replaced the gallows with the electric chair, which was installed at Eddyville in September 1910. Frankfort had no wish for the chair: the reformatory drew ten visitors for every one at the branch prison, and its managers did not want curiosity seekers coming to see it.

## The Water Rises

In 1936 the General Assembly approved a new prison at La Grange to ease overcrowding. No one expected a flood to hurry the move along. When the water came in 1937, prisoners who were not in the armory went to jails in towns including Lexington, Winchester, Georgetown and Danville. The ill were sent to the Federal Narcotics Farm at Lexington, and women prisoners were moved to the old prison school building until a new facility opened the following year in Shelby County. Governor A. B. Chandler ordered the Frankfort prison permanently closed, and the men in the hilltop camp were later transferred to a similar camp at La Grange to await the new Kentucky State Reformatory. The castle-like towers lingered for years; the prison's Wikipedia page says they were torn down in November 1950.

## Flight Context

The former penitentiary site lies at about 38.1998°N, 84.8719°W near High and Mero Streets in Frankfort, Kentucky, close to the Kentucky River that flooded it in 1937. Nothing of the prison remains to see, so use the river and the dome of the Kentucky State Capitol, just to the south, as landmarks from 2,000–2,500 feet AGL. Capital City Airport (KFFT) is about 2 nm west-southwest, so watch for pattern traffic; Blue Grass Airport in Lexington (KLEX) is about 16 nm southeast. Best in clear daytime conditions.
