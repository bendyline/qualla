# Rabbit Hash, Kentucky

An Ohio River hamlet of about 250 people with a name born of a supper dish, a beloved 1830s general store rebuilt after a 2016 fire, and a string of elected mayors who happen to be dogs.

**Location:** 38.9422, -84.8458

**Categories:** Census-designated places in Boone County, Kentucky, Unincorporated communities in Kentucky, Census-designated places in Kentucky, Unincorporated communities in Boone County, Kentucky, Kentucky populated places on the Ohio River

## Introduction

In November 2024, a Bluetick Coonhound named Boone won the mayor's office in Rabbit Hash, Kentucky, with 14,162 votes. The community he now leads had 254 residents at the 2020 census. The arithmetic is no mistake. Rabbit Hash, an unincorporated hamlet on the Ohio River in Boone County, has spent more than a quarter century electing dogs as its mayors, and its elections, which have doubled as fundraisers, draw vote totals many times larger than its population. It is a place famous for three things - its name, its canine mayors and a general store that has stood, in one form or another, since about 1831.

## What's in a Name

The hamlet was originally called Carlton. That name caused trouble, because mail kept getting mixed up with the larger community of Carrollton, some 25 miles away on the Ohio River, and the town was required to change it. The old name survives in the Carlton Voter Precinct. The new one may come from the local habit of eating rabbits; in the early nineteenth century, the town was well known for a rabbit hash meal. Folklore adds a livelier version. It is said that in December 1847, townsfolk were comparing plans for Christmas dinner, and one man answered that he would be serving rabbit hash. His neighbors nicknamed him "Rabbit Hash" as a joke, the nickname drifted onto the village itself, and steamboats on the river began stopping to order the hash the town was named for.

## The Best-Preserved Country Store

The heart of the hamlet is the Rabbit Hash General Store, built around 1831 and once described as "the best known and best-preserved country store in Kentucky." It joined the National Register of Historic Places on February 2, 1989. Fourteen years later, on December 4, 2003, the wider Rabbit Hash Historic District followed. It covers 330 acres along Lower River Road and includes 12 buildings, six structures and three objects. Not everything nearby is so quaint. The Duke Energy East Bend Generating Station, a coal-fired power plant, sits in the southeastern part of the census-designated place. And the hamlet has a literary footnote as well: Emma Bell Miles, the writer, poet and artist, lived in Rabbit Hash in her early childhood.

## Fire and the Rebuild

On February 13, 2016, the General Store burned. The Belleview-McVille fire chief first said the fire appeared accidental, with the old potbelly stove inside the most probable cause, but the cause was later reported as faulty wiring in a vintage Coca-Cola cooler. Only three walls and the floor survived, though the store's front sign and some larger pieces of merchandise were pulled from the wreckage. Rabbit Hash rebuilt on the original foundation. That year's mayoral election served as a fundraiser and brought in almost $9,000 toward the restoration. The store reopened on April 1, 2017, assembled from a combination of original material and donor lumber salvaged from other period-correct structures in the area. The care paid off in more than looks: the approach allowed the store to keep its historic designation.

## A Dynasty of Dogs

The first elected mayor in Rabbit Hash history was Goofy Borneman-Calhoun, an adopted dog "of unknown parentage" inaugurated in 1998 for a four-year term. His election was covered in the documentary Rabbit Hash (The Center of the Universe), and he died in office in July 2001 at 16. Junior Cochran, a black Labrador, took office in 2004. His tenure hit trouble when a visitor complained about animals in the General Store and the Northern Kentucky Health Department's scrutiny got him banned from the building, to the dismay of loyal supporters. In March 2008 his owner petitioned for an exemption for the mayor; by the end of May, Junior had died in office at 15. Lucy Lou, a border collie, won a special election that August to become the town's first female mayor.

## Madam Mayor and Her Successors

Lucy Lou made the most of the job. She took a "Talking Points" walk with Bill Geist of CBS Sunday Morning, accepted a $1,000 stimulus check from Reader's Digest, served as grand marshal of the Covington Paw-Rade and placed three years running in the Best Elected Official category of Cincinnati CityBeat's Best Of Cincinnati issue, winning in 2013. In 2015 her office announced she was considering a run for U.S. president. She also became the first Rabbit Hash mayor not to die in office, and lived until September 2018. Brynneth "Brynn" Pawltro, a pit bull, won in 2016 after raising $3,367. Wilbur Beast, a French bulldog, followed in 2020 with 13,143 of 22,985 votes, and Boone's 2024 total of 14,162 is the largest any candidate has received.

## Flight Context

Rabbit Hash lies at 38.9422°N, 84.8458°W on the Kentucky bank of the Ohio River in Boone County. The hamlet is only a handful of buildings along the river road, so plan on about 1,500-2,500 feet AGL and follow the Ohio River as your primary reference; the East Bend coal-fired generating station in the southeastern part of the community makes a useful reference point. Cincinnati/Northern Kentucky International Airport (KCVG) is only about 10.5 nm to the northeast, so expect heavy airline traffic and check current airspace charts before flying low along the river here. Cincinnati Municipal Lunken Field (KLUK) is about 22 nm east-northeast. River fog can linger in the valley on calm mornings.
