# Ark Encounter

Amish builders raised a 510-foot timber-framed ark in the hills of northern Kentucky for a creationist theme park that drew about a million visitors in its first year, fierce scientific criticism and a federal court fight over faith and taxes.

**Location:** 38.6222, -84.5923

**Categories:** Amusement parks in Kentucky, Tourist attractions in Grant County, Kentucky, Replicas and derivatives of Noah's Ark, Young Earth creationism, Timber framing

## Introduction

Before the ark could rise, the bats had to be considered. Late in 2012, crews outside Williamstown, Kentucky, cleared shagbark hickory trees from the site before endangered Indiana bats could migrate in to nest in them. What went up afterward was a 510-foot-long timber-framed ark, held 15 feet off the ground by concrete towers. Ark Encounter, a Christian theme park that opened in 2016, promotes faith-based young Earth creationist beliefs about the age of the Earth and the co-existence of humans and non-avian dinosaurs, ideas that mainstream science regards as pseudoscience. Since then it has drawn large crowds, sharp criticism and a federal lawsuit.

## Answering the Doubters

On December 1, 2010, the young Earth creationist group Answers in Genesis (AiG) and the for-profit Ark Encounter, LLC announced a park meant, in their words, to "lend credence to the biblical account of a catastrophic flood and to dispel doubts that Noah could have fit two of every kind of animal onto a 500-foot-long ark." They projected a $150 million cost, to be raised privately.

The money came slowly. In February 2012 AiG said it had raised only $5 million of the $24 million needed to begin, and the project was split into phases. In December 2013 Williamstown offered $62 million in tax increment financing bonds backed by the park's projected revenues, but by early January only $26.5 million had sold. Then, on February 4, 2014, AiG founder Ken Ham debated the viability of creationism with Bill Nye "the Science Guy." Ham later said the debate spurred bond sales, and construction could begin. AiG officials said the final cost at opening exceeded $100 million.

## Timber Frames and Building Codes

The Troyer Group of Mishawaka, Indiana, oversaw construction, and Amish builders raised the ark using traditional timber-framing techniques. More than 1,000 craftsmen worked on it. Where possible they used board pulling rather than steaming, though steel nails went in to satisfy building codes, and the electric lights inside were designed to look like oil lamps. The vessel contains approximately 3.3 million board feet of wood, much of it from renewable forests or beetle-infested trees. On the starboard side, the hull merges into three 80-foot masonry towers holding stairwells, elevators and restrooms.

The park was built with LEED-certified methods, including geothermal heating, rainwater capture and solar heating. The Washington Post saw in those choices "a fundamental shift in how religiously conservative Christians think of two basic biblical ideas: dominion and stewardship." During construction, former President Jimmy Carter toured the ark at the invitation of LeRoy Troyer, president of the Troyer Group.

## Seven-Seven

The park opened on July 7, 2016, a date chosen to match Genesis 7:7, the verse in which Noah and his family enter the ark. For its first 40 days and 40 nights, it extended its hours. Two days before opening, an estimated 7,000 donors toured the ark at a ribbon-cutting, and publicists reported 30,000 visitors in the first six days. About 150 opponents protested near the highway exit on opening day.

Inside, visitors begin on the lowest deck and climb ramps through the center of the hull, past bays of animal models meant to represent the "kinds" AiG believes were aboard, and dioramas of Noah's workshop. Displays placing humans alongside dinosaurs draw the sharpest objections; visiting scientists have called them not only ridiculous but harmful to young guests. The day after opening, Bill Nye accepted Ham's invitation to tour the ark, debating as they walked. Nye called it "all very troubling" and said "the absolutely wrong idea that the Earth is 6,000 years old" alarmed him. Ham replied, "My biggest concern is you're teaching generations of young people that they're just animals."

## Faith, Hiring and the Tax Code

Public money made the park a legal battleground. In 2011 Kentucky's Tourism Development Finance Authority approved incentives of up to $43.1 million, letting the project recoup part of its construction costs from sales taxes, and Williamstown created a district that returns 75 percent of sales and property taxes collected there to Ark Encounter for 30 years. Church-state separation groups criticized the arrangements. When AiG refused to give written assurance that it would not consider religion in hiring, the state withdrew the incentives in December 2014, saying the facility would be used for religious indoctrination.

Ark Encounter sued. On January 25, 2016, federal judge Gregory F. Van Tatenhove ruled in its favor, and the administration of Governor Matt Bevin, who had taken office weeks earlier, declined to appeal. Job applicants were required to sign a statement of faith, drawing criticism of the park's hiring as discriminatory. When the Freedom From Religion Foundation warned more than 1,000 public school districts against field trips, Ham responded by offering school groups $1 admission per child for the rest of 2016.

## Counting Visitors

AiG had predicted 1.4 to 2.2 million visitors a year. After the first year it reported about 1 million, blaming the mid-season opening, and Williamstown, which had based its budget on those projections, had to adjust downward. By 2020, though, its mayor said attendance was running about ten percent above forecast. In nearby Dry Ridge, the mayor reported hotel occupancy rising from 60 to 98 percent after the ark opened.

The Tri-State Freethinkers have staged protests on the anniversary of the opening, while readers of USA Today's 10Best named Ark Encounter the Best Religious Museum in 2020 and again in 2023. Rainbow lighting on the ark became permanent in 2017, and the grounds have added ziplining, a virtual reality theater and the Ararat Ridge Zoo.

## Flight Context

Ark Encounter sits at about 38.622 N, 84.592 W on the rolling hills outside Williamstown in Grant County, northern Kentucky, near the town's Interstate 75 interchange. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL, the enormous wooden hull is hard to miss: a long, boat-shaped structure raised on towers above open ground, surrounded by large parking areas. Gene Snyder Airport in Falmouth (FAA K62) is about 11 nm to the east-northeast, and Cincinnati/Northern Kentucky International Airport (KCVG) is about 26 nm to the north, so remain clear of its busy airspace. Capital City Airport in Frankfort (KFFT) lies about 30 nm to the south-southwest. The terrain is hilly and wooded, with morning valley fog common in cooler months.
