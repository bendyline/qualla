# Pigeon Roost State Historic Site

A limestone obelisk off U.S. 31 in southern Indiana marks Pigeon Roost, a frontier settlement on Shawnee land where a Native war party killed some two dozen settlers, fifteen of them children by the usual count, in September 1812, in an attack whose causes historians still debate.

**Location:** 38.6172, -85.7737

**Categories:** Indiana State Historic Sites, Indiana in the War of 1812, Landmarks of the War of 1812, Monuments and memorials in Indiana, Frontier history

## Introduction

The settlement took its name from the birds. Passenger pigeons lived in such great numbers in this corner of southern Indiana that the settlers who arrived in 1809 called the place Pigeon Roost. The pigeons are long gone now, the species extinct since 1914, and so is the village. What remains, at the end of a one-lane road off U.S. Route 31 between Scottsburg and Henryville, Indiana, is a tall stone obelisk. It stands for the roughly two dozen settlers, fifteen of them children by the usual count, who were killed here on September 3, 1812, a few months after the War of 1812 began.

## Cabins on Shawnee Land

Pigeon Roost was founded by William E. Collings, and most of its people came from Kentucky. The Collings family had held land grants in what is now Nelson County, Kentucky, signed by Virginia's governor, Patrick Henry, but those grants were deemed illegal. After the passage of the Northwest Ordinance, many squatters crossed the Ohio River and occupied Shawnee lands in southern Indiana, and Pigeon Roost was settled amid that migration. The settlement was a single line of cabins running north and south, about a mile north of present-day Underwood. The nearest Native village lay some 20 miles north, near the Muscatatuck River, and none of its people are believed to have taken part in what followed. For safety, settlers could look to two blockhouses: one to the north at Vienna, and one to the south, near today's Henryville, built by Zebulon Collings.

## A Day in September

On September 3, 1812, a small party of Native warriors, mostly Shawnee and possibly including some Delawares and Potawatomis, led by a man named Missilimetaw, made a surprise attack. It appeared to be coordinated with attacks that same month on Fort Harrison, near Terre Haute, and on Fort Wayne. The war party first came upon two hunters near the village and attacked them, then struck the cabins. Some settlers escaped to Zebulon Collings's blockhouse, but the Collings family lost many members. Henry Collings was killed, and his pregnant wife was stabbed to death. His brother Richard was away serving in the army under Gen. William Henry Harrison; Richard's wife and seven children were among the dead. The count most often repeated is twenty-four settlers killed, fifteen of them children, with two children taken captive; the state's historical marker says only that over twenty settlers died, and other accounts give somewhat lower totals. Four members of the war party are reported to have been killed.

## Stories Handed Down

Much of what is told about that day comes from family tradition, and there is little documented evidence for most of the accounts. William Collings's own actions are disputed. One version has him killing four attackers and holding off the rest with broken or unloaded rifles; another says he and his youngest son slipped out the back of his cabin and hid in a cornfield before escaping to the blockhouse; a family journal credits a visiting Capt. John Norris with saving lives in Collings's home. One of the most wrenching accounts concerns Mrs. Biggs, William Collings's sister, who hid in a thicket with her three children as raiders burned her cabin. When the youngest began to whimper, she pressed her shawl into the baby's mouth. The family reached safety, but according to that account, the infant had suffocated.

## Pursuit, and a Question of Why

The war party was gone before the militia at Charlestown could react, though more than one hundred militiamen mustered the next day. A force of Indiana Rangers from Washington County under Capt. Henry Dawalt caught up with the raiders at Sand Creek, in what is now Bartholomew County. One ranger, John Zink, was shot and later died; he is buried in Brock Cemetery in Salem. The war party escaped with few casualties. Why Pigeon Roost was attacked is still argued. Some historians point to earlier dealings between the attackers and their victims. According to a relative's journal, Henry Collings lived long enough to say that "little Kill Buck" shot him, suggesting he knew the man. Historian Lewis Baird argued that it was not a general raid at all but a local fight, rooted in bad blood between the Collings family and the Native people, including a fawn the Collings boys had taken and refused to return.

## An Obelisk and a Picnic

Pigeon Roost was rebuilt, but it was eventually abandoned. Most of the dead, including members of the Collings and Richey families, were buried in a mass grave. The state of Indiana appropriated $2,000 for a memorial, and the obelisk of Bedford limestone was dedicated on October 1, 1904; in 1929 the area became a state historic site. The state has since turned the site over to Scott County. Newer historic markers stand at the entrance on U.S. 31, along with a picnic shelter and a log cabin built in the style of the settlers' homes. A picnic is traditionally held here on the second Sunday in September, close to the anniversary of the attack.

## Flight Context

Pigeon Roost State Historic Site lies at about 38.6172°N, 85.7737°W in Scott County, Indiana, just off U.S. Route 31 between Scottsburg and Henryville, about a mile north of Underwood. The site itself is small, so use U.S. 31 as a guide at around 1,500–2,000 feet AGL. Nearest airports: Scottsburg Airport (FAA identifier 3R8; no ICAO code listed), about 2.5 nm; Clark Regional (KJVY) near Sellersburg, about 15 nm south; Madison Regional (KIMS, formerly Madison Municipal), about 17 nm east-northeast; Freeman Municipal at Seymour (KSER), about 20 nm north-northwest.
