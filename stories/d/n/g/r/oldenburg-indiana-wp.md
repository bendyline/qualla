# Oldenburg, Indiana

A German-founded Indiana town of fewer than 700 people, known as the "Village of Spires" for the churches, chapels and convent packed into its historic core.

**Location:** 39.3394, -85.2047

**Categories:** German-American history, Towns in Franklin County, Indiana, Towns in Indiana, 1837 establishments in Indiana, Populated places established in 1837

## Introduction

Oldenburg's nickname tells you where to look: up. This small town in southeastern Indiana is known as the "Village of Spires," a title earned by its churches and religious schools rather than by size or bustle. Its whole area covers just 0.43 square miles, and at the 2010 census it was home to 674 people. Yet within that compact footprint stand a stone church from the 1840s, a parish church from 1862, a Franciscan monastery and a convent chapel dated 1889 to 1901. Founded by German settlers and named for a German town, Oldenburg still wears its faith on its rooflines.

## Named for Home

The town began in 1837, when a group of German settlers founded it in what is now Ray Township, Franklin County, and named it after Oldenburg in Germany. The historic district shows how the community grew over the following century. Its buildings date from about 1837 to 1930, and the older ones include everyday places as well as sacred ones: Waechter's Cradle Shop from 1845, the Fischer Tavern from around 1850 and the Hackman Store, built in 1861 and 1862. Oldenburg was not formally incorporated until 1881, more than four decades after its founding. By then, several of the churches that would give it a nickname were already standing.

## Mother Theresa's Sisters

In 1851, Theresa Hackelmeier founded the Sisters of St. Francis of Oldenburg. Born in 1827, she was still in her twenties when she became Mother Theresa to the new community, and she died in 1860, less than a decade later. Her work outlasted her by far. The sisters went on to open numerous schools across the Midwest, carrying the influence of this small Indiana town well beyond Franklin County. At home, their mark is written in stone: the chapel of the Sisters of St. Francis convent, dated 1889 to 1901, stands within the historic district, and the religious educational institutions that grew up here are part of the reason the town is called the Village of Spires.

## Counting the Spires

The Oldenburg Historic District, listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1983, covers 103 acres and includes 106 contributing buildings, along with two contributing sites and six contributing structures. Its sacred buildings span most of the town's history. The Stone Church went up between 1846 and 1848, and Holy Family Church followed in 1862. A cemetery chapel dates to around 1880, and the Franciscan Monastery to 1894, while the Convent of the Immaculate Conception rose between 1889 and 1901. There were a first and a second Corpus Christi Church as well. For a place of a few hundred people, it is a remarkable concentration of churches and chapels, and walking among them it is easy to see how the nickname stuck.

## A Small Town by the Numbers

Oldenburg has stayed small. The 2000 census counted 647 residents; ten years later there were 674, living in 235 households. The town skews older, with a median age of 51 in 2010 and nearly three in ten residents aged 65 or over, and women made up 60.7 percent of the population. Its religious roots run through at least one of its natives, too. John Henry Tihen, born here in 1861, became a prelate of the Roman Catholic Church and lived until 1940. Nearly two centuries after its founding, Oldenburg remains compact enough to walk end to end and dense with the churches that made its name.

## Flight Context

Oldenburg sits at 39.3394°N, 85.2047°W in Franklin County, southeastern Indiana, at roughly 880 feet above sea level. The town is tiny, so descend to about 1,500-2,000 feet AGL and look for a tight cluster of church buildings and steeples in an otherwise rural landscape. Batesville Airport (KHLB) lies only about 2.5 nm to the west, making Oldenburg an easy first sight after departure. Cincinnati/Northern Kentucky International (KCVG) is about 30 nm southeast, and Indianapolis International (KIND) about 55 nm west-northwest. Low sun angles in morning or late afternoon cast the spires' shadows and make them easier to pick out.
