# Keeneland

Built on Jack Keene's farmland west of Lexington in 1936, Keeneland is both a tradition-bound racetrack and, by its own billing, the world's largest Thoroughbred auction house, where Derby winners are bought as yearlings.

## Introduction

For more than sixty years, no announcer's voice called the races over the public-address system at Keeneland. Horses thundered down the stretch while the speakers stayed silent on the action, and it was not until 1997 that the track finally began broadcasting race calls, reportedly the last in North America to do so. That stubbornness says a great deal about this place on the western edge of Lexington, Kentucky. Keeneland guards its traditions carefully, yet it has quietly shaped the modern Thoroughbred world, both on its racing oval and in its sales ring.

## Jack Keene's Farmland

Keeneland began as a nonprofit racing and auction venture on 147 acres of farmland west of Lexington. The land had belonged to Jack Keene, a driving force behind building the facility. Over the decades, Keeneland has plowed the proceeds from its races and auctions back into the Thoroughbred industry and into the surrounding community. Live race meets have been held every April and October since 1936. In 1986, Keeneland was designated a National Historic Landmark for its role in the growth of horse racing and in the sport's revitalization in Lexington, and it is also listed on the National Register of Historic Places.

## Spring and Fall

The racing calendar is short and rich. The 15-day spring meet is one of the richest in North America, with a card of graded stakes races led by the Blue Grass Stakes, a prep race for the Kentucky Derby. The 17-day fall meet counters with graded stakes of its own, including Grade One races used as preps for the Breeders' Cup. Between them, Keeneland says, the two meets offer about 40 graded stakes. Horses run on a dirt oval and on a seven-and-a-half-furlong turf oval installed in 1985, which can be set up two ways: the Keeneland Course, with a temporary rail set 15 feet out, and the Haggin Course, with none. For eight years the main track experimented with the proprietary Polytrack surface, installed in the summer of 2006, before returning to dirt in 2014. In 2009, the Horseplayers Association of North America rated 65 Thoroughbred tracks across the continent and ranked Keeneland first.

## A Queen, and a Film Set

Some of Keeneland's innovations arrived for royalty. Preparing for a visit by Queen Elizabeth II in 1984, the track built a trackside Winner's Circle and created the Queen Elizabeth II Challenge Cup Stakes, now run on the turf course. Hollywood came partly for the opposite reason: the track's "retro feel." Racing scenes in the 2003 film Seabiscuit, including the famous match race with War Admiral, were shot here (filming took place in 2002), and Keeneland also appeared in Dreamer in 2005 and in Secretariat in 2010, which staged key scenes here, including the Belmont Stakes in which the horse completes the Triple Crown. Tradition shows up among the regulars too. Since 1968 the Mark of Distinction has honored people who have contributed to Keeneland and the Thoroughbred industry, and each spring and fall members of the Keeneland Club wear buttons bearing the colors of their racing or farm silks.

## The Grand Slam

When Keeneland hosted the Breeders' Cup for the first time in 2015, many people in the horse industry were skeptical, arguing that the track and the town were too small for such a large event. Then American Pharoah, already the Triple Crown winner, took the Breeders' Cup Classic by six and a half lengths, becoming the first horse to win the unofficial Grand Slam of the Kentucky Derby, Preakness, Belmont and Classic. One Thoroughbred Daily News writer admitted, "I was wrong...it was spectacular." Keeneland was named host again for 2020, the year the COVID-19 pandemic cancelled its April spring meet and squeezed racing into five days in July. On November 7, 2020, Authentic, winner of that year's Kentucky Derby, led the Breeders' Cup Classic field home at Keeneland in a track-record time.

## Where Champions Are Bought

The racetrack is only half of Keeneland. Its sales division bills itself as the world's largest Thoroughbred auction house, holding several sales a year, among them the September Yearling Sale, the November Breeding Stock Sale and the January Horses of All Ages Sale. The roll of horses that passed through its ring is remarkable, including, by Keeneland's own counts as of 2023 and 2024, at least 24 Kentucky Derby winners and 27 Preakness winners, along with many Belmont Stakes and Breeders' Cup winners, Horses of the Year (Justify and Authentic among them) and winners of England's Epsom Derby. To keep buyers coming from abroad, the Keeneland team travels to more than 25 countries, and Keeneland is also known for its reference library.

## Flight Context

Keeneland sits at 38.0456°N, 84.6106°W on Lexington's western edge. Blue Grass Airport (KLEX) lies immediately to the south, about half a nautical mile from the track, so the racecourse is an obvious visual landmark on arrivals and departures; KLEX has an operating control tower and any low-level sightseeing here needs coordination with Lexington ATC. From 2,500-3,500 feet MSL, look for the racing ovals and grandstand just north of the airfield. Downtown Lexington is about 5 nm to the east. Other airports: Capital City Airport (KFFT) in Frankfort about 16 nm to the northwest, and Louisville Muhammad Ali International (KSDF) about 54 nm west. Racing meets run in April and October; summer haze can reduce visibility over the Bluegrass.
