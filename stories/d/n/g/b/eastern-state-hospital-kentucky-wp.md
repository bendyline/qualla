# Eastern State Hospital (Kentucky)

Dedicated by Henry Clay in 1817 but not opened until 1824, Lexington's Eastern State Hospital is often described as the second-oldest psychiatric hospital in the United States, with two centuries of reform, neglect and some 4,400 graves on its grounds.

**Location:** 38.0825, -84.4979

**Categories:** Psychiatric hospitals in Kentucky, National Register of Historic Places in Lexington, Kentucky, 1817 establishments in Kentucky, University of Kentucky buildings and structures, Teaching hospitals in Kentucky

## Introduction

In 1817, Henry Clay delivered the oration at a dedication ceremony in Lexington for a hospital that would never open its doors. The Fayette Hospital, founded the year before by a group of local citizens to help the poor, the disabled and those the era called "lunatic," stood half-built and empty for years. Its unfinished walls became the seed of something larger. Eastern State Hospital, which grew from that building, is often described as the second-oldest psychiatric hospital in the United States, and its two centuries tell the story of how America has treated people in mental distress: sometimes with compassion, often with neglect.

## Before There Was a Place

From 1792 until 1824, Kentuckians with mental illness were boarded out with private individuals at public expense, and a few were sent all the way to a hospital in Williamsburg, Virginia. The Fayette Hospital was meant to change that, but after Clay's oration the building was never finished or occupied. On December 7, 1822, the Kentucky General Assembly passed the Act to Establish a Lunatic Asylum. The state bought ten acres of land along with the abandoned shell of the Fayette Hospital, and on May 1, 1824, the first patient was admitted. As the only facility of its kind in the area, it soon took in people from all over Kentucky and from neighboring states.

## Custody or Care

The early years were bleak. In an 1828 dissertation, Samuel Theobald, a staff physician who also taught at Transylvania University's medical school, described the hospital's goal as "the custodial care of the insane and the protection of society," with patients sent there once they were "no longer tame enough to be kept at home." That was the language of the era, and it reflected reality: care barely met residents' minimal needs, and there was no medical staff of its own. Change arrived in 1844 with the first medical superintendent, John Rowan Allen. His administration brought "moral treatment," meaning compassionate and understanding care, and largely did away with straitjackets, leather cuffs and chains. The reformer Dorothea Dix, a campaigner for better treatment of people with mental illness, reportedly visited in 1847 and again in 1858.

## A Town Behind the Walls

The gains did not hold. After Transylvania's medical school closed in 1859 and the Civil War followed, the hospital's fortunes declined, crowding worsened and restraints returned. For decades the quality of treatment rose and fell with public interest and funding. The hospital became an isolated world of its own. Employees lived on the grounds, and residents did most of the labor: farming, cooking, laundry, sewing, and maintaining buildings and grounds. Most of the food was grown on site, across grounds that once reached 400 acres. By 1945 the population stood at 2,000. The 1940s brought Metrazol-shock and electric shock therapy, insulin therapy followed in the early 1950s, and after tranquilizing drugs arrived in 1954, insulin treatment faded out entirely by 1957. Along the way the institution cycled through a long string of names, most built around the words "Lunatic Asylum," before the General Assembly settled on Eastern State Hospital on January 2, 1912.

## The Unclaimed

Not everyone went home. From 1824 until 1954, patients who were unknown, whose families could not afford to bring them home, or who simply went unclaimed were buried on hospital property. By Wikipedia's account the cemeteries were moved at least twice before reaching their current location behind the Hope Center in 1984. In 2011, when a community college began building on the old campus, archaeologists exhumed an unmarked mid-nineteenth-century graveyard and, after two years of study, reburied the remains of 178 people. The state acknowledges about 4,400 remains there, though the true number may be higher, and no one knows how many unmarked graves lie across the property. As work continues on the land, any remains found are re-interred in this cemetery, a small act of dignity for people whose names were often lost.

## Three Towers, Old Names

The land shrank first: in 1956 more than 300 acres were sold to IBM. Then the twentieth century slowly pulled care outward. Community mental health centers spread across Kentucky in the 1960s, treating many people as outpatients, and the hospital, still home to over 1,000 residents as late as 1967, needed fewer beds. In 1993, as many states rapidly closed inpatient hospitals to contain costs, a nonprofit, Bluegrass Regional Mental Health-Mental Retardation Board, worried Kentucky might do the same and pursued running the hospital itself. It took over management in September 1995 under a state contract. On September 10, 2013, patients moved into a new $129 million facility on the University of Kentucky's Coldstream Research Campus: one large building with three three-story towers named Gragg, Allen and Wendell after buildings on the old Fourth Street campus. Months earlier, in January 2013, state officials had announced that UK HealthCare had signed a letter of intent to operate the hospital, with an initial state budget of $43 million a year. By December 2013, despite preservation efforts, most of the historic buildings had been demolished; only the administration and laundry buildings were kept, for reuse by the community college. Today the hospital, owned by the Commonwealth and managed by UK HealthCare, provides inpatient care; the 2013 plans called for 239 beds, and UK HealthCare now describes 195 acute-care beds.
