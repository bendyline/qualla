# Bryan Station

At Bryan Station, a frontier fort by Elkhorn Creek near Lexington, the women walked out to the spring under the eyes of a hidden besieging force in August 1782 and brought back the water that helped the fort hold.

**Location:** 38.0759, -84.4153

**Categories:** Geography of Fayette County, Kentucky, Former populated places in Kentucky, American Revolutionary War sites, Battles of the American Revolutionary War in Kentucky

## Introduction

The women walked out to the spring as if nothing were wrong. In August 1782, a large force of warriors and British-allied rangers lay hidden around Bryan Station, a fortified frontier settlement near present-day Lexington, Kentucky. The defenders knew they were there. Rather than tip off the attackers by changing their routine, the men let the women leave the fort to fetch water as usual. Led by Mary "Polly" Hawkins Craig, they carried back the water that would help the fort survive the siege, and later generations raised a monument in their honor.

## High Ground by Elkhorn Creek

Bryan Station, sometimes called Bryan's Station and often misspelled Bryant's Station, began as a camp at the spring in about 1775 or 1776 and was fortified as a station by 1779. Brothers Morgan, James, William and Joseph Bryan, together with their brother-in-law William Grant, came from the Yadkin River Valley in Rowan County, North Carolina. The family had close ties to a famous name: William Bryan was married to Mary Boone and William Grant to Elizabeth Jane "Betsy" Boone, both sisters of Daniel Boone. The party chose high ground near the southern bank of Elkhorn Creek, built a few cabins, planted sixty acres and cleared forest of blue ash, black walnut, honey locust and sycamore. A nearby spring supplied the water. The fort took the shape of an elongated parallelogram, much longer than it was wide, leaving generous room between the roughly forty cabins eventually built inside. Frontier life proved brutal. After a disastrous winter and attacks by Native Americans, the surviving Bryans abandoned the station in August 1780 and returned to North Carolina, but the remaining settlers stayed and withstood several more attacks.

## The Siege of 1782

The most important attack came in August 1782, during the American Revolutionary War. Warriors from the Wyandot, Lenape and Shawnee nations besieged the fort alongside a detachment of Butler's Rangers led by Captain William Caldwell and Simon Girty. Daniel Boone later estimated the force at 400 to 500 strong. None of the Bryan men were living in the garrison by then. Even so, the defenders were not ready for what they faced; they did not know how many warriors waited outside, or that the attackers had British support. The spring became the fort's lifeline. The women's trips for water guarded against dangerous dehydration in an unusually hot summer, and the water was also needed to fight the burning arrows the attackers used. Had the defenders collapsed from heat or had the fort burned, the women and children sheltering inside would have been exposed. Inside the walls, Betsy Boone Grant cared for the wounded, service the Sons of the American Revolution later recognized by naming her a "Patriot - Defender of Bryan Station."

## Echoes of the Siege

One moment from the siege would echo far into the future. According to later tradition, a flaming arrow lodged in a cradle near the head of an infant who grew up to be Colonel Richard Mentor Johnson. Decades later, in the War of 1812, the earliest accounts of the Battle of the Thames credited Johnson with killing Tecumseh, a claim historians still question. One account has him using a pistol loaded by his orderly, Captain Elijah Craig, who is said to have been at Bryan Station too, as an 18-year-old defender. The siege itself ended when Native scouts reported that Kentucky militia were on the way, and the attackers withdrew. The militia pursued Caldwell's force, only to be defeated three days later at the Battle of Blue Licks, about 33 miles to the northeast. In August 1896, the Lexington chapter of the Daughters of the American Revolution raised a monument commemorating the spring and the role it played in saving the fort; the spring has been described as lying on private property. The station's site lies along present-day Bryan Station Road, about three miles northeast of New Circle Road on the southern bank of Elkhorn Creek, near Briar Hill Road. A couple of miles to the west-southwest, Bryan Station High School carries the name forward, and its athletic teams compete as the "Defenders."
