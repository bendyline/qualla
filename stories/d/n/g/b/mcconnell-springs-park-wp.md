# McConnell Springs Park

The karst springs in Lexington, Kentucky, where frontiersmen in 1775 heard news of the Revolution's first battle and named their future town after it - now a park of roughly 26 acres where water sinks and rises again.

**Location:** 38.0553, -84.5278

**Categories:** Parks in Lexington, Kentucky, National Register of Historic Places in Lexington, Kentucky, Parks on the National Register of Historic Places in Kentucky, Springs of Kentucky

## Introduction

In June 1775, a party of frontiersmen camped beside a spring in the Kentucky wilderness received news that had travelled a long way to reach them. Word came from Fort Boonesborough that on April 19 the first battle of the American Revolution had been fought at Lexington, Massachusetts. William McConnell, a pioneer from Pennsylvania, his brother Francis and their companions chose to honor that battle in the name of the settlement they meant to build. They called it Lexington. The name stuck as the settlement grew into a town, which Virginia chartered in 1782, and the city that grew from it still carries the word chosen at this water's edge. Today the spring sits inside McConnell Springs, a natural park of roughly twenty-six acres where the water itself seems to play hide-and-seek.

## Water That Keeps Disappearing

The land McConnell claimed included a "sinking spring," and the phrase is literal. Beneath the park lies karst, a landscape shaped as water slowly dissolves soluble rock such as limestone, leaving behind sinkholes, caves, underground streams and springs. Here the result is a complex system of sinking springs. Artesian water rises to the surface, slips underground, reappears, runs in the open for a while and then vanishes again, only to emerge once more a third of a mile away. McConnell Springs is the only known site in Fayette County with a series of springs behaving this way. The phenomenon is local, but the geology is not: about a fifth of the United States is underlain by karst, and karst aquifers supply a large share of the groundwater Americans drink, a figure often put at about 40 percent. What looks like a quiet woodland stream is really a window into the hidden plumbing beneath a great deal of the country.

## Blue Hole, the Boils and the Final Sink

Three springs give the park its character. The first, the Blue Hole, is a circular pool whose water appears strikingly blue. The color is attributed to the pool's depth, roughly ten to fifteen feet depending on the source, and to the way its limestone-rich water filters sunlight. The second is the Boils, and its name misleads in a different way. Nothing here is hot. After heavy rain, water rushes up from underground with enough force that the surface churns as if it were boiling. The third is the Final Sink, a sinkhole that gathers all the water flowing through the park and sends it back below ground, where it travels on to resurface at Preston's Cave Spring. Standing at its edge, you are watching the end of one visible stream and the unseen beginning of another.

## Gunpowder, Dairy and Decay

In the years after McConnell's camp, the land around the springs was put to work. At various times it served agriculture, a gunpowder mill, a dairy, a railroad and assorted industrial uses. Eventually the birthplace of Lexington's name became a dilapidated, unused patch of ground. Recognition arrived before rescue did. McConnell Springs was added to the National Register of Historic Places on January 17, 1976, yet the property remained in disrepair until the early 1990s, when a community reclamation effort took hold, with Isabel Yates and Jim Rebmann among its key leaders. That work gave rise to the Friends of McConnell Springs and, in 1994, to the park's opening to the public. The spot where frontiersmen had once pitched camp was, after a long detour through industry and decay, somewhere people came on purpose again.

## A Sanctuary for Walking

The park sits within Lexington's city park system, and the nonprofit Friends of McConnell Springs work in partnership with the Lexington-Fayette Urban County Government's Division of Parks and Recreation. Its mission has three parts: to restore and preserve McConnell Springs as a national historic site, to serve as an educational resource for culture and environment, and to remain a passive recreational park. That last phrase shapes the visit. Nearly two miles of trails wind through the grounds, including a half-mile paved loop that makes the park accessible to most visitors, and an education center stands alongside them. Because the city protects the grounds as a sanctuary, pets and camping are not allowed - a small irony at a place whose written history begins with a campsite. The water's surface quality is monitored regularly to make sure the springs stay natural and free of pollutants.

## Flight Context

McConnell Springs Park lies at 38.0553°N, 84.5278°W, within the city of Lexington, Kentucky, about 4 nautical miles east-northeast of Blue Grass Airport (KLEX). At only about 26 acres, the wooded park is a small target amid the surrounding city; 2,000-2,500 feet AGL gives the best chance of picking out its tree canopy and pools. From KLEX, track roughly east-northeast for about 4 nm. Other nearby fields include Georgetown-Scott County Regional Airport (FAA LID 27K) about 12 nm north-northeast and Capital City Airport (KFFT) in Frankfort about 19 nm west-northwest. Clear, low-haze days give the best chance of spotting the spring pools under the trees.
