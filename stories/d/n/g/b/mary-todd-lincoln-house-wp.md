# Mary Todd Lincoln House

Once a tavern called the Sign of the Green Tree, then the girlhood home of Mary Todd and later a bawdy house, this Lexington townhouse became what is billed as the first historic site restored in honor of a First Lady.

**Location:** 38.0514, -84.5028

## Introduction

Before it was a future First Lady's home, the house was an inn called "The Sign of the Green Tree." Decades after she left, it became a bawdy house where a woman named Belle Brezing went to work. In between, from 1832 to 1839, it was where Mary Todd grew into adulthood, years before she married Abraham Lincoln. This Lexington, Kentucky, townhouse has held some very different lives. Today the fourteen-room house is a museum filled with period furniture, portraits and artifacts from the Todd and Lincoln families, and it tells the complicated story of the woman who lived here.

## The Sign of the Green Tree

The house went up around 1803 to 1806 as an inn and tavern. Mary's father, Robert Smith Todd, bought it for his family, and the Todds moved into the three-story home in 1832. Mary Ann Todd had been born in Lexington on December 13, 1818. Her mother died in 1825, and a year later her father married Elizabeth "Betsy" Humphreys. Mary grew up in comfort. The Todds were a wealthy, slave-holding family, and she attended Madame Mentelle's finishing school, where lessons centered on French and literature. The museum today begins its account of her complex life with that refined upbringing in a wealthy, slave-holding household.

## Mrs. Lincoln Comes Home

In October 1839, Mary moved to Springfield, Illinois, and on November 4, 1842, she married Abraham Lincoln. The couple came back to visit her family here, most notably for about three weeks in the fall of 1847, on their way to Washington. The house museum notes that census and tax records show an average of about five enslaved people, some owned and some rented, living and working in the Todd household between 1820 and 1849. One account says Robert Todd bought five enslaved people at Cheapside, Lexington's market a few blocks away, in 1846, but the museum's own account does not mention that purchase. The years ahead held heavy grief. Of the Lincolns' four sons, three died before their mother: Edward in 1850, William in 1862 and Thomas in 1871. Only Robert outlived her. In 1875, Robert began proceedings to have her institutionalized, and on May 20 of that year she was committed to a private asylum in Batavia, Illinois, though she was released after about three months. She spent her final years as a reclusive, mourning widow and died in Springfield on July 16, 1882, at the age of 63.

## Belle Brezing's Address

The house had a very different tenant later in the century. Starting in 1879, Belle Brezing worked here in a bawdy house run by Jenny Hill. She later became a madam in her own right, running her own brothel, and she is believed to have been the model for Belle Watling, the character in Margaret Mitchell's 1936 novel Gone With the Wind, although Mitchell denied it. It is an unexpected chapter for a building better known for a First Lady.

## The First Lady's House Restored

In the mid-1970s, Beula C. Nunn, wife of Governor Louie B. Nunn, led the effort to preserve and restore the house, working with the Kentucky Mansions Preservation Foundation, the group founded in 1968 that she led, and the Metropolitan Women's Club of Lexington to build support. It is billed as the first historic site restored in honor of a First Lady, and it opened to the public as a house museum on June 9, 1977, operated by the Kentucky Mansions Preservation Foundation. In June 1996, the Beula C. Nunn Garden was dedicated. Its enclosed beds hold trees, plants, herbs and shrubs chosen to represent what may have grown in the Todd family's garden in the early nineteenth century.
