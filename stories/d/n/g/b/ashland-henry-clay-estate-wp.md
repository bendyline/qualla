# Ashland (Henry Clay estate)

Henry Clay's Lexington plantation, built and worked by enslaved people, was torn down and rebuilt by his son, served as a university campus, and has been a museum since 1950, while the woman who sued Clay for her freedom is part of its story.

**Location:** 38.0286, -84.4800

## Introduction

In 1829, a woman Henry Clay held in slavery took him to court. Charlotte Dupuy sued the famous Kentucky statesman for her freedom and for that of her two children in the circuit court in Washington, D.C., 17 years before Dred Scott filed his better-known challenge. Her case is one thread in the story of Ashland, Clay's plantation in Lexington, in the Bluegrass region of central Kentucky. The buildings were raised by enslaved people, who also grew and harvested hemp, raised livestock, and cooked and cleaned for the Clays. The estate is a National Historic Landmark now, and their labor is as much a part of its story as his career.

## A House on Porous Brick

Around 1806, using the profits of forced-labor farming, Clay had enslaved people build him a Federal-style house. By 1809 he was calling the estate "Ashland," after the ash forest that stood on the site, though it is unclear whether he chose the name or kept an older one. The name would travel far: Ashland, Kentucky, in Boyd County, and towns called Ashland in Missouri, Virginia, Wisconsin and Pennsylvania were also named for the estate. Between 1811 and 1814 he added two wings designed for him by Benjamin Latrobe. The house had a weakness built in. Inferior materials, especially a porous kind of brick, left the structure unstable, and it was likely damaged by the New Madrid earthquake and its aftershocks in 1811 and 1812. Clay made many repairs, but none could fully stabilize it. Politics kept him in Washington for most of the years between 1810 and 1829, but Ashland remained the family's home from about 1806 until his death in 1852.

## Charlotte Dupuy's Claim

Charlotte Dupuy built her case on two grounds: her mother had been free, and a previous enslaver named Condon had promised to free her and her children. In 1830 the circuit court ruled against her, finding that the agreement with Condon did not apply to her later enslavement. She was imprisoned in Alexandria, and Clay had her removed from Washington and sent to New Orleans, to the home of his daughter and son-in-law, Martin Duralde Jr. She was held in slavery there for another decade. On October 12, 1840, Clay finally freed Charlotte Dupuy and her daughter, Mary Ann, in New Orleans, and he freed her son, Charles, in 1844. According to the 1860 census, she and her husband, Aaron Dupuy, were living together as free people in Fayette County, Kentucky.

## Torn Down and Built Again

Clay divided Ashland among three sons. After his death, his son James Brown Clay took the house and about 325 surrounding acres, and his widow, Lucretia Clay, moved out in 1854. James then had the house razed, which Wikipedia dates to 1854, though the estate's own history dates the rebuilt mansion only to 1857. Local architect Thomas Lewinski designed its replacement, completed by 1857, reusing the old footprint and foundation, the floorplan and the massing but updating the style. The result mixes Federal architecture with Italianate details. Inside, James added Greek Revival features and decorated lavishly with imported furnishings bought in New York City. James, a Confederate sympathizer, left Kentucky during the Civil War and died in Montreal in 1864, and in 1866 his widow, Susan Jacob Clay, sold the estate.

## Campus, Family Home, Museum

Ashland's next owner was a university. Kentucky University bought the estate and made it part of its campus, and its founder and regent, John Bryan Bowman, lived in the mansion and used part of it to house and display the University Natural History Museum. The Agricultural and Mechanical College, Kentucky A&M, occupied Clay's former farm. When Kentucky University split into what became Transylvania University and the University of Kentucky, it sold Ashland in 1882. Soon the house came back to the family. Clay's granddaughter Anne Clay McDowell and her husband, Henry Clay McDowell, bought about 325 acres with the outbuildings and moved in with their children in 1883, adding gas lighting and later electricity, indoor plumbing and a telephone. Their eldest daughter, Nannette McDowell Bullock, lived at Ashland until her death in 1948. She founded the Henry Clay Memorial Foundation, which bought and preserved the estate, and the house opened to the public as a museum in 1950.

## Flight Context

Ashland stands at 38.0286°N, 84.4800°W in Lexington, Kentucky, in the heart of the Bluegrass region. From 2,000 to 2,500 feet, look for the estate grounds and the mansion set amid Lexington's residential neighborhoods southeast of downtown. Blue Grass Airport (KLEX) is about 6 nm west and makes the natural departure point for a short sightseeing loop over the city. Check the airspace around Blue Grass Airport before descending.
