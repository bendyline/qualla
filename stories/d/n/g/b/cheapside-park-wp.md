# Cheapside Park

Lexington's Cheapside was the town's main marketplace and the site of a large slave market before the Civil War; in 2020 the city renamed the block Henry A. Tandy Centennial Park.

**Location:** 38.0480, -84.4981

**Categories:** Parks in Lexington, Kentucky, History of slavery in Kentucky, Slave markets in the United States, History of Lexington, Kentucky

## Introduction

The accounts of two slave traders named Downing and Hughes survive. They bought 13 people for $5,292.50, spent $257.72 carrying them to Natchez, Mississippi, and sold them there for $8,695.00, a return of about 57 percent. Behind every figure in those accounts was a person, carried far from home. In Lexington, Kentucky, the trade had a prominent home of its own: a single downtown block between Upper Street and Mill Street. Its name was Cheapside, and for most of the nineteenth century it was the town's main marketplace, where goods were bought and sold, and where, before the Civil War, human beings were too.

## A Name That Means "To Buy"

The block began as Public Square. Cheapside is a common English name for a marketplace, derived from the Old English "ceapan," meaning "to buy," and it turns up often in literature. In Lexington, its earliest recorded use dates to 1813, in an advertisement for the Todd and Smith Wholesale Grocery, owned by Robert Smith Todd, the father of Mary Todd Lincoln. That building still stands and now houses a bourbon bar, The Bluegrass Tavern. For much of the 1800s, Cheapside was where Lexington did its business. The market gathered local slave traders, slaveholders and others connected to the trade in one place.

## Lives Sold at Cheapside

The slave market at Cheapside was large, and it played a prominent role in the trade. Many of the enslaved people sold here were sent to the lower South; others were forced to labor in the surrounding area. Among those sold were young women of mixed race whom traders marketed as "fancy girls," women sold into sexual slavery. Many of them had been born and held within Kentucky itself. Debt fed the trade too. Henry Johnson, an attorney, often collected debts by selling enslaved people who had been pledged as collateral. One surviving deed shows him using a power of attorney to take a man named Bill and sell him to J.R. Megowan. Bill appears in the record only as property, but he was a man with a life of his own.

## A Future President in Town

By one account, in 1846 Robert Todd, the father of Lincoln's wife, purchased five enslaved people at Cheapside while Abraham Lincoln was visiting the family. The Mary Todd Lincoln House dates the Lincolns' documented Lexington stay to three weeks in the fall of 1847, and no source confirms that Lincoln attended any sale. The state had already made one attempt to rein in the trade. In 1833, the Kentucky General Assembly passed the Non-Importation Act, which banned bringing enslaved people into the Commonwealth for the purpose of selling them, in an effort to ban or at least cripple the business. The slave trade is said to have been outlawed in 1864, though slavery itself lasted in Kentucky until the Thirteenth Amendment took effect in December 1865. Cheapside's market continued long afterward, reportedly until 1922, when it was declared a public nuisance and banned.

## Taking Back Cheapside

The push to reimagine the block began in 2017 with a community organization called Take Back Cheapside. The city removed two statues celebrating Confederate figures, Gen. John Hunt Morgan and John C. Breckinridge, the Confederate Secretary of War, from the old courthouse lawn in October 2017; the Morgan memorial, erected in 1911 by the United Daughters of the Confederacy, had been paid for in part by the state. Then, in August 2020, the council voted to rename the area Henry A. Tandy Centennial Park. Tandy was an entrepreneur, a leader and a mason whose construction company laid the brick beneath the courthouse's stone façade, so the park now honors a man whose work is part of the courthouse itself. A historical marker commissioned by the Kappa Alpha Psi fraternity stands at the corner of Short and Upper Streets.
