# Federal Medical Center, Lexington

North of Lexington, the federal prison hospital known today as FMC Lexington began in 1935 as the United States Narcotic Farm, a first-of-its-kind addiction treatment center that left its mark on American literature and jazz lore.

## Introduction

The writer Alexander King remembered hearing "the best jazz ever played anywhere" at a hospital outside Lexington, Kentucky. The lineup changed constantly, and every player in it was a patient: famous jazz musicians who had come voluntarily to be treated for heroin addiction. The place King wrote about opened in 1935 as the United States Narcotic Farm, the first institution of its kind in the country. The institution still operates north of Lexington today, but it serves a very different purpose. It is now the Federal Medical Center, Lexington, a federal prison for inmates who need medical or mental health care.

## A Farm for Recovery

The Narcotic Farm opened in May 1935, on roughly 1,000 acres. Its purpose was to take in people with drug problems who had been admitted "voluntarily" and to treat them, mostly with experimental methods. The grounds included a farm where patients worked. The name did not last long. It soon became the U.S. Public Health Service Hospital, and in 1967 it was renamed again, as the National Institute of Mental Health Clinical Research Center. Through every change of name, it remained something of a hybrid, part hospital and part prison.

## Volunteers, So-Called

Over the years, about two-thirds of the people sent to the hospital were counted as volunteers. The word covered a lot of ground. Many traveled to Lexington on their own, seeking a way out of addiction. Others were volunteers in name only, choosing the hospital because the alternative was a federal sentence. The remaining third of the population was there on federal charges tied directly or indirectly to drug use. At its peak, that federal-charge population alone reached 1,499 inmates. Behind each number was someone trying, willingly or not, to get clean in a place that was still learning how to help them.

## Lexington on the Page

The hospital became famous enough to turn up in fiction and memoir. In Nelson Algren's novel The Man With the Golden Arm and its 1955 screen adaptation, the morphine-addicted Frankie Machine comes home to his Chicago neighborhood after being detoxed at the Lexington facility. William S. Burroughs sent the autobiographical hero of his book Junkie to "Lexington," where he checks himself in to quit heroin, and both Burroughs and his son, William Seward Burroughs III, were patients there in real life. Alexander King's Mine Enemy Grows Older recalls his own stays and all that remarkable jazz. In Gayl Jones's novel Corregidora, set in Kentucky, a character named Jeffrene is described late in the story as working at the narcotics hospital.

## From Hospital to Prison

In 1974, the institution became a federal prison, though it kept a "psychiatric hospital" title until 1998. That year, most psychiatric patients were moved to other federal medical centers as psychiatric care shifted toward the new Federal Medical Center in Devens, Massachusetts, which opened in January 1999. Today FMC Lexington is run by the Federal Bureau of Prisons, part of the U.S. Department of Justice. It is designated an administrative facility, which means it holds inmates of every security classification who need medical or mental health care, and the Bureau of Prisons announced in July 2026 that it will permanently close the adjacent minimum-security satellite camp, which local news reported as holding about 232 inmates, citing staffing shortages and deferred maintenance; no closing date was given. It sits on Lexington's northwest side, a little over 5 miles from downtown in a straight line (Wikipedia gives 7 miles north of Lexington) and about 18 miles southeast of Frankfort, the state capital.

## Flight Context

FMC Lexington lies at 38.1056°N, 84.5598°W, about 4.5 nm northwest of downtown Lexington, Kentucky, in the Bluegrass countryside. It is an active federal prison, so view it only in passing and avoid lingering or circling overhead; 2,500 to 3,000 feet gives a respectful perspective on the large institutional campus.
