# Comair Flight 5191

Before dawn on August 27, 2006, a regional jet bound for Atlanta took off from the wrong runway at Lexington's Blue Grass Airport, and 49 of the 50 people aboard never came home.

**Location:** 38.0379, -84.6154

**Categories:** Airliner accidents and incidents in Kentucky, Airliner accidents and incidents caused by pilot error, Airliner accidents and incidents involving runway overruns, Delta Air Lines accidents and incidents, History of Lexington, Kentucky, 2006 in Kentucky

## Introduction

The tower at Blue Grass Airport cleared Comair Flight 5191 for Runway 22, a strip of pavement 7,003 feet long. In the darkness before dawn on August 27, 2006, the jet lined up on Runway 26 instead. That runway was unlit, and at 3,501 feet it was barely half the length of the one they had been given. At around 6:07 in the morning, the Bombardier CRJ100 began its takeoff roll toward Atlanta, and Lexington, Kentucky, was minutes away from a tragedy that would send regulators back to a basic question: does the crew know which runway it is on?

## Half a Runway

Flight 5191 was a scheduled domestic run from Lexington to Atlanta, operated by Comair and sold under the Delta Air Lines brand as Delta Connection. The aircraft, registration N431CA, ran out of pavement before it could climb away. It struck a low earthen wall beside a ditch and briefly left the ground. Its landing gear clipped the airport's perimeter fence, and then it smashed into trees. The jet came to rest about 1,800 feet beyond the end of the runway and was destroyed in the fire that followed. Of the 50 people on board, 49 were killed: all 47 passengers and two of the three crew members, including the captain, 35-year-old Jeffrey Clay.

## The People on Board

Most of the passengers were Americans from the Lexington area, and they ranged in age from 16 to 72. Among them was a young couple who had been married the day before and were flying to California on their honeymoon. Comair released the passenger manifest two days after the crash, and the Lexington Herald-Leader published a list of the victims with short biographies, giving each name a life behind it. The city mourned in public. A memorial service was held on August 31 at the Lexington Opera House, and a second public service followed at Rupp Arena on September 10, 2006.

## One Survivor, No Memory

Only one person came out of the wreckage alive. James Polehinke, the 44-year-old first officer, was pulled from the aircraft by Lexington-Fayette and airport police officers. He had multiple broken bones, a collapsed lung and severe bleeding, and surgeons amputated his left leg. Doctors later determined that he had suffered brain damage and had no memory of the crash or of the events leading up to it. The one witness who had been in the cockpit could not tell anyone what happened there, so the answers would have to come from the cockpit voice recorder and the evidence on the ground.

## What the Recorder Heard

The National Transportation Safety Board released transcripts of the cockpit voice recorder, along with an engineering report, on January 17, 2007. In July, a Comair flight instructor testified that he would have failed both pilots for breaking sterile flight deck rules, which bar non-essential conversation during critical phases of flight. The board's final report, released later that month, cited the crew's "non-pertinent conversation" as a contributing factor. The board concluded that the probable cause was the crew's failure to use available cues and aids to identify the airplane's location on the airport surface, and their failure to cross-check and verify that they were on the correct runway before takeoff. The board could not determine whether fatigue played a part. Only one controller was on duty in the tower, contrary to the Federal Aviation Administration's own August 2005 guidance calling for two; investigators said a second controller was unlikely to have prevented the accident, and the board narrowly declined to list the controller's handling of an administrative task as a contributing factor. By spring 2007 the FAA had reminded pilots to positively confirm their position before crossing the hold-short line and again when starting a takeoff, and advised that training cover runway-lighting requirements for night departures.

## Forty-Nine Birds

The legal reckoning ran for years. Families of 45 of the 47 passengers sued Comair, and a federal judge ruled in 2008 that its parent company, Delta, could not be held liable because Comair ran its own management and employed its own pilots. Comair settled with most families, and a settlement with the federal government split liability 22% to the United States and 78% to Comair. In December 2009, a Lexington jury awarded $7.1 million in compensatory damages to the estate and daughters of 39-year-old Bryan Woodward. The airport changed too. Runway 8/26 closed in March 2009, and a new 4,000-foot Runway 9/27, built in a separate location not connected to Runway 22, opened on August 4, 2010, reportedly covering the ground where the jet came down. Across town, the Flight 5191 Memorial Commission chose the University of Kentucky Arboretum for a lasting tribute. Sculptor Douwe Blumberg created it: 49 stylized birds in flight, one for each of the lives lost that morning, above a base inscribed with the victims' names. The community gathered there on August 27, 2026, for the twentieth anniversary of the crash.

## Flight Context

The crash site lies at 38.0379°N, 84.6154°W, on the western side of Blue Grass Airport (KLEX), about half a nautical mile west of the airport's reference point; the ground is reportedly now covered by Runway 9/27, which opened in 2010. KLEX has an operating control tower, so any low pass over the field requires coordination with Lexington ATC; otherwise view it from 2,500-3,500 feet MSL on a published approach or while transiting with clearance. Keeneland Race Course, with its oval track and grandstands, sits immediately north-northeast of the site and is the easiest visual anchor, with downtown Lexington about 5-6 nm to the east. Other airports: Capital City Airport (KFFT) in Frankfort about 16 nm northwest, Central Kentucky Regional Airport near Richmond (no ICAO code listed; FAA identifier RGA) about 28 nm south-southeast, and Louisville Muhammad Ali International (KSDF) about 54 nm west. Summer haze can soften visibility over the Bluegrass; the accident itself happened before sunrise, a reminder to confirm your runway on any night departure here.
