# Lanier Mansion

A banker's 1844 Greek Revival showpiece on the Ohio River in Madison, Indiana, where a corkscrew staircase climbs to a skylit cupola and archaeologists are still peeling the house and gardens back to their original look.

**Location:** 38.7350, -85.3872

## Introduction

James F. D. Lanier reportedly paid $25,000 for a house that would one day be counted among the nation's finest examples of Greek Revival architecture, and then he lived in it for only about five years. The mansion he left behind still stands at 601 West First Street in Madison, Indiana, its south front facing formal gardens and a pasture that looks out toward the Ohio River. Lanier moved on to New York City. The house stayed, passed to his sons, and eventually to the public. Today it is a National Historic Landmark, and the patient work of returning it to its 1844 appearance continues.

## Brick, Limestone and Tulip Poplar

Lanier was one of Madison's pioneers. He arrived in 1817 and later practiced law, but his fortune came in the 1830s, when he turned to banking and finance. He became president of the Madison Branch of the State Bank of Indiana and a major investor in Indiana's first railroad. In 1840, with that success behind him, he commissioned the prominent local architect Francis Costigan to design and build a residence. Costigan chose the Greek Revival style, popular in the mid-nineteenth century, and drew on the pattern books of New York architect Minard Lafever. Much of the house came from its own surroundings. The brick, limestone and timber were manufactured nearby or on the premises, and the wood was largely tulip poplar, which was plentiful in the area. The mansion was completed and occupied in 1844. In 1849 Lanier left for New York, where he and Richard Winslow formed the investment firm Winslow, Lanier & Co.

## A Staircase to the Sky

Step into the entrance hall and the eye goes up. At the hall's midpoint, a self-supporting staircase trimmed with a Greek key design corkscrews all the way to the cupola, where a skylight pours natural light into the center of the house. The main floor was built to impress. The library's highly stylized ceiling is finished in gold leaf, deep red and blue. The dining room is papered in a botanical design from the French manufacturer Zuber, applied by a process described as "British Transfer," and its chandelier is adorned with Bacchus, the god of wine. Climb the stairs and the house grows plainer. The family's private rooms, with bedrooms in the four corners, a study at the north end of the hall and a nursery at the south, are less ornate, and the ceilings drop to 12 feet. The third floor, simply finished and just over six feet high, housed the smaller children, additional servants and storage.

## Alexander's Lost Gardens

In 1861 the deed passed to Lanier's son Alexander, who moved in and brought the house up to date with a coal furnace, gas lighting, a toilet and bathtubs. Outside, he developed large, elaborate gardens with greenhouses, tended by several professional gardeners. None of it lasted. Time and neglect took their toll, and when flooding left silt a foot deep, the old landscape was erased entirely. Today's gardens are a re-creation based on an 1876 lithograph of the site. The ten-acre grounds now fall into four areas: Craven Square, the Upper Terrace, the Lower Terrace and the Pasture. Craven Square, opposite the north side of the house, joined the grounds only in 1944, and the city closed the stretch of First Street in front of the house so the landscape runs uninterrupted for a full block. The Upper Terrace holds a walled sunken garden built in 1928. Below it, geometric parterres enclosed by boxwood hedges and gravel paths spread before the south facade, and beyond them the Pasture opens toward the river.

## Digging Back to 1844

The Laniers kept the house until 1917, when James's youngest son, Charles, donated it to the Jefferson County Historical Society. In 1925 the society, with the family's blessing, handed control to the state, which promptly opened it as a historic house museum, and it was declared a State Memorial in 1926. National Historic Landmark status followed in 1994. Since the 1990s, archaeologists and historians have been working toward the mansion's 1844 appearance, with much of the research funded by the National Society of the Colonial Dames of America and other grants. Their excavations have located the former dog kennels, the poultry house, greenhouses, cisterns and the carriage house, along with the original Lanier home, which faced Elm Street. In 2003 the carriage house rose again on its original foundation, rebuilt on the very footprint the archaeologists had uncovered.
