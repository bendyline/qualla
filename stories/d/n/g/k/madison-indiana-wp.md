# Madison, Indiana

An Ohio River town that was once Indiana's third-largest city, Madison kept its nineteenth-century downtown, a proud Underground Railroad past and what is billed as the world's only community-owned unlimited hydroplane, which once beat the best-funded teams in racing on its home water.

## Introduction

In 1971, through a technicality and a misunderstanding in the bidding process, racing's APBA Gold Cup landed in Madison, Indiana, a river town that even today has only about 12,000 residents. The hometown boat, Miss Madison, was billed as the world's only community-owned unlimited hydroplane, a low-budget team racing a hull that first raced in 1960 against the well-funded corporate "Thunderboats" of the day. On the Ohio River in front of its own people, Miss Madison won. The upset became the stuff of local legend and, three decades later, a movie. It also says a good deal about a town that has spent two centuries punching above its weight.

## Gateway on the River

Madison was platted in 1810, and John Paul sold the first lots in 1811. Heavy river traffic made its early years busy, and the town served as an entry point into Indiana. Directly across the water lay Kentucky, a slave state, and that position made Madison an important stop on the Underground Railroad. The barbershop of George DeBaptiste became a nerve center for the local network helping people escape slavery. By 1850 Madison was the third-largest city in Indiana, after New Albany and Indianapolis, and one of the hundred largest in the country. Indiana's first railroad, the Madison & Indianapolis, was built from here between 1836 and 1847. Its fortunes faded as river traffic declined and newer railroads linking Louisville, Indianapolis and Cincinnati bypassed the town, and Madison's growth never regained its pre-Civil War pace.

## A Downtown That Stayed

Slow growth turned out to be a kind of preservation. In the late nineteenth century, Madison's owners often updated old buildings with cast-iron storefronts and ornamental sheet-metal cornices rather than tearing them down, and many earlier structures survived with little change. The result is a streetscape containing examples of every major American architectural style of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, from Federal to Art Moderne. In 2006, 133 blocks of downtown were designated a National Historic Landmark as the Madison Historic Landmark District. The Lanier Mansion and the Charles L. Shrewsbury House are among the buildings on the National Register. Hollywood noticed the look of the place. Director Josef von Sternberg filmed Madison in 1943 for The Town, a wartime documentary presenting it as an ideal American community, and in 1957 Vincente Minnelli used it as the fictional Parkman for Some Came Running, bringing Frank Sinatra, Dean Martin and Shirley MacLaine to town.

## Thunder on the Ohio

Madison has raced powerboats since at least 1911. An annual race began in 1929, became the Madison Regatta by the late 1940s according to Wikipedia (the regatta itself counted its 75th edition in 2025), and has featured unlimited hydroplanes in early July since 1954. Each Fourth of July weekend, somewhere between 70,000 and 100,000 people crowd the riverfront, several times the town's population. Miss Madison began unlimited racing in 1961 and traditionally finished near the bottom of the standings, winning, by one count, just six races through 2004 with a variety of hulls. The 1971 Gold Cup made up for a great deal. The victorious hull retired at the end of that year in second place in the national standings, and the upset was retold in the 2001 film Madison, shot in the town. Later versions of the boat did better still, winning the H1 Unlimited national championship in 2008. When the boat was badly damaged in a crash at the 2011 Madison race, local fundraisers helped pay for the repairs, in keeping with Miss Madison tradition.

## Storm and Fire

During the Super Outbreak of April 3–4, 1974, one of the largest tornado outbreaks on record, an F4 tornado formed near Henryville and tore through Jefferson County. It leveled much of the neighboring town of Hanover, where all but one of Hanover College's 33 buildings were damaged, although no one on campus was killed or seriously injured. The storm killed eleven people in all, seven of them in Madison, where about 300 homes were destroyed. Three and a half decades later, disaster came from an unexpected direction. On May 20, 2009, as workers repainted the dome of the Jefferson County Courthouse for Madison's approaching bicentennial, a contractor's propane torch is reported to have set it alight. Smoke rose hundreds of feet, flames broke from the clock tower, and the fire burned for hours before it was brought under control. No one was seriously hurt, but the ruined dome had to be lifted off in two pieces on May 28.

## Falls on the City's Edge

Madison sits on the north bank of the Ohio, facing Milton, Kentucky, across the Milton–Madison Bridge. Its Main Street is Indiana State Road 56, part of the Ohio River Scenic Byway. On the city's western edge, Clifty Falls State Park protects the canyon of Big Clifty Creek and its tributaries, with several waterfalls and high ground rising 400 feet above the river valley. The railroad that once made Madison important never entirely left, either. When Conrail later bought the Pennsylvania Railroad, which had absorbed the old Madison line, the deal left out the 26-mile stretch from North Vernon to Madison, so the Madison Port Authority bought it and formed the Madison Railroad in 1975. Madison is billed as the largest city on the Ohio between Louisville and Cincinnati, and more than 55,000 people are said to live within 15 miles of its historic downtown.

## Flight Context

Madison lies on the north bank of the Ohio River at approximately 38.750°N, 85.395°W, opposite Milton, Kentucky. From 2,000–3,000 ft AGL, follow the river: the Milton–Madison Bridge carrying U.S. 421, the compact historic downtown along the waterfront, and the canyon of Clifty Falls State Park to the west are all clear references. Madison Regional Airport (KIMS, formerly Madison Municipal) is about 6 km west of downtown; North Vernon Airport (KOVO) is about 38 km to the north-northwest, and Bowman Field in Louisville (KLOU) about 63 km to the south-southwest. River fog is common on cool mornings, and summer brings haze and thunderstorms; during the Madison Regatta on the July 4 weekend, expect crowds on the river and check NOTAMs before flying over.
