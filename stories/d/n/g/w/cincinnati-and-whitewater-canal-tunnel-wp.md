# Cincinnati and Whitewater Canal Tunnel

A brick-lined 1837 canal tunnel in Cleves, Ohio, where boats passed through a hill while their animals climbed over it - one of only a handful of canal tunnels ever built in the United States.

**Location:** 39.1550, -84.7494

**Categories:** 1837 establishments in Ohio, Tunnels completed in 1837, Canal tunnels in the United States, Canals in Ohio, National Register of Historic Places in Hamilton County, Ohio, Tunnels in Ohio, Water tunnels on the National Register of Historic Places, Transportation buildings and structures on the National Register of Historic Places in Ohio

## Introduction

On the Cincinnati and Whitewater Canal, the boats and the animals that pulled them parted ways at a hill. The boats went through it, in a brick-lined tunnel about a third of a mile long, while the beasts of burden climbed up and over the top. Part of that tunnel still exists in the village of Cleves, in the southwestern corner of Ohio near Cincinnati. Finished in 1837, it has outlasted both the canal it was built for and the railroad that later borrowed it, and it survives as one of only a handful of canal tunnels ever built in the United States.

## Cincinnati Wants In

The tunnel was born of a neighbor's ambition. In the 1830s, Indiana began building the Whitewater Canal, and Ohio businessmen urged the construction of an extension to Cincinnati. The result was the Cincinnati and Whitewater Canal, completed in 1843. Its tunnel, built near the village of North Bend and now within Cleves, was completed in 1837, a full six years before the canal opened for business, and for those years it stood ready for boats that had no finished waterway yet to bring them.

## Through the Hill

The builders lined the tunnel with brick and framed its entrances with ashlar stonework portals and wing walls. When it was completed, the tunnel ran 0.3 miles, with more than 20 feet between its ceiling and the bottom of the canal. One feature set it apart from many tunnels of its kind. Unusually, it was built with room for a towpath instead of requiring the use of leggers. Even so, the towpath was not how most boats made the passage. The typical canal boat still relied on human muscle to get through, while the animals that normally pulled it took the overland route, climbing the hill above.

## Floods, Then Rails

The canal did not last. Despite all the effort spent building the Whitewater Canal, it was abandoned in 1856 after numerous floods had destroyed much of its length. The tunnel, left without a purpose, sat idle. That stagnation ended in 1863, when a railroad company took over the bore and began using it as an ordinary rail tunnel. Trains ran through the old canal passage for more than two decades, giving a structure designed for slow-moving boats a second career in a faster age. Once the railroad was gone, the tunnel silted up, and US 50 later cut through its North Bend end, so today only the Cleves entrance and part of the bore remain, nearly filled with silt.

## A Marker at the North Portal

Since the canal's closure, the tunnel has largely slipped out of public memory, but it has not been completely forgotten. In 2000, the Ohio Historical Society and a group of private organizations placed a historical marker beside its northern portal, and in May 2001 the tunnel was listed on the National Register of Historic Places. Its importance comes down to rarity. Only a handful of canal tunnels were ever built in the United States, and this brick-lined passage in Cleves, with its towpath and its stone portals, is one of them.

## Flight Context
