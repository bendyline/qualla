# Lochry's Defeat

In August 1781 a hundred Pennsylvania militiamen chasing George Rogers Clark down the Ohio River stopped to cook breakfast near present-day Aurora, Indiana, and Joseph Brant's warriors killed or captured every one of them.

**Location:** 39.0258, -84.8861

**Categories:** American Revolutionary War, Battles of the American Revolutionary War in Indiana, Battles involving Native American people, Dearborn County, Indiana, Ohio River

## Introduction

"My dear General. I arrived at this Post this moment. I find that there is neither Boats, provisions or ammunition left." Colonel Archibald Lochry wrote those lines at Wheeling on August 8, 1781, having missed George Rogers Clark's departure by only a few hours. He and roughly a hundred Pennsylvania volunteers would spend the next two weeks racing down the Ohio River to catch up. They never did. On August 24, near present-day Aurora, Indiana, warriors led by the Mohawk leader Joseph Brant killed or captured every one of them without losing a man. The engagement became known as Lochry's Defeat, or the Lochry massacre.

## A War for the Ohio Country

On the western frontier, the American Revolution pitted colonists south and east of the Ohio River against Native nations and their British allies to the north. The Shawnee, Mingo, Delaware and Wyandot hoped to drive settlers out of Kentucky and reclaim hunting grounds they had lost in the Treaty of Fort Stanwix and Lord Dunmore's War. Clark, the leading American commander in the region, believed the way to end the fighting was to capture Detroit, the British headquarters that supplied the war parties.

For 1781, Virginia governor Thomas Jefferson planned for Clark to lead 2,000 men there. Recruits were scarce. Militiamen did not want to leave their families exposed for six months or more, and many Pennsylvanians resented serving under a Virginian. Lochry, commander of the Westmoreland County militia, was an exception. "The Enemy are almost constantly in our County Killing and Captivating the Inhabitants," he wrote that July, seeing no defense but offensive operations. Even so, he could enlist only about 100 volunteers.

## A Few Hours Behind

Clark had waited at Wheeling five days longer than planned, but his men kept deserting, and he believed they would be less inclined to run off once they were farther from home. So he left. Writing from Middle Island the next day, he told Lochry he was "heartily sorry" and urged him on: "you cannot possibly pass us without our knowledge."

Lochry's men built boats and pushed downriver. On August 14 he reported them "in great spirits and determined to go where ordered," and they had even rounded up 16 deserters from Clark's army. But supplies were thin. On August 16 he sent Captain Samuel Shannon and seven men ahead with a letter asking Clark to leave more provisions, because he was short of flour and did not want to lose time sending out hunters. The next day he sent two hunters out anyway. They never returned.

## The Warrior Who Let Clark Pass

Joseph Brant, known also as Thayendanegea, had become a skilled partisan commander during the war and was perhaps the only Native leader commissioned as a British captain. Transferred from the ruined New York frontier to Detroit, he headed south in mid-August with George Girty and about 90 Iroquois, Shawnee and Wyandot warriors to oppose Clark.

On the night of August 18, Clark's boats floated past the mouth of the Great Miami River while Brant's party lay hidden on the northern bank. With too few men to take on Clark's larger force, Brant stayed silent. Historian Randolph Downes later wrote that Clark's biographers "have never sufficiently emphasized how close he and his expedition came to utter destruction." Three days later, Brant captured Clark's officer Major Charles Cracraft and six men, along with part of Shannon's detachment. Their letters revealed that Lochry was not far behind. "Whilst the enemy are scadred we can easy manage them," Brant wrote to the British agent Alexander McKee.

## Breakfast on the Riverbank

Lochry knew he was in hostile territory, but after two days of nonstop travel his men and horses had to eat. On the morning of August 24 the flotilla landed. The Pennsylvanians cooked fresh buffalo meat and cut grass for the horses, apparently without posting proper security. From the woods, Brant's men opened fire. Some Americans fought until their ammunition ran out; others did not have their weapons ready. Those who ran for the boats found warriors in canoes already waiting to cut off any retreat.

Seeing his men hopelessly trapped, Lochry called for surrender. Some of the dead, most by some accounts, were killed after they had surrendered, and according to some accounts Brant prevented still more prisoners from being killed. The dead were scalped and left unburied.

## Nearly Every Home

The 64 prisoners were divided among the tribes. A few were later killed. Some were taken home and ritually adopted to replace fallen warriors, while most were sold to the British at Detroit and sent to a prison in Montreal.

In Westmoreland County, nearly every home was touched by the loss. "This Country has got a severe stroke by the loss of Colonel Lochry and about one hundred (tis said) of the best men of Westmoreland County," wrote General William Irvine from Fort Pitt that December, adding that many talked of retreating east of the mountains. For Clark, the blow was fatal to his campaign. Short of men, Kentucky's militia officers overruled him and chose to stay on the defensive. "My chain appears to have run out," he wrote on October 1. He never mounted his expedition against Detroit.

## Flight Context

The site lies at about 39.026 N, 84.886 W on the Indiana bank of the Ohio River near Aurora, where a historical marker overlooks Laughery Creek. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL, follow the broad bends of the Ohio River, with the Kentucky shore on the opposite bank and wooded hills along the valley. Cincinnati/Northern Kentucky International Airport (KCVG) is only about 10 nm to the east, so plan around its busy airspace. Cincinnati West Airport (FAA I67) near Harrison, Ohio, is about 15 nm to the north-northeast. River fog is common on calm mornings in the valley.
