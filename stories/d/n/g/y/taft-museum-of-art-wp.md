# Taft Museum of Art

In a downtown Cincinnati house built around 1820, Robert S. Duncanson's painted walls, a Rembrandt and the Taft family's gift "to the people of Cincinnati" share one remarkable roof.

**Location:** 39.1025, -84.5033

## Introduction

Robert S. Duncanson did not bring canvases to this commission. Between 1850 and 1852 he painted eight large-scale landscapes directly onto the plaster walls of a Cincinnati home, turning its rooms into windows onto imagined scenery. More than 170 years later those walls are still here, recognized as the most significant pre-Civil War domestic murals in the United States, and the house around them has become the Taft Museum of Art. It is a small place with an outsized cast: a string of prominent Cincinnati owners, an art patron's painted rooms, the portico where a future president accepted his nomination, and finally a gift to an entire city.

## Landscapes on Plaster

The murals were commissioned by Nicholas Longworth, an art patron and horticulturist who lived in the house in the mid-nineteenth century. His choice of painter was significant. Duncanson was among the first African American landscape painters to rise to international acclaim, and here he worked at architectural scale, spreading his scenes across the walls themselves rather than framing them. The result was so important that when the building was designated a National Historic Landmark in 1976, the honor was given in recognition of the Duncanson murals. Today the museum counts them, after the house itself, among its largest works of art. Standing in those rooms, you are not looking at paintings hung on a wall. You are standing inside them.

## A House Passed Hand to Hand

The building at 316 Pike Street is now more than 200 years old, and it is downtown Cincinnati's oldest wooden residence still in its original location. It was built around 1820 for Martin Baum, then became the residence of Nicholas Longworth. After Longworth, the house was bought by David Sinton, who lived there with his daughter Anna. Anna married Charles Phelps Taft, a half-brother of the future president William Howard Taft, and the couple made the house their home from 1873 until their deaths in 1929 and 1931. It was the Tafts who decided what the building would become, and they filled it with art.

## A Nomination on the Portico

In 1908 the house stepped briefly onto the national stage. William Howard Taft accepted his presidential nomination there, speaking from its portico, and the moment tied the family home to the country's public life. Inside, Charles and Anna were building something more private and more lasting. They were avid collectors, and in 1927 they bequeathed both their home and its contents to the city. Their deed of gift spelled out the intention plainly: "We desire to devote our collection of pictures, porcelains, and other works of art to the people of Cincinnati," they wrote, so that it would be readily available to all. Neither lived to see the result. The Taft Museum opened to the public on November 29, 1932.

## Rembrandt on Pike Street

The collection would do credit to a far larger building. European old masters line the historic galleries, with works by Rembrandt, Frans Hals, Thomas Gainsborough, J. M. W. Turner, Jean Auguste Dominique Ingres and Jean-Baptiste-Camille Corot. Rembrandt's Portrait of a Man Rising from His Chair dates to 1633, and Turner's Europa and the Bull to around 1840 to 1850. The highlights range further still, from James Abbott McNeill Whistler's At the Piano to Frank Duveneck's The Cobbler's Apprentice and Henry Farny's Song of the Talking Wire. One highlight brings the story home, Joaquin Sorolla's 1909 Portrait of Mr. Taft, President of the United States. Beyond the paintings are Chinese porcelains, Limoges enamels, watches, sculpture and furniture, and a thirteenth-century Virgin and Child from the Abbey of Saint-Denis, dated to around 1260 to 1280.

## Old House, New Rooms

Keeping a two-century-old house working as a museum takes care. In May 2004 the Taft reopened after an extensive renovation and expansion that added a museum shop, the Carl H. Lindner Family Café, and a lecture and performance space called Luther Hall. The historic house remains the heart of it all: visitors still move through the rooms where the Sintons and the Tafts lived, with Duncanson's landscapes on the walls and the collection shown in the very house the Tafts gave along with it. It is listed on the National Register of Historic Places and is a contributing property to the Lytle Park Historic District, a reminder that this gracious home belongs to a neighborhood as well as to the history of American art.

## Flight Context

The Taft Museum of Art stands at 39.1025 N, 84.5033 W at 316 Pike Street on the eastern edge of downtown Cincinnati, within the Lytle Park Historic District and about half a nautical mile east of the Carew Tower. The house is small, so fly at 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL and use the downtown high-rises and the Ohio River just to the south to orient before picking out the museum among the streets of the Lytle Park Historic District. Cincinnati Municipal Airport Lunken Field (KLUK) is about 3.9 nm east, and Cincinnati/Northern Kentucky International Airport (KCVG) is about 8.3 nm west-southwest across the river, so plan around busy terminal airspace. River-valley haze and morning fog can reduce visibility.
