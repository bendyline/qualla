# Newport, Kentucky

Across the Ohio from Cincinnati at the mouth of the Licking River, Newport has been an army post, Kentucky's third-largest city, a Prohibition-era haven for bootleggers, the "Sin City" of casinos, and finally a riverfront of aquarium tanks and a stubborn county-seat fight.

## Introduction

In 1997, Newport, Kentucky, unveiled plans for a Millennium Tower more than 1,000 feet tall on its riverfront, to be built entirely with private money and finished by 2003. The investors pulled out, and nothing was ever built. The site is a parking lot today, next to the World Peace Bell. Big ambitions and sudden reversals are nothing new here. This small city at the meeting of the Ohio and Licking rivers has reinvented itself more than once.

## Named for a Sea Captain

Newport was laid out in 1791 and settled by James Taylor Jr., on land his father had bought from George Muse, who had received it as a grant. Despite its riverside setting, the name has nothing to do with a port: it honors Christopher Newport, who commanded the ships that carried the first English settlers to Jamestown, Virginia, in 1607. At the start of the nineteenth century, the military post at Fort Washington in Cincinnati moved across the river to become the Newport Barracks. A bridge linked Newport to neighboring Covington in 1853, and in 1866 the John A. Roebling Suspension Bridge, the first to span the Ohio to Cincinnati, opened nearby. Waves of German immigrants arrived in the 1880s and 1890s. By 1900 the city had 28,301 people and ranked third in Kentucky after Louisville and Covington, though both Newport and Covington were widely regarded as satellites of Cincinnati.

## Strikes, Stills and Sin City

Newport's history has a rough edge. In 1859 a mob destroyed the press of an abolitionist newspaper. Prohibition, beginning in 1920, brought smugglers supplying the city's residents and businesses with illegal liquor, and speakeasies, bribery and corruption became the norm. In 1921, after the steel industry slumped at the end of World War I, more than 2,000 steelworkers, about one in five of the town's workers, went on strike, largely over recognition for their unskilled union locals. The company armed the mill with machine guns, and state militia companies, and later tanks, were stationed in town into the spring of 1922. The strike was broken in practice by late April 1922, though it did not officially end until 1928. Later, upscale casinos on Monmouth Street earned Newport the nickname "Sin City." In 1961 an anti-corruption "Committee of 500" was formed, and racketeering investigations eventually pushed the casinos out, though peep shows and strip clubs took their place.

## Beer, Floodwater and Monmouth Street

The city's German character showed in its breweries: the John Butcher Brewery, later Wiedemann, was in business by 1869. Life along two rivers had its costs. Ohio River floods struck in 1898 and 1913, but the city's worst natural disaster came in 1937, when floodwaters covered a great part of the city. A flood wall completed in 1948 still shapes Newport's landscape. Monmouth Street, meanwhile, was a busy commercial street with men's stores, restaurants and ice cream parlors, until parking grew scarce and a newer commercial district opened on the hill in south Newport. Hollywood has found Newport, too: the toothpick-counting scene in the 1988 film Rain Man was shot at Pompilio's, an Italian restaurant in Newport.

## The County Seat Quarrel

Newport has also argued for generations about where Campbell County's business should be done. It served as county seat from 1797 to 1823 and again from 1824 to 1840, but most of the county's people lived in and around Newport and disliked traveling south to Alexandria through largely undeveloped country. In 1883 Newport persuaded the state legislature to create a special Newport Court House District, allowing a second courthouse and county business in Newport despite state law requiring a central county seat. The fight never quite ended. In 2009 a court ruled that Alexandria alone was the county seat. On November 24, 2010, the Kentucky Court of Appeals disagreed and granted Newport equal status, and on August 25, 2011, the Supreme Court of Kentucky declined to review the decision.

## From Sin City to Family Friendly

In the 1980s and 1990s, Newport set out to trade its old reputation for "family friendly" tourism. In May 1999 the $40 million Newport Aquarium opened on the riverfront. The historic Posey Flats apartments were torn down to make way for Newport on the Levee, a dining and entertainment complex on Third Street that opened in 2001 and now includes the aquarium. The Taylor–Southgate Bridge, opened in 1995, and the Daniel Carter Beard Bridge, opened in 1976, tie the city to Cincinnati. With 14,150 residents at the 2020 census, Newport remains an urban center of Northern Kentucky, a place that has been an army post, a steel town, a vice capital and a family destination, all within sight of the city across the river.

## Flight Context

Newport lies at approximately 39.089°N, 84.490°W on the south bank of the Ohio River, at the mouth of the Licking River directly across from downtown Cincinnati, with Covington on the opposite bank of the Licking. From 2,000–2,500 ft AGL, the river bridges, the Newport on the Levee complex and the flood wall along the waterfront are clear references, but you will be inside or beneath the Cincinnati/Northern Kentucky Class B airspace, so coordinate with ATC. Cincinnati Municipal Airport Lunken Field (KLUK) is about 6 km to the east-northeast, and Cincinnati/Northern Kentucky International (KCVG) about 16 km to the west-southwest. River fog is common on cool mornings, and summer brings haze and thunderstorms.
