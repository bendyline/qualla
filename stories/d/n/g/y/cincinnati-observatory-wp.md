# Cincinnati Observatory

The oldest professional observatory in the United States, founded with citizens' subscriptions in 1843, dedicated in John Quincy Adams's last public speech, and still sharing its 1845 telescope with the public from atop Mount Lookout.

**Location:** 39.1386, -84.4228

## Introduction

On November 9, 1843, John Quincy Adams stood on a hill above Cincinnati to preside over the laying of an observatory's cornerstone. The former president was 76 years old, and the speech he gave that day would be his last in public. The hill was renamed in his honor - Mount Ida became Mount Adams - but the observatory itself did not stay there. Today the Cincinnati Observatory, the oldest professional observatory in the United States, sits atop Mount Lookout, where visitors can still put an eye to a telescope built in 1845.

## Three Hundred Stargazers

The observatory was the project of Ormsby M. Mitchel, who turned to the public to pay for it. He organized the Cincinnati Astronomical Society, with 300 members paying $25 each, and set out for Europe to find a telescope worthy of the effort. Nicholas Longworth donated four acres of land at the peak of Mount Ida, a hill overlooking downtown Cincinnati. The observatory's refractor, made by Merz und Mahler, was in its day the biggest telescope in the United States. Mitchel's own story took a different turn. Nicknamed "Old Stars," he later served as a Union general, and in 1862, shortly after taking command at Hilton Head, South Carolina, he died of yellow fever in Beaufort.

## The Last Speech of John Quincy Adams

The cornerstone ceremony in 1843 drew a former president. Judge Jacob Burnet introduced former President John Quincy Adams, who presided over the occasion and spoke to the crowd. At 76, Adams had given his last public speech. Cincinnati marked the moment by renaming Mount Ida as Mount Adams, a name that has stuck. The original observatory site did not remain devoted to astronomy; the Holy Cross Monastery and Chapel stands there today. But the hill's name is a lasting reminder that the observatory began with a president's farewell.

## Escaping the Smoke

In 1871 the observatory came under the control of the University of Cincinnati, and two years later it moved. The reason was the city itself: in 1873 the observatory relocated to Mount Lookout to escape Cincinnati's smoke and dirt, on ground given to the city by John Kilgour in 1872. The firm of Cincinnati architect Samuel Hannaford designed the 1873 building, and a smaller structure, the Mitchel Building, holds the original telescope brought from Mount Adams. Jermain G. Porter directed the observatory from 1884 until about 1930, nearly half a century of watching the skies from its new perch. Locals came to call it the Mt. Lookout Observatory, and the University of Cincinnati still owns it.

## Two Telescopes, Still in Use

The observatory now operates as a working nineteenth-century observatory. The 1845 Merz und Mahler 11-inch refractor in the Mitchel Building is one of the oldest working telescopes in the world, and today it serves public education programs. Its companion, a 1904 Alvan Clark & Sons 16-inch refractor in the Herget Building, is used for public programs. Regular viewings through both historic instruments, along with tours and an extensive outreach program, bring astronomy to people across the Ohio, Kentucky and Indiana region. The site was declared a National Historic Landmark in 1997, and it is a contributing property to the Observatory Historic District. The asteroid 1373 Cincinnati was named for the observatory, whose staff computed most of its orbit, so a piece of the sky now carries the city's name as well.

## Flight Context

The Cincinnati Observatory stands at 39.1386°N, 84.4228°W on Mount Lookout, on Cincinnati's east side, about 5 nm east-northeast of downtown's high-rises. Cincinnati Municipal Lunken Field (KLUK) is only about 2.2 nm to the south, making the hilltop easy to find; from roughly 1,500-2,000 feet AGL, look for the two observatory buildings atop Mount Lookout. Cincinnati/Northern Kentucky International (KCVG) is about 12.6 nm west-southwest. Busy airspace surrounds both airports, so check current charts and coordinate with ATC before low sightseeing. Clear, dry evenings are when the observatory itself is at its best.
