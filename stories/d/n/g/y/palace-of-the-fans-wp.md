# Palace of the Fans

For ten seasons the Cincinnati Reds played in a neoclassical ballpark of Corinthian columns, opera boxes and carriage stalls, a palace so grand its designers forgot to give the players dugouts.

**Location:** 39.1169, -84.5367

**Categories:** Cincinnati Reds stadiums, Defunct Major League Baseball venues, Baseball venues in Ohio, Demolished sports venues in Ohio, Sports venues in Cincinnati

## Introduction

Its designers remembered almost everything. There were 22 hand-carved Corinthian columns, opera-style private boxes and stalls where wealthy fans could park their carriages beneath the grandstand. There was even the word "CINCINNATI" spelled across the facade behind home plate. What they forgot were dugouts and clubhouses for the players. For ten seasons, from 1902 through 1911, the Cincinnati Reds played in this magnificent, impractical ballpark at the corner of Findlay Street and Western Avenue, and its name said everything about its ambitions: the Palace of the Fans.

## Born of a Fire

The Reds had called the Findlay and Western block home since 1884, when League Park stood there. On May 28, 1900, the southwest grandstand of League Park burned to the ground, and the team spent most of that May and June on the road while the diamond was moved back to its old spot in the southeast corner. After a season there, owner John Brush announced that the club would build a new grandstand. Work began ambitiously in the spring of 1901, and the phrase "Palace of the Fans" first appeared that March. The club hoped to open by summer, but delays pushed the debut to the spring of 1902, and the Reds spent all of 1901 in the southeast corner as well.

## Columns, Boxes and Carriages

No grandstand had looked like this before, or has since. Designed in a neoclassical style reminiscent of Chicago's 1893 World's Columbian Exposition, the grandstand blended Roman and Greek styling in a way never before used for a ballpark. The covered grandstand was fronted by 19 "fashion boxes," each able to hold 15 or more well-to-do fans, and it sat atop carriage stalls so the wealthy could drive straight to the game, an early precursor of today's luxury suites. It was built largely of steel and concrete, an early use of those materials for a ballpark. The facade's "CINCINNATI" was of little use to anyone already inside, but it made sure that every photograph of the stands announced where they had been taken.

## Rooter's Row

Not everyone sat in a fashion box. Beneath the grandstand, at field level, was standing room for 640 more spectators in a rowdy section known as "Rooter's Row." It was so close to the field that fans could join in on the players' conversations, and it was strategically placed by the bar. Opening Day, April 17, 1902, drew some 10,000 spectators, who crowded in to watch the Reds lose 6-1 to the Chicago Orphans, the team that soon became the Cubs. Not every fan bought into the grandeur. Some kept calling the place League Park, which is why historians also know it as League Park III, and newspapers used both names, along with the occasional "Fans' Palace." Contemporary owners and modern historians alike treat the 1902 structure as a new ballpark.

## Lights in the Dark

The Reds won little during their stay, but one evening at the Palace pointed toward the future. In June 1909, an exhibition game between Elks lodge teams from Cincinnati and Newport, Kentucky, was played here under temporary lights developed by George F. Cahill. It was not the first attempt at night baseball, but this experiment was deemed a success. Nothing came of it until the 1930s, when night games came to be seen as necessary for boosting attendance and keeping clubs financially more secure, and night baseball would eventually return to this same site in a historic way. The Palace hosted other sports that year too: on October 14, 1909, a Cincinnati soccer team met a British touring side there, and the Gyms played Christ Church at football.

## The Palace Fades

Once hailed as "the handsomest grounds in the country," the Palace soon lost its luster. The seating area was too small, and as the years passed city inspectors began noting cracked girders, decayed supports and unsafe floors. The last game came on October 12, 1911, against the Cubs, the same franchise that had spoiled opening day. Newspapers reported that workmen would start demolition as soon as the final out was made. Even the old 1884 stand in right field, which had survived 27 years and the fire, came down to make way for "a modern and sumptuous stand, the equal of anything in the country." By opening day of 1912, Redland Field stood on the site, renamed Crosley Field in 1934, and the Reds would play at Findlay and Western until June 24, 1970.

## Flight Context

The ballpark's block, bounded by Findlay Street, Western Avenue, York Street and McLean Avenue, lies at 39.1169°N, 84.5367°W in Cincinnati's West End, roughly 1.5 nm northwest of the downtown high-rises and close to the I-75 corridor. Nothing of the Palace remains, so use downtown and the Ohio River bridges as references from 2,500-3,000 feet MSL. Cincinnati/Northern Kentucky International (KCVG) lies about 7 nm to the southwest across the river in Kentucky, and Cincinnati Municipal Airport Lunken Field (KLUK) about 6 nm to the east; the city sits within busy controlled airspace, so coordinate with ATC before any sightseeing. Morning fog is possible in the Ohio River valley.
