# Tyler Davidson Fountain

Cast in Munich from Danish cannon bronze and dedicated in 1871, the Genius of Water has rained down on Cincinnati's Fountain Square for more than 150 years as the city's symbol.

**Location:** 39.1016, -84.5124

**Categories:** Landmarks in Cincinnati, Fountains in Ohio, Statues in Cincinnati, National Register of Historic Places in Cincinnati, Tourist attractions in Cincinnati, 1871 establishments in Ohio

## Introduction

Rain falls from her fingers. High above Fountain Square in downtown Cincinnati, a bronze woman of heroic size stretches out her arms, and water streams from hundreds of jets pierced through her outstretched fingers. She is the Genius of Water, the crowning figure of the Tyler Davidson Fountain, and since her dedication in 1871 she has become the city's symbol and one of its most-visited sights. Each winter the water is shut off, and each spring it flows again in time for the Cincinnati Reds' first home game, a seasonal ritual that marks the end of winter downtown.

## A Memorial Found in Munich

The fountain began as an act of grief. When Tyler Davidson died, his brother-in-law and business partner, the Cincinnati businessman Henry Probasco, traveled to Munich in search of a fitting memorial. There he found designs that had been waiting years for a patron. The artist August von Kreling had worked with Ferdinand von Miller of the Royal Bronze Foundry of Bavaria on a fountain meant to rival the great fountains of Europe, yet to glorify mankind rather than fanciful creatures and mythic deities. Miller had never found a sponsor, and the plans languished until Probasco arrived with a similar theme in mind. Probasco asked for four extra figures with animals that would work as drinking fountains, and Miller's sons, Ferdinand and Fritz, designed them. The original miniature model survives in the Cincinnati Art Museum.

## Water and Its Blessings

Everything on the 43-foot fountain speaks of water, a tribute to the river city's debt to the Ohio. The central figure alone stands 9 feet tall and weighs 2 tons. Below her, four groups plead for or receive water's gifts: a workman on a burning roof begs for help, a farmer in a drought-stricken field prays for rain, a young girl offers water to an old man on crutches, and a mother leads her reluctant, naked little boy to his bath. On the pedestal, low reliefs show four uses of water: steam, water-power, navigation and fishing, from ironworkers at a trip hammer to a steamboat pulling away from a crowded shore. Around the outside, bronze boys ride a dolphin, play with ducks, wrestle a snake and perch on a turtle, and passersby can still drink from them. The inscription on the green granite base reads "To the People of Cincinnati."

## Cannon Metal and a Crowd of Thousands

The fountain holds roughly 24 short tons of cannon bronze bought from the Danish government and some 85 short tons of granite. It was cast in sections at the foundry and shipped across the Atlantic for assembly. To give it a stage, Probasco asked the city to clear away a dilapidated market along Fifth Street between Vine and Walnut, and architect William Tinsley, who had also designed Probasco's house in Clifton, built an esplanade down the middle of the street so that anyone passing could see the fountain. Ferdinand von Miller came to Cincinnati for the dedication on October 6, 1871, when an estimated 10,000 to 20,000 people turned out, and he and von Kreling enjoyed a measure of celebrity in the city. The fountain originally faced east, toward Europe, where so many Cincinnatians had come from.

## Turning to Face the City

The Genius of Water has changed direction more than once. Around 1970, during the fountain's first renovation, the plaza was redesigned for better traffic flow and the fountain was turned to face west, because Fifth Street had become a one-way street heading east and drivers could now see its front. After further repairs in 1999, a larger revitalization of Fountain Square followed, and during the work the fountain went on display at the Cincinnati Art Museum. When it returned, it was placed in a much wider space near the north end of the square, away from traffic and facing south. The square reopened on October 14, 2006, with bands, food, beer and fireworks. Today the square hosts concerts and, in winter, a heated tent called Winterhaus, which took over the space of the ice rink that stood here for twenty years before it moved to nearby Elm Street Plaza in 2025. Viewers far from Ohio know the fountain best from the opening credits of WKRP in Cincinnati, filmed at its former location.

## Flight Context

The fountain stands in Fountain Square at Fifth and Vine Streets in the heart of downtown Cincinnati, near 39.1016°N, 84.5124°W. From 2,000-3,000 feet MSL the square is hard to pick out among the downtown towers, so use the Ohio River bridges and the riverfront stadiums as references and look a few blocks north into the business district. Cincinnati Municipal Airport Lunken Field (KLUK) lies about 4 nm to the east, and Cincinnati/Northern Kentucky International (KCVG) about 8 nm to the southwest across the river in Kentucky; downtown sits within busy controlled airspace, so coordinate with ATC before any sightseeing. Morning fog is possible in the Ohio River valley.
