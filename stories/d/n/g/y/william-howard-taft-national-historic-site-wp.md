# William Howard Taft National Historic Site

The modest Greek Revival house on Auburn Avenue where William Howard Taft was born in 1857 passed through judges, a colonel and apartment tenants before the family, and finally the National Park Service, brought it home.

**Location:** 39.1197, -84.5086

**Categories:** Houses completed in 1835, Historic house museums in Ohio, Houses on the National Register of Historic Places in Ohio, Museums in Cincinnati, National Historic Landmarks in Ohio, National Historic Sites in Ohio, Presidential homes in the United States, National Register of Historic Places in Cincinnati, Taft family, William Howard Taft

## Introduction

In 1937, a group of admirers formed the William Howard Taft Memorial Association to buy the late president's birthplace for $12,000, and they failed. The money never came together, in large part because the family stayed away. Robert Taft, the president's son, felt it would look too opportunistic to memorialize the house his father grew up in, and without the Tafts behind it the effort stalled. Decades would pass before the two-story Greek Revival house at 2038 Auburn Avenue, a mile north of downtown Cincinnati, was rescued. Today it is the William Howard Taft National Historic Site, honoring the man who became both the 27th president and the 10th chief justice of the United States.

## A Lawyer from Vermont

The story begins with Alphonso Taft, a Vermont native who came to Cincinnati in the late 1830s to open a law practice. On June 13, 1851, he bought the house, then numbered 60 Auburn Street, with 1.82 acres, for $10,100. A family named Hopkins had probably built it between 1844 and 1847. Mount Auburn was a favored address for upper-class Cincinnatians escaping the sweltering heat and humidity of downtown summers, yet the Taft house was relatively modest beside its neighbors, which mixed Second Empire, Italianate and Georgian Revival styles. Alphonso, an early supporter of the Republican Party in Cincinnati, shared the home with his family and his parents. Tragedy came quickly: his wife Fanny Phelps Taft died in June 1852, a year after the move.

## The Boy in the Rear Ell

In December 1853 Alphonso married again, choosing Louise Torrey, a schoolteacher from Massachusetts. Their first child died of whooping cough at fourteen months. Their second, William Howard Taft, was born in the house on September 15, 1857, presumably in the first-floor nursery in the rear ell. He grew up in a household of six living children, two from Fanny's marriage and four from Louise's, and he stayed until he left for Yale University in 1874. After that, his father's career pulled the family away from the house, starting when Alphonso joined the administration of Ulysses S. Grant. Over the years he served as the 31st secretary of war and the 34th attorney general, then as U.S. minister to Austria-Hungary from 1882 and to Russia from 1884 until 1885. In 1877 a fire damaged the second floor and roof.

## Strangers at the Door

By 1889 William had married and his brothers had moved out, and Alphonso and Louise leased the house and moved to California, whose climate was thought to help those in declining health. Alphonso died in San Diego in May 1891 and was buried in Cincinnati, and the tenants graciously let mourners gather in the old house for his funeral. Louise tried for ten years to sell it outright, finally succeeding in 1899, when Judge Albert C. Thompson bought it; by then she had returned to her hometown of Millbury, Massachusetts. Within five years the front veranda was gone, replaced by a one-story porch, a conservatory had been added and the stable and other outbuildings demolished. Colonel Ernest H. Ruffner bought the house in 1912. By the 1940s it had been divided into apartments, and one owner, Elbert R. Bellinger, even considered selling it to become a funeral home serving the city's Black community.

## Bringing It Home

Robert Taft's death in 1953 changed the picture. With the family's political fortunes faltering and Charles Phelps Taft II available to lead the push, the memorial association bought the house at its assessed price of $35,000, well below the $75,000 Bellinger had demanded. It was in poor condition, and by 1961 restoration was estimated at $92,500. The house was declared a National Historic Landmark in 1964, celebrated at a ceremony on September 15, William's 107th birthday. The association gained full title in 1968 and handed the property to the National Park Service in 1969 to secure its future upkeep, and the federal government took title on November 1, 1970. Restorers leaned on an unexpected guide: letters and diary entries Louise Taft had written while living there, which helped them return the rooms to their appearance in William's boyhood.

## Rooms Frozen in Childhood

The National Park Service site has two main buildings, and at its heart is the original home of Alphonso and Louise Taft, restored to the years when young William lived in it. On the first floor, five rooms have been restored: the room where he was born and four others representative of the period. The furniture is made up of period pieces that did not necessarily belong to the Tafts, but the family portraits are genuine, as are many of the books on display. Upstairs, the second floor turns from boyhood to legacy, with exhibits on the accomplishments of the man who went on to lead two branches of the federal government.

## Flight Context

The William Howard Taft National Historic Site sits at 39.1197 N, 84.5086 W on Auburn Avenue in Cincinnati's Mount Auburn neighborhood, about 1.2 nm north of the downtown towers. The Harriet Beecher Stowe House in Walnut Hills lies about 1.3 nm to the northeast, making the two historic homes an easy pairing on a sightseeing loop. The house itself is small, so fly at 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL and use the downtown skyline and the Ohio River beyond it as references. Cincinnati Municipal Airport Lunken Field (KLUK) is about 4.3 nm east-southeast, and Cincinnati/Northern Kentucky International Airport (KCVG) is about 8.6 nm west-southwest, so expect busy surrounding airspace. River-valley haze and morning fog are common.
