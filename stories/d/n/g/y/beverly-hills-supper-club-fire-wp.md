# Beverly Hills Supper Club fire

On May 28, 1977, fire swept through an overcrowded supper club in Southgate, Kentucky, killing 165 people and exposing how a glamorous venue had been built and run without basic protections.

## Introduction

At 9:06 on the evening of May 28, 1977, a busboy stepped into the packed Cabaret Room of the Beverly Hills Supper Club in Southgate, Kentucky, interrupted the show, and ordered everyone out. Somewhere between 900 and 1,300 people had gathered there to see the singer John Davidson, in a room meant to hold about 600. Fire had been burning elsewhere in the building for several minutes. There was very little time left, and for many in that room it was not enough. By the time the last victim died, nine months later, the fire had taken 165 lives and injured more than 200 people.

## A Glamorous Club, Built Piecemeal

The site had a colorful past. It operated as an illegal gambling house from 1926, and by the 1970s it had become an elegant supper club that drew top entertainers. Between 1970 and 1976 the building was expanded several times into a sprawling complex. The frame and ceiling were non-combustible, but inside were highly flammable carpeting and wood paneling over wooden floor joists. The club had been built piecemeal, with inadequate roof support, no common ceiling space and no firewalls to stop a fire from spreading and drawing oxygen from other areas. It had no sprinkler system and no audible automatic fire alarm. The local volunteer fire department, according to the Cincinnati Enquirer, knew of the deficiencies but, under the law of the time, had no authority to force corrections.

## The Night of the Fire

Later estimates put around 3,000 people in the club that Saturday night, roughly double the 1,500 the fire code allowed. At about 8:59 p.m., an employee smelled smoke from a fire that had been smoldering in the Zebra Room near the main entrance. When employees opened the door to check on the smoke, they let in enough oxygen for what had been a smoldering fire to flash over. Seven minutes later came the warning in the Cabaret Room, where about a thousand people now had to find their way out. Most of those who died were found near the Cabaret Room's exits, where the crowd pressed so tightly that no amount of strength could free most of them. At 11:30 p.m., fearing the roof would soon collapse, fire commanders ordered all firefighters out of the building.

## The Long Count

By the next day, 159 bodies had been recovered, most from the area around the Cabaret Room. On the afternoon of June 1, two more were found in the rubble of the Viennese Room. Then the toll crept upward as the injured died in hospital: a 162nd victim on June 11, a 163rd on June 23, a 164th on July 1, and a 165th on March 1, 1978. Behind each of those numbers was a person with a family waiting for news, and more than 200 others survived with injuries. For the families, the count stretched the tragedy far beyond a single night.

## Reckoning

The investigation catalogued a building that had failed on almost every count: severe overcrowding; fewer than 17 exits, many of them poorly marked or hard to reach, where Kentucky law would have required 28 for such a crowd; wiring described as an "electrician's nightmare"; no firewalls; poor construction; and missing alarms and sprinklers. Richard Whitt of the Louisville Courier-Journal won the 1978 Pulitzer Prize for Local General or Spot News Reporting for his coverage of the fire and of the lack of enforcement of state fire codes. Questions lingered for decades. A 2012 book alleged that the fire had been set deliberately by organized crime, but a review team appointed by Kentucky Governor Steve Beshear had already examined those claims and called them "a very tiny shred of evidence of arson and a huge mountain of conjecture, unsupported speculation, and personal opinion." In 2011, Kentucky Attorney General Jack Conway ordered 30 boxes of post-fire photographs returned as public records.

## A Memorial at Last

For years the site was marked mainly by a state historic marker. In 2020 a private developer reached a deal with the City of Southgate to build a residential development there. First responders, families of the victims and others sued that September to halt the project, and in November the two sides reached an agreement that allowed it to proceed. In May 2023, a permanent memorial was dedicated near the development's northern access road, a lasting place to remember the 165 people who died.
