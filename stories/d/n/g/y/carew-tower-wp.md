# Carew Tower

Raised at record speed as the Depression began, Cincinnati's 49-story Art Deco Carew Tower was the city's tallest building for eight decades and is slated to be turned into apartments.

**Location:** 39.1008, -84.5132

**Categories:** Art Deco skyscrapers, National Historic Landmarks in Ohio, Historic Hotels of America, Buildings in Cincinnati, Adaptive reuse

## Introduction

By the time the problem surfaced, the monogrammed supplies had already been bought. The grand hotel attached to Cincinnati's newest skyscraper opened in January 1931 as the St. Nicholas Plaza, having ordered more than $300,000 in supplies bearing its initials. Then, on February 3, a court ruled that the St. Nicholas name belonged to the rival Hotel Sinton. Officials considered more than 2,000 alternatives before choosing "Starrett's Netherland Plaza," abbreviated on signage as "St.'s Netherland Plaza," so the initials still fit. The Netherland name survives today beside the 49-story Art Deco tower that was Cincinnati's tallest building for more than eighty years: Carew Tower.

## A City Within a City

The block had long been Emery territory. Industrialist Thomas Emery established the Emery Hotel & Arcade nearby in 1877, and in 1925 his widow, Mary Emery, bought the nine-story Carew Building, an 1891 Romanesque structure named for Joseph T. Carew of the Mabley & Carew department store. When Thomas Emery's Sons bought the last holdout, the Andrews Building, in March 1929, the family controlled a contiguous plot on Fifth Street between Race and Vine.

Realtor Walter S. Schmidt conceived the project, and John J. Emery developed it as a mixed-use "city within a city," with offices, shops, a hotel meant to rival the Waldorf-Astoria and a parking garage. Skyscraper pioneer William A. Starrett took over the planning, his firm Starrett Brothers became general contractor, and Walter W. Ahlschlager served as principal architect, with Delano & Aldrich as associate architect. Clearing the 13 structures on the site took 51 working days, the largest demolition in Cincinnati history at the time.

## Steel Against the Crash

Work began shortly before the stock market crash of October 1929. Emery had sold his stocks weeks earlier to finance the building, softening the blow, and in November a Starrett executive announced that the project would actually grow, a sign of the firm's "belief in the marvelous future that lies ahead of Cincinnati." On January 8, 1930, 400 workers began pouring the base and kept at it for 30 hours straight.

Steel began rising in March, but on March 24 ironworkers walked off in a sympathy strike over Starrett Brothers' use of non-union workers at the Empire State Building in New York. Al Smith, the former New York governor leading the Empire State project, tried without success to end it, and the strike lasted 49 days. Even so, 15,000 tons of steel went up over 61 days, reportedly a world record for speed at the time. Two workers had died in construction-related incidents by July 6, and on July 9 Emery's wife raised an American flag to mark the topping out. The tower opened to tenants on October 1, 1930. At $33 million, the project was reported to be the largest realty deal in American history at the time.

## The Hotel in the Low Land

The hotel opened on January 28, 1931, with 800 rooms and an opening dinner for almost 1,000 guests. Bandleader Wayne King composed the "St. Nicholas Plaza March" for the occasion, a title that lasted barely a week. The replacement name had its own local logic: "Netherland" referred to the hotel's setting in the low-lying basin of the Ohio River.

The decades were not always kind. A 1942 fire caused at least $400,000 in damage to the hotel and its retailers, and after Hilton took over the lease in 1956, a 1960s modernization concealed much of the original Art Deco. When Hilton declined to renew in 1981, the hotel closed for a $28 million remodel that restored much of the original architecture and trimmed the room count to 624. It reopened in 1983, became the Omni Netherland Plaza the next year, and has been the Hilton Cincinnati Netherland Plaza since 2002.

## A Jazz Age Statement

The tower and hotel share a five-story base. At street level the Carew Tower presents black granite and large windows framed in reeded white brass, while cast-metal bas-reliefs of transportation methods decorate the arcade entrance beneath a sculpted eagle perched atop a globe. The tower rises 49 stories and 574 feet, or 623 feet to the top of its flagpole. On completion it was Ohio's second-tallest building, behind Cleveland's Terminal Tower, and it remained Cincinnati's tallest until Great American Tower surpassed it in 2011. Its 27-story garage was among the tallest buildings in the world dedicated to parking before it closed in 1979 and was demolished.

Architectural historian Edward W. Wolner tied the mixed-use design to the reform ideals of the city's Charter Committee, in which Emery was a high-ranking member. Designating the complex a National Historic Landmark in 1994, the National Park Service called it "the most complete statement of the 1920s' Jazz Age, an embodiment of speed, high style, and a mass-market machine age."

## Apartments in the Clouds

Later years brought high vacancy and financial trouble, and by early 2019 nearly half the tower's retail and office space stood empty. In August 2022, New York developer Victrix Investments bought the tower, without the hotel, for $18 million and announced plans to make it a primarily residential building. The 2025 plan calls for 375 market-rate apartments on the 4th through 49th floors, with retail on the first three, at a projected cost of $162 million. That May, the city council unanimously approved a 30-year tax abatement that officials said the project needed in order to proceed. If the schedule holds, the conversion will be finished by the end of 2029, and people will be living inside Cincinnati's great Jazz Age landmark.

## Flight Context

Carew Tower stands at about 39.101 N, 84.513 W at Fifth and Vine streets in downtown Cincinnati, in the low-lying basin of the Ohio River. From 2,500 to 3,500 feet AGL, look for the Art Deco tower among the downtown high-rises, close to the taller Great American Tower with its distinctive tiara. Cincinnati Municipal Airport Lunken Field (KLUK) is about 4 nm to the east, and Cincinnati/Northern Kentucky International Airport (KCVG) about 8 nm to the west-southwest, so plan any downtown sightseeing around the busy surrounding airspace. The river valley often holds haze or morning fog.
