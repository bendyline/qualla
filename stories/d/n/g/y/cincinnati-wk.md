# Cincinnati: Queen City Where German Heritage Built a Chili Empire

Ohio's third-largest city where German immigrants created the largest Oktoberfest in America, where Cincinnati-style chili comes five ways on spaghetti, where Over-the-Rhine preserves America's largest National Historic District, and where the Roebling Suspension Bridge previewed the Brooklyn Bridge.

## Introduction

Cincinnati sits on hills above the Ohio River, a city that feels nothing like the flat Midwest surrounding it. The hills came first - then the Germans. By 1900, German immigrants had made Cincinnati one of America's most densely populated cities outside New York, building the ornate Victorian neighborhood of Over-the-Rhine with beer gardens, music halls, and breweries that made this the nation's brewing capital. World War I and Prohibition destroyed that culture, but the architecture survived, and today Over-the-Rhine is America's largest National Historic District, its revival one of the great urban comeback stories. The Germans also left Cincinnati-style chili - not Texas chili but a Macedonian-Greek invention served over spaghetti with cheese, beans, and onions, the 'five-way' that locals eat obsessively and visitors find baffling. The John A. Roebling Suspension Bridge crosses the Ohio to Kentucky, its design a preview of Roebling's later masterpiece, the Brooklyn Bridge. The Cincinnati Reds were America's first professional baseball team. Procter & Gamble and Kroger were founded here. The Queen City, they call it - and from the hills overlooking the river, the crown still fits.

## Over-the-Rhine Renaissance

Over-the-Rhine was named by the German immigrants who settled north of the Miami and Erie Canal, joking that crossing the canal was like crossing the Rhine into Germany. By 1900, the neighborhood was one of the most densely populated in America - 50,000 people in a square mile, five-story Italianate buildings lining streets that felt like Munich or Hamburg. The breweries made Cincinnati the nation's beer capital; the music halls brought opera and symphony; Findlay Market, opened in 1852, fed the neighborhood. Prohibition and World War I's anti-German hysteria destroyed the culture. The neighborhood declined for decades, bottoming out in the 2001 riots. But the architecture survived, and beginning around 2010, developers began restoring the Italianate buildings. Today Over-the-Rhine is Cincinnati's trendiest neighborhood - craft breweries reviving the brewing heritage, restaurants filling the ornate buildings, Findlay Market still operating as Ohio's oldest public market. The comeback isn't complete, but the bones of German Cincinnati endure.

## Cincinnati Chili

Cincinnati-style chili is not Texas chili. It was invented by Macedonian immigrants in the 1920s - Tom and John Kiradjieff at Empress Chili, Nicholas Lambrinides at Skyline - who created a thin, spiced sauce flavored with cinnamon, allspice, and cocoa. The chili is served over spaghetti: a 'three-way' adds shredded cheddar cheese; a 'four-way' adds onions or beans; a 'five-way' includes both. Cheese coneys - hot dogs with chili and cheese - are equally popular. Skyline and Gold Star are the dominant chains, but dozens of independent parlors have loyal followings. Visitors are often confused or repelled; locals are obsessive. Cincinnati has more chili parlors per capita than any American city. The chili is nothing like what you'd find in San Antonio - it's a Cincinnati invention, as local as the flying pig sculptures that commemorate the city's nineteenth-century pork-packing industry.

## Roebling's River Crossing

The John A. Roebling Suspension Bridge opened in 1866, connecting Cincinnati to Covington, Kentucky, and it immediately became the longest suspension bridge in the world. John Roebling, the German-born engineer, used the Cincinnati bridge as a prototype for his later masterpiece, the Brooklyn Bridge. The design is unmistakable - the Gothic stone towers, the diagonal cable stays, the graceful curve of the main span. The bridge still carries traffic across the Ohio, though the 1,057-foot span seems modest now compared to modern structures. Walking across provides views of Cincinnati's skyline rising up the hills, the riverboats passing below, Kentucky on the other side. The Purple People Bridge, a pedestrian span nearby, connects the Cincinnati riverfront to Newport, Kentucky. Cincinnati and northern Kentucky function as one metropolitan area, the Ohio River no longer the barrier it once was - but Roebling's bridge remains the most elegant crossing.

## First in Baseball

The Cincinnati Red Stockings of 1869 were America's first openly professional baseball team - paid players, traveling schedules, a business model that would become the national pastime. They went 57-0 that first season, barnstorming across the country and proving that professional baseball could draw crowds and make money. The modern Cincinnati Reds, founded in 1881, play at Great American Ball Park on the riverfront, where the skyline rises behind center field and the Ohio River curves past right. The Reds won the first World Series played under lights, in 1940; they won the Big Red Machine championships of 1975 and 1976 with Pete Rose, Johnny Bench, Joe Morgan, and Tony Perez. Opening Day in Cincinnati is an unofficial holiday - the Reds always play at home, the parade winds through downtown, and the city celebrates its claim as the birthplace of professional baseball.

## Queen City Heights

Cincinnati/Northern Kentucky International Airport (CVG), actually in Kentucky, serves as a Delta hub with connections across North America. I-71 runs northeast to Columbus and Cleveland; I-75 runs north to Dayton and south to Lexington; I-74 runs west to Indianapolis. The city climbs hills on both sides of the Ohio, the hilly terrain creating neighborhoods with views and the famous 400 hillside staircases that connect them. Mount Adams, perched above downtown, offers some of the best views and most walkable streets. Eden Park contains the Cincinnati Art Museum and Krohn Conservatory. From altitude, Cincinnati appears in a bowl of hills around the Ohio River's bend - the Roebling Bridge visible crossing to Kentucky, Great American Ball Park on the riverfront, Over-the-Rhine's dense Victorian streetscape spreading north of downtown. What appears from the air as a hilly river city is the Queen City where German immigrants built a brewing empire, where Cincinnati chili defies Texas, and where professional baseball was born.
