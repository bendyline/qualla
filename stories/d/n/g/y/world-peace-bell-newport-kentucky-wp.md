# World Peace Bell (Newport, Kentucky)

Cast in France, carried by ship and barge to Newport, Kentucky, and swung at the stroke of midnight to open the year 2000, the roughly 66,000-pound World Peace Bell now waits in storage for a new home.

**Location:** 39.0920, -84.4956

**Categories:** 1998 establishments in Kentucky, Buildings and structures celebrating the 3rd millennium, Buildings and structures completed in 1998, Buildings and structures in Campbell County, Kentucky, Individual bells in the United States, Landmarks in Kentucky, NBBJ buildings, Newport, Kentucky, Peace monuments and memorials, Tourist attractions in Campbell County, Kentucky

## Introduction

On August 1, 1999, a barge came up the Ohio River to northern Kentucky carrying a bell that weighed some 66,000 pounds, about 30 metric tons by local accounts, though its French foundry lists it at about 33. It had crossed an ocean and climbed two great rivers to get there. Exactly twenty-five years later, on August 1, 2024, workers and equipment removed the same bell from its home on York Street in Newport and trucked it to a temporary location. In between, the World Peace Bell rang in the year 2000 and stood as a secular monument meant to belong to no single group, but to all of humanity.

## A Tower That Never Rose

The original vision was far grander. In 1997, planners intended to cast the bell in an on-site foundry near Newport and hang it in a Millennium Monument tower 1,400 feet tall, where it would anchor an 85-bell carillon as its largest voice, all ready for New Year's Eve 1999. At first it was called "The Millennium Bell." The plans were later scaled back to a smaller tower with an accompanying museum. The Verdin Company managed the project on the American side on behalf of the Millennium Monument Company, while the bell itself became a collaboration with France, an international civic effort befitting a monument dedicated to peace.

## Poured in Nantes

The bell was cast on December 11, 1998, in the year the Universal Declaration of Human Rights turned fifty. The work took place on the premises of a ship propeller foundry in Nantes, France, under the strict supervision of staff from the bellfounders Fonderie Paccard of Annecy. Veteran bellfounder Pierre Paccard led the effort with his sons, Philippe and Cyril, in close collaboration with Master Founder Miguel Lopez. The result was a traditional Western-style swinging bell 12 feet wide. Its surface carries an inscription invoking the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, along with engravings marking important events from the past thousand years. Its makers and local accounts bill it as the largest free-swinging bell in the world, though by one account it held that title only until 2006, and it is billed as the largest bell dedicated to peace anywhere, though it is not part of the Japanese peace bell program, which has installed more than twenty Eastern-style temple bells around the world.

## The Long Way Home

The bell was first rung in Nantes in a public ceremony on March 20, 1999. Then came a journey worthy of its size. A sea voyage of a month and a half carried it to New Orleans, where it joined that city's Fourth of July celebration. From there it traveled by barge up the Mississippi and Ohio rivers, stopping in 14 cities along the way. Its arrival in northern Kentucky on August 1 came on the same stretch of the Ohio, shared by Cincinnati, Covington and Newport, that hosted the Tall Stacks riverboat festival that October. Not everything about the monument crossed the Atlantic. Its cast-iron striker, made of a special iron that would not damage the bell's rim, was produced by Cast-Fab Technologies in Cincinnati, and most of the tower and other components came from companies near Newport.

## Twelve Strokes, Then a Long Pause

The bell's widely broadcast dedication took place on December 31, 1999, in the hours before its big moment. At midnight Eastern time, as January 1, 2000 began, the World Peace Bell swung for the first time in the United States, ceremonially opening a new century. It was struck twelve times, and its peal reportedly carried for about 25 miles. For years afterward it hung at the World Peace Bell Center at 425 York Street, where the striker also allowed it to be chimed without swinging its full weight. Then, in May 2024, Newport's city commissioners announced that the bell would leave the York Street site to make way for a new hotel and office development, and that August it was trucked to a city public works lot on East 11th Street to await repairs. It had not rung since spring 2023, when a tension coil fell from the wheel that controls its swing. Local officials have talked of a more visible permanent home after restoration, possibly on the riverfront, which would return the bell to the waterway that first delivered it to Kentucky, but as of 2025 no permanent site had been chosen.

## Flight Context

The coordinates, 39.0920 N, 84.4956 W, mark the World Peace Bell's former site at 425 York Street in Newport, Kentucky, just across the Ohio River from downtown Cincinnati. The bell was removed in August 2024 and, as of the latest information available, had not been reinstalled, so there is currently no bell to see at this spot. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL, the Newport riverfront is easy to find opposite the Cincinnati skyline, and it is where city leaders hope the bell will eventually return. Cincinnati Municipal Airport Lunken Field (KLUK) is about 3.6 nm east, and Cincinnati/Northern Kentucky International Airport (KCVG) is about 8.4 nm west-southwest, so expect busy airspace. River haze and morning fog are common.
