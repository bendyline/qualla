# Miami and Erie Canal

For decades a 274-mile staircase of water linked the Ohio River at Cincinnati to Lake Erie at Toledo, and though railroads and the 1913 flood killed it, the canal still names a neighborhood and hides beneath a Cincinnati boulevard.

**Location:** 39.1008, -84.4969

**Categories:** Canals in Ohio, Canals opened in 1845, Transportation buildings and structures in Cincinnati, Butler County, Ohio, Montgomery County, Ohio, Shelby County, Ohio

## Introduction

The German immigrants of Cincinnati had a joke about the canal they crossed every day. The Miami and Erie Canal ran along the southern edge of the neighborhood where so many of them lived, and they called it the "Rhine," after the great river of their homeland. "The Germans live all together across the Miami Canal, which is, therefore, here jocosely called the 'Rhine,'" the traveler Ferenc Pulszky wrote in 1853. By 1875 writer Daniel J. Kenny noted that Germans and Americans alike loved to call the district "Over the Rhine." The name outlived the water. The canal itself had a far bigger job: carrying Ohio out of its isolation.

## A State Walled In

When Ohio became a state in 1803, getting anything in or out was a struggle. Most roads were poor, the Appalachian Mountains stood between Ohio and the East Coast, and goods from the Atlantic seaboard and Europe were scarce and expensive. Entrepreneurs who shipped goods down the Ohio River to New Orleans by sailing ship and keelboat faced a long, costly journey, and even after steamboats arrived, returning upriver was hard. George Washington was among those who had backed the idea of a national canal system. In 1807, Senator Thomas Worthington of Ohio, later the state's governor, asked the Secretary of the Treasury for money for roads and canals, without much success. Ohio's legislature labored for two decades before the Act of February 4, 1825, finally authorized a state canal system, largely paid for by selling land near the planned routes. Two canals would link the Ohio River and Lake Erie, and through Lake Erie, the Erie Canal and the Hudson River, reach all the way to New York City.

## A Staircase of Water

Construction began in 1825 and finished in 1845, at a cost to the state of $8 million. When complete, the Miami and Erie Canal ran 274 miles from Cincinnati to Toledo. Because Ohio is not flat, locks were arranged like a staircase: 103 of them at the height of the canal's operation, along with 19 aqueducts and three guard locks. From the Ohio River, boats climbed 513 feet, and from Lake Erie 395 feet, to reach the Loramie Summit, a 19-mile level stretching from New Bremen to lock 1-S in Lockington, north of Piqua. Supplying all that water required manmade reservoirs such as Grand Lake St. Marys and Lake Loramie, plus feeder canals, and Indian Lake in Logan County was greatly enlarged to supply the Sidney feeder. Standards varied by region, but the channel was typically built about 4 feet deep and 40 feet wide, with a 10-foot towpath where mules, horses or oxen pulled boats along at four to five miles per hour.

## Rails, Flood and a Subway

Competition arrived soon after the canal was finished. Railroads began reaching the area in the 1850s, and commercial use gradually declined through the late nineteenth century. A branch to Lebanon, the Warren County Canal, opened in 1840 and was abandoned in less than 15 years. The end came in 1913, when a historic flood severely damaged the waterway and it was permanently abandoned for commercial use. Cincinnati then tried to give the old channel a second life. Starting in 1920, six million dollars was spent using the canal bed to build a downtown subway; the two-mile tunnels were finished by 1923, but the money ran out before the Cincinnati Subway could be completed, and the route was paved over as Central Parkway, which opened in 1928. Farther north, long stretches were filled in to build parts of I-75, U.S. Route 24 and State Route 25. In Toledo, the Anthony Wayne Trail, originally named Canal Boulevard, follows the old right-of-way.

## Where the Water Remains

Urban development erased most of the canal, but pieces survive for those who look. Original Lock 17 stands in Carillon Historical Park in Dayton, and an unrestored but complete Lock 15 sits just off Main Street in Tipp City. East of Vandalia, the massive west abutment of the Old Nine-mile Aqueduct over the Great Miami River still rises near the Taylorsville Dam, anchoring a fairly intact segment that runs at least five miles north. In St. Bernard's Ludlow Park, the drained canal bed is still visible, and Lockland, a little farther north, was the site of the first set of locks. Between Delphos and St. Marys the canal still holds water and can be paddled by canoe or kayak. Near Spencerville, the Deep Cut was designated a National Historic Landmark in 1964, and the Johnston Farm & Indian Agency in Piqua (the Piqua Historical Area in older sources) keeps a replica canal boat, the mule-drawn General Harrison, while some old right-of-way now carries bike trails.

## Flight Context

The listed point, 39.1008°N, 84.4969°W, is on the east side of downtown Cincinnati, a short distance north of the Ohio River. From 2,500-3,500 feet MSL, the old canal's line through the city is traced by Central Parkway, the boulevard built over the canal bed, with Over-the-Rhine on its northern side. North of the city, I-75 and the Great Miami River valley roughly follow the canal's route toward Dayton, Piqua and the reservoirs of Grand Lake St. Marys and Lake Loramie, which are easy to spot from altitude. Cincinnati Municipal Airport Lunken Field (KLUK) lies about 4 nm to the east, and Cincinnati/Northern Kentucky International (KCVG) about 8.5 nm to the west-southwest; the downtown area sits within busy controlled airspace, so coordinate with ATC. Ohio River valley fog can linger on autumn mornings.
