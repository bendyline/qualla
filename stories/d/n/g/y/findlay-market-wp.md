# Findlay Market

Ohio's oldest continuously operated public market, an iron-framed hall begun in 1852 in Cincinnati's Over-the-Rhine where dozens of merchants, weekend farmers and the Reds' Opening Day Parade all gather.

**Location:** 39.1153, -84.5192

## Introduction

The bell in Findlay Market's tower once hung at Pearl Street Market, Cincinnati's oldest public market. Moving it here was a way of passing the city's market tradition from one generation of buildings to the next, and no other public market in Ohio has kept going as long. Begun in 1852 and open since 1855 in the Over-the-Rhine neighborhood just north of downtown, it is Ohio's oldest continuously operated public market, a place where Cincinnatians have been doing their shopping for more than 170 years.

## An Iron Frame in Over-the-Rhine

The market was founded on land donated by the estate of General James Findlay and his wife, Jane Irwin Findlay. Its builders used a new technology for the time: an iron framework. Findlay Market was one of the earliest structures in the nation built that way, and it is one of the few such structures that remain. Construction began in 1852, but disputes with contractors and problems with the new construction methods delayed the opening until 1855. Credited to city civil engineer Alfred West Gilbert (the National Register record names Williams, Adams & Co.) and built in the Italianate style, the market fits naturally into Over-the-Rhine, a historic neighborhood known for its dense concentration of Italianate architecture. The building was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on June 5, 1972, and its bell tower, with the old Pearl Street bell inside, gives the hall its signature feature.

## Dozens of Counters

Findlay Market is open year-round, Tuesday through Sunday, and more than 50 full-time merchants, by the market's own count, sell meat, fish, poultry, produce, flowers, cheese, deli fare and ethnic foods. On Saturdays and Sundays, the scene spills outdoors with a farmers' market, other outdoor vendors, street performers and special events, while small, independently owned shops and stands ring the market house. What makes the place matter to the city, though, is the crowd. People come from all over Cincinnati, and the market routinely draws shoppers who are socially, economically, racially and ethnically diverse.

## Parades and Pop Culture

Every year the market lends its name to one of Cincinnati's annual civic rituals. The Findlay Market Opening Day Parade for the Cincinnati Reds is an annual city tradition, a celebration of baseball's return anchored in a 19th-century market hall. The market has turned up in popular culture too. Johnny Cash visits Findlay Market in a scene from the movie The Pride of Jesse Hallam, and the local band The Seedy Seeds mentions it in their song "Oh, Cincinnati." For a city that takes its traditions seriously, it is fitting that its oldest market should also give its name to the celebration of a new baseball season.

## A District Renewed

The market's recent history is one of reinvestment. In 2004 the City of Cincinnati completed a renovation of the market, reported at $16 million to $17 million. Two years later, in June 2006, Cincinnati City Council named The Corporation for Findlay Market its preferred developer for 39 city-owned properties near the market, and the Findlay Market district is now a center of economic activity in Over-the-Rhine. By 2010 the market was fully occupied, and it has continued to grow. International recognition followed in 2019, when Newsweek named Findlay Market one of the top ten public markets in the world - a long way from a donated plot of land and an experiment in iron.
