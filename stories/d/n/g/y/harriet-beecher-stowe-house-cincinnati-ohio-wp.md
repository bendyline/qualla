# Harriet Beecher Stowe House (Cincinnati, Ohio)

Years before Uncle Tom's Cabin, a young Harriet Beecher lived in this Walnut Hills house built for the president of Lane Seminary, a home that later sheltered African American boarders as the Edgemont Inn and Tavern.

**Location:** 39.1330, -84.4877

**Categories:** Museums in Cincinnati, National Register of Historic Places in Cincinnati, Houses in Cincinnati, Houses on the National Register of Historic Places in Ohio, Historic house museums in Ohio, Ohio History Connection, Houses on the Underground Railroad, Biographical museums in Ohio, Literary museums in the United States, Women's museums in the United States

## Introduction

"If we gain the West, all is safe; if we lose it, all is lost." Lyman Beecher wrote those words to his daughter Catharine in 1830, already dreaming of Cincinnati, a city he called "the London of the West." The Congregationalist minister got his chance when he accepted a post at Lane Theological Seminary in Walnut Hills, and the seminary gave his family a new house built for its president. Harriet and most of her brothers and sisters came to live here with their father. At the time she was a young woman with no published book to her name. In 1852, as Harriet Beecher Stowe, she would publish Uncle Tom's Cabin.

## Eight Days Over the Alleghenies

Getting here was an adventure in itself. The Beechers had not been living under one roof, so the branches of the family in Boston and Hartford converged on New York to set out together, stopping in eastern cities along the way to raise money for the seminary. Isabella, one of the travelers, later remembered the leg west from Philadelphia with obvious delight: "we chartered a big, old-fashioned stage, with four great horses, for Wheeling, Virginia, and spent a week or more on the way, crossing the Alleghenies, before ever a railroad was thought of, and enjoyed every minute of the way." Wheeling now lies in West Virginia, but the mountains have not moved. A trip that normally took 48 hours stretched to eight days, and the family passed the long hours in the swaying coach by singing hymns.

## A Boomtown on the River

The city waiting for them was growing at a dizzying pace. Cincinnati's population leapt from 10,000 in 1820 to 25,000 in 1830, and by 1850, swelled by German and Irish immigrants, it had become the sixth-largest city in the United States. It was also a place where the abolitionist movement was active, a charged setting for a family of preachers and teachers. Catharine, eleven years older than Harriet, established the Western Female Institute in town. And it was here, in Cincinnati, that Harriet began her writing career. In 1834 a short story of hers won a prize from the city's Western Monthly Magazine, and in 1843 she gathered such tales into her first book, The Mayflower: Sketches of Scenes and Character Among the Descendants of the Pilgrims, its title looking back to New England even as its author was making a new life in the West.

## What She Saw in Maysville

Something else she carried from these years would matter far more. In 1833, while living in Cincinnati, Harriet traveled to Maysville, Kentucky, and witnessed a slave auction, where human beings were sold as property. The distress she felt stayed with her, and it became one of several experiences that inspired Uncle Tom's Cabin years later, the novel that made her an influential voice against slavery. Life in the Gilbert Avenue house went on. Harriet lived here at various times from 1833 until 1836, when she married Calvin Ellis Stowe, a professor. That same year her first children, twins named Eliza and Harriet, were born in the house. Her brother Henry Ward Beecher lived here too, and he went on to become a popular Protestant minister and an early leader in the women's suffrage movement.

## Many Lives, One House

The building itself is a solid 5,000 square feet, completed in 1833 specifically to house the president of Lane Seminary and provided by the seminary to the Beechers. Eleven Beecher children lived to adulthood, and most of them shared these rooms with their father at one time or another, a household of future preachers, teachers and writers under a single roof. Yet the Beechers are only part of the building's story. For a time it served as the Edgemont Inn and Tavern, a boarding house for African Americans. That chapter is easy to overlook beside a famous family name, and it is one of the reasons the site today tells more than one story. The same walls that housed a future antislavery novelist later gave lodging to Black residents of Cincinnati.

## Open Doors on Gilbert Avenue

Today the house at 2950 Gilbert Avenue is listed on the National Register of Historic Places and owned by the Ohio History Connection, with the Friends of the Harriet Beecher Stowe House, Inc. operating it. A small park and garden adjoin it. Inside, the museum tells a wider story than its name suggests: Stowe and her family, Lane Seminary, the abolitionists, the Underground Railroad, and the building's years as the Edgemont Inn and Tavern. It also presents African American history more broadly. Visitors who come for the author of Uncle Tom's Cabin leave with a fuller picture of the city, and the people, that surrounded her in the years before she became famous.

## Flight Context

The Harriet Beecher Stowe House stands at 39.1330 N, 84.4877 W on Gilbert Avenue in Cincinnati's Walnut Hills neighborhood, a little over 2 nm northeast of downtown and close to Interstate 71. The house itself is small from the air, so fly at 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL and use the downtown towers and the Ohio River to the southwest to orient before picking out the house and its adjoining park. Cincinnati Municipal Airport Lunken Field (KLUK) is about 3.7 nm east-southeast, Cincinnati/Northern Kentucky International Airport (KCVG) is about 9.8 nm west-southwest, and Butler County Regional Airport-Hogan Field (KHAO) is about 14 nm north, so expect busy surrounding airspace. River-valley haze and morning fog are common.
