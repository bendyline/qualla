# TWA Flight 159

On a November evening in 1967, a Boeing 707 aborted its takeoff at Greater Cincinnati Airport after mistaking jet blast from a stranded Delta DC-9 for a collision, and the investigation that followed asked a deceptively simple question: what does "clear of the runway" mean?

**Location:** 39.0500, -84.6667

**Categories:** Aviation accidents and incidents in the United States in 1967, Trans World Airlines accidents and incidents, Airliner accidents and incidents in Kentucky, Accidents and incidents involving the Boeing 707, November 1967 in the United States, 1967 in Kentucky, Cincinnati/Northern Kentucky International Airport, Aviation accidents and incidents involving runway overruns

## Introduction

"Yeah, we're in the dirt though." That was the reply from the crew of a Delta Air Lines DC-9 when the Greater Cincinnati tower asked, on the evening of November 6, 1967, whether his jet was clear of runway 27L. The controller passed the word along to TWA Flight 159, a Boeing 707 waiting to take off: the Delta plane was clear. It was not quite true, and the gap between what was said and what was meant would send a four-engine airliner off the end of the runway and down an embankment. Everyone aboard got out, but one passenger later died of injuries, and the investigation that followed turned on the meaning of a single phrase.

## Stuck in the Mud

Flight 159 was a regular New York to Los Angeles service with an intermediate stop at Greater Cincinnati Airport, today's Cincinnati/Northern Kentucky International Airport, and the first leg had gone without incident. At 18:38 Eastern Standard Time the tower told the 707 to taxi into position and hold on runway 27L. Delta Flight 379 had just landed on that same runway. Its crew received permission to turn 180 degrees and go back to an intersection they had passed, but the DC-9 could not complete the turn and ran off the pavement. Most of the jet ended up stuck in the mud well away from the runway, yet its tail sat only about 7 feet from the edge. Its rearmost exterior light was 45 feet from the edge, which may have made the plane look farther away than it was. At 18:39, the TWA jet was cleared for takeoff.

## A Bang at 145 Knots

The first officer was flying. He was 26 years old with 1,629 hours of flying time, seated beside a 45-year-old captain with 18,753 hours. Neither pilot initially saw how close the Delta jet was. As the 707 gathered speed, the captain noticed that the DC-9 was off the runway by only "five, six, seven feet or something of that nature." Then, as they passed it, the crew heard a loud bang. The flight controls moved and the aircraft yawed. Convinced he had struck the DC-9, the first officer tried to abort the takeoff, and both pilots worked the thrust reversers, brakes and spoilers. The airliner reached a peak speed of 145 knots. On a 707, V1, the highest speed at which a takeoff can be safely abandoned, is 132 knots. There was no longer enough runway left to stop.

## Over the Hill

The 707 overran the end of runway 27L, went over the edge of a hill and was briefly airborne for 67 feet. When it struck the ground again, the main landing gear sheared off and the nosewheel was forced rearward. The jet slid down an embankment and came to rest 421 feet beyond the runway's end, its fuselage ruptured and the structure of one wing broken. Fire broke out around the right wing. All 29 passengers and 7 crew members made it out. Eleven people were injured, nine passengers and two crew members, and one of the passengers died four days later. The airliner, which had flown 26,319 hours since its first flight in 1959, was damaged beyond repair.

## The Meaning of "Clear"

The 707 had never touched the DC-9. Investigators from the National Transportation Safety Board found that Delta's engines were still idling, blowing hot exhaust across the runway. That jet blast caused a compressor stall in the 707's number four engine, producing the bang, and it pushed on the flight controls. Neither FAA regulations nor the air traffic control procedures manual defined "clear of the runway," and the pilots of both planes and the controller each worked from a slightly different idea of it. The board judged the first officer's decision to abort reasonable, since he believed his plane was damaged, but faulted the crew for not executing it rapidly, and it named the crew's inability to stop at that speed as the probable cause, with the Delta crew's unchecked report that it was clear of the runway as a contributing factor. Board member Francis H. McAdams dissented. In his view the abort was both reasonable and necessary, and the probable cause lay elsewhere: in the Delta crew's failure to tell the tower how close they were to the runway, and in the tower's failure to ask for precise information before clearing the 707 to go.

## Lessons Written into the Rules

The board's recommendations reached well beyond Cincinnati. It urged the FAA to establish and publicize standards for safe clearance from runway edges, for aircraft and ground vehicles alike, that took jet exhaust into account, and it called for a fresh look at training manuals and abort procedures. That last point mattered. TWA's own manuals warned that a high-speed abort was dangerous and should be attempted only for an actual engine failure before V1. On Flight 159 the captain never called out V1, and the first officer believed the jet was at or near that speed, not well beyond it, when he made his split-second choice. The legal aftermath played out in civil court. The family of the passenger who died received a $105,000 settlement from Delta Air Lines, and TWA, which also sued Delta over the loss of its 707, settled for $2,216,000.

## Flight Context

The accident site lies at Cincinnati/Northern Kentucky International Airport (KCVG), at approximately 39.05 N, 84.667 W, on the Kentucky side of the Ohio River southwest of Cincinnati. The coordinates mark the airport itself rather than a precise wreck location, and the 1967 runway layout, including the runway then designated 27L, should not be assumed to match today's. KCVG is a busy airline hub, so any overflight must be coordinated with air traffic control; the airfield is best appreciated on an approach or departure, or from a respectful distance at 3,000 feet AGL or higher. Cincinnati Municipal Airport Lunken Field (KLUK) is about 12 nm east-northeast. Watch for haze and fog in the river valley, especially in the mornings.
