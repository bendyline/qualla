# Cincinnati Museum Center

Cincinnati's 1933 Art Deco Union Terminal reborn as a museum complex, where dinosaurs, a walk-through limestone cave and a pipe organ hidden behind old ticket-window grills share space with memories of the station's wartime heyday.

## Introduction

Inside a train station built for the age of the passenger railroad, you can walk through a limestone cave. It stretches 500 feet over two levels, with an underground stream and a vertical shaft 40 feet high, and it was sculpted by workers with deep knowledge of real caves. Nearby stand the bones of a Torvosaurus, and behind the grills that once fronted the ticket windows, a pipe organ waits to speak. This is the Cincinnati Museum Center, a complex of museums, a theater and a library operating out of Cincinnati Union Terminal, the Art Deco landmark that has stood in the city's Queensgate neighborhood since 1933.

## A Station Finds a Second Life

The museum center began as a search for space. In the early 1980s, the Cincinnati Historical Society and the Cincinnati Museum of Natural History were both looking for larger homes. Each traced its origins to the early and mid-1800s, and the historical society wanted to create a museum. Union Terminal, the Art Deco station designed by architect Alfred T. Fellheimer and now a National Historic Landmark, offered room for both. The complex opened on November 10, 1990, and held its grand opening day on November 2, 1991, when the two museums unveiled their first permanent exhibits there. Today it is home to the Cincinnati History Museum, the Museum of Natural History & Science, the Robert D. Lindner Family Omnimax Theater, the Cincinnati History Library and Archives, the Duke Energy Children's Museum and the Nancy & David Wolf Holocaust and Humanity Center.

## Dinosaurs and a Borrowed Cave

The natural history museum's Dinosaur Hall holds skeletons from the genera Galeamopus, Daspletosaurus and Torvosaurus. The Torvosaurus, installed in 2018, is the most complete skeleton of its genus, at 55 percent complete, and the only Torvosaurus skeleton on public exhibit. Then there is the cave. It began life in 1967 as "The Cavern" at the museum's Gilbert Avenue location and was reinstalled at Union Terminal around 1994 as "The Cave." Its designers modeled it on Mammoth Cave in nearby Kentucky, the longest cave system in the world, and workers with extensive cave knowledge sculpted it from gunite. Visitors follow a stream underground and peer up into dome pits, the tallest reaching 40 feet, all without ever leaving the station.

## The Organ Behind the Ticket Windows

The Grand E.M. Skinner Concert Organ is really two instruments joined together, and both were built in the late 1920s, around the time construction of the station began in 1929. The main instrument, Skinner Opus 660, was first installed at Immaculate Conception Roman Catholic Church in the East Germantown neighborhood of Philadelphia. The second, a smaller organ known as Opus 726, came from Pinecroft, the Cincinnati residence of Powel Crosley Jr., and now serves as an antiphonal division. The museum center bought Opus 660 in 1987 and installed it during its 1990s renovations. Its pipes speak from hidden chambers behind grills on either side of the hallway and stairs, the same grills that once fronted the terminal's ticket windows, while the Crosley organ sounds from a chamber behind a window above the history museum entrance.

## Presidents Passing Through

Union Terminal has long drawn the powerful. Franklin D. Roosevelt visited in 1936. Harry S. Truman came through five times between 1948 and 1952, and in 1952 Dwight D. Eisenhower was greeted by the public, prominent Republicans and a band. The museum era brought its own parade of politics. Vice President Dan Quayle visited in 1991, and in 2002 President George W. Bush made his case there for war with Iraq. John Kerry spoke in 2004, Barack Obama held a discussion on health care in 2008, Mitt Romney held his first rally there as the Republican Party's official presidential candidate in 2012, and in 2016 Hillary Clinton appeared alongside Elizabeth Warren. Few buildings can claim a guest list that spans eighty years of American politics.

## Swing Time at the Terminal

In recent years the museum center has rolled back the clock on the second weekend in August for 1940s Day, with a classic car show, swing dancing, big band music and a vintage costume contest. The celebration began during the terminal's 75th anniversary in 2008, and it honors the decade when Union Terminal saw its peak passenger traffic, especially during World War II. Holiday Junction, an annual rail-themed event, is another fixture on the calendar. The archives keep the station's own story, too: 14 of the architects' drawings, the silver trowel used to lay the cornerstone in 1931, the ceremonial gold key presented to Mayor Russell Wilson at the terminal's 1933 dedication, source photographs for the Winold Reiss murals that decorate the building, and the papers of Save the Terminal Inc.
