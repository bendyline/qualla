# Spring Grove Cemetery

Founded by Cincinnati gardeners in 1844 and reshaped by landscape architect Adolph Strauch, Spring Grove is a 733-acre garden cemetery where champion trees shade a Chief Justice, a grocery king and a roll call of Civil War generals.

## Introduction

On December 1, 1844, Salmon P. Chase and a handful of others prepared the Articles of Incorporation for a cemetery that did not yet hold a single grave. Chase would go on to become Chief Justice of the United States, and in the end he was buried here too, among the springs and groves that gave the place its name. Spring Grove Cemetery and Arboretum now spreads across 733 acres of Cincinnati, making it one of the largest cemeteries in the United States. Walk its lanes and it reads less like a graveyard than a vast, deliberately composed park, where ponds and Gothic Revival stonework sit among record-setting trees.

## An Idea Borrowed from Paris

The cemetery began with gardeners. In 1844, members of the Cincinnati Horticultural Society formed a cemetery association, and they had clear models in mind: contemporary rural cemeteries, Père Lachaise in Paris and Mount Auburn in Cambridge, Massachusetts. For a young city on the Ohio, it was an ambitious comparison, pitting a local project against celebrated burial grounds on two continents. The land they chose practically named itself, its numerous springs and groves suggesting "Spring Grove." Howard Daniels designed the grounds, and the cemetery was formally chartered on January 21, 1845. The first burial followed on September 1, 1845, and a Cincinnati institution had begun its long life.

## The Gardener Who Lowered the Monuments

In 1855 the cemetery hired the man who would define it. Adolph Strauch, a renowned landscape architect, was brought in to beautify the grounds, and he came with firm convictions. He set limits on private enclosures and on the heights of monuments, opening the landscape up so that lakes, trees and shrubs, rather than fences and competing stones, would set its character. It meant telling grieving families how grandly they could remember their dead, a delicate business, yet the results won Strauch praise in the United States and abroad, including from Frederick Law Olmsted and the French landscape architect Edouard André. The garden cemetery visitors see today is still essentially his. Of the 733 acres, about 450 are landscaped and maintained, with 12 ponds, many fine tombstones and memorials, and scattered examples of Gothic Revival architecture. The Spring Grove Cemetery Chapel is listed on the National Register of Historic Places in its own right.

## Champions Among the Graves

Spring Grove is an arboretum as much as a cemetery, and some of its trees hold records. As of 2005, it was home to two National Champion trees, a Kentucky yellowwood and a two-wing silverbell, both natives of the American Southeast. Its roster of state champions ran to eighteen species, and it reads like a world tour in timber. There was a cedar of Lebanon, a conifer from the mountains of the Eastern Mediterranean, and a dawn redwood, whose single living species is native to Hubei in China, alongside pines, firs, oaks and elms. It seems fitting that the botanist Emma Lucy Braun rests among them.

## Justices, Grocers and Generals

Reading Spring Grove's headstones is like paging through Cincinnati's biography. Salmon P. Chase lies here, as does his daughter Kate Chase, a Washington, D.C. socialite during the Civil War. Bernard Kroger, founder of the Kroger supermarkets, is here, along with William Procter, co-founder of Procter and Gamble, and the yeast manufacturer Charles L. Fleischmann. The Civil War left a heavy mark: generals Jacob Ammen, Kenner Garrard, Andrew Hickenlooper and Godfrey Weitzel are buried here, as is Manning Force, a brigadier general who ended the war as a brevet major general and a Medal of Honor recipient. Politics is represented by Joseph Benson Foraker, governor of Ohio and U.S. senator, and by the political boss George B. Cox. Baseball fans can find Miller Huggins, the Hall of Fame manager of the New York Yankees in the Babe Ruth era, and Reds Hall of Fame third baseman Heinie Groh. And there is Frances Wright, pioneering feminist, abolitionist and freethinker, alongside the poet Sarah Morgan Bryan Piatt.

## Where Grief Meets the Rules

Deciding what belongs on these lawns has never been entirely simple. In October 2013, a day after a large SpongeBob SquarePants headstone was erected on the grave of a U.S. Army corporal, cemetery staff told her family it had to come down, and it was removed along with a duplicate placed for her still-living twin sister. The family believed a worker had given them permission; management said that worker had erred. In February 2014 the two sides reached an agreement, and the statues were reinstated with granite slabs largely hiding them from passersby, a compromise that let the memorials stay while keeping them largely out of view. Back on March 29, 2007, Spring Grove had been designated a National Historic Landmark, recognized for its age, its architecture and the people buried within it.

## Flight Context

Spring Grove Cemetery lies at 39.1646 N, 84.5227 W along Spring Grove Avenue, roughly 4 nm north of downtown Cincinnati. At 733 acres it stands out from 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL as a broad green tract dotted with a dozen ponds, sharply different from the surrounding streets and buildings. Cincinnati Municipal Airport Lunken Field (KLUK) is about 6 nm southeast, Cincinnati/Northern Kentucky International Airport (KCVG) is about 10 nm southwest, and Butler County Regional Airport-Hogan Field (KHAO) is about 12 nm north, so expect busy airspace around the city. The Ohio River valley is prone to haze and morning fog.
