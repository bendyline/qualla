# Cincinnati Music Hall

Raised in 1878 on ground that had held an asylum and a pauper's cemetery, Cincinnati's Gothic Revival Music Hall has hosted a presidential convention, Richard Strauss and five of the city's great performing companies, along with, some say, a few permanent guests.

**Location:** 39.1094, -84.5189

**Categories:** Concert halls in Ohio, National Historic Landmarks in Ohio, Music venues in Cincinnati, Reportedly haunted locations in Ohio, Gothic Revival architecture

## Introduction

Before the ground on Elm Street held symphonies, it held the city's poorest dead. After the cholera outbreak of 1832, the land across from what is now Washington Park in Over-the-Rhine became a pauper's cemetery, and it served that purpose until 1857, when the growing city crowded in around it. Two decades later Cincinnati built a cathedral of music on the same spot. Music Hall, completed in 1878, is now home to the Cincinnati Symphony Orchestra, the Cincinnati Pops, the Cincinnati Opera, the Cincinnati Ballet and the May Festival Chorus, and its unusual foundations have helped earn it a reputation as one of the most haunted places in America.

## Asylum, Cemetery, Park

The city bought the plot on the west side of Elm Street, just north of 12th, from Jesse Embree in 1818 for $3,200. In 1821 the Ohio legislature established the Commercial Hospital and Lunatic Asylum of Ohio, among the state's earliest asylums for people with mental illness, which rose in Cincinnati on four acres of land. Then came the cholera years and the burials of people too poor for a family plot. After complaints from neighbors pushed the city's "Pest House" outside the limits, the land became Elm Street Park in 1859 and was used for expositions. In 1870, when Cincinnati hosted the North American Saengerbund, German musicians and exposition backers agreed to share a single huge wooden hall, 250 feet long, 100 feet wide and 80 feet tall, called Exposition Hall or Saengerfest Hall depending on the occasion.

## Springer's Challenge

Reuben Springer, a wealthy Cincinnatian of German ancestry, decided the city needed something permanent. In May 1875 he wrote to John Shillito, owner of Shillito's department store, offering $125,000 on two conditions: that the site be free from taxation, and that the community raise another $125,000. When only $106,000 came in, Springer added $20,000 more. Musical and industrial interests clashed from the start, so he offered a further $50,000 if the public would raise $100,000, money to build exposition wings on either side of the hall. Architects Hannaford and Procter took the contract, construction began in 1876, and the work was rushed to finish in time for the 1878 May Festival. The hall cost $300,962.78, with the exposition wings adding $146,331.51. Springer's name lives on in the main auditorium.

## Horns, Flowers and Gears in Stone

Samuel Hannaford's design is considered one of the last and best examples of Victorian Gothic Revival, with a steeply pitched gable roof, corbelled brick, traceried windows and a great rose window on the facade. It is really three separate buildings, the central hall flanked by North and South Exposition Buildings, joined from the start by second-story passageways, with carriage passages for arriving in bad weather. Sandstone carvings announce each building's purpose: musical instruments, including French horns, on the center; flowers and birds on the south, for horticulture; scientific tools on the north, for machinery. Not every detail survived. During a 1969 to 1975 renovation the exterior was sandblasted, destroying most of the carved and painted brickwork. In December 1974 the building was recognized as a National Historic Landmark.

## Opening Night to Convention Floor

On May 14, 1878, an estimated 6,000 people heard Gluck's opera Alceste and Beethoven's "Eroica" symphony at the first performance. President Ulysses S. Grant spoke to an overflowing hall on December 12, 1879, and later visitors included presidents Benjamin Harrison, William McKinley, Theodore Roosevelt and Dwight D. Eisenhower. From June 22 to 24, 1880, the Democratic National Convention met here and nominated Winfield S. Hancock for president, and Western Union telegraph wires were installed for the convention's use, something not done for major performance halls before. Richard Strauss conducted here in 1904. For decades the building was Cincinnati's main convention center, and at various times it housed the Cincinnati Art Museum, the College of Music, the television station WCET and, for more than 30 years, a roller skating rink in one of the exposition halls.

## Chandeliers and Night Visitors

Springer Auditorium seats 2,289 for the symphony beneath a Czechoslovak chandelier, 21 feet across and about 1,500 pounds, with 96 candle-shaped bulbs, bought by the Corbett family and installed in the early 1970s. In 2014 the National Trust for Historic Preservation put Music Hall on its list of America's 11 most endangered historic places. A 16-month, $143 million renovation, funded roughly half by private donors and half by public sources, added roughly 30,000 square feet of usable space, and the hall reopened on October 6 and 7, 2017. The stories of ghosts persist. The late Cincinnati Pops conductor Erich Kunzel recalled working through the night and meeting people out in the house, upstairs: "If you think I'm crazy just come here sometime at three o'clock in the morning. They're very friendly." Not everyone agrees. A longtime employee of 25 years and a facilities engineer both say they never experienced anything unusual, and the engineer suggested some strange sounds could come from the hall's ability to project sound.

## Flight Context

Music Hall stands at 1241 Elm Street in Cincinnati's Over-the-Rhine neighborhood, at approximately 39.109°N, 84.519°W, facing Washington Park just north of downtown. From 2,000–2,500 ft AGL (within the Cincinnati/Northern Kentucky Class B airspace, so coordinate with ATC), look for the long brick Gothic facade with its central gable and towers beside the green rectangle of Washington Park, north of the downtown skyline and the Ohio River. Cincinnati Municipal Airport Lunken Field (KLUK) is about 9 km to the east, and Cincinnati/Northern Kentucky International (KCVG) about 15 km to the west-southwest across the river. Expect haze in summer and river-valley fog on cool mornings.
