# Crosley Field

Cincinnati's quirky old ballpark at Findlay and Western hosted the first major league night game, a sloping left-field terrace that tripped Babe Ruth, and a rowboat on the pitcher's mound.

**Location:** 39.1167, -84.5353

**Categories:** Cincinnati Reds stadiums, Defunct Major League Baseball venues, Jewel Box parks, Demolished buildings and structures in Cincinnati, Baseball history

## Introduction

In 1937 the Mill Creek flooded and put Crosley Field under 20 feet of water. Reds pitcher Lee Grissom and traveling secretary John McDonald saw an opportunity. They climbed into a rowboat, crossed the left field fence and rowed out to the area of the pitcher's mound, while a photographer recorded it. The picture has circulated ever since, a fitting image for a ballpark that rarely did anything the ordinary way. From 1912 to 1970, Crosley Field, originally Redland Field, stood at Findlay and Western in Cincinnati's west end, and it became the first major league park with lights for night games.

## The Old Boomerang

The Reds had played at Findlay and Western since 1884, first at League Park and then at the Palace of the Fans. Between the 1911 and 1912 seasons their seating was cleared away for a steel-and-concrete stadium built for $225,000 by Harry Hake Sr.'s architectural firm. Its odd angles behind home plate and down the lines formed a distinctive V, earning it the nickname "The Old Boomerang." Groundskeeper Matty Schwab, hired in 1894, laid the sod just in time for opening day on April 11, 1912, when the Reds rallied from a 5–0 deficit to beat the Chicago Cubs 10–6.

The park was always among the smallest in the majors, seating about 20,700 at first and barely more than 30,000 at its peak. On August 3, 1919, some 43,000 fans, by press accounts, squeezed in to watch the pennant-chasing Reds face the New York Giants. That fall Cincinnati upset the Chicago White Sox in the World Series, a win tainted a year later when it emerged that some of the heavily favored Sox had thrown the Series.

## A Button in the White House

When local businessman Powel Crosley Jr. bought the struggling Reds in 1934, team president Larry MacPhail insisted the park be renamed for him. Arguing that without lights the club would fold, MacPhail persuaded the other owners to allow night baseball. Crews raised 632 lamps on eight metal stanchions. On May 24, 1935, President Franklin D. Roosevelt pressed a button in the White House that lit Crosley Field, and 20,422 fans watched the Reds beat the Philadelphia Phillies 2–1 behind Paul Derringer. The Phillies' Lou Chiozza became the first player to bat under the lights in a major league night game.

At a packed night game that July, fans spilled onto the field, and during a lull in the eighth inning a local nightclub entertainer named Kitty Burke picked up a bat and dared Cardinals pitcher Paul "Daffy" Dean to pitch to her. He lobbed a soft toss, and she grounded weakly. The umpires never counted it as an official at-bat.

## Uphill to the Wall

Crosley's most famous quirk was the terrace, a fifteen-degree incline in front of the left field wall. When the park was built for 1912, the city closed a lane of York Street so the field could grow, and rather than build a very high wall, the club built a shorter one at street level and let a slope make up the difference in grade. The terrace ran across the whole outfield and served as a warning track, and it frustrated many outfielders, mostly visitors. Babe Ruth, playing his final season with the Boston Braves, fell on his face climbing it on May 28, 1935.

Frank Robinson loved it so much that, as an Orioles executive in the early 1990s, he lobbied unsuccessfully for a terrace at Camden Yards. Other traps included a scoreboard well over 50 feet tall in left-center that turned would-be homers into doubles and a clothier's sign across York Street that rewarded any player who hit it with a suit. Wally Post won 11; Willie Mays led visitors with seven.

## Sun Deck and Moon Deck

All the outfield seats sat in the right field stands, known as the Sun Deck by day and the Moon Deck at night. The late 1930s brought prosperity: Crosley hosted Cincinnati's first All-Star Game in 1938, added upper decks during the 1939 pennant season, and watched the Reds win the 1940 World Series over the Detroit Tigers in seven games. In 1961 Roger Maris, fresh from his record 61 home runs, knocked one into the Moon Deck during the World Series. On April 7, 1969, the Dodgers' Bill Singer earned the first official save in major league history here.

The park served more than baseball. The Negro leagues' Cincinnati Tigers played here in 1936 and 1937, and on August 21, 1966, The Beatles performed on their final tour after a rainout the day before.

## Home Plate by Helicopter

Set in a dense neighborhood ill-suited to the automobile, Crosley struggled with parking and crime in its final years. The Reds agreed to move to the new Riverfront Stadium, and on June 24, 1970, they played their last game at Findlay and Western. Trailing the San Francisco Giants 4–3 in the eighth, Johnny Bench tied it with a home run, and Lee May won it with another. Afterward, Mayor Eugene P. Ruehlmann pulled home plate from the ground and carried it by helicopter to Riverfront.

On April 19, 1972, Pete Rose Jr. pulled a lever that sent a wrecking ball into the side of the old park. Today buildings and a street cover the site, and home plate's old spot is painted in an alley. The old terrace survives as a sloping parking lot beside York Street. In suburban Blue Ash, a replica field opened in 1988 with seats from the original park. Its organizer recalled a woman who hugged the ticket booth and kissed it.

## Flight Context

The former Crosley Field site lies at about 39.117 N, 84.535 W in the Queensgate area just west of downtown Cincinnati, near Interstate 75. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL, there is no stadium left to see; look instead for the Queensgate district beside I-75, with the Carew Tower and the downtown high-rises to the southeast and the Ohio River beyond. Cincinnati Municipal Airport Lunken Field (KLUK) is about 5.5 nm to the east, and Cincinnati/Northern Kentucky International Airport (KCVG) about 7.5 nm to the southwest, so expect busy airspace. Haze and morning fog are common in the river valley.
