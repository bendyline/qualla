# American Sign Museum

A Cincinnati museum where more than 600 rescued signs, from gold-leaf glass lettering to a spinning neon windmill, line an indoor Main Street called Signville.

**Location:** 39.1435, -84.5399

**Categories:** Museums in Cincinnati, Mass media museums in the United States, Design museums in the United States, Signage, Industry museums in Ohio, Neon lighting, Museums established in 1999, 1999 establishments in Ohio

## Introduction

A twenty-foot fiberglass genie, rescued from a Los Angeles carpet company, stands at the entrance of the American Sign Museum in Cincinnati's Camp Washington neighborhood. Past him lies a world built almost entirely out of advertising: gold-leaf lettering on glass, a spinning neon windmill, a Frisch's Big Boy with a slingshot in his pocket, and a whole make-believe town lit up by the signs that once shouted from America's roadsides and storefronts. The museum preserves, archives and displays signs and the equipment used to design and make them, and it treats commercial lettering as something worth saving.

## A Family Trade in Letters

The museum grew out of a trade journal. Tod Swormstedt's family owns Signs of the Times, a publication for the signage industry that has appeared since 1906. His grandfather, H.C. Menefee, was its first editor and bought the magazine for himself in 1911. Swormstedt spent more than twenty years working at the journal before the idea of a sign museum took hold in 1999. He began building the collection that year, and the museum opened to the public in 2005. Its subject is the craft his family's magazine had covered for generations, along with the equipment used to design and manufacture signs.

## From Gold Leaf to Neon

The collection spans the late nineteenth century to the 1970s, and it moves from delicate handwork to roadside spectacle. There are samples of gold-leaf lettering on glass. There is a Sputnik-like plastic orb from a shopping center in Anaheim and a rotating neon windmill from a Denver donut shop. A 1963 McDonald's sign features the Speedee character, and other pieces come from Big Bear Stores and Howard Johnson's. The sign from Johnny's Big Red Grill, a restaurant popular with Cornell University students in Ithaca, came down in 2009 and, after years of restoration, went on display at the museum in July 2024; the collection also holds the sign from Kona Lanes. Each piece was built to catch a passing eye, and together they chart how American businesses called for attention.

## A New Home in Camp Washington

The collection outgrew its first home. Many of the museum's signs were simply too large to fit the original exhibit space, so in 2007 the museum began purchasing a 42,000-square-foot property in Camp Washington. The building sits within the Oesterlein Machine Company-Fashion Frocks, Inc. Complex, itself listed on the National Register of Historic Places, which means the museum's historic signs now live inside a historic industrial building. Five years later, the museum opened in its new home in June 2012, with room at last for the oversized pieces it had been collecting. A $5.5 million expansion completed in July 2024 added another 20,000 square feet.

## Walking Through Signville

Today the museum shows more than 600 signs, and many of them are arranged along a faux streetscape, a town called "Signville," where visitors wander among the signs as if strolling through a small town from decades past. The museum is not only about looking back. Neonworks of Cincinnati moved its business into the new location and runs a live exhibit that shows visitors how old neon signs are restored. Watching glass tubes brought back to life beside the finished pieces makes the point of the whole place: these were made by skilled hands, and some of those skills are still in use.
