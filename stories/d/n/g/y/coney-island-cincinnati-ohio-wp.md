# Coney Island (Cincinnati, Ohio)

An apple orchard on the Ohio River became Cincinnati's beloved Coney Island, a riverboat-era amusement park that survived floods, a move to Kings Island and a half-century comeback before closing after the 2023 season.

**Location:** 39.0543, -84.4200

**Categories:** Defunct amusement parks in Ohio, Amusement parks in Ohio, Water parks in Ohio, Tourist attractions in Cincinnati, 2023 disestablishments in Ohio

## Introduction

Before the roller coasters, there were apples. In 1867, a farmer named James Parker bought a 20-acre apple orchard on the shore of the Ohio River, upstream from downtown Cincinnati. When he rented the land out for a private picnic in 1870, he realized it could become a popular gathering place for local residents and named it Parker's Grove. Over the next century and a half that orchard became Coney Island, one of the city's most cherished summer traditions, a park that floods soaked, a corporation stripped of its rides, and Cincinnatians kept coming back to anyway.

## The Coney Island of the West

In 1886 the Ohio Grove Corporation, a company headed by two steamboat captains, bought Parker's Grove for $17,500 and renamed it "Ohio Grove, The Coney Island of the West," borrowing the glamour of the famous New York resort. A year later the "Ohio Grove" was dropped and the name became simply Coney Island. Because the park sat on the river near the busy port of Cincinnati, the riverboat became the most popular way to get there. The first Island Queen joined the park's story in 1896, and a second Island Queen in 1925. Early novelties had a homespun charm. The Flying American, a 24-foot Ferris wheel of wood and iron with six carriages, arrived in 1888, and its operator, Albert Heninchen, took it apart every off-season and ran it inside a swim club in the city.

## Coasters, Castles and a Very Large Pool

The mid-1920s brought a burst of building. Sunlite Pool opened in 1925, an outdoor freshwater pool covering some 80,200 square feet, once the largest recirculating pool in North America. The same year brought the Moonlite Gardens dance pavilion, and in 1926 the Wildcat and Twister wooden roller coasters opened alongside a Philadelphia Toboggan Company carousel with 48 hand-carved wooden horses. Funhouses such as Bluebeard's Castle and Devil's Kitchen, where visitors entered through Satan's mouth, followed in 1927. The Land of Oz children's section came in 1934, and in the mid-1940s the old Clipper coaster was rebuilt as the Shooting Star. Later, a miniature railway carried riders across Lake Como on a 462-foot trestle, pulled by engines named for Mad Anthony Wayne and George Rogers Clark.

## Open to Everyone

For all its gaiety, the park was not open to all of Cincinnati. In 1952, Marian Spencer's young sons heard a radio advertisement for a celebrity visit to Coney Island and asked to go, but the park turned the family away because they were African American. Spencer filed suit with the NAACP and won, and the case desegregated the park. She went on to become the first female president of Cincinnati's NAACP branch in 1981 and, in 1983, the first African American woman elected to Cincinnati City Council, later serving as vice mayor. Her fight is a reminder that the summer pleasures in so many family memories were long denied to Black residents of the same city. The river posed a different kind of threat. The park's proximity to the water made it prone to frequent flooding; a large flood delayed the opening of the Devil's Dips coaster until June 22, 1913.

## Moving to Higher Ground

Those floods finally drove a dramatic decision. In 1968, park management began talks with Taft Broadcasting about building a new park on higher ground. Taft bought Coney Island outright in 1969 and started construction the next year in Deerfield Township, Warren County, 25 miles north of Cincinnati along Interstate 71. On September 6, 1971, Coney Island closed its amusements, and most of its rides, including the carousel, went north to the new Kings Island, which opened in 1972. Taft meant to sell the riverside land, but once the company decided to open Kings Dominion in Virginia and acquired Carowinds, redevelopment slipped down the list. Less than two years after closing, Coney Island reopened, a shadow of its former self but anchored by Sunlite Pool. In 1984 the park donated 15 acres, where the Wildcat and Shooting Star once ran, for Riverbend Music Center, summer home of the Cincinnati Symphony and Pops orchestras.

## The Last Summer

In 1991, Cincinnati businessman Ronald Walker bought the park and brought back the feel of a traditional amusement park, with a Tilt-A-Whirl, bumper cars, carnival games and musical shows. Sunlite Water Adventure kept adding attractions, from the Cyclone enclosed body slide in 2006 to a diving pool called Cannonball Cove in 2019 and a floating obstacle course in 2021. Then, on December 14, 2023, came the announcement that the park would be sold to Music and Event Management, Inc., a subsidiary of the Cincinnati Symphony Orchestra, which planned to build a new music venue on the site, described as a $118 million entertainment campus. A grassroots effort to save Sunlite Pool failed, and demolition went ahead in early 2024.

## Flight Context

The site lies at 39.0543°N, 84.4200°W on the north bank of the Ohio River, about 5 nm east-southeast of downtown Cincinnati, with Riverbend Music Center's amphitheater right beside it. Cincinnati Municipal Airport Lunken Field (KLUK) is only about 3 nm to the north, so this stretch of river is part of Lunken's traffic picture; contact the tower before maneuvering nearby. Cincinnati/Northern Kentucky International (KCVG) lies about 12 nm to the west, and its busy controlled airspace blankets the area. From 1,500-2,500 feet MSL (subject to ATC), follow the Ohio River upstream from the downtown bridges and look for the amphitheater on the Ohio shore. Kings Island, where many of Coney's rides went, is about 25 miles north along I-71. River fog is possible on cool mornings.
