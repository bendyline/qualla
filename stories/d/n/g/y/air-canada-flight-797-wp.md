# Air Canada Flight 797

In June 1983 a hidden fire aboard an Air Canada DC-9 forced an emergency landing at what is now Cincinnati/Northern Kentucky International Airport, where a flash fire claimed 23 lives and reshaped airliner safety rules worldwide.

**Location:** 39.0489, -84.6678

## Introduction

The first sign was a popping sound. At 6:51 on the evening of June 2, 1983, circuit breakers for the rear lavatory tripped aboard Air Canada Flight 797, a McDonnell Douglas DC-9 flying from Dallas/Fort Worth toward Toronto and Montreal. Nine minutes later a passenger reported an odor, and a flight attendant found smoke in the lavatory. Within half an hour the jet was on the ground at Greater Cincinnati International Airport, now Cincinnati/Northern Kentucky International, its pilots having brought it down through thickening smoke with most of their instruments gone. For 23 of the 46 people aboard, the landing came too late.

## Fire Behind the Panels

Flight 797 was an international service from Dallas/Fort Worth International Airport to Montréal-Dorval, with a stop in Toronto. The aircraft was a DC-9-32, originally registered C-FTLU, built on 22 March 1968 and delivered to Air Canada that April. It had logged 36,825 airframe hours. The fire that started around its rear lavatory did not stay where anyone could see it. It spread between the outer skin and the inner decor panels, filling the cabin with toxic smoke, and it burned through crucial electrical cables. At 7:02 the cockpit was told of a "fire in the washroom." At 7:06 the master caution light came on as electrical power failed, and at 7:08 the crew declared an emergency - "mayday, mayday, mayday" - and began an emergency descent, with most of their cockpit instruments disabled.

## Ninety Seconds on the Ground

In the cockpit were Captain Donald Cameron, 51, a pilot with 13,000 flight hours, and First Officer Claude Ouimet, 34, with 5,650. They diverted to Cincinnati/Northern Kentucky International and landed at 7:20. The doors were opened, and the evacuation began through the front doors and the overwing exits. The three flight attendants and 18 passengers got out that way. Then came the moment that decided everything else. About ninety seconds after the landing, as the doors stood open, the heat of the fire met a rush of fresh oxygen and produced a backdraft. The plane's interior was engulfed in flames almost instantly.

## Twenty-Three Lives

Twenty-three passengers, half of the people on board, were unable to get out. They died from smoke inhalation and burns. Among them was the Canadian folk singer Stan Rogers, who was 33. The 23 survivors were all five crew members and 18 passengers. The U.S. National Transportation Safety Board investigated, but it was ultimately unable to determine where the fire had begun. What the accident made painfully plain was how quickly a hidden fire had turned a controlled landing into a catastrophe, and how much the outcome had depended on the seconds available to get people out.

## What Every Flight Carries Now

Flight 797 became a watershed for aviation regulation around the world. In the aftermath, new requirements made smoke detectors in lavatories standard across the industry, along with strip lights marking the path to exit doors and increased firefighting training and equipment for cabin crews. Evacuation rules were updated as well, and passengers seated at overwing exits are now instructed to help in an emergency. The next time a flight attendant asks whether you are willing to assist at an exit row, or you notice the glowing strip along the aisle floor, you are looking at part of the legacy of that June evening in northern Kentucky.

## Flight Context

The coordinates, 39.0489°N, 84.6678°W, mark Cincinnati/Northern Kentucky International Airport (KCVG) in Boone County, Kentucky, where Flight 797 made its emergency landing on the evening of June 2, 1983. KCVG is a major airline airport, so it is best experienced as a destination or from an airliner window rather than on a low sightseeing pass; check current charts and coordinate with ATC before flying in the area. Cincinnati Municipal Lunken Field (KLUK) lies about 12 nm east-northeast.
