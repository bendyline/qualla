# Cincinnati Art Museum

The first purpose-built art museum west of the Alleghenies, Cincinnati's Eden Park landmark holds more than 73,000 works spanning 6,000 years, and it has been free to enter since 2003.

**Location:** 39.1139, -84.4969

## Introduction

In 2022, the Cincinnati Art Museum's chief conservator, Serena Urry, took Paul Cézanne's 1865 Still Life with Bread and Eggs down from an exhibition to check whether it needed any care. She noticed unusual patterns in the cracking of the paint. On a hunch, she had the canvas X-rayed, and beneath the still life a hidden portrait emerged. A museum founded in 1881 had found something new in a painting it thought it knew, and the discovery fits a place that has spent nearly a century and a half reinventing itself in Eden Park.

## West of the Alleghenies

In the late nineteenth century, public art museums were still a new idea, especially as far west as Cincinnati. After the success of the 1876 Centennial Exhibition in Philadelphia, the Women's Art Museum Association formed in Cincinnati to bring such an institution to the region for the benefit of all its citizens. By 1881 the Cincinnati Museum Association was incorporated, and the young collection moved temporarily into the south wing of Music Hall in Over-the-Rhine. The founders debated three sites: Burnet Woods, Eden Park or downtown on Washington Park. Charles West, the early museum's major donor, cast his votes for Eden Park and settled the question. On May 17, 1886, a Romanesque-revival building by Cincinnati architect James W. McLaughlin was dedicated with elaborate ceremonies, the first purpose-built art museum west of the Alleghenies and one of the oldest in the country.

## A Building That Would Not Sit Still

Prominent Cincinnatians, among them Melville E. Ingalls, gave so generously that the collection soon ran to tens of thousands of objects and demanded more room. Daniel Burnham designed the Schmidlapp Wing, which opened in 1907. In the 1930s the Emery, Hanna and French wings enclosed the courtyard and gave the museum its rectangular shape; the Emery Wing honors philanthropists Thomas J. and Mary Emery. Renovations in the late 1940s and early 1950s split the Great Hall into two floors and the present main entrance was created, and the Adams-Emery Wing followed in 1965. Then, in 1993, a $13 million project restored the grandeur of the interior architecture, uncovering long-hidden architectural details and renovating the Great Hall. Today visitors can arrive on foot by Art Climb, a staircase of multiple flights rising from the sidewalk near Eden Park Drive and Gilbert Avenue to the entrance, with room for outdoor artworks along the way.

## Six Thousand Years Under One Roof

The collection now numbers more than 73,000 works covering 6,000 years of human history, the largest in Ohio and one of the most comprehensive in the Midwest. European masters anchor the collection: Sandro Botticelli's Judith with Head of Holofernes, Peter Paul Rubens's Samson and Delilah, Hans Memling's Saint Christopher, Andrea Mantegna's A Sibyl and a Prophet, and a Frans Hals family portrait. Later centuries bring Corot, Renoir, Pissarro, Monet's Rocks at Belle Isle, Van Gogh and Picasso, alongside Thomas Gainsborough's Portrait of Ann Ford and Amedeo Modigliani's portrait of Max Jacob. The Decorative Arts and Design collection adds more than 7,000 works, including pieces by Paul de Lamerie, Jean-Pierre Latz, Kitaro Shirayamadani and the contemporary artist Karen LaMonte.

## A City Hangs Its Own Story

In 2003 the museum turned its attention homeward. The Cincinnati Wing, fifteen galleries and 400 objects across 18,000 square feet, became the first permanent display of a city's art history in the nation, tracing art made for Cincinnati or by its artists since 1788. Two of the largest pieces in the whole collection stand here: angels created by Odoardo Fantacchiotti in the late 1840s for the main altar of St. Peter in Chains Cathedral, among the first European sculptures to reach the city. The wing also holds the work of Frank Duveneck and Robert Scott Duncanson, Rookwood Pottery, Mitchell & Rammelsberg furniture and a tall case clock by Luman Watson. That same year, on May 17, 2003, exactly 117 years after the building's dedication, the museum eliminated its general admission fee for good, made possible by the Lois and Richard Rosenthal Foundation.

## Looking Inward

Money forced a rethink. The endowment slipped from about $80 million in 2008 to about $70 million by 2011, and spending as much as $2.5 million a year on special exhibitions made little sense when the museum's own holdings, from circus posters to Dutch contemporary design, sat largely unexploited. In 2010 the museum mounted See America, nine small shows exploring different regions of the country entirely through its own holdings. By 2011, attendance had risen 30 percent since the museum began emphasizing its permanent collection, and the portrait found hiding beneath a Cézanne is proof of how much a collection can still reveal. Since 2021 the museum has also belonged to the Monuments Men and Women Museum Network, launched by the Monuments Men Foundation for the Preservation of Art.

## Flight Context

The museum stands at 39.1139°N, 84.4969°W in Eden Park, about 1 nm northeast of downtown Cincinnati and a short distance north of the Ohio River. From 2,500-3,000 feet MSL, use the downtown skyline and the river bridges as references, then look just northeast to the green expanse of the park. Cincinnati Municipal Airport Lunken Field (KLUK) is only about 4 nm to the east-southeast, so the museum sits close to Lunken's traffic and controlled airspace; Cincinnati/Northern Kentucky International (KCVG) lies about 9 nm west-southwest across the river in Kentucky, and its busy controlled airspace covers the city, so coordinate with ATC before sightseeing.
