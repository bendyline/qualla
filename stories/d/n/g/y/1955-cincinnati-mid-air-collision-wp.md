# 1955 Cincinnati mid-air collision

On a cloudy January morning in 1955, a departing TWA airliner and a private DC-3 that had entered the Greater Cincinnati Airport's control zone without clearance collided over northern Kentucky, leaving no survivors.

## Introduction

At about nine o'clock on the morning of January 12, 1955, Trans World Airlines Flight 694 lifted off Runway 22 at Greater Cincinnati Airport in northern Kentucky - the field now known as Cincinnati/Northern Kentucky International. As the Martin 2-0-2 climbed in a right turn, it passed through a cloud base between 700 and 900 feet. Also in that sky was a privately owned Douglas DC-3, flying south under visual flight rules, that had entered the airport's control space without proper clearance. Moments later the two aircraft met. None of the people aboard either plane survived.

## Two Flights, One Morning

Flight 694 was a regularly scheduled service bound for Dayton, Ohio, on its way to Cleveland. Captain J. W. Quinn and co-pilot Robert K. Childress were at the controls, air hostess Patricia Ann Stermer looked after the cabin, and ten passengers were aboard. The DC-3 had a very different errand. Arthur "Slim" Werkhaven of Sturgis, Michigan, and his co-pilot, Edward Agner of Battle Creek, were flying from Battle Creek to Lexington, Kentucky, to pick up Mr. and Mrs. Van Lennep. Mrs. Van Lennep, the former Frances Dodge, was an officer of the firm that owned the plane. She had founded the Dodge Stables at Meadow Brook Farm and later moved them to Castleton Farm in Lexington, and the DC-3 was to carry the couple on to Delray Beach, Florida.

## Wing Against Wing

The collision came as the airliner climbed through the clouds on its assigned right turn. The right wing of Flight 694 struck the left wing of the DC-3. The blow tore the Martin's right wing away, and the DC-3 suffered damage to its fuselage, rudder and fin. Both aircraft fell out of control and struck the ground about two miles apart, and the wreckage of one came down along Hebron-Limaburg Road, two miles northeast of the town of Burlington. There were no survivors from either aircraft. Behind that bare sentence are real people: the ten passengers and three crew of Flight 694 and the two pilots of the DC-3, all of whom had set out that winter morning on what should have been a routine flight.

## No Flight Plan, No Clearance

The airport's control tower, run by the Civil Aeronautics Administration, reported that it had no record of a flight plan for either aircraft. A CAA spokesman said the TWA pilot had been cleared for takeoff and for a right turn out, and that radio messages from the airliner shortly after departure suggested the pilot was "alarmed and excited." The investigation settled on a clear probable cause: the DC-3 had been operated in a controlled zone as unknown traffic, having received no clearance and made no contact with the tower. TWA reportedly filed a $2 million damage suit against the Castleton Corporation of Kentucky. The field where it happened is now Cincinnati/Northern Kentucky International Airport, and the principle at the heart of that morning's probable cause - no aircraft in a controlled zone without clearance and contact with the tower - remains as basic as ever.

## Flight Context

The recorded coordinates, 39.0489°N, 84.6678°W, mark Cincinnati/Northern Kentucky International Airport (KCVG) itself, the field, then called Greater Cincinnati Airport, from which Flight 694 departed; the wreckage of one aircraft fell along Hebron-Limaburg Road about two miles northeast of Burlington, Kentucky. This is busy airline airspace today, so the area is best appreciated from an airliner window on arrival or departure, or from the ground, rather than on a low sightseeing pass; check current charts and coordinate with ATC before flying anywhere nearby. The collision happened at only 700-900 feet in a low cloud base, a reminder of how little room there is near a busy field in poor visibility.
