# Siege of Dunlap's Station

In January 1791, warriors of the Northwestern Confederacy besieged a small stockade of American settlers and soldiers on the Great Miami River in what is now southwest Ohio, a two-day fight shaped by a winter storm and remembered through rumor as much as record.

**Location:** 39.2931, -84.6553

**Categories:** Battles of the Northwest Indian War, Battles in Ohio, 1791 in the Northwest Territory, Native American history, Frontier history

## Introduction

"This night it rained, froze, and snow fell from four to five inches deep," one account of the siege recalls. The weather mattered. The warriors gathering around Dunlap's Station on the Great Miami River in January 1791 had planned to set the little stockade alight with blazing arrows and torches, and a coat of ice and wet snow made that nearly impossible. What followed was a two-day siege in the Northwest Indian War, one of the Native confederacy's few unsuccessful attacks of that period, and an episode that grew in the retelling until it became an emblem of frontier fear.

## A Station on Contested Ground

The Northwest Indian War began after the Revolutionary War, as American settlers holding purchased or granted titles pushed into lands north of the Ohio River that Native nations still lived on and defended. In the Northwest Territory, established in 1787, Judge John Cleves Symmes's Miami Company advertised land for sale and hired an Irish surveyor, John Dunlap, who led a party of men, women and children to the east bank of the Great Miami in early 1790. The station, later called Fort Colerain, was part military base, part land-speculation venture and part farm settlement. It stood beside far older works: the 2,000-year-old Colerain Earthwork and one or more Adena mounds. The settlers cleared land and raised crops their first summer, and neighboring Native people and settlers had once shared a Christmas feast. Still, this was primarily Shawnee land, and the settlers asked Fort Washington for a garrison.

## Twelve Soldiers and a Log Fence

Lieutenant Jacob Kingsbury arrived with a corporal and eleven men. Three blockhouses were built for the soldiers, along with a shelter for the hand mill, and ten settlers' cabins faced one another inside a fence of log pickets eight feet high that ran to the riverbank and enclosed about an acre. The defenses had gaps. A cleared line of fire was begun but not finished, and there were still open spaces between some of the logs. Across the region, the chiefs of the Northwestern Confederacy had concluded that the untrained American militias were vulnerable. In late 1790 they met with the British Indian Department to seek support for simultaneous raids on Dunlap's Station and Baker's Station. Scouts from the Shawnee, Miami, Lenape, Wyandot, Potawatomi, Odawa and Ojibwe nations moved toward the Great Miami.

## Spoons Melted for Bullets

The first blood came on January 8, 1791. Scouts surprised a small surveying party inspecting a nearby clearing. A man named Cunningham was killed, surveyor Abner Hunt was captured, and Captain John Sloan was wounded; John S. Wallace helped him back to the station. Inside, settlers and soldiers crowded into the blockhouses, and the women melted spoons to make bullets. In a moment of restraint amid the violence, the warriors allowed Wallace to recover Cunningham's body, which was buried where it lay. On January 10 the attackers appeared outside the walls, announcing that they were led by Simon Girty, and demanded surrender, using the captive Hunt as their interpreter. The parley lasted about an hour before gunfire broke out on the river side of the stockade, and shooting continued for two more hours. The attackers pulled back until evening, likely using the time to slaughter the settlers' cattle.

## A Captive's Fate

Abner Hunt was killed, and the circumstances remain disputed. Girty was accused of instigating the killing, but an 1843 report suggests that the Shawnee leader Blue Jacket led the attack while Girty was at Baker's Station. Kingsbury's written report of January 12 simply called Hunt's death a "murder," though allegations of torture may have been accurate. Wallace, meanwhile, slipped away to summon help. Fighting resumed at dawn on January 11, but the attackers had no siege weapons, and around 8 a.m. they withdrew. A relief force from Fort Washington arrived about two hours later. Kingsbury later boasted of the scalps his men had taken.

## How a Skirmish Became a Legend

The siege came just a week after the killings known as the Big Bottom massacre in southeast Ohio, and only months before the U.S. Army suffered a major defeat at the hands of the confederacy. In that climate, Hunt's apparent torture, with "white traitors" said to be leading the attack, was widely sensationalized as proof of Native inhumanity, and the press soon began embellishing the story. Reports reached Thomas Jefferson and George Washington. When Josiah Harmar praised Kingsbury on January 14, no mention was made of the warriors' decision to let Cunningham's body be recovered. The station itself proved hard to hold and was twice abandoned as too vulnerable. In 1881 a county history called it "the fiercest and longest sustained Indian attack recorded in the annals of Hamilton county." The fight had lasted barely two days; the story it became lasted far longer.

## Flight Context

The site of Dunlap's Station lies at approximately 39.293°N, 84.655°W on the east bank of the Great Miami River in Colerain Township, Hamilton County, Ohio, northwest of Cincinnati. Nothing of the stockade remains to be seen; from 2,000–3,000 ft AGL, follow the winding Great Miami and its broad floodplain fields as your reference. Butler County Regional Airport–Hogan Field (KHAO) is about 14 km to the northeast, Cincinnati West Airport (FAA identifier I67, no ICAO code) about 11 km to the west-southwest, and Cincinnati/Northern Kentucky International (KCVG) about 27 km to the south, so remain clear of its Class B airspace. River-valley fog is common on cool mornings; summer brings haze and thunderstorms.
