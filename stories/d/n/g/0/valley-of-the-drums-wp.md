# Valley of the Drums

A 23-acre hollow near Brooks, Kentucky, where thousands of waste drums piled up (17,051 on the surface in 1979, and more than 100,000 delivered over the years by one state estimate), a sight that helped persuade Congress to pass the Superfund law in 1980.

**Location:** 38.0822, -85.7236

**Categories:** Geography of Bullitt County, Kentucky, History of Louisville, Kentucky, Waste processing sites, Superfund sites in Kentucky, Environmental disasters in the United States

## Introduction

Love Canal is usually remembered as the disaster that gave America its Superfund law. But Lois Gibbs, the Love Canal activist, has pointed out a problem with that story: Love Canal looked like a suburban community. The Kentucky site did not. "Valley of the Drums," she is quoted as saying, "became the visualization of the problem." On 23 acres near Brooks, in northern Bullitt County near Louisville, drums of waste lay strewn across the land, giving the place its name. Officially it is the A.L. Taylor (Valley of Drums) Superfund Site, and it is known as one of the primary motivations for the Superfund Act of 1980.

## A Decade Without Rules

The site began collecting toxic waste sometime in the 1960s. State officials first took notice in the mid-1960s (1966 by one account, 1967 by EPA's), when some of the drums reportedly caught fire and burned for more than a week. Even then, there was little they could do. No laws yet addressed how toxic wastes should be stored or contained, and the site went on unregulated for another decade. In 1977, the owner, A.L. Taylor, died; the record infers that he was also the primary dumper. Who owned the property afterward is unclear, and county tax records show that its property taxes went unpaid for several years. In effect, no one was answering for the land, and the drums stayed where they were.

## One Hundred Thousand Drums

A 1978 investigation by the Kentucky Department of Natural Resources and Environmental Protection revealed the scale of what had happened. The investigation concluded that more than 100,000 waste drums had been delivered to the site over the years. By one account about 27,000 had been buried, and the contents of the rest had been discharged directly into pits and trenches. When EPA took its own inventory in 1979, it counted 17,051 drums on the surface, 11,629 of them empty. Over time, many drums on the surface deteriorated, spilling their contents onto the ground, where storm water runoff flushed them into a nearby creek. People living next to the property complained again and again about strong odors along the creek bed. For them the valley was not an abstraction or a policy debate. It was the smell along the creek beside their own property.

## The Picture That Moved Congress

In 1979, spring snowmelt carried large quantities of contaminants into the creek, and that finally brought the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency. EPA analysts found high levels of heavy metals and polychlorinated biphenyls, or PCBs, along with some 140 other chemical substances, in the property and the creek. The agency began an emergency cleanup of the worst leaking drums that same year, but workers on the ground quickly realized the scope of the problem was far beyond what they could handle. Once news of the site became public, members of Congress cited it as one of the reasons a proposed Superfund law was needed. The Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and Liability Act passed in 1980, and the image of this Kentucky hollow was part of the case for it.

## A Cleanup That Never Quite Ended

The site joined the Superfund National Priorities List in 1983. Cleanup stretched through the 1980s, with a clay cap and security fence installed in 1987, and was officially completed in 1990; EPA deleted the site from the list in 1996, and Kentucky's environmental agency now leads monitoring. The trouble did not disappear on schedule. Problems continued to be reported for years, and an environmental audit in 2003 reportedly found PCBs in the sediment around the site, prompting further testing. In December 2008, EPA inspectors discovered about four dozen rusted metal drums on land just outside the capped and fenced area, including part of Jefferson Memorial Forest, and new cleanup work was under consideration. That area became known as the Gully of the Drums, and in March 2026 Louisville's Metro Council approved $107,000 for an environmental firm to oversee its removal. The Valley of the Drums remains a lesson in how long careless dumping can last: the drums arrived over the course of years, and the consequences have outlasted the dumping by decades.
