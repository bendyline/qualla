# Bullitt's Lick

A salt lick west of Shepherdsville whose saltworks, begun in 1779, is often called Kentucky's first industry, boiling brine in iron kettles for markets as far off as New Orleans until cheaper Kanawha salt undercut it.

**Location:** 38.0006, -85.7639

**Categories:** Geography of Bullitt County, Kentucky, History of salt, Salt in the United States

## Introduction

What is often called Kentucky's first industry ran on fire and brine. In 1779, a year after Louisville was established, workers at a salt lick about 3 miles west of present-day Shepherdsville began boiling its salty water down in iron kettles set over a trench of fire. Bullitt's Lick is described as the first commercial supplier of salt in Kentucky, and its salt would travel by flatboat as far as New Orleans and Pittsburgh. It supplied jobs for many residents. It also relied on enslaved people, whose forced labor was part of how this early industry was built.

## Where the Buffalo Came

Long before anyone built a furnace here, animals knew the place. The water at the lick was far saltier than other sources nearby, and it drew buffalo and other animals in such numbers that their trails became natural roads for the humans who followed. Squire Boone noted killing a few buffalo by the lick in early 1779. Most salt deposits in what is now Kentucky held only enough to let a few settlers preserve their food. Bullitt's Lick was different. It sat within a larger concentration of salt that stretched from Bardstown Junction in the south, across the Salt River, to just north of present-day Fairdale, along the eastern side of the region's "Knobs." That concentration was what made an industry possible.

## A Surveyor's Find, a Tangled Inheritance

Captain Thomas Bullitt found the lick in 1773 while surveying land for Colonel William Christian, who had been granted land for his service in the French and Indian War. The Christian family owned the lick but did not work it themselves; others made the salt and paid the Christians rent in salt, the most prominent of them a sharecropper named Henry Crist. Christian himself did not reach the lick until 1785, and he was killed the following year in a skirmish during an expedition against Native peoples north of the Ohio River. His widow died soon after, and the property passed to their son, John Henry Christian, whose guardian and uncle, Patrick Henry, controlled it until John came of age. John also died young, leaving the lick to his five sisters, and through their marriages control passed to Alexander Scott Bullitt and William Pope Jr.

## Twenty-Five Kettles

The first real saltworks on the property went up in 1779, at a time when salt was hard to come by and there were few ways to move goods in or out. The method was brute force. Workers boiled the brine in 25 iron kettles set above a trench of fire. The first kettles weighed 100 pounds; later ones weighed as much as 200. Each 24-hour period produced three cycles of salt. At first the furnaces stood beside the lick, but as nearby timber was used up, it became cheaper to move the furnaces to fresh wood than to haul wood to the furnaces. For its early years, it is said to have been the only saltworks west of Pennsylvania's Allegheny Mountains. Joseph Field worked here briefly before joining the Lewis and Clark Expedition.

## Salt Down the Rivers

Salt from Bullitt's Lick soon reached well beyond Kentucky, into the Illinois Territory, the Tennessee Territory and faraway New Orleans. Flatboats carried it to New Orleans and to Pittsburgh. The business lasted until the 1830s, when improved navigation on the Kanawha River in Virginia (now West Virginia) allowed steamboats to deliver Kanawha salt throughout the Ohio and Mississippi river systems, undercutting the producers here. The industry left a mark on the land that outlived it. So much wood had fed the furnaces that, by one account, much of Bullitt County was deforested. Bernheim Arboretum and Research Forest, founded nearby in 1929, was created on land that earlier sale and iron ore mining had left nearly bare of trees, a reminder of how hard the region's industries were on its forests.
