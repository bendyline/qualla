# Shepherdsville train wreck

On December 20, 1917, a late-running express struck the rear of a local train at Shepherdsville, Kentucky, killing about 49 people in what is described as the deadliest train wreck in the state's history.

**Location:** 37.9869, -85.7147

**Categories:** Railway accidents in 1917, Railway accidents and incidents in Kentucky, 1917 in Kentucky, Bullitt County, Kentucky, Accidents and incidents involving Louisville and Nashville Railroad, December 1917 in the United States

## Introduction

Half a mile out of Shepherdsville, the engineer of the Flyer blew four short blasts on his whistle and looked ahead for the signal. "I could see the signal only dimly," he said later, "and it was green." Under the rules, green meant proceed only if he had watched it change from red. He had not seen it change, but he believed it already had. Moments later, on December 20, 1917, his express struck the rear of a local train at Shepherdsville, Kentucky. About 49 people died (counts run from 46 in the Interstate Commerce Commission's own report to 51 in some sources), in what is described as the deadliest train wreck in Kentucky's history.

## Two Trains, One Line

Local train No. 41, known as the Accommodation, left Union Station in Louisville at 4:35 that afternoon, bound for Springfield on the Louisville and Nashville Railroad. It was a short train: an engine pulling a baggage car, a combination car that served as the smoker and as the segregated car for Black passengers, and a first-class car. At Brooks, 14 miles out of Louisville, it arrived at 5:12, about six minutes late, and the dispatcher told its conductor to let the approaching Flyer pass at Shepherdsville unless he could reach Bardstown Junction first. The Flyer, a Cincinnati-to-New Orleans express of nine cars, most of them steel, had left Louisville at 4:53, running late (accounts range from about 23 minutes to nearly two hours behind schedule). Its crew intended to make up time on the run south.

## Minutes at the Depot

Rather than pull straight into the siding, the local's conductor chose to stop normally at the Shepherdsville station and confirm the Flyer's position before deciding what to do. The train arrived at 5:24, but the station operator had no news of the express, so the conductor hurried to the depot for information. Meanwhile the Flyer passed Brooks, sounded its four blasts, and received a green signal. When word came that the express was approaching fast, the local pulled forward so it could back into the siding at the switch. The Shepherdsville operator, seeing the express bearing down, threw the signal to stop and ran out with a lantern to flag it. The Flyer's engineer applied his emergency brakes, but it was too late. At an estimated 25 miles per hour, the largely steel express struck the all-wooden local from behind and pushed through the entire rear car and half the length of the smoker, driving the local 800 feet down the line.

## The Long Night

Help came as quickly as it could. A relief train reached Shepherdsville before 7 p.m. carrying 11 Louisville doctors and several surgeons, but the work of recovering the dead from the splintered wreckage went on until nearly midnight. The collision killed about 49 people and left dozens more injured, many of them severely. The list of the dead runs from Father Eugene A. Bertello to N. H. Thompson, the names of men and women whose lives ended that day, and whose names still tie this tragedy to real people rather than a number. Among those killed were the conductor and the flagman of the local train itself, the two men whose actions investigators would soon examine most closely.

## What the Investigators Found

The Interstate Commerce Commission assigned responsibility to the local train's conductor and flagman for failing to protect their train, calling the action of "these two experienced employees" in failing to do so "inexcusable." But the report did not stop there. A contributing factor was the express engineman's failure to properly observe the train order signal. More troubling was the system itself. Signals were held at stop until an approaching train, within 600 feet, sounded four short blasts to request clearance, and an engineman who failed to see the signal clear was required to stop. High-speed trains like the Flyer, though, needed far more than 600 feet to halt. With 44 trains scheduled in both directions every day, the commission concluded, traffic of that density could not be handled safely under the time-interval system, and it urged the railroad to "take immediate steps to provide an adequate block system for the protection of trains on this section of road." A separate Kentucky Railroad Commission report in February 1918 spread the blame wider, faulting the train dispatcher and several station operators as well, and finding the railroad's rules "too vague and indefinite."
