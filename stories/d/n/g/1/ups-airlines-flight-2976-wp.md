# UPS Airlines Flight 2976

On November 4, 2025, a UPS MD-11 cargo jet lost its left engine on takeoff from Louisville and crashed into an industrial area just beyond the airport, killing its three crew members and twelve people on the ground, the deadliest accident in the airline's history.

**Location:** 38.1472, -85.7344

**Categories:** Aviation accidents and incidents in 2025, Accidents and incidents involving the McDonnell Douglas MD-11, Airliner accidents and incidents in Kentucky, Louisville Muhammad Ali International Airport, 2025 in Louisville, Kentucky

## Introduction

At 5:14 in the evening, the members of Teamsters Local 89 fell silent. The union, which represents transportation and warehouse workers including those at UPS, had gathered at its Louisville hall for a candlelight vigil, and it chose that minute to mark the moment when UPS Airlines Flight 2976 came down. The crash, on November 4, 2025, killed fifteen people: the three crew members aboard and twelve more on the ground, among them a three-year-old girl and her grandfather. In a city where UPS runs its main air hub, the loss struck very close.

## Bound for Honolulu

Flight 2976 was a scheduled cargo run from UPS's hub at Louisville Muhammad Ali International Airport to Daniel K. Inouye International Airport in Honolulu, an 8.5-hour flight. The aircraft, registered N259UP, was a 34-year-old McDonnell Douglas MD-11F. It had been delivered to Thai Airways International in 1991, later converted to carry cargo, and joined UPS in 2006. It had logged about 92,992 hours and 21,043 cycles, and it was powered by three General Electric CF6 turbofans. Three experienced aviators were aboard. Captain Richard Wartenberg, 57, a retired U.S. Air Force lieutenant colonel who had served with the 445th Airlift Wing, was the pilot monitoring, with about 8,613 flight hours. First Officer Lee Truitt, 45, was flying the airplane, with about 9,200 hours. Relief officer Dana Diamond, 62, had about 15,250 hours, including 8,775 in the MD-11.

## Seconds After Liftoff

The jet began its takeoff from runway 17R, heading south, at about 5:13 p.m. local time. The weather was clear, with a light wind from the southeast. During the takeoff roll, the left engine separated from the aircraft. The MD-11 lifted off, but its left main landing gear struck the roof of a UPS Supply Chain warehouse, and seconds later the airplane crashed into an industrial area beyond the airport. Video analysis indicates the tail-mounted engine also malfunctioned, with bursts of flame from its exhaust; aviation experts have suggested that debris from the detached engine may have been ingested into it, causing it to surge. The debris field stretched about 3,000 feet south-southeast from the warehouse. Multiple buildings were set on fire or destroyed, and there were reports of people trapped inside.

## Fifteen Lives

In the hours and days that followed, families waited for news. Two employees of an auto yard were initially unaccounted for, no one knew how many customers had been on the premises, and fifteen families reported relatives missing. On November 6, Louisville Mayor Craig Greenberg reported that six of the missing had been accounted for; two days later, he said the missing were believed to have been found. Fifteen people were treated at UofL Health facilities. One of the injured later died, a death announced on December 25 that brought the toll to fifteen. It was the deadliest crash in the history of UPS Airlines, which had suffered two fatal accidents before, in 2010 and 2013, each taking the lives of the two crew members aboard. State flags flew at half-staff.

## A Fleet on the Ground

All flights at the airport were canceled that evening, and UPS temporarily suspended operations at its Worldport air hub. The fire was nearly contained by 10:30 p.m., freeing first responders to search for victims. Jefferson County's public schools closed the next day, and the runway reopened on the afternoon of November 6. On November 8, following instructions from Boeing, which inherited the design from McDonnell Douglas, UPS, FedEx and Western Global Airlines temporarily grounded their MD-11s, and the FAA issued an emergency airworthiness directive requiring inspections before flight; it later issued one covering the DC-10, the MD-11's predecessor, as well. Overnight cargo operations at several major U.S. airports were disrupted, all during a federal government shutdown that strained aviation agencies. On January 27, 2026, UPS announced the complete retirement of the MD-11 from its fleet.

## Cracks in the Pylon

The National Transportation Safety Board opened an investigation with the Federal Aviation Administration, sending a go-team of 28 people and recovering both the cockpit voice recorder and the flight data recorder; the voice recorder captured a persistent bell sound. On November 20, 2025, the NTSB's preliminary report described fatigue cracks in the lugs of the left engine pylon's aft mount. The mount's last visual inspection had been in October 2021, and more rigorous special inspections of its lugs were not yet due, because the aircraft's cycles were below the thresholds that would require them. A January 14, 2026 update from the NTSB pointed to cracks in the engine's spherical bearing assembly, the NTSB scheduled a two-day investigative hearing for May 19 and 20, 2026, and its investigation remained open, with no final report or safety recommendations published on its investigation page at last check. Lawsuits followed within days of the crash. For Louisville, though, the lasting mark is the fifteen people who never came home from an ordinary Tuesday evening.

## Flight Context

The crash site lies at about 38.1472°N, 85.7344°W, in an industrial area immediately south of Louisville Muhammad Ali International Airport (KSDF), roughly 1.6 nm from the airport reference point and off the departure end of runway 17R. This is inside the busy airspace of KSDF, home to the UPS Worldport hub, so any flight near here requires ATC coordination and respect for arrival and departure paths; please treat the area as a place of loss rather than a sightseeing target. Bowman Field (KLOU) lies about 6 nm to the northeast. The Ohio River to the north and the airport's parallel runways are the dominant visual references.
