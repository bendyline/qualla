# Howard Steamboat Museum

In Jeffersonville, Indiana, the red-brick mansion of the Howard shipbuilding family, with a staircase modeled on a steamboat, now tells the story of the yard that launched river vessels for 107 years.

**Location:** 38.2811, -85.7258

## Introduction

The grand staircase in the Howard mansion is said to have been fashioned after a steamboat, the J.M. White. It makes sense for the house. The family that built it earned its fortune launching river vessels from the Indiana bank of the Ohio, across from Louisville, and when Edmonds J. Howard built his home in the early 1890s, he brought the look of the boats indoors. Today the mansion is the Howard Steamboat Museum, also called the Howard National Steamboat Museum, and it tells the story of steamboats in general and of the Howard Shipyards of Jeffersonville in particular. As of fall 2026, its regular public hours are temporarily suspended during restoration work, though group tours and occasional walk-in days continue, so check with the museum before visiting.

## A Shipbuilder's Palace

Edmonds J. Howard inherited the family shipyard from his father, James Howard, and he spent $100,000 on a house worthy of it. The result is a three-story, 22-room red brick mansion of 15,000 square feet in the Richardsonian Romanesque style, built in what was then Port Fulton, Indiana, where the shipyard also stood. Inside, the wealth shows in every direction: carved woodwork, arches, and that grand staircase. The house uses 15 different types of wood, and its themed rooms include a library and a Moorish music parlor. The gasoliers still in place operate on both electricity and natural gas.

## The House the Hulls Built

Much of what visitors see today belonged to the Howards. About 90 percent of the furnishings are original, and many of them were bought at the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago. The family's real pride, though, lay elsewhere. The Howards prided themselves on their hulls, and a large collection of their half-breadths is displayed on the property, a reminder of where the money for this house came from. The house has weathered trouble of its own. On March 17, 1971, a fire broke out in the museum. Much of the building suffered only minor damage, the other areas could be restored, and the museum reopened the following year. In 1973 the mansion was listed on the National Register of Historic Places as the Howard Home.

## A Century of Launchings

The shipyard began in 1834, when James Howard started his first boat, the Hyperion. The Howard family controlled the yard for 107 years, and counts of the vessels it built range from about 550 to nearly 1,200. The yard struggled through the Depression and closed in 1940; in 1942 the U.S. Navy took over the property (accounts describe a sale or a condemnation and lease), and wartime output at the yard included 123 LSTs, the vessels known as "Landing Ship, Tank," along with 23 submarine chasers and numerous other craft. The Jeffersonville Boat and Machine Company, which had done that work for the Navy, took over the yard after the war and was eventually renamed Jeffboat. Jeffboat was described as the largest inland shipbuilder in the United States, and it closed permanently on April 23, 2018.
