# Pendennis Club

Louisville's Pendennis Club has hosted presidents, given the world Henry Bain's sauce and staged a young Cassius Clay's bout in its ballroom, while its famous claim to the Old Fashioned and its long record of exclusion tell a more complicated story.

**Location:** 38.2509, -85.7548

**Categories:** Gentlemen's clubs in the United States, Culture of Louisville, Kentucky, Clubhouses on the National Register of Historic Places in Kentucky, Colonial Revival architecture in Kentucky, Cocktails

## Introduction

Ask at the bar of Louisville's Pendennis Club and you may hear that the Old Fashioned was born here. Cocktail historians Robert Simonson, David Wondrich and Simon Difford flatly reject that claim. The phrase "old fashioned cocktails" appeared in the Chicago Daily Tribune in February 1880, more than a year before the club was founded, and the drink itself, liquor with bitters, sugar and water, was simply the traditional way cocktails had been made for decades. Legends cling to old institutions, though, and this private club on Muhammad Ali Boulevard has gathered more than its share since 1881.

## Named for a Fictional Gentleman

On June 28, 1881, Thomas Wilson Todd, Levi Bloom, John Smith Noyes and William Whits Hite met with sixteen others in Todd's office at Louisville City Hall to start a gentlemen's club on the London model. They first rented rooms above A. J. Ross's grocery at Fourth and Walnut, on the site where the Seelbach Hotel now stands. Their first president, Major John Montgomery Wright, was a West Point graduate and Union veteran who later became Clerk of the U.S. Supreme Court. For a name, the members chose Arthur Pendennis, the paradigm club man of William Makepeace Thackeray's 1850 novel. Thackeray had described Pendennis's crest but never pictured it, so the club drew its own, adopted the motto nec tenui penna, "with unfailing wings," and by year's end had the crest on bottles of Kentucky bourbon sold to members.

## A President for Dinner

In 1883 the club bought the Walnut Street home of William Burke Belknap, founder of Belknap Hardware, and it opened to members on August 1, the same day President Chester A. Arthur opened Louisville's Southern Exposition. That evening Arthur dined lavishly at the club with members of his cabinet, including Robert Todd Lincoln. Theodore Roosevelt, William H. Taft and Woodrow Wilson visited in later years, and according to founder Levi Bloom, Taft "felt more at home and would rather come to the Pendennis Club than any other in the country." On May 20, 1891, Frederick Law Olmsted, designer of New York's Central Park, dined here with leading citizens and park commissioners during the visit in which he proposed a park system for Louisville.

## Henry Bain's Sauce

In 1884 Henry Bain went to work at the club as its elevator operator. He rose to become perhaps its first maître d', and he left behind a legacy Louisville still buys by the bottle: a sauce for meat of his own creation. Bain sold the recipe to the club, which holds the trademark, and Henry Bain sauce is now sold in local stores. He retired in 1924 and died just months before the new clubhouse opened in 1928. A reputed nephew, Roland Hayes, made his debut at the club in 1910 as a classical lyric tenor. Drink was always central to club life. A 1911 Louisville book lists six Pendennis mixed drinks, among them the Pendennis Cocktail, a mint julep and an Old Fashioned Toddy, which may be the source of the later confusion. Prohibition barely slowed things down. When agents raided the club on August 9, 1930, they hauled away six carloads of liquor, and one remarked, "I never saw so many kinds of drinks in my life."

## Georgian Rooms, Closed Doors

The current Georgian Revival clubhouse, about a block from the original, went up in 1927 and 1928 at a reported cost of $1 million and opened on December 11, 1928. Its roughly 80,000 to 85,000 square feet (accounts differ) include a walnut-paneled library, two rooms of French woodblock wallpaper by Zuber & Cie, and one of the few grand billiard rooms in the country still used for billiards. Squash came to Louisville here in 1930. For all its polish, the club kept its doors narrow. Although founder Levi Bloom was Jewish, the club did not admit another Jewish member until 1974, and several prominent members resigned over rejected nominees. By 1991 it had no African American members. That year the Reverend Louis Coleman Jr., a local civil rights activist, protested by eating lunch at a table set up in front of the club and filed discrimination complaints with the state's human rights commission. In 2004 the Supreme Court of Kentucky ruled that while the club could choose its own members, the law denying members of discriminating clubs a tax deduction for their dues still applied, and the commission could investigate.

## Cassius Clay in the Ballroom

The grand ballroom has hosted debutante presentations, Derby-week parties and, since at least 1941, occasional stag boxing nights with a ring set up on its floor. By one account, on February 19, 1960, a young Louisville boxer named Cassius Clay climbed through those ropes and won by a third-round technical knockout. Later that year he fought in the Olympics and turned professional. It was a natural booking: reportedly at least five of the eleven Louisville men who sponsored his early career were club members. The young fighter who boxed that night before a club that, as late as 1991, still had no African American members would become Muhammad Ali, and the street outside, once Walnut, now bears his name.

## Flight Context

The Pendennis Club stands at 218 West Muhammad Ali Boulevard in downtown Louisville, Kentucky, at approximately 38.251°N, 85.755°W, a short walk from Fourth Street and the Seelbach Hotel and less than a mile south of the Ohio River waterfront. The building itself is hard to pick out from the air; at 2,000–2,500 ft AGL, use the downtown skyline and the Ohio River bridges as references, and remain aware of the Louisville Class C airspace. Bowman Field (KLOU) is about 8 km to the east-southeast and Louisville Muhammad Ali International (KSDF) about 9 km to the south. Expect summer haze and thunderstorms; river fog can form on cool mornings.
