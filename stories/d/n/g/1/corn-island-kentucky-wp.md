# Corn Island (Kentucky)

Louisville traces its birth to Corn Island, where George Rogers Clark planted a settlement in 1778 at the head of the Falls of the Ohio, and the island itself has since vanished beneath the river.

**Location:** 38.2622, -85.7592

**Categories:** Former islands of the United States, History of Louisville, Kentucky, River islands of Kentucky, Islands of the Ohio River, Landforms of Louisville, Kentucky, Former populated places in Kentucky

## Introduction

Louisville was founded on an island you can no longer stand on. Corn Island once lay in the Ohio River at the head of the Falls of the Ohio, just north of the city that grew from it. A survey in 1780 measured it at 43 acres, stretching from what is now Fourth Street to Fourteenth Street. Today it is gone, eroded, quarried and finally drowned. Yet the city still traces its founding in 1778 to the settlers who cleared fields on this vanished strip of ground.

## Dunmore's Island

The island first appeared on paper under another name. In 1773, a surveying party led by Virginia Captain Thomas Bullitt mapped it and called it Dunmore's Island, after John Murray, 4th Earl of Dunmore, the Crown Governor of Virginia. The timing was loaded. Surveying expeditions like Bullitt's helped provoke Dunmore's War the following year, and within five years the island would be drawn into a much larger conflict. Even its size refused to stay fixed. Estimates vary with the date they were made, because the island was steadily eroding, and the 43 acres recorded in 1780 were only a snapshot of a shape that kept shrinking.

## Clark's Colony

On May 27, 1778, in the middle of the American Revolutionary War, George Rogers Clark landed his militia on the island along with 60 civilian settlers. Clark had a campaign to fight in the Illinois Country, and he set up the farming colony here as a communication post to support it. When his force moved on on June 24, the civilians stayed behind. A few of their family names survive: Captain James Patton, Richard Chenoweth, John Tuel, William Faith and John McManus. Clark gave the island its new name, Corn Island, presumably reflecting how much the early settlement depended on farming. The name may have served a second purpose as well, helping to sustain the ruse that this was purely a farming settlement rather than a military post.

## Travelers and Firsts

The little settlement became a waypoint. Shortly after Clark left, Colonel David Rogers and Captain Robert Benham landed on the island on their way to New Orleans to meet the Spanish governor. They stopped again briefly on the return trip in October 1779, and a few days after leaving, their party was attacked by Simon Girty opposite present-day Dayton, Kentucky. The island also holds a place in family history. The Draper Manuscripts, which document its earliest pioneer settlers, name Isaac Kimbley, son of Andrew Kimbley and Sarah "Sallie" Bromley Kimbley, among the first documented non-Native American children born in Kentucky. His father had fought alongside Clark and later received a land grant in Kentucky from him.

## An Island Worn Away

The following year, the settlers crossed to the mainland and established the settlement named Louisville, and the island's life as a community ended. Farmers and hunters kept using it, but from as early as 1806, the island began to shrink. Deforestation stripped away the trees that had helped hold it together, adding to erosion, and its limestone was mined; in the 19th century the Louisville Cement Company extracted rock there for cement. Between 1889 and 1891, the Army Corps of Engineers is reported to have blasted and excavated the island's rock base, and a dam built in the 1920s flooded it. The ground where Louisville began now lies beneath the Ohio.

## Flight Context

The former site of Corn Island lies near 38.2622°N, 85.7592°W, in the Ohio River just north of downtown Louisville, Kentucky, at the head of the Falls of the Ohio. The island itself is submerged, so look instead for the river's edge along the downtown waterfront and the falls just downstream. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet the river and the city grid make the setting easy to read. Clark Regional Airport in Jeffersonville, Indiana (FAA code JVY) is about 6.5 nm north, Bowman Field (KLOU) about 5 nm east-southeast, and Louisville Muhammad Ali International Airport (KSDF) about 5.5 nm south-southeast. Check the airspace around Louisville Muhammad Ali International before descending over the river.
