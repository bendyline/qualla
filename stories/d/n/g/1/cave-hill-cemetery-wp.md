# Cave Hill Cemetery

Louisville's largest cemetery grew from a farm with a cave into a 296-acre Victorian garden of lakes and champion trees, where George Rogers Clark, Colonel Sanders, the sisters behind "Happy Birthday" and Muhammad Ali all rest.

## Introduction

Somewhere inside Louisville's largest cemetery is the cave that gave it a name, and nobody is supposed to go in. It can be entered for about 30 feet before it narrows to a marginal crawl space, and it is officially off limits. A spring near the cave feeds a stream that flows into the middle fork of Beargrass Creek, roughly splitting the grounds into an old western section and a new eastern one. Around that quiet spring spread 296 acres of hills, lakes and trees: Cave Hill Cemetery, a Victorian garden of the dead where roughly 120,000 people had been laid to rest by 2002.

## From Farm to Garden

Before it held graves, this was William Johnston's Cave Hill Farm, a rural property some distance east of Louisville. Johnston built the first brick house in Louisville here around 1788 and died in 1798. In the 1830s, city officials bought part of the land expecting to run a railroad through it, and a workhouse went up on the site. The railroad was built elsewhere, and the land was leased to local farmers. In 1846, Mayor Frederick A. Kaye began exploring the idea of a rural, garden-style cemetery on the grounds. Hartford, Connecticut, civil engineer Edmund Francis Lee looked over the property and saw its "undulating hills, promontories, and basins as perfectly suited for landscaping the rural aesthetic." The cemetery was chartered in 1848, and in 1888 the grounds were expanded and remapped to their modern size of nearly 300 acres.

## A Bell Tower That Drew Lightning

The signature entrance is on Baxter Avenue, though the cemetery itself calls it the "Broadway Entrance." Completed in 1892, the Corinthian-style building carries a 2,000-pound bell in its clock tower. That tower was reportedly once the tallest structure for miles, and it was frequently struck by lightning. It was last renovated in 2001. A second entrance on Grinstead Drive followed in 1913, while an entrance off Payne Street, built in 1910 closest to the military sections, is no longer in use. Inside the walls stand a stone office building near the lake and a Rustic Shelter House, built in 1892 for $565. There is a harder edge too. In the 1980s, razor wire was added to the brick walls to keep out after-hours visitors.

## Hundreds of Kinds of Trees

Cave Hill is also a recognized arboretum, and the living collection is as remarkable as the monuments. More than 500 species of trees and shrubs grow here (the cemetery's own arboretum page counts over 600), including numerous state champion trees. Some are natives, like the pignut hickory; others came from far away, like the Caucasian wingnut. Water shapes the grounds as much as the trees do. Five man-made lakes dot the landscape, and in 2008 the cemetery opened its first scenic overlook, at Twin Lakes. Between the lakes, the creek and the canopy, it is easy to see what Lee saw in these hills. Cave Hill was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1979, and it remains the largest cemetery in Louisville by both area and number of burials.

## Soldiers in Separate Rows

The Civil War left its own map on the hillsides. During the war, administrators sold several acres for the burial of Union soldiers, and patients who died at Brown General Hospital and other nearby army medical facilities were buried here. Cave Hill National Cemetery, which holds military graves, was added to the National Register in 1998. Local Confederate supporters bought nearby land as well. More than 200 Confederate soldiers lie in Section O, where the original wooden markers were replaced with stone in 1880 and 1881, and a number of those stones read only "unknown." Brigadier General Alpheus Baker is among them, and residents of the Kentucky Confederate Home who died around the turn of the 20th century rest in an addition to the section. The cemetery also holds three Union generals, the 32nd Indiana Monument, and a single British war grave: a soldier of the Machine Gun Corps who died in 1918 while serving with the British Military Mission to the United States.

## A Roll Call of Louisville

Walk the paths long enough and the names start to sound like a history of the city. George Rogers Clark, the Revolutionary War officer who founded Louisville, lies here. So do Patty and Mildred J. Hill, the sisters who composed the Happy Birthday song, and Colonel Harland Sanders, founder of Kentucky Fried Chicken. Muhammad Ali, the boxing champion born in Louisville, was interred on June 10, 2016. Other lives are woven through as well: suffragist Margaret Weissinger Castleman; Mary Ann Xantippe "Tip" Saunders, a portrait painter and cousin of Mark Twain; Mia Zapata, lead singer of the punk band The Gits; and Heisman Trophy winner Paul Hornung, buried in 2020. As of 2002 there was still room for 22,000 more graves.

## Flight Context

Cave Hill Cemetery is centered near 38.2456°N, 85.7158°W, about 2 miles east of downtown Louisville, Kentucky. From 2,000 to 2,500 feet its large green expanse, winding roads and small lakes stand out against the surrounding neighborhoods.
