# Bloody Monday

On election day, August 6, 1855, Know-Nothing mobs tore through Louisville's Irish and German Catholic neighborhoods, killing at least 19 people and burning homes and businesses, and no one was ever convicted.

## Introduction

The courthouse reportedly held the guns. In the summer of 1855, the old Jefferson County Courthouse in Louisville, today's Louisville Metro Hall and home of the Mayor's Office, is described as a stronghold of the Know-Nothing Party. Weapons and arms are said to have been stored there during the violence, and later so were the bodies of the dead. The day that filled it was August 6, 1855, an election day, when Protestant mobs attacked the Irish and German Catholic neighborhoods of a river city that immigration had remade. Historians put the death toll at 19 to 22 people. Louisville remembers the date by a blunt name: Bloody Monday.

## A City Remade by Newcomers

Like other major cities on the Ohio and Mississippi Rivers, Louisville had grown rapidly over the two decades before 1855, swelled by heavy immigration from Ireland and Germany. By then there were 11,000 immigrants in a white population of 36,000, and the Irish and Germans made up about a third of the city. Most were Catholic, though there was also a large German Lutheran community, and the vast majority voted Democratic. Against them stood the Know Nothings, officially the American Party, a nativist movement locked in a bitter rivalry with the Democrats. Their anger was stoked in print. George D. Prentice, editor of the Louisville Journal, published radical, inflammatory anti-immigrant writings that fed the movement in large part. By the summer of 1855, words had prepared the ground for what came next.

## Guarding the Polls

On election morning, the Know-Nothings formed armed groups to guard the polls. One newspaper account described Louisville as "in possession of an armed mob, the base passions of which were infuriated to the highest pitch by the incendiary appeals of the newspaper organ and the popular leaders of the Know Nothing party." Hundreds of voters were turned away by direct intimidation, and others stayed home out of fear of what might happen to them. Not everyone stood by. In the Sixth Ward, William Thomasson, a former US Representative from the district, appealed to the crowd to stop its disorder and violence. Someone struck him from behind, and he was beaten.

## Afternoon on Shelby Street

By afternoon the fighting had spread into a general row along Shelby Street, stretching from Main to Broadway. Some fourteen or fifteen men were shot, among them Officer Williams and Joe Selvage, and two or three were killed. Rioters broke into and pillaged a number of houses, chiefly German coffee houses. Around four o'clock, a vast crowd armed with shotguns, muskets and rifles set out to attack St. Martin of Tours, the new German parish on Shelby Street. It was turned aside by an unlikely figure. Mayor John Barbee was himself a Know-Nothing, yet he calmed the crowd, and it went back to the First Ward polls. The reprieve did not last. An hour later, the large brewery on Jefferson Street, near its junction with Green, was set on fire.

## Quinn's Row and the Long Memory

When the smoke cleared, more than 100 businesses, private homes and tenements had been vandalized, looted or burned. Among them was Quinn's Row, a block-long row of houses. The human cost is still disputed. Historians estimate that 19 to 22 people died, and scores more were injured. Catholics, including Bishop Spalding, put the toll at well over 100, and they spoke of entire families consumed in the fires. Bloody Monday was not an isolated outburst, either. Sporadic violence and attacks had broken out in the months leading up to August 6, and they continued for some time afterward. Justice never arrived. Five people were later indicted, none was convicted, and the victims were never compensated for what they had lost. A century and a half later, a series of commemorations marked the 150th anniversary. One organizer, Vicky Ullrich, whose German-speaking Swiss ancestors fled to Indiana, explained why the day still matters: "with another influx of immigrants increasing the diversity of Louisville, it's important that Bloody Monday be remembered so that a similar event does not happen again."
