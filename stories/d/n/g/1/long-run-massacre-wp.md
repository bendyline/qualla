# Long Run massacre

In September 1781, settlers fleeing Squire Boone's Painted Stone Station were ambushed where Long Run meets Floyd's Fork, and soldiers sent to bury them were attacked the next day.

## Introduction

For years, the historical markers told a bigger story. They said that at least 60 people were killed near Long Run in September 1781 and that only a few escaped, and at least one published report repeated the claim. The truth was smaller, and no easier to bear. On September 13, 1781, where Long Run Creek meets Floyd's Fork along the Falls Trace, in what is now eastern Jefferson County, Kentucky, about 15 settlers fleeing their frontier station were killed in an ambush. The next day, the soldiers who came to bury them were attacked too.

## Leaving Painted Stone

The settlers came from Painted Stone Station. Squire Boone, the younger brother of Daniel Boone, had brought 13 families to a tract called Painted Stone in 1780 and built a station there, the first permanent settlement in what is now Shelby County. The frontier was at war. In April 1781 the fort was attacked and Boone was shot, a wound whose complications left his right arm an inch and a half shorter than his left. Then, in September, the settlers learned that a large war party under the Loyalist captain Alexander McKee was about to raid them. Most decided to abandon Painted Stone for the better-defended stations near Beargrass Creek. The injured Boone stayed behind with only a few others.

## Thirteen-Mile Tree

The move did not go smoothly. Some settlers hesitated for two days before setting out toward Linn's Station, and along the way the party lost part of its military guard. At a spot known as thirteen-mile tree, eight miles short of Linn's Station, the war party struck. About 15 settlers died there, people who had packed up their frontier lives hoping to reach safety. The following day, Colonel John Floyd led soldiers back to bury their remains, and they were attacked in turn. About seventeen of Floyd's soldiers were killed, though accounts differ; a Kentucky Historical Society marker counts 16 of 25.

## How the Raid Ended

The second fight changed the course of events. During that engagement a Wyandot chief was killed, and his death led the Native forces to disperse, bringing McKee's raid to an end. The encounter is counted among the battles of the western theater of the American Revolutionary War, a Kentucky frontier clash between American settlers and a war party led by a Loyalist officer. Today the Painted Stone Settlers, a living-history group, hold a reenactment every year at Red Orchard Park in Shelbyville, in the county where Painted Stone Station stood, keeping alive the memory of the families who set out for safety in the late summer of 1781.
