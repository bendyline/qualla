# Big Four Bridge

Built at the cost of dozens of lives, rebuilt on its own piers in 1929 and stranded mid-river for decades as the "Bridge That Goes Nowhere," Louisville's Big Four Bridge now carries walkers and cyclists across the Ohio to Jeffersonville.

**Location:** 38.2656, -85.7389

**Categories:** Bridges over the Ohio River, Bridges in Louisville, Kentucky, Cyclist bridges in the United States, Former railway bridges in the United States, Rail trails

## Introduction

For almost four decades, a bridge on the Louisville riverfront led nowhere at all. Its six steel trusses still marched across the Ohio River toward Jeffersonville, Indiana, but in 1974 and 1975 the approaches that once carried trains up onto the main spans were torn away and sold for scrap, leaving the whole structure stranded above the river. It earned a nickname: the "Bridge That Goes Nowhere." Then, in 2013, the Big Four Bridge found somewhere to go again, reopening as a path for people on foot and on bicycles.

## Dozens of Lives

The bridge was first conceived in Jeffersonville in 1885. Indiana chartered the Louisville and Jeffersonville Bridge Company in 1887 and Kentucky followed in 1888. The riverboat industry, a big part of Jeffersonville's economy, wanted the bridge built farther upstream from the Falls of the Ohio, but the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers approved the site despite vocal protests. Construction began on October 10, 1888, and it was brutal. Accounts of the toll vary, but the figure most often cited is 37 deaths. On the evening of January 9, 1890, a caisson meant to hold back the river flooded, drowning workers; early reports counted 14, though other accounts say 12. A few months later, on May 14, 1890, more men died when a wooden beam broke at another caisson, with accounts giving four to six. The worst day came on December 15, 1893, when a truss carrying about 41 workers fell into the Ohio River and 21 of them died. The bridge was completed in 1895 and took its name from the Cleveland, Cincinnati, Chicago and St. Louis Railway, known as the "Big Four Railroad."

## Sleeping Engineers and Snowstorms

Its railway years produced some strange stories. On February 19, 1904, a Baltimore and Ohio train crossed the Big Four Bridge entirely by mistake: engineer Dick Foreman had fallen asleep and gone the wrong way at Otisco, Indiana, and the conductor finally noticed the error halfway across the river. The train had to back up all the way to Otisco. Interurban cars began using the bridge on September 12, 1905, and on January 14, 1918, two of them collided on the span at 5:30 p.m. in a blinding snowstorm. Three passengers were killed and 20 others were injured. Rail traffic kept getting heavier, and in June 1928 contracts were finalized to build a bigger bridge.

## A New Bridge on Old Piers

The replacement, which opened on June 25, 1929, was built on the piers of the old bridge, a novel process that saved time because the old structure helped reinforce the new one as it went up. While the work went on, rail traffic was routed over the Kentucky & Indiana Terminal Bridge, and interurban riders got off at Sellersburg, Indiana, and finished the trip into Louisville by bus. The result is the bridge that stands today: six spans totaling 2,525 feet, with 53 feet of clearance. Three spans of 547 feet each are riveted Pennsylvania through trusses, with riveted Parker through trusses at either end, one to the north and two to the south, and the whole bridge originally carried a single track. Its approach columns shaped the city around it too. When construction began in 1961 on the bridge that became the John F. Kennedy Memorial Bridge, the growing Kennedy Interchange had to thread around them, producing several two-lane ramps instead of one stretch of highway and helping earn the tangle its nickname, Spaghetti Junction.

## The Bridge That Goes Nowhere

The end came through corporate mergers. The Big Four Railroad's parent, the New York Central, was merged into the Penn Central in 1968, and the last train crossed on March 10, 1968, with its traffic shifted to Louisville's Fourteenth Street Bridge. From July 26, 1974, to January 1975, the Detzel Construction Company of Cincinnati removed both approach spans, making the Big Four the first Louisville bridge to fall out of use. The orphaned trusses drew some unusual interest. In 1988 Oscar Arias, then President of Costa Rica, asked Louisville mayor Jerry Abramson about buying the bridge to dismantle and reassemble in his country, believing it would be cheaper than building a new one. The city did not actually own it, and nothing came of the idea. On May 7, 2008, the bridge even caught fire, shortly after noon, a quarter mile north of the Louisville end.

## A Walk Across the Ohio

Converting the bridge for walkers and cyclists had been proposed since the 1990s. In February 2011, Kentucky, Indiana and the City of Jeffersonville committed $22 million to finish the job: $12 million from Kentucky for a new deck and a connection to the spiral ramp in Louisville Waterfront Park, up to $8 million from Indiana and $2 million from Jeffersonville for a ramp on the Indiana side. After unsuccessful litigation to stop the project, pedestrians stepped onto the bridge from the Kentucky ramp on February 7, 2013, and the Jeffersonville ramp opened on May 20, 2014. Until then, the George Rogers Clark Memorial Bridge had been the only bridge open to walkers and cyclists between Louisville and its Indiana neighbors. On the Kentucky side, computer-controlled lights can bathe the bridge in different colors, and during Thunder Over Louisville it marks how close private boats may get to the fireworks.

## Flight Context

The Big Four Bridge crosses the Ohio River at about 38.2656°N, 85.7389°W, linking downtown Louisville's Waterfront Park with Jeffersonville, Indiana. From 1,500–2,500 feet the six-span steel truss stands out among the cluster of downtown bridges, near the Kennedy Interchange's "Spaghetti Junction" on the Kentucky bank. This is busy airspace: Bowman Field (KLOU) is about 4 nm southeast and Louisville Muhammad Ali International (KSDF) about 6 nm south, so coordinate with ATC. Clark Regional Airport at Jeffersonville (FAA identifier JVY; no ICAO code listed) is about 6 nm north. The river corridor gives excellent visual orientation in clear weather.
