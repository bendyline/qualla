# Old Louisville

Built on the glow of an 1880s exposition, abandoned by the wealthy and nearly lost to the wrecking ball, Old Louisville survives as one of America's largest Victorian neighborhoods, a district of brick mansions, stained glass and pedestrian courts.

**Location:** 38.2299, -85.7630

**Categories:** Neighborhoods in Louisville, Kentucky, Historic districts on the National Register of Historic Places in Kentucky, Victorian architecture in Kentucky, History of Louisville, Kentucky, Tourist attractions in Louisville, Kentucky

## Introduction

In 1883, 4,600 electric lamps blazed inside a vast exhibition hall in Louisville's new southern suburb. The Southern Exposition, opened by President Chester A. Arthur on August 1, was lit by what was billed as the largest installation yet of Thomas Edison's new incandescent bulbs, and nearly a million people are said to have come in its first year to see what the future looked like after dark. When the fair closed, the ground it had lit up became the heart of a new suburb of turrets, towers and stained glass. Today that suburb is called Old Louisville, one of the largest historic districts in the United States.

## Picnic Grounds and Plank Roads

For most of the city's first century, the land south of Broadway was not much more than farmland. It began as three military land grants issued in 1773 and passed among speculators. As late as 1845, a 50-acre tract called Jacob's Woods was still a popular picnic ground, and the Oakland Race Track drew crowds. Country estates appeared from the 1830s, a mulecar line reached down Fourth Street to Oak in 1865, and in 1868 the city annexed the area all the way to its House of Refuge, where the University of Louisville campus stands today. A year later, architect Gideon Shryock called it "a growing and beautiful suburban locality." Its principal road, the Central Plank Road, became Third Street, and the new district went by a plain, practical name: the Southern Extension.

## The Exposition's Afterglow

Courier-Journal editor Henry Watterson pushed for the Southern Exposition to "advance the material welfare of the producing classes of the South and West," and the city built a 600-by-900-foot exhibition hall for it. The show ran until 1887, and it was among the first large spaces lit by incandescence at a time when electric light was still a novelty. After it ended, the area between Oak and Hill streets developed rapidly into one of the city's most fashionable neighborhoods. According to historian Young E. Allison, 260 homes worth a combined $1.6 million went up between 1883 and 1886, mostly in the Queen Anne and turreted Richardsonian Romanesque styles. At the neighborhood's peak in the late nineteenth century, nearly everyone in Louisville's Society Directory had an address here, and the directory even listed the days when leading ladies received callers, which varied by street: Tuesdays on Fourth, Thursdays on Second.

## Courts Without Cars

On the Exposition's former grounds rose St. James Court, begun in 1890 as a haven for the upper class and fully occupied by 1905, a place later described as "the epitome of Victorian eclecticism." Between 1891 and the 1920s, builders laid out eleven walking courts, where houses face each other across a grassy median instead of a street, giving Old Louisville what is described as the largest collection of pedestrian-only streets of any U.S. neighborhood. Belgravia and Fountain Courts came first, in 1891. Red sandstone trim became so fashionable by the 1880s that the Courier-Journal remarked that few new residences were going up without it. The grandest house of all was Edwin Hite Ferguson's mansion, designed by the architectural firm Dodd and Cobb, whose William J. Dodd also worked on the Seelbach Hotel. It took from 1901 to 1905 to build and cost $100,000, about ten times as much as its Victorian neighbors, making it Louisville's most expensive home at the time. Since 1986 it has housed The Filson Historical Society.

## Emptied Mansions

The decline came gradually. Wealthy families moved on to newer suburbs such as Cherokee Triangle, or to estates east of the city in Anchorage and Glenview. Houses built to need several servants became impractical once factory wages made servants unaffordable for all but the richest, and cars and interurban lines meant the wealthy no longer had to live near their businesses. During the Great Depression many mansions became boarding houses, and the Ohio River flood of 1937 reportedly pushed more wealthy households to higher ground. Landlords chopped homes into apartments, and after World War II, when new suburbs drew the middle class away, rents fell sharply. Between 1965 and 1971, 639 buildings were razed for schools, university expansion and industry, and in the 1960s Interstate 65 cut a barrier between the neighborhood and Shelby Park to the east. Low-income residents displaced from downtown by urban renewal found homes in the converted apartments, even as much of the city came to regard the area as undesirable.

## A Name Worth Saving

The revival started with a newspaperman. In 1960, Courier-Journal writer J. Douglas Nunn began a public campaign comparing the area to Georgetown and Beacon Hill, and in 1961 he took leave from his job to start Restoration, Inc., which restored ten homes on Belgravia Court. That same year the name "Old Louisville" first appeared in print. Activists moved in, and in 1975 the area became a historic preservation district. Today it is billed as the largest preservation district featuring almost entirely Victorian architecture, mostly brick, and is often said to have the country's highest concentration of homes with stained glass windows. Historian Theodore M. Brown called it "the only nineteenth-century segment of the city that is mostly intact." The Kentucky Shakespeare Festival plays in Central Park each summer, and every October, on the first weekend, the St. James Court Art Show fills the old Exposition grounds with visitors once again.

## Flight Context

Old Louisville lies at approximately 38.230°N, 85.763°W in central Louisville, Kentucky, between downtown and the University of Louisville, bounded by Kentucky Street to the north, Cardinal Boulevard to the south, Interstate 65 to the east and the CSX rail line to the west. From 2,000–2,500 ft AGL (observe the Louisville Class C airspace), look for the dense tree canopy and grid of large brick homes between I-65 and the rail line, with the university campus immediately south. Louisville Muhammad Ali International (KSDF) is only about 7 km to the south-southeast, and Bowman Field (KLOU) about 9 km to the east. Expect haze and afternoon storms in summer; the Ohio River valley can hold morning fog.
