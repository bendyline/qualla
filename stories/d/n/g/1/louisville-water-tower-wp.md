# Louisville Water Tower

Dressed as a Roman temple and crowned with zinc statues, the Louisville Water Tower, the nation's oldest standing ornamental water tower, brought clean water to a city once called the "graveyard of the west."

## Introduction

In the 1830s and 1840s, Louisville carried a grim nickname: the "graveyard of the west." After the second cholera pandemic reached the United States in 1832, residents drawing from tainted private wells suffered cholera and typhoid at epidemic levels. The city's answer stands by the Ohio River east of downtown, and it looks nothing like plumbing. The Louisville Water Tower rises from a pumping station disguised as a Roman temple complex, ringed by statues, and Louisville Water bills it as the nation's oldest standing ornamental water tower, older than the more famous Chicago Water Tower.

## A Cure No One Understood

Nobody in early Louisville knew what the water was doing to them. The link between contaminated water and cholera was not discovered until 1854, by the English physician John Snow, and it was not accepted as fact until decades later. Fire did as much as disease to build the case. Several devastating blazes in the 1850s convinced Louisvillians of the project's importance. The Kentucky Legislature formed the Louisville Water Company on March 6, 1854, but private investors showed little interest. Only 55 shares sold, and a first attempt to win voter approval to buy shares failed. After a wide promotional campaign, voters approved the purchase of 5,500 shares in 1856 and another 2,200 in 1859, turning the company into an almost completely government-owned corporation.

## Beauty and Efficiency

The builders refused to let a utility look like one. Their inspiration came from the French architect Claude Nicolas Ledoux, who merged "architectural beauty with industrial efficiency." Like the Fairmount Water Works in Philadelphia, the station hid its industrial purpose inside the shape of a Roman temple complex, and even the reservoir's gatehouse on the riverfront evoked the castles along the Rhine. The tower was not just pretty, though. The water tower began operating on October 16, 1860, and in 24 hours the station could produce 12 million gallons of water, sending it through 26 miles of pipe. The payoff came years later. Thanks to the water project, Louisville was free of cholera during the epidemic of 1873.

## Gods, Seasons and a Tornado

Ten zinc statues stand above the first level's balustrade, each on a pedestal over a Corinthian column. The plans originally called for urns, but figures took their place, and the first set included Ceres, Diana and a girl in a bonnet. The statues include Mercury, Flora, Summer, Autumn and a Danaide, along with a figure the sources call an "Indian hunter," a Native American man in the style of the period, carrying a tomahawk and holding a dog on a leash, who may represent the element earth. On March 27, 1890, a tornado irreparably changed the tower. It destroyed the original wood-paneled shaft that protected the iron pipe, which was replaced in cast iron, and it wrecked most of the original statues. Louisville Water bought the current set of ten zinc statues afterward, took them down for restoration in October 2020 and hoisted them back onto the tower in February 2024.

## Retired, Restored, Reopened

In the following decades Louisville built a new pumping station and reservoirs in Crescent Hill, and the original tower stopped pumping around 1909 (sources put it between 1909 and 1912). Its architecture outlived its job. The water tower and its pumping station are together designated a National Historic Landmark for their architecture, and the tower is also recognized as a Historic Civil Engineering Landmark. The pumping station was renovated in 2010, and in 2014 the Louisville WaterWorks Museum opened on the premises, opening the grounds that helped end the city's years as a graveyard to visitors. Visiting is limited today: Louisville Water lists public visits on the second Sunday of each month, from noon to 5 p.m., and says it stopped taking paid event rentals in August 2025 to focus on education.
