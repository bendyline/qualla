# Belle of Louisville

Built in 1914 as the Idlewild, this Louisville steamboat has been a Memphis ferry, a barge pusher, a floating USO nightclub and a wandering excursion boat, and she has survived a sabotage sinking to reach her second century.

## Introduction

She has answered to three names. Built in 1914 as the Idlewild, renamed Avalon in 1948 and rechristened the Belle of Louisville after Jefferson County bought her in 1962, this steamboat has carried ferry passengers, cotton, oil barges, soldiers and generations of excursion riders. Today she is owned by the city of Louisville, Kentucky, and run by the nonprofit Belle of Louisville Riverboats, and during her annual operating season she ties up at the downtown wharf beside the Riverfront Plaza/Belvedere. Her operators claim the title of "most widely traveled river steamboat of her size in American history," and she is a National Historic Landmark that still leaves the wharf to carry passengers.

## Pittsburgh-Built, Memphis-Bound

The James Rees & Sons Company of Pittsburgh built the Idlewild in 1914 for the West Memphis Packet Company. Her first job was humble and practical: running as a passenger ferry between Memphis, Tennessee, and West Memphis, Arkansas, while hauling cargo such as cotton, lumber and grain. In 1931 she came to Louisville, where she carried pleasure-seekers between the Fontaine Ferry amusement park near downtown and Rose Island, a resort about 14 miles upriver. From 1934 through World War II she ran a regular excursion schedule. The war added stranger duties. She was reportedly outfitted with special equipment to push oil barges along the river, and she served as a floating USO nightclub for troops stationed at military bases along the Mississippi.

## A Captain's Wish

In 1947 the Idlewild was sold to J. Herod Gorsage of Peoria, Illinois, and the next year she was renamed Avalon, honoring the deathbed wish of her longtime captain, Master Ben Winters. Under that name she became a wanderer. The Avalon visited ports along the Mississippi, Missouri, St. Croix, Illinois, Kanawha, Ohio and Cumberland rivers, calling at places as far apart as Omaha, Stillwater, Montgomery and Nashville. A boat built to shuttle passengers between Memphis and West Memphis had become a traveler of many rivers.

## Louisville's Own

In 1962, Jefferson County Judge Marlow Cook bought the steamboat for $34,000, and the city rechristened her the Belle of Louisville. On April 30, 1963, she made her first cruise in a race against the steamboat Delta Queen. Her toughest moment came in August 1997, when she was partially sunk at her moorings. A city water line had been left connected to a fitting that led into her hull, and a former crew member was later convicted of sabotage. The boat's crew and members of the community moved quickly, and the Belle was rescued, repaired and returned to service. Her business offices, meanwhile, are aboard another vessel, the Mayor Andrew Broaddus.

## A Second Century

Old boats still have hard days. On October 17, 2009, while turning around about halfway through a cruise, the Belle struck a dock near Six Mile Island on the Ohio River; an official blamed the wind. Tugboats pulled her to safety. About a third of the paddle wheel's bucket planks were damaged and the jockey bar, the main steel arm across the aft end of the wheel, was bent, but the crew made the repairs without drydocking, replacing the planks from stock. On October 18, 2014, she turned 100. Louisville threw a five-day festival, The Belle's Big Birthday Bash, along parts of Waterfront Park, and eight other riverboats came to join her, from the Anson Northrup to the General Jackson. There were cruises, fireworks, concerts and hot air balloon glows, in what was said to be the largest riverboat gathering Louisville had seen in more than 100 years. She has even had a life in fiction: Rudy Rucker's 1988 novel Wetware features an artificially intelligent Belle of Louisville in a story set on the Moon and in Louisville.
