# Muhammad Ali Center

On Louisville's riverfront, the six-story Muhammad Ali Center, founded by Ali and his wife Lonnie, tells the champion's story through boxing memorabilia and the six principles he tried to live by.

## Introduction

It started with a stolen bicycle. When a 12-year-old Louisville boy named Cassius Marcellus Clay Jr. lost his bike to a thief, a police officer, Joe E. Martin, suggested he learn to box before seeking revenge. That advice set the boy on a road that would carry him to an Olympic gold medal, the heavyweight championship of the world and a new name: Muhammad Ali. Half a century later, Ali came home in a different way. In 2005 he and his wife, Lonnie Ali, founded the Muhammad Ali Center, a non-profit museum and cultural center in the city where it all began.

## Louisville's Son

Clay was born in Louisville on January 17, 1942. At the 1960 Summer Olympics in Rome he won the light heavyweight gold medal, and in 1964 he took the heavyweight title from Sonny Liston. That same year he formally changed his name to Muhammad Ali, rejecting his birth name as a "slave name." In 1967 he refused induction into the military and was stripped of his title. On June 28, 1971, the Supreme Court of the United States overturned his conviction in Clay v. United States by a unanimous 8–0 decision. In 1974 he beat George Foreman to regain the championship, and he held it as undisputed champion until 1978.

## Six Stories on the River

The Muhammad Ali Center opened on November 19, 2005, in the city's West Main District. The six-story building of roughly 93,000 to 97,000 square feet cost about $80 million (some reports say $82 million), and it comes with a plaza and a two-level amphitheater of 40,000 square feet. On April 4, 2013, a new pedestrian bridge opened from the center's plaza, a walkway 170 feet long and nine feet wide, clad in metal panels that echo the plaza's design, linking the center to the Belvedere, the Waterfront and other downtown attractions. In 2020, the center and the SEEK Museum in Russellville were added to the U.S. Civil Rights Trail.

## Inside the Ring

The exhibits have been reworked since the center opened, and its own list now includes a timeline of Ali's career, a collection of his famous fights, a six-principles exhibit and a recreation of his Deer Lake Training Camp where visitors test their boxing skills. Earlier accounts describe an orientation theater that introduced Ali's life, a mock boxing ring based on that camp, the Pennsylvania camp where Ali trained for every fight from 1972 until his career ended in 1981, a two-level pavilion inside a large elliptical room holding his boxing memorabilia and history, a projector that threw the film The Greatest onto a full-sized boxing ring, and viewing booths where visitors could call up clips of his greatest fights, with pre- and post-fight interviews. Ali's Presidential Medal of Freedom has also been displayed.

## What Are You Fighting For?

Beyond the boxing, the center is built around principles. Its exhibitions explore six core values that Ali strove to be guided by throughout his life: confidence, conviction, dedication, giving, respect and spirituality. Earlier accounts describe an interactive terminal program that invited visitors to explore their sense of self, of others and of purpose, and Generation Ali Story Booths where they were encouraged to record what they are fighting for. Those accounts also place two rotating art galleries on the third floor, the LeRoy Neiman Gallery and the Howard L. Bingham Gallery; the center's website still names the Bingham Gallery. Ali died on June 3, 2016, at age 74, and was laid to rest in his hometown at Cave Hill Cemetery on June 10. Louisville's main airport now carries his name as well.
