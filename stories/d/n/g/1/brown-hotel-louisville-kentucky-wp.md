# Brown Hotel (Louisville, Kentucky)

Built in ten months to rival the Seelbach, Louisville's Brown Hotel gave the world the Hot Brown sandwich, sheltered flood refugees while boats rowed through its lobby, served as school offices, and came back to life at Fourth and Broadway.

## Introduction

In 1937, someone rowed a boat straight through the lobby of the Brown Hotel. Floodwater had filled the first floor, and one Louisville resident later remembered the scene: "We were rowing down Broadway and there was The Brown Hotel. The doors were open and the place was filled with water so we just rowed our boat in one door, went through the lobby and rowed out another." The bell captain, Elmer H. Weck, is recorded to have caught a two-pound fish in that same lobby, and the fish was mounted and hung there for years afterward. The 16-story hotel at the corner of Fourth and Broadway has weathered floods, depression, closure and rebirth, and it has a story for nearly every one of them.

## Ten Months to Rival the Seelbach

The Brown was born of competition. James Graham Brown, a local entrepreneur, wanted to take on The Seelbach Hotel just a few blocks down the street, so he funded and owned a $4 million rival designed by Preston J. Bradshaw. It opened in 1923, only ten months after construction began, with 600 rooms, ballrooms, shops, meeting rooms and restaurants. The hotel quickly became central to downtown Louisville's growing economy and to the social lives of its residents. The guests could be remarkable. David Lloyd George, former Prime Minister of the United Kingdom, was the first person to sign the guest register, and Queen Marie of Romania also stayed. The soprano Lily Pons was allowed to bring her pet lion, which roamed free in her room. And a young elevator operator named Victor Mature lost his job after abandoning his post, leaving a simple note, to go dance with a girl at one of the hotel's rooftop parties. He went on to become a famous actor.

## The Chef's Invention

In 1926, the hotel chef Fred K. Schmidt put something new on the menu. The Hot Brown was an open-faced turkey sandwich with bacon under a delicate Mornay sauce, and it became popular with locals and visitors alike. The dish has since carried the hotel's name well beyond Louisville, turning up in programs about regional cuisine such as the 2002 PBS documentary Sandwiches That You Will Like and a 2011 episode of the Travel Channel's Man v. Food. Schmidt's creation arrived just three years after the hotel opened, and it still bears the hotel's name.

## Hard Times and High Water

The early 1930s nearly finished the Brown. Prohibition and the Great Depression arrived together, the hotel defaulted on its loan, and the bank threatened foreclosure. Employees were asked to work for stretches without pay, and the Brown stayed open only because they were willing to do it. Then came the 1937 Flood. Water filled the first floor and the electricity was gone, yet the hotel kept its doors open, and many people whose homes were submerged found shelter inside. High above the water, WAVE radio broadcast from the 15th floor, and it was reportedly the only radio station to keep operating from its own facilities during the flood.

## Derby Week, and a Door Held Shut

Recovery came with the buildup to World War II. Thousands of soldiers passing through from Fort Knox stayed in the area, and the hotel was frequently full. The week of the Kentucky Derby was, and still is, the Brown's most successful week of the year, and its guests over the years have reportedly included the Duke of Windsor, Harry Truman, Elizabeth Taylor, Joan Crawford, Gene Autry, Eva Marie Saint and Muhammad Ali. Not everyone connected to the Derby was made welcome. In 1961, James Winkfield, a Black jockey who had won the Kentucky Derby twice, came to a Sports Illustrated reception at the hotel and was told he could not enter by the front door; he was admitted only after the magazine explained that he was an invited guest. The race that filled the Brown every year had been won twice by a man who was told, at its door, that he could not come in that way.

## Classrooms Upstairs, Then a Second Life

As downtown Louisville declined in the 1960s and 1970s, so did the Brown. A $1.5 million modernization in 1965, shared with another downtown hotel, did not bring the guests back. James Graham Brown died on March 20, 1969, and in 1971, after financial shortfalls, the hotel closed. The building was sold to the Louisville Public Schools and became the city school system's headquarters, and after the 1975 merger with the county schools it housed other administrative offices as the Brown Education Center. The schools took a toll on the building, but its grand public rooms survived. In the 1980s the Broadway Group acquired the Brown and began restoring it in 1983, reopening it as a Hilton. The Camberley Hotel Company bought it in 1993 and continued the work. During the restoration the dividing wall in every other room was removed to double each guest room's size, and the 600 rooms became roughly 294. In 2006 the hotel passed to 1859 Historic Hotels of Galveston, Texas. It is listed on the National Register of Historic Places and belongs to Historic Hotels of America, and in 2005 it appeared in numerous scenes of Cameron Crowe's film Elizabethtown.
