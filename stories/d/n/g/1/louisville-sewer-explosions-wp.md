# Louisville sewer explosions

Before dawn on Friday, February 13, 1981, hexane vapor released from a soybean plant ignited beneath Louisville, destroying more than 13 miles of sewer lines, wrecking streets above them and hurling manhole covers into homes.

## Introduction

It was Friday the 13th, and it was not yet dawn. At about 5:16 a.m. on February 13, 1981, a car was driving near the corner of 12th and Hill Streets in Louisville when a spark from it ignited fumes rising from the street and the sewers below. What followed was a cascading series of explosions that ripped through the southern part of Old Louisville, near the University of Louisville. Manhole covers flew like cannonballs, pavement buckled into the air, and by the time it was over, more than 13 miles of sewer lines in the center of the city had been destroyed, along with stretches of the streets above them.

## Solvent in the Sewers

The fuel came from a plant on Floyd Street. The site had processed cottonseed or soybeans for many decades (accounts date its operations to 1900 or to 1919), and by 1981 it was a soybean processing plant owned by Ralston-Purina, which used hexane as a solvent to pull oil from the beans. A containment system was designed to recycle used hexane back into the process, but that night it was not working, and a large quantity of the solvent poured into the sewers. The U.S. Government Accountability Office and the American Society of Civil Engineers later estimated the spill at thousands of gallons, roughly 10,000 liters or more, though the company disputed the amount. The company reported some leakage to the city the day before, and by one account called an inspector at about 1:30 a.m. He visited, checked for vapors on some nearby streets, and did not notice a serious problem.

## Iron from the Sky

Hours later, the streets erupted. One manhole cover smashed completely through the ceiling and floor of a third-floor apartment on Second Street. Another came down in a house at the corner of 9th and Hill Streets, narrowly missing a child. Sections of pavement were left standing on end beside gaping holes as much as 38 feet deep. Underground, the damage was even more sweeping: the destroyed lines included the entire two-mile main trunk sewer, a pipe between 7.5 and 12 feet across. Severed water and gas lines left residents without service for weeks. Astonishingly, no one was killed. Four people were injured, among them the women in the car whose spark set it off, and their injuries were not serious.

## An Admission, a Fine and a Lesson

Ralston-Purina admitted releasing hexane into the sewers but at first did not accept responsibility for the blasts. Eventually the company pleaded guilty to four counts of violating federal environmental laws and paid the maximum possible fine, $62,500. It rebuilt the facility in 1983 using more than $2 million in city industrial bonds, then sold it in 1984. The disaster outlived the plant's ownership as a warning. The United States Environmental Protection Agency has cited it as an example of the dangers of discharging hazardous waste into sewer systems, and the American Society of Civil Engineers and the Government Accountability Office have pointed to it in discussions of wastewater system security. Articles about it have appeared in the journals Environmental Geology and the Journal of the American Oil Chemists' Society.

## The Checkerboard Silos

For years the plant itself remained a landmark. Drivers heading south on I-65 passed its 22 large silos, each topped with a checkerboard logo. After the plant changed hands, the University of Louisville eventually bought the site, and for a while the silos became a welcome sign for the school, repainted with the university's name spelled out across them. In 2014 they were demolished, taking down a familiar reminder of the morning Louisville's streets blew apart.
