# Louisville and Portland Canal

Cut through solid rock to carry boats around the Falls of the Ohio, the only natural barrier between Pittsburgh and the Gulf, the Louisville and Portland Canal made its private owners rich, was bought out piece by piece by the federal government, and lives on as the McAlpine Locks and Dam.

**Location:** 38.2717, -85.7794

**Categories:** Canals in Kentucky, Ohio River, History of Louisville, Kentucky, Transportation in Louisville, Kentucky, Historic Civil Engineering Landmarks

## Introduction

Between Pittsburgh and the Gulf of Mexico, a boat on the Ohio River met only one natural obstruction: the Falls of the Ohio at Louisville. For most of the year, cargo had to come off the boats, travel around the rapids over land and be loaded again below them. Only during a few weeks of high water each spring could boats run the falls, which meant a glut of shipments every year and wild swings in prices up and down the river. Whole towns lived off that inconvenience. The Louisville and Portland Canal, 1.9 miles long, was built to end it.

## Towns Built on a Detour

Some of Kentucky's earliest cities, Louisville, Portland and Shippingport, grew out of the need to carry cargo around the rapids. Locals liked the business; merchants upriver in Pittsburgh and Cincinnati disliked the expense and hassle. The trustees of the Town of Louisville petitioned the Virginia General Assembly for the right to build a canal at their very first meeting, on February 7, 1781, but decades of false starts followed. Kentucky and Indiana both chartered canal companies in 1805, and nothing came of either. Treasury Secretary Albert Gallatin suggested national funding in 1808, and bills passed the Senate in 1810 and 1811 only to die in the House. Indiana's second attempt, chartered in 1818, ended with a failed dam and the Panic of 1819, amid rumors that the dam had been sabotaged, since a canal threatened much of Louisville's economy, from storage and shipping to hotels and entertainment.

## Digging Through Rock

In 1825 Kentucky chartered the private Louisville and Portland Canal Company, with James Guthrie as president, a toll of 20 cents per ton and the right to operate the canal in perpetuity. Shares cost $100, but buyers needed only $10 down, and in May 1826 Congress voted to purchase 1,000 shares as well. Construction began that year, and the planners soon learned the canal would have to be dug through solid rock. Costs climbed, some local investors stopped paying on their shares, and Congress put in another $133,500 in 1829. When a third round of federal funding came up, the newly elected President Andrew Jackson vetoed it, denouncing federal money for private corporations, and the company had to borrow $154,000 in 1830. That December the first vessel, the Uncas, passed through the partially completed locks. By one account the canal was fully finished in 1833, six years behind schedule, though the American Society of Civil Engineers' landmark page dates its completion to 1830, and it is described as the first major improvement completed on a major river of the United States.

## Too Small, and Very Profitable

At roughly 50 to 64 feet wide (sources differ), the canal was huge by the standards of its day, but steamboats kept growing, and it was nearly obsolete soon after it opened. The company raised its toll to 40 cents a ton in 1834 and 60 cents in 1837, and traffic rose anyway, from 170,000 tons in 1834 to 300,000 in 1839. Toll receipts jumped from $12,750.77 in 1831 to $145,424.69 in 1837. Rather than enlarge the canal or pay dividends, management used the profits to buy out private shareholders at a premium, steadily increasing the government's stake until the buyout was complete in 1855. By the 1850s, around 40 percent of the steamboats on the Ohio were too large for the canal. In the Civil War, at least one Confederate advocated destroying the works so that "future travelers would hardly know where it was," but Union control of Kentucky made the threat moot.

## Into the Engineers' Hands

A $865,000 improvement plan approved in 1860 was shelved almost at once by the war, and loans left the company $1.6 million in debt by 1866. The next year the Army Corps of Engineers was finally allowed to take over improvements, and two new locks, each 390 feet long and 90 feet wide, opened in February 1872. In May 1874 Congress gave the Corps full control. By 1877, despite the rise of railroads, traffic had tripled from any earlier level, mostly heavy, low-value cargo such as coal, salt and iron ore, and in 1880 Congress abolished the tolls entirely. A new lock followed in 1921, and after major modernization widened the canal to 500 feet, the works were renamed the McAlpine Locks and Dam (in 1960 by some accounts and 1962 by others).

## Portland and Shippingport

Louisville boomed from the canal, but its old partners did not. Portland incorporated separately in 1834, then in 1837 accepted annexation to Louisville in a compromise tied to canal widening and a planned rail link to its wharf on the Lexington and Ohio Railroad. When that railroad went bankrupt in 1840 after reaching only Louisville, the community broke away again from 1842 to 1852 before accepting reannexation. Much of it was destroyed by or razed after the floods of 1937 and 1945. Shippingport, cut off as an island by the canal, declined slowly until the federal government condemned its remaining private property in 1958 and the last families, some of whom had lived there for more than a century, had to leave. The old name endures. People still call the channel the Portland Canal, running between the Kentucky bank and Shippingport Island from about 10th Street down to the locks at 27th Street.

## Flight Context

The canal runs along the Kentucky bank of the Ohio River at about 38.2717°N, 85.7794°W in western Louisville, between the riverbank and Shippingport Island, from roughly 10th Street down to the McAlpine Locks and Dam at 27th Street. From 1,500–2,500 feet, the straight channel, the lock chambers and the Falls of the Ohio beside them are easy to pick out. This is busy airspace: Louisville Muhammad Ali International (KSDF) and Bowman Field (KLOU) are each about 6 nm away, and Clark Regional at Jeffersonville, Indiana (FAA identifier JVY; no ICAO code listed), is about 6 nm north-northeast across the river; coordinate with ATC. Best in clear daytime conditions.
