# Appalachian Trail

Nearly 2,200 miles of white-blazed footpath from Georgia to Maine, dreamed up by a grieving forester in 1921 and now walked by three million people a year.

**Location:** 38.0000, -78.0000

**Categories:** trail, outdoors, appalachia, national-scenic-trail

## Introduction

Benton MacKaye was a forester whose wife had just drowned herself in the East River. He spent the months after her death in 1921 thinking about the Appalachian ridgeline that runs from Georgia to Maine, and writing a planning paper titled An Appalachian Trail: A Project in Regional Planning. He imagined not a hiking trail exactly but a string of farms and study camps for tired city-dwellers - hiking was incidental to his actual vision. A newspaper editor at the New York Evening Post in 1922 reduced the idea to a headline that stuck: A Great Trail from Maine to Georgia. What MacKaye started in grief, others would finish in obsession. The Appalachian Trail is now the world's longest hiking-only footpath, almost 2,200 miles long, passing through 14 states, and walked in some piece by more than three million people every year.

## How a Footpath Got Built

MacKaye's original sketch attracted volunteers in waves. The first section opened in October 1923, from Bear Mountain west through New York's Harriman State Park to Arden. A 1925 conference in Washington founded the Appalachian Trail Conference - now the Appalachian Trail Conservancy - to coordinate the work. After MacKaye's friend Arthur Perkins died in 1932, a Maine lawyer named Myron Avery took over the project, and pushed it with the temperament of a man who finished things. Avery clashed with MacKaye over a road planned through Shenandoah National Park - MacKaye wanted to fight the road; Avery wanted to reroute the trail - and MacKaye left the organization. Avery walked the trail end to end himself in sections, the first person to do so. In August 1937, the final stretch was completed at Sugarloaf Mountain in Maine, and the volunteers shifted from cutting tread to defending what they had built.

## What 14 States Feel Like Underfoot

The trail crosses 14 states, but the experience is less a single corridor than a sequence of distinct landscapes. Georgia delivers 75 miles of hardwood ridgeline from Springer Mountain northward. North Carolina and Tennessee share the Smokies, climbing to the trail's highest point at 6,643 feet on Clingmans Dome (now spelled Kuwohi following its 2024 renaming). Virginia hosts a quarter of the entire trail - 550 miles - including the famous Roan Highlands grass balds and the Shenandoah ridges. Maryland gives 41 quick miles along South Mountain. Pennsylvania earned the nickname Rocksylvania for its 230 miles of sharp-edged stones that hikers say is where boots go to die. New Jersey and New York run gentler and lower; Connecticut and Massachusetts climb into the Taconic and Berkshire highlands. Vermont, New Hampshire, and Maine deliver the trail's hardest miles - the Whites' alpine ridges above treeline, the Mahoosuc Notch boulder mile that hikers call the hardest mile on the trail, and Maine's 100-mile wilderness, ending at Mount Katahdin's Baxter Peak.

## Thru-Hikers and Trail Magic

Of the roughly 3,000 to 4,000 hopefuls who set out each year to walk the whole trail in a single season, the Appalachian Trail Conservancy estimates about a quarter finish. Earl Shaffer, a Pennsylvania veteran of World War II, claimed the first thru-hike in 1948 - later challenged for shortcuts but never quite disproved - and walked it again as the first southbound thru-hiker. Emma Gatewood, a 67-year-old Ohio grandmother who told her family she was going for a walk, became the first solo woman to thru-hike, taking 146 days northbound in 1955. She did it again two years later. Hikers earn trail names - Gatewood was Grandma Gatewood - and sign shelter logbooks with them. Strangers along the route leave coolers of soda or cooked meals at road crossings, a tradition called trail magic. Most years the Conservancy registers about a thousand successful completions, including a mix of thru-hikers and section-hikers.

## The Trail in West Virginia

The Appalachian Trail crosses only four miles of West Virginia, almost all of them concentrated in and around Harpers Ferry - the only state where the trail does not climb a single significant ridge. The town is the headquarters of the Appalachian Trail Conservancy, and many hikers consider it the psychological midpoint of the journey, where they stop to be photographed for the Conservancy's hiker album. The actual midpoint sits 75 miles further north in southern Pennsylvania. A CSX freight derailment in December 2019 damaged the pedestrian bridge across the Potomac, severing the trail between West Virginia and Maryland; the crossing reopened in July 2020. For long stretches further south, the trail also marks part of the West Virginia-Virginia state line.

## Speed, Age, and the Limits of a Footpath

The trail is not a race, but it has become one for some. Tara Dower of North Carolina set the supported fastest known time southbound in September 2024 - 40 days, 18 hours, 6 minutes. Belgian dentist Karel Sabbe held the northbound record at 41 days. Self-supported, Jeff Garmire took 45 days in September 2025; Heather Anish Anderson held the women's self-supported record at just over 54 days. M.J. Nimblewill Nomad Eberheart became the oldest known thru-hiker at age 83 in November 2021. Juniper Netteburg, accompanied by family, is believed to be the youngest at age 4. The trail is relatively safe but not perfectly so: a small number of hikers have died from falls, exposure, or violence in its history, and an even smaller number have been victims of homicide on or near the trail. The trail's overwhelming risk, statistically, is still a bad ankle on Rocksylvania.

## Aviator's Notes

The Appalachian Trail's geographic anchors for aviation: southern terminus at Springer Mountain, Georgia (34.62 N, 84.19 W); psychological midpoint at Harpers Ferry, West Virginia (39.32 N, 77.74 W); northern terminus at Baxter Peak on Mount Katahdin, Maine (45.90 N, 68.92 W). From altitude the trail itself is largely invisible - a single track in the canopy - but the ridgelines it follows are reliable landmarks. The Allegheny ridges in Virginia and West Virginia, the Whites in New Hampshire, and Katahdin's isolated mass in north-central Maine all serve as visual anchors. Watch for icing over the high terrain in cold months and afternoon thunderstorms along the ridges in summer.

## Flight Context

The trail extends from 34.62 N (Springer Mountain, Georgia) to 45.90 N (Mount Katahdin, Maine), crossing 14 states along the Appalachian ridgeline. Notable elevations: Kuwohi (Clingmans Dome) at 6,643 feet, Mount Washington at 6,288 feet, Katahdin's Baxter Peak at 5,267 feet. The trail's eastern edge runs near major hubs - Atlanta, Washington/Dulles, Boston, Bangor. Recommended viewing altitude for ridgeline appreciation: 8,500-12,500 feet. Mountain weather varies dramatically; the Presidential Range in particular has some of the most severe weather in the lower 48.
