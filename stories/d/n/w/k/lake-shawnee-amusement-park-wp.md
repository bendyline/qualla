# Lake Shawnee Amusement Park

Built in 1926 on land with a long and painful past, the Lake Shawnee Amusement Park near Princeton, West Virginia, entertained coal-mining families for decades before closing for good in 1988, leaving behind a reputation for 'cursed land' and an archaeological record far older than the rides.

## Introduction

In the late 1980s or early 1990s (accounts differ), work crews on the grounds of a shuttered amusement park beside Lake Shawnee, near Princeton, West Virginia, began turning up Native American artifacts, some dating from before European settlers arrived. Archaeologists, including teams from Marshall University and Concord University, worked at the site over several years. Beneath the old playground of Mercer County's coal-mining families, they found graves. The discovery added another layer to a place already known for tragedy, and for the ghost stories that grew around it. Lake Shawnee's rides went quiet in 1988, but the land they stood on has been remembered and retold for far longer.

## Frontier Grief, 1783

The earliest recorded tragedy here predates the park by nearly a century and a half. In 1783, three children of landowner Mitchell Clay were killed in an attack by a raiding party that the park's own history attributes to the Shawnee. A daughter and a son died on the land, and another son was captured, taken to Chillicothe and killed there. What Clay did next depends on who is telling the story. By one account, he and other landowners went after the raiding party and killed several of its members. Other accounts say he did not seek revenge at all; wanting only to give his children a proper burial, he made an agreement with the Shawnee chief and the dispute ended there.

## A Summer Place for Miners' Families

In 1926 an entrepreneur named Conley Trigg Snidow Sr. bought the land and began building an amusement park for the people of Mercer County. It became a local favorite, especially among the families of coal miners who lived in the area. There was a Ferris wheel and a swing ride, a swimming pool, a race track, concession stands, a dance hall and cabins for anyone who wanted to stay the night. The swing ride, a simple pleasure in those early years, would later figure in the park's saddest chapter. For families whose breadwinners worked underground, a lakeside park with rides and a pool meant a day out in the open air.

## Losses and Closings

The park's history also holds real grief. Two people drowned there during Snidow's ownership, and a young girl died at the swing ride, in 1966 according to Wikipedia; WV Living says a delivery truck backed into her path. Wikipedia and one park history say the park closed in 1967 after failing a health inspection, though other accounts date the closing to 1966. It stayed dark for years. In 1985 a former employee bought the land and reopened the park, in the summer of 1987 by Wikipedia's account, but it closed again in 1988, this time for good; Wikipedia blames rising insurance rates. The new owner looked for other ways to use the property, including fishing tournaments and off-road motorsports, and it was during this period that his crews began finding the artifacts that brought the archaeologists.

## What the Ground Held

The archaeologists spent several years uncovering artifacts and graves, and reports count thirteen Native American burials on the property, many of them children. Their presence changes how the whole site reads. The violence of 1783 was only one chapter in a much longer human story here. Some of the artifacts found on the property date from before European settlers arrived, and the graves show that Native people were laid to rest on this ground. The site's best-known tales center on a settler family and on the park's own accidents. The graves are a reminder that the people who belong to Lake Shawnee's history also include Native communities whose presence here predates European settlement, and whose resting place deserves the same respect as any other.

## The Making of a Legend

Over time, the park's real losses fed urban legends that blamed its accidental deaths on "cursed land." Shortly after the excavations, the owner began offering guided paranormal tours during Halloween week, and television followed. The abandoned park appeared on Scariest Places on Earth on Fox Family in 2002, on Discovery Channel's Ghost Lab in 2010, on History Channel's The UnXplained in 2019 and on Portals to Hell in 2021, among other programs. Whatever one makes of the ghost stories, the documented history needs no embellishment. Two of Mitchell Clay's children died on this land in 1783, a young girl died here, and the ground held the remains of Native people, thirteen by most counts. That is reason enough to treat these grounds with care; the park's own site says visits are by appointment only.
