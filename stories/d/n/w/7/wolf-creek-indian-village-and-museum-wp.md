# Wolf Creek Indian Village and Museum

Rescued in a hurried 1970 dig ahead of Interstate 77, a Native American village from around 1480-1520 has been rebuilt near its original site in Bland County, Virginia, in the museum's words "pole for pole."

**Location:** 37.1807, -81.1373

**Categories:** Museums in Bland County, Virginia, Native American museums in Virginia, Archaeological sites in Virginia, Open-air museums

## Introduction

Some of the digging reportedly happened after dark, by the light of car headlights turned toward the soil. In 1970, crews building Interstate 77 near Bastian, Virginia, uncovered traces of a Native American village beside Wolf Creek, and the archaeologists had very little time. The creek was going to be diverted and the site destroyed by the highway. What they recorded in that rush became the blueprint for the Wolf Creek Indian Village and Museum, a life-sized reconstruction of a community that stood here roughly between 1480 and 1520.

## Racing the Road Graders

Construction halted while a rescue excavation and field survey took place. In May 1970, the archaeologist Howard MacCord led the dig, and the interstate work stopped only very briefly to allow it. The haste shaped the methods. Road graders scraped away the upper layers of dirt, and work continued into the evenings with car headlights aimed at the areas being examined. The site became the first officially state-recognized archaeological site in Bland County, numbered 44BD1, and it was named for Brown Johnston, who owned the farm where it lay. MacCord published his account of the Brown Johnston Site in the Quarterly Bulletin of the Archeological Society of Virginia in June 1971.

## What the Ground Remembered

The excavation revealed the remains of eleven circular buildings, or wigwams, along with storage huts and fire pits. Post holes, stains and molds in the soil traced the outline of a palisade around the village. The dig also recorded the remains of eleven people. Their graves held people of various ages and genders, some buried inside the palisade and some outside it. Carbon dating placed the site somewhere between 1480 and 1520. Who these villagers were remains unknown. They may have been Cherokee, or members of one of the Siouan-speaking peoples who frequented the area, but the evidence cannot say for certain. What survives is the pattern of their lives: where they built, where they cooked, where they stored food and where they laid their dead to rest.

## Pole for Pole

In 1992, local leaders including George Schaeffer and the Bland County Historical Society began work on the project, Virginia Tech's Community Design Assistance Center reportedly prepared a schematic design proposal for a Center for Eastern American Indian Culture in Bland County, and in 1996 the reconstruction, built near the original site, opened to the public. Working from the archaeological map and survey, the builders aimed for as authentic a result as possible; in the museum's own words, "Using the archaeology map, we have pole for pole and feature for feature recreated the village." The graves are accurately positioned and marked, each with a description of the burial position and of any objects placed with the person. Operated by the County of Bland since 2014, the village uses guides and living-history interpreters to show daily life, and guided tours cover homes and families, pottery and basket making, arrowhead and tool making, food and cooking, and hunting and hide tanning.

## Flight Context

The Wolf Creek Indian Village and Museum is at 37.1807 N, 81.1373 W near Bastian in Bland County, southwestern Virginia, beside Wolf Creek and Interstate 77. From 3,500-5,000 ft MSL, follow I-77 through the narrow Wolf Creek valley between long, parallel forested ridges; the village lies close to the highway corridor. Nearby airports: Mercer County Airport in Bluefield, West Virginia (KBLF), about 14 km north-northwest; Mountain Empire Airport (KMKJ), serving Marion and Wytheville, about 37 km south-southwest; and New River Valley Airport near Dublin (KPSK), about 41 km east. Ridges rise steeply on both sides of the valley, and mountain winds can bring turbulence.
