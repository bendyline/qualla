# Bramwell, West Virginia

Tucked into a horseshoe bend of the Bluestone River, the coal town of Bramwell once claimed more millionaires per capita than anywhere in America, and today fewer than three hundred people live among the mansions they left behind.

**Location:** 37.3256, -81.3125

**Categories:** Coal towns in West Virginia, Towns in Mercer County, West Virginia, West Virginia populated places on the Bluestone River, Historic districts, Populated places established in 1888

## Introduction

According to local historians, the janitor of the Bank of Bramwell, a man named Henry Wade, regularly pushed leather bags full of money in a wheelbarrow down the brick-paved streets to the train depot. It was that kind of town. In the late 1800s, Bramwell, West Virginia, gained a reputation for having the largest number of millionaires per capita in the United States, and the wealthy coal operators who made those fortunes built numerous large homes in town. Today fewer than three hundred people live in town, according to the 2020 census, and many of the houses are still standing.

## A Name for a Little Town

The Bluestone River loops around the site in a horseshoe, and early in the 1800s the place was known simply as Horse Shoe Bend, according to the town's history site. Its later name came from J. H. Bramwell, a New York civil engineer who arrived in 1883 and became the first postmaster. The post office needed a legal name, and he is remembered for supplying one with a rhyme: "Every little baby has a name, and this little town must have the same. I therefore name it Bramwell." Coal made the rest possible. Through the 1880s the region grew rapidly as a mining center, with several coal camps operating nearby, and Bramwell was officially established in 1888. Its namesake went on to become the first president of the Bank of Bramwell when it was incorporated in 1889.

## Millionaires on the Bluestone

The wealth came from the Pocahontas coalfield, and it flowed through Bramwell. Local historians say the Norfolk & Western once had 14 trains a day stopping in town, and that before 1900 Bramwell already had electric streetlights, a water company, telephone service and a weekly newspaper. The Bank of Bramwell was the financial center of southern West Virginia and was thought to be the wealthiest bank per capita in the country. The town's history site says that at one point in the early twentieth century, as many as thirteen millionaires lived in the town at once; other accounts give higher counts. Their fortunes did not last. The Depression brought an end to the Bramwell bank in 1933, along with the wealth of many of its millionaires.

## The Cold Morning of the Fire

On January 7, 1910, a cold wave and high winds swept in after an unusually warm day. That morning, fire broke out in Smith & O'Connor's poolroom, and the wind drove the flames into the buildings along Main Street. Coal companies along the railroad sent miners to fight it. At the telephone office, operator Susie Young stayed at her switchboard calling for help and was later named "the heroine of the fire." One eyewitness, then a 13-year-old schoolgirl, later recalled hearing that it was so cold the water froze as it was sprayed on the burning buildings. By the time the fire was out, twenty-one buildings had burned to the ground. Mayor J. A. Waddell estimated the loss at $200,000, and special trains carried sightseers up from Bluefield to see the damage. The town rebuilt. The big Bluestone Inn at the corner of Main and Bloch streets was repaired, and Edward Cooper replaced his damaged Victorian house with a copper-roofed Queen Anne mansion that, the town's history site says, has always stayed in the Cooper family.

## What the Coal Barons Left

The millionaires are gone, but their town kept its buildings. In 1983 the Bramwell Historic District was listed on the National Register of Historic Places, and many of its houses date from the early twentieth century. The Coal Heritage Trail Interpretive Center downtown has exhibits on the region's coal mining history and on Bramwell's coal operators and their families. The town covers barely more than half a square mile along the river, just east of the Virginia line, with U.S. Route 52 passing through its eastern part toward Bluefield to the south and Northfork to the northwest. Pinnacle Rock State Park lies just to the southeast. Bramwell also sits only 0.3 miles from an access point to the Pocahontas Trail system, part of the Hatfield-McCoy Trails, more than 700 miles of primitive roads that now draw riders on dirt bikes and ATVs into the old coal country.

## Flight Context

Bramwell lies at 37.3256°N, 81.3125°W in a tight horseshoe bend of the Bluestone River in Mercer County, West Virginia, about 6 miles northwest of Bluefield and just east of the Virginia state line. The town sits at roughly 2,300 feet above sea level among steep mountains, so plan on at least 4,500–5,000 ft MSL for a safe sightseeing pass; the river's horseshoe loop, with the town's two old brick main streets crossing inside it, is the best visual cue, with U.S. Route 52 running past the east side. Mercer County Airport (KBLF), field elevation 2,857 ft, is only about 5 nm east-southeast. Raleigh County Memorial Airport (KBKW) at Beckley is about 29 nm north-northeast. Mountain winds and low cloud can build quickly here, so check ceilings carefully before descending toward the river valleys.
