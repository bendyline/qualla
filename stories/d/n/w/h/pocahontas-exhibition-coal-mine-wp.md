# Pocahontas Exhibition Coal Mine

Opened in 1882 as the first mine in the Pocahontas Coalfield, the 'Baby Mine' fueled steel mills and the U.S. Navy before becoming, in 1938, what is billed as the country's first exhibition coal mine, one visitors could once drive straight through.

## Introduction

For years, visitors to Pocahontas, Virginia, could do something no other coal mine offered: steer their cars into the mountain. They entered through the fan opening, rolled through the old workings and came out through the original entry. The drive-through ended in 1970, when exhaust fumes were found to be damaging the mine's roof, but the mine itself still welcomes visitors on guided underground walking tours, in season. Pocahontas Mine No. 1, also known as the Baby Mine, was the first mine in the Pocahontas Coalfield when it opened in 1882, and in 1938 it became what is billed as the first exhibition coal mine in the United States.

## Tents in Powell's Bottom

Before there was a town, there was an outcrop. A local blacksmith named Jordan Nelson used coal from a large outcrop on his property and sold it by the bushel to anyone who came to buy. In 1881 the Southwest Virginia Improvement Company, financed by the same Philadelphia firm behind the Norfolk and Western Railroad, set out to build a mining town from scratch. In January 1882, two hundred Hungarian, Swedish and German immigrants recruited at Castle Garden in New York arrived and, alongside workers from Virginia, North Carolina and West Virginia, lived in tents among the cleared laurel thickets of a place called Powell's Bottom. That June the post office opened and Powell's Bottom became Pocahontas, the first company mining town in Virginia. The railroad reached it in March 1883, and the first carload of coal left on March 12. The whole two-state coal region would take its name from this small town.

## Smokeless Fuel

What came out of the Baby Mine was prized. Pocahontas coal was especially suitable for coking, and for years coke was produced right on site in beehive ovens, then shipped by rail to steel mills; coke and coal also rode the rails to Norfolk for transfer to eastern ports and overseas. The fuel played a significant role in the industrial development of the United States. It mattered at sea, too. Pocahontas coal fueled the U.S. Navy, and Wikipedia says this mine supplied a majority of the Navy's coal through both world wars; its smokeless quality let ships go undetected at a distance, giving the Navy an advantage over its enemies. The seam that made it possible is on view today: the exhibition mine shows off a coal seam 13 feet tall, part of the Pocahontas No. 3 coal this mine was the first to extract.

## Side by Side

Prosperity here came at a terrible cost. In 1884, just two years after the first miners arrived, an explosion on March 13 killed at least 114 miners. They were buried side by side in the Pocahontas Cemetery, and a memorial is held for them each year, keeping them part of the story of the town they helped build. The immigrant families who worked these seams also left gentler marks on the town. Hungarian immigrants who came for the mines built St. Elizabeth's Roman Catholic Church, whose ceiling and walls carry ten life-sized murals, and the parish has held an annual Hungarian cabbage roll dinner to honor the European heritage of the coalfield's workers.

## Headlights in the Dark

In 1938 the Baby Mine began welcoming a different kind of visitor, becoming what is billed as the first exhibition coal mine in the country. Advancing technology, which reduced the number of miners needed to do the work, helped make that possible. Its drive-through route turned a working landscape into a road trip, and visitors could see a coal seam from behind the wheel. The mine's working life was winding down: by the end of the Second World War its coal was waning, and in 1955 mining in Pocahontas ceased altogether. Visitors kept driving through until exhaust proved too much for the roof, and the practice stopped in 1970.

## What the Mountain Kept

The mine was declared a National Historic Landmark in 1994. On the surface, the spoil pile now covers the ground where the beehive ovens once stood, and only three oven ruins remain, overgrown with trees. Wikipedia says the town of Pocahontas owns and runs the Exhibition Mine and Museum, which offers guided tours into the mine and a museum of artifacts and photographs; Virginia Energy lists it as open April through September, and visitor counts vary, from more than 3,500 in 2021 according to Wikipedia to over a thousand a year in a 2025 local news report. The town itself, which peaked in population in 1920, counted 268 residents in the 2020 census. Walking into the dark of the Baby Mine today, visitors stand inside the place where the coalfield, and the town that named it, began.
