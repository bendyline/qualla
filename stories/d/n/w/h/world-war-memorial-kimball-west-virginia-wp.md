# World War Memorial (Kimball, West Virginia)

Built in 1928 after Black veterans in the coalfields of McDowell County petitioned for their own memorial, Kimball's hilltop World War Memorial was, by several accounts, the first memorial in the nation built to honor African-American veterans of World War I, and after decades of decline and a 1991 fire it has been restored as a museum and community hall.

## Introduction

It began with a memorial that left them out. After McDowell County honored its white World War I veterans with a memorial in the county courthouse, the county's African-American veterans petitioned the county commission for one of their own. They won it. On a hill in the coal town of Kimball, West Virginia, a columned building of stone, terra cotta and brick was dedicated in February 1928, described by West Virginia sources as the first memorial in the United States built to honor African-American veterans of World War I. It has since been a community hall, a Legion post, a burned-out ruin and, today, a restored museum telling the stories of the soldiers of the coalfields.

## Miners Who Went to War

In the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, many African Americans worked as coal miners, and by 1920, Wikipedia says, McDowell County had the largest African-American population in the Appalachian region. When the United States entered World War I, 1,500 African American men from the county served, among the hundreds of thousands of African Americans who served nationwide. When the county's memorial in the courthouse honored only its white veterans, Black veterans took their case to the McDowell County Commission, which eventually granted $25,000 toward a building of their own, and the local American Legion post raised another $19,500, bringing the construction budget to $44,500.

## Faith, Hope, Charity, Service

In 1927 the county hired a local architect, Hassel T. Hicks, to design the memorial, and it opened the following year in the Classical Revival style, with four columns. At the dedication on February 11, 1928, the speaker was G.E. Ferguson, described as the only African American from West Virginia to serve as a captain in World War I. Ferguson gave each of the columns a meaning. One stood for faith in America as a country. Another stood for hope in ending injustice, a pointed word at a dedication honoring men whose own county had first left them out. The third column represented charity toward others, and the fourth, service to the greater community. Those four ideas would describe the building's next several decades surprisingly well.

## Cab Calloway on the Hill

The memorial was never just a monument. Inside were a hundred-seat meeting room, a trophy room, a kitchen, a recreation center and a library, and local people of all races used them. The building worked as a community center and was frequently rented for social occasions. Among the entertainers who performed there was the popular jazz musician Cab Calloway. The memorial was also home to an African-American Legion post, Luther Patterson Post 36, named for Luther Patterson, described by the West Virginia Encyclopedia as one of the first Black casualties of the war. For a coal town, it was a place to gather, celebrate and remember, all under one roof on the hillside.

## A Ruin Worth Saving

Coal's fortunes shaped the memorial's own. In the later decades of the twentieth century, the declining coal industry shrank the local population, and the memorial fell into disrepair. Then, in 1991, a fire destroyed the interior, leaving only the outer walls standing. The building remained a ruin for more than a decade. Even so, its significance was clear enough that it was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1993, while still a shell. Restoration eventually came, at a reported cost of more than $1 million, and the War Memorial reopened in 2006. It received a Congressional Black Caucus Veterans Braintrust Award that year and an Honor Award from the West Virginia chapter of the American Institute of Architects in 2007, and, according to Wikipedia, the National Trust for Historic Preservation also recognized the project.

## Soldiers of the Coalfields

Today the building is again a working hall, hosting tours, classes, conferences, dinners, receptions and social gatherings. It also houses an interactive museum experience called "Forgotten Legacy: Soldiers of the Coalfields." Much of it grew from the work of a West Virginia University journalism professor who, beginning in 2010, led students in compiling an archive of interviews, photographs and artifacts from Affrilachian veterans. Those accounts are now open to the public. It is a fitting use for a building that began because veterans refused to be forgotten, and that stands restored on its hill in Kimball, still carrying Ferguson's four words: faith, hope, charity and service.
