# Federal Prison Camp, Alderson

America's first federal prison for women opened in the West Virginia hills in 1927 as a reform experiment built like a boarding school, and over the decades it held Billie Holiday, "Tokyo Rose" and Martha Stewart among thousands of less famous women.

**Location:** 37.7236, -80.6678

**Categories:** Women's prisons in West Virginia, Federal Prison Camps, Prisons in West Virginia, Buildings and structures in Alderson, West Virginia, Women in West Virginia

## Introduction

For the women who arrived from the streets of New York and Washington, the isolation could be a hardship of its own. At night, according to sociologist Esther Heffernan, they were woken by crickets and frogs. The Federal Prison Camp at Alderson sits in the hills of southern West Virginia, a minimum-security federal prison for women spread across 159 acres, straddling the line between Summers and Monroe counties. It was chosen for exactly that isolation: remote enough to discourage escapes, yet reasonably close to the nation's capital. When it opened in 1927, it was the first federal women's prison in the United States.

## Why Women Needed Their Own Prison

In the 1920s there was little federal prison space for women. Female offenders were either given alternative punishments or housed alone inside men's institutions, where girls and women were sexually exploited by staff and fellow inmates. Mabel Walker Willebrandt, then an Assistant U.S. Attorney General, first encouraged a separate facility, and it opened on April 30, 1927, as the Federal Industrial Institute for Women, part of a 1920s movement to reform female offenders. Later bureau lore claimed Alderson opened its doors to moonshining women from the West Virginia hills. In fact, 174 women had been sent there in its first year of operation, before its formal opening on November 14, 1928, and the vast majority of women held in those years were serving drug and alcohol sentences imposed under Prohibition.

## A Campus Without Walls

Willebrandt chose Mary Belle Harris as the first warden, and Harris, who had run women's institutions in New York and New Jersey, worked with the architects on the layout. The result looked nothing like a penitentiary. Styled after a boarding school, it had no armed guards and no fenced grounds. Fourteen cottages were set in a horseshoe on two-tiered slopes, each with its own kitchen and rooms for about 30 women, though the cottages were segregated by race. Every woman had a schedule and a job, and vocational training, including craft-shop work, was central from the start. Harris led the institution until 1941, and the systems she introduced here, including inmate classification and decentralized housing, were later adopted throughout the Federal Bureau of Prisons.

## Famous Names Among Many

Most of the women held at Alderson have been convicted of non-violent or white-collar crimes, and Heffernan described the population over the years as "a real mixture," from relatives of famous mobsters to "grandmotherly women who embezzled money from banks." Some names are known worldwide. Billie Holiday served time here in 1947 and 1948 after a narcotics arrest and was released early for good behavior. Iva Toguri D'Aquino, the American citizen whom Japanese forces made take part in Radio Tokyo's wartime propaganda broadcasts, was sent to Alderson after a 1949 treason conviction on charges built on false testimony forced from witnesses; she served more than six years before parole, and in 1977 President Gerald Ford granted her a full pardon. Mildred Gillars, known as "Axis Sally," was held until her parole in 1961. Elizabeth Gurley Flynn, a leftist leader and co-founder of the American Civil Liberties Union, who was imprisoned under the Smith Act from 1955 to 1957, was here too. So was journalist Claudia Jones, convicted under the same law, who served part of 1955 at Alderson before leaving the United States for Britain; there she organized the indoor Caribbean carnivals, beginning in 1959, that preceded London's Notting Hill Carnival.

## Camp Cupcake and the Reality

When Martha Stewart served a five-month sentence here after her 2004 conviction, reporters nicknamed the prison "Camp Cupcake," and, Wikipedia says, Stewart herself called it "Yale." Local residents have called it "the college campus." Daily life is less gentle than the nicknames suggest. The original cottages were closed in the 1990s after dormitories were built, and the West Virginia Encyclopedia gives the camp's capacity as 677 women. Since 2004, inmates have no longer been free to roam the whole campus, and that year a writer for The Independent described Alderson as following "a punitive rather than a rehabilitative model." Many women transfer here for its drug program, and Alderson once took part in Paws4prisons, which taught inmates to train shelter-rescue dogs and then highly skilled assistance dogs. Wikipedia calls the prison the largest employer in the Alderson area, about five hours' drive from Washington.

## Flight Context

FPC Alderson lies at 37.7236°N, 80.6678°W near the town of Alderson in southern West Virginia, on the border of Summers and Monroe counties, in hilly country. This is an active federal prison, so view it only in passing from a respectful altitude, around 4,000–4,500 ft MSL, and do not circle or loiter over the facility. Hinton-Alderson Airport (FAA identifier WV77, private use, permission required before landing, no ICAO code) at Pence Springs lies about 3 nm southwest, and Greenbrier Valley Airport (KLWB) at Lewisburg is about 15 nm northeast. Raleigh County Memorial Airport (KBKW) at Beckley is about 22 nm west. Watch for valley fog on calm mornings.
