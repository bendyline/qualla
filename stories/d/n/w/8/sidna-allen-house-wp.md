# Sidna Allen House

Sidna Allen spent lavishly on an eight-room Queen Anne house near Fancy Gap, Virginia, in 1911, then lost it after the 1912 Hillsville courthouse shooting turned him into a fugitive and, eventually, a prisoner.

**Location:** 36.6942, -80.6942

## Introduction

Oak floors, a living room of white maple, quarter-sawed oak wainscoting in the dining room, plaster on every wall and slate on the roof: Sidna Allen spared little on the house he built near Fancy Gap, Virginia, in 1911. It even had its own windmill and an acetylene generator to light the rooms. He and his wife, Bettie, lived in it for about a year. Then came March 1912, a gun battle in the Carroll County courthouse at Hillsville, and a chain of events that ended with Sidna in prison and the house gone from the family. He never lived there again.

## The Best Wood Around

Sidna Allen was 35 when he married Bettie Mitchell on January 27, 1901, and the couple decided to build a home of their own. The book The Courthouse Tragedy records the result in detail. The house had eight rooms, all built of the best wood around. "The floors were of oak, except the floor of our living room, which was white maple," the account says, and "in the dining room we used quarter-sawed oak, finished in natural color wainscoting." The roof was slate, and all of it, wood and stone alike, was very expensive at the time. The house, completed in 1911 in the Queen Anne style, was valued at $13,000 that year. The Allens were then Carroll County's most prominent family, with large tracts of farmland, a prosperous general store and an active role in local Democratic politics.

## Trouble on the Road Outside

The trouble that cost Sidna his house started close to it. In December 1910 (some sources say 1911), a deputy and his driver were taking two of the Allens' nephews to the courthouse under arrest, one handcuffed and one tied, when their buggy met Sidna's brother Floyd on the road south of Sidna's home. Floyd blocked the road at Sidna's store, beat the deputy with his own pistol and freed the young men, though he turned them over to the court a few days later. A grand jury indicted Floyd, Sidna and another relative for interfering with the deputies. Sidna was never tried on that charge. Floyd was, and on March 13, 1912, after close to a year of delays, his trial opened at Hillsville.

## March 14, 1912

The next day, with many members of the Allen family watching from the spectators' benches, most of them armed, the jury found Floyd guilty and the judge sentenced him to a year in the penitentiary. Floyd rose and said he was not going. Shooting broke out, and accounts still differ over who fired first. When it ended, the presiding judge, the county's prosecutor and its sheriff had been fatally wounded, along with a juror and a 19-year-old witness who had testified against Floyd. Sidna fled with his nephew Wesley Edwards. It took Baldwin-Felts detectives several months to track them to Des Moines, Iowa. Floyd Allen and his son Claude were executed in 1913, and the conviction has been debated in Carroll County for decades. Sidna was convicted and sentenced to 35 years in prison. Liens placed on his property for the victims' heirs led to its seizure and sale at auction, and Bettie and their two small daughters lived in rented quarters and worked menial jobs until Governor Harry Flood Byrd granted Sidna a conditional pardon in April 1926.

## An Empty Showplace

The house outlasted the scandal that emptied it. Its plaster and hardwood, its windmill and the generator that gave it light all belonged to a brief moment, barely a year, when Sidna and Bettie Allen made it their home. It was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1974. Today it belongs to the Carroll County Historical Society, which was deeded the house in December 2014 by its owner; the society's website says the house is currently closed to the public and in need of a total restoration. Seen now, it is a record of two things at once: the prosperity one mountain family had built by 1911, and how quickly a single day in a county courtroom could take it away.
