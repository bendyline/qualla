# Burke's Garden, Virginia

Named as a joke after a surveyor's discarded potato peelings sprouted, Burke's Garden is a high, mountain-ringed bowl in Tazewell County, Virginia, where, by local tradition, farmers once refused to sell to the Vanderbilts.

**Location:** 37.1022, -81.3431

**Categories:** Valleys of Virginia, Unincorporated communities in Tazewell County, Virginia, Geography of Tazewell County, Virginia, National Register of Historic Places in Tazewell County, Virginia, Amish communities

## Introduction

A handful of potato peelings named this valley. Around 1748 a team of surveyors working for landowner James Patton came into a high mountain bowl in what is now Tazewell County, Virginia, and one of them, an Irishman named James Burke, is said to have thrown away some peelings while cooking. When the party returned a year later, potatoes were growing where the peels had landed. Someone dubbed the place Burke's Garden as a joke, and the name stuck.

## A Bowl Among the Ridges

The joke landed on an extraordinary piece of ground. Burke's Garden is an oval, bowl-like upland valley about 8.5 miles long and 4 miles wide, set around 3,000 feet above sea level, which makes it one of the highest valleys in Virginia, and the highest by some accounts. Garden Mountain surrounds it. Geologically, the valley is a dome-shaped up-warp, an anticline, that exposed older Ordovician limestone, rock that erodes far more easily than the younger Silurian sandstone of the surrounding ridges. The result is a sheltered basin known for its fertile land. Wolf Creek, a tributary of the New River, drains the basin, flowing out of the bowl toward the northeast.

## Frontier Outpost

Indigenous peoples occupied the area for a long time before the surveyors arrived. In 1749, the year the potato story came to its punchline, William Ingles went to Burke's Garden to build a house with his uncle, John Ingles. Later in the 18th century the valley became an outpost of German immigrants settling the backcountry frontier. Far from the transportation corridors of the major rivers, it stayed relatively isolated. Its churches tell a story of their own. Burke's Garden Central Church, now Lutheran and described as the county's oldest church, originally served several denominations and was called Union Church. When the congregation learned that it had joined the Lutheran denomination, most members reportedly left and established Methodist and Presbyterian churches instead.

## Not for Sale, Not Even to Vanderbilts

Local tradition holds that in the late 19th century, agents for the Vanderbilt family approached local farmers about buying land for a large estate in the valley. Nobody wanted to sell. The Vanderbilts went elsewhere and built their Biltmore Estate near Asheville, North Carolina. Burke's Garden kept its farms, and it kept its stories. In 1952 the community was terrorized by the "Varmint of Burke's Garden," a large coyote that killed many local sheep and did a great deal of damage before it was finally killed in February 1953.

## Scooters at the Schoolhouse

In the 1990s a few Amish families moved to Burke's Garden, but they later left, unable to buy enough land or attract enough other families to form a viable community. The Amish have since returned and now have a community that AmishAmerica's Chris Wesner estimated at about 100 people in 2021. They run their own school, and lines of scooters can be seen daily outside the Burkes Garden School and Community Center. The valley as a whole is home to about 300 people. The entire valley is listed on the National Register of Historic Places as the Burke's Garden Rural Historic District, and the Burke's Garden Central Church and Cemetery is listed as well.

## Flight Context

Burke's Garden is unmistakable from the air: an oval valley about 8.5 miles long and 4 miles wide, surrounded by the ridge of Garden Mountain, with open farmland on the floor at around 3,000 ft MSL. The valley's center, and the story's coordinates, are near 37.10°N, 81.34°W. Cruise at 5,500-7,000 ft MSL for the full view of the bowl, and allow for the surrounding ridges when descending. Nearby airports: Mountain Empire Airport (KMKJ), serving Marion and Wytheville, about 12 nautical miles south; Mercer County Airport in Bluefield, West Virginia (KBLF), about 13 nautical miles north-northeast; and Tazewell County Airport (KJFZ) near Richlands, about 22 nautical miles west. Mountain winds can produce turbulence over the ridges.
