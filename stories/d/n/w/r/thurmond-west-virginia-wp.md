# Thurmond, West Virginia

Once a booming C&O railroad town in the New River Gorge with two banks, two hotels and a poker game that reportedly ran for fourteen years, Thurmond is now West Virginia's smallest municipality, where Amtrak trains still stop.

**Location:** 37.9611, -81.0817

**Categories:** Coal towns in West Virginia, Ghost towns in West Virginia, Historic districts in Fayette County, West Virginia, National Coal Heritage Area, National Register of Historic Places in New River Gorge National Park and Preserve

## Introduction

By Wikipedia's account, in the city elections of June 14, 2005, six of Thurmond's seven residents sought office. It is hard to find a more complete picture of civic participation, or of how far a town can shrink. Thurmond, on the New River in Fayette County, is the least-populous municipality in West Virginia, with five people counted in the 2020 census. A century ago it was a prosperous railroad town at the heart of the New River Gorge coal country, with hotels, banks and a railyard that handled coal from the surrounding hills. Most of it now belongs to the National Park Service, and all of it is history.

## Paid in Land

The town takes its name from Captain W. D. Thurmond, who came to the Fayette County area as a young man in the mid-1840s. In 1873 he received the 73-acre site as payment for a surveying job. He served as a captain in Thurmond's Rangers, an irregular Confederate unit, and died in 1910 at 89. For decades the settlement stayed small. The post office opened in 1888, the same year the railroad station was built, but real growth began in 1892, when Thomas G. McKell of Glen Jean negotiated with the Chesapeake and Ohio Railway for a crossing at Dunloup Creek. The coal trade did the rest. Thurmond became an important switching center for the C&O, a place where short trains from mines in the area were assembled into longer ones bound for markets on the main line. The town was incorporated in 1903.

## A Main Street Made of Rails

Thurmond's strangest feature is still plain to see. The town occupies a narrow strip of flat land beside the tracks, and for much of its history it had no street at all. Its commercial buildings front directly onto the railroad, and until 1921 the only way in was by train. Even today, a single-lane road crosses the New River on a single-track railroad bridge, crosses the main line, and climbs the hill behind the town, running 150 feet above it before dropping back down beside the tracks. The rail line was everything. Houses came in three or four standard types matched to their occupants' positions in the railroad hierarchy, and the town's level ground is still almost entirely taken up by railroad operations. Despite having no road, the commercial district thrived, supporting two hotels, two banks and a number of other businesses.

## Dry Land and a Legendary Poker Game

Captain Thurmond banned alcohol from his lands, which made up the town as originally incorporated. The McKell family had no such scruples. Their Dun Glen Hotel, on McKell land just outside the town, opened in 1901 with 100 rooms and became a nationally known resort. It also became the setting of a legend: a poker game said to have lasted fourteen years, billed as the longest in history. The National Park Service has found no firsthand account of it, and reports that Guinness says it has never had the record in its system. Nearby, on the south side near the Dun Glen, a district called "Ballyhack" gained a reputation as Thurmond's red-light district. Closer to the tracks stood the town's other hotel, the Lafayette, which locals nicknamed the "Lay-flat." The Dun Glen burned down in 1930, and hard times followed.

## Diesel, Decline and a Visitor Center

The Thurmond National Bank closed in 1931, and the McKells' New River Bank moved to Oak Hill in 1935. In the 1950s, diesel locomotives replaced steam, and because they could travel much farther without stopping for coal and water, they no longer needed Thurmond. A second decline came in the late 1980s, when the National Park Service began buying homes and properties from willing sellers, promising to turn the town into a living history museum. Thurmond became the filming location for John Sayles' 1987 film Matewan, because it still looked like a 1920s Appalachian coal town. The C&O depot was renovated in 1995 as a Park Service visitor center, and the entire town is now a historic district. Trains still come. Amtrak's Cardinal stops three days a week, on request, at the unstaffed station, which served 466 passengers in 2023, making it one of the least-used stops on the network.

## Flight Context

Thurmond sits at 37.9611°N, 81.0817°W deep in the New River Gorge in Fayette County, West Virginia, inside New River Gorge National Park and Preserve. The town is a thin line of commercial buildings squeezed between the New River and the rail line, with the single-track railroad bridge crossing the river beside it. The gorge walls rise steeply on both sides, so stay high, around 4,000–4,500 ft MSL, and follow the river rather than descending into the canyon. Raleigh County Memorial Airport (KBKW) at Beckley is about 11 nm south-southwest, Greenbrier Valley Airport (KLWB) at Lewisburg is about 33 nm east, and Yeager Airport (KCRW) at Charleston is about 35 nm northwest. Morning fog can fill the gorge and hide the river until it burns off.
