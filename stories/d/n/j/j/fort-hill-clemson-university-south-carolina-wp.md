# Fort Hill (Clemson University, South Carolina)

John C. Calhoun's plantation house now stands at the heart of Clemson University, preserved under the terms of a will that ordered it never be torn down, and remembered too for the enslaved families whose labor built it.

**Location:** 34.6779, -82.8389

**Categories:** National Historic Landmarks in South Carolina, Clemson University campus, Historic house museums in South Carolina, Plantations in South Carolina, Calhoun family, Houses completed in 1803

## Introduction

Thomas Green Clemson's will, in force when he died in 1888, had one house in mind. He left more than 814 acres of the Fort Hill estate to the State of South Carolina for an agricultural college, but the dwelling came with conditions. It "shall never be torn down or altered," he wrote, must be kept in repair with its furniture, and "shall always be open for inspection of visitors." The college, today Clemson University, was established on the plantation site the following year. The house it was bound to protect had been the home of John C. Calhoun, the seventh Vice President of the United States and a noted proponent of slavery and constitutional Nullification.

## From Esseneca to Fort Hill

Before the Revolutionary War, the Cherokee called this land Esseneca. Around 1776, Fort Rutledge was built nearby, and its name would outlast the fort itself. About 1803, Dr. James McElhenny, pastor of Hopewell Presbyterian Church, built a modest four-room house on the site and called it Clergy Hall. In 1825 it became the home of John C. Calhoun and his wife, Floride. The property was bought by her mother, Floride Bonneau Colhoun, and Calhoun did not own it himself until 1836. Ten rooms were added to the four-room parsonage, making fourteen, and from about 1830 the family called the expanded house Fort Hill, after the old fort. What emerged was a house in the Greek Revival style with Federal detailing, simple rather than showy inside. For a quarter century, until Calhoun's death in 1850, it was home to a vice president remembered as a champion of slavery and of the doctrine that a state could nullify federal law.

## The Families Behind the Fields

Everything at Fort Hill rested on forced labor. Clemson University's own history puts it plainly: life at Fort Hill, and all of its financial growth, was only possible because of the enslaved African Americans who worked its 450 cultivated acres, kept the household running and did the plantation's construction. Written records of their lives are scarce, but a few family units are documented. There was Sawney, his wife Tiller, and at least two of their children, Sawney Jr. and Issey. There was Daphne, her husband Bill Lawrence, and their children Susan Clemson Richardson and Benjamin. Sharper lived there with his wife Caroline and his daughter Tiller, and Nelly with her son Andy. When Calhoun died in 1850, the law counted fifty enslaved people at Fort Hill as part of his estate, and they passed to his widow along with the land.

## An Inheritance Tangled in Court

What followed was a family struggle that dragged on for more than two decades. Floride Calhoun was to share the property with three of her children: Cornelia, John, and Anna Maria, who had married Thomas Green Clemson. Anna sold her share to her mother. Floride then sold the plantation to her son Andrew Pickens Calhoun, holding the mortgage herself, and when Andrew died in 1865 she filed for foreclosure against his heirs. She died the next year. After lengthy legal proceedings, the plantation was auctioned at Walhalla in 1872. The executor of Floride's estate won, and the land was divided among her surviving heirs. Anna Clemson received the residence with about 814 acres, while Floride's great-granddaughter, Floride Isabella Lee, received about 288. Thomas and Anna Clemson moved into Fort Hill that same year. Anna died in 1875, and the house passed to her husband.

## Open for Inspection of Visitors

Clemson's 1888 bequest gave the state about three-fourths of the Fort Hill plantation, along with $80,000, for a public scientific and agricultural college. The university has kept to the terms. The house and its immediate parcel, all that remains of an estate that once spread across more than 1,000 acres, are maintained as a house museum. Fort Hill was designated a National Historic Landmark in 1960. Later it went through a two-year restoration and reopened in spring 2003, and the Save America's Treasures program named it a national treasure, with federal grants and matching funds supporting a comprehensive conservation of its artifacts. The old parsonage now sits inside the campus that grew out of its fields, still open to anyone who comes to look, as the will demanded.

## Flight Context

Fort Hill stands at about 34.678°N, 82.839°W on the Clemson University campus in Pickens County, South Carolina, near the upper reaches of Lake Hartwell. The house itself is small from the air; at 2,000-3,000 feet AGL the university campus and the lake beside it are the landmarks to find. Oconee County Regional Airport (KCEU) at Clemson is about 2.5 nm away, making it the obvious base. Other nearby fields: Pickens County Airport (KLQK, about 10 nm), Anderson Regional Airport (KAND, about 13 nm) and Greenville Downtown Airport (KGMU, about 26 nm). Clear days give the best view of the campus and shoreline.
