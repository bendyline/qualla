# Faith Cabin Library at Seneca Junior College

A small T-shaped log cabin in Seneca, South Carolina, stocked with books from Oberlin College students, is one of the last standing reminders of a grassroots movement that built libraries for Black communities shut out by segregation.

**Location:** 34.6808, -82.9596

**Categories:** African-American history of South Carolina, Historically segregated African-American schools in South Carolina, School buildings on the National Register of Historic Places in South Carolina, Libraries, Seneca, South Carolina

## Introduction

"We didn't have anything to go on but faith." A woman in Saluda, South Carolina, said it about the first library her Black community had built, and the phrase became a name. By 1937 the idea had reached Seneca, where a T-shaped log cabin went up beside Seneca Junior College, at what is now 298 South Poplar Street. It was built to serve the town's African American community, and its shelves held a gift from far away: books donated by the students of Oberlin College in Ohio.

## Nowhere to Borrow a Book

In the early twentieth century, library access anywhere in rural South Carolina was limited. For Black South Carolinians, segregation made it close to nonexistent; outside Charleston and Columbia, there was essentially nowhere to borrow a book. The answer came from an unlikely pair of childhood friends. Willie Lee Buffington was a white mill worker, and Euriah Simpkins was an African American teacher. Together they conceived a program of libraries for Black communities and filled the shelves by writing to preachers and asking for donations. A Methodist church in New York responded with a large gift of about 1,000 books, enough to justify a building. African Americans in Saluda built the first library themselves, and it opened in 1932. That woman's remark about faith gave the Faith Cabin Libraries their name.

## A Movement Crosses the State Line

Buffington kept going. He finished high school, went on to Furman University, and later taught at two historically Black colleges, Benedict College in Columbia and Paine College in Augusta, Georgia. As his career moved, the program moved with him, spreading into Georgia. In the end about thirty Faith Cabin Libraries were built in South Carolina and about seventy in Georgia. Each unit carried its own identity within the network, and the one in Seneca was named for the people who had helped fill it.

## The Oberlin Unit

In the Faith Cabin system, the Seneca library was the Oberlin Unit, honoring the students of Oberlin College in Oberlin, Ohio, who donated its books. The name carried history with it. In 1835, Oberlin became one of the first colleges in the United States to admit African Americans, and about a century later its students were sending books to a Black junior college in the South Carolina Upstate. That college had deep roots of its own: Seneca Institute - Seneca Junior College was established in 1899 as a historically Black school in Seneca, a railroad town founded in 1873 where the Blue Ridge Railroad met the Atlanta and Charlotte Air Line. The library, though, arrived late in the college's life. It stayed open for only two years, undone both by the Great Depression, which forced Seneca Junior College to close, and by the construction of a new high school for African Americans in Seneca.

## Logs on a Stone Foundation

The building is plain and sturdy. The main part of the T-shaped cabin sits on a stone foundation, while the rear wing rests on concrete piers, all of it under a gabled roof of V-crimped metal. Double-hung sash windows flank the front door, a stone chimney rises on the left side, two more windows open the right side, and one lights the rear wing. The gables are sheathed in horizontal wooden planks and hold a window. Everything else from Seneca Junior College is gone. The Seneca Institute Family Life Center now occupies the site with a brick and metal building from 1978, which is not part of the historic listing. The log cabin is one of only two free-standing Faith Cabin Library buildings that local officials say remain in South Carolina, along with the one in Pendleton, and on November 14, 2012, it was added to the National Register of Historic Places.

## Flight Context

The library stands in Seneca, South Carolina, at about 34.68°N, 82.96°W, in the Upstate between Lake Keowee and Lake Hartwell, near Clemson. The cabin itself is far too small to pick out from the air; at 2,000-3,000 feet AGL the town grid of Seneca and the surrounding lakes are the useful references.
