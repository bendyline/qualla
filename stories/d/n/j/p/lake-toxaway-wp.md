# Lake Toxaway

North Carolina's largest private lake was born as a turn-of-the-century mountain resort, drained when its dam burst in 1916, and was painstakingly refilled more than four decades later.

**Location:** 35.1345, -82.9408

**Categories:** Reservoirs in North Carolina, Bodies of water of Transylvania County, North Carolina, Tourist attractions in Transylvania County, North Carolina, Dam failures in the United States, Historic resorts

## Introduction

After a summer of storms, including a deluge reported at more than 20 inches in less than 24 hours, the Toxaway River was swollen on August 13, 1916. Just above Toxaway Falls, an earthen dam 500 feet long and 60 feet high held back a mountain lake that had been built with no way to control its water level. Under the strain, it gave way. More than five billion gallons of water crashed over the falls and down into South Carolina, and the lake that had turned this corner of Transylvania County, North Carolina, into a retreat for the rich and famous drained away in a single disaster. Remarkably, no people died; the only casualty reported was a mule.

## The Switzerland of America

The lake began with a visitor's eye for potential. E. H. Jennings came to Transylvania County from Pennsylvania in 1890 and founded several businesses, among them the Toxaway Company. Visiting the valley in the 1890s, he saw what it could become, and in 1902 he filled Lake Toxaway, one of the largest artificial lakes in the Appalachian Mountains. None of it would have worked without the Southern Railway, which built a line from Asheville that arrived in step with the new lake. Jennings ringed the area with resorts - the Fairfield Inn, the Sapphire Inn, the Lodge, the Franklin Hotel and the Toxaway Inn - and the mountains here came to be known as the "Switzerland of America." Holding it all together was that earthen dam, 20 feet wide at its top.

## Five Stories Above the Water

The Toxaway Inn opened in 1903 and was the jewel of the enterprise. It rose five stories above the lake, and its woodwork came from more than 40 species of trees cut on the property. Five hundred guests paid $17.50 a week and up for central heat, electricity, private indoor plumbing, long-distance telephones and elevators. French chefs cooked, and diners ate from imported crystal and dinnerware with sterling silver and fine linens. After dinner there was a ballroom with a large orchestra, a billiard parlor, a bowling alley, a bar and a gazebo for outdoor concerts. By day guests went boating, swimming, fishing, riding and hunting, or played golf and tennis. The frequent guests read like a directory of American wealth: Henry Ford, Harvey Firestone, the Vanderbilt family, John D. Rockefeller, James Buchanan Duke, R. J. Reynolds and Thomas Edison.

## Granite Laid Bare

Rain is no stranger here. Lake Toxaway is among the wettest places in North Carolina, with about 96 inches of precipitation a year, and sits within the Appalachian temperate rainforest. That August deluge, though, overwhelmed a dam never engineered to handle it. The Toxaway Inn survived the flood itself, but the loss of its lake was its undoing. It never reopened, stood empty for decades, and was demolished in 1947. The falls below still carry the evidence of that night. Along Toxaway Falls, granite that the torrent scoured bare remains exposed for a great distance down the slope, a scar left by five billion gallons racing over the falls.

## A Tent on the Peninsula

One family stayed. Around 1910, the Armstrongs of Savannah visited the Toxaway Inn and decided to build a summer home. George F. Armstrong suggested his wife camp on the site she had chosen to be sure she liked it, so in 1913 Lucy Camp Armstrong, helped by eleven servants, pitched a tent with a hardwood floor on the knoll of a short, oak-dotted peninsula. By 1915 her six-level house stood there, modeled on a Swiss mountain cottage, with casement windows, flower boxes, balconies with small triangular cutouts and native-stone foundations. The lake vanished a year later, but she kept coming. George died in an automobile accident in the 1920s, and Lucy added a kitchen, a freestanding library, stables and a pool, turning the retreat into a year-round home. She remarried in the 1930s, lived in the house for 47 years in all, and sold it in 1963. In 1985 it opened as the Greystone Inn.

## Filling the Valley Again

For more than four decades the valley sat empty of its lake. Then, in 1960, a group of investors headed by R. D. Heinitsh Sr. bought the 9,000-acre tract that had surrounded the old resort and formed the Lake Toxaway Company. Heinitsh cleared the entire lakebed, rebuilt the dam, and brought the water back to its original level of 3,010 feet above sea level. A golf course followed in 1963, along with the Lake Toxaway Country Club. Today the lake covers 640 acres with 14 miles of shoreline, the largest privately held lake in North Carolina. Hawk Mountain, Panthertail Mountain, Cold Mountain and Mount Toxaway rise around it, alongside wilderness areas totaling more than 10,000 acres, and the water fills again with boaters, anglers, swimmers, skiers and sailors.

## Flight Context

Lake Toxaway lies at about 35.13°N, 82.94°W in Transylvania County, North Carolina, near the South Carolina line, with the lake surface at about 3,010 feet above sea level and higher peaks close by, including Mount Toxaway and Panthertail Mountain. Stay at or above 6,500 feet MSL for terrain clearance and a good overview; look for the branching 640-acre lake with U.S. Highway 64 winding past it and the outflow dropping over Toxaway Falls on the Toxaway River. Nearby airports: Jackson County Airport at Sylva (FAA identifier 24A, no ICAO code listed, about 17 nm), Pickens County Airport (KLQK, about 23 nm), Macon County Airport at Franklin (FAA identifier 1A5, about 24 nm), Asheville Regional Airport (KAVL, about 27 nm) and Hendersonville Airport (FAA identifier 0A7, about 27 nm). This is one of the wettest places in North Carolina, so expect low cloud and mountain obscuration; pick a clear day.
