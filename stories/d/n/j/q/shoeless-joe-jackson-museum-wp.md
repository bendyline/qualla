# Shoeless Joe Jackson Museum

Across from Fluor Field in Greenville, the small brick house where banned baseball star "Shoeless" Joe Jackson lived and died now holds his museum, whose campaign to clear his name outlasted a commissioner's refusal.

**Location:** 34.8415, -82.4073

**Categories:** Buildings and structures in Greenville, South Carolina, Baseball museums and halls of fame, Museums established in 2008, 2008 establishments in South Carolina, Museums in Greenville County, South Carolina, Sports museums in South Carolina

## Introduction

The address is a batting average. The Shoeless Joe Jackson Museum stands at 356 Field Street in Greenville, South Carolina, numbered in honor of Jackson's lifetime .356, one of the highest in major-league history. The brick house, where Jackson lived and died, sits across from Fluor Field, the city's ballpark. Inside are a few of his personal belongings and a baseball research library. It is a modest place for a man whose name still stirs arguments a century after the scandal that drove him out of the game.

## The Boy in His Socks

Jackson learned his baseball in the mills. Born in 1887, he grew up in Brandon Mill, a company town on the outskirts of Greenville, and started playing for the mill's team in 1900, when he was 13. By his own account, his nickname came from one of those games. As he recalled in a 1949 magazine interview, a new pair of cleats had given him blisters so painful that he took his shoes off before batting. A heckling fan spotted him running to third base in his socks and shouted, "You shoeless son of a gun, you!" The name stuck for the rest of his life, though some biographers credit a Greenville newspaper reporter with coining it in 1908. In 1908 he began his professional career with his hometown Greenville Spinners, and from there he climbed to the major leagues.

## The Series That Never Ended

Jackson's fame is inseparable from the Black Sox Scandal, in which eight members of the 1919 Chicago White Sox took part in a conspiracy to fix the World Series. Jackson's role in the scandal has been fiercely debated ever since, not least because of how he played in that Series: he led both teams in several statistical categories, had 12 hits, a World Series record at the time, and hit the only home run of the Series. Commissioner Kenesaw Mountain Landis nonetheless banned all eight players from professional baseball permanently in 1921, after a jury had acquitted them. Jackson eventually came home. He and his wife returned to Greenville in 1933, and he died in 1951, at the age of 64, of a heart attack. He is buried at Woodlawn Memorial Park near the Bob Jones University campus, and many museum visitors make the trip to his grave as well.

## A House That Moved to the Ballpark

The museum began with a relocation. In 2006, Jackson's original home was moved from Wilburn Avenue to a site next to Fluor Field in downtown Greenville and restored, a project chronicled on the TLC reality show The Real Deal. On June 21, 2008, the house opened to the public as the Shoeless Joe Jackson Museum and Library. In 2020 it was moved again, about 100 yards, to the corner of South Markley and Field streets, when a developer building apartments on the old site paid for the move and an addition that roughly doubled the museum's size. The museum has opened to the public on Saturdays and offered private tours during the week, drawing visitors who want to see where this fiercely debated figure lived and died. Its collection is small and personal rather than grand, a few of Jackson's belongings alongside a library of baseball books, which suits a house that was never meant to be a monument.

## Petitioning the Commissioner

The museum did more than preserve the house. In 2015, it formally petitioned Commissioner of Baseball Rob Manfred to reinstate Jackson, arguing that he had "more than served his sentence" in the 95 years since Landis banished him. After an official review, Manfred said no. "The results of this work demonstrate to me that it is not possible now, over 95 years since those events took place and were considered by Commissioner Landis, to be certain enough of the truth to overrule Commissioner Landis' determinations," he wrote. The story did not end there. In May 2025, Manfred announced that a person's time on baseball's permanently ineligible list ends upon their death, and Jackson was removed from the list, making him eligible for the Hall of Fame once again.
