# Petersburg, Georgia

Once the third-largest city in Georgia, the tobacco town of Petersburg rose at the fork of the Broad and Savannah Rivers, emptied within a generation, and now lies mostly beneath Clarks Hill Lake.

**Location:** 33.9633, -82.5703

**Categories:** Ghost towns in Georgia (U.S. state), Submerged places in the United States, Geography of Elbert County, Georgia, Savannah River, History of Georgia (U.S. state)

## Introduction

When Clarks Hill Lake (federally named J. Strom Thurmond Lake) drops low, old foundations surface near Bobby Brown State Park, close to where the Broad River meets the Savannah. They are what remains of Petersburg, Georgia - a town that at its peak ranked third in the state, behind only Savannah and Augusta, and then emptied so completely that its ground went back to farmland long before the water covered it. Dionysius Oliver founded Petersburg in 1786, naming it after Petersburg, Virginia, and laying out 86 lots across about 40 acres on the point of land between the two rivers. Its purpose was plain: to serve the rapidly growing Broad River Valley, and to get that valley's tobacco to market.

## Risen Out of the Woods

Travelers struggled to believe it. Writing in his 1801 Pinelands of Georgia, Sibbald ranked Petersburg "second only to Augusta" in situation and commercial consequence, calling it "a handsome well built Town" that presented "to the view of the astonished traveller, a Town which has risen out of the Woods in a few years, as if by enchantment." It had two warehouses for the inspection of tobacco, and it was not alone. Across the Broad River sat a smaller town - Sibbald called it Lincoln, though it may have been Lisbon - with its own tobacco inspection and some stores. On the far shore of the Savannah, in South Carolina, stood Vienna, with houses, stores and yet another inspection. That inspection was the whole point. Planters packed their crop in casks, and a Petersburg inspection was vital to getting a good price for it. Pole boats then carried the cargo down the Savannah River to Augusta.

## Grog Shops and a Billiard Table

A few years later, sometime between about 1806 and 1809, Longstreet came to know the town through the annual exhibition at Moses Waddel's nearby Willington Academy, and his portrait is livelier. Petersburg, he wrote, "was quite an active, busy, commercial little town," with some eight or ten stores, "the usual supplement of grog shops, and the very unusual supplement of a billiard-table." In spite of those temptations, its citizens were "remarkable for their refinement, respectability, intelligence and hospitality," and the dwelling houses far outnumbered the stores and shops. He was less generous to the neighbors. Lisbon, he believed, "could never boast of more than two stores and a groggery." Vienna surpassed Lisbon in everything, though exactly how far he could not say - except for John Glover's house and store, "which had no match in Lisbon."

## A Valley of Senators

Petersburg was never large. The 1810 census counted 332 people, a figure that included the enslaved, and the population may once have been about twice that. Yet the Broad River Valley around it produced a striking run of public men. William Wyatt Bibb practiced medicine in Petersburg before winning election to Congress from Georgia. He served in the U.S. Senate from 1813 to 1816, went to Alabama as its territorial governor by presidential appointment, and in 1819 was elected the first governor of the new state. Charles Tait, brought to the area with his family in 1783, sat in the Senate from 1809 to 1819, which made Petersburg the home of two concurrent senators. George Rockingham Gilmer, born in Wilkes County and a pupil at Waddel's academy, went to Congress in the 1820s and served twice as governor of Georgia, from 1829 to 1831 and again from 1837 to 1839.

## The Pull of the West

The boom was brief. Petersburg had not been developed until after the Revolutionary War, and after 1810 its population began to fall. Several explanations have been advanced. Cotton squeezed out the tobacco monopoly, and cotton needed no inspection station at all; it was "thrown upon boats all along the river without being inspected." Steamboats arrived, but they were not practicable above Augusta. Later, the same rivers that had made the town became obstacles to railroads, which after 1850 were considered essential to a town's economic life. Above all, new land opening to the west kept drawing people onward. The last known sale of a numbered lot came in 1837. The Petersburg post office moved to nearby Lisbon in 1844 and closed in 1855. Across the Savannah, Vienna declined and disappeared too. After the last person left, the buildings deteriorated and the site reverted to agricultural land.

## Beneath the Lake

What outlived the town was a boat. The "Petersburg boat," a pole boat that carried roughly ten tons, suited the 75 river miles between Petersburg and Augusta so well that it stayed in common use until well after the Civil War, long after the town that named it had gone quiet. The site, 305 river miles above Savannah, is now mostly submerged by Clarks Hill Lake. Maritime historians and archaeologists have carried out a multidisciplinary investigation of the drowned ruins, the subject of a 1998 East Carolina University master's thesis. When the water falls, the foundations reappear, marking the place where a town once rose out of the woods "as if by enchantment" and then quietly let the woods, the fields and finally the lake take it back.

## Flight Context

The Petersburg site lies at about 33.96°N, 82.57°W, at the confluence of the Broad and Savannah Rivers on the Georgia-South Carolina line, now mostly under the waters of Clarks Hill Lake. From 3,000-5,000 feet AGL, look for the point where the Broad River arm joins the main body of the lake on the Georgia shore, near Bobby Brown State Park; the old town foundations show only at low water, so the lake itself is the landmark. Nearby airports: Elbert County Airport-Patz Field at Elberton (FAA identifier EBA, GPS code KEBA, about 15 nm), Washington-Wilkes County Airport (KIIY, about 16 nm), Greenwood County Airport (KGRD, about 27 nm) and Augusta Regional Airport at Bush Field (KAGS, about 46 nm). Choose a clear day to trace the lake's branching arms back to the old river fork.
