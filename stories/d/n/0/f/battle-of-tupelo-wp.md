# Battle of Tupelo

In the July heat of 1864, Union infantry dug in at the ghost town of Harrisburg near Tupelo, broke a series of Confederate assaults, and kept Nathan Bedford Forrest's cavalry away from the railroad feeding Sherman's march on Atlanta.

**Location:** 34.2557, -88.7370

**Categories:** American Civil War, Battles, Tupelo, Mississippi, Military history, 1864 in Mississippi

## Introduction

On July 14, 1864, dismounted Confederate cavalrymen went forward on foot against Union infantry dug in at Harrisburg, a ghost town two miles west of Tupelo, Mississippi. Many were cut down by the fire of a larger, entrenched force. Many others simply dropped, felled by exhaustion in the great heat of a July sun. By a little after noon the attacks were over, and the Battle of Tupelo, also called the Battle of Harrisburg, had turned into a Union victory that shaped a campaign hundreds of miles away.

## Guarding a Single Track

In the spring and summer of 1864, Mississippians had their attention fixed on the fighting in Virginia and Georgia. Yet what happened in their own northeast corner was bound tightly to the fighting in Georgia. William T. Sherman's army, pushing toward Atlanta, drew its supplies over a single-track railroad through middle Tennessee. Lieutenant General Stephen D. Lee and Major General Nathan Bedford Forrest commanded Confederate cavalry in north Mississippi capable of striking into that region and wrecking the line. Keeping them busy became a Union priority, and the battles of Brice's Crossroads and Tupelo were both fought to protect the military railroad.

## Mules, Wagons and a Running Fight

Major General Andrew J. Smith led the Right Wing of the 16th Army Corps out of La Grange, Tennessee, on July 5, with roughly 14,000 to 16,000 men, depending on the count, and 24 guns, including a brigade of United States Colored Troops under Colonel Edward Bouton. The columns met little opposition until they neared Pontotoc, where Confederate Brigadier General James R. Chalmers checked the advance. Lee, also with the Confederate forces, assumed general command, to Forrest's deep resentment. After three attempts to push forward were blocked, Smith turned toward Tupelo on July 12, and a running fight rolled on for ten miles. Near Barrow's Shop, Chalmers and Rucker's Brigade dashed onto the Union wagon train and held it long enough to kill all the mules, forcing Smith's men to burn seven wagons, a caisson and two ambulances before superior numbers drove the raiders off. Smith reached Harrisburg and began reinforcing his position for battle the next morning.

## Heat and Fire at Harrisburg

On the morning of the 14th, Smith held a strong, entrenched position. Lee formed his lines with Roddey's Brigade on the right, Mabry's on the left and Crossland's in the middle, with Bell's beside Mabry. Every man was dismounted, and the Confederates numbered roughly 8,000 to 9,000 men, most of them cavalry. The plan seemed to call for swinging the right, but one brigade became engaged first and fell back with heavy losses. Mabry and Bell were repulsed, and Rucker's Brigade followed with an assault just as futile. Soldiers were swept away by the Union fire, and in the heat many fell from exhaustion. A little after noon the Confederates pulled back and entrenched. Smith did not pursue them. His troops instead tore up the railroad around Tupelo and burned what remained of Harrisburg.

## Old Town Creek

On the 15th, when it became clear that Smith would not attack, Brigadier General Abraham Buford made a demonstration against the Union left. Soon afterward Smith began to withdraw, and the Confederates set off in vigorous pursuit. At Old Town Creek, Buford ran into Union troops drawn up in line of battle and was driven back in confusion. McCulloch's Brigade, sent in regiment by regiment, was quickly repulsed. Forrest was wounded in the foot there and was out of action for about three weeks, and command at the front passed to Chalmers. The pursuit went on, but with only light skirmishing. By the American Battlefield Trust's estimate the two days cost roughly 650 Union and 1,300 Confederate casualties, and other tallies break those totals down differently. Officers fell alongside their men: Colonel Alexander Wilkin was killed leading a Union brigade, and several Confederate colonels were among the wounded.

## A Victory That Left Sherman Irritated

Sherman, closing in on Atlanta, was irritated that Smith had not pressed Forrest harder and let him escape. A Forrest raid into middle Tennessee before Atlanta fell could have been disastrous for the Union. Still, although Smith failed to destroy Forrest's cavalry, he had done his job: Sherman's supply line stayed safe. Forrest recovered from his wound and rallied his men for more daring raids, including one into Memphis that August. Tupelo's growth has since obscured many of the sites where it happened. The Tupelo National Battlefield, first set aside as a War Department site on February 21, 1929, covers just one acre, and the American Battlefield Trust and its partners had preserved another 12 acres by mid-2023.

## Flight Context

Tupelo National Battlefield sits at 34.256N, 88.737W on the west side of Tupelo, Mississippi, near the old Harrisburg ground two miles west of downtown. From 2,000-3,000 feet, the small preserved parcel is lost among modern streets; orient instead on downtown Tupelo to the east and the rail lines running through town. Tupelo Regional Airport (KTUP) is only about 1.8 nm west-northwest, so the battlefield lies close to its traffic pattern; coordinate with Tupelo traffic before maneuvering overhead. Summer brings hazy skies and afternoon thunderstorms.
