# Pink Palace Museum and Planetarium

Piggly Wiggly founder Clarence Saunders lost his pink marble mansion before he could finish it; since 1930 Memphis has filled it with a museum of natural and local history, a planetarium and a replica of the original Piggly Wiggly, widely regarded as the first self-service grocery store.

## Introduction

Clarence Saunders never got to live in his pink marble palace. The Memphis grocer who pioneered the self-service grocery store began building a grand residence of pink Georgia marble in 1922, but a losing battle on Wall Street bankrupted him in 1923, before it was finished. The unfinished mansion later passed to the City of Memphis. What the city did with it proved more lasting than any private home. Since 1930 the Pink Palace has housed a museum, and the museum reports more than 240,000 visitors a year coming to see its fossils, its history galleries, its planetarium and a replica of the store that made Saunders famous.

## The Man Who Invented the Supermarket

In 1916, Memphian Clarence Saunders opened the original Piggly Wiggly, widely regarded as the first self-service grocery store. With it, Saunders is credited with launching the self-service revolution that led to the modern supermarket, and by the early 1920s he was ready to build on a grand scale. In 1922 construction began on his new residence, faced in pink Georgia marble, a color that gave the house its enduring nickname. But Saunders's fortunes turned. A losing battle on Wall Street drove him into bankruptcy in 1923, and the house was still incomplete when it left his hands; the house and about ten surrounding acres were later given to the City of Memphis. The man who pioneered self-service grocery shopping never got to live in the palace he had set out to build.

## A Museum in an Unfinished Mansion

The city turned the empty mansion into a public institution. In March 1930, months after the Stock Market Crash, the Memphis Museum of Natural History and Industrial Arts opened inside it. Four years later, in 1934, the Public Works of Art Project of President Franklin D. Roosevelt's administration commissioned Memphis artist Burton Callicott to paint a three-panel mural for the original main entrance lobby. Part of a wide program of art and public works projects that employed artists during the Great Depression, the murals depict Hernando de Soto and his men arriving at the Mississippi River near the site of Memphis, and their encounters with Native Americans. The murals present that arrival as a "discovery," the language of their time. Callicott went on to help found the Memphis Academy of Art, later the Memphis College of Art, where he taught, and died in 2003.

## From Pottery to Piggly Wiggly

The museum's galleries range from archaeology to chemistry. One exhibit recreates the original Piggly Wiggly store, commemorating Saunders's invention in the very house he lost. Other permanent exhibits include 15th-century Native American pottery, pre-Columbian artifacts, fossils and dinosaurs, mounted animals and Clyde Parke's Miniature Circus. History exhibits explore the roles of music and cotton in shaping Memphis, the Civil War, the changing roles of women and the lives of historic Black Memphians. Several special exhibits arrive each year as well. The mix is eclectic by design, a local and natural history museum that tries to tell the story of a whole region, from 15th-century potters to Memphis musicians to the grocer who pioneered self-service shopping.

## Stars Under the Dome

The Pink Palace also looks upward. The Sharpe Planetarium, housed at the museum, seats 145 people in a theater-in-the-round auditorium, where public shows project star fields, images and laser lights across a domed ceiling. On January 21, 1995, the Spotlight Giant Theater, now called the Giant Screen Theater, opened, with a movable screen four stories high. The museum belongs to the Memphis Museums of Science and History (MoSH), a group of historic, educational and technological attractions maintained by the City of Memphis and Memphis Museums, Inc. Its name has shifted along the way. In 2021 the family of museums was rebranded as MoSH, and the museum was briefly styled the Museum of Science and History - Pink Palace, but its Pink Palace name is in use again. The pink marble, after all, had always made the building's real name obvious.

## Flight Context

The Pink Palace stands at 35.1256°N, 89.9599°W in the Chickasaw Gardens neighborhood of midtown Memphis, Tennessee. From 2,000–3,000 feet, look for the pink marble mansion in the midtown grid; the University of Memphis campus lies about 1.2 nm east-southeast, and 34-story Clark Tower on Poplar Avenue rises about 3.5 nm east-southeast. Memphis's main airport, Frederick W.
