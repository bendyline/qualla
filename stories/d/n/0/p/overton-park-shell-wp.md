# Overton Park Shell

Built by the WPA in 1936 for less than $12,000, the Overton Park Shell in Memphis hosted Elvis Presley's first paid concert in 1954 and survived one demolition plan after another to become a home for free music under the stars.

**Location:** 35.1457, -89.9948

**Categories:** Amphitheaters in the United States, Music of Memphis, Tennessee, Buildings and structures in Memphis, Tennessee, Music venues

## Introduction

On July 30, 1954, the headliner at the band shell in Memphis's Overton Park was Slim Whitman. The opening act was a young singer named Elvis Presley, and it was his first paid concert. That night he performed in what some music historians call the first-ever rock and roll show. The stage he played on was an open-air amphitheater built eighteen years earlier as a New Deal public works project, and it has gone by many names since: the Memphis Open Air Theater, the Shell Theater, the Levitt Shell and, once again, the Overton Park Shell. Today it is a concert and community venue, and it has come remarkably close to disappearing more than once.

## A New Deal Band Shell

The City of Memphis and the Works Progress Administration built the Shell in 1936 for $11,935, as part of the New Deal. The architect Max Furbringer modeled it on similar shells in Chicago, New York and St. Louis. The WPA is said to have built 27 band shells in all, and the Overton Park Shell is described as one of only a few that still remain. Through the 1930s and 1940s it was home to the Memphis Open Air Theater's orchestral shows, along with light opera and musicals. Then came that July night in 1954, when a very different sound rang out from the same stage and gave the Shell its most famous claim to history.

## Saved Again and Again

Fame did not guarantee survival. In the mid-1960s the Shell was turned over to the Memphis Arts Center, which planned to raze it to build a $2 million theater. Noel Gilbert, the long-time conductor of the Memphis Concert Orchestra, led a campaign that gathered 6,000 signatures to prevent its destruction. In 1972 the Shell was nearly removed to make way for a parking garage, and again community outcry saved it. In 1982 the National Conference of Christians and Jews proposed a restoration and renamed the Shell in honor of Raoul Wallenberg, but the group could not raise the necessary funds, and by 1984 the parking plan had returned. This time Mayor Richard Hackett stepped in, pledging to fund a renovation if a private group would lead an arts program. In 1985 the Shell lay dormant for the first time in its history.

## Free Music Under the Stars

Private citizens took up the cause. In 1986 they formed a corporation called Save Our Shell, Inc., and over the next 20 years it presented hundreds of free programs on the old stage. After a partnership with the Levitt Foundation that began in 2005, the Shell was renamed the Levitt Shell at Overton Park, and the Levitt Foundation funded a large-scale renovation carried out by the Memphis firm Askew Nixon Ferguson Architects, with state-of-the-art audio and visual design. When the work was completed in early September 2008, free concerts returned, and the Shell has offered shows on Thursday through Sunday nights during its spring and fall seasons; its 2026 calendar mixes free series with ticketed benefit concerts and wellness classes. In the summer of 2011, Indie Memphis added a Concert Film Series of free weekend screenings. On March 3, 2022, the venue took back its historic name, the Overton Park Shell, continuing its mission of free music for the entire community.
