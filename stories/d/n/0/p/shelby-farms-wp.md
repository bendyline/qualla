# Shelby Farms

The ground beneath one of America's largest urban parks has been an emancipation experiment, a family homestead and a county penal farm; today it is 4,500 acres of lakes, forest, trails and bison in East Memphis.

**Location:** 35.1388, -89.8325

**Categories:** Parks in Memphis, Tennessee, Protected areas of Shelby County, Tennessee, Urban parks, Abolitionism, Rail trails

## Introduction

Few parks have lived as many lives as Shelby Farms. Its ground in East Memphis has held a reformer's experiment in emancipation, a 19th-century homestead, and a county penal farm where prisoners raised crops for 35 years. Today it is a public park of about 4,500 acres, one of the largest urban parks in the United States and more than five times the size of New York's 843-acre Central Park. Lakes, natural forests and the wetlands of the Wolf River spread across its hills, and a herd of bison grazes in a park ringed by commuter routes.

## Nashoba

In 1825 the humanist reformer Frances Wright, an opponent of slavery, founded the Nashoba experiment on 1,940 acres of woodland along the Wolf River. About 670 of those acres, north of the river, now lie within Shelby Farms Park; the rest is in today's Germantown, and other accounts put the commune's acreage differently. Wright gathered enslaved people, free Black people and white residents in a multi-racial commune meant to prepare the enslaved for freedom through education in practical and cultural skills. Under the plan, the enslaved residents were to buy their freedom through their collective labor, a scheme that still asked people held in bondage to pay for their own liberty. As rumors of interracial marriage spread, the commune ran into growing financial trouble, and it was collapsing by about 1828; sources date its end between 1828 and 1830. Wright then chartered a ship herself and, in January 1830 by one account, carried Nashoba's enslaved people, about thirty of them, to Haiti, where she emancipated them.

## What the Manns Left Behind

At the park's eastern tip, a later chapter lies half-hidden in the woods. Here are the remains of a late 19th-century settlement: the ruins of a house, a disintegrated barn, old fences, car wrecks from the 1950s and 1960s, and a family burial ground. By 2005, only one grave marker still stood in the Mann family plot. It records two deaths just ten days apart, Robert W. Mann on November 25, 1891, and Mary S. Mann on December 5, 1891. Five or more other graves are marked only by their base stones. In a park built for bikes, boats and picnics, these quiet ruins are a reminder that people lived, built homes and buried their dead on this land long before it became a place to play.

## The Penal Farm Years

In 1928, Shelby County acquired 1,600 acres here for a penal farm. From 1929 until 1964, people held at the Shelby County Corrections Center worked the fields, raising food for inmates and staff, with surplus sold for profit on behalf of the state of Tennessee. When the farm closed, the land's future was wide open. In 1966 the county planning commission proposed turning it into a planned community, and in 1967 city and county officials heard nine proposals from companies with different ideas for the site. In 1970 the county created a nine-member Shelby Farms Development Board to oversee it, and during that decade the land opened for recreation. Protection came later. In December 2006, the Shelby County Commission granted the Shelby Farms Park Alliance a conservation easement restricting commercial and residential development for 50 years, and in 2007 an agreement with County Mayor A C Wharton created the Shelby Farms Park Conservancy to run the park day to day and plan its future.

## A Million Trees

The Conservancy held a competition for a master plan, won by James Corner Field Operations of New York, which partnered with Marlon Blackwell Architects on a half-dozen new buildings. The plan took shape in 2008 and came to fruition with a $52 million expansion whose ribbon was cut on September 1, 2016. Patriot Lake was greatly enlarged, to nearly 80 acres, and renamed Hyde Lake, new walking and biking trails wound through the grounds, a new visitors center opened, and new trees and meadows were planted under a master plan that spoke of "One Million Trees." In the year after this Heart of the Park project opened, park staff estimated that visitation had more than doubled. One long-running threat also faded. Since the 1990s there had been plans for a road running north from Walnut Grove Road, which bisects the park, to Whitten Road. Friends of Shelby Farms sued in 1999 to halt it pending further environmental study, and the Conservancy later backed a version with pedestrian paths and a ban on tractor-trailers. The project was reportedly scrapped in 2023, and local news attributes the decision to a vote of the region's metropolitan planning organization.

## Bison and the Greenline

Beavers, deer, turtles and many kinds of birds live here, and on nearly 50 acres of pasture roams a bison herd that the park says usually numbers around 15 animals; park superintendent Tommy Hill brought the first six to the park in 1989. Visitors can rent boats on Hyde Lake, circle it on a 2.5-mile paved trail, or head into the woods on the unpaved, 6.08-mile Tour de Wolf. There are horses for hire, a runway for radio-controlled planes, an 18-hole disc golf course, a BMX track and a large off-leash area for dogs. The park also reaches toward the city. The Shelby Farms Greenline, a paved rail trail of about 11 to 13 miles depending on the extension (the park's own pages give 10.65 and 12.94 miles), runs from Tillman Street in Midtown to Macon Road in Cordova. Its first section opened in October 2010 and an extension to Cordova in June 2016, and it is billed as the longest continuous urban trail in Shelby County.
