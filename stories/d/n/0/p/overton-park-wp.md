# Overton Park

Memphis's great Midtown park survived an interstate, a wall and a zoo expansion, keeping its old-growth forest, its WPA band shell where Elvis played his first paid concert, and a Doughboy statue funded in part by schoolchildren and the public.

**Location:** 35.1464, -89.9892

**Categories:** Parks in Memphis, Tennessee, Historic districts on the National Register of Historic Places in Tennessee, Works Progress Administration in Tennessee, Protected areas of Shelby County, Tennessee, Civil rights history

## Introduction

What does it take to stop an interstate highway? In Memphis, it took a small group of Midtown residents and a case that went all the way to the U.S. Supreme Court. In the 1960s and 1970s, highway planners slated part of Overton Park for demolition so Interstate 40 could run through it and carry suburban commuters downtown. The neighbors formed Citizens to Preserve Overton Park, sued, and won the landmark case Citizens to Preserve Overton Park v. Volpe. The park they saved holds an old-growth forest, the Memphis Zoo, and the band shell where Elvis Presley gave his first paid concert.

## From Lea's Woods to Overton

Memphis bought the land, then known locally as Lea's Woods, on November 14, 1901, for $110,000; at the time it sat on the city's eastern and northern edge. Landscape architect George Kessler designed the park as part of a comprehensive plan that also produced Riverside Park and the Memphis Parkway System. The name came from a contest run by the Evening Scimitar newspaper, in which readers chose among three of the city's founding fathers: Andrew Jackson, James Winchester and John Overton. Overton won, and the naming became official on July 25, 1902. The park was established in 1906. The Formal Gardens were laid out in 1904 and 1905, and Kessler's Picnic Pavilion, built in 1904, still stands under a nearly continuous canopy of mature oaks in the East Picnic Area. The Main Pavilion, the park's principal gathering place from 1902, did not last; a storm damaged it beyond repair in 1936.

## The Highway That Never Came

The Supreme Court victory did not end the fight. The City of Memphis and the Tennessee Department of Transportation kept proposing ways to route Interstate 40 through the park, including a tunnel and a deep trench. In 1978, Citizens to Preserve Overton Park successfully nominated the park to the National Register of Historic Places, which meant federal money could not be spent on projects that damaged its historic integrity without approval from the U.S. Department of the Interior. The Memphis Commercial Appeal called the listing the "final nail in the coffin" for the interstate plan. Meanwhile, starting in June 1974, the interior roads closed to motor vehicles on weekends and holidays, dubbed "People's Days." There were objections at first, but the idea caught on, and on April 13, 1987, the closures became permanent for everyone but official vehicles.

## Who the Park Belongs To

Not every battle over Overton Park ended in the public's favor. When the entirely white Hein Park community walled off access to the park through West Drive, predominantly Black neighbors from across Jackson Avenue sued under the Civil Rights Act of 1866. In 1981, a narrowly divided Supreme Court found the barrier legal. Justice Thurgood Marshall dissented, warning that "a group of white citizens has decided to act to keep Negro citizens from traveling through their urban 'utopia' and the city has placed its seal of approval on the scheme." Later conflicts turned on the land itself. In February 2008, the Memphis Zoo cleared old-growth forest for its Teton Trek exhibit. Park advocates answered with a 16-month campaign that ended in 2011 with the Tennessee General Assembly creating the Old Forest State Natural Area, minus some forest beside the zoo that was left out to ease opposition from the mayor and zoo officials. In December 2011, the nonprofit Overton Park Conservancy was founded to manage much of the park, and it still does so under an agreement with the city; a 2022 deal between the Conservancy, the zoo and the city kept zoo parking off the Greensward and protected part of the forest from further zoo development.

## Songs in the Shell

On July 30, 1954, Elvis Presley gave his first paid concert at the park's band shell. The Shell itself was a New Deal project, built in 1936 by the City of Memphis and the Works Progress Administration for $11,935. Architect Max Furbringer modeled it on similar shells in Chicago, New York and St. Louis, and it is described as one of only a few still standing of the 27 band shells the WPA is said to have built. After passing through various owners, it became the Levitt Shell at Overton Park after a partnership with the Levitt Foundation that began in 2005, and a renovation underwritten by the foundation was completed in early September 2008, bringing free concerts back; on March 3, 2022 it returned to its historic name, the Overton Park Shell. The Memphis Brooks Museum of Art, founded in 1916 and describing itself as the oldest and largest art museum in Tennessee, grew here to a collection of more than 10,000 works before announcing in 2017 that it would move downtown. Its Overton Park building is now closed, and the museum, renamed the Memphis Art Museum, has said its new riverfront home will open to the public in December 2026. The Old Forest remains one of the few old-growth forests left in the state, with more than 300 plant varieties and markers naming 32 tree species.

## A Doughboy for the Park

The hallmark of Veterans Plaza, south of the zoo, is a bronze Doughboy statue put up in September 1926. The local chapter of the Daughters of the American Revolution raised the money with help from the public and schoolchildren and hired sculptor Nancy Coonsman Hahn to make it. Nearby, the Memory Grove memorial, created in 1932 by the Memphis chapter of the American War Mothers to honor their sons killed in World War I, bears 27 names on a bronze plaque. Limestone walls carry the names of Shelby County's dead from World War II, Korea, Vietnam and Desert Storm, and a plaque pays honor to 1,525 Shelby County veterans killed in the wars of the 20th and 21st centuries. Just beyond lies the Greensward, one of the largest open spaces in Memphis with no purpose other than outdoor recreation. There are no assigned spots. People simply stake a temporary claim to an empty patch of grass for frisbee, kites, music or a picnic.
