# Nashoba Community

In 1825 Frances Wright founded Nashoba on the Wolf River near today's Germantown, Tennessee, as a test of a plan for enslaved people to earn their freedom; it collapsed within a few years, and Wright herself carried the community's enslaved people to freedom in Haiti.

**Location:** 35.1465, -89.8449

**Categories:** Utopian communities in the United States, Owenism, Shelby County, Tennessee, Populated places established in 1825

## Introduction

For a brief time during World War I, the Memphis suburb of Germantown, Tennessee, set aside its name as a protest against Germany and called itself Neshoba (a spelling also written Nashoba) instead. The new name was old. Nashoba, sometimes spelled Neshoba, is the Chickasaw name for the nearby Wolf River, and it had already been attached to an ambitious and deeply troubled experiment. In 1825 Frances "Fanny" Wright founded the Nashoba Community on woodland along the Wolf River, beside present-day Germantown (a tract that began at about 320 acres and later grew to some 2,000), to show that slavery could be ended without slaveholders losing money.

## A Plan to Buy Freedom

Wright's idea was a full-compensation emancipation plan. Through a system of unified labor, enslaved people would earn the price of their own freedom, so that no slaveholder would lose money, and once free they would be transported to Haiti or to the settlements that became Liberia. Nashoba was to be a small-scale demonstration, a place to educate enslaved people and prepare them for freedom and colonization abroad. Wright believed that slaveholders were "anxious to manumit their people, but apprehensive about throwing them unprepared into the world," and that if emancipation came without financial loss, Southern planters would adopt it. If the experiment worked, she imagined, its methods could be applied throughout the nation.

## Property Until Paid For

Reality fell short. Wright raised funds and recruited settlers, among the first of them the Englishman George Flower and his family, who had founded another settlement in Albion, Illinois. But she could not raise enough money, and she ended up spending a good portion of her own fortune to buy the land and the enslaved people who would work it. Nashoba is sometimes remembered as an egalitarian, interracial community, but it never reached those goals. The men and women who labored there remained Wright's property until they could buy themselves out, their freedom deferred by a debt they had not chosen. By one account the community had only 20 members at its largest, though other accounts count about thirty enslaved people there, nearly half of them children. The writer Frances Trollope visited Wright there around the turn of 1828 and later described Nashoba briefly in her 1832 book Domestic Manners of the Americans.

## Collapse and a Voyage to Haiti

The community's interim managers introduced the concept of free love, and in practice the relationships that followed crossed racial lines but were far from egalitarian, in a community where many residents were still legally the property of its founder. The experiment was collapsing by about 1828, and sources date its end between 1828 and 1830. Wright did not abandon the people bound to her project. In January 1830, by one account, she personally chartered a ship, carried Nashoba's enslaved people (about thirty, nearly half of them children) to Haiti and emancipated them there, keeping at least the final promise of her plan.

## A Name That Stayed

Nashoba left little on the ground but a great deal in memory. The name still turns up around Germantown, on local places such as Neshoba Road, which runs between Kirby Parkway and Kimbrough Road. In 1992 a new congregation in the area where the commune once stood took the name as well, founding the Neshoba Unitarian Universalist Church, which meets in nearby Cordova. Far away in Virginia, the Twin Oaks Community, an intentional community founded in 1967, names its buildings after earlier communities, and one of its residences is called Nashoba.

## Flight Context

The Nashoba Community site lies at about 35.1465°N, 89.8449°W, in the woods along the Wolf River on the edge of Germantown, east of Memphis, Tennessee. Nothing of the commune is visible from the air, so use the winding, tree-lined course of the Wolf River and the suburban grid of Germantown as references from 1,500–2,500 feet. Memphis's main airport, Frederick W. Smith International (KMEM), is about 9 nm southwest, and its busy airspace covers much of the area; Charles W. Baker Airport (FAA identifier 2M8, no ICAO code) is about 9 nm north-northwest and Olive Branch Airport (KOLV) about 11 nm south-southeast. Expect summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms.
