# Libertyland

Memphis's Libertyland opened on July 4, 1976, gave Elvis Presley's favorite wooden coaster a new name, and after closing in 2005 scattered its rides from Wisconsin to the Philippines.

**Location:** 35.1185, -89.9824

**Categories:** Amusement parks, Defunct amusement parks, Memphis, Tennessee, Roller coasters, Elvis Presley

## Introduction

Elvis Presley's favorite wooden roller coaster now runs, rebuilt from the original's design, in Green Bay, Wisconsin. The park's looping steel coaster was reassembled at an amusement park in the Philippines, and a carousel built in 1909, which arrived at the fairgrounds in 1923, turns today on the Children's Museum of Memphis campus. All of them once shared a patch of the Mid-South Fairgrounds as Libertyland, a Memphis amusement park that opened on July 4, 1976, the nation's bicentennial, and closed in 2005. Where it stood, a youth sports complex now hosts tournaments.

## Opening Day on the Fourth

Libertyland was owned by the Mid-South Fair, and it did not start from scratch. Several new rides joined attractions that had already been operating on the fairgrounds, including two that had been on the fairgrounds since 1923: a wooden coaster called the Pippin, built in 1913 at East End Park and reopened at the fairgrounds in 1923, and the Grand Carousel, built in 1909 and bought by the city's park commission in 1923. With the park's opening, the Pippin became the Zippin Pippin, and it was widely popular as Elvis Presley's favorite wooden coaster. The carousel was a classic that visitors widely appreciated. Around them grew a lineup that included the Old Hickory Log Flume, Twain's Twister, the Tennessee Tilt and Casey's Cannonball Train Ride. Later came the Revolution, a loop-and-corkscrew steel coaster built by Arrow Development in 1979.

## Crowds, but Never Profits

Libertyland gradually drew decent crowds but never made much money. It kept adding rides, including a Top Spin called the Tidal Wave in the late 1990s, replaced in April 2002 by the Rebellion, a drop tower that brought larger crowds. It was not enough. For years the park's financial troubles were widely known. Few new attractions arrived, and attendance began to slip. When the vote came to close it in 2005, the reasons given were the lack of profit, the steady decline in attendance and a wish to extend the midway for the annual fair, which ran for ten days each October next door. The closing followed that of the Adventure River water park, which shut in September 1998, by less than ten years.

## Save Libertyland!

Memphians fought back. A group called Save Libertyland! formed to push the Mid-South Fair to reopen the park, arguing that it created hundreds of jobs for local teenagers and gave families an affordable place to spend time. The group went to court to block the auction of the rides, arguing that the City of Memphis owned them, and the city ended up claiming ownership of the Zippin Pippin and the Grand Carousel. The group also recruited companies willing to run the park, chiefly Joyland, Inc., known for buying struggling amusement parks and turning them around. The fair held its auction anyway, on June 21, 2006. In December of that year Joyland sent the city a letter of intent to build a new park on the site under a three-year lease, but after seeing the grounds in person it withdrew, citing extensive damage to the wiring, plumbing and other infrastructure.

## Where the Rides Went

Most of the rides sold at auction and scattered around the country and beyond. The Zippin Pippin was bought by the city of Green Bay, whose city-owned Bay Beach Amusement Park rebuilt it from the original's plans after the old structure had deteriorated too far; it opened there on May 21, 2011, and is on the park's 2026 ride list. The Revolution went first to DelGrosso's Amusement Park, which never reassembled it, and eventually to a park in the Philippines, where it reopened in 2014 as Zimerman. The Rebellion drop tower reopened in May 2007 at the Maggie Valley, North Carolina, park then called Ghost Town Village, as the Geronimo Drop; that park has since closed and been abandoned. The Grand Carousel stayed home: leased and restored by the Children's Museum of Memphis, it began turning again in a new pavilion in late 2017. In 2022 the 227,000-square-foot Memphis Sports and Events Center, with two adjacent soccer fields, was built on the site of the amusement park.

## Flight Context

The former Libertyland site lies at about 35.119N, 89.982W on the old Mid-South Fairgrounds in Memphis, Tennessee, about 4 miles east-southeast of downtown. From 2,000-2,500 feet, look for the large stadium and the broad Tiger Lane green space and parking beside it; the new sports complex and its soccer fields occupy the old park ground. Frederick W. Smith International Airport (KMEM), formerly Memphis International, the city's main airline and cargo airport, is only about 4.5 nm south, so this area sits under busy arrival and departure paths; coordinate with Memphis approach before maneuvering. General DeWitt Spain Airport (FAA identifier M01, no ICAO code) is about 6 nm northwest.
