# Orange Mound, Memphis

Built in the 1890s on the grounds of a Memphis plantation, Orange Mound became a proud center of Black life, weathered decline and the crack era, and keeps renewing itself through its churches, schools and music.

**Location:** 35.1078, -89.9720

**Categories:** Neighborhoods in Memphis, Tennessee, African-American history in Memphis, Tennessee, Populated places in Tennessee established by African Americans, Civil rights history, Music

## Introduction

During W. W. Herenton's first term as mayor of Memphis, Tyler Glover presented him with an orange key. It was the "key to Orange Mound," handed over by the man neighbors called the "Mayor of Orange Mound," whose restaurant, Tyler's Place on Park Avenue, served as the community's unofficial city hall. "This is the greatest community in the world," Glover once said. "I have had numerous opportunities to move some place else, but there is no other place in the world I want to live, but Orange Mound, Tenn." His neighborhood carries a remarkable distinction: it is often described as the first US neighborhood built by and for African Americans.

## Lots for Less Than $100

The ground had been a plantation. Between 1825 and 1830, John Deaderick bought 5,000 acres in what is now southeastern Memphis and built a stately house near today's Airways Boulevard, between Carnes and Spottswood. In 1890, a developer named Elzey Eugene Meacham (spelled Meachem in some sources) bought land from the Deaderick family and began laying out a subdivision for African Americans, selling lots for less than $100 to give people of modest means a chance at affordable land and homes. The typical house of the 1890s was a small, narrow shotgun. Tradition holds that the name came from the Osage orange hedges, or in some tellings mock-orange shrubs, on the grounds of the old homeplace. Memphis annexed the community in 1919. Orange Mound has been home to roughly 14,000 people, about 95 percent of them African American, bounded by Semmes Street on the east, Lamar and Kimball Avenues on the south, and the Southern Avenue railroad tracks that run past the Mid-South Coliseum.

## A Refuge With Unpaved Streets

Orange Mound's early streets were unpaved, but the community that grew along them was vibrant, a mix of homes, businesses, churches and cultural centers. It became a refuge for Black people arriving in the city for the first time from rural areas, and by the 1970s it was billed as "the largest concentration of blacks in the United States except for Harlem in New York City." Churches anchored it, developing community leaders and fostering stability. Mount Moriah Missionary Baptist Church has stood at the corner of Carnes Avenue and David Street since 1926. Mt. Pisgah CME Church, at Park Avenue and Marechalneil, played a role in the Civil Rights Movement by assisting activists jailed for their work in support of racial equality. During the era of desegregation, though, the neighborhood entered a period of decline as younger residents began to move away.

## Hard Years, Hard Work

Drugs and alcohol had troubled the neighborhood for years, but crack brought violence and drug dealing that devastated poor and middle-class families alike. Since the 1990s, Orange Mound has improved considerably as crime has fallen and residents and partners have worked to revitalize it. The Orange Mound Collaborative, funded by a Ford Foundation grant and built around "education through empowerment," launched an Early Childhood Institute and an oral history project that recorded videotaped interviews with the neighborhood's older residents. In 2003 Orange Mound was named one of 21 Memphis areas in the S.M.A.R.T. Revitalization Plan, a public-private partnership for declining neighborhoods. A year later, Robert Lipscomb, director of the city's Housing and Community Development division, wrote that code enforcement, tenant education and cleanup efforts had brought much progress. With help from the Mid-South Peace & Justice Center, residents also planted the Orange Mound Community Garden, meant to beautify the streets, supply nutritious food, teach leadership and encourage self-reliance.

## Friday Nights and Fresh Beats

On Fridays in football season, the neighborhood gathers at Melrose Stadium to cheer the Golden Wildcats, and Melrose High School and Dunbar Elementary remain sources of pride; in the fall of 2009 the school opened its stadium with a new field, bleachers and state-of-the-art technology. Music runs deep here too. Orange Mound hosts a growing underground rap scene, and the hit duo 8 Ball & MJG, Premro Smith and Marlon Jermain Goodwin, grew up in the neighborhood. Author Jay Fingers, another native, made it the title and setting of his novel Orange Mound. In 2021, plans were announced to turn the abandoned United Equipment Building, an old feed mill, into the Orange Mound Tower, a proposed $50 million multi-use project led by local arts organizer Victoria Jones and music producer IMAKEMADBEATS, who grew up in the neighborhood. By April 2024 the project had finished predevelopment and entered its design and schematics phase, and we found no report of construction since. Tyler Glover died in September 2019, but the neighborhood had already named his successor in 2007: Melrose veteran and Hall of Famer Jason Smith.

## Flight Context

Coordinates: 35.1078 N, 89.972 W, in southeastern Memphis, Tennessee, a few miles north of Frederick W. Smith International Airport (KMEM), formerly Memphis International. The neighborhood sits directly beneath KMEM's Class B airspace and approach paths, so any flyover must be coordinated with Memphis Approach. Look for the Mid-South Coliseum and the Liberty Bowl stadium at the fairgrounds along the neighborhood's northwestern edge, and the University of Memphis district just east of Semmes Street. Nearby airports: KMEM about 4 nm south; General DeWitt Spain Airport (FAA identifier M01; no ICAO code) about 7 nm northwest; Millington-Memphis (KNQA) about 16 nm north. Summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are common.
