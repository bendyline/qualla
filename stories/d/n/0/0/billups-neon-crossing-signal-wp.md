# Billups Neon Crossing Signal

For about three decades, drivers approaching an Illinois Central crossing in Grenada, Mississippi, were greeted by a neon skull and crossbones flashing "STOP - DEATH - STOP" and an air raid siren, Alonzo Billups's experimental answer to a deadly crossing.

**Location:** 33.8055, -89.8016

**Categories:** Illinois Central Railroad, Railway signaling in the United States, Level crossings, Buildings and structures in Grenada County, Mississippi, Individual signs in the United States, 1930s establishments in Mississippi

## Introduction

STOP. DEATH. STOP. When a train approached the Illinois Central crossing on the highway at Grenada, Mississippi, those words blazed out in alternating blue and red neon beneath a glowing skull and crossbones, stretched across the road on a giant gantry. An air raid siren wailed in place of the usual bells, and flashing neon arrows pointed the way the train was coming. Locals called it the "Skull and Crossbones." It was the invention of Alonzo Billups, installed in the second half of the 1930s (one account says 1940) at a crossing notorious for collisions, and it was unlike anything drivers were likely to have seen anywhere else in the country.

## A Crossing That Kept Killing

The crossing where Mississippi Highway 7, now Mississippi Highway 332, met the Illinois Central Railroad had a grim record. Collisions between trains and motor vehicles were numerous, and concern was growing. In the second half of the 1930s (his design patent was issued in 1936) the inventor Alonzo Billups installed his experimental signal there. His answer was to make the warning impossible to ignore. Billups built a large gantry that spanned the entire highway, putting the warning directly in drivers' line of sight. It was likely the first use of a gantry-style crossing signal of the kind now relatively common across the country, a style of crossing that later came into relatively common use.

## Neon, Skulls and Sirens

Every element of the Billups signal was designed to shock. As a train approached, a giant neon sign lit up in alternating blue and red with the words "STOP - DEATH - STOP" beneath a skull and crossbones. Flashing neon arrows showed which direction the oncoming train was traveling. Instead of the bells used at typical crossings, an air raid siren, the kind built for civil defense, sounded the alarm. Billups did not rely on theatrics alone. As a backup, standard railway flashers were mounted below the neon display, so drivers still saw the familiar warning even if the neon failed. The combination of light, symbol and sound earned the Grenada crossing its local nickname, and it was a warning built to be felt as much as seen.

## Undone by War and a Stuck Siren

The Billups signal never spread beyond its prototype. World War II made neon scarce, and the signal also suffered from maintenance problems, which often showed up as the siren sounding continuously until a crew arrived to shut it off. Together, the neon shortage and the upkeep headaches meant that no further signals were ever produced. The original stayed in place for decades, eventually joined by standard railway crossing signals installed alongside it. Around 1970 the neon gantry was removed entirely, and the standard signals took over the job alone. The Skull and Crossbones has since found an afterlife in miniature: in the 2020s, Lionel has produced models of the Billups crossing for O-gauge toy train layouts, letting hobbyists light up "STOP - DEATH - STOP" on their own tabletop railroads.

## Flight Context

The Billups crossing site is at 33.8055°N, 89.8016°W on Mississippi Highway 332 (formerly Highway 7) at the former Illinois Central line (now the Grenada Railroad) on the north side of Grenada, Mississippi, whose center is about 2 nm south-southwest. The neon gantry is long gone, so look for the highway and the rail line crossing north of town; the old Grenada railroad station lies about 1.4 nm south along the tracks, and the waters of Grenada Lake on the Yalobusha River spread to the east-northeast. Grenada Municipal Airport (KGNF) is only about 1.6 nm north, so the crossing sits close to its traffic pattern; view it from pattern altitude or above and watch for arriving and departing aircraft. Expect summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms typical of north Mississippi.
