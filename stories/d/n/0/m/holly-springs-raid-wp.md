# Holly Springs Raid

On December 20, 1862, Earl Van Dorn's cavalry surprised Grant's supply depot at Holly Springs, burning $1.5 million in supplies, turning back a Union advance on Vicksburg and delaying Grant's order expelling Jews from his district.

**Location:** 34.7733, -89.4464

**Categories:** Battles of the American Civil War in Mississippi, Battles of the western theater of the American Civil War, Holly Springs, Mississippi, Conflicts in 1862, Jewish American history

## Introduction

When Confederate cavalry set fire to Ulysses S. Grant's supply depot at Holly Springs, Mississippi, on December 20, 1862, the damage reached far beyond one smoking railroad town. Earl Van Dorn's raid, together with Nathan Bedford Forrest's strikes in West Tennessee, forced Grant's army to withdraw to Memphis. It also cut Grant's communications for weeks, obstructing the full implementation of General Order No. 11, which three days earlier had expelled Jews as a class from his military district. Because the order was slow to spread, some Jewish families escaped expulsion, though about thirty were expelled from Paducah, Kentucky.

## A Railroad to Vicksburg

That autumn, Grant had taken command of the Department of Tennessee and begun the Vicksburg campaign, planning to march south along the Mississippi Central Railroad with 40,000 troops. Across the lines, Van Dorn had been defeated at the Second Battle of Corinth in October, and Jefferson Davis had placed John C. Pemberton above him. A prisoner exchange swelled Van Dorn's force to 24,000, but many of his men were in poor health. He fell back from Holly Springs on November 9 to a line behind the Tallahatchie River, and in December behind the Yalobusha, covering Grenada. Grant followed, and Holly Springs became his supply base. Then Lieutenant Colonel John S. Griffiths of the 6th Texas Cavalry proposed a bold answer: raid that base. Pemberton liked the idea and gave Van Dorn three brigades of cavalry to try it. Meanwhile, William Tecumseh Sherman was preparing to take 32,000 troops from Memphis down the Mississippi to strike Vicksburg from the north.

## The Long Way Around

Van Dorn left Grenada on December 16 with 3,500 cavalrymen. The Texas brigade, 1,500 strong, rode under Griffiths' temporary command; William Hicks Jackson led 1,200 Tennesseans, and Robert McCulloch led 800 horsemen from Arkansas, Mississippi and Missouri. The column first swung east to get around the Union flank, reaching Houston, Mississippi, at noon on December 17 and Pontotoc the next day. After passing New Albany, the raiders turned west toward Holly Springs. The town's Union commander, Colonel Robert C. Murphy of the 8th Wisconsin Infantry, was warned that a Confederate force was loose, but he failed to alert his garrison. That September, as Confederates approached Iuka, Mississippi, Murphy had abandoned a depot there and lost a large store of supplies, but a court-martial acquitted him and he stayed in the service. His troops were now spread across three camps, none close enough to support the others: cavalry on the edge of town, infantry at the courthouse and at the train station.

## Over by Eight O'Clock

Van Dorn split his force into three columns, one for each camp. At dawn on December 20, two of them overran the infantry, while the third caught the Union cavalry in the middle of an inspection; the Federal horsemen fought briefly before they were overwhelmed. By 8:00 a.m. it was over. The raiders broke into the food stores and helped themselves to brand-new weapons, and the spoils included three full freight trains waiting to be unloaded. The Confederates captured 1,500 Union soldiers and paroled them on the spot; only 130 escaped capture. Then roughly $1.5 million worth of supplies went up in flames. "I want those fellows caught, if possible," Grant said, and he sent columns in pursuit. Van Dorn pushed north along the railroad instead, wrecking track and bridges almost to Bolivar, Tennessee. Only at Davis' Mills was he checked, where Colonel W. H. Morgan's 300 men, defending a fortified Indian mound beside a trestle over the Wolf River, reportedly killed 22 raiders, wounded 30 and captured 20 while suffering three wounded. The raiders slipped past their pursuers and were back in Grenada on December 28.

## Living Off the Land

Historian Shelby Foote summed up Grant's predicament: "Van Dorn having destroyed his supplies on hand and Forrest having made it impossible for him to bring up any more," the Union general had to retreat. His army still had to eat. "It could not be expected that men, with arms in their hands, would starve in the midst of plenty," Grant remarked, and he sent escorted wagons to take food and forage from the country on either side of the railroad. He was skeptical at first. The wagons came back loaded with food seized from local inhabitants, enough, he found, to have fed his soldiers for perhaps two months. "This taught me a lesson," he wrote. With Grant's thrust stopped, Pemberton was free to shift troops against Sherman, who was repulsed at Chickasaw Bayou on December 29 with 1,700 casualties. Murphy protested that "my force was inadequate," but he was dismissed from the army, without a court-martial, over the loss of the depot.

## An Order Delayed

The raid also collided with a controversial order Grant had just issued. His General Order No. 11 of December 17, 1862, expelled Jews as a class from his military district within 24 hours, because he believed Jewish traders were violating Treasury Department rules on the cotton trade. It swept up every Jewish person in the district, not only the traders he suspected. The Confederate raids tangled the order's path. Forrest's West Tennessee raid, begun in mid-December, had destroyed communications along fifty miles of the Mobile and Ohio Railroad, and Van Dorn's strike disrupted Union lines for weeks. The order was not fully enforced at Paducah, Kentucky, until December 28, 11 days after it was issued, when about thirty Jewish families were expelled; local Jewish leaders then appealed directly to President Abraham Lincoln. Lincoln had General-in-Chief Henry Halleck order Grant to revoke it. When Grant ran for president in 1868, the order proved such an embarrassment that he disavowed it.
