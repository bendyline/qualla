# Rowan Oak

William Faulkner bought a run-down house built in the 1840s in Oxford, Mississippi, renamed it for two trees, wrote his way to a Nobel Prize there, and left the outline of a novel on his office walls.

**Location:** 34.3598, -89.5247

**Categories:** Historic house museums, William Faulkner, Greek Revival architecture, National Historic Landmarks, Oxford, Mississippi, Literary landmarks

## Introduction

The driveway runs between rows of Eastern red cedars, planted in an era when people believed cedar trees purified the air of yellow fever. At the end of the alley stands a primitive Greek Revival house built around 1844, and inside, on the walls of a small office, is the outline of a novel, written directly on the walls by William Faulkner. He lived at Rowan Oak for more than three decades, and the house has been kept almost exactly as he left it.

## Before Faulkner

The house was built around 1844 (sources give dates from about 1840 to 1848) for Robert B. Sheegog, an Irish immigrant farmer from Tennessee, on four landscaped acres now surrounded by 29 acres of woods known as Bailey Woods. It was designed in an L shape, with a 450-square-foot center hall joining a parlor and dining room on one side to a library on the other, and a quarter-turn stair climbing to three bedrooms. Several antebellum features survive: the cedar alley, a concentric circle garden, a post oak barn and a dwelling where enslaved people lived, a reminder that the house's first years rested on their bondage; university curators cite records showing at least nine enslaved people living and working on the property. The Sheegogs stayed until 1872, when the Bailey family bought the place. Around the turn of the century Julia Bailey added an indoor kitchen, a pantry, a front porch and a bathroom, and enclosed the dogtrot hallway in the servants' area. The Baileys lived there until 1923.

## Two Trees for a Name

Faulkner bought the house in 1930, when it was in disrepair, and in 1931 he renamed it Rowan Oak after two trees: the rowan of Scotland, for peace and security, and the live oak, for strength and solitude. Then he set about making it livable. Through the 1930s he installed plumbing and electricity, framed the front portico with brick terraces and balustrades, and added a porch off the dining room, a porte-cochere on the west side, a fourth bedroom, a butler's pantry and a kitchen, along with other structural changes. The 1844 house became a working writer's home, with the woods pressing close around it.

## A Novel on the Walls

Faulkner settled here with his wife, Estelle, and her two children from a previous marriage, Malcolm and Victoria, and within a few years their daughter Jill was born. He kept horses on the property for riding, jumping and, occasionally, fox hunting. The years at Rowan Oak were productive: Faulkner received the Nobel Prize in Literature (the 1949 prize, awarded in 1950), and in 1955 his 1954 novel A Fable brought him both the Pulitzer Prize and the National Book Award. In 1951 he added a private office on the ground floor, along with a closet and bathroom, and he spent the last decade of his career writing there. On its walls he wrote the outline of A Fable, where visitors can still see it. Rowan Oak remained the family home until Faulkner's death in 1962.

## Kept as He Left It

The University of Mississippi bought Rowan Oak in 1972 and has preserved it as it was at the time of Faulkner's death, with about 90 percent of the original furnishings still in place. It became a National Historic Landmark in 1968 and is open to visitors year-round, except Mondays. A long list of writers has made the trip: John Updike, Czesław Miłosz, Alice Walker, Salman Rushdie, Richard Ford and the Coen brothers are among the visitors. One, the writer Mark Richard, even repaired a faulty doorknob on the French door to Faulkner's study. The house still held surprises after it opened. Its first curator, Ole Miss alumna Bev Smith, is said to have found a large number of Faulkner's original manuscripts hidden in the closet under the stairs.

## Flight Context

Rowan Oak sits at 34.360N, 89.525W on the south side of Oxford, Mississippi, just south of downtown and east of the University of Mississippi campus. From 1,500-2,000 feet the house itself is hard to spot beneath its cedars and the surrounding Bailey Woods; look for the dark patch of forest south of town rather than the building. University-Oxford Airport (KUOX) is about 1.6 nm north-northwest, close enough that overflights should respect its traffic pattern; Panola County Airport (KPMU) at Batesville is about 18 nm west. Summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are common in north Mississippi.
