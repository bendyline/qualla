# Ole Miss riot of 1962

When Air Force veteran James Meredith arrived to enroll at the University of Mississippi in 1962, a segregationist mob besieged the federal marshals guarding him, two men died, and nearly 31,000 troops were mobilized before he walked into his first class.

**Location:** 34.3655, -89.5360

**Categories:** Civil rights movement, Riots, University of Mississippi, Oxford, Mississippi, 1962 in Mississippi, National Historic Landmarks

## Introduction

Around the time Chief Marshal McShane ordered his officers to fire tear gas into the crowd outside the Lyceum at the University of Mississippi, President John F. Kennedy went live from the Oval Office. It was the evening of Sunday, September 30, 1962, and the president thanked the southerners who had "contributed to the progress of our democratic development" by admitting Black students to state universities elsewhere in the South. His aides had not been able to tell him before airtime that the violence was beginning. By the time it ended, two men were dead and more than 300 people had been injured, all over whether one Black student would be allowed to attend class.

## "Which One Is Meredith?"

James Meredith had served in the Air Force from 1951 to 1960 and completed coursework at Jackson State College (now Jackson State University). Shortly after Kennedy's 1961 inauguration, he applied to Ole Miss, which had never enrolled an African American student. Mississippi's governor, Ross Barnett, set out to stop him in person. At one registration attempt, Barnett read a proclamation on states' rights. At another he delivered a rehearsed quip: "Which one is Meredith?" Another attempt was turned back by Lieutenant Governor Paul B. Johnson Jr. and lines of state troopers. The standoff ended only when the federal government sent Meredith to campus under guard of U.S. marshals, setting up a direct confrontation between state and federal power.

## Siege at the Lyceum

Meredith and dozens of marshals arrived on campus that Sunday evening. Twenty-four of them escorted him to Baxter Hall, a guarded dormitory, while the rest turned the Lyceum, the university's administration building, into their operational headquarters. At about 7:25 p.m. the state highway patrol, which had been holding the crowd back, began to withdraw through the influence of Barnett's ally, State Senator George Yarbrough. The mob swelled to about 2,500. Rioters assaulted reporters and hurled Molotov cocktails and acid bottles at the marshals and border patrolmen ringing the building, and at 7:50 McShane ordered the tear gas. Edwin Walker, a former Army major general, appeared on campus to encourage the mob after broadcasting a radio appeal for 10,000 volunteers to "rally to the cause of freedom." At 11 p.m. Barnett went on the radio himself: "We will never surrender!" By the end, by one count, about 160 marshals had been injured, 28 of them with gunshot wounds; other tallies differ.

## Two Lives Lost

Two civilians did not survive the night. Paul Guihard, a French journalist on assignment for Agence France-Presse, was found behind the Lyceum with a gunshot wound in his back. Ray Gunter, a 23-year-old white jukebox repairman who had come to campus out of curiosity, was found with a bullet wound in his forehead. Law enforcement officials described both as execution-style killings. The FBI examined 450 guns belonging to the marshals, border patrolmen and federal prison guards and found that the fatal bullets matched none of them, though the personal weapons of agents who quit their services after the riot were never tested. In 2009 the Society of Professional Journalists dedicated a bench to Guihard on the campus where he died.

## An Army Comes to Oxford

Shortly after 2 a.m., the first Army detachment, from the 503rd Military Police, reached the campus. More followed: soldiers of the 82nd and 101st Airborne Divisions flew into Oxford aboard military transport aircraft, machine-gun nests went up along the roads, and a radio observation post appeared on the roof of Oxford Elementary School. After a plea from the mayor, the last of the rioting was quelled late in the morning of October 1. That day Meredith enrolled and attended his first class, in American Colonial History, the first Black student to attend the University of Mississippi. More than 30,000 personnel were mobilized in all, described as the largest force for a single disturbance in American history, and at the peak 20,000 troops camped on campus, outnumbering students five to one. Walker was arrested and charged with insurrection, though the charges were later dropped. On October 1, soldiers raiding a campus fraternity house found a large weapons cache.

## The Last Battle of Massive Resistance

The danger did not end with the riot. The FBI learned that the Ku Klux Klan planned to lynch Meredith once security eased, and hundreds of troops guarded him around the clock. To appease local sensitivities, Robert Kennedy is reported to have secretly ordered some 4,000 Black soldiers removed from the force, a move Meredith decried. He persisted and graduated on August 18, 1963, with a degree in political science. According to historian William Doyle, his admission "crushed forever" the segregationist strategy of massive resistance, though federal power would be called on again, when President Kennedy federalized the Alabama National Guard at the University of Alabama in 1963 and the Guard was federalized again to escort the Selma-to-Montgomery march in 1965. At the University of Alabama in 1963, Governor George Wallace stepped aside after having signaled privately that he wanted to avoid the kind of violence seen at Ole Miss. Ira B. Harkey Jr. won the 1963 Pulitzer Prize for Editorial Writing for his coverage, and Bob Dylan sang about it in "Oxford Town." A statue of Meredith was dedicated on campus in 2006, and in 2008 the site of the riot was designated a National Historic Landmark.

## Flight Context

The riot centered on the Lyceum and the Circle at the heart of the University of Mississippi campus in Oxford, at about 34.365N, 89.536W. From 2,000-3,000 feet, look for the Lyceum near the center of the campus on the west side of town, with downtown Oxford just to the east. University-Oxford Airport (KUOX) lies only about 1 nm north of campus, so plan on overflying the pattern with care; Panola County Airport (KPMU) at Batesville is about 18 nm west. North Mississippi summers bring haze and afternoon thunderstorms.
