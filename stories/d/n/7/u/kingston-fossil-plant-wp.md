# Kingston Fossil Plant

Built to power Oak Ridge and once billed as the largest coal-fired plant in the world, TVA's Kingston Fossil Plant on Watts Bar Lake is remembered for the 2008 failure of an ash pond, the largest accidental coal fly ash release in the United States.

**Location:** 35.8983, -84.5189

**Categories:** Coal-fired power stations in Tennessee, Tennessee Valley Authority, Environmental disasters in the United States, Buildings and structures in Roane County, Tennessee, Chimneys in the United States

## Introduction

Birdwatchers know the Kingston Fossil Plant for its ponds. Waterfowl gather on the settling and treatment ponds beside this coal-fired power station on the shore of Watts Bar Lake, just outside Kingston, Tennessee. The rest of the country learned the plant's name because of a pond that failed. Sometime between midnight and 1 a.m. on December 22, 2008, an ash impoundment here gave way and released 1.1 billion gallons of coal fly ash slurry. It was the largest accidental release of coal fly ash in the United States, and its consequences reached far beyond the riverbanks it buried.

## Power for Oak Ridge

Construction began on April 30, 1951, and the Tennessee Valley Authority built the plant primarily to supply electricity to the nearby Oak Ridge National Laboratory. When it was completed in 1955, Kingston was billed as the largest coal-fired power plant in the world, and it was formally dedicated on November 17 of that year. Its nine generating units came online in two waves: Units 1 through 4, rated at 175 megawatts each, entered service in 1954, and Units 5 through 9, at 200 megawatts each, followed in 1955. Together they give the plant a total capacity of 1,700 megawatts, or 1,398 net. In a typical year Kingston, still commonly called the Kingston Steam Plant, has burned some five million tons of coal to produce about ten billion kilowatt-hours of electricity. Its future has swung back and forth: in April 2024 TVA decided to retire the coal units by the end of 2027 and build a gas-fired energy complex on the site, but on February 11, 2026 its board voted to keep the coal units running alongside the new gas plant.

## A Thousand Feet of Chimney

The plant's skyline has changed over the decades. In 1976 the original nine smokestacks were taken out of service, though left standing, and replaced by a pair of chimneys 1,000 feet tall, one serving Units 1 through 5 and the other Units 6 through 9. The two tall stacks were later replaced by a single stack connected to scrubbers installed in 2007. All nine units carry selective catalytic reduction systems that cut the nitrogen oxide emissions that help form ozone. In April 2011 the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency announced a settlement with TVA resolving alleged Clean Air Act violations at 11 of its coal-fired plants in Alabama, Kentucky and Tennessee, and under that agreement all nine Kingston units must run those systems continuously.

## The Night the Pond Gave Way

The failure came in the dark, days before Christmas. A report released in June 2009 traced its main cause to the slippage of an unstable layer of fine, wet coal ash beneath the pond. Some 5.4 million cubic yards of slurry poured out, spreading across up to 300 acres under as much as six feet of sludge. The flood covered 12 homes, pushed one entirely off its foundation, left three uninhabitable and damaged 42 residential properties. It flowed into the Emory and Clinch rivers, tributaries of the Tennessee. Remarkably, no one was reported injured or in need of hospitalization from the spill itself. TVA went on to buy 180 properties and 960 acres from affected landowners, and the Emory River was reopened in late spring 2010.

## The Workers Who Cleaned It Up

The cleanup cost approximately $1.134 billion and was completed in 2015, but its human toll ran longer. As early as 2012, cleanup workers began to report illnesses they believed had been caused by the work. Within ten years of the spill, more than 30 cleanup workers had died, and hundreds had been sickened. On November 7, 2018, a federal jury ruled in favor of workers seeking compensation, finding that the contractor Jacobs Engineering had failed to keep them safe from environmental hazards and had misled them about the dangers of coal ash. On May 23, 2023, it was announced that the company, by then rebranded as Jacobs Solutions, had reached a confidential settlement to resolve the workers' lawsuits, reported to cover more than 200 workers, its terms undisclosed. Their story belongs to this shoreline now, as surely as the stack and the waterfowl.

## Flight Context

Kingston Fossil Plant sits at 35.8983°N, 84.5189°W on the shore of Watts Bar Lake just outside Kingston, Tennessee, in Roane County. From 3,000–4,000 feet AGL the plant's stack, powerhouse and settling ponds are easy to pick out against the lake; the Emory and Clinch rivers, which carried the 2008 spill, feed the lake here. Stay well clear of the stack and avoid overflying the plant itself. Oak Ridge, the plant's original customer, lies nearby. Rockwood Municipal Airport (KRKW) is about 8.5 nm west, and McGhee Tyson Airport serving Knoxville (KTYS) is about 26 nm east. Plumes from the stack and morning river fog can reduce visibility over the lake.
