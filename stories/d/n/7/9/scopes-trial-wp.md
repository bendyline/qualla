# Scopes trial

In the summer of 1925, a publicity scheme hatched in a Dayton drug store put a willing young teacher on trial for teaching evolution and brought William Jennings Bryan and Clarence Darrow face to face on the lawn of the Rhea County Courthouse.

**Location:** 35.4949, -85.0127

**Categories:** Scopes Trial, 1925 in Tennessee, Christianity and evolution, William Jennings Bryan, Clarence Darrow, American Civil Liberties Union litigation

## Introduction

On April 5, 1925, at Robinson's Drug Store in Dayton, Tennessee, a few local men sat down to talk about a brand-new state law. George Rappleyea, local manager for a coal and iron company, convinced school superintendent Walter White and attorney Sue K. Hicks that a trial over the Butler Act, which banned teaching human evolution in Tennessee's public schools, would bring Dayton much-needed publicity. "As it is, the law is not enforced," he said, according to Robinson. "If you win, it will be enforced. If I win, the law will be repealed. We're game, aren't we?" They sent for a 24-year-old teacher named John T. Scopes. By July, journalists from around the world had come to town.

## A Law Nobody Expected to Enforce

The act's author, State Representative John Washington Butler, was a farmer and head of the World Christian Fundamentals Association. "I didn't know anything about evolution," he later said; he had read in the papers that children were coming home telling their parents the Bible was nonsense. Governor Austin Peay signed the bill on March 21, 1925, to win rural legislators' support, believing it would never be enforced. In response, the American Civil Liberties Union offered to defend anyone charged under it. Rappleyea pointed out the catch: the state required teachers to use George William Hunter's Civic Biology, which described and endorsed evolution, along with scientific racism and eugenics. Scopes, a science and math teacher who had substituted in biology, could not remember whether he had actually taught evolution, though he had gone through the textbook's evolution chart and chapter with the class. He agreed to stand trial, coached students to testify against him, and was indicted on May 25.

## Two Giants in a Small Town

William Jennings Bryan, a three-time Democratic presidential nominee and former secretary of state, joined the prosecution, though he had not tried a case in thirty-six years. Clarence Darrow, the famed labor and criminal lawyer, then offered his services to the defense, though the ACLU had hoped to keep him out, fearing his staunch agnosticism would hurt the case. Rappleyea, chasing headlines, even invited British novelist H. G. Wells to help; Wells declined, noting he had no legal training in Britain, let alone America. The trial opened on July 10 before Judge John T. Raulston, who quoted Genesis and the Butler Act and told the jury to judge the violation, not the law. When the defense's expert witnesses were threatened with exclusion, Darrow's co-counsel Dudley Field Malone answered with a speech widely considered the oratorical climax of the trial, promising there would be no duel, because "there is never a duel with the truth." The courtroom went wild.

## The Bible on the Witness Stand

By the sixth day the defense had run out of witnesses, and the judge had declared its testimony on the Bible irrelevant. So on the seventh, Darrow took the unorthodox step of calling Bryan himself as an expert on the Bible. He had prepared the night before, with Harvard geologist Kirtley Mather, a devout Baptist, playing Bryan in rehearsal. Raulston had moved court to a stand on the courthouse lawn, ostensibly because he feared for the packed building but probably because of the stifling heat. For about two hours, Darrow pressed Bryan on Eve and Adam's rib, on the population of ancient Egypt, and on where Cain found his wife; Bryan said he would "leave the agnostics to hunt for her." Bryan charged that the purpose was "to cast ridicule on everybody who believes in the Bible." Darrow retorted that it was "preventing bigots and ignoramuses from controlling the education of the United States." Raulston then ruled the whole examination irrelevant and ordered it expunged.

## Nine Minutes and a Technicality

Darrow then asked the jury to find his client guilty so the case could go to a higher court. Waiving his closing speech also barred the prosecution from summing up, so Bryan's prepared summation was never delivered in court. The jury took nine minutes. On July 21 Scopes was convicted and fined $100, and he then spoke in court for the only time: "I feel that I have been convicted of violating an unjust statute." Bryan died suddenly five days later. On appeal, the Tennessee Supreme Court upheld the law but set aside the conviction, because judges could not then set fines above $50 and the jury should have set it. Chief Justice Grafton Green saw "nothing to be gained by prolonging the life of this bizarre case," and the state never retried it. The Butler Act was repealed in 1967; a year later, in Epperson v. Arkansas, the U.S. Supreme Court ruled that such bans violated the First Amendment's Establishment Clause.

## A Circus and a Holy War

Time magazine called Dayton "the fantastic cross between a circus and a holy war." Twenty-two telegraphers reportedly sent out 165,000 words a day, and it became the first trial in American history broadcast nationally by radio. H. L. Mencken of The Baltimore Sun gave it the name "Monkey Trial" and mocked the townspeople, though he confessed Dayton was "a country town full of charm and even beauty." Vendors sold Bibles and stuffed monkeys, and a trained chimpanzee named Joe Mendi performed on the courthouse lawn. The 1891 Rhea County Courthouse, a National Historic Landmark since 1976, now has its courtroom restored to its 1925 appearance and a basement museum holding the microphone that broadcast the trial. For more than three decades, locals have reenacted key moments there each July, and on the lawn stand statues of both adversaries, Bryan since 2005 and Darrow since 2017.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 35.4949°N, 85.0127°W, the Rhea County Courthouse in downtown Dayton, Tennessee. The town sits in the Tennessee Valley on the western side of Chickamauga Lake, with the long escarpment of Walden Ridge and the Cumberland Plateau rising to the west; the courthouse square is in the middle of the town grid. A pass at about 2,500-3,500 feet MSL gives a good view of the town, the lake and the ridge. Mark Anton Airport at Dayton (FAA identifier 2A0, no ICAO code) is about 4 nm away; Cleveland Regional Jetport (KRZR) is about 20 nm southeast and Chattanooga Metropolitan / Lovell Field (KCHA) about 29 nm south-southwest. Expect summer haze and afternoon storms, and watch the rising terrain west of the valley.
