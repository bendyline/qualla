# 1990 Interstate 75 fog disaster

On a December morning in 1990, sudden fog near Calhoun, Tennessee, turned a stretch of Interstate 75 into a 99-vehicle chain of collisions that killed 12 people and reshaped how the highway guards against fog.

**Location:** 35.3246, -84.7824

**Categories:** Road incidents in the United States, Interstate 75, 1990 disasters in the United States, Transportation disasters in Tennessee, Road incidents involving fog, Transportation in McMinn County, Tennessee

## Introduction

"It was like somebody throwing a blanket across your windshield," one survivor said afterward. On the morning of December 11, 1990, fog settled over Interstate 75 near Calhoun, Tennessee, so thick that a man standing beneath the State Route 163 overpass could not see the bridge above him. Within minutes a chain of collisions spread through both directions of traffic. By the end, 99 vehicles had crashed, 12 people were dead and 42 had been treated for injuries. By vehicle count, it was reportedly the largest motor vehicle collision in United States history when it happened.

## Where the Fog Gathers

This stretch of highway runs through two valleys bounded by ridges in the Ridge-and-Valley Appalachians, split by the Hiwassee River. Creeks feed the river near the Interstate crossing, and the reservoir behind Chickamauga Dam backs up into the Hiwassee, spreading into broad lakes where the creeks converge. Beside the road lay the wastewater settling ponds of the Bowater paper mill in Calhoun. Fog here is common, and the road learned it quickly. The section opened on Christmas Eve 1973, and on March 9, 1974, the first multi-vehicle collision in heavy fog involved 18 vehicles and killed three people. More pileups followed, among them a 62-vehicle crash in November 1978, until the last one before 1990, on April 15, 1979. After it, the Tennessee Department of Transportation put up warning signs in each direction reading "Extreme Dense Fog Area Next 5 Miles," fitted with lights meant to flash when fog was detected.

## Ten Minutes Past Nine

On the morning of the disaster, the northbound sign's lights were reportedly not working, while the southbound sign had been flashing continuously for three days. Around 9:10 a.m., a tractor-semitrailer slowed in the southbound lanes near the State Route 163 interchange, less than a mile north of the Hiwassee River bridges. A second truck struck it from behind; both drivers climbed out unhurt. Then a passenger car hit the second truck and was itself struck by another semi, and fire consumed all three vehicles, killing the people in the car. Across the median, a northbound car slowed near the wreckage, was rear-ended, and was then hit by a pickup truck. From there the chain reaction ran in both directions until the crash zone stretched half a mile north and south of where it began. Seventy-two of the vehicles had been heading south and 27 north. Explosions followed, and some fires burned hot enough to damage the asphalt.

## Into the White

The first 911 call reached the Bradley County Sheriff's Office at 9:14 a.m. The first officer on scene arrived three minutes later, while collisions were still happening. He radioed for backup and emergency medical services and pleaded for the Interstate to be closed. Many vehicles were damaged or burned beyond recognition; others came through with only minor damage. More than 200 rescue workers eventually arrived, some from as far as Chattanooga and Knoxville, and the injured were taken to hospitals in Cleveland, Athens, Chattanooga and Knoxville. Early reports counted at least 15 dead and more than 50 hurt, but investigators later settled on 12 lives lost and 42 people treated for injuries. It was reportedly the largest vehicle accident in Tennessee history and the second deadliest, behind the 1972 Bean Station bus-truck collision that killed 14.

## The Argument Over the Fog

The National Transportation Safety Board issued its report on September 28, 1992, finding the probable cause to be "drivers responding to the sudden loss of visibility by operating their vehicles at significantly varying speeds." The report said the Bowater settling ponds and steam from the plant may have played a role in forming the fog, without confirming it. Bowater denied any responsibility, arguing that dense fog had occurred naturally before the plant was built and faulting the state for not installing fog detection equipment. In January 1994 the company reached a $10 million out-of-court settlement with 44 victims and family members of victims, and agreed to limit its use of the 235-acre pond closest to the Interstate. The state transportation department paid $800,000 to settle negligence lawsuits.

## Gates Against the Fog

The eight miles between mileposts 31 and 39 became a designated fog advisory zone, marked by large warning signs as drivers enter and leave (TDOT's wider fog-zone operations now run from mile marker 27 to 44). A $4.5 million computerized fog detection system followed, going into operation in December 1993. When visibility drops below a quarter mile, it cuts the speed limit from 70 to 50 miles per hour, and below 480 feet it drops to 35; below 240 feet, swing gates close off six entrance ramps. A multimillion-dollar upgrade around 2006 (reported as $6.6 million or $6.8 million) added video cameras. As of a 2012 newspaper review, no major multi-vehicle wreck had occurred on this stretch in fog since the system went live, and local news reported the road's first fog closure since the 1990 crash on December 15 and 16, 2015. The morning of 1990 lives on in a 1997 episode of Forensic Files titled "Killer Fog" and in books on Tennessee's disasters.

## Flight Context

The crash site is at 35.3246°N, 84.7824°W on Interstate 75 in McMinn County near Calhoun, Tennessee, just north of the I-75 bridges over the Hiwassee River and near the State Route 163 interchange, about 35 miles northeast of Chattanooga and 65 miles southwest of Knoxville in a straight line. From 2,000–3,000 feet AGL, follow the Interstate to where it crosses the broad Hiwassee, with the long ridges of the Ridge-and-Valley country running northeast–southwest on either side. The same valley geography that fogs the highway can fog the airfields: expect low visibility on calm, cool mornings and check conditions before planning an early arrival. Cleveland Regional Jetport (KRZR) is about 7 nm south; McMinn County Airport at Athens (KMMI) is about 12 nm east-northeast; Chattanooga Metropolitan Airport (KCHA) is about 27 nm southwest.
