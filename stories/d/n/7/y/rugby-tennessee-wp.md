# Rugby, Tennessee

Founded in 1880 by Tom Brown's School Days author Thomas Hughes as a utopia for England's landless younger sons, Rugby failed as a colony but survives as a restored Victorian village on the Cumberland Plateau.

**Location:** 36.3611, -84.7003

**Categories:** Utopian communities in the United States, Unincorporated communities in Morgan County, Tennessee, Populated places established in 1880, Historic districts on the National Register of Historic Places in Tennessee, Open-air museums in Tennessee

## Introduction

Inside a village library on the Cumberland Plateau, about 7,000 books sit much as they did in 1882. The Thomas Hughes Library is the least changed of all the buildings in Rugby, Tennessee, and most of its original collection is still on the shelves, the oldest volume reportedly dating to 1687. The collection was assembled primarily by Boston booksellers Estes & Lauriat and donated on the condition that the library be named for Hughes, the English author who founded this village as a utopia. The utopia did not last. The library, and a surprising amount of the village around it, did.

## A Colony for Second Sons

Thomas Hughes was born in Uffington, Oxfordshire, in 1822 and attended Rugby School in the 1830s, where he fell under the influence of its progressive headmaster, Thomas Arnold. School and headmaster both figure prominently in his 1857 novel, Tom Brown's School Days, which espoused Christian socialism and the cooperative ownership of community businesses. By the 1860s, Hughes had grown disenchanted with the materialism of late Victorian England. He was troubled by the waste of its younger sons: under primogeniture, the eldest son inherited the family land, and an economic downturn left many of the others jobless and idle. Hughes imagined a colony where these second sons of the gentry could own land and live free of the social and moral ills of English cities, practicing equality and cooperation instead.

## Spotted from the Train

The site was found by rail. In 1878, Franklin Webster Smith, president of the Board of Aid, and Cyrus Clarke, an agent of the new Cincinnati Southern Railway, were riding the line's new tracks across the plateau when they were struck by its virgin forests, clear air and scenic gorges. Clarke secured options on hundreds of thousands of acres. Back in Boston, Smith found few families willing to move there, so he told Hughes, who formed a partnership with British lawyers Sir Henry Kimber and John Boyle and bought the Board of Aid. Hughes was on hand for the colony's opening on October 5, 1880, and gave a speech laying out his plans. Houses rose within months, along with the three-story Tabard Inn. By 1884 Rugby had more than 400 residents, 65 frame public buildings and houses, a tennis team, a social club, and a literary and dramatic society.

## Typhoid and Thin Soil

The trouble began almost at once. In the summer of 1881 a typhoid outbreak killed seven colonists and forced the Tabard Inn to close; the inn burned down altogether in 1884. Lawsuits over land titles tangled the colony's footing, and the settlers, many of them unused to hard manual labor, struggled to coax crops from the poor soil of the plateau. By late 1887 most of the original colonists had died or moved away. Edward Bertz, the German-born colonist who served as Rugby's first librarian, later published a book about his time there, Das Sabinergut, in 1896. Yet Rugby never quite emptied. A few colonists carried on into the 20th century, and the village kept a small, continuous population through the decades that followed.

## Carpenter Gothic in the Woods

The church, at least, never left. Christ Church Episcopal was established on October 5, 1880, the same day the colony opened, and first met in the schoolhouse. Its present building, in the Carpenter Gothic style, was built in 1887 by Cornelius Onderdonk, who put up many of Rugby's original buildings, and consecrated in 1888 by Bishop Charles Quintard. English carpenter Henry Fry, who had worked on churches around London, designed its alms basin, and its reed organ, built in 1849, is one of the oldest in the United States. The parish has met there regularly ever since. Nearby stands Kingstone Lisle, a Queen Anne cottage built in 1884 as a home for Hughes. He stayed there only briefly on one of his annual visits, usually lodging at the Newbury House instead, and in the late 1880s gave it to the church's priest as a rectory.

## A Village Put Back Together

In the 1960s, residents, friends and descendants of the colonists began restoring Rugby's original design and layout, and in 1966 preservationists formed Historic Rugby to care for the surviving buildings. They restored Kingstone Lisle with period furniture and reconstructed structures that had been lost. In 1972 the historic area joined the National Register of Historic Places as the Rugby Colony historic district, and new homes in the Beacon Hill section must follow the village's Victorian aesthetic. The area remains remote. On Rugby's north side, the Clear Fork meets White Oak Creek in a natural pool called the Meeting of the Waters, a favorite hike since the colony's early days, and beyond it the 125,000-acre Big South Fork National River and Recreation Area spreads across the country to the north.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 36.3611°N, 84.7003°W, atop the Cumberland Plateau, where the community straddles the Morgan and Scott county line. The historic village strings along State Route 52, with the deep, forested gorges of the Clear Fork and the Big South Fork National River and Recreation Area immediately to the north; the contrast between flat plateau and cut gorges makes the area easy to spot. A cruise around 3,500-4,500 feet MSL gives a good view of the village and gorges. Scott Municipal at Oneida (KSCX) is about 8 nm northeast, and Jamestown Municipal (FAA identifier 2A1) about 12 nm west; the nearest larger airport is Crossville Memorial-Whitson Field (KCSV), about 31 nm southwest. Morning fog often fills the gorges, and the plateau can hold low cloud after rain.
