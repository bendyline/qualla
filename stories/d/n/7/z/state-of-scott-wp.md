# State of Scott

When Tennessee left the Union in 1861, Unionist Scott County declared itself the Free and Independent State of Scott, a secession from the secession that was only formally undone in 1986.

## Introduction

When Tennessee left the Union in 1861, Scott County left Tennessee. The county had voted overwhelmingly against secession, and when the state went anyway, its county court responded with a resolution declaring the "Free and Independent State of Scott." Neither the Confederacy nor the Union ever recognized it. Yet the declaration stayed on the books for 125 years, until the county finally repealed it in 1986 and asked, ceremonially, to be let back into Tennessee.

## More Than Twenty-Seven to One

Scott County's position on secession was not close. In Tennessee's June 8 referendum, the county voted against leaving the Union by about 540 to 19 (Wikipedia gives 541 to 19, while a Scott County history gives 521 to 19). It was part of a broader Southern Unionist movement, and in June 1861 the argument came to its doorstep. On June 4, United States Senator Andrew Johnson spoke on the courthouse steps in Huntsville, the county seat, and argued against separation from the Union. When Tennessee seceded anyway, Scott County chose to follow its own vote rather than the state's.

## A State of Its Own

The county court voted to approve a unanimous resolution declaring Scott County's own secession, this time from Tennessee. The Tennessee Encyclopedia describes it as a protest resolution, and the roadside marker near Huntsville puts it plainly: the county court "by resolution seceded from the state and formed the Free and Independent State of Scott." The new "state" had no recognition from anyone. Its edict was never officially acknowledged by either the Confederacy or the Union, which left it in an odd position: loyal to a nation that did not formally accept its independence, and in open defiance of a state that regarded it as part of its territory.

## Soldiers on the Huntsville Road

Tennessee did not ignore the challenge. Governor Isham Harris reportedly gathered 1,700 soldiers to march on Huntsville and put down what the state treated as a rebellion. They met extreme resistance and were forced to retreat before reaching the town. The war in Scott County was far from over, though. At the Battle of Huntsville on August 13, 1862, Union Colonel William Clift, whose roughly 250 men were mostly new recruits, faced a Confederate force that his own report put at 1,500 to 2,000. His men left the breastworks in confusion until only about 20 remained, and Clift was driven from the town into the back woods. His regiment was later pieced back together, and for much of the rest of the year it fought more as a guerrilla unit than a conventional one.

## Readmitted After 125 Years

The proclamation itself lingered for well over a century. It was only in 1986 that Scott County formally repealed its declaration of independence. At the same time, the county petitioned the state of Tennessee for readmission, and that request was ceremonially granted, closing a quarrel that had outlived everyone involved in it. The story is still marked on the landscape. A roadside marker on State Route 63, near Huntsville, recalls Johnson's 1861 speech and the county court's resolution, a small reminder that in 1861 not every corner of Tennessee followed the state out of the Union.
