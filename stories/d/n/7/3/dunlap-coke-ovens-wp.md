# Dunlap coke ovens

At the foot of Fredonia Mountain in Tennessee's Sequatchie Valley, 268 stone beehive ovens once baked coal into coke for Chattanooga's furnaces, and today they share a park with a museum, a hiking trail and a bluegrass festival that helped save them.

**Location:** 35.3797, -85.4017

## Introduction

Once the coal was in and leveled, a worker sealed the side window of the oven with clay, leaving a gap about an inch wide to let in air. Then the waiting began. For about 72 hours the coal baked inside a dome of firebrick and sandstone, twelve feet across, as its volatile material cooked away. When the seal was broken, what came out was coke, roughly one ton for every two tons of coal, bound by rail for the iron furnaces of Chattanooga. Near Dunlap, in Tennessee's Sequatchie Valley, 268 of these beehive ovens stand in five batteries at the foot of Fredonia Mountain, cold now for about a century.

## Iron Hunger Reaches the Valley

Coke, a fuel made by carbonizing coal, was used mainly to make pig iron, and the rise of the steel industry sent demand soaring. For the central Sequatchie Valley, whose economy had long rested on subsistence farming, the ovens meant drastic change. In 1900 the Douglas Coal and Coke Company bought a 14,000-acre tract around the base of Fredonia Mountain. By 1902 it had built its first ovens (accounts put the number between 24 and 50), opened several coal mines, laid an incline railway, and set up a company town with a commissary and a clubhouse. The venture struggled from the start. Douglas suffered labor problems and never really worked out how to separate the dirt from its coal, and in 1904 it halted operations.

## Seventy-Two Hours in the Beehive

The layout worked like a production line. Every oven had the same two openings, one in the top of its dome and a "window" in its side. Railroad tracks ran across the top of each battery, and an incline railway connected the ovens with a mine higher up the slope. Cars carried coal down from the mine and dumped it straight into the openings on the oven tops. A laborer then leveled the pile through the side window with a scraper before the clay went on and the long bake began. At the end, the coke was pulled out and loaded onto a train for an iron furnace in Chattanooga.

## Boiler Blast and Bust

The Chattanooga Iron and Coal Company bought the Douglas operation in 1905 and the next year added a steam-powered coal washer and more ovens. The company owned quarries in Georgia and a blast furnace in the Tannery Flats section of Chattanooga, where coke from Dunlap turned Georgia iron ore into pig iron. The operation was profitable until 1916, when a boiler explosion destroyed the coal washer. The company built a new one and added still more ovens, but the new washer's cost and poor performance drained its finances, and in 1919 it sold the entire iron and coke operation to the Southern States Iron and Coal Company. Southern States made coke at Dunlap until about 1922, then shut down. Brothers E.P. and E.K. Rosamund bought the property in 1928, but their plans for a revival failed, and the ovens went quiet for good.

## Saved by Pickers

The site eventually passed to the Bowater Corporation, which in 1985 donated it to the Sequatchie Valley Historical Association. That same year the ovens joined the National Register of Historic Places. The association cleared tons of trash from the grounds, and Dunlap Coke Ovens Park opened in 1987. Local bluegrass musicians were part of the effort from the beginning, lending labor, connections and music to the cleanup, construction and fundraising. To pay for staff and upkeep, the association built an amphitheater beside one of the oven batteries and started the Coke Ovens Bluegrass Festival, held each June.

## Walking the Incline

In 1989, $71,000 from the estate of Rhea County historian David Henry Gray paid to rebuild the old company commissary on its original foundation, and the building now houses the Coke Ovens Museum. Around it, the park holds the oven batteries themselves along with a replica coal mine and mining machinery, the ruins of the 1906 coal washer, a replica of the Dunlap depot and a caboose. A hiking trail follows the old incline railway grade up the mountainside to the former mining areas atop Fredonia Mountain, retracing the route the coal once took down to the ovens. The music is part of the landscape now, too. In 2008, filmmaker Charli Wyatt's half-hour program about the park's volunteers and musicians, named for a song by festival music director Ed Brown, aired on Chattanooga's PBS station, and local fans raised a stone monument inscribed with the names of bluegrass musicians who have played the park and the valley.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 35.3797°N, 85.4017°W, on the western edge of Dunlap in the long, narrow Sequatchie Valley, where the ovens sit at the foot of Fredonia Mountain on the Cumberland Plateau side of the valley. From the air, follow the straight valley floor between the plateau escarpments; the town of Dunlap is the landmark, and the oven park lies at the base of the slope just west of town. A cruise around 3,500-4,500 feet MSL keeps you clear of the ridges on both sides. Dunlap Airpark (FAA identifier 3TN4, no ICAO code; private use, permission required before landing) is about 3 nm south-southwest; Chattanooga Metropolitan / Lovell Field (KCHA) is the nearest major airport, about 23 nm south. Valley fog is common on still mornings, and the ridgelines can hide in low cloud.
