# Cumberland Homesteads

A New Deal plan to resettle struggling farmers, miners and workers atop Tennessee's Cumberland Plateau failed as a cooperative, yet its crab orchard stone houses and tower still stand south of Crossville.

**Location:** 35.9061, -84.9828

**Categories:** New Deal subsistence homestead communities, New Deal in Tennessee, Historic districts on the National Register of Historic Places in Tennessee, Geography of Cumberland County, Tennessee

## Introduction

As a cooperative experiment, Cumberland Homesteads failed. The community store and cannery struggled, the factories planners hoped to attract never came, and by 1945 the federal government had withdrawn from the project entirely. Yet the community survived, and so did its houses. Built of a locally quarried sandstone known as "crab orchard" stone, most of the original 251 houses still stand in the hills just south of Crossville, Tennessee, and in 1988 several hundred of the community's original buildings became a historic district covering more than 10,000 acres of the Cumberland Plateau.

## Hard Times on the Plateau

By the early 1930s, decades of poor farming practices had left many small farms in East Tennessee untenable, and the Great Depression had thrown thousands of coal miners and other industrial workers out of work. In Cumberland County, farm agent Robert Lyons led a local committee that drew up a proposal for a homestead community, shaped by earlier back-to-the-land experiments on the Plateau and by relief efforts from local Quakers. Tennessee Valley Authority chairman Arthur Morgan, a proponent of cooperative living, lent his influence, and in January 1934 the New Deal's Division of Subsistence Homesteads accepted the plan. The Civil Works Administration immediately hired several hundred local people to prepare the newly acquired land, and, by one account, their wages effectively ended the Great Depression in Cumberland County.

## A Model Community on Paper

Franklin D. Roosevelt had grand expectations for the program. He wanted the homes to be comfortable and the communities to approach a kind of utopia. The Division planned three kinds of settlements: part-time farmers living near wage work, farmers resettled from worn-out land, and "stranded" industrial workers such as miners and loggers. Cumberland Homesteads eventually fell into that last category, the one that drew the most criticism, since many doubted such communities could ever support themselves. The initial 233 families selected included distressed farmers, unemployed miners and textile workers, and struggling professionals. According to the museum's director, some homesteaders worked with two-thirds of their credited hours going toward the purchase of a home. First Lady Eleanor Roosevelt, who took a special interest in these projects, visited the Homesteads in the mid-1930s; sources date her visit to 1934 and to 1935.

## Houses Cut from the Hills

The builders were intent on using local resources, and the Plateau supplied them in stone. William Macy Stanton, a TVA architect who had designed buildings in the planned town of Norris, drew up the basic designs; fifteen distinct housing designs have been identified, eleven of them repeated across the community. The landmark is the Homesteads Tower, a cross-shaped building wrapped around an eight-story octagonal water tower, which once housed the project's offices and supplied the community's water. Two stone arch bridges, on Deep Draw Road and Old Mail Road, count as contributing structures in the historic district, alongside more than a dozen structures in Cumberland Mountain State Park, which was developed at the same time as a recreation area. Byrd Creek, a tributary of the Obed River, winds through the community and drains much of it.

## The Experiment Ends, the Neighbors Stay

The economics never worked. A community-owned store, the "Trading Post," and a cannery opened in 1934 but struggled under inexperienced management. Wage-paying industries never relocated to the Homesteads, and the homesteaders' own attempts at a coal mine and a sorghum mill failed. Through the 1930s the project passed among a succession of agencies with differing philosophies, and it drifted without a clear purpose. By 1945 Washington had extricated itself, after allowing the remaining homesteaders to buy their farms. The people stayed anyway: more than half the farms remained in the hands of original homesteaders through the 1950s. Today the Cumberland Homestead Tower Association preserves the story in the tower itself. Charles Tollett, an association volunteer, has been adamant that the Homesteads were never a government handout but a chance for Tennesseans to work hard for a place to live.

## Flight Context

Cumberland Homesteads centers on 35.9061°N, 84.9828°W atop the Cumberland Plateau, just south of Crossville, Tennessee. From 2,000–3,000 feet AGL, look for U.S. Route 127 running south from Crossville into the community, State Route 68 ending at that highway and heading south-southeast toward Spring City, and the woods of Cumberland Mountain State Park within the district. The plateau sits well above the surrounding valleys, so field elevations here run around 1,800–1,900 feet. Crossville Memorial Airport–Whitson Field (KCSV) is about 6 nm northwest and is the obvious base; Rockwood Municipal Airport (KRKW) lies about 14 nm east. Low cloud and fog can cap the plateau when the valleys below are clear.
