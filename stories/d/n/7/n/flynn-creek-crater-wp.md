# Flynn Creek crater

Some 360 million years ago an object from space struck a shallow sea in what is now Jackson County, Tennessee, leaving a 3.8-kilometer crater that a Devonian sea then sealed and preserved.

**Location:** 36.2833, -85.6667

**Categories:** Impact craters of the United States, Devonian impact craters, Geology of Tennessee, Landforms of Jackson County, Tennessee

## Introduction

Some 360 million years ago, the ground south of present-day Gainesboro, Tennessee, lay under shallow water, perhaps 15 meters deep at most. Then an object from space, estimated at 90 to 190 meters across, struck it. The blast carved a saucer-shaped crater 3.8 kilometers wide and roughly 150 to 200 meters deep (estimates vary), heaved the bedrock at its center upward, and flung at least half of the shattered rock clear of the hole. The scar it left is the Flynn Creek crater, and a quirk of timing made it one of the best-preserved ancient impact structures presently known.

## Rock Broken to Its Roots

The impact pulverized more than two square kilometers of flat-lying limestone and dolomite, churning it to a depth of 200 meters. Geologists call the result breccia, rock broken into fragments and cemented back together, and at Flynn Creek it forms a lens averaging about 40 meters thick. The fragments range from small grains to megabreccia blocks as long as 100 meters. Below all this chaos, undisturbed strata lie 100 meters beneath the original crater floor. The crater's outline is roughly polygonal rather than round, and the zone of disturbed rock reaches, on average, about a kilometer out from the crater wall.

## A Hill Thrown Up from Below

At the heart of the crater stands a large central hill. It formed when a sequence of Middle Ordovician limestone and dolomite, now steeply tilted, folded, faulted and brecciated, was uplifted 100 meters. Deeper rock of the Knox group was raised roughly 250 meters above its normal position and locally carries shatter cones, the distinctive fracture patterns left by intense shock. The rim tells the same violent story. There, Ordovician limestone was pushed up and folded into anticlines and synclines that curve around the crater walls, compressing the rock by as much as 35 percent. In the southeastern rim, a large thrust block was shoved outward and lifted about 50 meters, partly riding over a tilted block that dropped roughly 100 meters toward the crater. Flynn Creek sits at the lowermost size limit for complex craters, the kind with a central uplift.

## Sealed by the Chattanooga Sea

What saved Flynn Creek was a sea. Erosion after the impact stripped away the ejecta, except for a layer of breccia that survives over the down-dropped rim block, apparently the remains of the blanket of debris that once surrounded the crater, and it lowered the regional surface by less than 30 meters. Then, within a few million years, the whole structure was buried under shale deposited by the Late Devonian Chattanooga Sea. Conodonts, tiny fossils found in the crater's immediate infill, confirm the setting was marine. Much later, erosion along the Flynn Creek drainage cut back into the structure and exposed a large part of it. Karst processes have since riddled the area with caves tied to impact features, among them Hawkins Impact Cave, described as the only cave known to occur in the central uplift of an impact structure.

## Kilometers of Core in Flagstaff

The crater's value to science lies in what it can reveal about how impacts work. Between 1967 and 1979, U.S. Geological Survey scientist Dr. David Roddy ran a drilling program here that produced more than 3.8 kilometers of nearly continuous core from 18 separate drill holes. Those samples now fill 2,621 standard core storage boxes at the USGS in Flagstaff, Arizona, where they preserve, core by core, the record of the moment some 360 million years ago when the sky fell into a shallow sea.

## Flight Context

The Flynn Creek crater is centered near 36.2833°N, 85.6667°W in Jackson County, Tennessee, about 8 km (5 miles) south of Gainesboro. There is no crisp rim to spot from the air; erosion along Flynn Creek is what exposed the structure, so follow the creek's valley and look for the large central hill at the crater's heart. A pass at 2,000–3,000 feet AGL gives the best sense of the 3.8-kilometer structure. Jackson County Airport at Gainesboro (FAA identifier 1A7, no ICAO code listed) is about 7 nm north; Upper Cumberland Regional Airport near Sparta (KSRB) is about 15 nm south-southeast. Morning fog can settle in the valleys of this hilly country.
