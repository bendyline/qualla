# Pikeville Cut-Through

To rid downtown of coal dust and floods, Pikeville, Kentucky, spent 14 years carving a 523-foot-deep notch through a mountain and moving its highway, its railroad and its river into it.

**Location:** 37.4753, -82.5389

**Categories:** Transportation in Pike County, Kentucky, Tourist attractions in Pike County, Kentucky, Cuts (earthmoving), U.S. Route 23, Pikeville, Kentucky

## Introduction

Most towns route a highway around a mountain. Pikeville, Kentucky, cut straight through one, and sent its railroad and its river through the gap as well. The Pikeville Cut-Through is 1,300 feet wide, 3,700 feet long and 523 feet deep, a notch in the Appalachian skyline built by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers over 14 years. Today a four-lane divided highway, a CSX rail line and the Levisa Fork of the Big Sandy River all run side by side through the same man-made canyon.

## The Mayor Who Wanted Clean Air

The idea began in 1960 with Dr. William Hambley, a Pikeville native and longtime mayor. Coal-hauling trains passed through the city every day, and with them came coal dust. Hambley proposed moving the railbed out of town. In 1963 Pikeville won a $38,000 federal grant to study relocating the railroad, and the city was later named a "Model City" by the federal Model Cities Program, created in 1966. By 1965 the plan had grown to include Corridor B of the Appalachian Development Highway System. Then came a bolder decision. The Levisa Fork, which snaked through downtown and flooded it almost every year, would be moved too, and its old bed reclaimed as land for the city to grow on.

## Blasting Peach Orchard Mountain

Work began on November 26, 1973. In the first of four phases, crews blasted nearly 13 million cubic yards of rock out of Peach Orchard Mountain to open a channel wide enough for a road, a railroad and a river together. That phase alone cost $17,250,000. The finished dimensions tell the scale: a gap nearly a quarter-mile wide and more than 500 feet deep, opened through a ridge right beside a mountain city.

## A Downtown Built on a Riverbed

Phase II started on March 4, 1980, and it changed the face of downtown. The coal tipples and railroad tracks came out, a bridge went up across the cut, and the river was rerouted into its new channel while the old riverbed was filled in. Moving 5 million cubic yards of soil created 240 acres of usable land in the heart of town, at a cost of $22,200,000. The final two phases began together on March 15, 1983, adding downtown interchanges, flood walls, another new bridge and 150 more acres of property for $19,700,000. On the reclaimed land, the city built Hambley Boulevard, a lasting tribute to the mayor who had first imagined the project.

## Three Lines Through One Gap

The Pikeville Cut-Through was dedicated on October 2, 1987, at a total cost of $77.6 million. Its canyon now carries three very different kinds of traffic. The four-lane highway is Corridor B, signed as U.S. Route 23, US 119, US 460 and Kentucky Route 80. Beside it runs CSX's Big Sandy Subdivision rail line, and alongside both flows the Levisa Fork, no longer winding through downtown streets. The railroad still runs, but through the mountain rather than down the middle of town.

## Flight Context

The Pikeville Cut-Through is at 37.4753 N, 82.5389 W on the edge of Pikeville in Pike County, eastern Kentucky. From 2,500-4,000 ft AGL it is an unmistakable human-made landform: look for a broad, straight notch through a forested ridge carrying a divided highway, a railroad and the Levisa Fork together, with Pikeville's downtown on the reclaimed former riverbed beside it. Pike County Airport-Hatcher Field (KPBX) lies about 10 km north-northwest, close enough to use the cut as a visual reference. Also nearby are Big Sandy Regional Airport (FAA identifier SJS, GPS code KSJS) about 32 km north-northwest and Paintsville-Prestonsburg-Combs Field (9KY9, private use, permission required before landing) about 37 km northwest. Surrounding terrain rises steeply from the valleys, and fog can settle along the river on cool mornings.
