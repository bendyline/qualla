# Natural Tunnel State Park

A cave carved by a creek through a Virginia ridge, so large that trains have been running through it since the 1890s.

**Location:** 36.7030, -82.7430

**Categories:** State parks of Virginia, State parks of the Appalachians, Parks in Scott County, Virginia, Railroad tunnels in Virginia, Protected areas established in 1967, 1967 establishments in Virginia, Caves of Virginia

## Introduction

Most railroad tunnels are blasted and bored. This one was dug by a creek. In the mountains of Scott County, Virginia, groundwater and a small stream spent more than a million years hollowing out a passage through a limestone ridge, and when the railroad arrived in the 1890s, the engineers found the job largely done for them. Trains still roll through the Natural Tunnel today, dwarfed by a cave that Virginia's state parks describe as more than 850 feet long and ten stories high. William Jennings Bryan, who served as the nation's 41st Secretary of State, called it the "Eighth Wonder of the World."

## Written in Limestone

The tunnel began to form more than a million years ago, in the Pleistocene, when groundwater carrying carbonic acid seeped through cracks in the rock and slowly dissolved the limestone and dolomite bedrock. A small river, now called Stock Creek, was diverted underground and kept cutting for many millennia, widening the passage it had found. The ridge it pierced is Purchase Ridge, made of Ordovician Chepultepec Limestone and sitting near the axis of the Purchase Ridge syncline, on a slab of rock known as the Hunter Valley Thrust Sheet, between the Clinchport and Hunter Valley thrust faults. Virginia's state parks describe the result as more than 850 feet long and ten stories high. The walls hold traces of much older life, too. Many fossils have been found in the creek bed and in the tunnel walls.

## The Eighth Wonder

The Natural Tunnel has drawn sightseers for more than a century. Daniel Boone is reputed to have been the first European to see it, in the eighteenth century. The landscape around the tunnel is as dramatic as the tunnel itself, a wide chasm between steep stone walls ringed by pinnacles known as "chimneys," and naturally formed amphitheaters that rise as high as 600 feet. The highest pinnacle carries a legend. As the popular story goes, a Cherokee maiden and a Shawnee warrior, forbidden to marry by their people, leaped together to their deaths from the highest point above the tunnel. The spot is known today as Lover's Leap, and the park's trails lead to it.

## Trains Through a Cave

In the early 1890s the South Atlantic and Ohio railroad ran its line through the Natural Tunnel. Accounts differ on the dates: railroad histories give 1893 for the tracks and 1894 for the first train, while a Virginia rail history places the line's completion at 1890. In 1899 the tunnel was bought by the Tennessee & Carolina Iron and Steel Company. For years the line carried passenger trains, but that era has passed. Today the tracks are still open and operated by the Norfolk Southern Railway, with CSX Transportation also running trains under trackage rights, and the only cargo is coal. The trains that pass through now run on a route that nature surveyed first, through a cave older than any railroad by more than a million years.

## A Park Built Around a Hole in the Mountain

Virginia created Natural Tunnel State Park in 1967 and opened it to the public in 1971. Getting down to the tunnel is part of the experience. A chairlift, the only one in Virginia's state park system, carries visitors to the tunnel floor on a seasonal schedule, and a level boardwalk of about 500 feet leads to the mouth of the tunnel, accessible to guests with disabilities. Trails branch across the park to the tunnel floor, Lover's Leap, Tunnel Hill and Gorge Ridge. There are two campgrounds, cabins, picnic areas and a visitor center, as well as the Wilderness Road historic area. Guests can also take cave tours and canoe trips on the nearby Clinch River. Stock Creek, the stream that carved the tunnel in the first place, still runs through the park, where anglers can fish a limited stretch of it.

## Flight Context

Natural Tunnel State Park is at 36.703°N, 82.743°W near Duffield in Scott County, southwestern Virginia, in the ridges of the Appalachian Mountains. From the air, look for the railroad line threading through Purchase Ridge and the deep, cliff-walled chasm around the tunnel; 2,000 to 3,000 feet above the ridges gives a good view without crowding the terrain. Lonesome Pine Airport in Wise (KLNP) is about 20 nm north-northeast, Tri-Cities Airport (KTRI) about 21 nm southeast, and Virginia Highlands Airport in Abingdon (KVJI) about 34 nm east. Mountain winds can make for turbulence over the ridges, and morning valley fog is common, especially in spring and fall.
