# Battle of the Grapevine Creek

The January 1888 clash on a West Virginia creek that became the last major fight of the Hatfield–McCoy feud, and led to trials that reached the U.S. Supreme Court.

**Location:** 37.5801, -82.1150

**Categories:** History of Kentucky, History of West Virginia, History of the Southern United States, Culture of the Southern United States, Folklore of the United States, Society of Appalachia, Hatfield family, Feuds in the United States, Folklore of the Southern United States, Conflicts in 1888

## Introduction

On January 19, 1888, two armed parties met along Grapevine Creek, on the West Virginia side of the Tug Fork, and began shooting at each other. One side was a posse out of Pike County, Kentucky, with McCoys in its ranks. The other was made up of Hatfields and their allies. It was the last major offensive clash of the Hatfield–McCoy feud, the long and bloody quarrel between two mountain families that has since become America's shorthand for any rivalry that will not die.

## Two Families, One River

The feud played out along the Tug Fork of the Big Sandy River, which separates West Virginia from Kentucky. The Hatfields lived mostly on the West Virginia side and were led by William Anderson "Devil Anse" Hatfield. The McCoys lived mostly on the Kentucky side and were led by Randolph "Randall" McCoy. Between 1880 and 1891, the feud claimed more than a dozen members of the two families, and at one point the governors of West Virginia and Kentucky even threatened to send their militias into each other's states. The violence came to a head at the start of 1888. On New Year's Night, a group of Hatfields surrounded the McCoy family's cabin and opened fire. Randolph McCoy and most of his children escaped, but two of his children, Calvin and Alifair, were killed.

## The Posse Crosses the Line

Within days, a posse led by Pike County Deputy Sheriff Frank "Bad Frank" Phillips rode across the state line into West Virginia to hunt down the Hatfield men. McCoys rode with him, among them Bud McCoy and Randolph's son James "Jim" McCoy. The posse's first target was Jim Vance, who was killed after he refused to be arrested. Raids on Hatfield homes and supporters followed; the posse captured many men and, by some accounts, killed three more Hatfield supporters. The Hatfield side, meanwhile, was preparing to strike back. By one account, the Hatfields were already on the move when word of their plans reached the McCoy side, and Phillips gathered every able man he could to intercept them.

## The Fight at Grapevine Creek

The two groups met around Grapevine Creek on January 19 and opened fire. Accounts of the fighting differ. One describes a group from the McCoy side luring the Hatfields into one part of the battlefield while another group moved around to outflank them, leaving the Hatfields with multiple casualties and forcing them to retreat. A number of Hatfield men could not get away and were taken prisoner. Accounts of Bill Dempsey's death differ, and some are of uncertain reliability: one holds that he, a deputy riding with the Hatfields, was wounded and then killed by Phillips after the Hatfield party surrendered, while another says only that Phillips killed him in the firefight. What the accounts agree on is the outcome. The Hatfield side lost, prisoners were taken back to Kentucky, and the feud's era of open raids and ambushes was drawing to a close.

## Justice Across a State Line

The prisoners, along with others captured before the battle, were put on trial in Kentucky for crimes committed during the feud, including the killing of Alifair McCoy. Because they had been seized in West Virginia without proper extradition, the case went all the way to the United States Supreme Court. In Mahon v. Justice (1888), the court ruled 7–2 for Kentucky, holding that no federal law prevented a fugitive from being tried even if he had been brought back illegally. All the defendants were found guilty, and most received life in prison; sources count seven or eight life sentences. Ellison "Cottontop" Mounts, who pleaded guilty to shooting Alifair, was sentenced to death and hanged in Pikeville on February 18, 1890, where thousands gathered. Some accounts describe Mounts as intellectually disabled and see him as a scapegoat. Fighting between the families eased afterward, and the feud is generally said to have ended in 1891. More than a century later, in 2003, descendants of both families signed an official truce in Pikeville.

## Flight Context

The battle site is given at 37.580°N, 82.115°W, along Grapevine Creek on the West Virginia side of the Tug Fork, the river that forms the Kentucky–West Virginia line. This is deep Appalachian terrain of steep, forested ridges and narrow valleys; fly at least 2,000 feet above the ridgetops and use the winding Tug Fork as your guide. Southern West Virginia Regional Airport (KEBD) is about 6 nm north, Logan County Airport (FAA: 6L4) about 19 nm north-northeast, and Pike County Airport-Hatcher Field in Kentucky (KPBX) about 22 nm west. Morning valley fog is common, and emergency landing options among these ridges are very limited.
