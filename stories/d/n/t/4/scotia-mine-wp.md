# Scotia Mine

Two explosions two days apart in March 1976 killed 26 coal miners and federal mine inspectors at the Scotia Mine in Letcher County, Kentucky, and helped bring about the Federal Mine Safety and Health Act of 1977.

**Location:** 37.0480, -82.8293

**Categories:** Coal mines in the United States, Coal mining disasters in Kentucky, Letcher County, Kentucky, 1976 disasters in the United States, Mine safety

## Introduction

Jimmy W. Sturgill was 20 years old. Richard M. Sammons was 55. Between them, on the list of 26 men who died in the Scotia Mine in March 1976, runs almost every age of a working life: 23, 24, 29, 31, 40, 45, 47. They died in two explosions two days apart, in a mine tucked into the community of Oven Fork in Letcher County, Kentucky, and a state historical marker nearby now calls the loss one of the worst mine disasters in U.S. history.

## A Shaft Still Being Finished

The Scotia Mine opened in 1962 as a subsidiary of the Blue Diamond Coal Company of Knoxville, Tennessee, at Oven Fork, about four miles south of Whitesburg. It was first driven into the Imboden coalbed. In 1975 the operation added a new opening, a concrete-lined shaft 376 feet deep. Its lining was completed on July 21, 1975, and crews began installing an automatic elevator. By March 9, 1976, that work was still unfinished, and the shaft served only as an opening to draw fresh air into the mine.

## Two Days, Two Blasts

At about 11:45 a.m. on March 9, 1976, an explosion of coal dust and gases tore through the Scotia Mine, killing 15 miners. Two days later a second explosion took 11 more lives. The dead included coal miners and federal mine inspectors. According to the Mine Safety and Health Administration's narrative, the first blast was methane ignited by an electric arc or spark from a battery-powered locomotive; for the second, investigators could not narrow the ignition to a single source among five possibilities. A lack of ventilation figured prominently in the disaster. The marker's own wording is blunt: faulty equipment ignited methane and coal dust due to lack of proper ventilation. Eleven of the dead could not be brought out for months. On November 20, 1976, The New York Times reported that their bodies had finally been recovered after 253 days entombed in the mine.

## Twenty-Six Names

The men who died were Glenn Barker, Dennis Boggs, Everett Scott Combs, Virgil Coots, Don Creech, Larry David McKnight, Earl Galloway, David Gibbs, Robert Griffith, John Hackworth, J. B. Holbrook, Kenneth B. Kiser, Roy McKnight, Lawrence Peavy, Carl Polly, Richard M. Sammons, Tommy Ray Scott, Ivan Gail Sparkman, James Sturgill, Jimmy W. Sturgill, Monroe Sturgill, Kenneth Turner, Willie D. Turner, Grover Tussey, Denver Widner and James Williams. Half of them were 29 or younger. Three shared the surname Sturgill, two were Turners and two were McKnights.

## The Law That Followed

The explosions at Scotia led to the passage of the Federal Mine Safety and Health Act of 1977, which strengthened the earlier 1969 law. It brought coal and non-coal mining under a single set of federal health and safety rules, strengthened miners' rights and their protection from retaliation, and moved responsibility for mine safety from the Department of the Interior to the Department of Labor, where the new agency was named the Mine Safety and Health Administration. Historical Marker #2314 in Letcher County records the chain directly: the twin explosions of March 9 and 11 led to the passage of the 1977 Act.

## The Widows Go to Court

In 1977 the miners' widows, who became known as the Scotia widows, sued Blue Diamond Coal Company. U.S. District Judge Howard David Hermansdorfer of the Eastern District of Kentucky ruled that the company was exempt from tort liability under Kentucky's Workmen's Compensation Act and dismissed the case. The Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit disagreed, holding in Boggs v. Blue Diamond Coal Co. in 1979 that a parent corporation is not immune from tort liability to its subsidiary's employees for its own, independent acts of negligence. Gerald M. Stern later told the story in his book The Scotia Widows: Inside Their Lawsuit Against Big Daddy Coal.

## Flight Context

The Scotia Mine site is near 37.048 N, 82.8293 W, in the Oven Fork community of Letcher County, Kentucky, about four miles south of Whitesburg in steep, heavily forested Appalachian terrain. From 3,500-4,500 ft AGL look for narrow creek valleys and winding roads between high ridges; there is little to distinguish the site from the air, so this is a place to remember rather than to spot. Nearby airports: Lonesome Pine Airport in Wise, Virginia (KLNP), about 27 km east-southeast; Tucker-Guthrie Memorial Airport near Harlan (FAA identifier I35, no ICAO code listed), about 52 km west-southwest; and Wendell H. Ford Airport near Hazard (KCPF), about 53 km northwest. High ridges surround the valleys, so keep generous terrain clearance, and expect valley fog on cool mornings.
