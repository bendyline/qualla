# Lynch, Kentucky

Built by a U.S. Steel subsidiary in 1917 as a coal company town, Lynch once held 10,000 people in a narrow valley beneath Kentucky's highest mountain and now counts fewer than 700.

**Location:** 36.9650, -82.9167

**Categories:** Company towns in Kentucky, Coal towns in Kentucky, Cities in Harlan County, Kentucky, U.S. Steel, Populated places established in 1917

## Introduction

At its peak, which sources place around the 1940s, some 10,000 people lived in Lynch, crowded into a narrow mountain valley in eastern Kentucky with a hospital and a movie theater of their own. By the 2020 census, 658 remained. The town sits in the valley of Looney Creek, pressed between ridges that climb well above 3,000 feet, the nearest city to Black Mountain, the highest point in Kentucky. Everything about Lynch, from its name to its rise and its long decline, traces back to coal and to the steel company that needed it.

## A Town Drawn on Company Paper

Lynch did not grow on its own. It was laid out in 1917 by the U.S. Coal and Coke Company, a subsidiary of U.S. Steel, as a company town to house the workers at its nearby coal mines. The company named it for Thomas Lynch, then its head. For decades, the town's fortunes rose and fell with the mines in the hills around it. Lynch was once one of the largest coal mining towns in the nation, and its hospital, its movie theater and its thousands of residents all depended on the work underground.

## Many Peoples in One Valley

The mines drew workers from far beyond Harlan County. Lynch became home to a Black community and an immigrant community. Part of that story is told in The Eastern Kentucky Social Club, which Wikipedia credits to PBS of Eastern Tennessee in 2012; the club itself is an annual gathering of African Americans whose families came from Kentucky's coal camps. That diversity endured long after the boom years: at the 2000 census, roughly a quarter of residents were Black. Lynch's notable people include Juanita M. Kreps, who served as U.S. Secretary of Commerce, the politician Louise Slaughter, and Leland Byrd, a college basketball player and coach.

## When the Work Changed

The decline came with new ways of mining. In the 1960s and 1970s, as the industry shifted to less labor-intensive methods, Lynch's population fell dramatically. The railroad tracks that had served Lynch and neighboring Benham were officially abandoned in 1996. The losses left their mark on the people who stayed. In 2000, 37.8 percent of residents lived below the poverty line, including more than half of those under 18. By 2010 the population was 747, and by 2020 it had dropped to 658 in a town covering just 0.3 square miles.

## Beneath Black Mountain

Geography defines Lynch. The city sits at about 1,716 feet above sea level, with 3,488-foot Looney Ridge to the north and 3,640-foot Benham Spur to the south. Benham adjoins it to the west, and the town of Cumberland lies 4 miles west along Kentucky Route 160, Lynch's main road. Heading the other way, KY 160 climbs southeast up the Looney Creek valley to the Virginia line on the crest of Black Mountain, at about 3,700 feet, before descending to Appalachia, Virginia, 16 miles from Lynch. The mountain itself rises to 4,145 feet, just above the town.

## Flight Context

Lynch lies at approximately 36.97°N, 82.92°W in the Looney Creek valley of eastern Harlan County, Kentucky, just below Black Mountain (4,145 feet), the state's highest point, on the Virginia border. The town sits at about 1,716 feet with ridges above 3,400 feet on both sides, so fly high: 6,000 to 6,500 feet MSL gives safe clearance to see the valley, the town and neighboring Benham along KY 160. Tucker-Guthrie Memorial Airport (FAA: I35, no ICAO code listed) near Harlan is about 22 nm west, Lonesome Pine Airport (KLNP) at Wise, Virginia, about 19 nm east, and Tri-Cities Airport (KTRI) about 38 nm southeast. Mountain fog, low ceilings and turbulence over the ridges are common; plan for rapidly changing weather.
