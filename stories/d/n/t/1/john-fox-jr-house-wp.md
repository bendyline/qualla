# John Fox Jr. House

The Big Stone Gap home where John Fox Jr. wrote The Trail of the Lonesome Pine, and where an opera star once came to live after falling for the mountains in his books.

**Location:** 36.8658, -82.7783

**Categories:** Historic house museums in Virginia, Museums in Wise County, Virginia, Houses on the National Register of Historic Places in Virginia, Houses completed in 1890, Houses in Wise County, Virginia, National Register of Historic Places in Wise County, Virginia, Literary museums in the United States, Biographical museums in Virginia

## Introduction

Fritzi Scheff read her way to Big Stone Gap. The Austrian singer, a star of the Metropolitan Opera, fell for the Appalachian mountains through the stories of John Fox Jr., and she eventually married the author and came to live with him in his house on Shawnee Avenue in this small Virginia town. The mountains on the page, it turned out, were more thrilling than the ones outside the window. But the house where Fox wrote those stories still stands, and it keeps his name and his world alive.

## Speculators Turned Storyteller

Fox was born in Stony Point, Kentucky, and studied at Harvard, graduating in 1883 before working as a reporter in New York for the New York Times and The Sun. He first visited the Cumberland Gap area while still a Harvard student. His two older brothers, James and Horace, owned coal mines in Jellico, Tennessee, and in 1888 the three of them came to this corner of the mountains as speculators and mineral developers. Business was the plan. But the more John Fox explored the region, the more its people fascinated him, and eventually he abandoned his real estate interests for writing. His books tapped into a curiosity among American readers about the Appalachian people, and they made him a best-selling author.

## The House on Shawnee Avenue

The original section of the house went up around 1890 as a four-room cottage. Over time it grew into a two-story frame house of 20 rooms on a stone foundation, and Fox lived here from 1890 until his death in 1919. He traveled often. He covered the Spanish–American War in Cuba for Harper's Weekly, where he served with the Rough Riders, and later reported on the Russo-Japanese War for Scribner's. Yet his most productive writing years were spent in this house, and it was here that he wrote his most famous books. The Little Shepherd of Kingdom Come appeared in 1903 and The Trail of the Lonesome Pine in 1908, and both reached the New York Times list of the year's top ten best-selling novels. At least one character is said to have come straight from local life. Devil Judd Tolliver, in The Trail of the Lonesome Pine, is said to have been based on "Devil John" Wesley Wright, a Wise County lawman.

## An Opera Star in the Mountains

Scheff came to Big Stone Gap because of those books. Fascinated by the region Fox described, she divorced her husband, married Fox in 1908 and moved into the house with him. The two became local celebrities, a famous singer and a famous novelist living in a small town in the Virginia hills. The romance of the mountains did not last for her. She found everyday Appalachian life less exciting than she had expected, and the marriage lasted just over four years; the couple divorced in 1913. Fox stayed. He died in 1919 of pneumonia, during the global influenza epidemic, in Big Stone Gap, and he was buried in the family plot in Paris, Kentucky.

## The Lonesome Pine Lives On

Fox's stories outlived him on screen. Cecil B. DeMille directed a film of The Trail of the Lonesome Pine in 1916, and Henry Hathaway made another in 1936. The Little Shepherd of Kingdom Come was filmed in 1920, again in 1928 and once more in 1961. Many of his novels were historical romances or period dramas set in this region. The house itself became a museum in 1970, after the death of Fox's sister, and it was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1974. Today it is run by the Lonesome Pine Arts and Crafts Association, and the town of Big Stone Gap says it is currently open only by scheduled appointment. Walking through its rooms, you are in the place where a Kentucky-born newspaperman turned a mineral-speculating trip into a literary career, and where the trail of the lonesome pine first found its way onto paper.

## Flight Context

The John Fox Jr. House is in Big Stone Gap, Wise County, Virginia, at 36.866°N, 82.778°W, in the mountains of far southwestern Virginia. The town lies among forested Appalachian ridges; 2,500 to 3,500 feet above the terrain gives a clear view while leaving room for the rising ground.
