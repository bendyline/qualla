# Darby Mine No. 1 disaster

In the early hours of May 20, 2006, a methane explosion behind a faulty seal at a Harlan County coal mine killed five miners, and one man survived by following a power cable through the smoke.

**Location:** 36.8758, -82.9528

**Categories:** Coal mining disasters in Kentucky, Harlan County, Kentucky, 2006 mining disasters, 2006 disasters in the United States, 2006 in Kentucky

## Introduction

Seconds after the afternoon crew stepped out of the portals, the mine exhaled behind them. A gust of air, dust and debris burst from the openings, and at first the men thought a massive roof fall had happened, or that the highwall had collapsed. Then the smell of burned coal reached the portals, and they understood. It was about 1:00 a.m. on Saturday, May 20, 2006, at Darby Mine No. 1 in Harlan County, Kentucky. Six men were still underground, and only one of them would come out alive.

## A Wall With Steel Through It

Darby Mine No. 1 lay about 26 miles east of Harlan on State Route 38, a non-union underground mine that had begun producing coal in 2001 and employed 31 people underground and three on the surface. About two months before the explosion, the company had finished mining its A Left section and sealed off the worked-out area with three walls of Omega 384 blocks. Metal roof straps, installed as roof support, had never been removed when the seals went up, and four of them passed straight through the No. 3 Seal. After their shift ended, foreman Amon Brock and shuttle car operator Jimmy Lee stayed behind, loaded a personnel carrier with oxygen and acetylene cylinders and a cutting torch, and rode down the return airway to remove the straps. There was no way to sample the air behind the No. 3 Seal. Cutting a strap that passed through it ignited methane on the other side, and the blast killed both men and destroyed the seals.

## Into the Smoke

Four men were on the maintenance shift deeper in the mine: foreman George "Bill" Petra, electrician Roy Middleton, mechanic Paris Thomas and roof bolter Paul Ledford. They heard the explosion. Petra gathered them together and warned that something might have happened because Brock and Lee had taken tanks and a torch into the return. The crew headed out along the primary escapeway and, about four crosscuts along, drove into dense smoke. They stopped, put on their self-contained self-rescuers and kept going, though none of them carried a detector that could sense carbon monoxide. After roughly 300 feet their personnel carrier jammed on the wreckage of a blasted overcast, so they climbed off and continued on foot.

## Following the Cable

Near a power center, Ledford and Middleton pulled out their mouthpieces to talk. Ledford had found the high-voltage power cable and meant to follow it to the surface. Middleton said he was going back to find the power center. After a short exchange, Ledford set off with the cable as his guide, and he never saw the others again. He walked about 1,050 feet before he collapsed. Around 3:05 a.m. he came to and crawled into the next entry, and a few minutes later rescuers walking the intake saw a light and found him. He rode out on a personnel carrier and was taken to Lonesome Pine Hospital in Big Stone Gap, Virginia. Petra, Middleton and Thomas were found at different points along the way out, each overcome by carbon monoxide.

## What the Investigators Found

On May 22, investigators entered the mine for the first time. At a news conference in Holmes Mill, Chuck Wolfe of Kentucky's Environmental and Public Protection Cabinet said chief investigator Tracy Stumbo was "pretty satisfied it was a methane explosion," with no reason to think coal dust played a part. Governor Ernie Fletcher, leaving the Capitol that day for the victims' funeral visitation, said the blast appeared to have begun in a contained area that was leaking, and that the state had set a new protocol to check non-conventional seals. The federal Mine Safety and Health Administration later concluded that the accident happened because the operator did not observe basic mine safety practices and violated critical safety standards: the seals were improperly built, safe procedures were not ensured while employees tried to correct them, and miners were not adequately trained in escape routes and self-rescuer use.

## Penalties Left Unpaid

After the explosion, delinquent fines against Kentucky Darby stood at $593,107, and the companies its owners controlled together or separately carried some $2.3 million more. In 2014, reporting by Mine Safety and Health News and National Public Radio found that the penalties for the 2006 disaster remained unpaid; NPR put the amount at about $500,000 and said the former operators had not paid despite a federal court order. MSHA's director told NPR the agency has no authority to shut down a mine over unpaid penalties alone, and NPR found nothing in the law to stop the mine's former operators from starting another company. Five men did not come home from Darby Mine No. 1: Amon Brock, Jimmy Lee, Bill Petra, Roy Middleton and Paris Thomas.

## Flight Context

The Darby Mine No. 1 site is near 36.8758 N, 82.9528 W, around Holmes Mill in eastern Harlan County, Kentucky, roughly 26 miles east of the town of Harlan along State Route 38. The terrain is steep, forested Appalachian ridge-and-hollow country; from 3,000-4,000 ft AGL look for narrow valleys threaded by winding roads between forested ridges. Nearby airports: Lonesome Pine Airport in Wise, Virginia (KLNP), about 40 km east-northeast; Tucker-Guthrie Memorial Airport near Harlan (FAA identifier I35, no ICAO code listed), about 36 km west; and Lee County Airport near Jonesville, Virginia (FAA identifier 0VG, no ICAO code listed), about 34 km southwest. Ridges rise sharply above the valleys, so keep terrain clearance in mind, and expect morning fog in the hollows.
