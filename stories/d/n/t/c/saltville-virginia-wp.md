# Saltville, Virginia

A small Virginia valley town named for its salt marshes has fed Confederate armies, witnessed a wartime massacre, lost nineteen neighbors to a chemical flood, and made hydrazine rocket fuel credited with helping power Apollo 11 to the moon.

**Location:** 36.8736, -81.7608

**Categories:** Towns in Virginia, Towns in Smyth County, Virginia, Company towns in the United States, American Civil War sites, Industrial disasters

## Introduction

When Apollo 11 carried the first humans to the moon, hydrazine rocket fuel made at Olin Mathieson Chemical Corporation's plant in Saltville is credited with helping power the mission. It is an unlikely footnote for a town of 1,824 people at the 2020 census, tucked into Smyth and Washington counties in southwest Virginia. Saltville takes its name from its salt marshes, and long before rockets, those marshes drew animals, then hunters, then armies. Each of them left a mark on this quiet valley.

## Where the Animals Came to the Salt

Before European settlement, the marshes attracted wildlife, and the record reaches deep into the past. Excavations at an archaeological site known as SV-2 have recovered several well-preserved skeletons of now-extinct species dating back to the last ice age. Indigenous peoples of varying cultures later hunted at the marshes. In 1992, archaeologists proposed that a prehistoric "Saltville Complex Petty Chiefdom" had flourished here, with its principal village at the Northwood High School site, and reported distinctive "Saltville style" gorgets along with iron and copper materials scattered across the region. The historic Native people of the area were the Chisca. In the spring of 1567, the Spanish conquistador Hernando Moyano de Morales led 15 to 20 soldiers north from Fort San Juan at Joara, in what is now western North Carolina. They attacked and burned the Chisca village of Maniatique, which may have stood at or near the site of Saltville.

## Salt for a Hungry Army

Three centuries later the salt mattered to a war. During the American Civil War, Saltville was one of the Confederacy's main saltworks, and the product was considered vital because it preserved meat for soldiers and civilians alike. That made the town a target. On October 2, 1864, Union forces under Brigadier General Stephen G. Burbridge attacked in the First Battle of Saltville. Their ranks included two of the few Black cavalry units in the United States Colored Troops. The Confederates held, and the Union attack was defeated. Two months later, the Union cavalry commander General George Stoneman led a second attack, the Second Battle of Saltville. This time the Confederate defenders lost and Union troops destroyed the saltworks, a loss considered a major blow to the Confederacy's dwindling resources.

## The Men Who Did Not Come Home

The first battle had a terrible aftermath. After the fighting, Confederates murdered a number of wounded Black soldiers, in what became known as the Saltville Massacre. These were cavalrymen of the U.S. Colored Cavalry, men who had enlisted to fight for the Union, and they were killed after the battle while lying wounded. Historians' counts vary widely. Thomas Mays, in his 1995 book The Saltville Massacre, argued that 46 soldiers were killed, and another analysis of National Archives records puts the number of men from the 5th and 6th U.S. Colored Cavalry who were murdered at 45 to 50, while William Marvel considers five to about two dozen more likely. A Union surgeon left behind reported that five to seven wounded Black soldiers, along with a white lieutenant, Elza C. Smith, were killed in the hospital at Emory and Henry College. Shortly after the war, the guerrilla leader Champ Ferguson was tried by a military court in Nashville for this and other killings, found guilty, and hanged at the Tennessee State Prison on October 20, 1865.

## A Company Town and a Christmas Eve Flood

Saltville became a company town, and industry shaped both its fortunes and its worst day. The Mathieson Alkali Company stored liquid chemical waste behind what would become the infamous "Muck Dam." On December 24, 1924, the dam broke, sending a huge volume of waste into the North Fork of the Holston River and killing nineteen people who lived along its banks. The river remained polluted and virtually dead for decades. Industry persisted all the same, and the Olin Mathieson plant is where the hydrazine credited with helping power the Apollo 11 mission was made. The ground beneath the town has a job too. Saltville's salt caverns have been used to store natural gas since the Saltville Gas Storage Company began commercial operation in 2003, and Wikipedia describes them as the only such caverns serving the Mid-Atlantic states. The facility is described as high-deliverability storage, because gas can be injected and withdrawn quickly to meet sudden demand.

## Songs, Clay and Memory

Downtown, the Museum of the Middle Appalachians gathers these threads under one roof, with exhibits on the region's geological history, the Civil War, the company town era and the Woodland Indians. The town's notable names range widely. Ceramic artist Georgia Blizzard made works that are now in the permanent collections of the Smithsonian American Art Museum and other museums. Folk singer Texas Gladden and her brother Hobart Smith, remembered as an old-time music legend, are both counted among Saltville's own, as is Robert Porterfield, the founder of the Barter Theater. Much earlier, Elizabeth Henry Campbell Russell, a sister of Patrick Henry, introduced Methodism to Southwest Virginia; a church and a cemetery in Saltville still bear her name.

## Flight Context

Saltville sits at 36.8736°N, 81.7608°W in a valley in southwest Virginia, straddling the Smyth–Washington county line along the North Fork of the Holston River. Surrounding terrain is hilly to mountainous, so a sightseeing altitude of about 4,500–5,500 ft MSL keeps a safe margin while still showing the town and the river corridor. Virginia Highlands Airport (KVJI) at Abingdon lies about 17 nm to the southwest. Tazewell County Airport (KJFZ) is about 12 nm north across the ridges, and Mountain Empire Airport (KMKJ) near Rural Retreat is about 20 nm east. Tri-Cities Airport (KTRI) is about 39 nm southwest. Valley fog is common on still mornings; check ceilings before descending toward the valley floor.
