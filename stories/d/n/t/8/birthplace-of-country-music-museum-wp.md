# Birthplace of Country Music Museum

A Bristol, Virginia, museum devoted to the 1927 Bristol Sessions, the two weeks of recording that introduced the Carter Family and Jimmie Rodgers to the world.

**Location:** 36.5963, -82.1829

## Introduction

"They wander in," the record producer Ralph Peer remembered decades later. "He's dressed in overalls and the women are country women from way back there. They looked like hillbillies. But as soon as I heard Sara's voice, that was it." The family was the Carters, the year was 1927, and the place was a temporary studio in a hat company building on State Street in Bristol, the street that marks the line between Tennessee and Virginia. The recordings made there, the Bristol Sessions, are considered by some the "Big Bang" of modern country music. A few blocks away, the Birthplace of Country Music Museum now tells their story.

## Twelve Days on State Street

Peer was a producer for the Victor Talking Machine Company, and Bristol was one stop on a two-month, $60,000 trip through the South in search of new music. Electrical recording, introduced in 1925, had made equipment somewhat more portable and able to capture softer instruments like guitars, dulcimers and jaw harps. Ernest Stoneman, a musician from Galax, Virginia, urged Peer to come to Bristol, which, together with Johnson City and Kingsport, formed what Wikipedia calls the largest urban area in the Appalachians at the time. Between July 25 and August 5, 1927, Peer recorded in the Taylor-Christian Hat Company building at 408 State Street, on its upper floors. Artists were paid $50 in cash for each side they cut, plus two and a half cents for every record sold. Stoneman went first, on July 25. The site of that building is now a parking lot, not far from the museum.

## The Word Gets Out

At first, Peer's newspaper advertisements drew little response, and the early acts filled only the first week. Then a local paper ran a story about Stoneman that stressed the $3,600 in royalties he had earned in 1926 and the $100 a day he was making in Bristol. That did it. Dozens of artists poured into town, many of whom had never been to Bristol before, and Peer added night sessions to keep up. One of the newcomers was Jimmie Rodgers, who had fallen out with his band over what name to record under; he recorded solo, and the band recorded as the Tenneva Ramblers. The Carters came too. Peer had written to the family earlier that summer, and once he heard Sara sing, he knew. In all, the 1927 sessions captured 19 performers or groups and, by Wikipedia's count, 76 songs. They also produced two new stars, the Carter Family and Jimmie Rodgers, both of whom recorded for the first time in Bristol before gaining prominence.

## A Museum for the Big Bang

The Birthplace of Country Music Museum opened in Bristol, Virginia, in 2014. Its focus is how evolving sound technology shaped the success of the 1927 sessions, and how the region's musical heritage still thrives across Appalachia and beyond. Theater experiences, interactive displays and live performances bring the story to life, and a Special Exhibits Gallery hosts rotating exhibitions from guest curators and institutions including the Smithsonian. The museum also keeps a collection of objects, photographs and ephemera, along with a digital archive. Music doesn't just hang on the walls here. Radio Bristol, the low-power station WBCM-LP, went live from inside the museum on August 27, 2015, and current listings describe it as still broadcasting live from the building. The recording industry has returned the favor: Orthophonic Joy: The 1927 Bristol Sessions Revisited, an album on which Dolly Parton, Vince Gill, Marty Stuart and others reinterpret songs from the sessions, came out on May 12, 2015, and Wikipedia describes it as a Sony Legacy Recordings release benefiting the museum.

## Flight Context

The Birthplace of Country Music Museum is in downtown Bristol, Virginia, at 36.596°N, 82.183°W, a short walk from State Street, which forms the Tennessee–Virginia line. From 2,500 to 3,500 feet AGL, look for State Street running through downtown, with the twin cities spreading out on either side. Virginia Highlands Airport in Abingdon (KVJI) is about 9 nm northeast, and Tri-Cities Airport (KTRI) is about 13 nm southwest, so expect airline traffic in the area. The surrounding Appalachian ridges can produce gusty winds, and valley fog often lingers on cool mornings.
