# Virginia Creeper Trail

A mountain railroad named for the way its trains crept up steep grades became a rail trail of roughly 34 miles from Abingdon to Whitetop, saved from the scrap contractors in the 1970s and now rebuilding after Hurricane Helene.

**Location:** 36.7090, -81.9713

**Categories:** Rail trails in Virginia, National Recreation Trails in Virginia, Mount Rogers National Recreation Area, Bike paths in Virginia, Southwest Virginia

## Introduction

Nobody is entirely certain how the Virginia Creeper got its name. The explanation most people accept, according to the trail's conservancy, is that the old mountain trains had to creep up and down this line, its route winding and its grades steep, much as the Virginia creeper vine meanders over signposts, trees and outbuildings across rural southwest Virginia. The trains are long gone. In their place runs a rail trail of roughly 34 miles (sources give anywhere from 33 to 35) for hikers, cyclists and horseback riders, stretching from Abingdon through Damascus to Whitetop, near Mount Rogers National Recreation Area and the North Carolina line.

## Stone and Earth, Moved by Hand

The path is older than the railroad that finally used it. In the 1880s, speculators organized as the Abingdon Coal and Iron Railroad, betting on an iron-ore boom in the region. Much of the manual labor on the 16-mile right-of-way between Damascus and Abingdon was done by African-American men from a state prison, who moved enormous quantities of stone and earth to cut the grade. According to old Norfolk & Western documents cited by the conservancy, some of those men died while working and were buried near where they died, in unmarked graves along the right-of-way. Their graves are unmarked, but every rider who rolls along this stretch travels on their work. The boom never came. The company failed without ever opening the line, and the right-of-way passed to the Virginia–Carolina and Southern Railway and then the Virginia–Carolina Railway, which began operating in Damascus in February 1900.

## Timber, Passengers and a Photographer's Eye

By 1912 the line reached Whitetop, and by the end of that decade it ran to Elkland, North Carolina, a place now called Todd. In 1919 the Norfolk & Western, which had helped fund construction, took control and named it the Abingdon Branch. Service to Todd ended in 1933, when the terminus moved to West Jefferson. The last steam engine retired in 1957, replaced by diesels, and the conservancy records the final passenger train in 1962. This colorful, rustic branch was a favorite of railroad photographer O. Winston Link. He was famous for night scenes shot in black and white under synchronized arrays of flashbulbs, yet on the Abingdon Branch he worked in daylight and in color. Many of his celebrated images are now housed in the former Norfolk & Western passenger station in Roanoke, which today is the O. Winston Link Museum.

## The Injunction That Saved the Trestles

By 1974 the Norfolk & Western had petitioned the Interstate Commerce Commission to abandon the line, effective April 1, 1977. The last train ran on March 31, 1977, and hard rains that year flooded and damaged most of the line; the damage was never repaired. Removal of the track followed quickly, and the trestles very nearly went with it. A Chicago contractor had bought the line's rails, bridges and trestles and had already begun taking them out when Dr. French Moore Jr., an Abingdon dentist who was then the town's vice-mayor, joined other supporters in obtaining an emergency injunction that halted the removal of the bridges and trestles. In North Carolina, the old right-of-way went back to the landowners. In Virginia, the Forest Service and the towns of Abingdon and Damascus secured the corridor. In June 1987, the conservancy records, it was dedicated as a National Recreation Trail (the national trails registry lists the certification as 1985), and by mid-summer 1989 repairs and construction from Abingdon to Whitetop Station were complete.

## Seventeen Miles Downhill

The elevation profile shapes how people ride it. From Abingdon, the trail drops about 300 feet to the South Holston River near Damascus, then climbs nearly 2,000 feet to Whitetop, so before Helene, shuttle services in Damascus carried cyclists up the mountain for a 17-mile descent back to town. While the upper half is closed, Damascus outfitters shuttle riders to the Abingdon trailhead for the open Abingdon-to-Damascus stretch. Along the way the route follows Laurel Creek, passes through National Forest, crosses the Appalachian Trail and rolls over restored trestles. Some stretches cross private farmland, where cyclists open and close gates behind them. The old stops read like a timetable: Watauga, Alvarado, Damascus, Straight Branch, Taylors Valley, Creek Junction, Green Cove, Whitetop. The original 4-8-0 steam locomotive No. 433 stands beside the Abingdon trailhead, and cabooses sit at Damascus, the trail's midpoint, and at Taylors Valley. Races have used the corridor too, among them the Down the Mountain Marathon and the Dam Yeti 50.

## Floodwater, Again

In September 2024, Hurricane Helene brought the kind of water that had ended the railroad. Flooding washed away seventeen miles of the trail and damaged 30 of its 31 historic trestles, eighteen of them destroyed, forcing a partial closure. The Abingdon-to-Damascus half stayed open. For a trail built on salvaged bridges and a line that floods had already killed once, the losses cut deep. Reconstruction has been underway since, and the conservancy says the upper section is scheduled to reopen on March 31, 2027. The date carries an echo. In the conservancy's own history, the last train on the Virginia Creeper ran on March 31, 1977, which means that if the schedule holds, the mountain section will return exactly fifty years after the whistle went quiet.

## Flight Context

The Abingdon trailhead sits at 36.709°N, 81.971°W in downtown Abingdon, Virginia (town elevation about 2,087 ft). From there the trail runs roughly 10 nm east-southeast to Damascus (about 36.63°N, 81.79°W), then climbs east toward Whitetop and the Mount Rogers high country. Whitetop Mountain rises to about 5,525 ft just beside the upper section, with Mount Rogers, Virginia's highest point, close by, so check the sectional chart and keep well above those summits when tracing the upper corridor; the South Holston River near Damascus and the town itself make good waypoints. Virginia Highlands Airport (KVJI) is about 3 nm west-southwest of the trailhead, and Tri-Cities Airport (KTRI) at Blountville, Tennessee, is about 25 nm southwest. Mountain weather changes quickly here, and low cloud can hide the upper section even when Abingdon is clear.
