# Martha Washington Inn

Built in 1832 as a general's family home, Abingdon's Martha Washington Inn has been a women's college, a Civil War barracks and hospital, a lodging for Barter Theatre actors and a hotel, and it has gathered a ghost story for nearly every chapter.

**Location:** 36.7096, -81.9755

**Categories:** Hotels in Virginia, American Civil War hospitals, Historic Hotels of America, Reportedly haunted locations, Buildings and structures in Washington County, Virginia

## Introduction

In a parlor of an Abingdon hotel stands a Dutch-baroque grandfather clock more than nine feet tall. One of General Francis Preston's daughters had it shipped from England, and it has outlasted almost every purpose the house around it has served. Preston, a hero of the War of 1812, built this brick residence in 1832 for his family of nine children. Since then it has been a college for women, a Civil War barracks and hospital, a boarding house for actors and, since 1935, the Martha Washington Inn, a hotel that now offers spa treatments alongside its rooms and has gathered a ghost story for nearly every chapter of its past.

## A General's House Becomes a College

Preston spent just under $15,000 on the house. His family kept it until 1858, when it sold for $21,000 to the founders of Martha Washington College, and the rooms where the Prestons had lived began a long public life. They have held up well. The building's architecture has been preserved for more than 150 years, and the family's original living room now serves as the inn's main lobby, so travelers arriving with their luggage walk straight into the room where the general's family once lived. The college was devoted entirely to women. It educated them in these halls for about seventy years before the Great Depression finally closed its doors, and the towering clock in the parlor remains one of the original Preston belongings still in the house.

## Rifles on the Lawn, Wounded in the Halls

During the Civil War the campus took on a harder purpose. It served as the training ground for a Confederate unit, the Washington Mounted Rifles, and the building became both barracks and hospital. After skirmishes between Union and Confederate forces, the wounded were brought to the school for treatment. That stretch of the house's history left a deep mark on its legends, because many of the ghosts said to walk here wear a soldier's uniform or wait for one. After the college closed, the building found yet another life. The Martha Washington Inn opened in 1935 and has operated as a hotel ever since. It also housed actors performing at the Barter Theatre next door, although today the company's actors usually stay at the Barter Inn. Wikipedia lists the hotel as a member of Historic Hotels of America, the official program of the National Trust for Historic Preservation.

## The Stain That Keeps Coming Back

One of the inn's saddest legends is a love story. A young Confederate soldier in Abingdon, the tale goes, was assigned to carry important papers about the location of the Union army to General Robert E. Lee. He was hopelessly in love with a young woman at the college, and knowing the danger ahead, he felt he had to say goodbye before he left. He made his way through the caves beneath Abingdon and climbed a secret stairway into the college. As he took his leave, two Union officers came up the stairs. He had no way out. They shot him in front of the woman he loved, and his blood stained the floor where he fell. According to the legend, that stain has never stayed gone. Carpets laid over the spot are said to develop mysterious holes, and even after the floorboards have been refinished, the mark returns.

## Hoofbeats, Mud and a Sealed Tunnel

Other stories cluster around the house like moths. On moonless nights, a phantom horse is said to roam the grounds and wait by the front steps for its master, a Union soldier shot in front of the house in 1864. Inside, people have reported a soldier hobbling down a hallway on a crutch, leaving a trail of mud behind him, his head split open by a shot, long past any help the old hospital could have given. Why he lingers is anyone's guess. Then there is the tunnel. A passage once linked the inn to the Barter Theatre, and actors who used it in the 1930s and 1940s described a malevolent presence, never seen but strongly felt. Some believe it was a man killed when the tunnel collapsed in 1890, others a Confederate soldier who smuggled ammunition out of the basement. The inn's end of the tunnel has been sealed for years, though the theater's section still carries electrical cables.

## What the Stone Walls Hold

The darkest story belongs to the basement. According to local lore, enslaved Black people were held in an underground chamber there, and some of them were buried within its stone walls. The lore is thinly documented, and accounts of the inn's ghosts differ from teller to teller. It sits uneasily beside the romantic tales of doomed sweethearts and loyal horses, because whatever its literal truth, it points to the real history of enslaved people in this region, whose names the story does not record. The town's lore is the subject of Donna Akers Warmuth's 2005 book, Legends, Stories and Ghostly Tales of Abingdon and Washington County, Virginia. Whatever a visitor makes of the ghosts, the building itself carries every era it has lived through, from a general's parlor to a schoolroom, a sickroom and a hotel lobby, all under one brick roof.

## Flight Context

The Martha Washington Inn stands on West Main Street in downtown Abingdon, Virginia, at 36.7096°N, 81.9755°W, with the Barter Theatre directly adjacent. The town sits at about 2,087 feet above sea level in the valleys of Southwest Virginia, so a pass at roughly 3,500–4,500 feet MSL gives a good view of the historic downtown. Virginia Highlands Airport (KVJI, field elevation 2,087 ft) lies only about 3 nm west-southwest of the inn, making the downtown easy to spot on departure or approach; stay clear of its traffic pattern. Tri-Cities Airport (KTRI) at Blountville, Tennessee, is about 25 nm to the west-southwest. Abingdon is also the terminus of the Virginia Creeper Trail rail-trail. Expect hilly terrain around the town, and check for morning valley fog before a low sightseeing flight.
