# Waverly Hills Sanatorium

Built on a Louisville hilltop to fight tuberculosis, the "White Plague," Waverly Hills grew from open-air pavilions into a five-story hospital, emptied once antibiotics arrived, outlived a string of failed schemes, and now draws visitors as one of America's most famous "haunted" buildings.

**Location:** 38.1301, -85.8417

**Categories:** Defunct hospitals in Louisville, Kentucky, Tuberculosis sanatoria, National Register of Historic Places in Louisville, Kentucky, Reportedly haunted locations, Medical history

## Introduction

The name came from a schoolteacher's bookshelf. In 1883 Major Thomas H. Hays bought the land now known as Waverly Hill as his family home, and because it lay far from any existing school, he opened a one-room schoolhouse on Pages Lane for his daughters and hired Lizzie Lee Harris to teach there. Harris loved Walter Scott's Waverley novels, so she named it the Waverley School. The romantic name stuck to the hill. A generation later it would belong to something far grimmer: a hospital built to fight the disease people called the White Plague.

## Fresh Air Against the White Plague

In the early 1900s tuberculosis swept through Jefferson County, and in 1910 Waverly Hills Sanatorium opened to contain it. The first building was modest: a two-story wooden structure with an administrative building and two open-air pavilions, each housing 20 patients, for the treatment of "early cases." The need quickly outran it. On August 31, 1912, every tuberculosis patient from Louisville's City Hospital was moved to temporary quarters in tents on the Waverly grounds while a hospital for advanced cases was finished, and that December it opened for another 40 patients. In 1914 a children's pavilion added 50 beds, bringing the known capacity to around 130. It served not only sick children but also the children of tuberculosis patients, youngsters who otherwise could not be properly cared for while a parent fought the disease. Planners hoped to add a building every year, so there may have been more beds than the records show.

## Five Stories and a Tunnel

The wooden buildings needed constant repair, and there were never enough beds; people were being turned away for lack of space. In March 1924 construction began on a five-story building designed to hold more than 400 patients, and it opened on October 17, 1926. Beneath the hill ran a 500-foot tunnel built to carry steam and supplies between the heating plant at the bottom of the slope and the main building, with a cable car to haul loads up and down. According to a number of stories and anecdotes, for a limited time between the 1920s and 1940s the tunnel also carried the bodies of patients who had died down to a waiting ambulance, so that the living on the wards would not learn that someone else had lost the fight. Then medicine changed everything. After streptomycin, discovered in 1943, reached patients here (in 1949, by one account), tuberculosis cases gradually fell until the great hospital was no longer needed. It closed in 1961, and its remaining patients were sent to Hazelwood Sanatorium in Louisville.

## Plans That Never Took Hold

The building reopened in 1962 as the Woodhaven Geriatric Center, a nursing home for older people, including patients with dementia; it was later troubled by overcrowding, staff shortages and reports of neglect before it, too, closed. In 1983, the year the sanatorium buildings joined the National Register of Historic Places, Simpsonville developer J. Clifford Todd bought the property for a reported $3,005,000. He and architect Milton Thompson wanted to turn it into a minimum-security state prison, but dropped the idea after neighbors protested, then proposed apartments that hinged on Jefferson Fiscal Court buying about 140 acres from them for $400,000. In March 1996 Robert Alberhasky bought the site, and his Christ the Redeemer Foundation announced the most audacious plan of all: what it billed as the world's tallest statue of Jesus, 150 feet tall and 150 feet wide, standing on the sanatorium's roof. In a year, the national fundraising drive raised only $3,000, and the project was canceled in December 1997.

## An Afterlife on Screen

Today tours are conducted inside the old hospital, with a 2026 season that runs from March through August and a Halloween haunted house on weekend nights through October 31, and it is marketed as haunted. The reputation has travelled far. The television show Ghost Hunters devoted a March 2006 episode, "Haunted Sanatorium," to Waverly Hills, and Ryan Bergara and Shane Madej visited in the second season of BuzzFeed Unsolved, then returned for the premiere of Ghost Files on their independent Watcher channel. The 2006 horror film Death Tunnel was shot here, and the video game Project Zomboid, set in Kentucky, features a fictionalized version called the "Sunderland Hills Sanatorium." On August 11, 2007, the building even hosted the final show of the touring Sounds of the Underground festival, with metal bands including Lamb of God, Hatebreed and Gwar, though complaints from local residents make a repeat unlikely. Behind all the ghost stories stand real people: the patients, children among them, who came to this hill hoping that rest and open air would save them.

## Flight Context

Waverly Hills Sanatorium stands at 38.1301°N, 85.8417°W on a hilltop in the Waverly Hills neighborhood of Louisville, Kentucky. The long five-story building is visible from 2,000–2,500 feet above the ground but sits just southwest of Louisville Muhammad Ali International (KSDF), about 6 nm away, so any sightseeing pass is inside busy airline airspace and needs ATC coordination. Other nearby airports: Bowman Field (KLOU), about 10 nm, and Godman Army Airfield at Fort Knox (KFTK), a military field about 15 nm southwest. The Ohio River to the west and the airport's runways make good orientation references.
