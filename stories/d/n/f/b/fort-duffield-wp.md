# Fort Duffield

Raised by Union troops on a steep hill above West Point, Kentucky, in the first winter of the Civil War, Fort Duffield's unusual serpentine earthworks sat empty by the time John Hunt Morgan's raiders came through the region, and volunteers later pulled them back out of the forest.

**Location:** 37.9931, -85.9419

**Categories:** American Civil War forts, Kentucky in the American Civil War, National Register of Historic Places in Hardin County, Kentucky, Parks in Kentucky, Hiking

## Introduction

The timing could hardly have been worse. Union soldiers spent the first winter of the Civil War building an earthen fort on a steep hill above West Point, Kentucky, and about 950 troops were reportedly stationed behind its walls. Then they were sent to the front lines, and by the fall of 1862 Fort Duffield had seen its last use, abandoned because it seemed the war would never come near it. In July 1863 John Hunt Morgan led his Confederate raiders through this part of Kentucky on the way to the Ohio River crossing at Brandenburg, downriver, and by Wikipedia's account rode right past the fort. With a garrison still on the hill, the raiders might have been challenged there.

## Sherman's Supply Line

The fort began with a worry. William Tecumseh Sherman was concerned about Confederate positions at Bowling Green and Columbus, and he sent the 37th Indiana and the 9th Michigan Infantry Regiment to West Point, a river town at the confluence of the Ohio and Salt rivers. The plan was to turn West Point into a Union supply base for Elizabethtown, and a supply base needed protecting. Sherman ordered a fort built on Pearman Hill, high above both the town and the route inland. Construction began on November 3, 1861, and by late 1861 the earthwork stood largely ready. The fort was named for the Rev. George Duffield of Detroit, whose son, Colonel William W. Duffield, commanded the 9th Michigan. It was a family honor carried a long way from home, to a Kentucky hilltop that the regiment would not hold for long.

## A Wall That Snakes

The typical Civil War fort in Kentucky was built in a star shape. Fort Duffield is different: it is mostly a long, zigzagging earthen wall, shaped like a wide W with ten angles of fire for artillery and an open rear above the steep slope, and its earthworks are well preserved. When the soldiers were here, the view was stark: a clearing reportedly a mile wide separated the fort from the nearest trees, leaving no cover for an approaching enemy. A fresh water spring rose inside the fort itself, a precious thing for a garrison on a hilltop. After the fort was abandoned, the forest grew back and closed around the walls, and by the 1990s they sat in thick overgrowth. Fort Duffield was added to the National Register of Historic Places on January 31, 1994.

## Out of the Overgrowth

The fort's second life began in 1995, when a team of volunteers led by Billy Morris and Dennis Wren started clearing away the overgrowth. By 1996 Fort Duffield was open as a tourist attraction. Visitors could catch a bus in West Point and ride it to the top of the steep hill, where an information station displayed a map of the fort and its paths. Wooden walkways led up past small cabins and tents where reenactors gave demonstrations, and a trail continued to a lookout with a clear view of the Ohio River and the town below. From there a smaller path wound past the natural spring that soldiers had drawn from during the war, before looping back to where the tour began.

## Hiking the Hill Today

As of 2021, the buses and reenactments had stopped, but the hill was still open to anyone willing to climb it. The trails and pathways lead past the little cabins to the lookout, where a bench faces the river. The spring is now too dangerous to approach and has been excluded from the public trail. The park belongs to the City of West Point, and volunteers, including the West Point Historical Society and the West Point Historical Museum, help keep the fort in shape. On the hillside below the fort lies a Civil War cemetery for soldiers who died of disease in the winter of 1861 and 1862, cleared for visitors and marked with memorial stones. At the bottom of the hill, a parking lot doubles as the starting point for several biking trails, and a boat dock and campground off Dixie Highway (31W) put paddlers into the Salt River.
