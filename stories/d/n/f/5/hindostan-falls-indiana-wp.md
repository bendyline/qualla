# Hindostan Falls, Indiana

Named by a veteran of the British East India Company, Hindostan was briefly one of frontier Indiana's largest towns before an epidemic and an economic collapse emptied it, leaving only cemeteries, a restored church and holes drilled into a riverside rock.

**Location:** 38.6244, -86.8508

**Categories:** Ghost towns, Former populated places in Indiana, Martin County, Indiana, Frontier history, Epidemics

## Introduction

Captain Caleb Fellows had seen India before he ever saw Indiana. The English immigrant had served with the British East India Company on the subcontinent, and when he came to the United States and invested in land on the raw Indiana frontier, he gave his new town a name that carried the far side of the world with it: Hindostan. For a few bright years the name looked like a promise. By 1820 Hindostan was the largest community in what was then still Daviess County and the most promising town on the White River. Within a decade it was emptying, and today not a single building stands.

## A Town on the Stagecoach Road

Location made Hindostan. The settlement sat on the original stagecoach route between New Albany and Vincennes, one of the only roads in a state that had been a territory until 1816. Settlers surging toward new land on the Indiana and Illinois frontiers passed right through, and by 1820 about 1,200 people lived here, making Hindostan one of the largest settlements in frontier Indiana. Not all of them lived on solid ground. Many made their homes on houseboats on the East Fork of the White River, close to the falls. The same traffic that fed the town's growth carried a hidden cost. A crossroads for travelers was also a crossroads for whatever they carried, and Hindostan was constantly open to the diseases that moved along the road and the river with them.

## The Fever Year

An epidemic broke out around 1820, though the date is not settled: Visit Indiana puts it in the early 1820s, while the state's historical marker dates a 'Great Sickness' to 1828. Whether it was yellow fever or cholera is not certain, but the pattern was familiar across the Midwestern frontier. Towns clustered on rivers for easy transportation, which often meant building on flood plains that bred insects in huge numbers, and water- and insect-borne illnesses followed. What set Hindostan apart was the ferocity of the outbreak: people died, or they packed up and left. Money trouble compounded the sickness. An economic depression struck around the same time, and families who had bought land on credit defaulted and fled. Hindostan may have lost as many residents to the depression as to disease. Those who stayed often could not pay their taxes, and county and local creditors foreclosed on their property. By 1824 fewer than half of the town's people remained, though many seem to have stayed somewhere in the county.

## The Tax Collector's Iron Pot

Hindostan's legend involves buried money. According to rumor, a county tax collector was holding several thousand dollars in revenue when he fell ill during the epidemic. He was thought to have buried the gold and silver coins in an iron pot for safekeeping until the sickness passed. He died instead, and if the pot exists, its location died with him. A state tourism account tells it differently, with a town treasurer hiding civic funds in a well. The official record of the town's decline is more prosaic. In 1828 the county seat was relocated to Mt. Pleasant, several miles away. The post office, established in 1819, was discontinued on December 29, 1830. Mt. Pleasant itself was abandoned by most of its residents in 1853 for a nearby site now called Loogootee, four miles from Hindostan, while the county seat, which had left Mt. Pleasant in 1844, passed through short-lived locations including Trinity Springs and then, in 1871, settled at Shoals. Shoals counted 677 people at the 2020 census, a little more than half of what Hindostan held at its peak.

## Holes in the Rock

The site of the once-prosperous town eventually became farmland, and today it is a state-owned fishing and wildlife area. A historic marker at State Highway 550 and County Road 55 stands about six-tenths of a mile north of where the town was. No buildings survive. What remains is scattered and quiet: a few pioneer cemeteries nearby, a restored church, and a large flat rock along the river pocked with numerous square holes, some 128 of them by the state's count. They were drilled to support a mill at Hindostan, one owned by Frederick Shulz (also spelled Schultz), and they can still be found below the falls, whose look changes with the level of the East Fork. Of everything the town left behind, those sockets may be the most telling. Somebody drilled them into solid stone, in a town they expected to last.

## Flight Context

Hindostan Falls lies at 38.6244°N, 86.8508°W on the East Fork White River in Center Township, Martin County, in southern Indiana. A low pass at 1,500–2,500 feet AGL is best for picking out the river, the falls and the State Highway 550 corridor, where a historic marker stands about six-tenths of a mile north of the old town site; Loogootee is about four miles away. Nearby airports: French Lick Municipal (KFRH), about 12 nm; Daviess County Airport at Washington (KDCY), about 14 nm; Virgil I. Grissom Municipal at Bedford (KBFR) and Huntingburg (KHNB), each about 23 nm. The river and its falls are easiest to spot in clear daytime conditions.
