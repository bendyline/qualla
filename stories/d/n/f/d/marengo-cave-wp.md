# Marengo Cave

Two children with two candles crawled into a sinkhole in Marengo, Indiana, in 1883, and a landowner's quick ban on souvenir-breaking kept the glittering cave they found nearly untouched for the tours that began within days.

**Location:** 38.3755, -86.3399

**Categories:** Caves of Indiana, Show caves in the United States, National Natural Landmarks in Indiana, Limestone caves, Tourist attractions in Crawford County, Indiana

## Introduction

Blanche Hiestand was fifteen and worked as a cook at the Marengo Academy, where she overheard some boys talking about a sinkhole in the woods. They suspected it led to a cave and planned to come back with lanterns. Blanche decided to beat them to it. On September 6, 1883, as the cave's staff tell the story, she ran home, enlisted her eleven-year-old brother Orris, took two candles, and headed for the woods. The children crawled about 50 feet down a narrow passage and became the first people known to set foot in Marengo Cave.

## Diamonds by Candlelight

What they saw in that brief, wavering light was enough to frighten them back to the surface. It was also enough to convince them they had found diamonds, for the flowstone walls sparkled as the candles passed. Three days later the children told the landowner, a Mr. Stewart, whose property they had been trespassing on. Stewart, believing the cave might hold diamonds, quickly organized a group of men from town to explore. The sparkle turned out to be flowstone rather than gems, but the cavern was rich with glittering mineral formations, and within days of its discovery, guided tours were under way at a quarter a head. Public tours have run ever since, and Marengo is one of just a handful of show caves in Indiana and the one its owners bill as the state's most visited.

## Three Stories, One Hole

Not everyone remembers the discovery the same way. A county history tells a livelier tale: on August 18, 1883, a few boys chasing a rabbit pulled loose rocks from its den until the hole opened into the mouth of a great cavern, and a crowd of men and boys with lanterns and strings went back to explore. An early state account names the Hiestand children and the September 6 date, and notes that the rabbit story had spread, though Stewart and others connected with the cave emphatically denied it, crediting "childish curiosity" alone. That account adds a quieter explanation for why the hole appeared at all. Shortly before the discovery, the virgin timber had been cut from the hillside, and Stewart believed the increased gullying and runoff that followed had opened the sink.

## The Rooms Nobody Noticed

The first explorers were not gentle. Stewart saw people coming out of his cave carrying formations they had broken from the walls and ceiling, and, realizing how unusual the place was, he banned the practice. According to the state account, that early protection is largely why the interior remains nearly unmarred. Luck helped too. To the right of the old entrance lie the Pillared Palace, the Queen's Palace and the Crystal Palace, rooms described as literally filled with mineral formations. The first party walked straight past them and on through the length of the cave. By the time anyone found those rooms, Stewart's rules were in place, and they survived intact, touched only slightly by the effects of a hard winter freeze.

## Beyond Blowing Bat Crawl

The Stewart family kept the cave until 1955, when Floyd Denton bought it with ambitious plans, cut short by his death from a stroke in 1961. The current owners bought it in 1973, just as Interstate 64 was being built through southern Indiana, and better roads, more mobile travelers and steady improvements brought the business back to life. Designated a National Natural Landmark in 1984, the cave still had surprises to give. On June 14, 1992, a small crawlway called Blowing Bat Crawl was finally broken through, revealing the cave's stream level. The discovery added about 3.5 miles of passage, including what is described as the largest room of any cave in Indiana, and brought Marengo to almost 5 miles of known passageway, among the longer caves in the state. Today it is open nearly all year, closed only on a few holidays, with the mile-long Dripstone Trail tour and the shorter Crystal Palace tour covering entirely different sections. Filmmakers have noticed, too: the cave's own press release confirms that Madison (2001) shot an exploring scene underground here, and Wikipedia also lists Abby (1974) and Fire from Below (2008), which other sources do not confirm.

## Flight Context

Marengo Cave lies at approximately 38.376°N, 86.340°W, in the small town of Marengo in Crawford County, southern Indiana. The cave is invisible from the air, so at 2,000–3,000 ft AGL use the town of Marengo itself as the reference. French Lick Municipal Airport (KFRH) is about 30 km to the west-northwest; Paoli Municipal Airport (FAA identifier I42, no ICAO code) is about 26 km to the north-northwest; and Louisville Muhammad Ali International (KSDF) is about 57 km to the east-southeast. Expect summer haze and afternoon storms; morning fog is common in the valleys in autumn.
