# West Baden Springs Hotel

Built in under a year out of spite after a fire, the West Baden Springs Hotel raised what was then considered the world's largest dome over a southern Indiana spa, then survived life as a seminary, a college and a near-ruin before reopening in 2007.

**Location:** 38.5672, -86.6181

**Categories:** National Historic Landmarks in Indiana, Historic Hotels of America, Historic Civil Engineering Landmarks, Domes, Buildings and structures in Orange County, Indiana

## Introduction

Lee Wiley Sinclair built what was then considered the largest dome in the world because a rival turned him down. After fire struck his West Baden Springs Hotel on June 14, 1901, Sinclair offered to sell the property to Thomas Taggart, the new owner of the competing French Lick Springs Hotel a mile down the road. Taggart refused and boasted that he would simply expand to take in more guests. Outraged, Sinclair resolved to build something fireproof, circular and crowned with an enormous dome, and to open it within a year. The new hotel opened about fifteen months after the fire, and its dome, roughly 200 feet across, still floats over the atrium today.

## Mile Lick Becomes West Baden

Long before any hotel, bear, deer and buffalo came to the salt licks here along the Buffalo Trace, and Native Americans hunted the grounds. French traders gave the area the name French Lick, and in 1778 George Rogers Clark camped less than a mile from the springs. A local physician, William A. Bowles, bought land with the springs and built a small inn. Part of his land held another mineral spring, Mile Lick, a mile to the north, surrounded by marshy ground that flooded every year. John A. Lane, a physician and patent-medicine salesman, saw a business district there that could outshine French Lick. He bought 770 acres from Bowles in 1851, built a sawmill and a bridge over Lick Creek, and opened the Mile Lick Inn around 1852. When the settlement was renamed West Baden in 1855, in reference to a German spa town famed for its mineral springs, Wiesbaden or Baden-Baden, the inn took the new name too. The rivalry between the two springs had begun.

## The Carlsbad of America

In 1887 the Monon Railroad extended its line to the springs, and by the late 1800s guests were arriving from across the country on seven separate railroads, drawn by relaxation and the alleged curative powers of the mineral water. In 1888 an investment group that included Sinclair, of Salem, Indiana, and E. B. Rhodes bought the West Baden hotel and 667 acres for $23,000. Fire destroyed the hotel in 1891, but Sinclair rebuilt, bought out his partners, and advertised the result as the Carlsbad of America. It had a casino, an opera house, and a covered, two-deck, one-third-mile oval track for bicycles and ponies. On the lighted baseball diamond inside the track, major league teams including the Cincinnati Reds, Chicago Cubs and Pittsburgh Pirates held spring training.

## A Dome the Experts Doubted

Most building professionals rejected the idea of a 200-foot dome. Harrison Albright, an architect from West Virginia, took on the design anyway, and bridge engineer Oliver Westcott worked out the dome's trusses. To beat the first anniversary of the fire, a crew of 500 men worked six days a week in ten-hour shifts, at a total cost of $414,000. The hotel opened on September 15, 1902, to rave reviews, and advertisements called it the Eighth Wonder of the World. From 1902 to 1913 its dome was considered the largest in the world, and it is said to have remained the largest free-spanning dome in the United States until the Coliseum in Charlotte, North Carolina, was completed in 1955. The famous followed. Paul Dresser is believed to have composed at least part of Indiana's state song, "On the Banks of the Wabash, Far Away," during a stay at West Baden in 1897, in the inn that preceded the dome; boxers John L. Sullivan and James J. Corbett trained here; and Diamond Jim Brady and Al Capone were reported to be frequent visitors.

## Two Million Squares of Marble

After Sinclair's health failed, his daughter Lillian and her husband, Charles Rexford, ran the hotel, and after his death in 1916 it was theirs to manage. When a fire on February 11, 1917, destroyed the bottling plant, opera house, bowling alley and hospital, Lillian overrode her husband's opposition and launched a grand Greco-Roman restoration. Between 1917 and 1919, Italian artisans laid a mosaic floor of two million one-inch marble squares in the atrium, and the fireplace was refaced with Rookwood Pottery tiles. For part of that time, in 1918 and 1919, the hotel served as a U.S. Army hospital for wounded soldiers returning from World War I. Lillian sold the property to Edward Ballard for $1 million in 1923. The Great Depression emptied luxury hotels, and Ballard closed West Baden in June 1932. Two years later he donated the $7 million resort to the Jesuits.

## Seminary, College, Near-Ruin

The Jesuits stripped away the luxury to create West Baden College, an austere seminary affiliated with Loyola University Chicago. The crumbling Moorish towers came down, and the mineral pools were filled with stone and capped with concrete. The seminary's cemetery received thirty-nine interments before low enrollment and rising costs closed the school after the 1963–64 year. From 1968 to 1983 the building served as a campus of Northwood Institute, a business college. Then came a sale, a bounced $250,000 payment, a bankruptcy and nearly a decade of litigation. Declared unsafe, the building closed to visitors in 1989, and in 1992 the National Trust for Historic Preservation named it one of America's most endangered places. The Historic Landmarks Foundation of Indiana matched an anonymous $70,000 gift to stabilize a partially collapsed wall and patch the roof. A coalition of preservationists, businesses and Orange County citizens lobbied so persistently for casino gaming that they became known as "The Orange Shirts," after T-shirts reading "Save French Lick and West Baden Springs." Legislation passed in 2003, and after a restoration costing almost $100 million, a gala on June 23, 2007, reopened the hotel seventy-five years after it had closed.

## Flight Context

The West Baden Springs Hotel stands at approximately 38.567°N, 86.618°W in West Baden Springs, Orange County, southern Indiana, about a mile north of the French Lick Springs Hotel. From 1,500–2,500 ft AGL the great circular building and its dome are unmistakable, set within landscaped grounds near Lick Creek. French Lick Municipal Airport (KFRH) is only about 7 km to the south-southwest, making a short scenic circuit easy; Paoli Municipal Airport (FAA identifier I42) is about 13 km to the east, and Monroe County Airport in Bloomington (KBMG) is about 64 km to the north. Summer haze and afternoon storms are common; morning fog can linger in autumn.
