# French Lick Springs Hotel

A southern Indiana salt lick became a buff-brick grand hotel built on laxative mineral water, Democratic politics, championship golf and gambling that the law chased for decades.

**Location:** 38.5542, -86.6208

**Categories:** Historic Hotels of America, Hotel buildings on the National Register of Historic Places in Indiana, Orange County, Indiana, Resorts, Golf, Baseball history

## Introduction

"If Nature Won't, Pluto Will." The slogan was not subtle, and neither was the product. Pluto Water, drawn from mineral springs in the hills of southern Indiana, was sold as a laxative, served at the hotel's Pluto Bar just off the main entrance and bottled across the street for shipment across the country and abroad. Around that unglamorous spring water grew a grand resort. The French Lick Springs Hotel drew fashionable, wealthy and notable guests, hosted presidential politicking and a PGA Championship, and harbored a gambling reputation the state spent decades trying to stamp out. Writer Chris Bundy has observed that French Lick and neighboring West Baden "were the Disney World of their time."

## Where the Buffalo Came to Lick

Long before any hotel, animals traveling the Buffalo Trace stopped at a salt lick here, and Native Americans hunted nearby. The name came from French traders and settlers who lived nearby. In 1826 the state authorized salt mining, but the brine proved too weak for large-scale production, and in 1832 Dr. William A. Bowles, a Paoli physician and land speculator, and his brother Thomas bought 1,500 acres that included the mineral springs.

Bowles built a modest inn, believed to date from around 1845, that operated in the summer months. His lessee, John A. Lane, later bought land at Mile Lick, a mile to the north, and built the rival West Baden Springs Hotel, launching a competition that lasted decades. In 1869 a Madison physician, Joseph G. Rogers, named the largest spring Pluto's Well, after the mythological god of the underworld. In 1887 the Monon Railroad extended its line to carry guests to both resorts.

## Tom Taggart's Buff-Brick Palace

In 1901 an investment group that included Thomas Taggart, a three-term mayor of Indianapolis who also chaired the Democratic National Committee, bought the property. By around 1905 Taggart owned it outright, and he remade it wing by wing: a Mediterranean Revival main wing, a fireproof west wing, a seven-story deluxe wing and a six-story north wing, all unified by the hotel's distinctive buff-colored brick. He brought in electricity, fresh water and trolley service, persuaded the Monon to run a spur to the grounds with daily passenger service to Chicago, and the grounds gained a Japanese garden, built around 1920 by Chicago garden builder T.R. Otsuka, complete with waterfall and bronze cranes.

At its height the hotel checked in roughly 150 to 200 guests a day and earned Taggart more than $2 million a year. With Taggart at the head of the Democratic National Committee, it became the party's unofficial headquarters, and in 1931 Franklin D. Roosevelt came to a Democratic governors' conference here seeking support for his presidential bid. Tomato juice, the story goes, was first served here in 1917, when chef Louis Perrin ran out of orange juice.

## The Plutos

The resort was a major employer of African-American workers, most of whom came from nearby Kentucky. Historian Paul Debono traced how the booming resorts needed laborers: the town's African-American population grew from one in 1880 to 124 in 1900, and by 1920 it was 325, with more than 100 listing their occupation as waiter, followed by bellboy and porter. Two-thirds had come from Kentucky.

Those waiters likely made up much of the hotel's baseball team, the French Lick Plutos, which began as a mixed team in 1908 and was all-Black by the next year. Its great rival was the West Baden Sprudels from the hotel up the road. Games were played at the resort to amuse spectators, yet they were fiercely competitive. According to Debono, the tradition lasted until the Great Depression, "when most of the blacks at the resort lost their jobs." Professional teams, meanwhile, came to French Lick for spring training into the 1940s.

## Dice in the Garden

A two-story wooden building in the middle of the Japanese gardens may have served as a casino in the early twentieth century, though promotional materials described it as a place for bowling and dice games. Taggart denied any link to illegal gambling. In July 1906 Indiana's Republican governor, Frank Hanly, had state police raid the hotel and seize its gambling equipment, and the state sued both Taggart and West Baden's owner, Lee Sinclair. The case dragged on past Hanly's term, which ended in January 1909, and a jury ruled for the hotels in 1910. Gambling simply moved across the street to Brown's hotel, where it continued until Governor Henry Schricker got authorities to raid French Lick's operations in 1949 and Brown's casino finally closed.

The irony arrived in 2006, when legal casino gambling came to French Lick as part of the restored resort.

## Hagen's Hill and a Second Act

By the 1920s the resort was best known for its sports, above all golf. Donald Ross and his associates designed the Hill Course, a plan dating to about 1917 and completed in 1920 on about 300 acres of reshaped, hilly ground, and in 1924 it hosted the PGA Championship, won by Walter Hagen. After Taggart's death in 1929 and the stock market crash that same year, the resort declined but kept operating until it closed for renovation in November 2005. His son sold it in 1946, and a long string of owners followed. Pluto Water production ended in 1971.

A partnership that included billionaire Bill Cook applied for a state gambling license, and in April 2005 the Cook Group of Bloomington bought the hotel; the license followed that summer. Listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 2003, the restored hotel reopened in 2006. Today it anchors a resort of more than 3,000 acres with several golf courses, including the Donald Ross and Pete Dye championship courses, a spa, stables and miles of hiking trails.

## Flight Context

The French Lick Springs Hotel sits at about 38.554 N, 86.621 W in the wooded hills of Orange County, southern Indiana. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL, look for the long buff-brick hotel on the edge of the small town of French Lick, with the West Baden Springs Hotel about a mile to the north and the resort's golf courses spread across the surrounding hills. French Lick Municipal Airport (KFRH) is only about 3 nm to the south-southwest, Orleans Airport (FAA 7I4) about 10 nm to the northeast, and Virgil I. Grissom Municipal Airport in Bedford (KBFR) about 19 nm to the north-northeast. The terrain is hilly and heavily forested, and valley fog is common on calm mornings.
