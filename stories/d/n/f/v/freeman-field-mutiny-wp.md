# Freeman Field mutiny

In April 1945, Black officers of the 477th Bombardment Group walked into a whites-only officers' club at Freeman Field, Indiana, and then refused to sign away their objection, a quiet act of defiance that led to 162 arrests and became a milestone on the road to an integrated military.

**Location:** 38.9247, -85.9083

**Categories:** African-American history of the United States military, History of African-American civil rights, Indiana in World War II, Mutinies in World War II, Tuskegee Airmen

## Introduction

Late on the afternoon of April 5, 1945, the last group of Black officers from the 477th Bombardment Group arrived at Freeman Army Airfield near Seymour, Indiana, and began walking, a few at a time, toward Club Number Two. Officially it was the club for "instructors." In practice, every instructor was white and every "trainee" assigned to the other club was Black. These men were training to fly bombers into combat for their country, and they had decided in advance to push the question of that separate door to the point of arrest if necessary.

## Trained to Fight, Barred at the Door

The 477th grew out of the Tuskegee Airmen experiment. After pressure from Black civilian leaders, the Army reactivated the group on January 15, 1944, to train African American aviators on the B-25J Mitchell medium bomber. Segregation followed them everywhere. At Selfridge Field near Detroit, the officers' club was closed to Black officers even though Army Regulation 210-10 required such clubs to be open to all, and the War Department reprimanded the base commander. On May 5, 1944, possibly out of fear of a repeat of the previous summer's race riot in Detroit, the group was abruptly moved to Godman Field at Fort Knox, Kentucky, an airfield poorly suited to the B-25. Morale sagged as Black officers, including combat veterans of the 332nd Fighter Group, were passed over for command. By early 1945 the group reached full strength and was scheduled to enter combat on July 1, so it moved once more, to Freeman Field.

## Two Nights at Club Number Two

Word of the two clubs reached the officers still at Godman, and a group led by Second Lieutenant Coleman A. Young, an experienced labor organizer and the future mayor of Detroit, planned their response. An earlier attempt on March 10 had ended with Black officers simply refused service. On April 5, the club officer, Major Andrew M. White, turned away the first three men. Later groups met the Officer of the Day, First Lieutenant Joseph D. Rogers, posted at the door with a holstered .45 on Colonel Robert Selway's orders. When 19 officers, Young among them, entered anyway and would not leave, White placed them under arrest in quarters, and they returned to their rooms. Seventeen more were arrested that night, including Second Lieutenant Roger C. Terry, whom Rogers claimed had shoved him. The next evening 25 more went in and were arrested. Apart from that alleged shove, no one on either side used force.

## The Paper No One Would Sign

An Air Inspector recommended dropping charges against all but Terry and two others, and 58 officers were released. Colonel Selway then wrote a new order, Base Regulation 85-2, meant to make the segregation legally airtight. On April 10 his deputy read it to the trainees and told each man to sign a statement that he had read and fully understood it. No one signed. A board of two Black and two white officers then interviewed the holdouts one by one, offering them the chance to sign or to write their own certificate, and warning that refusing a direct order in wartime under the 64th Article of War could technically be punished by death. On April 11 (some accounts say April 13), 101 officers still refused and were arrested. Pressure from African American organizations, labor unions and members of Congress built quickly, and the 101 were released in late April (accounts give April 23 or April 25), though each received an administrative reprimand in his file. Accounts also differ on who ordered it: Wikipedia credits Army Chief of Staff General George C. Marshall, while the National WWII Museum says Major General Hunter intervened.

## Thurgood Marshall's Defense

In July, the three officers accused of jostling Lieutenant Rogers faced a general court-martial. Thurgood Marshall, future justice of the U.S. Supreme Court, directed the defense from behind the scenes. Theodore M. Berry, later mayor of Cincinnati, served as lead counsel, assisted by Lieutenant William T. Coleman Jr., who would go on to become U.S. Secretary of Transportation. Second Lieutenants Marsden A. Thompson and Shirley R. Clinton were acquitted. Terry was cleared of disobeying an order but convicted of jostling Rogers; he was fined $150, lost his rank and received a dishonorable discharge. Across the whole affair, the arrests numbered 162, some men arrested twice, and a single conviction stood.

## Fifty Years to Vindication

The protest sent the 477th back to Godman Field, where it was reorganized as the 477th Composite Group. On July 1, 1945, Colonel Benjamin O. Davis Jr. took command, and Black officers replaced white ones in its command and supervisory positions. Japan surrendered on August 14, before training was complete, and the group never saw combat. In 1948, President Harry S. Truman's Executive Order 9981 called for the integration of the armed forces. Freeman Field itself had closed and been declared surplus in December 1946, and 2,241 acres of it became Freeman Municipal Airport, the land formally sold in November 1948. In 1995, responding to requests from 477th veterans, the Air Force removed the reprimands from the files of 15 of the 104 officers charged and promised to remove the rest on request. Roger Terry's conviction was set aside, and the National Park Service says he later received a full pardon, his rank restored and his $150 refunded. Historians of the civil rights movement regard the protest as an important step toward full integration of the armed forces, and a model for later efforts to integrate public facilities through civil disobedience.

## Flight Context

The site of the protest is today's Freeman Municipal Airport (KSER) in Seymour, Indiana, at approximately 38.925°N, 85.908°W; 2,241 acres of the former Freeman Army Airfield became this airport. From 2,000–3,000 ft AGL the field's runways are easy to spot beside the city of Seymour, and pilots can land on the same ground where the 477th trained. Columbus Municipal Airport (KBAK) lies about 38 km to the north, and North Vernon Airport (KOVO) about 30 km to the east-northeast. Southern Indiana weather is generally good for VFR flying outside summer haze and thunderstorms; watch for low morning clouds and fog in autumn and winter.
