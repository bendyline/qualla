# Seymour, Indiana

Built where two railroads cross, Seymour has seen the Reno Gang's celebrated peacetime train robbery, a secret wartime helicopter school and John Mellencamp's boyhood.

**Location:** 38.9564, -85.8900

**Categories:** Cities in Jackson County, Indiana, Railroad towns, World War II airfields, American Civil War, Indiana history

## Introduction

Seymour was laid out in 1852 at a spot where two railroads were about to cross, and it would owe nearly everything to the junction. Today Seymour, a city of 21,569 in Jackson County, sits where two major lines, one running north–south and one east–west, cross in the middle of downtown, and both still operate. People call it the "Crossroads of Southern Indiana," and over more than 170 years that crossing has drawn soldiers, train robbers and, eventually, warplanes.

## Where the Rails Cross

The north–south line, the Jeffersonville, Madison and Indianapolis, was built in the 1840s to connect Indianapolis with the Ohio River at Jeffersonville. The Ohio and Mississippi followed in the 1850s, linking Cincinnati with St. Louis. Meedy and Eliza Ewing Shields laid out a town at the junction on April 27, 1852. One account holds that Captain Meedy Shields persuaded Hezekiah Cook Seymour to route the east–west railroad through his land and named the new town in Seymour's honor.

Shields set about attracting settlers to his new town. The Travis Carter house on Indianapolis Avenue, built in 1852, is described as the oldest building still standing in Seymour.

## A Waypoint to the Front

Those same rails made Seymour an important waypoint for men and supplies during the Civil War. Despite strong Copperhead sentiment across southern Indiana, Seymour and Jackson County sent 2,571 volunteers to the Union cause, many organized at Camp Heffren in town. W. A. Carter later recalled a flag ceremony on the platform of the Ohio and Mississippi station, where Seymour women presented a silk American flag to Fielder Jones when his company was organized. "The enemy will never get this flag while I live," Jones declared.

In July 1863, as John Hunt Morgan's Confederate cavalry sacked nearby Salem, Maj. Gen. John Love of the Indiana Legion arrived to protect the vital railroad crossing. He gathered about 1,000 men here and marched them toward Vernon to meet Morgan, leaving Meedy Shields in charge of Seymour's defenses. In Love's words, Shields "rendered invaluable service."

## The Renos and the Express Car

Peace brought a different kind of trouble. On October 6, 1866, just east of town, the local Reno Gang struck the Adams Express Company car of the Ohio and Mississippi Railroad in what is often described as the first robbery of a moving train in the United States during peacetime. Masked vigilantes later hanged gang members at Hangman's Crossing outside town, and the three Reno brothers, lynched in New Albany in December 1868, are buried in Seymour's city cemetery.

Around 1876, some 500 railroad men at Seymour and North Vernon, unpaid by the railroad for two and a half months, went on strike, led by armed brakemen and engineers. A paper reported both towns armed and in revolt, all service was suspended, and U.S. Marshals and detectives were sent from Cincinnati to end it. For its 1952 centennial, Seymour restaged the Reno robbery with a locomotive borrowed from the B&O Railroad's Baltimore museum.

## Secrets at Freeman Field

During World War II, the federal government bought 2,500 acres southwest of town for Freeman Army Airfield, which operated from 1942 to 1946. It began as a twin-engine training school, and by its last graduation in May 1944 it had turned out 4,245 cadets in 19 classes on some 250 Beechcraft AT-10 Wichita trainers. Graduates went on to fly B-24s, B-17s, B-29s and transports. Future astronaut Gus Grissom, from nearby Mitchell, was inducted as an aviation cadet in 1944. Freeman was also the first helicopter base in the United States, and preparations for helicopter training were made in great secrecy, since in 1944 very few people had ever seen one.

Near the war's end, the field became the Foreign Aircraft Evaluation Center for Army Air Technical Intelligence, receiving German and Italian aircraft collected by "Operation Lusty." When the base closed, some of the captured aircraft left at the field were destroyed or buried. The city received the field in 1946, and it remains a civilian airport today.

## Crossroads Culture

Seymour's stories run in every direction. The town fielded a minor-league baseball club, the Seymour Reds, from 1900. Frank B. Shields, a Seymour native and former MIT professor, invented Barbasol shaving cream. Two weeks before the 1908 election, William Howard Taft spoke to 10,000 people at the Chestnut Street railroad crossing. John Mellencamp grew up here, and since 2019 a large mural of him by Indianapolis artist Pamela Bliss has overlooked downtown from the side of a music store.

The Dutch and German Lutherans who arrived in the late 1800s are remembered in an Oktoberfest held every year since 1973, and since about 1989 Indigenous Chuj families from San Sebastián Coatán, Guatemala, have built new lives here. Seymour has held a Victory over Japan Day parade every August since 1946, a tradition that Wikipedia says ended in 2019.

## Flight Context

Seymour lies at about 38.956 N, 85.890 W on the flat Scottsburg lowlands of southern Indiana, roughly 58 miles south of Indianapolis and 49 miles north of Louisville in a straight line. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL, look for the downtown railroad crossing, the former Blish Mill grain towers (described as the tallest structures in town), and the meandering East Fork of the White River. Freeman Municipal Airport (KSER), the former Freeman Army Airfield, is just 2 nm to the south-southwest, with North Vernon Airport (KOVO) about 14 nm to the east-northeast and Columbus Municipal Airport (KBAK) about 18 nm to the north. Virgil I. Grissom Municipal Airport in Bedford (KBFR) is about 27 nm to the west-southwest. The terrain is flat farmland; watch for low river fog on calm mornings.
