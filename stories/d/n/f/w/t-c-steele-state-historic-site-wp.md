# T. C. Steele State Historic Site

On a Brown County hilltop, Hoosier Group painter T. C. Steele and his wife, Selma, built the House of the Singing Winds, a home, studio and garden that Selma gave to Indiana in 1945 along with more than 300 of his paintings.

**Location:** 39.1306, -86.3386

**Categories:** Indiana State Historic Sites, Artists' studios in the United States, Art museums and galleries in Indiana, National Register of Historic Places in Brown County, Indiana, Gardens

## Introduction

The Steeles named their house for the sound it made. Breezes moved through the screened porches of the cottage on a Brown County hilltop, and T. C. and Selma Steele called it the House of the Singing Winds. Below the fireplace mantel, their artist friend Gustave Baumann carved an old Gaelic saying that could have been the painter's creed: "Every morning I take off my hat to the beauty of the world." Today the house, its studio and its gardens form the T. C. Steele State Historic Site, one and a half miles south of Belmont, between Bloomington and Nashville, Indiana.

## From Portraits to Landscapes

Theodore Clement Steele was born in Owen County, Indiana, in 1847 and began studying art at an early age. He attended Waveland Collegiate Institute, studied painting briefly in Chicago and Cincinnati, and settled in Indianapolis as a portrait painter. From 1880 to 1885 he trained at the Academy of Fine Arts in Munich, then returned to Indianapolis to resume his career. Portraits paid the bills, but it was his landscapes that made his name. Steele became a member of the Hoosier Group of American regional impressionists, alongside William Forsyth, J. Ottis Adams, Richard B. Gruelle and Otto Stark. He served as president of the Society of Western Artists, and in 1913 he was elected an Associate Artist of the National Academy of Design in New York, confirming a standing that one source calls the most famous Hoosier artist of his time. Two years later, three of his paintings were accepted into the Panama–Pacific International Exposition in San Francisco.

## A Hilltop in Brown County

Around 1905 or 1906, while searching for new landscapes to paint, Steele came upon a scenic and isolated corner of Brown County. In 1907 he bought 60 acres there and had a hilltop home and studio built, moving in that August with Selma Neubacher Steele, his second wife. It began as a summer residence. The couple developed it slowly: in 1908 they added a west wing to serve as his studio, in 1911 they bought more land to bring the property to 211 acres, and in 1912 it became their year-round home. Steele still kept a studio in Indianapolis, and after Indiana University named him its artist-in-residence in 1922, he also kept a winter home in Bloomington and a studio on campus. But the hilltop remained his primary residence.

## A Red Barn Full of Paintings

The house itself is a vernacular design with high ceilings, screened porches, a pyramid-shaped roof and a central fireplace. Steele's first studio was in what is now the living room; he worked in the west wing for seven years and also used another outbuilding. In 1916 a large, barn-like studio went up, painted dark red, 30 by 50 feet, with a wall of windows along its north side. Because Steele was primarily a plein-air painter who worked outdoors, the big studio served mostly as a gallery where he could exhibit and sell his work, and his paintings still hang there today. Inside the house, Selma mixed styles freely, pairing Arts and Crafts pieces with Rococo Revival.

## Selma's Gardens, and Her Gift

Selma ran the property while her husband painted, and she shaped its landscape over the years, introducing new trees, shrubs and masses of flowers to the hilltop. In spring, daffodils covered the hillsides. Steele died in 1926, and Selma kept the place for nearly two more decades. In July 1945, shortly before her own death, she donated the property to the Indiana Department of Conservation, forerunner of today's Department of Natural Resources, to create a historic site in his memory. The gift included the house and its furnishings, the studio, the outbuildings and more than 300 of his paintings. Listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1973, the site is operated by the Indiana State Museum and Historic Sites, which offers guided indoor tours of the house and large studio at set times, Wednesday through Sunday, with registration required. Visitors can wander lily ponds and gardens, walk trails with names like the Trail of Silences and Inspiration Ridge, and explore the 92-acre Selma Steele Nature Preserve, dedicated in 1990.

## Flight Context

The T. C. Steele State Historic Site sits on a hilltop at 39.1306°N, 86.3386°W in rural Brown County, Indiana, about one and a half miles south of Belmont, between Bloomington and Nashville. This is rural hill country, so the site is best found by following the roads between the two towns at around 2,000 feet AGL; the dark red studio barn is a small but useful visual cue.
