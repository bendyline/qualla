# Fontaine Ferry Park

For more than six decades Fontaine Ferry Park thrilled Louisville with wooden roller coasters on the Ohio River, while for most of that time barring the city's African Americans, a contradiction that ended in a riot on opening day in 1969 and a park that never truly recovered.

**Location:** 38.2642, -85.8269

**Categories:** Defunct amusement parks in the United States, Former buildings and structures in Louisville, Kentucky, Racial segregation in the United States, African-American history, Amusement parks opened in 1905

## Introduction

Two public parks flanked it. Before integration, Shawnee Park, on one side, was designated for white residents, and Chickasaw Park, on the other, for Black residents. Between them, on 64 acres beside the Ohio River in western Louisville, stood Fontaine Ferry Park, with its wooden roller coasters, swimming pool and skating rink. For nearly 60 years its gates were largely closed to the city's African Americans. Long after its last ride stopped, Fontaine Ferry is still cited bitterly as a reminder of racial segregation in Louisville.

## From Ferry Landing to Midway

The name goes back to Aaron Fontaine, who bought the land in 1814 as part of his estate and ferry landing. In 1887 it was sold to Thomas Landenwich, who built a hotel and other attractions, and in May 1905 Fontaine Ferry Park opened to the public at the western end of Market Street, originally called Fontaine Ferry Road, on what is now Southwestern Parkway in the Shawnee neighborhood. Until the 1940s, visitors could arrive by steamboat from downtown Louisville. The park grew to offer more than 50 rides and attractions, along with a theatre and a grand carousel. Its greatest draw was its wooden roller coasters; at least four of them were built over the years.

## A Reminder of Jim Crow

For most of its life, Fontaine Ferry was off-limits to the city's Black residents. When Louisville's West End became integrated in the 1960s, the park remained a constant reminder of Jim Crow to African Americans, even after it was racially integrated in 1964. The surrounding Shawnee neighborhood was integrating too, and white flight was occurring heavily there, especially after the 1968 riots in the West End. Then, on May 4, 1969, the first day of the operating season, a race riot broke out at the park, and Fontaine Ferry was heavily vandalized. It never reopened as Fontaine Ferry Park. The property was sold that year and tried to begin again, first as Ghost Town on the River in 1972 and then as River Glen Park in 1975, which proved to be its last season by Wikipedia's account, though other sources date the final closure and a destroying fire to 1976 or 1977. After several fires, the city bought the land in 1981. The park's Dentzel carousel, built in 1922 according to the ride's own history, found a second home at Marriott's Great America near Chicago, where it reopened as the Ameri-Go-Round, a ride that park removed at the end of the 2003 season.

## Houses Where the Coasters Ran

According to Wikipedia, part of the site is now a residential development called Fontaine Estates, where the first houses were sold in 1996. A year later a $1.2 million Shawnee Park Sports Complex was reportedly built on former Fontaine Ferry land, next to the public park that had once been reserved for white residents; other sources describe the site simply as part of Shawnee Park. Houses and playing fields now stand where wooden roller coasters were once the most popular attraction in the park. What lingers is the contradiction at the heart of the place: for nearly 60 years, a riverside amusement park that people could reach by steamboat from downtown told the city's Black residents they were not welcome.

## Flight Context

The former Fontaine Ferry Park site lies at 38.2642°N, 85.8269°W on the Kentucky bank of the Ohio River in Louisville's West End, at the western end of Market Street where Southwestern Parkway runs between Shawnee Park and Chickasaw Park. The river bend and the green of the two parks are the best landmarks from 2,000–2,500 feet. The site sits within the busy airspace of Louisville Muhammad Ali International (KSDF), about 7 nm southeast, so coordinate with ATC. Other nearby airports: Clark Regional at Jeffersonville, Indiana (KJVY), about 7 nm north-northeast across the river, and Bowman Field (KLOU), about 8 nm east-southeast.
