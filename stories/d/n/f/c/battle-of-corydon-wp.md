# Battle of Corydon

On July 9, 1863, roughly 400 to 450 Indiana militiamen and townspeople behind a wall of logs faced John Hunt Morgan's Confederate raiders south of Corydon, in the only pitched Civil War battle fought in Indiana, and lost a fight whose delay helped doom the raid.

**Location:** 38.2000, -86.1300

**Categories:** Morgan's Raid, Battles of the American Civil War in Indiana, Corydon, Indiana, National Register of Historic Places in Harrison County, Indiana, 1863 in Indiana

## Introduction

At 11:30 on the morning of July 9, 1863, the men waiting behind a wall of logs south of Corydon, Indiana, saw Confederate cavalry coming up the Mauckport Road. Most of them had never seen a battle. They were locals, farmers among them and older men who had joined at the last minute, from a town and countryside of about a thousand people, the town having been Indiana's capital until 1825, and the town had spent the night building a breastwork on a ridge. By early afternoon they would be routed, and Corydon would be in the hands of John Hunt Morgan's raiders. It remains the only pitched battle of the Civil War fought in Indiana.

## Steamboats at Brandenburg

Morgan had set out from Tennessee in June with a cavalry division of roughly 2,400 to 2,500 men. Gen. Braxton Bragg had told him to raid Kentucky but not to cross the Ohio River into the Union. Morgan was already planning to disregard that order. At Brandenburg, Kentucky, his men ambushed the steamboat John T. McCombs as it landed with the mail, then used it to fake a distress call and capture the fast sidewheeler Alice Dean. On July 8 they began ferrying troops into Indiana. A company from Leavenworth had brought its town's ceremonial 8-pound cannon to the Indiana bank, but the men had only ever fired it at celebrations. Confederate artillery answered from the higher Kentucky shore, and two of the defenders were mortally wounded. A Union gunboat dueled with the raiders for about an hour and ran out of ammunition. The crossing took seventeen hours, and when it was done the Alice Dean was burned and sunk.

## A Wall of Logs

All the roads Morgan was likely to take north met at Corydon. The townspeople worked all night on a breastwork of criss-crossed logs between four and five feet high, stretching 2,500 feet across a high ridge and straddling the Mauckport Road. Leaders in New Albany had promised a thousand reinforcements, but Morgan's men tapped the telegraph lines and sent false reports that the raiders were heading for New Albany, and no help came before the fight. About 450 defenders of the Indiana Legion, the state militia (the state historical marker says about 400), held the line under Col. Lewis Jordan, an elderly veteran of the War of 1812. That morning, as the raiders advanced up a county road, a shot rang out and killed one of their soldiers. Confederate scouts opened fire on a nearby house they assumed it had come from, killing a Lutheran minister and wounding his son.

## Henry Rifles and a White Flag

The first Confederate attack on the wooded western wing was thrown back three times. On the open eastern wing, thirty Legion men armed with Henry rifles, which could fire fourteen rounds before reloading, kept the attackers pinned in a field for about twenty minutes. Then the numbers turned. Two more Confederate regiments arrived, and at about 12:40 p.m. artillery began to pound the works. Col. Richard Morgan, the general's brother, sent his men around both ends of the line. Jordan ordered a retreat, and it quickly became a rout; this second phase was over in about twenty-five minutes, and the whole battle lasted about an hour. Fleeing men threw their guns into Indian Creek. About a hundred ran up the Corydon Pike, straight into Morgan's main body, and were captured. After two warning shells fell on the town, Jordan, who did not "desire to see the unnecessary loss of life," ran up a white flag. Casualty counts vary by source. Wikipedia gives the defenders four killed, ten to twelve wounded and 355 captured, and the raiders eleven dead and forty wounded, with three civilians killed; the state historical marker counts three Hoosiers and eight Confederates killed and 345 men surrendered.

## Ransom and Plunder

Morgan's men rode into Corydon, and a county commissioner who stepped out of the courthouse carrying a rifle was shot dead, the final casualty of the day. The captured militiamen were paroled on a promise not to take up arms, and the county jail was emptied, freeing the Confederates captured the day before. Then the raiders turned to money. Morgan demanded $1,000 from each of three mills to spare them from the torch, and the owners bargained him down to $500. The county treasurer paid $690 to save the courthouse, and two leading stores paid $600 each. Soldiers took everything from ice skates and bonnets to guns and horses, losses mostly reimbursed later by the state. The raid's human costs reached further. An African American man from Corydon was forced to serve as a guide for two days before he escaped and made his way home. The Confederate dead were buried in unmarked graves on a farm at the edge of town, and their wounded were cared for in a local Presbyterian church.

## The Cost of Six Hours

Corydon had delayed Morgan by six crucial hours, and the pursuing Union cavalry, numbering six thousand men, was soon only a few miles behind him. He raided Salem, then turned east into Ohio as the raid became a flight. At Buffington Island, Union gunboats ended his attempt to recross the Ohio with only a few hundred of his men across, and Morgan surrendered on July 26 near West Point, Ohio, close to Salineville, a spot considered the northernmost reached by an officially organized Confederate force. In the mid-1970s the battlefield was preserved as the Corydon Battle Site, now the Battle of Corydon Memorial Park, and it joined the National Register of Historic Places on July 9, 1979, the battle's anniversary. Today the five-acre park, run by Harrison County Parks, is reported to be part of the John Hunt Morgan Heritage Trail.

## Flight Context

The Corydon Battle Site lies at about 38.20°N, 86.13°W, just south of downtown Corydon in Harrison County, Indiana, along the old Mauckport Road. From 2,000–3,000 feet AGL, follow the road south from town to the ridge; Brandenburg, Kentucky, and the river crossing lie about 12 nm (14 miles) to the south-southwest. Nearby airports: Louisville Muhammad Ali International (KSDF), about 19 nm east, inside busy airline airspace; Godman Army Airfield at Fort Knox (KFTK), a military field about 19 nm south-southeast. Hilly Ohio River country; best viewed in clear daytime conditions.
