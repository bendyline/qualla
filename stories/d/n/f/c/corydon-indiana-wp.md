# Corydon, Indiana

Indiana wrote its first constitution in the shade of an elm in this small southern town, which served as the state's first capital and later endured Morgan's Raid.

**Location:** 38.2106, -86.1264

**Categories:** Former state capitals in the United States, County seats in Indiana, Harrison County, Indiana, American Civil War, Indiana history

## Introduction

In June 1816 the log cabin was too small and the summer too hot. Forty-three delegates had come to Corydon, a young town in the far south of the Indiana Territory, to consider statehood and draft Indiana's first constitution. The courthouse meant to host them was not yet finished, so they crowded into an unfinished log cabin nearby, and when the heat and the cramped quarters became too much, they often sought refuge outside, in the shade of a giant elm. They finished their work in nineteen days. The tree became known as the Constitution Elm, and Corydon, a town that had been named for a shepherd in a hymn, became the first capital of the state of Indiana.

## A Shepherd's Name

Edward Smith and his family were the first Americans to settle on the site, arriving in 1803, and for a time they were its only recorded inhabitants. In 1804 William Henry Harrison bought a tract where Big Indian Creek and Little Indian Creek join to become Indian Creek and decided to build a town. Tradition says Harrison asked Smith's daughter Jenny to name it, and she chose Corydon, the shepherd mourned in his favorite hymn, "The Pastoral Elegy."

Founded in 1808, the town took the territorial capital from Vincennes in 1813, as Harrison's opponents sought to move government away from his Knox County stronghold. Dennis Pennington, speaker of the territorial legislature's lower house, helped win the selection by pointing to a new limestone courthouse planned for the public square, whose construction he then supervised. In 1814 Paul and Susannah Mitchem, who had become Quakers, arrived from North Carolina with 107 enslaved people and freed them. Some moved on, but the group became one of the largest communities of free Black people in the state.

## Nineteen Days Under the Elm

President James Madison signed an Enabling Act on April 19, 1816, calling for a convention at Corydon to consider statehood. The delegates, five of them from Harrison County, met from June 10 to June 29, and their preamble still names the place: "We the Representatives of the people of the Territory of Indiana, in Convention met, at Corydon, on Monday the tenth day of June..." Article XI, Section 11, made Corydon the seat of government "until the year eighteen hundred and twenty-five, and until removed by law." The new constitution took effect the day they adjourned.

On November 4, 1816, the Indiana General Assembly met at Corydon for the first time, and on December 11 Madison signed the resolution admitting Indiana as the nineteenth state. The famous tree eventually died, but a portion of its trunk survives, surrounded by a sandstone memorial the WPA built during the Great Depression.

## Capital of a Frontier State

Statehood brought officials, and officials needed beds. Corydon had only three taverns for lodging in 1813; the much larger Kintner House Tavern opened in 1819. Three successive governors and their staffs moved to town and built large homes, and in 1819 President James Monroe and Andrew Jackson came to visit. The limestone courthouse, now called the Old Capitol, housed state government, and a Masonic lodge hall billed as the state's first was built here that same year.

The arrangement was never popular in rival towns, and critics warned that a capital in Indiana's far south would grow inconvenient as the population shifted north. Pennington and his Harrison County allies held them off until Governor William Hendricks signed a bill in 1824 moving the capital to Indianapolis, effective January 10, 1825. Corydon settled into life as a county seat and market town for the surrounding farms.

## Morgan Crosses the Ohio

On July 9, 1863, Confederate Brig. Gen. John Hunt Morgan led more than 2,000 cavalrymen across the Ohio River into Indiana. About 450 members of a hastily assembled home guard met them outside Corydon in what is considered the only official pitched battle fought in Indiana during the Civil War. The defenders were quickly beaten, and the town surrendered.

Morgan's men plundered Corydon, reportedly in retaliation for Union looting in Kentucky. They took $690 from the county treasury, paid to spare the courthouse, and released the inmates of the local jail, and Morgan demanded hundreds of dollars from each mill and shop owner to keep their buildings from being burned (accounts differ on the sums). One local tradition adds a strange note of courtesy: a Corydon miller is said to have overpaid by $200, and Morgan promptly returned it. A local group hosts a Battle of Corydon reenactment each July, held at Hayswood Park, though in recent years it has sometimes been scaled back to a living-history day.

## What the Old Capitol Witnessed

The decades after the war brought neglect and violence. By 1871 the first statehouse had grown so filthy and flea-infested that many people refused to enter it, until a campaign by local journalists won it a thorough renovation in 1873. In 1889 a mob of 150 mounted men, led by twenty masked white cap vigilantes, came to the county jail for James Devin and Charles Tennyson, two men being held on charges of attempted murder. The leaders threatened to burn the town if refused. When the sheriff would not hand over the keys, the mob broke down the jail doors with hammers, dragged the two men to the old Western Bridge and hanged them before they could ever be tried.

The state bought the old capitol in 1917, and it opened as a state memorial in 1929–30. In 1973 part of downtown was listed on the National Register as the Corydon Historic District. And every year since 1860, Corydon has held its county fair, billed as the oldest continuously held county fair in Indiana.

## Flight Context

Corydon lies at about 38.211 N, 86.126 W near the center of Harrison County in far southern Indiana, roughly 20 miles west of downtown Louisville. From 2,500 to 3,500 feet AGL, look for the courthouse square and the limestone Old Capitol in the middle of town, Interstate 64 running east-west about 13 miles to the north (Exit 105 at Palmyra), State Road 62 through town, and Indian Creek winding southwest toward the Ohio River. Louisville Muhammad Ali International Airport (KSDF) is about 19 nm to the east, with Clark Regional Airport in Jeffersonville (KJVY) about 20 nm to the east-northeast and Bowman Field (KLOU) about 22 nm to the east. Louisville's busy airspace lies just to the east, and summer haze often hangs over the Ohio River valley.
