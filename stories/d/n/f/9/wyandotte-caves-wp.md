# Wyandotte Caves

Beneath the forests near the Ohio River in southern Indiana, Wyandotte Caves hold ancient Native American mines, War of 1812 saltpeter vats, a 135-foot underground mountain and tens of thousands of hibernating bats.

**Location:** 38.2281, -86.2961

**Categories:** Caves of Indiana, Show caves in the United States, National Natural Landmarks in Indiana, Archaeological sites in Indiana, Indiana in the War of 1812, State parks of Indiana

## Introduction

The first miners came with torches of hickory bark and grape vine. By that flickering light, Native Americans worked their way into Wyandotte Cave for thousands of years before Europeans arrived, prying out aragonite for pipes and necklaces and chert for stone tools. One widely repeated carbon-dating claim puts human activity here as early as 8000 BC, but the best-documented use runs back nearly 4,000 years. The marks of their quarrying are still visible on tours today, deep inside a cave system in Crawford County, Indiana, where the air holds at a steady 52 degrees Fahrenheit all year.

## Written in Limestone

The rock here began as the floor of a shallow inland sea that covered Indiana during the Mississippian epoch, some 360 to 325 million years ago. The caves themselves started forming about two million years ago, as water dissolved that limestone and left hollow spaces behind. Ice played a part, even though the glaciers never quite reached this far south. Their advance and retreat destroyed the ancient Teays River, and the Ohio River formed to drain the land in its place. As the ice melted, cold water rushing toward a much higher Ohio carved out caves like Wyandotte. Today the Ohio flows only minutes away, and the main entrance of Wyandotte Cave sits 220 feet above the Blue River.

## Saltpeter for an Army

European settlers are believed to have found the cave around 1798, and it quickly proved valuable. Its walls yielded saltpeter, a key ingredient of gunpowder, as well as Epsom salts. Mining peaked during the War of 1812 under Dr. Benjamin Adams, whose vats and hoppers still stand along the tour route, and the cave also stored supplies for William Henry Harrison's army. Guides point out magnesium sulfate, the chemical name for Epsom salt, glittering on the walls. Before it settled on a name, the cave was called the Mammoth Cave of Indiana, the Epsom Salts Cave and the Indiana Saltpetre Cave. Governor David Wallace finally named it after the river then called the Wyandotte, known today as the Blue. Henry Peter Rothrock bought the land above the cave in 1819, but the family did little with it until a large new section was discovered and they began offering commercial tours, in 1850 by Wikipedia's account, though a 1985 Christian Science Monitor feature gives 1856. By the earlier date, Wyandotte is billed as the fourth-oldest commercial cave in the United States.

## An Underground Mountain

Wyandotte Cave is a place of long open passageways and large rooms, with about 9.2 miles of surveyed passages spread across five levels, which ranks it as the fifth-longest cave in Indiana (older advertising claimed 25 miles). Its signature feature is Monument Mountain, a 135-foot rise considered to be the world's largest underground mountain. The cave also holds a great many helictites, which are considered rare, and a stalagmite known as the Pillar of the Constitution, visible only on crawling tours. Its smaller neighbor, Little Wyandotte Cave, also known as Siberts Cave, was discovered in 1851 and named for the man who found it. It is wetter and gentler, dense with stalactites, columns, soda straws, popcorn, rimstone dams, draperies and iron-stained flowstone known as cave bacon. Its easy 30- to 45-minute tour has no long stairways, while a demanding two-hour, 1.5-mile trek into the big cave leads to the prehistoric flint quarries and Monument Mountain.

## Thirty Thousand Sleepers

The cave's most important residents are its bats. Nine of Indiana's 12 native bat species live here, more than 30,000 animals in all, and the most common is the endangered Indiana bat, followed by the little brown bat. Their winter hibernation is the main reason the caves close to the public in the cold months; the Indiana Department of Natural Resources now runs guided tours on weekends and holidays from Memorial Day weekend through Labor Day weekend. To slow the spread of white-nose syndrome, a disease affecting bat populations, the caves were closed entirely from 2009 to 2016. When they reopened, visitors had to walk through decontamination stations at the end of each tour, and the long closure had taken such a toll on the site's buildings that many were torn down. A $1.7 million project replaced the main building and rewired the cave, and by 2018 its lights were working again. The caves were sold to the Indiana Department of Natural Resources in 1966, along with 1,174 acres of woodland, and are now part of O'Bannon Woods State Park.

## Flight Context

Wyandotte Caves lie at approximately 38.228°N, 86.296°W in Harrison–Crawford State Forest and O'Bannon Woods State Park, Crawford County, Indiana, about 3.5 miles (straight-line) northeast of Leavenworth and roughly 9.5 miles west of Corydon, close to the Ohio River and the Blue River. The caves themselves are invisible from the air; from 2,000–3,000 ft AGL, use the Ohio River and the forested state park and forest lands along it as your reference. Nearby airports include Louisville Muhammad Ali International (KSDF), about 49 km to the east; Perry County Municipal Airport in Tell City (KTEL), about 42 km to the southwest; and French Lick Municipal (KFRH), about 43 km to the northwest. Summer haze and thunderstorms are common, and river fog can form on cool mornings.
