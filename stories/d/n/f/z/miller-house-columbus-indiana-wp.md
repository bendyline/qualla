# Miller House (Columbus, Indiana)

Eero Saarinen, Alexander Girard and Dan Kiley designed this flat-roofed modernist home for J. Irwin and Xenia Miller, whose five children held sleepovers in its famous sunken conversation pit.

**Location:** 39.2272, -85.9231

**Categories:** Eero Saarinen buildings and structures, Modernist architecture in Indiana, Historic house museums in Indiana, National Historic Landmarks in Indiana, Landscape architecture

## Introduction

Eero Saarinen's team first proposed building the house on the Flatrock River. Xenia Miller rejected the idea immediately, joking that her children would have to row to school. The first drawings put the house on stilts, and she sent those back too, worried about mosquitoes. Saarinen reportedly drew up a dozen versions before the design settled into its final form: a single-story house on a terrace beneath a flat roof, set in a 13.5-acre garden in Columbus, Indiana. The Miller family moved in during March 1957. The Miller House and Garden, now a museum and a National Historic Landmark, has been praised for its design ever since.

## The Perfect Client

J. Irwin Miller was the longtime leader of his family's business, the engine company Cummins, and by the late 1940s he also led the Irwin Union Trust Bank. He married Xenia Simons in 1943, while serving in the U.S. Navy. Saarinen was Miller's favorite architect, and Saarinen in turn regarded Miller as "the perfect client," ultimately designing four buildings for him. The house was the third, after a 1952 cottage in Canada and the Irwin Union Bank downtown.

Miller bought the site in September 1953 for $30,000. The design became a collaboration: interior designer Alexander Girard worked closely with the Millers to furnish the residence, landscape architect Dan Kiley shaped the grounds, and Saarinen's associate Kevin Roche contributed as well. Architectural drawings credit Saarinen and Girard equally. The house, its interiors and its gardens were designed in tandem, so every part relates to the others.

## A Room Within a Room

Inside the 6,838-square-foot house, four zones branch from a central living room, one for the parents, one for the children, one for guests and servants, and one for service areas. The communal zones sit near the entrance, while the family's private rooms are placed at the rear. A band of skylights runs uninterrupted around the perimeter, lighting a facade the eaves would otherwise shade, and the ceiling skylights rest on pairs of steel beams, a detail Roche suggested.

The heart of the house is its sunken conversation pit, attributed to Girard, who had used a similar feature in his own home. Before it was built, Girard displayed a mockup in a New York storefront and asked passersby to sit on it. Critic Blair Kamin called the pit a "room within a room." Nearby, a storage wall is divided into compartments, each lined in a different material to suit the objects it displays. The dining table, a custom version of Saarinen's Pedestal Collection, had a fountain at its center. The children's zone drew on Saarinen's impressions of children's rooms in Finland, where he was born.

## Worth the Wait

The Taylor Brothers Construction Company won the contract on April 18, 1955, agreeing to build the house for $352,800, and hired craftsmen from across the Midwest. Irwin Miller wanted the house built relatively quickly, but material shortages, the family's insistence on high-quality craftsmanship and the heavy workload of Saarinen's firm all slowed progress. At one point Miller wrote that he did not think construction could continue until steel arrived. The house missed its contract completion date of July 31, 1956, and a project manager reported that the contractors' morale was low, worn down by delayed specifications and Miller's unrelenting demands. The final cost was never disclosed. Saarinen, who usually specialized in larger buildings, later turned down requests from Bahrain's royal family and a Minnesota family to design houses for them; he had designed this one as a favor to a friend.

## Sleepovers in the Pit

For all its refinement, this was a family house. The five Miller children, ranging from 11 months to 13 years old when they moved in, hosted sleepovers in the conversation pit and roller-skated and played touch football outside. At Christmas, Xenia set out her crèches, which numbered at least a hundred. The walls held works by Kandinsky, Bonnard, Chagall, Matisse, Monet, Picasso and Rothko, yet the Millers' son Will said his parents "saw art as an essential element of leading a good life" rather than as a collection. Their eldest child, Margaret, recalled that only one artwork was ever damaged, when a guest fell onto the Bonnard while trying to look at it. Afterward, the Millers placed plants to keep visitors at a distance.

Guests included First Lady Lady Bird Johnson, who stayed overnight in 1967, as well as David Rockefeller and New York mayor John Lindsay. As the Millers aged, Roche added a handrail inside the pit in 1994, which the museum later removed during restoration. Irwin died in the house in 2004, and Xenia in 2008.

## Allées by the River

Kiley designed the landscape as an extension of the house, with a meadow, two allées and groves of trees stretching between Washington Street and the Flatrock River. The Guardian called it Kiley's first "mature project," and The Washington Post described it as the culmination of his work. The house left its mark on furniture, too: the Eames Aluminum Group chairs were originally produced specifically for it, and Saarinen devised an early version of his tulip chair here. It was the first residence designated a National Historic Landmark while its original owners still lived there and its original landscape architect was still alive.

In November 2008 the family agreed to donate the house to the Indianapolis Museum of Art (now part of Newfields), and it opened to the public in May 2011, drawing 2,600 visitors in its first three months. Today it can be seen only on a guided tour, for adults and children over 10, and because parking is restricted near the house, visitors still arrive by shuttle from the Columbus Area Visitors Center downtown.

## Flight Context

The Miller House and Garden sits at about 39.227 N, 85.923 W on the east bank of the Flatrock River, north of downtown Columbus, Indiana. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL, look for a low, flat-roofed rectangle set among long rows of trees and an open meadow running down to the river. Columbus Municipal Airport (KBAK) lies only about 2.5 nm to the north-northeast, so expect traffic in the pattern, and Freeman Municipal Airport in Seymour (KSER) is about 18 nm to the south. The surrounding terrain is flat, making the city's grid and the river corridor easy to follow.
