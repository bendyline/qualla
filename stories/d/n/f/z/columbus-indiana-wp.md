# Columbus, Indiana

A southern Indiana engine town that shed its founder's name became a worldwide showcase of modern architecture, with landmark buildings by Saarinen, Weese, Pei and more.

**Location:** 39.2140, -85.9111

**Categories:** Cities in Bartholomew County, Indiana, County seats in Indiana, Modern architecture, Public art, Industrial heritage

## Introduction

This town on the White River was first called Tiptona. General John Tipton, who bought the land with Luke Bonesteel in 1820 and built a log cabin on a small hill overlooking the swampy, heavily forested valley, lent it his name. On March 20, 1821, it became Columbus instead. Many people believe Tipton took offense, though no evidence proves it; either way, he left the new town and never returned. Later, as Indiana's highway commissioner building a road from Indianapolis to Louisville, he detoured it around the west side of Columbus, a route described as the first bypass road ever built. It is a wry beginning for a city that people now travel a long way to see.

## Where Two Rivers Become One

The Driftwood and Flatrock rivers meet here to form the East Fork of the White River, and a ferry below the confluence gave the settlement its start: three or four log cabins around the landing, then a store in 1821. Later that year the state legislature organized Bartholomew County, named for the Hoosier militiaman General Joseph Bartholomew. For years, local history books said Tipton had donated the land for the town. In 2003, Historic Columbus Indiana acquired a deed showing that he had sold it.

Then came the railroad. The Madison and Indianapolis Railroad, described as the first operable steam railroad completed in Indiana, was built north from Madison and reached Columbus in 1844; it later became part of the Pennsylvania Railroad system. Three more lines reached the city by 1850, and the traffic helped grow Columbus into one of the largest communities in the state.

## Engines and Oddities

Industry followed the tracks. Around 1911 the local Reeves company produced two unusual automobiles, the four-axle Octoauto and the twin rear-axle Sextoauto. In the 1930s, Noblitt-Sparks Industries built radios here under the Arvin brand. Above all, Columbus became home to the engine company Cummins, founded by inventor Clessie Cummins and still headquartered in the city and one of the region's largest employers.

The town kept its cultural landmarks too. The Crump Theatre, built by John Crump in 1889, is called the oldest remaining theater in Indiana by the state's economic development agency. After years of sitting mostly unused, and a 2019 spot on Indiana Landmarks' list of most endangered places, it is slated for a $21.85 million renovation and expansion as the Crump Center for the Performing Arts, backed by state arts-and-culture investment funds. Cummins Bookstore, open from 1892 until late 2007, was billed as the oldest continually operated bookstore in the state. And a young Chuck Taylor played basketball at Columbus High School before setting out to promote the sport and his now-famous shoes, a career that eventually earned him a place in the Naismith Memorial Basketball Hall of Fame.

## A Bank Without Bars

The architecture that made Columbus famous worldwide had a patron. J. Irwin Miller, then president and chairman of Cummins, launched the Cummins Foundation, a charitable program that helps subsidize a large number of architectural projects across the city by up-and-coming architects and engineers.

The Irwin Union Bank, designed in 1954 and opened in 1955, shows what that ambition looked like. Traditional banks of the day mostly echoed imposing neoclassical buildings of brick or stone, with tellers behind iron bars, removed from their customers. Eero Saarinen designed something else: a glazed banking hall set off against the blank background of a brick office annex, a building meant to welcome customers rather than intimidate them. Steel-and-glass vestibules connect the hall to the three-story annex, the larger part of which was added in 1973 by Kevin Roche John Dinkeloo and Associates. In 2000 the bank was designated a National Historic Landmark in recognition of its unique architecture.

## Modernism on Main Street

Seven buildings in Columbus, constructed between 1942 and 1971, are National Historic Landmarks, and about 60 others sustain the city's reputation as a showcase of modern architecture. Harry Weese designed First Baptist Church without windows, under a high-pitched roof and skylight; it was dedicated in 1965. Myron Goldsmith of SOM designed the former home of the newspaper The Republic, finished in 1971 and, since 2018, the home of Indiana University's Master of Architecture program. First Christian Church, North Christian Church, the Mabel McDowell School and the Miller House and Garden complete the landmark list.

The other names read like an architecture syllabus: I. M. Pei's Cleo Rogers Memorial Library, César Pelli's Commons Centre, Robert Venturi's Fire Station Number 4, and Gunnar Birkerts's St. Peter's Lutheran Church and Lincoln Elementary School. Public art fills the spaces between them, from Henry Moore's Large Arch and Jean Tinguely's Chaos I to Dale Chihuly's Yellow Neon Chandelier and Rudolph de Harak's Exploded Engine, a fitting sculpture for an engine town.

## A Legacy Still Unfolding

Outsiders have noticed. National Public Radio once devoted an article to the town's architecture, and in late 2008 National Geographic Traveler ranked Columbus 11th on its list of historic destinations, calling it "authentic, unique, and unspoiled." The city is also the basis for independent filmmaker Kogonada's 2017 film Columbus, shot on location over 18 days in the summer of 2016.

Today Columbus is a city of 50,474, about 40 miles south of Indianapolis. In 2016, Landmark Columbus launched Exhibit Columbus, an annual program that alternates between symposium and exhibition years, as a way to carry the ambitious traditions of the past into the future. The town that once lost its founder to a name change has found a far more lasting identity in what it chose to build.

## Flight Context

Columbus lies at about 39.214 N, 85.911 W in Bartholomew County, roughly 40 miles south of Indianapolis, where the Driftwood and Flatrock rivers join to form the East Fork of the White River. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL, look for the meeting of the two rivers beside the city, Interstate 65 bypassing the city to the west, and Indiana Route 46 running east-west through the southern part of town. Columbus Municipal Airport (KBAK) is about 3 nm north of the city, with a roughly 6,400-foot Runway 5-23 and a roughly 5,000-foot crosswind runway 14-32. Freeman Municipal Airport in Seymour (KSER) is about 17 nm to the south. The surrounding terrain is flat, open farmland with good visibility on most days.
