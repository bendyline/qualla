# Tombstone Junction

Near Cumberland Falls, a homegrown Wild West park built around a full-size steam train drew country music's biggest stars before fire after fire, and hard times in coal country, brought it down.

**Location:** 36.8348, -84.3580

**Categories:** Defunct amusement parks in the United States, Amusement parks in Kentucky, Heritage railroads, Tourist attractions in McCreary County, Kentucky, Country music

## Introduction

The last fire at Tombstone Junction happened by accident, after the park was already gone. A scrap crew was cutting up the old wooden coaches when a torch set them ablaze. The worker, afraid of being blamed for damaging the steam locomotive still coupled to them, pulled the coupling and cut the safety cables so the burning cars would roll free. Roll they did, all the way down the steep grade out of the park, until they reached the stretch where railway crews had already pulled up the rails. There the flaming cars piled onto one another and burned in a heap. It was a fitting, if sad, final scene for a Western town that fire had been chasing for years.

## A Railroad Grows a Town

It began with a train. In the 1960s, Millard and Morris Stephens of nearby Whitley City built the Cumberland Falls Scenic Railroad as an attraction to complement their Falls Motel near Cumberland Falls State Resort Park. The track ran around the edges of a large cut between two mountains, filled in just enough to lay an L-shaped loop. Similar scenic railroads of the era grew into parks, such as the Rebel Railroad in Pigeon Forge, the ancestor of Dollywood, and Tweetsie Railroad in North Carolina, but those used smaller narrow-gauge equipment. The Stephens' line was full-size standard gauge. With help from local residents, the family added buildings, shops and the Red Garter Saloon for the second season, and a frontier town took shape: a fort-style entrance, a jail, a train station, a tilted-floor fun house called Pa's Cabin and a mock graveyard called Boot Hill. Locals called it simply "The Junction."

## Old #77 and the Eight Percent Grade

The star was Old #77, a 90-ton 0-6-0 switcher built by ALCO in Schenectady in January 1944 for Pennsylvania's Union Railroad. It later worked Kentucky's Morehead and North Fork Railroad as #14; after that line switched to diesel in 1963, the park bought it and gave back its old number. Two Army-surplus Porter tank engines had been bought first, but the grades proved too much for them. The ride was rough, a close echo of a hastily laid early-20th-century line. Halfway around, "bandits" stopped the train until the sheriff rode up to face their leader in a draw, and the winner varied. The staged robbery also gave the crew time to build a full head of steam. The climb back rose from 4 to 6 to 8 percent, ending in a 180-degree turn so tight that the tender's grab irons scraped the cab. Once, with the sanders accidentally left on, #77 lifted itself right off the rails beside the amphitheater, just before a Loretta Lynn show.

## Sundays with the Stars

The park ran no mechanical rides besides the train, so it made up the difference with people. Local residents played the townsfolk, gunfighters mingled with visitors, and the shows invited audiences to join in. On Sundays from April to October, a famous recording artist or band took the outdoor stage, and the roster read like a country music hall of fame: The Judds, Randy Travis, Dolly Parton, Kenny Rogers, Barbara Mandrell, Conway Twitty and Loretta Lynn, the last two regulars. The octagonal amphitheater had a roof on posts, benches in three tiers and a gravel floor. Two magicians who would become world-class performers, Lance Burton and Mac King, began their professional careers here as a two-man act, performing three shows a day, and the magician Whit Haydn is also reported to have worked the Red Garter's stage. The station office held a collection of autographed photos from the stars who came through.

## Fire, Coal and Competition

The losses came in waves. The original Red Garter burned near the end of the 1974 season, and Millard Stephens died that November. Morris Stephens died in August 1976, leaving family control of the park uncertain until a sale in early 1989. A July 1983 fire destroyed five buildings, including the jail and bank, at a cost of more than $100,000. The region's economy rested almost entirely on coal, and when Kentucky's coal industry began shedding jobs in the late 1980s, local ticket sales dropped sharply. Meanwhile, Dollywood and the booming resort towns of Pigeon Forge and Gatlinburg, a couple of hours away, drew tourists the small park could not match. Late in 1989, another fire destroyed the Red Garter and several town buildings. On October 2, 1991, fire took nearly everything that remained except a few outbuildings and the train. The decision to close came almost immediately, and an absolute auction was held in May 1995.

## What the Woods Kept

Little remains. There is a two-level parking lot, a rusting entrance gate, the saloon's foundation, the standpipe that once filled the locomotive's boiler, and scattered crossties and ballast. Vandals burned the train station in the early 2000s and the repair barn around 2005; Pa's Cabin stood until 2007 before its roof caved in. Old #77 survived, bought at auction by Harmon Taylor of Stearns to keep it in McCreary County. A later restoration costing more than $1.5 million failed, and as of 2023 the engine sat disassembled in the old Kentucky and Tennessee Railway steam shop in Stearns.

## Flight Context

The site of Tombstone Junction lies at approximately 36.83°N, 84.36°W along Kentucky Route 90 in McCreary County, Kentucky, a short distance from Cumberland Falls State Resort Park. Little survives on the ground, so use the Cumberland Falls area and KY 90 as visual references, viewed from 2,500 to 3,500 feet AGL over the wooded hills. McCreary County Airport (FAA: 18I) is about 8.5 nm south, Williamsburg-Whitley County Airport (KBYL) about 8 nm east, Lake Cumberland Regional (KSME) about 18 nm northwest, and London-Corbin/Magee Field (KLOZ) about 20 nm northeast. Valley fog is common on cool mornings.
