# Cumberland Falls

Kentucky's "Niagara of the South" has crept some 45 miles upstream over the ages, carved a gorge up to 400 feet deep, and on clear full-moon nights throws a rare moonbow across its mist.

**Location:** 36.8380, -84.3444

**Categories:** Waterfalls, Cumberland River, State parks, Sacred waters, Geology, Kentucky

## Introduction

Come on a clear night near the full moon and this waterfall does something most waterfalls never do. The mist rising off the falling water catches the moonlight and forms a lunar rainbow, a moonbow, over the Cumberland River. Cumberland Falls in southeastern Kentucky, sometimes called the Little Niagara, the Niagara of the South or simply the Great Falls, is one of the few places in the Western Hemisphere where a moonbow can be seen with any regularity. The Kentucky Department of Parks even publishes schedules so visitors can be there when it happens.

## A Curtain Across Two Counties

By daylight the falls are impressive enough. The Cumberland River pours over a resistant sandstone bed in a drop that averages 68 feet high and 125 feet wide, spanning the river right at the border of McCreary and Whitley counties. Below the ledge the water gathers in a wide plunge pool before moving on downstream. The falls are the central feature of Cumberland Falls State Resort Park, and the river here belongs to Kentucky's Wild Rivers System, which the Office of Kentucky Nature Preserves oversees. A second waterfall, Eagle Falls, pours down nearby on the west bank, and farther downstream the rapids of Center Rock keep the river restless.

## A Waterfall That Walked Upstream

The falls have not always stood here. It is believed that Cumberland Falls began far downstream, at the Pottsville Escarpment near modern-day Burnside, Kentucky, and retreated approximately 45 miles upstream through erosion. As it moved, the waterfall cut the Cumberland River gorge behind it, reaching depths of up to 400 feet in places. The journey cost the falls both height and power. At its original location it stood taller than it does today, eroding lower as it crept upstream. It also carried more water, because two major tributaries, the Laurel River and the Rockcastle River, join the Cumberland between the escarpment and the modern falls. What visitors see now is a waterfall that has been slowly walking away from where it was born.

## Sacred Water, Surveyed Land

People were drawn to this water long before the park existed. By 1650 the area had been visited by members of the Shawnee, Cherokee, Chickasaw and Creek peoples, and both Cumberland Falls and nearby Eagle Falls were considered sacred by many. Later came deeds and surveyors. The first recorded landowners of the falls were Matthew Walton and Adam Shepard in 1800. They surveyed the land in 1814 and were issued a land patent in 1828. From then on the falls passed through multiple private owners, a place of power and beauty that could be bought and sold like any other tract.

## The Campaign for a Park

The 1920s brought the falls new attention. In 1927 the Corbin Kiwanis Club built a road and wooden bridge to the falls from nearby Corbin, Kentucky, and Governor William J. Fields attended the opening. The next year, a proposal to accept the falls as a park reached the floor of the Kentucky Senate and was defeated. The falls found their champion in T. Coleman du Pont, a Kentucky native who offered in 1927 to buy the falls and the land around them in order to create a state park. In March 1930 the General Assembly approved the arrangement, and du Pont's family turned the land over to the state; du Pont himself died that November. The state dedicated the park on August 21, 1931, making the falls the heart of what is now Cumberland Falls State Resort Park.

## Why the Moon Paints Here

Moonbows need a particular set of conditions, and this gorge happens to supply them. During or near a full moon on clear nights, the water of the falls can form a lunar rainbow. The steep gorge walls reduce how much wind disperses the waterfall's mist, so the spray lingers in the air. At the same time, the gorge is wide enough to let in increased levels of moonlight. Put those together and the falls can hold a faint arc of moonlight in their spray. The Kentucky Department of Parks publishes moonbow schedules for visitors, turning a trick of geology and light into a night out.

## Flight Context

Cumberland Falls: 36.838°N, 84.344°W, on the Cumberland River at the McCreary-Whitley county line in southeastern Kentucky, inside the Daniel Boone National Forest region. A viewing altitude of about 2,500-3,000 ft AGL (roughly 3,500-4,000 ft MSL) shows the falls, the wide plunge pool and the forested gorge winding away downstream to the north. Nearest airports: Williamsburg-Whitley County Airport (KBYL), about 7 nm east-southeast; McCreary County Airport at Pine Knot (FAA identifier 18I, no ICAO code), about 9 nm south-southwest; London-Corbin Airport/Magee Field (KLOZ), about 19 nm northeast; Lake Cumberland Regional at Somerset (KSME), about 18 nm northwest. River fog can fill the gorge on calm mornings; midday light shows the falls best.
