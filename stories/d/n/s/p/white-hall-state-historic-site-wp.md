# White Hall State Historic Site

The Italianate mansion of emancipationist publisher Cassius Marcellus Clay near Richmond, Kentucky, holds the contradictions of a family that built its fortune on enslaved labor and produced both an anti-slavery crusader and suffrage leaders.

**Location:** 37.8328, -84.3522

**Categories:** Kentucky State Historic Sites, Historic house museums in Kentucky, Abolitionism in the United States, Women's suffrage in the United States, Italianate architecture in Kentucky, Houses on the National Register of Historic Places in Kentucky

## Introduction

While Cassius Marcellus Clay served as President Lincoln's minister to Russia during the Civil War, his wife was remaking their Kentucky home. Mary Jane Warfield Clay oversaw the transformation of Clermont, a modest Georgian house of eight rooms, into White Hall, an Italianate mansion of 44. The work, designed by architect Thomas Lewinski, is said to have cost $40,000. Even the front door moved, turned from south to east. The house she made still stands near Richmond, southeast of Lexington, and it holds the story of one of Kentucky's most complicated families.

## Rainwater and Georgian Bones

Cassius Clay's father, General Green Clay, built Clermont in 1798 and 1799, and a kitchen on the property dates to 1790. That stone kitchen served as the main cooking space, with food carried to a warming kitchen in the house, and it contains what is believed to be the largest surviving fireplace from the 19th century. The renovation layered Italianate style over the Georgian original. White Hall was notable inside, too. Rainwater collected from the roof was fed down to a toilet and a copper bathtub, giving the family indoor plumbing. Today the restored house sits within a 14-acre state historic site.

## A Fortune Built on Others' Labor

Green Clay was an early Kentucky settler and land surveyor who made his fortune by keeping a portion of the land he surveyed as payment. By his death, he was one of the largest landholders and slaveholders in Kentucky. That wealth rested on the forced labor of enslaved people, and their presence is still visible here. The larger of White Hall's two surviving outbuildings served as living quarters for the enslaved people who lived and worked on the estate. Their lives are as much a part of White Hall's history as the grand rooms of the main house.

## The Emancipationist

Cassius Clay turned against the system that had enriched his family. Although his father was among the largest slaveholders in the state, he supported the gradual freeing of enslaved people, a position unusual for a man of his class, and he faced violent attacks over it. In Lexington he published an anti-slavery newspaper, The True American, and was later forced to move its production to Cincinnati. He served in the Mexican War and came home to a hero's welcome, helped found the Republican Party in Kentucky, and represented the United States in Russia under Lincoln, Johnson and Grant. He also granted land to the abolitionist John Gregg Fee, who founded Berea College on it in 1855. The Society of Professional Journalists lists White Hall among its Historic Sites in Journalism, recognizing Clay's career as a publisher.

## Daughters of White Hall

Clay's daughters grew up here too, and they carried the family's reforming streak in a new direction. Mary Barr Clay was an early supporter of women's suffrage, and she deeply influenced her younger sisters, especially Laura, who went on to lead in the suffrage movement. Laura Clay became one of the first women to have her name placed in nomination for president at a major party's national convention, in 1920. Another sister, Sally, lived at White Hall as well. Cassius's son Brutus Junius Clay II served as a commissioner to the 1900 Paris Exposition and as U.S. minister to Switzerland, and donated a home in Richmond in memory of his wife to help found the city's first hospital.

## Bringing the Pieces Home

When Cassius Clay died, his belongings were sold at an estate sale at White Hall on October 8, 1903. Decades later, the buyers' names in the bank's sale book and county records helped the state track pieces down. After the Madison County Garden Club helped lead a preservation push and Governor Edward Breathitt agreed to buy the property, Clay's heirs sold the surrounding land to Kentucky for $18,000 in 1968 and donated the house. First Lady Beula C. Nunn led the restoration, and White Hall opened to the public in 1971. Treasures have trickled back since: Clay's appointment to Russia signed by Abraham Lincoln, pieces of his 104-piece diplomatic china, two tapestry rugs found in a kitchen stovepipe, likely placed there to keep out soot, and a Louis XV table that General Lafayette gave to Green Clay. Cassius prized that table so much that he let no one touch it and repaired a broken leg himself. In 2019, Eastern Kentucky University accepted the site's transfer from the state, and by 2026 the City of Richmond had taken over day-to-day operations, reopening the house for tours that June.

## Flight Context

White Hall State Historic Site lies at approximately 37.83°N, 84.35°W in Madison County, Kentucky, north of Richmond and southeast of Lexington. The mansion and its outbuildings sit on a 14-acre site; a viewing altitude of 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL works well. Central Kentucky Regional Airport (KRGA) at Richmond is about 12 nm south, and Blue Grass Airport (KLEX) in Lexington about 17 nm northwest. Fort Boonesborough State Park lies about 6 nm to the northeast. Expect summer haze and occasional morning fog.
