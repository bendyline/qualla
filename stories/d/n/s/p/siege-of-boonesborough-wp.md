# Siege of Boonesborough

In September 1778, Shawnee chief Blackfish laid siege to Daniel Boone's Kentucky fort for more than a week, months after capturing and adopting Boone, and the fort's survival ended with Boone himself facing a court-martial for disloyalty.

**Location:** 37.9083, -84.2719

**Categories:** Battles of the American Revolutionary War in Kentucky, Sieges of the American Revolutionary War, Daniel Boone, Shawnee history, Madison County, Kentucky

## Introduction

On September 9, 1778, outside the log walls of Boonesborough, the Shawnee chief Blackfish sat down to a shared meal with the settlers he had come to drive from Kentucky. Then he asked the question at the heart of the whole conflict: "by what right had the white people taken possession of this country." Daniel Boone answered that they had bought the land from the Cherokees at Sycamore Shoals, and a Cherokee chief confirmed it. The two sides signed a treaty that does not survive. As the two sides moved to shake hands on it, a scuffle broke out, marksmen opened fire, and the siege began in earnest.

## Hunting Grounds and Treaty Lines

The trouble had been building for years. After Virginia defeated a mostly Shawnee coalition in Dunmore's War in 1774, a treaty set the Ohio River as the boundary between Shawnee lands to the north and Virginia to the south. Many Native leaders never accepted it, since it gave away their prime hunting grounds. In 1775, Richard Henderson of North Carolina bought a large tract of Kentucky from the Cherokees, and his employee Daniel Boone blazed the Wilderness Trail and built Fort Boone, soon renamed Boonesborough. As the Revolution spread west, Henry Hamilton, the British lieutenant governor at Detroit, began arming Native war parties in 1777. Blackfish joined willingly, hoping to push the Americans out and reclaim his people's hunting grounds. That year alone, by one tally, Native war parties brought 129 scalps and 77 prisoners to Hamilton at Detroit.

## Big Turtle

With crops burned and cattle killed, Boonesborough needed salt to preserve its meat. In January 1778, Boone led thirty men to the salt springs on the Licking River, and on February 7 he was captured while hunting by warriors under Blackfish. Greatly outnumbered, Boone persuaded his men to surrender, then talked Blackfish out of attacking the weakened fort by promising it would surrender willingly in the spring. It was improvisation, but his men did not know that, and many concluded he had gone over to the British. At Chillicothe, Boone was adopted into a Shawnee family, perhaps Blackfish's own, and given the name Sheltowee, meaning "Big Turtle." When he learned in June that Blackfish was preparing a large force, he slipped away and covered 160 miles to Boonesborough in five days.

## Stalling for Time

On September 7, Blackfish arrived with a force Boone put at roughly 400 to 450 Native warriors, mostly Shawnees along with Cherokees, Wyandots, Miamis, Delawares and Mingos, plus 12 French-Canadian militiamen from Detroit. It was the largest force yet sent against the Kentucky settlements, but it had no artillery. Blackfish reminded Boone of his promise and offered Hamilton's guarantee of good treatment in Detroit. Inside, the settlers chose to fight and to stall, since reinforcements from Virginia were expected. Boone and Major William Bailey Smith told Blackfish they feared the journey would be too hard on the women and children; the chief replied that he had brought 40 horses for them. The chief believed at least 200 militiamen were inside. In fact there were only some 40 to 60 effective gunmen, depending on the account, and some of the women put on men's clothing and carried weapons to keep up the illusion. What happened when the treaty was sealed remains unclear. Some say the Shawnees tried to seize the American leaders, but historian John Mack Faragher and others see little evidence of that.

## Tunnels in the Riverbank

For days, gunfire crossed the walls. Boone urged the defenders to save powder. At night, warriors ran up with burning torches to throw onto the cabin roofs, and became easy targets. On September 11, Lieutenant Antoine Dagneaux de Quindre of the Detroit militia had his allies begin tunneling from the riverbank toward the fort, planning to blow a gap in the wall with gunpowder. The defenders heard the digging and dug a countermine, and the diggers on both sides shouted taunts at each other underground. Heavy rain collapsed the attackers' tunnel first. Boone's brother Squire, an inventor, built a wooden cannon bound with iron that fired once or twice before cracking, along with squirt guns made from old musket barrels to douse roof fires. The final assault came on September 17, and it cost the attackers more dead than any day before. The next day they gradually broke off the siege, scattering into war parties that raided other settlements. Two people inside the fort died: London, an enslaved man, and a settler named David Bondurant.

## The Hero on Trial

Victory did not bring Boone gratitude. Colonel Richard Callaway and Captain Benjamin Logan, both of whose nephews were still prisoners from the salt party, charged him with favoring the British. The court-martial found him not guilty and promoted him to major, but Boone was humiliated and rarely spoke of it again. He founded a new settlement, Boone's Station, rather than resettle where he had been court-martialed. Blackfish was shot in the leg defending Chillicothe in 1779 and died when the wound became infected, and Callaway was killed by Shawnees outside Boonesborough in 1780. Today the siege is reenacted every year at Fort Boonesborough State Park.

## Flight Context

The site of Boonesborough lies at approximately 37.91°N, 84.27°W on the Kentucky River in Madison County, Kentucky, now Fort Boonesborough State Park on the Kentucky River, southeast of Lexington. The park has a living reproduction of the fort, built on a hillside away from the original site, and holds the yearly reenactment of the siege. From 1,500 to 3,000 feet AGL, follow the Kentucky River to the park. Central Kentucky Regional Airport (KRGA) at Richmond is about 17 nm south, and Blue Grass Airport (KLEX) in Lexington about 18 nm west-northwest; check the sectional chart for Lexington's Class C airspace. River fog can linger in the valley on autumn mornings.
