# Middlesboro crater

An entire Kentucky town sits inside a 3-mile meteorite crater that helped open the Cumberland Gap to westward travel, and it is often called the only place in the world where coal is mined inside an impact crater.

**Location:** 36.6167, -83.7333

**Categories:** Impact craters, Geology, Cumberland Gap, Coal mining, Kentucky

## Introduction

Alexander A. Arthur chose the site for a new town in 1886: a basin in the Appalachian Mountains of southeastern Kentucky, set among iron and coal deposits worth exploiting. What he apparently did not know was how the basin got there. Middlesboro was built inside the scar of an impact from space, a crater about 3 miles wide that the city now largely fills. Its streets and homes sit on the floor of an ancient impact site, and the crater itself takes its name from the town that grew up inside it.

## A Blow From the Sky

The Middlesboro crater, also called an astrobleme, is approximately 3 miles wide by the figure on the state historical marker, and the Earth Impact Database lists it at about 6 kilometers. Its age is estimated at less than 300 million years, placing it in the Permian. Estimates put the object that made it at about 100 meters across, roughly the length of a football field, though the impactor itself is not known, and its impact left fingerprints that geologists can still read in the rock. The most telling are shatter cones, a rock-shattering pattern that forms naturally only during impact events. They are found in abundance around Middlesboro, and shocked quartz, another telltale sign, has been identified here as well. The crater lies between the Cumberland Mountains and Pine Mountain, and in September 2003 the Kentucky Society of Professional Geologists designated it a Distinguished Geologic Site.

## Two Moments of Recognition

The town was more than seventy-five years old before anyone put a name to what lay beneath it. Accounts credit two moments of discovery. In the early 1960s, K. J. Englund and J. B. Roen, working for the U.S. Geological Survey, proposed that an impact had formed the basin. Then, in 1966, Robert Dietz found shatter cones in the local sandstone, and that find led to the further identification of shocked quartz. Between them, the geologists turned a familiar valley into something far stranger. The people of Middlesboro had been living, mining and building for generations in a place shaped by a collision from space, and only now did the rocks tell them so.

## The Crater That Opened a Gateway

The impact did more than dent the landscape. It helped shape a critical passage west. The 12-mile-long Cumberland Gap consists of four geologic features: the Yellow Creek valley, the natural gap in the Cumberland Mountain ridge, the eroded gap in Pine Mountain, and the Middlesboro crater. Together they made the Cumberland Gap a critical westward passage during the settlement of Kentucky and the Ohio Valley in the late 18th and early 19th centuries. By one account, the crater's part was essential. Without it, packhorses would have had difficulty navigating this gap, which formed through differential erosion along a cross fault, and wagon roads would have been improbable at such an early date. By that account, settlers who led horses west through the Gap owed part of the path to a rock from space.

## Coal From an Impact Site

Middlesboro claims a singular distinction: it is often called the only place in the world where coal is mined inside an impact crater. The crater's strata are complicated, and special mining techniques are said to be needed to work them. Coal mining has long been the town's economic mainstay, and local leaders have hoped to turn the crater itself into a tourist destination. The town has another advantage. The Cumberland Gap Tunnel makes Middlesboro a convenient place for visitors to Cumberland Gap National Historical Park to stock up on supplies, and anyone who stops there is standing inside the very feature that helped make the Gap a gateway.

## Flight Context

Middlesboro crater: 36.6167°N, 83.7333°W, the roughly 3-mile-wide basin occupied by the city of Middlesboro, Bell County, Kentucky, between Pine Mountain to the north and the Cumberland Mountain ridge to the south and east, near the Cumberland Gap and the Kentucky-Tennessee-Virginia border. A viewing altitude of about 4,500-5,500 ft MSL clears the surrounding ridges and shows the basin's rounded outline filled by the town. Middlesboro/Bell County Airport (FAA identifier 1A6, no ICAO code) sits inside the crater, less than 1 nm from these coordinates. Other nearby fields: New Tazewell/Wayne Coffey Municipal (FAA identifier 3A2, no ICAO code), about 15 nm southeast; for larger airports, McGhee Tyson at Knoxville (KTYS) is about 50 nm south-southwest. Ridge-top winds can cause turbulence and valley fog is possible on calm mornings.
