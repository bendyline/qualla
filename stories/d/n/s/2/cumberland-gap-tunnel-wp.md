# Cumberland Gap Tunnel

Forty years in the making, the twin-bore Cumberland Gap Tunnel carries U.S. 25E beneath a national historical park between Tennessee and Kentucky, replacing a deadly stretch of road drivers called 'Massacre Mountain.'

**Location:** 36.6058, -83.6769

**Categories:** Tunnels in Kentucky, Tunnels in Tennessee, Cumberland Gap, U.S. Route 25, Tunnels completed in 1996

## Introduction

The old road over the Cumberland Gap had a grim nickname: "Massacre Mountain." The highway through the pass, where Kentucky, Tennessee and Virginia meet, had earned that nickname with its hazardous design and high rate of crashes. Since 1996, traffic on U.S. Route 25E has gone under the mountain instead, through the Cumberland Gap Tunnel, a pair of bores running beneath Cumberland Gap National Historical Park between Cumberland Gap, Tennessee, and Middlesboro, Kentucky. It is often described as one of only two mountain vehicular tunnels in the United States that cross a state line, the other being the East River Mountain Tunnel between Virginia and West Virginia. Getting it built took forty years of proposals, budget fights and a mountain full of surprises.

## A Pass Too Important to Pave

The Cumberland Gap, a mountain pass important in American colonial history, lies only about 0.4 miles northeast of the tunnels at its closest point, and the tri-point of the three states is closer still, about 0.15 miles from the tunnel. For most of the 20th century, the road went straight through the historic ground. In 1908 the federal government built a macadamized road called the Government Pike from Middlesboro, Kentucky, to Cumberland Gap, Tennessee, partly through the gap itself. In 1916, Tennessee, Virginia and Kentucky added a road through the pass that connected to it. Safety improvements followed in the 1960s, but the National Park Service had already proposed a tunnel in 1956. Studies over the following decade kept recommending one, because widening the surface road to four lanes would damage the park's historically sensitive areas. A tunnel became the only viable choice.

## Stopped, Then Started

Congress began funding the tunnel in the late 1970s, with an initial $1.1 million, and geologists examined the rock in 1979. A contract for initial construction was awarded on November 20, 1984 - and less than a month later, on December 14, the administration of President Ronald Reagan abruptly ordered the project halted, reportedly to help reduce the federal budget deficit. Regional lawmakers pushed back, among them Representative Hal Rogers, whose district included the Kentucky side of the tunnel. On May 15, 1985, Interior Secretary Donald P. Hodel announced that the project would go ahead. The Eastern Federal Lands Highway Division of the Federal Highway Administration ran the work on behalf of the National Park Service, with funding from both agencies and from Kentucky and Tennessee, which widened their own approaches to four lanes.

## A Lake Inside the Mountain

Starting in December 1985, crews drilled a pilot tunnel 4,100 feet long and 10 feet square. What they found inside changed the project. The mountain held thick clay infillings, limestone formations, caves, multiple underground springs and streams, and a lake, and water leaked into the workings at 450 gallons per minute. At the time, planners expected the tunnel to open by 1991 at a cost of about $160 million. The water made the job harder and more expensive, and in the end the tubes were lined with a thick layer of PVC to keep it out. Excavation of the main tubes began on June 21, 1991, with crews digging from both sides at once. The two headings met on July 9, 1992. The tunnel opened to traffic on October 18, 1996, at an overall cost, including the four-lane approach roads and other work, that sources put between $265 million (the National Park Service) and about $280 million.

## Watched Around the Clock

Each of the two tubes runs 4,600 feet, 32 feet wide and 16.5 feet high, carrying two lanes. Cross-passages with fire extinguishers and phones link the tubes every 300 feet, and ventilation fans hang from the roof every 600 feet. At each portal, an "illumination zone" lets drivers' eyes adjust to the dimmer light inside. The posted speed limit is 45 mph, lower in bad weather or during closures, and lane changes are prohibited. Trucks carrying explosives are banned outright, while other hazardous cargo and wide loads wait at the portals to be escorted through one at a time. Pedestrians and bicyclists may not use the tunnel without a special use permit. Cameras cover the full length, and a control system ties together fans and carbon monoxide, smoke and heat detectors. Identical control rooms sit above each portal, staffed 24 hours a day by the Cumberland Gap Tunnel Authority, whose staff of roughly three dozen full-time employees even runs its own emergency medical service, fire department and towing crew.

## Flight Context

The Cumberland Gap Tunnel runs beneath the Cumberland Mountains at approximately 36.6058 N, 83.6769 W, between Middlesboro, Kentucky, to the north and the town of Cumberland Gap, Tennessee, to the south, with the Virginia line just to the east. From 4,500-5,500 ft MSL, the tunnel portals, the U.S. 25E approach roads and the notch of the historic gap about 0.4 mile to the northeast are all visible, along with the town of Middlesboro. Ridges nearby rise well above the valley floors, so keep a healthy terrain margin. Nearby airports: Middlesboro/Bell County Airport (FAA identifier 1A6, no ICAO code listed; about 3 nm west), New Tazewell/Wayne Coffey Municipal Airport (FAA identifier 3A2, no ICAO code listed; about 13 nm south-southeast) and London-Corbin Airport/Magee Field (KLOZ; about 35 nm northwest). McGhee Tyson Airport (KTYS) near Knoxville is about 50 nm south-southwest. Valley fog can linger on autumn and winter mornings.
