# Hensley Settlement

High on Brush Mountain, a family settlement that never had modern utilities grew to about a hundred people, emptied until one man remained, and was rebuilt as a living record of Appalachian mountain life.

**Location:** 36.6694, -83.5283

**Categories:** Appalachian culture, Living history museums, Cumberland Gap National Historical Park, Log buildings, Kentucky

## Introduction

For two years, Sherman Hensley was the only person left on top of Brush Mountain. By 1949 everyone else had gone from the settlement where he had lived for nearly half a century, a community that once held about a hundred people, and in 1951 he finally left too. Behind him stood log cabins, a one-room schoolhouse and a blacksmith shop in a place that had never developed modern infrastructure or technology. Today it is Hensley Settlement, an Appalachian living history museum inside Cumberland Gap National Historical Park in Bell County, Kentucky.

## Five Hundred Acres Above the Valleys

The story of the land begins decades before the Hensleys arrived. In 1845 Governor William Owsley deeded 500 acres on top of Brush Mountain, in the Appalachian Mountains. The brothers C. and R.M. Bales, who received the land from Owsley, leased the acreage to John Nichols and Jim Nelson, who mostly used it for livestock. Nichols and Nelson cleared the property and made improvements, including shake-roofed log cabins built of chestnut. That clearing was a first, practical footprint on the summit, and it made the ridge into land that could support a household. Later families would build on what those livestock men began.

## A Family Divides the Mountain

Burton Hensley Sr. bought the mountaintop land and divided it among his extended family; the National Park Service dates the purchase to about 1900, while other accounts say 1903, the year Sherman Hensley moved his family up the mountain. Sherman, a hog farmer, and his wife Nicey Ann, Burton's daughter, moved into an existing log cabin on the twenty-one acres allotted to her. Willy Gibbons and his wife Nancy, Nicey's niece, soon followed. Most of the people who lived here belonged to either the Hensley or the Gibbons family. Life on the mountain was almost entirely self-made. Everything was grown, raised or made by hand in the settlement, and people traveled on foot or on horseback. A spring house served for food storage. On that remote ridge, two intertwined families built a working community almost entirely with their own hands.

## Lessons in a Lean-To

Children needed schooling, and the settlement found a practical way to get it. The first school house was little more than a lean-to, put up so that the Bell County Superintendent of Schools would agree to send a teacher. It worked. In 1908 Bell County provided a teacher for the newly erected one-room school, which taught students through the eighth grade. By the time the school closed in 1947, four different structures had served as the Brush Mountain school. The last was a log cabin warmed by a cast-iron stove burning wood and coal, set in the middle of the room. There was no indoor plumbing and no electricity. Students sat at desks made of wood and cast iron, learning their lessons on a mountaintop the modern world had not yet reached.

## The Long Emptying

The settlement's population peaked at about 100 people, by most accounts in the 1920s. After that, life on the mountain began to thin. Nicey Ann Hensley died in 1937. During World War II, residents moved away, some to join the military and others to work in the coal mines. The population kept dwindling until 1949, when Sherman Hensley was the only resident left. A mountaintop that had once held about a hundred people now held one. For two years he stayed on alone. When he finally abandoned the property in 1951, the settlement began to fall into disrepair, its cabins and fields left to the weather and the woods.

## Brought Back to Life

The mountain's story did not end with its last departure. On July 4, 1959, the settlement was dedicated as part of Cumberland Gap National Historical Park. In 1965 the National Park Service and the Job Corps began making improvements, restoring the school and dozens of other settlement structures, along with the agricultural environment, to the community's prime. Today the settlement includes homestead log cabins, the one-room school house, a blacksmith shop and a restored spring house. The Park Service runs it as a living history museum, and park rangers lead guided tours of the settlement from mid-May through October. The tours leave from the park visitor center in Middlesboro by four-wheel-drive van and need a reservation. Visitors can walk among the buildings the Hensley and Gibbons families once called home, on the ridge where they made their lives.

## Flight Context

Hensley Settlement: 36.6694°N, 83.5283°W, on the crest of Brush Mountain in Cumberland Gap National Historical Park, Bell County, Kentucky, near the Kentucky-Virginia line. A viewing altitude of about 5,000-6,000 ft MSL gives safe clearance over the ridges and shows the settlement's cleared fields as an opening in the forest along the mountaintop; the town of Middlesboro and the Cumberland Gap lie to the west-southwest. Nearest airports: Middlesboro-Bell County Airport (FAA identifier 1A6, no ICAO code), about 11 nm west-southwest; Lee County Airport at Jonesville, Virginia (FAA identifier 0VG, no ICAO code), about 15 nm east; Tucker-Guthrie Memorial Airport at Harlan (FAA identifier I35, no ICAO code), about 14 nm northeast. For larger airports, Tri-Cities Airport (KTRI) is about 55 nm east-southeast. Mountain ridges here can hold low cloud and turbulence, so plan for good VFR conditions.
