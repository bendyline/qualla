# Hindman Settlement School

Founded in 1902 on Troublesome Creek by two women a local elder asked to stay, Hindman Settlement School became what is described as America's first rural settlement school and has spent more than a century teaching, reading and writing in the Kentucky mountains.

**Location:** 37.3344, -82.9758

**Categories:** Settlement schools, Appalachian culture in Kentucky, Educational institutions established in 1902, Museums in Knott County, Kentucky, DAR schools, Hindman, Kentucky

## Introduction

Solomon Everage watched the two women for some time before he said anything. Katherine Pettit and May Stone had been coming into the Kentucky mountains every summer since 1899 to do settlement work, and to the people of Knott County they were outsiders: "quare fotched-on women from the level land," in a phrase that later gave a published edition of their journals its title. Eventually the local elder made his request. Would they establish a permanent school in the Troublesome Creek area? In 1902 they did, and Hindman Settlement School became what is widely described as the first rural settlement school in America.

## The Camp That Stayed

The summers came first. Under the auspices of the Kentucky Federation of Women's Clubs, Pettit and Stone spent three seasons working at Camp Cedar Grove, Sassafras Social Settlement and a place called Camp Industrial, which would later become the Hindman Settlement School. When they committed to something permanent in 1902, the financing came through the Kentucky Woman's Christian Temperance Union, whose president, Frances Beauchamp, is credited as its backer, and the school stayed under the union's sponsorship until 1915. More than a century on, Hindman is a DAR-approved school, and its mission has long kept a distinctly local accent: "to provide educational and service opportunities for the people of the mountains, while keeping them mindful of their heritage." That second clause matters: the aim was to educate without asking the mountains to forget themselves.

## Ballads in the Hollows

Because the school set out to weave traditional culture into education, it opened its doors to visitors who wanted to record what the mountains sang. During the years of the First World War, two fieldwork teams arrived: Loraine Wyman working with Howard Brockway, and Cecil Sharp with Maud Karpeles. Around Hindman they found a wealth of beautiful melodies and texts, gathered from singers at the school itself and from the surrounding neighborhood. That instinct to treasure local craft still shapes the campus. The Marie Stewart Museum & Craft Shop sells traditional Appalachian crafts to support the school's work, and upstairs a small museum tells the story of the settlement and the region's handiwork.

## The Librarian Who Never Left

Some people came to Hindman and simply stayed. The poet, folklorist and novelist James Still, who lived from 1906 to 2001, arrived after his friend Don West offered him a job organizing recreational programs at a settlement school in Knott County. Still gladly accepted, soon became librarian at the Hindman Settlement School Library, and spent the rest of his days in the county. He is buried on the campus. Another figure brought the wider world with him. Fred Williams, principal in the mid-1940s, had been a Methodist missionary in India, where he is said to have befriended Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi and worked on sanitation and against caste-based discrimination.

## Words on the Page

The literary program has nurtured the Appalachian writing tradition for more than a century, and its calendar reads like a map of creative refuge: the Appalachian Writers Workshop, the Ironwood Writers Studio, the Troublesome Creek Writer's Retreats, the Winter Burrow Literary and Arts Conference and the Oak Ledge Writing Residency. Reading itself became a mission in 1980, when community members led by Lois Combs Weinberg founded an Orton-Gillingham based program to tutor children struggling with dyslexia. It offers afterschool tutoring and a five-week intensive summer school. When the COVID-19 pandemic pushed classes online, the virtual format proved popular and reached more students, so it continued alongside in-person teaching. Since 2020, in partnership with AmeriCorps, the program has delivered in-school reading intervention in 20 schools across five counties, reaching more than a thousand students by 2024.

## When Troublesome Creek Rose

The creek that gave the founders their first foothold turned on the town in the summer of 2022. On July 28, Governor Andy Beshear declared a state of emergency after several Eastern Kentucky counties were hit by a thousand-year flood event. Troublesome Creek overflowed and affected nearly every building in downtown Hindman. The settlement school sustained significant damage, with its archive rooms and other spaces submerged under several feet of water. Today its work reaches beyond the classroom: its website lists programs in dyslexia intervention, literary arts, traditional arts and foodways, aimed at literacy, food insecurity and access to traditional arts.

## Flight Context

Hindman Settlement School sits at 37.3344 N, 82.9758 W in the town of Hindman, Knott County, along Troublesome Creek in the mountains of eastern Kentucky. From 3,000-4,000 ft AGL the town appears as a thin ribbon of roofs and roads following the creek between steep, forested ridges; follow the creek valley to locate the downtown and campus. Nearby airports: Wendell H. Ford Airport near Hazard (KCPF) about 14 nm west, Julian Carroll Airport in Jackson (KJKL) about 23 nm northwest, and Pike County Airport-Hatcher Field near Pikeville (KPBX) about 24 nm northeast. Ridge-top terrain rises well above the valley floors, and morning valley fog is common in the mountains; plan for low clouds in narrow hollows.
