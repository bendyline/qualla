# Great Saltpetre Cave

A Rockcastle County cave that supplied saltpeter for gunpowder during the War of 1812, later hosted dances and weddings, and is now a protected karst preserve.

**Location:** 37.3669, -84.2875

**Categories:** Caves of Kentucky, Kentucky in the War of 1812, Protected areas of Rockcastle County, Kentucky, National Register of Historic Places in Rockcastle County, Kentucky, Nature reserves in Kentucky, Landforms of Rockcastle County, Kentucky, Daniel Boone National Forest, Limestone caves, Mining in Kentucky

## Introduction

Oxcarts once rolled through this cave. A stream ran through the Great Saltpetre Cave, and the way was wide enough for oxcarts to haul their loads up to daylight, while workers deep inside dug by torchlight. What they were digging out was dirt, but not ordinary dirt. It held the raw material for saltpeter, a vital component of gunpowder, and during the War of 1812 this limestone cave in Rockcastle County, Kentucky, became an important source of it.

## Gunpowder from the Ground

A man named Baker discovered the cave in the late eighteenth century. Like most saltpeter caves, it lies beneath a plateau covered in hardwood forest, and inside, calcium nitrate, also called niter, is leached from the dry soil. That niter was the start of a supply chain. The saltpeter produced here was typically sent to Lexington, Kentucky, to be made into gunpowder, the black powder of the day. The cave also mattered to science. In 1806, Dr. Samuel Brown, a Transylvania University professor of anatomy, chemistry and surgery, gave the first scientific description of Kentucky's saltpeter production in Philadelphia, and he drew mainly on what was happening at the Great Saltpetre Cave. Over the years the cave collected other names, too: Kincaid's Cave and The Great Cave on Crooked Creek.

## A War Fought Underground

When war with Britain came in 1812, British blockades cut off saltpeter shipments from overseas, and the young country had to find its own. Sixty to seventy men were employed to mine this cave during the war, and many of them were enslaved people, forced to do the hard, dark work of digging and hauling. The cave's usefulness did not end with that conflict. It was mined again, though to a lesser degree, during the Mexican–American War and the American Civil War. During the Civil War, Union soldiers who worked the cave are said to have lived in it too. The reason, as the story goes, was simple comfort. The cave holds a steady 58 °F all year, a relief from both the cold of Kentucky winters and the heat of its summers.

## A Ballroom Underground

Long after the mining ended, the cave found a gentler kind of business. For a time in the later twentieth century, until it closed to the public in 1985, it was a commercial show cave, open for tours and also, reportedly, for ballroom dances, a museum and weddings. The crowds came at a cost. Guests often broke off cave formations to take home as souvenirs, and some left their names in the cave. One of the names is said to be Daniel Boone's. The cave's cool chambers have even served as a film set: parts of the 1997 Steven Seagal movie Fire Down Below were shot here. In 2013 the Great Saltpetre Cave was listed on the National Register of Historic Places.

## A Cave Given Back to the Karst

Today the cave is owned by the Rockcastle Karst Conservancy, a non-profit dedicated to protecting caves and karst lands in and around Rockcastle County, which has owned and cared for it since 2006. It forms part of the Great Saltpetre Preserve, and it lies within the boundary of the Daniel Boone National Forest. The preserve's own site says the cave is normally closed to the general public except during an annual Open House Weekend and is managed for historical and educational purposes; educational visits can be arranged, and anyone who visits or camps at the preserve must have a liability waiver on file. The mining days have not vanished entirely: artifacts from that era are reportedly still visible at the site. The oxcarts are gone and the torches long out, but the cave still holds its steady temperature, and the soil that once fed Lexington's gunpowder makers is now part of a protected landscape.

## Flight Context

The Great Saltpetre Cave lies at 37.367°N, 84.288°W in Rockcastle County, Kentucky, within the boundary of the Daniel Boone National Forest. The cave entrance itself is hidden under forest, so fly this as a scenic overflight of the wooded plateau and hollows of the preserve; 2,500 to 3,500 feet AGL keeps you comfortably above the ridges. Central Kentucky Regional Airport in Richmond (KRGA) is about 16 nm north, London-Corbin Airport/Magee Field (KLOZ) about 20 nm south-southeast, and Lake Cumberland Regional (KSME) about 25 nm southwest. Blue Grass Airport in Lexington (KLEX), where the cave's saltpeter was once sent to become gunpowder, is about 43 nm north-northwest. Valley fog is common on cool mornings in this hilly country; late morning usually brings better visibility.
