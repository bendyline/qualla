# Hurricane Creek mine disaster

On December 30, 1970 - one year to the day after a landmark federal mine safety law - an explosion at the Finley Mine near Hyden, Kentucky, killed 38 men, and a mountain community has honored them with a memorial of 38 wooden gates.

**Location:** 37.1287, -83.3478

**Categories:** Coal mining disasters in Kentucky, 1970 mining disasters, 1970 disasters in the United States, Leslie County, Kentucky, Memorials

## Introduction

The timing was noticed at once. The Federal Coal Mine Health and Safety Act of 1969, a law meant to make American mines safer, had been signed on December 30, 1969. Exactly one year later, on December 30, 1970, shortly after noon, an explosion tore through the Finley Mine near Hyden, Kentucky, and killed 38 men. Coverage of the disaster pointed out the anniversary again and again. The Hurricane Creek mine disaster was the deadliest in the United States since the Farmington Mine disaster of 1968, and it left the families of Leslie and Clay counties to grieve in the snow of an Appalachian winter.

## A Small Mine on Leased Land

The explosion hit shafts 15 and 16 of a "truck mine" just outside the town of Hyden. The mine belonged to Charles and Stanley Finley. It had opened only the previous March on leased land, although their company had been mining in the area for ten years. It was a small operation, with 39 men underground that day, and its miners were not members of the United Mine Workers. Most of the men who worked there came from Clay and Leslie counties, two of the poorest in Kentucky. The New York Times summed up the local economy in a single line: "there is little industry but coal."

## Warnings That Went Unchecked

The danger had been identified, though accounts differ on the details. Inspectors cited the mine repeatedly in 1970. A contemporary newspaper account says an inspector found an "imminent danger" from loose coal and coal dust in June and halted mining for three days, and a Kentucky historical marker says safety recommendations made in November were ignored or only indifferently followed. Later accounts say that hazards - excess accumulation of coal dust and electrical spark hazards among them - were found on November 19, and that the mine was ordered to clean them up by December 22. But the Bureau of Mines was short of inspectors and could not return on that date to reinspect, as the law required. After the explosion, investigators found dynamite and Primacord, both banned under the new law, and concluded there had been a "nearly absolute failure" to enforce the new safety laws. Primer cord was reportedly also found at the site of an earlier blast at the mine on December 22 - the very day the cleanup was due. The safety law was a year old, and the agency charged with enforcing it did not have enough inspectors to keep up.

## Snow on the Mountain Roads

The weather made everything harder. Snow lay on the rural mountain roads around Hyden at the time of the accident, complicating the recovery. The bodies were brought to a grade school gymnasium in town. Many had been so badly damaged by the blast that the men could only be identified by the Social Security numbers written on their belts. Word of the tragedy reached Washington quickly. President Richard Nixon issued a statement offering sympathy to the families and friends of the dead miners, promised to take "every appropriate step" to prevent future disasters, and dispatched some of his top aides, including Robert Finch.

## Thirty-Eight Gates

Musicians remembered Hyden in song. Tom T. Hall recorded "Trip to Hyden" about the disaster. J. D. Jarvis issued "The Hyden Miners' Tragedy" as a 45 RPM single on the independent Sunrise label of Hamilton, Ohio, and Wikipedia lists The Band's "The Caves of Jericho," from its 1993 album Jericho, among the songs tied to the disaster, though its lyrics name a fictional Jericho, Kentucky. A permanent memorial took longer. Friends and families of the lost miners planned it, and in 2010, the 40th anniversary year, the state legislature passed House Bill 420, designating the mine site a state historic site and calling for a monument to the miners, with the names of the 38 who died and the survivor. The Hurricane Creek Mine Memorial was dedicated on October 8, 2011, on the site of the Finley Mine. A walkway passes thirty-eight wooden gates, each with a coal miner's hat hanging from it, one for every man who died. A bronze statue of a coal miner stands nearby, and two walls carry the names of the thirty-eight along with information about each of them. The disaster had one survivor, and he is honored too, with a plaque bearing his name placed on the statue.

## Flight Context

The Hurricane Creek Mine Memorial and the site of the Finley Mine are at approximately 37.1287 N, 83.3478 W, just outside Hyden in Leslie County, in the steep, forested mountains of eastern Kentucky. From 4,000-5,000 ft MSL, follow the narrow valleys near Hyden; the terrain is rugged, ridge-and-hollow country with few landing options, so keep generous altitude margins. Nearby airports: Wendell H. Ford Airport, Chavies (KCPF; about 16 nm north-northeast), Tucker-Guthrie Memorial Airport, Harlan (FAA identifier I35, no ICAO code; about 16 nm south) and Julian Carroll Airport, Jackson (KJKL; about 28 nm north). London-Corbin Airport/Magee Field (KLOZ) is about 35 nm west. Winter weather can bring low ceilings and snow, as it did on the day of the disaster, and morning valley fog is common.
