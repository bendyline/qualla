# Red River Gorge

A Kentucky canyon system with more than 100 sandstone arches, rock shelters that preserved ancient moccasins, and a river that survived three decades of plans to dam it.

**Location:** 37.8340, -83.6079

**Categories:** Canyons and gorges of Kentucky, Climbing areas of Kentucky, Daniel Boone National Forest, Natural arches of Kentucky, National Natural Landmarks in Kentucky

## Introduction

Ask for directions in the Red River Gorge and there is a good chance the answer will begin at a pizza restaurant. Miguel's Pizza, in Slade, Kentucky, is widely regarded as the hub of the area's climbing culture, and most guidebooks and websites start their route descriptions from its door. Beyond lies a canyon system on the Red River in east-central Kentucky, part of the Pottsville Escarpment, crowded with high sandstone cliffs, rock shelters, waterfalls and more than 100 natural arches. Climbers, who count it among the world's top rock climbing destinations, simply call it the Red.

## Stone Bent Into Bridges

Erosion and weathering have worked this sandstone for millions of years, and the result is described as the highest concentration of natural arches of any region east of the Mississippi River. Look closely at a cliff face and the rock itself is striped. Groundwater has carried iron-rich minerals through the Corbin Sandstone and left them behind in red, orange and brown Liesegang bands, patterns that even influence how hard the stone is. Natural Bridge State Park sits immediately next door and holds one of the largest natural bridges in the area. The Sky Bridge made its way onto movie screens in The Kentuckian, the 1955 film that Burt Lancaster both directed and starred in. Gray's Arch, reached by its own loop trail, tempts hikers to scramble up its back side, an activity considered extremely dangerous that can lead to injury or death.

## What the Shelters Kept

Long before climbers arrived, people lived beneath these overhangs. Rock shelters across the Red River Gorge Geological Area hold artifacts of indigenous occupation reaching back to the Paleoindian period. Because the overhangs protect the ground from rain-driven weathering, their dry, nitrate-rich soils preserve things that would rot away almost anywhere else, including plant materials, woven mats and leather moccasins. The gorge preserves living history too. Experts believe plants of Canadian origin became established here during the glaciation that began about 2.58 million years ago, and they still grow in its sheltered canyons: purple fringed orchid, Canadian lily and Canadian yew. The white-haired goldenrod, which Kentucky lists as endangered, grows only in the Red River Gorge area of three Kentucky counties; the federal government removed it from the threatened list in 2016 after its recovery. Hawks, owls, woodpeckers and hummingbirds share the forest with deer, foxes and black bears, along with two venomous snakes, the copperhead and the timber rattlesnake.

## The Dam That Never Came

The river that carved all this also flooded the people downstream, and in 1962 the "Great Flood of Clay City," the worst in 102 years, sent officials and residents to lobby the state legislature and the Kennedy administration for a flood-control dam. Congress approved and funded it, and the Army Corps of Engineers went to work. Many feared the reservoir would drown the gorge's unique ecosystem. The Sierra Club spearheaded the opposition, and on November 18, 1967, Supreme Court Justice William O. Douglas joined a Dam Protest Hike at the junction of Swift Camp Creek and the Red River, drawing local and national attention. In 1971 the University Press of Kentucky commissioned the farmer and author Wendell Berry to write The Unforeseen Wilderness, a book arguing for the gorge's preservation. The struggle lasted decades and involved two proposed dam sites. It ended on December 2, 1993, when the Red River Designation Act (Public Law 103-170), sponsored by Representative Hal Rogers, became law, placing 19.4 miles of the river in the National Wild and Scenic River system and ruling out any dam.

## Through the Tunnel, Onto the Rock

One road into the gorge runs straight through a ridge. Nada Tunnel, a 900-foot logging tunnel, was cut around 1910 to 1911 with dynamite, steam drills and hand tools, and local legend holds that one worker died building it when frozen dynamite he had set near a fire to thaw exploded. The shortcut it gives motorists has earned it the name "Gateway to Red River Gorge." Beyond it, the gorge lies within the Daniel Boone National Forest as the Red River Gorge Geological Area, around 29,000 acres, with the 12,371-acre Clifty Wilderness (the Forest Service's figure) inside it. The upper gorge's stretch of river is also a designated Kentucky Wild River. Climbing here has its own written history, beginning with Frank T. Becker's Red River Gorge Climber's Guide in 1975; new routes kept appearing until Ray Ellington's guide had to split into two volumes, north and south. Backcountry campers must display a Daniel Boone National Forest recreation pass in their vehicle, and the Forest Service prohibits camping within 100 feet of the base of any cliff or the back of any rock shelter, and within 300 feet of any developed road or trail, except at designated campsites. The Gladie Visitor Center, on Sky Bridge Road in the heart of the gorge, offers interpretive displays on the gorge and the Red Wild and Scenic River, with a pioneer cabin and barn on its grounds.

## Flight Context

The Red River Gorge Geological Area centers near 37.834 N, 83.6079 W in east-central Kentucky, on the rugged, forested Pottsville Escarpment. From 3,500-5,000 ft AGL look for the winding Red River threading through deeply cut valleys lined with sandstone cliff bands; Natural Bridge State Park adjoins the geological area. Nearby airports: Stanton Airport (FAA identifier I50, no ICAO code listed) about 21 km west, Julian Carroll Airport in Jackson (KJKL) about 37 km southeast, Mount Sterling-Montgomery County Airport (KIOB) about 41 km northwest, and Blue Grass Airport in Lexington (KLEX) about 90 km west. Ridges and cliffs create turbulence on windy days, and morning fog often fills the gorge bottoms in cooler months.
