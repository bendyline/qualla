# Battle of Camp Wildcat

On October 21, 1861, Union troops dug in on the hills of Laurel County, Kentucky, turned back Felix Zollicoffer's Confederates at Camp Wildcat - one of the first Union victories of the Civil War.

**Location:** 37.2634, -84.1996

**Categories:** Battles of the American Civil War in Kentucky, Union victories of the American Civil War, 1861 in the American Civil War, National Register of Historic Places in Laurel County, Kentucky, Military history

## Introduction

Colonel Theophilus T. Garrard reported that he was outnumbered seven to one. In October 1861, he had been sent to the Rockcastle Hills near London, Kentucky, to set up a camp at the base of Wildcat Mountain and hold the ford on the Rockcastle River. His task was to obstruct the Wilderness Road against a Confederate army pushing north out of Tennessee. Help reached him just in time. On October 21, 1861, the Union force at Camp Wildcat turned back the attack in what is considered one of the first Union victories of the American Civil War.

## A Neutral State Tips

Kentucky had tried to stay out of the war. When President Abraham Lincoln called for volunteers on April 15, 1861, pro-Confederate Governor Beriah Magoffin refused to send troops. But most members of the Kentucky General Assembly were pro-Union, and the state did not secede. On May 20, following a legislative committee's recommendation, Magoffin proclaimed Kentucky neutral. Neutrality did not last. In elections on August 5, voters returned veto-proof pro-Union majorities to both houses, and a day later a Union post, Camp Dick Robinson, was established. On September 2, the General Assembly raised the U.S. flag over the State Capitol at Frankfort. The next day, Confederate Major General Leonidas Polk ordered Brigadier General Gideon Pillow to seize Columbus, Kentucky, on the Mississippi River. The border state had become a battleground.

## Zollicoffer Comes Through the Gap

In the east, Confederate Brigadier General Felix Zollicoffer pushed out of Tennessee with 5,400 men, aiming to gain control of central Kentucky. On September 14, 1861, ten days after leaving their base, his troops occupied the Cumberland Gap and took position at Cumberland Ford, near present-day Pineville. Five days later, about 800 of his men under Colonel Joel A. Battle struck Camp Andrew Johnson near Barbourville, where Unionists had been training recruits from Kentucky and east Tennessee. Most of the recruits had already left for Camp Dick Robinson. About 300 pro-Union Home Guards under Captain Isaac J. Black tore the planking off a bridge to slow the attackers, but the Confederates' greater numbers prevailed. The Union side lost one man killed, one wounded and 13 captured; seven Confederates died. On September 28, another Confederate detachment seized 200 barrels of salt and destroyed the Goose Creek Salt Works in Clay County.

## The Fight for Round Hill

Union Brigadier General George H. Thomas answered by sending Garrard to Camp Wildcat, 30 miles north of the salt works, and then reinforcing him with Brigadier General Albin F. Schoepf and what amounted to a brigade. That brought Union strength to about 7,000. Schoepf arrived on October 20, a day before Zollicoffer moved against the camp, and that evening Union pickets skirmished with the Confederates and returned to camp to report a large force nearby. Schoepf placed four companies of the 33rd Indiana Infantry, 350 men, on Round Hill, a steep high point along the road about three-quarters of a mile east of camp. Zollicoffer sent most of two regiments against them - the 11th Tennessee Infantry and several companies of the 17th Tennessee. After an hour of fighting the 11th Tennessee fell back, and later assaults on the South Rim, across the road from Round Hill, failed as well.

## Retreat and Remembrance

With casualties mounting to no effect, Zollicoffer called off the attack. His Confederates withdrew during the night and continued their retreat to Cumberland Ford, which they reached on October 26. The reported losses were small, but each number was a person, and the tallies differ by source. The American Battlefield Trust estimates 25 Union and 53 Confederate casualties, the commonly cited breakdown being five Union soldiers killed and 20 wounded, and 11 Confederates killed with 42 missing or wounded, while the Kentucky Historical Society lists four Union killed and 18 wounded. The Kentucky Historical Society calls it the first major engagement in Kentucky; by one count it was only the second clash of troops in the state, after Barbourville. Today the battlefield lies partly on land held by the Daniel Boone National Forest, partly on tracts acquired for preservation, and partly in private hands. Nearly 130 acres were listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1979, and another 440 acres were added in 2006. The American Battlefield Trust says it and its members have saved more than 264 acres of the ground where a Confederate advance was turned back in the Kentucky hills.

## Flight Context

The Camp Wildcat battlefield lies at approximately 37.2634 N, 84.1996 W in the forested hills of northern Laurel County, Kentucky, north of London near the Rockcastle River. From 3,500-4,500 ft MSL, look for the wooded ridges of Wildcat Mountain along the historic Wilderness Road corridor; much of the surrounding land is Daniel Boone National Forest. Nearby airports: London-Corbin Airport/Magee Field (KLOZ; about 12 nm south-southeast), Central Kentucky Regional Airport near Richmond (KRGA; about 23 nm north-northwest) and Lake Cumberland Regional Airport (KSME; about 24 nm west-southwest). Blue Grass Airport, Lexington (KLEX) is about 50 nm north-northwest. Morning fog is common in the valleys, and terrain rises sharply from the creek bottoms.
