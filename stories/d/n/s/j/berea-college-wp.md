# Berea College

Founded by an abolitionist in 1855, Berea became the South's first coeducational, racially integrated college, and today its students pay no tuition and work their way through school.

**Location:** 37.5724, -84.2919

**Categories:** Liberal arts colleges in Kentucky, Work colleges, 1855 establishments in Kentucky, History of civil rights in the United States, Education in Madison County, Kentucky, Appalachia

## Introduction

In 1866, the first full year after the Civil War, Berea College enrolled 187 students. Ninety-six were Black and 91 were white. Only seven years earlier, slavery supporters had driven the college's founder out of the area, which made that roll call a statement in itself. Berea became the first college in the Southern United States to be both coeducational and racially integrated, and its motto still states the conviction behind it, taken from Acts 17:26: "God has made of one blood all peoples of the earth."

## Of One Blood

The abolitionist John Gregg Fee founded Berea in 1855 on land granted to him by the politician and fellow abolitionist Cassius Marcellus Clay. The school's first articles of incorporation were adopted in 1859, but that same year slavery supporters forced Fee and the teachers out of the area. The school revived after the war, and by 1866 its classrooms were integrated. A. A. Burleigh became the first African American adult to attend and graduate from Berea, finishing in 1875. Julia Britton Hooks, one of the first African American women to attend college in Kentucky, studied at Berea and later taught music there, and Carter G. Woodson, the historian whose Negro History Week grew into Black History Month, is counted among its notable alumni. The college was founded by Protestant Christians and keeps a Christian identity independent of any one denomination.

## The Law That Split the Campus

Integration lasted nearly four decades. Then, in 1904, a new Kentucky law, the Day Law, forced the college to segregate. Berea fought it, challenging the law in state court and carrying the case to the U.S. Supreme Court as Berea College v. Kentucky. The challenge failed, and Berea had to become an all-white school. But the college raised money to establish the Lincoln Institute in Simpsonville, Kentucky, founded in 1912 to educate Black students. In 1950, when the Day Law was amended to allow integration at the college level, Berea resumed its integrated policies. Four years later, on February 4, 1954, Irvine Shanks took the court for Berea against Wilmington College and broke the color barrier in Kentucky college basketball. Today, approximately half of Berea's students identify as people of color.

## Tuition Paid in Labor

Berea charges its students no tuition. Pell Grants, other grants and scholarships go toward the cost, and federal work-study and work college programs cover the rest. Behind it all stands one of the largest per-student financial reserves of any American college, an endowment of more than $1.5 billion in recent years. In exchange, everyone works. Berea is one of nine federally recognized work colleges in the country, and every student puts in at least 10 hours a week on campus. They bus tables at the college-owned Boone Tavern Hotel, lead campus tours, tend grounds, work in information technology and make brooms, ceramics and weavings in Student Craft, work that has helped make the town a center for arts and crafts. In the 2025-26 school year, the college's handbook set hourly pay at $6.48 to $8.15, depending on the job level, alongside an annual work grant of $9,100 applied toward the cost of education. Most students come from Kentucky and the wider Appalachian region. In 2024, Washington Monthly ranked Berea first among national liberal arts colleges for its contribution to the public good.

## Mountain Day and Country Dances

Some traditions here run deep. Since 1875, Berea has celebrated Mountain Day, when classes stop and students make a sunrise hike to the top of the Pinnacle, then spend the day on games, performances and food. Labor Day, established in 1921, honors the value of student work. Folk dance is woven into campus life too. Physical education instructor Oscar Gunkler brought it to Berea in the 1920s, Frank Smith founded the Berea College Country Dancers in 1938, and the troupe danced at the White House in 1963. The college still holds community folk dances most weekends. In Hutchins Library, the Southern Appalachian Archives preserve records, oral histories and photographs of the region, and the writer bell hooks served at Berea as Distinguished Professor in Residence in Appalachian Studies.

## Flight Context

Berea College lies at approximately 37.57°N, 84.29°W in the town of Berea, Madison County, Kentucky. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL, look for the campus within the town; the Pinnacle, destination of the college's Mountain Day sunrise hike, is a local landmark. Central Kentucky Regional Airport (KRGA) at Richmond is about 4 nm north-northwest. Blue Grass Airport (KLEX) in Lexington is about 32 nm northwest, and London-Corbin/Magee Field (KLOZ) about 31 nm south. Expect haze in summer and valley fog on cool mornings.
