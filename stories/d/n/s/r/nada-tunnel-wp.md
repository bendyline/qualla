# Nada Tunnel

A single-lane, unlit logging tunnel blasted through solid sandstone in 1910–1911, now the backdoor into Kentucky's Red River Gorge.

**Location:** 37.8172, -83.6808

**Categories:** Buildings and structures in Powell County, Kentucky, Transportation infrastructure in Appalachia, Tunnels in Kentucky, 1911 establishments in Kentucky, Tunnels completed in 1911, Road tunnels in the United States, Transportation in Powell County, Kentucky

## Introduction

The first trainload of logs did not fit. The tunnel had been blasted through a sandstone ridge in eastern Kentucky, twelve feet wide and twelve feet high, and when the first loaded train rolled in, it jammed. Workers had to blast it free. The fix was to raise the ceiling a foot, to thirteen feet, and the timber began to flow. More than a century later, cars still squeeze through the same 900-foot bore one at a time, headlights on, heading for the canyons of the Red River Gorge.

## Two Crews, One Ridge

Nada Tunnel was built for the Dana Lumber Company between 1910 and 1911. Crews attacked the ridge from both sides, with teams working inward from each end, blasting solid sandstone with dynamite and digging it out with steam machinery and hand tools. The tunnel took its name from Nada, then a logging town about ten miles past its entrance, and locals still pronounce it "nay-duh." The work was dangerous. Local legend says one man was killed during construction when frozen dynamite he was thawing near a fire exploded, though no record confirming the death turned up. The tunnel that came out of all that effort was 900 feet long, a dark bore through the rock that would carry the region's forests out to market.

## Timber on Narrow Rails

Once the ceiling was raised, the narrow-gauge steam locomotives of the Big Woods, Red River & Lombard Railroad settled into their routine. They hauled timber cut from the vast forests of the Red River Valley through the tunnel and on to a sawmill fifteen miles away in Clay City. The trains ran until there was nothing left to haul. When the forests had been cleared, the timber companies pulled out. The rails were torn up and a dirt road was laid through the unlit tunnel for horses and people on foot. In time it was paved to carry a single lane of traffic, and today it is part of Kentucky Route 77, still without lights. With room for only one lane, drivers take turns at the portals.

## Gateway to the Gorge

The tunnel is often called the "Gateway to Red River Gorge" for the shortcut it gives motorists into the gorge's canyons in the Daniel Boone National Forest. What lies beyond is a landscape of high sandstone cliffs, rock shelters and waterfalls. The Red River Gorge Geological Area covers around 29,000 acres and holds more than 100 natural sandstone arches. It is a National Natural Landmark, and its overhanging, pocketed cliffs draw rock climbers from all over the world. In 1993 part of the Red River entered the National Wild and Scenic River system. People were here long before the loggers, too. Two prehistoric Native American rock art sites carry the tunnel's name, the Nada Tunnel 1 Petroglyphs and Nada Tunnel 2, both listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1992.

## The Worker Who Never Left

Nada has its ghost story, too. Local legend holds that the tunnel is haunted by a construction worker who died when a stick of dynamite exploded as he thawed it by a nearby fire. A Red River Historical Society volunteer, retelling the tale, presents the death as legend rather than documented fact. Whether you believe the story or not, the setting does its part. The bore is twelve feet wide, room for one car at a time, and inside, the only light is the light you bring. Drive it slowly. Somewhere in the middle, it is easy to understand how a place built with dynamite and sweat came to be remembered with a ghost story.

## Flight Context

Nada Tunnel sits at 37.817°N, 83.681°W on Kentucky Route 77 in Powell County, on the road into the Red River Gorge in the Daniel Boone National Forest. The tunnel itself is hidden in the ridge, but the gorge beyond is spectacular from the air: sandstone cliff lines, forested canyons and the winding Red River. Fly at least 2,000 feet above the ridges, around 3,500 to 4,500 feet MSL, and respect the national forest and wilderness areas below. Stanton Airport (FAA: I50) is about 8 nm west-northwest, Mount Sterling-Montgomery County Airport (KIOB) about 20 nm northwest, and Blue Grass Airport in Lexington (KLEX) about 46 nm west-northwest. Valley fog often fills the gorge on cool mornings and can make for a dramatic view once it starts to lift.
