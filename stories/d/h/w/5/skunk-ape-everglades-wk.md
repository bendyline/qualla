# Skunk Ape: The Everglades Bigfoot

Florida's contribution to North American cryptid folklore - a seven-foot, foul-smelling swamp ape whose legend, cryptozoology writers claim, is older than Bigfoot's.

**Location:** 25.8700, -81.0700

**Categories:** united-states, florida, folklore, cryptid, everglades, roadside-attraction

## Introduction

Somewhere southeast of Naples, the road runs out of dry land and the Tamiami Trail enters one of the strangest stretches of asphalt in Florida - some 80 miles across the Everglades with sawgrass to the horizon on both sides and not much else. Drive it after dusk and you will pass a roadside sign for the Skunk Ape Research Headquarters. Dave Shealy founded it. He grew up on this piece of road. He says he saw his first skunk ape when he was ten years old, in 1974, while deer hunting with his older brother, Jack, in the swamp behind the family home. He has been looking for the creature ever since. Whether the skunk ape is a leftover ape, a misidentified bear, or pure folklore depends on who is talking. What is not in dispute is that the swamps south of here are immense, almost roadless, and capable of swallowing a small aircraft without leaving a trace. If you wanted to hide something seven feet tall, the Everglades would be a generous place to try.

## Esti Capcaki

Cryptozoology writers like to say that long before any Floridian thought to compare a swamp ape to Bigfoot, the Seminole and Miccosukee were telling stories about something tall and hairy in the wet country. The name those writers give is Esti Capcaki, translated as roughly Tall Man, or Hairy Giant. The claim is hard to pin down. Researchers who have gone looking have found the phrase in cryptozoology books rather than in Seminole sources, and Seminole-language speakers asked about it did not recognize it. The tribes' stories are theirs to tell, and this one has not been shown to be among them. The earliest skunk-ape report that can be traced in the press dates from 1971. Whatever the skunk ape is or is not, it did not begin in 1974 with Dave Shealy. Where it did begin is part of the mystery.

## The Smell

The thing that distinguishes the skunk ape from its better-known cousins in Washington and British Columbia is the odor. Witnesses do not just describe a tall, hair-covered biped; they describe a stench. Rotten eggs. A skunk's spray. Swamp gas. Often the smell is reported before the sighting - a wave of it drifting through still air over a slough or a hammock of cypress, followed by branches breaking somewhere out of sight. Skeptics offer reasonable explanations. The Everglades produce hydrogen sulphide naturally as vegetation decomposes underwater. Methane bubbles up from peat. A startled wild hog or a feral pig wallow smells exactly the way witnesses describe. Believers reply that they know what a hog smells like, and this was something else.

## The 1997 Footage

The modern skunk-ape era took off in July 1997, when the Ochopee fire chief photographed a large, dark upright figure he had seen cross a road in Big Cypress - he never claimed to know what it was - and a run of sighting reports followed. The creature's most famous moment came at the end of 2000, when an anonymous letter writer in Sarasota County mailed photographs to the sheriff's office showing what she said was a large ape that had been taking apples from her back porch at night. The pictures - two grainy frames of something tall and hunched on the edge of palmetto scrub - became known as the Myakka skunk ape photographs and have been argued over ever since. The letter itself suggested an escaped orangutan. Skeptics suggested a costume. The photographer never came forward. The photographs are real; what is in them is not settled. The same can be said of most skunk-ape evidence. Casts of four-toed footprints, smears on game cameras, distant howls captured on cellphones - none of it amounts to a body, and the absence of a body is the thing that mainstream science keeps coming back to.

## Dave Shealy

The Skunk Ape Research Headquarters sits beside US-41 in Ochopee, a community whose other claim to fame is the smallest post office in the United States, a former irrigation-pipe shed of about 61 square feet. The Research Headquarters, run by the Shealy family, is part gift shop, part reptile zoo, and part field station. Dave Shealy is a real person doing what is, on his own terms, real work. He has baited and watched sites across Big Cypress for decades. He has cast footprints and collected eyewitness reports and given many interviews. The headquarters charges admission to its animal exhibit, sells T-shirts, and books pole-boat tours - nobody is pretending the operation does not have a tourist side. But Shealy's conviction is unmistakable. He grew up here. He knows the country. Whether or not he is right about what walks in it, he is the genuine article.

## The Country Itself

Big Cypress National Preserve covers 729,000 acres of cypress swamp, wet prairie, hardwood hammock, and pine flatwood, and it sits next to the 1.5 million acres of Everglades National Park immediately to the south. Most of it has no road, no trail, and no easy way in. Airboat tracks fade in a season. Helicopter overflights see nothing but a green plain. Florida panthers, almost extinct fifty years ago, slipped through the country so quietly that around 1970 they were feared gone; field surveys then found a remnant of twenty or thirty, and today, counted largely by trail cameras, they number an estimated 120 to 230 adults. The lesson cryptid enthusiasts draw from the panther story is that big animals can hide in this landscape for a long time. The lesson scientists draw is that when there are big animals, eventually the cameras find them. Decades of skunk-ape stories have produced no body, no bone, no hair sample that survived genetic analysis. The Everglades keep the secret, if there is one to keep.

## Flight Context

The skunk ape's reported range centres on Big Cypress National Preserve and the western Everglades, roughly between 25.90 N, 81.30 W (Ochopee) and the southern tip of mainland Florida. From altitude, the country is unmistakable: a vast pale-green sheet of sawgrass cut by darker bands of cypress strands and the dead-straight scar of the Tamiami Trail (US-41). Dade-Collier Training and Transition (TNT), a long, oddly isolated training runway about 22 nm east of Ochopee, was taken over for a state immigration detention center in 2025 and returned to Miami-Dade County in July 2026, with no date then set for reopening to pilots - check NOTAMs. Everglades Airpark (X01) at Everglades City and Marco Island (KMKY) are the closest small-aircraft options. Naples (KAPF) handles general aviation to the northwest, and Miami Executive (KTMB) and Miami International (KMIA) lie roughly 57 and 64 miles east of Ochopee. Afternoon thunderstorms develop almost daily in summer and can build to 50,000 feet within an hour; low-altitude flying over the preserve is best in early morning.
