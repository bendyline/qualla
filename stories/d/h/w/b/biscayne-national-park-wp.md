# Biscayne National Park

Ninety-five percent underwater, this national park south of Miami protects coral reefs, mangrove shorelines, and a chain of limestone keys that developers once tried to bulldoze into a rival Miami Beach.

**Location:** 25.4694, -80.1861

**Categories:** national-park, marine, coral-reef, conservation, florida-keys

## Introduction

In 1968, when developers realized their chain of tropical islands was about to become a national monument, they bulldozed a six-lane highway straight down the center of Elliott Key, destroying the forest for its entire length. They called it Elliott Key Boulevard publicly. Privately, they called it the Spite Highway. They hoped the devastation would make the land worthless for conservation. It did not. President Lyndon B. Johnson signed the Biscayne National Monument into existence that same year, and the subtropical forest grew back over the scar. Today, the only significant hiking trail on Elliott Key follows that old path of defiance - nature's quiet rebuttal to the idea that a bulldozer could settle the argument. Biscayne National Park is ninety-five percent water, a shimmering expanse of shallow bay, mangrove shoreline, limestone keys, and offshore coral reef stretching south of Miami in Miami-Dade County. It is a park you cannot drive to - not really. You drive to Convoy Point, and then you get in a boat.

## The Keepers of the Keys

The human history of Biscayne reads like a novel with too many characters to be plausible. The Tequesta people lived on these shores for millennia, eating fish and shellfish without farming. Juan Ponce de Leon arrived in 1513. Spanish treasure fleets wrecked on the reefs - 44 documented shipwrecks lie within the park, from the 16th through the 20th centuries. Pirates named Black Caesar - two different ones - used Elliott Key as a hideout in the 18th century, lending their name to Caesar's Creek. In 1897, Israel Lafayette Jones, an African-American property manager, bought Porgy Key for $300 and began growing limes and pineapples. His sons Lancelot and Arthur became fishing guides at the Cocolobo Cay Club on Adams Key, where their clients included future presidents Kennedy, Johnson, and Nixon. When the national monument was established, Lancelot Jones sold his family's land for a third of its development value. He lived on Porgy Key until Hurricane Andrew in 1992 forced him to Miami, where he died in 1997 at 99 years old.

## A Park That Almost Wasn't

The fight over Biscayne Bay pitted environmentalists against some of the most powerful forces in mid-century Florida. Billionaire shipping magnate Daniel K. Ludwig proposed SeaDade, an industrial seaport with an oil refinery on the bay's mainland shore, requiring a dredged shipping channel cut straight through the coral reef. Developers created the City of Islandia on Elliott Key in 1960, envisioning a causeway connecting the keys to the mainland and a community to rival Miami Beach. The CIA used Elliott Key as a training ground for Cuban exiles preparing for the Bay of Pigs invasion. Against this stood Lloyd Miller of the Izaak Walton League, Miami Herald reporter Juanita Greene, ecologist Art Marshall, Congressman Dante Fascell, and Governor Claude R. Kirk Jr. They formed the Safe Progress Association and argued that the reefs, islands, and bay were worth more alive than developed. They won. The Spite Highway was the losers' last gesture, and even that the forest eventually erased.

## Four Worlds in One

Biscayne contains four distinct ecosystems layered from shore to sea. The mainland edge is dominated by mangrove forest - red, black, and white mangroves whose tangled aerial roots create nurseries for fish, crustaceans, and molluscs. Biscayne's mangrove forest is the longest on Florida's east coast, with two to three times the carbon sequestration capacity of terrestrial forests. Beyond the mangroves lies the shallow lagoon of Biscayne Bay itself, averaging just a few feet deep, its floor covered by seagrass meadows that shelter manatees, juvenile fish, and hundreds of species. The limestone keys rise from the water - ancient coral reefs lifted above sea level, home to endangered Sargent's cherry palms and the semaphore prickly-pear cactus, reduced to roughly 20 individuals outside one colonial population of 570 discovered in 2001. Beyond the keys, the seafloor drops and rises again into a nearly continuous coral reef, harboring more than 200 species of fish and about 4,000 individual patch reefs.

## The Underwater Trail

Most national parks ask you to hike. Biscayne asks you to swim. The Maritime Heritage Trail is the only underwater archaeological trail in the National Park Service system, linking the wrecks of ships that broke apart on the same reefs that make the park spectacular. The Arratoon Apcar sank in 1878, the Erl King in 1891, the Alicia in 1905, the Lugano in 1913, and the Mandalay in 1966. Ranger-led snorkeling tours guide visitors over hulls colonized by coral and fish, where human ambition met geological reality. Above water, Boca Chita Key draws the most visitors to its ornamental lighthouse - built by industrialist Mark C. Honeywell in 1938 from coral rock, it looks like a lighthouse but never guided a ship. The Fowey Rocks Light, a genuine skeleton-frame cast iron structure from 1878, still stands offshore. And scattered across the keys are the ruins of plantations, fishing clubs, and homesteads, all slowly surrendering to hurricanes and time.

## The Vulnerable Edge

Every acre of land in Biscayne National Park sits just a few feet above sea level. Park Service studies project that much of this land will be lost over the coming centuries as seas rise. The Turkey Point Nuclear Generating Station sits on the park's southwestern boundary, its cooling canals threading along the edge of the preserved bay. Drainage canals built during the 20th century to control the Everglades have altered the bay's salinity, channeling agricultural runoff where rain and groundwater once flowed. Coral reef coverage has declined across the entire Florida Reef tract, taking fish species richness down with it. Seagrass beds scar from boat propellers, needing up to 15 years to recover, with 200 such incidents documented yearly. And yet Biscayne endures. Hurricanes have hammered these keys since long before humans arrived, and the natural systems are adapted to the battering. It is the human additions - the buildings, the docks, the altered water flows - that prove fragile. The park's greatest argument may be its simplest: some places work better when left alone.

## Flight Context

Located at 25.47°N, 80.19°W, Biscayne National Park stretches south of Miami as a vast expanse of turquoise and emerald water. From altitude, the park is strikingly visible - the shallow Biscayne Bay appears as a lighter band between the dark mainland mangroves and the chain of keys running north-south. Elliott Key, the largest island, is a thin dark green ribbon roughly 7 miles long. Look for the distinctive Stiltsville houses on pilings in the shallows near the north end of the park. The Turkey Point Nuclear Generating Station with its cooling canal grid marks the southwestern boundary. Homestead Air Reserve Base (KHST) is immediately west. Miami International Airport (KMIA) is roughly 25nm north. The offshore coral reef line is sometimes visible as a color change from deep blue to lighter water east of the keys. Best viewed at 3,000-5,000 feet AGL for the full mosaic of bay, keys, and reef. Conditions are frequently hazy in summer; winter offers the clearest visibility.
