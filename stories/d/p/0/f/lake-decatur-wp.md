# Lake Decatur

Built in the early 1920s to feed Decatur's thirsty corn mills, the reservoir of roughly 3,000 acres on the Sangamon River became central Illinois's playground for speedboats and anglers, even as prairie silt has steadily filled it in.

**Location:** 39.8167, -88.9333

**Categories:** Decatur, Illinois, Reservoirs in Illinois, Tourist attractions in Macon County, Illinois, Bodies of water of Macon County, Illinois

## Introduction

When A. E. Staley campaigned for Decatur to borrow $2 million and dam the Sangamon River, he was not being altruistic. Staley was a corn refiner, and corn wet-milling is a water-intensive industrial process. Decatur needed a dependable source of water, above all for its corn refining industry, and a reservoir would provide it. The city agreed, and between 1920 and 1922 Lake Decatur took shape east of downtown (the city says it was built in 1922 and dedicated in 1923): about 2,800 acres of water by the figure usually given, though the city now cites about 3,090, the largest lake on the Sangamon and, reportedly, the largest artificial lake in Illinois at the time it was built. What began as an industrial water tank soon became something more, the lake the whole region came to play on.

## Water for the Mills

The water still flows to the same purpose a century later. The corn wet-milling that Staley depended on continues in Decatur, carried on by Primient, which took over the North American corn-processing business of Tate & Lyle, Staley's successor, in 2022, and by its local competitor, Archer Daniels Midland. Lake Decatur water is also used to refine corn into ethanol. In 2007, about 76 percent of the water drawn from the lake went to commercial and industrial users. The reservoir sits 613 feet above sea level and gathers water from a watershed of 925 square miles, some 592,000 acres reaching into parts of seven counties. Every rain that falls on that vast swath of farmland eventually drains toward the dam, and toward the mills and taps of Decatur. Lake and city share the same namesake, the U.S. Navy commodore Stephen Decatur.

## Speedboats on the Prairie

With a 30-mile shoreline, the new lake quickly became a focus of recreation for all of mid-Illinois. The timing was right. The invention of the gasoline-powered speedboat had made sheltered reservoir waters attractive to Americans living far from any coast, and suddenly central Illinois had a large one of its own. The Decatur Park District was founded in 1924 specifically to take advantage of the opportunities the lake opened up. Today the shoreline borders two of Decatur's largest parks, Nelson Park and Faries Park, as well as Scovill Zoo. Anglers work the water for bass, crappie and walleye, and public boat ramps, listed at Nelson Park, Faries Park, the Sportsman's Club, and on Lost Bridge and Country Club Roads, put boats in the water. The city has scheduled the Nelson Park ramp to close at midnight on October 18, 2026 for an $800,000 rebuild with longer, wider launching lanes and new floating docks.

## The Slow Filling

The same prairie that made Lake Decatur possible is slowly taking it back. The lake was designed to sit atop relatively flat land, and it receives silt eroded from farm fields throughout the 925-square-mile upper Sangamon River drainage. From 1923 to 1983, the lake lost an estimated 35 percent of its designed storage volume. Dredging slowed that trend but did not stop it; by 2004 the lake's average depth was described as only eight feet. City officials weighed building a second reservoir for water storage, then decided against it. The city has since run a much bigger dredging program; its latest project, completed in 2019 according to the city (Wikipedia says 2018), removed about 11.1 million cubic yards of sediment and increased the lake's storage capacity by 30 percent, and the city now describes an average depth of 10 to 25 feet. For now, Lake Decatur remains both the city's workhorse and its playground, a shallow sheet of water doing a great deal of work.

## Crossing the Lake

Many visitors see the lake without ever setting foot on a dock. U.S. Highway 36 crosses it on a causeway bridge on the city's southeast side, and U.S. Route 51 spans it on the south side. In all, five crossings carry traffic over the water: Reas Bridge Road, William Street (Illinois 105), US 36 with Illinois 121, Lost Bridge Road, and US 51 with Illinois 105. Boaters need city registration, and the city's 2026 fee schedule lists a $15 day pass. The city's Lake Office on South Lake Front Road, which handles lake questions, lists hours of 6 a.m. to 10 p.m. daily from Memorial Day to Labor Day, with extended hours depending on staffing, and 7 a.m. to 3:30 p.m. Monday through Friday the rest of the year. Call 217-424-2837 to confirm before you go.

## Flight Context

Lake Decatur is centered near 39.8167 N, 88.9333 W at 613 feet above sea level, on the east and south sides of Decatur, Illinois. From 2,000-4,000 feet AGL, it is the dominant landmark around Decatur: a reservoir winding along the Sangamon River, crossed by five bridges including the U.S. 36 causeway on the southeast side and the U.S. 51 bridge on the south side. Nelson Park, Faries Park and Scovill Zoo line the shore. Decatur Airport (KDEC), which has a control tower, is only about 3 nautical miles east-northeast, so check the sectional chart and expect to work with the tower or approach control. University of Illinois Willard Airport (KCMI) lies about 33 nautical miles east-northeast and Abraham Lincoln Capital Airport (KSPI) at Springfield about 34 nautical miles west. Summer haze can wash out the shoreline; the water surface itself usually remains visible.
