# Decatur Airport

Owned by the Decatur Park District, this central Illinois airport pairs an 8,496-foot runway with a small commercial terminal, an Army National Guard aviation facility and a long fight to keep subsidized airline service.

**Location:** 39.8344, -88.8656

**Categories:** Airports in Illinois, Buildings and structures in Decatur, Illinois, Essential Air Service

## Introduction

Most park districts look after swimming pools, ball diamonds and picnic shelters. The Decatur Park District also owns an airport, with a main runway more than a mile and a half long. Decatur Airport spreads across 2,100 acres in Macon County, five miles east of the city. It is a place of contrasts: an airline terminal with baggage claim and rental car counters that ranks near the bottom of Illinois's commercial airports by traffic, an Army National Guard aviation facility, and a string of federal orders deciding who would fly its passengers to the wider world.

## Three Runways on the Prairie

The airport sits at an elevation of 682 feet, and its three runways are all 150 feet wide. Runway 6/24, the longest, stretches 8,496 feet in asphalt and concrete. Runway 12/30 runs 6,799 feet, also asphalt and concrete, and Runway 18/36 adds 5,298 feet of asphalt. Three runways pointing in three directions give pilots options when the prairie winds shift. The Federal Aviation Administration's National Plan of Integrated Airport Systems for 2021 to 2025 categorized Decatur as a regional general aviation facility, and among Illinois's twelve commercial airports it is the eleventh busiest. Its 24,000-square-foot passenger terminal holds airline counters, a restaurant, a baggage claim area and car rental desks, and public transit has reached the door too: the Decatur Public Transit System listed Route 12 from downtown Monday through Saturday and Route 3 on Sundays, though the city added its on-demand Decatur Moves service in September 2025, so check current options before counting on a bus.

## The Fight for a Flight

Decatur's airline service has long run through the federal Essential Air Service program, and the airport's paperwork trail reads like a roster of regional carriers. In 2006 the Department of Transportation selected RegionsAir, flying as American Connection, at a subsidy of $1,242,250 a year. In 2007 it chose Great Lakes Aviation. In 2009 the job went to Multi-Aero, doing business as Air Choice One, with a subsidy of $3,082,403 for Decatur, and in 2011 the department re-selected Air Choice One for another year. SkyWest, flying as United Express, took over the route from Cape Air in March 2020. The most dramatic moment came in March 2022, when SkyWest gave the Department of Transportation 90 days' notice that it intended to end its Essential Air Service at 29 communities, Decatur among them, citing a pilot shortage. The department ordered SkyWest to keep flying until a replacement could be found, and in 2023 it took new proposals from Southern Airways Express and Contour Airlines. A December 2024 federal order renewed SkyWest's jet service as United Express, according to local reporting, and United Express flights to Chicago–O'Hare are the airport's published link to the airline network; check the current schedule before planning to fly commercially.

## The Guard Next Door

Not every aircraft at Decatur carries paying passengers. The Illinois Army National Guard operates an Army Aviation Support Facility here, occupying 10 acres with three permanent buildings and a temporary one. Military aircraft have used the field for decades. On August 3, 1950, a United States Air Force Douglas C-47D was destroyed by fire after an accident on takeoff, yet all five people aboard survived both the crash and the fire. More than half a century later, on October 2, 2006, an Air Force Learjet C-21A was flying a simulated approach on a training mission at Decatur when its speed unexpectedly dropped. The jet rolled sharply to the right, struck the pavement and skidded across the runways. Both people aboard survived.

## A High-Speed Taxi

Not every story here ended with survivors. On December 2, 2010, a single-engine home-built aircraft, a Southerland Freebird Litesport Ultra, took off from Decatur Airport and crashed about a mile and a half east of the field. The pilot, the only person on board, was killed. The National Transportation Safety Board traced the crash to a flight that was never supposed to happen. It concluded that the probable cause was "the pilot's reported inability to control his amateur-built airplane for undetermined reasons after the airplane inadvertently became airborne while conducting a high-speed taxi." In other words, a run down the runway that was never meant to leave the ground did, and the pilot could not bring it back safely.

## Flight Context

Runways: 6/24, 8,496 x 150 ft asphalt/concrete; 12/30, 6,799 x 150 ft asphalt/concrete; 18/36, 5,298 x 150 ft asphalt. Expect Illinois Army National Guard aviation activity and occasional military training approaches alongside airline and general aviation traffic. The field has a control tower (AirNav lists Decatur Tower as part-time, 0600-2200 local), so check the sectional chart and expect to work with the tower or approach control. Visual references: the city of Decatur about 4 nautical miles west and Lake Decatur about 3 nautical miles west-southwest. Nearby airports: University of Illinois Willard Airport (KCMI), about 30 nautical miles east-northeast; Taylorville Municipal (KTAZ), about 28 nautical miles southwest; Abraham Lincoln Capital Airport (KSPI) at Springfield, about 38 nautical miles west. Central Illinois summers bring haze and afternoon thunderstorms; winter can bring low ceilings and icing.
