# Battle of Virden

On October 12, 1898, striking coal miners and armed company guards fought a gun battle, which accounts put at between ten and twenty minutes, around a train of Black strikebreakers and their families at Virden, Illinois, a clash the union commemorated for decades and one that helped drive Black miners out of Macoupin County.

## Introduction

The people on the train had no guns. When it stopped outside the mine stockade at Virden, Illinois, on October 12, 1898, the African American miners recruited from Birmingham, Alabama, along with their wives and children, could only drop beneath the seats as the shooting began. For somewhere between ten and twenty minutes, by differing accounts, bullets crossed the tracks between striking union miners and the company's armed guards, and the windows of all five cars were blown out. By the time the train pulled away, at least a dozen men had been killed and dozens more wounded, and a small coal town had written itself into the history of both American labor and American racism.

## A Strike Against the Holdouts

The conflict began as a contract fight. The Illinois District of the United Mine Workers of America had reached settlements with almost every coal operator, but the Chicago-Virden Coal Company and a few others broke away. Rather than meet the union's terms, they recruited African American miners from the South to work as strikebreakers. The company's first attempt to bring in strikebreakers, in late September, failed. For the second attempt, the company prepared for trouble: it had a stockade built around the mine and hired about fifty guards. Detectives from the Thiel Detective Service Company boarded the train at East St. Louis, armed with Winchester rifles and orders to protect the recruits and their families as the train carried them north toward the Virden mine. Counts of the recruits range from about 75 to just over 100.

## Gunfire at the Stockade

The train stopped on the Chicago & Alton tracks just outside the minehead stockade. The strikers, who were armed as well, tried to surround it, and firing broke out from all sides. Inside the five cars, the strikebreakers and their families had nothing to fight with, so they sheltered under the seats while glass exploded around them. The engineer was shot in the arm. After the shooting, which accounts put at ten to twenty minutes, he pulled the train away from the minehead, keeping the passengers aboard, and ran on to Springfield. Eight union miners had been killed and about 30 wounded. Accounts of the guards' losses differ, with four or five killed and five wounded. Black strikebreakers on the train were wounded too, at least one by the record, but not every casualty among them was ever written down.

## Abandoned in Springfield

At Springfield the dead and injured were taken off the train. Union officials promised the Alabama families care and transportation and persuaded them to come to the union hall. The next day, October 13, the union abruptly announced it would stop protecting or caring for them after six o'clock that evening. Two Black miners who tried to run from the union hall to the train station were caught by white miners and badly beaten. A mob gathered and threatened to lynch the strikebreakers until Springfield's mayor, Loren Wheeler, calmed the crowd and arranged to put the Birmingham miners on the next train to St. Louis. There they were abandoned, without money, food or warm clothing, hundreds of miles from the homes they had left for the promise of work.

## Gatling Guns and a Sundown Town

Governor John Riley Tanner responded by ordering the Illinois National Guard to keep more strikebreakers from arriving and warned that if another rail car carried them into the state, he would "shoot it to pieces with Gatling guns." The Guard captain at Pana promised to order his men to fire if Black miners brought there refused orders to retreat. The violence did not end at Virden. At Pana on April 10, 1899, seven people died in a gunfight between union strikers and Black strikebreakers, five of them Black, including a miner's wife, and two white men, one of them a bystander; at Lauder that June, strikers fired on a train carrying strikebreakers, killing a woman (named in one account as Anna Karr, a strikebreaker's wife) and wounding about twenty others; and at Carterville in September, white miners killed five African American residents of a nearby strikebreakers' camp. Less than a month after Virden, a mob of about a hundred miners took F. W. Stewart, a Black man from Toluca, from the jail at Lacon and hanged him. He had been accused of assaulting a miner's daughter, an accusation never tested in court, and he had stayed in Toluca after many Black residents left amid the racial tension that followed the strike. Virden itself gained a reputation as a sundown town. Most Black miners were driven out of Macoupin County, and by one account only one African American was left among its 3,123 miners by 1908.

## Whose Martyrs?

For the union, Virden became a founding memory. Four of the miners killed that day came from Mount Olive, and when the owner of that town's private cemetery refused to have them buried there, the local union bought land. In September 1899 the United Mine Workers established the Union Miners Cemetery on a single acre, described as the only union-owned cemetery in the United States. Its tall granite monument memorializes Mother Jones, whose grave lies at its base, and plaques there honor miners killed at Virden and in other labor struggles. Yet the traditional commemorations of the Battle of Virden have been questioned, and the issue of reparations has been raised. The strikers fought for their union and paid with their lives. The families on the train paid too, with their safety and, in the years that followed, with any place for Black miners in Macoupin County's coal country. Both truths stand on the same stretch of track.

## Flight Context

Virden lies at about 39.501°N, 89.767°W in Macoupin County on the flat farmland of central Illinois, roughly 20 nm south-southwest of Springfield. From 2,000-3,000 feet AGL, look for the small town grid along the north-south rail corridor toward Springfield, surrounded by open fields. Abraham Lincoln Capital Airport (KSPI) at Springfield is the nearest major field, about 21 nm to the north, and St. Louis Regional Airport (KALN) at Alton lies about 39 nm to the south-southwest. Expect good visibility over the prairie on clear days, with summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms.
