# Benjaminville Friends Meeting House and Burial Ground

A plain 1874 Quaker meeting house and its carefully divided burial ground are all that remain of Benjaminville, once called one of the strongest Friends settlements in Illinois.

**Location:** 40.4765, -88.8061

## Introduction

Inside the meeting house, the Friends of Benjaminville did something said to be unusual even for their faith: they left the partition open, so that men and women could worship together in the same room. Step outside, though, and the rules change. The burial ground behind the building is strictly divided, not by sex but by belief, with one section for Quakers, one for non-Quakers, and a newer one for descendants of both. Together, the plain building and its orderly rows of graves are all that remain of a vanished village on the McLean County prairie.

## A Village of Friends

Quakers settled this stretch of prairie east of Bloomington, and in 1856 the village of Benjaminville was founded. More Friends followed, and a first meeting house went up on the site in 1859. Quakers kept arriving through the 1860s. In 1874 they raised the meeting house that stands today. For several years afterward, the building was the focus of the whole settlement. By 1879 one author described Benjaminville as "one of the strongest settlements of Friends that is to be found anywhere in the state." The land they chose sits on a relatively elevated rise near the community of Holder, and when they arrived it was flat, treeless prairie.

## Plain Walls, One Room

The Society of Friends built its meeting houses to a pattern that stayed virtually unchanged from the colonial American period through the nineteenth century, and Benjaminville is a well-preserved example. Its design contains many of the major elements associated with the style, and the building has seen little change since it went up in 1874. That continuity is the point. Standing here on a rise of central Illinois farmland, you are looking at a design that colonial-era Friends would have recognized. One feature reportedly set it apart from the pattern: an open partition in its single worship room, where men and women could gather together instead of meeting behind movable shutters.

## Sorted by Belief

The burial ground covers most of the site, a plot of roughly 32,000 square feet planted with grass and trees, and every grave is oriented east to west. Its geography tells a story of faith and family. Non-Quaker burials were originally confined to the northern section, directly behind the meeting house. Members of the Society of Friends were laid to rest in the middle portion, today encircled by a loop of the gravel road that runs through the site. The most recent burials lie in the southernmost section, farthest from the building, and there the old divisions soften: the newer ground holds a mix of Quaker and non-Quaker descendants of the people buried nearby. The founders drew firm lines, but in the end their descendants share the same ground.

## The Last Building Standing

Benjaminville itself is gone. The meeting house is the last surviving structure of the village, and the burial ground and building together are all that mark where it stood. The land around it, once open prairie, is now mostly farmland, with a few residences nearby. To the east, the prairie has taken on a newer landmark: the Twin Groves Wind Farm, whose turbines spread across Arrowsmith, Cheney's Grove and Dawson townships in eastern McLean County. On December 13, 1983, the Benjaminville Friends Meeting House and Burial Ground was listed on the National Register of Historic Places, recognized for both its history and its architecture.

## Flight Context

The meeting house stands at 40.4765°N, 88.8061°W, north of the rural community of Holder and east of Bloomington in McLean County. It is a single building beside its tree-planted burial ground and a gravel loop road, so descend to around 1,500 to 2,000 feet AGL for a look, and pick out the cemetery's trees set against open farm fields. Central Illinois Regional Airport (KBMI) at Bloomington-Normal is only about 5 nautical miles to the west, and KBMI has a control tower, so check the sectional chart and expect to work with the tower or approach control before orbiting.
