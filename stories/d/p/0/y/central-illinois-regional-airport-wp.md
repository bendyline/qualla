# Central Illinois Regional Airport

Bloomington–Normal's airport began in 1928 as a farmer's sod field where an escape artist slipped a straitjacket in flight, moved to a New Deal site on Route 9 in 1934, and now flies nonstop to Atlanta, Dallas/Fort Worth and Florida.

**Location:** 40.4772, -88.9158

**Categories:** Airports in Illinois, Transportation buildings and structures in McLean County, Illinois

## Introduction

On May 30, 1928, some 10,000 people gathered at a farm field in Normal Township to watch the sky. Local and visiting pilots staged what The Pantagraph called "stunts and jumps and aerial tricks," and Nathan B. Winslow, an escape artist raised in Bloomington, freed himself from a straitjacket while in flight. The crowd had come for the dedication of the community's first airfield. Nothing of that field survives today. Its successor, Central Illinois Regional Airport, sits three miles east of Bloomington on land chosen during the Great Depression, and from there flights leave nonstop for Atlanta, Dallas/Fort Worth and Florida.

## A Stone Circle for a Signpost

The field belonged to Herman Will, a farmer who in 1927 opened a tract of 70-plus acres in rural Normal Township. The newly paved Illinois 2, today U.S. 51, bordered it on the west and the Illinois Central Railroad, now the Constitution Trail, on the east. The Bloomington Flying Club, which rallied the public behind "heavier-than-air" flight, owned a "Jenny"-type open-cockpit biplane and evidently helped pay for a hangar and the field's daily operations. Will offered to rent the field to Bloomington for $1,000 a year, but there was little enthusiasm for a municipal airport. By 1928 the field had a six-plane hangar, a fuel pump, a wind cone and, so pilots could pick it out from above, a circle of crushed stone 100 feet across. A few weeks after the dedication, the U.S. Department of Commerce added it to its list of officially recognized airports. On July 11, 1929, it hosted the Central Illinois Air Derby, billed at the time as the greatest such event in Illinois outside Chicago.

## Too Soft for a Tri-Motor

Scheduled passengers arrived next, though not for long. On October 1, 1931, Century Air Lines inaugurated service, adding Bloomington to its Chicago-to-St. Louis route with ten-passenger Stinson tri-motors. Then came the wet winter of 1931-1932, which left the sod field too mushy for the heavy airplanes. American Airways took over the following spring and ended service after just two days, May 1 and 2. The field was too small, and its turf too soggy, for passenger aircraft. Today a historic marker near the north end of the Constitution Trail, on the trail's west side beside a Rivian Automotive warehouse, is all that marks the site of Herman Will's field.

## A New Deal on Route 9

Opposition to a city-run airport weakened once federal dollars were on offer through the Civil Works Administration, one of Franklin D. Roosevelt's New Deal programs. In mid-December 1933, at the height of the Great Depression, officials settled on 164 acres east of Bloomington along Illinois 9, land that had once been the Corn Belt Nursery. The CWA contributed $90,000. Bloomington Municipal Airport opened with two asphalt runways, each 75 by 1,500 feet, laid out diagonally, and its dedication on October 28, 1934 was one of the biggest events in the city's history up to that time. The Pantagraph's coverage boasted a crowd of 60,000, flights by 83 planes and an appearance by Illinois Governor Henry Horner. A permanent passenger terminal followed in 1964. Its Arnie's Restaurant & Lounge was a favorite for brunch and dinner and a draw for pilots flying in, while carriers from Ozark and Britt to Midwest Express and Frontier came and went.

## Nine Gates and a Museum

As Bloomington-Normal kept growing, a $14 million terminal opened on November 5, 2001, three times larger than its predecessor, with nine gates, six of them fitted with jetways. The airport now covers 1,968 acres, twelve times the original Route 9 site, and its longest runway, 2/20, stretches 8,000 feet, more than five times the length of the 1934 strips. Federal records counted 262,846 passenger boardings in 2008 and 164,672 in 2023, when it ranked 198th in the nation. The 2013 federal sequester would have closed its control tower had the FAA not reversed course. Today Delta Connection flies to Atlanta, recently the busiest route, American Eagle serves Chicago O'Hare and Dallas/Fort Worth, and Allegiant flies to Orlando/Sanford and St. Petersburg/Clearwater, with seasonal service to Phoenix/Mesa. FedEx Express carries freight to Memphis. On the west side of the field, the Prairie Aviation Museum displays former U.S. Air Force and Navy aircraft.

## Two Miles From the Runway

Not every flight home has ended on the runway. After midnight on April 7, 2015, a twin-engine Cessna 414 returning from the NCAA basketball championship game in Indianapolis came down in a soybean field about 2 miles east-northeast of the Runway 20 threshold during an instrument approach in low visibility. All seven people aboard died: the pilot, Thomas Hileman; Torrey Ward, Illinois State University's associate head basketball coach; Aaron Leetch, a deputy director in its athletic department; business owner Scott Bittner; sports bar owner Terry Stralow; Jason Jones, a former Illinois Wesleyan basketball and baseball player; and Andrew Butler, who worked for Sprint. Investigators found that a poorly secured antenna cable had left the pilot without glideslope guidance, that the pilot likely had been awake for 18 hours, and that the airplane was loaded aft of its balance limit. The probable cause was the pilot's failure to maintain control in night instrument conditions, which led to an aerodynamic stall and spin. Eleven years earlier, on the night of March 21, 2004, a Bloomington flying club's Piper carrying six central Illinois residents home from a NASCAR race in Darlington, South Carolina, vanished from radar; searchers found the wreckage on the side of Little Black Mountain in Kentucky, and all six aboard had died.

## Flight Context

Central Illinois Regional Airport (KBMI/BMI) lies at 40.4771°N, 88.9159°W, 3 miles east of downtown Bloomington, at a surveyed field elevation of 871 feet. Runway 2/20 is 8,000 x 150 feet of grooved concrete and Runway 11/29 is 6,525 x 150 feet of grooved asphalt and concrete. The tower is part-time (0600-2200) on 124.6, which also serves as the CTAF when the tower is closed; ATIS is on 119.575. Illinois Route 9 runs east from the field toward the Twin Groves Wind Farm, whose 240 turbines are centered about 9.5 nm east (near 40.48°N, 88.71°W) and make a prominent visual reference; treat them as tall obstructions. For a sightseeing pass over Bloomington-Normal, 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL works well; check the sectional chart and expect to work with the tower or approach control. Nearby alternates include Logan County Airport (KAAA), 27 nm southwest, Pontiac Municipal Airport (KPNT), 30 nm northeast, Pekin Municipal Airport (C15), 35 nm west, and Rantoul National Aviation Center (KTIP), 37 nm east. Night approaches in low ceilings and reduced visibility demand care.
