# Towanda, Illinois

Platted by Jesse Fell in 1854 with its main street facing the railroad, Towanda survived fires, a hatchet-wielding temperance raid and the rerouting of Route 66, then turned a stretch of the abandoned highway into a parkway.

**Location:** 40.5642, -88.8989

**Categories:** Villages in McLean County, Illinois, Populated places established in 1875, 1875 establishments in Illinois, Villages in Illinois

## Introduction

The women of Towanda had seen enough. Fed up with how much money their menfolk were spending on whiskey, a group of them gathered at the local hardware store, where they were handed hatchets. Then, in the 1860s, they marched into the Buena Vista Tavern, smashed the bottles and furniture and hurled bottled goods through a window while the barman looked on. Local history records two different outcomes: a fine of one dollar each, or no conviction at all. It may be the most exciting event in the history of this small McLean County village, home to 431 people at the 2020 census, but it is far from the only story Towanda has to tell.

## A Town the Railroad Couldn't Found

The land under Towanda was first entered in 1853 by Peter A. Badeau, a civil engineer who had attended Wesleyan University and worked on the Alton and Springfield Railroad. One account holds that Illinois law at the time barred railroads and their officials from founding new towns. Whatever the reason, Badeau donated the original forty acres, and Towanda was laid out by two McLean County real estate developers, Jesse W. Fell and Charles W. Holder, who filed its plat on December 7, 1854. Fell, a native of Chester County, Pennsylvania, helped found Clinton, Dwight, Normal and Pontiac, and was the driving force behind the school that became Illinois State University. He also knew how to bend a railroad to his will. Local histories say that at least twice he altered its path, adding a curve so it would pass through Normal and nudging the survey so it ran through Pontiac instead of rival Richmond.

## Main Street Faces the Tracks

Central Illinois towns of the 1830s, like Bloomington and Lexington, were usually built around a central square. Towns of the 1850s, Towanda among them, were built around the railroad. Towanda's main street ran diagonally, parallel to the tracks, with building lots on only one side, so that the storefronts looked across the street at the trains. The same design appears, with small variations, in Dwight, Gardner, Odell and McLean along the old Chicago and Alton line. It had a side effect: odd triangles of land where the diagonal street met the town's north-south and east-west grid. Fell gave Towanda two of these triangles, one on each side of the tracks. The original deed called them "plaza," and both have served the town as parks ever since.

## Fences, Hogs and a Missing Jail

Photographs from the 1870s show a town crisscrossed with fences that looks rather like the set of a western movie. Cattle and hogs were driven into town at night and penned before shipping by rail, and houses were fenced to keep stray livestock out of the gardens. Most homes were wooden I-cottages, single-story or story-and-a-half frame houses with symmetrical fronts. Along the tracks stood long rail corn cribs and a simple board-and-batten station, and the most striking structure of all was a stone tank house topped with a windmill. Wooden sidewalks went down in 1873 and 1874, brick in 1891 and concrete in 1916. The first jail, built in the North Park, was evidently not much of a building: in 1901 the town fathers had to appoint a committee to find out what had happened to it.

## Fire, Freight and the Mother Road

Towanda has been plagued by fire. A large part of downtown burned in 1905, and the original railroad station went up in flames in 1917. Rail traffic was never heavy; in 1887 just one freight train stopped each way, and passenger service had ended by the 1940s. The road took over. The route beside the tracks became State Bond Route 4 in the 1910s, U.S. Route 66 from 1926 into the early 1970s, and then Interstate 55. In 1955, Route 66 was widened to four lanes here, with a grass median between the northbound and southbound lanes. When I-55 construction bent the highway around the northwest side of town in 1977, part of the old southbound roadway was closed to cars and abandoned.

## Parkway and Parade

Towanda found a new use for the abandoned pavement. Beginning in 1998, a student and community project turned a stretch of old Route 66 into the Towanda Historic Route 66 Parkway, which also saved the Money Creek Bridge north of town after a 2001 plan to demolish it. Along the way, an outdoor exhibit called "Historic Route 66: A Geographic Journey" traces the highway through all eight of its states, alongside picnic areas, birdhouses, a three-flagpole garden, and a pond at the south end with parking for catfish and bass fishing. Each July 3 the village holds a spaghetti supper and a street dance, and its July 4 parade draws crowds from Bloomington, Normal, Hudson and Lexington, with a flea market filling both parks. The best-known person from Towanda was Bonnie Lou (1924 to 2015), born Mary Joan Kath, whose rock and roll and country hits, including "Seven Lonely Days" and "Tennessee Wig Walk," made her a star in the 1950s, and who became a popular television host and performer in Cincinnati.

## Flight Context

Towanda sits at 40.5642°N, 88.8989°W in central McLean County, about 5 nautical miles northeast of Normal and 6.5 nautical miles northeast of Bloomington. Interstate 55 crosses the village's northwest corner at Exit 171, and the old Route 66 alignment runs alongside, so the I-55 and Route 66 pair makes an easy line to follow northeast from Bloomington-Normal. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL, look for the town's diagonal main street running parallel to the railroad, the grain silo complex, and the Route 66 parkway stretching northeast from the small pond at the south tip of town (near 40.5577°N, 88.9089°W). Central Illinois Regional Airport (KBMI) is about 5 nautical miles to the south and has a control tower (staffed 0600 to 2200 local), so check the sectional chart and expect to work with the tower or approach control near it. Terrain is flat and open, with good visibility outside summer haze and winter overcast.
