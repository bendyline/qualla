# Colfax, Illinois

Named for Ulysses S. Grant's vice president, Colfax began as the end of a railroad line in 1880, dug nearly 400 feet down for coal, by one local count lost eleven miners to the work, and settled into life as a grain town that is now restoring its own past.

**Location:** 40.5664, -88.6153

**Categories:** Villages in McLean County, Illinois, Populated places established in 1880, 1880 establishments in Illinois, Schuyler Colfax, Villages in Illinois

## Introduction

On July 4, 1880, the first train rolled into Colfax, and then it had nowhere else to go. For two years the new railroad ran only from Kankakee to this brand-new town in eastern McLean County, so a turntable was installed to spin the locomotives around and send them back east. Colfax had been laid out that March by James E. Wood, one of a string of towns planted along a line that local farmers had helped pay for with cash and right-of-way. The northeastern townships of the county were among the last to get a railroad. When it finally came, Colfax was, for a while, the end of the line.

## Uncle Billy and the Vice President

The town took its name from Schuyler Colfax, Speaker of the U.S. House of Representatives during the Lincoln and Andrew Johnson administrations and later vice president under Ulysses S. Grant. It shares the honor with several other Colfaxes, including those in California, Indiana, Louisiana, North Carolina and Washington. Its most devoted booster, though, was a local man. William H. Anderson, born in Indiana, had come to McLean County around 1855 as State Missionary for the Christian Church. He stayed, and became a farmer, a banker and a lifelong promoter of Colfax. Anderson also raised Standard Bred and Road Bred horses and kept a half-mile horse track just east of town. To the people who knew him, he was simply "Uncle Billy."

## A Hundred Lots Facing the Tracks

The original town was a tidy rectangle on the north side of the railroad: twelve blocks of eight or sixteen small lots each, plus four blocks along the western edge that each held a single larger lot, for a hundred lots in all. They faced a widened strip of railroad land running the full length of the town. No park or square was set aside, but Main Street, one block north of the tracks and parallel to them, was built wider than the rest. The depot stood on the north side, with the grain elevator, lumber yard and stock yard across the tracks to the south. William Rinker opened the first store, selling groceries. Wilson and Santee followed, and then D.A. Green, who moved his business in from Potosi, near the Livingston County line, and built a two-story store selling groceries, dry goods and drugs.

## Four Hundred Feet Down

In 1886 a company was formed to open a coal mine just west of town, and it quickly learned how hard the ground could be. Frank Seymour and Sons, paid fourteen dollars a foot to sink the shaft, fought water and cave-ins and then had to blast through layers of limestone. The railroad could not bring in enough shoring, so extra timber came by wagon. At 140 feet the diggers struck a narrow vein of coal, but they had to go all the way to 392 feet to find a seam worth working. The first coal came up in June 1888. A second shaft, east of town, was even worse: organized in December 1891, it did not start digging until June 1894, and it battled water and layers of quicksand before hitting coal in 1895. Financing troubles then delayed production for two more years.

## The Price of Coal

When the east mine opened, some 200 miners flooded into Colfax and found almost nowhere to live. The shortage eased only after a large number of small miners' cottages went up, and a few of them may still be found in town. Unions were organized, and strikes followed. In 1901 and 1902 the mines were producing almost 60,000 tons of coal, yet the work was deadly: one local history counts eleven miners who died between 1889 and 1902, most of them crushed by falling rock. Money troubles dogged the operation from start to finish. The west shaft, which produced most of the coal, closed in 1904. It sat idle for years, reopened briefly as a cooperative from 1921 to 1924, and finally became a rubbish dump before it was filled in.

## Good Ole Days

Colfax grew quickly in its first decades. A tile factory went up in the northeast corner of town in 1892, and, as often happened in central Illinois, its grounds eventually became a small park. There was a cigar factory, too, and a little plant that made tin cans. Once the mines closed, Colfax settled into a quieter role as the commercial hub of a prosperous grain-growing district. Its population peaked in 1900 and has barely changed since 1910; the 2020 census counted 996 residents. Lately the village has turned to its own history. The Colfax Restoration Project, formed in June 2017, says it has raised more than $100,000 for projects that include turning the vacant Old Town Hall into a boutique shop, creating Colfax Plaza with murals and historical signs on a former empty lot, and planning a Main Street pavilion for concerts and farmers markets. Its website also promotes the annual Good Ole Days Festival, which in 2026 filled a weekend with a parade, baseball, live music and carnival rides.

## Flight Context

Colfax sits at 40.5664°N, 88.6153°W in eastern McLean County. Illinois Route 165 runs along the village's northern edge, leading west-southwest toward Bloomington, about 18 nautical miles away, and east toward Sibley, about 11 nautical miles away, and makes a good line to follow in. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL, look for the compact grid of about half a square mile set in flat grain country; the smaller village of Cooksville lies roughly 5 nautical miles to the west-southwest along the same corridor. Central Illinois Regional Airport (KBMI) at Bloomington-Normal is about 15 nautical miles to the west-southwest and has a control tower, so check the sectional chart and expect to work with the tower or approach control if you fly that way. Pontiac Municipal Airport (KPNT) is about 21 nautical miles to the north. Terrain is flat and open, with visibility typically limited only by summer haze or winter overcast.
