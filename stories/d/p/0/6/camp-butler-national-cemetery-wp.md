# Camp Butler: From Training Camp to National Cemetery

By one account, a carriage ride in 1861 chose the site of an Illinois training camp, and the ground now holds Union and Confederate dead side by side in a national cemetery.

**Location:** 39.8338, -89.5575

**Categories:** American Civil War army posts, American Civil War prison camps, Cemeteries on the National Register of Historic Places in Illinois, Defunct prisons in Illinois, Forts in Illinois, Historic American Landscapes Survey in Illinois, Illinois in the American Civil War, National Register of Historic Places in Sangamon County, Illinois, Protected areas of Sangamon County, Illinois, United States national cemeteries

## Introduction

In 1861, after President Lincoln called for troops in April, Illinois needed a place to train them. Governor Richard Yates turned to State Treasurer William Butler, and by one long-told account Butler and Secretary of State Ozias M. Hatch took a carriage ride with General William T. Sherman, about five and a half miles northeast of downtown Springfield, to look over the land. The Department of Veterans Affairs' history says the War Department dispatched Sherman to Springfield to select the site, but local historian Mark Flotow cautions that Sherman did not visit Springfield until September 1861, after the camp had already been operating under Butler's name for more than a month, and that the exact reason it was named for Butler is unknown. The site was near Riverton, a community then known as Jimtown, short for Jamestown. Today part of that ground is Camp Butler National Cemetery, administered by the Department of Veterans Affairs, where 19,825 interments had been counted by the end of 2005.

## More Than Two Hundred Thousand

A Union training facility was officially established there on August 2, 1861, and it filled fast. The Department of Veterans Affairs' history of the site counts 5,000 men in camp by the end of that first month. Camp Butler was meant to train Illinois troops and muster them into service, and by the war's end nearly 200,000 Union soldiers had passed through it, according to the state's historical marker. Only one training camp in the state was larger: Camp Douglas in Chicago.

For its first months, then, the camp did the ordinary work of an army filling its ranks. In February 1862, that changed.

## The Prisoners of Fort Donelson

On February 16, 1862, Fort Donelson in Tennessee surrendered, and approximately 2,000 Confederate soldiers were taken prisoner. Camp Butler, built to train Illinois volunteers, was quickly pressed into service to house them.

Smallpox and other diseases were rampant in the camp in 1862, and the poor living conditions the prisoners endured made the situation worse. The Department of Veterans Affairs says roughly 700 prisoners died in the smallpox epidemic of that summer, and the Sangamon County Historical Society, citing a state marker, counts more than 800 Confederate dead buried at the camp in all. They were buried in the cemetery, in a Confederate section of their own. Behind those figures are individual soldiers who died of disease while held as prisoners in Illinois.

## Pointed Headstones

Today the national cemetery holds 866 graves of Confederate prisoners, and they are easy to pick out: the headstones are pointed. Local lore holds that the shape was meant to keep the devil from sitting on the graves. Beside them, side by side, lie 776 graves of Union soldiers and enlistees, which brings the total to 1,642 Civil War graves.

Men who wore opposite uniforms share the same ground, and what most plainly sets their graves apart is the shape of the stone. The VA's history adds that a Confederate monument was erected in December 2005 by the United Daughters of the Confederacy and Sons of Confederate Veterans.

## Every War After

The Civil War did not end the cemetery's work. Veterans who lost their lives in the Spanish–American War, both World War I and World War II, the Korean War and the Vietnam War are buried at Camp Butler too, along with about three dozen enemy prisoners of war from World War II, mostly Germans plus some Italians and a Korean soldier, who died in U.S. camps and whose graves were moved here when older military cemeteries closed. According to the VA, it also became the final resting place of many remains returned from overseas after World War II.

A few of the names stand out. Seaman John H. Catherwood (1888–1930) received the Medal of Honor for action in the Philippines in 1911. Lieutenant Colonel Otis B. Duncan (1873–1937) was the American Expeditionary Forces' highest-ranking African American officer at the end of World War I, according to the VA. Two U.S. Representatives, Frank S. Dickson (1876–1953) and Jack D. Davis (1935–2018), are buried here as well.

The cemetery lies near Riverton, about six miles northeast of Springfield, in Sangamon County, and it was placed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1997. By one account, a carriage ride in 1861 chose a place to train soldiers. The wars that followed, and the soldiers who died in them, decided what the place would become.

## Flight Context

Camp Butler National Cemetery sits at 39.83°N, 89.56°W, near Riverton and about six miles northeast of Springfield in Sangamon County, Illinois. Fly low and slow (roughly 1,500 to 2,500 feet above ground) for the best look at the grounds in clear daytime weather. Abraham Lincoln Capital Airport (KSPI) is about 5.5 nautical miles to the west, so check the sectional chart and expect to work with the tower or approach control.
