# First Presbyterian Church (Springfield, Illinois)

The Springfield congregation where the Lincolns paid $36 for a pew now glows with seven Tiffany windows, a 1940s window showing a young Abraham Lincoln, and a hand-built organ shaped around a stained-glass dove.

**Location:** 39.7985, -89.6468

## Introduction

The price was $36, by the Papers of Abraham Lincoln's count; another source says $50. For that sum, Abraham and Mary Lincoln held a pew at Springfield's First Presbyterian Church, and they sat in it regularly through the 1850s. The pew outlasted the building it stood in: saved from the wrecking ball, it was moved in 1912 to the congregation's current sanctuary at Seventh Street and Capitol Avenue, a building Lincoln himself never visited. Around it now glow seven windows from Louis Comfort Tiffany's studios, one of them a panel that first shone at a world's fair in Chicago.

## A Grieving Family Finds a Pew

The congregation is almost as old as Illinois. It was founded in 1828, ten years after statehood, and its first building stood on the block between Third and Fourth Streets and Washington and Monroe. The Lincolns' ties to it began with loss. In 1850 their second son, Edward, died, and the family asked the church's minister, Rev. James Smith, to conduct the funeral. His service made a deep impression on both grieving parents. From then until Lincoln left for Washington in 1861, the family kept their pew and attended regularly. Mary Todd Lincoln became a member in 1852. Her husband never formally joined, though he kept coming, a small reminder that Lincoln's religious life resists tidy labels.

## A Funeral Planned Eight Years Ahead

The present church was not built for this congregation. Its cornerstone was laid in 1866 for Springfield's Third Presbyterian Church, and the building was dedicated in 1868; Third Presbyterian later sold it to First Presbyterian. Outside, it has changed little beyond the loss of its original tall spires and the addition of a steel superstructure that now braces it. It was here, on July 19, 1882, that Mary Todd Lincoln's funeral was held. By then she had outlived three of her sons and seen her husband assassinated. In a letter to her son Robert in 1874, she had already set out specific instructions for a funeral still eight years away. Historians suggest she welcomed death in many ways, and it is hard to read that letter's foresight without feeling the weight of all that came before it.

## Light by Tiffany

In the 1890s the church began installing memorial windows, and prominent families worked directly with Tiffany Studios on designs to their own instructions, so no two are alike. The oldest, the Angel of the Resurrection, has a central panel that was part of the Tiffany Chapel at the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago. Church members bought it there, and it was dedicated not by a family but by the students of Mary Mickey Holmes, principal of a finishing school called the Betty Stuart Institute. Saint Barnabas, installed in 1906, is the only Tiffany window here with a male figure. In the 1920 Angel with Cherubs, Tiffany's artists laid clear glass over the cherubs' faces to create an illusion of distance. The Dove window, near the peak of the ceiling, was made by the congregation for the congregation. Up to three layers of glass give it depth, and its border sparkles with sapphire and emerald jewel-like glass. On a bright morning, ministers say, its blaze of light inspires them at the pulpit.

## Lincoln in the Glass

A major remodeling in the 1940s pushed the sanctuary toward a Gothic look, replacing the exposed organ pipes in the chancel with new windows. The centerpiece above the chancel, installed in 1946 and designed by Willet Studios, is built of nine panels in a three-by-three grid that reads like ancient mosaic from a distance. Jesus preaches and washes his disciples' feet in the top row. Below, St. Francis blesses the animals, and Dorcas, a biblical woman who used her wealth to serve the poor and widows, takes her place beside St. Paul. At the center of the bottom row stands a young Abraham Lincoln, above the words "with malice toward none" from his second inaugural address, flanked by Florence Nightingale and Clara Barton, each ministering to a soldier. The choice is an unusual one, since Lincoln never formally joined a church.

## Pipes Around a Dove

In 1992 the church signed a contract with John Brombaugh & Associates of Eugene, Oregon, for a custom organ, and construction began in 1997. The result has three manual keyboards and a pedalboard, 46 stops and roughly 3,250 pipes, in a case 30 feet long and 29 feet high yet slightly less than a yard deep. Brombaugh based its design on late Renaissance models, framed it in unfinished, oiled Appalachian white oak and gilded the mouths of its facade pipes with 23-carat gold leaf. One requirement shaped everything: the round Tiffany Dove window had to stay visible, so the pipes were designed to fit around it. The organ was dedicated at Pentecost 2001.

## Flight Context

The church stands at 39.7985N, 89.6468W at Seventh Street and Capitol Avenue in downtown Springfield, Illinois. From 1,500-2,500 feet AGL, look for a Gothic Revival sanctuary among the downtown blocks; with its original spires removed it is a subtle target, best found by working outward from the larger government buildings nearby. Abraham Lincoln Capital Airport (KSPI) lies about three nautical miles to the north-northwest, so check the sectional chart and expect to work with the tower or approach control. Central Illinois is flat and usually offers long visibility outside summer haze.
