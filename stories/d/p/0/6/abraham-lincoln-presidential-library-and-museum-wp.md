# Abraham Lincoln Presidential Library and Museum

Built partly on schoolchildren's pennies, Springfield's Lincoln museum pairs rotating Lincoln originals, including at times a handwritten Gettysburg Address, with ghost shows and life-size dioramas, and historians still argue over whether that mix is a triumph or a gimmick.

**Location:** 39.8032, -89.6473

**Categories:** Abraham Lincoln National Heritage Area, Museums established in 2005, Library buildings completed in 2004, Libraries in Illinois, Museums in Springfield, Illinois, Presidential libraries, Presidential museums in Illinois, 2005 establishments in Illinois

## Introduction

Before there was a building, there were pennies. Illinois schoolchildren collected them in a drive organized by Lura Lynn Ryan, then the state's first lady, and the coins added up to $47,000 toward a home for Abraham Lincoln's story. Ryan had launched the fundraising herself by raising $250,000, and she became the library's first chairwoman. What rose in downtown Springfield is the Abraham Lincoln Presidential Library and Museum, a place that pairs serious scholarship with the kind of showmanship more often found in a theme park. Visitors have come in their millions. Historians are still arguing about whether they should approve.

## Stepping Into the Frame

The museum does not ask you to look at Lincoln so much as walk through his life. Life-size dioramas recreate his boyhood home, rooms of the White House and the presidential box at Ford's Theatre, and the permanent galleries split his life into two halves: "Journey One: The Pre-Presidential Years" and "Journey Two: The Presidential Years." A third space, the Treasures Gallery, shows originals from the library's Lincoln collection on a rotating basis. The state's tourism listing counts a handwritten Gettysburg Address among them, though the document is light-sensitive and not always on view; it has been shown, for example, on Lincoln's birthday weekend, so check before you go. Elsewhere, "The Civil War in Four Minutes" compresses the whole war into an animated map, its battle lines shifting across the screen as the years race by. Two special-effects theater shows, Lincoln's Eyes and Ghosts of the Library, complete the experience. Nearly all of it came from BRC Imagination Arts of Burbank, California, led by Bob Rogers, which produced the permanent exhibits, the theaters, the music and the lifelike figures.

## Showmanship on Trial

That approach set off a debate among museum designers and historians. Traditionalists disapproved of telling Lincoln's story through theatrics and reproductions rather than the display and interpretation of real artifacts. Others applauded. Writing in the Journal of American History, John R. Decker argued that rather than "merely pandering to the public or dumbing down history," the museum "intelligently and compellingly uses visual culture" to open complex history to a wider audience than conventional means could reach. The spectacle was not built on guesswork. State Historian Thomas Schwartz assembled a content team that included leading Lincoln scholars, Pulitzer Prize-winning historians and Illinois schoolteachers from the fourth, seventh and eleventh grades, working with the designers and the Illinois Historic Preservation Agency. Their stated aim was to spark personal interest in Lincoln, and the gift shop suggests it worked: shop sales reportedly reached $1 million within three months of opening.

## Three Blocks of Lincoln

The complex sits in Springfield's historic downtown among other Lincoln sites, spread across three structures that each take up a full city block. The library opened on October 14, 2004, and the museum followed on April 19, 2005. The two main buildings, both designed by the architectural firm HOK, face each other across a street and are joined by an enclosed walkway above it. Each entrance opens into a rotunda that echoes the dome of the Old State Capitol, where Lincoln served as a legislator. The third building is the former Springfield Union Station. It was first adapted as a visitor center, but from early 2014 it housed "Lincoln: History to Hollywood," displaying two sets along with props and costumes from Steven Spielberg's 2012 film Lincoln, lent by Spielberg himself. Museum officials credited the film with a rise in visitors in 2013.

## Crowds, Custodians and Quarrels

Since opening, the museum has been described as America's most visited state-controlled presidential museum. It welcomed its millionth visitor in less than twenty-one months and its three millionth in August 2012. That state control sets it apart: unlike the presidential libraries in the National Archives system, it is not affiliated with the National Archives and Records Administration. It became an independent state agency in 2017, and its library also holds the collection of the Illinois State Historical Library, founded in 1889. Stewardship has not always been smooth. In September 2019 Governor J.B. Pritzker fired executive director Alan Lowe; an inspector general's report that November said Lowe had overstepped his authority by lending the museum's handwritten Gettysburg Address in 2018 to Glenn Beck's Mercury One for a Texas exhibit, for a reported $50,000. Questions have been raised about the provenance of a stovepipe hat said to be Lincoln's and valued at $6.5 million, and in 2024 vexillologists suggested that a 21-star flag bought as an 1818 statehood relic may instead date from the Civil War. Even the quarrels, in their way, show how much people care about getting Lincoln right.

## Flight Context

The museum complex stands at 39.8032N, 89.6473W in the historic downtown of Springfield, Illinois, near several other Lincoln sites. From 2,000-3,000 feet AGL, look for the dense grid of the capital's historic core; the museum and library appear as two large buildings facing each other across a street, joined by an elevated enclosed walkway, with the former Union Station alongside. Abraham Lincoln Capital Airport (KSPI) lies about three nautical miles to the north-northwest, so downtown sits close to its traffic; check the sectional chart and expect to work with the tower or approach control. The surrounding prairie is flat and open, and visibility is usually good outside summer haze and winter overcast.
