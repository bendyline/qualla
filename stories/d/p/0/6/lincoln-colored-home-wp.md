# Lincoln Colored Home

Turned away by every orphanage in Springfield, Black children found a home at 427 South 12th Street, where Eva Carroll Monroe built one of the nation's first orphanages for African American children with $125 she and her sister raised and help from a mayor's widow.

**Location:** 39.7976, -89.6397

**Categories:** 1904 establishments in Illinois, Houses completed in 1904, Historic house museums in Illinois, Residential buildings on the National Register of Historic Places in Illinois, Illinois State Historic Sites, National Register of Historic Places in Springfield, Illinois, Orphanages in the United States, Houses in Springfield, Illinois

## Introduction

Neighbors called it the "Haunted House." When Eva Carroll Monroe put a down payment on the property at 427 South 12th Street in Springfield, Illinois, she and her sister Olive Price had raised just $125, two years after Monroe arrived from Kewanee, and many townspeople thought she was foolish to try. Her reason was simple and urgent. Springfield already had three orphanages, and none of them would take in children of color. On March 8, 1898, Monroe opened the Lincoln Colored Old Folks and Orphans Home, one of the first orphanages for African American children in the United States.

## A Mayor's Widow Answers

Good intentions did not pay the mortgage. Monroe fell behind, and the home faced foreclosure until her work came to the attention of Mary Lawrence, widow of former Springfield mayor Rheuna Lawrence. Lawrence paid $1,400 to bring Monroe's debts current, and she used her community connections to bring the home political attention it had never had. Then she pushed for something bolder. The old "Haunted House" should come down, she argued, and a new building should rise in its place. Monroe agreed, and a home built for its purpose went up on the same site in 1904. Monroe and the home's 37 residents moved in on New Year's Day that year.

## Doors From a Wright Remodel

The new house carried pieces of another Springfield story inside it. Mary Lawrence's daughter, Susan Lawrence Dana, had hired the architect Frank Lloyd Wright to remodel the family's Victorian mansion on East Lawrence Avenue, which some say was the highest commission he had taken on at that point in his career. As Wright's work transformed the Lawrence house, its older doors, windows, stained glass and chandeliers found a second life at the orphans' home on South 12th Street. Children who had been turned away elsewhere now walked through doors taken from the Lawrence family's own mansion.

## Dedicated in Mourning

Mary Lawrence did not live to see the home formally dedicated. She died suddenly, and on March 11, 1906, the first anniversary of her death, the new Lincoln Colored Old Folks and Orphans Home was dedicated to her memory. The household that had moved in two years earlier numbered 29 children and eight elderly women. Her commitment reached beyond the house itself; she also worked as a probation officer and social worker, serving Black children and widows in a segregated city. Susan Lawrence Dana carried on her mother's support, both financial and political, and that steady backing became a big part of why the home endured for as long as it did.

## When the Rules Changed

What ended Monroe's work was not a single crisis but a shift in how the country thought about care. From the mid-1920s, Americans increasingly favored foster parenting over institutions for children without parents, and many came to believe that anyone running a facility like the Lincoln Colored Home should hold a college degree. Those changes cost the home its license to care for the elderly. In 1933 the State of Illinois would not grant it a new license to care for orphans. The 32 children living there were sent back to their own families or to boarding homes. After thirty-five years, the refuge Monroe had built from $125 and stubborn conviction closed its doors.

## A House Still Waiting

The building outlasted its purpose. In 1944, by one account, the house and land were sold at auction, and the proceeds went toward Susan Lawrence Dana's medical bills. It changed hands a few times after that and was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1998. It later belonged to Lyman Hubbard Sr., a former Tuskegee Airman who died in 2012; Illinois Times reported in 2021 that his estate owned it. In recent reports the house stands empty and in poor repair, though efforts to restore it have been made, and Hubbard's son Lee has said he wants to get traction to repair the home.

## Flight Context

The house stands at 39.7976N, 89.6397W at 427 South 12th Street, on the east side of Springfield, Illinois, a short distance east of the downtown core. The house sits among residential blocks and will not stand out from altitude; use downtown Springfield as your reference and look just to its east-southeast.
