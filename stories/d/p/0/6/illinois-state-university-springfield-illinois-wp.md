# Illinois State University (Springfield, Illinois)

A small Lutheran college lured from Hillsboro to Springfield counted Robert Todd Lincoln and John Hay among its students and Abraham Lincoln among its trustees, then collapsed under debt, doctrinal splits and the Civil War before being reborn as Carthage College.

## Introduction

Abraham Lincoln did not pay the $300 up front. When Springfield's new Illinois State University offered perpetual scholarships in 1852, buyers could either hand over the full sum or pay 6 percent interest on it every year, and Lincoln chose the second route. On October 1, 1852, he secured a scholarship for his son Robert, who by one account entered the school's preparatory department at eleven, in the fall of 1854. The college he was investing in had big ambitions and shallow pockets, and within two decades it would be gone from Springfield entirely.

## An Academy on the Prairie

The story starts in Hillsboro, Illinois, where residents raised money in 1835 for a school building called the Hillsboro Academy. John Tillson gave the most, and he also guaranteed the teachers' pay and donated equipment. Classes for both boys and girls began in November 1837. In 1847, at the trustees' request, the state transferred the academy's charter to the grandly named Literary and Theological Institute of the Lutheran Church in the Far West. It became Lutheran College, though townspeople, who supported it well, simply called it Hillsboro College. To build a $10,000 endowment, the college sold scholarships by subscription: $125 bought twelve years, $200 bought twenty-five, and $400 bought a scholarship in perpetuity, transferable to someone else.

## The Capital Comes Calling

In 1852 leading citizens of Springfield made an offer: move the college to the state capital, and they would provide $37,000 for a building and scholarships. The trustees accepted, even though their college was debt-free and its Hillsboro building was worth $6,000. The academy stayed behind under its old name. A new charter as The Illinois State University allowed departments not only of theology, medicine and law but of agriculture and "mechanical philosophy." Only the theology department was meant to teach Lutheran doctrine; the rest were to follow the principles of general Protestant Christianity. Robert Todd Lincoln became a freshman at thirteen. John Hay, later Abraham Lincoln's private secretary, also studied here. And on June 28, 1860, Abraham Lincoln himself was elected to a one-year term as a trustee.

## Promises Unkept

The money that lured the college to Springfield never fully arrived. By 1855 many residents' pledges remained unpaid, scholarship payments lagged, and hoped-for help from the state's Seminary and College Fund never came. Some blamed the Rev. Francis Springer, president since 1847, and he resigned. The building debt stood at $15,000, with another $3,000 owed to interim president S. W. Harkey. Enrollment fell from 160 in 1854 to 114 in 1856, and by 1858 the school owed a further $2,682 in unpaid faculty salaries. A proposal to move the college again shook loose more local donations, but only for a while.

## When the Scandinavians Left

Through the 1850s, German and especially Scandinavian Lutheran immigrants filled the region and the classrooms. In 1858 Lars Paul Esbjorn was appointed professor of Scandinavian languages. On March 31, 1860, he abruptly resigned and advised his Scandinavian students to go home, and all but two did. Esbjorn said he had not been allowed to give them proper religious guidance, though growing nativist feeling among American-born students had likely made them feel unwelcome. Beneath it lay a doctrinal rift over how strictly to hold to the Lutheran Confessions. That June the Scandinavian Lutherans left the Synod of Northern Illinois to form the Augustana Synod, and Esbjorn and others founded Augustana College and Seminary in Chicago. Then came the Civil War. Students left to enlist, discipline among those who stayed was lacking, and enrollment dropped below 70 by 1862. Meanwhile Illinois's growing public school system drew away the high-school-age students who once filled the academy department.

## Many Lives of One Building

By 1867 even the new synod that gained control of the college could not support it, and Wittenberg College in Ohio turned down a plan to merge seminaries. Illinois State University closed in 1869, by the account of a state historical marker (Wikipedia gives 1868). Its building was leased to two Presbyterians who opened Springfield College there, then sold at a sheriff's auction and used briefly by Rev. William Passavant as an orphanage. In 1874 the Lutheran Church–Missouri Synod bought the property for $7,474.38 and moved students from its practical seminary in St. Louis onto the campus. That seminary, now Concordia Theological Seminary, stayed in Springfield until 1976. The old college building was razed in 1931. The college itself had already found a second life: in 1870 former faculty reopened it as Carthage College in Carthage, Illinois, and today it lives on in Kenosha, Wisconsin.

## Flight Context

The former campus site lies at 39.8087N, 89.6378W on the northeast side of Springfield, Illinois, about a nautical mile from downtown. The original college building was razed in 1931 and the grounds now belong to the Illinois Department of Corrections, so there is no historic structure to spot; treat this as a point over the city grid rather than a landmark. From 2,000-3,000 feet AGL, the capital's downtown core lies just to the southwest. Abraham Lincoln Capital Airport (KSPI) sits about three nautical miles to the northwest, so check the sectional chart and expect to work with the tower or approach control. Flat central Illinois terrain usually gives long visibility outside summer haze.
