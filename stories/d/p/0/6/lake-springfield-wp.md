# The Valley Under the Lake

In the 1930s Springfield dammed Sugar Creek, and the valleys of Sugar and Lick Creeks became the largest municipally owned lake in Illinois.

**Location:** 39.7594, -89.6003

**Categories:** Bodies of water of Sangamon County, Illinois, Protected areas of Sangamon County, Illinois, Reservoirs in Illinois, Springfield, Illinois, 1935 establishments in Illinois

## Introduction

A hundred and ten properties were purchased to make room for Lake Springfield. Most of the owners were willing sellers, thanks partly to the Great Depression, but not all. Leander Shoup had to be “escorted from his land by the sheriff and ten deputies,” according to a City Water, Light and Power history of the lake. What the city built on that acquired land is now a 3,965-acre reservoir in the southeast of Springfield, the largest municipally owned body of water in Illinois.

## Before the Water

The first historically documented settlement of the valleys of Sugar and Lick Creeks, now part of Lake Springfield, took place in the 1810s. The Edwards Trace, an ancient road between Kaskaskia and Peoria, ran along the eastern side of the valley. Near the junction of the two creeks, the town of Cotton Hill took shape around a mill. Also known as “Crow’s Mill,” it was the site of a quarry that produced the limestone for the Old State Capitol in Springfield.

Route 66 came through later. It passed through the valley and the town of Cotton Hill until it was rerouted east, to the present-day alignment of Interstate 55.

## Sugar Creek Wins

Talk of building a lake in Springfield began in the 1920s: according to City Water, Light and Power, the official recommendation reached the City Commission on May 31, 1923, and was formally adopted in 1924. Engineers weighed three water sources, the Sangamon River, the South Fork of the Sangamon and the Sugar Creek Valley, and determined that Sugar Creek was the most economical. The effort was led by Willis J. Spaulding, the city's longtime utilities commissioner, who oversaw Springfield's electric and water departments from 1909 until 1943 and gave his name to the dam. Voters approved a bond referendum in 1930 to pay for part of the lake. In the end, federal relief programs such as the Works Progress Administration paid much of its $5.64 million cost.

Then the weather got involved. NPR Illinois reports that Dust Bowl conditions delayed the filling. Lake Springfield reached “full pool” on May 2, 1935, twelve years after the recommendation first reached the City Commission, at 560 feet above sea level. Soon afterward, water began flowing over the spillway, re-watering lower Sugar Creek and completing the dam-interrupted hydrology.

## When the Lake Fell

The lake has known both drought and flood over its 90 years. The worst drought, from 1952 to 1955, drew the lake down more than 12 feet below full pool and cut its water storage to 7.4 billion gallons, threatening operations at both the power plant complex and the water treatment plant. The city responded with an emergency connection to the South Fork to supplement the lake.

The same drought led to plans for a second lake as a long-term backup water source. Nearly all the land for Lake II, later named Hunter Lake after former utilities commissioner John Hunter, had been purchased by the early 21st century. Bureaucratic hurdles and continuing public doubt about the need for it left the lake unbuilt as of 2022.

## Summer on the Water

Boating is popular on Lake Springfield, especially in summer, and the activities run from skiing, wakeboarding and windsurfing to sailing, tubing, kayaking and paddle boarding. Coves around the lake let boaters tie up away from the main boating areas, and a private marina at the south end offers boat and kayak rentals and a restaurant. It is also a highly ranked fishing lake. Blue catfish, black crappie, green sunfish, freshwater drum, redear sunfish, carp, walleye, saugeye and both black and yellow bullhead are among the fish found there.

Eight city parks sit around the lake, most with pavilions open to the public and many with docks for fishing. The Abraham Lincoln Memorial Garden, a local arboretum on the east side, offers trails and exhibits. The lake also borders the Henson Robinson Zoo and lies close to the campus of the University of Illinois Springfield. The public beach closed in the 2000s and never reopened, though the historic Beach House can still be rented by reservation.

The calendar fills up too: fish fries and slo-pitch softball tournaments at the clubs, an annual summer triathlon, and boat races during various stretches since the 1980s, the last in 2019. A large fireworks show lights the south end of the lake around the Fourth of July, and a second show at the north end is reported for Labor Day weekend. In June 2025, the lake celebrated its 90th birthday.

## Flight Context

Lake Springfield lies at 39.76°N, 89.60°W, a 3,965-acre reservoir in the southeast of Springfield, Illinois, with a full-pool surface about 560 feet above sea level. Fly roughly 2,500 to 3,500 feet above ground to take in the shoreline in one view; the University of Illinois Springfield campus, the Abraham Lincoln Memorial Garden and the Henson Robinson Zoo all sit along its edges, and Interstate 55 runs to the east. Abraham Lincoln Capital Airport (KSPI) is about 6 nautical miles to the northwest and has Class C airspace, so check the sectional chart and expect to work with the tower or approach control. Best viewed in clear daytime weather.
