# Springfield, Illinois

Illinois's capital began as a frontier settlement called Calhoun, won the statehouse with help from Abraham Lincoln and the "Long Nine," and today mixes Lincoln pilgrimage sites and state government with horseshoe sandwiches, Cozy Dogs and chili spelled with two l's.

## Introduction

The first name did not stick. Settlers on this stretch of the Sangamon country called their community Calhoun, after Senator John C. Calhoun of South Carolina, a nod to their cultural ties. By 1832 Calhoun had fallen out of public favor, and the town renamed itself Springfield. According to local history, the new name came from the wife of John Kelly, whose cabin was the first built here, after Spring Creek, which ran through the land people knew as "Kelly's Field." From that humble clearing grew the capital of Illinois, the adopted hometown of Abraham Lincoln, and a city that still claims the corn dog.

## Kelly's Field to Statehouse

Trappers and fur traders reached the Sangamon River in 1818. Two years later John Kelly, drawn by plentiful deer and other game, built his cabin on a hill above a creek later called the Town Branch; a stone marker on Jefferson Street still marks the spot. In 1821 the young settlement became the seat of Sangamon County, prized for its location, fertile soil and trading opportunities, and settlers arrived from Kentucky, Virginia and North Carolina. The real prize came in 1839. Illinois had governed first from Kaskaskia, then from Vandalia, but that year Springfield was named the third state capital, largely through the efforts of Abraham Lincoln and his associates, nicknamed the "Long Nine" for their combined height of 54 feet. Not everyone who passed through in those years came by choice. In 1838 the Potawatomi Trail of Death crossed Springfield, as Potawatomi people were forced west under the federal government's removal policy.

## Twenty-Four Years With Lincoln

Lincoln was not born here, but he is the city's most famous resident. He moved to Springfield in 1837 after six years in nearby New Salem, and for the next 24 years he lived and worked here as a lawyer and politician. He delivered his Lyceum address in Springfield, and his farewell address on leaving for Washington remains a classic of American oratory. Home did not always vote for him: as waves of European immigrants aligned with the Democrats, the once-Whig city shifted, and in the 1860 presidential election Lincoln barely carried it. Today his footprints are everywhere. The Lincoln Home National Historic Site preserves his house and the surrounding neighborhood, and the Lincoln circuit continues through the Lincoln-Herndon Law Offices, the Old State Capitol, the Lincoln Depot where he departed for his inauguration, the Presidential Library and Museum, and his tomb. In 2007 Barack Obama chose the grounds of the Old State Capitol to announce his own run for president.

## Camps, Rails and a Hard Reckoning

During the Civil War, Springfield became a major center of Union activity. Illinois regiments trained here, the first under Ulysses S. Grant, and Camp Butler, about seven miles northeast of town, opened in August 1861 to train Illinois soldiers and also held Confederate prisoners of war through 1865. Many residents who came to see the camp's excitement found themselves moved by the suffering of ill and dying prisoners. After the war the city grew into a major hub of the Illinois railroad system, and by 1900 it had added coal mining to government and farming. The new century brought the city's darkest days. In August 1908, White mobs attacked Springfield's predominantly Black neighborhoods, including the Levee and the Badlands, in the Springfield race riot. Two Black men, Scott Burton and William Donnegan, were lynched. Counts of the dead vary: the Sangamon County Historical Society says at least eleven people died, more of them white than Black, while Wikipedia's article on the riot says at least seventeen. It took thousands of state militiamen to restore order after the violence that began on August 14.

## Horseshoes, Cozy Dogs and Chilli

Springfield eats with a distinct accent. The city claims the corn dog was invented here under the name "Cozy Dog," though the snack's origin is debated. The horseshoe sandwich, little known outside central Illinois, was born here too, and the Maid-Rite Sandwich Shop, which dates to the 1920s, claims the first drive-up window in the United States. Local chili shops often spell the dish "chilli," which one interpretation explains as a nod to the "Ill" in Illinois. For 117 years, from 1849 to 1966, the same family brewed Reisch Beer here. The city's cultural roots run wide. Poet Vachel Lindsay was born here in 1879, and Frank Lloyd Wright's Dana–Thomas House, built for patron Susan Lawrence Dana, is among the best preserved of his early Prairie houses. Protestant exiles from Madeira, driven out by ostracism in their Catholic homeland, settled here in 1849 by way of Trinidad, forming one of the Midwest's earliest Portuguese communities. And the ill-fated Donner Party began its journey west from Springfield.

## A Capital on the Plain

Modern Springfield counted 114,394 people in 2020, making it the most populous city in Central Illinois. State government anchors the economy, and as of 2002 the State of Illinois employed 17,000 people across Sangamon County. Each August the Illinois State Fair fills the fairgrounds, and the city lies along historic Route 66. The land around it is remarkably flat. Springfield sits on a till plain laid down by continental ice sheets, where relief rarely exceeds twenty feet. South of the city spreads Lake Springfield, a 3,965-acre reservoir created in 1935 by damming Sugar Creek, which supplies the city's drinking water and is a popular spot for boating and fishing.

## Flight Context

Springfield centers on 39.7975N, 89.6450W on the flat till plain of central Illinois, at roughly 560 feet above sea level. From 3,000-4,500 feet AGL the city is easy to read: the Illinois State Capitol anchors downtown near the western start of the numbered street grid, the Sangamon River flows past the northern outskirts, and the broad water of Lake Springfield lies just south of the city. Interstate 55 runs north-south past the city and I-72 runs east-west, both useful for navigation. Abraham Lincoln Capital Airport (KSPI), with scheduled service to Chicago O'Hare, sits about 3 nautical miles northwest of downtown and has Class C airspace, so check the sectional chart and expect to work with the tower or approach control. Summer thunderstorms are common, as Springfield lies at the far reaches of Tornado Alley, while the flat terrain usually offers long visibility in settled weather.
