# The Only House Lincoln Ever Owned

The Springfield house Abraham and Mary Todd Lincoln bought in 1844 and grew to twelve rooms, the only home he ever owned, and the four preserved blocks around it.

**Location:** 39.7972, -89.6450

**Categories:** National Historic Landmarks in Illinois, National Historic Sites in Illinois, Presidential homes in the United States, Illinois in the American Civil War, Historic house museums in Illinois, Museums in Springfield, Illinois, Presidential museums in Illinois, Protected areas established in 1971, National Register of Historic Places in Springfield, Illinois, Protected areas of Sangamon County, Illinois

## Introduction

Abraham Lincoln owned exactly one home in his life. He and Mary Todd Lincoln bought it in 1844 as a five-room cottage, and it grew into the twelve-room, two-story house at 413 South Eighth Street, on the corner of Jackson Street in Springfield, Illinois. They lived there until 1861, just before he became the 16th president of the United States. Three of their children were born in the house, and one, Eddie, died there. Today it anchors a four-block historic district administered by the National Park Service, where the streets and sidewalks use period materials and the neighbors' houses have been restored on the outside to the way they looked while Lincoln lived among them.

## From New Salem to Eighth Street

Lincoln moved to Springfield in 1837 from New Salem, at the start of his law career. At her sister's home in town he met Mary Todd, and the two married in 1842. Two years later they bought the house on Eighth Street, and over the years that followed the Lincolns completely renovated and expanded it. It was a political house as much as a family one. The Lincolns often hosted political meetings under its roof, and the large north parlor is where, on May 19, 1860, a Republican committee led by George Ashmun formally notified Lincoln of his presidential nomination. During his time there he was elected to the House of Representatives in 1846 and, in 1860, to the presidency.

## A Parlor Rebuilt from Photographs

The Lincoln Home has been completely restored to its 1860 appearance, though few original artifacts remain inside. Conservators recreated the furniture and design from historical pictures. Out back, the outhouse in the rear yard is not the original structure either, but the period building stands in the original spot and belonged to family friends of the Lincolns. The restoration carries a quiet weight. The Lincolns fully intended to return to this house after his presidency, and Lincoln was assassinated before that could happen.

## A Gift with Conditions

In 1887, Lincoln's son Robert Todd Lincoln donated the family home to the State of Illinois, with a condition attached: it would forever be well maintained and open to the public at no charge. The terms had a cause. Some tenants had charged people who wanted to visit Lincoln's home, and many tended to leave it in disrepair. Official recognition came in stages. The home and the Lincoln Tomb, also in Springfield, were designated National Historic Landmarks on December 19, 1960, and were automatically listed on the National Register of Historic Places on October 15, 1966. On August 18, 1971, the National Historic Site was authorized, and on October 9, 1972, the home and the adjacent district officially became part of it, administered by the National Park Service.

## The Neighbors on Eighth Street

Lincoln's was never the only house that mattered on this block. Several other structures in the four-block area are preserved with it, and two are open to visitors with exhibits of their own: the Dean House ("What a Pleasant Home Abe Lincoln Has") and the Arnold House ("If These Walls Could Talk"). Signs tell the neighborhood's other stories, among them the Jameson Jenkins site, where a home stood that was on the Underground Railroad. The park's buildings occupy 12 acres in all, and other homes in the district remain privately owned and lived in. A "log cabin" wagon has been displayed along the street, modeled on a promotional vehicle used in political parades in Lincoln's time.

Across Eighth Street from the Lincoln Home is the Lyon House, where Samuel Rosenwald's family moved around 1869. It became the boyhood home of his son Julius, who went on to become president of Sears Roebuck and Company and a major philanthropist, helping to fund thousands of schools across the segregated South. The house was renamed in his honor in 2020.

## The Rest of the Springfield Years

Rangers staff the modern visitor center, where a gift shop sits beside displays of Lincoln-related artifacts. There, a film of about 25 minutes, Journey to Greatness, recreates his Springfield years between the 1840s and 1860, both personal and professional. It was filmed on location at the home and in the neighborhood, at New Salem, in the Old Capitol and elsewhere. At selected times, local historical reenactors portray Abraham Lincoln, Mary Todd Lincoln and their neighbors, or demonstrate period skills and entertainment within the park. The story spills past the four blocks, too. Nearby in Springfield stands the Old State Capitol, where Lincoln served as a state legislator; the building that housed the law offices of Lincoln and his partner William Herndon from 1844 until 1852; and the Lincoln Depot, from which Lincoln left the city for his 1861 inauguration.

## Flight Context

Lincoln Home National Historic Site sits at 39.7972 N, 89.6450 W in downtown Springfield, Sangamon County, Illinois. The four-block district is compact, so 2,000-3,000 feet AGL gives a clear view of the street grid; the Old State Capitol and the Lincoln Depot lie nearby in the same city. Nearby airports: Abraham Lincoln Capital Airport (KSPI), about 3 nm north-northwest, with Class C airspace, so check the sectional chart and expect to work with the tower or approach control; Taylorville Municipal (KTAZ), about 22 nm southeast; Jacksonville Municipal (KIJX), about 27 nm west; Decatur Airport (KDEC), about 36 nm east.
