# The Fair That Bought Chicago's Dome

The Illinois State Fair roamed Illinois for forty years, sat out 1893 for Chicago's world's fair, then settled in Springfield under a dome bought from that same exposition.

**Location:** 39.8360, -89.6420

**Categories:** Illinois State Fair, Landmarks in Illinois, State fairs, August, Fairs in Illinois, Tourist attractions in Springfield, Illinois, Festivals established in 1853

## Introduction

In 1893 there was no Illinois State Fair. Chicago was hosting the World's Columbian Exposition, a far larger showcase of agricultural products and machinery, and the state fair sat out the year. Within two years, though, the fairgrounds in Springfield had a piece of that exposition for their own: a glass-domed building bought from the Chicago World's Fair and reconstructed in Springfield. The fair that yielded to Chicago had, by then, found a permanent home. It has been celebrated almost every year since 1853, and today it fills 11 days in mid-August at the Illinois State Fairgrounds.

## Farmers Comparing Notes

The 1850s were a golden age of agricultural journalism, with a wide variety of editors offering suggestions, well-founded or not, to increase farm productivity. The first state fairs, in Illinois and other states, were created and organized by farmers who wanted to compare notes with their colleagues and tell good advice from bad. Illinois held its first fair in Springfield in 1853. A year later, according to the fair's own history, the main attractions were Senator Stephen Douglas and Abraham Lincoln, with Lincoln delivering a rebuttal to Douglas's speech the day after.

After the Civil War, the rules of agricultural judging became standardized, and more farmers began to show their farm products. Growing knowledge of genetics inspired the breeding and showing of purebred farm animals at county fairs and at the state fair. In the first half of the 20th century, the internal combustion engine revolutionized life on the American farm, and manufacturers of agricultural machinery eagerly used occasions like the Illinois State Fair to demonstrate their new products.

## No Fixed Address

For its first forty years, 1853 to 1892, the fair kept moving, as many state fairs did. It was celebrated as far north as Freeport and as far south as Du Quoin, and by the count in the fair's own history it passed through twelve different cities. In 1858, according to that history, two farm children in Centralia accidentally ascended into the sky when a hot-air balloon tied to their father's fence broke loose. They were recovered safely 18 miles away.

The fair was suspended only on a few occasions, and 1893 was one of them.

## A Home on the Northern Edge

In January 1894, the fair's board chose Springfield as its permanent site. The State of Illinois began to use a 156-acre parcel on the northern boundary of the city, which became the heart of the permanent Illinois State Fairgrounds. The first fair there opened on September 24, 1894, with admission set at 50 cents for adults, 75 cents for one person on horseback, and $1.25 for a carriage load of four.

A grandstand and racetrack were built, and the first auto races were reportedly held at the Illinois State Fairgrounds Racetrack in 1910. In 1924 the state bought another 208 acres, and the grounds now cover about 366 acres. The grounds work beyond August, too: motorcycle and car racing, horse shows and rodeos share the site through the year.

## The Glass Dome

The building the fair bought from the 1893 Chicago exposition cost a reported $69,000 to $69,500, depending on the account, and it stood on the fairgrounds from 1895 to 1917. Its glass dome measured 222 feet across and has been described as the world's second-largest unsupported dome. The building could hold 10,000 people.

In August 1917, about 1,500 soldiers waiting to go to war were temporarily housed inside it. On the evening of August 17, just before the fair was due to open, the Dome Building caught fire, and the huge glass dome collapsed. None were injured, and no cause was ever determined.

## Butter, Corn Dogs and Crowds

Starting in 1946, the fair is credited with a key role in popularizing the corn dog. It has long been noted, too, for its annual butter cow, a life-size animal formed of pure butter applied to an armature by a sculptor wielding an oversized palette knife. Among the butter cow's more ironic admirers was the author David Foster Wallace, who covered the 1993 fair for Harper's. His report on the yellow bovine and other fair sights is reprinted in A Supposedly Fun Thing I'll Never Do Again (1997).

Attendance has swung over the years. In 2019, the fair reversed declining trends with an estimated 509,000 fairgoers and record revenues, with Snoop Dogg and Reba McEntire among the headliners. In 2020, as a safety measure during the COVID-19 pandemic, the main shows were removed, the first cancellation since World War II. Agricultural and motorsport events moved to September and early October: the animal shows became a Junior Livestock Expo, and the Allen Crowe 100 and Bettenhausen 100 races were held in October. Attendance recovered after the pandemic: the fair that ran through August 17, 2025, counted 723,079 fairgoers, reported as its second-largest turnout, and the August 2026 fair drew about 714,000, reported as the third highest in its history.

## Flight Context

The Illinois State Fairgrounds lie at 39.836°N, 89.642°W, 366 acres on the northern boundary of Springfield, Illinois. The grandstand and racetrack are the features to look for from the air; 1,500 to 2,500 feet above ground gives a clear view in good daytime weather. Abraham Lincoln Capital Airport (KSPI) is about 2 nautical miles to the west-northwest, so check the sectional chart and expect to work with the tower or approach control. The fair itself runs for 11 days in mid-August.
