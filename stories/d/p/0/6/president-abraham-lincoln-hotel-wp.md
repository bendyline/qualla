# President Abraham Lincoln Hotel

Built in 1985 with Italian marble, a Murano-glass chandelier and a state-backed mortgage, Springfield's convention hotel stopped paying the state, fell into its hands by foreclosure, and was sold for a fraction of its cost before becoming a DoubleTree.

**Location:** 39.8009, -89.6458

**Categories:** DoubleTree hotels, Buildings and structures in Springfield, Illinois, Hotel buildings completed in 1985, 1985 establishments in Illinois

## Introduction

For a time, Illinois state employees were barred from spending the night in a hotel the State of Illinois had helped pay for. The building was the Ramada Renaissance, opened in downtown Springfield in 1985 with Italian marble and Venetian glass, and financed with a mortgage the state itself had backed. By 2008 the state had foreclosed, and the ban had already been lifted. The hotel, now the President Abraham Lincoln Hotel, a DoubleTree by Hilton, has changed names, chains and owners so often that its history reads like a ledger of Springfield's ambitions and their costs.

## Tied to the Convention Center

The hotel was born as a companion to something bigger. In the early 1970s, the Springfield Metropolitan Exposition and Auditorium Authority started planning a convention center, later known as the Prairie Capital Convention Center, and broke ground in mid-1975. Proposals were sought for an adjacent hotel; of eleven interested companies, only two submitted plans. In March 1979, New Frontier Development, headed by William F. Cellini, won with a bid to build a 300-room hotel for about $10 million, and the center opened that fall. The hotel's link to the convention center, a tunnel that reportedly makes it the only hotel connected to the complex now called the Bank of Springfield Convention Center, is said to be what allowed it to be financed with state-backed loans. The developers' venture borrowed about $24 million, including a $15.5 million state-backed mortgage, a $3.1 million low-interest city loan funded through a federal urban development grant, and roughly $5.4 million from a bank in Hinsdale. The developers added about $7 million of their own.

## Marble, Murano and a Poet's Painting

Plans for a Radisson gave way in April 1983 to Ramada's more upscale Renaissance brand, and construction began in February 1984 to designs by Nagle Hartray and Associates of Chicago. At the grand opening on Friday, August 30, 1985, Governor James R. Thompson joined a crowd of officials and business leaders. Guests walked across marble floors imported from Italy beneath a chandelier of Murano glass, made on an island off Venice, over Axminster carpet woven in Great Britain. One of the two restaurants, Lindsay's Gallery, honored the Springfield poet Vachel Lindsay, and an eight-foot rendition of his 1913 painting "The Wedding of the Rose and the Lotus" greeted diners at its entrance. An upper-level bar took the name of the Globe Tavern, the Springfield inn where Abraham Lincoln would occasionally have stopped. Rooms started at $65 a night. Construction had cost about $89,000 per room.

## When the Payments Stopped

The elegance proved hard to pay for. In 1990 the state restructured the loan so that payments were due only when the hotel made money, and in 1997 regular payments stopped altogether; just two payments, of less than $143,000, were made in 2002. That same year of 1997, Marriott bought the Renaissance chain and the word Ramada disappeared from the sign. When Marriott declined to renew the franchise in 2005, the hotel became the President Abraham Lincoln Hotel and Conference Center. In 2008 the state foreclosed, with $30 million in principal and interest owed, according to the state treasurer's office. A court-appointed receiver from Hostmark Hospitality Group took over operations, and the state spent a further $375,000 on repairs, new furnishings and replacement mattresses. Governor Rod Blagojevich had lifted the ban on state employees staying there earlier, before the foreclosure.

## Changing Hands

In 2009 the state put the hotel up for sale and drew five bidders. Steve Horve, whose family owned hotels in Decatur, Forsyth, Champaign and Dearborn, Michigan, won with a bid of $6.5 million, a fraction of what the building had cost to build. The sale closed in January 2010, and renovations that followed took nearly two years, down to replacing all the laundry equipment. In 2013 the hotel joined Hilton as a DoubleTree property. Then, in October 2014, came an unsolicited offer from an unlikely direction: the Al Habtoor Group, led by businessman Khalaf Ahmad al Habtoor, paid $9.3 million, according to the county recorder's office. The seller said it would be the group's first purchase in the United States, and the hotel was to remain under Hilton management.

## Two Lincoln Hotels

This is the second Springfield hotel named for the city's most famous resident. The first, the thirteen-story Abraham Lincoln Hotel, known locally as "the Abe," opened in the mid-1920s (sources give 1925 or 1926) and closed in June 1964. On December 17, 1978, it came down in a controlled implosion, to make way for a state courts complex and legal education center that never materialized, and its site has stayed undeveloped, a parking lot by one local account. Its successor is still standing and still working. The twelve-story building holds 310 guest rooms above a street-level floor with the lobby, the Presidential Ballroom, Lindsay's Restaurant and The Globe Tavern, while a grand staircase rises to meeting rooms on the second floor. Down on the concourse level, past the fitness center and indoor pool, the tunnel still runs straight to the convention center, the connection that made the whole venture possible.

## Flight Context

The hotel stands at 39.8009N, 89.6458W at 701 East Adams Street in downtown Springfield, Illinois. From 2,000-3,000 feet AGL, look for a twelve-story tower in the downtown core alongside the broad bulk of the convention center it connects to by tunnel. Abraham Lincoln Capital Airport (KSPI) lies about 3 nautical miles to the north-northwest and has Class C airspace, so check the sectional chart and expect to work with the tower or approach control. The flat prairie around Springfield generally offers long visibility, with haze common in summer.
