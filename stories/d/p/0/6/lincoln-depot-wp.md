# The Steps of the Lincoln Depot

A resilient railroad station in Springfield, Illinois, that evolved from the site of Lincoln's farewell to a hybrid law office and museum.

**Location:** 39.7993, -89.6424

**Categories:** Abraham Lincoln, Railway stations in the United States opened in 1852, National Register of Historic Places in Springfield, Illinois, Railway stations on the National Register of Historic Places in Illinois, 1852 establishments in Illinois, Former Wabash Railroad stations, Former railway stations in Illinois

## Introduction

On a dark, gloomy morning in February 1861, more than a thousand Springfield residents gathered at the Great Western Railroad Depot. President-elect Abraham Lincoln had ridden a cart from The Chenery House hotel to the station, where an office served as a reception room for neighbors and friends who filed past to take his hand. Shortly before 8 a.m., Lincoln walked through the crowd to the back of the train that would take him to Washington D.C. From its rear platform he delivered an impromptu farewell address and requested prayers before embarking on his mission. Today, the second floor of the Lincoln Depot houses a professional law practice, while the first floor remains a public exhibit.

## Separate Waiting Areas

The Great Western Railroad opened the modest structure in 1852, providing an office and separate waiting areas for males and females. In 1857, a fire damaged the depot, necessitating extensive remodeling. Over time, passenger operations moved to a building at Tenth and Washington Streets, and the old building was operated as a freight house. The structure grew physically around 1900 when a second story was added to the building.

## The Mid-Century Echoes

By the middle of the twentieth century, the depot became a place of performance and symbolic send-offs. On Lincoln's 139th birthday in 1948, hundreds of supporters gathered to see off The Lincoln Friendship Train, which carried food aid to citizens of World War II-ravished Western Europe. Governor Dwight Green dedicated the train, and State Representative G. William Horsley portrayed Lincoln during the send-off. The depot later served as a television set in 1956; on Lincoln's birthday, the program "Wide Wide World" filmed a reenactment of the farewell to Springfield with G. William Horsley again portraying Lincoln and Floyd Barringer as Judge Bollingreen.

## Fire and Fragility

The depot's history is marked by periods of preservation and decay. A local group operated the site as a paid entry museum between 1965 and 1976. However, disaster returned in December 1968 when a fire severely damaged the building; while arson was suspected, it was not proven. The blaze left few of the depot's contents unscathed, and the warehouse section—which had represented the height of the building during Lincoln's departure—was torn down following the fire. Some parts of the structure dating to Lincoln's era survived. From 1977 to 1980, Sangamon State University operated the restored depot as part of its curriculum.

## A Modern Hybrid

Then, in 2012, the building was sold to become the home of the Noll Law Office. Following extensive renovation and rehabilitation, the Noll Law Office moved its operations to the mezzanine and second floor in March 2013. The building was added to the National Register of Historic Places on August 25, 2014. The first floor is a tourist site operated by the Noll Law Office and the Lincoln Home National Historic Site, where National Park Service Rangers provide interpretation during the open season. Inside the visitor's center, a physical remnant is preserved: the steps Abraham Lincoln used to enter the Depot manager's office on his last day in Springfield.
