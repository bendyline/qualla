# Robert Allerton Park

An art-loving stockyard heir turned a stretch of Sangamon River farmland into a Georgian Revival mansion ringed by sculpture gardens and wild floodplain forest, then gave it all to the University of Illinois for the people to enjoy.

**Location:** 39.9980, -88.6500

## Introduction

In 1929 Robert Allerton ordered a reduced-size copy of a statue for his Illinois estate: Swedish sculptor Carl Milles's Sun Singer, a figure of Apollo. The artist sent the full-size casting instead: sixteen feet and 2,300 pounds of bronze. Allerton kept it, and in 1932 his designer John Gregg, later his adopted son, created a three-tiered platform for it, styled after the Altar of Heaven at Beijing's Temple of Heaven. The god still stands there today, a helmet crowned with Pegasus, the nine Muses in relief around the base, rising out of the farm country of Piatt County. It is only one of the surprises in Robert Allerton Park, 1,517 acres of gardens, woods and prairie on the upper Sangamon River near Monticello.

## The Heir Who Chose Art

Robert Henry Allerton was born in 1873, heir to a Chicago banking and stockyard fortune built by his father, Samuel Allerton, one of the founders of Chicago's Union Stock Yards. Robert was drawn to art from his youth and studied painting, drawing and sculpture abroad, at academies in Munich and Paris. In 1897 he came home to Illinois and settled on one of the family's farms near Monticello. Construction of his house began on June 13, 1899, and was mostly finished a year later at a cost of about $50,000. The Farms is a 40-room, 30,000-square-foot Georgian Revival mansion of Dutch brick laid in Flemish bond and Indiana limestone. One of its models was Ham House in England, and a spring-fed reflecting pond was designed to mimic the River Thames' reflection of that house. Over the next forty-seven years, with activity peaking in the 1920s and early 1930s, Allerton turned the farm into a central Illinois showplace.

## Gardens Gathered From the World

The estate has been described as "a vast prairie turned into a personal fantasy land of neoclassical statues, Far Eastern art, and huge European-style gardens surrounding a Georgian-Revival mansion." The description fits. In the Chinese Maze Garden, hedges are trimmed into a variation of the ancient Chinese symbol Shou, with fish fountain statues that Allerton bought in Peking (now Beijing) in 1930 at their centers. The Sunken Garden, created in 1915 on the site of the estate's old garbage dump, is framed by four Balinese-style gateways topped with bronzed shachi, guardian fish thought to protect a home from fire, modeled on originals Allerton saw at Nagoya Castle in Japan. A 1917 octagonal tower, the House of the Golden Buddhas, holds two gilded teakwood Buddhas from Thailand. Along the Avenue of the Musicians, a dozen statues play flutes, drums and strings. Deeper in the forest, along the Orange Trail, hikers stumble on Emile-Antoine Bourdelle's Death of the Last Centaur, a surprise that visitors love.

## Giving It Away

In 1932 Allerton hired John Gregg, who had worked for Chicago society architect David Adler until the 1929 crash cut into house commissions, to design new gardens and interiors and help run the farms. Allerton legally adopted him in 1960, and Gregg took the Allerton name in 1965. By the 1940s, the Great Depression, World War II and federal income taxes had made great country houses hard to staff and run. In 1946 the Allertons deeded the Piatt County property to the University of Illinois and moved to Lawai-Kai, now Allerton Garden, on Kauai in Hawaii. The gift came with a twist. Allerton land had supplied a fifth of Willow Branch Township's tax revenue, and when the university claimed exemption, the township protested; an Illinois Supreme Court ruling eventually settled on an annual payment in lieu of taxes to Piatt County. About 3,600 acres of farmland beyond the estate came with the gift, worked by tenants to help support the park. After a gala garden party in May 1947, the grounds opened to the public in 1949.

## The River Bottom Saved

South of the Sangamon, Allerton left the land almost entirely natural, and in the 1960s much of it was threatened by a dam. Oakley Dam, a U.S. Army Corps of Engineers project on the Sangamon downstream that Congress authorized in 1962, would have meant condemning a substantial portion of the park's riverine bottomland. A grassroots group, the Committee on Allerton Park, campaigned against it; its funding was halted in 1975, and the project was deauthorized in 1985. In 1970 the southern section and adjacent bottomland, about 1,000 acres, became the Allerton Natural Area, a National Natural Landmark, with riverine bottomland described as "an exceptional floodplain forest of silver maple, cottonwood, and sycamore." A 24-hour Biodiversity Blitz in 2001 recorded more than 2,000 species of plants and animals. The estate itself joined the National Register of Historic Places in 2007, when the park was drawing roughly 100,000 visitors a year.

## A Stone in the Pond

Robert Allerton died in 1964, and his ashes were scattered over Lawai-Kai Bay in Hawaii. There is no grave marker. Instead, in 1965, a granite boulder was set in the reflecting pond with an inscription that serves as his epitaph: "Robert Allerton he gave his woodland home to the University of Illinois for the people to enjoy and to enrich their lives." The house he built now serves as a conference and retreat center and hosts weddings and overnight guests, and scientists have gathered here for decades, from a 1961 symposium on self-organization to the Allerton Conference on Communication, Control, and Computing, which began here in 1963.

## Flight Context

Robert Allerton Park is at about 39.998 N, 88.650 W along the upper Sangamon River in Piatt County, Illinois, roughly 4 nm west-southwest of Monticello. From 2,000-3,000 feet AGL, look for the dark ribbon of floodplain forest following the river through open farmland; the mansion, formal gardens and reflecting pond lie north of the river, and the Allerton Natural Area bottomland spreads to the south. Caution: the WILL AM/FM/TV transmitter tower stands on former Allerton farmland north of the park, so keep a safe altitude when circling. Nearby airports: University of Illinois Willard Airport (KCMI), about 17 nm east; Decatur Airport (KDEC), about 14 nm southwest; both have control towers, so check the sectional chart and expect to work with the tower or approach control near them. Summer haze and thunderstorms are common.
