# Champaign–Decatur combined statistical area

A former census region that bound a university town and an industrial city across four prairie counties, the Champaign–Decatur area is home to a flagship university, historic theaters, a homegrown rock scene and a long list of Nobel laureates, inventors and artists.

## Introduction

Some regions exist on the ground long after they vanish from the paperwork. The Champaign–Decatur Combined Statistical Area was a Census Bureau creation that tied together four central Illinois counties, Champaign, Macon, Piatt and Ford, with about 343,000 people at the 2010 census, ranking around 104th among such areas in the country. By 2020 the statisticians had split it apart, defining Decatur as its own metropolitan area and folding the other three counties into a Champaign–Urbana metropolitan area, which the 2023 definitions pair with Danville in a combined area of its own. On the prairie, though, the connections remain: two anchor cities, a flagship university, and a web of farm towns in between.

## Two Cities and a Campus

The region's weight is uneven. Champaign County counted 201,081 residents at the 2010 census and Macon County, home to Decatur, 110,768, while Piatt and Ford counties together held just over 30,000. At the heart of the eastern half sits the University of Illinois at Urbana–Champaign, the flagship campus of the University of Illinois system, and its students are included in the census counts. Development around the campus has pushed Champaign's skyline upward. Memorial Stadium underwent a major renovation with new stands, clubs and luxury suites, and Campustown filled with towers such as 309 Green, a 24-story, 256-foot apartment building. Decatur, to the west, brings its own institutions: Millikin University, on a 75-acre campus founded by James Millikin, and Richland Community College, which hosts the Farm Progress Show every other year, trading off with Boone, Iowa.

## Where the Suburbs Meet the Corn

The outer edges of this region do not look like the suburban skirts that ring most American metros. Instead, the urban areas run right up against broad stretches of farmland, dotted with small and medium-sized villages that began as farming communities. As professionals have grown more willing to commute, new housing has sprung up on the edges of those villages. Most of the outlying communities, such as Savoy, Mahomet and St. Joseph, and arguably Rantoul and Monticello, depend on Champaign and Urbana for economic and infrastructure support, and because many of their residents are well-paid professors, doctors and technology workers who can afford cars and single-family homes, they often post higher median household incomes than the cities themselves. The growth has a price in local politics. Annexation, which shifts property tax revenue from townships to cities, has been a point of constant strife between the cities and the surrounding townships.

## Stages and Screens

For a region of this size, the arts run remarkably deep. The Krannert Center for the Performing Arts, built in 1969 on the university campus, covers more than four acres with four theatres and an amphitheatre and hosts over 400 performances a year. Downtown Champaign's Virginia Theatre, commissioned in 1921, served first as a venue for films and live shows, then mainly as a movie house, before closing to regular films on February 13, 1992, with Steve Martin's Father of the Bride. Revived as a live venue and now owned and run by the Champaign Park District, it hosts Roger Ebert's Film Festival each April, and its original Wurlitzer pipe organ, revived by organist Warren York's volunteer restoration in the 1980s and fully restored in 2012, is still played regularly. The Art Theater began in 1912 as the Park, Champaign's first theatre devoted to movies, and the 1914 Orpheum by the architects Rapp and Rapp now houses a children's science museum.

## A Sound From the Prairie

The region has sent a steady stream of musicians into the world, starting with REO Speedwagon, Head East and Dan Fogelberg and continuing through bands such as Hum, Poster Children and Braid. The Pygmalion Music Festival, founded in 2005 by Seth Fein and Justine Bursoni, brought acts including Yo La Tengo, Andrew Bird and Questlove. Homegrown media keep the scene alive. WEFT 90.1 FM, a community station, has broadcast music, news and public affairs with an all-volunteer staff since 1981, and in November 2005 hundreds of volunteers from the region and around the country built low-power station WRFU-LP for Radio Free Urbana at the ninth Prometheus Radio Project barnraising.

## Minds and Makers

The list of people tied to the region, as natives or as University of Illinois students, reads like a survey of twentieth-century achievement. John Bardeen won the Nobel Prize in Physics twice. Nick Holonyak Jr. invented the visible light-emitting diode. Marc Andreessen co-wrote the Mosaic web browser. Roger Ebert, the film critic, went to high school in Urbana alongside the actor David Ogden Stiers. Red Grange and Dick Butkus starred in football, and Bonnie Blair raced to Olympic speed skating fame. Visitors can trace the region's wider story at the Spurlock Museum, with more than 50,000 artifacts on human culture, at the Monticello Railway Museum's operating tourist railroad, and at Robert Allerton Park, a donated private estate of gardens, woods and prairie. Decatur, once dubbed "Park City U.S.A.," adds a lake and 2,000 acres of parks.

## Flight Context

The area's nominal coordinates, 40.0 N, 88.667 W, fall in open farmland between Decatur and Champaign, about 4 nm west-southwest of Monticello. From 5,000-8,000 feet the region reads clearly: Decatur and Lake Decatur to the southwest, Champaign-Urbana and the University of Illinois campus to the east, and between them a grid of fields dotted with farm villages. Nearby airports: University of Illinois Willard Airport (KCMI), about 18 nm east near Savoy, and Decatur Airport (KDEC), about 13 nm southwest; both have control towers, so check the sectional chart and expect to work with the tower or approach control near them. Abraham Lincoln Capital Airport (KSPI) at Springfield is about 47 nm west. Summer haze and thunderstorms are common; flat terrain makes low-level navigation easy but offers few natural landmarks beyond the towns and water.
