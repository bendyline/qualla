# Next Stop, Vinegar Hill

A town named for a Polish Revolutionary War hero lost its county seat when the railroad passed it by, kept its Lincoln-era courthouse, and earned the nickname Vinegar Hill.

**Location:** 40.0100, -89.2844

**Categories:** Cities in Illinois, Cities in Logan County, Illinois, Populated places established in 1836, Polish-American culture in Illinois, 1836 establishments in Illinois

## Introduction

As the train neared town, the conductor would call out, “Vinegar Hill next stop.” In the Prohibition era, local histories say, Mount Pulaski stayed “wet” longer than the dry towns around it, with thirteen prosperous saloons and a few bootleggers, and visitors would arrive with empty flasks and leave with full ones. That nickname is the mischievous part of the town’s past. The rest is quieter and odder: a prairie settlement named for a Polish hero of the American Revolution, a county seat that followed the railroad out of town, and a brick courthouse where the circuit lawyer Abraham Lincoln argued cases and which Illinois later restored as a historic site.

## A Polish Name on the Prairie

Mount Pulaski dates to 1836. According to the Illinois Times, former Springfield merchant Jabez Capps and several others established the town, and they chose a name honoring Casimir Pulaski, the Polish nobleman who came to America during the Revolutionary War. The town sits atop a glacial ridge dating to the Illinoian Stage, near the geographic center of the state.

It is a small place by any measure: 1.13 square miles, all of it land, with 1,566 people at the 2010 census and 1,537 at the 2020 census. In the middle of the nineteenth century, though, it was the center of Logan County’s legal life.

## The Seat the Railroad Skipped

Before 1848, Logan County used a frame courthouse at Postville. Wooden courthouses burned too easily, so county leaders went looking for a community that would help pay for a masonry one. Mount Pulaski’s citizens combined to contribute $2,700 toward the $3,000 required, and the result was a two-story brick Greek Revival building, with pediments spanning its length and pilasters at each corner. From 1848 to 1855 the town was the county seat.

Lincoln had been representing central Illinois in the U.S. House when the courthouse went up. Back in Illinois in 1849, he resumed his practice on the Eighth Circuit and often visited Mount Pulaski, which lies about 25 miles northeast of Springfield, a day’s journey by the horse and light buggy he used.

Then the railroad arrived, and it chose another route. In the early 1850s developers built a line northeast from Springfield through Logan County, one that would become the Chicago and Alton Railroad. It became the focus of county development, and it did not go near Mount Pulaski. The railroad and county leaders agreed to move the seat to a spot near the county’s center, directly on the new tracks, and the new county seat was named Lincoln, after the lawyer, who by then had made many friends throughout the county. Voters affirmed the move in an 1853 referendum, and by 1855 the court and county government had gone.

## Brick With a Second Act

When the county seat left, the building’s days as a courthouse ended, but it found other work. It became a school, then Mount Pulaski’s city hall and jail, and then its post office. County leaders’ fear of fire, meanwhile, was borne out in 1857, when a blaze destroyed the relocated county records, including the dockets of the circuit court. As a result, very little is known of Lincoln’s specific cases in Logan County during the Mount Pulaski years, although two patent disputes argued in this courtroom are still remembered: the Cast Iron Tombstone Case and the Horological Cradle Case.

In the 1930s, Illinois Governor Henry Horner launched a statewide search for surviving public buildings that Lincoln had known. Mount Pulaski turned its old courthouse over to the state in 1936, for a single dollar according to one account (the state’s own site says it bought the building in 1935). The state then spent 1936 to 1939 restoring it to resemble a working county courthouse. It joined the National Register of Historic Places on August 3, 1978, and a member of the Logan County planning commission at the time said it “ranks among the finest and best-preserved pre-1850 buildings in the state.” It is one of only two remaining courthouses from the Eighth Circuit, and visitors can take guided tours of its recreated county offices and courtroom.

## Names on the Hilltopper Roster

Mount Pulaski has one high school, home of the Hilltoppers, and the town’s list of notable people runs well past what a population of about 1,500 would suggest. Vaughn De Leath, born here in 1894, broadcast “Swanee River” from Lee de Forest’s New York station in January 1920, a debut sometimes billed as the first by a woman on radio, although de Forest’s earlier experimental broadcasts had also featured women singers. The artist and Disney Imagineer Herbert Ryman (1910–1989) is listed among the town’s notable people as having lived here in his youth, though his own biography places his boyhood in Vernon and Decatur. Nicholas L. Hubbard, born in town, worked as a grain dealer and farmer and served as an Illinois state legislator, while Bob Trumpy, who is listed as born here (accounts of his birthplace differ), played in the NFL and became a broadcaster.

The list also includes John Schlitt, lead singer for Head East and Petra; Henry Pierson Crowe (1899–1991), a Marine Corps colonel who attended high school in town; and Dennis Werth, a 1971 graduate of Mount Pulaski High School who later played in Major League Baseball. The county seat followed the railroad. What stayed behind was a brick courthouse, a ridge-top town and a nickname with a story of its own.
