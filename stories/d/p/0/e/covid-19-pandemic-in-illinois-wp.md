# COVID-19 pandemic in Illinois

From the nation's second confirmed case, a Chicago woman, to a fall surge that filled hospitals, the COVID-19 pandemic reshaped Illinois in 2020, closing its schools and streets, falling hardest on its Black and Latino communities, and reaching every one of its 102 counties.

## Introduction

She had spent the holidays in Wuhan, China, visiting a hospitalized relative and other family members who were sick with respiratory illnesses. After she returned to Chicago in January 2020, the woman in her sixties began to feel ill herself. On January 24, Illinois health officials announced that she had the novel coronavirus, making her the state's first confirmed case and only the second in the United States. Days later her husband tested positive too, and the CDC confirmed that his was the first known human-to-human transmission of the virus in the country. Both recovered. What followed over the next year touched every one of Illinois's 102 counties.

## The First Weeks

The couple were isolated at St. Alexius Medical Center in suburban Hoffman Estates, released to home isolation on February 7, and cleared a week later. A third Illinois case did not appear until February 29. Then, on March 8, officials confirmed a case in a Cook County man in his sixties who had neither traveled to an affected area nor been in contact with a known patient. It was the first evidence that the virus was spreading within the community. The count climbed fast: 11 cases on March 9, 46 by March 13, 105 by March 16. On March 9, Governor J. B. Pritzker issued a disaster proclamation, the state's equivalent of a state of emergency, and began holding daily press briefings with the Illinois Department of Public Health. Cases soon appeared downstate, in Woodford, Cumberland and St. Clair counties, and the state's first death, a Chicago woman in her sixties, was announced on March 17.

## A State Stays Home

Chicago canceled its St. Patrick's Day parade and the dyeing of the Chicago River. The governor closed every school in the state, shut bars and restaurants to sit-in diners, and banned gatherings of fifty or more. On March 20 he announced a stay-at-home order starting the next day; first set to end April 7, it was extended to April 30 and then to May 29. Workers began converting McCormick Place into an alternate care facility for 3,000 patients, a $15 million project paid for by FEMA. The Illinois State Fair canceled most of its program for the first time since World War II. People found other ways to be together. On March 21, thousands of residents of Chicago's Rogers Park neighborhood joined a #chicagosingalong of Bon Jovi's "Livin' on a Prayer," and Bon Jovi sent congratulations on Instagram. By mid-April, light shows had become a nightly 8 p.m. ritual in the South Loop.

## The Weight of Loss

Every number in the daily reports was a person. On March 28 the governor announced that an infant in Chicago had died after testing positive, which state health officials said was the first such infant death in the United States. On April 16 the state reported 125 additional deaths in a single day, bringing the toll past a thousand. The virus moved through prisons and jails; the first cases at Cook County Jail were announced on March 23, and even as hundreds of detainees were released as a precaution, more than 200 detainees there had tested positive by early April. At the LaSalle Veterans' Home, an outbreak that began on Veterans Day, November 11, infected most of the residents, and by December 21 thirty-four of them had died. Each of them had a family and a history that no daily tally could hold.

## An Unequal Burden

The pandemic did not strike evenly. In early April, news organizations analyzing state, county and city data found that African Americans accounted for 42 percent of coronavirus deaths and 30 percent of confirmed cases in Illinois while making up only 14 percent of the state's population. Latino residents, too, tested positive at higher rates than other groups throughout 2020. Chicago Mayor Lori Lightfoot announced a racial equity rapid-response team and more bus service for the South and West sides, and Black activists and elected officials called for rent and mortgage holidays, utility freezes and better paid sick leave. Federal health officials emphasized that the disproportionate toll was not a matter of genetics but of social conditions that exposed people of color to more risk. When vaccines arrived, the Chicago Tribune reported that Black and Latino residents were being vaccinated at half the rate of white residents, and U.S. Rep. Jesús "Chuy" García pointed to language barriers, healthcare deserts and fear of deportation.

## The Second Surge

In May the governor unveiled Restore Illinois, a five-phase reopening plan, and by June 26 the whole state had reached Phase 4, with restaurants and bars open again under distancing and mask rules. The reprieve did not last. On October 30 the state recorded 6,943 new cases, its highest yet, and records kept falling, reaching 15,415 on November 13, when Illinois passed New York for the fourth-most cases of any state. By November 11 more than 5,000 Illinoisans were hospitalized with coronavirus-like illness, 951 of them in intensive care. On November 20 the entire state entered Tier 3 restrictions, closing indoor dining, casinos, theaters and museums. By then more than 10,000 Illinois residents had died, and the grim reports that followed Thanksgiving, swelled in part by a holiday reporting backlog, brought the deadliest single day of reporting yet.
