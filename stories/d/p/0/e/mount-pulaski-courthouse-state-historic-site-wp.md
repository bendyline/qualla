# The Courthouse That Missed the Train

A brick courthouse built to withstand fire lost its county seat to a railroad, then found a second life as a state historic site.

**Location:** 40.0091, -89.2845

**Categories:** Government buildings completed in 1848, Abraham Lincoln National Heritage Area, Former courthouses in Illinois, Former post office buildings, Former school buildings in the United States, Greek Revival architecture in Illinois, Illinois State Historic Sites, Museums in Logan County, Illinois, Law museums in Illinois, Defunct schools in Illinois

## Introduction

Logan County's leaders feared fire, and in the end they were proved right. The county's courthouse before 1848, at Postville, was a frame building, and frame courthouses were especially vulnerable to flames, so county leaders went looking for a community that would help pay for brick. Mount Pulaski stepped forward. The fire came anyway, in 1857, when a blaze destroyed the relocated county records, the dockets of the circuit court among them. That is one reason relatively little is known about the cases Abraham Lincoln argued in the brick courthouse that Mount Pulaski built.

## Brick for a Frame County

Before 1848 the people of Logan County used a frame courthouse at Postville, close to the center of the county. Citizens of Mount Pulaski combined to contribute $2,700 toward the $3,000 needed for a new masonry structure, and local residents built it in 1847 and 1848. The result was a two-story brick Greek Revival building with pediments spanning the length of the building and a pilaster at each corner. Six county offices filled the first floor, among them the circuit clerk, the sheriff, the surveyor, the treasurer and the school commissioner. Upstairs were the courtroom, the jury room and the judge's chamber.

## Riding the Circuit

When the courthouse was new in 1848, Abraham Lincoln was serving central Illinois in the U.S. House of Representatives. Back in Illinois in 1849, he resumed practicing law on the Eighth Circuit, a route that kept him on the road for about six months of each year. Mount Pulaski lies about 25 miles northeast of Springfield, a day's journey by the horse and light buggy he used, and he often visited the courthouse, where in the second-floor courtroom he argued, won and lost cases. Other lawyers who rode the circuit, among them Stephen Douglas, William Herndon and John Todd Stuart, practiced there too. Two cases remembered from the courthouse were patent disputes, the Cast Iron Tombstone Case and the Horological Cradle Case. In the second, Lincoln was hired to defend two brothers who had licensed the patent for a self-rocking cradle run by clockwork. The patent turned out to protect only the painted decoration, and when the case reached the Illinois Supreme Court, it ruled against his clients.

## The Railroad Decides

In the early 1850s developers built a railroad northeastward from Springfield through Logan County, on a line that would become the Chicago and Alton. It did not go near Mount Pulaski, which sits in the southeastern quarter of the county. The railroad became the focus of county development, and the railroad and county leaders agreed to move the county seat to a spot very near the center of the county and directly on the line. The new seat was named Lincoln, after the lawyer, who by then had made many friends throughout the county. County voters affirmed the move in a referendum in 1853, and the court and county government had left Mount Pulaski by 1855.

## Second Lives

The brick building did not stand empty. It served as a school, as the Mount Pulaski city hall and jail, and as the local post office, and one account adds a library and a community building. In the 1930s Illinois Governor Henry Horner launched a statewide search for surviving public buildings that Lincoln would have known. Mount Pulaski turned its old courthouse over to the state, in 1936 by most accounts (the state's own site says it bought the building in 1935), and the state restored it extensively between 1936 and 1939 to resemble its life as a county courthouse. The Illinois Department of Natural Resources operates the building as a state historic site, with guided tours of the recreated county offices and courtroom, and upstairs visitors walk on the original floor. The courthouse joined the National Register of Historic Places on August 3, 1978, for both its local history and its architecture. A member of the Logan County planning commission said at the time that it "ranks among the finest and best-preserved pre-1850 buildings in the state." It is also one of only two remaining courthouses from the Eighth Circuit, the other being in Metamora.
