# Le Roy, Illinois

Sold in 1835 with a promise that no site could surpass it for beauty, LeRoy survived the town-founding boom, built its own "Pumpkin Vine" railroad to fight high freight rates, and became a home base between two university towns.

**Location:** 40.3472, -88.7622

**Categories:** Cities in Illinois, Cities in McLean County, Illinois, Populated places established in 1835, 1835 establishments in Illinois

## Introduction

"No situation can well surpass it for beauty." That was the promise in the advertisement Asahel Gridley and Merritt L. Covell placed in the Sangamon Journal in November 1835, selling lots in a brand-new town on the Illinois prairie. They described an eminence gently descending in every direction, an open expanse of prairie to the north and west, and occasional groves of timber breaking the view. Then they closed with a flourish of confidence: "A further description is deemed unnecessary, as all who wish to make investments will doubtless visit it previous to investing." The bidding, later sources said, was spirited. The town they laid out that month was LeRoy, and unlike most of its rivals, it is still here, a McLean County city of 3,512 at the 2020 census.

## Two "Generals" and a One-Store Rival

Gridley and Covell were Bloomington businessmen who had served together in the Black Hawk War, Covell as a captain and Gridley as a lieutenant. Both men were popularly called "General," a title that for Gridley came from his later rank as brigadier general of the county militia. Gridley went on to serve in the Illinois General Assembly, starting in 1840, and by 1859 was recognized as McLean County's first millionaire. They laid out LeRoy on November 28, 1835, on a low mound where the Bloomington-to-Danville state road crossed the road from Shelbyville to Chicago. It was the first of eight towns laid out within the county's present boundaries during the Illinois town-founding boom that peaked in 1836. Their chief problem lay a mile and a half to the southwest: Munroe, a place consisting of a single store run by John W. Baddeley, an immigrant from Whitchurch, Shropshire, England, who had quickly entered a thousand acres in Empire Township. The founders offered him twenty-seven prime lots to move his store to LeRoy. Baddeley agreed. He lost most of his fortune in 1837 but kept doing business in LeRoy for many years.

## Around the Public Circle

Like most central Illinois towns of the 1830s, LeRoy was built around a central "Public Circle," with streets joining the square midway along each side. Apart from some rounding at the corners to ease traffic, the square keeps its 1835 shape today. The original town held twenty-five blocks, and its street names, from Cedar and Cherry to Walnut and Vine, are virtually identical to those of Lexington, which Gridley also co-founded, though LeRoy has no South Street. Growth was slow at first. Log cabins went up in the fall of 1836, Edgar Conkling built a frame store and, with a partner, laid out a vast addition that tripled the town's size, and Hiram Buck came to run a hotel and became postmaster in 1838. Two early mills burned. Still, by 1850 LeRoy was reported to be the second-largest town in the county, and it became a city on August 10, 1874. Of the eight McLean County towns born in the 1830s boom, only LeRoy, Danvers and Lexington survive.

## The Railroad They Built Themselves

After the Civil War, LeRoy's citizens were convinced only a railroad could secure their future. They led the campaign for what became the Indiana, Bloomington and Western Railway, voted 202 to 6 in 1867 to tax themselves for it, and eventually subscribed $75,000 in bonds. The first train steamed into town on May 1, 1870. Disappointment followed fast. The bonds were promptly hypothecated, used as security to borrow money, so residents kept paying each year with no say in how the line was run. The railroad sank into legal and financial trouble, service was poor and rates were exorbitant. Rather than simply complain, the 1879 county history says, LeRoy built its own. Begun in the mid-1870s, a narrow-gauge line ran down the center of Oak Street and east to Rantoul, where it met the Illinois Central, and on toward West Lebanon, Indiana. The idea, as that history tells it, was to force freight rates down by giving the I.B. & W. some competition; other accounts credit Rantoul attorney Benjamin F. Gifford, who was unhappy with Illinois Central rates, and call the line the Havana, Rantoul and Eastern. Local people did much of the labor and called it the "Pumpkin Vine." It never carried much traffic, but it was a great source of local pride, and it was eventually sold to the Illinois Central and widened to standard gauge.

## From Depot to Interstate Exit

By 1900, LeRoy had 1,629 people, two newspapers, four churches, a hotel, two grain elevators, three doctors, three lawyers and twenty-five stores. Then, slowly, it stopped being a railroad town. Passenger service on the Pumpkin Vine ended in 1931, and on the Bloomington–Urbana line in 1957. The depot came down in 1967, the last train ran in 1980, and the tracks were torn up soon after. The highway took over. When Interstate 74 was completed in the early 1970s, new businesses grew up near the exit, and LeRoy increasingly became a residential center for both Bloomington–Normal and Champaign–Urbana. After growing just under twelve percent between 1900 and 1950, the population more than doubled by the century's end.

## Festivals and Famous Voices

Modern LeRoy keeps several parks and playgrounds and the Replex, a community recreation center with indoor and outdoor pools, a weight room, a gymnasium and meeting rooms. Every August, the town throws its annual LeRoy Fall Festival, getting a head start on the season. The town's notable names range widely. John Allen Sterling, born near LeRoy, served as a U.S. Representative from 1903 to 1913 and from 1915 to 1918. Actress Betty Jane Watson and outfielder Tim Hendryx are counted among its people, and Bret Iwan, who became the voice of Mickey Mouse, attended LeRoy High School from 1996 to 2000. Downtown, commerce still clusters along Center Street just east of the square, exactly where it began in the 1830s, which suggests the founders chose their ground well.

## Flight Context

LeRoy lies at 40.3472°N, 88.7622°W in southeastern McLean County, Illinois, southeast of Bloomington. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL, look for the square "Public Circle" at the center of the town grid, with Center Street's commercial blocks just to its east, and Interstate 74 passing close by with its exit-side businesses. The railroad tracks are long gone, so the interstate and the square are the best guides. Central Illinois Regional Airport (KBMI) at Bloomington is about 11 nautical miles to the northwest, and University of Illinois Willard Airport (KCMI) near Champaign is about 29 nautical miles to the southeast. The surrounding prairie is flat, with long sightlines on clear days.
