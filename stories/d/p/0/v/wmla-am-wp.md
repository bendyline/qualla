# WMLA (AM)

For 28 years a small AM station at 1440 kHz, transmitting from fields near Downs, served Bloomington–Normal under a parade of call letters and owners, surviving a courtroom feud and a badge scandal before going silent in 1990.

**Location:** 40.4236, -88.8750

## Introduction

Radio stations are supposed to be known by their call letters. The one at 1440 on the AM dial, licensed to Normal, Illinois, went through so many that its history reads like an alphabet puzzle: WIOK, WAKC, WRBA and WMLA, and then WIRE and WBCI, the last of them assigned after the station had already gone quiet. Between 1962 and 1990 it passed from a Beardstown couple to a Rockford buyer, a Tulsa broadcaster, a former CBS News correspondent, a Watseka businessman and two radio groups. Along the way its owners feuded in court, one was charged with impersonating a police officer, and the last one simply decided not to turn the AM transmitter back on.

## A Daytimer North of Downs

It began with Robert and Margareta Sudbrink of Beardstown. Through their McLean County Broadcasting Company, they won a construction permit on November 27, 1961, for a new daytime-only AM station at Normal. WIOK debuted on September 17, 1962, with full-service programming, a Mutual Broadcasting System affiliation and a transmitter north of Downs. Two years later the Sudbrinks bought property on South Main Street in Normal and moved the studios there, and later that year the station was approved for nighttime service from a second transmitter site farther north in Downs. Selling proved harder. An Illinois Broadcasting Company bid filed in 1966 was dismissed by the Federal Communications Commission in January 1967, because WIOK's signal overlapped impermissibly with WSOY in Decatur, which that company also owned. Three months later John R. Livingston of Rockford paid $265,000. In those years WIOK's sports director was Joe Tait, who went on to become the radio play-by-play voice of the Cleveland Cavaliers.

## Country Music and a Newsman's Station

In November 1969 Livingston sold to WIOK, Inc., owned by S. Carl Mark, who also owned KAKC in Tulsa, Oklahoma. In 1971 Mark renamed the Normal station WAKC to match his Tulsa holding and switched it to country music. Three years later he sold it to Great Oaks Broadcasting, a partnership headed by Allan Jackson, a former CBS News correspondent. That September WAKC traded its Mutual affiliation for CBS Radio, and it later moved to a 24-hour broadcast day. Jackson did not get long to enjoy it. After two weeks in ill health, he died on April 26, 1976, and his death opened a turbulent chapter in the station's life.

## The Feud at 1440

The dispute pitted Jackson's son Stephen against Great Oaks' other half-owner, Douglas H. Donoho. Donoho said that shortly before Jackson died, the two partners had made a pact giving a surviving owner the option to buy out the other. According to Donoho, the Jackson family refused to honor that agreement, mismanaged the station until it was losing listeners and facing possible foreclosure, and kept him away from the premises and business records by posting an armed guard at the station. In July, Donoho won a preliminary injunction against the Jacksons. The conflict finally ended in 1977 with yet another sale, to Robert Bivens and associates, operating as the Iroquois County Broadcasting Company of Watseka. When they took over on October 24, the call letters became WRBA and network programming was dropped.

## Cubs Games, Women on the Mic and a Badge

Under Bivens, WRBA became the area's Chicago Cubs Radio Network affiliate at the start of the 1978 season, and by 1980 it had rejoined both CBS and Mutual. It broadened into a full-service adult contemporary format, and in 1983 it put an all-female announcer lineup on the air during the day, along with what was purportedly the only female play-by-play announcer in the United States. Then came an odd headline. In April 1984, Bivens was charged with impersonating a police officer after two McLean County sheriff's deputies found he was carrying an expired Iroquois County deputy badge, which he used to get quickly to his radio stations if they failed. He was already on his way out. Three months earlier he had applied to sell the station for $376,000 to Withers Broadcasting of Mount Vernon, owner of WMLA at 92.7 FM. That fall Withers moved the WMLA call letters and country format to the AM side, while the FM became rock outlet WTWN, both fed by the Satellite Music Network.

## Signing Off Without Fanfare

In 1987 Withers sold WMLA-AM-FM for $700,000, plus $250,000 more if the FM frequency were upgraded, to the David Keister Stations group, also known as Mid America Radio, of Martinsville, Indiana. On June 2, 1990, the company took both stations off the air to overhaul the FM operation. The overhaul brought the FM a format change, a power increase and a new frequency. The AM never returned. The company claimed the FCC had found 1440 "not feasible for the area," even though it still held a valid license. The call letters kept moving even in silence. At the end of 1989 the FCC had quietly granted the station the call sign WIRE, letting Mid America hold on to heritage letters from the Indianapolis market, and in September 1990 the license became WBCI, swapped with Mid America's station in Lebanon, Indiana, whose listeners had been confusing WBCI with Indianapolis's WIBC.

## Flight Context

This story is pinned at 40.4236°N, 88.8750°W, in the area north of Downs in McLean County, Illinois, where the station's transmitters stood, on the southeastern edge of Bloomington–Normal. Nothing of the station remains to see, so use it as a waypoint rather than a target. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL, look for Downs just to the south, between U.S. Route 150 and Interstate 74, with the Bloomington–Normal urban area spreading to the northwest. Central Illinois Regional Airport (KBMI) is only about 4 nautical miles to the north-northwest, so expect airport traffic here and observe KBMI's airspace requirements. Visibility is generally good over the flat terrain.
