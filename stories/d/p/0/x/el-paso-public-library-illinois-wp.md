# Stone Turrets on First Street

After fire destroyed El Paso's volunteer-run library in 1894, the town won a Carnegie grant and in 1907 dedicated a turreted Richardsonian Romanesque library that still sits at the core of the expanded building.

**Location:** 40.7396, -89.0186

**Categories:** Libraries on the National Register of Historic Places in Illinois, Romanesque Revival architecture in Illinois, Library buildings completed in 1907, National Register of Historic Places in Woodford County, Illinois, Carnegie libraries in Illinois

## Introduction

In 1894 fire destroyed the Eagle Block building in El Paso, Illinois, and the town's library burned with it. The books had been kept in one of the building's rooms by the Ladies' Library Association, which had been running a library in El Paso since 1873. Thirteen years later the town dedicated a two-story library of buff stone at 149 West First Street, its entrance framed by a pair of round turrets. Built with Carnegie money and listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1994, the building is still the core of the town's library.

## The Ladies' Library

El Paso's library began as a volunteer effort. The Ladies' Library Association formed in 1873 and, before it had a building of its own, ran the library from various locations around town, among them that room in the Eagle Block. The fire wiped the slate clean. Afterward a new library started over in a temporary home while the association weighed two questions at once: where a permanent building might come from, and whether the city should take control. The second question was settled first. In 1904 the association voted to donate its library to the city, and in 1905 the city council backed a library tax to support a public library.

## Six Thousand Dollars and Eighteen Hundred Books

The money for a building came from Andrew Carnegie: a $6,000 grant that a list of Illinois Carnegie libraries dates to September 1905. The library's own history places the moment in 1906 and describes it as the association realizing its "thirty year dream" of a permanent public library. Construction finished the following year, and the library was dedicated on February 22, 1907, though a 1954 local history dates the dedication to 1906. Its shelves opened with 1,300 books from the Ladies' Library Association and 500 new ones ordered for the new building, and El Paso's citizens donated another $1,000 to buy more.

## A Chicago Model in Buff Stone

The design came from Paul O. Moratz of Bloomington, an architect who also designed the entrance gates and a house in his home city's White Place neighborhood. Moratz chose the Richardsonian Romanesque style and, by one account, drew his inspiration from the Morgan Park Library in Chicago. The result is a two-story building with a buff stone exterior over a brick foundation. Round turrets rise on either side of the front entrance, linked by a segmental arch above the door. Overhead, the hip roof shows exposed rafter tails all the way around, and dentils trim the eaves above the entrance. When the library joined the National Register of Historic Places in 1994, it was listed as part of a multiple property submission covering Illinois's Carnegie libraries, with significance in architecture, education and social history.

## Room to Grow

Nearly a century on, the library was outgrowing its building. The library board began discussing how to balance the historic character of the building with growing needs for room, technology and access, and in 2003 it hired a consultant to assess the building and plan an addition. The plans called for making every level accessible while keeping the distinctive look of the original. Fundraising ran through 2012 and 2013, construction began in 2013, and the expanded library celebrated its grand opening on May 3, 2014, when hundreds of people came to explore it. According to the library, the expansion was paid for entirely with grants and donations.

## Rooms with Names

Inside, several rooms now carry family names. The Edward and Marjory Heiken Children's Room gave the children's library far more space, with thousands of picture books and what the library calls its "infamous dollhouse." The Drake Family-Heartland Bank Community Room hosts library programs and community organizations alike, with seating for up to 75. The original building holds the Lester Pfister History and Genealogy Room, home to a rotating collection of local artifacts and a computer for reading digitized El Paso newspapers from 1888 to 2003. Upstairs in the old building, a bequest from Dorothy Yambert's estate created a cozy reading room. Now called the El Paso District Library, it stands in Jefferson Park at the heart of town, close to the elementary, middle and high schools.
