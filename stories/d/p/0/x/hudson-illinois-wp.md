# Hudson, Illinois

Founded in 1836 by a pooled-money "colony" of strangers, Hudson carried New England habits onto the Illinois prairie, from its ring of out lots to a village herdsman who, local histories say, rounded up the town's cattle every morning until 1913.

**Location:** 40.6058, -88.9872

**Categories:** Villages in McLean County, Illinois, Populated places established in 1836, 1836 establishments in Illinois, Villages in Illinois

## Introduction

At six o'clock every morning, a man walked the streets of Hudson collecting cows. It was an official post. As village herdsman, he gathered the cattle kept in town and drove them out to pasture beyond the town limits, following a custom carried west from old New England, and by local accounts Hudson kept the office going until 1913. That stubborn attachment to older ways runs all through the story of this McLean County village, home to about 1,750 people at the 2020 census. Hudson began as a business plan, drawn up by people who were largely strangers to one another.

## Strangers With a Shared Purse

In the 1830s, "colonies" were a popular way to settle the West. Rather than migrating alone and buying land piecemeal, participants pooled their money, sent a committee to choose a large tract, and divided it among themselves. The Illinois Land Association formed on those terms in Jacksonville in February 1836. Each member paid $235 into the common pool and, by the usual account, was promised four kinds of property: three lots in town, one "out lot," 160 acres of prairie for farming, and 20 acres of timber for fences, firewood and building. Any land left untaken would be sold, with the profits shared. The word colony implied no common faith or cause. Several of Hudson's founders came from New York, but others came from Maine, Massachusetts, Pennsylvania and Illinois, and many had no connection to one another before they signed on. Hudson is one of two such colonial settlements in McLean County; the other, the Rhode Island colony, lay in the county's southwest.

## A Town Without a Square

Most central Illinois towns of the 1830s were laid out around a public square. Hudson's 1836 plan had none. Instead it borrowed an arrangement centuries old in New England, where farmers disliked keeping their livestock far from town: a core of standard "in lots" encircled by a ring of slightly larger "out lots" of varying sizes. Why the colonists chose such an old-fashioned design remains unclear. The original town held 30 blocks of in lots, eight lots to a block, and because each member drew several lots, houses in the older part of Hudson still stand farther apart than in other towns founded at the same time. Broadway was planned as the main street, 120 feet wide, while every other street got 80. In time the distinction faded, and in lots and out lots alike became simply places to build a house.

## Short on Timber, Long on Patience

The colony arrived just as the ground gave way. In 1837 the land market soured, settlement slowed and Illinois sank into a deep depression. The organizers discovered they could not buy the promised 20 acres of timber per member, because earlier settlers had already claimed most of the woodland. A few families got the full share, but most received only 2.5 acres, too little to meet their needs. Arguments broke out over land-sale profits, and only about twenty families actually moved onto the colony's land. They built well anyway, putting up a number of substantial frame houses, and there was a school nearby and two churches. What Hudson lacked was business. That changed in 1854, when the Illinois Central Railroad passed just west of town. Empty lots filled, and a new commercial district grew along the west-side out lots facing the tracks.

## Black Soil and a Steady Climb

With the railroad in place, Hudson's future was secure. The tallgrass prairie around it proved to be some of the most fertile farmland in the world, and settlers poured in through the 1850s and 1860s. Early farms raised corn and oats alongside a great deal of livestock; by the mid-twentieth century livestock was slowly declining and soybeans had replaced oats as the second crop. The village grew slowly but steadily. Hudson incorporated in 1873, had telephone service by 1883, switched on its first electric lights in 1912 and built a new town hall in 1916. Late in the twentieth century, its closeness to Bloomington and Normal made it an increasingly popular place to live, and Interstate 39, opened in stages between 1989 and 1992, tied Hudson directly to Normal, five miles south, and El Paso, nine miles north.

## A Name With a Question Mark

Tradition holds that Hudson was named for Hudson, New York, in Columbia County, supposedly the home of its first settlers. Ruth Bitting Hamm, in her 1976 book on the Hudson Colony, found a problem with that story: some settlers did come from Queens County, New York, but none came from anywhere near the town of Hudson. She suggests the colonists more likely named their town for the Hudson River itself. The village has sent a few names of its own into the wider world. Ben Corbett, a film actor known mainly for westerns, was born here in 1892. The journalist Melville Elijah Stone, born here in 1848, founded the Chicago Daily News and later led the Associated Press, and the essayist Elbert Hubbard, who grew up in the village, is also counted among Hudson's notable people.

## Flight Context

Hudson lies at 40.6058°N, 88.9872°W in northwestern McLean County, about 5 nautical miles north of Normal. From 2,500 to 3,500 feet AGL, look for Interstate 39 running along the village's western boundary, with the Exit 5 interchange marking the town; the compact old grid sits just east of the highway, surrounded on every side by flat, squared-off corn and soybean fields. Follow I-39 south to pick up the Bloomington-Normal urban area. Central Illinois Regional Airport (KBMI) at Bloomington-Normal is about 8 nautical miles to the south-southeast, making Hudson an easy first landmark on a departure north from KBMI. KBMI has a control tower, so check the sectional chart and expect to work with the tower or approach control near the airport. The prairie terrain is flat and open, so visibility is usually limited only by summer haze or winter low overcast.
