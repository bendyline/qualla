# The Town the Winter Built

A hard winter stopped railroad crews at Joseph Schrock's farm in 1887 and a village grew at the end of the tracks; in 1999 another winter gave Congerville the coldest reading in Illinois history up to then.

**Location:** 40.6142, -89.2067

**Categories:** Villages in Woodford County, Illinois, Peoria metropolitan area, Populated places in the United States established in 1888, Villages in Illinois

## Introduction

Winter has made Congerville twice. In 1887, as local history tells it, a severe winter stopped railroad crews at the edge of Joseph Schrock's farm in Woodford County, Illinois, and a town took shape where the tracks ended. More than a century later, on January 5, 1999, the thermometer here read −36°F, at the time the coldest temperature ever officially recorded in Illinois. The village had 497 residents at the 2020 census and lies east of Peoria, within the Peoria metropolitan area.

## A Wagon from Ohio

The family that first gave the village its name came from Lorraine. Joseph Schrock was born in 1828 and crossed to America with his parents in 1831, at the age of three. When his father, Johannes, moved the family from Ohio to Illinois, it was 22-year-old Joseph who drove the horses, cattle and household goods west on his own. Many years later he showed his children and grandchildren the American elm along the road west of Bloomington where he had camped under his wagon on the last night of the trip.

In 1852 he married Magdalena Guingrich. Her father, remembered by descendants as "Gold Rush Guingrich," came home with $18,000 in gold coins and invested it in farmland for his seven children, and the Schrock homestead was part of that purchase. Joseph first came to Woodford County in 1864, buying land that year and adding more as he fenced and planted it, until he owned about 320 acres on Section 22 of Montgomery Township, divided into two farms.

## Loss and Long Sundays

The prairie could be cruel. In 1855 cholera reached the log cabin of Christian and Magdalena Smith, Schrock relatives who lived in the country near the future town. Christian died first. Three days later Magdalena died, followed by their nineteen-year-old daughter, Barbara, and a few hours after that by their six-year-old son, John.

For Joseph and Magdalena Schrock, faith meant long Sundays. They were among the earliest converts to the New Amish movement, later called the Apostolic Christian Church. Services were held at Dillon, near Tremont, and at Partridge Prairie, near Metamora, each 22 miles from their farm. The couple set out at four in the morning with a team and wagon, forded the Mackinaw River because there was no bridge, and came home the same day. Their son Jonathan remembered ice floating on the Mackinaw when he was baptized.

## Where the Tracks Stopped

In 1887 a railroad that the Schrock family remembers as the Nickel Plate was being built from Bloomington to Peoria. "According to legend and history," as a Schrock family account puts it, the contractors had laid track as far as Schrock's farm when a severe winter halted them. Railroad workers and contractors built housing at the end of the line to wait out the cold. Others moved in too, many hoping to work on the railroad once building resumed. On January 2, 1888, the site was surveyed for Joseph Schrock, and on January 7 a plat was recorded naming the community the Town of Schrock.

## Three Names in Seven Months

The Schrock name did not last. On February 27, 1888, the town was renamed Conger, after Ben Conger, an early settler who owned a considerable amount of land and had a reputation as a fine hunter. On July 31 it became Congerville. Schrock descendants tell an "inside" story that the change may have had something to do with the division between the Amish Mennonites and the New Amish, or Apostolic Christians.

Whatever the reason, the family stayed. Joseph had built a house on the farm in the early 1880s, and he died in 1901 at 73. His son Jonathan lived on the Schrock farm until his death in 1947, when, the family history says, the whole south end of Congerville belonged to the Schrocks, including five houses and an office building. That same year Jonathan's sons Joseph and Alvin started the Schrock Hybrid Corn Company.

## Thirty-Six Below

For most of its history Congerville was simply small. Then came the morning of January 5, 1999. The −36°F reading took the state record from Mount Carroll, which had recorded −35°F in 1930, and Congerville held the title for twenty years, until Mount Carroll took it back on January 31, 2019, at −38°F. The 2000 census, taken the year after the record, found 466 residents with a median age of 30, and about three-quarters of the village's 163 households were married couples.

The village covers just over a square mile, nearly all of it land. Its notable people include Andy Studebaker, who grew up in Congerville and played linebacker for the Philadelphia Eagles, Kansas City Chiefs and Indianapolis Colts, among other teams; the farmer and politician Simon E. Lantz, who lived there; and the geneticist Herman E. Brockman, who died in Congerville in 2025.

## Flight Context

Congerville sits at 40.6142°N, 89.2067°W in Woodford County, Illinois, at roughly 740 feet above sea level. The village covers just over a square mile, so look for its small grid set in open farmland. Central Illinois Regional Airport (KBMI) at Bloomington-Normal is about 16 nautical miles to the southeast, and General Wayne A. Downing Peoria International Airport (KPIA) is about 22 nautical miles to the west; both have control towers. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL the whole village fits in one view. Flat terrain gives long sightlines in clear weather, but this is the place that once held the state's cold record, so in winter expect bitter temperatures and plan for icing in cloud.
