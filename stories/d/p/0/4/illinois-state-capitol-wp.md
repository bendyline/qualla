# Illinois State Capitol

Illinois's sixth capitol, a zinc-domed giant that took twenty years to build, stands taller than the U.S. Capitol, and waited well over a century for the statues its own legislators once judged too risqué.

## Introduction

The legislators of the 1870s would not have the statues. Architect Alfred Piquenard had designed a pair of scantily clad female figures to hold lamps on the Illinois State Capitol's grand staircase, and lawmakers found them too risqué. Plain lamps went in instead. The maidens intended for Springfield were installed at the Iowa State Capitol in Des Moines, which Piquenard was also designing, and there they remained. Only during the west wing renovation of the early 2010s did Illinois finally get its statues, in the form of replicas copied from the Iowa originals after more than a century in exile.

## Five Capitols Before This One

The current building is the sixth to serve as the Illinois capitol since statehood in 1818. The first was a leased two-story building in Kaskaskia, on the east bank of the Mississippi, rented for $4 a day. Wanting a capital in the interior, the state moved its government northeast to Vandalia, which became the capital in 1820. That capitol soon burned; a third went up in 1824 for $15,000. Within a decade many Illinoisans wanted a capital nearer the center of the state. A referendum in the 1830s picked Alton, but the legislature judged the margin too small and ignored it. In 1836 a young lawyer named Abraham Lincoln and his colleagues pushed for Springfield. Vandalia's residents responded by tearing down their 12-year-old capitol and building a fourth, of brick, for $16,000, hoping to keep the government. It didn't work. On February 25, 1837, the General Assembly voted to move the capital to Springfield.

## Lincoln's Capitol

The first brick of Springfield's first capitol was laid on July 4, 1837. Designed by John F. Rague in the Greek Revival style and built of stone quarried six miles away, it was serving as the capitol by 1840 and cost a reported $240,000 to $260,000, depending on the source, many times what any earlier capitol had cost. For years it was considered the largest and most extravagant capitol among the western frontier states. It is also closely associated with Lincoln. He argued cases there, served in the General Assembly there, and delivered his "House Divided" speech there in June 1858. In 1865, after his assassination, his body returned to lie in state in the same building. When the legislature moved out in 1876, the old capitol became the Sangamon County courthouse, which it remained until 1966. The state had taken it back by the early 1960s, and from 1966 to 1969 workers numbered its exterior stones, took the building apart and rebuilt it as the Old State Capitol State Historic Site.

## Twenty Years and $4.5 Million

After the Civil War the old building grew crowded, and on February 24, 1867, the General Assembly voted to build a larger one. Ground was broken on March 11, 1868. The Chicago architect John C. Cochrane won the design competition and partnered with Alfred Piquenard on the French Renaissance and Italianate design, with William Boyington joining the project later. Piquenard died in 1876, the same year the legislature moved in and twelve years before the building was complete. It was finished in 1888 at a total cost of $4.5 million. Its footprint is a cross with four equal wings. The architectural scholar Jean A. Follett called it "monumental in scale and rich in detail," and the inside of the dome bears her out: a plaster frieze painted to look like bronze circles the rotunda with scenes from Illinois history, and at the very top, in the oculus, a stained-glass replica of the state seal looks down.

## Taller Than Washington's

The Capitol rises 361 feet, or 405 feet to the top of its flagpole. That makes it, by Wikipedia's account, the tallest non-skyscraper capitol in the United States, and its dome stands well above the U.S. Capitol's in Washington, D.C. Its central rotunda, upper dome and tower roofs are sheathed in a zinc alloy chosen to give a silvery face that does not weather or corrode. Inside are the chambers of both houses of the General Assembly, the House of Representatives and the Senate, along with a ceremonial office for the governor, other executive offices, legislators' offices and committee rooms. Outside, the grounds hold statues and plaques, among them Andrew O'Connor's 1918 statue of Lincoln at the East Front and a bronze of Everett McKinley Dirksen, the longtime U.S. senator from Illinois.

## A Building Still Being Finished

A capitol this old is never really done. The west wing project of the early 2010s, which brought back Piquenard's maidens, cost a reported $50 million and earned LEED Gold certification. In December 2021 the state began a second, far larger phase in the north wing, first estimated at $224 million. By September 2026 the cost was put at $350 million, and the state's Architect of the Capitol said the work would wrap up that winter, about two years behind schedule. The plan called for updating fire protection, electrical and climate systems, improving accessibility, and removing asbestos installed decades earlier. It also called for tearing out mezzanine levels squeezed between floors in the 1960s and 1970s to make room for legislators and staff, restoring the original decorative paint schemes, and adding an underground parking garage and a visitor screening area, so that surface parking lots could give way to landscaped grounds for people. The mezzanines are out and the 435-space garage opened in spring 2026, while the first and second floors, a new welcome center and an underground conference center were still being finished.

## Flight Context

The Illinois State Capitol stands at 39.7983 N, 89.6547 W in downtown Springfield, Illinois. At 361 feet, with its silvery zinc-clad dome, it is a prominent landmark; from 1,500-3,000 feet AGL look for the cross-shaped building beneath the dome. The Governor's Mansion lies a few blocks to the east-southeast and the Dana–Thomas House a little farther to the southeast. Nearest airport: Abraham Lincoln Capital Airport (KSPI), about 3 nautical miles to the north-northwest; KSPI has Class C airspace, so check the sectional chart for its limits and expect to work with approach control, and remember the structure reaches 405 feet at the top of its flagpole. Summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are common over central Illinois.
