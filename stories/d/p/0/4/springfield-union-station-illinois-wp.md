# Springfield Union Station (Illinois)

Springfield's ornate 1898 Richardson Romanesque Union Station lost its clock hands, then its tower, then its trains, before reopening in 2007 as part of the Abraham Lincoln Presidential Library and Museum complex.

**Location:** 39.8036, -89.6486

## Introduction

The clock lost its hands first. In 1936, as an economy move, the hands were taken off the faces of Springfield Union Station's tower so that no one would have to keep maintaining the clock mechanism. Ten years later, in the summer of 1946, the tower itself came down to its base. For a building whose three-story, 110-foot clock tower had once made it an instantly recognizable landmark on the Springfield skyline, it was a slow unmaking, and it was only the start of the station's long journey from rail terminal to part of a museum campus.

## Built to Impress

Union Station was designed in 1896 as a shared passenger terminal for several railroads: the Baltimore and Ohio, the Chicago, Peoria and St. Louis, the Illinois Central, and the St. Louis, Peoria and Northern. The Illinois Central was the dominant carrier, and its chief architect, Francis T. Bacon, drew the plans in the Richardson Romanesque style. Construction ran through 1897 and 1898 at a cost of $75,000, and the station opened for business at 500 East Madison Street on January 2, 1898. Springfield was served by five significant rail terminals, but this one was different. The others, including the Gulf, Mobile and Ohio, Great Western, Wabash and Illinois Terminal stations, were utilitarian, while Union Station was built in a far more ornate style, a statement building for a state capital.

## Backing In on Madison Street

For all its grandeur, Union Station worked awkwardly. The Illinois Central ran it as a stub-end terminal, and passenger trains had to back into the station along tracks that ran east on Madison Street to the railroad's main line. That arrangement reflected the station's origins, when the Illinois Central served Springfield only on a route running northeast to Clinton, Gilman and Chicago, with the capital as the end of the line rather than a stop along the way. In 1899 the Illinois Central bought the Springfield to St. Louis segment of the old St. Louis, Peoria and Northern, and on June 17, 1900, it launched two premier trains, the Daylight Special and the Diamond Special, to compete with the Chicago and Alton's St. Louis-Springfield-Chicago service. By 1936 the Illinois Central's named trains on that route were the Green Diamond and the Night Diamond.

## Tenants Come and Go

The other railroads drifted away one by one. Baltimore and Ohio service through Springfield was always secondary, running from Indianapolis and Decatur on one line and between Flora, Pana, Springfield and Beardstown on another. After buying the Alton Railroad in 1931, the B&O moved its trains to the former Chicago and Alton station. The Indianapolis service was cut west of Decatur during World War II, leaving a single motorcar train on the Flora to Beardstown line, which briefly returned to Union Station after the Alton was sold to the Gulf, Mobile and Ohio in 1947 before it was discontinued on March 24, 1951. The Chicago, Peoria and St. Louis, which ran trains to Peoria and to St. Louis by way of Waverly and Alton, evolved into the Chicago and Illinois Midland. It built a smaller depot almost two miles north, and in 1937 withdrew its last passenger trains from Union Station.

## The Governor's Special

Through most of the 1960s, only one train still served the station, a shortened version of the Illinois Central's Green Diamond known as the Governor's Special. On April 30, 1971, the creation of Amtrak ended it, and with the last Illinois Central passenger train between Springfield and Chicago went the station's 73 years of active service. Amtrak consolidated Springfield's service at the former Chicago and Alton depot, about three blocks west. Union Station carried on as offices for Illinois state government and several private businesses, and in 1978 it was listed on the National Register of Historic Places. In 2007, with a rebuilt clock tower, it reopened to the public as a visitor center for the Abraham Lincoln Presidential Library and Museum complex across the street. That role has ended: the museum's own Union Station page now says the building "is currently closed to the public," and gives no reopening date, though the rebuilt 110-foot clock tower still stands.

## Flight Context

Springfield Union Station stands at 39.8036°N, 89.6486°W at 500 East Madison Street in downtown Springfield, Illinois, beside the Abraham Lincoln Presidential Library. From 1,500 to 2,000 feet AGL, look for the station's Romanesque bulk within the museum complex, just north of the Old State Capitol's square; Springfield's active Amtrak station is about three blocks to the west on the rail line through downtown. Abraham Lincoln Capital Airport (KSPI) is about 2.8 nautical miles to the north-northwest, so downtown sits close to its traffic; KSPI has Class C airspace, so check the sectional chart and expect to work with approach control. Clear days show the downtown grid well, and the domed Illinois State Capitol to the southwest is the easiest reference point.
