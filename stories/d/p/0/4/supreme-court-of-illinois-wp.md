# Supreme Court of Illinois

Illinois's highest court is elected district by district in partisan races, and its seven justices live at home, traveling to a 1908 Springfield courthouse that keeps living quarters upstairs for their stays.

**Location:** 39.7979, -89.6527

**Categories:** Springfield, Illinois, Supreme Court of Illinois, 1818 establishments in Illinois, Courts and tribunals established in 1818, State supreme courts of the United States

## Introduction

The third floor of the Illinois Supreme Court Building is closed to the public, and for good reason: people live there, at least part of the time. In many states the justices of the highest court move to the capital for their terms. In Illinois they stay home, keeping chambers in their own appellate districts and traveling to Springfield to hear oral arguments and deliberate. When they come, the 1908 building at 200 East Capitol Avenue gives them living quarters upstairs, a courthouse that doubles, a few days at a time, as a residence for the people who decide the state's hardest cases.

## A Courthouse of Its Own

For its first decades in Springfield, the court borrowed its rooms. Its earliest sessions in the city were held in an Episcopal church at the southeast corner of Third and Adams streets. It later sat in what is now the Old State Capitol, and after that in the present State Capitol, until 1908. That year the court finally got a building of its own. Designed by architect W. Carbys Zimmerman and erected at a cost of $450,500, it was dedicated when Chief Justice John P. Hand accepted the keys on February 4, 1908. The layout tells the story of how the court works. Offices for the clerk of the court fill the first floor. The second floor holds the Supreme Court's courtroom, an appellate courtroom and a law library, whose collection covers the laws of Illinois, the other states and the federal government. The living quarters sit above it all.

## Seven Justices, Five Districts

Article VI of the Illinois Constitution creates a court of seven justices elected from the state's five appellate judicial districts. Three come from the First District, Cook County, and one from each of the other four. Each serves a ten-year term, and the court chooses its own chief justice from among its members for three years at a time. Its reach is broad. The court has final appellate jurisdiction on Illinois state law, hears cases that challenge the constitutionality of laws, and takes discretionary appeals from the Illinois Appellate Court; until Illinois abolished the death penalty in 2011, it was required to hear capital cases. Along with the legislature it writes the rules for every state court, oversees lawyer discipline, and governs initial licensing through the Illinois Board of Admissions to the Bar. Its decisions are published in the official law report, Illinois Reports.

## Why Illinois Elects Its Judges

Under the state's first constitution, the legislature appointed the justices. The framers had deliberately kept that power away from the governor out of fear of "executive tyranny." Citizens soured on the arrangement after legislative Democrats carried out a court-packing scheme in 1841; the court's roster shows five justices joining that year, among them Stephen A. Douglas. The constitutional convention of 1847 answered by putting judges on the ballot, partly to strengthen the separation of powers and partly in the spirit of Jacksonian democracy. The 1848 constitution created three justices, each elected from a single-member district, and let the legislature switch to at-large elections if it chose. But as Chicago's population exploded, legislators preferred to keep geographic districts to protect the voting power of downstate Illinois. A 1962 amendment kept partisan first elections but moved sitting judges to retention votes, and in December 1970, voters deciding on a new constitution also chose to keep electing judges rather than adopt a Missouri Plan-style "merit selection" system.

## Lines on the Map

The result is a system with few counterparts among state supreme courts. By a 2020 count, Illinois is one of 21 states that elect supreme court justices by popular vote and one of only six that use partisan elections, and Ballotpedia counted it in 2021 as one of just four states, with Kentucky, Louisiana and Mississippi, whose supreme court justices represent geographic districts. A new justice wins a partisan race for a ten-year term; after that, keeping the seat means winning at least 60 percent in a nonpartisan retention election. The district lines themselves dated from 1963 and went nearly sixty years without an update, even though the four districts outside Cook County are supposed to have substantially equal populations. By 2018 estimates, the Second District held about 3,189,000 people and the Fourth about 1,320,000. The legislature redrew the map in 2021, Governor J. B. Pritzker signed it, and the new districts took effect for the 2022 elections. Only the First District stayed exactly the same.

## Flight Context

The Illinois Supreme Court Building stands at 39.7979°N, 89.6527°W at 200 East Capitol Avenue in downtown Springfield, Illinois, immediately east of the Illinois State Capitol grounds. From 1,500 to 2,000 feet AGL, the State Capitol's large dome is the landmark to find; the court building sits just east of it, with the Old State Capitol's square about a quarter of a nautical mile to the northeast. Abraham Lincoln Capital Airport (KSPI) is about 3 nautical miles to the north-northwest, so downtown lies close to its traffic; KSPI has Class C airspace, so check the sectional chart and expect to work with approach control. Clear days make the capitol complex and the downtown grid easy to read from well outside the city.
