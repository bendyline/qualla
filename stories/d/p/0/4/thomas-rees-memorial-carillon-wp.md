# Thomas Rees Memorial Carillon

A newspaper publisher's lifelong love of Low Country bells became a 132-foot brutalist tower in Springfield's Washington Park, home of an International Carillon Festival once called the world's best known.

**Location:** 39.7893, -89.6835

**Categories:** Bell towers in the United States, Brutalist architecture in Illinois, Buildings and structures in Springfield, Illinois, Carillons, Modernist architecture in Illinois, Tourist attractions in Springfield, Illinois, Towers completed in 1962, Towers in Illinois

## Introduction

Thomas Rees died in 1933, and it took twenty-nine years for his wish to become a tower. The Springfield newspaper publisher had fallen for the carillons of Belgium and the Netherlands on his travels through Europe, and he left very specific instructions in his will: how many bells there should be, and where they should hang. In June 1962, the Springfield Park District finally dedicated the result, a 132-foot campanile of concrete, brick and steel rising from Washington Park, carrying Dutch-cast bells within its open interior for anyone below to hear.

## A Publisher's Passion

Rees published the Illinois State Register from 1881 until his death, and in 1902 he was elected to a single term in the Illinois Senate. During World War I he served on the International Board of Arbitration for newspapers and later for unions, work that took him across Europe. There he found the instruments that would define his legacy. Rees attributed his great interest in bells to visiting carillons in Belgium and the Netherlands, although his first spark of interest reportedly came from articles he read in National Geographic and other publications. He did more than admire them. He provided $200,000 from a trust fund to build a carillon of his own for Springfield. The Park District carried out the project, and the bells were cast by the Dutch foundry Petit & Fritsen, in one of the two countries whose carillons had captivated him.

## Brutalism in the Park

The architect was Bill Turley, who left several marks on Springfield, including the Hoogland Center for the Arts, still standing as of 2022, and the former Springfield YMCA, demolished in 2021. For the carillon he worked in concrete, brick and steel, and the tower is an excellent example of the brutalist style. At 132 feet it is free-standing, with an open interior that holds three observation decks. The bells are hung dead, fixed in place rather than swinging, and they are spread up the height of the tower, the lowest at 52 feet and the highest at 105. Around the base, gardens and a reflecting pool soften all that concrete, so the tower rises from the park as much as from its foundations. Wikipedia says an elevator carries visitors to the top for views of Springfield, but the carillon's own website does not list tours, so call ahead.

## Keeping the Tower Ringing

A carillon is a working instrument, and this one has needed regular care. Its transmission cables, the linkage between the player and the bells, were overhauled twice, first in 1978 and again in 1987. In 1993 the carillon closed for a time while it underwent a major structural renovation. Each round of work kept the tower in service for the event that has made it famous among bell lovers. The International Carillon Festival is held each year and brings world-renowned carillonneurs to Springfield for evening concerts; long held in June, it moved to Memorial Day weekend in 2025, and in 2026 it ran May 21 to 24. In its 2007 edition, Chase's Calendar of Events called it "the world's best known carillon festival," high praise for a gathering in a Midwestern city park that exists because one man never forgot the sound of Low Country bell towers.

## Visiting the Tower

The carillon's own website, carillon-rees.org, lists its concert schedule, and the concerts are free: from May through September they run most evenings and weekend afternoons, with fewer in the fall and none from January through March. The festival's evenings, now held over Memorial Day weekend, are the time to come, when the bells carry across Washington Park and the music comes from an instrument spread through a tower more than a hundred feet tall. The site does not list tour times or fees, so call (217) 546-3853 before planning a climb. If you do get to the top, look out over the city whose newspaper Rees published for more than half a century, and consider that this unusual concrete landmark began, by one account, with a magazine article read by a newspaperman who wanted his hometown to hear what he had heard in Europe.

## Flight Context

The Thomas Rees Memorial Carillon rises 132 feet at 39.7893°N, 89.6835°W in Washington Park on the west side of Springfield, Illinois. From 1,500 to 2,000 feet AGL, look for the large park and the slim concrete tower standing near the gardens and reflecting pool. Downtown Springfield and the domed Illinois State Capitol lie about 1.5 nautical miles to the east-northeast. Abraham Lincoln Capital Airport (KSPI) is about 3.3 nautical miles to the north, so the park sits close to its traffic; KSPI has Class C airspace, so check the sectional chart and expect to work with approach control. At 132 feet the tower is an obstacle worth respecting at low altitude, and clear, low-sun conditions help its shadow stand out on the park lawns.
