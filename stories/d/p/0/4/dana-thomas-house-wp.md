# Dana–Thomas House

The Springfield mansion where a widowed heiress gave a young Frank Lloyd Wright his first blank check, and where a medical publisher later kept the Prairie School masterpiece largely intact until the state could save it.

**Location:** 39.7943, -89.6514

**Categories:** Houses completed in 1904, Frank Lloyd Wright buildings, National Historic Landmarks in Illinois, National Register of Historic Places in Springfield, Illinois, Houses on the National Register of Historic Places in Illinois, Historic American Buildings Survey in Illinois, Houses in Springfield, Illinois, Historic house museums in Illinois, Illinois State Historic Sites, Museums in Springfield, Illinois

## Introduction

Susan Lawrence Dana asked Frank Lloyd Wright to remodel her family's mansion. He gave her a new house instead. The commission was the largest Wright had yet received, and recognizing a kindred spirit in his client, he stretched the boundaries of the contract until a remodeling job on Springfield's "Aristocracy Hill" became, in effect, an entirely new house. The result, built from 1902 to 1904 at 301 East Lawrence Avenue, is the Dana–Thomas House, a Prairie School landmark still filled with the glass and furniture Wright designed for it.

## An Heiress With Her Own Ideas

Susan Lawrence Dana was independent-minded and rich. Her family fortune included silver mines in the Rocky Mountains, and after her husband Edwin died in 1900 and her father in 1901, she inherited roughly $3 million. Widowed, she had complete control over her household and her money, and she knew what she wanted to do with both. Dana was eager to express her own personality and to become a leading philanthropic figure in Springfield, the state capital. Her plan began at home: she would completely remodel the Lawrence family's Italianate mansion in the city's fashionable Aristocracy Hill neighborhood. What she needed was an architect whose ambitions matched hers, and in 1902 she was introduced to one.

## Wright's Blank Check

Frank Lloyd Wright was then the rising leader of the Prairie School, a movement of "organic architecture" that stressed harmony between a building's interior and its surroundings. The Dana house was his 72nd building and, as the foundation that supports the house today describes it, his first "blank check" commission. Patron and architect shared a love of Japanese prints and drawings, and the house reflects that, along with the flat landscape of Illinois itself. Guests entered through an arched doorway into a sequence of spaces that open outward from vestibule to reception hall, a sense of expanding space that Wright repeated throughout. The house holds about 12,000 square feet in 35 rooms spread over 16 levels. Some 250 art glass doors and windows, with motifs that include sumac and butterflies, filter the light, and in the entry hall stands a life-size terracotta sculpture designed by Wright and Richard Bock, one of his studio collaborators, The Flower in the Crannied Wall, inspired by Tennyson's poem.

## The Party and the Quiet

The house was built for display and entertainment, and at first Dana used it exactly that way. In 1906 she threw a party for more than 1,000 people. She remarried twice after Edwin's death but had no children. By the 1920s her life had turned inward: she became increasingly reclusive and absorbed in spiritualism, and around 1928, after the death of her cousin Florence, she closed the great house and moved to a cottage across the street. In her last years Dana struggled with age-related dementia, and her fortune was largely spent. In 1944 the house was sold to Charles C. Thomas. Susan Lawrence Dana died in 1946. The woman who had once filled these rooms with more than a thousand guests had long since left them behind.

## The Publisher Who Kept It

The house could have been gutted by its next owner. Instead, the Charles C. Thomas Publishing Company, a medical publishing firm run by Charles and Nanette Thomas, bought the property, reportedly for $12,250, and used it as offices for decades, making minimal changes to the structure. That restraint mattered. The house was designated a National Historic Landmark on January 7, 1976, and in 1981 the Thomases sold it to the State of Illinois, which approved $1 million for the purchase. It opened as a museum on July 1, 1982, then underwent a $5 million restoration from 1987 to 1990, reopening in September 1990. By 1998 the Dana–Thomas House Foundation and the state's historic preservation agency had acquired almost all the remaining major pieces of original furniture, and today the house holds what its foundation calls the largest collection of site-specific, original Wright art glass and furniture.

## A Great Place, Sometimes Closed

Even a landmark can fall victim to a budget. The state closed the house to the public from December 1, 2008, until April 23, 2009, as part of a round of cuts, and it closed again for 11 months in 2011 for renovations to its interior and exterior finishes and its mechanical and security systems. In 2018, as Illinois marked its bicentennial, the American Institute of Architects' Illinois chapter reportedly named the Dana–Thomas House one of the Illinois 200 Great Places. It is owned and operated today by the Illinois Department of Natural Resources as a historic house museum, a few blocks from the Capitol, in the city where Susan Dana once set out to become a leading philanthropic figure.

## Flight Context

The Dana–Thomas House stands at 39.7943 N, 89.6514 W, 301 East Lawrence Avenue in Springfield, Illinois, about a quarter of a nautical mile southeast of the Illinois State Capitol and just south of the Illinois Governor's Mansion. From 1,500-2,500 feet AGL over downtown Springfield, use the Capitol's tall dome as the anchor and look a few blocks to the southeast for the house's long, low roofs among older residential blocks. Nearest airport: Abraham Lincoln Capital Airport (KSPI), about 3 nautical miles to the north-northwest; KSPI has Class C airspace, so check the sectional chart for its limits and expect to work with approach control.
