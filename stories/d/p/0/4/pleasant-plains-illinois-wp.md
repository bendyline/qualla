# Pleasant Plains, Illinois

A Sangamon County village that took its name from Peter Cartwright's Methodist church, grew up where the Springfield–Beardstown stage road crossed Richland Creek, and bought its Lincoln-era stagecoach stop to save it.

**Location:** 39.8742, -89.9200

**Categories:** Villages in Sangamon County, Illinois, Springfield metropolitan area, Illinois, Villages in Illinois

## Introduction

The first time the name "Pleasant Plains" turned up, it belonged to a church rather than a town. It was attached to a small Methodist church built in 1838 by the Reverend Peter Cartwright, a circuit-riding preacher who had beaten Abraham Lincoln at the ballot box once and would lose to him once. The village that eventually took the name grew up beside a stagecoach road west of Springfield, and today its 800-plus residents live in a place that has worked hard to hold on to that frontier past.

## The Preacher Who Beat Lincoln

Peter Cartwright, who lived from 1785 to 1872, moved in 1824 to the area that became Cartwright Township. He rode the circuit as a Methodist preacher, opposed slavery, and did not confine himself to the pulpit. In 1832 he defeated Abraham Lincoln, among other candidates, in a race for the Illinois House. Fourteen years later the two met again, this time for a seat in Congress, and in 1846 it was Lincoln who won. It was a rivalry that ended with one win apiece. Cartwright remains the first name on the short list of notable people linked to Pleasant Plains, remembered there as both a politician and a leader of the Methodist church.

## Where the Stage Road Crossed the Creek

From the 1830s to the 1850s, a stagecoach line ran between Springfield and Beardstown, and the settlement that became Pleasant Plains grew up at the spot where the line crossed Richland Creek. The first European settlers had arrived near the site around 1819. A mile east of the present village, Moses Broadwell's inn at Clayville was the first stagecoach stop for travelers heading west out of Springfield. The rails came later. The Pana, Springfield and Northwestern Railroad was built through Pleasant Plains in 1872, and the village kept a depot until 1962, when it closed. Today the village sits within the Springfield metropolitan area.

## Buying the Stagecoach Stop

In 2009, mayor Jim Verkuilan and a group of friends set out to reconnect the village to its roots. They founded the Pleasant Plains Historical Society, with a stated purpose "To acquire, restore and maintain historical sites surrounding the community." The society moved quickly. Its first goal was Clayville, the Lincoln-era stagecoach stop that a predecessor of the University of Illinois Springfield had operated from the 1970s until 1992, and in 2010 the society completed the purchase. Its affiliate, Clayville Historic Site, now runs the stagecoach stop and the Broadwell Inn and Tavern, first built in 1824, rebuilt in the 1830s after a fire, and now on the National Register of Historic Places. For a village of a few hundred households, owning and caring for a tavern with roots in 1824 is no small commitment.

## The Village Today

Pleasant Plains counted just over 800 people at the 2020 census, living in roughly 300 households, and its small grid covers about 1.4 square miles. Beyond Cartwright, the village's notable names include William J. Frey, an Illinois state legislator who was also a farmer and businessman, and James F. Short Jr., a sociologist. The historical society's work has given the village a tangible link to the years when this was a stop on the road west. The stagecoach stop still stands a mile down the highway, and visitors can see the ground where travelers bound for Beardstown once paused on their way.

## Flight Context

Pleasant Plains lies at 39.8742°N, 89.9200°W in western Sangamon County, Illinois, about 13 nautical miles west-northwest of downtown Springfield. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL, look for a compact village grid set in open farmland along the course of Richland Creek, with Illinois Route 125 running east toward Clayville, the restored stagecoach stop about a mile east of town. Abraham Lincoln Capital Airport (KSPI) is roughly 11 nautical miles to the east. The surrounding terrain is flat, open farmland, so the village stands out well in clear conditions; haze in summer can soften the view of small features such as the Clayville grounds.
