# Illinois House of Representatives

The lower house of the Illinois General Assembly, where Abraham Lincoln began his political career and where, for 110 years, voters elected three members per district using a cumulative-vote system that a 1980 voter revolt swept away.

**Location:** 39.7981, -89.6550

**Categories:** Illinois General Assembly, State lower houses in the United States, 1818 establishments in Illinois

## Introduction

For 110 years, every Illinois voter walked into the booth with three votes for the state House. They could give one vote each to three candidates, a vote and a half to two, or pile all three onto a single favorite, a move known as a bullet vote. From 1870 to 1980 that system of cumulative voting shaped the Illinois House of Representatives, the lower chamber of the General Assembly, which meets beneath the dome of the Illinois State Capitol in Springfield. In most districts, it all but guaranteed the minority party a seat at the table. Then, in a single November election, voters threw it out.

## Lincoln's First Chamber

The House is as old as the state. It was created by Illinois's first constitution, adopted in 1818. In the 1830s its candidates sorted themselves into political parties, first Democrats and Whigs; in the 1850s the Whigs reorganized as Republicans. One of those Whigs arrived in 1834: a young Abraham Lincoln, who began his political career in the Illinois House and served there until 1842. Republicans held a majority of the House after 1860, but at the next election control swung back to the Democrats, whose legislature worked to frame a new state constitution that voters ultimately rejected. It was in this chamber that a future president first learned the trade of lawmaking.

## Three Seats, Four Candidates

Under the arrangement that lasted from 1870 to 1980, the House had 153 members, later 177. The state was divided into 51, and later 59, legislative districts, each electing one senator and three representatives. Nothing in the constitution required it, but the two parties had an informal agreement to run only two candidates per district, so most races featured four candidates for three seats. The math all but guaranteed that the district's minority party would win a seat, especially outside Chicago, and it usually assured each party at least a third of the chamber, 59 of 177 seats. By Wikipedia's account, the only exceptions to that floor came in 1875 and during World War I. The one break from cumulative voting itself was the 1964 election, held statewide at large.

## The Cutback

The end came with the Cutback Amendment, approved by voters on November 4, 1980, with 68.7 percent of those voting on the measure in favor. It was largely led by Pat Quinn, who would later become governor, along with the Coalition for Political Honesty, the League of Women Voters and Citizens for Constitutional Reform, and it was popularly seen as a way to punish legislators for voting themselves a 40 percent raise. The amendment abolished cumulative voting and multi-member districts and cut the House from 177 seats to 118. Each of the 59 Senate districts was split in half, a method called nesting, so every senator is now associated with two representatives. Not everyone celebrated. In 1995 the Chicago Tribune editorialized that the old multi-member districts produced better legislators, and in 2001 a task force led by former Governor Jim Edgar and former federal judge Abner Mikva called for reviving cumulative voting, in part because it appears to increase the representation of racial minorities.

## Firsts in the Chamber

The House's roll of firsts traces who Illinois has let into power, and when. John W. E. Thomas, first elected in 1876, and George French Ecton, elected in 1886, were the first two African-American legislators in the state. Lottie Holman O'Neill became the first woman elected to the House in 1922. Floy Clements became the first African-American woman to serve as a state representative in 1958, and Joseph Berrios the first Hispanic American in 1982. Theresa Mah became the first Asian American member when she was sworn in on January 11, 2017. On January 11, 2023, Abdelnasser Rashid and Nabeela Syed became the first Muslim members of the General Assembly, and Rashid the first Palestinian American to serve in it.

## How the House Works Now

Today's House has 118 members elected from single-member districts for two-year terms, with no term limits. Districts are redrawn every ten years, and after the 2010 census each representative spoke for roughly 108,734 people. Members must be at least 21 and must have lived in their district for at least two years. The House convenes on the second Wednesday of January each year. Together with the Senate and the governor, it makes the state's laws, writes its budget, acts on amendments to the U.S. Constitution and proposes amendments to the state's own. It also holds the power to impeach executive and judicial officials. Democrats won a majority in 1982, and apart from two years of Republican control from 1995 to 1997, they have held it ever since.

## Flight Context

The Illinois House meets in the Illinois State Capitol at 39.7981 N, 89.6550 W in downtown Springfield, Illinois. From 1,500-3,000 feet AGL, the Capitol's dome is easy to pick out among the surrounding blocks, with the Governor's Mansion a few blocks to the east-southeast and the Lincoln Home National Historic Site nearby. Nearest airport: Abraham Lincoln Capital Airport (KSPI), about 3 nautical miles to the north-northwest; KSPI has Class C airspace, so check the sectional chart for its limits and expect to work with approach control. Summer brings haze and afternoon thunderstorms to central Illinois.
