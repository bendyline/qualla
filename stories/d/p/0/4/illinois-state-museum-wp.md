# Illinois State Museum

Founded in 1877 as a showcase inside the Capitol, the Illinois State Museum gathers the state's fossils, art and archaeology a block from the dome, while working through the harder task of returning what it holds that was never its own.

## Introduction

Somewhere between the fossils and the dioramas, the Illinois State Museum keeps a collection of glass paperweights. It is a small clue to how wide the museum's brief really is. A state law charges it with collecting and preserving objects representing past and present fauna and flora, "the life and work of man," geological history, natural resources, and the manufacturing and fine arts, and then explaining all of it to the public. The result, one block southwest of the Illinois State Capitol in Springfield, is a museum of the life, land, people and art of Illinois.

## A Showcase in the Capitol

The museum was founded on May 25, 1877, as a showcase inside the sixth Illinois State Capitol, the domed building that still anchors downtown Springfield. Its first curator was Amos Henry Worthen. As state government grew and needed room, the collection moved out of the Capitol in 1923 and into the newly built Centennial Building, now known as the Michael Howlett Building. The collections kept growing, and the state wanted a dedicated place for tourists and school groups, so in 1961 it began building the first purpose-built state museum. Completed in 1962 and dedicated in 1963 at Spring and Edwards Streets, that building is the Illinois State Museum today, still standing in the Capitol's shadow nearly a century and a half after the collection began inside it.

## Fossils to Paperweights

Inside, the museum ranges across time and subject. There are local fossils and exhibits on mining. There are household displays furnished for different historic periods, dioramas of Native American life, and archaeological and ethnographic artifacts, alongside the museum's art holdings and those glass paperweights. Natural history shares the building with the state's cultural and artistic heritage, which is precisely the point: the museum's job is not one discipline but one state. It is now a division of the Illinois Department of Natural Resources, where Governor Jim Edgar placed it by executive order in 1995, and it has its own 11-member board appointed by the governor with the advice and consent of the state Senate.

## Satellites, and a Year in the Dark

Over the decades the museum added satellite sites. Dickson Mounds, near Lewistown, which came to the museum in 1965 and was dedicated as a museum in 1972, focuses on the Native American history of the Illinois River valley from the Ice Age to the 19th century; its 230-acre grounds include the Eveland Village, the excavated remains of three early Native American buildings. The Lockport Gallery, which opened in 1987, occupies the 1850 Norton Building, which once held grain, a supply store and a dormitory for canal crews, and processed grain until the 1950s. Galleries in Chicago, opened in 1985, and near Rend Lake, dedicated in 1990, showed Illinois art and crafts. Then came the state's budget crisis. The whole museum system closed to the public on September 30, 2015, for the first time in its 138-year history. The Springfield museum and Dickson Mounds reopened on July 2, 2016, at first with an adult admission fee, and the Lockport Gallery followed on September 24, 2016. The Chicago Gallery and the Southern Illinois gallery near Rend Lake did not reopen. Admission to the Springfield museum is free today.

## Returning What Was Taken

Some of what the museum holds was never rightfully its to keep. In October 2019 it returned 42 culturally significant Aranda and Bardi Jawi objects to Australian First Nations, and the museum says it was the first institution in the world to repatriate objects under the Australian government's Return of Cultural Heritage Project. Representatives of the Aranda people of Central Australia and the Bardi Jawi people of Western Australia took custodianship at a handover in Springfield. The University of Chicago linguist Gerhardt Laves had collected most of the objects between 1929 and 1931, and the university transferred them to the museum in 1942. The harder work is closer to home. Despite the Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act of 1990, the museum still holds thousands of Native American ancestors. A May 2025 report by Capitol News Illinois counted 5,789 remains held by the museum and Dickson Mounds together, the second-largest such collection of any U.S. institution, with notices published for about a quarter of them and a federal deadline for the rest in 2029. Each of those numbers was once a person.

## Flight Context

The Illinois State Museum is at 39.7967 N, 89.6554 W, at Spring and Edwards Streets in downtown Springfield, Illinois, one block southwest of the Illinois State Capitol. From 1,500-2,500 feet AGL, use the Capitol's silvery dome as the reference and look just to its southwest. The Governor's Mansion is a few blocks to the east and the Dana–Thomas House a short distance to the southeast. Nearest airport: Abraham Lincoln Capital Airport (KSPI), about 3 nautical miles to the north-northwest; KSPI has Class C airspace, so check the sectional chart for its limits and expect to work with approach control. The museum's Dickson Mounds branch lies near Lewistown, well to the northwest of Springfield near the Illinois River. Summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are common over central Illinois.
