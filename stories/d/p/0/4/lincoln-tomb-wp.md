# Lincoln Tomb

The granite obelisk in Springfield's Oak Ridge Cemetery where Abraham Lincoln, Mary Todd Lincoln and three of their sons lie, a tomb that survived an 1876 plot to steal the president's body and ended with his coffin encased in cement ten feet below the floor.

**Location:** 39.8232, -89.6557

**Categories:** 1865 establishments in Illinois, 1874 establishments in Illinois, Abraham Lincoln National Heritage Area, Abraham Lincoln, Assassination of Abraham Lincoln, Buildings and structures completed in 1874, Burial monuments and structures in Illinois, Illinois in the American Civil War, Illinois State Historic Sites, Mausoleums on the National Register of Historic Places

## Introduction

On November 7, 1876, counterfeiters tried to steal Abraham Lincoln's body. Their plan was to take the dead president from his tomb in Springfield's Oak Ridge Cemetery and hold him for ransom. They failed, and were caught ten days later, but the attempt changed the tomb forever. Lincoln's coffin would be hidden, moved and reburied more than once over the next quarter century, until, at the request of his son Robert, it was sealed in a concrete vault ten feet below the floor. Today the Lincoln Tomb, a granite obelisk rising 117 feet over the cemetery, is where Lincoln, his wife Mary Todd Lincoln and three of their four sons, Edward, William and Thomas, finally rest.

## Home to Springfield

Two days after Lincoln was assassinated, on April 16, 1865, a group of Springfield citizens formed the National Lincoln Monument Association and began raising money for a memorial. The funeral train arrived on May 3, and Lincoln lay in state for one night in the Illinois State Capitol, the building now preserved as the Old State Capitol. After the funeral and burial services the next day, his coffin, along with that of his son William, went into a public receiving vault at Oak Ridge Cemetery, the site Mary Lincoln had requested. That December they were moved to a temporary vault. The permanent tomb was designed by Larkin Goldsmith Mead. In September 1871, Lincoln and his sons Edward and William were moved into the unfinished tomb, and on October 15, 1874, it was dedicated before a large crowd, with Richard J. Oglesby and Ulysses S. Grant among the speakers.

## The Plot and the Hiding

Two years after the dedication came the attempted theft. The counterfeiters failed to take the body, but the fright was enough. Members of the monument association hid Lincoln's coffin elsewhere inside the building, leaving the sarcophagus empty. Mary Todd Lincoln died in Springfield in 1882 at the age of 63 and was buried in the tomb that July. In 1887 the Lincolns' remains were moved again, into a new brick vault beneath the floor. The State of Illinois acquired the tomb in 1895, and from 1899 to 1901 it was rebuilt, with all five caskets moved to a nearby underground vault during the work. When it was finished, at the request of Robert Todd Lincoln, the president's coffin was placed in its final resting place: on September 26, 1901, it was secured in a concrete-and-steel reinforced chamber ten feet below the burial room floor.

## Granite and Bronze

The tomb is built of granite, quarried in Biddeford, Maine, and dressed at Quincy, Massachusetts. A story-and-a-half base supports the obelisk, with a semicircular entrance at one end and a semicircular burial room at the other. Four flights of balustraded stairs climb to a terrace, now closed to the public. Around the base runs a chain of 40 stone shields, 37 carved with the abbreviations of the states that existed when the tomb was built and three marked U, S and A, each linked to the next by raised bands so that the chain never breaks. Bronze statues representing the infantry, navy, artillery and cavalry of the Civil War stand at the corners, and a full-length statue of Lincoln rises above the entrance. Out front, on a pedestal, sits a bronze recasting of Gutzon Borglum's head of Lincoln, whose marble original is in the U.S. Capitol.

## Now He Belongs to the Ages

In 1930 and 1931 the state rebuilt the interior in an Art Deco style, and President Herbert Hoover spoke at its rededication on June 17, 1931. Inside, marble from Minnesota, Missouri, Massachusetts, Arkansas, Utah, Italy, Spain, France and Belgium lines the rotunda and corridors. Eight statues by prominent sculptors trace phases of Lincoln's life, and bronze tablets carry his Farewell Address, the Gettysburg Address and part of the Second Inaugural. Gold stars in sets of twelve mark the 48 states of 1930. The burial room has black and white marble walls and a gold-leaf ceiling. At its center stands a seven-ton block of reddish marble inscribed with Lincoln's name and years, marking the burial vault 30 inches behind it and ten feet down. Nine flags form a semicircle around it: the United States flag, the presidential flag, and the flags of seven states tied to Lincoln and his ancestors. Above a stained glass window are the words reputedly spoken by Edwin Stanton at Lincoln's death: "Now he belongs to the ages."

## Later Wars Remembered

The State of Illinois owns and runs the tomb as the Lincoln Tomb State Historic Site. It became one of the first National Historic Landmarks in 1960. A short distance from the tomb, and also overseen by the state, stand three war memorials. The Vietnam Veterans Memorial, dedicated in 1988, honors the almost 3,000 Illinoisans killed or listed as missing in that war, with their names carved into black granite set in five gray granite walls around an eternal flame. The Korean War Memorial, dedicated on June 16, 1996, honors 1,748 Illinoisans killed there with a twelve-foot bronze bell. And the World War II Illinois Veterans Memorial, dedicated in December 2004, remembers the 987,000 Illinois men and women who served and the 22,000 who died, gathered around a 22-ton white concrete globe.

## Flight Context

The Lincoln Tomb stands in Oak Ridge Cemetery at 39.8232 N, 89.6557 W on the north side of Springfield, Illinois, about 1.5 nautical miles north of the Illinois State Capitol. From 1,500-2,500 feet AGL, look for the 117-foot granite obelisk rising from the cemetery grounds; the Capitol's silvery dome to the south is the best reference. Abraham Lincoln Capital Airport (KSPI) lies only about 1.6 nautical miles to the northwest, so the tomb sits close to the airport's traffic; KSPI has Class C airspace, so check the sectional chart for its limits and expect to work with approach control. Summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are common over central Illinois.
