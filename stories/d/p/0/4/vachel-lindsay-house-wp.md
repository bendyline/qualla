# Vachel Lindsay House

An 1848 frame house on South 5th Street where the Lincolns once visited family, and where the chanting poet Vachel Lindsay was born, wrote, and died.

**Location:** 39.7958, -89.6497

**Categories:** National Historic Landmarks in Illinois, Houses completed in 1848, Historic house museums in Illinois, Illinois State Historic Sites, Museums in Springfield, Illinois, Houses on the National Register of Historic Places in Illinois, National Register of Historic Places in Springfield, Illinois, Literary museums in the United States, Houses in Springfield, Illinois

## Introduction

Before it belonged to a poet, the frame house at 603 South 5th Street belonged to Abraham Lincoln's in-laws. Clark M. Smith is believed to have moved in soon after marrying Anna Maria Todd, the younger sister of Mary Todd Lincoln, and in the days before the Lincolns left Springfield for Washington, both of them came here often as visitors. Decades later, a boy born in the same house would grow up to write a poem called "Abraham Lincoln Walks at Midnight."

## A House on Fifth and Edwards

Built in 1848, the house stands at the southwest corner of South 5th and East Edwards streets, a short walk from downtown Springfield. It is a two-story wood-frame building with a front-facing gable roof and clapboard siding, five bays wide across the front. A single-story porch with a hipped roof runs across the center three bays, carried on square posts beneath a dentilled cornice. Look closely at the entrance and you can read the house's age in layers: the surround is Greek Revival, but the doorway inside it is a later Victorian addition. Nothing about it is grand. It is a comfortable family house of its era, which is exactly what makes the lives lived inside it so vivid.

## The Boy Who Wanted to Paint

Nicholas Vachel Lindsay was born here on November 10, 1879, and it was the home he kept coming back to, apart from years away in Ohio, Chicago, New York and Spokane, Washington. As a young man he studied at Hiram College in Ohio and then at art schools in Chicago and New York, and at first set out to become an artist, only to discover that his real talent lay in poetry. His earliest books were self-published, and in 1913 Poetry magazine ran "General William Booth Enters into Heaven." The next year brought "The Congo," the poem that made him famous, and that has since drawn criticism for its racist, dehumanizing depiction of Black people. Lindsay's approach was unusual for its time. He wrote in colloquial language, and when he appeared in public he delivered his work in a sing-song, chanting style. Springfield ran through his writing, from The Golden Book of Springfield to his Lincoln poem. The state now remembers him as a poet, an illustrator and a pioneering film critic.

## Rooms Full of Evidence

Inside, the house has been decorated in the late Victorian style, and it holds the kind of objects that make a writer's life tangible. Downstairs are an entry hall, two parlors, a library, a dining room, a kitchen, a bathroom and a pantry. Upstairs, off a central hall, are a sewing room, five bedrooms, a bathroom and a trunk room. Among the furnishings are the bed in which Lindsay was born, a desk he built, and the table at which he did much of his writing. One account of the house places the writing of many of his poems in the second-floor room at the northwest corner. Stand there and the distance between the poems and the place shrinks to almost nothing.

## An Ending and an Afterlife

Lindsay's life in the house ended in sorrow. In 1931, suffering from what was then called melancholia, he took his own life here, in the home where he had been born. He was buried in Springfield's Oak Ridge Cemetery. His heirs later sold the house to a non-profit foundation, which, according to Wikipedia, opened it as a house museum in 1960. In 1971 it was declared a National Historic Landmark. Today the Illinois Department of Natural Resources, which now runs the state's historic sites, operates the home, with tours that emphasize Lindsay's poetry and his art; the state's current listing says tours are by appointment only and reservations are required, so plan ahead. It is a quiet visit, and a moving one, in a house where Lincoln's in-laws once welcomed him and Mary, and where a poet later brought Lincoln walking back into verse.

## Flight Context

The Vachel Lindsay House stands at 39.7958°N, 89.6497°W at the corner of South 5th and East Edwards streets, just south of downtown Springfield, Illinois. From 1,500 to 2,000 feet AGL, the domed Illinois State Capitol is the landmark to find; the house lies a few blocks to its southeast, and the Old State Capitol's square is roughly a third of a nautical mile to the north. At this scale the house itself is a small residential building in the street grid, so use the capitol complex to orient. Abraham Lincoln Capital Airport (KSPI) is roughly three nautical miles to the north-northwest, putting downtown close to its traffic; check the sectional chart and expect to work with the tower or approach control.
