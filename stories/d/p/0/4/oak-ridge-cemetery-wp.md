# Oak Ridge Cemetery

On an oak-covered ridge above Spring Creek, Springfield's municipal cemetery holds the Lincoln Tomb, a century and a half of Illinois leaders, and the boy who lived to be called the last living person to have seen Abraham Lincoln's face.

**Location:** 39.8236, -89.6578

## Introduction

Fleetwood Lindley was fourteen years old on September 26, 1901, the day Abraham Lincoln's casket was opened for the last time. His father, Joseph, belonged to the Lincoln Guard of Honor, and so, by his own later account, the boy stood close enough to look upon the face of the sixteenth president before the coffin was lowered into a concrete-and-steel vault ten feet below the floor of the tomb. When he died in 1963, the county historical society says, he was the last living person to have seen it. Lindley now lies in the same ground, on the same oak-shaded ridge in Springfield, Illinois, where he kept that strange appointment with history.

## A Ridge Above Spring Creek

The city dedicated Oak Ridge Cemetery on May 24, 1860. It was the third public cemetery in Springfield, after the City Cemetery and Hutchinson's Cemetery, and hundreds of burials were later moved here from those two. Today it is the city's municipal cemetery. The site was chosen for its shape. William Saunders designed the grounds in the Rural Cemetery Landscape Lawn style, which depends on rolling hills, and this piece of land had them: a ridge crowded with the oaks that gave the cemetery its name, falling away to low-lying Spring Creek. It is a landscape unusual in central Illinois, and the curving lanes make the most of it. The newest section, in the southwest corner, opened after 1945 and follows a different philosophy altogether. Built in the Memorial Park style, its roadways are wide enough for motor vehicles. Walk from the old lanes into the new section and you cross from one era's idea of how the living should visit the dead into the next.

## Keeping Watch Over Lincoln

The Lincoln Tomb is the cemetery's center of gravity. Abraham Lincoln rests there with his wife, Mary Todd Lincoln, and all but one of their children. Guarding him has never been a purely ceremonial job. In 1876 a gang of counterfeiters tried to steal Lincoln's body, and from then on a group of men known as the Lincoln Guard of Honor watched over his casket. The tomb's first custodian, John Carroll Power, is buried at Oak Ridge too, and the tomb grounds still include a custodian's residence. In 1901 Robert Lincoln, the president's surviving son, asked that his father be given a secure burial. Before the casket went into its final vault, it was opened so the remains could be verified, and five men signed a public notice certifying them: four Guard of Honor members, Joseph Lindley among them, and one representative of the National Lincoln Monument Association. That is how his teenage son came to be standing in the room, and why Fleetwood Lindley's own grave carries a footnote no other in the cemetery can claim.

## A Roll Call of Illinois

Lincoln draws the visitors, but the hillside reads like an index of the state's history. Ninian Edwards, the only governor the Illinois Territory ever had, is buried here. So is John T. Stuart, a congressman and lawyer who was once Lincoln's law partner, and Governor John Riley Tanner, whose tomb is here as well. Shelby Moore Cullom, William Henry Bissell, Daniel Pope Cook and Alexander Starne, who served as both Illinois Secretary of State and Illinois Treasurer, share the ground with them. Not every name belongs to politics. Nellie Grant, daughter of President Ulysses S. Grant, rests at Oak Ridge. John L. Lewis, who led the United Mine Workers from 1919 to 1960, lies here, and so does the Springfield poet Vachel Lindsay. Arthur Harrison Wilson, a Medal of Honor recipient, is buried on the same slopes, as are the brothers Jacob Bunn and John Whitfield Bunn. Taken together, they make a quiet census of the people who built Illinois.

## Memorials on the Hillside

Oak Ridge also holds the state's collective grief for its wars. A Korean War memorial stands on the grounds, with a separate memorial to the Battle of Chosin Reservoir. The World War II Illinois Veterans Memorial has a side devoted to the European Theater, and the Illinois Vietnam Veterans Memorial completes the trio. All of this gives the cemetery a double identity: a pilgrimage site for one president, and a gathering place for the ordinary soldiers of three later wars. Just beyond the gates, the Springfield and Central Illinois African-American History Museum stands next to the cemetery, carrying the city's story in yet another direction. Come in the late afternoon, when the light slants low through the oaks and the ridge falls into shadow toward the creek. The older lanes belong to whoever is willing to wander them.

## Flight Context

Oak Ridge Cemetery lies at 39.8236°N, 89.6578°W on the north side of Springfield, Illinois. From 1,500 to 2,000 feet AGL, look for a patch of wooded, rolling ground and curving cemetery lanes along the low course of Spring Creek, with downtown Springfield and the Illinois State Capitol dome about 1.5 nautical miles to the south. Abraham Lincoln Capital Airport (KSPI) lies about 1.5 nautical miles to the northwest, so the cemetery sits close to its traffic; KSPI has Class C airspace, so check the sectional chart and expect to work with approach control. Clear weather with a low sun helps the wooded ridge stand out from the surrounding city grid.
