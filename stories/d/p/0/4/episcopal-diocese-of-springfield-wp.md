# Episcopal Diocese of Springfield

Born in 1877 when the Episcopal Diocese of Illinois split in three, the Springfield diocese stretches from Bloomington to Cairo and began with a bishop who had been rejected once and said no once before he said yes.

**Location:** 39.7933, -89.6539

**Categories:** Dioceses of the Episcopal Church (United States), Episcopal Church in Illinois, Religious organizations established in 1877, Anglican dioceses established in the 19th century, 1877 establishments in Illinois, Province 5 of the Episcopal Church (United States)

## Introduction

The first bishop of Springfield had already been turned down once, and he turned the job down himself before he finally took it. George Franklin Seymour was elected Bishop of Illinois in 1874, but the church's House of Deputies refused to confirm him. When the new Episcopal Diocese of Springfield elected him its first bishop in December 1877, he declined in April 1878, then accepted when the diocese elected him again that May. He went on to lead it for 28 years.

## One Diocese Becomes Three

The Episcopal Diocese of Springfield was born of division. In 1877 the old Episcopal Diocese of Illinois split into three separate dioceses: Springfield, Quincy and Chicago. Springfield's share of the map is large. It takes in the part of Illinois east of the Illinois River and south of Woodford, Livingston, Ford and Iroquois counties, a territory that runs from Bloomington and Normal in the north to Cairo at the far southern end of the state. Its parishes are scattered across dozens of towns: Alton and Havana, Carbondale and Champaign, Danville and Decatur, Mattoon and Mount Vernon. At the center, in the state capital, the diocese keeps its offices on South Second Street, a few blocks south of the Illinois State Capitol.

## The Reluctant First Bishop

Seymour came to Springfield as an East Coast churchman and academic. Born in New York on January 5, 1829, he had served as a missionary at Annandale-on-Hudson and became the first warden of St. Stephen's College, now Bard College. In 1865 he was elected professor of church history at the General Theological Seminary, and in 1875 he became its dean. After the false start of 1874 and his own hesitation in 1878, he was consecrated at Trinity Church in New York City on June 11, 1878. He served as bishop from 1878 until 1906 and died in Springfield of pneumonia on December 8, 1906. He was buried at Green-Wood Cemetery.

## A Short Line of Bishops

Since Seymour, the diocese has had eleven more bishops, and their tenures vary wildly. John Chanler White served from 1924 to 1947, nearly as long as Seymour. His successor, Richard T. Loring, served only from 1947 to 1948. Peter H. Beckwith led the diocese from 1992 to 2010, and Daniel Hayden Martins from 2011 until June 30, 2021. On December 11, 2021, the diocese elected the Very Rev. Brian K. Burgess of Woodbury, New Jersey, as the 12th Bishop of Springfield, and he has served since 2022. Twelve names across nearly a century and a half make a short list, and a handful of long episcopates account for much of it.

## Thirty-Three Churches

The numbers today are modest. The diocese lists 33 congregations; in 2023 its parishes reported 3,098 members, and in 2024 an average Sunday attendance of 1,152. Springfield itself has three congregations: the Cathedral Church of St. Paul, Christ Church and St. Luke's. Champaign has two, Emmanuel Memorial and the Chapel of St. John the Divine. Most other towns on the list have a single parish, and the same names repeat from town to town across the diocese: St. John's in Albion, Centralia and Decatur; Trinity in Jacksonville, Lincoln, Mattoon and Mount Vernon; St. Paul's in Alton, Carlinville and Pekin.

## Flight Context

The diocese's offices are in downtown Springfield, Illinois, at about 39.7933 N, 89.6539 W on South Second Street, a few blocks south of the Illinois State Capitol, whose dome makes a good landmark from the air. The diocese itself covers much of central and southern Illinois east of the Illinois River, from Bloomington-Normal south to Cairo. For a low pass over downtown Springfield, 1,500-2,500 feet AGL gives a clear view of the Capitol complex. Nearest airport: Abraham Lincoln Capital Airport (KSPI), about 3 nm to the north-northwest; downtown lies inside the airport's Class C airspace, so check the sectional chart and expect to work with the tower or approach control before overflying. Summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are common over central Illinois.
