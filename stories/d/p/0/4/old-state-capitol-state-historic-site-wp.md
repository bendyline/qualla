# Old State Capitol State Historic Site

Springfield's yellow-limestone Greek Revival statehouse heard Lincoln's House Divided speech, held his body as 75,000 mourners filed past, and was later taken apart stone by stone and rebuilt.

**Location:** 39.8011, -89.6486

## Introduction

Every stone in the walls of Springfield's Old State Capitol has been taken down and set back in place. In 1966 workers dismantled the building piece by piece, made room beneath it for an underground public parking lot and offices, and rebuilt it, restoring its public rooms to look much as they did around 1860. Nobody goes to that much trouble for an ordinary government building. They did it because of what happened inside this one, in a single second-floor room where Abraham Lincoln declared that a house divided against itself could not stand, and where, seven years later, his body lay in state.

## Pulling the Capital to the Center

From 1820 until 1839, the capital of the young state of Illinois was Vandalia, a small village in the south-central part of the state on the National Road. As northern Illinois opened to settlement in the 1830s, pressure grew to move the seat of government closer to the geographic center. A caucus of nine lawmakers, including a young Whig lawyer named Abraham Lincoln, pushed for the Sangamon County village of Springfield. They won in 1837, when the General Assembly passed a law setting up a two-year transition. Construction ran from 1837 to 1840 on Springfield's central square. Architect John Francis Rague designed the building in the Greek Revival style, using yellow limestone from nearby Crow's Mill, and the project cost $240,000, of which the city of Springfield paid $50,000. Inside were chambers for both houses of the General Assembly, offices for the governor and other executive officials, and a courtroom for the Illinois Supreme Court.

## What Representatives Hall Heard

Lincoln served his final term as a state legislator here in 1840 and 1841, and for the next two decades he argued cases before the state supreme court in the same building. The defining moment came in June 1858. In Representatives Hall, announcing his candidacy for the U.S. Senate, Lincoln delivered the House Divided speech: "I believe this government cannot endure, permanently half slave and half free." He lost that race to Stephen A. Douglas. Two years later he was nominated for president instead, and when well-wishers came calling, he received them in the second-floor Governor's reception room because his own law office was too small. After Lincoln's inauguration in 1861, Douglas stood in Representatives Hall and urged a cheering, nonpartisan crowd to set aside political differences to save the Union. Four years after that, Lincoln's body lay in the same room while a crowd of 75,000 mourners filed past to pay their last respects.

## Courthouse Years

The building served as the statehouse from 1840 to 1876. Economic growth spurred by the Civil War and the industrialization that followed left it too small by the 1870s, so Illinois built its sixth and current capitol four blocks to the southwest and handed the old one to Sangamon County. For ninety years, from 1876 to 1966, it was the county courthouse. The county was not sentimental about it. In 1898 and 1899, workers raised the whole structure 11 feet, added a third floor beneath it, and demolished and reconfigured the interior. The site was designated a National Historic Landmark in 1961, chiefly for its association with Lincoln and his rival Douglas, and the painstaking dismantling and reconstruction followed from 1966 to 1969. Today it is one of two preserved former Illinois capitol buildings; the other stands in Vandalia, where the story began.

## A Backdrop for Beginnings

The restored building still does ceremonial work, and modern politicians have come here to borrow a little of Lincoln's light. In February 2007, then-U.S. Senator Barack Obama officially announced his candidacy for President of the United States here, and in August 2008 he introduced his running mate, Joe Biden, with the old capitol as his backdrop. The building has even gone into Americans' pockets. A one-cent piece minted for general circulation in 2009 to celebrate Lincoln's work as a lawyer shows the Old State Capitol, whose Supreme Court courtroom was where he pleaded key cases in his legal career. Every May, the Springfield Old Capitol Art Fair is held around the building. Visitors can explore it on their own or join a guided tour; the state says there is no charge for the tour, though donations are appreciated. Climb to the hall where a Springfield lawyer once told Illinois exactly what he thought the country was facing.

## Flight Context

The Old State Capitol stands at 39.8011°N, 89.6486°W in the middle of downtown Springfield, Illinois, on the city's central square. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL, look for the pale limestone building set in an open block of the downtown grid; the far larger, domed current Illinois State Capitol rises four blocks to the southwest and is the easiest reference point in the city. Abraham Lincoln Capital Airport (KSPI) lies about 3 nautical miles to the north-northwest, so downtown sits close to its arrival and departure paths; KSPI has Class C airspace, so check the sectional chart and expect to work with approach control. Clear days give the best contrast between the two capitol domes.
