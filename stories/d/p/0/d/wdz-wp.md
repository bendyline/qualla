# WDZ

Born in 1921 as a ten-watt grain-price service for a Tuscola grain broker, WDZ survived a federal threat to its license, launched Smiley Burnette's career, and still broadcasts sports from Decatur under one of Illinois's rare three-letter call signs.

**Location:** 39.8097, -88.9992

**Categories:** Radio stations established in 1921, 1921 establishments in Illinois, Decatur, Illinois, Douglas County, Illinois, Radio stations in Illinois, Sports radio stations in the United States, Fox Sports Radio stations

## Introduction

WDZ did not begin with music or ball games. It began with grain prices. On March 17, 1921, a ten-watt transmitter in Tuscola, Illinois, began sending Chicago Board of Trade quotations out across the prairie so that local grain elevators could decide whether to sell. More than a century later, the station that grew from that humble service still broadcasts from Decatur on 1050 AM, now carrying Cubs and Bears games instead of grain quotes. It is one of the oldest radio stations in Illinois, and one of the few that still carry a three-letter call sign, keeping company with Chicago's WGN and WLS.

## Grain Prices on the Air

James L. Bush was a commodity broker who ran the James Bush Grain Company in Tuscola. In the early 1920s his firm received prices from the Chicago Board of Trade by private telegraph wire, and a telegraph operator named Clyde E. Wiley took down the reports and telephoned them, one by one, to area elevators. Wiley also had extensive experience as a radio operator. He installed a 10-watt transmitter, and under an amateur license, 9JR, issued to Bush, the service went on the air in March 1921. It caught on quickly, and that success brought trouble. Western Union and Postal Telegraph tried to have Bush expelled from the Board of Trade, claiming he was infringing on their exclusive right to distribute prices. After a review, the Board sided with Bush. By November the station was sending market reports every half hour from 9:30 in the morning until a final report at one in the afternoon.

## Three Letters and a Contested Birthday

At the end of 1921, the Department of Commerce began requiring broadcasters to hold a Limited Commercial license. On April 5, 1922, Bush received one with the randomly assigned call letters WDZ, authorized for the 360-meter wavelength, though the transmitter still ran on just 10 watts. Exactly how old WDZ is has been a matter of some pride and some argument. Station publicity has called it the oldest radio station in Illinois, dating to an experimental agricultural station in 1917, but evidence for any start before 1921 is lacking, and Chicago's WBBM traces its own history back to 1911. Wiley, for his part, later claimed that with a 1921 start WDZ was "the second broadcasting station in the country, (some say third, but what difference does it make now?)"

## Holding On to the Dial

In 1928 the station's future was in doubt. On May 25, the new Federal Radio Commission issued General Order 32, telling 164 stations, WDZ among them, that it could not find their continued operation served the public interest. WDZ made its case and kept its license. That November, a sweeping reallocation moved it to 1070 kHz, a clear-channel frequency where WDZ had to share time with WCAZ in Carthage and sign off at night to protect Cleveland's WTAM. A 1936 move to 1020 kHz ended the time-sharing and raised power from 100 to 250 watts, though it stayed a daytime station to protect KYW in Philadelphia, which had left Chicago for Philadelphia in 1934. By 1939 WDZ was running 1,000 watts from a new 252-foot tower. It was also doing something unusual for a rural station: remote broadcasts, relayed by a truck called the "WDZ White Relay Truck" with a 100-watt transmitter and by two-watt battery transmitters worn on assistants' backs. The 1941 NARBA reallocation put WDZ at 1050 kHz, where it has stayed ever since.

## How Lester Became Smiley

In 1929 a furniture store employee named Lester Burnette came to WDZ to do an advertising spot for his employer and ended up with a job. He did a bit of everything, from spinning records and directing music to sweeping the floors. Since much of his audience was children, he read the day's newspaper comics aloud in different voices with sound effects. While reading Mark Twain's "The Celebrated Jumping Frog of Calaveras County" on the air, he borrowed one of its character names, Smiley, for a character in a new children's program he was creating. The name ended up sticking to him, and so did "Frog." In December 1933, Smiley Burnette left for Chicago's WLS to work with Gene Autry. Two decades later his wife, Dallas, wrote that Mr. and Mrs. James L. Bush, "Mommie and Uncle Jim, as Smiley and I call them," were like a second set of parents to the couple.

## Fox Sports 1050

WDZ moved from Tuscola to Decatur in 1949, keeping a remote studio in Tuscola until 1958. Today its studios are on North Water Street, and it airs a sports format as "Fox Sports 1050," having returned to the Fox Sports Radio network on January 4, 2021. Alongside network shows it carries Chicago Cubs baseball, Chicago Bears football, local high school football and Millikin Big Blue football. 1050 kHz is a clear-channel frequency reserved for Mexico, so WDZ runs 1,000 watts by day and must drop to 250 watts at night to avoid interfering with other stations. It shares a single tower with sister station WSOY. In 2024, Champaign Multimedia Group bought Neuhoff Media's Danville and Decatur stations for $2 million, making WDZ a sibling of WDZQ, WKIO, WSOY and WSOY-FM.

## Flight Context

WDZ's listed coordinates are 39.8097 N, 88.9992 W, on the southwest side of Decatur, Illinois; the station's transmitter stands on St. Louis Bridge Road at Wesley Road, where WDZ shares a vertical radiator with WSOY. Treat the tower as an obstruction when flying low over the area. From 2,000-3,000 feet AGL, the city of Decatur and Lake Decatur, east of downtown, make the best references. Decatur Airport (KDEC), which has a control tower, is about 6 nautical miles east-northeast; Abraham Lincoln Capital Airport (KSPI) at Springfield is about 31 nautical miles west, and University of Illinois Willard Airport (KCMI) about 36 nautical miles east-northeast. Tuscola, the station's original home, is about 33 nautical miles east. Check the sectional chart and expect to work with the tower or approach control near the airports. Summer haze and thunderstorms are the main visibility concerns.
