# The Nerve Center of the Wabash

A story of the Decatur station's transition from a railroad headquarters to a sanctuary for antiques.

**Location:** 39.8472, -88.9464

**Categories:** Railway stations on the National Register of Historic Places in Illinois, Neoclassical architecture in Illinois, Railway stations in the United States opened in 1901, Buildings and structures in Decatur, Illinois, Railway Express Agency, National Register of Historic Places in Macon County, Illinois, Former Wabash Railroad stations, Former Amtrak stations in Illinois, Former railway stations in Illinois, Railway stations in the United States opened in 1981

## Introduction

Four hundred incandescent lights burned inside the building and train sheds during the dedication on June 18, 1901. Outside, twelve arc lights glowed, including one hanging in the tower to showcase its beauty. General superintendent H. L. Magee viewed the building as one of the most impressive on the line, remarking that "if the Wabash had a few more Decaturs scattered along the line, we would have electric fans and a barbershop on every train."

## Four Hundred Lights

The railroad spent $70,000 on the project, which St. Louis architect Theodore Link designed in the Classical Revival style. Yellow brick and limestone form the two unconnected structures—the station and the Railway Express Agency building—while terra cotta and sandstone provide the trim. Ionic pilasters flank the main and east entrances, which are topped by a pediment with ornamental modillions. Arched sandstone frames the first-floor windows, and limestone quoins mark the corners of the building.

This was more than a passenger depot; it served as maintenance offices and division headquarters for the Wabash Railroad, and later the Norfolk and Western. As the only station in Decatur to house railroad division offices, it served as home to one of the largest forces of dispatchers in the country.

## The Banner Blue

Passenger train service reached its peak in 1907. At this peak, seventy-two daily passenger trains brought travelers to and from the city, while $350,000 to $400,000 worth of freight shipped through the station yearly. Among the noteworthy Wabash trains that served the station, the Banner Blue and the Blue Bird ran between St. Louis and Chicago, while the Wabash Cannon Ball connected St. Louis to Detroit.

## The City of Decatur

The peak did not last. Passenger service began to decline in the 1920s, and as the years passed, the Wabash merged into the Norfolk & Western. The scale of operations shrank; in 1968, the Blue Bird was truncated to a shorter route called the City of Decatur, running only between Decatur and Chicago. This service persisted until 1971, the same year that Amtrak was formed and Norfolk & Western passenger service at the station ended. The Wabash Cannon Ball also ceased its run in 1971.

## The Final Stop

A brief revival followed. In 1981, Amtrak attempted to restart service with the Illini, but that effort lasted only until 1983. Then the station closed for good. On February 4, 1994, it was added to the National Register of Historic Places; it is the only remaining historic railroad station in Decatur. It was restored in 2002. Today, the building sits close to the WABIC railroad crossover, where the quiet of a historic monument has been replaced by the activity of the Wabash Depot Antique Mall.
