# The Octagon of Lincoln Square

A transit hub that once anchored the movement of an entire city before being moved to make way for the highway.

**Location:** 39.8425, -88.9533

**Categories:** Commercial buildings on the National Register of Historic Places in Illinois, Romanesque Revival architecture in Illinois, Octagonal buildings in the United States, Buildings and structures completed in 1896, Buildings and structures in Decatur, Illinois, National Register of Historic Places in Macon County, Illinois

## Introduction

By 1913, a survey claimed that 730 cars passed through Lincoln Square daily, including 82 interurbans and streetcars running at a rate of 36 per hour. The Transfer House, a two-story building designed by Chicago architect William W. Boyington, who also designed the Chicago Water Tower, served as a transfer point for these streetcars and interurbans. To provide more interior space without jutting too far into surrounding streets, Boyington gave the structure an octagonal plan. He drew from several Victorian styles, especially Richardsonian Romanesque. Thick limestone arches form the first-floor windows and entrances.

## 730 Cars a Day

Businessmen offered free train rides and souvenirs while an orchestra played for crowds that exceeded expectations during the building's opening. Once operational, the Transfer House became a focal point of Decatur because nearly every streetcar traveler in the city had to stop there to transfer. Passengers waited for their connections under a wide canopy and bell-shaped roof.

## The Roof as a Stage

While the first floor handled transit, the second floor consisted entirely of an open-air bandstand covered by a cupola. This elevated platform hosted speeches from Presidential Candidate William Jennings Bryan and Presidents Theodore Roosevelt, William Taft, and Woodrow Wilson. On August 14, 1945, when victory and peace in World War II were announced, people celebrated Victory over Japan Day by crowding the streets and climbing atop the roof of the Transfer House.

## The Conflict with the Car

In 1936, City Bus Lines made the Transfer House their official central transfer point. After streetcar and interurban systems ceased operations in the 1950s, the building was reduced to a bus terminal and lost most of its passenger volume. The Illinois Department of Transportation recommended that the city move the building because it interfered with traffic on highway US 51.

## A Moved Landmark

The city complied in 1962 and moved the structure to Central Park. The Transfer House transitioned into an official symbol of the City of Decatur and was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 2002. It has continued to serve as a civic landmark for the community. For example, during the 2020 Christmas season, the building was decorated to provide Santa Claus a safe and warm place to talk with the children of Decatur.
