# The Vanishing Homestead

Abraham Lincoln's first Illinois home lasted a single hard year, and the cabin itself later vanished from the record.

**Location:** 39.8028, -89.1008

**Categories:** Protected areas of Macon County, Illinois, State parks of Illinois, Abraham Lincoln National Heritage Area, Monuments and memorials in Illinois, Protected areas established in 1938, 1938 establishments in Illinois

## Introduction

The cabin that sheltered Abraham Lincoln during his first year in Illinois later went on tour. By one widely repeated account, in 1865 it was dismantled for public viewing and shipped to Chicago, then to Boston, and finally to a museum in New York City run by P.T. Barnum. There the paper trail ends, and what became of it is unknown. It had begun as a roughly 18-by-18-foot house of local logs on the north side of the Sangamon River, and the family that built it stayed only a year.

## Where Timber Met Prairie

Thomas Lincoln moved his household from Indiana in March 1830: about twelve members of his extended family, among them his grown son Abraham, then twenty-one. They settled west of Decatur, at a spot Abraham later described as the place where timber met prairie, about ten miles from town. Using local logs, they raised the cabin that would shelter them through their first Illinois year.

## Rails, Sod and Fever

Abraham split rails for his father's ten-acre field, and he also hired out to split rails for neighboring pioneer farmers. The work would later inspire his political nickname, the Rail Splitter. Pioneer farmers used split-rail fences to confine their stock, and to keep free-ranging livestock from getting into a crop field and damaging it. In his own 1860 account, Lincoln recorded that the family made enough rails to fence ten acres and raised a crop of sown corn there that same year. The harvest disappointed. The corn had been planted directly in the sod of the tallgrass prairie, and the yield was poor. Then illness arrived, and many members of the family developed severe cases of malaria, which is associated with living in the Illinois wetlands.

## The Winter of the Deep Snow

Then came the winter of 1830-1831, which pioneers called the Winter of the Deep Snow. Lengthy periods of sub-zero temperatures set in, and the snow reached about three feet on the level, with drifts of four to six feet. The Lincoln clan faced serious hunger, and according to one report, "Abraham rode to nearby homes seeking food for his family." Lincoln himself, looking back from 1860, called it "the winter of the very celebrated 'deep snow' of Illinois." Illness and the harshness of that winter sent the family on to Coles County in March 1831, only a year after they had arrived.

## The Whitleys' Mill

The abandoned farmstead did not stay empty. The Whitley family settled there and lived at the site for several generations. They built a dam across the Sangamon River to power a small flour mill, and they established a pioneer cemetery. In 1938 the state created a memorial on the Whitley site, and it was formally dedicated in 1957. Today the state park near Harristown serves as a park and picnic area for the greater Decatur metropolitan area, with hiking, canoeing, fishing and picnicking on offer. It holds the Whitleys' pioneer cemetery and the remains of their flour mill and dam, set among mature second-growth bottomland timber that includes black walnut trees. Illinois DNR's current closures list shows a park roads project at the site in 2026, including a temporary closure in early August, so check it before visiting.

## Looking for the Footprint

The memorial is believed to contain the site of the 1830 homestead, and "believed" is the operative word. Archaeologists have reportedly not definitively located the cabin, which may lie within the memorial's boundaries or slightly beyond them. A marker placed by the Decatur chapter of the Daughters of the American Revolution in 1904 notes the approximate location of the homestead. A replica cabin once stood at the site but has since burned, and visitors find markers where a building might have been. What the park does have is trees. Oak and hickory grow here, with redbud, dogwood and pawpaw beneath them, and bluebells, larkspur and wild ginger among the wildflowers. An American linden considered to be over 300 years old would have stood here in Lincoln's time. If that estimate holds, it is a living witness to the winter of 1830-1831.

## Flight Context

The memorial sits at 39.8028 N, 89.1008 W on the Sangamon River in Macon County, near Harristown and roughly seven nautical miles west of central Decatur. From 1,500-2,500 feet AGL, follow the wooded Sangamon River corridor west of Decatur to find the park. Nearby airports: Decatur Airport (KDEC), about 11 nm east; Abraham Lincoln Capital Airport (KSPI) in Springfield, about 27 nm west; Logan County Airport (KAAA) in Lincoln, about 24 nm north-northwest. KDEC and KSPI have control towers, so check the sectional chart and expect to work with the tower or approach control. Check current METARs at KDEC and KSPI for conditions before descending to low altitude.
