# Lytleville, Illinois

A McLean County mill village laid out by a zealous preacher in 1836 was said to have come within a single committee vote of becoming the county seat, then faded when the railroad went three miles west.

**Location:** 40.3417, -88.9556

**Categories:** Unincorporated communities in McLean County, Illinois, Unincorporated communities in Illinois, Populated places established in 1836, 1836 establishments in Illinois

## Introduction

On Sundays, when the water was up, the old gate-saw at Lytleville kept running. John Baldwin, the preacher who had bought the mill, would go down to reason with his partner about working on the Sabbath. While the two men argued, the saw kept cutting, and the sawyer had a knack for getting Baldwin to lend a hand turning down the log and setting its tail end. None of those kind acts converted the partner, and in the end the partnership dissolved. The scene belongs to a village that was busy and ambitious in its day. Lytleville, about three and a half miles northeast of Heyworth in McLean County, Illinois, once had roughly 100 residents and dreams of greatness. Very little of it remains.

## A Village Around a Sawmill

The mill came before the town. James Hedrick built it in 1833, and it may have been the first water-powered sawmill and grist mill in McLean County. Three years later he sold it to Baldwin, who in 1836 laid out a community around his sawmill in what is now Randolph Township. He named it for a friend, Robert T. Lytle, a Democrat who had served as a U.S. Congressman from Ohio from 1833 to 1835. The gate-saw at the heart of it all was an old-fashioned machine, so slow that there seemed to be some excuse for running it every hour the water allowed. That excuse was exactly what the partner leaned on each Sunday, and exactly what Baldwin could not accept.

## The Preacher Who Prophesied

Baldwin had not always been devout; he had been a wicked and boisterous man before his conversion. Afterward he became a preacher and threw himself into revival work with great spirit and zeal. Many people considered him eccentric, but he stayed faithful to his calling. Stories were told of his prophesying when he was under strong religious excitement, though there is no record that any of the prophecies came true. His brother, James Baldwin, was the community's earliest doctor and lived on a farm east of the mill property. Between them, the Baldwin brothers tended to the souls and the bodies of a young settlement, one with the preaching and the other with the healing.

## One Vote Short

For a time, Lytleville was a bustling trading center for dry goods, groceries and whiskey. It competed hard with Waynesville and Bloomington to become the leading town in this part of the county. A post office opened, stores and trade grew up, and the village reportedly lacked just one committee vote to become the county seat of McLean County. That detail came from Dave Hartson, born in Lytleville in 1834, who died in July 1922, six weeks after an author visited him. It is best read as local tradition: McLean County was created in December 1830 and Bloomington became its seat in 1831, years before Lytleville was laid out. Local controversy later grew around a bolder claim, that Lytleville had once been considered for the Illinois state capital. The only chance would have been in 1836 and early 1837, when Springfield and several other towns were competing before the legislature chose Springfield in February 1837, and that seems unlikely for a village that had barely been laid out.

## Schoolhouses and a Fire

The village's schools trace its whole life. Lytleville School No. 18 began in the 1830s as a log house with crude benches and slabs on pegs for seats. In the 1840s a frame schoolhouse went up just across the road to the north. A Methodist church, built in 1874 at the south end of State Street, was sold to the school district in 1886 and served as the schoolhouse, remodeled and standardized in 1921, until it burned on January 15, 1931. That summer a new schoolhouse went up for about $2,800. Enrollment had dwindled from the large classes of earlier years, but the building remained a place for public meetings until Lytleville's school joined the Heyworth Community Unit School District in 1948. It was then sold, and it still stands as a private home.

## Three Miles From the Tracks

What undid Lytleville was a railroad that never came. In the 1850s, as the Illinois Central built its main line through McLean County, a heated contest broke out over the exact route. Local history says Campbell Wakefield, the founder of Heyworth, donated land to railroad officials to draw the line through his property, and the company eventually built there. The tracks ended up about three miles west of Lytleville, and the village declined rapidly as people moved to the new town of Heyworth. Today only a scattering of homes marks the spot, none of them original to the village. The rebuilt schoolhouse from the 1930s is the most tangible link to a place that, by local tradition, once came one vote from becoming the county seat.

## Flight Context

Lytleville's coordinates, 40.3417°N, 88.9556°W, put the community roughly 2.5 to 3 nautical miles northeast of Heyworth's center in Randolph Township, McLean County, Illinois. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL, find Heyworth first, where U.S. Route 136 runs through the village center and the four-lane U.S. Route 51 passes along its west side, then look to the northeast for a scattering of rural homes; there is no village grid to spot. Bloomington–Normal lies about 9 to 10 nautical miles to the north. Central Illinois Regional Airport (KBMI) at Bloomington is about 8 nautical miles to the north-northeast, and Decatur Airport (KDEC) is about 31 nautical miles to the south. The flat terrain gives good visibility on clear days.
