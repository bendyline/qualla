# Tornado outbreak of March 28–31, 2007

A late-March storm system spun up about 80 tornadoes across the central United States in 2007, brushing central Illinois with two harmless twisters before devastating Holly, Colorado, and taking five lives on the High Plains.

**Location:** 40.2800, -89.2300

**Categories:** F3, EF3 and IF3 tornadoes, Tornadoes of 2007, Tornadoes in Colorado, Tornadoes in Illinois, Tornadoes in Iowa, Tornadoes in Kansas, Tornadoes in Missouri, Tornadoes in Nebraska, Tornadoes in Oklahoma, Tornadoes in Texas

## Introduction

Just after 3 p.m. on March 28, 2007, a tornado touched down in an open field north of Atlanta, Illinois. Fourteen minutes later another spun up southwest of McLean, a few miles to the northeast. Each traveled about a tenth of a mile, no more than ten yards wide, and neither damaged a thing. They are among the quietest entries in the record of a storm system that was about to erupt far to the west. Over four days, the tornado outbreak of March 28–31, 2007, also known as the Late-March 2007 tornado outbreak, produced about 80 confirmed tornadoes, hail as large as softballs and straight-line winds as strong as 90 mph. Five people died. It arrived four weeks after the deadly February 28–March 2 outbreak farther east.

## A Dry Line Ignites

The engine was a powerful mid- and upper-level storm system that tracked from the Great Basin into the central Rocky Mountains, carrying terrifically strong winds aloft over the southern and central Great Plains. Ahead of it, a dry line stretched south from a low-pressure area over extreme northeastern Colorado and the Nebraska Panhandle into western Texas. South and southeasterly winds spread increasingly humid air across the western Plains through the afternoon, and unseasonably warm temperatures added lift and fuel. Tornado watches went up from South Dakota to Texas. Late in the afternoon, the advancing dry line and an eastbound cold front combined to set off explosive supercell development, and by early evening severe storms stood in a nearly continuous line from near Lubbock, Texas, to Rapid City, South Dakota. Before those storms merged into a squall line late that evening, at least 63 tornadoes had been reported, many of them very large and potentially destructive. The sparse population of the region prevented widespread death and destruction.

## Three Places, Five Lives

The deaths came in small places. In southeastern Colorado, a large tornado formed in Prowers County and rapidly intensified to EF3 strength, devastating the town of Holly. Up to 200 structures were damaged or destroyed, some block-foundation homes were swept away, and two people died. Its path ran 28 miles, and at its widest it measured 900 yards across. Farther south, a long-lived, multiple-vortex EF2 tornado caused no damage in Texas before crossing into Oklahoma, where it destroyed a house. The couple inside had taken refuge in their bathroom, and both were killed. In Hemphill County, Texas, a large EF3 tornado moved through an oil drilling location and blew away a fifth-wheel trailer, fatally injuring the person inside; one other person was hurt. Five people lost their lives that day: two in a small Colorado town, two sheltering together in their own home and one at work in a drilling-site trailer.

## No Time to Warn

March 29 was supposed to be quieter. Forecasters had called the day very uncertain, because so much depended on whether the dry line would fire up again, and the squall line pushing east from the night before kept a major daytime outbreak from developing. Only a slight risk of severe storms was issued. Yet the squall line stayed intact as it moved, and tornadoes formed anyway, the weather ignoring the careful language of the outlook. Two of them struck the Oklahoma City metropolitan area with almost no advance warning, causing significant damage. At least two people were hospitalized when their RV was hit, and four others were injured by a tornado ten minutes later.

## The Long Fade East

The system spent two more days drifting east and north. On March 30, a moderate risk covered south-central Texas into the Mexican state of Coahuila and was later extended into northern Texas and south-central Oklahoma, while the Oklahoma tornado watch stretched into eastern Kansas. On March 31, a moderate risk for central Texas was downgraded to slight as the storm line pushed northeast. Tornado warnings went out in southeastern Arkansas, and a thunderstorm watch even reached Louisiana. By evening the system had arrived in the north-central states, prompting warnings in Wisconsin, Illinois and Iowa and a watch in northwestern Indiana. Then it weakened, and no more tornadoes formed. Illinois took one last blow. Just before 10 p.m. CDT, in what National Weather Service officials later confirmed as a microburst, violent winds struck Carol Stream and Glendale Heights in DuPage County, just west of Chicago. Roofs and windows were heavily damaged on buildings including an apartment complex and a church, along a path about half a mile long and 250 yards wide. The outbreak that had touched central Illinois so gently on its first afternoon left its final mark on the Chicago suburbs.

## Flight Context

This story is pinned at 40.28°N, 89.23°W, in open country between Atlanta in Logan County and McLean in McLean County, Illinois, where the outbreak's two brief EF0 tornadoes touched down in open fields on March 28, 2007. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL, Interstate 55 is the obvious guide, running northeast–southwest past both towns; Atlanta lies to the southwest along the highway and McLean to the northeast. Logan County Airport (KAAA) at Lincoln is about 9 nautical miles to the southwest, and Central Illinois Regional Airport (KBMI) at Bloomington is about 19 nautical miles to the northeast. The land is flat and open, giving wide views in clear weather. Spring convective weather can build quickly over the prairie, so check forecasts closely before flying here in storm season.
