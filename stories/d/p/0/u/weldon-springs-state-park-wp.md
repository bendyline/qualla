# The Spring on the Chautauqua Circuit

A spring fed through glacial sand and gravel on the flat Grand Prairie drew Helen Keller, William Jennings Bryan and former President Taft to its Chautauqua stage before becoming an Illinois state park.

**Location:** 40.1206, -88.9236

**Categories:** Chautauqua, Protected areas of DeWitt County, Illinois, Protected areas of Piatt County, Illinois, State parks of Illinois, Springs of Illinois, Protected areas established in 1936, 1936 establishments in Illinois

## Introduction

Helen Keller spoke here. So did William Jennings Bryan, Carrie Nation, the Rev. Billy Sunday and former President William Howard Taft. From 1901 until 1921, a spring outside Clinton, Illinois, was a recognized stop on the Chautauqua circuit, and for ten days each year inspirational speakers and entertainers took top billing on the outdoor amphitheatres of its spring garden. The circuit is long gone, but the campgrounds and amphitheatres of those days are still kept in readiness for use at what is now Weldon Springs State Park.

## Rain From Two Thousand Years Ago

A spring is an unusual thing on the relatively flat Grand Prairie of central Illinois. The water that emerges here began as rain and snowmelt that seeped into the ground about 2,000 years ago, according to the Illinois Department of Natural Resources, then migrated about a mile through layers of sand and gravel before surfacing at the springs. That thick deposit of silty sand and gravel was left by a continental-size glacier that covered the Clinton area 10,000 to 25,000 years ago. The springs became a wetland, and one account says it holds some of the northernmost bald cypress trees in Illinois.

The park's main unit covers 550 acres and centers on Salt Creek, where a tributary, Weldon Springs, was impounded to form a reservoir, Weldon Spring Lake. The lake and Salt Creek are constantly fed by the same source as the springs.

## The Judge's Fishing Camp

In the 1800s the springs were the fishing camp of a leading local citizen, Judge Lawrence Weldon. Weldon practiced law in Clinton from 1854 to 1861 and was an associate of Abraham Lincoln, who appointed him United States Attorney for the Southern District of Illinois; Weldon named a son in Lincoln's honor. He later served more than two decades as a judge of the federal Court of Claims.

In 1900 the judge leased the springs to the Weldon Springs Company, which raised $7,500 to redevelop them as a Chautauqua. A branch line of the Illinois Central Railroad served the grounds, and from 1901 the spring garden took its place on the circuit, a ten-day season of uplift and entertainment staged in the open air.

## Cars, Movies and a Locked Gate

The circuit could not outlast the new century's distractions. As personal motor vehicles became more available and movies spread, the Chautauqua circuit collapsed in the early 1920s. The Weldon family donated the springs to Clinton, the county seat, in 1936, and in 1948 the parcel moved into state ownership. It remains the heart of the park, which the Illinois Department of Natural Resources supervises.

Its gates did close for a while. Weldon Springs was one of eleven state parks slated to close indefinitely on November 1, 2008, because of budget cuts by then-Governor Rod Blagojevich. After a delay that restored funding for some of the parks, a plan to close seven state parks and a dozen state historic sites, Weldon Springs among them, went ahead at the end of that month, with the parks closed by December 1, 2008. After Blagojevich's impeachment, the new governor, Pat Quinn, reopened the closed state parks in February 2009.

## A Schoolhouse Named for the Union

The park's visitor center started life in another county. When nearby Logan County built its first pioneer public schools in the 1860s, one of the one-room schools was named the Union School (1865), after the side Illinois took in the Civil War. It served rural Logan County for more than 80 years until it was consolidated about 1950, and in 1988 the building was moved to Weldon Springs and restored as the park's museum and visitor center.

More than 7 miles of trails fan out from the schoolhouse and parking lot. Down in the Salt Creek bottomlands, the oaks and hickories of the upland forest give way to silver maple, sycamore and hackberry. The 1.3-mile Schoolhouse Trail circles a restored tallgrass prairie where more than 30 butterfly species have been identified, and more than 80 birdhouses are kept up for bluebirds, which can be seen from late February through October. On the spring-fed lake, managed for largemouth bass and channel catfish with panfish too, gasoline motors are not allowed.

## Downstream on the Sangamon

Weldon Springs has a second, separate unit near Monticello. The Piatt County Unit comprises two parcels in the county of the same name, next to Allerton Park, part of which is a designated National Natural Landmark. Together they protect 770 acres along 2.3 miles of the Sangamon River, and most of that land is designated a Land and Water Reserve by the Illinois Nature Preserves Commission. Silver maples, cottonwoods, sycamores and black walnuts grow in its upland and bottomland forests, and the site is open to hunting, hiking and nature observation.

## Flight Context

Weldon Springs State Park's main unit lies at 40.12°N, 88.92°W, near Clinton in DeWitt County, Illinois, where 550 acres follow Salt Creek around Weldon Spring Lake. From 2,000 to 3,000 ft above ground, look for the lake and the creek bottoms set into otherwise flat prairie country. Decatur Airport (KDEC) is about 17 nm to the south, Central Illinois Regional Airport at Bloomington-Normal (KBMI) about 21 nm to the north, and University of Illinois Willard Airport (KCMI) about 30 nm to the east. The separate Piatt County Unit lies near Monticello along the Sangamon River. Best viewed in clear daytime weather.
