# Your Radio Active Neighbor

Finished seven years late at nearly ten times its budget, the Clinton Power Station in central Illinois has been shut down, sold for about $20 million, scheduled for closure and twice pulled back from the edge; it is now licensed to run until 2047.

**Location:** 40.1722, -88.8350

**Categories:** Energy infrastructure completed in 1987, Buildings and structures in DeWitt County, Illinois, Nuclear power stations with proposed reactors, Nuclear power plants in Illinois, Exelon

## Introduction

In April 1989, the radio station in Clinton, Illinois, gave itself a new motto: "WHOW, your radio active station." The joke was aimed at the town's new neighbor. Clinton Power Station had begun commercial operation on November 24, 1987, with a single General Electric boiling water reactor, which after a later uprate can produce up to 1,092 megawatts. It arrived seven years behind schedule, at a final cost of $4.25 billion against an original budget of $430 million, nearly ten times as much. In the decades since, the plant has been shut down, sold for a sliver of that price, scheduled for closure and twice pulled back from the edge.

## A Troubled Start

The early years were rough. The plant went down for maintenance frequently and was out of service for almost half the time between September 1988 and October 1989. After less than a decade of operation, its original owner, Illinois Power, had to shut it down in 1996, following technical problems and safety violations that brought a $450,000 fine, and in 1997 it was said to be producing "some of the highest electric rates in the midwest." The buyer, AmerGen, a joint venture of PECO Energy and British Energy that Exelon would later take over, agreed in 1999 to pay a far more modest sum, up to $20 million, and took on full responsibility for operating and eventually decommissioning the plant. The new owner soon asked more of the machine. In 2001 AmerGen sought a 20 percent power uprate, from 2,894 to 3,473 megawatts thermal, worth 193 megawatts of electric output. As of July 2012, the Energy Information Administration still counted it as the largest extended uprate the Nuclear Regulatory Commission had approved.

## Cobalt in the Core

Clinton's reactor has also been drafted into medicine. In January 2010, GE-Hitachi announced that the station would begin producing cobalt-60, a radioactive isotope used in cancer therapy, the sterilization of medical equipment, food irradiation and materials testing. The method is simple in outline. A target rod rich in ordinary, non-radioactive cobalt-59 goes into the reactor core, where it captures free neutrons and becomes cobalt-60, which processing extracts once the rod is retrieved. More than 80 percent of the world's supply had traditionally come from one aging research reactor, Canada's NRU at Chalk River, and the plan would make Clinton the only light water reactor then producing it. "We view this as an opportunity for Exelon to support an important medical technology that saves people's lives," said Exelon Nuclear president Charles Pardee. A September 2011 announcement added a feasibility study, commissioned by GE-Hitachi and Exelon, into making molybdenum-99 at the reactor. Molybdenum-99 decays into technetium-99m, which is used in tens of millions of diagnostic imaging procedures every year.

## Closing Time, Cancelled

For a while, Clinton looked set to grow. In September 2003 Exelon applied for an Early Site Permit for a possible second reactor, and it was issued on March 15, 2007. The permit did not license construction, though; it only opened a path toward approval. Instead the opposite happened. By June 2016 the second reactor had never advanced beyond the permit, and Exelon said the existing reactor would shut down in June 2017, because the plant was struggling to compete in wholesale electricity markets and had lost millions of dollars in recent years. Rescue came that December. With natural gas pushing rates down, Illinois voted to subsidize Exelon with about $235 million a year for 10 years to keep Clinton and the Quad Cities Nuclear Generating Station open. The legislature passed and the governor signed SB 2814, the Future Energy Jobs Bill, which provides Zero Emission Credits for the plants' carbon-free electricity, and the closure was called off.

## Twenty More Years

The 2016 law bought time, not a future. The reactor's operating license, issued April 17, 1987, was due to expire on April 17, 2027. In October 2022, Constellation Energy, which became owner and operator after its spin-off from Exelon, said it would ask for a 20-year extension, and it applied in February 2024 to run the plant until 2047. The economics followed. In 2025 Constellation signed a 20-year agreement with Meta, Facebook's parent company, to buy electricity from the plant beginning in June 2027, as the state's zero emission credit support runs out. The deal is to add 30 megawatts through uprates, and Constellation said it would preserve 1,100 local jobs. On December 16, 2025, the Nuclear Regulatory Commission renewed the license, extending operations to April 17, 2047. Constellation now calls the plant the Clinton Clean Energy Center.

## Within Fifty Miles

The plant itself occupies only around 150 acres of a 14,300-acre site. The operator owns that land and the roughly 5,000-acre cooling reservoir, Clinton Lake, but both host the Clinton Lake State Recreation Area, open to the public for a wide range of outdoor activities. WHOW was not the last to make the joke. Inspired by the lake's purpose, the Clinton Lake Sailing Association hosts the Glow In The Dark Regatta, which in 2018 drew 34 teams from eight states. The Nuclear Regulatory Commission draws a 10-mile emergency planning zone around every plant, and in 2010 it held 14,677 people here. The 50-mile ingestion zone held 813,658, taking in Champaign, Decatur, Bloomington-Normal and parts of Springfield and the Peoria area. An NRC study published in August 2010 put the yearly risk of an earthquake strong enough to damage Clinton's reactor core at 1 in 400,000.

## Flight Context

Clinton Power Station stands at about 40.17°N, 88.84°W in DeWitt County, Illinois, beside Clinton Lake, its roughly 5,000-acre cooling reservoir. The sky-blue reactor building is the landmark to look for; from 2,500 to 3,500 feet above ground the plant and the lake's arms are easy to pick out. Give the plant a wide berth rather than circling overhead. Central Illinois Regional Airport at Bloomington-Normal (KBMI) is about 19 nautical miles to the north, Decatur Airport (KDEC) about 20 nautical miles to the south, and University of Illinois/Willard Airport (KCMI) about 27 nautical miles to the east. Summer haze and thunderstorms are common over central Illinois.
