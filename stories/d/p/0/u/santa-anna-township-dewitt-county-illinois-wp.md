# A Borrowed Name on the Prairie

Since 1859 this central Illinois township, once Mt. Pleasant, has carried the name Santa Anna, which Wikipedia ties to a Comanche war chief who turned from resistance to peace, though local history names no honoree.

**Location:** 40.2397, -88.6486

**Categories:** Townships in DeWitt County, Illinois, 1858 establishments in Illinois, Populated places established in 1858, Townships in Illinois

## Introduction

Santa Anna Township covers about 29 square miles of DeWitt County, Illinois, and all but a sliver of it is land. Its name, though, comes from a long way off. On June 7, 1859, what had been Mt. Pleasant Township was renamed Santa Anna, and Wikipedia says the name honors a Comanche war chief who became a peace advocate. He had died almost ten years earlier, among his own people in Texas. The local record is thinner than that claim. The name was already in use for the village post office before 1859, proposed by a settler named John Smith, and the 1882 county history does not say whom it honored. Another Santa Anna, the Mexican general of the Alamo and San Jacinto, was far better known to Americans then, so the Comanche connection is best treated as possible rather than proven. Whoever the name was meant for, the Comanche chief's life is worth following.

## A Voice for Resistance

Santa Anna was born around 1800 on Edward's Plateau in Texas and belonged to the Penateka division of the Comanche, in the same area as the war chiefs Buffalo Hump and Yellow Wolf. He rose to prominence in the years after the Texas Revolution. Then came the deadly Council House Fight, where, the Comanche felt, whites had slaughtered their envoys despite the promise of a white treaty flag. Clashes with migrating Anglo-Texans grew more frequent, and Santa Anna argued for armed and bitter resistance to the invasion of the Comancheria.

For roughly the next five years he joined Buffalo Hump and other war chiefs in raids on Anglo settlements, including the Great Raid of 1840, when the Comanche attacked Victoria, sacked and burned Linnville and raided all the way to the sea. His own part cannot be traced with any precision today. He probably took part in the attacks on Linnville and Victoria, and he may have been at the Battle of Plum Creek. Before 1845, he stood firmly with the faction of his tribe that opposed accommodation with whites.

## What the Treaty Table Revealed

The turn came at a negotiating table. In the latter part of 1845, Santa Anna was persuaded to attend treaty talks held by United States officials, and there he saw for the first time the true numbers and weaponry of the whites. Convinced that his people could neither defeat them nor resist them for long, he began to argue for peace. In May 1846 he was one of the Comanche chiefs who signed a treaty promising peace between his people and American citizens in Texas. The geologist Ferdinand von Roemer, present at treaty talks with the chiefs, described him as "a powerfully built man with a benevolent and lively countenance."

Later he traveled to Washington, D.C., with a party of chiefs from several tribes in Texas, the first of his tribe to make such a journey. He was recorded as overwhelmed by what he saw, above all by the sheer numbers of whites. From then on he believed that continued armed resistance against the United States was tantamount to suicide for his people, and he spent his prestige as a noted war chief trying to secure a lasting peace.

## The Cost of Peace

Peace cost him. Among Comanche who still favored war, his conversion reduced his standing. Apparently tired of that reduced position, he led several raids into Mexico in 1848 and 1849 to regain his former glory, until the U.S. Army and the Indian agent Robert S. Neighbors persuaded him to stop.

Disease then did what war had not. In late December 1849, a cholera epidemic killed more than 300 Penateka Comanche in a few weeks, and Santa Anna was among the dead. Buffalo Hump, also ill, survived, as did Yellow Wolf. Afterward the Penateka, apart from the division Buffalo Hump commanded, came apart, and the survivors joined other Comanche groups. About nine and a half years later, east of the Mississippi, a township in DeWitt County took the name Santa Anna, whether or not it was meant for him.

## Too Many Mount Pleasants

The old name had a practical problem of its own. Before settlers arrived, the Kickapoo and Potawatomi lived on the prairie and in the woodlands around Salt Creek and other local streams. Later, part of the settlement that grew into Farmer City became known as Mt. Pleasant. Mail delivery began in 1837, but with another Mt. Pleasant in Illinois, the name changed to Santa Anna. The 1882 county history says a settler named John Smith proposed it for the post office and became its first postmaster, without saying whom it honored. In the early 1850s, DeWitt County lay in the Eighth Judicial District, and the lawyer Abraham Lincoln traveled through the area many times on his circuit.

The town eventually took a name of its own. Farmer City, the township's one city, counted 1,828 residents in 2020. Interstate 74, paralleled by US 150, and State Route 54 intersect near town, with Norfolk Southern and Illinois Central routes running alongside. Farmer City even earned a footnote in linguistics: a 1980 University of Illinois dissertation, "Sound Change in Farmer City," studied local speech using acoustic data. Beyond the city lie the unincorporated community of Watkins and five cemeteries: Campground, Farmer City, Greenleaf, Maple Grove and Saint Josephs Catholic.

## Twenty-Nine Square Miles, 2,276 Neighbors

The 2020 census counted 2,276 people in Santa Anna Township, living in 959 households, with 1,085 housing units in all. The population leans toward middle age and beyond: the median age was 49.3, some 35.8 percent of residents were between 45 and 64, and another 22.0 percent were 65 or older. Married couples made up 59.02 percent of households, and about a third of households were people living alone. Two school districts serve the township: Blue Ridge Community Unit School District 18, which keeps its high school in Farmer City and shares facilities with nearby Mansfield and Bellflower, and Deland-Weldon Community Unit School District 57. Of its 29.02 square miles, just 0.13 is water.
