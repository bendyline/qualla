# Shelbyville, Illinois

A Kaskaskia River county seat where a glacier stopped, a dishwasher and a hay baler were born, and a 20-sided wooden Chautauqua hall still stands in the park.

**Location:** 39.4081, -88.7997

**Categories:** Populated places established in 1827, Cities in Illinois, Cities in Shelby County, Illinois, County seats in Illinois, 1827 establishments in Illinois

## Introduction

Among the marvels on display at the 1893 Columbian Exposition in Chicago was a machine for washing dishes, and it won "the highest award." Its inventor, Josephine Garis Cochran, had created it in 1886, one of the first mechanical dishwashers ever made, and her home was Shelbyville, a small county seat on the Kaskaskia River in central Illinois. She was not the town's only inventor to change how people work. A restored house on West Main Street and a national engineering landmark designation tell the story of another Shelbyville machine, one whose basic mechanism has been used in more than fifteen million farm balers.

## A Cabin Becomes a Courthouse

Shelbyville was founded in 1827 and named for Isaac Shelby, a hero of the Revolutionary War and a governor of Kentucky. Its history begins with Barnett Bone, a Tennessean who built a log cabin along the Kaskaskia River. The 1827 law that created Shelby County made Bone's home its temporary county seat, and the county's first courthouse, a two-story log building finished in 1828, followed. The first businesses followed the needs of a frontier crossroads: blacksmith shops, a general store, a stagecoach stop and a grist mill to turn the settlers' grain into flour. Politics arrived early, too. In 1856 Anthony Thornton, who would later serve in Congress and on the Supreme Court of Illinois, debated Abraham Lincoln here. Samuel Wheeler Moulton, a congressman considered the father of public education in Illinois, made Shelbyville his home from 1849 to 1905.

## Machines Born on the Prairie

Cochran's dishwasher earned the headlines, but Shelbyville's other great invention reshaped farming. The first commercial pick-up baler was designed and developed here by Raymore McDonald, conceived and financed by Horace M. Tallman and his two sons, Leslie and Gentry. The Ann Arbor Machine Company of Shelbyville marketed the balers for many years. The idea of processing forage right in the field made a significant contribution to the efficiency and economy of harvesting in agriculture worldwide, and the basic pick-up mechanism has since been used in more than fifteen million balers. The American Society of Agricultural and Biological Engineers named the Ann Arbor Baler one of its historic landmarks of agricultural engineering, a list of roughly sixty in the United States. Tallman's home on West Main Street has been restored, listed on the National Register of Historic Places, and folded into the Shelby Inn.

## Twenty Sides and No Pillars

In Forest Park stands one of Shelbyville's most unusual buildings. The Chautauqua Auditorium, built in 1903, is an icosagonal wooden meeting hall, meaning it has twenty sides, and it is described as the largest building of its kind anywhere in the world. Built by a local bridge builder, H.B. Trout, who patterned it on the Rock River Assembly tabernacle in Dixon, a design by architect Morrison H. Vail, it uses a structural support system that leaves no interior pillars to block the view of the stage. On that stage stand three female Grecian statues representing Art, Music and Drama, the work of Shelbyville native Robert Root, a painter known for his tonalist and impressionist canvases. The hall joined the National Register of Historic Places on January 30, 1978. Its survival has not been guaranteed: in 2009, Landmarks Illinois named it one of the ten most endangered historic places in the state. Since then, Shelbyville voters backed a March 2020 referendum to fund maintenance, and a restoration followed in 2021.

## Where the Ice Stopped

The landscape around Shelbyville records a moment when the last great glacier reached its limit. The terminal moraine of the Wisconsin glacier, a ridge of debris left where the ice sheet halted, lies near town and is known as the Shelbyville Moraine. The Kaskaskia River cuts through that ridge, and the river was dammed at the breach. The Army Corps of Engineers broke ground in 1963, and construction was finished in the early summer of 1970, creating Lake Shelbyville. The glacier's farthest reach and the modern reservoir now meet in the same spot, one left behind by the retreating ice and the other raised in about seven years of construction.

## Life in a County Seat

Shelbyville counted 4,674 residents in the 2020 census, and its roles are larger than its size. HSHS Good Shepherd Hospital, in town, is the only hospital in Shelby County. Shelbyville Community Unit School District 4 runs the local schools, including Shelbyville High School, and for just over a century the town was also home to Sparks College, a business trade school that opened in 1908 and closed in 2009. The list of people with ties to the city is long for a place this size. Jesse Monroe Donaldson served as Postmaster General of the United States from 1947 to 1953. Augusta Cottlow became a concert pianist, and Orval Caldwell, a landscape painter, served as president of the Chicago No-Jury Society in 1940 and 1941.
