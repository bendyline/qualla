# 2013 Washington, Illinois tornado

On a Sunday morning in November 2013, a high-end EF4 tornado carved a 46-mile path from East Peoria through Washington, Illinois, destroying whole neighborhoods yet killing three people, and leaving a city to rebuild through a brutal winter after FEMA refused to cover local governments' costs.

## Introduction

It was a Sunday morning, and in Washington, Illinois, many people were not at home. Some were at church; others had gone shopping. When forecasters later tried to explain how a tornado with winds of 190 miles per hour could tear through densely populated neighborhoods on November 17, 2013, and kill only three people, that was part of their answer. The rest lay in basements and storm shelters, in the wider spread of weather warnings, and in a threat that had been heavily publicized before the storm arrived.

## A Warning Heard

The Storm Prediction Center had watched the system for days. Early on November 17 it upgraded parts of Illinois, Indiana, southwestern Michigan and western Ohio to a high risk for severe weather, then expanded the zone to cover a region of the Midwest home to nearly 19 million people, with at least a 30 percent chance of tornadoes. Over central Illinois, a supercell developed a rotating mesocyclone and held it for more than 50 miles. Its first tornado was brief, a two-minute EF2 that crossed the Illinois River into Pekin, ripped roofs from brick homes and injured ten people. A few minutes after it lifted, at 10:59 a.m., the same storm put down the tornado that would be remembered for Washington.

## Forty-Eight Minutes

It touched down about 2.5 miles east of North Pekin, near West Muller Road, and strengthened as it moved northeast across Interstate 474 and then Interstate 74, reaching EF3 intensity near East Peoria, where it caused $110 million in damage. Then it turned violent. At the Georgetown Commons apartment complex it reached EF4 strength, destroying the top two stories and wrapping a truck from the parking lot around a tree; sixteen apartment buildings and a nearby restaurant were later torn down. Crossing into Washington, it leveled an Advance Auto Parts store with employees inside, all of whom escaped injury. On Fayette Avenue, where numerous homes were destroyed, it gravely injured an 82-year-old woman, who survived the storm itself but died of her injuries a week later. In the Trail Edge subdivision the winds peaked at an estimated 190 miles per hour.

## Bare Foundations

The worst damage came along Mackenzie Street, where a row of well-built homes was obliterated and the debris swept away entirely, leaving only bare foundations. On Holborn Court four rows of homes were leveled. At 11:07 a.m., Doppler radar showed a debris ball north of Washington, accompanied by an intense velocity couplet. A $50 bill from Washington was later found 100 miles away in Minooka. Beyond the city, the tornado held its 190 mph winds as it destroyed farmsteads, then weakened past Roanoke, Minonk and Dana before lifting a few miles east of Long Point at 11:47 a.m. In 48 minutes it had traveled 46.2 miles, reached half a mile across at its widest, killed three people, injured 125 and caused an estimated $935 million in damage, $800 million of it in Washington alone.

## Rebuilding in the Cold

Thousands of residents were left homeless. Firefighters from the Illinois Air National Guard's 182nd Airlift Wing searched 36 leveled structures and shut off gas lines, Army Reserve soldiers blocked roads into the city, and a 6 p.m. curfew held for a week. Volunteers poured in: Samaritan's Purse had 854 people clearing debris and tarping roofs within days, and Operation BBQ Relief cooked more than 25,600 meals. Six days after the storm, fire trucks and ambulances from across central Illinois lined up to welcome the Washington Panthers football team home from the state semifinals. Then winter struck hard. Peoria recorded a record 57.6 inches of snow that season, and four months after the tornado little cleanup had been done. When Illinois asked FEMA to help cover local governments' costs, the agency ruled the devastation in Washington not "severe" enough to merit federal public assistance, although individual aid for households had already been approved. After the state's final appeal was rejected in March 2014, Governor Pat Quinn announced a $45 million state-funded relief plan less than 12 hours later.

## What Grew Back

Rebuilding came slowly but steadily. Six months after the storm, more than 650 building permits had been issued, and one in 20 homes rebuilt in Pekin, East Peoria and Washington included a basement safe room with 18-inch reinforced concrete walls. At Harry LaHood Park in Trail Edge, a new restroom building doubles as a tornado shelter built to withstand high-end EF4 winds. On September 22, 2015, the city unveiled the bronze Community Spirit sculpture and plaza at Five Points, paid for by private donations. Emotional recovery took longer. More than a year later, counselors were seeing increases in domestic problems and alcohol abuse; adult survivors had trouble sleeping and concentrating, and some children grew more fearful because of flashbacks. By 2020, Washington's population had grown to 16,071, and ten years after the tornado its destroyed neighborhoods had largely been rebuilt, though some homesites remained empty.

## Flight Context

The marker at 40.622 N, 89.5678 W sits near where the tornado touched down, about 2.5 miles east of North Pekin in Tazewell County, Illinois. From 3,000-4,000 feet AGL, trace the path northeast across I-474 and I-74 on the East Peoria side of the Illinois River valley to the city of Washington, roughly 9 nautical miles to the northeast, whose rebuilt subdivisions mark the heart of the damage path; the track then continued over farmland toward Long Point. General Wayne A. Downing Peoria International Airport (KPIA, Class C) is about 6 nautical miles to the west-northwest, so check the sectional chart and expect to work with the tower or approach control. November brings low ceilings and gusty frontal weather; spring and fall both carry severe-storm risk in this region.
