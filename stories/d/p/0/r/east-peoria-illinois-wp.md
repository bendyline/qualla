# East Peoria, Illinois

Once the floodplain towns of Fondulac and Bluetown, East Peoria grew on brick clay and Caterpillar tractors, then turned an old factory slab into its Levee District and its winters into a Festival of Lights.

**Location:** 40.6667, -89.5333

## Introduction

Before it was East Peoria, it was Hilton, and before that it was two towns. One was Fondulac, from the French for "bottom of the lake," a fitting name for a settlement on a swampy floodplain. The other was Bluetown, a name said to trace back to Joseph Schertz, who "always wore blue smocks at their work." The city across the Illinois River from downtown Peoria changed its name three times between 1884 and 1919, and its identity even more, from brickyards to tractors to a riverfront built on a factory's concrete slab.

## Bottom of the Lake

A few years after Illinois became a state in 1818, William Blanchard, Charles Sargeant, Theodore Sargeant and David Barnes crossed the Illinois River from Fort Clark, at Peoria, to its eastern side. The land was swampy floodplain, but they staked their claim anyway, built huts and grew corn and tomatoes in the fertile soil. The settlement was named Fondulac Township, and more settlers followed. In 1855 Seth Abbott petitioned to plat the Town of Fondulac, with Mill Street, Main Street, Depot Avenue, a sawmill and a stretch of the Peoria & Oquawka Railroad, which by 1857 reached as far east as Chenoa and carried the town's farm products. Bluetown was platted in 1864. On July 1, 1884, voters in both towns agreed, 30 to 12, to merge as the Village of Hilton. In 1889 it became the Village of East Peoria, and in 1919 a city.

## Clay, Brick and Caterpillar

The ground itself was a resource. East Peoria's clay and shale fed numerous small brickmaking plants in the 1880s, and one of them, begun when Frederick R. Carter bought the Spurck Paving Brick Plant in 1899, is still in business as the Peoria Brick Company. It started with 60 workers making up to 40,000 bricks a day, added a plant on Cole Hollow Road in 1909 with ten domed kilns 30 feet across, and reached 65,000 bricks a day by 1939. Brickmaking ended in 1982, but the Carter family's firm carried on in masonry and landscaping supplies. A bigger name arrived in 1909. The Holt Manufacturing Company bought the assets of the Colean factory, on which at least $450,000 had been spent, for a $50,000 note, and opened its eastern plant here on February 15, 1910, with 12 employees. Holt registered "Caterpillar" as a trademark in 1911, and in 1925 merged with C. L. Best to form the Caterpillar Tractor Co., which moved its headquarters to Peoria in 1930.

## A Riverfront Reborn

For most of the 20th century, the land just across the river from downtown Peoria was a Caterpillar manufacturing site. In 1998 it was razed, leaving nothing but a concrete slab, and in 2000 the city bought it for redevelopment. The result is the Levee District, a mixed-use hub of restaurants, shops and lodging that finally let East Peorians dine and shop without crossing to Peoria. Its civic heart came later. The Fondulac District Library, whose old basement filled with thousands of gallons of floodwater in April 2013, opened a new 32,000-square-foot building in the Levee District on November 1 of that year, sharing an atrium with a City Hall completed in 2015. On the river, the Par-A-Dice Hotel and Casino moved over from Peoria in 1993; its riverboat cruised daily until 1999 and set out again in 2010 for the first time in 11 years.

## The Festival of Lights

Every year after Thanksgiving, East Peoria lights up. The Festival of Lights began in the mid-1980s as a Parade of Lights (the city counts the 2026 parade as its 42nd), with floats wrapped in Christmas lights driven through town. In 1986 a float called the E.P. Noel Riverboat debuted, built from pencil steel, and it proved so popular that every float since has been made the same way, with animated effects added as lighting improved. Since 1993 the floats and displays have stayed put for the season in a Winter Wonderland drive-through that draws thousands of visitors a year. For three decades the Festival Wreath was a Ferris wheel strung with fluorescent tubes. In 2014 a new wreath replaced it, a welded frame carrying 80 tube-shaped lights, 1,000 bulbs and rope lighting, set atop Fon du Lac Drive where it overlooks the city.

## Hometown Names

Illinois Central College anchors the city's northeastern edge. Central Illinois voters approved a tax levy to create it in May 1966, by 16,870 to 8,421, and when its first semester began in the fall of 1967, 2,486 students enrolled. By the 1970s enrollment had reached 9,000, and the college now serves all or parts of ten counties from campuses in East Peoria, Peoria and Pekin. The city is also home to most of the Peoria area's commercial television outlets, led by NBC affiliate WEEK-TV on Springfield Road. Its list of notable people includes Joe Girardi, who caught for four major-league teams and managed the New York Yankees; comedian Sam Kinison, who attended East Peoria Community High School; and REO Speedwagon guitarist and songwriter Gary Richrath. The 2020 census counted 22,484 people in East Peoria.

## Flight Context

East Peoria is centered near 40.6667 N, 89.5333 W on the east bank of the Illinois River in Tazewell County, directly across from downtown Peoria. From 2,000-3,500 feet AGL, look for the river crossings that tie the two cities together: Interstate 74 on the Murray Baker Bridge, Illinois 40 on the Bob Michel Bridge and Illinois 8 and 116 on the Cedar Street Bridge. The Levee District sits on the flat riverfront, with the Illinois Central College campus at the city's northeastern edge. General Wayne A. Downing Peoria International Airport (KPIA, Class C) lies about 7 nautical miles to the west, so check the sectional chart and expect to work with the tower or approach control. Watch for river fog on cool mornings and summer thunderstorms.
