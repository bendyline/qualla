# Pekin, Illinois

Named for a city its founders believed lay on the far side of the globe, the Illinois River town of Pekin holds a tangled history: a freedom case argued by Lincoln, a Civil War divided in whispers, a Klan-owned newspaper, a sundown-town reputation, and a senator who helped pass the Civil Rights Act.

## Introduction

When Ann Eliza Cromwell chose a name for the new town on the Illinois River, she reached for the other side of the world. It has been said she called it Pekin because she believed Peking lay exactly opposite on the globe; in the 1800s, China and the United States were thought to be antipodes, and towns were sometimes named for their supposed mirror points. Nearby Canton, Illinois, is another. The name stuck, and Pekin grew into the seat of Tazewell County, a city of 31,731 people at the 2020 census and the second-largest in the Peoria metropolitan area.

## A Ridge Above the River

Jonathan Tharp, a farmer from Ohio, built a log cabin in 1824 on a ridge above the Illinois River near the present foot of Broadway Drive, the first non-Native resident of what became Pekin. His father Jacob followed in 1825. They were not alone on the land: nearby, along Gravel Ridge on the eastern shore of today's Pekin Lake, stood Chief Shabbona's large village of about 100 wigwams, populated primarily by Potawatomi. County surveyor William Hodge laid out a town site in 1827, and in 1829 the plat was auctioned in Springfield to Major Isaac Perkins, Gideon Hawley, William Haines and Major Nathan Cromwell. Cromwell named many streets for the wives and daughters of early settlers. The first post office opened in 1832, and two years later cholera killed several settlers, who were buried in the old Tharp cemetery.

## Nance and the Lawyer from Springfield

Nathan Cromwell brought someone else to Pekin: Nance Costley, a young woman he claimed as his property; one account says he bought her at auction in 1827. Before his death in 1836, Cromwell sold her to David Bailey, a local merchant with abolitionist leanings, for a promissory note. Bailey let her live as a free woman and did not pay, and Cromwell's estate sued him on the note in Tazewell County. He lost there, and the case went to the Illinois Supreme Court, where in 1841 his friend, the attorney Abraham Lincoln, argued it, citing the Illinois Constitution and the Northwest Ordinance. Justice Breese reversed the lower court, voided the note and declared Nance free, writing that "it is a presumption of law, in the State of Illinois, that every person is free, without regard to color." She is often described as the first enslaved person freed with Lincoln's help. Nance remained in Pekin with her husband and children, and William H. Bates's 1870 history of Pekin, printed in the city directory, wrote of her as a longtime resident. Lincoln returned in 1843 for a Whig convention in Pekin, where rivals agreed to take turns in Congress. His one term, won in 1846, was his only Washington experience before the presidency.

## Whispers in Wartime

Illinois was a free state, but much of its south and center had been settled by Southerners, and Pekin leaned pro-slavery. "Pekin was a pro-slave city for years," as the town's 1949 centenary history put it; Stephen A. Douglas, not Lincoln, was the local hero. Abolitionists pushed back. Dr. Daniel Cheever ran Underground Railroad activities from his office at Capitol and Court streets, and the brothers Samuel and Hugh Woodrow joined the cause; Catherine and Amanda streets are named for their wives. German immigrants who arrived after the Revolutions of 1848 bolstered that side, since the German community disapproved of slavery. Their numbers were climbing: residents listed as coming from Germany made up nearly 14 percent of Pekin's population in 1850 and 22 percent by 1860. During the Civil War the secessionist Knights of the Golden Circle openly supported secession and slavery in Pekin, and Union loyalists, the centenary recalled, "spoke often in whispers." On June 25, 1862, Cheever and ten other men met at 331 Court Street to found the first council of the Union League of America.

## Shadows and Change

Pekin's twentieth century carried its own darkness. From September 1923 to June 1925 the Pekin Daily Times was owned by Oscar W. Friedrich, a Ku Klux Klan Grand Titan, and its headlines followed the Klan's meetings and plans. In August 1924 the Pekin Klan hosted one of the largest rallies ever held in Illinois, drawing an estimated 25,000 to 45,000 people. The city also carried the identity of a sundown town, hostile to Black residents, and few settled there; in a 1967 newspaper article, Peoria attorney Joe Billy McDade alleged that a sign at Pekin's border had warned Black people, in a racial slur, not to let the sun set on them in town. Yet some Pekin pastors marched for civil rights in the 1960s, and Pekin's own U.S. Senator Everett Dirksen was integral to passing the Civil Rights Act of 1964. In 2011, Laurie Barra became the city's first female mayor.

## Marigolds and Mine Shafts

Dirksen left a gentler mark as well. He tried to have the marigold named the national floral emblem, and although he failed, his hometown became known as the "Marigold Capital of the World." Since 1973, the Marigold Festival has filled the first weekend after Labor Day with a parade, the crowning of Miss Marigold, music and crafts. That same year President Richard Nixon came at the request of Dirksen's widow to dedicate the cornerstone of a new public library, which first housed the Dirksen Congressional Center. Other layers lie underfoot. At least four coal mines once operated in Pekin, and one of them, run by the Pekin Coal Mining Company, extended under ground now occupied by Broadmoor Junior High, Willow Elementary and Schramm School. Above it all, the Pekin Park District tends well over a dozen parks, Coal Miners' Park among them.

## Flight Context

Pekin is centered near 40.5681 N, 89.6264 W on the east bank of the Illinois River in Tazewell County, Illinois, about 8 nautical miles south-southwest of downtown Peoria. From 2,000-3,500 feet AGL, follow the Illinois River south from Peoria: Pekin Lake lies in the city's northwest, and the John T. McNaughton Bridge carries traffic across the river to the small annexed area in Peoria County. General Wayne A. Downing Peoria International Airport (KPIA, Class C) is about 6.5 nautical miles to the north-northwest, so check the sectional chart and expect to work with the tower or approach control; the city-owned Pekin Municipal Airport sits about 5 nautical miles south of the central business district. Expect river valley fog on cool mornings and summer thunderstorms.
