# 1932 Moweaqua Coal Mine Disaster

On Christmas Eve 1932 a methane gas explosion killed 54 miners in Moweaqua, Illinois. The disaster, one of the worst in the state's coal history, shaped the town's identity and is commemorated by a museum and a memorial near the former shaft.

## Introduction

Christmas Eve should be a day of preparation. In Moweaqua, Illinois, on 24 December 1932, it became a day of loss. A methane explosion in the town's coal mine killed 54 miners, and the community on US 51, about 20 miles south of Decatur, has lived with that memory ever since. The story is not just a number. It is a mine opened to keep work alive, a local Christmas party that was cancelled, and a small museum that still holds the newspaper coverage of the tragedy.

## A mine built to keep work alive

Coal mining in Moweaqua dates back to 1891. In 1932 a group of local businessmen formed the Moweaqua Coal Corporation to keep a mine operating after the Pana Coal Company intended to close it. The mine was leased from Pana and organized by the Progressive Miners of America, a breakaway from the United Mine Workers that was active in Illinois at the time. The workers were also shareholders in the company. Less than three months after the corporation was created, the mine would be the site of a disaster.

## Christmas Eve, 1932

The explosion occurred on Christmas Eve, 1932. Methane gas ignited underground and killed 54 miners. The Herald-Review later described the event as one of the worst disasters in the history of the coal industry. Contemporary newspaper coverage recorded how close the tragedy came to a community celebration. The Moweaqua News on 11 January 1933 reported that Tom Jackson, 54, had been selected to impersonate Santa Claus at the annual Moweaqua Christmas party for children in town and had been helping with preparations. He was to have worked only until noon that Saturday and then come home to prepare for the party. He was among the first groups of men to enter the mine that morning and was trapped with the others. Merchants and miners who were sponsoring the party called it off. About three hundred pounds of candy and several boxes of fruit that had been prepared for distribution were given out later.

## Aftermath and memory

The mine reopened a year later and ceased operation in 1935. Memory of the 54 men persisted in families. Decades later, Mike Potsick Jr. recalled that his father and brother died in the explosion, and that the event was rarely discussed when he was growing up. Mary Ann Gorden, whose father died in the explosion, described the community's resolve to move on after the loss. A memorial and marker now sits near the mine shaft that was the site of the disaster. On 25 May 1986 the Moweaqua Coal Mine Museum was dedicated at 129 S. Main to commemorate the 1932 disaster and to present the town's coal mining history. The museum holds historic documents, photographs, artifacts and extensive newspaper coverage of the tragedy. It was established explicitly to recall the lives lost, though it does not have regular hours and is difficult to visit.
