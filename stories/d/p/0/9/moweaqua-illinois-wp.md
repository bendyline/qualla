# Moweaqua, Illinois

A small central Illinois village whose name is said to mean "she that weeps," Moweaqua lost 54 coal miners in a methane explosion on Christmas Eve 1932, a disaster that Illinois's historical marker says marked the end of the era of open-flame carbide lights in the mines.

**Location:** 39.6253, -89.0178

**Categories:** Villages in Shelby County, Illinois, Villages in Christian County, Illinois, Villages in Illinois

## Introduction

Tom Jackson had an appointment on the evening of December 24, 1932. He was scheduled to play Santa Claus at a party for his fellow townspeople in Moweaqua, Illinois. That morning he went down into the village's coal mine with the other miners, and a methane explosion killed all fifty-four men who entered it that day. Moweaqua is a quiet place today, a village of 1,764 people straddling the line between Shelby and Christian counties, but that Christmas Eve still shapes how it remembers itself, and a museum founded in 1986 keeps the memory of those miners.

## She That Weeps

The name came before the tragedy, which makes it all the more haunting. Moweaqua was named for a small stream about a mile and a half south of the station. Native people are said to have called the stream Moweaqua, a Potawatomi word said to mean "she that weeps." Whoever first gave the creek that name could not have known how fitting it would become. The village itself is compact, covering just 2.30 square miles of flat land with no water inside its limits, and it sits across a county line, part of it in Shelby County and part in Christian County. In 2020 it counted 742 households, about a quarter of them with children under eighteen.

## Christmas Eve Underground

Coal shaped Moweaqua for more than four decades. From 1891 until 1935, a mine here worked seams of coal laid down in Pennsylvanian-age strata, and it had a reputation as a gassy mine, meaning methane collected in its workings. On the morning of December 24, 1932, that gas ignited. The state's historical marker says an unprecedented drop in barometric pressure let methane escape into the mine, and open-flame carbide lights ignited it at about 8 a.m. The explosion killed fifty-four miners, all of the men who had entered the mine that day. For a village this size, the loss of so many men in a single morning was staggering. Tom Jackson's story lingers because of its cruel detail: a man who expected to spend Christmas Eve handing out holiday cheer to his neighbors died underground before the party began. The mine reopened about a year later and stopped producing coal in 1935.

## A Lantern Goes Dark

The Moweaqua explosion arrived at a moment of political change. The month before, in November 1932, voters had elected the pro-labor Seventy-Third Congress. The disaster and the new Congress together are credited with helping bring about mine safety legislation and the phaseout of open-flame carbide lanterns in United States coal mines, and Illinois's historical marker says the disaster marked the end of the era of open-flame carbide lights. Those lanterns burned with a live flame, and in a mine where methane could collect, a naked flame was a constant hazard. The deaths of fifty-four men in one central Illinois village thus rippled outward into safer working conditions for miners. Moweaqua has not let that story fade. In 1986, the Moweaqua Coal Mine Museum opened to commemorate local coal miners, especially the men who died on that Christmas Eve. A University of Illinois guide says the museum has no regular hours, so check with the village office at 217-768-3036 before planning a visit.

## Raiders, Farmers and a Film Crew

Today the village is served by the Central A&M School District, established in 1993, which also includes the city of Assumption and the surrounding rural areas. Central A&M High School and Gregory Intermediate School share one building on the northern edge of the village, where a few hundred high schoolers compete as the Raiders. The Central A&M FFA chapter has been successful in section, district and state competitions and in community service. Hollywood passed through, too. The 2009 film The Informant!, starring Matt Damon, was partially shot in Moweaqua. Its plot drew on the ADM price-fixing scandal of the 1990s, bringing a tale of corporate intrigue to the streets of a former coal town.

## From Moweaqua Outward

A handful of people with roots here made their names far from home. Tom Candy Ponting was a cattleman who, with a partner, is credited with driving the first herd of Texas cattle to New York City in 1853 and 1854, an undertaking that spanned half a continent. Cecil Coombs played briefly in the outfield for the Chicago White Sox in 1914. More recently, Brett Adcock became the chief executive of Figure AI. It is a wide spread of lives for a village of fewer than two thousand people: a drover, a ballplayer and a technology executive. Set beside the fifty-four miners the museum honors, they show how many directions the lives of one small prairie village can take, some ending underground and some reaching far beyond it.

## Flight Context

Moweaqua lies at 39.6253 N, 89.0178 W on flat central Illinois farmland, straddling the Shelby-Christian county line. From 2,000-3,000 feet AGL the village appears as a compact grid of about 2.3 square miles surrounded by cropland; the Central A&M school building marks its northern edge. On a clear day the city of Decatur and Lake Decatur are visible to the north-northeast. Nearby airports: Decatur Airport (KDEC), about 14 nautical miles north-northeast, with a control tower; Taylorville Municipal (KTAZ), about 15 nautical miles west-southwest; Abraham Lincoln Capital Airport (KSPI) at Springfield, about 33 nautical miles west-northwest. Check the sectional chart and expect to work with the tower or approach control near Decatur and Springfield. Expect summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms over the prairie.
