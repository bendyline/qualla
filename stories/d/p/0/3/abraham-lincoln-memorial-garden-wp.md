# Abraham Lincoln Memorial Garden

A 100-acre woodland and prairie garden on the shore of Lake Springfield in Springfield, Illinois, designed by Jens Jensen as a living memorial to Abraham Lincoln and stewarded by volunteers and the Abraham Lincoln Memorial Garden Foundation.

## Introduction

In the 1930s Springfield was building a 4,000-acre lake. Civic leader Harriet Knudson asked what a garden in Lincoln’s hometown could be. The answer became a living memorial made of plants native to Kentucky, Indiana and Illinois, the three states Lincoln lived in, and a landscape that still grows through volunteer hands.

## An idea takes root

The garden began as an idea in 1934, credited to Harriet Knudson, a member of the Springfield Civic Garden Club. She persuaded the city of Springfield to donate land along the shore of the new Lake Springfield and convinced the Garden Clubs of Illinois to adopt the project. She also asked 74-year-old Jens Jensen, a leader of the Prairie School of landscape architecture and one of Illinois’ earliest conservationists, a contemporary and associate of Frank Lloyd Wright, to design it.
Jensen chose a 63-acre site of farm fields and sparsely wooded areas where intermittent streams flowed toward the emerging lake. His plan used connected paths bordered by native plantings, eight council rings of circular stone benches, groves of oaks and hickories, open meadows of sun-loving prairie species, and small flowering trees and shrubs forming borders between meadow and woodland. The design was intended to evoke the Midwestern landscape familiar to Lincoln, even though Lincoln probably never walked the area. Because many species Jensen proposed were unavailable commercially, school children and garden clubs collected and planted acorns and seeds from areas being lost to new construction.
The first planting occurred on November 14, 1936, when Boy Scouts and Girl Scouts planted acorns that have grown into today’s towering oaks. Volunteers were essential from the start; hundreds worked for several years on initial plantings, with nearly 50 garden clubs and organizations from around the state helping. Women in the ankle-length dresses of the day carried shovels, buckets and drinking water to remote locations for long days transplanting material gathered from roadsides, railroad rights-of-way and other natural areas.

## Woodland, prairie and council rings

The Jensen section borders Lake Springfield and anchors the present 100-acre site, which is owned by the city of Springfield and managed by the Abraham Lincoln Memorial Garden Foundation, an organization established for that purpose in 1952. The garden was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1992 and was one of Jensen’s last major public landscape commissions.
Today the site features six miles of interconnected trails winding through restored prairies, woodlands and wetlands. The trails include a dozen footbridges, a pond, and eight stone council rings, most with views of the lake. Trail surfaces are wood-chipped or grass, and benches, many inscribed with Lincoln quotes, are placed throughout for rest and contemplation.
Seasonal change is part of the experience. Spring brings dogwoods in bloom, summer brings colorful prairie wildflowers, autumn brings burnished leaves, and winter shows snow-covered maples. The Jensen unit includes white oak, the state tree of Illinois, sugar maple, dogwood and redbud, while the newer Ostermeier Prairie Center is characterized by prairie grasses such as big bluestem and fire-resistant specimens like bur oak. Admission is free to visitors except during special events, and the garden is open year-round sunup to sundown.

## Stewardship by the community

Growth of the garden has been driven by local support. Myrtle Walgreen, a friend of Harriet Knudson and wife of Walgreen Drug Store founder Charles Walgreen, sponsored an annual garden tour of the Walgreen estate in Dixon, Illinois, to raise funds. That effort helped build the Nature Center, which first opened in 1965 and was remodeled and updated in 2003. The Nature Center provides a center for educational programs and houses the Split Rail Shop.
Expansion continued with community involvement. In 1995 the garden added the 29-acre Ostermeier Prairie Center at the south edge, with a prairie restoration area, seasonal wildflowers, a small pond, public rest rooms, a covered picnic area and a half-mile accessible trail. In August 2000 the garden added the six-acre Cawley Meadow across the street from the main entrance; it now hosts the garden’s biggest annual event, Indian Summer Festival, reducing wear on the historic garden. In 2013 a team of University of Illinois Extension Master Naturalists and Master Gardeners established a native wildflower demonstration garden behind the Ostermeier House, with plans underway for a second demonstration shade garden.
The garden receives no direct taxpayer support and relies on donations to the 501(c)3 foundation. Volunteers remain central to its upkeep, continuing a tradition that began with the initial plantings and that sustains the self-governing character of the woodland and prairie landscape.
