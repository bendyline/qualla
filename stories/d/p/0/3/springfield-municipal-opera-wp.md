# The Magic in the Wheat Field: The Story of Springfield's Muni Opera

From a nomadic series of park performances to a 55-acre wheat field, the Springfield Municipal Opera overcame devastating fires and financial hurdles to become one of the Midwest's largest self-supporting community theaters.

**Location:** 39.7215, -89.6363

**Categories:** Springfield, Illinois, Tourist attractions in Springfield, Illinois

## Introduction

Before the costumes, the spotlights, and the roar of thousands of spectators, there were only 55 acres of waving wheat. In the late 1940s, a stretch of land off East Lake Drive in Springfield was nothing more than farmland—until a music educator with a vision for outdoor theater saw it as the perfect stage. Today, that transformation lives on in the Springfield Municipal Opera, a community jewel where the magic of musical theater unfolds under the open sky.

## A Nomadic Beginning

The dream began with E. Carl Lundgren, a man determined to make outdoor musicals a permanent fixture of Springfield’s cultural life. In 1935, Lundgren directed two shows on a temporary stage to mark the dedication of Lake Springfield. For the next few years, his productions moved between venues. In 1938, a performance of *Good News* drew 1,200 people to a baseball field at Lanphier Park; later shows like *Sweethearts* and *Firefly* found homes at the Illinois State Fairgrounds. While these early successes proved there was an appetite for the arts, Lundgren knew that for the tradition to survive, it needed a place to call its own.

## The Dollar-a-Year Dream

The breakthrough arrived in April 1950. Lundgren convinced Springfield Utilities Com. John Hunter to give the Springfield Municipal Opera Association a lease at $1 per year on 55 acres of wheat fields off East Lake Drive. The catch was simple but strict: the new Springfield Municipal Opera Association had to produce at least one play annually or risk losing the land. The community didn't hesitate. On June 17, 1950, thousands of people gathered for the opening night of *The Merry Widow*, marking the official birth of the "Muni." The wheat was gone, replaced by an outdoor amphitheater that promised a new kind of summer tradition.

## Trial by Fire

Success was not a straight line. By the mid-1950s, the Muni faced a series of setbacks and a major fire in 1963 destroyed part of the facilities. A major fire in 1963 tore through the facilities, and a second blaze the following year destroyed nearly everything that remained. The theater was gone, but the spirit of the organization survived through a gamble. In 1964, the Muni staged a test production of *Bye Bye Birdie* in Douglas Park to gauge public interest. The response was an overwhelming surge of enthusiasm, fueling a rapid rebuild that allowed the theater to return to its lake site in 1965 with productions of *The Music Man* and *South Pacific*.

## A Standing Ovation

From the ashes of the 1960s, the Muni grew into a Midwest powerhouse. By 1972, it had expanded to its first four-production season, featuring hits like *The Sound of Music* and *1776*. The audience grew steadily over the decades, reaching a historic milestone in the summer of 2006 when the theater welcomed its one-millionth patron during a run of *Aida*. Remarkably, the Muni remains one of the largest self-supporting community theaters in the country, operating without grants or tax dollars. It survives on the passion of a dedicated corps of volunteers and the loyalty of nearly 30,000 fans who return every summer to the lake.
