# Cotton Hill, Illinois

Cotton Hill was a tiny railroad hamlet in Sangamon County that was erased by the creation of Lake Springfield in the 1930s. Named for an early cotton-growing experiment, it lived as a station, store and post office before being submerged beneath the lake that now bears its memory in a nearby park.

**Location:** 39.6962, -89.6301

**Categories:** Ghost towns in Illinois, Unincorporated communities in Sangamon County, Illinois

## Introduction

Lake Springfield covers the site where Cotton Hill once stood. The former village lies about 20 feet below the water, roughly where the lake meets the shore east of Interstate 55. It was the only community wiped from the map by the lake’s construction in the 1930s, and today only the name remains — on park signs and in county histories. The story is less about what was lost in size than about why a place so small was remembered at all.

## A Name From Cotton

The name comes from a pioneer experiment. In an editorial in November 1933, the Illinois State Journal recalled that Kentuckians who came to Sangamon County in 1835 brought cotton seed with them and demonstrated that cotton would grow there. A patch on the hillside in the fork of Brush and Horse creeks gave the site its name. The Journal recalled the 1835 experiment, though other histories place the attempt earlier, before the so-called Deep Snow of 1830-31. The name stuck to the hamlet and later to the post office that served it.

## A Station on the Line

Cotton Hill’s main distinction was its place on the Illinois Central Railroad. In 1918 the Illinois State Register described the hamlet in a single line: Cotton Hill is a station on the Illinois Central, just north of the line between Ball and Woodside townships. Maps show the village just south of that township line, close enough that the depot may have been on the other side. The village was in Ball Township, not Cotton Hill Township to the east. From the mid-1800s into the 1900s the hamlet had a store that doubled as a post office and, for a period, a blacksmith. It was on the South Sixth Street Road, also known as State Route 126, a road that for a time was designated part of U.S. 66. Students from the area attended school elsewhere, and Cotton Hill never incorporated.

## Drowned by the Lake

Cotton Hill sat on the north side of Sugar Creek, one of the watercourses that feed Lake Springfield. The community was about 540 feet above sea level. When planners set the new lake to crest at 560 feet, the hamlet was doomed. The village’s last use was in 1933, when a camp housing 170 Civilian Conservation Corps workers was located there. The workers quarried stone and rip-rapped part of the lake’s banks as part of federal Depression-era work. After the reservoir filled, the site disappeared beneath water. In 1933 the State Journal urged that the new Route 66 bridge west of the old village be named for Cotton Hill, calling the name homely but tied to a fascinating pioneering story. The bridge was not named for the hamlet. Instead the city named a 20-acre park on the south side of the lake, approximately in line with the drowned village, after Cotton Hill. West Cotton Hill Park is a popular fishing location, while the area labeled East Cotton Hill Park remains relatively unimproved wildlife area, both entered off East Lake Drive east of I-55.
