# Loami, Illinois

A Sangamon County village that says it is named for the loam beneath it, where a farming couple's bequest has put 120 acres of tallgrass prairie back onto former farmland just outside town.

**Location:** 39.6753, -89.8467

**Categories:** Villages in Sangamon County, Illinois, Springfield metropolitan area, Illinois, Villages in Illinois

## Introduction

Plenty of towns are named for a founder, a railroad man or a city somebody left behind. Loami, by the village's own account, is named for the dirt. The village in Sangamon County, Illinois, says it takes its name from the loam soil it sits on, though the county historical society calls the origin of the name unknown, and just outside town a stretch of that same soil has been handed back to the plants that held it before the plow arrived: tallgrass prairie, replanted seed by seed on ground that had been farmed.

## A Square Mile of Loam

Loami became an incorporated village on July 29, 1875. It has never grown large. The village covers just over one square mile, almost all of it dry land, and the 2020 census counted about 810 residents, up from 745 in 2010. Today it counts as part of the Springfield metropolitan area, tied to the state capital to the northeast, but the name keeps pointing downward rather than outward. Where other places borrowed grandeur from Europe or the Bible, this one, by the village's account, looked at the ground and named itself after what it found there. An older folk tale, reported in an 1896 newspaper that doubted it, has mill builder William Colburn surveying the ruins of his burned mill and exclaiming "Low am I." It is an unusually honest piece of branding for a farm town on the Illinois prairie.

## The Nippers' Gift

The prairie outside town exists because of one farm family. Frank Nipper was a farmer, and when he died in 1989 he left land and money to create a wildlife sanctuary. His widow, Gladys, oversaw the creation of the land trust that would run it before her own death in 1995. The Nipper Wildlife Sanctuary was established in 1992 (a current University of Illinois Extension page says 1995) on 120 acres along Lick Creek, about two miles southeast of the village, and a private trust, with volunteer help, runs it. The Nippers did not simply fence off a field and let it go wild. They endowed it, which meant the work of turning cropland back into prairie could actually be paid for.

## Seventy Percent Grass

Before farming remade it, Sangamon County was roughly 70 percent tallgrass prairie. Agriculture eliminated nearly all of that native habitat, so restoring it meant finding the pieces that survived. Beginning in 1999, about 150 species of flowers and plants were replanted in the sanctuary's former farmland. Plants typical of central Illinois prairies had survived in nearby remnants, scraps of land that for one reason or another escaped the plow. The plantings have drawn prairie insects and birds back to the site. In late summer it offers something close to a reconstruction of the country that surrounded Loami before the plow, grown on the same loam the village says gave the town its name.

## A Village of Young Families

The census portrait of Loami is of a family town. In 2000 the village held 804 people in 300 households, and more than 40 percent of those households had children under 18 living in them. Married couples made up 58 percent of households. The median age was 35, and nearly three in ten residents were under 18. The count slipped to 745 by 2010 and rose to about 810 in 2020. Those household figures are two decades old, but the character of the place is easy to read in them: a small village of young families within Springfield's orbit, with a restored prairie a short drive down the road.

## Flight Context

Loami sits at 39.6753 N, 89.8467 W in Sangamon County, roughly 12 nautical miles southwest of downtown Springfield, at about 633 feet (193 m) above sea level. From 2,500-4,000 feet AGL, look for the small village grid set in open cropland, with Interstate 72 running to the north and the Springfield skyline visible to the northeast in clear weather. The Nipper Wildlife Sanctuary lies about two miles southeast of the village along Lick Creek; its unplowed grassland stands out against the surrounding row crops in the growing season. Nearest major airport: Abraham Lincoln Capital Airport (KSPI) on Springfield's north side, about 13 nautical miles to the northeast. Summer thunderstorms can build quickly over central Illinois.
