# The Sanctuary of Submarginal Sand

A prehistoric geological anomaly in Illinois, where early farming proved difficult, now serves as a rare biological refuge for Southwestern species.

**Location:** 40.4114, -89.8661

**Categories:** 1939 establishments in Illinois, Civilian Conservation Corps in Illinois, Illinois River, Illinois state forests, Protected areas established in 1939, Protected areas of Mason County, Illinois

## Introduction

In the heart of Illinois, the prickly pear cactus grows in a landscape that once defeated the ambitions of early settlers. This landscape began 15,000 years ago when flood waters from the last glaciation period receded down the Illinois River valley, leaving a vast deposit of sand stretching from near Pekin past Beardstown and as far west as San Jose. Shifting winds sculpted this residue into dunes 100 feet high, which now appear as the wooded ridges for which Sand Ridge State Forest is named.

## The Resistance of the Sand

A large forest existed on these dunes until European settlement occurred in the 1830s. Settlers attempted to impose traditional agriculture on the terrain, but they found that farming on the deep, sandy soil proved difficult. The land resisted their efforts to cultivate it, and by the 1930s, most of these farms were abandoned.

## A Forest by Decree

In 1939, the state of Illinois purchased 5,504 acres of this "submarginal sandy farmland" to be managed as an experimental forest and for conservation purposes. To stabilize the earth, the Civilian Conservation Corps planted pine trees. This government experiment was designed to control erosion and demonstrate the viability of growing a commercial tree crop in sandy soil.

The area became known as Sand Ridge State Forest in 1971 when the Division of Land Management took over management. The deep, sandy soil that proved difficult for early settlers to farm now supports a variety of rare species. Today, it is one of the few places in Illinois that supports plants and animals more associated with the American Southwest than the Midwest, including the pocket gopher, badger, prickly pear cactus, and silvery bladderpod.

In June 1985, the forest's unique ecology provided a male specimen of a new species of wolf spider for study. A 2011 study found approximately 70 endangered and threatened species, with 40 of them restricted to glacial drift sand habitats. In 2013, a study recorded the threatened sand prairie flatsedge and two endangered species: silvery bladderpod and bent milk vetch.

## The Lasting Legacy of Sand

Now the largest state forest in Illinois, the park covers more than 7,000 acres of native oak-hickory woodland, pine plantations, and unique sand prairies. For those exploring the park on two wheels, this geological quirk remains a physical hurdle; the biking trails are exceptionally challenging due to the sand.
