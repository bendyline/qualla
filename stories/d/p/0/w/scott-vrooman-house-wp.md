# A Century on Taylor Street

Julia Scott Vrooman was born in this Bloomington mansion in 1876 and died in the same room 104 years later, and in between her family made it a crossroads of farm reform, war relief and national politics.

**Location:** 40.4775, -88.9836

**Categories:** Buildings and structures in Bloomington–Normal, National Register of Historic Places in McLean County, Illinois, Houses in McLean County, Illinois, Houses on the National Register of Historic Places in Illinois

## Introduction

Julia Scott Vrooman was born in 1876 in the house at 701 East Taylor Street in Bloomington, Illinois. She died there in 1981, at 104, in the same room where she was born, according to the mansion's own history. In between, the house sheltered two generations of formidable women and a crusader for scientific farming. Listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1983, it now takes in overnight guests as the Vrooman Mansion bed and breakfast.

## Bought on Prairie Money

The house came to the family through the younger Julia's parents. Her mother, Julia Green Scott, was born in Danville, Kentucky, in 1839, the daughter of the Presbyterian minister Lewis W. Green, who headed Centre College there. In 1859 she married Matthew T. Scott, a land speculator, and the couple were among the founders of the prairie town of Chenoa. After a short stay in Springfield, they settled in Bloomington in the early 1870s and bought the Taylor Street house, which had been built in 1869. The McLean County Museum of History counts it among the most elegant homes of its day in the city. Two daughters arrived in the 1870s: Letitia, named for her aunt, and Julia, who would call the house home for the rest of her life.

## The Widow's Wing

Matthew Scott died in 1891, and his widow took over the business. Julia Green Scott managed the family's farmland in Illinois and Indiana and became the principal stockholder, and eventually the president, of the McLean County Coal Company. An advocate of conservation, she sent 40 of her tenant farmers to the University of Illinois College of Agriculture to learn newer methods.

She also remade the house. Between April 1900 and October 1901 she enlarged it by about 60 percent, retaining the local architect Arthur Pillsbury. The work brought Tiffany stained-glass windows bearing the family coat of arms near the staircase, carved red oak, a cherry-paneled library, electric lights with gas backups, and an office with a large wall safe that had her name painted on it. On October 29, 1901, when she and her local Daughters of the American Revolution chapter welcomed visiting officers from around the country, more than 300 guests moved through rooms decorated in red, white and blue. The Bulletin reported that the house was "so spacious and admirably devised that there was no crush."

## Medals and Orphans

Patriotic societies ran in the family. Julia Green Scott's younger sister, Letitia, married Adlai E. Stevenson I, Vice President under Grover Cleveland, and was a founding member of the national DAR and one of its early presidents. Julia herself was elected president general of the society in 1909. During World War I she headed its War Relief Committee and led the drive to raise money for French war orphans. In 1921 the French ambassador, Jusserand, presented her with the Medal of French Gratitude of the First Class in recognition of her work to rehabilitate the French town of Tilloloy and the adoption of 4,000 war orphans. It was her last public appearance. She lived in the Taylor Street house until her death in 1923.

## A Dirt Farmer in Washington

The younger Julia met Carl Schurz Vrooman in 1894 while traveling in Europe. The mansion's history says she counted his proposal, made on a canal in Venice, as the most memorable moment of her life. They married on December 28, 1896, and later co-wrote a travel book, The Lure and Lore of Travel. Carl managed some of the Scott landholdings, called himself a "dirt farmer," and became Assistant Secretary of Agriculture under Woodrow Wilson. He believed farming should be run scientifically and as a business. He promoted the Smith-Lever Act of 1914, which created a national cooperative extension service, calling it "agricultural learning democratized, made practical, and given to farmers of the country without money and without price." His 1916 pamphlet, Grain Farming in the Corn Belt with Live Stock as a Side Line, was in such demand that nearly a million copies were printed. On his own land he was blunter: tenants who ignored his order to plant alfalfa to recharge the soil found themselves put off it. After the war, the American Farm Bureau chose him to head a relief mission charged with shipping nearly a million bushels of corn to Austria, Poland and Czechoslovakia. The Polish government decorated him for it.

## Jazz at the Front, Bread for a Queen

Julia wanted her own part in the war. "I have no children. I am in perfect health. I am fortunate enough to be able to speak both French and Italian," she said when asked why. From August 23, 1918, until late 1919 she worked for the YMCA with American soldiers in Europe, forming a jazz band from soldiers of the army of occupation to lift morale in France, Germany and Belgium and holding "cocoa parties" for homesick Illinois soldiers. According to the county museum, the house served as a hospital during the 1918-1919 flu pandemic.

The Vroomans' circle took in Woodrow and Edith Wilson, Franklin and Eleanor Roosevelt, William Jennings Bryan, playwright Rachel Crothers and the poets Sara Teasdale and Vachel Lindsay; Julia loved to throw parties and served her guests at the dining room table that bed and breakfast guests still use today. Julia sent her famous "Vrooman bread" to the Queen of England and Eleanor Roosevelt. During World War II the couple turned the third floor into apartments for soldiers and their families. Carl died in 1966, shortly before their 70th anniversary; Julia stayed on fifteen more years.

## Flight Context

The Scott-Vrooman House stands at 40.4775°N, 88.9836°W, at 701 East Taylor Street in Bloomington, Illinois, within the city's street grid. Central Illinois Regional Airport (KBMI) lies only about 3 nautical miles due east and has a control tower, so check the sectional chart and expect to work with the tower before any low pass over the neighborhood. General Wayne A. Downing Peoria International Airport (KPIA) is about 34 nautical miles to the west. For a sightseeing view of the house among Bloomington's older blocks, 1,500 to 2,000 feet AGL works well, and the flat central Illinois terrain gives long sightlines on clear days.
