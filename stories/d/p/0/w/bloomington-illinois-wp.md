# Bloomington, Illinois

The McLean County seat where Lincoln gave his famous Lost Speech grew from Blooming Grove into an insurance capital, the only home of Beer Nuts, and a crossroads of three interstates.

**Location:** 40.4842, -88.9936

## Introduction

On May 29, 1856, Abraham Lincoln rose to speak at Major's Hall in Bloomington, Illinois, and according to the story that has followed the speech ever since, the reporters in the room laid down their pencils and forgot to take notes, as if hypnotized. No full transcript survived. The Anti-Nebraska convention that day culminated in the founding of Illinois's state Republican Party, and Lincoln's words, believed to have been an impassioned condemnation of slavery's expansion, became known simply as the Lost Speech. A version published forty years later was eventually debunked. The hall is gone now, but the city that heard him has grown to 78,680 people at the 2020 census, side by side with its neighbor, Normal.

## From Keg Grove to County Seat

Before Euro-American settlers arrived in the early 1820s, this was the edge of a large grove occupied by the Kickapoo. The settlement that grew up here was first called Keg Grove, later Blooming Grove, and when McLean County was created on December 25, 1830, Bloomington was named its seat. Lots went on sale at a well-attended and noisy auction on July 4, 1831. The young town became part of Lincoln's world: David Davis, his friend and mentor, lived here. Two Adlai E. Stevensons are buried in the city's Evergreen Cemetery: the first a vice president under Grover Cleveland, the second a governor of Illinois. In 1900, an officer on patrol discovered a fire in a laundry. It destroyed most of downtown, especially the blocks north and east of the courthouse, and local architects rebuilt the heart of the city. Later, the legendary US 66 ran directly through that downtown before moving to a bypass on the east side.

## Insurance and Beer Nuts

In June 1922, a retired farmer named George J. Mecherle founded State Farm in Bloomington as a mutual automobile insurance company. State Farm still calls Bloomington home, and so do Country Financial and the Illinois Agricultural Association. The city makes something smaller, too. Bloomington is the only town where Beer Nuts brand snacks are produced: the company says it still makes every product at its 100,000-square-foot plant here, and the plant has its own store. Just next door in Normal, the original Steak 'n Shake opened in 1934. By the mid-2000s, Bloomington and McLean County were described as the fastest-growing metropolitan area in Illinois. A special census in February 2006 counted 74,975 people in the city, a 15.7 percent jump in less than six years.

## A City of Stages

Bloomington keeps a lot of curtains rising. The Bloomington Center for the Performing Arts anchors the Cultural District, hosting more than 400 performances and community events each year, including concerts by the Illinois Symphony Orchestra. The Castle Theatre, which opened in 1904 and moved into its East Washington Street building in 1916, calls itself a 1,000-seat house from Balaban & Katz and has been restored with a $1.5 million renovation. The Community Players Theater, opened in 1923, is one of the region's oldest community theaters and is in its 104th season. Each summer the Illinois Shakespeare Festival stages plays in an open-air, Elizabethan-style theater at historic Ewing Manor, a Channel-Norman house designed by Phil Hooten with gardens by landscape architect Jens Jensen. Audiences are encouraged to picnic first, entertained by strolling madrigal singers and jesters. One tradition has ended. The American Passion Play, long billed as the longest-running Passion Play in the United States, closed after its 100th year in spring 2023.

## Trails, Tigers and Hockey

Outdoors, the Constitution Trail, which the Town of Normal counted at 37 miles by 2014 and which has grown since, follows dedicated right-of-way through much of the city, its north–south spine laid on an abandoned Illinois Central Gulf railroad. Walkers, cyclists, skaters and wheelchair users share it, and in winter, when it is left uncleared, skiers take over. Miller Park Zoo keeps Sumatran tigers, red pandas and red wolves, among other animals. Every August the McLean County Fair, billed as the "Home of the World's Largest County 4-H Fair," fills the fairgrounds with livestock shows and tractor pulls. Hockey has come and gone and come back again: since 2024 the Bloomington Bison have played in the ECHL, and for 2026-27 they are affiliated with the NHL's Winnipeg Jets, after two seasons as a New York Rangers affiliate.

## Books, Scholars and Trains

Learning runs deep here. Illinois Wesleyan University, founded in 1850, is a private residential university of about 1,600 students. The public library began in 1857, opened by the Ladies' Library Association and supported by donated books and membership fees. It closed for lack of funds in 1880 until residents raised $1,100 to reopen it, and in 1888 it moved into a new building on land donated by Sarah B. Withers, taking her name as Withers Library. Its bookmobile dates to 1926, when it rolled out as the Library on Wheels. Rail still matters here, too. Amtrak's Bloomington–Normal station, with about 230,000 boardings and alightings in fiscal year 2025, is the third-busiest in Illinois after Chicago and Champaign–Urbana.

## Flight Context

Bloomington lies at 40.4842°N, 88.9936°W in central McLean County, Illinois, about 105 nautical miles southwest of Chicago and 125 nautical miles northeast of St. Louis. From 3,000 to 4,000 feet AGL, the city and neighboring Normal form one large urban area on open prairie. Interstates 39, 55 and 74 all meet here, so the highway web is the best guide, and US 51, US 150 and Illinois Route 9 cross the city as well. Central Illinois Regional Airport (KBMI), on Route 9 on the east side of town, is only about 4 nautical miles from downtown, so expect airline traffic and observe its airspace. General Wayne A. Downing Peoria International Airport (KPIA) is about 34 nautical miles to the west-northwest. Flat terrain gives clear views in good weather.
