# The Domes and Bungalows of the Square

Bloomington's courthouse square holds 1850s Greek Revival buildings, a copper-domed 1903 courthouse that now houses the McLean County Museum of History, and a 1926 building that opened with a fully furnished Spanish-style bungalow on its seventh floor.

## Introduction

When it opened in 1926, the Ensenberger Building held a fully furnished six-room Spanish-style bungalow on its seventh floor, a strange luxury in downtown Bloomington. This eccentricity is just one layer of the square's history, where a copper dome said to be modeled after St. Peter's Basilica in Rome tops the courthouse.

## Early Foundations

Long before the skyscrapers rose, Greek Revival buildings went up on the square. The Kersey H. Fell Building, dating from 1856, stands as one of the oldest structures in downtown Bloomington; it is claimed that Jesse Fell first suggested Abraham Lincoln should run for president within the building's second-floor office. Nearby, Rudolph Richter designed the Benjamin & Shermerhorn building, completed in 1857. During its construction, workers struck a well that provided a natural cooling system for perishables stored in the basement.

## A Monument to Power

The ground beneath the current courthouse has seen four different structures since the early 1830s. The first was built in 1831 (some accounts say 1832) and the second in 1836, followed by a third completed in 1868 that suffered major fire damage on June 19, 1900. In its wake, William Reeves and John M. Baile of the Peoria firm Reeves and Baile designed a replacement to embody community and power.

Rebuilt in 1903, the courthouse is an American Renaissance structure of solid masonry clad in limestone. Its symmetrical, rectangular plan follows the Corinthian order, with identical entrances on all four sides featuring a pediment topping an entrance arch dominated by columns. Above, a limestone drum and copper dome rise from the roof; the dome, once oxidized green, was later restored to its original copper color. Inside, the dominating feature is a rotunda rising over 100 feet to an allegorical painting of peace and prosperity.

## The Rise of the Skyscrapers

Surrounding this formal center, the square shifted toward modern ambition. In 1903, Bloomington architect George Miller designed the Livingston Building, an example of Chicago Style architecture. As the first steel frame construction building in Illinois outside of Chicago, it was considered Bloomington's first "skyscraper," and during the early 1900s, its roof garden became a known site for dances and musical performances.

The Ensenberger Building opened in 1926, designed by local architect Arthur Pillsbury in an eclectic interpretation of the Art Deco Style. Because Pillsbury was killed in an automobile accident in October 1925, he never saw the building open. It remained his final design.

## Remnants and Restoration

Some pieces of the courthouse that burned in 1900 found new lives outside the square. Certain remnants became lawn ornaments for several Bloomington homes, while others were used to build the bridge at Miller Park.

In 1988, the McLean County Historical Society moved to put its museum in the courthouse; the museum has operated there since 1991. Today, all four floors are occupied by the McLean County Museum of History for its exhibits, collections storage, and offices. This commitment to preservation was recognized on October 15, 2006, when the Landmark Preservations Council of Illinois presented an "Outstanding Restoration" award to the museum for its efforts to restore the copper dome.
