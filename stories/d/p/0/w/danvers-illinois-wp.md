# The Rival a Mile Away

Founded in 1836 as Concord, Danvers outlasted its neighboring rival, threw a picnic for its first train, and once housed an unusual willow-bark treatment for drinkers.

**Location:** 40.5289, -89.1769

**Categories:** Villages in McLean County, Illinois, Populated places established in 1836, 1836 establishments in Illinois, Villages in Illinois

## Introduction

For a time, the people of Concord had to go to their rival town to get their mail. Wilkesborough lay just over a mile to the east, founded the same year on the same stage route, and for several years nobody could tell which of the two would succeed. Concord won. By the late 1870s Wilkesborough had vanished completely, and by then Concord had a new name as well. Today it is Danvers, a McLean County village of 1,089 people at the 2020 census.

## Two Towns, One Stage Road

Israel W. Hall and Matthew Robb laid out Concord on February 20, 1836, and Hall named it after Concord, Massachusetts. On June 3 of the same year, James O. Barnard laid out Wilkesborough, with a public square of its own and, depending on the source, 70 or 96 lots. Both belonged to a great town-founding surge in Illinois that peaked in 1836; between 1835 and 1837, eight new towns and many additions were laid out in McLean County. Pairs of towns founded close together and competing for the same locational advantage were fairly common, and these two shared the stage route from Bloomington to Peoria and Pekin. Wilkesborough did quite well, with 15 heads of family by 1859, but Concord had three times that number.

## A Square Cut in Four

Like many towns laid out in the 1830s, Concord was planned around a central public square. Unlike most, such as Lexington and LeRoy, its plat showed with dashed lines how the streets entering the square midway along each side could be extended to meet at the center. That unusual feature made it possible to divide the square into four smaller rectangles, and this was soon done. One has always been a park. The other three have served civic uses, including sites for the library, the fire station, the water tower and the school gymnasium.

## Nearly Lifeless

Growth came slowly at first. Hall and Robb both lived in the area, but neither lived in the town. Nicholas Conger put up the first building in 1837, though he does not seem to have lived there himself; the first actual resident was Asa Rankin, and eventually Hobson and Havens built a small mill. "For more than six years the place was nearly lifeless," the 1879 county history put it. A post office opened in 1848 or 1849, and the town grew more rapidly in the late 1840s and early 1850s.

Back in 1836, Illinois had no way to regulate town names, and popular ones were often repeated; at least two other places in the state were called Concord. So in 1861 the town became Danvers, after Danvers in Essex County, Massachusetts. It was incorporated as a village under a special charter on May 7, 1869.

## Chicken, Oysters and Coffee

In the late 1850s and 1860s, farmers moved onto the surrounding prairie, and Danvers became the center of an exceptionally productive agricultural district. Shipping grain meant having a railroad, yet the Illinois Central and the Chicago and Alton had crossed McLean County without coming close. A town meeting decided that Danvers would subscribe to $50,000 in railroad bonds. Many Illinois communities took similar action, and most of them came to regret it: the bonds were quickly sold in Europe or the East, leaving taxpayers to pay for railroads that often gave indifferent service.

On November 25, 1869, Danvers celebrated its first train with a great public picnic and consumed large amounts of chicken, oysters and coffee. Scheduled rail service lasted until December 1982. In 1907 Danvers also became a stop on the electric interurban, its tracks laid along Exchange Street through the center of town, and the Illinois Traction System kept running long after similar lines elsewhere had closed. In 1942, as the government scoured the country for surplus metal for war needs, the tracks were threatened, but citizens protested that the line was vital to local people and saved great amounts of gasoline. Service finally ended on February 21, 1953.

## Willow Bark and Buried Gas

Beginning in 1880, Dr. F. J. Parkhurst ran a distinctive alcohol-treatment facility here, the Willow Bark Institute. He sought to treat addiction to strong spirits with small amounts of alcohol, followed by a stiff dose of foul-tasting willow-bark extract. It operated until about 1920 and was revived by others from 1935 to the early 1950s. Below ground, Danvers had drift gas: vegetation buried by ice-carried soil in the last glacial epoch decayed into gas that shallow wells could tap for some domestic uses, and at one time the area had more than seventy such wells.

The population was 356 in 1870, 607 in 1900 and 762 in 1950. By 2000 it was over 1,100, meaning that, unlike many similar places, Danvers had grown faster in that last stretch than in any earlier period. Much of the increase came from people who found Danvers a pleasant home base while working in Bloomington-Normal or Peoria. The village's own website describes a community with strong ties to agriculture.

## Flight Context

Danvers sits at 40.53°N, 89.18°W in western McLean County, Illinois, about 9 nautical miles northwest of Bloomington, with Illinois Route 9 passing just south of the village on its way southeast to Twin Grove and west to Mackinaw. From 2,000 to 3,000 ft above ground, look for the village street grid with the highway along its southern edge. Central Illinois Regional Airport at Bloomington-Normal (KBMI) is about 12 nm to the east-southeast, and General Wayne A. Downing Peoria International Airport (KPIA) about 25 nm to the west-northwest. Best viewed in clear daytime weather.
