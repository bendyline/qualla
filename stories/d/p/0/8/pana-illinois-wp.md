# Pana, Illinois

A railroad crossing and coal town in Christian County, Pana endured a deadly 1899 mine riot, reinvented itself as the "City of Roses" with more than a hundred greenhouses, and now draws crowds of up to 15,000 to its Labor Day parade.

**Location:** 39.3867, -89.0817

**Categories:** Cities in Illinois, Cities in Christian County, Illinois, Populated places established in 1845, 1845 establishments in Illinois

## Introduction

Coal built Pana, but roses named it. At one time this Christian County town is said to have counted 109 greenhouses, and so many major florists and growers set up shop here that local newsmen, the Jordan Brothers, dubbed it the "City of Roses." It is an unexpected title for a place that began as Stone Coal Precinct and grew up around mine shafts and railroad tracks. Beneath the flowers lies a harder history, including a deadly mine riot in 1899, and today a town of about 5,200 people that has learned to throw a Labor Day parade drawing crowds of up to 15,000.

## Fuel and Water at the Crossing

The area was first organized as Stone Coal Precinct in 1845. When the county's precincts became townships in 1856, it became Pana Township, and the village of Pana was incorporated the following year. The name is believed to derive from the Pawnee, an Indigenous nation. What made the town was geography of another kind: it developed where east-west and north-south railroads crossed, and it had the supplies of fuel and water that steam locomotives needed. Trains needed both, and a town grew up around the crossing. Most of Pana lies in Christian County, though a small portion spills into neighboring Shelby County.

## April 1899

By the late 19th century, Pana had become a center of coal mining, and among its miners were Black replacement workers recruited from Alabama. On April 10, 1899, a gunfight broke out between striking White union miners and the Black replacement workers. Accounts differ on how it began and on who fired the first fatal shot, but a White man was killed, and White union miners then turned on the Black workers. Most accounts count seven dead in all, five of them Black and two White, and six more Black miners were wounded; one University of Illinois history page says only that the number of dead remains uncertain. The National Guard quelled the violence, but the hostility Black families faced in Pana did not end. Many were driven out or left over the following months; by one historian's count, 211 Black residents moved west, primarily to Weir, Kansas, to work at another mine, while only 63 went back to Alabama and the Jim Crow South.

## The City of Roses

Pana would also cultivate a gentler reputation. Many major florists and growers set up shop here, and at one time the town is said to have held 109 greenhouses, the source of its enduring nickname. Two Pana landmarks are listed on the National Register of Historic Places: Kitchell Park, added in 1992, and the Louis Jehle House, listed in 1995. Pana also sent notable people out into the world. War correspondent and author Vincent Sheean was born here, as was Warren Amling, a 1984 College Football Hall of Fame inductee. Albert Marsh, co-inventor of the alloy chromel, grew up in Pana after his family moved here in 1884, and Thomas Henry Carter, later a U.S. senator from Montana, spent his youth here after the Civil War.

## A Town That Loves a Parade

Every Labor Day, Pana's population swells. The annual parade draws crowds of up to 15,000 people and has been described as the largest Labor Day parade in Illinois, a title other towns, such as Naperville, also claim, and in 2022 Pana hosted its 88th. The 2011 parade was led by 343 firefighters marching in honor of the firefighters, paramedics and police officers who died in the September 11 attacks. The celebration hardly stops there. The Tri-County Fair runs for six days, opening on the Wednesday before Labor Day and closing on the holiday itself, with carnival rides, vendors and games drawing an average of 7,000 visitors a day. Earlier in the season, on the last weekend of May, Pana Heritage Days close off the streets for vendors, fair rides and live band music.

## Flight Context

Pana sits at 39.3867N, 89.0817W in southeastern Christian County, Illinois, on the Shelby County line. From 2,500-3,500 feet AGL, look for a compact town grid amid flat farmland, built where east-west and north-south railroads crossed. Moweaqua lies about 14 nautical miles to the north. Decatur Airport (KDEC) is roughly 29 nautical miles to the north-northeast, and Abraham Lincoln Capital Airport (KSPI) at Springfield lies about 39 nautical miles to the northwest; both have control towers, so check the sectional chart and expect to work with the tower or approach control near them. Central Illinois is flat and usually clear, though summer haze and thunderstorms can reduce visibility.
