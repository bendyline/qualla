# Lincoln-Berry General Store

The frontier store a 23-year-old Abraham Lincoln bought on credit in New Salem failed within two years and left him in debt until 1848, yet it helped earn him the name Honest Abe.

**Location:** 39.9791, -89.8415

**Categories:** General stores in the United States, Abraham Lincoln National Heritage Area, 1832 establishments in Illinois, Abraham Lincoln

## Introduction

Years later, writing about himself in the third person for an 1860 autobiography, Abraham Lincoln still sounded faintly amazed by it. "Before long, strangely enough, a man offered to sell, and did sell, to Abraham and another as poor as himself, an old stock of goods, upon credit. They opened as merchants; and he says that was the store." The store in question stood in the frontier village of New Salem, Illinois. It made him briefly a thriving merchant, then a partner in a failing tavern, and finally a debtor for more than a decade.

## An Old Stock of Goods, Upon Credit

In 1832, after the Black Hawk War, the 23-year-old Lincoln and William F. Berry, who had served in Lincoln's militia company during that war, bought one of New Salem's general stores from James and Rowan Herndon. Neither man had money. They signed personal notes to buy the business and signed more when they later acquired another store's inventory. The store sold general merchandise: apparel, dry goods, hardware, home furnishings and a selection of food, including takeout meals for stagecoach passengers passing through. For a short time the partners did well. Lincoln often slept in the back room after a long night of reading, which says a good deal about where his mind was even while he stood behind the counter. The reconstructed store at Lincoln's New Salem is said to be the only frame building in the village; the others are log buildings.

## A Grocery, in the Old Sense

To bring in more money, Berry applied for a liquor license, which cost seven dollars, and the store became a tavern as well. As licensed bartenders, the partners sold liquor at 12 cents a pint. It helped, but not enough. By 1833 New Salem had stopped growing. The Sangamon River proved inadequate for commercial transportation, and no roads or railroads gave easy access to other markets. Berry, meanwhile, by several accounts drank heavily and was often too drunk to work, leaving Lincoln to run the store alone. Local tradition held that disagreement over the liquor sales broke up the partnership. Lincoln sold his share to Berry in 1833, and the store closed the next year. When Berry died in 1835, Lincoln was left with a business debt of more than $1,000 that he joked about as his "national debt" and could not pay off until 1848, while he was serving in the U.S. House of Representatives. His opponents did not forget. At the first Lincoln-Douglas debate, in Ottawa, Illinois, on August 21, 1858, Stephen Douglas mocked him for having run a "grocery," a polite word for a tavern.

## Honest Abe Behind the Counter

The store failed, but it left Lincoln with something more durable than profit. He told customers plainly when a product was not very good. According to local legend, he once took six cents too much from a customer and walked three miles to return the money. It is the kind of story that small communities hold on to, and it stuck to him. Lincoln's work as an everyday store clerk and trustworthy business owner helped earn him the nickname "Honest Abe," a reputation that followed him out of New Salem and into national politics. The long debt makes the nickname more pointed, not less: the man who walked miles over six cents also spent more than a decade paying off a partnership that had collapsed around him.

## The Store on Canvas and Screen

The little store became a fixture in the way Americans picture young Lincoln. A 1928 painting by Frank Schoonover of Lincoln working there is reported to hang in the Norman Rockwell Museum. The 1930 film Abraham Lincoln set its first act partly in his days as a storekeeper here, and the 1933-1934 Chicago World's Fair built a replica of the store as an exhibit. In 1939, the film Young Mr. Lincoln set an early scene inside "Lincoln's store." Today the reconstructed building stands at Lincoln's New Salem State Historic Site among the log cabins of the recreated village; check the state's current hours before you go.

## Flight Context

The Lincoln-Berry General Store stands at 39.9791°N, 89.8415°W within Lincoln's New Salem State Historic Site in Menard County, Illinois, on a wooded ridge above the Sangamon River about 13 nautical miles northwest of downtown Springfield and roughly 2 nautical miles south of Petersburg. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL, look for the Sangamon's winding course and the clearing of the reconstructed village on the ridge to its west; Petersburg's grid lies to the north. Abraham Lincoln Capital Airport (KSPI) in Springfield is about 11 nautical miles to the southeast, so check the sectional chart and expect to work with approach control. The village is partly screened by woodland, so late-autumn and winter light, when leaves are down, give the clearest view of the cabins and the store.
