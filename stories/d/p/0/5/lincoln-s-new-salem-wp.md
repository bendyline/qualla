# Lincoln's New Salem

A frontier mill village on the Sangamon that lived barely a decade, shaped the young Abraham Lincoln, and was rebuilt log by log from memories, documents and archaeology.

**Location:** 39.9737, -89.8397

## Introduction

New Salem barely lasted a decade. It was founded in 1829, it grew fast, and by 1840 its ridge above the Sangamon River was nearly deserted. It might have vanished from memory entirely if not for a 22-year-old who arrived on a flatboat in 1831 and stayed for about six years. Abraham Lincoln left New Salem for Springfield in 1837. The village he left behind was later rebuilt from the recollections of his old neighbors, and it is now Lincoln's New Salem State Historic Site.

## A Mill Town on the Ridge

The village began with a gristmill. In 1829 James Rutledge and John Camron built one on the Sangamon River, then surveyed and sold lots for shops and homes along the ridge that stretches west above it. For a few years New Salem boomed. When Lincoln lived there, it had a cooper shop, a blacksmith shop, a wool carding mill, four general stores, a tavern, a grocery, two doctors' offices, a shoemaker, a carpenter, a hat maker, a tanner and a combined schoolhouse and church, along with homes, common pastures and kitchen gardens. Somewhere between 20 and 25 families lived there at any one time. This was no sleepy farm hamlet but a commercial village full of young businessmen and craftsmen trying to start new lives on the frontier. It could not hold. The county seat went to nearby Petersburg, the Sangamon proved poorly suited to steamboats, and New Salem shrank until, by 1840, it was abandoned.

## Six Years That Made Lincoln

In 1830 Thomas Lincoln moved his family from Indiana to Macon County, Illinois, and later on to Coles County; in 1831 his 22-year-old son struck out on his own. Abraham reached New Salem by flatboat and made his living any way he could: as a shopkeeper, a soldier in the Black Hawk War, a general store owner, a postmaster, a land surveyor, a rail splitter and a taker of odd jobs around town. As far as historians know, he never owned a home here, which was typical of single men at the time. He often slept in the tavern or in his store and took his meals with a nearby family. Politics found him here too. In 1832 he ran for the Illinois General Assembly, carrying his New Salem precinct handily but losing the countywide race. He tried again in 1834 and won. By around 1837 he had moved on to Springfield, and the young man who had arrived on a flatboat left as a state legislator.

## The Talisman Comes Upriver

In 1832 the river briefly seemed to promise everything. The Talisman, a 136-foot, 150-ton steamer chartered to Springfield businessman Vincent Bogue, came all the way from Cincinnati, down the Ohio, up the Mississippi and the Illinois, and into the Sangamon. Lincoln joined the axmen who cleared obstructions from the river near New Salem, then served as assistant pilot to Rowan Herndon on the steamer's return trip to Beardstown. The Sangamon never became the highway New Salem needed. In the summer of 1961 the story repeated itself. A new sternwheeler named Talisman, built by Dubuque Boat & Boiler Works in Iowa, came to New Salem by way of the Mississippi, Illinois and Sangamon rivers. At 73 feet and 40 tons, with an upper cabin and a 60-horsepower diesel, it was a scaled-down tribute, and it carried tourists on short trips from a landing beside the old mill site. Like the original, it was defeated by low water. By the late 1990s, with the river dam at Petersburg abandoned, the water had reportedly become too low to navigate.

## Rebuilding From Memory

After Lincoln's death in 1865, historians and biographers went looking for New Salem. They collected the memories, documents and village plans of former residents and neighbors, and studied the site's archaeological remains. In 1906 William Randolph Hearst bought the village land and deeded it to the Old Salem Chautauqua Association, which gave it to the State of Illinois in 1919. The site opened to the public on May 19, 1921. Most of the reconstruction was done by the Civilian Conservation Corps during the Great Depression, with buildings raised on the original foundations. Today 23 buildings, mostly log, stand in the park, furnished with period tools and furniture, many gathered from area farms and homes, and in season costumed interpreters demonstrate life in the 1830s. Twenty-two are reconstructions. The log buildings are aging, and in March 2025 the state committed $8 million to repair up to 23 of them and update the outdoor theater. The Onstot Cooper Shop is original: Henry Onstot moved it to Petersburg in 1840, and in 1922 it came home to what archaeologists believe was its original foundation. The 700-acre park around the village adds extensive woodland, a visitor center with an exhibit and theater, trails and campsites.

## Flight Context

Lincoln's New Salem lies at 39.9737°N, 89.8397°W in Menard County, Illinois, about 13 nautical miles northwest of downtown Springfield and roughly 2 nautical miles south of Petersburg. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL, follow the winding Sangamon River and look for the village clearing on the wooded ridge just west of the river, with the 700-acre park's woodland around it and Petersburg's street grid to the north. Abraham Lincoln Capital Airport (KSPI) in Springfield is about 11 nautical miles to the southeast, so check the sectional chart and expect to work with approach control. Woodland partly screens the log buildings, so leaf-off seasons and clear, low-sun conditions give the best view of the reconstructed village.
