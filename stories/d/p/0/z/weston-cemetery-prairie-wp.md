# Weston Cemetery Prairie

Set aside as a township cemetery in 1870, a few acres near Weston escaped the plow and now preserve one of the rare surviving fragments of the tallgrass prairie that once blanketed Illinois.

**Location:** 40.7468, -88.6142

**Categories:** Grasslands of Illinois, Nature reserves in Illinois, Protected areas of McLean County, Illinois

## Introduction

Tallgrass prairie once covered some 22 million acres of Illinois. Here, half a mile east of the tiny McLean County community of Weston, survives a patch measured in single acres. Its story begins in 1870, when early residents set this ground aside for the dead. More than 80 people were laid to rest here between about 1870 and the 1950s, and part of that parcel of black-soil prairie escaped the plow. Today the Weston Cemetery Prairie is an Illinois Nature Preserve, dedicated in 1972, and a Category I site on the Illinois Natural Areas Inventory, a living sample of the landscape the settlers found.

## Ground Set Aside

Weston was laid out in 1867, and a year or two later four prominent local men organized the Weston Cemetery Company. In 1869 or 1870 it bought five acres east of the village to serve as a township cemetery, though the deed was not recorded until 1884. The earliest known burial is believed to date from 1870 or 1871 and the latest from 1955 or 1956, though most took place between 1880 and 1915. One of them was three-year-old Iva "Pearl" Adams, who burned to death when her clothes caught fire at home in Weston on December 4, 1900, and was interred here two days later.

## What the Plow Missed

As the surrounding prairie was broken for farming, part of the cemetery parcel was left untouched, and it was still there in the 1970s, when surviving fragments of Illinois tallgrass prairie were being located and inventoried. The main 3.5 acres of high-quality prairie are owned by Yates Township and managed by the ParkLands Foundation. In 1982 Flora Kelleher donated a narrow buffer strip, running back toward Weston along the southern edge of the train tracks, to the Natural Land Institute, which passed it on to the ParkLands Foundation in 1986. Sources disagree on the preserve's exact size, giving the whole cemetery as anywhere from 5 to 5.6 acres, with roughly 3.2 to 3.8 acres of that in prairie, but either way it is tiny, and that makes it precious. It is actively managed with prescribed burns and exotic species control.

## A Census of Wildflowers

For its size, the prairie is remarkably diverse. A count by botanist Roger Anderson, a distinguished professor emeritus at Illinois State University, found 71 native prairie plant species here, along with 47 introduced ones. The native wildflowers read like a field guide: dwarf prairie rose, prairie shooting star, spiderwort, prairie gentian and downy gentian, compass plant and wood betony. There are four asters, sky blue, heath, smooth and hairy; three sunflowers, sawtooth, false and prairie; and four goldenrods, tall, grass-leaved, early and rigid. Cream wild indigo, spiked lobelia, violet wood sorrel, false dragonhead and common mountain mint bloom among them. Among them grow the grasses that give tallgrass prairie its name and texture: big bluestem, little bluestem, prairie dropseed and Indian grass, along with yellow star grass and blue-eyed grass.

## Tread Lightly

Getting here takes some intent. From U.S. Route 24, a small sign marks a grass lane that leads north, about 0.3 mile, to a small parking area in the preserve's southeast corner, and there are no trails once you arrive. Visitors are asked to tread lightly, large groups are discouraged, and collecting plants or seeds is prohibited. The nearest interstate interchange is Exit 187 on Interstate 55. What waits at the end of the lane is modest to the eye, a few acres of grass and flowers around old graves, but it is also a rare, unplowed glimpse of what much of Illinois looked like before the farms.

## Flight Context

Weston Cemetery Prairie lies at 40.7468°N, 88.6142°W, about half a mile east of the unincorporated community of Weston in northeastern McLean County, beside a rail line and reached by a short grass lane north from U.S. Route 24. The preserve is only a few acres, so it is best viewed low and slow, around 1,500 feet AGL, as a small unplowed patch amid cultivated fields. Chenoa, at the junction of I-55 and US-24, is about 5 nautical miles to the west and makes the easiest reference. Pontiac Municipal Airport (KPNT) is about 11 nautical miles to the north, and Central Illinois Regional Airport (KBMI) at Bloomington-Normal is about 21 nautical miles to the southwest. The terrain is flat farmland; in the growing season the prairie's mixed grasses and wildflowers may stand out against the surrounding row crops.
