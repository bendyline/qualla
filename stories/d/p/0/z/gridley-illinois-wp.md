# Gridley, Illinois

Named for a Bloomington banker who later took his own town to court, Gridley survived a tornado, a devastating 1901 fire and a deadly 1946 railroad strike to become a quiet farm village with a telephone museum.

**Location:** 40.7428, -88.8806

**Categories:** Villages in McLean County, Illinois, Populated places established in 1869, 1869 establishments in Illinois, Villages in Illinois

## Introduction

Most towns are grateful to their namesakes. Gridley spent years resenting its own. Asahel Gridley, the Bloomington banker, lawyer and land developer the town was named for, bought the entire place within a year of its being laid out, then, years later, surprised everyone by announcing he would re-plat the whole town and subdivide the open ground along the railroad, land the town had been treating as public. He took his neighbors to court, and he lost. The Illinois Supreme Court ruled against him in 1877, and in the village of 1,456 people (2020 census) that bears his name, the suit left a great deal of ill feeling.

## A Banker's Name on the Prairie

Gridley was laid out in late 1856 by Thomas Carlyle and George Washington Kent, both of whom were working for Asahel Gridley at the time. Kent, Carlyle and Gardner, the last for a governor of Massachusetts, were all considered as names before the founders settled on their employer's. Gridley himself was a Whig politician and land investor who once hired Abraham Lincoln to defend him in a slander suit. Like many central Illinois towns, this one was born when a railroad crossed the state, in this case the Peoria and Oquawka, which soon became the Toledo, Peoria and Western. The first train arrived on February 28, 1857.

## The Fight Over the Depot Grounds

At first glance Gridley's plan looked ordinary: a 100-foot strip for the railroad, flanked on the north and south by long, narrow rectangles labeled "Depot Grounds," with a grid of 38 blocks beyond. In most towns such strips became parks or other public spaces, and at Gridley half of the northern one was dedicated as a long, narrow "Public Square." The rest of the depot grounds caused trouble. The town grew quickly, and the railroad and private owners built on them; the townspeople even put their jail there. Then Gridley argued that although the plat said "depot grounds," he had never actually dedicated the land to the railroad or to public use, and he asked the courts to eject the others. Gridley v. Hopkins went all the way to the Illinois Supreme Court, which ruled against him in 1877: because he had sold lots by the original plat, it held, he was estopped from reclaiming the grounds, and the others kept their buildings.

## Trees From the Mackinaw

Early growth was rapid. J.M. Mitchell built the first store, on the corner just north of the small depot, and a house and blacksmith shop went up south of the tracks. In 1858 a tornado tore through town, damaging houses and twisting the newly laid rails, but building quickly resumed. George Washington Kent, the co-founder, built the first grain elevator and then enlisted in the Union Army, serving as a sergeant and later a lieutenant. When he came home, he helped guide the town's next stage of growth, digging up trees along the Mackinaw River and hauling them to town to plant in the public square. In 1884 Fred Donner opened a tile factory, and by 1900 Gridley had 735 residents.

## The Afternoon Center Street Burned

Gridley thought it was ready for fire. After a serious blaze in July 1891, the town had several cisterns and a dedicated corps of volunteer firefighters. Then, on a hot and windy afternoon in May 1901, at 3:40 p.m., smoke rose from the alley behind Cohn's store. Within a short time, stores along both sides of Center Street were engulfed. Firefighters pumped water from several cisterns, then ran hoses to the flooded pit of the tile factory. Every able-bodied person in town, and many from the surrounding countryside, joined the fight. A telegram went to Peoria for help, but the blaze had subsided before it could arrive. The state bank and many other buildings burned to the ground, and damage was estimated at $100,000, a severe blow for a small town. Rebuilding began at once.

## Hard Road, Hard Times

In the early 1920s, six passenger trains ran each way through Gridley. Then, in June 1924, the newly paved Route 8, today's U.S. 24, opened, and the hard road quickly drained away rail passengers; by 1929 passenger service had ended. The town's darkest day came in February 1946, during a strike against the Toledo, Peoria and Western. The railroad sent a train with fourteen workers and four armed guards to keep the line open. Strikers followed in cars along U.S. 24, pelting the train with rocks whenever it slowed, and when it stopped at Gridley, shooting broke out. Two strikers died and three were wounded; according to local accounts, a jury later found the guards had acted in self-defense. Most years were gentler, filled with Fourth of July races and Saturday night band concerts in the park. The old tile-factory clay pits became a town skating rink in 1937, and in 2001 the Gridley Telephone Museum opened.

## Flight Context

Gridley sits at 40.7428°N, 88.8806°W in northern McLean County, with its northern edge on the Livingston County line and U.S. Route 24 crossing the south side of the village. Follow US-24 and the parallel railroad east about 7 nautical miles to Chenoa or west about 6 nautical miles to El Paso; the village lies roughly midway between Interstate 39 to the west and Interstate 55 to the east. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL, look for the village grid straddling the rail line, with US-24 crossing its south side. Pontiac Municipal Airport (KPNT) is about 16 nautical miles to the northeast, and Central Illinois Regional Airport (KBMI) at Bloomington-Normal is about 16 nautical miles to the south. The terrain is flat, open farmland with good visibility outside summer haze and winter overcast.
