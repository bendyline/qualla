# Lexington, Illinois

Nearly abandoned before the railroad arrived in 1854, Lexington hosted Lincoln and Douglas at a log tavern, launched a railroad-rate case that reached the Illinois Supreme Court, turned out to see Theodore Roosevelt's train, and spent half a century as a Route 66 stop.

**Location:** 40.6425, -88.7853

**Categories:** Cities in Illinois, Cities in McLean County, Illinois, Populated places established in 1836, 1836 establishments in Illinois

## Introduction

According to local history, on November 21, 1860, a train brought Abraham Lincoln to Lexington, and he spoke a few words to his old friends there. Less than five years later, in the early hours of May 3, 1865, another train passed through town. This one carried Lincoln's body. Between those two trains lies much of what made Lexington what it is: a town on the road between Springfield and Chicago, where travelers, politicians and, eventually, presidents came and went, and where the townspeople learned that their fortunes rose and fell with whatever passed along that route.

## A Square at the Edge of the Timber

Asahel Gridley and James Brown laid out Lexington in January 1836, at the height of a real estate boom that swept the nation; within a few months, seven more new towns appeared in McLean County. Gridley was a Bloomington lawyer and banker who would become one of the wealthiest men in the county. Brown was born in Lexington, Kentucky, which gives one theory for the town's name; the other links it to the Revolutionary War's Battle of Lexington, which a 1914 local account tied to Gridley's family. Like other towns of the 1836 boom, Lexington was built around a central public square, with 36 blocks of six lots each and streets running true north-south and east-west. It sat right on the line between woodland and prairie, on higher ground a mile from the Mackinaw River. The founders' advertisement promised "a fine rolling prairie, near a large and inexhaustible body of the best timber the country affords."

## Cattle on the Town Square

Then came the crash. From 1837 to 1854, a long national depression left Lexington's survival in doubt. The founders' first building, a store, failed within a year and was hauled away to Bloomington, and the first house was moved to the rival town of Clarksville downstream. By the early 1850s more than 300 unsold lots were being offered to cover unpaid taxes, and cattle grazed on the town square. A few believers stayed. Jacob Spawr built a dog-trot house, essentially two log cabins facing each other under one roof, and used it as home, post office and tavern. Because Lexington sat halfway between the county seats of Bloomington and Pontiac, Spawr's place made a convenient stop, and Abraham Lincoln and Stephen Douglas were frequent guests. Even so, by 1854 only about a dozen families lived in town.

## Whiskey in the Mud

On July 4, 1854, the railroad that would soon be known as the Chicago and Alton reached Lexington, and everything changed. A grain warehouse built by Thomas Kincaid was hauled across the tracks, fitted with a passenger platform and pressed into service as the station. Dawson and McCurdy built a grain elevator, three denominations raised churches, and the Mahan brothers built a windmill downtown. According to a 1924 county history, when the town formed its own government, its first act was to buy up the stock of the two local whiskey sellers and pour the contents of the bottles into the mud of Main Street. A new hotel went up, and by 1865 Lexington had its first brick block.

## Lumber by the Thousand Feet

Lexington also became the test case in a fight over who could regulate the railroads. Once railroads reached the Midwest, it became clear that a line free to set its own freight rates could drain most of the wealth from nearby farmers, and in 1870 a new Illinois constitution told the legislature to pass laws against unjust discrimination and extortion by railroads. The state's railroad commissioners went after the Chicago and Alton over lumber. The railroad charged $5.65 per thousand feet to ship it from Chicago to Lexington, 110 miles away, but only $5.00 to ship it 126 miles to Bloomington. The railroad answered that more competition at Bloomington forced it to charge less there. A McLean County court stripped the company of its franchises, but in January 1873 the Illinois Supreme Court reversed that judgment. It held that the legislature may prohibit unjust discrimination, but that this act was flawed because it treated any difference in rates as conclusive proof of guilt. Bloomington lawyer Reuben M. Benjamin was among the lawyers who argued for the state.

## Teddy, Trains and the Mother Road

Of all the days in Lexington's history, the best remembered may be the day President Theodore Roosevelt's train stopped in town. Local sources date it variously to 1902, 1903 or 1904 and describe the crowd as anything from a small gathering at the depot to twenty thousand people. Roosevelt stayed aboard and spoke briefly from the train. Local histories say it was the only visit by a sitting president. Rail travel slowly faded after that, and passenger service ended in the fall of 1946. The road took its place. A paved road along the old stagecoach route reached town in 1915, became Route 66 in 1926, and for the next 50 years made Lexington a popular stop. When Interstate 55 opened in the late 1970s, traffic moved to it, but near Lexington much of the old highway can still be driven.

## Flight Context

Lexington lies at 40.6425°N, 88.7853°W in McLean County, about a mile from the Mackinaw River on the old Chicago-to-Springfield corridor. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL, look for the town's grid squared to the compass, with the railroad, old Route 66 and Interstate 55 running parallel nearby; the Mackinaw River, about a mile away, makes a useful reference. Towanda lies to the southwest and Chenoa to the northeast along the same corridor. Central Illinois Regional Airport (KBMI) at Bloomington-Normal is about 12 nautical miles to the south-southwest, and Pontiac Municipal Airport (KPNT) is about 18 nautical miles to the north-northeast. The terrain is flat to gently rolling farmland with good visibility outside summer haze and winter overcast.
