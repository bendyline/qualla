# Chenoa, Illinois

Matthew T. Scott bet that two railroads would cross on this empty prairie, platted a town there in 1856, walled off his rival with an 'imaginary wall', and left behind a city whose very name is still misunderstood.

**Location:** 40.7431, -88.7200

**Categories:** Cities in Illinois, Cities in McLean County, Illinois, 1856 establishments in Illinois

## Introduction

Between the two halves of early Chenoa ran a wall that did not exist. Matthew T. Scott had laid out his town on the west side of the railroad, and his rival and former friend W. M. Hamilton had laid out East Chenoa on the other. Scott controlled a narrow strip of land between them, and he refused to divide it into streets or lots. Anyone who walked the short distance from one town to the other was, technically, trespassing on Scott's property. He called the strip his "imaginary wall." It was never a physical barrier, but it says a great deal about the man who built Chenoa.

## A Bet on a Crossing

Scott, the son of a Kentucky banker, arrived in Illinois as an experienced land developer at the head of a well-financed group of investors. In 1854 he began buying thousands of acres of prairie here. The Chicago and Alton Railroad was already running trains, but the survey for the east-west Toledo, Peoria and Oquawka line, soon to become the Toledo, Peoria and Western, had not yet reached McLean County. Scott guessed where the two lines would cross and laid out a town on that spot on May 13, 1856. His advertisement for a lot sale on May 15 ran under the heading GREAT SALE OF LOTS IN THE TOWN OF CHENOA, promising "the only crossing of railroads likely to be made within McLean County within four or five years." Coal would sell for twelve cents a bushel. Anyone who built a house worth $400 to $500 within six months got two years of interest-free credit. Anyone who sold liquor would forfeit the title to their land.

## Breaking the Sod

The great problem was that nobody lived out here yet. The land around Chenoa was unbroken prairie, and the Native peoples who had long hunted and lived in the Illinois country had already been removed by the U.S. government to lands west of the Mississippi River. Scott set out to fill the emptiness himself. He plowed and cultivated 16,000 acres, built two hundred houses and planted twenty-seven miles of hedge fences. The first structures in town were two little dugouts, half sod and half board, that served as depot, freight house and shelter for railroad section hands, one of the very few mentions of sod houses anywhere in McLean County. J. B. Lenney, who came from Pennsylvania and put up a frame building called "The Farmer's Store," took such an active part in building the town that he is remembered as the "Father of Chenoa." In 1864 Chenoa, East Chenoa and several additions finally united under one government.

## The Name Nobody Can Pin Down

What does Chenoa mean? Mid-nineteenth-century histories said it was a Native American word for Kentucky, translatable as "Dark and Bloody Ground," but linguist Edward Callary points out that the meaning is unknown in Kentucky itself. Others claim it is Cherokee for "dove" or "white dove," yet the Cherokee words are waya and unega waya. A popular story holds that Scott wanted "Chenowa" and the railroad dropped the w by mistake; in fact the railroad had no say, and Scott recorded the spelling exactly as it stands. The likeliest clue is that "Chenoka" or "Chenoa" was one of many Native American names for the Kentucky River, which may explain why Scott, a Kentuckian's son, believed it meant Kentucky.

## A Vice President's Wedding

Scott's own home still stands. The rear of the Matthew T. Scott House is an 1855 post-and-beam I-cottage; the more formal front, added in 1863, is a balloon-frame structure built around a central hall with an impressive staircase, both resting on Joliet dolomite. It was here that Adlai E. Stevenson I, who would serve as the 23rd vice president of the United States from 1893 to 1897, courted Scott's sister-in-law, Letitia Green. They were married in the house in 1866. By 1980 the house was in poor condition and seemed likely to be torn down, until Elizabeth Stevenson Ives, a great-niece of Matthew Scott, bought and restored it. It joined the National Register of Historic Places on February 10, 1983.

## Fire, Trains and Route 66

Chenoa's fortunes rose and fell with transportation. The Chicago and Alton was double-tracked in 1890, electric lights arrived in 1891, and railroad traffic peaked in 1911, when twenty-four trains came through. Disaster struck twice. In July 1894 the entire business district east of the railroad burned, and very few buildings were insured. On November 11, 1918, the union station the two railroads shared burned down during the Armistice celebrations. As cars and trucks replaced trains, Chenoa became a stop on Route 66, and today it sits where Interstate 55, Historic Route 66 and U.S. Route 24 meet. Schuirman's Drug Store, now Chenoa Pharmacy, and Union Roofing have both been noted for their longevity. The town's last grain elevator reportedly came down in December 2020, and the Bright Stalk Wind Farm, a 205-megawatt project in operation since 2019, runs its operations and maintenance office from Chenoa. At the 2020 census, 1,695 people called it home.

## Flight Context

Chenoa lies at 40.7431°N, 88.7200°W in northeastern McLean County, where Interstate 55, Historic Route 66 and U.S. Route 24 meet. The I-55 and US-24 junction makes the town easy to find, and from 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL you can pick out the two railroad corridors whose crossing Matthew Scott bet on in 1856, with his original town on the west side of the railroad. Gridley lies about 7 nautical miles (8 road miles) west along US-24, and the hamlet of Meadows sits between the two. Pontiac Municipal Airport (KPNT) is about 12 nautical miles to the north-northeast, and Central Illinois Regional Airport (KBMI) at Bloomington-Normal is about 18 nautical miles to the south-southwest. The surrounding land is flat, heavily farmed prairie with long visibility on clear days.
