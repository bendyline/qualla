# The Airfield That Taught the Air Force

Built in about two months for World War I pilots, Chanute Field in Rantoul, Illinois, spent more than seven decades as a school for the people who keep aircraft flying, from the first ground crews of the Tuskegee Airmen to Minuteman missile technicians.

## Introduction

The squadron had no pilots, and that was the point. On 22 March 1941 the 99th Pursuit Squadron, the first all-Black fighter squadron, was activated at Chanute Field, on level ground just south of Rantoul, Illinois. It was formed to train an officer corps and the ground crews every fighter unit depends on. More than 250 enlisted men learned their trades here as airplane mechanics, supply clerks, armorers and weather forecasters, and that small group became the core of the squadrons later formed at Tuskegee and Maxwell fields in Alabama: the men history remembers as the Tuskegee Airmen. The moment suited Chanute, a base whose main mission across its whole existence was technical training, the work of keeping aircraft in the air rather than flying them.

## Sixty Days on Level Ground

After the United States entered World War I in 1917, Congress appropriated $640 million to build up the Air Service. The War Department chose Rantoul because it was one of the few level sites in Illinois close to both the Illinois Central Railroad and the ground school at the University of Illinois. English Brothers Construction of Champaign signed the contract on 22 May 1917, with sixty days to finish. At the peak, 2,000 men, 200 teams of horses, three steam shovels and several steam tractors worked the site, on a weekly payroll of about $96,000, and visitors came just to watch. The first airplanes arrived on 4 July. Curtiss JN-4 "Jenny" trainers began flying from dawn until dusk in mid-July, and the field was completed on 22 July at a cost of about $1 million. Within a month the sightseers had become such a distraction to the pilots that the field was closed to them. Named Rantoul Aviation Field, it became Chanute Field within weeks, honouring Octave Chanute (1832–1910), the engineer who advised the Wright brothers and whose 1896 biplane glider, braced on the truss principle of his own railroad bridges, served as a prototype design for airplanes.

## The School That Would Not Close

As an eight-week primary flying school, Chanute had trained several thousand pilots by the armistice in November 1918. Then the airplanes were flown out, and the base shrank to a storage depot for OX-5 aircraft engines and paint, minded by about 30 people. A recommendation to close it was made in Washington in August 1919. Instead, Congress approved money to buy the field in February 1920, and in January 1921 the Air Service Mechanics School arrived from Kelly Field, Texas. The photography school from Langley Field, Virginia, and the communications school from Fort Sill, Oklahoma, followed in 1922, and from then until 1938 Chanute provided all technical training for the Army Air Corps. Its wooden buildings were a fire hazard, and after several fires in the early 1930s the Air Corps named Chanute one of four bases to be rebuilt. The "Great Renaissance" that followed brought massive hangars, a hospital and a 300,000-gallon water tower, most of the $13.8 million cost paid by the Works Progress Administration. The work was finished in 1941, just months before Pearl Harbor.

## Tents, Jet Engines and Missile Silos

Pearl Harbor turned the school into a crush. Recruits poured in, the new quarters built for 15,000 men proved too small, and many soldiers lived for a time in large tents; in January 1943 the student load peaked at 25,000. When the Air Force became a separate service in 1948, Chanute Field became Chanute Air Force Base, and its classrooms followed the technology: by mid-1948 almost half the students were in a new course for jet-propulsion airplane and engine mechanics. In the 1960s the base was the prime training center for the LGM-30 Minuteman intercontinental ballistic missile, with training silos for maintenance crews housed in a hangar on the flight line. Air Force firefighters and welders trained here too, alongside weather specialists from the Air Force, Navy and Marine Corps. Once the training mission no longer needed aircraft support, the Air Force closed the last active runway, in 1971.

## Tractors, Motocross and Contamination

Closure had been discussed many times before; this time it stuck. On 29 December 1988 the Department of Defense recommended its closure as part of that year's Base Realignment and Closure Commission, and the base closed on 30 September 1993. Some of what remained found new purposes. The airfield is now Rantoul National Aviation Center / Frank Elliott Field, named for Major General Frank Worth Elliott Jr., a former Chanute Technical Center commander who later advised the village on economic development. B-52 Hangar MX runs an indoor motocross track inside one of the big hangars, and in odd-numbered years the grounds host the Half Century of Progress, described as the largest working antique tractor show in the world. Other parts fared worse. For years many buildings stood empty and deteriorating, White Hall was demolished in 2015 and 2016, and asbestos and toxic chemical dumps forced the condemnation of parts of the base. The Air Force says it has spent more than $200 million on cleanup, with about $76 million still to go at the time of the 2023 handover; the EPA proposed the site for the Superfund National Priorities List in 2000 and withdrew that proposal in 2021. In January 2026 the village reported that, for the first time since closure, every remaining structure was leased or loaned, with former barracks slated for demolition that year. Part of it is listed on the National Register of Historic Places as the Chanute Field Historic District. On 25 October 2023 the Air Force handed its final buildings and land to the Village of Rantoul, at a ceremony celebrating 106 years of partnership between the base and the town.

## Flight Context

The former base lies at about 40.29°N, 88.14°W, directly south of and adjoining Rantoul in Champaign County, Illinois, roughly 100 nautical miles south-southwest of Chicago. Its airfield is still active as Rantoul National Aviation Center / Frank Elliott Field (KTIP), an uncontrolled general aviation airport at 737 feet elevation with two asphalt runways, 9/27 (5,001 ft) and 18/36 (4,894 ft). From 2,000-3,000 feet the cluster of large hangars and former base buildings stands out clearly on the flat surrounding terrain, with the village of Rantoul immediately north. University of Illinois Willard Airport (KCMI) lies about 16 nautical miles south-southwest. There is no high terrain nearby; haze and passing weather, not obstacles, are the usual limits on visibility.
