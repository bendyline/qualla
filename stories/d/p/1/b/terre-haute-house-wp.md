# Terre Haute House

From Chauncey Rose's Prairie House to a ten-story 1928 landmark of high rollers and rumors, a hotel held the corner of Seventh and Wabash until thirty-five years of failed rescues ended with a one-dollar sale and a wrecking ball.

## Introduction

When the demolition crews arrived in the autumn of 2005, the keys to every room of the Terre Haute House were still in the basement, along with the china, the pots and pans, an antique drill press and other tools. The hotel at the northeast corner of Seventh Street and Wabash Avenue had been closed to guests for 35 years, and in that time one plan after another to bring it back had come to nothing. Many people wanted this landmark saved. Nobody managed to save it.

## A House in the Prairie

It began with Chauncey Rose, who in 1831 bought 320 acres on both sides of what is now Wabash Avenue. In the late 1830s he built a hotel there and called it the Prairie House, because it stood "in the prairie" several blocks east of the village. When federal money for the National Road dried up in 1840 and the Army Corps of Engineers that supervised the highway left town, the hotel closed and seems to have served for a time as a boarding house. With the Wabash and Erie Canal on its way, Rose reopened in 1849, renamed the place the Terre Haute House in 1855 and added a fourth floor of guest rooms. By 1866 he was tired of the business and thought of giving the property away for educational purposes, an idea that sat so badly with local businessmen that they formed the Terre Haute Hotel Company and bought it instead. Rose Polytechnic Institute, today Rose-Hulman Institute of Technology, was named in his honor.

## Ten Stories at Seventh and Wabash

The hotel passed through a string of owners, among them the brothers Charles and Jacob Baur, before Crawford Fairbanks bought it in the early 1900s and promised to replace it with something far grander. He died in 1924 with the promise unkept, and his heirs saw it through. On July 2, 1928, the new Terre Haute House opened: ten stories of Renaissance Revival on the corner where Wabash Avenue carried U.S. Highway 40. Into the 1950s it was a high-class address in a city whose illegal gambling and other businesses of ill repute drew high rollers. Legends followed. Al Capone is rumored to have stayed here, though the story seems to trace back to a biography of Benjamin "Scatman" Crothers and cannot be proven. It has also been said that John Dillinger stayed at the hotel, though some dispute it.

## Last Call in the Prairie Room

By the time Tony Hulman bought the hotel in 1959, along with the Terre Haute House Garage and the Terre Haute Opera House, that era of vice was on its way out. Hulman, the Terre Haute businessman who owned the Indianapolis Motor Speedway, wanted to breathe new life into the old hotel. Terre Haute, though, was moving away from downtown. Construction of Interstate 70 along the city's south side drew stores and businesses away, and newer hotels and motels rose along U.S. 41, including a large Holiday Inn that opened in 1962. According to Richard Van Allen, who managed the hotel, Hulman lost a great deal of money keeping it open, and toward the end he sold food at cost and let civic groups meet free, just to bring people through the doors. On June 18, 1970, Van Allen gathered the staff in the Prairie Room and told them the hotel would close within two weeks. It shut to guests on July 4, exactly 42 years and two days after it opened.

## Thirty-Five Years of Almost

Several tenants stayed on, evidently hoping for a reopening; the last, World Wide Travel Service, moved a block south to Ohio Street in the fall of 1980. In March 1984 Mayor P. Pete Chalos threatened to condemn the property to force the Hulman family to sell. The threat failed, though it did seem to spur developers' interest. The most serious bid came in the mid-1990s, when Texas developer Don Daseke proposed reopening the building as a Radisson convention hotel. He signed a purchase agreement in 1995, but bank underwriting terms he later called "onerous" stood in the way, and in January 1997 he withdrew. That year Tony George, Hulman's grandson, called the building architecturally and historically significant, then added: "If no one develops it, we'll probably demolish it." Kevin Burke, who campaigned on tearing the hotel down, called it "an impediment to downtown revitalization" in 2003, and in May 2005 a city committee announced that none of four rehabilitation plans met the city's financial criteria.

## Sold for a Dollar

That September, Seventh & Wabash, LLC, owned by local businessman Greg Gibson, agreed to buy the hotel and two neighboring buildings on its block. The price was $1, and filings with the Vigo County auditor showed that the Hulmans paid Gibson a "development fee" to take the property. "Clearly the property sold has a negative value," said family spokesman Fred Nation, "and the sale terms reflect that." Implosion was considered, but owners and tenants nearby feared a blast could damage their own buildings, and in the end a crane swung a 6,000-pound wrecking ball. The last of the façade, the lower floors of the southwest corner, fell on December 28, 2005. Half of the Marine Room's copper-clad bar, the Mayflower Room's mural and two grand pianos were saved; walnut paneling, marble and hundreds of varnished doors went into the rubble. A Hilton Garden Inn opened on the site in 2007; plans announced in 2005 called for the old stone pediment reading "TERRE HAUTE HOUSE" to be reused over its entrance.

## Flight Context

The Terre Haute House stood at the northeast corner of Seventh Street and Wabash Avenue (U.S. 40) in downtown Terre Haute, Indiana, at about 39.4667°N, 87.4067°W; a Hilton Garden Inn now occupies the site. The Wabash River forms the city's western border and is the easiest landmark to find. From about 1,500 to 2,500 feet AGL, look for the downtown grid a short way east of the river, where Wabash Avenue crosses Seventh Street. Interstate 70 passes about 5 miles south of Wabash Avenue. Terre Haute Regional Airport (KHUF), still also known as Hulman Field, lies about 5 nautical miles east of the site; ground was broken for it in 1943 on land donated to the city by Tony Hulman, the same man who bought and later closed the hotel.
