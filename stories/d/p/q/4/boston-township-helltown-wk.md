# Boston Township ('Helltown')

An Ohio village emptied by the federal government, abandoned for forty years, and rebranded by teenagers as a haunted place - a story of real eminent domain hiding under invented satanic legend.

**Location:** 41.2650, -81.5660

**Categories:** Cuyahoga Valley National Park, Ghost towns in Ohio, Townships in Summit County, Ohio, Urban legends, National Park Service, Eminent domain, Krejci Dump

## Introduction

The story teenagers tell themselves in the parking lot of Boston Mills involves a school bus full of children, a satanic church, a mutant python, and a stretch of road locals call the Highway to Hell. The story their grandparents tell is shorter and grimmer. In 1974, the federal government drew a boundary across northeast Ohio for a new national park. Inside that boundary stood a Cuyahoga River village called Boston - settled 1806, post office since 1815, canal port since 1827 - and most of the families who lived there were not asked whether they would like to stay. Houses were bought. Then houses were emptied. Then, for forty years, the houses stood. The legends grew in the gap between what happened and what the next generation could see with their own eyes.

## A Real Town, Not a Stage Set

Before any of this, Boston was simply a place that worked. Founded by New England families in 1806 at a wide bend of the Cuyahoga River between Akron and Cleveland, it became, in the 1820s, a port on the Ohio and Erie Canal. There were paper mills upstream, locks and lock-tenders, a general store, a Methodist church, and a small Roman Catholic parish called Mother of Sorrows that put up a stone building in 1880. Families like the Stanfords, the McKissons and, later, the Krejcis worked the river bottom for five and six generations. The canal eventually failed, the railroad came and went, the mills closed, and the population thinned through the twentieth century - but Boston did not die. It just got quieter. As late as 1970, you could still buy a sandwich at the village store and walk home down Boston Mills Road without seeing a stranger.

## The 1974 Map

On December 27, 1974, President Gerald Ford signed legislation creating the Cuyahoga Valley National Recreation Area, eventually thirty-three thousand acres set aside to protect the river and the open land between two of the Rust Belt's largest cities. The National Park Service began acquiring property along the river the next year. Some sellers were willing; many were not. Eminent domain - the federal power to force a sale at market value - filled in the gaps. The Krejci family's salvage business was bought out. The Stanfords lost the farmland they had worked since the canal era. Mother of Sorrows held on for a while; most of its parishioners did not. The Park Service later acknowledged that some of the takings had been more aggressive than necessary, that families with no intention to develop their land had nonetheless been pushed out, and that the agency had not had a workable plan for what to do with the buildings it now owned. So the houses stayed where they were. Empty.

## How a Town Becomes a Legend

Empty houses in the woods, lined up along quiet roads, with the windows boarded and the lawns going to milkweed - this is the raw material of every American ghost story. In the 1980s and 90s, teenagers from Akron and Cleveland began driving out to Boston Mills Road at night, daring each other across the porches of houses whose owners they did not know had ever existed. Each strange thing they saw acquired an explanation. Stanford Road, which dead-ends near the river and has a steep blind dip in the middle, became the Highway to Hell - the dip 'where cars stall and won't restart.' Mother of Sorrows Church, whose architecture happens to feature an inverted cross on its steeple (a traditional Catholic symbol of the crucifixion of Saint Peter), became proof of a satanic cult. A National Park Service hazardous-waste cleanup at the old Krejci salvage yard, with its yellow tape and protective suits, became 'the boa pit' - a federal cover-up of mutant snakes escaped from a chemical spill. An abandoned school bus near a barn became the bus where the children died. None of this happened. All of it spread.

## What the Cleanup Was Actually About

The boa pit was, in fact, the Krejci Dump. From 1948 to 1980, John Krejci's salvage yard had accepted drums of industrial waste from Ford, General Motors, Chrysler, 3M, Chevron and others - some legally, much of it not. When the Park Service acquired the property in 1985, it inherited an unregulated chemical dump bad enough to qualify as a Superfund site. The cleanup ran from 2000 until March of 2021. Workers in moonsuits hauled out drums by the thousand and excavated forty-seven acres of poisoned soil down to the groundwater. The corporate polluters settled for tens of millions of dollars. The mutant snake was always a story, but the dump it covered was real, and it was the kind of real that costs a generation and a fortune to fix.

## What Remains

Most of the abandoned houses are gone now. The Park Service's long-delayed demolition program finally cleared the last of them in 2016. The school bus was removed at the same time. A handful of buildings are still standing - the Boston Mill Visitor Center occupies one - but the rest of the village has gone back to meadow and second-growth woods. The Cuyahoga River runs cleaner here than it has in a century. The towpath of the old canal is a popular trail. Mother of Sorrows still holds Mass on Sundays for a small parish in nearby Peninsula. And the families - the Stanfords, the McKissons, the Krejcis - mostly live elsewhere now, some still bitter, some philosophical about it, all aware that their grandparents' homes are part of an urban legend told by people who never met them. Drive Stanford Road today at noon and the only thing you will hear is wind in the sugar maples. The horror story was always cover for an older, quieter American sadness about who gets to keep their land and who does not.

## Flight Context

Boston Township lies along the Cuyahoga River at roughly 41.265 degrees N, 81.566 degrees W, inside Cuyahoga Valley National Park between Akron and Cleveland in northeast Ohio. From 4,000 feet AGL the valley reads as a continuous green corridor cutting through the otherwise dense suburban grid of the Rust Belt; the Boston Mill Visitor Center sits at the river crossing, the Ohio Turnpike (I-80) cuts overhead north of town. The closest controlled field is Akron-Canton (KCAK), eleven nautical miles southeast; Cleveland-Hopkins (KCLE) sits twenty nautical miles northwest. Bravo airspace stacks over Cleveland; Class C surrounds Akron-Canton. Lake-effect cloud bases can drop quickly off the lake in winter - check Cleveland TAFs even when flying over inland Ohio.
