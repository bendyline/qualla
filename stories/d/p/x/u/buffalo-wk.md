# Buffalo: The Queen City That Rose and Fell and Rises Again

The city that was America's eighth-largest in 1900, where Frank Lloyd Wright built houses, where chicken wings were invented in 1964, and where abandonment is slowly yielding to rebirth.

**Location:** 42.8864, -78.8784

**Categories:** new-york, buffalo, city, architecture, rust-belt

## Introduction

In 1900, Buffalo was the eighth-largest city in America - an industrial powerhouse at the western terminus of the Erie Canal, the nation's greatest grain-milling center, home to more millionaires per capita than any American city. Pan-American Exposition visitors marveled at electric lights; McKinley was assassinated at the fairgrounds. A century later, Buffalo had lost over half its population, its steel mills closed, its grain elevators abandoned, its neighborhoods emptying block by block. The decline was spectacular even by Rust Belt standards. But Buffalo possessed something many declining cities lacked: extraordinary architecture that poverty preserved. Frank Lloyd Wright's houses, Louis Sullivan's buildings, Frederick Law Olmsted's parks - Buffalo couldn't afford to demolish its heritage. Now that heritage is driving rebirth.

## The Erie Canal's End

When the Erie Canal opened in 1825, it transformed Buffalo from a frontier village into a major port. Goods flowing from the Great Lakes to New York City passed through Buffalo; the city became America's gateway to the West. Grain elevators - Buffalo invented the modern grain elevator in 1842 - lined the harbor, transferring wheat from lake boats to canal boats. By 1900, Buffalo milled more grain than any city in the world. The St. Lawrence Seaway, opened in 1959, bypassed Buffalo entirely, ending its role as a transfer point. The elevators stand empty now, monumental concrete ruins that architects and urban explorers find fascinating and that ordinary Buffalonians regard with complicated feelings.

## Frank Lloyd Wright's Laboratory

Frank Lloyd Wright built five houses in Buffalo between 1903 and 1906, the largest concentration outside Chicago. The Darwin D. Martin House Complex, his most ambitious Prairie-style work, sprawls across over 29,000 square feet - main house, pergola, conservatory, carriage house, gardener's cottage. Wright called it his 'opus.' Neglected for decades, the Martin House has been meticulously restored and offers tours. The Graycliff estate, built for Darwin Martin's summer retreat overlooking Lake Erie, and three other Wright houses survive. Buffalo became Wright's laboratory for Prairie house ideas; the city's wealthy industrialists gave him freedom to experiment. That these buildings survived Buffalo's decline is remarkable; that they're now destinations is revival.

## The Buffalo Wing

Teressa Bellissimo invented Buffalo wings at the Anchor Bar on October 30, 1964 - or so the story goes. Her son and his friends arrived late and hungry; she fried up chicken wings (usually discarded or used for stock), tossed them in hot sauce and butter, and served them with celery and blue cheese dressing. The combination became iconic. The Anchor Bar still serves them; competitors like Duff's Famous Wings argue they're better. Buffalo wings spread across America, becoming a sports bar staple and Super Bowl tradition, though Buffalonians maintain that anything called a 'Buffalo wing' outside Buffalo is inferior. The city takes its invention seriously; the National Buffalo Wing Festival draws 50,000 annually.

## Olmsted's Parks

Frederick Law Olmsted, who designed Central Park, created Buffalo's park system in the 1870s - the first planned municipal park system in America. The Olmsted Parks include Delaware Park (the main landscape park), Front Park (designed for Niagara River views), and South Park (with its conservatory). A connected parkway system links them, creating green corridors throughout the city. Like much of Buffalo, the parks declined and are now being restored. The Buffalo Olmsted Parks Conservancy has raised millions for rehabilitation. Olmsted designed Buffalo as a city within parks; seeing his vision renewed is part of Buffalo's broader renaissance.

## The Queen City

Buffalo Niagara International Airport (BUF) lies east of downtown. Niagara Falls is 20 miles north, an easy day trip. Downtown Buffalo contains City Hall (an Art Deco tower with a stunning observation deck), the Guaranty Building (Louis Sullivan's masterpiece), and the theater district anchored by Shea's Buffalo Theatre. Canalside, on the former Erie Canal terminus, has been redeveloped with ice skating, concerts, and waterfront access. The Elmwood Village and Allentown neighborhoods offer walkable streets and independent shops. From altitude, Buffalo appears as urban development where the Niagara River leaves Lake Erie, the grain elevators visible along the harbor - the Queen City that rose and fell and, improbably, rises again.
