# Michigan Central Station

For thirty years the gutted shell of Detroit's grandest train station stared out over Roosevelt Park as the city's most photographed ruin - then Ford spent nearly a billion dollars putting it back together.

**Location:** 42.3287, -83.0775

**Categories:** architecture, transportation-history, detroit, restoration, beaux-arts

## Introduction

For most of three decades, the eighteen-story tower stared out over Roosevelt Park with nearly every one of its windows punched out. Trees took root in the abandoned building. Graffiti climbed the walls where announcers once called trains for Chicago and New York. Photographers came from around the world to shoot it, and every shot said the same thing about Detroit: this is what collapse looks like. The strange part is that the building was never knocked down. It just waited.

## Built to Last a Century

When Michigan Central Station opened in 1913, the firms that had just finished Grand Central Terminal in New York - Warren and Wetmore, with Reed and Stem - drew its plans. The result was a Beaux-Arts station hall vaulted with Guastavino tile, faced in marble and bronze, and topped by an eighteen-story office tower that made it briefly the tallest train station in the world. By the First World War more than two hundred trains a day departed, and in the early 1940s more than four thousand travelers passed through daily. So many newcomers arrived through these doors that the station was sometimes called Detroit's Ellis Island. Presidents Hoover, Truman and Roosevelt all passed through the Grand Hall. The station was built for an American century that had not quite arrived yet, and for a Detroit that assumed it would never stop growing.

## The Last Train

The decline took longer than the rise. Cars replaced trains, then the freeway system bypassed the station's neighborhood, then passenger service dwindled to a few Amtrak trains. On January 6, 1988, the last train pulled away. The doors closed. The building's owners walked. What followed was a thirty-year argument about what to do with one of the largest abandoned buildings in North America. In 2009 the city council even voted to demolish it, but a preservation lawsuit followed and the wrecking ball never came. Scrappers stripped the copper and brass. Water came through the roof. Pigeons moved in. Hollywood discovered it - the place ended up in films about war zones and apocalypses, because no set designer needed to add anything. The hollow station became the visual shorthand for what had happened to American cities.

## Ruin Porn and the Photographs

In 2010, photographers Yves Marchand and Romain Meffre published a book called The Ruins of Detroit. Their photograph of Michigan Central's facade was on the cover. The genre that followed got a nickname - ruin porn - and the station was its cathedral. Detroiters had complicated feelings about that. The pictures were genuinely beautiful, light streaming through broken windows onto cracked marble, but they framed a living city as a corpse. A few thousand people still lived in the surrounding Corktown neighborhood. They were not aesthetic. They were trying to get the city to do something about the building before something terrible happened in it.

## Ford Buys a Ruin

In June 2018, Ford Motor Company announced it had bought the station. The price was undisclosed but reported around ninety million dollars. The plan was audacious: spend close to a billion dollars restoring the building and the thirty acres around it into a mobility innovation campus - autonomous vehicle research, startups, a hotel, retail, the works. The restoration itself ran six years and consumed roughly 1.7 million labor hours. Crews restored or replicated 8 million bricks. Twenty-three thousand square feet of marble flooring was salvaged or matched. Workers restored 29,000 original Guastavino tiles in the Grand Hall ceiling, recreating those that could not be saved using the same century-old technique. The result is not a museum. It is a working building again.

## The Concert

On the night of June 6, 2024, twenty thousand people filled Roosevelt Park in front of the station. The lineup was Detroit royalty: Diana Ross, Big Sean, Jack White, Eminem - the last appearing in a surprise set backed by the Detroit Symphony Orchestra and giving his new single Houdini its first live performance. The station behind them was floodlit, open again thirty-six years after the last train left. The symbolism was almost too on the nose, and nobody cared. Detroit had been told a story about itself for a long time - the decline, the bankruptcy, the ruin photographs - and the city was tired of the story. The reopening was the first chapter of a different one. The building still has the tower visible from miles across the freeway, still anchors the skyline at the western edge of downtown, but the windows are no longer empty.

## Flight Context

Michigan Central Station sits at 42.3287 N, 83.0775 W, on the western edge of downtown Detroit in the Corktown neighborhood. From the air the eighteen-story tower is unmistakable, set back from the Detroit River and aligned with Roosevelt Park out front. Closest major airport is Detroit Metropolitan Wayne County (KDTW), about 16 miles west-southwest. Coleman A. Young Municipal (KDET) sits about 6.5 miles north-northeast. Recommended viewing altitude 2,500-3,500 feet AGL; the building's roofline is the high point of the immediate skyline, with the Ambassador Bridge a little over a mile to the south. Best light is late afternoon, when the stone goes warm and the tower throws a long shadow east across Corktown.
