# Indianapolis: The Racing Capital of the World

Indiana's capital where the Indianapolis 500 has drawn hundreds of thousands since 1911, where more interstate highways converge than anywhere in America, where the NCAA makes its home, and where a planned city on the White River became the Crossroads of America.

**Location:** 39.7684, -86.1581

## Introduction

On Memorial Day weekend 1911, Ray Harroun won the first Indianapolis 500, averaging 74.6 miles per hour around the 2.5-mile brick oval that gave the Indianapolis Motor Speedway its 'Brickyard' nickname. The race has run every year since except during the World Wars, becoming America's largest single-day sporting event - 300,000 spectators filling grandstands and infields to watch cars that now exceed 230 mph blast past a remaining yard of exposed bricks at the start-finish line. Indianapolis exists because Indiana needed a central capital; legislators chose a spot on the White River and laid out a city in 1821. The location proved prophetic: more interstate highways converge here than any other American city, earning Indianapolis its 'Crossroads of America' motto. The city leveraged that centrality into sports tourism - hosting the NCAA, building Lucas Oil Stadium, attracting the Colts from Baltimore. But everything comes back to the Speedway, the 500, and one day each May when the whole city stops to watch.

## The Indianapolis Motor Speedway

The Speedway complex covers 559 acres on the city's west side - the oval itself could hold Vatican City, the Roman Colosseum, and Churchill Downs simultaneously. Carl Fisher built it in 1909 as a testing ground for the infant automobile industry; the original surface of crushed stone and tar proved deadly, so workers laid 3.2 million paving bricks in 63 days. Most bricks are now covered with asphalt, but the yard of original bricks at the start-finish line remains - drivers kiss them after winning. The Indianapolis Motor Speedway Museum displays winning cars from across the decades; the Hall of Fame enshrines racing legends. Track tours take visitors around the oval itself, feeling the banking, imagining the speed. The Month of May transforms the city: qualifying, practice, the parade, and finally the race itself, 200 laps and 500 miles of speed and strategy.

## Sports Capital

Indianapolis built its modern identity around sports. The NCAA moved its headquarters here in 1999; the NCAA Hall of Champions celebrates college athletics in White River State Park. Lucas Oil Stadium hosts the Colts; Gainbridge Fieldhouse (formerly Conseco) hosts the Pacers. The city hosts more major amateur sporting events than anywhere else in America - the Olympic swimming trials, the Big Ten tournaments, the Men's and Women's Final Four. Victory Field, home of the Triple-A Indians, is consistently rated among baseball's finest minor league parks. The Indiana Sports Corporation, a nonprofit, recruits events that generate hundreds of millions in economic impact. Indianapolis made a strategic bet that sports tourism could replace lost manufacturing jobs; the bet paid off.

## Monument Circle

The Soldiers and Sailors Monument rises 284 feet from the center of Monument Circle, the literal and symbolic heart of Indianapolis. The limestone memorial, completed in 1902, honors Indiana's Civil War veterans; an observation deck offers downtown views. The circle itself functions as the city's central plaza - office workers eating lunch, visitors photographing the monument, Christmas lights in December. The neighborhoods radiate outward: Mass Ave (Massachusetts Avenue) with its galleries and restaurants in restored buildings, Fountain Square's hipster bars and vintage shops, Broad Ripple's college-town atmosphere along the canal. The Cultural Trail connects these districts with 8 miles of protected bike and pedestrian paths, making Indianapolis unexpectedly walkable for a Midwestern city planned around the automobile.

## White River and Beyond

White River State Park spreads along the downtown waterfront, a 250-acre urban park holding the Indianapolis Zoo (one of few in America accredited by both zoo and aquarium associations), the Eiteljorg Museum of American Indians and Western Art, the Indiana State Museum, and the NCAA Hall of Champions. The Central Canal, part of a failed 19th-century transportation project, survives as a pedestrian waterway through the park. The Indianapolis Museum of Art at Newfields, north of downtown, holds significant collections in a 152-acre campus that includes the Lilly House, a 1930s estate, and outdoor gardens. The Children's Museum of Indianapolis is the world's largest, five floors of interactive exhibits drawing families from across the Midwest.

## Crossroads Position

Indianapolis International Airport (IND) offers major airline service; the city's central location makes it hub for FedEx's second-largest sorting facility. I-65, I-69, I-70, and I-74 converge here - more interstate miles meeting than any other American city. The geography is flat, the skyline modest, the scale manageable. Indianapolis spread into suburban counties that consolidated with the city in 1970, creating the 'Unigov' merged government that covers most of Marion County. From altitude, Indianapolis appears as the hub of a wheel, interstate spokes radiating to Chicago, Cincinnati, St. Louis, Louisville. The Speedway's distinctive oval is visible northwest of downtown. What appears from the air as a planned Midwestern capital is the Racing Capital of the World, the Crossroads of America, and a city that transformed itself through sports into something more than geography would suggest.
