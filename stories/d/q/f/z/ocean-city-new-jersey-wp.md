# Ocean City, New Jersey

A barrier-island city founded by Methodist ministers as a Christian seaside retreat, where alcohol has been prohibited since 1879 and the boardwalk runs 2.5 miles.

**Location:** 39.2653, -74.5936

**Categories:** seaside resorts, dry towns, methodist history, new jersey, boardwalks

## Introduction

In 1879, four Methodist ministers stood on a deserted barrier island just south of Atlantic City and decided to build a religious resort. Their names were Ezra B. Lake, James E. Lake, S. Wesley Lake, and William H. Burrell - the three Lake brothers and a fellow Methodist minister. They wanted a place free from what they considered the moral hazards of the boardwalk culture down the coast - particularly alcohol. The Lakes founded the Ocean City Association and wrote alcohol prohibition into the original property deeds. The city was incorporated in 1884. The ministers' choice has held. Ocean City remains the largest dry municipality in the country - no liquor stores, no bars, no restaurant wine lists, no alcohol sold within the city limits, all 147 years later.

## The Methodist Plan

The Lake brothers were not amateurs at building Methodist communities. The family had already been involved in church camp meetings across the Mid-Atlantic, and they understood the model: buy land, lay out lots, build a tabernacle, sell deeds with religious restrictions, attract like-minded buyers. Ocean City was an industrial-scale version of the camp-meeting town. The Ocean City Tabernacle, founded with the city itself, still stands at 6th and Wesley Avenues. The streets were named after Methodist leaders - Wesley, Asbury, Simpson, Haven. The Sabbath was strictly observed. The early city was, by most accounts, peaceful, prosperous, and entirely lacking in the kinds of trouble that the Methodist founders had wanted to avoid. The dry-town policy survived every legal challenge for the next century. A 2011 push to allow BYOB at restaurants drew enough public opposition that the city council refused to schedule the vote.

## Eight Miles of Sand

Ocean City occupies most of an eight-mile-long barrier island between Great Egg Harbor Inlet to the north and Corson's Inlet to the south. The southern boundary is Corson's Inlet State Park, established in 1969 to preserve one of the last truly undeveloped stretches of the New Jersey coast. The northern boundary - 4th Street and the inlet - faces Atlantic City directly across the water. From the boardwalk on a clear evening you can watch the casino lights blink on while the Ocean City beach stays dark. The contrast is intentional and ancient: Atlantic City sold what it sold, and Ocean City sold the opposite. The beach is divided into 56 separately named lifeguarded sections by street number. Beach tags, required during the season, are checked daily by patrols who walk the sand.

## The Boardwalk Pizza Wars

Mack's Pizza, founded in 1953, and Manco and Manco Pizza, founded in 1956 (originally Manco's), have been Ocean City's two competing pizza institutions for more than seven decades. Both make New York-style slices in plain view of the customer - the dough being stretched, the sauce being ladled, the cheese being scattered, the pie sliding into the oven. The two parlors have spent generations attracting partisan loyalty from local families, with the local question "Mack's or Manco's?" carrying roughly the political weight of a primary endorsement. Both still operate on the boardwalk today. Their pies are recognizably similar and recognizably different. Visitors choose a side or hedge by buying from both. The argument has outlasted most of the founders themselves.

## Storms and Sand

The barrier island that Ocean City sits on has been reshaped repeatedly by storms over the past century. The Ash Wednesday Storm of March 1962 hit Ocean City as hard as any of the southern Cape May barrier islands - the boardwalk was damaged, the dunes were stripped, and many beachfront properties were destroyed. Hurricane Gloria in 1985 came back through and gusted at over 100 mph in nearby Atlantic City. Hurricane Sandy in 2012 brought tide surges that overwhelmed sections of the boardwalk and flooded the back bays. Each storm has been followed by aggressive beach replenishment - sand pumped from offshore and pumped onto the shore in volumes that rebuild the protective dune line. The boardwalk itself has been rebuilt, in sections, more times than the city advertises. The wood is replaced before it fails. The amusements stay open.

## The Year-Rounders

Roughly 11,000 people live in Ocean City year-round. The summer population pushes past 100,000. The off-season is, in some ways, the city's quieter true self - boardwalk shops closed, beaches mostly empty, the Methodist tabernacle running its winter program, the year-round restaurants serving locals. The downtown along Asbury Avenue between 6th and 14th Streets has been deliberately preserved as a family-business district. Hospitality Night each December kicks off the holiday shopping season. The Doo-Dah Parade in April honors basset hounds and Comedians of the Year, all of which is exactly as strange as it sounds. The Ocean City Pops orchestra plays summer concerts at the Music Pier. The Miss New Jersey pageant has been held on the boardwalk since the 1950s. The town is genuinely a year-round town, and the dry-town policy has done nothing to prevent that.

## Flight Context

Ocean City sits at approximately 39.27 degrees north, 74.59 degrees west on a barrier island between Great Egg Harbor Inlet (north) and Corson's Inlet (south). From cruising altitude, the city occupies the central portion of an eight-mile-long barrier island just south of Atlantic City. Atlantic City International (KACY) sits about ten nautical miles northwest. Ocean City Municipal (26N) is a small general aviation field on the mainland west of the city. The three bridges connecting Ocean City to the mainland are visible from cruise: 34th Street, 9th Street, and the northern bridge to Somers Point. The boardwalk shows as a darker strip along the Atlantic shore.
