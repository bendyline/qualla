# Atlantic City Boardwalk

The world's first boardwalk, built in 1870 as a temporary summer fix for hotel owners tired of sand on their carpets, and still the longest and busiest boardwalk in the world.

**Location:** 39.3546, -74.4358

**Categories:** boardwalks, atlantic city, new jersey, seaside resorts, landmarks

## Introduction

The Atlantic City Boardwalk was originally a complaint about housekeeping. In 1870, the hotel owners and railroad operators of the new resort town were tired of tracking beach sand into their lobbies, their parlor cars, and their guest rooms. A railroad conductor named Alexander Boardman and a hotelier named Jacob Keim proposed a temporary wooden walkway along the beachfront, to be put up each summer and dismantled each fall. The first boardwalk in the world opened on June 26, 1870. It was supposed to be a small practical fix. It became, instead, the model for every American boardwalk that followed, and over the next century-and-a-half it grew into the longest and busiest boardwalk on Earth.

## Eight Generations of Wood

The original 1870 boardwalk was rebuilt eight times. The first version was a foot-and-a-half off the sand on portable supports - it lasted one season, came up in fall, went back down in spring. It was succeeded by progressively more permanent structures: a two-foot-high walkway in 1880, a four-foot version in 1890, and the still-iconic concrete-and-steel substructure with wood planking that was first installed around the turn of the twentieth century and has been maintained ever since. The current boardwalk is roughly four miles long, running from Absecon Inlet at the north end of Atlantic City to the southern city limit, where it continues into Ventnor City. The width varies. In some sections it is sixty feet across - wide enough for rolling-chair operators, pedestrians, joggers, surreys, and bicycles to move in mostly separate lanes.

## The Piers

Atlantic City's piers were the boardwalk's first big draw. Applegate's Pier opened in 1884, the first commercial pier on the boardwalk. Steel Pier opened in 1898 and would eventually bill itself as The Showplace of the Nation - the home of the famous diving horse, of the big-band radio broadcasts, of the marathon performances by Frank Sinatra and Al Jolson and the Beatles. Young's Million Dollar Pier opened in 1906. Heinz Pier - sponsored by the H.J. Heinz Company and used as a giant ketchup advertisement - opened in 1898. Steeplechase Pier opened in 1899. At one point Atlantic City had at least six major piers extending into the Atlantic, each with its own attractions, its own neon, its own crowds. Most are gone now. The 1944 Great Atlantic hurricane destroyed Heinz Pier. Fires have destroyed others, in 1912, 1944, 1949, 1982, and 1988. Steel Pier survives. Central Pier, rebuilt from the remnants of Young's Ocean Pier after a 1912 fire, also survives.

## Edison's Garden

Captain John L. Young opened the Million Dollar Pier in 1906 and built himself a mansion at the seaward end called No. 1 Atlantic Ocean. The mansion was real - a marble building fronted by a formal garden. The garden's lighting and landscaping were designed by Thomas Alva Edison, who was a longtime personal friend of Young's. Edison provided the electrical illumination that made the pier glow at night, when seen from the boardwalk or from passing ships - a small spectacle of artificial daylight reaching out into the Atlantic. The mansion was demolished in 1953. The pier has been five different things since. The Edison illumination is gone. But the marketing language of Atlantic City - which has always been about scale, about claim, about the implausible promise - still bears traces of what Young and Edison were trying to do.

## Sandy and South Inlet

Hurricane Sandy made landfall just south of Atlantic City on October 29, 2012. The headlines reported that the Atlantic City boardwalk was destroyed. The reality was more complicated. The main casino-front boardwalk, the section most often photographed and shown on television, survived the storm with relatively minor damage. The northern section of the boardwalk, fronting Absecon Inlet in the residential neighborhood called South Inlet, was destroyed - but that section had already been slated for demolition and replacement before the storm, and the boards that came up in the news photographs were the ones the city had been planning to remove anyway. The misreporting became a small political issue. The casino industry resented being painted as ruined when its core infrastructure had held. The boardwalk's actual vulnerabilities became, briefly, a national policy conversation about coastal infrastructure.

## Rolling Chairs and Lights

The rolling chair - a wicker-and-canvas push-chair piloted by a uniformed attendant - is the boardwalk's signature mode of transportation. It dates from 1887, when M.D. Shill began renting them to wealthy invalids. By the 1920s they were a luxury accessory, then a tourist novelty, then a working-class job, then a tourist novelty again. They still run. The boardwalk's other signature - the neon - went up in the 1920s and grew through the 1930s and 1940s into a continuous wall of light. Most of the original neon is gone, replaced by LED displays at the casinos. The boardwalk itself is still wood. The planks are still nailed to a heavy concrete-and-steel substructure that resists storms and tides. The Atlantic, four feet below your feet at high tide, has been trying to take this thing away for 155 years. The boardwalk is still there.

## Flight Context

The Atlantic City Boardwalk runs along the Atlantic Ocean shore of Absecon Island from Absecon Inlet (north) to the Ventnor City line (south), at approximately 39.35 degrees north, 74.44 degrees west. From cruising altitude, the boardwalk shows as a darker strip along the Atlantic edge of Atlantic City's casino district. The major piers - Steel Pier (north) and ACX1 Studios pier (south) - extend visibly into the water. Atlantic City International (KACY) lies about 7 nautical miles northwest. The Absecon Lighthouse at the north end and the Tropicana at the south end mark the boardwalk's ends.
