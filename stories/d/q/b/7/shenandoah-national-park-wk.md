# Shenandoah National Park

A 105-mile ridge of billion-year-old rock turned national park, where Skyline Drive runs the spine and over 450 families were displaced to make the wilderness America wanted.

**Location:** 38.5333, -78.3500

**Categories:** National Parks of the United States, Blue Ridge Mountains, Appalachian Trail, Virginia

## Introduction

Skyline Drive runs 105 miles along the crest of the Blue Ridge, milepost 0 at Front Royal to milepost 105 at Rockfish Gap, posted at 35 miles per hour the whole way. The drive opened in pieces during the 1930s while President Herbert Hoover ran his summer White House on the Rapidan River and Civilian Conservation Corps crews built the trails and the overlooks. The road and the park beneath it required something less photogenic to come first: more than 450 families had to be pushed off the mountain. They did not want to go.

## Deep Time on the Ridge

The oldest rocks in the Blue Ridge formed over a billion years ago, when magma deep in the Earth's crust pushed upward and slowly cooled into granite. Younger metamorphic layers stacked on top, then everything was crumpled by mountain-building forces and ground down again by glaciers, water, and time. What remains is one of the oldest mountain ranges on Earth - hills with the deep-shoulder geometry of weariness. The park stretches about 200,000 acres along the central Appalachians, rising above the Virginia Piedmont to the east and the Shenandoah Valley to the west. Two peaks exceed 4,000 feet. Hawksbill and Stony Man are the high points, but the whole ridge is high enough to keep its own weather - cooler and wetter than the lowlands by a noticeable margin. Big Meadows, at the park's center, gets 132 centimeters of precipitation a year, including roughly 94 centimeters of snow.

## Who Lived Here First

Human beings arrived on this ridge eight to nine thousand years ago - barely a heartbeat in geological time. Native communities visited seasonally to hunt, gather nuts and berries, and find the right stone for tools. European settlers did not start moving into the lower hollows until the 1750s, near springs and streams where small farms could survive. Over the next century and a half hundreds of families built homesteads, mills, and stores; planted orchards; ran small mining operations; and eventually opened mountain resorts that promised vacationers the cool breezes and views. By 1900 the Blue Ridge here was settled country - not wilderness but worked land, with a culture and a way of life. That made what came next harder.

## The Park That Was Carved Out

When Congress authorized the first national parks in the eastern United States in the 1920s, the federal government decided that Shenandoah would become one of them. Authorization came in 1926. To assemble the land, the state of Virginia bought out - or condemned - the property of more than 450 mountain families. Many fought. Some had lived on the same land for five or six generations. Some were paid; some were paid almost nothing; some were physically removed. A few elderly residents were granted life tenancies and allowed to stay until they died. The park was officially established in December 1935. The Civilian Conservation Corps moved in to build trails, lodges, picnic grounds, and the overlooks along Skyline Drive. Facilities were initially racially segregated, with a separate area at Lewis Mountain set aside for Black visitors; integration came only in the 1950s. The mountain houses were torn down or left to rot. The orchards went wild. The forest came back.

## What the Mountain Holds Now

Today the park is the largest fully protected area in the mid-Appalachian region and one of the most-visited national parks in the East, with well over a million visitors a year. The Appalachian Trail runs through it for more than 100 miles, weaving in and out of Skyline Drive. There are over 500 miles of marked trails - waterfalls in the hollows, balds with views in every direction, talus slopes still littered with stone. Black bears live here in numbers; rangers expect every visitor to know not to feed them. White-tailed deer are everywhere. There are over 200 bird species, 50 mammals, 51 reptiles and amphibians, and 30 fish. The lodges at Skyland, Big Meadows, and Lewis Mountain book out months in advance, especially in mid-October when the fall foliage runs scarlet and gold along the entire ridge. Spring, with the wildflowers and migratory songbirds, is the quieter pleasure.

## From the Air

Seen from the air, Shenandoah is a long narrow ribbon of forest running roughly southwest to northeast, a single uninterrupted ridge with Skyline Drive winding along its crest. The Shenandoah Valley to the west reads as a band of farmland and small towns; the Virginia Piedmont to the east is broader, rolling, and dotted with horse farms. Two airports flank the park: Shenandoah Valley Regional (KSHD) sits in the valley to the west, while Charlottesville-Albemarle (KCHO) lies east of the southern end. The Blue Ridge Parkway picks up the ridge at Rockfish Gap and continues south for 469 miles into North Carolina. From above, the foliage line in October is unmistakable - a wave of orange and red moving south along the ridge, the same wave that fills Skyline Drive's seventy-five overlooks with stopped cars. Below the wave, in the hollows where the mountain families once lived, the forest now covers what was once cleared. The trees grew back. The people did not.

## Flight Context

Shenandoah National Park stretches roughly southwest to northeast along the Blue Ridge crest at about 38.53 N, 78.35 W, with peaks exceeding 4,000 feet. Recommended viewing altitude is 5,000 to 7,000 feet AGL for the best look at the long ridgeline, the Shenandoah Valley to the west, and the Virginia Piedmont to the east. The two main airports are Shenandoah Valley Regional (KSHD) in the valley to the west and Charlottesville-Albemarle (KCHO) about 20 nm east of the southern end. Front Royal-Warren County (KFRR) sits at the park's northern end. Mountain wave turbulence is common on west-wind days, especially in afternoon - plan early-morning passes for the smoothest air. October fall foliage is the famous spectacle from above; the color wave moves north to south along the ridge over about two weeks in mid- to late October. Skyline Drive is visible as a thin paved line tracing the crest for 105 miles.
