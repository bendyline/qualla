# Harpers Ferry

Where the rivers meet and history collides - the town where John Brown's 1859 raid ignited the Civil War, where floods have destroyed it repeatedly, and where West Virginia, Virginia, and Maryland come together in spectacular geography.

**Location:** 39.3252, -77.7286

**Categories:** west-virginia, john-brown, civil-war, confluence, floods

## Introduction

Harpers Ferry sits at the confluence of the Potomac and Shenandoah rivers, where water gaps cut through the Blue Ridge Mountains in a setting Thomas Jefferson called 'worth a voyage across the Atlantic.' But the town's fame comes from bloodier history. On October 16, 1859, abolitionist John Brown led 21 men in a raid on the federal armory here, hoping to seize weapons and spark an uprising against slavery. The raid failed; Brown was captured by Robert E. Lee, tried, and hanged. But his martyrdom helped ignite the Civil War that freed the enslaved. The town changed hands eight times during the war and was devastated. It was devastated again by floods in 1870, 1889, 1924, 1936, 1942, 1972, and 1996. Harpers Ferry keeps getting destroyed and keeps coming back, its strategic position at the rivers' meeting point both blessing and curse.

## The Setting

Water gaps - places where rivers cut through mountain ridges - are rare and strategically important. At Harpers Ferry, the Potomac and Shenandoah rivers join after cutting separate gaps through the Blue Ridge. The result is spectacular scenery and a natural transportation hub. George Washington recognized this in 1799, establishing a federal armory to manufacture weapons. The town grew around the armory, becoming an industrial center. The Baltimore & Ohio Railroad and the Chesapeake & Ohio Canal both passed through. Harpers Ferry controlled routes between East and West, North and South.

## The Raid

John Brown was convinced that armed insurrection was necessary to end slavery. On the night of October 16, 1859, he led 21 followers - including five Black men - in seizing the Harpers Ferry armory. Brown expected enslaved people to rise up and join him. None did. Local militia pinned down his force in the fire engine house. On October 18, U.S. Marines under Colonel Robert E. Lee stormed the building. Ten of Brown's men were killed, including two of his sons; Brown was captured. He was tried for treason against Virginia, convicted, and hanged on December 2. His last words predicted that slavery would end only through bloodshed.

## The War

Brown's raid terrified the South and energized abolitionists. Eighteen months later, the Civil War began. Harpers Ferry's strategic position - controlling a river crossing and rail junction - made it a military prize. The town changed hands eight times. Stonewall Jackson captured it in 1862, along with 12,500 Union soldiers - the largest surrender of American troops until Bataan. By war's end, the town was devastated, the armory destroyed, the population scattered. It would never regain its antebellum importance.

## The Floods

The same geography that made Harpers Ferry strategic makes it vulnerable. The town sits at the low point where two rivers merge in a narrow gap. Floods have destroyed it repeatedly: 1870, 1889, 1924, 1936, 1942, 1972, 1996. After each flood, residents debate whether to rebuild. They always do. The lower town, now a National Historical Park, has been raised and reinforced. But the threat remains. Climate change is increasing flood frequency and severity. The question isn't whether Harpers Ferry will flood again, but when.

## Visiting Harpers Ferry

Harpers Ferry National Historical Park encompasses the lower town where rivers meet, the heights where John Brown was captured, and surrounding battlefields. The lower town is closed to private vehicles; a shuttle runs from the visitor center. John Brown's Fort (the fire engine house, relocated) stands near its original site. Museums interpret the raid, the Civil War, and African American history. The Appalachian Trail passes through; its headquarters is here. The confluence viewpoint from Maryland Heights requires a steep hike but offers the view Jefferson praised. Washington Dulles International Airport (IAD) is 55 miles east; Baltimore/Washington (BWI) is 65 miles. The town has restaurants and B&Bs; flooding is possible October through April.
