# Chesapeake and Ohio Canal National Historical Park

The Grand Old Ditch, saved from becoming a highway by a Supreme Court justice's eight-day hike.

**Location:** 38.9971, -77.2479

**Categories:** national-park, history, canal, maryland, outdoors

## Introduction

In January 1954, the Washington Post ran an editorial endorsing a plan to pave the entire 184.5-mile towpath of the abandoned Chesapeake and Ohio Canal and convert it into a scenic highway. Supreme Court Justice William O. Douglas read the editorial and wrote a letter to the editor inviting the Post's editors to walk the canal with him before they made up their minds. The editors accepted. On March 20, 1954, Douglas set off from Cumberland, Maryland with thirty-seven hikers. Eight days and 184.5 miles later, only nine of them finished the walk into Georgetown — the so-called Immortal Nine. The Post editors did not complete the full distance, but the experience was enough. The Post reversed its editorial position. The towpath was not paved. Today the canal is the most visited national historical park in the country.

## The Grand Old Ditch

Construction on the C&O Canal began in 1828 with a groundbreaking by President John Quincy Adams, and reached Cumberland in 1850, twenty-two years later. The original plan had called for it to continue another two hundred miles across the Allegheny Mountains to Pittsburgh, but by 1850 the Baltimore and Ohio Railroad had already reached Cumberland with cheaper, faster transport, and the canal's western extension was abandoned. The B&O reached Cumberland in 1842, eight years ahead of the canal. Even so, the canal remained competitive for bulk coal transport from the western Maryland mines for another quarter century. Only in the mid-1870s, when larger steam locomotives and air brakes finally let the railroad undercut canal rates, did the C&O begin its long decline. The canal operated from 1831 to 1924. Repeated floods finally rendered it economically unrepairable.

## How It Got Saved

In 1938 the federal government bought the dead canal from the B&O Railroad in exchange for a loan made through the Reconstruction Finance Corporation. The plan was to use Civilian Conservation Corps labor, including a primarily African-American workforce, to restore the canal as a recreation area and provide Depression-era jobs. By 1940 the first twenty-two miles, from Georgetown to Violette's Lock, were rewatered and the first mule-driven canal-boat rides began in 1941. World War II interrupted everything. The war years saw the strange suggestion from a citizen named Harry Athey that the canal could be paved over and used as a bomb shelter with the roof becoming a landing strip; the periodic flooding of the Potomac killed that idea quickly. The Army Corps of Engineers, more seriously, proposed building fourteen dams that would have permanently inundated seventy-four miles of towpath. The dams were not built. Douglas's hike in 1954 finally consolidated public support for preservation.

## Two Designations

Dwight Eisenhower made the canal a National Monument under the Antiquities Act in January 1961, in one of his final acts as president. The designation actually hardened opposition from those who still wanted the highway plan, and for the next decade the canal's status was contested. The compromise that emerged was the National Historical Park, with broader recreational use and a less restrictive interpretive mandate. Richard Nixon signed the Chesapeake and Ohio Canal National Historical Park Act on January 8, 1971. The park grew to nearly twenty thousand acres along its narrow ribbon of the Potomac, with six visitor centers spaced along the route: Georgetown, Great Falls Tavern, Brunswick, Williamsport, Hancock, and Cumberland. In 2013 the towpath was designated the first segment of U.S. Bicycle Route 50.

## Floods and Repairs

The Potomac has continued to flood, and the park has continued to be damaged and rebuilt. The winter of 1996 brought two separate catastrophes. A January blizzard followed by heavy rain produced a snowmelt flood that damaged eighty to ninety percent of the canal and towpath. Eight months later, in September, Hurricane Fran inundated long stretches again. Volunteers, the C&O Canal Association founded in 1954, and the newer C&O Canal Trust founded in 2007 work continuously with the National Park Service to repair eroded sections and rewater historical reaches. Current restoration includes the Conococheague Aqueduct, Locks 3 and 4, repairs at Great Falls and Swain's Lock, and a second phase of work at the Paw Paw Tunnel that began in May 2019. The work is never finished. The river will keep flooding.

## Why Five Million People Come

The C&O receives about five million recreational visits a year, more than any other national historical park. Most come for an afternoon, walking the towpath out of Georgetown, riding the replica Charles F. Mercer canal boat behind a pair of mules at Great Falls Tavern, or hiking the demanding Section A of the Billy Goat Trail along Mather Gorge. Robert F. Kennedy hiked from Great Falls to Harpers Ferry along the towpath on February 9, 1963 as part of his brother's fitness craze. The route connects with the Appalachian Trail near Harpers Ferry National Historical Park, where the two systems briefly share a path. The thirty hiker-biker campsites spaced about every five miles let through-cyclists run the entire 184.5 miles in a week or so, then continue another 150 miles on the Great Allegheny Passage to Pittsburgh. None of this would exist if William O. Douglas had not finished his walk.

## Flight Context

The park runs as a thin band from Georgetown at 38.9028 degrees north, 77.0584 degrees west, west and northwest to Cumberland at the far end. The midpoint near Great Falls Tavern is at 38.9962 degrees north, 77.2479 degrees west. Best viewed at 2,500 to 4,500 feet AGL, following the Potomac corridor. Dulles International (KIAD) is twelve nautical miles south of Great Falls; Reagan National (KDCA) sits at the eastern end. The corridor sits inside Washington Class B veil at its eastern end; overflight requires ATC coordination. Spring and autumn provide the best light along the river.
