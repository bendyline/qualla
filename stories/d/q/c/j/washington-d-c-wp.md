# Washington, D.C.

Three founders ate dinner in June 1790 and decided where to put the United States capital. The compromise placed it on the Potomac, near Washington's plantation, on land surveyed by a Black astronomer whose father had been enslaved.

## Introduction

In June 1790, James Madison, Thomas Jefferson, and Alexander Hamilton sat down to dinner at Jefferson's rented house on Maiden Lane in Manhattan and made a bargain that determined where the United States of America would have its capital. Hamilton needed northern support for his plan to have the federal government assume the states' Revolutionary War debts. Madison and Jefferson needed southern support for placing the new capital somewhere on the Potomac River, on the boundary between Maryland and Virginia, where southern states' interests could be defended from a slowly more populous and politically northern Atlantic seaboard. They traded what each side wanted. The Residence Act was passed on July 16, 1790. The Compromise of 1790 is what put Washington, D.C., on the Potomac, near George Washington's plantation at Mount Vernon. The Hamilton musical retold this bargain as 'The Room Where It Happens.' For Washingtonians, the room where it happened is the room their city was born in.

## Benjamin Banneker and the Survey

Andrew Ellicott led the team that surveyed the original ten-mile-square boundary of the federal district in 1791 and 1792. Among Ellicott's deputies was Benjamin Banneker, a free Black mathematician and astronomer from Maryland whose father had been enslaved. Banneker, who was 59 years old when he joined the survey, took most of the celestial observations at the base camp, calculating the precise positions of the stars that fixed the boundary stones. The southernmost stone, at Jones Point in Alexandria, was placed in a ceremony on April 15, 1791. The boundary stones were set at every mile mark around the perimeter - forty stones in all, of which thirty-six survive. Banneker later wrote to Thomas Jefferson directly in 1791, sending him a copy of his published almanac and using the occasion to argue with Jefferson over slavery: 'I freely and Cheerfully acknowledge that I am of the African race, and... of that color which is natural to them of the deepest dye. We are a race of beings who have long laboured under the abuse and censure of the world.' Jefferson sent the almanac to the Royal Academy of Sciences in Paris. He did not free his own enslaved workers.

## Built by Enslaved Hands

The construction of Washington's federal buildings - the Capitol, the White House, the Treasury, and many of the early government structures - relied heavily on the labor of enslaved African Americans. Slave receipts and payroll records, recovered in the 1990s by historian Edward Lawler and the Architect of the Capitol's research staff, document hundreds of enslaved workers hired from their owners at fixed daily rates for the federal construction projects. Many of the workers cut and quarried sandstone at Aquia Creek in Virginia and limestone in Maryland; others fired bricks, dug foundations, hauled materials, plastered walls. The District itself was a slave-holding jurisdiction from its founding in 1790. The slave market at Robey's Tavern on 7th Street was one of the largest in the Upper South before the Civil War. Slave coffles - chains of enslaved people being walked from Virginia and Maryland to be sold to the deep South - regularly passed through the streets of Washington under the very dome of the Capitol where Congress was debating the laws that maintained the system. President Lincoln signed the Compensated Emancipation Act on April 16, 1862, freeing approximately 3,100 enslaved people in the District of Columbia - nine months before the Emancipation Proclamation.

## Retrocession and the Diamond That Was Lost

L'Enfant's original district was a perfect diamond, ten miles on each side, including land from Maryland on the north and Virginia on the south. In the 1830s and 1840s, the residents of Alexandria - the largest town on the Virginia portion of the district - grew anxious that the abolitionist sentiment in Congress would eventually end the slave trade in the District (which it did, in 1850, as part of the Compromise of 1850). Alexandria petitioned Virginia to take its land back. The Virginia General Assembly agreed in February 1846. Congress voted in July 1846 to return all of the Virginia-donated territory. The retrocession reduced the District from 100 square miles to about 68 square miles and shaved off the southwestern third of the original diamond. The boundary stones planted by Ellicott and Banneker still stand inside present-day Arlington County and Falls Church, Virginia - federal markers in a state that has not formally been part of the District for almost 180 years. The boundary stones at Falls Church and at the Pentagon's southwest corner are now historic monuments preserved by the National Park Service.

## Home Rule and the Statehood Question

For most of its history Washington was governed not by its own residents but by Congress directly, through appointed commissioners and territorial governors. Walter Washington became the first elected mayor in 1975 after Congress passed the District of Columbia Home Rule Act in 1973 - more than 170 years after the city's founding. The District's residents still have no voting representation in Congress; the city sends a non-voting delegate (currently Eleanor Holmes Norton, in office since 1991) to the House. District residents have voted in presidential elections only since 1961, when the Twenty-third Amendment gave the District three electoral votes - the same as the smallest state. The District's license plates carry the slogan TAXATION WITHOUT REPRESENTATION as a permanent civic protest. The D.C. statehood movement has been active since the 1980s. In 2016 District voters approved statehood by 85 percent. In April 2021 the House passed the Washington, D.C. Admission Act, which would have created a new state called Washington, Douglass Commonwealth - keeping the abbreviation D.C. while honoring Frederick Douglass. The Senate did not advance the bill. The statehood question remains unresolved as of 2026.

## Diplomatic City

Washington today hosts 177 foreign embassies, the most of any city in the world after Brussels. Massachusetts Avenue between Scott Circle and the Naval Observatory is universally called Embassy Row - a stretch of about two miles where Cosmos Club and country residences from the Gilded Age now serve as the official Washington residences and chancellaries of dozens of nations. The World Bank, the International Monetary Fund, the Organization of American States, the Inter-American Development Bank, and a constellation of other multilateral organizations are headquartered in the District. The diplomatic presence has shaped the District's restaurant scene (the largest Ethiopian and Salvadoran communities in the United States are both centered in the Washington area), its housing market, and its civic life. Washington's 2022 visitor count of nearly 22 million domestic and international tourists made it the country's seventh most-visited city, narrowly behind Las Vegas. The federal government remains the largest single employer in the metropolitan area, with nearly 200,000 federal civilian employees in the District itself and another 150,000 in the immediate Maryland and Virginia suburbs. The contracting industry, the lobbying industry, the law firms, the think tanks, the universities, and the international organizations all complete what residents simply call the federal city.

## Flight Context

Washington, D.C. occupies 68 square miles centered near 38.9072 degrees N, 77.0369 degrees W on the north bank of the Potomac River. From the air the federal city's monumental core - the Capitol dome at the eastern end of the Mall, the Washington Monument's 555-foot obelisk at the center, the Lincoln Memorial at the western end, and the White House just north of the central axis - is unmistakable, with the Potomac River bending around the southern and western edges of the city. Best viewed at 2,500 to 5,000 feet AGL; the entire city lies within the Washington FRZ and the prohibited area P-56A, the most restrictive airspace in the United States. Special Federal Aviation Regulation 94 requires every general aviation pilot operating within the SFRA to complete additional online training. Nearest airports are Ronald Reagan Washington National (KDCA) 3 nm south, College Park (KCGS) 7 nm northeast, and Washington Dulles (KIAD) 23 nm west.
