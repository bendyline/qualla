# Fort Meade

Originally a World War I training cantonment named for the Union general who won at Gettysburg, Fort Meade is now home to the National Security Agency, U.S. Cyber Command, and 54,000 employees - the largest single workforce in Maryland.

**Location:** 39.1080, -76.7480

**Categories:** Military installations in Maryland, Anne Arundel County, Maryland, U.S. Army facilities

## Introduction

The man the post is named for - George Gordon Meade, the Pennsylvania-born Union general who commanded the Army of the Potomac at Gettysburg in July 1863 - would scarcely recognize what carries his name today. Fort George G. Meade sits on about 5,000 acres between Washington and Baltimore, just east of Interstate 95 and west of the Baltimore-Washington Parkway. Behind its gates sit the National Security Agency, U.S. Cyber Command, the Defense Information Systems Agency, the Defense Information School, and dozens of other military and intelligence organizations. The base employs more than 54,000 people, making it the largest single employer in the state of Maryland and the second-largest Army installation by workforce in the country. The signals intelligence and cyber operations conducted here touch nearly every adversarial and allied government on Earth. The man who fought Robert E. Lee in a Pennsylvania town in 1863 would have recognized none of it.

## Camp Admiral to Camp Meade

The base opened in 1917 as Camp Admiral, established on nearly 30 square miles of land acquired by the Army for World War I training. It was renamed Camp Meade in 1918. During the First World War the 154th Depot Brigade trained at the post, and the Tank Corps moved its training school to Camp Meade in 1919 - one of the formative units of American armored warfare, briefly commanded after the war by a young captain named Dwight D. Eisenhower. The fort was renamed Fort Leonard Wood for thirteen months between February 1928 and March 1929, then renamed Fort Meade again after Wood's name was given to a new Missouri installation. In World War II it was a recruit training post, a prisoner-of-war camp, and a holding center for about 384 Japanese, German, and Italian immigrants arrested as potential fifth columnists. The Second U.S. Army headquartered there in 1947. The transformation from training camp to intelligence hub began that year, when the post became home to what would become the National Security Agency.

## Cold War Sky

From the 1950s into the 1970s, Fort Meade was an active node in the air defense of Washington. The first Nike Ajax surface-to-air missiles in the country were deployed at Meade in December 1953, becoming operational in May 1954. In 1955 a Battery C, 36th Antiaircraft Artillery Missile Battalion fired one of those missiles accidentally - it was an event that helped recalibrate the safety protocols of the entire Nike system. The Martin AN/FSG-1 Antiaircraft Defense System operated at Meade as part of the layered defense around the capital. During the Cuban Missile Crisis in October 1962, the 6th Battalion (Hawk) of the 65th Artillery, headquartered at Meade as part of U.S. Strike Command, was deployed to Miami and Key West to provide air defense against potential Soviet attack from Cuba. Fort Meade's bomb disposal experts were also sent to Savage Mountain in January 1964 to secure the two thermonuclear weapons aboard the B-52 that crashed there in a snowstorm.

## The Black Building

The National Security Agency, founded in 1952, established its headquarters at Fort Meade in 1957. The main building, OPS-2A and OPS-2B - dark glass and the signature obsidian curtain wall that gives the complex its nickname, the Black Building - is one of the most photographed and least visited structures in the federal government. The 2005 Base Realignment and Closure process designated Fort Meade to gain 5,700 positions, most of them tied to NSA expansion. U.S. Cyber Command was established at Fort Meade in 2009 and elevated to a unified combatant command in 2018. The Defense Information Systems Agency moved its headquarters to a 95-acre Meade complex in April 2011. The intelligence agencies clustered here employ tens of thousands of mathematicians, linguists, network engineers, cryptographers, and analysts. The black SUVs entering and exiting the gates outnumber the personal vehicles on most weekdays.

## The Refuge Next Door

In the early 1990s, the Army transferred 12.7 square miles of Fort Meade land to the Patuxent Research Refuge - the only U.S. National Wildlife Refuge created specifically for wildlife research. The transfer doubled the refuge's size and gave the federal government an unusually large wooded buffer between Fort Meade's perimeter and the suburbs of Laurel, Odenton, and Columbia. The refuge holds populations of bald eagles, black bears, and migratory waterfowl that nest in the marshes near where the Patuxent River winds through the protected land. In 2007 the EPA ordered an assessment of fourteen hazardous waste sites on Fort Meade, including an ordnance disposal area, a 1940s waste dump, and a closed sanitary landfill. A September 2007 environmental impact report concluded that adding two proposed golf courses would represent a significant threat to the biological and territorial integrity of the adjacent refuge. The Army said it would take steps to limit damage. The boundary between the most secretive intelligence installation in the country and one of the most protected ecological corridors in the mid-Atlantic remains literally adjacent.

## What Happens at the Gate

Twice in three years, drivers attempted to ram National Security Agency entry points and were stopped by NSA Police gunfire. On March 30, 2015, an SUV was driven toward a restricted gate on the NSA campus; two officers and a passenger were injured. The FBI determined the incident was not terrorism-related, though investigations into the driver's motives never produced a clear public answer. On February 14, 2018, a different SUV rammed a barricade near another NSA gate, and police shot and wounded the driver. Both incidents underscored what's never visible to the millions of commuters who pass Fort Meade on the Baltimore-Washington Parkway: the perimeter of the installation is one of the most heavily defended in the country, with policing capabilities that include explosive ordnance disposal, K-9 units, counter-drone systems, and special-response teams. The town outside the gates - Odenton, with its small downtown and old MARC train station - looks like a normal Maryland suburb. What sits inside the gates is one of the most consequential intelligence apparatuses in the world.

## Flight Context

Fort Meade is centered at approximately 39.108 N, 76.748 W in Anne Arundel County, Maryland. The base is bordered by the Baltimore-Washington Parkway (MD 295) to the west and is about 5 miles east of Interstate 95. BWI Marshall (KBWI) is 8 miles east; Tipton Airport (KFME), a public general aviation field, sits on Fort Meade property at 39.085 N, 76.760 W. The base sits well outside the Washington Flight Restricted Zone but inside the Special Flight Rules Area; sensitive overflight restrictions and a temporary flight restriction protect the NSA and Cyber Command facilities. From altitude, the dark glass of the NSA's Black Building is visible from the parkway, and the gridded street pattern of the residential cantonment is distinct from the surrounding suburbs.
