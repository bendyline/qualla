# Coro Synagogue

A former lieutenant governor's residence on Talavera Street became one of Latin America's oldest synagogues when Sephardic traders from Curacao transformed it into a house of prayer, its sand-covered floors echoing a tradition carried across the Caribbean.

**Location:** 11.4080, -69.6764

**Categories:** historic-site, religious-heritage, museum, colonial-architecture, jewish-heritage

## Introduction

Sand covers the floor. Not beach sand hauled in for decoration, but fine grains from the Medanos de Coro, the vast dune field that sprawls north of the city toward the Caribbean. The practice mirrors the sand-covered floors of the Mikve Israel-Emanuel Synagogue in Willemstad, Curacao, where the Jewish community that built this prayer house originally worshiped. Walk into the former synagogue on Talavera Street in Santa Ana de Coro, and you walk into a centuries-old conversation between two coastlines, two islands of faith separated by a narrow stretch of sea.

## A Governor's Walls, A Trader's Vision

The building on Talavera Street began as something entirely different. In the first half of the 18th century, Don Francisco Campuzano Polanco, the lieutenant governor of Coro, built it as his personal residence. For roughly a century the house served colonial authority, its thick walls and Spanish colonial architecture reflecting the power of the crown. Then, on July 30, 1847, a Sephardic trader named David Abraham Senior purchased the property. Senior had come from Curacao, part of a growing Jewish community that had been establishing roots in northern Venezuela. Before he bought the house, the community gathered at the home of David Valencia to pray -- roughly twenty people assembling for Shabbat and daily services in whatever space they could find. Senior's purchase changed that. He and his family lived in the house while dedicating one of its rooms as a permanent prayer hall, giving the community its first real synagogue.

## Faith Carried Across the Sea

The Sephardic Jews who settled in Coro traced their heritage through centuries of displacement. Expelled from Spain in 1492, their ancestors had scattered across the Mediterranean and the Caribbean, carrying liturgical traditions and communal practices wherever they landed. Curacao, a Dutch island barely 130 kilometers north of Coro, became one of the most significant Sephardic communities in the Western Hemisphere. The Mikve Israel-Emanuel congregation there, established in the 1650s, is the oldest continuously active Jewish congregation in the Americas. From Curacao, traders like Senior crossed to the Venezuelan mainland, bringing goods and faith alike. The sand on the synagogue floor was not mere imitation. It connected this small prayer room on Talavera Street to the broader Sephardic diaspora, a physical reminder that worship continues regardless of where the winds carry you.

## Generations in the Prayer Hall

Isaac Senior, David Abraham's son, continued the family tradition. He and his descendants lived in the Talavera Street house and maintained the prayer room through the decades, a quiet continuity in a city buffeted by political upheaval and economic change. By the 1880s, however, the community had shifted. Venezuela's Jewish population was dispersing to larger cities, and the prayer hall on Talavera Street fell silent. The house passed through other hands, its religious purpose fading from memory even as its walls endured. For over a century the building sat in Coro's old quarter, indistinguishable from the other colonial-era residences lining the narrow streets -- a piece of living history waiting to be recognized.

## From Silence to Museum

Recognition came in 1986 when the Venezuelan government purchased the house. More than a decade later, on August 3, 1997, the government of Falcon State reopened the building as the Casa de Oracion Hebrea -- the Hebrew Prayer House -- acknowledging it as a significant piece of the Sephardic heritage woven into northern Venezuela's cultural fabric. Today the former synagogue forms part of the Alberto Henriquez Museum of Art, administered by the Universidad Francisco de Miranda. In 2009, the university reached out to the Israelite Association of Venezuela to seek support for restoring and maintaining the site. The effort speaks to something larger than preservation of old walls: it is an attempt to keep visible a chapter of Venezuelan history that might otherwise vanish entirely, a small community's determination to worship freely in a house that once belonged to the colonial governor himself.

## Flight Context

Located at 11.41N, 69.68W in the historic center of Santa Ana de Coro, Falcon State, Venezuela. The city sits on a coastal plain south of the Paraguana Peninsula, flanked by the striking white dunes of Medanos de Coro National Park to the north. Nearest major airport is Jose Leonardo Chirino Airport (SVJC). From the air, look for the colonial old quarter's tight street grid near the cathedral. The Caribbean coast and the island of Curacao are visible to the north on clear days. Recommended viewing altitude: 3,000-5,000 feet AGL for the historic center context.
