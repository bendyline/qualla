# Caribbean

Hundreds of islands strung between two continents, shaped by sugar and slavery, colonized by six empires, and home to a musical and culinary culture found nowhere else on Earth.

## Introduction

Count the languages and you begin to understand. English on Jamaica, Spanish on Cuba, French on Martinique, Dutch on Curacao, Haitian Creole in Port-au-Prince, Jamaican Patois in Kingston - and those are just the ones spoken on the radio. The Caribbean is not one place but hundreds of islands shaped by the competing ambitions of six European empires, by the forced migration of millions of enslaved Africans, by the later arrival of Indian indentured laborers, and by the indigenous Arawak and Carib peoples who were here first. What binds it all together is water: the warm, turquoise, hurricane-prone Caribbean Sea that gives the region its name and its livelihood.

## Sugar, Empire, and the Human Cost

The Caribbean's colonial history is written in sugarcane. European powers fought hard over these islands - sometimes disproportionately hard for their size - because sugar plantations generated staggering wealth. That wealth depended on the labor of enslaved Africans, who were brought to the Caribbean in enormous numbers through the Atlantic slave trade. The enslaved people lived under brutal conditions: hard labor, violence, disease, and starvation defined their daily existence.

Slavery's end came through different paths on different islands. In Haiti, the enslaved population rose up and won both abolition and independence in a single revolution. Elsewhere, abolition arrived through legislation, though exploitation continued in other forms - the British, Dutch, French, and Spanish colonizers brought indentured laborers from India to replace enslaved workers, creating the significant Indian communities found today in Trinidad and Tobago, Jamaica, and elsewhere. This layered migration shapes everything from the food to the music to the religious practices of the modern Caribbean.

## Rhythms That Travel

Caribbean music moves faster than Caribbean politics. Charanga from Cuba, merengue from the Dominican Republic, ska and reggae and dancehall from Jamaica, calypso and soca from Trinidad and Tobago - each island developed its own sound from the collision of African rhythms, European instruments, and indigenous traditions. Steel pan, invented in Trinidad from discarded oil drums, turned industrial waste into an orchestra.

The religious landscape carries similar complexity. Most of the Caribbean is nominally Christian, but indigenous and African spiritual traditions have blended with Christian practice to create entirely new belief systems. Santeria and Vodun, rooted in the Yoruba religion that came to the Americas with enslaved Africans, are practiced openly in Cuba and Haiti. Jamaica gave birth to Rastafari. Small Jewish communities on islands like Curacao are among the oldest in the Western Hemisphere, and Hindu and Muslim minorities reflect the Indian diaspora. No single faith dominates the spiritual conversation.

## Island by Island

The Greater Antilles - Cuba, Jamaica, Hispaniola, and Puerto Rico - account for most of the Caribbean's landmass and population. Cuba alone is larger than all the other Caribbean islands combined. To the north, the Lucayan Archipelago includes the Bahamas and the Turks and Caicos Islands. To the east, the Lesser Antilles curve southward in a long volcanic arc from the Virgin Islands to Trinidad, each island small enough that you can drive across it in an afternoon.

Some of the Americas' oldest colonial cities stand here. Havana's crumbling grandeur, San Juan's fortified harbor, the pastel waterfront of Willemstad in Curacao - these are places where Columbus and his successors first established European footholds in the New World. Several remain dependencies of France, the Netherlands, or the United Kingdom. Others, like Navassa Island, require special permission to visit. And on Saba, you can land on the shortest commercially used runway in the world - an experience that redefines what you thought a runway could be.

## Coconut, Rum, and the Sea

The not-so-secret ingredient in Caribbean cooking is coconut. It appears in curries and stews, in rice dishes and desserts, in drinks and sauces, across islands that agree on little else culinarily. Indian-influenced dishes like roti and curry have become staples in Trinidad and Jamaica, a direct legacy of the indentured laborers who brought their foodways across the ocean. The Bahamas claim cracked conch - deep-fried mollusk with rice - as a national dish, while every island has its own version of jerk seasoning, pepper sauce, or seafood preparation.

Rum, distilled from sugarcane, is the spirit that ties it all together. Some of the world's most acclaimed distilleries operate in the Caribbean, and rum-based cocktails have become the region's most successful cultural export - the mojito from Cuba, the pina colada from Puerto Rico. But the drink's origins are inseparable from the plantations that produced the sugarcane and the enslaved people who worked them. In the Caribbean, even a cocktail carries history.

## Between Hurricanes

Hurricane season runs from June through November, and it is no abstraction. Warm Atlantic water feeds cyclones that can intensify from tropical depression to Category 5 in days, stripping roofs, flattening vegetation, and reshaping coastlines. Islands built on tourism find themselves rebuilding with startling regularity.

Outside those months, the Caribbean delivers the weather that sells postcards - trade winds, warm water, consistent sun. Cruise ships decant thousands of visitors daily into ports from Nassau to Bridgetown. But beyond the resort fences, a different Caribbean persists: fishing villages where the catch comes in by hand, mountain interiors thick with cloud forest, and communities where creole languages and African-rooted traditions remain the fabric of everyday life.

## Flight Context

The Caribbean Sea stretches roughly from 10N to 25N latitude and 60W to 88W longitude, bounded by the Greater Antilles to the north, the Lesser Antilles to the east, and the Central and South American mainland to the south and west. Major aviation hubs include Miami (KMIA), San Juan (TJSJ), Havana (MUHA), Kingston (MKJP), Nassau (MYNN), and Curacao (TNCC). Island-hopping by air is common via Caribbean Airlines and regional carriers. Expect tropical weather year-round with particular caution during hurricane season (June-November). Trade winds generally blow from the east-northeast. The deep Caribbean basin reaches depths exceeding 7,000 meters in the Cayman Trench. From cruising altitude, the contrast between the deep blue open sea and the turquoise shallows surrounding each island is one of the most striking sights in aviation.
