# Armero tragedy

On November 13, 1985, a foreseen eruption of Nevado del Ruiz sent lahars down into the Colombian town of Armero. More than twenty thousand people died; the warnings had come for weeks.

**Location:** 4.9633, -74.9056

**Categories:** Lahars, 1985 in Colombia, 1985 natural disasters, 20th-century volcanic events, Natural disasters in Colombia, Populated places in Tolima Department, Landslides in Colombia

## Introduction

The hazard map existed. Hazard maps had existed for weeks. Geologists had warned authorities and media outlets about the danger of Nevado del Ruiz; officials knew the volcano had woken up after 69 years of dormancy; evacuation plans had been drafted. On the afternoon of November 13, 1985, a severe storm rolled in over the Tolima cordillera, and communications became difficult. When the volcano's crater erupted around 9:08 p.m., most of the people of Armero were at home, some having been reassured by radio announcements that the ashfall earlier in the day had subsided. Four lahars — superheated mixtures of mud, ice-melt, rock, and debris — poured down the mountain's glaciers and into river valleys. Around 11:30 p.m. they reached Armero. More than 20,000 of the town's roughly 29,000 inhabitants died.

## A Mountain That Gives and Takes

Armero was built on an alluvial fan — volcanic sediment laid down by previous eruptions. The soil made the land remarkably fertile; the town had grown into the third-largest in Tolima Department, producing roughly a fifth of Colombia's rice crop along with large shares of cotton, sorghum, and coffee. Nevado del Ruiz rises to 5,321 metres, a glacier-capped stratovolcano visible on clear days from much of the Magdalena River valley. The volcano had erupted destructively in 1595 and again in 1845. The 1595 event produced three Plinian eruptions and lahars that killed 636 people. The 1845 event produced similar mudflows through the same valleys. The warning signs of what geologists call a "volcano-glacier interaction" were carved into the mountain itself, and written into the geological record — a record that future mapmakers would later turn into a hazard map specifically for the area around the volcano.

## The Warnings

In late 1984, geologists noticed increasing seismic activity near the summit. Fumaroles grew more active. Sulfur deposits accumulated around vents. Small phreatic — steam — explosions continued through September 1985; a major one on September 11 drove a column of steam high into the air. Activity slackened in October, probably as the new magma finished its ascent into the volcano's edifice. By then, a hazard map had been finalised — it correctly identified the valleys that the lahars would eventually flow through, including the one leading to Armero. The map, however, was poorly distributed. Local seismographs had been delivered to officials without instructions on how to use them. Communication between the scientists, the national government, and the towns around the volcano was patchy. Some warnings reached local radio stations, but many evacuation plans were never put in motion. In November, magma neared the surface; sulfur dioxide emissions climbed, and spring waters around the volcano became enriched with magnesium, calcium, and potassium leached from the rising melt.

## The Evening of November 13

Ashfall began in the afternoon. Some residents left the town; others stayed, many because they had been told on the radio that the event had peaked. A severe storm reached the area in the evening, rain drowning out sound and knocking out communications. The main eruption — a Plinian column from the summit — occurred at about 9:08 p.m. Pyroclastic flows melted large portions of the summit ice cap. The resulting water rushed into the gullies that feed the Lagunillas, Azufrado, and Gualí rivers, picking up sediment as it went and transforming into dense, fast-moving lahars. By the time they reached the low country, they were moving at tens of metres per second. Around 11:30 p.m. the first lahar reached Armero. It swept through the town in a series of surges, some up to five metres deep, carrying trees, cars, and entire houses. Much of the population was asleep or had only minutes to react. By morning, two-thirds of the town had been buried.

## Omayra Sánchez

Thirteen-year-old Omayra Sánchez Garzón survived the initial lahar, but she was pinned beneath the rubble of her home, her legs trapped in the collapsed concrete. For roughly sixty hours, rescuers and journalists watched and tried to help while Omayra remained conscious, composed, and kind — asking for food, speaking to her mother through the cameras, comforting those around her. The diving gear and pumps needed to free her legs were not available. On November 16, Omayra died in the mud. Photographer Frank Fournier's photograph of her large brown eyes in the water won the World Press Photo of the Year in 1986, and the image went around the world. It became inseparable from the tragedy itself. Omayra was one person among tens of thousands, but the photograph made an abstract death toll concrete, and made it impossible to look away. She deserves to be named, as do the thousands of others who cannot be named here — they were farmers, teachers, grocers, mothers, children, neighbours.

## A Preventable Disaster

At a mass funeral in Ibagué, mourners carried a banner that read, "The volcano didn't kill 22,000 people. The government killed them." The judgment has largely stood. This was a foreseeable catastrophe, and the failure was primarily one of communication and political will, not of science. Relief efforts after the event faced enormous difficulty — the lahar mud was nearly impossible to move through, and twelve hours passed before most rescue teams reached Armero; many seriously injured survivors died before they could be extracted. The overall death toll, including fatalities at the town of Chinchiná and elsewhere, reached around 23,000–25,000 people. It was the second-deadliest volcanic disaster of the twentieth century, surpassed only by the 1902 eruption of Mount Pelée in Martinique. In the aftermath, Colombia established a national disaster management system, and the US Geological Survey formed the Volcano Disaster Assistance Program, which in 1991 helped evacuate roughly 75,000 people from the area around Mount Pinatubo in the Philippines before its eruption. The dead of Armero bought those later survivors the time they needed.

## What Remains

Pope John Paul II visited the site of Armero in July 1986 and prayed at the memorial. The town itself was not rebuilt where it had stood — the ground had become, in effect, a cemetery, and Colombia left it that way. A memorial park marks the site now, with a cross where the town's main church once stood. Nevado del Ruiz continues to erupt periodically; when it did so in 1989, more than 2,300 people in the surrounding area were evacuated. In 2023 the volcano's alert level was raised again, and tens of thousands of people in the valleys below were prepared to leave. As geologist Stanley Williams observed in 1988, with the possible exception of Mount St. Helens in Washington State, no other volcano in the Western Hemisphere is watched so elaborately as Nevado del Ruiz. That vigilance is the most important thing Armero left behind.

## Flight Context

The former town of Armero sits at approximately 4.96°N, 74.91°W, in Tolima Department, Colombia, at roughly 350 metres elevation on the Magdalena River valley floor. Nevado del Ruiz stratovolcano rises to 5,321 metres some 45 km west-southwest of the site. The nearest commercial airport is Perales Airport at Ibagué (SKIB), about 60 km south-southwest; El Dorado International Airport at Bogotá (SKBO) is roughly 130 km east. From altitude, look for the distinct glacier-capped summit of Nevado del Ruiz in the Central Andes, the broad north–south corridor of the Magdalena Valley, and the visible scars of the lahar paths in the Azufrado, Lagunillas, and Gualí river valleys. Up to 500,000 people still live in valleys that could be affected by a similar-size event; hazard-zone maps are active and consulted.
