# Jardin

Three priests fleeing a civil war founded a town in a valley of white snakewood. A hundred and sixty years later, the paint still gets touched up every year.

**Location:** 5.5983, -75.8197

**Categories:** Pueblos Patrimonio, Antioquia municipalities, Colonial architecture, Heritage preservation

## Introduction

The story the founders tell begins with a view. Settlers from the village of Sietecueros climbed the Alto de las Flores, the Flower Hilltop, and looked down into a valley they had never seen before. Jungle, laced by two creeks, full of white snakewood trees. They turned to each other and said: un jardin. A garden. That was the 1860s, and the town that grew there kept the name. Today, Jardin is one of only seventeen Colombian municipalities officially designated as Pueblos Patrimonio, heritage towns, and the only second in Antioquia to earn the title. Walk the main plaza and you will see why. The paint on the balconies has been refreshed continuously for a hundred and sixty years.

## Three Priests and a Civil War

The earliest human settlement in this valley came from the Chamies people, who spoke Catio and were known as Docatoes after the nearby Docato River (now called the San Juan). Their graves are the oldest evidence of life here. Colonization by Antioquian settlers hit its peak in the 1860s. In 1863, a settler named Indalecio Pelaez claimed vast tracts of land between two streams called Volcanes and El Salado. The formal founding of Jardin came a year later in unusual circumstances. In 1864, Dr. Jose Maria Gomez Angel, a prominent priest, and two other priests arrived in the area after fleeing General Tomas Cipriano de Mosquera, who controlled Medellin during the Colombian civil war then underway. The priests met with Pelaez and other early settlers and proposed establishing an independent village. The settlers agreed. The town was declared a parish in 1871 and elevated to formal town status in 1882 by Luciano Restrepo, then president of Antioquia State.

## The Basilica of River Stones

The parish declared in 1871 needed a church, and the town eventually built one worth the wait. The Basilica of the Immaculate Conception, which dominates the main plaza, is a neo-Gothic structure made from hand-cut stones quarried from the Taparto River nearby. It was declared a National Monument of Colombia in 1980 and elevated to a minor basilica in 2003. The plaza itself, known as the Parque Principal or El Libertador, earned National Monument status in 1985. Its floor is paved with stones from the same Taparto. Its colored wooden chairs sit under gardens of flowering plants. Around the edge, more than twenty cafes and bars open onto the square, making the plaza the town's permanent living room. Declaring a plaza a national monument is not a common thing. Jardin earned it by preserving the plaza as it was built.

## The Architecture That Refused to Change

For one hundred and forty years, Jardin has looked like Jardin. While other Antioquian towns updated and tore down, Jardin's colonial architecture survived. White-washed building walls with doors, balconies, and chairs painted in reds, blues, yellows, greens. Many of the chairs are hand-painted with rural scenes and landscapes, a tradition still carried on by local artisans. The town's 13,541 people (2018 census) are predominantly Mestizo and white (89.3 percent), with a 10 percent Indigenous population, largely Embera-Chami, living just outside the center. That Indigenous presence is continuous with the Chamies who lived here before Spanish arrival, a thread unbroken across centuries of conquest and migration. The cemetery at Morro Amarillo still holds their ancestors' graves.

## Coffee to Cable Cars

For generations, Jardin's economy ran on the land. Farmers grew coffee, plantains, sugarcane, and beans on the slopes. Nuns in nearby convents made artisan crafts. Trout caught from the rivers that flow through the municipality, the San Juan, the Claro, the Dojurgo, became a regional dish. That agricultural life still continues, but tourism has transformed the town. With more than forty hotels operating, Jardin has several times the lodging capacity of larger municipalities. In 2007, the town completed two cable car lines. They were built partly to help farmers from mountain villages like La Selva, La Linda, and La Salada move their goods into town, partly to move tourists to the scenic viewpoints at the Alto de las Flores and La Garrucha on opposite ridges. The first one, to Cristo Rey, has been out of operation since around 2018; the second still runs. Either can also be reached on foot.

## What Surrounds the Town

The countryside around Jardin is mountainous cloud forest, laced with waterfalls and nature reserves. Where the La Herrera and La Bonita ravines merge, the water pools into a formation known as Charco del Corazon, the Heart Puddle, for its shape. The Cueva del Esplendor, the Cave of Splendor, sits ten kilometers from town and features a waterfall dropping through a hole in the cave's ceiling. The Yellow-eared Parrot Nature Reserve protects one of Colombia's most endangered bird species, the Ognorhynchus icterotis, which had nearly vanished before conservation efforts in this region helped it recover. Trout farms like Estadero El Arka let visitors catch their own meal. The Clara Rojas Museum holds nineteenth-century furniture and relics from the Colonizacion Antioquena era, the same settlement movement that brought Jardin's founders into the valley in the first place. The Casa de la Cultura, opened in 1880, houses the ashes of Colombian novelist Manuel Mejia Vallejo, whose work drew heavily on the Antioquian mountain life that Jardin has been slow to abandon.

## The Valley, Held Still

Rainfall is abundant year-round in the subtropical highland climate, and mornings often break with mist along the ridges. Temperatures average 19 degrees Celsius. The 224 square kilometers of the municipality include both the town and a ring of country fincas that grow coffee and raise cattle and host weekenders escaping Medellin. The municipal anthem is called Himno al Jardin. The national government designated Jardin a Pueblo Patrimonio in 2012, confirming what the residents had already decided for themselves generations earlier: that keeping a town as it was is itself a kind of work, and one worth doing. Three priests arrived here on the run from a civil war, told a group of farmers there should be a town, and walked into a valley full of white snakewood. What followed was a century and a half of not letting go.

## Flight Context

Located at 5.60 degrees North, 75.82 degrees West, in the southwest region of Antioquia, Colombia. The municipality covers 224 km squared, bounded by the San Juan River and a branch of the Western Cordillera (Cordillera Occidental). Neighboring municipalities: Andes, Jerico, and Tamesis to the north; Caldas department to the south. Elevation at the town is roughly 1,750 meters. Nearest major airport: Jose Maria Cordova International (SKRG) at Rionegro, serving Medellin, about 150 km northeast. Recommended viewing altitude: FL100 to FL150 for the San Juan valley and surrounding coffee slopes; the Cordillera Occidental rises toward 3,800 meters west of town. Morning typically offers the clearest visibility before afternoon cumulus buildups.
