# Coffee production in Colombia

How a parish priest's penance, a cooperative of half a million smallholders, and a mustachioed fictional farmer made Colombia the world's arabica powerhouse.

**Location:** 5.4717, -75.6817

**Categories:** Agriculture in Colombia, Coffee in Colombia, Coffee production by country, Economy of Colombia, Products with protected designation of origin

## Introduction

In the town of Salazar de las Palmas in the 1790s, a priest named Francisco Romero listened to confessions and handed out an unusual penance: plant coffee. Parishioners did as they were told. Generations later, the slopes above that town near the Venezuelan border were green with coffee trees, and the crop had begun its slow march west across the Colombian Andes. Today Colombia produces roughly 11.5 million 60-kilo bags a year - third in the world after Brazil and Vietnam, but first among producers of the arabica bean. The country's coffee story is less about volume than about structure: more than half a million farming families, most working a few hectares each, have built a national identity out of a fruit that takes five years from planting to first harvest.

## The First Bags

Coffee reached what is now Colombia long before anyone thought to export it. The oldest written mention comes from José Gumilla, a Jesuit missionary who recorded the crop in 1730 near where the Meta River meets the Orinoco, in his book The Orinoco Illustrated. Half a century later, the archbishop-viceroy Caballero y Góngora noted coffee growing near Girón in Santander and Muzo in Boyacá. The first documented commercial shipment left in 1808 - a hundred green bags exported from the port of Cúcuta, hauled down to the Venezuelan frontier. That northeastern corner of the country remained the coffee belt for most of the 19th century. But fragile export booms in tobacco, quinine, leather, and cattle all collapsed when international prices fell. When Colombian coffee prices dropped at the turn of the 20th century and the Thousand Days' War (1899-1902) devastated the eastern haciendas, the industry's center of gravity shifted west.

## Smallholder Country

The crisis of the big estates opened room for small farms. As early as 1875, family-scale coffee producers had begun to multiply in Santander and parts of Antioquia. In the Paisa region of the western cordillera - what Colombians know as Viejo Caldas, roughly today's Caldas, Quindío, and Risaralda - internal migrants colonized new land in the first decades of the 20th century. Coffee suited them. Unlike tobacco or sugar it required no major technology, permitted subsistence crops on the same plot, and kept the land productive year-round. By 1913 Colombia exported a million bags; by 1930, three million; by 1953, more than six. The country was, briefly, the world's second-largest coffee producer. Just as importantly, it was producing coffee with a different social geometry than its neighbors - grown in small farms rather than huge plantations, processed grain by grain, owned by the people who picked it.

## The Federation and Juan Valdez

That smallholder model needed representation in global markets. The National Federation of Coffee Growers, founded in 1927, became one of the most effective agricultural cooperatives in the world. Today it represents more than 500,000 producers, runs research institutes, sets quality standards, and negotiates with international buyers on behalf of farmers who would otherwise face wholesalers alone. In 1959 it introduced Juan Valdez - a fictional Paisa farmer in carriel, poncho, and aguadeño hat, accompanied by his mule Conchita. The character first appeared on American television in 1983 and became one of the most recognized advertising figures of the 20th century. The point of the campaign, first developed in 1981, was simple: distinguish 100% Colombian coffee from blended beans. The effort worked. In 2007 the European Union granted Colombian coffee a protected designation of origin, and in 2011 UNESCO inscribed the Coffee Cultural Landscape of the Eje Cafetero on its World Heritage List.

## Heat, Rust, and Adaptation

The coffee tree is a particular plant. It likes altitude, shade, a narrow band of temperature, rain that falls at the right moments. Climate change unsettles all of that. As temperatures rise, coffee leaf rust - a fungus that used to stay down in warmer lowlands - has pushed higher into the mountains, attacking plants that had no evolutionary reason to resist it. Colombia's response has run through the Federation's research arm Cenicafé, which has bred new varieties resistant to rust and better adapted to shifting rainfall. Farmers have experimented with shade management, soil practices, and price hedging. Not everyone has weathered the change equally: the 2013 Colombian coffee growers' strike, which shut down highways across the coffee region, exposed just how thin margins had become for small producers squeezed between climate volatility and international price swings.

## Indigenous Producers

Coffee's expansion into Indigenous territories began in the late 1800s as settlers pushed into the Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta, where the Arhuaco, Kankuamo, Kogui, and Wiwa peoples had long tended their own crops. The popular story - that settlers hired Indigenous labor to grow coffee for them - is only partly accurate. Arhuaco and Kogui farmers ran their own farms, sold their own beans, and today produce some of the most prized coffees in the country. Cafés Arhuaco and related cooperatives sell organic, shade-grown beans whose reputation travels on their origin story, not despite it.

## Flight Context

The Eje Cafetero (Coffee Axis) is centered near 5.47°N, 75.68°W in the Andean departments of Caldas, Quindío, and Risaralda. Recommended viewing altitude 8,000-12,000 ft AGL to see the terraced plantations on the western slopes of the Cordillera Central. Major airports include José María Córdova (SKRG) near Medellín, Matecaña (SKPE) in Pereira, and El Edén (SKAR) in Armenia. Flying from Bogotá westward over Ibagué, the land drops into the Cauca Valley then rises again into coffee country - an hour's flight that crosses three distinct ecological zones.
