# Cauca Valley montane forests

A 32,000 square kilometer ecoregion climbing the slopes above Colombia's Cauca River holds 200 butterflies, 500 birds, what may be the world's tallest palm, and the genetic traffic of two continents that met when Panama closed.

**Location:** 4.8390, -75.6310

**Categories:** Neotropical tropical and subtropical moist broadleaf forests, Ecoregions of Colombia, Andean forests, Geography of Antioquia Department, Geography of Caldas Department, Geography of Cauca Department, Geography of Huila Department, Geography of Quindío Department, Geography of Risaralda Department, Geography of Valle del Cauca Department

## Introduction

Three million years ago, give or take, the Isthmus of Panama finally closed. Animals and plants that had evolved separately on two continents walked, flew, and blew into each other's territory for the first time. The Cauca Valley sat directly in their path. North American oaks, alders, magnolias, and walnuts pushed south into the tropical Andes; South American species pushed north. What settled onto the slopes above the Cauca River became one of the most biologically intricate places on Earth, a belt of montane forest nearly 32,000 square kilometers in size where species have diverged at different elevations and along different slopes with a precision that still astonishes biologists.

## Between Two Ranges

The Cauca Valley montane forests, catalogued as NT0109, wrap the flanks of the Cauca River valley as it runs 600 kilometers from south to north between the Cordillera Central and the Cordillera Occidental of the Colombian Andes. The ecoregion covers 3,211,585 hectares. Below it, along the river itself, runs the narrower strip of Cauca Valley dry forests; above it, at the crests of the cordilleras, it gives way to the Northern Andean páramo. On the west it meets the Northwestern Andean montane forests; on the east the Magdalena Valley montane forests lie on the other side of the Cordillera Central. Geology set the stage: the Cordillera Central began forming in the late Paleozoic, the Cordillera Occidental at the end of the Mesozoic, and the whole three-range configuration, separated by two great valleys, arose from folding in the Miocene between 23 and 5 million years ago. The mountains reached present elevations in the Pliocene and Pleistocene. The Cordillera Central still carries snow-capped volcanoes above 5,000 meters, its soils volcanic and fertile; the Cordillera Occidental reaches 4,000 meters over laterite and limestone.

## The Tallest Palm in the World

Walk through these forests from 1,000 meters upward, and you move through a vertical patchwork of plant families. Below 1,500 meters, legumes of the Fabaceae family and the mulberry-family Moraceae dominate. From there up, laurels, melastomes, and madders take over. Above 3,000 meters the heaths and asters carry the canopy down to shrub. Through it all, rising improbably above the rest of the forest, stand the wax palms of the genus Ceroxylon. The quindío wax palm, Ceroxylon quindiuense, the national tree of Colombia, may be the tallest palm species in the world. Heights can reach 60 meters. In the Cocora Valley in Quindío, they rise out of misty cloud-forest pasture like lances. North American arrivals from the great three-million-year exchange, trees of the genera Alnus, Quercus, Talauma, and Juglans, are also present, differentiated here into local species after generations of isolation at different elevations. Orchids, bromeliads, and other epiphytes in the Gesneriaceae, Orchidaceae, Rubiaceae, and Melastomataceae families have diversified on an explosive scale.

## A Catalog of Feathers and Fur

More than 500 bird species have been recorded here. Twelve are restricted-range; four are found nowhere else on Earth. The Cauca guan, Penelope perspicax, survives only in a few remaining pockets of humid forest on both sides of the valley. Other species of note include the brown-banded antpitta, the moustached antpitta, the multicolored tanager, and the golden-plumed parakeet, Leptosittaca branickii, which moves regionally along the Andean slopes. Endangered birds include the black-and-chestnut eagle and the yellow-eared parrot. Among 120 mammal species, the spectacled bear and the mountain tapir face elevated conservation concern; the black-headed spider monkey, Geoffroy's spider monkey, the white-footed tamarin, and Handley's slender opossum are also endangered. Among the 92 recorded frog species, 60 are endemic to the region, with populations divided finely by elevation and slope. Endangered amphibians carry names that read like a small poem: Angelito stubfoot toad, Malvasa stubfoot toad, painted stubfoot toad, Sonson frog, Johnson's horned treefrog. Some 200 butterfly species have been counted; among them, the Pronophilina satyrs have produced closely related species at each elevation tier.

## The Forests That Are Not There Anymore

From 1,000 to roughly 2,000 meters most of the original forest has been cleared, almost entirely for agriculture. What remains at those lower elevations is a scatter of isolated fragments. The largest, the Yotoco Forest Reserve, covers just 519 hectares. The World Wide Fund for Nature ranks the ecoregion as Critical/Endangered. The reality of this loss is simple: most of the creatures listed above now survive only because of a few remaining higher-elevation forest blocks. That the ecoregion remains a center of endemism at all is partly a matter of vertical luck, steep terrain that was simply too hard for farming.

## Where the Forest Still Stands

Higher up, protected areas hold larger stretches. Farallones de Cali National Park protects steep, cloud-soaked slopes directly west of the city of Cali. Tatamá National Natural Park preserves a high, cloud-wrapped massif in the Cordillera Occidental. Los Nevados National Natural Park protects the snow volcanoes of the central range, including Nevado del Ruiz, Nevado del Tolima, and Nevado de Santa Isabel. Smaller regional reserves such as Ucumarí Regional Park in Risaralda complement the national-level protection, and the Cinturón Andino Cluster Biosphere Reserve, recognized by UNESCO, overlays part of the ecoregion. The forests outside the national parks continue to be logged. Large birds and mammals continue to be hunted illegally. And yet, in the forest blocks that remain, the Cauca guan and the golden-plumed parakeet still survive, alongside wax palms that may be the tallest on the planet. The forest is not gone. It is still there, watching the valley.

## Flight Context

Located at 4.84°N, 75.63°W in Colombia's coffee zone, covering the slopes of the Cauca River valley between the Cordillera Central and Cordillera Occidental. Nearest major airports include Pereira Matecaña (SKPE), Armenia El Edén (SKAR), and Cali Alfonso Bonilla Aragón (SKCL). The ecoregion spans elevations roughly 1,000 to 3,500 meters above sea level, with snow-capped volcanoes above 5,000 meters on the Cordillera Central. Mornings usually offer the clearest views of the long green valley, wax-palm ridges, and the Cocora Valley; afternoon convective buildups over the ranges are routine. Flight level 350 will show the narrow Cauca corridor with cordilleras rising steeply on both sides.
