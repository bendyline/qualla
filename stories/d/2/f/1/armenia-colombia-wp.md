# Armenia, Colombia

The capital of the Quindío coffee country, a jeep-parading city founded by a jaguar hunter that rebuilt itself in fifteen years after the catastrophic 1999 earthquake.

**Location:** 4.5389, -75.6725

**Categories:** Armenia, Colombia, Municipalities of Quindío Department, Coffee culture, Capitals of Colombian departments, UNESCO World Heritage Coffee Cultural Landscape

## Introduction

Jesús María Ocampo was a jaguar hunter. In the mountain valleys of Colombia's Quindío, he'd killed enough big cats — called tigres locally — to earn the nickname Tigrero, the tiger killer. On October 14, 1889, he paid a hundred pesos in gold coins to a man named Antonio Herrera for a patch of mountain ground, planning to build a fonda — a roadside trading post for other settlers drifting in from Anaime, Salento, Antioquia, and Manizales. Within six months the settlement had a hundred inhabitants. By November 30, 1889, the name Armenia had been put to a vote and adopted. A century and change later the city Ocampo founded sits at 1,483 metres in the Colombian coffee-growing axis, its historic centre part of a UNESCO World Heritage Coffee Cultural Landscape since 2011.

## How a Coffee Town Got Its Name

The name Armenia has attracted romantic explanations. One popular story claims it was chosen in sympathy with the Armenian people then suffering in the Hamidian massacres in the Ottoman Empire of 1894–97, or the Armenian genocide of 1915–23. The dates make this impossible: the Colombian town had already been named in 1889, years before those atrocities began. Other historians have argued the name refers to the historical Kingdom of Armenia from biblical antiquity; Colombian settlers frequently drew place names from the Bible. At the founding meeting Villa Holguín had been proposed, in honour of then-president Carlos Holguín Mallarino, but the colonists voted it down in favour of Armenia. What everyone agrees on is that the town grew fast and rough. Transport was almost entirely by mule, due to the steep terrain, until the first asphalt road reached Zarzal in the Valle del Cauca in 1927.

## La Ciudad Milagro

Armenia earned the nickname Ciudad Milagro — Miracle City — for the speed of its twentieth-century growth. The nine-block pedestrian spine through the city centre, lined with shops and restaurants connecting two central plazas, is one of its best-known urban features. But the "Miracle" took on a harder meaning at 1:19 p.m. on January 25, 1999, when a magnitude 6.2 earthquake struck with its epicentre 17 kilometres south of the city. Armenia was the hardest-hit place in Colombia; the quake was felt across Risaralda, Valle del Cauca, Tolima, Antioquia, and Cundinamarca, and the death toll reached roughly 1,000. Entire neighbourhoods collapsed. Over the following fifteen years the city rebuilt itself almost completely — new buildings constructed to higher seismic standards, public spaces re-imagined, and a renewed identity as a tourism and coffee destination. El Edén International Airport, ten kilometres from town on the road to La Tebaida, now runs daily flights to Bogotá, Cartagena, Medellín, Panama City, and Fort Lauderdale.

## The Coffee Belt

Armenia sits near the centre of the Colombian coffee-growing axis, or Eje Cafetero, alongside Pereira and Manizales. The traditional economy is still built on coffee, plantains, and bananas. Coffee farms — fincas — dot the hillsides in every direction; many now offer bed-and-breakfast accommodation and ecotourism packages, letting visitors walk among the glossy green rows, learn to pick a cherry, and taste the difference altitude makes. To the south, as the land drops toward sea level, the climate warms and the agricultural mix shifts. Country clubs, fishing resorts, and theme parks cluster in that warmer zone. The region's most famous natural landmark is the Cocora Valley, a short drive from Salento, where Colombia's national tree — the wax palm, Ceroxylon quindiuense, growing over 60 metres tall — stands in dramatic scattered groves against mist-hung green hills. Los Nevados National Park opens to the north, where central Colombia's highest peaks rise above 4,500 metres into perpetual snow.

## Yipao and Chapoleras

Armenia's folk traditions are lived, not curated. After World War II the town received a shipment of surplus American jeeps — designated for the difficult mountain paths the mules used to handle. The jeeps never really retired. The Yipao, literally a "jeep-load," became a parade in which overloaded jeeps stacked absurdly high with coffee sacks, plantains, furniture, and people roll through the streets on festival days. The Chapoleras beauty pageant, featuring women in late-19th-century coffee-harvesting dress, is nationally known. The city is the home of "cuyabros," a demonym derived from the cuyabra, or totumo — the dried shell of a local bottle gourd once used as a container and now preserved as hand-painted folk craft. The traditional paisa leather handbag, the carriel, is still made and sold here, along with crafts in guadua, the enormous local bamboo that frames buildings and sculptures throughout the city.

## The Climbing Cyclists of Quindío

Ask a European cycling fan where Colombia's best climbers come from, and Quindío's mountains will be part of the answer. The punishing gradients around Armenia have produced generations of world-class climbing cyclists — riders who grow up on roads that would break most training programs. Many have gone on to compete in the Tour de France and other grand tours. The city's sports centres — Centenario Stadium and the Coliseo del Café — host national and international football, including matches of Deportes Quindío, the city's professional football club. The University of Quindío, in the northern part of the city, anchors regional higher education. And the Quimbaya Museum, designed by celebrated Colombian architect Rogelio Salmona, holds permanent exhibitions of pre-Columbian ceramic and gold pieces — a reminder that long before Tigrero arrived with his gold coins, these mountains had their own distinguished dynasty.

## Flight Context

Armenia sits at approximately 4.54°N, 75.67°W, at 1,483 metres elevation in the Colombian coffee-growing axis. El Edén International Airport (SKAR, IATA AXM) is approximately 10 km south on the road to La Tebaida. The city is about 290 km west of Bogotá and is flanked by the Central Andes to the east. Average temperature range 16–22 °C. From altitude, look for the characteristic rolling green hills of coffee country, the dramatic wax palms of the Cocora Valley to the north-east near Salento, and the snow-capped peaks of Los Nevados National Park further north. Weather can be changeable; afternoon convective buildups over the cordillera are common.
