# Calarcá

Named for a Pijao war chief, built by coffee, rebuilt after a 1999 earthquake, Calarcá is a Colombian hill town where poets and Jeep Willys share the same balconies.

**Location:** 4.5292, -75.6408

## Introduction

The town was named for a man who died fighting the Spanish. According to the tradition passed down in the Quindío hills, the Pijao chief Calarcá fell on the cliffs of Peñas Blancas, killed by an Indigenous man who had converted to Catholicism, a battle over which people the land would belong to. Four centuries later a ring of coffee farms would climb those same slopes. The town that took Calarcá's name wears it as a kind of inheritance, its balconies crowded with geraniums, its streets full of the sound of old Jeep Willys climbing toward the farms above.

## The Cacique's Cliffs

Peñas Blancas, whose bright white walls come from calcite and limestone, rises on the western slopes of the Cordillera Central just outside town. The crag holds three vertical rock faces, a tangle of solutional caves, and rock shelters said, by locals who have passed the story for generations, to hide a treasure concealed by the Pijao chief before his last battle. Treasure or not, the cliffs are a landmark visible from Armenia and Calarcá both. They rise eighty meters where climbers set their anchors. A forty-meter zip line crosses the air above camping grounds and a pair of ecological trails, one of 3.2 kilometers and one of 9. From the foot of the cliffs you can see the red-tiled roofs of Calarcá laid out against the folds of the mountains, a town where geology, history, and myth keep company.

## Coffee and the Antioquian Wave

Calarcá was founded on June 29, 1886, by Segundo Henao and Román María Valencia, explorers from Salento who had come south during the closing phase of the Antioquian Colonization, the great nineteenth-century migration that pushed families out of overcrowded Antioquia into the mountain valleys to the south. The first economy was mining and commerce. Coffee arrived in force around the 1930s and changed everything. By the middle of the twentieth century Calarcá sat squarely in the center of Colombia's coffee-growing axis, the Eje Cafetero, and in 2011 UNESCO added the wider region, including Calarcá, to the World Heritage List as the Coffee Cultural Landscape. Coffee wealth paid for balconies and music, for schools and cafés. It also paid for poets. Calarcá earned the nickname La Villa del Cacique in homage to its writers, and the town still hosts the annual Encuentro Nacional de Escritores Luis Vidales, named for the poet Luis Vidales (1904–1990), who was born there.

## The Yipao Parade

Every year the town hosts the Desfile del Yipao, and nothing else in Colombia is quite like it. Jeep Willys, the flat-fendered American four-wheel drive vehicles left over from the Second World War, became the essential workhorse of the coffee zone after the 1950s: light enough for mountain roads, tough enough for the harvest. Owners here decorate theirs with elaborate arrangements of coffee sacks, plantain stalks, furniture, livestock, even whole families, stacked impossibly high, and drive them in a procession through the main streets. Some lift the front wheels off the ground on the steepest incline to show how well the old jeeps still balance. Spectators line the sidewalks with cups of tinto, the small black coffees of the region. It is a celebration of coffee, and of the particular engineering the coffee life required.

## After the Shaking

On January 25, 1999, a magnitude 6.2 earthquake struck the Eje Cafetero, centered near Armenia, four kilometers west. In Calarcá the ground rolled and many buildings collapsed. The wider disaster killed more than a thousand people and injured thousands more across the region. In Calarcá the town lost part of its colonial and republican architecture, old houses of bahareque and tapia pisada whose walls could not absorb the shock. Not all was gone. The Casa de los Téllez, the Hospital la Misericordia building, and the Rafael Uribe Uribe school still stand, along with stretches of balconied streets whose wood is painted in the bright oranges, yellows, and blues characteristic of the region. What was rebuilt was built with some of the same sensibility. The town remembers.

## A Garden of Wings

Just outside town lies the Quindío Botanical Garden, opened in 1985. Its centerpiece is a butterfly house shaped, from above, like a butterfly: 640 square meters of enclosure that holds more than 1,200 species of butterflies native to Colombia. Visitors walk through curtains of blue morpho and yellow sulphur, with clearwing and swallowtail brushing past their shoulders. The garden also holds collections of medicinal plants, heliconias, and cacti, grown alongside ornamental beds. Between the butterfly house and the cliffs of Peñas Blancas, between coffee farms at a thousand meters and the high Campanario páramo above 3,600 meters, Calarcá sits in the middle of one of the most vertically compressed landscapes in Colombia. Poets come for the quiet. Climbers come for the rock. Everyone else comes for the coffee.

## Flight Context

Located at 4.53°N, 75.64°W in Colombia's Quindío Department, about 4 kilometers east of Armenia on the western slopes of the Cordillera Central. The nearest airport is El Edén International (SKAR) in Armenia, about 10 km west. Pereira Matecaña (SKPE) lies 40 km to the north. Elevation in the urban area is roughly 1,530 meters, climbing to 3,667 meters at El Campanario. Expect rapid afternoon buildups over the ridges; mornings generally offer the clearest views of the red-tiled town set against the dark green cordillera and the white cliffs of Peñas Blancas.
