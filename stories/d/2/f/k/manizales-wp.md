# Manizales

Capital of Caldas department, Manizales was founded in 1849 by twenty Antioquian settlers and grew into Colombia's coffee and university city, perched between the Nevado del Ruiz volcano and the Cauca Valley.

**Location:** 5.0675, -75.5100

**Categories:** Cities, Colombia, Coffee region, University towns, Volcanic landscapes

## Introduction

The topography was called abrupt by the first surveyors, and the word has stuck. Manizales rides a narrow ridgeline of the Cordillera Central in central Colombia, with steep drops on both sides and a 5,321-meter active volcano, Nevado del Ruiz, looming to the southeast. That the city exists at all is a story of persistence. Twenty Antioquian settlers, remembered as the Expedición de los Veinte, founded it on October 12, 1849, after walking south from Neira and Salamina in search of land. What they chose was dramatic: a mountain spine high enough to escape the heat, fertile enough for coffee, and exposed enough that every building afterward had to be engineered for the shaking and the mudslides.

## The Coffee Axis

Manizales anchors the northern end of the Eje Cafetero, Colombia's coffee-growing axis, together with nearby Pereira and Armenia. The city's core economy has revolved around coffee for more than a century, and the surrounding fincas produce some of the country's most prized Arabica. Institutions like Cenicafé - the National Coffee Research Center - and the Departmental Committee of Coffee Growers of Caldas make this a coffee capital in more than the marketing sense. Almacafé handles processing. Threshers, cooperatives, and exporters form a local industry that supports the rural economy across the department. In 2010, the World Bank ranked Manizales the easiest Colombian city to do business in, citing efficient procedures and reliable infrastructure - an unusual distinction for a place built on a knife-edge ridge.

## Building on a Moving Mountain

Because the city sits on the Cordillera Central, it sits in seismic country. Mudslides and earthquakes have shaped architecture here as much as aesthetics. Buildings must flex. Retaining walls hold up the slopes. The main thoroughfares - Avenidas Santander, Paralela, and Kevin Ángel - all run parallel along the ridgeline because running east-west follows the spine, while going north-south means sharp descents into valleys. In 2009, the city added a new piece of infrastructure: the Manizales Aerial Tramway, a gondola lift built by Italian manufacturer Leitner Ropeways that runs 2.1 kilometers between downtown and the regional bus terminal. A 2013 extension added service to Villamaría. The system can carry 2,100 passengers per hour and has become both a commute and a view - the city's best aerial tour costs the price of a ticket.

## A Cathedral and a Theatre Festival

The Cathedral of Manizales, a Gothic Revival church whose spire reaches 113 meters, is often called the third-tallest in Latin America. Built by the city itself over decades, its stained glass and gold-leaf canopy drew worshippers and architecture students alike - until the magnitude 7.4 earthquake of August 10, 2026, which killed five people in Manizales and brought one of the cathedral's towers down onto the nave. Steel has been pledged for its reconstruction. Across town, Plaza de Bolívar holds the government buildings of Caldas department, of which Manizales is capital. The Manizales International Theatre Festival, founded in 1968 by Carlos Ariel Betancur, is among the oldest and most important theatre gatherings in Latin America. The Manizales Jazz Festival draws musicians from around the world every September and October. The Feria de Manizales, which grew out of the city's centenary celebrations and was first held in full in 1955, runs each January with parades, bullfights (banned nationwide from July 2027), horseback riding, trova concerts, and the International Coffee Beauty Pageant, a contest now held annually since 1957 that draws contestants from coffee-producing countries worldwide.

## Universities, Football, and the Volcano

Locals claim more universities per capita than anywhere else in Colombia. Seven institutions, about 30,000 students. Universidad Nacional, Universidad de Caldas, Universidad Católica, Universidad Autónoma, and others share the young population that gives Manizales its café-filled, music-loud character. The football club Once Caldas plays at the 31,611-seat Palogrande Stadium and famously won the South American Copa Libertadores in 2004, beating Boca Juniors in the final. Four Colombian league titles round out their trophy case. Over all of it looms Nevado del Ruiz. Its 1985 eruption triggered a lahar that destroyed the town of Armero to the east, killing more than 23,000 people - one of the deadliest volcanic disasters of the twentieth century. The mountain remains active. The Colombian Geological Service watches it closely. On clear mornings its snowy cone is the first thing you see from any Manizales window.

## Flying Into Ruiz Country

La Nubia Airport (SKMZ) serves the city with a 1,400-meter runway but is often closed by fog. Pereira's Matecaña International (SKPE) and Armenia's El Edén (SKAR) provide alternates. The new Coffee Airport (Aerocafé) in nearby Palestina, at 1,525 meters, began construction in April 2026; its first phase has a 1,460-meter runway, to be lengthened in later stages for larger aircraft. On approach, the key visual features are the ridge itself, the 113-meter cathedral spire, and the snow-capped cone of Nevado del Ruiz to the southeast. In 2011, Manizales was one of the host cities of the FIFA U-20 World Cup.

## Flight Context

The 113 m cathedral spire is a visible landmark (one of its towers collapsed in the August 2026 earthquake). Afternoon convective cloud typical over cordilleras; morning flights preferred.
