# Patía, Cauca

A hot valley in southern Cauca where descendants of runaway enslaved Africans built free towns, told of a lake wept from a hill, and made a rhythm called bambuco patiano their own.

**Location:** 2.1142, -76.9831

**Categories:** cities, afro-colombian, palenques, music, colombia, cauca

## Introduction

Springs rise on Cerro Manzanillo and feed the Valerio ravine below. Local tradition says the water is not geology but memory - the tears of enslaved Africans who escaped their captors, climbed the hill hoping to glimpse their ancestral Africa, and, unable to see it, wept until their tears formed a lake. It is a story told in the Patia Valley, a hot depression in southern Cauca department where the Panamerican highway cuts through palenques - self-liberated Black towns - that were founded on exactly that grief and the refusal to live with it.

## A Valley Between the Ranges

The Patia Valley is framed by the Western and Central Andean cordilleras, about 82 kilometers by road southwest of the Cauca department capital of Popayan. Its administrative centre is the town of El Bordo, which sits at 910 meters above sea level with an average temperature of 23 degrees Celsius. The municipality covers 732 square kilometers, rising from the valley floor into the Manzanillo hill that hems it in. The Patia River runs north to south and extends into the Narino department, one of the largest rivers draining this stretch of South America's Pacific coast. Where it passes through the valley center, the riverbank forms one of Colombia's dry-forest ecoregions, the Patia Valley dry forests, an unusual patch of arid vegetation in a country famous for green.

## The Palenques

Before the Spanish arrived, the valley belonged to the Bojoles, Chapachugas, Sindaguas, and the Patias themselves - indigenous peoples whose names now survive mostly in archaeology and placenames. Colonial violence decimated them. Into the emptied landscape came two new populations: Catholic missionaries building doctrinal chapels, and enslaved Africans brought to work the farms and gold mines along the Patia, Guachicono, and Juanambu rivers. When enslaved people escaped, they did what escaped people across the Americas did - they built palenques, fortified free towns hidden in terrain the slavers could not reach. The Patia Valley palenques thrived along the rivers, exploiting the same gold mines their former captors had worked, now for themselves. Their descendants remain a large part of the valley's population today.

## Gold, Watermelons, and Coal

The old economies of the valley are still legible in its villages. In the depression known as The Hoyo, near the Quilcace and Esmita rivers, artisanal coal mining continues where gold panning once dominated. Around the town of Patia - reached 30 minutes from El Bordo along the Panamerican highway - the specialty is watermelon and kumis, the thick fermented dairy drink. The township of El Estrecho sits at a fork in the highway where travelers choose between Balboa, popularly called 'the Patia balcony' for its long view, and Mojarras, where another branch heads south toward Narino. Past Mojarras lies Mercaderes, the valley's southern gate.

## Bambuco Patiano

Bambuco is Colombia's national rhythm, a 6/8 time that swings like a rocking chair. The Patia Valley has its own form, bambuco patiano, traditionally in a minor key, and it is more than music. It is a whole system of song, dance, and improvised theater that locals use to recount weddings, mock the government, mourn the dead, lament the community's health problems, and needle their neighbors. Performers play it on stringed instruments - violin, guitar - joined by drums, percussions, and cununos, plus the charango and the valley's own violin patiano, once built by self-liberated musicians from local materials such as guadua cane and totumo gourds. The Luis Angel Arango Library in Bogota has displayed a 19th-century Patia violin.

## Women Who Keep the Valley

Patia's economy rests, as in so many Afro-Colombian rural communities, on women's labor. Subsistence farming - corn, plantains, cassava, the basic foodstuffs the valley calls comida de pancoger - is mostly done by women to feed their families, and the totumo tree underpins much of the local economy. Women also carry the valley's songs: the Cantaoras del Patia, a group of Afro-Colombian women singers with more than 30 years on stage, perform its arrullos, alabaos and bambuco patiano.
