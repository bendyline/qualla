# Patía, Cauca

A hot valley in southern Cauca where descendants of runaway enslaved Africans built free towns, wept a lake from a hill, and invented a rhythm called bambuco patiano.

**Location:** 2.1142, -76.9831

**Categories:** cities, afro-colombian, palenques, music, colombia, cauca

## Introduction

On the rock face of Cerro Manzanillo, springs still flow. Local tradition says the water is not geology but memory - the tears of enslaved Africans who escaped the colonial plantations of the Cauca, climbed the hill looking east hoping to glimpse their homeland, and, seeing only more Colombia, wept until their crying filled the streambed of the Valerio ravine below. It is a story you will hear across the Patia Valley, a hot depression in southern Cauca department where the Panamerican highway cuts through palenques - self-liberated Black towns - that were founded on exactly that grief and the refusal to live with it.

## A Valley Between the Ranges

The Patia Valley is framed by the Western and Central Andean cordilleras, about 82 kilometers south of the Cauca department capital of Popayan. Its administrative centre is the town of El Bordo, which sits at 910 meters above sea level with an average temperature of 23 degrees Celsius. The municipality covers 732 square kilometers, rising from the valley floor into the Manzanillo hill that hems it in. The Patia River - named for the valley or the valley for the river, nobody remembers which came first - runs north to south and extends into the Narino department, the largest river draining this stretch of the South American Pacific. Where it passes through the valley center, the riverbank forms one of Colombia's dry-forest ecoregions, the Patia Valley dry forests, an unusual patch of arid vegetation in a country famous for green.

## The Palenques

Before the Spanish arrived, the valley belonged to the Bojoles, Chapachugas, Sindaguas, and the Patias themselves - indigenous peoples whose names now survive mostly in archaeology and placenames. Colonial violence decimated them. Into the emptied landscape came two new populations: Catholic missionaries building doctrinal chapels, and enslaved Africans brought to work the farms and gold mines along the Patia, Guachicono, and Juanambu rivers. When enslaved people escaped, they did what escaped people across the Americas did - they built palenques, fortified free towns hidden in terrain the slavers could not reach. The Patia Valley palenques thrived along the rivers, exploiting the same gold mines their former captors had worked, now for themselves. Their descendants are the majority of the valley's population today.

## Gold, Watermelons, and Coal

The old economies of the valley are still legible in its villages. In the depression known as The Hoyo, near the Quilcace and Esmita rivers, artisanal coal mining continues where gold panning once dominated. Around the town of Patia - reached 30 minutes from El Bordo along the Panamerican highway - the specialty is watermelon and kumis, the thick fermented dairy drink you can buy roadside in clay cups. The township of El Estrecho sits at a fork in the highway where travelers choose between Balboa, popularly called 'the Patia balcony' for its long view, and Mojarras, where another branch heads south toward Narino. Past Mojarras lies Mercaderes, the valley's southern gate.

## Bambuco Patiano

Bambuco is Colombia's national rhythm, a 6/8 time that swings like a rocking chair. The Patia Valley is its cradle - specifically, a form called bambuco patiano that is more than music. It is a whole system of song, dance, and improvised theater that locals use to recount weddings, mock their politicians, mourn the dead, satirize the national health system, and needle their neighbors. Performers play it on stringed instruments - violin, guitar - joined by drums, percussions, and cununos, plus instruments specific to the valley: the violin patiano, the charango, and the charrasca, a scraper made from the hollow shell of the totumo fruit that grows everywhere here. The Luis Angel Arango Library in Bogota keeps a 19th-century Patia violin on display. The instrument still makes the instrument new every weekend somewhere in El Bordo.

## Women Who Keep the Valley

Patia's economy rests, as in so many Afro-Colombian rural communities, on women's labor. Subsistence farming - corn, plantains, cassava, the basic foodstuffs the valley calls comida de pancoger - is mostly done by women to feed their families, while the cash economy of cattle and transport moves along the highway. This gendered division is old and unglamorous, but it is also the reason that the music, the cooking, and the oral history of the palenques have survived into the 21st century. Walk the plaza of El Bordo on a Sunday morning and you will hear aunties teaching their nieces exactly how to pound the rhythm out of a charrasca carved from the fruit of the tree next door.
