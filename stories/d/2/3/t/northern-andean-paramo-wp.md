# Northern Andean Páramo

Between the treeline and the snow, a ribbon of grassland runs along the Andes where about 86% of the flowering plants are found only in the páramo.

**Location:** 2.3440, -76.3840

**Categories:** Ecoregions, Páramos, Andes, Colombia, Ecuador, Conservation

## Introduction

There is a band of the Andes where the trees give out and the snow has not yet begun. For well over a thousand vertical meters, between roughly the cloud forest and the glacier line, grass and wet peat and strange, woolly rosette plants take over. This is páramo, and the Northern Andean version of it - stretching from the cordilleras of north-central Colombia south into central Ecuador - holds some of the most distinctive flora on the planet. Temperatures can drop below freezing at night and climb toward 30 °C by noon. In a single afternoon you can walk through rain, snow, fog, and hard tropical sun. About eighty-six percent of the flowering plants growing here grow nowhere but the páramo.

## A Sky Island Archipelago

The Northern Andean páramo ecoregion covers about 3,004,386 hectares - not a continuous blanket, but a scatter of peaks, ridges, and high basins separated by warmer valleys of cloud forest. Ecologists call these sky islands. Near the end of the last ice age, about 14,000 years ago, when temperatures here were six to seven degrees colder than today, the páramo belt reached down to around 2,000 meters and spread into a connected ocean of high grassland across the northern Andes. When the Holocene warmed, the belt climbed uphill, lost ground, and broke apart into the archipelago that exists now. Each isolated high peak became its own laboratory. Each evolved its own endemic species. Walking from one páramo to another is, genetically, like walking from one island to another: same genera, different species, the same story told in slightly different words.

## The Weather Is a Single Afternoon

The Köppen classification for the Northern Andean páramo is Cfb - warm temperate, fully humid, warm summer. That tidy label does not prepare anyone for the reality. Humidity comes as rain, as persistent cloud, as horizontal fog that sweeps up the valleys from the cloud forest below. Annual rainfall runs from about 500 mm in drier sectors to about 3,000 mm where the trade winds lift air straight up a cordillera face. Temperatures plunge after sunset. In the middle of a single afternoon a páramo walker can stand in hard sunshine, watch fog roll in over a ridge, get hailed on, and emerge back into cobalt sky - all before starting the descent. The plants have adapted accordingly: dense wool on leaves, mantles of dead leaves kept as insulation, growth habits that hug the ground.

## Rosettes, Frogs, and Bogotá Rails

The signature plants of the páramo are frailejones - Espeletia species with tall, woolly trunks crowned in silver rosettes, looking like small yetis frozen mid-step. Below them grow tussock grasses of Calamagrostis and Festuca, clumps of small herbs, and in wetter patches cushion plants that hold water like sponges. The fauna is stranger still. Endangered amphibians alone include the harlequin frogs Atelopus arthuri, A. bomolochos, A. ebenoides, A. exiguus, and A. muisca - that last one named for the indigenous people of the Bogotá savanna. Mountain tapirs (Tapirus pinchaque) browse the edges of the grassland. Bogotá rails (Rallus semiplumbeus), secretive and endangered, hunt through high wetlands. Black-breasted pufflegs - tiny, critically endangered hummingbirds - feed at high-mountain flowers.

## The Water Tower

The páramo does work that extends far beyond its own borders. Its soils - andosols, histosols, rich in organic matter, peat-like at the middle elevations - absorb rainfall and release it slowly through the year. That slow release is what feeds the rivers of the Colombian and Ecuadorian highlands and the lowlands they drain into. Much of Bogotá's water comes from the Chingaza páramo to the east, and Quito also draws on páramo water. Strip away the cushion plants and the peat, as overgrazing and cultivation have done in places, and the rivers begin to flash-flood in the rainy season and disappear in the dry. The World Wildlife Fund's assessment rated the ecoregion Relatively Stable / Intact, but the pressure is real. Potato and bean cultivation requires draining the boggy soils. Cattle compress the peat. Pine plantations change the water budget.

## Protected Territory

Much of the best páramo is inside protected areas. On the Colombian side: Chingaza National Natural Park, Sumapaz Páramo National Park - protecting part of what is often called the world's largest páramo - Sierra Nevada del Cocuy Chita o Guican, Lake Iguaque, Los Nevados, and Las Hermosas. On the Ecuadorian side: the Cayambe-Coca Ecological Reserve. The Cinturón Andino Cluster Biosphere Reserve ties several units together. In Colombia, a 2016 Constitutional Court ruling (C-035) struck down the exceptions that had let mining continue in páramos. Humans have shared the páramo since pre-Columbian times, and since Spanish colonial times mostly as graziers of cattle and sheep; cutting and burning have helped keep some of the grassland treeless. The páramo that looks natural is partly a human landscape. That is not a problem to solve; it is a fact to work with.

## Flight Context

The Northern Andean páramo is not a single point but a ribbon of high grassland running along the Colombian and Ecuadorian cordilleras from roughly 8°N to 2°S. A useful reference point is the southern portion near the Colombian Massif at 2.34°N, 76.38°W. Páramo begins at about 3,000-3,500 m and runs up to the snowline near 5,000 m. Major nearby airports include Bogotá El Dorado (SKBO), Quito Mariscal Sucre (SEQM), and Popayán (SKPP). Mountain weather: expect rapid cloud formation through the day; views are often clearest early in the morning.
