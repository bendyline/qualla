# Francisco Jose de Caldas

The self-taught Colombian scientist who invented a way to measure altitude with boiling water, mentored with Humboldt, and was executed by Spanish firing squad with a Greek letter chalked on his cell wall.

**Location:** 2.4427, -76.6114

**Categories:** historical-figure, scientist, colombia, independence, popayan

## Introduction

Before they shot him, Francisco Jose de Caldas wrote a single Greek letter on the wall of his cell: theta. In classical Athens, theta stood for *thanatos* - death. His countrymen interpreted it as a final sentence: *Oh larga y negra partida!* - Oh long and dark departure! He was 48 years old. He had been a lawyer against his will, a merchant by his father's order, a geographer by obsession, an inventor by accident, and an independence movement journalist by choice. General Pablo Morillo, the Spanish commander sent to reconquer New Granada, signed his execution order on October 28, 1816. When the people watching pleaded for the scientist's life, Morillo said: *Spain does not need savants*. Two centuries later, the country Caldas helped imagine named its university after him, its highway department's elevation surveys after him, and a whole Colombian department after him. Spain's opinion of savants turned out to be irrelevant.

## The Reluctant Lawyer

Caldas was born October 4, 1768, in Popayan - a colonial town tucked into the Andes of what is now the Cauca Department of Colombia. His parents, Jose de Caldas and Vicenta Tenorio, were modestly prosperous and prosperous-minded. His cousin Camilo Torres Tenorio, who would become one of the most important figures of the independence movement, studied with him at the Seminary of Popayan. So did Francisco Antonio Zea. The social circles of colonial South America were small; the independence circles smaller still. In 1788 his father pressed him to move to Santafe - present-day Bogota - and study law at the Colegio del Rosario. He earned his degree in 1793. He hated every minute of it. What Caldas wanted was mathematics, astronomy, and natural history, and he gave the law back as soon as he could, returning to Popayan to run the family business, at which he was - by every surviving account - terrible.

## The Broken Thermometer

On his business trips to Bogota, Caldas found ways to avoid business. He carried a barometer, a thermometer, and a compass everywhere he went, measuring everything he could measure. He mapped the course of the Prado river in Tolima. He wrote notes on medicinal trees. He described the pre-Hispanic stone hieroglyphs in Aipe and the monumental statues at San Agustin. He conducted experiments to determine whether insects were venomous. Then one day a thermometer broke. Working with what remained of the equipment, Caldas realized something: the boiling point of water changes with altitude, and if you can measure the boiling point precisely you can calculate elevation. He built the first hypsometer - an altitude-measuring device using water temperature - and used it to triangulate elevations across the northern Andes. It was a genuine innovation, and it came out of the mountains of colonial Popayan, produced by a merchant's son who was supposed to be selling things.

## The Meeting with Humboldt

On December 31, 1801, Caldas traveled to Ibarra, in what is now Ecuador, to meet Alexander von Humboldt and Aime Bonpland - the Prussian and French naturalists then conducting one of the most ambitious scientific expeditions in history. Humboldt had every reason to dismiss a local amateur. He did the opposite. Humboldt was impressed - genuinely impressed - by Caldas's altitude data and his mapping work, and the three men did fieldwork together in the country around Quito. Caldas wanted to continue with Humboldt's expedition. Humboldt wanted him to come. But circumstance kept Caldas in the Andes while Humboldt moved north. The correspondence they maintained afterward placed Caldas in the small circle of South American scientists that European natural philosophy took seriously. He returned to his memoirs, his measurements, and his writing.

## The Diario Politico

When the independence movement crystallized in 1810, Caldas was ready. He founded the *Diario Politico de Santa Fe* - the Political Diary of Santa Fe - and used it to defend the new junta and argue for independence from Spain. During the first republican period, he served as an engineer, colonel in the revolutionary army, architect of powder mills and gun factories, and instructor at the Academy of Engineers in Medellin. He designed artillery. He minted coins. When the Spanish Reconquista arrived in 1815-1816, bringing General Pablo Morillo and his pacification campaign, Caldas was on the list. He was captured, tried, and sentenced to death.

## The Wall

*Espana no necesita sabios.* Morillo's sentence is quoted in every Colombian history textbook because it captured the imperial project in a single phrase. Whether or not Morillo ever said it exactly is debated. What is certain is that on October 28, 1816, Caldas was shot in Bogota. Before the firing squad, in the cell where he spent his final hours, he chalked a theta on the wall. His body was buried at the Church of Veracruz, later consecrated as the Panteon Nacional. Decades later, after independence, his remains were moved to the Panteon de los Proceres in Popayan - back to the town where he had been born, had broken that thermometer, had measured the altitude of every church steeple he passed.

## The Department of Caldas

Caldas's legacy ripples outward. The Colombian department of Caldas, carved from the central Andes, is named for him. The Francisco Jose de Caldas District University - a major public institution in Bogota - carries his name. Colciencias, the national science agency, offers a Francisco Jose de Caldas scholarship for Colombian doctoral students. His face once appeared on the 20-peso banknote. Colombian engineers call him the father of Colombian engineering. And his hypsometer, the invention born of a broken thermometer, found continued use for a century as a field tool for cartographers measuring peaks without any easier option. Caldas never lived to see the country whose independence he helped to launch. But the work he did in the spare hours of a merchant's life outlasted the empire that killed him.

## Flight Context

Caldas was born in Popayan (2.44 degrees N, 76.61 degrees W, elevation 1,738 m), capital of Cauca Department, where his remains now rest in the Panteon de los Proceres. He was executed in Bogota (4.60 degrees N, 74.08 degrees W). Popayan's Guillermo Leon Valencia Airport (SKPP) serves the birthplace city; Bogota's El Dorado International (SKBO) serves the execution site. The terrain Caldas mapped - central and southern Colombian Andes - is visible on any flight between Bogota and Popayan, or between Cali (SKCL) and Bogota. From a cruising altitude over the Cauca Valley, the cordilleras he measured stand in clear relief on both sides.
