# Francisco Jose de Caldas

The self-taught Colombian scientist who devised a way to measure altitude with boiling water, met and worked with Humboldt, and, legend says, drew a Greek letter on the wall before a Spanish firing squad shot him.

**Location:** 2.4427, -76.6114

**Categories:** historical-figure, scientist, colombia, independence, popayan

## Introduction

Before they shot him, the story goes, Francisco Jose de Caldas drew a single Greek letter on the wall: theta. In Greek usage, theta could stand for *thanatos* - death. His countrymen interpreted it as a final sentence: *Oh larga y negra partida!* - Oh long and dark departure! He was 48 years old. He had been a lawyer against his will, a merchant by his father's order, a geographer by obsession, an inventor by accident, and an independence movement journalist by choice. General Pablo Morillo, the Spanish commander sent to reconquer New Granada, ordered his execution in October 1816. When people pleaded for the scientist's life, Morillo reportedly answered: *Spain does not need savants*. Two centuries later, the country Caldas helped imagine has named a university after him, and a whole Colombian department. Spain's opinion of savants turned out to be irrelevant.

## The Reluctant Lawyer

Caldas was born October 4, 1768, in Popayan - a colonial town tucked into the Andes of what is now the Cauca Department of Colombia. His parents, Jose de Caldas and Vicenta Tenorio, were modestly prosperous and prosperous-minded. His cousin Camilo Torres Tenorio, who would become one of the most important figures of the independence movement, studied with him at the Seminary of Popayan. So did Francisco Antonio Zea. The social circles of colonial South America were small; the independence circles smaller still. In 1788 his father pressed him to move to Santafe - present-day Bogota - and study law at the Colegio del Rosario. He earned his bachelor's degree in 1793. He had little love for it. What Caldas wanted was mathematics, astronomy, and natural history, and he gave the law back as soon as he could, returning to Popayan to run the family business, at which he had little success.

## The Broken Thermometer

On his business trips to Bogota, Caldas found ways to avoid business. He carried a barometer, a thermometer, and a compass everywhere he went, measuring everything he could measure. He mapped the course of the Prado river in Tolima. He wrote notes on medicinal trees. He described the pre-Hispanic stone hieroglyphs in Aipe and the monumental statues at San Agustin. He conducted experiments to determine whether insects were venomous. Then one day a thermometer broke. Working with what remained of the equipment, Caldas worked out for himself, independently of European scientists, that the boiling point of water changes with altitude, and if you can measure the boiling point precisely you can calculate elevation. He devised a hypsometer - an instrument that finds altitude from the temperature at which water boils - and used it to measure elevations across the northern Andes. It was a genuine innovation, and it came out of the mountains of colonial Popayan, produced by a merchant's son who was supposed to be selling things.

## The Meeting with Humboldt

On December 31, 1801, Caldas traveled to Ibarra, in what is now Ecuador, to meet Alexander von Humboldt and Aime Bonpland - the Prussian and French naturalists then conducting one of the most ambitious scientific expeditions in history. Humboldt had every reason to dismiss a local amateur. He did the opposite. Humboldt was impressed - genuinely impressed - by Caldas's altitude data and his mapping work, and the three men did fieldwork together in the country around Quito. Caldas wanted to join Humboldt's expedition, but Humboldt chose the young Quito aristocrat Carlos Montufar instead and moved on toward Peru without him. The botanist Jose Celestino Mutis then brought Caldas into the Royal Botanical Expedition, and he went on to direct the Astronomical Observatory in Santa Fe. He returned to his memoirs, his measurements, and his writing.

## The Diario Politico

When the independence movement crystallized in 1810, Caldas was ready. With Joaquin Camacho he founded the *Diario Politico de Santa Fe* - the Political Diary of Santa Fe - first published on August 27, 1810, and used it to defend the new junta and argue for independence from Spain. During the first republican period, he served as an engineer, colonel in the revolutionary army, architect of powder mills and gun factories, and instructor at the Academy of Engineers in Medellin. He designed artillery. He minted coins. When the Spanish Reconquista arrived in 1815-1816, bringing General Pablo Morillo and his pacification campaign, Caldas was on the list. He was captured, tried, and sentenced to death.

## The Wall

*Espana no necesita sabios.* Morillo's line is widely quoted in Colombia because it captured the imperial project in a single phrase. Whether or not Morillo ever said it exactly is debated. What is certain is that in late October 1816 - on the 29th by most accounts, though some sources give the 28th - Caldas was shot in Bogota's Plaza de San Francisco, today Santander Park. Before the firing squad, tradition holds, he drew a theta on the wall. His body was buried at the Church of Veracruz, later consecrated as the Panteon Nacional. Long after independence, in the early twentieth century, remains believed to be his were moved to Popayan, where they rest in the Panteon de los Proceres - back in the town where he had been born.

## The Department of Caldas

Caldas's legacy ripples outward. The Colombian department of Caldas, carved from the central Andes, is named for him. The Francisco Jose de Caldas District University - a major public institution in Bogota - carries his name. Colciencias, the national science agency that became the Ministry of Science in 2019, long named a doctoral scholarship after him, and the national science fund still bears his name. His face once appeared on the 20-peso oro banknote. Some authors call him the father of Colombian engineering. And boiling-point hypsometers like the one born of his broken thermometer remained a field tool for surveyors for generations. Caldas never lived to see the country whose independence he helped to launch. But the work he did in the spare hours of a merchant's life outlasted the empire that killed him.

## Flight Context

Caldas was born in Popayan (2.44 degrees N, 76.61 degrees W, elevation about 1,760 m), capital of Cauca Department, where remains believed to be his rest in the Panteon de los Proceres. He was executed in Bogota (4.60 degrees N, 74.08 degrees W). Popayan's Guillermo Leon Valencia Airport (SKPP) serves the birthplace city; Bogota's El Dorado International (SKBO) serves the execution site. The terrain Caldas mapped - central and southern Colombian Andes - is visible on any flight between Bogota and Popayan, or between Cali (SKCL) and Bogota. From a cruising altitude over the Cauca Valley, the cordilleras he measured stand in clear relief on both sides.
