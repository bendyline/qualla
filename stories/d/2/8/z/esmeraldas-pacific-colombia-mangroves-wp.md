# Esmeraldas-Pacific Colombia Mangroves

A 6,500-square-kilometer tidal forest where rivers meet the Pacific, trees can top 40 meters, and core samples show mangroves that have held their ground for 4,500 years.

**Location:** 4.2090, -77.5090

**Categories:** ecoregion, mangrove, colombia, ecuador, pacific, conservation

## Introduction

The mangroves here can grow more than 40 meters tall. That fact alone separates this place from nearly every other tropical coast. Where most mangrove forests are stunted, twisted tangles at the edge of the sea, the ecoregion stretching from Colombia's Gulf of Tribuga down into Ecuador's Mompiche Bay produces trees tall enough to mistake for rainforest. The explanation lies upstream: rains of up to 8,000 millimeters a year in the north pour into rivers that carry fresh water, sediment, and nutrients out to the Pacific in staggering quantities. South of the Gulf of Tribuga, sediment cores show *Rhizophora* mangroves that have held their ground for about 4,500 years, and they are still growing.

## A Tidal Forest 400 Miles Long

From Cabo Corrientes in Colombia's Choco department south to Esmeraldas in Ecuador, a more-or-less continuous strip of mangrove runs along the Pacific coastline - the largest mangrove zone in northwestern South America. The ecoregion totals about 6,500 square kilometers. Major rivers form the deltas that feed it: the San Juan, the Naya, the Guapi, the Mira, the Patia, the Sanquianga, the Esmeraldas. Smaller streams - about 25 of them - add their own deltas. Tides rise up to four meters. In the south, where the coastal plain is flat and sedimentary, the forest reaches as much as 20 kilometers inland. In the north, where the coastal hills rise to about 1,500 meters close to the sea, the continental shelf is narrow and the mangroves cling to a thin ribbon between mountain and ocean.

## Exuberant Growth

Compared to Caribbean mangroves, the Pacific Colombian stands have lower salinity. Rainfall dilutes the tidewater. Tides push enough seawater in to keep the soil brackish. The result is unusually fast and tall growth, with trees topping 40 meters. Bromeliads and orchids colonize the trunks. Branches catch fallen leaves and hold them in tiny tanks of their own. Each hectare of mature mangrove releases 8 to 15 tons of organic material per year - a steady rain of detritus that feeds fish, crabs, and shellfish downstream. Red mangroves (*Rhizophora mangle*) dominate the outer fringe. Further inland, transitions through black mangrove (*Avicennia germinans*), white mangrove (*Laguncularia racemosa*), and tea mangrove (*Pelliciera rhizophorae*) blur into freshwater swamp dominated by *Mora oleifera* and clusters of *Euterpe* palms.

## Life Among the Prop Roots

The ecology of a mangrove is, in a sense, an ecology of edges. Above the waterline, threatened birds such as the bare-throated tiger heron and the American yellow warbler take refuge. Below the surface, the mangrove cockle (*Anadara tuberculosa*) works its shell deep into the mud - the backbone of an artisanal fishery on the south-central coast. American crocodiles, spectacled caimans and crab-eating raccoons shelter here too. The mangrove tree crab (*Aratus pisonii*) is everywhere, climbing the trunks and roots. Oncillas, deer, lowland pacas, white-lipped peccaries, and Neotropical otters pass through the upper reaches where the trees meet dry ground.

## The Pollen Record

Scientists drilling sediment cores in and around the Gulf of Tribuga found something remarkable. South of the gulf, *Rhizophora* mangroves have dominated for about 4,500 years - a continuous ecological community outlasting the Chibcha goldsmiths, the Spanish conquest, and the founding of the modern republics. In one area of the gulf itself, the mangroves are much newer, colonizing terrain that became viable only recently. In another part of the northern gulf, *Pelliciera rhizophorae* has been present for about 2,600 years. This is not a frozen landscape - it shifts with sea level, sediment, and storms - but it is a remarkably persistent one. The forest you see from the air today is, in many places, the same forest Francisco Pizarro's ships sailed past in the 1520s.

## Critical, Endangered

The World Wide Fund for Nature lists this ecoregion as Critical/Endangered. Mangroves have long been harvested for fuel, construction timber, fishing gear, and tannin. Now the pressures are heavier: aquaculture ponds carved out of the forest, coconut plantations clearing tidal land, expanding towns, overfishing, pollution drifting down from agricultural zones upstream. Colombia protects portions of the ecoregion through Sanquianga National Natural Park and Utria National Natural Park. Civil Society Natural Reserves - privately stewarded conservation lands - are adding smaller protections in the north. It is not a solved problem. But looking down on the coast from above, the green strip is still remarkably continuous, still breathing with the tides, still releasing tons of organic material with every ebb and flow.

## Flight Context

The Esmeraldas-Pacific Colombia mangroves stretch along the Pacific coast from approximately 5.6 degrees N (Gulf of Tribuga) to 0.6 degrees N (Mompiche Bay, Ecuador), centered near 4.21 degrees N, 77.51 degrees W. Prominent river deltas mark the Mataje, Mira, Patia, Sanquianga, San Juan, and Esmeraldas river mouths. Nearest airport to the center point: Buenaventura (SKBU), about 70 km southeast; farther south along the belt are Guapi (SKGP), Tumaco (SKCO) and, in Ecuador, Esmeraldas (SETN). Cloud cover and rainfall are frequent year-round. The mangrove fringe reads as a distinct dark-green line where muddy river water meets the Pacific.
