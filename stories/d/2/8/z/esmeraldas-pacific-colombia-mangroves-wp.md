# Esmeraldas-Pacific Colombia Mangroves

A 6,500-square-kilometer tidal forest where rivers meet the Pacific, the trees grow 40 meters tall, and core samples show stands that have stood for 4,500 years.

**Location:** 4.2090, -77.5090

**Categories:** ecoregion, mangrove, colombia, ecuador, pacific, conservation

## Introduction

The mangroves here grow more than 40 meters tall. That fact alone separates this place from nearly every other tropical coast. Where most mangrove forests are stunted, twisted tangles at the edge of the sea, the ecoregion stretching from Colombia's Gulf of Tribuga down into Ecuador's Mompiche Bay produces trees tall enough to mistake for rainforest. The explanation lies upstream: monsoonal rains dumping up to 8,000 millimeters a year into rivers that carry fresh water, sediment, and nutrients out to the Pacific in staggering quantities. At the south end of the Gulf of Tribuga, pollen cores show stands of *Rhizophora* mangroves that have held their ground for 4,500 years - older than any human civilization on the continent, and still growing.

## A Tidal Forest 400 Miles Long

From Cabo Corrientes in Colombia's Choco department south to Esmeraldas in Ecuador, a more-or-less continuous strip of mangrove runs along the Pacific coastline - the largest mangrove zone in northwestern South America. The ecoregion totals about 6,500 square kilometers. Major rivers form the deltas that feed it: the San Juan, the Naya, the Guapi, the Mira, the Patia, the Sanquianga, the Esmeraldas. Smaller streams - about 25 of them - add their own deltas. Tides rise up to four meters. In the south, where the coastal plain is flat and sedimentary, the forest reaches as much as 20 kilometers inland. In the north, where the Andes tumble straight into the sea, the continental shelf is narrow and the mangroves cling to a thin ribbon between mountain and ocean.

## Exuberant Growth

Compared to Caribbean mangroves, the Pacific Colombian stands have lower salinity. Rainfall dilutes the tidewater. Tides push enough seawater in to keep the soil brackish. The result is unusually fast and tall growth, with trees topping 40 meters. Bromeliads and orchids colonize the trunks. Branches catch fallen leaves and hold them in tiny tanks of their own. Each hectare of mature mangrove releases 8 to 15 tons of organic material per year - a steady rain of detritus that feeds fish, crabs, and shellfish downstream. Red mangroves (*Rhizophora mangle*) dominate the outer fringe. Further inland, transitions through black mangrove (*Avicennia germinans*), white mangrove (*Laguncularia racemosa*), and tea mangrove (*Pelliciera rhizophorae*) blur into freshwater swamp dominated by the endemic *Mora oleifera* and clusters of *Euterpe* palms.

## Life Among the Prop Roots

The ecology of a mangrove is, in a sense, an ecology of edges. Above the waterline, the bare-throated tiger heron hunts in daylight and the American yellow warbler nests among the bromeliads. Below the surface, the mangrove cockle (*Anadara tuberculosa*) works its shell deep into the mud - the backbone of an artisanal fishery that supports communities all along the coast. American crocodiles and spectacled caimans haul out on sandbars. Crab-eating raccoons hunt fiddler crabs at low tide. The mangrove tree crab (*Aratus pisonii*) is everywhere: climbing trunks, waving in and out of the water, doing what its Spanish nickname implies - eating anything it can reach. Oncillas, lowland pacas, white-lipped peccaries, and coypu pass through the upper reaches where the trees meet dry ground.

## The Pollen Record

Geologists drilling sediment cores in the Gulf of Tribuga found something remarkable. In one area, *Rhizophora* mangroves have dominated for about 4,500 years - a continuous ecological community outlasting the Chibcha goldsmiths, the Spanish conquest, and the founding of the modern republics. In another area of the same gulf, the mangroves are much newer, colonizing terrain that became viable only recently. *Pelliciera rhizophorae* stands have held their ground in one spot for about 2,600 years. This is not a frozen landscape - it shifts with sea level, sediment, and storms - but it is a remarkably persistent one. The forest you see from the air today is, in many places, the same forest Cabeza de Vaca would have seen.

## Critical, Endangered

The World Wide Fund for Nature lists this ecoregion as Critical/Endangered. Mangroves have always been harvested for fuel, construction timber, fishing poles, and tannin. Now the pressures are heavier: aquaculture ponds carved out of the forest, coconut plantations clearing tidal land, expanding towns, overfishing, pollution drifting down from agricultural zones upstream. Colombia protects portions of the ecoregion through Sanquianga National Natural Park and Utria National Natural Park. Civil Society Natural Reserves - privately stewarded conservation lands - are adding smaller protections in the north. It is not a solved problem. But looking down on the coast from above, the green strip is still remarkably continuous, still breathing with the tides, still releasing tons of organic material with every ebb and flow.

## Flight Context

The Esmeraldas-Pacific Colombia mangroves stretch along the Pacific coast from approximately 5.6 degrees N (Gulf of Tribuga) to 0.6 degrees N (Mompiche Bay, Ecuador), centered near 4.21 degrees N, 77.51 degrees W. Prominent river deltas mark the Mataje, Mira, Patia, Sanquianga, San Juan, and Esmeraldas river mouths. Nearest airports: Guapi (SKGP) on the Colombian coast and Esmeraldas General Rivadeneira (SETE) in Ecuador. Cloud cover and rainfall are frequent year-round, with lowest rainfall typically in February-March. The mangrove fringe reads as a distinct dark-green line where muddy river water meets the Pacific.
