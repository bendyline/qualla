# Piamonte

On the narrowest strip of land between the Pacific Ocean and the Amazon, a Colombian frontier town of oil wells, coca plots, and Inga territorial councils only 30 years old.

**Location:** 1.1158, -76.3261

**Categories:** cities, indigenous, amazon, oil, colombia, cauca

## Introduction

From the municipality of Piamonte in southwest Cauca, the Pacific Ocean is 335 kilometers away. That doesn't sound remarkable until you realize you are already in the Amazon. Piamonte sits at the single place in all of South America where the Amazon basin and the Pacific coast come closest - a narrow corridor where the Central Cordillera, the Eastern Cordillera, the Magdalena Valley, and the Amazon rainforest all meet. The town that sits on this geographic pressure point was founded in 1959 by a man named Aristides Perez at a spot called La Barbasca, and recognized as a municipality only on November 18, 1996. It is one of the youngest municipalities in Colombia, and the land it sits on is among the most fought-over.

## The Baja Bota Caucana

Look at a map of Colombia's Cauca department and its southeastern corner narrows to a boot-shape: the Baja Bota Caucana, Piamonte's geographic home. It borders four departments - Huila to the north, Caqueta to the east, Putumayo to the south, and the rest of Cauca to the west - and the Rio Caqueta cuts through it. The climate is tropical rainforest in the Koppen sense: hot, humid, rain almost every day of the year, totals that would drown lesser landscapes. The vegetation is dense, the biodiversity is extreme, and the terrain is so broken that the first roads didn't reach the interior until the 1950s. Before the roads, there were only trails - one of which today is called the Carretera Marginal de la Selva, the Peripheral Road of the Jungle.

## Waves of Arrival

Piamonte's population is a record of successive Colombian crises. The Inga indigenous people were here first, displaced from Putumayo before memory, settling at Guayuyaco and other Andean foothill sites. In the 1930s came the quinine boom - cinchona bark harvested for the anti-malarial drug - which pulled migrants from across the country. Then La Violencia in the 1950s and 1960s sent refugees fleeing political killings. Later arrived what Wikipedia politely calls 'the oil fever.' The original town of Napoles, the valley's oldest, had to be moved in the early 1970s after the Caqueta River flooded it. Today's population of roughly 7,241 people (2012 count) is 12.24% Inga, with a significant Afro-descendant community and settlers from Caqueta, Putumayo, Narino, and the rest of Cauca.

## Oil, Coca, and a Broken Promise

Piamonte's economy is an open argument between extraction and subsistence. Under its subsoil sits part of a vast oil deposit that stretches through Ecuador and Peru. From 1993 to 2008, the Canadian-owned Gran Tierra Energy (then operating as Argosy Energy International) drilled five wells here - Mary 1 through Mary 5 and Miralor 1 - and by 2002 production was 2,290 barrels per day. Above ground, families grow bananas, cassava, corn, and chontaduro, plus the Amazonian fruits araza and borojo, while others cultivate coca. Law 141 of 1994 was supposed to send 12.5 percent of oil royalties back to Piamonte for social investment, with 80 percent required for electricity and sanitation. The law has mostly not been honored. Only Piamonte's town center and Miraflor have recent electrical connections; many villages still do not.

## The Inga Resguardos

The Inga people of Piamonte are organized into nine traditional councils - Bajo Chuspizacha, San Gabriel, Alto Suspizacha, Rumiñawi, San Jose del Inchiyaco, Ambiwasi, Musurrunacuna, Caucapapungo, and Aukawasi - and hold six legally recognized resguardos, or indigenous territorial reservations: Guayuyaco, Inga Wasipanga, La Floresta Espanola, La Leona, Las Brisas, and San Rafael. These are not symbolic designations. A resguardo is a collective land title that Colombian law treats as inalienable, giving Inga authorities a real say over what happens on their territory even when oil and coca economies press in from every side. The mountainous slice of the municipality holds the Serrania de Los Churumbelos Auka-Wasi National Natural Park, a protected stretch of Amazonian jungle where puma and spectacled bear still pass through.

## The Corridor

The Carretera Marginal de la Selva that crosses Piamonte is no ordinary road. It is part of IIRSA, the Initiative for the Integration of Regional Infrastructure in South America - a continental highway project meant to knit together road networks from the Caribbean coast to Patagonia. From Piamonte the road runs to Villagarzon and Puerto Asis and out toward Ecuador; in the other direction, from Villagarzon toward Pasto and the Pacific port of Tumaco. For a town that spent decades being at the geographic edge of four departments, this corridor is what makes it, perhaps inevitably, a crossroads. Whether that status helps or harms its Inga councils and its settlers remains, like so much else in this municipality, an argument being written in real time.

## Flight Context

Located at 1.12 N, 76.33 W in the Baja Bota region of southeast Cauca, at the transition between the Andes and the Amazon rainforest. Nearest airports: Antonio Narino (SKPS) in Pasto and Guillermo Leon Valencia (SKPP) in Popayan, both 100+ km away. The municipality sits 335 km from the Pacific - the narrowest land passage between the Pacific Ocean and the Amazon basin in South America. Expect near-constant cloud cover from tropical rainforest convection; visibility for overflight is best just after dawn.
