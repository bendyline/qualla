# Lago Agrio oil field

A name meaning 'Sour Lake' became infamous as one of the Amazon's worst ecological disasters, where oil development upended the lives of Cofán, Siona, and Kichwa communities.

**Location:** 0.0746, -76.7579

**Categories:** Oil fields, Environmental disasters, Indigenous history, Ecuador, Amazon basin

## Introduction

Sour Lake is not a translation. It is an accusation. When Texaco arrived in northeast Ecuador in 1964, its engineers named their base camp after a Texas town, Sour Lake, and the Spanish rendering, Lago Agrio, stuck. Six decades later, that bitter name reads like prophecy. The oil field sits near the Colombian border in Sucumbíos Province, on land long inhabited by Cofán, Siona, Kichwa, and Tetete peoples. Oil came in 1967. Full production started in 1972. And somewhere in the decades between the first drill bit hitting the Cretaceous Napo formation and today, roughly 19 billion gallons of toxic produced water flowed into unlined pits, into streams, into the drinking water of roughly 30,000 people.

## A Base Camp Called Nueva Loja

The town now known as Nueva Loja began as a Texaco outpost. The Texaco Petroleum Company and Gulf Oil formed a consortium in 1965 to drill this tract of Amazon rainforest, and the small camp of corrugated metal and clapboard grew into a frontier city. The consortium struck oil in 1967 in the Cretaceous Napo and Hollin formations, and by 1972 it was pumping at industrial scale. Ecuador's national oil company took a 25 percent interest in 1974, bought out Gulf's share, and by 1976 the government held a majority stake. Yet TexPet ran the drilling. That technical authority would later become the heart of the lawsuit: who designed the pits, the pipelines, the dumping practices that would come to define the region. Oil flowed out through the 498-kilometer SOTE pipeline, crossed the Andes, and reached the Pacific terminal at Balao. Wealth flowed out with it. Much else stayed behind.

## The Pits in the Rainforest

Judith Kimerling's 1991 report for the Natural Resources Defense Council, Amazon Crude, is still one of the most cited accounts of what happened. She described 880 unlined open pits. She described produced water with petroleum concentrations of 500 to 5,000 parts per million, draining into the streams that local families drank from, bathed in, fished. She described an average of 4,165 cubic meters of toxic drilling fluid accumulating at each well site. In the United States, produced water had been reinjected underground since the 1970s. Here it was poured into the earth. A Petroecuador manager told one researcher that at some points the rivers were like the sea, meaning the salinity. A 1987 earthquake ruptured the trans-Ecuadorian pipeline. A 1989 landslide spilled another 210,000 gallons, and the Napo River ran black as far downstream as Peru. The pits kept filling.

## Aguinda v. Texaco

In 1993, the class action began. It was filed in New York on behalf of 30,000 inhabitants of the affected region, the named plaintiff a Cofán woman, Maria Aguinda. The case was eventually moved to Ecuadorian courts at Texaco's insistence. In 2011, an Ecuadorian court ordered Chevron, which had acquired Texaco in 2001, to pay eight billion dollars. The Ecuadorian Supreme Court confirmed the verdict in 2013 at 9.5 billion. Chevron refused to pay. The case became one of the most tangled environmental lawsuits in history, rulings and counter-rulings stretching across Argentina, Canada, Brazil, the Netherlands, and the United States. In 2018, the Permanent Court of Arbitration in The Hague ruled unanimously for Chevron, finding the Ecuadorian judgment had been obtained through fraud, bribery, and corruption. Steven Donziger, the American lawyer leading the plaintiffs' case, was found by a U.S. court to have paid bribes and ghostwritten expert reports. He was disbarred. The legal victory collapsed. The pollution did not.

## What Remains in the Forest

The Tetete people are gone. Ecuadorian government officials have stated that Texaco's encroachment on Tetete land contributed to their extinction. A similar fate was declared for the Tagaeri in 2003. Pablo Fajardo, who represents the plaintiffs and won the 2008 Goldman Environmental Prize alongside activist Luis Yanza for this work, has said the pollution caused 2,000 cancer deaths by 2019. Five peer-reviewed studies have shown elevated cancer rates in the region. Chevron funded a study that found no link. Subsequent audits, in 2003 and 2009, documented 58 waste pits left uncovered, petroleum hydrocarbon concentrations far above Ecuador's legal limit. Of 31 trees planted over the first remediated pit, the writer William Langewiesche reported, most had died within ten years. Community bioremediation efforts, seeding contaminated soil with microbes that eat hydrocarbons, are quiet ongoing work done by people who did not choose this fight.

## Flying Over Sucumbíos

From altitude, the Western Oriente Basin looks like a green carpet cut by brown rivers, the Amazon headwaters braiding south toward Peru. Gas flares still burn at production sites, small points of orange at night. The nearest major airport is Quito to the west, well over the Andes. Nueva Loja has its own strip. What a pilot sees is the shape of what happened: a pattern of clearings and service roads, a lattice of pipelines, the darker band of the Aguarico River where the Cofán still fish what they can.

## Flight Context

Located at 0.07°N, 76.76°W in the Western Oriente Basin of Ecuador's Amazon lowlands, near the Colombian border. The nearest major airport is Mariscal Sucre International (SEQM) in Quito, about 250 km west across the Andes. Nueva Loja has a regional airstrip (SENL). Cruising altitude 10,000-30,000 ft offers views of rainforest, the Napo and Aguarico river systems, and the eastern slopes of the Cordillera Real. Cloud cover is heavy most afternoons; mornings offer clearer conditions.
