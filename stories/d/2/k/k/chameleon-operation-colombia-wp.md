# Chameleon Operation (Colombia)

On June 13, 2010, Colombian commandos finished a ten-day crawl through the Guaviare jungle and freed four hostages who had been held by FARC for nearly twelve years.

**Location:** 2.1639, -72.5950

**Categories:** Guerrilla warfare, Guerrilla organizations, Military of Colombia, FARC actions, Hostage taking, Hostage rescue operations, National Army of Colombia, 2010 in Colombia, June 2010 in South America

## Introduction

For almost twelve years, General Luis Herlindo Mendieta, Colonels Enrique Murillo and William Donato Gómez, and Sergeant Arbey Delgado lived as hostages in the deep jungle of Colombia's Guaviare Department. They moved every eight to fifteen days, from one guerrilla camp to another, always deeper into a forest that was effectively a prison without walls. On June 13, 2010, soldiers of the Colombian National Army's special forces stepped out of Black Hawk helicopters into a clearing in the vereda of Salto Gloria, and began crawling the last two kilometers of a ten-day approach. They called the mission Operación Camaleón. Twelve years of captivity were about to end.

## The Men in the Jungle

General Mendieta and Colonel Murillo had been prisoners of the Revolutionary Armed Forces of Colombia, the FARC, since the Siege of Mitú in November 1998, when roughly 500 FARC guerrillas overran a police anti-drug base in the remote jungle town. Sixteen members of the public forces and three civilians were killed. Seventy-five military personnel and fifty-four police officers were kidnapped. The Colombian army recaptured Mitú in less than 48 hours, with support from Brazil that let Colombian reinforcements fly in from an air base near the town, since the guerrillas had burned the airstrip. But many of those taken that night were pulled deeper into the forest. Mendieta, the highest-ranking FARC detainee, was among them. Colonel Donato had also been captured at Mitú. Sergeant Delgado's path to captivity, like that of many of the people FARC held for long stretches, had wound through other operations in the late 1990s. All four men had grown old while their country changed around them.

## An Intelligence Campaign

Operation Chameleon was not an ambush; it was the end of years of intelligence work. Army and police agents had infiltrated FARC and retrieved data from the field on a weekly basis, penetrating the group's security systems and copying the internal communications keys and frequencies that FARC had changed after Operation Jaque, the 2008 deception operation that had freed fifteen hostages including the French-Colombian politician Íngrid Betancourt. The information reached General Javier Flórez, commander of the Fuerza de Tarea Conjunta Omega, and then climbed the chain. In February 2010 three experienced military intelligence colonels, one of whom had worked Jaque, brought General Óscar Enrique González Peña a concrete picture of where the hostages were being held. The planning was based on material gathered since 2007, including data from Jhon Frank Pinchao, a police officer who had escaped FARC after almost nine years of captivity. On March 12, 2010, a FARC guerrilla named Marcos Parrilla was captured and resigned from the organization. He gave investigators the coordinates of the camp, and confirmed the hostages moved every 8 to 15 days.

## Eight Days

On June 10 at 5:30 p.m., President Álvaro Uribe authorized the operation. Some 300 special forces soldiers were sent to the Joaquín París Battalion at San José del Guaviare, and by 1 p.m. on Saturday June 12, what the commanders called operation zero hour, air force aircraft were attached to the battalion. The final stage had actually begun on June 2: soldiers dropped roughly thirty kilometers from the FARC camp at Salto Gloria in the municipality of El Retorno, advancing on average three kilometers per day. They reached the camp's perimeter after ten days. The last two kilometers they covered at night, crawling. General Freddy Padilla de León, commander of the military forces, later said the operation had been planned for a fortnight but actually lasted eight days, with 36 of those hours rated critical and extremely high-risk.

## Black Hawks

At 10 a.m. on June 13, men from the Comando de Operaciones Especiales, COPES, descended from Black Hawk helicopters. At 12:40 p.m., General Mendieta and Colonel Murillo were the first two hostages freed, escorted out of the combat zone by a thirty-commando motorcade while other soldiers searched for Sergeant Delgado and Colonel Donato. All four hostages were eventually brought out alive. According to intercepted communications, FARC high command had given orders that the guerrillas guarding hostages were to kill their prisoners if threatened; that this did not happen was partly timing and partly the speed of the assault. President Uribe announced the release while presiding over a televised community council in Quibdó. Across Colombia, a World Cup match being played in South Africa was interrupted so that the country could hear the news. Chameleon was the first successful hostage rescue since Operation Jaque on July 2, 2008.

## Credits, Costs, and Questions

After the operation, army commander General Óscar González announced that Marcos Parrilla and three other informers had been rewarded with 2.5 billion Colombian pesos for their role. U.S. Ambassador William Brownfield described the operation as "magnificent and sensational" and acknowledged that his government had shared in the intelligence work. "This was a Colombian concept, plan, and operation," he said, while pointing out that Colombia and the United States had been cooperating in intelligence for more than a decade. Colombian Defense Minister Gabriel Silva initially denied that the operation had any outside help. The family of Corporal Edward Milciades Guzmán Barón, a Colombian soldier shot by a FARC sniper in San José del Guaviare who died of his wounds on June 17, said he had been sent to guard the perimeter of the target area, a claim that complicated the official account. The operation fell one week before the second round of Colombia's presidential election on June 20, 2010, which Juan Manuel Santos, Uribe's former defense minister and a central figure in Operations Phoenix and Jaque, won decisively. Critics said the timing was too convenient; Uribe denied political motivation. What was not in dispute was the human outcome. Four men who had been held in the jungle for almost twelve years walked out. For them, for their families, that answered everything else.

## Flight Context

Located at 2.16°N, 72.59°W in the Guaviare Department of southeastern Colombia, a vast Amazonian frontier of rivers and forest near the headwaters of the Inírida. The nearest airfield of significance is San José del Guaviare (SKSJ); El Retorno itself has only a small airstrip. The region lies at approximately 200 meters elevation, covered by dense tropical rainforest with the braided channels of the Guaviare River visible from cruise altitude. Weather is dominated by convective buildups most afternoons; mornings offer clearer views of the vast green canopy through which the operation's special forces advanced on foot.
