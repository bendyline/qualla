# American Airlines Flight 965

On December 20, 1995, a Boeing 757 bound for Cali struck a mountain near Buga, Colombia, killing 159 people and reshaping how the aviation industry thinks about terrain awareness and automation.

**Location:** 3.8459, -76.1047

**Categories:** Aviation accidents and incidents in Colombia, Airliner accidents and incidents involving controlled flight into terrain, American Airlines accidents and incidents, Accidents and incidents involving the Boeing 757, 1995 in Colombia

## Introduction

At 6:35 p.m. Eastern Standard Time on December 20, 1995, American Airlines Flight 965 rolled down the runway at Miami International, already nearly two hours behind schedule after waiting for connecting passengers and then for congestion at Miami to clear. On board were 155 passengers, eight crew, and a Christmas-season manifest of families flying home to Colombia for the holidays. Three hours and six minutes later, the Boeing 757 struck trees at about 8,900 feet on the east face of El Deluvio, a ridge of roughly 9,000 feet near Buga, Colombia, about 45 kilometres northeast of the airport it was trying to reach. One hundred and fifty-nine people died. Four passengers and a dog survived.

## The Crew and the Night

Captain Nicholas Tafuri was 57 years old, with more than 13,000 hours of flight experience — 2,260 of them on the Boeing 757/767. First Officer Donald "Don" Williams was 39, with almost 6,000 hours total and 2,286 on the type. By any reasonable measure, both pilots were experienced and capable. Cali's Alfonso Bonilla Aragón International — ICAO code SKCL — sits in the Cauca River valley, with mountains rising to as much as 14,000 feet on either side. The normal approach uses a series of VOR radio beacons to guide aircraft around the terrain. On the night of the crash, Cali's air traffic controllers had no functional radar: their surveillance radar had been destroyed in a 1992 bombing. The approach controller could follow Flight 965 only through position reports from the pilots themselves.

## A Wrong Turn

The crew was running late, and Cali's controller offered a direct routing to the Rozo non-directional beacon, which would shorten the approach. The captain accepted. When he typed "R" into the Flight Management System — the identifier printed for Rozo on his chart — the computer listed matching beacons by distance and put Romeo, a beacon in Bogotá some 130 miles to the east-northeast, first. The crew selected it without recognising the discrepancy. The autopilot dutifully swung the aircraft east, into the wall of mountains paralleling the valley. The published charts listed Rozo under the identifier "R"; the FMS database used a different naming convention. That mismatch — invisible to the crew, buried in the navigation database — is one of the most studied details in aviation safety history. When the pilots realised something was wrong and tried to get back on course, precious minutes had already passed, and the airplane was over terrain that should not have been beneath it.

## GPWS

The ground proximity warning system activated with its insistent automated cry. Within one second the first officer disengaged the autopilot. Within two seconds take-off power had been selected and the nose pulled hard up; within three seconds the pitch was 20.6 degrees nose-high, enough to activate the stick shaker warning of an imminent stall. The aircraft clawed for altitude with every tool available — except one. Neither pilot remembered to retract the speed brakes, which had been deployed earlier in the descent and were still fully extended. They dragged the climb rate down by a critical margin. At about 9:41 p.m. EST the 757 struck trees at about 8,900 feet on the east face of the ridge, nose high, at roughly 187 knots. The wreckage went over the top and came to rest on the west side, a few hundred feet below the crest. The crash site lies near the summit of El Deluvio, about 33 miles northeast of the Cali VOR.

## The Victims and the Survivors

The dead included entire families travelling home for Christmas. Paris Kanellakis, a computer scientist at Brown University, died alongside his wife and two children. Benny Ramírez, a former professional wrestler and NWA Texas Heavyweight Champion, died with his wife; their daughter Mercedes was one of the four human survivors. Five passengers survived the impact, and one of them later died in hospital. The survivors spent the night on the mountain before a helicopter sighted the wreckage at first light, and Colombian rescue teams brought them down. Survivor Mercedes Ramírez Johnson went on to speak publicly about what happened and why; survivor Michelle Dussan later sat for a podcast interview recounting the same. The accident is featured prominently in Colombian author Juan Gabriel Vásquez's 2011 novel "The Sound of Things Falling," which takes the crash as a lens on his generation's experience of Colombia during the 1990s. A 2021 documentary film, "American 965," directed by a former British Airways captain, revisited the case a quarter-century later.

## What Changed

The Colombian Civil Aeronautics investigation was direct about the probable causes: the crew failed to adequately plan and execute the approach; failed to discontinue when numerous cues should have warned them off; lost situational awareness regarding terrain and navigation; and failed to fall back on basic radio navigation when FMS-assisted navigation became confusing in a critical phase of flight. Contributing factors included the pressure to avoid further delays, the speed brake oversight during the escape maneuver, and the FMS naming discrepancies. In a third-party lawsuit American brought against the navigation system vendors, a 2000 jury found Jeppesen 17 percent at fault, Honeywell 8 percent, and American Airlines 75 percent. The industry's response was structural. The Enhanced Ground Proximity Warning System (EGPWS) — which uses a terrain database to provide earlier warnings — was introduced in 1996; US regulators eventually required advanced terrain awareness warning systems on all US-registered turbine aircraft with six or more passenger seats built since 2002. American Airlines still flies the Miami–Cali route, under different flight numbers (recently 919 and 921). The mountain near Buga remains what it has always been: a dark silhouette on the horizon northeast of the airport, and a reason the industry works the way it now works.

## Flight Context

Crash site at approximately 3.85°N, 76.10°W, on the east face of a mountain near Buga, Valle del Cauca, Colombia, at about 8,900 feet elevation. Destination airport was Alfonso Bonilla Aragón International (SKCL), about 45 km southwest of the crash site in the Cauca River valley at about 960 metres. Origin was Miami International (KMIA). The accident aircraft was a Boeing 757-223, N651AA, delivered new to American in 1991 and powered by Rolls-Royce RB211-535E4B engines. The Cauca valley is flanked on both sides by Andean cordilleras rising above 10,000 feet; night-time approaches to SKCL require strict adherence to published procedures, and the lessons learned here have shaped terrain-awareness equipment and crew training worldwide.
