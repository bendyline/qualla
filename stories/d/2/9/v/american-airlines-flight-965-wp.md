# American Airlines Flight 965

On December 20, 1995, a Boeing 757 bound for Cali struck a mountain near Buga, Colombia, killing 159 people and reshaping how the aviation industry thinks about terrain awareness and automation.

**Location:** 3.8459, -76.1047

**Categories:** Aviation accidents and incidents in Colombia, Airliner accidents and incidents involving controlled flight into terrain, American Airlines accidents and incidents, Accidents and incidents involving the Boeing 757, 1995 in Colombia

## Introduction

At 6:35 p.m. Eastern Standard Time on December 20, 1995, American Airlines Flight 965 rolled down the runway at Miami International, already nearly two hours behind schedule because of a winter storm in the Northeast. On board were 155 passengers, eight crew, and a Christmas-season manifest of families flying home to Colombia for the holidays. Three hours and six minutes later, the Boeing 757 struck trees at 8,920 feet on the east face of an unnamed 9,000-foot mountain near Buga, Colombia, 28 kilometres north of the runway it was trying to reach. One hundred and fifty-nine people died. Four passengers and a dog survived.

## The Crew and the Night

Captain Nicholas Tafuri was 57 years old, with more than 13,000 hours of flight experience — 2,260 of them on the Boeing 757/767. First Officer Donald "Don" Williams was 39, with almost 6,000 hours total and 2,286 on the type. By any reasonable measure, both pilots were experienced and capable. Cali's Alfonso Bonilla Aragón International — ICAO code SKCL — sits in the Cauca River valley, with mountains rising to over 14,000 feet on either side. The normal approach uses a series of VOR radio beacons to guide aircraft around the terrain. On the night of the crash, Cali's air traffic controllers had no functional radar: their surveillance radar had been destroyed in a 1992 bombing. Controller Nelson Rivera Ramírez was watching Flight 965 only through position reports from the pilots themselves.

## A Wrong Turn

The crew was running late, and Cali's controller offered a direct routing to the Rozo non-directional beacon, which would shorten the approach. The captain accepted. When he typed "R" into the Flight Management System — reasoning that Rozo was the nearest navaid whose name began with R — the computer instead offered Romeo, a beacon near Bogotá, far to the northeast. The crew selected it without recognising the discrepancy. The autopilot dutifully swung the aircraft east, into the wall of mountains paralleling the valley. The published charts listed Rozo under the identifier "R"; the FMS database used a different naming convention. That mismatch — invisible to the crew, buried in the navigation database — is one of the most studied details in aviation safety history. When the pilots realised something was wrong and tried to get back on course, precious minutes had already passed, and the airplane was over terrain that should not have been beneath it.

## GPWS

The ground proximity warning system activated with its insistent automated cry. Within one second the first officer disengaged the autopilot. Within two seconds the captain had selected maximum take-off power and pulled hard up; within three seconds the pitch was 20.6 degrees nose-high, enough to activate the stick shaker warning of an imminent stall. The aircraft clawed for altitude with every tool available — except one. Neither pilot remembered to retract the speed brakes, which had been deployed earlier in the descent and were still fully extended. They dragged the climb rate down by a critical margin. At 9:41:28 p.m. EST the 757 struck trees at 8,920 feet on the east face of the mountain, at 187 knots and 28 degrees nose-up. The wreckage went over the summit and came to rest on the far side. The crash site lies 9.7 kilometres south of the Tulúa VOR and 28 kilometres north of the approach end of runway 19 at SKCL.

## The Victims and the Survivors

The dead included entire families travelling home for Christmas. Paris Kanellakis, a computer scientist at Brown University, died alongside his wife and two children. Benny Ramírez, a former professional wrestler and NWA Texas Heavyweight Champion, died with his wife; their youngest child was one of the four human survivors. Twelve hours of painful, heroic work by Colombian rescue teams on the freezing mountainside saved those four lives. Survivor Mercedes Ramírez Johnson went on to speak publicly about what happened and why; survivor Michelle Dussan later sat for a podcast interview recounting the same. The accident is featured prominently in Colombian author Juan Gabriel Vásquez's 2011 novel "The Sound of Things Falling," which takes the crash as a lens on his generation's experience of Colombia during the 1990s. A 2021 documentary film, "American 965," directed by a former British Airways captain, revisited the case three decades later.

## What Changed

The Colombian Civil Aeronautics investigation was direct about the probable causes: the crew failed to adequately plan and execute the approach; failed to discontinue when numerous cues should have warned them off; lost situational awareness regarding terrain and navigation; and failed to fall back on basic radio navigation when FMS-assisted navigation became confusing in a critical phase of flight. Contributing factors included the pressure to avoid further delays, the speed brake oversight during the escape maneuver, and the FMS naming discrepancies. In a third-party lawsuit American brought against the navigation system vendors, a 2000 jury found Jeppesen 30 percent at fault, Honeywell 10 percent, and American Airlines 60 percent. The industry's response was structural. The Enhanced Ground Proximity Warning System (EGPWS) — which uses a terrain database to provide earlier warnings — was introduced in 1996; US regulators eventually required advanced terrain awareness warning systems on all US-registered turbine aircraft capable of carrying more than six passengers built since 2002. American Airlines still flies the Miami–Cali route; the flight numbers are now 919 and 921. The mountain near Buga remains what it has always been: a dark silhouette on the horizon north of runway 19, and a reason the industry works the way it now works.

## Flight Context

Crash site at approximately 3.85°N, 76.10°W, on the east face of a mountain near Buga, Valle del Cauca, Colombia, at about 8,920 feet elevation. Destination airport was Alfonso Bonilla Aragón International (SKCL), 28 km south of the crash site in the Cauca River valley at about 960 metres. Origin was Miami International (KMIA). The accident aircraft was a Boeing 757-223, N651AA, the 390th 757 built, powered by Rolls-Royce RB211 engines. The Cauca valley is flanked on both sides by Andean cordilleras rising above 10,000 feet; night-time approaches to SKCL require strict adherence to published procedures, and since 1995 the lessons learned here inform approach design for every similarly-situated airport worldwide.
