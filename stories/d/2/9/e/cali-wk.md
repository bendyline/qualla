# Cali

The self-declared salsa capital of the world is a hot Andean valley city where nightclubs open at dusk, rivers come down from the cordillera, and two million people live by a beat measured in 220 to 250 per minute.

**Location:** 3.4400, -76.5197

**Categories:** Cities in Colombia, Valle del Cauca, Salsa music, Tourism in Colombia

## Introduction

In Cali, salsa is not a genre; it is a clock. Bands play at 220 to 250 beats per minute, noticeably faster than Havana or New York, and dancers here have trained their feet to match. The Menga district to the north and Juanchito across the Cauca River are the traditional heartland of the scene, but salsa drifts out of corner bars in Granada and the Barrio de San Antonio and gets piped through the MIO bus system and into any taxi you flag down on Avenida Sexta. Cali sits at 1,003 meters in a wide green valley with the Farallones mountains rising to the west, and it declares itself, confidently, the world capital of salsa.

## A City That Stays Warm

Cali is the capital of Valle del Cauca, a city of about two million people, the main urban and industrial hub of southwestern Colombia. The temperature averages 26°C, with daytime highs running from 19 to 34°C, which means shorts and short sleeves year-round. Dusk falls fast in the tropics and arrives around six in the evening; the cool comes with it, a relief you can feel on the climb up to the Cerro de las Tres Cruces or the hill of Cristo Rey, where a statue reminiscent of Rio's Cristo Redentor looks out over the whole valley. For the best view without the safety concerns that sometimes surround Cristo Rey, locals recommend the neighborhood around the Estatua de Belalcázar, which honors the Spanish conquistador Sebastián de Belalcázar who founded both Cali and nearby Popayán in the 1530s. From there the city grid unrolls below, broken by the thin green line of the Cauca River.

## Colonial Corners and a Gold Museum

Cali is more than 450 years old, and its downtown holds the evidence. La Ermita, a slender Gothic-style church on the river, was first built in 1678 and rebuilt in 1930; it has become the city's visual signature, its pale blue and white spire often framed by the palms of Parque del Gato del Río. Museo del Oro del Banco de la República on Calle 7 holds pre-Columbian gold and pottery from the Calima cultures that lived in these valleys before the Spanish arrived, and admission is free. The Museo Arqueológico La Merced, housed in an old colonial church downtown, tells a similar story through other objects. La Tertulia, a modern art museum, runs an art-house cinema that shows foreign films you will not find at the mall. Up the hill in the Barrio de San Antonio, an old colonial church tops a rise that fills on Thursday, Friday, and Saturday nights with storytellers, street performers, craft sellers, and travelers. Expats teaching English often choose to live here. Café terraces glow with candles, and from the park in front of the church you can see the whole city laid out below.

## The Sound After Dark

Salsa dancing is the reason many travelers come. Zaperoco, just off Avenida Sexta, is a legendary salsa club: serious dancers, serious band booking, Thursday through Saturday nights. Tin Tin Deo draws an older, more discerning crowd for classic salsa and Caribbean rhythms played almost like a concert. Chango, out in Juanchito on the far side of the Cauca River, has the reputation of the biggest salsa party in the city; locals take taxis out and make a night of it. Every December the city throws the Feria de Cali, a week of salsa concerts, parades, horse shows, and bullfights at the Plaza de Toros that turns the entire calendar year toward the festival's final days. Even on an ordinary Tuesday, salsa classes run all afternoon in Juanchito and San Antonio; many travelers take one or two lessons before going out, on the theory that the humiliation on a club dance floor is worth avoiding.

## Food From the Cauca Valley

Cali's food sits where the Andes meet the Pacific Coast. Sancocho de gallina, a chicken soup that simmers for hours with cassava and plantain, is best found in the small town of Ginebra, about an hour from the city. Arroz atollado is almost a risotto, loose and moist, studded with chicken, pork, sausage, and potatoes. Cholados, an invented-in-Jamundí treat fifteen minutes south of the city, stack shaved ice with exotic fruit, raspberry syrup, and sweet condensed milk under a thin wafer cookie. Street vendors sell pandebonos, small chewy rings of cheese bread, still warm from the oven; buñuelos, cheese-fritter doughnuts fried crisp; and peach-palm fruit, the chontaduro, which locals eat with salt or honey. To drink: guarapo, pressed sugar-cane juice served cold with lime; champus, a pineapple-and-lulo concoction with corn and limoncillo; or aguardiente Blanco del Valle, the licorice-flavored spirit that is the regional badge of identity.

## Getting In, Getting Out

Cali's Alfonso Bonilla Aragón International Airport sits 16 km northeast of downtown and handles domestic flights to every major Colombian city along with a modest roster of international destinations: Miami, Madrid, Panama City, San Salvador, Quito, Guayaquil, Esmeraldas. Buses reach Bogotá in about twelve hours, Medellín in nine, Buenaventura in three, and Popayán in three. The MIO (Masivo Integrado de Occidente) system of air-conditioned blue buses in dedicated lanes is the cleanest way to move around, with fares paid by smart card. Taxis use a unit-based meter; when in doubt, call for one rather than hailing on the street. For day trips, consider Calima Lake, ninety minutes out in the mountains and one of South America's top wind- and kite-surf spots thanks to steady year-round trades; the pilgrimage town of Buga, one hour northeast; or the humpback whale migration off Isla Gorgona, reachable through Buenaventura, where roughly 1,200 whales arrive each June and July from Antarctic waters to mate and calve. Cali can feel bigger and more complex than first-time visitors expect. Stay alert, use taxis at night, and give the city the weekend it deserves.

## Flight Context

Located at 3.44°N, 76.52°W in the Cauca River valley at 1,003 meters (3,290 feet) elevation. Alfonso Bonilla Aragón International Airport (SKCL) lies 16 km northeast near Palmira. The Farallones de Cali range rises sharply to the west above 4,000 meters, with the Cordillera Central climbing east. Expect afternoon convective buildups over both ranges, especially during the April–May and October–November rainy seasons; mornings generally give the best visibility of the long green valley and the distinctive silhouette of the three crosses on Cerro de las Tres Cruces.
