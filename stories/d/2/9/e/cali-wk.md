# Cali

The self-declared salsa capital of the world is a hot Andean valley city where nightclubs open at dusk, rivers come down from the cordillera, and more than two million people live by a fast salsa beat.

**Location:** 3.4400, -76.5197

**Categories:** Cities in Colombia, Valle del Cauca, Salsa music, Tourism in Colombia

## Introduction

In Cali, salsa is not a genre; it is a clock. Cali's dancers are famous for their speed, with footwork quicker than the Havana or New York styles, and they have trained for years to get it. The Menga district to the north and Juanchito across the Cauca River are the traditional heartland of the scene, but salsa drifts out of corner bars in Granada and the Barrio de San Antonio and into many a taxi on Avenida Sexta. Cali sits at 1,003 meters in a wide green valley with the Farallones mountains rising to the west, and it declares itself, confidently, the world capital of salsa.

## A City That Stays Warm

Cali is the capital of Valle del Cauca, a city of more than two million people, the main urban and industrial hub of southwestern Colombia. The temperature averages around 25°C, ranging from about 19°C at night to 34°C on hot afternoons, which means shorts and short sleeves year-round. Dusk falls fast in the tropics and arrives around six in the evening; the cool comes with it, a relief you can feel on the climb up to the Cerro de las Tres Cruces or the hill of Cristo Rey, where a statue reminiscent of Rio's Cristo Redentor looks out over the whole valley. For a view without the safety concerns that sometimes surround Cristo Rey, try the neighborhood around the Estatua de Belalcázar, which honors the Spanish conquistador Sebastián de Belalcázar who founded both Cali and nearby Popayán in the 1530s. From there the city grid unrolls below, broken by the thin green line of the Cauca River.

## Colonial Corners and a Gold Museum

Cali, founded in 1536, is nearly 490 years old, and its downtown holds the evidence. La Ermita, a slender Gothic-style church on the river, stands where a colonial chapel stood until a 1925 earthquake; the present church opened in 1942 and has become the city's visual signature, its pale blue and white spires often framed by the palms along the Cali River. Museo del Oro del Banco de la República on Calle 7 holds pre-Columbian gold and pottery from the Calima cultures that lived in these valleys before the Spanish arrived, and admission is free. The Museo Arqueológico La Merced, housed in an old colonial church downtown, tells a similar story through other objects. La Tertulia, a modern art museum, runs an art-house cinema that shows foreign films you will not find at the mall. Up the hill in the Barrio de San Antonio, an old colonial church tops a rise that fills on Thursday, Friday, and Saturday nights with storytellers, street performers, craft sellers, and travelers. Expats teaching English often choose to live here. Café terraces glow with candles, and from the park in front of the church you can see the whole city laid out below.

## The Sound After Dark

Salsa dancing is the reason many travelers come. Zaperoco, just off Avenida Sexta, is a long-running salsa bar with serious dancers. Tin Tin Deo draws an older, more discerning crowd for classic salsa and Caribbean rhythms played almost like a concert. Chango, out in Juanchito on the far side of the Cauca River, has long had the reputation of the best salsa party in the city; locals take taxis out and make a night of it. Every December the city throws the Feria de Cali, a week of salsa concerts, parades, horse shows and - until Colombia's bullfighting ban takes full effect in 2027 - bullfights at the Plaza de Toros. Salsa schools are everywhere, and many travelers take one or two lessons before going out, on the theory that the humiliation on a club dance floor is worth avoiding.

## Food From the Cauca Valley

Cali's food sits where the Andes meet the Pacific Coast. Sancocho de gallina, a chicken soup that simmers for hours with cassava and plantain, is best found in the small town of Ginebra, about an hour from the city. Arroz atollado is almost a risotto, loose and moist, studded with chicken, pork, sausage, and potatoes. Cholados, a treat credited to Jamundí, just south of the city, stack shaved ice with exotic fruit, raspberry syrup, and sweet condensed milk under a thin wafer cookie. Street vendors sell pandebonos, small chewy rings of cheese bread, still warm from the oven; buñuelos, cheese-fritter doughnuts fried crisp; and peach-palm fruit, the chontaduro, which locals eat with salt or honey. To drink: guarapo, pressed sugar-cane juice served cold with lime; champus, a pineapple-and-lulo concoction with corn and limoncillo; or aguardiente Blanco del Valle, the licorice-flavored spirit that is the regional badge of identity.

## Getting In, Getting Out

Cali's Alfonso Bonilla Aragón International Airport sits about 20 km northeast of downtown, near Palmira, and handles domestic flights to every major Colombian city along with international routes to destinations such as Miami, Fort Lauderdale, Madrid, Panama City, Mexico City and Santiago de Chile. Buses reach Bogotá in about twelve hours, Medellín in nine, Buenaventura in three, and Popayán in three. The MIO (Masivo Integrado de Occidente) system of buses in dedicated lanes is the cleanest way to move around, with fares paid by smart card. Taxis use a unit-based meter; when in doubt, call for one rather than hailing on the street. For day trips, consider Calima Lake, about ninety minutes to two hours out in the mountains and a well-known wind- and kite-surf spot thanks to steady winds; the pilgrimage town of Buga, one hour northeast; or the humpback whale migration on the Pacific coast, reachable through Buenaventura, where whales arrive from Antarctic waters roughly between July and October to mate and calve. Cali can feel bigger and more complex than first-time visitors expect. Stay alert, use taxis at night, and give the city the weekend it deserves.

## Flight Context

Located at 3.44°N, 76.52°W in the Cauca River valley at 1,003 meters (3,290 feet) elevation. Alfonso Bonilla Aragón International Airport (SKCL) lies about 20 km northeast near Palmira. The Farallones de Cali range rises sharply to the west above 4,000 meters, with the Cordillera Central climbing east. Expect afternoon convective buildups over both ranges, especially during the April–May and October–November rainy seasons; mornings generally give the best visibility of the long green valley and the distinctive silhouette of the three crosses on Cerro de las Tres Cruces.
