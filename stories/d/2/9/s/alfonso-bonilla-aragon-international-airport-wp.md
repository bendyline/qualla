# Alfonso Bonilla Aragón International Airport

Cali's 24-hour international airport in the Cauca River valley, built for the 1971 Pan American Games and still one of the few Latin American secondary airports open around the clock.

**Location:** 3.5431, -76.3814

**Categories:** Airports in Colombia, Buildings and structures in Valle del Cauca Department, Cali, Cauca River valley

## Introduction

The 1971 Pan American Games built Cali an airport. The city had been getting by with three aging airfields — military, commercial, and one built by a breakaway airline — but hosting thousands of international athletes meant the national government had to commit to something bigger. The result sits 20 kilometres northeast of the city, between Palmira and Cali, on a long flat stretch of the Cauca River valley: Alfonso Bonilla Aragón International Airport, inaugurated on July 24, 1971. More than half a century later, with roughly 5.6 million passengers a year and enough stable weather to stay open 24 hours a day, it has outgrown every "too big, too expensive" critique its first architects ever heard.

## From a $9,500 Prize to Three Airports

Aviation in Cali began with a prize. On April 21, 1921, Italian pilot Ferruccio Guicciardi flew his Macchi-Hanriot HD-1 biplane, nicknamed "Telegraph I," from Guayaquil, Ecuador, to Cali, stopping in Quito and Pasto on the way. His was the first aircraft ever to land in the city, and he collected a 9,500-peso purse that the Department of Valle and the Cali City Council had put up for whoever managed it. After Colombia's 1932 border war with Peru, President Enrique Olaya Herrera asked SCADTA engineers to find a site that could support military operations. The German pilot Herbert Boy picked a piece of ground called "El Guabito," the bulldozers came in, and operations began on September 21, 1933. Two more airports followed in the 1940s — the commercial "Calipuerto" in Candelaria and VIARCO's "El Limonar" in the Pasoancho district — giving Cali, like Bogotá, three active airfields for a time.

## Palmaseca

Building the new airport took money the project did not originally have. Civil Aeronautics had already purchased land from Hacienda Palmaseca in Palmira, but construction ran into operational and financial trouble; the central government had to inject an additional 35 million US dollars to finish to international safety standards. When Palmaseca International Airport opened in July 1971 — during the government of President Misael Pastrana Borrero — it had a 3,000-metre runway, full taxiways, a parking apron, a main terminal with airline counters, restaurants, shops, even a small hotel and a nondenominational prayer chapel for nervous passengers. Critics called it oversized. Traffic caught up quickly. The airport was designated an alternate for Bogotá's El Dorado International, and in June 1989 it was renamed after Alfonso Bonilla Aragón, a Cali civic leader and journalist who had pushed hard for the project. The renaming was deeply unpopular locally — the Business Guilds and the Cali Chamber of Commerce formally asked the government to change it back. They did not win.

## Always Open

One thing Palmaseca — the name many in Cali still use — got spectacularly right: location. Far enough from both Cali and Palmira to have no noise restrictions, and blessed with year-round stable weather thanks to its position in the Cauca River valley between the Western and Central Andean cordilleras, the airport is one of the very few secondary airports in Latin America that operates 24 hours a day. Non-stop flights reach the United States, Spain, Panama, Ecuador, the Dutch Antilles, Mexico, and Chile. It is roughly a five-hour flight from Santiago de Chile, 50 minutes from Quito, and about three hours eighteen minutes from Miami. Cargo operations handle up to 747 freighters at a dedicated dock with bonded warehouses, a Free Trade Zone, and the usual air-freight apparatus.

## Difficult Years

The airport's story is not uncomplicated. Parts of the international terminal satellite collapsed in the mid-1980s, forcing Civil Aeronautics to rebuild and reinforce the structure in 1986. The general aviation area, built in the same period, became — as the airport's own history acknowledges — a clandestine centre for drug trafficking flights during the worst years of Colombia's cocaine trade; the Aeroclub del Pacifico eventually moved its operations to land near the Free Trade Zone to distance itself from that reputation. And the airport's runway is the final landfall, or should have been, for more than one tragedy: Copa Airlines Flight 201 from Panama City crashed in the Darién Gap in June 1992, and American Airlines Flight 965 from Miami crashed into a mountain near Buga on December 20, 1995, killing 159 people. Flight 965 remains the deadliest aviation disaster in Colombia's history.

## Gateway to the Valley

What travellers see now — arriving late at night, because the airport is always awake — is a modern terminal stretched between two cordilleras of the Andes. The flat valley floor runs north and south toward sugar cane fields and the industrial outskirts of Yumbo; Cali's skyline appears on the horizon to the southwest. Avianca's executive lounge, on the top floor of the Domestic Satellite Terminal, offers the now-standard conveniences — Wi-Fi, cable TV, snacks, charging ports. The airport's ICAO code is SKCL, and it remains, as it was designed to be half a century ago, the alternate when Bogotá's El Dorado weather turns sour: the backup plan for a capital perched two kilometres higher up in the clouds.

## Flight Context

ICAO: SKCL. Located at approximately 3.54°N, 76.38°W, between Palmira and Cali in the Cauca River valley at about 960 metres elevation. Runway 01/19 is 3,000 metres long. The airport is roughly 20 km northeast of downtown Cali. Expect year-round stable meteorological conditions; the airport operates 24 hours without noise restrictions. It serves as an alternate for SKBO (El Dorado, Bogotá) and other Colombian airports. From the air, look for the wide flat Cauca valley bracketed by the Western (Cordillera Occidental) and Central (Cordillera Central) Andes ranges; the terrain north and east of the field climbs quickly — the deadly ridges that figured in the 1995 American Airlines 965 accident lie to the north near Buga.
