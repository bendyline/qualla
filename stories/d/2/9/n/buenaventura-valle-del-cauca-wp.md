# Buenaventura, Valle del Cauca

Colombia's main Pacific port handles a large share of the country's international cargo from a rainy, majority Afro-Colombian city that has endured violence, neglect, and a civic uprising that forced the government to listen.

**Location:** 3.8772, -77.0267

**Categories:** Municipalities of Valle del Cauca Department, Populated places established in 1545, Port cities and towns in Colombia, Ports and harbours of Colombia, 1545 establishments in the Spanish Empire

## Introduction

Six to seven thousand millimeters of rain fall on Buenaventura in an average year - around twenty feet. The clouds rarely clear. Container ships slide in from the Pacific through mist and mangrove, unloading cargo that will travel up the Andes to Cali, Bogotá, and Medellín. By Transport Ministry estimates, around 40 to 45 percent of Colombia's international cargo passes through this one harbor (a smaller share once coal and oil exports are counted). Most of the city's urban life is packed onto a small humid island called Cascajal. The name means good fortune. The city's history has argued with the name.

## A Port With No Dry Season

Buenaventura was founded on July 14, 1540, by Juan Ladrillero under orders from Pascual de Andagoya, the Spanish conquistador who gave the word Perú to European ears. The land belonged to the Buscaja people. The first town was destroyed by Indigenous communities before 1600 and later rebuilt. For centuries the port was a minor affair. Then the Panama Canal opened in 1914 and Buenaventura's geography, once a disadvantage, became a strategic asset: it was the Pacific doorway closest to the heart of Colombia. The port grew, the city grew, and by the 1950s Buenaventura was a regular stopover for the international jet set. Most urban development still takes place on Cascajal Island. The majority of the municipality's land, though, is rural, a vast green sweep of mangrove estuaries and scattered villages belonging to ten Indigenous reservations of Emberá, Wounaan, and Chamí communities.

## A Majority Afro-Colombian City

The 2018 national census counted 235,064 people in Buenaventura (DANE's adjusted estimates put the population above 300,000), and about 85 percent identified as Afro-Colombian. The numbers are the residue of history: Spanish colonists brought enslaved Africans to the Pacific coast to work gold mines and river ports, and when abolition came in 1851 the freed people and their descendants stayed and built their own towns, their own music, and their own cuisine. In 2017 UNESCO added Buenaventura to its Creative Cities Network as a City of Gastronomy, recognizing a Pacific cooking tradition built around fish, coconut, plantain, and the dense seafood stew known as encocado.

## The Harbor and the Violence

The same geography that makes Buenaventura indispensable to Colombia's economy has also made it indispensable to its illicit economy. Cocaine leaves from here. So do the fortunes behind it. Colombian authorities once seized nearly twenty-eight million US dollars in cash from shipping containers routed through Buenaventura and linked to the trafficking brothers Luis Enrique and Javier Antonio Calle Serna, known as the Combas. Human Rights Watch reported in 2014 that the official homicide rate had fallen from 121 per 100,000 in 2006, the highest in Colombia, to 48 in 2013, while warning that many disappearances were never counted as homicides. The violence was not abstract. Community activist Víctor Hugo Vidal, who later served as the city's mayor, said at the time that the authorities might call the city safer, but for those who lived there nothing had changed. In the 2020s the fight passed to two local gangs, the Shottas and the Espartanos, whose church-brokered truces have cut killings for a time and then broken down, as in early 2025. Behind those numbers are human beings: fishermen, mothers, dockworkers, teachers. Their neighborhoods carried the weight, and often the grief, of a trade fought over by armed groups.

## The City That Shut Itself

In May 2017 Buenaventura did something rare. Tired of extreme poverty amid record port revenues, tired of a hospital without equipment, tired of fresh water that did not reach their homes despite rain that never stopped, residents launched a Paro Cívico, a civic strike. The city shut down for 22 days, from May 16 to June 6. Cargo backed up at the port as marches filled the streets. "To live with dignity and in peace in our territory" became the slogan of a movement led by grassroots Afro-Colombian organizers. After three weeks the national government signed an agreement promising infrastructure, schools, and clean water. Implementation has been uneven, and many of the 2017 commitments remain partly unfulfilled. But the strike itself changed the conversation. It proved that a long-neglected Pacific port city could bring Colombia's most important trade artery to a halt until someone in Bogotá listened.

## Beyond Cascajal

Away from the container cranes, Buenaventura opens onto one of South America's most extraordinary coastlines. Bahía Málaga, just to the north, is a deepwater bay where humpback whales arrive from Antarctic waters roughly between July and October to mate and calve in warm tropical waters, a journey of about 8,000 kilometers each way. Uramba National Natural Park protects 47,000 hectares of that bay. The beaches of Juanchaco, Ladrilleros, and La Barra draw Colombian travelers for long weekends, reachable only by boat from the Buenaventura pier. Inland, reached by a small improvised train, lies San Cipriano, a village tucked into a nature reserve where the river runs so clear you can swim above rounded stones. The Gerardo Tobar López Airport has limited flights. For most visitors, though, Buenaventura is reached by road from Cali, 116 kilometers to the southeast, down out of the Andes through green canyons slick with rain, to a port city that has earned the right to tell its own story.

## Flight Context

Located at 3.88°N, 77.03°W on Colombia's Pacific coast, across tidal estuaries and mangrove on Cascajal Island. Gerardo Tobar López Airport (SKBU), about 7 km southeast of the center, has limited scheduled service; Cali Alfonso Bonilla Aragón International (SKCL) is about 80 km east-southeast across the Cordillera Occidental (116 km to Cali by road). The region has one of the wettest tropical rainforest climates on Earth, with roughly 6,000-7,000 mm of annual rainfall and persistent low overcast; pilots should expect IFR conditions much of the year. Look for the harbor, Bahía Málaga to the north, and the snaking black ribbons of mangrove channels against dense green forest.
