# Buenaventura, Valle del Cauca

Colombia's main Pacific port handles roughly 60 percent of the country's seaborne trade from a rainy, majority Afro-Colombian city that has endured violence, neglect, and a civic uprising that forced the government to listen.

**Location:** 3.8772, -77.0267

**Categories:** Municipalities of Valle del Cauca Department, Populated places established in 1545, Port cities and towns in Colombia, Ports and harbours of Colombia, 1545 establishments in the Spanish Empire

## Introduction

Six thousand millimeters of rain fall on Buenaventura in an average year, which is more than twenty feet. The clouds rarely clear. Container ships slide in from the Pacific through mist and mangrove, unloading the sneakers, phones, and auto parts that will travel up the Andes to Cali, Bogotá, and Medellín. Roughly sixty percent of all Colombian seaborne imports and exports pass through this one harbor, which sits on a small humid island called Cascajal where most of the city's life is packed into a grid of streets that cannot seem to drain. The name means good fortune. The city's history has argued with the name.

## A Port With No Dry Season

Buenaventura was founded on July 14, 1540, by Juan Ladrillero under orders from Pascual de Andagoya, the Spanish conquistador who gave the word Perú to European ears. The land belonged to the Buscaja people. The first town was destroyed by Indigenous communities before 1600 and later rebuilt. For centuries the port was a minor affair. Then the Panama Canal opened in 1914 and Buenaventura's geography, once a disadvantage, became a strategic asset: it was the Pacific doorway closest to the heart of Colombia. The port grew, the city grew, and by the 1950s Buenaventura was a regular stopover for what newspapers then called the international jet set. Most urban development still takes place on Cascajal Island. The majority of the municipality's land, though, is rural, a vast green sweep of mangrove estuaries and scattered villages belonging to ten Indigenous reservations of Emberá, Wounaan, and Chamí communities.

## A Majority Afro-Colombian City

The 2018 national census counted 235,064 people in Buenaventura, and recorded that about 85 percent identified as Afro-Colombian. The numbers are the residue of history: the Spanish Crown brought enslaved Africans to the Pacific coast to work gold mines and river ports, and when abolition came in 1851 the descendants of those workers stayed and built their own towns, their own music, and their own cuisine. In 2017 UNESCO added Buenaventura to its Creative Cities Network as a City of Gastronomy, recognizing a Pacific cooking tradition built around fish, coconut, plantain, and the dense seafood stew known as encocado. On any evening along the Pier, the smell of grilled piangua clams and toasted coconut milk carries across the water as street vendors set up.

## The Harbor and the Violence

The same geography that makes Buenaventura indispensable to Colombia's economy has also made it indispensable to its illicit economy. Cocaine leaves from here. So do the fortunes behind it. Colombian authorities once seized nearly twenty-eight million US dollars in cash from shipping containers routed through Buenaventura that belonged to brothers Luis Enrique and Javier Antonio Calle Serna, the traffickers known as the Combas. Between 2008 and 2010 the number of homicides in the city doubled. By 2010 the murder rate was 175 per 100,000 people, about five times the national average. The violence was not abstract. Community activist Víctor Hugo Vidal has said simply that if you ask the authorities the city is safer, but for those who live there nothing has changed. Behind those numbers are human beings: fishermen, mothers, dockworkers, teachers. Their neighborhoods carried the weight, and often the grief, of a trade fought over by cartels.

## The City That Shut Itself

In May 2017 Buenaventura did something rare. Tired of extreme poverty amid record port revenues, tired of a hospital without equipment, tired of fresh water that did not reach their homes despite rain that never stopped, residents launched a Paro Cívico, a civic strike. The city shut down for 22 days. Container traffic stopped at the port. Marches filled streets normally owned by cartels. "We want to live in dignity on our own land," became the slogan of a movement led by grassroots Afro-Colombian organizers. After three weeks the national government signed an agreement promising infrastructure, schools, and clean water. Implementation has been uneven, and many of the 2017 commitments remain partly unfulfilled. But the strike itself changed the conversation. It proved that a long-neglected Pacific port city could bring Colombia's most important trade artery to a halt until someone in Bogotá listened.

## Beyond Cascajal

Away from the container cranes, Buenaventura opens onto one of South America's most extraordinary coastlines. Bahía Málaga, just to the north, is a deepwater bay where humpback whales arrive from Antarctica each June to July, sometimes more than a thousand of them at once, to mate and calve in warm tropical waters. They travel up to 17,000 kilometers one way. Uramba National Natural Park protects 47,000 hectares of that bay. The beaches of Juanchaco, Ladrilleros, and La Barra draw Colombian travelers for long weekends, reachable only by boat from the Buenaventura pier. Inland, reached by a small improvised train, lies San Cipriano, a village tucked into a nature reserve where the river runs so clear you can swim above rounded stones and watch river otters. The Gerardo Tobar López Airport handles regional flights. For most visitors, though, Buenaventura is reached by road from Cali, 116 kilometers east, down out of the Andes through green canyons slick with rain, to a port city that has earned the right to tell its own story.

## Flight Context

Located at 3.88°N, 77.03°W on Colombia's Pacific coast, across tidal estuaries and mangrove on Cascajal Island. Gerardo Tobar López Airport (SKBU) serves regional flights; Cali Alfonso Bonilla Aragón International (SKCL) is 116 km east across the Cordillera Occidental. The region has one of the wettest tropical rainforest climates on Earth, with roughly 6,000 mm of annual rainfall and persistent low overcast; pilots should expect IFR conditions much of the year. Best visual approach in early morning at low altitudes to pick out the harbor, Bahía Málaga to the north, and the snaking black ribbons of mangrove channels against dense green forest.
