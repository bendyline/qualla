# Farallones de Cali

A craggy chain of Andean peaks rising to over 4,000 meters west of Cali, home to 300+ bird species and a paramo reported to have no frailejones.

**Location:** 3.3400, -76.6958

**Categories:** mountain, national-park, colombia, andes, biodiversity, paramo

## Introduction

Step into the street in almost any neighborhood of Cali and look west. On clear mornings, before the afternoon clouds climb the Cauca Valley and swallow the skyline, the Farallones stand up sharp and blue against the sky - a line of rocky crags rising from the edge of the valley to over 4,000 meters. *Farallon* means cliff in Spanish, and the name fits. What the view from the city does not convey is what happens on the other side of those cliffs. The west slope falls away toward the Pacific Ocean through some of the wettest, most biodiverse forest in the Americas. The same ridge that defines the Cali skyline is also the edge of the Choco bioregion, where the rain does not really stop.

## Two Slopes, Two Worlds

The mountains split the weather in half. The eastern slope, facing Cali and the Cauca Valley, is drier - it supplies rivers like the Cali, the Pichinde, the Melendez, the Lili, and the Pance, which tumble down past the city and into the Cauca. The western slope catches the Pacific rain full-force. The Anchicaya and Dagua rivers carve deep canyons on that side, with a hydroelectric dam on the Anchicaya feeding power to the region. The temperature range is remarkable: tropical heat at the foothills, freezing air on the highest summits. Elevation changes everything in less than a day's walk.

## The Park Nobody Else Has

Parque Nacional Natural Farallones de Cali was established in 1968 and is the largest national park in Valle del Cauca. It covers multiple ecosystems in one: tropical wet forest at the lowest elevations, subtropical cloud forest blanketed in fog and draped in moss, montane Andean forest, and paramo at the top. The paramo is the strange one. Many Colombian paramos are dominated by *frailejones* - tall, fuzzy, daisy-family plants with leaves like felt. The Farallones paramo is reported to have none, though accounts of its vegetation disagree. It is geographically isolated from its neighbors, and its plant communities differ from theirs. It is the same ecosystem, but composed of different species - a little pocket of alpine wetland that does not match any of the paramos around it.

## A Birder's Mountain

More than 300 species of birds have been recorded in the park, and the Anchicaya watershed on the west slope is a celebrated birding area. Three birds found only in Colombia define the place: the multicolored tanager, its body painted in green, yellow, red, and black; the crested ant-tanager; and the chestnut wood-quail, a plump ground bird that sings at dawn. The Andean cock-of-the-rock holds its leks in the Pichinde River valley, where males gather on traditional display perches to court females with bright orange plumage and strange bows. Birders come specifically for this. The leks around Penas Blancas are the most famous.

## Spectacled Bears and Spider Monkeys

The western slope holds suitable habitat for the spectacled bear, *Tremarctos ornatus*, South America's only native bear - listed as vulnerable, shy, rarely seen. Five primate species share the forest: white-headed capuchins, red howlers, mantled howlers, gray-bellied night monkeys (called *marteja* in Colombia), and the Colombian spider monkey (*marimonda*). The spider monkey and the night monkey are both listed as threatened. At lower elevations, poison frogs and cane toads navigate the leaf litter. *Oophaga lehmanni* - a small, red-banded poison frog - is found only in a small part of this stretch of the Western Cordillera.

## Pico de Loro and the Climb

The park's most popular summit is Pico de Loro (Parrot's Peak) at 2,832 meters - a day hike from the ranger station at El Topacio, offering views of Cali and the Cauca Valley; it is one of the few routes the park authorizes for ecotourism. Punta Pance, at 4,020 meters, was long a one- or two-day climb with camping at a spot called Balconies, where a spring runs near a flat spot for five to ten tents; it is not among the park's open routes today. From that summit, on the rare windows the clouds offer up, climbers have seen Buenaventura, the Pacific, and the Nevado del Huila rising to the southeast. Most of the year, though, the mountain is in cloud. You hike up into the white, and you come back down into the white, and somewhere in between you pass through a world that has not quite given up its secrets.

## Threats to the Green

The park is under pressure. Illegal gold mining is the most urgent threat - operators have dug into the high ground between the Cali and Anchicaya river basins with mercury and explosives, poisoning streams on both slopes. Commercial logging, agriculture creeping up the foothills, hunting, unregulated tourism, and roads pushed into remote areas all take pieces of the forest each year. Regional authorities have raised alarms about the reactivation of illegal mining camps. For the city of Cali, the stakes are tangible: these mountains feed rivers that supply part of its water. The rivers that wash down the east slope run straight through the city. Protecting the Farallones is not abstract conservation. It is municipal infrastructure with scenery.

## Flight Context

Farallones de Cali is centered near 3.34 degrees N, 76.70 degrees W, with summit elevations up to about 4,050 meters. The ridge runs roughly north-south, immediately west of Cali (pop. ~2.3 million). Alfonso Bonilla Aragon International Airport (SKCL) is the nearest hub, about 40 km northeast in Palmira, across the Cauca Valley. Maintain a significant terrain clearance - mountain wave and orographic buildups are common, especially in the afternoon. The Anchicaya hydroelectric dam lies on the western slope. On clear mornings, the full ridge is visible against the sky above Cali; by afternoon, orographic clouds typically cap the summits.
