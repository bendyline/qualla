# Farallones de Cali

A craggy chain of Pacific-facing Andean peaks rising over 4,000 meters above the city of Cali, home to 300+ bird species and the only paramo in Colombia without frailejones.

**Location:** 3.3400, -76.6958

**Categories:** mountain, national-park, colombia, andes, biodiversity, paramo

## Introduction

Step into the street in almost any neighborhood of Cali and look west. On clear mornings, before the afternoon clouds climb the Cauca Valley and swallow the skyline, the Farallones stand up sharp and blue against the sky - a line of granite cliffs twenty kilometers long, rising from the floor of the valley to over 4,000 meters. *Farallon* means cliff in Spanish, and the name fits. What the view from the city does not convey is what happens on the other side of those cliffs. The west slope falls away toward the Pacific Ocean through some of the wettest, most biodiverse forest in the Americas. The same ridge that defines the Cali skyline is also the edge of the Choco bioregion, where the rain does not really stop.

## Two Slopes, Two Worlds

The mountains split the weather in half. The eastern slope, facing Cali and the Cauca Valley, is drier - it supplies rivers like the Cali, the Pichinde, the Melendez, the Lili, and the Pance, which tumble down past the city and into the Cauca. The western slope catches the Pacific rain full-force. The Anchicaya and Dagua rivers carve deep canyons on that side, with a hydroelectric dam on the Anchicaya producing much of the electricity that lights Cali at night. The temperature range is remarkable: tropical heat at the foothills, freezing air on the highest summits. Elevation changes everything in less than a day's walk.

## The Park Nobody Else Has

Parque Nacional Natural Farallones de Cali was established in 1968, making it the largest national park in Valle del Cauca. It covers multiple ecosystems in one: tropical wet forest at the lowest elevations, subtropical cloud forest blanketed in fog and draped in moss, montane Andean forest, and paramo at the top. The paramo is the strange one. Every other Northern Andean paramo is dominated by *frailejones* - tall, fuzzy, daisy-family plants with leaves like felt. Farallones de Cali has no frailejones at all. The paramo here is geographically isolated from its neighbors, and over time the dominant plants simply diverged. It is the same ecosystem, but composed of different species - a little pocket of alpine wetland that does not match any of the paramos around it.

## A Birder's Mountain

More than 300 species of birds have been recorded in the park, and the Anchicaya watershed on the west slope is considered one of the world's premier birding destinations. Three endemic species define the place: the multicolored tanager, its body painted in green, yellow, red, and black; the crested ant-tanager, which mobs army-ant swarms on the forest floor; and the chestnut wood-quail, a plump ground bird that sings at dawn. The Andean cock-of-the-rock holds its leks in the Pichinde River valley, where males gather on traditional display perches to court females with bright orange plumage and strange bows. Birders come specifically for this. The Penas Blancas lek is the most famous, accessible from a trailhead on the northern edge of the park.

## Spectacled Bears and Spider Monkeys

The western slope holds suitable habitat for the spectacled bear, *Tremarctos ornatus*, South America's only native bear - listed as vulnerable, shy, rarely seen. Five primate species share the forest: white-headed capuchins, red howlers, mantled howlers, gray-bellied night monkeys (called *marteja* in Colombia), and the Colombian spider monkey (*marimonda*). The spider monkey and the night monkey are both vulnerable. At lower elevations, poison frogs and cane toads navigate the leaf litter. *Oophaga lehmanni* - a small, bright-red banded poison frog - lives here and nowhere else in the world.

## Pico de Loro and the Climb

The park's most popular summit is Pico de Loro (Parrot's Peak) at 2,832 meters - a day hike from the ranger station at El Topacio, offering views of Cali, the Cauca Valley, and, on rare clear days, snowcapped peaks on the far side of the Central Cordillera. More ambitious hikers take on Punta Pance at 4,020 meters over one or two days. From the summit, on the rare windows the clouds offer up, you can see Buenaventura Bay, the Pacific itself, ships at anchor off the port, and the Nevado del Huila rising to the southeast. Camping is possible at Balconies, where a spring runs near a flat spot for five to ten tents. Most of the year, though, the mountain is in cloud. You hike up into the white, and you come back down into the white, and somewhere in between you pass through a world that has not quite given up its secrets.

## Threats to the Green

The park is under pressure. Illegal gold mining is the most urgent threat - operators work the western slopes with mercury and sluice rigs, poisoning streams that flow toward the Pacific. Commercial logging, agriculture creeping up the foothills, hunting, unregulated tourism, and roads pushed into remote areas all take pieces of the forest each year. Regional authorities have raised alarms about the reactivation of illegal mining camps. For the city of Cali, the stakes are tangible: these mountains are the water supply. The rivers that wash down the east slope are the rivers the city drinks from. Protecting the Farallones is not abstract conservation. It is municipal infrastructure with scenery.

## Flight Context

Farallones de Cali is centered near 3.34 degrees N, 76.70 degrees W, with summit elevations up to approximately 4,100 meters. The ridge runs roughly north-south, immediately west of Cali (pop. ~2.2 million). Alfonso Bonilla Aragon International Airport (SKCL) is the nearest hub, in the Cauca Valley north of the city. Maintain a significant terrain clearance - mountain wave and orographic buildups are common, especially in the afternoon. The Anchicaya hydroelectric dam lies on the western slope. On clear mornings, the full ridge is visible against the sky above Cali; by afternoon, orographic clouds typically cap the summits.
