# Marquetalia Republic

In May 1964, the Colombian army attacked a remote peasant commune called Marquetalia. Forty-eight armed defenders escaped into the mountains and, two years later, founded the FARC.

**Location:** 3.0400, -75.8700

**Categories:** History, Colombia, Civil conflict, Rural communities, 20th century

## Introduction

The helicopters came first. In May 1964, up to a thousand Colombian soldiers - FARC accounts later put the number at 16,000 - moved on a mountain settlement called Marquetalia. The enclave covered roughly 800 square kilometers of steep Andean country in southern Tolima, around 6,000 feet above sea level, with heavy rains and a population of about a thousand people. Forty-eight of them carried rifles. The army brought helicopters, aircraft and a counterinsurgency plan shaped with US military advisers from Fort Bragg. The villagers scattered into the jungle. The first combat came on May 27, the date the FARC would later mark as its birth. Two years later, the organization they founded would be called the Revolutionary Armed Forces of Colombia. FARC.

## After La Violencia

To understand Marquetalia you have to understand the decade before it. On April 9, 1948, the populist Liberal leader Jorge Eliécer Gaitán was shot dead in downtown Bogotá. His killing triggered the Bogotazo riots, then the slow partisan bloodletting known as La Violencia, which killed between 200,000 and 300,000 Colombians over the next ten years. Liberal and communist peasants organized self-defense groups in the countryside to survive attacks by Conservative paramilitaries and army units. When General Gustavo Rojas Pinilla seized power in 1953 and offered an amnesty, most groups demobilized. Some did not. A handful of remote enclaves kept their weapons and their partial self-government even after Rojas fell in 1957 and the Liberal-Conservative National Front began sharing power. These were the places Conservative congressmen took to calling independent republics. Marquetalia was one.

## Life in the Commune

Jacobo Arenas, one of the surviving leaders, wrote Diario de la resistencia de Marquetalia in 1972. The book describes the settlement not as a military base but as an improvised commune where Communist Party ideologues, peasant fighters, their families, and their friends worked the land together. The villagers extorted local landowners from time to time - the diary does not hide this - and skirmished occasionally with government forces. But for most of the post-La Violencia years, Marquetalia was simply a place where about a thousand poor peasants had decided they would govern themselves, and the government in Bogotá, for a time, left them to it.

## Plan LASO

Washington changed the equation. In October 1959, a US Special Survey Team arrived in Colombia to assess internal security. Among its recommendations: aid should be sterile and covert in nature, kept clear of interventionist charges. In February 1962, a Fort Bragg Special Warfare team led by General William Yarborough made a second visit. The result was Plan Lazo, launched later that year, which combined military operations against the independent republics with civic action programs for the cleared populations. Under pressure from Conservative politicians and from Washington, President Guillermo León Valencia authorized the assault on Marquetalia. It became, in Arenas's bitter framing, a Pentagon operation fought by Colombian bloodhounds commanded by American hawks. Operation Marquetalia was the centerpiece of Plan Lazo.

## Forty-Eight Men

When the attack began in May 1964, the armed peasants who fought back - forty-eight of them, in the FARC's own telling - were led by a commander then known as Manuel Marulanda Vélez, whose real name was Pedro Antonio Marín. He would later become known as Tirofijo, Sureshot, and would lead the FARC until his death in 2008. Arenas was his political counterpart, the Communist Party intellectual who provided ideological framework to what was, at root, a peasant self-defense movement. The fighting lasted nearly two months before the fighters slipped away into the mountains rather than hold the ground. That July the survivors gathered in an assembly, proclaimed an agrarian program and decided to fight on - not as a village defending itself but as a guerrilla force operating across the country. In 1966, at their Second Guerrilla Conference, they formally adopted the name Fuerzas Armadas Revolucionarias de Colombia. The FARC was born.

## Fifty Years in the Mountains

What happened next became one of the longest running armed conflicts in the Western Hemisphere. By 1982, under the Seventh Guerrilla Conference, the group had added People's Army to its name and expanded into dozens of fronts across Colombia. By the 1990s, with drug-trade revenue fueling its operations, FARC fielded thousands of fighters and controlled vast swaths of rural territory. The 2016 peace accord, signed by President Juan Manuel Santos and FARC commander Rodrigo Londoño, ended the state's war with the FARC. Dissident factions and other armed groups remain active. Marquetalia itself - the hillside, the streams, the scattered homesteads - returned to being simply a remote place, its meaning now historical. For the roughly 450,000 people Colombia's Truth Commission counts as killed in the conflict between 1985 and 2018 alone, the question of whether that 1964 assault was necessary or catastrophic remains unsettled. For the more than seven million displaced, it is not a question so much as a long, unfinished answer.

## Flight Context

Located at 3.04°N, 75.87°W in southern Tolima, Colombia, near the Huila border, at about 6,000 feet elevation in the Cordillera Central. Gateway airport: Benito Salas (SKNV) in Neiva, Huila. The terrain is steep Andean country with heavy rainfall - cloud cover frequent in afternoons. Nevado del Huila (5,364 m, active) rises to the southwest as a major visual landmark in clear weather. Access historically by small strips and roads; the area remains remote.
