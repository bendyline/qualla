# Marquetalia Republic

In May 1964, the Colombian army attacked a remote peasant commune called Marquetalia. Forty-eight armed defenders escaped into the mountains and, two years later, founded the FARC.

**Location:** 3.0400, -75.8700

**Categories:** History, Colombia, Civil conflict, Rural communities, 20th century

## Introduction

The helicopters came first. In May 1964, more than a thousand Colombian soldiers - some sources say 16,000 when including support elements - moved on a mountain settlement called Marquetalia. The village was roughly 800 square kilometers of steep Andean country in the department of Huila, around 6,000 feet above sea level, with monsoon rains and a population of about a thousand peasant families. Forty-eight of them carried rifles. The army carried what the Pentagon was willing to send: helicopters, aircraft, counterinsurgency doctrine developed at Fort Bragg. The villagers scattered into the jungle. On May 27 the survivors held a meeting. Two years later, the organization they founded would be called the Revolutionary Armed Forces of Colombia. FARC.

## After La Violencia

To understand Marquetalia you have to understand the decade before it. On April 9, 1948, the populist Liberal leader Jorge Eliécer Gaitán was shot dead in downtown Bogotá. His killing triggered the Bogotazo riots, then the slow partisan bloodletting known as La Violencia, which killed between 200,000 and 300,000 Colombians over the next ten years. Liberal and communist peasants organized self-defense groups in the countryside to survive attacks by Conservative paramilitaries and army units. When General Gustavo Rojas Pinilla seized power in 1953 and offered an amnesty, most groups demobilized. Some did not. A handful of remote enclaves kept their weapons and their partial self-government even after Rojas fell in 1958 and the Liberal-Conservative National Front began sharing power. These were the places Conservative congressmen took to calling independent republics. Marquetalia was one.

## Life in the Commune

Jacobo Arenas, one of the surviving leaders, wrote Diario de la resistencia de Marquetalia in 1972. The book describes the settlement not as a military base but as an improvised commune where Communist Party ideologues, peasant fighters, their families, and their friends worked the land together. Children attended makeshift schools. Coffee and plantains and maize came out of cleared mountain terraces. Disputes got settled by assembly rather than by the police who did not come up the roads. The villagers extorted local landowners from time to time - the diary does not hide this - and skirmished occasionally with government forces. But for most of the post-La Violencia years, Marquetalia was simply a place where several hundred poor families had decided they would govern themselves, and the government in Bogotá was content to leave them to it.

## Plan LASO

Washington changed the equation. In October 1959, a US Special Survey Team arrived in Colombia to assess internal security. Among its recommendations: aid should be sterile and covert in nature, kept clear of interventionist charges. In February 1962, a Fort Bragg Special Warfare team led by General William Yarborough made a second visit. The result was Plan Lazo, launched later that year, which combined military operations against the independent republics with civic action programs for the cleared populations. Under pressure from Conservative politicians and the Kennedy administration, President Guillermo León Valencia authorized the assault on Marquetalia. It became, in Arenas's bitter framing, a Pentagon operation fought by Colombian bloodhounds commanded by American hawks. Operation Marquetalia was the centerpiece of Plan Lazo. The people who lived there did not get to vote on it.

## Forty-Eight Men

When the attack began in May 1964, the forty-eight armed peasants who fought back were led by a commander then known as Manuel Marulanda Vélez, whose real name was Pedro Antonio Marín. He would later become known as Tirofijo, Sureshot, and would lead the FARC until his death in 2008. Arenas was his political counterpart, the Communist Party intellectual who provided ideological framework to what was, at root, a peasant self-defense movement. The villagers who were not armed were forced from their homes. Most lost everything. The forty-eight fighters retreated into the mountains along with their families and what supplies they could carry. Some did not survive the retreat. Those who did held a meeting on May 27 and decided to fight on - not as a village defending itself but as a guerrilla force operating across the country. In 1966, at their Second Guerrilla Conference, they formally adopted the name Fuerzas Armadas Revolucionarias de Colombia. The FARC was born.

## Fifty Years in the Mountains

What happened next became one of the longest running armed conflicts in the Western Hemisphere. By 1982, under the Seventh Guerrilla Conference, the group had added People's Army to its name and expanded into dozens of fronts across Colombia. By the 1990s, with drug-trade revenue fueling its operations, FARC fielded thousands of fighters and controlled vast swaths of rural territory. The 2016 peace accord, signed by President Juan Manuel Santos and FARC commander Rodrigo Londoño, ended most of the organized conflict. Dissident factions remain active. Marquetalia itself - the hillside, the streams, the scattered homesteads - returned to being simply a remote place, its meaning now historical. For the 260,000 Colombians killed in the five decades between the attack and the accord, the question of whether that 1964 assault was necessary or catastrophic remains unsettled. For the 5.9 million displaced, it is not a question so much as a long, unfinished answer.

## Flight Context

Located at 3.04°N, 75.87°W in the department of Huila, Colombia, at about 6,000 feet elevation in the Cordillera Central. Gateway airport: Benito Salas (SKNV) in Neiva, Huila. The terrain is steep Andean country with monsoon rainfall - cloud cover frequent in afternoons. Nevado del Huila (5,364 m, active) rises to the north as a major visual landmark in clear weather. Access historically by small strips and roads; the area remains remote.
