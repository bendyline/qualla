# Magdalena Valley dry forests

A 19,748 km² ecoregion of cactus, thorn forest, and Miocene fossils, the Magdalena Valley dry forests are an evolutionary refuge with no national park to protect them.

**Location:** 4.0000, -74.9800

**Categories:** Ecoregions, Dry forests, Colombia, Endemic species, Paleontology

## Introduction

There is a desert in the middle of Colombia. Most people are surprised to hear this, because Colombia is supposed to be green - Amazon to the south, Pacific rainforest to the west, Andean cloud forest in the middle. But in a valley between two mountain ranges, where the winds run dry and the rain falls somewhere else, the land grows cactus instead of cloud. The Magdalena Valley dry forests ecoregion, 19,748 square kilometers of thorn scrub and ochre ground, sits in the upper reaches of the Magdalena River. And the most dramatic piece of it is the Tatacoa Desert, which isn't a desert in the geological sense but reads as one to the eye - red badlands, weathered arroyos, a night sky so clear it draws astronomers from across the continent.

## Why It Is Dry

The Andes are the reason. As moisture-bearing winds push in from the Pacific and the Amazon, they climb the western and eastern cordilleras and drop their water on the slopes. By the time the air reaches the valley floor, around 450 meters above sea level, it has been wrung out. Rainfall in the Magdalena dry forest zone ranges from more than 2,000 millimeters a year at the wetter edges down to about 830 on the dry valley floor, and under 700 in the heart of the Tatacoa. Mean annual temperature sits near 27 degrees Celsius, climbing to almost 30 in July and August. The valley floor is flat, coated with alluvial soils and volcanic ash from Nevado del Huila and the Puracé volcano - the two active peaks that still dust this corner of Colombia when they erupt.

## Bones in the Badlands

The Tatacoa Desert has been mined for Miocene fossils since the 1920s. Some thirteen million years ago, this was a warmer, wetter place full of giant sloths, terror birds, marsupial saber-tooths, and mammal lineages long since extinct. Paleontologists working the La Venta fossil beds of the Honda Group here have described primates, giant caimans, sloths and many species known from nowhere else, one of the richest Miocene fossil assemblages in South America. The same weathering that makes the Tatacoa look lunar is what exposes the bones. Wind and rain chew at the sandstone, and every few years a skull, a jaw, a vertebra emerges from the ground. Modern visitors come at night for the sky. In a country where most cities sit in damp highlands, the Tatacoa offers one of the few reliably cloudless nights, and an observatory near Villavieja draws amateur astronomers when the Milky Way arcs clearly overhead.

## Life That Learned to Wait

The thorn forest reads like a Sonoran landscape transplanted. Opuntia prickly pear, barrel-shaped Melocactus, Armatocereus humilis, the columnar Stenocereus griseus - these cacti handle the heat. Above them, umbrella-shaped trees under 15 meters tall - Pithecellobium bogotense, Prosopis juliflora, Vachellia farnesiana - throw shade during the long dry season. Endemics hide in the mix. Steriphoma colombiana and Amaria petiolata grow nowhere else. And in the transition between dry and moist forest grows the May flower, Cattleya trianae, Colombia's national flower and also highly endangered. Some scientists think this valley and its surrounding forests served as a Pleistocene refugium: during the cooler, drier ice ages the dry forest expanded, and the region became a centre of endemism. That may explain the odd concentration of endemics here: orchids, birds, butterflies all speciated in isolation.

## Turtles, Tapirs, and the Loss

The Magdalena River turtle, Podocnemis lewyana, is critically endangered. The white-footed tamarin, Saguinus leucopus, is endangered too. Mountain tapirs, Tapirus pinchaque, survive in the highest corners of the ecoregion. The tropical rattlesnake Crotalus durissus still hunts in the drier corners. Bird surveys have found 297 species in the region, though only 35 are directly tied to the forest. Yellow-eared parrots, long thought extinct, were rediscovered in the wider region. The blue-billed curassow was not so lucky - it is no longer present in the ecoregion. The World Wildlife Fund rates this habitat Critical/Endangered. As of 2001, 69 percent of the original landscape had been transformed by human use, most of it into farmland, and none of it was protected; no national park covers it today. Early-2000s maps showed about 31 fragments, averaging just 155 hectares each - stands of thorn forest in Tolima and along the banks of the Cabrera River.

## Flying the Dry Valley

From the air, the Magdalena Valley dry forests look nothing like the rest of Colombia. The land is tawny where the rest is green, striped with irrigation canals and rice paddies where farmers have taken what water they can divert. The Tatacoa reveals itself as a gash of red and gray, eroded into fingers and gullies that photograph beautifully at sunrise. Neiva's Benito Salas Airport (SKNV) is the closest gateway to the Tatacoa. Flying upriver toward the Colombian Massif, the valley narrows and the mountains close in from both sides, a reminder that this pocket of desert exists only because of the rain shadow geography.

## Flight Context

Located at roughly 4°N, 74.98°W in the upper Magdalena Valley of Colombia, 450 m average elevation. The Tatacoa Desert near Villavieja is the most striking aerial feature. Gateway airport: Benito Salas (SKNV) in Neiva, Huila. Best visibility morning; afternoon convective activity builds over surrounding cordilleras. The tawny valley floor contrasts sharply with the forested flanks of the Cordillera Central (west) and Oriental (east).
