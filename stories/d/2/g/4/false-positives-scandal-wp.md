# The False Positives Scandal

Colombian soldiers lured poor civilians with promises of work, killed them, and dressed the bodies as guerrillas for promotions and bonuses - a pattern that eventually claimed thousands of lives.

**Location:** 4.5781, -74.2144

**Categories:** human-rights, colombia, history, armed-conflict, extrajudicial-killing

## Introduction

They were poor men, mostly. Some had intellectual disabilities. One recruiter would later testify that he was paid 500 US dollars per person he delivered. The men thought they were leaving Soacha, on the southern edge of Bogota, for work they had been promised - construction jobs, farm labor, something that paid. They were driven hundreds of miles north to Ocana, near the Venezuelan border. There they were murdered by Colombian Army soldiers, dressed in fatigues or rebel insignia, and reported up the chain as guerrillas killed in combat. The Colombian term for this is *falsos positivos* - false positives. The practice did not begin in 2008, and it did not end when the killings in Ocana were exposed. But in 2008, for the first time, the country could no longer pretend it was not happening.

## How the Scheme Worked

The details are simple and brutal. A recruiter - sometimes a civilian, sometimes a soldier in plainclothes - approached a man in a poor neighborhood with an offer of work. He promised transportation and pay. The victim boarded a bus or a truck. He was driven to a remote location. Soldiers killed him on arrival. The body was redressed, placed near weapons, and photographed. Paperwork was filed claiming a guerrilla had been eliminated in combat. The soldiers who reported the kill received time off, promotions, cash bonuses, extra leave. The recruiter was paid per head. Colombian investigative agencies have found cases as far back as 1988. A declassified 1990 cable from US Ambassador Thomas McNamara described an incident involving nine men killed by the military and presented as guerrilleros. The killings continued throughout the 1990s. What changed after 2005 was the scale.

## The Directive and the Body Count

In November 2005, Defense Minister Camilo Ospina Bernal signed Permanent Ministerial Directive No. 29. The directive laid out specific benefits that Colombian soldiers could receive for producing verifiable combat kills of guerrilla fighters. Later analysis by the Catholic research center CINEP, examining 951 documented cases of extrajudicial killing, found that at least 730 of the victims died during President Alvaro Uribe Velez's presidency - with a 50 percent increase in his second term, the term that began after Directive 29 took effect. A 2018 study cited in The Guardian estimated 10,000 victims total between 2002 and 2010. The International Federation for Human Rights put the count at more than 3,000 civilian victims between 2002 and 2008, and asked the International Criminal Court to consider an investigation.

## Soacha, 2008

The men from Soacha disappeared over a span of months in 2008. There were 22 of them. When their bodies turned up in Ocana - several hundred miles from where they had last been seen - their families started asking questions. The questions got louder. Journalists followed the story. The recruiter broke, testified about the 500-dollar payments, and a pattern that had been quietly denied for years surfaced all at once. Defense Minister Juan Manuel Santos - who would be elected president two years later - fired 27 officers, including three generals. The commander of the Colombian Army, General Mario Montoya, resigned on November 4, 2008. President Uribe ordered that the cases be handled by civilian rather than military courts. In June 2012, six soldiers received long prison sentences for the Soacha killings.

## A Colonel's Confession

In 2011, a Colombian army colonel was sentenced to 21 years in prison. He had admitted to his role in the killing of two peasants who were then presented as guerrilleros. Before sentencing, he admitted something larger: his unit had carried out 57 similar murders. He said he learned of the practice when he arrived at the unit, and that Defense Minister Santos himself had warned him to produce measurable combat results or lose his position. The colonel later testified at other false positive trials. In 2013, a Colombian radio station played tape of him offering silence in exchange for money from other soldiers who might be called to testify. The details matter because this was never a single rogue unit. Orders flowed from above. Bonuses flowed from above. The killings, when they happened, happened under the supervision of lieutenants, captains, colonels, and generals who understood what body counts meant for their careers.

## The Jurisdiction for Peace

In August 2016, after decades of war, the Colombian government and FARC signed a peace agreement. One of its products was the Special Jurisdiction for Peace - the JEP - a transitional justice system designed to document crimes committed during the conflict and to try those responsible. By the time the JEP was established, about 800 people, mostly low-ranking soldiers, had been convicted in false-positive cases. Military courts had handled most of them, suppressing evidence in some cases and punishing whistleblowers in others. The JEP eventually charged General Mario Montoya himself, along with eight other officers, with war crimes and crimes against humanity for lying about combat kills, pressuring subordinates to produce bodies, and the 130 assassinations and forced disappearances attributed to their units in Medellin between 2002 and 2003. The work of naming the dead, counting them accurately, and identifying who ordered their killing is still in progress.

## The Weight of What Remains

What was done to these men was a betrayal at every level. They were betrayed by a state that existed to protect them, by officers who murdered them for promotions, by a system of incentives that turned poor Colombian lives into bookkeeping entries. Many families searched for years for sons and brothers before finding them in anonymous graves. Some families never stopped searching. Father Raul Carvajal spent years demanding justice for his son, a soldier who had refused to participate in the scheme and had been killed himself - Carvajal died in 2021 still pressing his case. The Comision de la Verdad, the Truth Commission that operated alongside the JEP, collected the testimony of survivors and relatives. Former President Santos, who had been defense minister when much of the killing accelerated, publicly apologized in 2019 to the victims' families: *Pido perdon desde lo mas profundo de mi alma* - I ask forgiveness from the depths of my soul. The apology did not return anyone. But after decades of denial, it put on record what the country had done to its own.

## Flight Context

The scandal is associated with sites across Colombia. The killings of the Soacha victims were committed in Ocana, Norte de Santander (approximately 8.24 degrees N, 73.35 degrees W), several hundred miles north of Bogota. Soacha itself lies at 4.58 degrees N, 74.21 degrees W, immediately south of the capital. Additional documented false-positive killings are concentrated in Antioquia (near Medellin), Sucre, Cesar, and the Colombian-Venezuelan border region. Nearest major airports: Bogota El Dorado (SKBO), Medellin Jose Maria Cordova (SKRG), and Ocana Aguas Claras (SKOC).
