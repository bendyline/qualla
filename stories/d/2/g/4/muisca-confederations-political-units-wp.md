# Muisca Confederations

On the high plains where Bogotá now stands, four Muisca confederations traded emeralds, counted in twenties, and gave the Spanish the legend of El Dorado.

**Location:** 4.7175, -74.2094

**Categories:** Indigenous history, Pre-Columbian Americas, Colombian history, Chibcha cultures, Muisca

## Introduction

The number twenty had a name of its own: gueta, from gue, meaning house. The Muisca counted in twenties because twenty is what a human body carries - ten fingers, ten toes, the whole census of flesh. Above twenty, they counted houses: gue-bosa was two houses, forty; gue-hisca was five houses, a hundred. This is the kind of detail that reveals a people. Before the Spanish arrived on the Altiplano Cundiboyacense in 1537, the Muisca had built four regional confederations across the high Andean plateau where Bogotá now stands, and they had arranged the world so that counting, trading, ruling, and remembering all hung together by the same threads of kinship and custom.

## Four Confederations on the High Plains

The Muisca territory covered roughly 22,000 square kilometers of the Eastern Colombian Andes - today's departments of Boyacá, Cundinamarca, and parts of Santander. Four confederations shared this highland: Bogotá to the south, Tunja in the center, Duitama (Tundama) to the north, and Sogamoso (Iraca) to the east. These were not a single empire but independent regional unions, each built around a principal chiefdom whose authority smaller ones chose to recognize. Bogotá, ruled by the zipa, was the most powerful at the time of Spanish contact. Tunja, ruled by the zaque, held the dense cluster of towns across Boyacá - places like Ramiriquí, Villa de Leyva, and Sutamarchán. Between and around them, independent caciques at Guatavita, Ubaté, Chiquinquirá, and Ubaque held their own ground.

## Houses, Lineage, and the Shape of Power

Muisca chiefdoms were woven from matrilineal units the Spanish called capitanías - smaller uta groups of houses, larger sibyn groups that often functioned as villages. Power did not run down from a throne. It ran through alliances between rulers and households, which meant a chief's authority was a daily negotiation, renewed through gift, custom, and the concept of gue - the house. Succession followed rules; consent mattered. Religious specialists maintained what the Muisca considered the equilibrium of the world, since the cosmos was held in place by ritual rather than force. Gold, so ravenous in the Spanish imagination, was not money here. The Muisca had no currency. Gold had religious significance; what chiefs displayed for prestige was exotic cloth and elaborate palisaded enclosures, the cercados that marked the seat of a cacique.

## The Lake That Became El Dorado

At Lake Guatavita, in the mountains northeast of Bogotá, a new zipa was inaugurated in one of the most consequential rituals in the history of the Americas. The incoming ruler was covered in gold dust and rowed onto the lake, where he offered gold and emeralds to the waters. Spanish conquistadors heard this story, garbled it, and turned it into El Dorado - the golden man, the golden city, the golden kingdom that would lure expeditions into the Amazon for centuries. The lake is still there, a perfect dark circle in the mountains. It was one of many sacred sites: Lake Iguaque, where the goddess Bachué was said to have emerged from the water carrying a boy who would father the human race; the Tequendama Falls outside Soacha; El Infiernito near Villa de Leyva, where standing stones tracked the sun.

## A Working Landscape

The confederations were economies before they were politics. Somondoco supplied the emerald mines. Gachantivá held copper. Sutamarchán and Tinjacá specialized in ceramics. Turmequé and Villa de Leyva were important market towns; the salt of Zipaquirá moved through networks that reached far beyond Muisca territory. Roads connected the chiefdoms to each other and to neighboring peoples - the Panche and Muzo (the Emerald People) to the west, the Guane to the north who grew cotton for mantles, and the Guayupe, Achagua, and Tegua to the east. Warfare with the Panche was frequent. Trade with the Guane opened routes to the Magdalena River and ultimately to sea shells from the Caribbean coast. The Muisca script existed only for numbers, but the territory itself was a kind of text, read in trails and market days.

## The Conquest and After

Gonzalo Jiménez de Quesada and his brother Hernán reached the altiplano in 1537. Bogotá fell that year. Iraca (Sogamoso) fell in early September of the same year. Tundama held out longer - Baltasar Maldonado took Duitama in late December 1539. The confederations did not return, but the Muisca did not vanish. Their descendants live across Cundinamarca and Boyacá today, and their language, Muysccubun, is being reconstructed and taught by communities who never fully let it go. The words for the land - Bogotá itself derives from Bacatá - still carry the places forward. Every time someone counts past twenty in the old system, a house is named.

## Flight Context

The heartland of the Muisca confederations covers the high Andean plateau of Cundinamarca and Boyacá. Bogotá's El Dorado International Airport (SKBO) sits inside the old territory of the zipa. Lake Guatavita, still visible as a dark circular crater 50 km northeast of Bogotá, is best viewed above 3,000 m on a clear morning. For the Tunja heartland, aim for coordinates near 5.53°N, 73.36°W. Mountain weather turns quickly on the altiplano; morning light is usually the best.
