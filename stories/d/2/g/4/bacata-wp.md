# Bacatá

Bacatá — in Chibcha, Muyquytá — was the seat of the zipa who ruled the southern Muisca Confederation before the Spanish conquest. The name survives inside the word Bogotá.

**Location:** 4.7164, -74.2122

**Categories:** Populated places of the Muisca Confederation, History of Bogotá, Altiplano Cundiboyacense, Cundinamarca Department, Muisca archaeological sites

## Introduction

The word "Bogotá" is not Spanish. It is a transliteration — Bogothá in an early chronicle, Bacatá to the conquistadors, Muyquytá or Muequetá in the original Chibcha language of the people who built the city long before the Spanish arrived. In the Muisca language the root bac meant "outside," ca meant "enclosure," and tá meant "farmfield." The full sense is something like "the place at the edge of the farms," a name for the main settlement at the heart of the southern Muisca Confederation, where the zipa — the ruler of the Bogotá savanna — held his seat. Modern Bogotá is a successor city; it was founded by the Spanish in 1538 on different ground, at the western foothills of the Eastern Hills, but it inherited the older name because there was no sensible alternative.

## The People Who Named It

The Muisca had lived on the Altiplano Cundiboyacense for centuries when the Spanish came — one of the four great civilisations of the pre-Columbian Americas, alongside the Aztec, Maya, and Inca. Their confederation was not a strict empire in the Mexican or Peruvian mould. Modern anthropologists like Jorge Gamboa Mendoza have argued persuasively that it was a looser arrangement: a collection of small chiefdoms with related languages and common traditions, organised around four major cacicazgos. The zaque ruled from Hunza, today's Tunja. The iraca held Suamox, now Sogamoso. The Tundama leader governed the north. And the zipa held the southern reaches — the Bogotá savanna and its adjacent valleys — from his main seat at Bacatá. The villages dependent on the zipa stretched across the savanna: Zipaquirá, Nemocón, Guatavita, Guachetá, Chocontá, Ubaté, Suba, Fusagasugá, Pasca, Cáqueza. Each had its cacique. Each paid or traded or owed something to Bacatá.

## The Salt People

The Muisca economy rested on agriculture, goldwork, and — most distinctively — salt. Brines pooled at Zipaquirá and Nemocón, and Muisca women boiled them down in large ceramic pots over fires, producing high-quality salt that the people traded across vast distances. This practice earned them one of their names: The Salt People. Their other famous commodity was gold. The tunjos — small votive figurines — are the most common surviving Muisca gold artefacts, offerings made to deities at sacred lakes across the altiplano. The most important of those lakes was Guatavita, a nearly perfect circle of water at 3,000 metres northeast of present-day Bogotá. At the initiation of a new zipa, according to chroniclers, the ruler would cover himself in gold dust and dive from a raft into the lake's cold waters — an offering to the gods. Over centuries, enough gold settled into Guatavita's bottom sediment to seed the European legend of El Dorado, the City of Gold. The famous Muisca raft in Bogotá's Museo del Oro depicts that ceremony, and remains one of the most recognisable pre-Columbian artworks in the Americas.

## The Last Zipas

Because the Muisca had no written script, only the most recent zipas are known to history. Meicuchuca, whose story is partly mythological, reigned at Muyquytá roughly 1450–1470. His nephew Saguamanchica followed, subduing the Sutagao people to the south in the Battle of Pasca and later dying around 1490 in the Battle of Chocontá against the zaque Michuá. Nemequene succeeded him and, according to later biographers, ruled with strict severity — the Nemequene Code was a set of laws with harsh punishments for those who broke them. Nemequene died around 1514. He was followed by Tisquesusa. It was Tisquesusa who received the news, early in 1537, that strange men had crossed the mountains from the north. They had horses — unknown animals in the Andes — and large dogs, and armour. A cacique had earlier prophesied that the zipa would die "bathing in his own blood." Tisquesusa fled Muyquytá as the Spaniards approached. On April 20, 1537, Gonzalo Jiménez de Quesada and his surviving men found the place abandoned and formally founded the Spanish village of Funza there, ending the zipa's rule at Bacatá.

## The Prophecy, and What Came After

The prophecy held. Tisquesusa was stabbed by a Spanish soldier and fled into the western hills above the savanna, dying alone in the hills of Facatativá. His body was discovered later. His brother Sagipa — also called Zaquesazipa — took the rulership, breaking the Muisca succession custom, which would have passed the role to the eldest son of the previous zipa's sister. The Spanish exploited the anomaly, pressuring Sagipa into paying tributes and drawing him into the Battle of Tocarema on August 19–20, 1537, where 12,000 to 20,000 Muisca guecha warriors, allied with roughly 50 to 100 Spaniards, defeated the Panche people. After the victory, the Spanish demanded Muisca treasures — gold, emeralds, cotton mantles. Sagipa would not or could not produce them in sufficient quantity. Gonzalo Jiménez de Quesada left the colony in May 1539 in the hands of his younger brother, Hernán Pérez de Quesada, who — with other conquistadors — tortured Sagipa by burning his feet. The last zipa of Muyquytá did not survive the torture. He died in the Spanish camp at Bosa in 1539. The foundation of Santafé de Bogotá on August 6, 1538, formally ended Muyquytá's period as capital of the southern Muisca.

## A Name That Survived Everything

The bloodline of Muyquytá did not vanish entirely. Sagipa's daughter, recorded as Magdalena de Guatavita, married the conquistador Hernán Venegas Carrillo in one of the earliest mestizo unions in the New Kingdom of Granada; they had four children. Their son Alonso Venegas — grandson of the last independent zipa — killed the Spanish conquistador Gonzalo García Zorro in a duel in 1566, a small and pointed historical twist. The name Bacatá has had a long afterlife of its own. BD Bacatá, the tallest skyscraper in Colombia, carries it in the heart of modern Bogotá's business district. Etayoa bacatensis, an ancient ungulate fossil found in the Bogotá Formation, bears a scientific name drawn from the Muisca word. Pegoscapus bacataensis, a fig wasp endemic to the Bogotá savanna, carries it too. In every case, the word points back to the same flat green plateau — the enclosure at the edge of the farms — and to the people whose descendants still live on it.

## Flight Context

Bacatá's historical core is identified with Funza, on the Bogotá savanna at approximately 4.72°N, 74.21°W, about 2,550 metres elevation. Bogotá's El Dorado International Airport (SKBO) sits roughly 10 km to the east, on the same plain. From altitude, look for the broad flat savanna ringed by the Eastern Cordillera, the meandering Bogotá River crossing the plateau, and the dramatic Tequendama Falls where the river plunges southwest off the altiplano's edge. Lake Guatavita — the sacred lake central to the El Dorado legend — lies about 50 km northeast, a near-perfect circle at 3,000 metres elevation. Average temperatures around 14 °C; afternoon hailstorms common. The savanna is densely populated today; Bogotá's metropolitan area fills most of the southern half.
