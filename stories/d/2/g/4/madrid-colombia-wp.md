# Madrid

A town on the Bogotá savanna named twice, first for a small mountain range and then for a Cuban-born politician, shares a name with Spain's capital only by way of his surname.

**Location:** 4.7306, -74.2639

**Categories:** Cities, Colombia, Muisca heritage, Bogotá metropolitan area

## Introduction

The name was not chosen to honor Spain. Madrid, Colombia, sits 21 kilometers west of Bogotá at 2,554 meters on the high savanna, and it has been called by that name since 1875, when the local community petitioned the legislature of what was then the Sovereign State of Cundinamarca to rename the town in honor of Pedro Fernández Madrid, a Cuban-born politician who had spent his final years in what was then called Serrezuela. The link to Spain runs only through his surname. Spain's Madrid took its name from an Andalusi Romance word meaning mother stream, a reference to the little waterway that once ran through Segovia Street. Colombia's Madrid, by chance, sits beside the Subachoque River.

## Before It Was Madrid, Before It Was Serrezuela

Long before the Spanish arrived with an encomienda grant in 1559, the Herrera Culture worked this savanna. These were among the earliest sedentary societies on the Sabana de Bogotá, clustered near the Subachoque River and Lake La Herrera. The Herrera Culture eventually gave way to the Muisca, whose confederation made this region, then called Tibaitatá, a breadbasket. Local tradition holds that a cacique named Sugasuca governed these farmlands, though no documents confirm it; the zipa's seat was nearby Bacatá, today's Funza. The fertile plains that drew Muisca farmers drew the conquistadors for the same reason. The conquest redistributed the land. The farming continued, with different hands on the tools.

## Two Names, Two Returns

Alonso Díaz founded the colonial settlement as Serrezuela in 1559, a diminutive meaning little mountain range, referring to the Tibaitatá hill. The name held for more than three centuries. Then, after Pedro Fernández Madrid died in February 1875, the community petitioned to honor him. Law 14 of November 17, 1875 made the change official. But municipal identity is stubborn. Serrezuela was restored in 1945, replaced with Madrid again in 1946, and in 1973 the Departmental Assembly tried again to reinstate Serrezuela under a 1920 law requiring towns to adopt indigenous, ancient, or historical names. The Decree No. 14 of November 16, 1976 settled things, sort of: Madrid, once and for all. Historian Roberto Velandia noted that the name was adopted somewhat incidentally, pulled forward by the political and social standing of the man it honored.

## The Roll Call of the Famous

For a commuter town of 140,000, Madrid has hosted an unreasonable share of Colombian history. Simón Bolívar passed through. Antonio Nariño, the independence thinker whose translation of the Rights of Man helped spark the republican movement, spent time here. José Hilario López, the president who abolished slavery in Colombia in 1851, has ties to Madrid. So does Rafael Reyes, the modernizing early-twentieth-century president, and Alfonso López Pumarejo, whose reformist presidency in the 1930s and 40s reshaped the country's labor and land laws. The philologist Rufino José Cuervo and the literary critic José María Vergara y Vergara added intellectual weight. Charles Lindbergh himself landed here in 1928 on his Latin American goodwill tour, adding an unexpected aviator to the register of visiting worthies.

## The Air Base and the Savanna

Madrid holds a specific place in Colombian aviation. The Comando Aéreo de Mantenimiento here, founded in 1924, is described by the Air Force as its oldest unit, and the Escuela de Suboficiales trains the Colombian Air Force's non-commissioned officers. The pattern of the town - rail line, highway, airfield - reflects a century of being the logistical backstop for a capital city that ran out of flat ground inside its own boundaries. Cerro Tibaitatá rises to the southeast, between 2,550 and 2,750 meters, near the historic Hacienda Casablanca. To the north, the Valle del Abra natural reserve shelters bird species, insects, and plants that the rest of the savanna has lost to wheat and onion fields.

## Flying Over the Savanna

At 2,554 meters, the air is thin and the temperature stays mild. Average 14 degrees Celsius year round, fog and drizzle common. From altitude, the Bogotá savanna reads as a green-gray mosaic, Madrid's grid visible along the railway and the main highway to Puerto Salgar. The nearby Facatativá and Funza municipalities press close, the western ring of Bogotá's metropolitan area. Bogotá's El Dorado International Airport (SKBO) is the obvious gateway. Guaymaral (SKGY), on Bogotá's northern edge, serves small aircraft. Watch the afternoon clouds that build quickly off the Eastern Cordillera.

## Flight Context

Located at 4.73°N, 74.26°W at 2,554 m elevation on the western Sabana de Bogotá. Main airport is El Dorado International (SKBO) in Bogotá, about 13 km east. The savanna's distinctive flat expanse, rimmed by the Eastern Cordillera, is clearly visible from cruising altitude. Afternoon convective clouds over the mountains are common; morning offers best visibility of the rectangular street grid and surrounding agricultural patchwork. Temperatures average 14°C.
