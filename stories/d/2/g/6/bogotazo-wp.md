# Bogotazo

The assassination of Jorge Eliécer Gaitán on April 9, 1948 set off ten hours of riots that destroyed downtown Bogotá and pushed Colombia into decades of civil conflict.

**Location:** 4.5956, -74.0776

## Introduction

Just after one o'clock in the afternoon on April 9, 1948, Jorge Eliécer Gaitán stepped out of his office in central Bogotá for a lunch with friends. A lone gunman fired several times at him from the front. Gaitán fell to the sidewalk. He was the leader of the Liberal Party, the most popular politician in Colombia, a practicing lawyer who only that morning had returned from court in triumph. Within minutes of his death, radio stations controlled by his followers were calling the city to arms. What followed was ten hours of rioting known ever since as El Bogotazo, from Bogotá and the Spanish suffix -azo, which signals violent augmentation.

## The Man on the Sidewalk

Gaitán was an extraordinary orator. Two months earlier, on February 7, he had addressed a march of roughly 100,000 people with a speech known as the Prayer for Peace, asking President Mariano Ospina Pérez to halt the official persecution of Liberals and bring political struggle back inside constitutional limits. "We are not cowards," he told the silent crowd, "we are descendants of the brave who annihilated tyrannies on this sacred ground." A week later, in Manizales, he gave the Prayer for the Humble as a tribute to twenty Liberals massacred in the department of Caldas. His killing on April 9 was personal for many Colombians. He was the one they believed could stop the bleeding.

## A Mob, a Drugstore, a Stranger

The gunman ran south, pursued by an enraged crowd. A policeman, Carlos Alberto Jiménez Díaz, caught him and locked both of them inside the nearby Granada drugstore. Some witnesses later told El Tiempo and El Espectador that the man Jiménez seized was not the same one eventually pulled out of the drugstore, confusion made worse because both men wore gray hats. The prisoner, asked why he had killed Gaitán, is reported to have answered only: "Oh Lord, powerful things! Oh Virgin of Carmen, save me!" The doors were then forced, the crowd took him, and his mutilated body was left in Plaza de Bolívar outside the presidential palace. A bystander, Gabriel Restrepo, gathered the remains of his clothes and found documents identifying him as Juan Roa Sierra, aged 26, unemployed, from a poor family with a history of mental illness. He had repeatedly tried to get a job in Gaitán's office and been turned away. Investigators pointed out that the gun had been bought two days earlier, and that Roa Sierra had no known training with firearms. Theories about conspirators have never been corroborated.

## Downtown Burning

Radio Estación Últimas Noticias, run by Gaitán's followers, broadcast within minutes: "The Conservatives and the Ospina Pérez government have just assassinated doctor Gaitán, who fell in front of the door of his office, shot by a police officer. People, to arms! To the streets with clubs, stones, shotguns, whatever is at hand! Break into the hardware stores and take the dynamite, gunpowder, tools, machetes." Instructions for making Molotov cocktails followed. People rushed downtown from every neighborhood, many of them homeless Colombians who had come to Bogotá fleeing rural violence. At 1:20 p.m. President Ospina learned of the killing and called his cabinet. After the crowd dumped Roa Sierra's body outside the Casa de Nariño, it turned on the palace with stones and bricks. Cars, buses, and streetcars burned. Within hours violence spread to Medellín, Ibagué, and Barranquilla.

## The City the Next Morning

Darío Echandía, nominated by the Liberal Party to replace Gaitán, pleaded from a balcony for the rioting to stop. It did not. The army confronted the mobs at the Casa de Nariño and killed many. The offices of the Ministry of Government and the newspaper El Siglo were set on fire. Hardware stores were raided, especially in the San Victorino district. Some police joined the mob; others stood waiting for orders that never came. Around three o'clock, a crowd broke into police headquarters. Major Benicio Arce Vera came out unarmed to beg the people to leave, and ordered his men not to shoot. The mob trampled him and seized the weapons. One of the young men said to have taken arms that afternoon, according to an interview Arce later gave to Bohemia magazine in 1983, was a 21-year-old Cuban student visiting Bogotá for a youth congress: Fidel Castro. Liberal leaders were still at Clinica Central beside Gaitán's body, too afraid to cross the streets, when a phone call invited them to the palace. Only with a military escort did they finally reach it. President Ospina was astonished to see them; he had not authorized the meeting. They talked through the night without agreement.

## The Violence That Did Not End

Downtown Bogotá was gutted, its ministries sacked and its streets strewn with shattered glass, blood, and the bodies of people who had come downtown that morning with no idea the city would come apart by nightfall. The full death toll has never been settled, but it reached into the thousands in Bogotá alone. What the Bogotazo triggered was larger still. Scholars treat April 9, 1948 as the opening of La Violencia, a decade of partisan killing between Liberals and Conservatives that killed perhaps 200,000 Colombians, mostly peasants, and pushed the country into a cycle of armed conflict that shaped the rest of the twentieth century. Today the corner where Gaitán fell, at Carrera Séptima and Avenida Jiménez, carries a small plaque. Colombians passing it know what happened there. They also know it did not stop.

## Flight Context

Located at 4.60°N, 74.08°W, in the heart of downtown Bogotá at roughly 2,640 meters (8,660 feet) elevation on the Andean altiplano. El Dorado International Airport (SKBO) is about 13 km west. The city sits in a high mountain basin ringed by the Cerros Orientales to the east, with Monserrate and Guadalupe peaks rising directly above La Candelaria. Typical cruise-altitude overflights show the grid of the historic center and the green Eastern Hills on a clear morning; afternoons bring convective buildups over the cordillera.
