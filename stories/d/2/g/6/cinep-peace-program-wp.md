# CINEP / Peace Program

A Jesuit-founded research center in Bogotá that has spent six decades documenting human rights in Colombia's long armed conflict.

**Location:** 4.6201, -74.0651

**Categories:** Jesuit development centres, Organizations established in 1972, Charities based in Colombia, Research institutes in South America, Peace organizations, Human rights

## Introduction

On a quiet street in central Bogotá, a research library holds one of the most detailed records of conflict victims in Latin America. Every entry - the village, the date, the name when a name is known - was gathered by hand by researchers and community members who refused to let the numbers dissolve into statistics. The institution keeping that record is called CINEP/PPP, short for Centro de Investigación y Educación Popular / Programa por la Paz. For sixty years it has done three stubbornly linked things: listen to Colombians in conflict zones, document what has happened to them, and accompany them in the slow work of repair.

## A Jesuit Idea

The thread runs back further than the acronym suggests. In 1944, Colombia's Society of Jesus organized a National Coordination of Social Action, focused on training community leaders and advising what were then called popular organizations - labor unions, peasant cooperatives, parish councils. In 1966, a group of Jesuits and lay colleagues founded CIAS-CINEP to add research and teaching to that pastoral work. The Peace Programme was later integrated into the same institution, producing the cumbersome but descriptive name it still carries. The Jesuit character matters: the Society of Jesus has long argued that faith without justice is a hollow thing, and CINEP has lived out that argument in paperback reports and community workshops rather than sermons. Today it calls itself a research school, training young social scientists in what its staff describe as "research with, by and for people."

## Documenting the Undocumented

The archive is the quiet engine. CINEP's documentation covers human rights violations, breaches of international humanitarian law, environmental and territorial disputes, peace initiatives, social movements - anything that tells Colombians something true about what is happening in their country. Its periodic bulletin Noche y Niebla, launched in 1996, catalogs political violence by region, perpetrator, and pattern. National and international courts have relied on the center's data. So have the United Nations, the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights, and universities across the Americas. The library itself began when Jesuits pooled their personal collections on religion, church social doctrine, and economics. As CINEP's research broadened into politics and rural development, the collection grew with it. By the 1980s it required professional librarians; today it is considered one of the strongest specialized collections in Colombian social sciences.

## Accompaniment

CINEP does more than archive. It offers technical and strategic support to communities pressing legal claims for land, water, and human rights. In the framework of Colombia's transitional-justice process - set up after the 2016 Havana Peace Agreement between the government and the FARC-EP - the center has supported ethnic communities whose territories were occupied, mined, or depopulated during the war. Its pedagogical materials reach teachers and organizers with tools shaped by gender, ethnic, generational, and regional perspectives. The primers that emerge from this work have titles that read like small manifestos: Aja, y por qué no? Men committed to gender equity; Transformación noviolenta de conflictos sociales en Colombia. The center also builds and accompanies spaces for dialogue - multi-actor negotiations involving communities, companies, and government, particularly in regions where the peace accord's promises have been slow to materialize.

## Bogotá Address, National Reach

CIAS took the name CINEP in 1976, and the institution works from longtime headquarters at the corner of 33rd and 5th. The building still anchors the program's identity, but its work has always extended far beyond the capital. Researchers travel to Chocó on the Pacific, to the Magdalena Medio river region where paramilitaries and guerrillas fought for control of oil and coca corridors, to Wiwa and Arhuaco territories in the Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta. One CINEP document - Does Cerrejón Always Win? - examines corporate impunity at one of the world's largest open-pit coal mines. Other titles ask why negotiating with the ELN has proved so stubbornly difficult, and how Colombia might reform its agrarian laws in the 21st century. These are not neutral questions. CINEP has never pretended they are.

## Flying Over

CINEP's offices sit in the heart of Bogotá, Colombia's sprawling capital tucked against the Eastern Hills at 2,640 meters. From the air, the city looks like a dense urban grid pressed up against a wall of green mountains, with the Monserrate sanctuary rising above. The neighborhood around 33rd and 5th is mixed residential and institutional - universities, clinics, embassies - a fitting setting for an organization that speaks between many worlds.
