# Casa de Nariño

Colombia's presidential residence stands on the exact plot where Antonio Nariño was born in 1765, beside an observatory that planned independence and a guard battalion that defended the building through the Bogotazo.

**Location:** 4.5956, -74.0775

## Introduction

On April 9, 1765, a child was born in a grand house halfway along Calle de la Carrera in Santa Fe de Bogotá. His name was Antonio Nariño, and he would grow up to translate the Rights of Man into Spanish, serve as a revolutionary general, and become one of the national heroes of Colombian independence. Nearly a century and a half after his birth, on the same plot of ground, the country built the house where its presidents would live. The Casa de Nariño dedicated in 1908 is not just named for him; it stands on the very plot where he took his first breath.

## Before the Palace

The grand house on Carrera Séptima was purchased by Don Vicente Nariño in 1754 for 5,200 patacones, a small fortune in the currency of the viceroyalty. Vicente and his wife Catalina Álvarez lived there with their children for about thirty years, and it was in that house, as the third of eight children, that Antonio was born. When Vicente died in 1778, the family inherited the property. Over the nineteenth century it passed through several owners. Early Colombian presidents, including Simón Bolívar, governed from a different building, the Palacio de los Virreyes on the western side of Plaza de Bolívar. On November 16, 1827, one of the strongest earthquakes in Bogotá's recorded history damaged that older palace, and Bolívar authorized the purchase of the San Carlos Palace instead. Then in October 1885, President Rafael Núñez bought the Nariño house to make it a presidential residence, citing its beauty, its historical weight, and its nearness to the National Capitol.

## A Frenchman, a Colombian, and a Swiss Sculptor

The building today is largely the work of two architects commissioned in 1906 by General Rafael Reyes: the French-born Gastón Lelarge, a former pupil of Charles Garnier, architect of the Paris Opera; and the Colombian Julián Lombana. They demolished parts of the old house, extended the property to Carrera Octava, cut the interior into two full levels, added wide halls, and faced the exterior with carved stone. The Swiss sculptor Luigi Ramelli carried out the ornamentation. On July 20, 1908, the finished building was officially returned to government use as the Casa de Nariño, also called the Palacio de la Carrera. During the presidency of Eduardo Santos in the late 1930s, a third level and a roof terrace were added. In 1954 General Gustavo Rojas Pinilla moved the presidential office out to the chancellery, and the building waited. When President Julio César Turbay brought the presidency back in 1980, a new round of restoration, directed by architect Fernando Alsina at a cost of 250 million pesos, gave the palace the form it still has today.

## The Oldest Observatory in the Americas

Tucked in the palace gardens stands a small octagonal tower that predates the presidential residence by more than a century. The Observatorio Astronómico de Bogotá was designed and built between March 24, 1802 and August 20, 1803 by the Capuchin friar-architect Domingo de Petrés, with José Celestino Mutis, the great botanist of the Royal Botanical Expedition to New Granada, as its first director. It is often called the oldest observatory in the Americas. It is also where the leaders of the July 20, 1810 conspiracy met to plan what would become Colombia's independence movement. On the Plaza de Armas in front of the observatory now, the 37th Infantry Presidential Guard Battalion performs the daily changing of the guard. Antonio Nariño's statue, carved in 1910 by the French sculptor Henri-Léon Gréber, watches from the front of the square.

## Rooms and What They Remember

Inside, the Casa de Nariño reads as a compressed museum of Colombian history. The Baules Room holds the travel trunks Simón Bolívar used to carry his war documents. The Salón Luis XV has furniture in that style and a Murano glass chandelier with bronze inlays. The Patio de los Novios, where carriages once entered, centers on a fountain and Édgar Negret's sculpture Anudamiento; behind it, a gallery holds portraits of the last twenty-five presidents, with only Bolívar's portrait never moved. Upstairs in the Yellow Room, foreign ambassadors present their credentials on an original bamboo floor decorated by Ramelli. The Council of Ministers Room once held Alejandro Obregón's painting El Cóndor, stolen on March 9, 2007 by a sergeant of the presidential guard and recovered hours later from a house in the San Victorino neighborhood. The Salón Virreinal holds a small Meissen vase with a beetle at its base and a private chapel used by Pope John Paul II during his 1986 visit to Colombia. Manuelita Sáenz's German piano sits upstairs, too, alongside marble busts of Bolívar and Santander by the Italian sculptor Pietro Tenerani.

## The Guard at the Door

The 37th Infantry Presidential Guard Battalion was formally created by presidential decree on September 7, 1927, under President Miguel Abadía Méndez, and raised by Lieutenant Colonel Roberto Perea Sanclemente on August 16, 1928. Today about 1,400 soldiers and officers make up its ranks, organized into companies named for Colombian heroes: Córdova, Rondón, Ricaurte, Caldas, and Wafersson. Their slogan, En Defensa del Honor Hasta la Muerte, remembers two of the most harrowing episodes in the palace's history. On April 9, 1948 the mob of the Bogotazo, grieving the assassination of Jorge Eliécer Gaitán, assaulted the building with stones and bricks; the battalion held them off, and many on both sides died. On November 6, 1985 the Palace of Justice Siege unfolded only blocks away, one of the darkest days in modern Colombian history. The Casa de Nariño has stood through earthquakes, uprisings, and a 2002 rocket attack during a presidential inauguration. Its doors, its observatory, its statue of Nariño, and its daily changing of the guard continue a civic ritual that ties a country back to a baby born in this spot more than two and a half centuries ago.

## Flight Context

Located at 4.60°N, 74.08°W in the historic La Candelaria district of central Bogotá, at approximately 2,640 meters (8,660 feet) elevation. El Dorado International Airport (SKBO) lies about 14 km northwest. The palace sits directly below the dark forested ridge of the Cerros Orientales, with Monserrate rising sharply to the east. Airspace over the Casa de Nariño is tightly restricted. Overflights of the capital are best viewed at cruise altitudes on clear mornings before afternoon thunderstorms build over the cordillera.
