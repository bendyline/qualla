# El Dorado International Airport

At 8,360 feet in the Andes, Colombia's main airport handles nearly half the country's air traffic and carries a name that began as a conquistador's rumor.

**Location:** 4.7014, -74.1469

**Categories:** airport, colombia, bogota, aviation, infrastructure

## Introduction

The runway sits at 8,360 feet above sea level, which means the air over Bogota is thin enough that jets need every bit of its 3,800 meters to get off the ground. El Dorado International Airport was named for the conquistador dream that sent Europeans hacking into the Andes looking for a city of gold - a city that never existed, but whose rumor shaped the continent. The airport itself is very real, very busy, and very important: it handles 49 percent of Colombia's air traffic, moves more cargo than any other hub in Ibero-America, and ranks as the 20th most-connected airport in the world. The jets that depart here climb toward passes in the Cordillera on all sides. The gold is elsewhere.

## Rojas Pinilla's Airport

El Dorado was a dictator's project. General Gustavo Rojas Pinilla, who ran Colombia from 1953 to 1957, wanted an airport that matched his vision of a modernized country, and construction began in 1955. The old Techo Airport, which had served Bogota since 1930, was aging badly. By December 1959 the new facility was open, and the capital had finally caught up to what Barranquilla's Soledad International had long provided: a proper airline gateway. Techo was demolished in the years that followed. What remains of that earlier aviation era is CATAM, the military air base adjacent to the civilian runway - inaugurated on May 28, 1963, still operating as the Colombian Air Force's main transport hub, and home to the Air Force Museum.

## The Hub of the Americas

Geography does most of the work at El Dorado. Sitting almost exactly on the equator, within one flight band of both North American and South American population centers, it functions as what the industry calls a natural hub. Avianca - Colombia's flag carrier and the second-oldest airline in the world - opened its Bogota hub here in December 1998 and now flies 52 destinations out of the airport. LATAM, Copa, and Satena base operations here too. The Swiss company that runs Zurich Airport, Flughafen Zurich AG, operates El Dorado through a consortium called OPAIN. Skytrax ranks it among the best airports in South America year after year. The passengers shuffling through Terminal 1, shaped from above like a lowercase h, feed one of the largest air-cargo operations in Latin America: 760,000 metric tons in a single year pass through these warehouses, mostly flowers bound for Miami and beyond.

## Puente Aereo

In 1981, President Julio Cesar Turbay Ayala inaugurated something called Puente Aereo - Air Bridge - a dedicated Avianca terminal for high-frequency domestic routes. For decades, it was the architectural embodiment of the old way of flying in Colombia: walk across the tarmac, climb a set of portable stairs, board. In 2018, Avianca migrated its domestic flights to the new Terminal 1, and Puente Aereo - now Terminal 2 - became home to Satena and EasyFly. The patched-together feel of its walkways and aluminum-and-plastic gate expansions, built as temporary fixes during the larger 2007-2017 airport overhaul, remains. It is the working-class terminal: a reminder that this whole place was once much humbler.

## The Day Avianca Flight 203 Exploded

On November 27, 1989, a Boeing 727 left El Dorado headed for Cali's Alfonso Bonilla Aragon Airport. It never arrived. A bomb detonated in mid-air over Soacha, south of Bogota. All 107 people on board were killed, and so were three people on the ground. The bomber was Pablo Escobar. The target was supposed to be presidential candidate Cesar Gaviria Trujillo, who was not on the plane. Gaviria was elected president the next year and spent much of his term hunting the Medellin cartel that had killed the 110 people on his behalf. Avianca 203 is one chapter in a dark ledger of losses associated with this airport - Avianca 052, a Boeing 707 that ran out of fuel over Long Island in 1990; Air France 422 in 1998, which struck a mountain east of Bogota two minutes after takeoff - but it is the chapter that changed Colombian history.

## El Dorado Max

El Dorado is running out of room. It was designed to serve 16 million passengers annually. It now moves closer to 40 million. In 2023 the government announced plans to double capacity to 60 million, with a contract awarded by 2025. The latest proposal, unveiled in April 2025, carries the name El Dorado Maximo Desarrollo - El Dorado Max - with a second airport, El Dorado II, eventually connecting to the main facility by commuter rail. The name still points back to a conquistador's rumor, but the airport underneath it is an economic fact: Colombia's hinge to the world, perched high in the Andes, always bigger than it was the last time you passed through.

## Flight Context

El Dorado International Airport (ICAO: SKBO, IATA: BOG) sits at 4.70 degrees N, 74.15 degrees W, elevation 8,360 feet (2,548 m), in Bogota's Fontibon district. Two parallel 3,800-meter runways (13L/31R and 13R/31L) support high-altitude jet operations. The airport handles approximately 49 percent of Colombia's air traffic. High-elevation approaches require careful speed and fuel planning; thunderstorms over the Cordillera are a frequent factor. CATAM, the Colombian Air Force transport base, lies immediately adjacent. Nearest diversion options include Alfonso Bonilla Aragon (SKCL) in Cali and Jose Maria Cordova (SKRG) near Medellin.
