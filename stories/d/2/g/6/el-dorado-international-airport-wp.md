# El Dorado International Airport

At 8,360 feet in the Andes, Colombia's main airport handles nearly half the country's air traffic and carries a name that began as a conquistador's rumor.

**Location:** 4.7014, -74.1469

**Categories:** airport, colombia, bogota, aviation, infrastructure

## Introduction

The runway sits at 8,360 feet above sea level, which means the air over Bogota is thin enough that jets need long takeoff runs; both of its runways stretch 3,800 meters. El Dorado International Airport was named for the conquistador dream that sent Europeans hacking into the Andes looking for a city of gold - a city that never existed, but whose rumor shaped the continent. The airport itself is very real, very busy, and very important: it handles 49 percent of Colombia's air traffic, moves more cargo than any other hub in Ibero-America, and ranks among the most-connected airports in the world. The jets that depart here climb toward passes in the Cordillera on all sides. The gold is elsewhere.

## Rojas Pinilla's Airport

El Dorado was a dictator's project. General Gustavo Rojas Pinilla, who ran Colombia from 1953 to 1957, wanted an airport that matched his vision of a modernized country, and construction began in 1955. The old Techo Airport, which had served Bogota since 1930, was aging badly. By December 1959 the new facility was open, and the capital had finally caught up to what Barranquilla's Soledad International had long provided: a proper airline gateway. Techo was demolished in the years that followed. Next door is CATAM, the military air base adjacent to the civilian runway - inaugurated on May 28, 1963, and still operating as the Colombian Air Force's main transport hub.

## The Hub of the Americas

Geography does most of the work at El Dorado. Sitting just north of the equator, within one flight band of both North American and South American population centers, it functions as what the industry calls a natural hub. Avianca - Colombia's flag carrier, often called the second-oldest airline in the world - opened its Bogota hub here in December 1998 and by 2023 flew to 52 destinations from the airport. LATAM, Copa, and Satena base operations here too. El Dorado is operated by OPAIN, a consortium whose members have included Flughafen Zurich AG, the Swiss company that runs Zurich Airport. Skytrax ranks it among the best airports in South America year after year. The passengers shuffling through Terminal 1, shaped from above like a lowercase h, feed one of the largest air-cargo operations in Latin America: 760,000 metric tons passed through these warehouses in 2022, and more than 800,000 in 2024, mostly flowers bound for Miami and beyond.

## Puente Aereo

In 1981, President Julio Cesar Turbay Ayala inaugurated something called Puente Aereo - Air Bridge - a dedicated Avianca terminal for high-frequency domestic routes. For decades, it was the architectural embodiment of the old way of flying in Colombia: walk across the tarmac, climb a set of portable stairs, board. In 2018, Avianca migrated its domestic flights to the new Terminal 1, and Puente Aereo - now Terminal 2 - became home to Satena and EasyFly. The patched-together feel of its walkways and aluminum-and-plastic gate expansions, built as temporary fixes during the larger 2007-2017 airport overhaul, remains. It is the working-class terminal: a reminder that this whole place was once much humbler.

## The Day Avianca Flight 203 Exploded

On November 27, 1989, a Boeing 727 left El Dorado headed for Cali's Alfonso Bonilla Aragon Airport. It never arrived. A bomb detonated in mid-air over Soacha, south of Bogota. All 107 people on board were killed, and so were three people on the ground. The bombing was ordered by Pablo Escobar's Medellin Cartel. The target was supposed to be presidential candidate Cesar Gaviria Trujillo, who was not on the plane. Gaviria was elected president the next year and spent much of his term hunting the Medellin cartel that had killed 110 people trying to kill him. Avianca 203 is one chapter in a dark ledger of losses associated with this airport - Avianca 052, a Boeing 707 that ran out of fuel over Long Island in 1990; Air France 422 in 1998, which struck a mountain east of Bogota two minutes after takeoff - but it is the chapter that changed Colombian history.

## El Dorado Max

El Dorado is running out of room. A 2015 plan aimed to raise its capacity to 40 million passengers a year; in 2024 it handled nearly 46 million. In 2023 the government announced plans to double capacity to 60 million, with a contract expected by 2025. The latest proposal, unveiled in April 2025, carries the name El Dorado Maximo Desarrollo - El Dorado Max - and would lift capacity to about 73 million; by late 2026 its contract had still not been awarded. The name still points back to a conquistador's rumor, but the airport underneath it is an economic fact: Colombia's hinge to the world, perched high in the Andes, always bigger than it was the last time you passed through.

## Flight Context

El Dorado International Airport (ICAO: SKBO, IATA: BOG) sits at 4.70 degrees N, 74.15 degrees W, elevation 8,360 feet (2,548 m), in Bogota's Fontibon district. Two parallel 3,800-meter runways (14L/32R and 14R/32L) support high-altitude jet operations. The airport handles approximately 49 percent of Colombia's air traffic. High-elevation approaches require careful speed and fuel planning; thunderstorms over the Cordillera are a frequent factor. CATAM, the Colombian Air Force transport base, lies immediately adjacent. Nearest diversion options include Alfonso Bonilla Aragon (SKCL) in Cali and Jose Maria Cordova (SKRG) near Medellin.
