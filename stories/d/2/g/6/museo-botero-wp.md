# Museo Botero

A Colombian painter's private collection, hauled out of Swiss vaults and Italian studios, became one of Latin America's finest free museums in a colonial mansion in La Candelaria.

**Location:** 4.5967, -74.0734

**Categories:** Museums in Bogotá, Art museums and galleries in Colombia, Fernando Botero, La Candelaria

## Introduction

Fernando Botero wanted to give his collection to Medellín, the city he had grown up in. Medellín's officials took too long. So in 1999 he picked up the phone, called Enrique Peñalosa - then the mayor of Bogotá - and said yes to Bogotá instead. A year later, a colonial mansion in La Candelaria opened its doors, free to anyone who walked in, and Bogotá found itself in possession of 123 Boteros and 85 works the artist had quietly collected over thirty years: a Picasso, a Monet, a Miró, a Dalí, a Renoir, a Toulouse-Lautrec, a Degas, a Freud. Not a bad second choice, for a city.

## The Collection Before the Collection

Botero began collecting pre-Columbian pieces in the late 1960s, when he was already famous for the plump, volumetric figures that would become his signature. He moved on to colonial art, then to modern drawing, painting, and sculpture. For decades the collection was scattered - his apartment in New York, his studios in Paris and Monte Carlo, his workshop in Pietrasanta, and a Swiss bank deposit stored, of all places, in Bogotá. A Picasso in a New York closet. A Monet in Tuscany. Two Renoirs somewhere in Monaco. The Mona Lisa he had reimagined in Boterian dimensions was in there too, eventually designated by Colombia's Ministry of Culture as one of his most important paintings.

## A Colonial Mansion, Rewired

The Bank of the Republic acquired a seventeenth-century mansion in La Candelaria, the historic core of Bogotá, and Botero himself supervised its adaptation into a museum. He wanted galleries that breathed, not the tight white cubes of New York. The finished rooms open onto a central courtyard where, during his funeral in September 2023, his coffin lay in repose - the artist returned to the building he had shaped, in the city he chose. Since November 1, 2000, the museum has been free of charge. Over 500,000 people visit each year; about 2,000 students come each month. For Colombians who might never afford a Paris museum ticket, a Monet, a Degas, and a Picasso wait twelve blocks from the Plaza de Bolívar.

## Botero on Violence

The collection includes the first series Botero painted on Colombian violence - heavy, unavoidable canvases with titles like Pablo Escobar, Tirofijo, Carrobomba, and La masacre de Mejor Esquina. He painted them because no one else was going to render the cartel wars and the guerrilla kidnappings in oil on the scale they deserved. The second series, painted later, hangs in the National Museum of Colombia a short walk away. Put together, the two series form a kind of civic ledger: a famous artist, world-collected, spending his last decades painting what his country was doing to itself, and giving those paintings to his country for free.

## The Guest List

The private collection Botero donated reads like a guided tour of modernism. Two paintings and two drawings by Picasso, plus a lithograph. A Monet (The Geldersekade in Amsterdam, winter 1871-74). A Degas drawing and sculpture. Two Renoirs. Matisse, Chagall, Braque, Miró, Dalí, Léger, Calder, Giacometti, Bacon, Freud, Klimt. A Rauschenberg, a Warhol-adjacent pop corner. Sculptures from Rodin (via Medellín), Moore, Ernst. The room arrangements pair these works with Botero's own inflated, sensuous forms so that the international modernists and the Colombian master look across the galleries at each other. It is, by design, an argument: that Latin American art belongs in this conversation, and has always belonged.

## The Candelaria Corner

The Museo Botero sits inside a dense cultural complex run by the Bank of the Republic: the Gold Museum with its pre-Columbian metalwork a few blocks away, the Luis Ángel Arango Library (the busiest in Latin America by many measures) next door, the Miguel Urrutia Art Museum across the courtyard, and the Museo Casa de Moneda - the old royal mint - connected through a series of linked patios. A single free afternoon can carry a visitor from Quimbaya goldwork to Monet to Colombian banknote history without leaving the block. La Candelaria itself is the old city: uneven cobbles, colonial balconies, the white walls of the Plaza de Bolívar at the hill's foot, and the green flank of Monserrate rising behind.

## Flight Context

The Museo Botero is in La Candelaria, Bogotá, at approximately 4.5967°N, 74.0734°W. Bogotá sits at 2,640 m (8,660 ft) on the altiplano; El Dorado International Airport (SKBO) is 13 km west of the museum. Expect morning mist clearing by mid-morning, with the Cerro de Monserrate (3,152 m) forming a distinctive green ridge directly east of the historic center.
