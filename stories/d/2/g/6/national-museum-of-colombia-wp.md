# National Museum of Colombia

The oldest museum in Colombia lives inside a prison. It opened in 1823 - and took over a panopticon built to surveil its inmates a century later.

**Location:** 4.6155, -74.0683

**Categories:** Museums in Bogotá, National museums, History museums, Colombian history, Archaeology museums

## Introduction

It looks like a fortress because it was built to be one. The Panóptico of Bogotá was designed in 1850 by Danish architect Thomas Reed to be Colombia's largest and most important prison - a radial stone structure laid out on Jeremy Bentham's principle that a single guard could watch every inmate at once. It held prisoners for seventy years. When the last of them were moved out to La Picota in 1946, the building did not become a hotel or a government office. It became what Colombia's oldest museum had needed for 123 years: a home. The National Museum opened inside the former prison in May 1948, and it has been sorting the country's history behind those thick walls ever since.

## Older Than the Country

Established by an act of Congress on 28 July 1823, the National Museum of Colombia is older than most of the countries it shares a continent with. Only three years had passed since the Battle of Carabobo sealed independence. The nation was still figuring out what to keep, what to throw away, and what to call itself. The first donations were idiosyncratic: large bones of unknown animals, a mummy found near Tunja, a silver medal commemorating the battles of Junín and Ayacucho - deposited in the museum by a congressional decree in February 1825. Over 20,000 pieces now fill its four collections: archaeology, art, ethnography, and social history. It is not subdivided into satellite institutions the way the Smithsonian is. Everything lives in one building.

## The Day the Opening Was Canceled

The inauguration of the museum inside the former prison was scheduled for April 9, 1948. That morning, the liberal leader Jorge Eliécer Gaitán was assassinated on a Bogotá sidewalk, and the city erupted into what Colombians still call the Bogotazo - days of riots, fires, and lootings that reshaped twentieth-century Colombian politics. The museum opening was postponed. Twenty-three days later, on May 2, the doors finally opened: archaeology and ethnography on the first floor, history on the second, fine arts on the third. The building that had once held prisoners now held, on its third floor, paintings by José María Espinosa and Andrés de Santa María; on its first floor, 10,000 years of pre-Columbian craftsmanship in gold, ceramic, and stone.

## Ten Thousand Years of Hands

The archaeological collection ranges from stone tools dated between 10,500 and 9,000 BC to the complex societies the Spanish encountered in the sixteenth century. Objects from the Muisca, Quimbaya, San Agustín, Calima, and Tairona cultures are assembled here - a gold pectoral with a human figure, a ceramic offering vessel that once held emeralds and tunjos, an exquisite Tumaco-La Tolita figure. The collection began as eighteenth-century curiosities, became nineteenth-century antiquities, and - through the work of scholars like Paul Rivet and Gregorio Hernández de Alba in the 1940s - was reclassified as the cultural record of the people who made them. It is the difference between a relic and a testimony. The pieces are the same. The framing changed everything.

## A Country's Paintings and a Country's Ledger

The painting collection holds about 900 works, centered on Colombian artists of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries - Epifanio Garay, Ricardo Acevedo Bernal, Andrés de Santa María, Ignacio Gómez Jaramillo - with later donations from Guillermo Wiedemann, Fernando Botero, and Enrique Grau. It also holds a rare Flemish panel painting, Escena campesina (1575) by Marten van Cleve, depicting a European rural scene that somehow ended up in Bogotá. The numismatic collection tells a parallel story through coins: macuquina and cordoncillo from the colonial mints in Potosí, Lima, and Popayán; the instability of nineteenth-century Republican issues; the bonds and vouchers of the Thousand Days' War (1899-1902), when Colombia's economy fractured into multiple competing paper currencies. Coins as evidence of what a country trusted, or stopped trusting.

## The Weight of the Testimonial Objects

The testimonial objects room is where history becomes touchable. The silver medal for Junín and Ayacucho. The Royal Standard of Castile with which Francisco Pizarro sailed into Peru in 1531, captured during the Peruvian War of Independence in 1824 and sent north as a trophy by Antonio José de Sucre. A civic gold garland that the people of Cusco offered Simón Bolívar in 1825. Letters - eight signed by Bolívar himself, thirty-seven addressed to him - acquired in the 1950s and still legible. Fifteen documents tied to José Celestino Mutis, botanist of the 1783 Royal Botanical Expedition. The collection also holds a cannon used in the Thousand Days' War, a piece whose smoke, in some sense, never cleared - Colombia's twentieth century opened with that war and spent a hundred years trying to close it.

## Flight Context

The National Museum stands in the Santa Fe locality of central Bogotá at 4.6155°N, 74.0683°W - 1.5 km north of the Plaza de Bolívar. El Dorado International Airport (SKBO) is 14 km to the west. At 2,640 m (8,660 ft), Bogotá is one of the highest capital cities in the world; mountain weather typically produces afternoon clouds rolling down from Monserrate (3,152 m), directly east of the museum.
