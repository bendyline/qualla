# National Library of Colombia

Founded in 1777 from books confiscated from expelled Jesuits, Colombia's national library is generally considered the oldest in the Americas.

**Location:** 4.6096, -74.0687

**Categories:** Libraries in Bogotá, National libraries, Colombian history, 1777 establishments, Ministry of Culture

## Introduction

In 1767, King Charles III of Spain signed an order that expelled every Jesuit from every corner of the Spanish Empire. Across the Americas, schools emptied, missions closed, and entire libraries changed hands overnight. Viceroy Manuel de Guirior ordered the books the Jesuits had left behind in Santa Fe de Bogotá turned into a public library, and in January 1777, ten years after the expulsion and after Guirior had moved on to Peru, it opened as what is generally considered the oldest national library in the Americas. The collection started as seized property. It has since grown, through two centuries of law and gift, into more than two million volumes.

## From Confiscation to Public Trust

The expulsion of the Jesuits stripped Spanish America of some of its best-stocked libraries. In Bogotá, those books became the seed of a public institution. The library was formally reorganized in 1825, during the early years of independence, when Francisco de Paula Santander - Bolívar's vice president, one of the founders of Gran Colombia - moved it to the campus of the Colegio de San Bartolomé and gave it the name it carries today. Nine years later, on March 25, 1834, Colombia passed its first legal deposit law: every book printed in the country had to send a copy to the library. That single decree turned a collection of confiscated Jesuit texts into the active memory of a nation.

## The Law of the Copy

Legal deposit is a quiet kind of magic. A printer in Medellín runs a novel; two copies go to Bogotá. A publisher in Cali presses a record; a copy arrives at the library. Film, video, sheet music, pamphlets, academic journals, protest broadsides - all of it flows, by law, into the stacks. The modern rules are set out in Law 44 of 1993 and its implementing decrees, but the principle has held since 1834. About a quarter of the library's two million volumes come from legal deposit; the rest come from donations. The library also holds 47 incunabula - books printed before 1501, when the press was still new - and 610 manuscript volumes, including rare and curious books gathered over generations.

## The People Behind the Shelves

The most valuable parts of the collection trace back to individual lives. Miguel Antonio Caro, the grammarian and former president. Rufino José Cuervo, whose Diccionario de construcción y régimen of the Spanish language is still cited. Jorge Isaacs, author of María, the nineteenth-century novel that shaped Colombian literary taste. Manuel del Socorro Rodríguez, founder of the country's first newspaper. Eduardo Santos, a twentieth-century president. Germán Arciniegas, essayist and diplomat. Each of these libraries-within-the-library arrived as a donation. The collection also holds watercolors from the Chorography Commission of the 1850s - Colombia's first systematic attempt to see itself - and the Aviso del Terremoto, considered the first periodical published in what became the Republic of Colombia.

## The Building on Independence Park

The current building sits in the southeast corner of Parque de la Independencia, inaugurated in 1938 and remodeled in 1978 by architect Jacques Mosseri - though the façade and decorative elements of the original were preserved. Four stories rise around reading rooms and exhibition halls. The first floor holds the general reading rooms; the second houses periodicals and researchers; the upper floors are given to storage and administration. It is, among its other functions, the training center for Colombian librarians and a lender to other countries' libraries. The brief is enormous: recover, preserve, and give access to the collective memory of Colombia, in any physical form, for the individual and collective development of Colombians.

## What the Library Is For

National libraries are easy to imagine as mausoleums - vast, refrigerated, slowly forgetting. The Biblioteca Nacional is not that. It directs Colombia's public library network, shapes national reading policy, and works with the Ministry of Culture on the bibliographic heritage of the country. When a new generation of writers starts publishing in Cartagena or Leticia, the legal deposit copies eventually arrive. The library is the shelf on which a country keeps a record of itself, and Colombia has been keeping that record - with some interruptions, some losses, some arguments about which books belong - for 249 years.

## Flight Context

The National Library sits at the southeast corner of Parque de la Independencia in central Bogotá, at 4.6096°N, 74.0687°W. Bogotá's altiplano elevation is 2,640 m (8,660 ft). El Dorado International Airport (SKBO) is about 13 km to the northwest. On a clear day the Cerros Orientales form a dramatic green wall directly east of the library; Monserrate and Guadalupe are the two peaks most visible.
