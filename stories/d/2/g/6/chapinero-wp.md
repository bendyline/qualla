# Chapinero

Named for shoemakers who specialized in footwear for pigeon-toed Bogotanos, Chapinero grew from a country road into Bogotá's financial district, its university quarter, and Latin America's most visible LGBTQ neighborhood.

**Location:** 4.6458, -74.0633

**Categories:** Localities of Bogotá

## Introduction

The name came from a shoe. In Spanish, a person with pigeon toe is called a chapín, and the shoemakers who settled the area in the early nineteenth century made corrective shoes and boots for them. The barrio took the trade as its title: Chapinero, the place of the bespoke shoemakers. From that small craftsmen's quarter on the road between Bogotá and the villages to the north came, over two centuries, the financial capital of Colombia, its densest cluster of universities, and the most openly gay district in Latin America.

## From Tram Lines to Towers

Chapinero was settled in 1812 by workers in the industries that supplied Bogotá with its daily needs. By the end of the nineteenth century the area was filling with mansions and country estates belonging to families who wanted cleaner air than the crowded colonial center offered. A mule-pulled tramline extended north from Bogotá in 1875; electric tracks followed in 1910, binding Chapinero firmly to the growing city. When the Special District of Bogotá was formed in 1955, Chapinero was recognized as a locality and absorbed the El Lago, Chicó, and Cataluña neighborhoods. The physical boundaries have stayed the same since the 1991 reorganization into the Capital District: Calle 39 in the south, Avenida Caracas in the west, Calle 100 in the north, and the Cerros Orientales, the eastern hills, rising directly above. Today the locality covers 3,899 hectares, about 20 percent residential, 35 percent urban, and 21 percent protected rural land on the hills above.

## The Money and the Universities

Between Calle 72 and Calle 100, Chapinero holds the central business district of Bogotá. Banking and financial headquarters stretch along Calle 72, Carrera 7, and Calle 100. Shopping runs along Carreras 11 and 15, including modern malls like Centro Andino, Atlantis Plaza, El Retiro, and Avenida Chile. Four major universities have campuses in the locality: Pontificia Universidad Javeriana, Catholic University of Colombia, Universidad Piloto de Colombia, and the National Pedagogic University. The result is a density of young professionals, lawyers, students, and executives that makes Chapinero feel busier than the rest of the city, and gives it the population to support Bogotá's most famous restaurants and bars. Zona Rosa, Zona G (the G stands for Gourmet), and the tree-lined Parque de la 93 anchor the high-end dining scene. Sidewalk cafés on El Chicó fill up after six. Along southern Chapinero, large Victorian houses survive from the early twentieth century, traces of the European architectural fashions that shaped Bogotá's elite when the city was still small.

## A Neighborhood of One's Own

Chapinero is the center of Bogotá's LGBTQ community, with more than one hundred bars, clubs, and discos catering to gay, lesbian, bisexual, and trans Colombians. Theatron, located in the heart of the locality and occupying a former cinema, is often described as the largest gay club in Latin America. Its thirteen themed rooms and indoor-outdoor spaces hold thousands of people on weekend nights. Chapinero also hosts a community center focused on LGBTQ residents, the first of its kind in Latin America, and a former locality mayor, Blanca Inés Durán Hernández, is openly lesbian. The openness has not been without tension in a country where cultural attitudes can be conservative, but Chapinero's rainbow flags, visible on ordinary Thursday afternoons along Carrera 13, announce a civic identity that other neighborhoods have come to accept as part of what this part of Bogotá is.

## The Night of El Nogal

On February 7, 2003, a car bomb detonated at the El Nogal Club, an elite social club on Carrera 7 in the northern Chapinero neighborhood of the same name. It was a Friday evening. Families with children were inside, using the pool, the gym, the dining rooms. Thirty-six people were killed. More than two hundred were injured. The Colombian government attributed the attack to FARC, which had placed the explosive inside the building with the help of an insider, targeting a place that symbolized the establishment Colombia's oligarchy was accused of protecting. The building was rebuilt, and a memorial inside honors those who died. For people who live in Chapinero, the date is still remembered. The violence that touched many Colombian neighborhoods during the long civil conflict reached El Nogal on a quiet Friday evening, and thirty-six members of a community were lost. They were mothers and fathers, children and club staff, and they were killed going about the ordinary errands of an ordinary evening.

## Rivers Under the Streets

Chapinero's eastern edge runs straight up into the forested Cerros Orientales, today heavily reforested after decades of bare slopes through the 1930s. Rivers and streams descend through the locality, many now running inside man-made canals: the Arzobispo River joining the San Francisco to feed the Bogotá River, and the Quebrada El Virrey flowing into the Salitre system. Chapinero is divided into five units of zonal planning and 50 neighborhoods, from Chapinero Alto at the top of the hill to the cluster of Chicós at the north, from Quinta Camacho and Antiguo Country in the middle to the informal settlements of Bosque Calderón Tejada and El Paraíso on the slopes. Some of those hillside neighborhoods began as squatter settlements and remain among the poorest in a district otherwise dominated by upper-middle-class wealth. It is a locality of striking contrasts held inside one set of borders: gleaming glass towers and century-old Victorian mansions, high-end restaurants and the crowded TransMilenio buses moving people across Avenida Caracas, rainbow flags and church steeples, a financial district and an unbroken view of the green mountain wall that rises above it all.

## Flight Context

Located at 4.65°N, 74.06°W in the north-central part of Bogotá at approximately 2,640 meters (8,660 feet) elevation. El Dorado International Airport (SKBO) lies 13 km west. The locality sits directly beneath the Cerros Orientales, the forested eastern hills climbing past 3,200 meters, with Monserrate visible a few kilometers south. Cruise-altitude overflights of Bogotá on clear mornings show Chapinero as the dense cluster of glass towers immediately north of La Candelaria, with the green Andean ridge wall rising sharply on the east side. Afternoon convection over the cordillera is routine.
