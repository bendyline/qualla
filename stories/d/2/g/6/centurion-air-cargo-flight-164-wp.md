# Centurion Air Cargo Flight 164

A 27-year-old Boeing 747 freighter leaving Bogotá with a cargo of flowers lost two engines on rotation in the predawn darkness of July 7, 2008, crashing into a ranch house and killing a father and his 13-year-old son on the ground.

**Location:** 4.7111, -74.0722

**Categories:** Aviation accidents and incidents in Colombia, Aviation accidents and incidents in 2008, 2008 in Colombia, Accidents and incidents involving the Boeing 747, Colombia–United States relations, July 2008 in South America, Airliner accidents and incidents caused by engine failure

## Introduction

At 3:57 a.m. local time on July 7, 2008, a taxi driver refueling at a petrol station along the Bogotá–Madrid road saw a Boeing 747 trailing sparks as it skimmed low over the highway wires. The aircraft was Centurion Air Cargo Flight 164, bound for Miami with a hold full of cut flowers. It had lifted off El Dorado International Airport less than a minute earlier. The pilots were already declaring an emergency, already trying to turn back. They would not make it. The airplane skidded across a field and slammed into a ranch house, killing a father and his thirteen-year-old son who were asleep inside.

## The Aircraft

The Boeing 747-209BSF registered as N714CK was twenty-seven years old. It had flown 90,613 airframe hours. Four Pratt & Whitney JT9D-7AQ engines hung from its wings. The plane had been delivered to China Airlines in 1981 as B-1886, converted to freighter service as B-18753 when transferred to China Airlines Cargo in 1999, and grounded by the Republic of China in May 2002 after the loss of China Airlines Flight 611 south of Penghu Island. Kalitta Air acquired the airframe in November of that same year. By the summer of 2008 it was wet-leased by Centurion Air Cargo for flower flights between Bogotá and Miami, a route carrying roses, carnations, and chrysanthemums grown in the high Andean sun and flown overnight to American florists.

## The Crew

Eight crew members were on board. The pilot flying was 51-year-old Captain Bryant Beebe, with 8,874 total flight hours and 2,874 on the 747. The pilot monitoring was 49-year-old First Officer Ivan Dankha, 11,373 total hours, 2,853 on type. The 59-year-old flight engineer, Joseph Kendall, had 10,665 total hours with 2,665 on type. These were not inexperienced aviators. They had flown into El Dorado before, knew the procedures, and understood how an aircraft loaded near limits behaves in the thin air of a runway sitting 2,548 meters above sea level, where engines produce less thrust and everything takes longer.

## Rotation

The 747 was configured for takeoff with the flaps set at 10 degrees. The engines were producing an Engine Pressure Ratio of between 1.69 and 1.72 when the aircraft accelerated through Vr, 152 knots, and rotation was initiated. As the nose came up through 13 degrees and the airspeed exceeded V2 at 162 knots, the number 4 engine, the outboard right, failed. The EPR dropped from about 1.7 to 1.0 in two to three seconds. The engine surged four times in that interval. Investigators would later determine that the high-pressure turbine installed at the January 2008 shop visit had a blade tip clearance that was too large, a reduced chord, and wear on the leading edges of the fan blades; the estimated power loss was 5.8 percent. About twenty seconds after that first failure, the crew declared an emergency, reported a fire in engine 4, and requested return to runway 14R. Bogotá Tower cleared them. They started the left turn specified in the engine-out procedure. Then, about twenty seconds after engine 4 failed, engine 1 on the far left wing also failed.

## No Thrust

The failure in engine 1 originated in the third stage of the low-pressure turbine. Engineers believe it started with the loss of a number of guide vanes or with the loss of a large piece of outside air seal due to thermal damage. Parts ejected through the exhaust. Although an overboost condition existed briefly outside regular operating range, the application of such power for a short time should not have caused an engine failure; the exact triggering cause could not be determined. Whatever initiated it, the 747 had now lost both outboard engines, the two that provide the longest levers of thrust to fight asymmetric drag. Engine 2, the inboard left, began surging as well, five cycles of decline and recovery at 87, 33, 13, and 3 seconds before impact. The aircraft no longer had the thrust to climb. The crew recognized immediately that they could not reach the runway and committed to an off-airport landing in the dark.

## The House at the End of the Field

The 747 cleared trees, touched down in open ground, and skidded across a field. It struck the wires along the highway and threw sparks. Then it slammed into a ranch house. Two people inside were killed: Pedro Suarez, age 50, and his 13-year-old son Edwin. They had been asleep. The aircraft broke apart into several sections. Rescuers reached the crash site within minutes. All eight crew members survived, though one was in critical condition; at least five were seriously injured. Two were flown to a hospital in Madrid, Cundinamarca. The others were taken to the Central Police Hospital in Bogotá. The Colombian Grupo de Investigación de Accidentes opened the investigation; the U.S. National Transportation Safety Board sent five investigators, with the FAA, Boeing, and Pratt & Whitney also participating. The final report was published on August 22, 2011. The conclusion: the crash was caused by the sequential failure of engines 4 and 1, the aircraft unable to sustain flight with two engines malfunctioning and a third beginning to surge. It was the second Kalitta Air Boeing 747 crash of 2008; Kalitta Air Flight 207 had struck birds on takeoff from Brussels two months earlier. The flower roses that the Suarez family did not get to see bloom the next morning made it a small fraction of the way to Miami florists. The grief did not.

## Flight Context

Located at 4.71°N, 74.07°W near Bogotá's El Dorado International Airport (SKBO), the main Colombian hub at 2,548 meters (8,360 feet) elevation. Runway 14R/32L, from which Flight 164 departed, is one of two parallel runways. The crash site lay in a rural stretch of Madrid, Cundinamarca, west of the airport. The high field elevation means reduced engine performance on takeoff, especially for heavy four-engine freighters operating near maximum gross weight, and any engine failure near V2 demands immediate, precise procedural execution. Overflights of the El Dorado approach corridor pass over the Sabana de Bogotá plateau at around 9,000 feet MSL.
