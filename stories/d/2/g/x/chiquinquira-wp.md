# Chiquinquirá

The pilgrimage town in Boyacá where Colombia's patroness was born from a faded canvas that believers say renewed itself in 1586.

**Location:** 5.6175, -73.8164

**Categories:** Municipalities of Boyacá Department, Populated places of the Muisca Confederation, Pilgrimage sites, Catholic Colombia

## Introduction

The Muisca called it Chiquinquirá - "place of swamps covered with fog" - and on a cool morning in the Andean highlands 2,556 meters above the sea, the name still fits. Mist drifts across the savanna north of Bogotá while pilgrims climb the steps toward the Basilica of Our Lady of the Rosary. They come for a painting. Four and a half centuries ago, according to local tradition, a woman named María Ramos prayed before an abandoned canvas hanging in a chapel here, and the faded image of the Virgin brightened before her eyes. That event - known simply as la renovación, "the Renewal" - made Chiquinquirá the spiritual capital of Colombia, and it has drawn believers ever since.

## Before the Renewal

Long before Spanish friars ever walked these hills, the Chiquinquiraes people lived along the Terebinto hill in the hamlet of Coca, a community of the Muisca Confederation governed by their own cacique. They grew crops on the cool plateau and defended themselves, often with help from the neighboring Saboyaes, against raids by the Muzo from the emerald country to the west. The colonial town that would eclipse them grew from something much smaller: a residence ordered by Catalina Díaz de Islos, widow of the encomendero Antonio de Santana, on the left bank of the Chiquinquirá River. It was in that modest complex, in 1586, that believers say the painting restored itself. Around the chapel built on the site of the renewal, merchants and landowners gathered. The settlement became a parish in 1588, a municipality in 1636, and a city in 1651.

## The Painting and the Pilgrims

The image at the center of everything is surprisingly small - an oil painting on cloth of the Virgin of the Rosary, flanked by Saint Andrew and Saint Anthony. Tradition holds that colonial damp and neglect had worn it nearly blank by the time María Ramos began her prayers. When the colors returned, pilgrims began arriving. They have not stopped. On July 3, 1986, Pope John Paul II flew into the Sucre Infantry Battalion airfield, rode the popemobile to Bolívar Square, and celebrated Mass for roughly 200,000 Catholics at what is now John Paul II Park. Colombia's bishops had named the Virgin of Chiquinquirá the country's patron saint long before that visit, but the pope's presence sealed the town's place in the national imagination. The Dominican friars who began building the first convent here in 1640 would not have imagined the crowds that still fill the basilica on pilgrimage days.

## The Poisoned Bread

Chiquinquirá's modern history is not only luminous. In November 1967, a Bogotá truck driver delivered sacks of flour that had been contaminated with parathion, a powerful organophosphate insecticide. A local bakery used the flour. Within hours, 800 people were poisoned and 78 died - 74 of them children. The tragedy was so stark that it changed Colombian medicine: physicians here were trained in clinical toxicology for the first time, and the country moved to tighten legislation on the handling of poisonous substances. Murder charges were filed against the driver and the bakery's owner. Three decades later, the town suffered another unprecedented incident. On May 15, 2000, a woman named Elvia Cortés was killed when a bomb collar attached to her neck detonated during a failed disarmament attempt; a police subintendent also died, and four others were injured. Colombians still remember the case by its chilling shorthand: el collar bomba.

## A Working Town in the Andes

Strip away the miracles and the tragedies and Chiquinquirá is what it has been for centuries - a regional market town. Commerce dominates: roughly 2,000 establishments line the streets, and the city serves as the collection and distribution hub for the eighteen municipalities of Western Boyacá. Farmers bring milk, potatoes, wheat, corn and vegetables down from the rural parishes that ring the urban core. The Chiquinquirá Sandstone, named after the town, crops out in the surrounding hills, where clay and construction stone still feed local quarries. Between 1987 and 2000, the municipality absorbed waves of displaced people - most from the nearby emerald belt, where violence over mine control drove families from their homes. The city's only second-level referral hospital serves those eighteen surrounding municipalities, which is why the birth rate here runs nearly twice the national average: mothers from across the region arrive to deliver.

## Flying Over

From the air, Chiquinquirá reveals itself as a compact grid tucked into the cool highlands of western Boyacá, the basilica's twin bell towers rising from Bolívar Square. The rural parishes - seventeen of them - spread outward into farm country, hedgerows and dairy pastures following the contours of the Andean savanna. To the west, the land drops toward Muzo and the emerald-bearing foothills. To the east, the road climbs over ridges toward Tunja and eventually Bogotá.

## Flight Context

Located at 5.62°N, 73.82°W, in the Western Boyacá Province at 2,556 m elevation. Recommended viewing altitude 8,000-12,000 ft AGL to see the basilica and surrounding farm country. Nearest major airport is El Dorado International (SKBO) in Bogotá, about 115 km to the south. Tunja's Gustavo Rojas Pinilla Airport (SKTJ) is closer but smaller. The cool high-plateau climate (average 58°F) often brings morning fog that burns off by mid-morning - the feature the Muisca named the town for.
