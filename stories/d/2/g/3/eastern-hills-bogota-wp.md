# Eastern Hills (Bogotá)

The green wall that frames every view in Bogotá - sacred to the Muisca, pilgrimage climb for Catholics, and the only thing that keeps the city from spreading east forever.

**Location:** 4.6300, -74.0400

**Categories:** Mountains of Colombia, Geography of Bogotá, Muisca religion, Pilgrimage sites, Andean ecology

## Introduction

From the Monserrate summit above Bogotá, on a clear morning before the afternoon clouds build, the city spreads west across the savanna toward a horizon of distant mountains. This is the view that anchors a Colombian capital whose metropolitan area holds more than ten million people. The ridge you are standing on is called the Cerros Orientales - the Eastern Hills - a 52-kilometer chain running north-northeast to south-southwest along the eastern edge of the Bogotá savanna. For the Muisca who lived here before Spanish conquest, these were sacred places where Sué the Sun rose. For Colombians today, Monserrate remains one of the country's most visited pilgrimage sites. The green wall is a constant. The city at its foot changes every decade.

## Sacred Sunrises

Before the Spanish conquest of 1537-38, the Muisca people held the Eastern Hills as sacred. Seen from Bolívar Square, which now anchors the historic center of Bogotá with a cathedral on its east side, the hilltops of Guadalupe and Monserrate mark exactly where the sun rises on the December and June solstices. That archaeoastronomical alignment tells you something about what the hills meant. Temples and shrines dotted the slopes. The dead were buried in the hills. The highest ridges led up toward the sun. Excessive deforestation followed Spanish settlement of the savanna, stripping the slopes of their native forest. By the 1930s and 1940s the Colombian government was executing large reforestation programs to restore what colonial timber-cutting had taken, planting eucalyptus and pine that still dominate parts of the range today.

## The Stones Underneath

Geologically, the Eastern Hills are the front edge of a tectonic pile-up. The Bogotá Fault pushed Late Cretaceous rocks of the Chipaque Formation and Guadalupe Group up and over younger Paleocene and Eocene sediments. The sandstones that cap the ridges - the Labor and Tierna members of the Guadalupe Group - are hard enough to resist erosion, which is why they stand as cliffs while the softer formations below them eroded to form the savanna. In the Pleistocene, glaciers covered the highest parts of the range and fed meltwater into a great paleolake - Lake Humboldt - that filled the Bogotá savanna for thousands of years. The wetlands you can still find on the city's outskirts are that lake's last remnants. Moraines in the central Eastern Hills have been dated to 18,000 to 15,500 years before present, the tail end of the last glacial maximum.

## Climbing to Monserrate

At 3,152 meters Monserrate rises about 500 meters above the city, topped by a whitewashed sanctuary founded in the 17th century, rebuilt in the early 20th, and dedicated to El Señor Caído - the Fallen Christ. Pilgrims have climbed here for centuries. Three ways up serve the faithful and the tourists: a funicular railway, a cable car, and a steep stone path up the hillside. On Sunday mornings the path fills with Bogotanos climbing in their exercise clothes, Colombian families making the climb as a kind of moving prayer, and vendors selling chicha, arepas, and hot agua de panela. From the summit the whole city unfurls. Guadalupe Hill, slightly higher at 3,317 meters, carries a large white statue of the Virgin of Guadalupe and looks down on Monserrate from the south. Together the two peaks frame the old city like bookends.

## Life in the Forest

The hills contain several overlapping ecosystems. The highest ridges sit in páramo terrain - the characteristic Andean alpine grassland of frailejones, bunch grasses, and peat. Below the páramo line, high-altitude cloud forest still survives in patches. Researchers have documented 443 plant species in the Eastern Hills, with 156 species of vascular plants belonging to 64 families. Birds include Andean species that sweep down from the higher ranges, and butterflies such as the Julia and the common green-eyed white. The Parque Entrenubes - "park between the clouds" - covers the northernmost reach of the Sumapaz Páramo, on the city's southeastern edge. Large parts of the Eastern Hills are officially designated as a protected area, though protection has been uneven. Mining, illegal construction on the lower slopes, contaminated creeks, and frequent dry-season forest fires remain constant pressures.

## The Wall That Keeps Bogotá In

Bogotá has grown dramatically over the last century - from roughly 330,000 people in 1938 to nearly 8 million today - but the Eastern Hills have kept the city's footprint rectangular. The metropolis could not expand east because the hills were too steep, too protected, too essential as watershed and aesthetic backdrop. So instead Bogotá spread west and north across the savanna. That geography gave the capital its signature view: no matter where you are in the old city, you look east and see the green wall. It also made the hills something shared - a pilgrimage site and a running path, a geological feature and a political issue, a piece of sacred Muisca topography still visible every morning as the sun rises over Guadalupe.

## Flight Context

The Eastern Hills run north-northeast to south-southwest at approximately 4.6°N, 74.05°W. Recommended viewing altitude 9,000-12,000 ft AGL to see the ridge against the Bogotá savanna. Monserrate and Guadalupe peaks reach 3,152 and 3,317 m respectively. El Dorado International Airport (SKBO) lies on the western side of the city. Morning flights offer the clearest visibility; afternoon orographic clouds often build against the hills. The ridge is a significant obstacle for VFR traffic east of Bogotá and should be considered in any approach planning.
