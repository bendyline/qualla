# Altiplano Cundiboyacense

The high Andean plateau where the Muisca civilisation flourished, a 2,600-metre expanse of lakes, páramos, and ancient settlement that remains Colombia's historical heartland.

**Location:** 5.5322, -73.3629

**Categories:** Altiplano Cundiboyacense, Plateaus of the Andes, Landforms of Colombia, Muisca heartland, Natural regions of Colombia

## Introduction

Above the jungle and below the snowline, tucked high inside the Eastern Cordillera of the Colombian Andes, lies a plateau that should not exist in a country otherwise made of mountains and river basins. The Altiplano Cundiboyacense — a compound word stitching together the two Colombian departments it straddles, Cundinamarca and Boyacá — averages 2,600 metres above sea level and stretches wider than most visitors expect. It is made of three flat valleys: the Bogotá savanna in the south, the Ubaté-Chiquinquirá basin in the middle, and the Duitama-Sogamoso valleys in the north. Bogotá sits at its southern edge. Everything that has mattered in central Colombia for the last twelve thousand years has played out on this high ground.

## A Floor Made from a Sea

The altiplano's geological story starts underwater. From the Early Cretaceous into the Eocene, the region lay beneath a warm inland sea that covered much of northern South America. Ichthyosaurs and pliosaurs cruised those waters; fossils of the pliosaur Monquirasaurus boyacensis (long known as Kronosaurus boyacensis) and the four-nostrilled Muiscasaurus have come out of the Paja Formation near Villa de Leyva. Later, as the Eastern Cordillera folded and rose, hot fluids moving through fractures in the old seabed left emeralds — the reason Colombia is so famous for its green stones. During the Late Pleistocene, glacial lakes filled the plateau's valleys. The largest, Lake Humboldt, covered what is now the Bogotá savanna — some 4,000 square kilometres of water, roughly seventy times the size of Lake Tota, Colombia's biggest surviving natural lake. Lake Humboldt drained about 30,000 years ago. What remains of it are Lake Herrera, the Bogotá wetlands, the Bogotá River — and the impossibly fertile soil that made everything else possible.

## Twelve Thousand Years of People

The earliest dated evidence of human occupation on the altiplano comes from El Abra, in the municipality of Zipaquirá, where stone tools and chopper cores have been carbon-dated to about 12,400 years before present. Tibitó, Tequendama, and Checua follow across the next four thousand years. At each of these rock shelters, small groups of hunter-gatherers tracked white-tailed deer, guinea pigs, and armadillos across a landscape that was colder and wetter than today's. By around 5,000 years ago, people had begun moving out of the shelters and onto open ground — Checua and Aguazuque are the classic examples; at Aguazuque they built circular houses ringed with upright deer bones or wooden posts and painted some floors red. Agriculture arrived slowly: pollen evidence from the Herrera Period (800 BCE to 800 CE) shows the first maize cultivation. Maize would become the heart of Muisca cuisine; they had dozens of words for it, for its kernels, and for the processes of grinding and cooking. The cacicazgos — independent chiefdoms — were self-sufficient, terracing flooded fields and trading surplus abitago maize with warmer-climate neighbours for avocados, pineapples, and sweet potatoes.

## The Salt People

By the eleventh century the altiplano was Muisca country. The confederation — which modern anthropologists such as Jorge Gamboa Mendoza see as a looser collection of peoples than the Spanish chroniclers described — dominated the plateau from the zipa's seat at Bacatá in the south to the zaque's capital at Hunza (today Tunja) and the iraca of Sugamuxi at Sogamoso in the north. The Muisca were extraordinary traders. Salt, mined as brine from wells in Zipaquirá and Nemocón and boiled down in ceramic pots over fires, gave them one of their best commodities and their other name: the Salt People. They worked gold with a sophistication still on display at Bogotá's Museo del Oro — the tiny votive figures called tunjos, the miniature Muisca raft that has become the emblem of the El Dorado legend. And they built astronomical observatories — horizon markers for sun and moon that tracked the year with precision.

## The Conquest

In April 1536, more than 900 men left Santa Marta on the Caribbean coast under the command of Gonzalo Jiménez de Quesada, with his brother Hernán second in command. They were hunting El Dorado, and the jungle and rivers hunted them back. By the time the expedition reached Muisca territory in March 1537, only about 170 Spaniards were still with it. They founded Chipatá on March 8, pressed on through Guachetá and Lenguazaque, crossed into the Bogotá savanna at Suesca, and reached Funza in April. The zipa Tisquesusa fled, was wounded by a Spanish soldier's sword in a night attack near Facatativá, and died of his wounds — as a mohan, a Muisca priest, had reportedly prophesied — bathing in his own blood. In August, De Quesada's troops captured the zaque Quemuenchatocha in his bohío at Hunza. A few weeks later, in the Iraca Valley, two of De Quesada's soldiers accidentally set fire to the Sun Temple at Sogamoso. The altiplano was lost.

## Páramos and Living Cities

Today the altiplano carries one of the world's most specialised ecosystems: the páramo, a treeless Andean tundra found only in the high tropics. Colombia holds roughly half or more of all the páramo on Earth, and Boyacá alone holds about a fifth of Colombia's total páramo area. Sumapaz, forming the plateau's southern boundary, is often called the largest páramo in the world. Ocetá, in the northeast, is routinely called the most beautiful. Chingaza supplies much of Bogotá's water. The same plateau supports the Colombian capital and, from the northeast down to the southwest, a string of historic Muisca and colonial cities: Sogamoso, Duitama, Chiquinquirá, Villa de Leyva, Tunja, Ubaté, Suesca, Tocancipá, Zipaquirá, Cajicá, Chía, Facatativá, Soacha. Lake Tota sparkles in the northeast; the Tequendama Falls tumble off the plateau's southwestern edge. The altiplano is not a relic — it is where most of central Colombia still lives.

## Flight Context

The Altiplano Cundiboyacense stretches roughly from 4.5°N to 6°N, 73°W to 74.5°W, at an average elevation of 2,600 metres, ranging from 2,500 to 4,000 metres. El Dorado International (SKBO) at Bogotá sits on the southern savanna; smaller airports serve Tunja and Sogamoso. Average temperature around 14 °C, ranging from 0 °C to 24 °C. Driest months December–March; wettest April–May and September–November. Hail is common. From altitude, look for the three distinct flat valleys stitched between higher ridges, the lakes of Tota (northeast) and Fúquene (northwest), the Tequendama Falls plunging off the plateau's southwestern edge, and the Sumapaz Páramo forming a high moorland boundary to the south.
