# Colombian War of Independence

It began with a borrowed flower vase and ended, nine years and uncounted deaths later, at a river crossing called Boyacá where Bolívar's army decided the fate of a continent.

**Location:** 5.4504, -73.4303

**Categories:** Colombian War of Independence, 1810s conflicts, 1820s conflicts, Colonial Colombia, Independence of Colombia

## Introduction

On the morning of July 20, 1810, two Criollos in Bogotá went to the shop of a Spanish merchant named José González Llorente and asked to borrow a vase. The vase was a pretext. The Criollos knew Llorente disdained them, knew he would refuse publicly and probably rudely, knew a crowd was already gathering in the street. When he refused, as planned, one of the conspirators - Francisco José de Caldas - turned to the crowd and repeated the insult back loudly. The day ended with a junta; by the next evening the crowds had forced the arrest of the Spanish viceroy, and July 20 is what Colombians still celebrate as their Independence Day. The borrowed vase started a war that would last almost a decade and reshape half of South America.

## A Colony on the Edge

The conditions for rebellion had been accumulating for decades. Charles III of Spain had raised taxes to fund his support of American independence from Britain, triggering the Revolt of the Comuneros in New Granada as early as 1781. The Crown excluded Criollos - locally-born descendants of Spaniards - from senior administrative posts, a slight that the lawyer Camilo Torres Tenorio cataloged in his Memorandum of Grievances, arguing for equality between Americans and Europeans "upon the basis of justice." Then Napoleon changed everything. In 1808 his brother Joseph Bonaparte was installed as king of Spain after forced abdications, and Spanish authority fractured. Across the Americas, cabildos in Caracas, La Paz, Quito, Cartagena, and finally Bogotá formed their own juntas - initially swearing loyalty to the deposed Ferdinand VII but refusing to obey the viceroys Spain had sent.

## The Foolish Fatherland

After July 20, the first years of independence were chaotic enough that Colombians themselves named them la Patria Boba - the Foolish Fatherland. Each province, and sometimes each city, established its own junta. Two political camps hardened into bitter rivals: federalists in the United Provinces of New Granada and centralists in the Free and Independent State of Cundinamarca. Between 1812 and 1814 they fought a civil war even as Royalist forces still held territory in the north and south. Antonio Nariño led a southern campaign that collapsed in 1814 when he was captured at Pasto. In neighboring Venezuela, Simón Bolívar founded a Second Republic, only to see it destroyed by llanero cavalry still loyal to Spain. The internal feuds weakened everything. When Spain finally came back, the Patriots had little left to resist with.

## Reconquest and Terror

Ferdinand VII, restored to the Spanish throne by a December 1813 treaty with Napoleon, sent about 10,000 troops across the Atlantic in sixty ships in 1815 - the largest force Spain had yet dispatched to the Americas. General Pablo Morillo, a veteran of the Peninsular War, led the Reconquest. Cartagena fell after a punishing siege in 1815. Spanish forces advanced on three fronts and crushed the Patriot armies at the Battle of Cuchilla del Tambo in June 1816. What followed was a campaign of terror. Morillo's courts-martial sent more than a hundred patriot leaders to execution on charges of treason, including the scientist Francisco José de Caldas - the same man who had inflamed the crowd in Bogotá on July 20, 1810. Survivors like Francisco de Paula Santander fled to the eastern plains, near the Venezuelan border, to regroup.

## The Crossing

By 1819 Bolívar had rebuilt an army. Setting out that May, in the rainy season, he led perhaps 2,500 soldiers - Colombian and Venezuelan patriots alongside a contingent of British and Irish volunteers - over the Cordillera Oriental. The march across the soaking Páramo de Pisba killed horses and men; the llaneros from the hot lowland plains suffered terribly in the cold. But the Spanish defenders of Bogotá had assumed no army could cross that range in the rainy season. In early July, Bolívar's troops climbed out of Casanare and came down from the mountains at Socha, in Boyacá. At the Battle of Vargas Swamp on July 25, they fought off a Royalist army trying to reinforce Bogotá. Then, on August 7, 1819, at the Battle of Boyacá - a short, decisive engagement on the road to Bogotá - the bulk of the Spanish army in New Granada surrendered. Viceroy Juan de Sámano fled the capital. Three days later, Bolívar entered Bogotá without resistance.

## Who Fought, and What It Cost

Independence was not won only by generals. Criollo officers like Bolívar, Santander, and Nariño led the campaigns, but llanero cavalry from the Venezuelan and Colombian plains did much of the fighting - horsemen whose skill with a lance had been honed herding cattle. Afro-Colombians, some of them formerly enslaved, fought on both sides; the Cartagena declaration of independence on November 11, 1811 had included the right of all men to vote regardless of ethnicity, a radical principle for its time. Indigenous communities were divided by the war and suffered heavily from it. One estimate puts total deaths at about 400,000 across New Granada, Venezuela and Ecuador - roughly 15 percent of the region's 1810 population. The Republic of Colombia that emerged in 1819 eventually dissolved in 1830-31 into Venezuela, Ecuador and New Granada (which included Panama until 1903), but the battle at Boyacá is remembered as the turning point in the liberation of a continent.

## Flight Context

The Battle of Boyacá was fought at approximately 5.45°N, 73.43°W, near the Teatinos River crossing in the department of Boyacá, about 110 km northeast of Bogotá in a straight line and about 12 km south-southwest of Tunja. Recommended viewing altitude 5,000-8,000 ft AGL to see the rolling Andean terrain between Tunja and Bogotá. The nearest major airport is Bogotá's El Dorado International (SKBO); Tunja has a small airfield (SKTJ). The high plateau of Boyacá - site of both the Vargas Swamp battle and Boyacá itself - sits around 2,500-3,000 m, and morning mist often settles in the high valleys where so much of this history was decided.
