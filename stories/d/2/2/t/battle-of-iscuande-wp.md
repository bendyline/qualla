# Battle of Iscuandé

On a river mouth in Colombia's Pacific jungle, a ragtag patriot fleet with one working cannon defeated a Spanish squadron and helped decide the fate of the Colombian coast.

**Location:** 2.4538, -77.9850

**Categories:** Colombian War of Independence, 1812 in Colombia, Nariño Department, Battles involving Spain, Naval battles

## Introduction

The patriot flotilla had four old cannons, and only one of them worked. Against them sailed a Spanish squadron with a 24-pound bronze cannon nicknamed El Invencible, a gunboat clad in copper, two brigantines, and 200 soldiers. On the clearest day Iscuandé had ever seen, between mangroves on Colombia's Pacific coast, those odds were about to invert. On January 28 and 29, 1812, Captain Rodríguez and his outgunned crew opened fire from the riverbank and fought the first naval engagement of the Colombian War of Independence to a stunning finish.

## The Road to a River Mouth

The battle grew out of a chain of patriot victories farther inland. After the republicans won at Bajo Palacé, Spanish Colonel Miguel Tacón was pushed out of Popayán and eventually all the way to the Pacific coast, where he set up headquarters in Barbacoas. From there he wrote to the governor of Guayaquil and the Viceroy of Peru, laying out an ambitious plan: raise militias along the coast, press enslaved workers from the royal mines at Guapi and Timbiquí into service, seize the port of Buenaventura, then march inland to retake Pasto, Cali, and Popayán. Guayaquil and Lima agreed to help, sending a gunboat and limited support. Seventy thousand pesos in gold, much of it taken from Popayán before Tacón's defeat, bought weapons and ammunition.

## A Tidal Trap

On January 22, 1812, Tacón's fleet set sail from Tumaco. The brig San Antonio, also called El Morreño, led the squadron, along with the gunboat Justicia, two feluccas, two ceibos, and smaller boats. Bronze and iron cannons, swivel guns, and two hundred soldiers made it, as one officer put it, a force capable of controlling the coast from Buenaventura to Guayaquil. On January 28 the Spanish reached the mouth of the Iscuandé River and began bombarding the town. The patriots, who had been advancing from Cali, fell back to Iscuandé and dug in. That first afternoon, their return fire was enough to drive the royalists to anchor in the shelter of Sequionda. That night Rodríguez paddled close to the enemy ships, fired his blunderbuss into the dark, and slipped back without ever seeing clearly what he was up against.

## Six Hours of Fire

The main engagement lasted from ten in the morning until four in the afternoon on January 29. The royalists fired broadside after broadside. The patriots answered with one culverin and two or three reassembled cannons, but their marksmen were steady and the coast was theirs. Then the tide went out. Tacón's gunboat and its escorts found themselves stranded in shallow water, unable to reach deep channels or to swing their guns. At the same moment, Tacón and his lieutenant Ramón Pardo were quarreling over how to fight the battle. Seeing the paralysis, the patriots placed two boats in front of the gunboat to draw its attention, then opened rifle fire from cover in the trees along the northern shore. The first volleys killed two royalists and wounded several more. Lieutenant Pardo was severely wounded and captured.

## The Cost and the Song

By the end of the day, about eighty royalists were dead, wounded, or missing. Twelve patriots were killed and more than fifteen wounded. The victors captured the copper-clad gunboat and the brig San Antonio, along with eighty rifles, twenty pairs of pistols, and eleven quintals of gunpowder. Rodríguez wrote to his comrade José Díaz that Our Lord Jesus Christ had fully protected them there. But another memory survived as well. The historian Sergio Elías Ortiz collected a marimba song on Iscuandé from the lips of a centenarian Black man who had known the fight in his youth. Its call and response stitched the victory into the coastal culture: "Do you remember, brother, do you remember / When the great Tacón came / Saying he would take Iscuandé / With many men and cannons?" The people who sang it on the river were descendants of enslaved Africans brought to work those same royal mines Tacón had hoped to turn against the patriots. The story of Iscuandé is also their story.

## What a River Decided

The victory at Iscuandé liberated the Pacific coastline between Tumaco and Buenaventura from Spanish control. Barbacoas soon proclaimed independence. Colonel Tacón slipped away to Tumaco, then Guayaquil, and finally to the port of Callao in the Viceroyalty of Peru, where he reached the harbor on March 20 and went on serving Spain until 1819. The gunboat he had named El Invencible sailed under new colors. Today Iscuandé is a quiet river town on Colombia's Pacific coast, surrounded by mangroves and estuaries. The tide that helped decide the battle still drops twice a day through the same channels, sweeping past the place where, for six hours on a bright January afternoon, a single functioning cannon and a handful of sharp-eyed riflemen rewrote the map of the coast.

## Flight Context
