# Eastern Frontal Fault System

A 921-kilometer seam where the North Andes microplate grinds against South America, and the earthquakes that follow keep rewriting the map.

**Location:** 4.2010, -73.6800

**Categories:** Seismic faults of Colombia, Thrust faults, Strike-slip faults, Active faults, Plate boundaries

## Introduction

A quiet valley between the Andes and the Llanos looks peaceful enough from the road. A cornfield, a white house, a stand of palms. What you cannot see is the geological stress accumulating beneath the soil. The Eastern Frontal Fault System runs here - a 921-kilometer chain of thrust and strike-slip faults cutting Colombia from Ecuador to Venezuela, marking the line where the North Andes microplate tears itself away from the South American Plate. Every major earthquake in Colombia's eastern foothills for at least two centuries has come from somewhere along this system. Geologists have mapped its segments carefully. The land remembers them less carefully.

## A Plate Boundary in Slow Motion

The Eastern Frontal Fault System is not a single fault but an assembly of them - at least nine major segments plus a dozen minor ones, linked end-to-end along the base of the Cordillera Oriental. Their combined mapped length is 1,821 kilometers. To the geologist's eye they tell a story that began in the Late Jurassic, when a shallow sea covered much of central Colombia and deposited the shales and sandstones that today's faults slice through. The mountains rose later, in the Neogene, pushed up by compression between tectonic plates. The initial normal faults, formed by extension, reversed and became thrust faults, shoving older Paleozoic rocks up and over younger sediments. What we see today as the abrupt wall of the Eastern Andes above the Llanos Orientales is the surface expression of that long geological wrestling match.

## The Segments and Their Names

Geologists subdivide the system by location. The Guaicáramo Fault splits into three sections totaling 343 kilometers; it is the most prominent thrust fault above the Llanos, characterized by what scientists call neotectonic morphology - freshly-faulted landforms that reveal recent motion. The Yopal Fault, 220 kilometers long, is the easternmost active thrust known in northeastern Colombia, placing Neogene rocks against Quaternary alluvium along the Casanare plains. The Servitá-Santa María Fault runs for nearly 296 kilometers east of Bogotá. The Algeciras Fault crosses the southward-narrowing Eastern Ranges at an oblique angle. The Garzón-Pitalito Fault creates a pull-apart basin near the town of Pitalito. Each name corresponds to a real hillside, a real river that got offset, a real village with a story about the ground shaking.

## When the Ground Moved

The historical record is long enough to show the system at work. On November 16, 1827, the Timaná earthquake struck at magnitude 7.7, dammed the Suaza River with landslides, and rearranged the topography of southern Huila. In 1834 the Sibundoy earthquake in Putumayo hit magnitude 7.0. The 1917 Sumapaz earthquake, magnitude 7.3, shook Bogotá and the surrounding highlands. On February 9, 1967, the Neiva earthquake damaged buildings across Huila at magnitude 7.2. In 1995 the Tauramena earthquake struck Casanare at magnitude 6.5, damaging an oil pipeline. The 2008 El Calvario event in Meta was smaller at magnitude 5.6 but rattled the Bogotá region. These are not random events. Each corresponds to a documented segment of the Eastern Frontal Fault System.

## Cities on the Edge

Ten of Colombia's 32 departments lie along the fault system: Nariño, Putumayo, Cauca, Huila, Caquetá, Cundinamarca, Meta, Boyacá, Casanare, and Arauca. Several departmental capitals - Mocoa in Putumayo, Florencia in Caquetá, Villavicencio in Meta, Yopal in Casanare - sit directly on or immediately adjacent to active fault traces. Villavicencio, gateway to the Llanos and home to more than half a million people, straddles the Guayuriba Fault at the foot of the mountains. Yopal sits along the thrust fault that bears its name. Every construction project in these cities negotiates with geology in some form, from building codes to insurance rates. The 2017 Mocoa landslide disaster - when monsoon rains sent a cascade of mud and rock through the city, killing more than 300 people - was not a direct fault movement, but the steep, fractured terrain the Mocoa Fault had produced made the catastrophe possible.

## Reading the Landscape

From the air the fault system is astonishingly legible. The abrupt change of slope from Andean mountain to flat Llanos is a single long cliff running from Venezuela south into Ecuador. Scarps, offset streams, narrow pull-apart basins, long linear ridges - all betray recent motion. The Guaicáramo segment, in particular, has what geologists describe as some of the most outstanding neotectonic morphology in Colombia: the landscape itself is still adjusting, still young. Satellite imagery and high-resolution digital elevation models have let researchers trace the fault traces with precision. What a farmer might know as "the ridge above the creek" turns out to be the surface scar of a thrust fault that moved a few thousand years ago and will move again.

## Flight Context

The fault system runs diagonally across central Colombia. A representative point is 4.20°N, 73.68°W, near Villavicencio and the front of the Cordillera Oriental. Recommended viewing altitude 6,000-10,000 ft AGL to see the abrupt mountain-front transition from the Andes into the Llanos plains - one of the most dramatic tectonic boundaries visible anywhere on Earth. Key airports along the system include Vanguardia (SKVV) at Villavicencio, El Alcaraván (SKYP) at Yopal, Gustavo Artunduaga (SKFL) at Florencia, and Cananguchal (SKMU) at Mocoa. Morning flights typically offer best visibility before afternoon convection builds over the mountain front.
