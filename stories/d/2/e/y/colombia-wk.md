# Colombia

Pick your climate, pick your region - from Caribbean beaches to Amazon jungle to Bogota bookshops, Colombia packs more landscapes into one country than most continents.

**Location:** 4.6000, -74.0800

**Categories:** Countries in South America, Travel destinations, Andean region, Caribbean coast

## Introduction

Pick a climate and drive an hour. That is the short version of traveling in Colombia. The country sits at the northwest corner of South America, twice the size of France, with coastlines on both the Caribbean and the Pacific, three Andean ranges running up its spine, and the Amazon spreading across its southeast. A morning that starts in the light-jacket chill of Bogotá can end by a pool on a lowland hacienda. A week here can cross deserts, paramos, cloud forests, mangroves, and reefs without crossing a border. After decades when the country's reputation kept most travelers away, Colombia is being rediscovered - and travelers are finding a place that was never defined by its headlines.

## Six Regions, One Country

Colombians think of their country in regions before they think of it as a whole. The Andean interior holds most of the population and the major cities: Bogotá at 2,600 meters, cool and intellectual, with more bookstores than almost any capital in Latin America; Medellín at a perpetual spring temperature, famous for innovation and the El Poblado nightlife; Cali down in the Cauca Valley, claiming the title of world salsa capital. The Caribbean coast is another country in tone - walled Cartagena in its pastel old town, Barranquilla in carnival fever each February, Santa Marta at the foot of the world's highest coastal mountain range. The Pacific is wetter and wilder; the Llanos plains stretch east toward Venezuela; the Amazon covers the south. Each region cooks differently, dances differently, speaks Spanish differently.

## What to Taste

Bandeja paisa is the dish tourism brochures love: rice, beans, fried plantain, arepa, fried egg, chorizo, and chicharrón arranged on a tray the size of a platter. It belongs to Antioquia and the coffee region. Bogotá's ajiaco is gentler - a chicken soup made with three kinds of potato, fresh corn, and the herb guasca, served with capers, avocado, and cream. On the Caribbean coast the rice arrives flavored with coconut, and sancocho of fish replaces the highland sancocho of hen. Breakfast is its own world: hot chocolate served with a piece of cheese dropped in to melt, tamales wrapped in plantain leaves, arepas with scrambled egg, and occasionally changua, a milk-based soup that divides opinion even among Colombians. Pre-Columbian civilizations cultivated about 200 varieties of potato here, and the legacy shows up in almost every meal.

## Getting Around

There are no intercity passenger trains in Colombia. The mountains are too steep, distances too long. Air travel fills the gap - Bogotá-Medellín was the busiest air route in all of Latin America in 2024, carrying 3.8 million passengers on a flight that covers just 239 kilometers. Avianca, LATAM Colombia, Jetsmart, and a handful of regional carriers connect every city of any size. On the ground, long-distance buses run everywhere, cheaply, though slowly; main highways are two-lane, and landslides in the rainy season can close them for days. Colombia's cities pioneered BRT systems in Latin America - Bogotá's TransMilenio, Cali's MIO, Medellín's Metroplús - and Medellín's metro (still the country's only one) climbs into the hills via cable cars that double as commuter transit. Cable cars also reach mountain towns like Jardín and Jericó, and a spectacular one crosses the Chicamocha canyon in Santander.

## Things to Do That Aren't in the Guidebook

The Lost City (Ciudad Perdida), built by the Tayrona between the 8th and 14th centuries, sits deep in the jungle of the Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta. Reaching it takes a four-day trek with local guides - some of them Kogui or Wiwa descendants of the people who built it. Providencia, a Caribbean island halfway to Jamaica, sits above the Western Hemisphere's second-largest barrier reef and was designated a UNESCO Biosphere Reserve. The Zipaquirá salt cathedral, an hour north of Bogotá, is a working church carved from an old salt mine. In the Amazon around Leticia, boats weave through flooded forests full of pink river dolphins. The Cocora Valley in Quindío is the home of the wax palm, Colombia's national tree, which grows to 60 meters. Coffee farms across the Eje Cafetero now welcome travelers for overnight stays and harvest days.

## Respect and Practical Notes

Colombians are formal by Latin American standards. Please and thank you matter. In parts of Boyacá, strangers still address one another as su merced, "your mercy," a phrase that would sound medieval elsewhere. Race is not a hot-button topic in the way it is in the United States; nicknames like mono (blond, literally "monkey"), moreno (dark), and chino (a Chibcha-origin word for "kid" that also means Chinese) carry no sting in context. Same-sex marriage has been legal since April 2016, and Bogotá's Chapinero neighborhood holds what may be the country's largest LGBT scene. The security situation has improved dramatically since the 1990s - Colombia's violent crime rate is now lower than Mexico's or Brazil's - but rural areas near the Darién Gap, in Putumayo, or Caquetá are still active conflict zones. Stick to the main roads and the cities, and Colombia in 2026 feels different from the country your parents read about.

## Flight Context

Colombia stretches from 12°N at the Guajira Peninsula to 4°S in the Amazon, and from 67°W to 79°W. Major international airports include El Dorado (SKBO) in Bogotá, José María Córdova (SKRG) near Medellín, Rafael Núñez (SKCG) in Cartagena, and Alfonso Bonilla Aragón (SKCL) in Cali. The country's highest peak, Pico Cristóbal Colón in the Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta, reaches 5,775 m. Highland weather in Bogotá is cool and stable; the Caribbean coast is hot and humid with a November-through-April dry season that is ideal for flying.
