# Colombia

The only country named for Columbus, home to 10% of Earth's species, two oceans, and a 60-year war that finally has an end.

**Location:** 4.0000, -72.0000

**Categories:** Countries in South America, Megadiverse countries, Former Spanish colonies, Andean states, Caribbean countries

## Introduction

About one in ten species on Earth lives in Colombia. Think about that for a moment. A country roughly the size of France and Spain combined, sitting in the northwest corner of South America, contains more than 1,900 species of birds - more than Europe and North America combined - along with the world's highest number of orchids, butterflies, and amphibians ranked among the top few. It is one of 17 countries the United Nations calls megadiverse. It is also named for a man who never set foot here. Christopher Columbus reached Panama on his fourth voyage, not Colombia proper, but in 1819 Simón Bolívar's new republic took his name anyway - a reference not to a person but to the entire New World.

## Before the Conquest

Humans have lived in what is now Colombia for at least 14,500 years. Paleoindian hunters left traces at Pubenza and El Totumo in the Magdalena Valley; the oldest pottery in the Americas - dating to around 5000 BCE - was made at San Jacinto on the Caribbean plain. By the centuries before Spanish contact the country held sophisticated chiefdoms: the Muisca on the high plateau of today's Cundinamarca and Boyacá, the Tairona in the Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta, the Quimbaya in the Cauca Valley, the Zenú on the Caribbean lowlands. The Muisca farmed maize, potato, quinoa, and cotton, and traded gold, emeralds, coca, and rock salt across the Andean highlands. A site at Serranía de la Lindosa, revealed in 2020, contains eight miles of rock paintings dated to roughly 10,500 BCE - some of them depicting now-extinct Ice Age animals that the painters had seen with their own eyes.

## Conquest, Colony, Independence

Alonso de Ojeda, who had sailed with Columbus, reached the Guajira Peninsula in 1499. In 1510 the Spanish founded Santa María la Antigua del Darién - the first stable European settlement on the mainland of the Americas. Gonzalo Jiménez de Quesada pushed into the Andean interior in 1536, provisionally founding Bogotá in 1538 near the Muisca settlement of Muyquytá. The Viceroyalty of New Granada, established in 1717, eventually administered today's Colombia, Panama, Ecuador, and Venezuela from the capital. The colonial order relied on forced labor, Indigenous tribute, and the trans-Atlantic trade in enslaved Africans. Declarations of independence followed July 20, 1810 - still Colombia's national day - and after nearly a decade of war, Bolívar's decisive victory at Boyacá in August 1819 ended Spanish rule. Between 250,000 and 400,000 Colombians, as much as 20% of the pre-war population, died in the independence struggle.

## La Violencia and the Long Conflict

Colombia's 20th century was shadowed by political violence. In April 1948 the Liberal presidential candidate Jorge Eliécer Gaitán was assassinated on a Bogotá street, triggering riots known as El Bogotazo and a ten-year partisan war called simply La Violencia. At least 180,000 Colombians died. Out of that trauma came the National Front, a bipartisan coalition that alternated the presidency between Liberals and Conservatives for 16 years - and out of the National Front's exclusions came new guerrilla movements, chiefly the FARC and the ELN, founded in 1964. The armed conflict that followed lasted more than half a century and was tangled further by the rise of cocaine cartels in the 1970s and by right-wing paramilitary groups that emerged in the 1980s. A 2016 peace accord between the Santos government and the FARC-EP ended the largest insurgency; the same year President Santos was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize. The work of implementation continues, uneven and contested, across rural Colombia.

## The Country of Water

Geography explains much about Colombia. The Andes split into three ranges as they enter the country, creating four long north-south valleys. The Magdalena River, draining the eastern valley, was the main artery of trade for four centuries. Colombia holds coastlines on both the Caribbean and the Pacific - the only South American country to do so. To the east, the Llanos plains roll toward Venezuela; to the southeast the Amazon rainforest begins. The Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta, rising to 5,775 meters at Pico Cristóbal Colón, is the highest coastal mountain range in the world. Colombia ranks sixth globally in renewable freshwater supply. About 70% of its electricity comes from hydropower, powered by rivers that have carved the country's topography for millions of years.

## A Diversified Economy

Colombia's economy is the third-largest in South America, with GDP (PPP) reaching about US$1 trillion in 2023 according to IMF estimates. Coffee remains iconic, but Colombia is also one of the world's five largest producers of avocado and palm oil, the second-largest cut-flower exporter in the world after the Netherlands, a top-20 petroleum producer, and the world's largest producer of emeralds. Services - finance, technology, tourism - now account for 65% of the workforce. Bogotá anchors the financial sector, Medellín the textile and fashion industries, Cartagena the booming cruise and conference tourism trade. Tourism has grown from 600,000 annual foreign visitors in 2007 to more than four million a decade later. With the long conflict receding, Colombia is rediscovering itself in the eyes of the world.

## Flight Context

Colombia spans latitudes 12°N to 4°S and longitudes 67°W to 79°W, covering roughly 1.14 million square kilometers. Recommended viewing altitude for Andean features is 10,000-14,000 ft AGL; for Amazon and coastal scenery, 6,000-10,000 ft works better. Primary international airports include El Dorado International (SKBO) in Bogotá, José María Córdova (SKRG) near Medellín, Rafael Núñez (SKCG) in Cartagena, and Alfonso Bonilla Aragón (SKCL) in Cali. The three Andean ranges - Occidental, Central, and Oriental - create striking parallel ridges visible from altitude. Weather varies dramatically by region; highland mornings typically offer the clearest flying conditions.
