# Soyuz at the Guiana Space Centre

For a decade, Russian rockets launched from French Guiana on a joint program that worked largely as intended until geopolitics ended it.

**Location:** 5.1690, -52.6900

**Categories:** space, rockets, french-guiana, international-cooperation, kourou

## Introduction

On 21 October 2011 at 10:30 UTC, a Russian Soyuz rocket lifted off from French Guiana. It was a first: no Soyuz had ever launched outside the territory of the former Soviet Union. A rocket designed for the frozen steppes of Kazakhstan was now roaring into the sky over a South American rainforest, and for the next decade, Russia and Europe would share a launchpad on the Atlantic coast of the Amazon.

## Why French Guiana

The Guiana Space Centre near Kourou sits close to the equator, and that geography is rocket fuel in itself. Launches from equatorial sites get a free boost from Earth's rotation - about 460 meters per second of extra velocity to the east, which translates directly into payload capacity. Europe had Ariane 5 for heavy lifts and was developing Vega for light ones, but needed a medium-lift rocket to fill the gap. Rather than designing one from scratch, Europe made a deal with Russia: Russia would supply Soyuz-2 rockets, modified for the tropical climate and called Soyuz-ST-A and ST-B, and launch them from a new pad built at Kourou. The joint facility, the Ensemble de Lancement Soyouz, broke ground in the 2000s. Components shipped from Saint Petersburg first arrived by sea in November 2009.

## Adapting the Rocket

Soyuz had flown from desert for decades. Flying it from an equatorial jungle required changes. The tropical humidity and heat meant new treatments for the airframe. The launch infrastructure took on a European look: unlike Soyuz pads in Russia, the ELS included a mobile service tower that allowed payloads to be integrated with the rocket vertically, on the launchpad itself - the Ariane way rather than the Soyuz way. A European Safeguard Kit was added to track the rocket in real time and, if necessary, destroy it if it went off course over populated or ecologically sensitive areas. The Fregat upper stage, optional on standard Soyuz-2s, was mandatory for the ST variants. Between 2011 and 2022, 27 Soyuz-ST rockets launched from the CSG, with 26 successful missions. Nine used the smaller ST-A; the rest used the more powerful ST-B.

## The Galileo Anomaly

The one failure was instructive. On 22 August 2014, flight VS09 carried two satellites for Europe's Galileo navigation constellation. The Soyuz performed normally. Then the Fregat upper stage, during its long first burn, delivered the satellites to the wrong orbit. The investigation, published 7 October 2014, found a thermal bridge between propellant and helium feed lines had caused hydrazine to freeze - the lines ran so close together that cold helium, pressurizing the main tanks, chilled the adjacent hydrazine line below its freezing point during the long burn. The flight control system did not detect the problem and completed its maneuver with the stage oriented wrong. The satellites were salvaged with a complex 15-maneuver orbit-raising operation that concluded in March 2015, leaving them in a mirrored pair of unusual orbits that repeated the same ground track every 20 days. They still fly in those orbits today, reminders that a single cold propellant line can rewrite a mission plan.

## The End

The Russian invasion of Ukraine in February 2022 ended the partnership abruptly. Russia withdrew its personnel from Kourou. European satellites that had been planned for Soyuz flights - including replacement Galileo spacecraft - were left without a rocket. Arianespace scrambled to move payloads onto other vehicles or wait for new ones. The timing was almost tragic: the coming Ariane 6, whose smaller Ariane 62 version was designed to take over Soyuz's medium-lift role, and the upgraded Vega C were about to make Soyuz largely redundant anyway. The European-Russian collaboration in French Guiana had been, by any technical measure, a success. Twenty-six successful missions, two stranded satellites recovered, one decade-long program that worked. But it ended not because of engineering failure but because of a war thousands of kilometers away. The ELS then stood idle for years, a piece of infrastructure built for a partnership that no longer existed.

## What Remains

The pad lies about 10 kilometers northwest of the old Ariane 5 complex, in the commune of Sinnamary. When the Russian teams left in 2022, much of their equipment stayed behind. In September 2024 the French space agency CNES chose MaiaSpace, an ArianeGroup subsidiary, to take the site over for its Maia launcher; the old Soyuz service tower was demolished with explosives in April 2026, and Maia's first flight is expected no earlier than 2027. The Soyuz era at Kourou is now a monument to a particular kind of international cooperation: technical, pragmatic, productive, and vulnerable to forces that had nothing to do with engineering. From the cruising altitude of an overflight, the complex looks like a clearing in the rainforest with a few buildings - a small footprint for a program that once, briefly, made Russia and Europe launch together.

## Flight Context

The Ensemble de Lancement Soyouz sits at the Guiana Space Centre northwest of Kourou in the commune of Sinnamary, at about 5.30 degrees N, 52.83 degrees W. Recommended viewing altitude 3,000-6,000 feet to observe the launch complex and its Atlantic proximity. Cayenne-Felix Eboue (ICAO: SOCA) is the nearest major airport, about 75 km southeast. The space centre has multiple launch pads; the Soyuz complex lies northwest of the Ariane pads. Airspace restrictions apply during launch operations.
