# Guiana Space Centre

Europe's spaceport sits five degrees north of the equator on the jungle coast of French Guiana, launching Ariane and Vega rockets from the same stretch of shore that once held Devil's Island.

**Location:** 5.2372, -52.7606

**Categories:** Spaceport, French Guiana, European Space Agency, Aerospace, Launch Sites

## Introduction

Five degrees above the equator, the Earth spins faster than it does anywhere humans have built a launch pad. That extra rotational kick is why a rocket lifting off from Kourou in French Guiana can loft more than a third more payload to geostationary orbit than the same rocket leaving Baikonur. It is also why, in 1964, France chose this stretch of coast - swampy, unpopulated, facing 4,000 kilometers of open Atlantic - to replace its Algerian launch sites. On Christmas Day 2021, an Ariane 5 carried the James Webb Space Telescope out of this same jungle. You could have watched it go from a beach where, a century earlier, convicts from Devil's Island dreamed of escape.

## Why Here

Equatorial launch sites are geometry. A rocket aimed east picks up roughly 1,670 kilometers per hour of free velocity at the equator, and that number falls off with latitude. Cape Canaveral sits at 28.5 degrees north, which locks it out of orbits with inclinations lower than 28.5 degrees unless the rocket spends propellant bending its path. Kourou, at 5.2 degrees, can reach inclinations as low as 6. The math is why the Ariane 5 from French Guiana can push more than 10,000 kilograms to geostationary transfer orbit while a similarly-sized Russian Proton from the steppe manages 6,270. The second reason is what lies east of the launch pad: nothing. No villages, no shipping lanes, nothing but open ocean for any stage that falls where it should not.

## A Shared Pad

France opened the Centre Spatial Guyanais in 1968 with its own Diamant rocket. In 1975 it offered the facility to the newly-formed European Space Agency, which today covers roughly two-thirds of the site's annual budget. The land is managed by CNES, the French national space agency. The launch infrastructure belongs to ESA. Operations are run by Arianespace, which sells slots to commercial customers, while the rockets themselves are built by ArianeGroup for Ariane 6 and by Italy's Avio for Vega. On paper this is a jurisdictional knot. In practice it works because everyone involved has spent decades learning to share a jungle.

## The Pads, Present and Past

ELA-1 began life in the 1960s as a Europa-II pad, hosted one catastrophic guidance failure in 1971, and was rebuilt for Ariane 1. It later became ELV for the Vega rocket, whose first flight left here on 13 February 2012. ELA-2 hosted Ariane 4 from 1988 until 2003, and its service tower was demolished with explosives in September 2011. ELA-3 launched Ariane 5 from 4 June 1996 to its final flight on 5 July 2023. ELA-4 was inaugurated on 28 September 2021 for Ariane 6, which made its maiden flight on 9 July 2024. An older Diamant pad is being reactivated as ELM for a generation of microlaunchers - Isar Aerospace's Spectrum, PLD Space's Miura 5, Rocket Factory Augsburg's RFA One, and half a dozen others. The Soyuz pad at Sinnamary, built with Russia and opened in 2011, went silent on 26 February 2022 when Roscosmos walked away over Ukraine sanctions. In September 2024 MaiaSpace was chosen to take it over for a new two-stage launcher.

## What Launches From Here

The James Webb Space Telescope left Kourou on 25 December 2021 aboard Ariane 5. The BepiColombo mission to Mercury followed in October 2018. The JUICE probe departed on 14 April 2023 for an eight-year transit to Jupiter's icy moons - Ganymede, Callisto, and Europa. In between, the spaceport has lofted communications satellites for almost every country that has ever wanted one: ARSAT-1 for Argentina, Sky Muster for Australia's NBN network, GSAT-7 for the Indian Navy, SaudiGeosat-1, Hispasat 36W-1. The first Diamant-B left the pad on 10 March 1970 carrying two small satellites. One, DIAL/MIKA, failed during launch but still reached orbit at 111 kilograms of effectively dead weight. The other, DIAL/WIKA, sent back data for about two months.

## The Islands in the Trajectory

Northeast of the launch complex, under the path every geosynchronous payload traces, lie the Îles du Salut - the Salvation Islands, including the one the world calls Devil's Island. For most of a century this was a French penal colony where Alfred Dreyfus was held, and where Henri Charrière set Papillon. The last prisoners left in 1953. Today the islands are a tourist site, but whenever a rocket goes up the visitors get boated back to the mainland, because the launching trajectory runs directly over them. A modern Europe lifts its most advanced science off a shore whose colonial inheritance it still carries. Local activists make this point regularly: French Guiana is 20 percent unemployed, dependent on imported food, and home to one of the most expensive rockets on Earth. The 2017 social unrest - 30 labor unions and the Collective of 500 Brothers blockading the spaceport - ended only after Paris authorized an emergency relief package of up to 2.1 billion euros.

## Flight Context

Located at 5.24°N, 52.76°W, on the coast between Kourou and Sinnamary. Cayenne – Félix Eboué International (SOCA / CAY) lies 50 km to the southeast and is the nearest major airport. Airspace over CSG is closed during launch windows; check NOTAMs before filing any flight plan in the region. Recommended viewing altitude 3,000-5,000 ft outside restricted periods. The Salvation Islands (Îles du Salut) lie 14 km north of Kourou, directly beneath the primary launch corridor.
