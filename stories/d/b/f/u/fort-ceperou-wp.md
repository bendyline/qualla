# Fort Cépérou

The ruined hilltop fort at the western edge of Cayenne still carries the name of the Kalina chief who once sold the French the ground beneath it - a transaction whose cost his people bore for generations.

**Location:** 4.9376, -52.3366

**Categories:** Forts, French Guiana, Colonial history, Historic sites

## Introduction

What stands on Mont Cépérou today is modest: some old walls, a bell tower, a pagoda rescued from collapse in 2016. It is not, at first glance, much of a fort. But the name is everything. Cépérou was the Kalina (Carib) chief who, in 1643, sold the hill at the mouth of the Cayenne River to a French commander named Charles Poncet de Brétigny. The sale gave the French the hill on which Cayenne would grow. The three centuries of French colonial history that followed - the disasters and re-foundings, the sieges by the Dutch and the English and the Portuguese, Vauban's ramparts and their destruction - all circle back to that hill and the man whose name it still carries.

## Before the Fort

Daniel de La Touche, seigneur de la Ravardière, had been the first Frenchman to seriously reconnoitre this coast, in 1604. The Spanish and Portuguese had not settled it, though rumors of El Dorado nudged at the edges of every plan. The Dutch were already settling on the Essequibo, Berbice and Demerara rivers. The English showed up at the Suriname River in 1630. Cardinal Richelieu authorized colonization of Guiana in 1626. For decades French attempts came and broke apart against the climate, against tropical diseases, against peoples who already lived there. Merchants from Rouen built an early fort on the northwestern shore of Cayenne Island in 1638. It would not last.

## The Sale and the Revolt

In 1643 Brétigny arrived with four hundred settlers under the banner of the Compagnie du Cap du Nord. He bought the hill at the mouth of the Cayenne River from the Kalina and named it after Cépérou, the chief who sold it. A wooden fort went up on the summit; a village grew below. Then Brétigny showed what kind of man he was. He was ruthless, despotic, cruel to the settlers under his command and crueler to the Kalinas from whom he had just bought his hill. He persecuted them. He enslaved some. In 1644 the Kalinas revolted, killing many of the colonists. A Carib warrior killed Brétigny with an axe to the head. Twenty-five Frenchmen survived the uprising. None of the four hundred who had come with Brétigny would leave Guiana alive. The story of Fort Cépérou opens, in other words, with a man who came as a buyer and behaved as a tyrant, and the people he wronged closing the account.

## Changing Hands

The pattern of the next fifty years was a rhythm of failure and arrival. In 1652 twelve seigneurs of the Compagnie de la France équinoxiale landed 800 men and found the 25 survivors of the previous expedition. The king's engineer Jean de Laon replaced the wooden walls with a stone bastion called Fort Saint Michel. Within a few years the new settlers too had been killed by the Kalina or had fled to Barbados. By 1660 no Frenchmen remained in Guiana. In 1664 Colbert sent 1,200 men to retake Cayenne from a small Dutch colony that had meanwhile established itself. In August 1667 an English fleet under Rear-Admiral Sir John Harman sacked the town and destroyed the fort. On 5 May 1676 the Dutch vice-admiral Jacob Binckes landed 900 troops near what was now called Fort Saint Louis; the French surrendered. On 18 December of that same year a French force under vice-admiral Jean II d'Estrées took the city back. The fort's name cycled - Cépérou, Saint Michel, Saint Louis, Cépérou again - but the hill outlasted every flag.

## Vauban's Walls and Their End

Between 1689 and 1693 the great French military engineer Sébastien Le Prestre de Vauban laid out an elaborate new line of fortifications around the whole town. The original small fort on the hill remained, now wrapped in more elegant geometry. Then came fire. In 1701 the fort's structures burned, and the fire leapt to the palm-thatched huts below. During the Napoleonic wars, from 1809 to 1817, Portuguese forces occupied Guiana. They left, but not before destroying Vauban's ramparts and knocking down the fort's bastions. Only three dilapidated buildings remained of the fort itself. Governor Tardy de Montravel built a lighthouse on Mont Cépérou in 1862. By 1864 the fort was almost abandoned.

## What Remains

In August 1888 a great fire destroyed the southeast part of Cayenne. The bell in the fort's pagoda rang for eight days as a warning and a rallying point until, on 12 August, it split from the strain. A public clock was installed in the first half of the twentieth century. Today some walls, a bell tower and the pagoda are what is left standing. The pagoda is classified as a *monument historique*; in 2016 work was done to save it from collapsing. In February 2009 the armed forces formally transferred the site to the city of Cayenne. The most recent chapter written into Mont Cépérou came on 9 June 2024, when the Olympic flame passed through the fort during the "Ocean Relay" leg of the 2024 Summer Olympics torch relay on its way to the Place des Palmistes. The hill that had changed hands so many times under different banners received a quieter visit - a flame, a crowd, a moment. Behind it all, the name of a chief long dead still holds the ground.

## Flight Context

Fort Cépérou sits at 4.94°N, 52.34°W on the western edge of Cayenne, French Guiana, above the mouth of the Cayenne River. Visible from approach to Félix Eboué Airport (SOCA / CAY), about 13 km to the south-southwest. From the air, look for the small hill at the northwest corner of the old island of Cayenne, where the Cayenne River meets the Atlantic.
