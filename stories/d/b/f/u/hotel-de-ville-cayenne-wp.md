# Hôtel de Ville, Cayenne

Cayenne's neoclassical city hall, completed in 1924 on Rue de Rémire, grew out of a gold merchant's house, a quartermaster's office, and a mayor's stubborn insistence on brick.

**Location:** 4.9382, -52.3350

**Categories:** City Halls, Cayenne, French Guiana, Neoclassical Architecture, Civic Buildings

## Introduction

For more than a century the town hall of Cayenne moved around the block. It started, in the eighteenth century, as the home of a wealthy gold merchant named Victor Ceïde. It moved to an old quartermaster's office. It spent a spell in a building called Maison Vitalo on the corner of Rue Arago and Rue Barrat while the bricklayers worked next door. Only in 1924, under mayor Eugène Gober and the assistant engineer of public works Paul Amusant, did the neoclassical mairie you see today finally open its doors on Rue de Rémire - a clock above the center bay, iron railings on the balcony, and the seal of a city that had been moving since the last days of the French colonial sugar economy.

## The Gold Merchant's House

Maison Ceïde, the first town hall, stood on what is now Avenue du Général de Gaulle. It was timber-framed in the eighteenth-century Creole manner, and by 1842 it had evolved into a symmetrical composition of a main block with two square pavilions flanking a courtyard. A round-headed opening in the center allowed carts to pass through to the yard inside. The first floor's covered walkway jettied out over the pavement - a bit of local climate engineering that kept the rain off pedestrians without giving up space upstairs. Victor Ceïde himself had grown rich on the gold trade that ran through Cayenne in the nineteenth century, and his former house later became the Galerie des Trois-Fontaines, named after the three fountains that in 1867 first piped fresh water down from the reservoir on Mount Cépérou. One of the fountains stood in the courtyard. The stones are long gone, but the fountain idea persists in the name of a shopping arcade that still occupies the site.

## The Quartermaster's Office

By the late nineteenth century, the Ceïde house was too small. The council moved a short distance southwest to the old quartermaster's office on Rue de Rémire. This was a handsome military building: an eleven-bay frontage with the central three bays recessed around a doorway, a mix of square-headed and round-headed windows, shuttered on the ground floor, with four dormers poking out of the attic. It served the town through the end of the 1800s and into the 1900s, though it was timber-framed, which in the climate of French Guiana is not a recipe for longevity.

## Mayor Gober's Brick

Population pressure at the start of the twentieth century pushed the council toward something more permanent. Mayor Eugène Gober wanted brick. The timber-framed town hall on Rue de Rémire came down. The council decamped to Maison Vitalo while construction proceeded. Paul Amusant, assistant engineer of public works, supervised the design - a neoclassical brick building finished with cement render and completed in 1924. The frontage runs seven bays wide. A segmental-headed doorway with stone surround and keystone marks the center, framed by casement windows on both floors. The central five bays are fronted by a balcony with iron railings. Above the doorway, a clock. At the roofline, two dormers. It is a confident building in a modest idiom, exactly the kind of public architecture France exported to its colonies in the 1920s, adapted to the heat and rain of Cayenne.

## Bureaux and Salle

Inside, the arrangement is as straightforward as the outside. The mayor's office - the Bureaux de Maire - occupies the ground floor. The council chamber, the Salle du Conseil, sits upstairs. This is where Cayenne's elected representatives still debate the affairs of a city of 65,000 people, the capital of France's only South American department. Since 2002 the building has carried a listing on the Inventaire général des monuments maintained by the French Ministry of Culture - not a historic-monument classification in the strict sense, but official recognition that this unassuming brick building is part of the architectural heritage France cares enough about to document.

## Flight Context

Located at 4.938°N, 52.335°W, on Rue de Rémire in central Cayenne, four blocks from the waterfront. The city is identifiable from altitude by the fort-capped hill of Mount Cépérou and the dark estuary of the Mahury River to the east. Cayenne – Félix Eboué International Airport (SOCA / CAY) lies 17 km southeast. Low-level approaches into Cayenne pass the city at roughly 1,500 ft; the city hall is in the grid of 18th-century streets just south of the fort.
