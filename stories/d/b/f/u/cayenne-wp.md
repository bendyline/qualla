# Cayenne

The prefecture of French Guiana and largest French-speaking city in South America, founded by settlers in 1643 on a former island at the mouth of the Cayenne River - a city whose history runs through four empires and a notorious penal colony.

**Location:** 4.9386, -52.3350

**Categories:** capital cities, French Guiana, colonial history, penal colonies

## Introduction

Cayenne's municipal motto is fert aurum industria - work brings wealth. It was adopted by a city whose history could fairly be read as a skeptical reply. Founded by French settlers in 1643 on a former island at the mouth of the Cayenne River, the town was burned, abandoned, captured, recaptured, used as a slave-economy port, used as a penal colony for most of a century, and finally transformed - by immigration, birth rates, and the steady growth of French overseas administration - into a metropolitan area of 153,884 people. It is the prefecture of French Guiana, the largest Francophone city of the South American continent, and it stands today on a coast where the first French settlers tried to hold a town in 1604 and failed.

## Four Empires on One Island

The Spanish explorers who reached the area in the 16th century found it too hot and moved on. The French tried to settle in 1604; the Portuguese destroyed that outpost in the name of the Treaty of Tordesillas. The French returned in 1643, founded Cayenne - and were forced out again by the local indigenous population, whose territory this had been for thousands of years. In 1664, on the third serious attempt, France established what would become a permanent settlement. Even then the town changed hands: over the following decade it shifted among French, Dutch, and English control before returning to France. In 1809 an Anglo-Portuguese force captured Cayenne during the Napoleonic Wars, and the city was administered from Brazil until 1817, when it was returned to French control. Through every transfer, the city kept growing around the mouth of the Cayenne River, and its colonial grid slowly filled with the Caribbean-style wooden houses that still define downtown today.

## Penal Colony

From the 1850s until new transports ended in 1938, France used Cayenne - and the infamous offshore Îles du Salut, Devil's Island among them - as a penal colony. Tens of thousands of prisoners were shipped from metropolitan France, condemned to labor in a climate that killed the unprepared. Many never returned home; even those freed at the end of their sentences were required to stay in the colony for a period equal to their sentence - or for life, if they had been sentenced to eight years or more - a rule that left thousands stranded in Cayenne and Saint-Laurent-du-Maroni. The prisoners who suffered under this system included political exiles as well as common convicts; the journalist Albert Londres's 1923 reporting from the colony helped create the public pressure that eventually closed it. Papillon, Henri Charrière's 1969 book, presented as memoir though much of it is contested and later adapted to film, carried the penal colony's memory into global popular culture. The system shaped Cayenne for nearly a century, and its physical traces - forts, walls, administrative buildings - remain part of the downtown today.

## Geography of a Former Island

Cayenne stands on the estuary of the Cayenne River where it meets the Atlantic. The city occupies part of what used to be a true island; subsequent sedimentation has altered some of the boundaries. By road it is 268 kilometers from Saint-Laurent-du-Maroni on the Surinamese border, and 64 kilometers from Kourou, the rocket-launch town that gives French Guiana its place in the space age. In strict metric terms: Paris is 7,100 kilometers away; Fort-de-France in Martinique, 1,500 kilometers; Paramaribo, 342 kilometers to the northwest; Macapá in Brazil's Amapá state, 554 kilometers to the southeast. The climate, which the commune's roughly 63,000 residents and the 153,884 of the 2023 metro census have both learned to live with, is tropical monsoon, bordering on equatorial - heavy rain most of the year, temperatures clustered around 27 degrees Celsius with almost no seasonal variation.

## A City Built on Immigration

Cayenne's metropolitan population has grown dramatically over the decades, driven by two forces: a high birth rate and significant immigration, primarily from the West Indies and Brazil. The result is one of the most ethnically plural cities in South America. French Creole, Brazilian Portuguese, Haitian Kreyol, Hmong, Surinamese, Lebanese, Chinese, and Javanese communities all contribute to the city's character. This diversity shows up in food markets, neighborhoods, religious festivals, and the municipal politics of a commune that sits inside a French overseas department with full European Union membership and the euro as its currency. Cayenne is both a French city and a South American city, and neither label feels quite adequate to describe the place.

## Governing Cayenne

Cayenne is a commune of the French Republic, administered by a mayor and municipal council. Sandra Trochimara became mayor in October 2020 after her predecessor, Marie-Laure Phinéra-Horth, won the French Senate race the month before - French law prevents senators from simultaneously holding mayoral office - and her list won the March 2026 municipal elections. Phinéra-Horth had been mayor since 2010 and is herself the daughter of Stéphan Phinéra-Horth, who presided over the General Council of French Guiana from 1994 to 1998. The commune of Cayenne is too small to cover the entire urban area, so an intercommunal authority - the communauté d'agglomération du Centre Littoral - groups Cayenne with five suburban communes. It handles refuse collection, water, sewage, urban planning, and public transport for the metro area. Until 2015 Cayenne was divided into six cantons; those administrative subdivisions were abolished when the French Guiana department and region were dissolved and replaced by a single Territorial Collectivity.

## Flying Into the Prefecture

From the air, Cayenne is a tight urban cluster on a dark green headland, flanked by mangrove swamps and the brown outflow of the Cayenne River. To the north and east, the Atlantic stretches murky and vast. To the west, rainforest unrolls almost unbroken toward Suriname. Kourou's rocket pads lie just over the horizon, and far beyond, the Brazilian Amazon. A French city, in a climate no French city in Europe ever knew - and the largest French-speaking place on the South American continent.

## Flight Context

Located at 4.94°N, 52.34°W. Cayenne-Félix Eboué International (SOCA) is the main airport, about 13 km south-southwest of downtown. Saint-Laurent-du-Maroni (SOOM) is about 200 km west-northwest at the Surinamese border; Kourou (SOOK) is about 47 km northwest near the European spaceport. Recommended viewing altitude: 2,000-6,000 feet AGL to appreciate the city, the estuary geometry, and the surrounding forest-mangrove interface. Expect tropical rain and convective buildups year-round; ceilings often below 5,000 feet.
