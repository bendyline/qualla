# Roura

A Jesuit-founded commune in French Guiana where a Lafayette-backed emancipation experiment once failed, which Portuguese troops occupied for eight years, and where a village founded by Hmong refugees from Laos runs a Sunday market.

**Location:** 4.7283, -52.3242

**Categories:** french-guiana, communes, hmong-diaspora, colonial-history

## Introduction

On Sundays the market at Cacao, a Hmong village in the rainforest southwest of Cayenne, draws visitors from across the department for its food stalls and Hmong embroidery. Cacao is a small village. It is also part of Roura, a sprawling commune about four and a half degrees north of the equator in French Guiana, a department of France on the Atlantic edge of South America. The arc of Roura's history is the arc of the Atlantic itself: Jesuits, an emancipation experiment backed by Lafayette, a Portuguese occupation, the end of slavery, and a group of refugees from Laos who arrived in 1977 to farm land where living and working conditions resembled home.

## A Sprawling Commune

Roura was founded in 1675 by Jesuits. The commune itself is enormous by metropolitan French standards, occupying a broad coastal-to-inland wedge of French Guiana that borders Matoury and Montsinéry-Tonnegrande to the north, Kourou and Saint-Elie to the west and northwest, and Régina to the south and east. Its territory runs from the low-lying, mangrove-studded coastal strip — where most of the department's population lives — into the Guiana Shield's deeper equatorial rainforest. Kaw Mountain rises in the broader Roura region, a forested escarpment that gives the commune its most characteristic aerial profile: green water on one side, green hills on the other, and the thin red thread of the Route nationale 2 and the RD6 connecting everything.

## Lafayette's Failed Experiment

In the mid-1780s, the Marquis de Lafayette, a few years after returning from the American Revolutionary War, attempted something unusual on the savanna Gabriel near Roura. On the estate he bought there, with nearly seventy enslaved Africans, he forbade their sale, had them paid for their work and schooled, and planned their gradual emancipation. The experiment was real; it was also doomed by the colonial economy around it, which depended on forced labor, and by Lafayette's own deteriorating position in France. When he was imprisoned in 1792 his property was confiscated and the people on the estate were sold back into slavery. Slavery in French Guiana, abolished in 1794 and restored by Napoleon in 1802, would not end for good until 1848. The episode is one of the earliest attempts in any French colony to imagine a way out of the plantation system — made by a man who was better at fighting for other people's freedom than for the freedom of the people he himself held in bondage.

## When Roura Was Brazil

Between 1809 and 1817, an eight-year interlude, Roura was captured by Portuguese troops during the upheavals of the Napoleonic Wars and administered as part of the Portuguese Empire — after 1815, the United Kingdom of Portugal, Brazil and the Algarves. The peace settlements of 1814-1815 ordered the colony's return, and in 1817 French Guiana reverted to Paris. The episode is a reminder that these coasts were never stable property. Cayenne and its hinterlands had already passed between French, Dutch and English hands in the 17th century, and for eight years in the early 19th they were governed in Portuguese.

## The Hmong Villagers of Cacao

In 1977, the French government resettled a group of Hmong refugees from Laos in a cleared patch of forest in the Roura commune. They called it Cacao. Many Hmong had fought alongside French forces in the First Indochina War and with American forces in Laos's 'Secret War', and many fled persecution after the Pathet Lao took power in 1975. The choice of French Guiana was deliberate: living and working conditions were similar to those the Hmong had known in Laos. The experiment worked. Cacao today is a thriving agricultural village, supplying a significant share of the vegetables consumed in Cayenne. The weekend market draws visitors from across the department. The Hmong language is still spoken.

## Marshland and Mountain

Two protected areas anchor the commune's deep interior. The Kaw-Roura Marshland Nature Reserve, established in 1998, covers 94,700 hectares of coastal wetland that spill across Roura and Régina — a habitat for scarlet ibis, black caiman, and a dense concentration of the region's waterbirds and reptiles. The Trésor Regional Nature Reserve, a smaller 2,464-hectare reserve on the flank of Kaw Mountain, received regional nature reserve status in 2010 and has a public botanical trail. Between these two reserves and the Palikur Amerindian community at Favard on the Oyack River, Roura holds together as a patchwork of older and newer ways of living: Jesuit-founded town, Hmong market village, Palikur river community, and conservation district — all inside one commune of an overseas department of France.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 4.728°N, 52.324°W, in central coastal French Guiana, about 23 km south of Cayenne. Recommended viewing altitude 2,000–3,500 feet to see the Comté River, the town of Roura, and the forested shoulders of Kaw Mountain rising to the southeast. The Kaw-Roura Marshland spreads east of the town. Nearest airport is Cayenne–Félix Eboué (SOCA) about 11 km north-northwest. Equatorial climate year-round; the drier season runs August–November.
