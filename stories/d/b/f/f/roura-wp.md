# Roura

A Jesuit-founded commune in French Guiana where a Lafayette-backed emancipation experiment once failed, where Portuguese troops raised a Brazilian flag, and where a Laotian Hmong village supplies Cayenne with its weekend pho.

**Location:** 4.7283, -52.3242

**Categories:** french-guiana, communes, hmong-diaspora, colonial-history

## Introduction

On Sundays at the Cacao market, French Guianese families from Cayenne line up for pho, steamed buns, and Hmong embroidery under the roof of an open-air pavilion in a rainforest village fifty kilometers south of nowhere familiar. The smell is unmistakable — star anise, lemongrass, rice vermicelli — and the accents ricocheting around the stalls are equal parts Hmong, French Creole, Portuguese, and metropolitan French. Cacao is a small village. It is also a commune-within-a-commune of Roura, which is itself a sprawling commune of French Guiana, a department of France located three degrees north of the equator on the Atlantic edge of South America. The arc of Roura's history is the arc of the Atlantic itself: Jesuits, an emancipation experiment backed by Lafayette, a Portuguese occupation, the end of slavery, and a group of refugees from Laos who arrived in 1977 because the rainforest reminded them of home.

## A Commune of Seven Borders

Roura was founded in 1675 by Jesuits — one of the order's many Latin American experiments in combining missionary work with cattle and smallholder agriculture. The commune itself is enormous by metropolitan French standards, occupying a broad coastal-to-inland wedge of French Guiana that borders Matoury and Montsinéry-Tonnegrande to the north, Kourou and Saint-Elie to the west and northwest, and Régina to the south and east. Its territory runs from the low-lying, mangrove-studded coastal strip — where most of the department's population lives — into the Guiana Shield's deeper equatorial rainforest. Kaw Mountain rises in the broader Roura region, a forested escarpment that gives the commune its most characteristic aerial profile: green water on one side, green hills on the other, and the thin red thread of the Route nationale 2 and the RD6 connecting everything.

## Lafayette's Failed Experiment

In 1786, the Marquis de Lafayette — three years after returning from the American Revolutionary War — attempted something unusual on the savanna Gabriel near Roura. He proposed to give the enslaved Africans on his colonial holdings the chance to work small plots of their own, a step toward emancipation through economic self-sufficiency. The experiment was real; it was also doomed by the colonial economy around it, which depended on forced labor, and by Lafayette's own deteriorating position in France. The scheme was abandoned in 1796. Slavery in French Guiana would not end for another half century, until the decree of abolition reached the colony in 1848. A brief historical note that reads as a single sentence in the records is, in fact, one of the earliest attempts in any French colony to imagine a way out of the plantation system — made by a man who was better at fighting for other people's freedom than for the freedom of the people he himself held in bondage.

## When Roura Was Brazil

Between 1809 and 1817, an eight-year interlude, Roura was captured by Portuguese troops during the upheavals of the Napoleonic Wars and administered as part of the Portuguese Empire — and briefly, after 1815, as part of the Kingdom of Brazil. The occupation ended with the Treaty of Vienna and French Guiana reverted to Paris. The episode is a reminder that these coasts were never stable property. Cayenne and its hinterlands passed between French, Dutch, English, and Portuguese control multiple times in the 17th and 18th centuries, and for a few years in the early 19th the postcards would have been in Portuguese.

## The Hmong Villagers of Cacao

In 1977, the French government resettled a group of Hmong refugees from Laos in a cleared patch of forest in the Roura commune. They called it Cacao. The Hmong had fought alongside American and French forces during the Vietnam War and its Laotian continuation, and many fled persecution after the Pathet Lao took power in 1975. The choice of French Guiana was deliberate: the climate and terrain were similar to the mountain agriculture the Hmong had practiced in Laos, and the French state needed people willing to do exactly that kind of smallholder farming on cleared rainforest land. The experiment worked. Cacao today is a thriving agricultural village, supplying a significant share of the vegetables consumed in Cayenne. The weekend market draws visitors from across the department. The Hmong language is still spoken. A small museum in the village documents the migration.

## Marshland and Mountain

Two protected areas anchor the commune's deep interior. The Kaw-Roura Marshland Nature Reserve, established in 1998, covers 94,700 hectares of coastal wetland that spill across Roura and Régina — a habitat for scarlet ibis, black caiman, and a dense concentration of the region's waterbirds and reptiles. The Trésor Regional Nature Reserve, a smaller 2,464-hectare reserve on the flank of Kaw Mountain, received formal protection in 2010 and contains a research station and public botanical trails. Between these two reserves and the Palikur Amerindian community at Favard on the Oyack River, Roura holds together as a patchwork of older and newer ways of living: Jesuit-founded town, Hmong market village, Palikur river community, and conservation district — all inside one commune that is, on paper, a department of France.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 4.728°N, 52.324°W, in central coastal French Guiana, about 30 km south of Cayenne. Recommended viewing altitude 2,000–3,500 feet to see the Comté River, the town of Roura, and the forested shoulders of Kaw Mountain rising to the southeast. The Kaw-Roura Marshland spreads east of the commune. Nearest airport is Cayenne–Félix Eboué (SOCA) about 25 km north. Equatorial climate year-round; best light November–March.
