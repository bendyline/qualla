# Revolt of Saint-Joseph

In October 1894, French authorities on a tropical prison island encouraged a plot that historians say was a trap to justify killing the anarchists they had exiled — and when the trap failed, guards and soldiers killed them anyway.

**Location:** 5.2813, -52.5831

**Categories:** history, anarchism, penal-colonies, massacre, french-guiana

## Introduction

On the morning of 22 October 1894, a French soldier found an anarchist named Charles Simon hiding in a tree on Saint-Joseph Island in the Salvation Islands off French Guiana. The soldier asked whether Simon preferred to be shot in the head or somewhere less dignified. Simon answered, "Long live anarchy," and was executed where he stood. Later that day, Léon Léauthier and two companions were cornered by the same hunting party near the coast. Unarmed, they embraced, tore open their shirts, and waited. They shouted the same words as they were killed. At least twelve convicts would die in the massacre that week, and the French press would report it as a spontaneous riot — a version that historians now believe hid a premeditated killing.

## How They Got to the Island

The men killed that week had come to French Guiana because Paris had decided they were too dangerous to live in France. After the Paris Commune of 1871, French authorities had intensified their repression of anarchists — a repression that hardened after the Clichy affair and the 1891 Fourmies massacre, in which soldiers opened fire on striking workers. Some anarchists answered with the Ère des attentats, 1892 to 1894, a wave of bombings and assassinations. The French state responded with the lois scélérates of 1893 and 1894, laws written expressly to crush the movement. Hundreds of anarchists were tried and sentenced. A number of the most notorious — including Charles Simon, a friend of the bomber Ravachol, and Léon Léauthier, who had stabbed a diplomat in a restaurant — were sent to the bagne at the Salvation Islands. There they were housed in huts on Saint-Joseph, the southernmost island of the three, watched by guards who considered French justice to have been too soft on men the guards wished had gone to the guillotine.

## The Trap That Backfired

The escalation began when a guard named Mosca shot and killed an imprisoned anarchist, Jean-Baptiste Briens, during a roll call in September 1894. The act was cold, and the anarchists hated him for it. A shadowy convict named Eugène Allmeyer — notorious for his escapes but privately useful to the authorities — began nudging the anarchists toward a plot: kill Mosca, escape, disappear into the jungle. What the anarchists did not know at first was that another convict in the circle, a man called Plista, was feeding every detail to the administration. The chief overseer of Royale Island, Courtois, spoke openly on 20 October of the "organized massacre" planned for the next day. Troops were already in position to intervene. But the anarchists had seen Plista for what he was. They pulled out of the plan. Some had even requested transfers three weeks earlier, sensing the trap; their requests were denied. On 21 October, the uprising the authorities wanted simply did not happen.

## Twenty-Four Hours of Killing

Mosca and another guard, Crétaillaz, had spent most of 21 October waiting for a revolt that never began. Frustrated, they took two convict underlings with them to one of the anarchist huts and murdered the first two men they found inside. That was the spark. About fifteen prisoners — led by Simon and a man named Marpeaux — seized improvised iron spikes and attacked. Both guards died. The underlings were killed or wounded. The remaining guards barricaded themselves in their quarters and waited for the army, which arrived by 10 P.M. The camp governor, Billet, ordered a retaliation. Because military doctors had arrived with the troops and might witness it, the massacre inside the huts did not happen. Instead, at daybreak on 22 October, a manhunt crossed the island. Simon was executed in his tree. Léauthier and his companions were executed on the rocks. Two men who had hidden in a cave were smoked out and shot while fleeing. By the time it was over, at least twelve convicts were dead, including a man named Pigache, shot a few days later.

## The Cover Story and What Survived It

The French press, led by Le Petit Journal, ran the authorities' version: 800 anarchists had spontaneously attacked their guards and been put down. Every number in that sentence was wrong. About fifteen men had fought back after two of their comrades were murdered in their hut. The overwhelming majority of the roughly 800 convicts on the island had sheltered in place. Four guard deaths were reported; modern research suggests only the two guards died, and their two convict accomplices were wounded. Historians such as Hélène Millot treat the Revolt of Saint-Joseph as the closing event of the Ère des attentats — the state's last answer to these anarchists was not prosecution but summary execution, carried out in a place where no one was expected to see. The names of the men the record preserves — Simon, Léauthier, Thiervoz, Meyrueis, Chevenet, Boésie, Garnier, Marpeaux, Lebeault, Maservin, Pigache, and Briens, shot weeks before it all began — belong in the same sentence as the lie that was told about them.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 5.281°N, 52.583°W, on Saint-Joseph Island — the southernmost of three small islands of volcanic origin (Devil's, Royale, Saint-Joseph, from north to south) that make up the Îles du Salut, roughly 14 km north of Kourou, French Guiana.
