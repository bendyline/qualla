# Penal colony

For four centuries, empires solved their prison problem by sending the people they refused to execute to the ends of the earth — a policy that built nations, destroyed tens of thousands of lives, and still echoes in the geography of modern detention.

**Location:** 5.2936, -52.5831

**Categories:** history, penal-colonies, empire, human-rights

## Introduction

The idea is simple and cold: take the people society will not kill but refuses to keep, and put them somewhere the ocean does the guarding. Call it exile, call it transportation, call it penal settlement — the bookkeeping changes but the logic doesn't. Between the early 1700s and the middle of the 20th century, empires moved hundreds of thousands of men and women across entire oceans under sentence. Some of those places became nations. Most of them became mass graves with a postal address. A few are open still, under new names, under new flags, and everywhere you find one of these sites today you find the same stubborn question underneath the paperwork: what does a state owe to people it has given up on?

## The British Solution

It began with the Transportation Act of 1717. For the next six decades, British merchants auctioned convicts to Chesapeake planters the way they auctioned indentured servants, which on the ground was often the same thing. Some 50,000 people made that crossing before the American Revolution slammed the door shut in 1776. Then the prisons in London filled past capacity. In 1786, William Pitt's government decided the answer lay in the Pacific, and on 13 May 1787 the First Fleet sailed from Portsmouth carrying roughly 800 convicts and 250 marines toward Botany Bay. Between 1788 and 1868, about 162,000 convicts were transported from Britain and Ireland to Australia. Among them were 3,600 political prisoners — Tolpuddle Martyrs, Luddite weavers, Irish nationalists from the Society of United Irishmen and Young Ireland. They hoed colonial soil and built colonial roads. Much of modern Australia's infrastructure rests on their labor.

## The French Answer

On 27 March 1852, Louis-Napoléon Bonaparte, months from becoming Emperor Napoleon III, signed off on a different version of the same idea. France would send its condemned to the tropics. Louisiana had already received French criminals a century earlier. The real apparatus began in 1852, when the first prisoners left Brest for the Salvation Islands off French Guiana. The system would eventually ingest some 80,000 men between 1852 and 1939. Devil's Island, the smallest and northernmost speck in the archipelago, earned its nickname honestly: by some accounts the mortality rate at its worst reached 75 percent, and the bagne became known as the Dry Guillotine — slower than the blade but no less final. New Caledonia and its Isle of Pines received Communards from the 1871 revolt and Kabyle rebels from Algeria between the 1860s and 1897. The empire called this colonization. The men dying of fever in the huts called it what it was.

## The Americas and the World

Every empire that could afford a boat built one of these places. Spain stranded some 9,000 or more French prisoners of war, most of them taken at the Battle of Bailén in 1808, on the tiny island of Cabrera from 1809 and left them to starve; fewer than half survived. Portugal, under Salazar, ran Tarrafal in Cape Verde, where anti-fascists died from 1936 onward. Imperial Russia used Siberia and, from 1857, Sakhalin; the Soviet Gulag inherited and industrialized that machinery. Ecuador exiled people to the Galápagos. Argentina sent them to Ushuaia, on the Beagle Channel, from 1902 to 1947. Chile used Punta Arenas. Japan's Meiji government opened Abashiri Prison in Hokkaido in 1890. Apartheid South Africa used Robben Island to cage the men who would one day govern the country — Nelson Mandela's cell is now a pilgrimage site. Vietnam's Côn Đảo Island was French from 1861 and then held prisoners of the Republic of Vietnam through the Vietnam War. Taiwan had Green Island under Chiang Kai-shek's White Terror. North Korea operates labor camps today. Guantánamo Bay, in this taxonomy, is the United States' own entry on the list.

## Who Was Sent, and Why It Matters

The statistics flatten people who were not flat. The convicts on the First Fleet had names and ages and children they would never see again. The Communards in New Caledonia were schoolteachers, typesetters, women who had defended barricades. The bagnards of French Guiana included forgers and thieves but also Alfred Dreyfus, framed on forged evidence and held on Devil's Island itself, and Henri Charrière, who escaped and wrote Papillon. Robben Island held Mandela, Sobukwe, and generations of activists the state called criminals. Every penal colony was built on a legal fiction — that the people inside had forfeited their humanity along with their freedom. The places endure as warnings precisely because the fiction never quite held. People kept writing letters home. People kept surviving. People kept being people, even when the system was designed, very deliberately, to stop them.

## Flight Context

This entry ranges globally, but its coordinates 5.29°N, 52.58°W point to the Salvation Islands off French Guiana — the iconic French bagne. Recommended viewing altitude 3,000–5,000 feet to see Royale, Saint-Joseph, and Devil's Island as a small arc in the Atlantic swell, about 15 km off the coast near Kourou. The nearest major airport is Cayenne–Félix Eboué (SOCA / CAY), about 60 km to the southeast; the Guiana Space Centre sits on the coast at Kourou. Tropical climate year-round; the rains run from about December to July, broken by a short dry spell in March, and August to November is drier.
