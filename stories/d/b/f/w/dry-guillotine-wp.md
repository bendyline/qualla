# Dry Guillotine

Prisoner slang for the French penal colony at Cayenne became the title of a 1938 memoir that helped shame an empire into closing it.

**Location:** 5.2939, -52.5833

**Categories:** Prison writings, Memoirs, French Guiana, 20th century history

## Introduction

*La guillotine sèche*. The dry guillotine. It was the phrase the bagnards used among themselves, in the camps and on the work details, for the place they had been sent to die. The blade of the real guillotine, in Paris, fell in an instant. The dry one took years. It killed by yellow fever, by malaria, by the heat and the water and the food, by the punishment cells and by the stones the men carried in the sun. Prisoner number 46,635 - a small-time thief from Paris named René Belbenoît - would take that name, carry it out of the jungle wrapped in oilskin, and set it down on a bookshelf in New York, where the world would finally read what happened.

## The Phrase Before the Book

The term was already in circulation before Belbenoît wrote his memoir. The American explorer and geographer Charles W. Furlong, a Fellow of the Royal Geographical Society, used it in 1913 as the title of a 14-page article for *Harper's Magazine*: "Cayenne - the Dry Guillotine." Furlong had gone to French Guiana himself. His article detailed the cruel and often intentionally lethal conditions the *bagnards* endured, and he listed young prisoners by name - small-time offenders sentenced to what amounted to execution by time. It reached American readers, but it reached them in a magazine; it did not shake anything loose. What was needed was a voice from inside the wire.

## A Child of the Prison System

René Belbenoît was born in Paris in 1899. Separated from his parents as a child, he was raised by his grandparents and came back to Paris at twelve, after they died. The thefts that sent him to the bagne were small - money and pearls taken from employers and businesses, nothing violent, the kind of crimes that in another life would have earned a short sentence and a chance to start again. Instead, in 1922, he was sentenced to eight years of hard labour and sent to the prison of Saint-Laurent-du-Maroni in French Guiana, and from there through the camp system that Parisians called by the name of its most famous island. He started writing.

## The Manuscript in Oilskin

Belbenoît wrote *Dry Guillotine* in pieces, on whatever paper he could get, and wrapped the growing manuscript in oilskin to protect it from tropical storms and the soakings that came with every river crossing. While he was imprisoned in Cayenne, the American travel writer Blair Niles met him; she bought several of his pieces to use in her own 1928 book, *Condemned to Devil's Island*, and the money went toward another escape attempt. The manuscript itself made it out with him. After multiple escapes and recaptures, a last flight by sea to Trinidad and a long journey through Colombia, Panama and El Salvador, Belbenoît finally reached the United States in 1937, carrying papers he had protected, and rewritten where the water ruined them, across oceans and rivers. He was in New York when E. P. Dutton published *Dry Guillotine: Fifteen Years Among the Living Dead* in 1938.

## The Book That Changed Public Opinion

It sold. Fourteen additional printings followed in the first year alone; Blue Ribbon Books reprinted it in 1940. *Dry Guillotine* was not the first exposé of the French penal colonies, but it was the one that finally caught in the throat of a reading public increasingly used to thinking of European empires as civilized enterprises. Belbenoît wrote in plain, unsparing prose about men he had known by name - men who starved, men who were driven mad in solitary, men who threw themselves into the sea. The French government was already moving: the Popular Front had decided in 1936 to stop the convoys, and a decree of June 1938, the year the book appeared, ended transportation to the *bagne de Cayenne*, leaving the camps to wind down. World War II intervened and delayed the shutdown, but between 1946 and 1953 the prisons were closed one after another. Belbenoît, deported from the United States in 1940 and jailed for slipping back in, eventually settled in California, became an American citizen in 1956, and died there in 1959.

## After the Book

Belbenoît's was not the only memoir of the bagne. Henri Charrière's *Papillon* came thirty-one years later and became the more famous of the two - a wilder, more cinematic escape story that researchers now believe drew much of its material from other prisoners rather than from Charrière himself, Belbenoît possibly among them. The facts of the penal colony were dramatic enough that invention was hardly necessary, but invention sold more books. What remains of *Dry Guillotine* is something the film adaptations never quite captured: the voice of a man writing not for Hollywood but for the men he had left behind in the cells. He had escaped, and they had not, and his book was the ladder he could lower back to them. He could not pull them out, but he could make sure that when history looked back at the jungle camps of Cayenne, it would not be able to say it had not been told.

## Flight Context

The penal colony this book describes spread across the Salvation Islands and mainland camps of French Guiana at roughly 5.29°N, 52.58°W. The Cayenne region is served by Félix Eboué Airport (SOCA / CAY). From the air, the three small green islands off Kourou and the Maroni River prison at Saint-Laurent-du-Maroni (about 105 km to the west) mark the furthest edges of the camp system Belbenoît survived.
