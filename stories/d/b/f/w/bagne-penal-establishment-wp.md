# Bagne (Penal Establishment)

The French penal system of forced-labor camps - from royal galley convicts in Mediterranean ports to the brutal Guiana colonies of Papillon and Devil's Island - with transportation abolished only in 1938 and the last convicts brought home in 1953.

**Location:** 5.2831, -52.5831

**Categories:** France, French Guiana, Penal colonies, Legal history, Historical sites

## Introduction

The word comes from a Livorno bathhouse. Italian bagno meant 'bath,' and a prison built on the site of an old Roman therma in that Tuscan port took the name bagno - the bath. The French borrowed the word as bagne, and it came to describe something specific and terrible: a system of forced-labor prisons that France ran for nearly five centuries, first chained to oars on royal galleys, then housed in Mediterranean naval arsenals, and finally shipped overseas, above all across the Atlantic to penal colonies in French Guiana where the heat, the fevers, and the jungle killed a huge share of the men who arrived. The last convicts came home in 1953. The scars of the system are still visible on the towns that housed them and in the descendants of those who survived.

## Chained to the Oars

The French monarchy began using condemned men as galley rowers in the 15th century under Jacques Cœur. Louis XIV, urged on by his minister Colbert, made the practice systematic, especially after 1685 when the Edict of Fontainebleau revoked religious tolerance and Protestant men could be condemned to the galleys for refusing to convert. The arsenal at Marseille housed the galley convicts - chained to benches, several men to an oar, sleeping where they rowed. Louis XV finally abolished the galley corps in 1748, and the men were moved into shore prisons at Toulon, Brest, and Rochefort to work the dockyards. These were the harbor bagnes. By the end of the 18th century Toulon alone held around 3,000 men. They worked the Arsenal's earthworks and construction, the toughest chained two by two, branded under the Ancien Régime with red-hot iron, fed mostly on dried beans and vegetables.

## Transportation Across the Atlantic

In the 1840s French politicians argued increasingly that criminals should be removed from metropolitan France entirely. Napoleon III signed the law of May 30, 1854, ordering hard labor sentences to be served in colonial penal establishments. The first convicts shipped to Cayenne, French Guiana, in 1852, and to New Caledonia in 1864. The law created something called 'doubling': a man sentenced to less than eight years of hard labor had to remain in the colony for an additional term equal to his original sentence; a man sentenced to eight years or more had to stay for life. The official purpose was to develop the colonies. The practical result was that very few convicts ever returned to France. Transport ships like the Loire and later the Martinière carried them west; the administration kept first-time and repeat offenders apart, and the convicts' slang for the two groups - 'those below' and 'those above' - is thought to come from where each was held in the ship's holds.

## The French Guiana System

The Guiana penal establishment was not a single prison but a scattered archipelago of camps. The main administration ran from Saint-Laurent-du-Maroni on the Suriname border and from Cayenne itself. Men worked logging camps deep in the jungle, cleared land that refused to stay cleared, and built roads that the vegetation swallowed within a season. The Îles du Salut - Royale, Saint Joseph, and the small Île du Diable (Devil's Island) - held the highest-security prisoners. Alfred Dreyfus, convicted of treason in 1894 and later proven innocent, spent four years alone on Devil's Island. Henri Charrière, who claimed to have escaped repeatedly, published Papillon in 1969 - presented as memoir, though journalists who checked the prison records found much of it borrowed from other convicts or invented - and made the system globally infamous. The reality was simpler and worse than the adventure story: of the roughly 70,000 convicts sent to French Guiana between 1852 and 1938, huge numbers died of yellow fever, malaria, dysentery, exhaustion, or violence. The mortality rate was catastrophic, and critics called the bagne a 'dry guillotine'.

## Children's Prisons

From 1836 onward, France operated separate prisons and penal colonies for juvenile offenders. Petite-Roquette in Paris opened for children that year. In 1840, the first private agricultural penal colony for minors opened at Mettray. Dozens more followed across the French countryside. On the Île du Levant in the Mediterranean, the Sainte-Anne agricultural colony, open since 1861, received 65 children transferred from the Ajaccio juvenile prison on September 28, 1866. Four days later, on October 2, the children revolted. More than a dozen children died before army and gendarmerie detachments restored order on October 4. A memorial at the site today lists the names of the children buried there. On Réunion Island, the Îlet à Guillaume penal colony, run by a missionary order from 1864, confined up to 4,000 children before its closure was finally enforced in 1879. The 1934 revolt at Belle-Île-en-Mer, in which dozens of child inmates escaped and islanders and holidaymakers hunted them down for a reward on each child returned, inspired Jacques Prévert's poem La chasse à l'enfant - The Child Hunt - and helped force the 1945 reform that abolished juvenile penal colonies entirely.

## Abolition

The end came slowly. Gaston Monnerville, a French Guianese politician serving as undersecretary for the Colonies, pushed the decree-law of June 17, 1938, signed by President Albert Lebrun, which abolished transportation to the colonies. But men already in the penal colony system remained there - the war intervened, shipping stopped, and the Vichy government had other priorities. Postwar France finally evacuated the last camps. On August 1, 1953, the last convicts and their supervisors returned to France. The abandoned camps in Guiana, New Caledonia, and Madagascar are now historic sites, some reclaimed by the forest and some preserved as museums. The Toulon penal colony's last buildings, which had survived into the 20th century, were largely destroyed in 1944. The bagne, as a living institution, is gone. Its echo survives in French cinema, novels, and the peculiar vocabulary of a system that treated human beings as expendable labor for nearly five hundred years.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 5.28°N, 52.58°W (Îles du Salut, off Kourou). The French Guiana penal system's main surviving site is the Camp de la Transportation at Saint-Laurent-du-Maroni on the Maroni River, opposite Albina in Suriname. The Îles du Salut - including Devil's Island - lie about 14 km off Kourou, now the site of the Guiana Space Centre. Cayenne-Félix Eboué Airport (SOCA) serves the region. From altitude, the main visual markers are the Maroni River border with Suriname, the Atlantic coastline, dense rainforest inland, and the distinctive cluster of Îles du Salut visible as small islands off the coast near Kourou. Many camp sites deep in the interior are now reclaimed by jungle and difficult to locate even at low altitude.
