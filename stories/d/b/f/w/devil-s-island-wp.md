# Devil's Island

For 100 years, France shipped men it wanted forgotten to this speck of jungle-green rock off the South American coast - and for most of them, forgetting was the whole point.

**Location:** 5.2939, -52.5833

**Categories:** Former penal colonies, French Guiana, History, Islands

## Introduction

At its worst, three of every four men sent to the penal colony of Cayenne died there. They were tailors, tradesmen, burglars, political agitators, soldiers who had made the wrong enemy - nearly 80,000 convicts over a century, most of whom would never leave. The French called it the *bagne*. The prisoners, in the bitter shorthand of the condemned, called it *la guillotine sèche* - the dry guillotine. The blade was slow, made of fever and bad water and the sun, but it cut just as deep.

## An Archipelago of Punishment

Devil's Island itself - Île du Diable - is the smallest and most isolated of three small islands lying about fifteen kilometers off the coast of Kourou. The prison system that took its popular name stretched far beyond this one rocky outcrop. Île Royale, the largest, served as the reception point where general-population convicts lived in a kind of sunburnt semi-freedom, walled in by sharks and swift currents rather than stone. Saint-Joseph held the Reclusion, a silent hell of stone cells where men were sentenced to solitary confinement in darkness for attempted escape or infractions inside the colony. Devil's Island proper was reserved for political prisoners, and never held more than about a dozen men at a time. Three larger facilities sat on the mainland - at Kourou, at Cayenne, and at Saint-Laurent-du-Maroni, 160 kilometers to the west. Together they received convicts from every corner of the Second French Empire.

## Why Here

The choice of French Guiana was not an accident of geography but a confession of failure. Since 1604 France had tried and failed to colonize this coast. In one disastrous attempt in 1763, some nine thousand of twelve thousand colonists died in their first year, mostly from tropical disease. By the 1850s the survivors were down to a handful. When Napoleon III closed the old prison hulks rotting in French harbors after his 1851 coup, he needed somewhere to send the men inside them. Algeria was ruled out. Haiti, Cuba, Texas - all discussed, all rejected. French Guiana was the place that had already killed settlers by the thousand. Three thousand volunteer convicts answered the first call. Many were doublage prisoners, condemned by law to work an additional term as free laborers equal to their sentence after its completion. In practice, doublage meant most of them would never leave.

## The Famous and the Forgotten

The world remembers Devil's Island mostly through one man's disgrace. In 1895 Captain Alfred Dreyfus, a Jewish artillery officer framed for treason, was shipped to the tiny island and kept alone in a stone hut for nearly five years while Émile Zola's *J'accuse* slowly turned French public opinion. But Dreyfus was an exception both in his innocence and his eventual release. Most who arrived were ordinary thieves and forgers. Prisoner number 46,635, René Belbenoît, had stolen small amounts from employers. He wrote his memoir in the camps, carrying the manuscript wrapped in oilskin through storms and river crossings until he reached California in 1937. His book *Dry Guillotine* became an international bestseller in 1938 and turned public stomachs against the colony. Henri Charrière's later *Papillon* told a wilder story of escape that penal-colony records show he mostly fabricated - yet the cruelties he described were real enough, borrowed from the lives of others.

## One Experiment, Many Wounds

Near the middle of the nineteenth century the administration tried an experiment. Fifteen women, brought from French prisons and guarded by nuns, were sent to the islands with the stated hope that they would encourage the men to settle, to marry, to live like citizens. No families formed. Rum changed hands. A syphilis epidemic followed. Tell that story honestly and it is neither a tale of moral weakness nor of exotic curiosity - it is what happens when a government treats people, men and women alike, as raw material for an experiment. The administrators who thought it up went home to France. The people left behind, on both sides of the wire, paid the bill.

## After the Silence

Belbenoît's book helped shame France into action. Closure was announced, then delayed by the war. Between 1946 and 1953 the prisons were shut one at a time, with Devil's Island itself the last to go dark. Some freed men - unable, after decades, to imagine any other life - chose to stay in Guiana rather than return to a country that no longer remembered them. In 1965 France handed most of the Salvation Islands to the newly founded Guiana Space Centre at Kourou, whose Ariane rockets now arc eastward to geostationary orbit directly over the old cells. The islands must be evacuated during each launch. Today more than 50,000 tourists a year visit Îles Royale and Saint-Joseph, where some prison buildings have been restored as museums. Devil's Island itself stays closed. The cable car that once carried supplies across to it has long since rusted away, and you can only see it from a boat - a green knuckle of rock in a restless sea, holding the ghosts of men France preferred to forget.

## Flight Context

Devil's Island and the Salvation Islands lie at 5.29°N, 52.58°W, about 15 km off the coast of Kourou, French Guiana. Nearest airport is Cayenne - Félix Eboué (SOCA / CAY), roughly 50 km southeast. The tiny islands are clearly visible from cruising altitude in clear weather; their airspace is restricted during Ariane launches from the Guiana Space Centre just onshore.
