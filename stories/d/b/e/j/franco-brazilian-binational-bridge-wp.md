# Franco-Brazilian Binational Bridge

It took nearly twenty years from first announcement to first car - a 378-meter span that turned a ferry crossing into a highway, and made it possible, for the first time, to drive from Cayenne to Macapá.

**Location:** 3.8569, -51.8256

**Categories:** Bridges, International crossings, French Guiana, Brazil

## Introduction

The *New York Times* once called it "a bridge for no one." For six years after its completion in August 2011, the Franco-Brazilian Binational Bridge stood across the Oyapock River with its lights on and its lanes empty, a 378-meter cable-stayed span of Italian engineering with Brazilian concrete piers and French customs booths, waiting for two governments to finish their paperwork. On 18 March 2017 the ribbons were finally cut. Two days later, on the morning of 20 March, the first ordinary cars crossed from Saint-Georges-de-l'Oyapock in French Guiana to Oiapoque in Amapá, Brazil - the first land crossing ever opened between France and Brazil, and the first terrestrial border in or out of French Guiana's neighbors anywhere in northern South America.

## An Engineering Object

The bridge is cable-stayed, its two towers rising to 83 meters above the brown current of the Oyapock. The deck spans 378 meters in all, about 200 of them over water, with a vertical clearance of 15 meters to pass the riverboats that still come and go beneath it. There are two traffic lanes of combined 9 meters width and a 2.5-meter pedestrian sidewalk. The designer was Mario de Miranda, an Italian structural engineer with a reputation for elegant cable-stayed bridges; the builders were a partnership of EGESA and Consórcio Metropolitano de Transportes, both headquartered in Brasília. The Professor Carlos Augusto Bittencourt Foundation of Niterói was brought in to oversee environmental engineering. As a structure, it is pleasing to the eye - white towers, harp of cables, a slender deck curving across muddy water where, not long before, ferries had run.

## A Long Political Crossing

The bridge was announced in 1997. It took nearly ten years of talks and delays before French president Nicolas Sarkozy and Brazilian president Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva unveiled a model of it on 14 February 2008. An international treaty between France and Brazil had been signed in July 2005, and the bridge was folded into the Guianese Shield Hub of the Initiative for the Integration of the Regional Infrastructure of South America - an ambitious plan to stitch together the continent's isolated interiors. Construction wrapped in August 2011. Everyone expected the first drivers in early 2012. Instead the bridge sat closed because the Brazilian road on the far side, BR-156, was still an unfinished dirt track in places, and because the Brazilian customs facility was not built. Year by year the opening slid: end of 2013, no; January 2015, no. The structure became the subject of faintly embarrassed newspaper profiles in both countries.

## The Card of the Frontier

While the bridge waited, people on both banks kept crossing as they had for generations - by small boat, by arrangement, by familiarity. In 2014 the governments agreed on something called the *carte de frontalier*, a local border-crossing card with a biometric chip, valid for up to two years, letting residents of Oiapoque and Saint-Georges-de-l'Oyapock visit each other's town visa-free for up to 72 hours at a stretch, but no further inland. It came into effect on 1 January 2015. By October that year, over 200 cards had been issued to Brazilians resident in Oiapoque. The French government described the arrangement as one with which the Brazilian government was "largely satisfied," which is diplomatic language for something that mostly worked.

## Inauguration Without a Minister

By early 2017 French pressure finally forced the issue. Even though the Brazilian customs facility was still incomplete, the inauguration was set for 18 March. The French Minister of Ecology, Sustainable Development and Energy, Ségolène Royal, was supposed to attend. She did not. The official reason was the absence of her Brazilian counterpart; local media in French Guiana reported that the real reason was a string of protests she had encountered during her visit, which led her to cut the trip short and fly back to Paris on 17 March. The inauguration proceeded anyway, with the governor of Amapá, Waldez Góes, the prefect of French Guiana Martin Jaeger, and the mayor of Saint-Georges-de-l'Oyapock, Georges Elfort, cutting the ribbons. On 20 March the bridge opened to the public.

## What the Bridge Changed

The French side is staffed by three agencies - the Border Police, Customs, and the Directorate for Food, Agriculture and Forestry - and is open 08:00-12:00 and 14:00-18:00 on weekdays, closed Saturdays, Sundays and Brazilian public holidays. The Brazilian side is still not staffed at the bridge itself; drivers arriving in Brazil must stop at the Federal Revenue office and the Federal Police in Oiapoque to formalize entry. For now the bridge carries only passenger vehicles; cargo and buses are not yet permitted. But it is now possible, in a way it was never possible before, to drive from Cayenne to Macapá - a long journey across French Guiana and down through northeastern Brazil on BR-156, parts of which remain unpaved as of the bridge's opening. Further west, the Maroni ferry still links French Guiana to Suriname at Albina. The dream mapped by the Guianese Shield Hub is an unbroken road from Macapá to Venezuela and Colombia, with a single ferry crossing. For now, this one cable-stayed span across the Oyapock is the first piece of it - a bridge, at last, that is for someone.

## Flight Context

The Franco-Brazilian Binational Bridge crosses the Oyapock River at 3.86°N, 51.83°W, linking Saint-Georges-de-l'Oyapock in French Guiana to Oiapoque in Amapá, Brazil. Nearest airports: Saint-Georges-de-l'Oyapock (SOOG) on the French side; Oiapoque Airport (SBOI) on the Brazilian side; Cayenne - Félix Eboué (SOCA / CAY) roughly 185 km northwest. From the air, the two white towers and their harp of cables are clearly visible against the brown ribbon of the Oyapock.
