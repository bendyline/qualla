# Amapá Mangroves

Along 1,550 square kilometers of the Brazilian Amazon coast, mangrove forests reach 40 kilometers inland and rank among the best-preserved mangrove ecosystems in the Americas.

**Location:** 3.0960, -51.0280

**Categories:** Brazil, Amapá, Mangroves, Ecoregions, Amazon biome

## Introduction

The tides build the forest. Twice a day along the coast of Amapá, water rises more than five meters against the low coastal plain, pushes far inland along a network of natural canals, and retreats again - leaving behind fresh deposits of fine gray clay carried up from the mouth of the Amazon by the northbound coastal current. On these shifting mudflats, mangroves colonize within seasons. Trunks fork into stilt-like prop roots. Green canopies close over the waterways. Over a few centuries a new mangrove forest grows out of nothing but mud, current, and saltwater. The Amapá mangroves cover 1,550 square kilometers of Brazil's northernmost Atlantic coast, and they are counted among the best-preserved mangroves in the Americas; the World Wildlife Fund rates the ecoregion relatively stable and intact.

## A Forest Built by Rivers

Most mangroves grow in well-defined belts along tidal coasts. The Amapá mangroves grow strange: they reach 40 kilometers inland, wrapping estuaries and riverbanks wherever tidal influence penetrates. The Amazon's own discharge - one-fifth of all freshwater flowing into the world's oceans - creates a plume so massive that strong coastal currents sweep its sediment north along Amapá's shore. The mud settles, islands form, and mangroves take hold. Tidal range here runs to 5.2 meters. Annual rainfall reaches 4,000 millimeters, with only a two-month dry season. The constant freshwater input keeps salinity low, so freshwater and saltwater plants grow mixed together in ways unusual elsewhere. Palms like açaí root alongside red mangroves; the giant aroid Montrichardia arborescens lines channels where saltwater and river water meet.

## Three Kinds of Mangrove

The forest is layered by salinity and tide. Closest to open sea, the black mangroves dominate - Avicennia germinans and Avicennia schaueriana, the latter reportedly growing up to 45 meters tall. Their pneumatophores - aerial breathing roots - poke up through the mud like thousands of inverted pencils so the trees can get oxygen through waterlogged soils. Behind them, Rhizophora mangle (the red mangrove, recognizable by its arched stilt roots) takes over. Farther inland, Laguncularia racemosa and Conocarpus erectus fringe the freshwater edges. The region holds about 13 percent of all Brazilian mangrove formation. In its deepest zones the canopy rises 15 meters or more, threaded with muddy tidal channels.

## A Late-Holocene Story

Sediment cores taken near the town of Calçoene tell the forest's history. Deposits from 2,100 years ago show no sign of mangroves at all - just mud filling tidal channels. Then mangroves began to establish along channel edges, while grasses colonized the higher ground. A phase followed when the mangroves actually retreated and freshwater várzea forest pushed outward, indicating a shift toward less sea influence and more river input. The current phase shows both biomes expanding at once - probably because sea level is rising faster than the land. The forest is still actively growing outward as new mudflats accrete. This is not a static ecosystem. It is a construction project still in progress.

## Jaguars, Manatees, and the Scarlet Ibis

Amapá's remoteness has kept the fauna list astonishingly complete. Howler monkeys and black-bearded sakis move through the canopy. Jaguars still hunt these forests; so do pumas, ocelots and margays. Capybaras wade through the shallows, South American tapirs plow trails through the undergrowth, and giant otters - top predators of the Amazon river system, up to 1.8 meters long - hunt in the channels. West Indian manatees browse quietly in the estuaries. Tucuxi dolphins, smaller than their more famous pink river cousins, surface along the coast. Leatherback and green sea turtles also use the mangrove coast. And the birds - the scarlet ibis in flocks that fill a tree with vermilion when they roost at sunset, the tiny semipalmated sandpiper that migrates here from Arctic breeding grounds, the rufous crab hawk that specializes in catching the mangrove's most abundant prey.

## The Largest Protected Mangroves in the Americas

The Amapá Biodiversity Corridor ties together Cabo Orange National Park, the Maracá-Jipioca Ecological Station, and other conservation units into a single protected swath - the largest stretch of protected mangrove in the Americas. Maracá-Jipioca alone covers 602 square kilometers across two islands about 5 kilometers offshore. Local artisanal fishermen harvest crabs and seafood here, using mangrove wood for fuel, boats and dwellings, and stripping bark to make tannin for dyeing sails. The serious threats are unsustainable exploitation - commercial logging, over-harvesting of shrimp and other seafood, conversion of land to pasture - and offshore oil exploration, now under way: in 2026 Petrobras reported oil at an exploration well about 175 kilometers off the Amapá coast, and a major spill in the Amazon plume would be catastrophic. For now, the forest holds.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 3.10°N, 51.03°W (approximate center). The Amapá mangroves stretch along Brazil's northern Atlantic coast from just north of the Amazon mouth to the Cassiporé River. From the air, the coastline appears as a complex maze of channels, islands, and mudflats, with dense dark-green mangrove canopy. The low coastal plain gives way to Amazonian rainforest inland. Nearest significant airfield is Macapá International (SBMQ) at the Amazon delta, with Oiapoque (SBOI) serving the far northern end. The water appears muddy-gray due to heavy sediment in the Amazon plume. Cabo Orange National Park borders the northern section.
