# Tacky's Revolt

In April 1760, a Fante royal named Tacky led the largest uprising of enslaved people in the eighteenth-century British Caribbean, shaking Britain's hold on Jamaica.

**Location:** 18.3700, -76.8900

**Categories:** history, jamaica, resistance, slavery, caribbean, 18th-century

## Introduction

Before dawn on Easter Monday, 7 April 1760, a man named Tacky led a column of trained soldiers out of the cane fields of St. Mary Parish and changed what the British Empire understood about the cost of slavery. Tacky had been a king in West Africa - a paramount chief of Eguaafo, in what is now Ghana's Central Region - before a rival army and the Dutch defeated him near Elmina and sold him into the Atlantic slave trade. In Jamaica the British called him property. The men who followed him that morning called him something else.

## A King Across the Ocean

The people who rose with Tacky were Coromantee - a British catch-all name for Akan-speaking captives shipped from the Gold Coast - and many of them had been soldiers before they were sold. Tacky himself spoke English, as the ruling Fante class often did, and had planned the uprising for a year in a cave with his lieutenants Quaw Badu, Sang, Sobadou, Fula Jati, and Quantee. The plan was simple in ambition and enormous in scale: take the island, expel the British, and establish a free state. The inspiration was close to hand. A generation earlier, Queen Nanny had led the Windward Maroons to a treaty that forced the colony to recognize their freedom. If she could do it, so could they.

## Easter Monday

They moved in the dark and the first hours went their way. Frontier and Trinity plantations fell quickly; some of the white overseers were killed. The rebels marched to Fort Haldane, killed the storekeeper, and carried off roughly four barrels of gunpowder and forty firearms - enough to equip a small army. By sunrise hundreds of others had slipped away from the cane fields to join them. At Ballard's Valley they stopped to celebrate. One person from Esher estate slipped off during the celebration to raise the alarm. That small decision turned the tide. On 9 April, Lieutenant Governor Sir Henry Moore dispatched a detachment of the 74th Regiment - 70 to 80 mounted militia - from Spanish Town. With them, bound by treaty to suppress such rebellions, came the Maroons of Moore Town, Charles Town, and Scott's Hall - the same communities whose own freedom had inspired Tacky in the first place.

## The Marksman and the Cave

The rebels fought for a week in the hills above Port Maria. At Rocky Valley on 14 April, Maroon forces led by the superintendent Charles Swigle routed them. Tacky ran through the woods with what remained of his men, pursued by a Maroon marksman named Davy. Davy shot him on the run and cut off his head as proof, for which he was richly rewarded. The head was mounted on a pole in Spanish Town until a follower took it down in the middle of the night. The rest of Tacky's men were found in a cave near what is now called Tacky Falls. They had chosen death over return to bondage.

## The Western Movement

Tacky's death did not end the rebellion - it moved it west. On 25 May, on the Masemure estate in Westmoreland, a man named Apongo rose in revolt. Thomas Thistlewood, the infamous English enslaver whose own diaries record years of torture and rape, wrote that Apongo had been a prince of a West African state tributary to a kingdom he called 'Dorme', long read as Dahomey. Apongo's forces were disciplined and well-supplied. When British troops stormed the 'Rebel's Barricade' in June, they found more than seventy hogsheads of gunpowder and chests of clothing, ruffled shirts, and laced hats - a government in exile, not a desperate band. For months the rebels held out in the forests. When capture came, the sentences were designed as theater: a man named Davie was left in an iron gibbet for a week until he starved; Apongo was strung up in chains for three days before his scheduled burning, dying in the cage before the final act. Militia accounts boasted that about seven hundred rebels were killed in the western campaign. A woman named Cubah, crowned 'Queen of Kingston' by the Coromantee in that city, was banished, bribed her ship's captain to put her ashore in western Jamaica, joined the rebels there, and was eventually captured and executed.

## What They Changed

The historian Trevor Burnard has argued that only the American Revolution surpassed Tacky's War in its shock to the eighteenth-century British imperial system. The Jamaican Assembly passed new and harsher laws, banned the West African religious practice of Obeah, and tightened the chains everywhere they could. But the uprisings did not stop. In 1765 a man named Abruco - who had been acquitted of any role in 1760 for lack of evidence - led another rising that set fire to Zachary Bayly's Whitehall estate. In 1766 thirty-three people in Westmoreland rose up and killed nineteen white colonists before they were defeated. In western Jamaica, survivors held out in the mountain forests of St Elizabeth. The island's next great rising came in 1831 - the Baptist War, still the largest British Caribbean slave uprising, which helped hasten abolition. In May 2023, a plaque in a Dorset church that had praised a plantation owner's role in suppressing the rebellion was finally removed and moved to the Dorset Museum - two hundred and sixty-three years after the revolt.
