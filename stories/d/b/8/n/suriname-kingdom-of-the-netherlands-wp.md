# Suriname (Kingdom of the Netherlands)

For 21 years, a South American country was an equal partner in the Kingdom of the Netherlands. Then the Dutch decided they wanted out.

**Location:** 4.0000, -56.0000

**Categories:** suriname, netherlands, colonial-history, independence, political-history

## Introduction

Between 1954 and 1975, a country on the northeast coast of South America was a constituent partner of the Kingdom of the Netherlands, equal on paper with the Netherlands itself and the Netherlands Antilles. Suriname had full autonomy over everything except defense and foreign policy. Its delegates attended Dutch cabinet meetings when relevant. It was, briefly, the experiment in post-colonial equality that European powers rarely tried. It lasted twenty-one years.

## Queen Wilhelmina's Promise

The idea began with propaganda. On 7 December 1942, Queen Wilhelmina delivered a radio speech from her London exile. The Dutch government-in-exile was feeling the pressure of American opinion, which had turned against European colonialism during the war. The queen promised that after liberation, the Netherlands would convene a conference to settle the status of its overseas territories on a basis of equality. The main audience was really the Dutch East Indies - what would soon become Indonesia - and the hope was that a promised post-war reform would calm unrest there. It did not work: Indonesia fought and won its independence anyway. But the promise, once spoken on the BBC, could not easily be withdrawn for the Dutch colonies in the Caribbean. When Indonesia was gone, the Netherlands pivoted to a smaller, lighter arrangement: a charter, signed in 1954, that bound the Netherlands, the Netherlands Antilles, and Suriname together as equal constituent countries.

## The Charter of 1954

Under the charter, Suriname gained real autonomy. A Minister Plenipotentiary represented Surinamese interests in The Hague and joined Dutch cabinet meetings when decisions affected the kingdom as a whole. Surinamese delegates could participate in sessions of both chambers of the Dutch parliament. An overseas member could join the Council of State. Suriname could amend its own Staatsregeling - its basic law - on its own authority. What the charter did not recognize was a unilateral right to leave. Dissolution required mutual consent. In practice, the charter worked: Suriname governed itself in virtually all domestic matters while remaining inside a Dutch-Caribbean-South American federation that functioned on paper and mostly in reality. For a Caribbean-South American territory once run as a plantation colony with enslaved African labor, this was a genuine constitutional revolution.

## When the Dutch Changed Their Minds

The irony was that in the 1950s, the Dutch opposed Surinamese independence. They had built the charter specifically to keep Suriname within the kingdom. By the 1960s the Dutch mood had flipped. The Netherlands New Guinea crisis of 1962 had ended with the Dutch losing their last East Indies territory to Indonesia. The 1969 Curacao uprising showed that Caribbean unrest could still cost the metropole. The former colonies had become a fiscal burden, and the Dutch government had lost real oversight of their internal administration. The Dutch Labour Party added an ideological argument: colonialism was wrong, and any remnant of it - including the charter - had to go. The Den Uyl cabinet took office in May 1973 promising that the Caribbean partners would become independent during its term. The Netherlands Antilles refused. Suriname's prime minister Jules Sedney argued that the Dutch were rushing, that independence needed longer preparation. But Henck Arron, winning the 1973 Surinamese election, accepted Den Uyl's invitation. Independence would come by the end of 1975.

## The Night of 24 November

On 21 November 1975, the statue of Queen Wilhelmina was removed from Paramaribo's Oranjeplein. The Surinamese flag went up in its place, and the square was renamed Independence Square. On the evening of 24 November, the Dutch flag was lowered for the last time. At midnight, parties erupted across Paramaribo. The first day of the independent Republic of Suriname, 25 November 1975, was celebrated with Princess Beatrix and Prince Claus present, alongside Prime Minister Den Uyl. Johan Ferrier, the former governor, was sworn in as president. In The Hague, Queen Juliana signed the treaty of sovereignty. Attached to the transition was a severance package of 3.5 billion Dutch guilders in development aid - the largest per capita decolonization package in history. In the months before independence, roughly one-third of Suriname's population had emigrated to the Netherlands, keeping their Dutch citizenship and voting with their feet about what the new republic might bring.

## Dutch Guiana and Its Ghosts

The term Dutch Guiana was used informally to describe the territory, by analogy with British Guiana and French Guiana. Officially the country was always Surinam or Suriname, in both Dutch and English. Before 1814 there had been several Dutch colonies in the Guianas - Suriname, Berbice, Essequibo, Demerara, Pomeroon - but the last four passed to British control in 1814 and were merged into British Guiana in 1831. What ended in 1975 was not just a constitutional arrangement but a particular story the Netherlands had been telling itself since the 17th century. The experiment of 1954 - of trying to turn a slave colony into an equal partner - had proved that constitutional reform could outrun neither history nor the economic realities that had made the colony profitable in the first place. On that November midnight in Paramaribo, a new country was born. On the same midnight, an older one finally admitted what it could not undo.
