# Suriname

The smallest country in South America, a rainforest republic whose history runs from slavery to military coup to a first woman president.

**Location:** 4.0000, -56.0000

**Categories:** suriname, countries, south-america, rainforest, caribbean

## Introduction

Over 90 percent of Suriname is covered by rainforest - the highest proportion of any country in the world. The government runs a carbon-negative economy because the trees do more work than the people can undo. It is the smallest independent country in South America by both population and area. It is also the only country in the Americas where Dutch is the language of government and business. How a small corner of the Guiana Shield ended up this way is a story about slavery, sugar, a swap with Britain, and a coup that never quite ended.

## New Amsterdam for New York

In 1667, in the negotiations that produced the Treaty of Breda ending the Second Anglo-Dutch War, the Dutch traded places with the English. The English had taken New Amsterdam - the Dutch settlement at the tip of Manhattan Island. The Dutch had taken the nascent plantation colony of Surinam. When the treaty was signed, each side kept what they had grabbed. England renamed its prize New York after the Duke of York. The Dutch kept Suriname because, at the time, a Caribbean sugar colony was worth more than a small trading post on a northern river. The trade would look different by the 20th century. In 1667, the arithmetic favored sugar.

## The Plantation Economy

Approximately 300,000 enslaved Africans were brought to Suriname during the transatlantic slave trade. The Dutch historian C.R. Boxer wrote that man's inhumanity to man just about reached its limits in Surinam - and he was writing about treatment that was extreme even by the standards of a hemispheric system built on cruelty. Enslaved people worked sugar, coffee, cocoa, and cotton plantations along the rivers, under a Dutch Society of Suriname chartered in 1683 by Amsterdam, the Van Aerssen van Sommelsdijck family, and the Dutch West India Company. Many people escaped into the rainforest, linked up with indigenous communities, and built sovereign Maroon nations that the colonial authorities could not militarily defeat. By the 18th century, Dutch administrators were signing peace treaties with Maroon groups - Saramaka, Ndyuka, Paramaka, Aluku, Kwinti, Matawai - that granted sovereign trade rights in their interior territories. Runaway slaves had negotiated, at gunpoint in the forest, what the Dutch would not grant through law.

## Abolition and Indenture

Slavery was abolished in Suriname in 1863 - later than in the British empire, later than in the United States. Even then, formerly enslaved people were required to work on plantations for a ten-year transition period at minimal pay, which was described as partial compensation to their former enslavers. True freedom came in 1873. Many of the freedmen moved to Paramaribo, refusing to remain on plantations where their ancestors had been held. To replace their labor, the Dutch recruited indentured workers from British India - starting in 1873 and continuing until 1916 - and from the Dutch East Indies, now Indonesia, until 1936. Smaller numbers came from China and the Middle East. These arrivals, forced and voluntary in different measures, created the ethnic landscape of modern Suriname. The capital-city commemoration of 1 July is still called Keti Koti - the chains are cut - and the public memory does not let the date settle into mere celebration.

## Independence and Its Troubles

On 25 November 1975, Suriname became independent. Within five years, on 25 February 1980, a military coup led by Desi Bouterse overthrew the civilian government. Opponents attempted counter-coups in April and August 1980, in March 1981, and again in March 1982. In the pre-dawn hours of 7 December 1982, Bouterse's military rounded up thirteen prominent critics of the regime, held them at Fort Zeelandia in Paramaribo, and executed them over the next three days - the December Murders. A civil war between the army and Maroons loyal to rebel leader Ronnie Brunswijk began in 1986 and weakened Bouterse through the 1990s. Democracy returned, was overthrown by a telephoned dismissal of ministers in 1990 - the Telephone Coup - and returned again. At the 1988 Seoul Olympics, Anthony Nesty won the 100-meter butterfly, giving Suriname its first Olympic medal. Bouterse was convicted in absentia by a Dutch court for drug trafficking and then, in 2019, convicted at home for the December Murders and sentenced to twenty years. He died in December 2024, a fugitive. On 6 July 2025, Jennifer Geerlings-Simons was elected Suriname's first woman president by the National Assembly.

## The Shield of Trees

Suriname sits on the Guiana Shield, one of the oldest and most stable pieces of continental crust on Earth - rocks that were here when the continents were still figuring out where to go. The country has six terrestrial ecoregions, a forest cover of 90.2 percent, and a 2019 Forest Landscape Integrity Index score of 9.39 out of 10 - ranking fifth globally out of 172 countries. The Central Suriname Nature Reserve is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Julianatop rises to 1,286 meters in the country's south. Illegal gold mining with mercury continues to damage the Lawa and Maroni border rivers. Climate change is producing warmer temperatures and more extreme weather, though the country's contributions to it have been minimal. What Suriname has - forest, rivers, biodiversity - is what the global system needs more of. What Suriname has struggled to hold is what the global system cares less about: stable institutions, a past that settles without returning, a future agreed on by the many communities who call this corner of the Guiana Shield home.

## Flight Context

Suriname is centered near 4.00 degrees N, 56.00 degrees W. Recommended viewing altitude 5,000-12,000 feet for topographic context. The country ranges from Atlantic coast at sea level to Julianatop at 1,286 meters in the south. Johan Adolf Pengel International (ICAO: SMJP) is the main international airport, 45 km south of Paramaribo. Paramaribo sits on the Suriname River; most population lives within a coastal strip a few tens of kilometers wide. The Central Suriname Nature Reserve dominates the interior as a vast roadless rainforest.
