# Suriname

A small South American republic where Dutch is the official language, Hindi-speaking Hindus live next door to Javanese Muslims, and the Amazon starts a short drive from the capital.

**Location:** 4.0000, -56.0000

**Categories:** suriname, countries, south-america, caribbean, amazon

## Introduction

Dutch is the official language of a country on the northern coast of South America. Hindu temples stand on the same streets as mosques and Moravian churches. Most people are descended from Africans brought in chains, or South Asians brought under indenture contracts, or Javanese brought from the Dutch East Indies. Suriname, which declared independence in 1975, is a country that exists because of the Atlantic slave trade and the migrations that followed it - and its current face, one of the most culturally dense on Earth, is what grew from that ground.

## A Country of Invitations and Forced Arrivals

Suriname's 576,000 people - half of them in the capital Paramaribo - inherit a staggering mix of origins. The Dutch colonized from the 17th century and built a plantation economy that depended on enslaved African labor. Hundreds of thousands of Africans were brought across the Atlantic, worked to death in fields of sugarcane and coffee, and replaced. When slavery ended in 1863, plantation owners turned to indentured labor, inviting workers from British India and Java. Many stayed. Today Indians - mostly Hindu, some Muslim - are the largest ethnic group at about a quarter of the population. Maroons, descendants of enslaved Africans who escaped into the rainforest and founded their own sovereign nations, are another 21 percent. Javanese are about 14 percent; Chinese 7 percent; and a substantial number of Surinamese claim mixed heritage. No group forms a majority. The country is known for the tolerance between them - a feature of modern Surinamese identity rather than an accident of demographics.

## Sranang Tongo

On the streets of Paramaribo, you hear Dutch used in formal settings and in schools. You also hear Sranang Tongo, an English-based creole that is now the country's real lingua franca. The language developed on plantations where the Dutch forbade enslaved Africans from speaking Dutch, and English-derived vocabulary carried over from a brief earlier period of British administration. For centuries, Dutch authorities suppressed Sranang Tongo, calling it nengre or negerengels. They lost that fight. Since 1986 it has had an officially codified spelling. It is the native language of most Surinamese people and spoken by every ethnic group as a common ground. You will also hear Sarnami Hindi, Javanese, Mandarin and Hakka Chinese, Portuguese along the Brazilian-origin community. Suriname is a country that speaks in several voices at once and does not apologize.

## The Rainforest and Its Absent Roads

Eighty percent of Suriname is tropical rainforest - one of the highest forest-cover percentages in the world. The coastal strip holds nearly the entire population; almost no one lives in the interior, and the few who do are mostly Maroon or Amerindian communities on rivers that double as highways. You can drive to Pokigron, also called Atjoni, and that is where the road ends. From there it is boats, guides, and hammocks. Brownsberg Nature Park sits 130 kilometers south of Paramaribo on a cool plateau overlooking the Brokopondo Reservoir. The Central Suriname Nature Reserve, a UNESCO World Heritage site, protects 1.6 million hectares - roughly the size of New Jersey - of primary tropical forest, including the flat-topped Tafelberg and the granite dome of the Voltzberg. Jaguars still hunt in these forests. Leatherback turtles lay eggs on the beach at Galibi. The Amazon begins, effectively, just outside the capital.

## Paramaribo and Its Ghosts

The historic inner city of Paramaribo is a UNESCO World Heritage Site for its extraordinary collection of Dutch colonial architecture. Wooden houses with steep Dutch gables sit in tropical heat; the Saint Peter and Paul Cathedral is the largest wooden building in South America. Fort Zeelandia, at the edge of the Suriname River, is the physical center of Surinamese history - the place the Dutch built to defend what they had taken. In December 1982, it became the site of the December Murders, when the military dictatorship of Desi Bouterse executed fifteen prominent critics over three days. The building is now a museum. The weight in its walls is real. Down the block, the Central Market sells Sranang tonics beside South Asian spices beside Javanese sambal, and the Waterkant's food stalls serve pom, saoto, roti, and cassava. The city is a kitchen. It is also a memorial.

## Keti Koti

Every 1 July, Suriname marks Keti Koti - Sranang Tongo for the chains are cut. The holiday commemorates the 1863 abolition of slavery, when roughly 33,000 enslaved people across the Dutch Caribbean were formally freed - though full freedom came only after a mandatory ten-year transition during which freedpeople still had to work plantations for minimal pay. Keti Koti is celebrated with music, feasts, traditional dress, and an insistence that the past is not behind but still in the room. Other holidays reflect the country's layered arrivals: Indian Arrival Day on 5 June, the Day of Javanese Arrival on 9 August, Day of the Maroons on 10 October, Divali as a national holiday since 2010. Keti Koti is the oldest and the heaviest. It is what a country chooses to remember, and how, and out loud.

## Flight Context

Suriname is centered at roughly 4.00 degrees N, 56.00 degrees W. Recommended viewing altitude 5,000-12,000 feet. The capital Paramaribo sits on the Suriname River near the northern coast. Johan Adolf Pengel International Airport (ICAO: SMJP) is 45 km south of the capital. Zorg en Hoop Airfield (ICAO: SMZO) handles domestic flights. The country's northern coast is low and heavily settled; the southern 80% is dense rainforest with few roads.
