# Tapanahony

A Suriname resort the size of a small country, where the Ndyuka granman holds court and a sacred oracle still speaks from the village of Diitabiki.

**Location:** 3.3408, -55.1289

**Categories:** suriname, maroon, ndyuka, rainforest, indigenous, history

## Introduction

Roughly a quarter of Suriname fits inside a single administrative resort called Tapanahony, and the paramount chief of its Ndyuka people does not sit in the capital. He sits at Diitabiki, a village on the Tapanahoni River where the forest closes in. Since 1950, Diitabiki has been the residence of the granman of the Ndyuka people. It is also where the Ndyuka oracle resides.

## Forest as Refuge

The Ndyuka are the descendants of people who refused to remain enslaved. In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, their ancestors were carried from Africa to Dutch-owned plantations in Suriname, and as soon as they could they ran for the rainforest. The forest hid them, fed them, and slowly became theirs. After decades of guerrilla war that the Dutch could not win, a peace treaty was signed in 1760 granting the Ndyuka autonomy - one of the earliest such agreements made between a European colonial power and a Maroon community anywhere in the Americas. From 1761 onward, the Ndyuka pushed steadily southward, founding camps along the Tapanahoni River. This migration had a cost that the record should not smooth over: the indigenous Tiriyó people already lived there, and they were displaced.

## A Militarized Frontier

The river that defines Tapanahony was for more than a century a political border as real as any drawn on a European map. People who had recently escaped slavery on Armina and the upper Commewijne were stationed at Poeloegoedoe, near the confluence of the Tapanahoni and the Lawa, to guard against Aluku attacks. In December 1791, a Dutch officer named Philip Stoelman built a military outpost on an island in the river - Stoelmanseiland - turning that stretch of brown water into a formal frontier between Ndyuka territory and the Colony of Suriname. In 1857 the colonial government began paying the granman an annual stipend of one thousand guilders. Even then, respect had its limits. Gerard Versteeg, who led a 1904 expedition into the interior, fumed in his diary about waiting four full days for the granman's audience before he was permitted to continue his journey.

## The Diitabiki Oracle

Diitabiki is a small place with a large role. Its older map name, Drietabbetje, still appears on some maps; it is a Dutch rendering of the Ndyuka name Diitabiki, which means 'three islands.' Since the mid-twentieth century the village has housed both the granman and the Ndyuka oracle - a ritual institution through which spiritual powers are consulted on matters affecting the community, from illness to disputes. The Moravian Church reached the river long ago, and many Ndyuka are Christian, but the older framework has not been replaced by it. It runs alongside.

## Many Peoples, One River

Tapanahony's population - 13,808 at the 2012 census, before the resort was split - is spread across villages that belong to several distinct nations. Diitabiki, Godo Holo, Manlobi, and Moitaki are Ndyuka. Apetina, Kawemhakan, and Kumakahpan are Wayana, and Paloemeu is home to Wayana and Tiriyó families. Cottica is Aluku. Pelelu Tepu is Tiriyó. Each people has its own language, its own governance, and its own relationship to the rivers. Some are reachable by scheduled flight, some only by dugout canoe pushing upriver for days. In 2019, the northern portion of the old Tapanahony resort was split off to create a separate resort, Pamacca, inhabited mainly by the Paramaccan people - another Maroon nation whose story parallels the Ndyuka's but runs on a different thread.

## Looking Down from Above

Flying over Tapanahony, the forest goes on so long that the eye stops trying to measure it. Then a river shows itself - the Tapanahoni, or the Lawa, or the Marowijne into which they eventually feed - and the canopy breaks open for just long enough to show a village: a cluster of thatched roofs and cleared gardens and a dugout on a sandbar, then back to green. The disputed territory between the Malani and Litani rivers, claimed by both Suriname and French Guiana, belongs to this resort administratively though not always in fact. It is one of the emptier quarters of a sparsely populated continent - though along the Lawa, informal gold mining, much of it using mercury, has drawn miners from Suriname, Brazil and French Guiana and polluted the rivers the villages depend on.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 3.34°N, 55.13°W (central Tapanahony resort, southeastern Suriname; Diitabiki lies about 100 km to the north-northeast). Recommended viewing altitude 3,000-5,000 ft for river navigation. The nearest airstrip is Paloemeu (Vincent Fayks, SMPA), about 35 km west; Drietabbetje airstrip (SMDA) at Diitabiki has scheduled flights from Paramaribo. Paramaribo's Johan Adolf Pengel International (SMJP) is about 235 km due north. Dense tropical rainforest; expect towering cumulus and afternoon thunderstorms in the wet season (April-August).
