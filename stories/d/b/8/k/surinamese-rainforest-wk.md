# Surinamese Rainforest

Three inland districts cover most of Suriname, most of them untouched forest, with one reservoir the size of Rhode Island and mountains barely anyone has climbed.

**Location:** 3.6724, -55.7501

**Categories:** rainforest, suriname, amazon, protected-areas, unesco-heritage, maroon-communities

## Introduction

Suriname's three inland districts - Brokopondo, Para, and Sipaliwini - hold what lies behind the coastal strip: mostly uncut Amazon, a reservoir big enough to show up on world maps, a mountain called Julianatop that tops 1,280 meters, and more than 15,000 Saramaka Maroons living along rivers in villages their ancestors founded after escaping Dutch plantations three hundred years ago. This is where the country gets away from the city and starts being, mostly, trees.

## The Drowned Forest

Lake Brokopondo, officially the Prof. Dr. Ir. W.J. van Blommesteinmeer, is one of the largest reservoirs in the world at 1,560 square kilometers. It formed after 1964 when the Afobaka Dam blocked the Suriname River to generate electricity for aluminum refining. Villages along the Sara Creek and surrounding areas had to be resettled. Most of the trees were never cleared before the water rose. Their dead tops still punch through the surface today, a forest that died standing up and refuses to fall. Former hilltops are now islands, some with tourist facilities. The town of Brokopondo sits on the reservoir's western shore, just north of the dam, with a beach on the Suriname River and boats running out to the islands. It is a landscape where you can swim in what used to be a hilltop, sleep on what used to be a mountainside, and look across water toward trees whose roots are still in their original soil under 30 meters of lake.

## Central Suriname Nature Reserve

The UNESCO World Heritage Site known as the Central Suriname Nature Reserve protects 1.6 million hectares of primary tropical forest - an area roughly the size of New Jersey. It links three older reserves - Raleighvallen in the north, Tafelberg in the center, and the Eilerts de Haan mountains in the south - into a single continuous corridor. About 80 percent of Suriname is covered in this kind of moist highland forest. The Raleighvallen reserve sits along the Coppename River, where the Raleigh Falls drop in a series of rapids near Fungo Island. Climb the Voltzberg, a granite sugarloaf that rises 150 meters above the forest canopy, and you see monkeys, toucans, macaws, and the spectacular cock-of-the-rock bird. Jaguars live here but rarely show themselves to visitors. In 2005 scientists discovered twenty-five species new to science in a three-week expedition to eastern Suriname. The forest is still giving up its secrets because so few people have ever gone looking.

## Villages on the Upper Suriname

The Upper Suriname region begins south of Lake Brokopondo and extends along the Gran Rio and Pikin Rio rivers, which rise in the Eilerts de Haan Mountains. Here live about 15,000 Saramaka Maroons, descendants of Africans who escaped Dutch plantations from the late 17th century and built sovereign communities in the forest. Their long isolation meant many West African cultural practices survived more intact here than almost anywhere in the Americas. Pokigron, also called Atjoni, is the last place a road reaches from Paramaribo - a 180 kilometer ride, about four to five hours over mostly dirt. From Pokigron you take a boat. River taxis run between villages for about 10 euros per hour of travel, give or take, and along the riverbank from Atjoni to Awarradam you can find dozens of small lodges and stay in the homes and villages of Saramaka families for days or weeks. This is not a theme park of rainforest tourism. It is daily life in communities that negotiated their freedom centuries ago and have been maintaining it since.

## Kwamalasamutu and the Werehpai Grottoes

Deep in the far southwest, in Sipaliwini district, is the Amerindian village of Kwamalasamutu - home to about 1,000 people from around ten different tribes. The location is extreme: visitors arrive by a two-hour flight from Paramaribo to an inland airstrip, then boat 20 minutes through jungle. What waits at the end is the Werehpai grottoes, a set of caves containing rock engravings estimated to be 5,000 years old. Modern science did not discover them until the year 2000. The carvings are still being studied. The Iwaaana Saamu Rainforest Lodge, small and owned by the local community, accommodates the few tourists who make the trip. A four-day visit includes meeting the shaman, a music night, canoe trips on the Sipaliwini River, and the grottoes themselves. The cost - around 1,200 euros including the flight - reflects the logistics, not the attraction. The attraction is the kind of experience that does not get inventoried at most prices.

## The Settlement That Time Buried

About 50 kilometers from Paramaribo sits Jodensavanne - the Jewish savanna - the ruined historic settlement of Sephardic Jews who first arrived in 1650 and built a thriving plantation community around the synagogue Berachah Veshalom, completed in 1685. For over a century it was one of the most important Jewish communities in the Americas. As Paramaribo developed, residents drifted to the city, returning only for holidays. A 1832 fire gutted most of the buildings. The synagogue limped on until around 1860, when the community effectively ended. The jungle took the ruins back. Restoration began in the 1970s. Since 2023 the Jodensavanne Settlement and the nearby Cassipora Creek Cemetery have been inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The stone synagogue foundations, visible today, are what remains of 300 years of Jewish Surinamese life - built, flourished, dispersed, overgrown, and remembered in that order.

## Flight Context

The Surinamese Rainforest covers most of the country south of the coastal strip, centered around 3.67 degrees N, 55.75 degrees W. Recommended viewing altitude 6,000-12,000 feet gives a sense of the canopy's continuous extent. The Brokopondo Reservoir is a prominent feature at 4.5 degrees N, 55.0 degrees W with visible dead-tree canopies. Johan Adolf Pengel International (ICAO: SMJP) is the nearest major airport. Inland airstrips served by Blue Wing Airlines, Gum Air, and Caricom Airways provide access to remote reserves. Julianatop (1,280 m) is in the southern mountain ranges.
