# Surinamese Rainforest

Three inland districts cover most of Suriname, most of them untouched forest, with a reservoir of some 1,560 square kilometers and mountains barely anyone has climbed.

**Location:** 3.6724, -55.7501

**Categories:** rainforest, suriname, amazon, protected-areas, unesco-heritage, maroon-communities

## Introduction

Suriname's three inland districts - Brokopondo, Para, and Sipaliwini - hold what lies behind the coastal strip: mostly uncut Amazon, a reservoir big enough to show up on world maps, a mountain called Julianatop that tops 1,280 meters, and tens of thousands of Saramaka Maroons living along rivers in villages their ancestors founded after escaping plantation slavery some three hundred years ago. This is where the country gets away from the city and starts being, mostly, trees.

## The Drowned Forest

Lake Brokopondo, officially the Prof. Dr. Ir. W.J. van Blommesteinmeer, is one of the largest reservoirs in the world at 1,560 square kilometers. It filled after 1964, when the Afobaka Dam blocked the Suriname River to generate electricity for aluminum refining. The flooding forced about 5,000 people, mostly Saramaka and Ndyuka Maroons, from their villages; most rebuilt downstream, often under the old village names. Most of the trees were never cleared before the water rose. Their dead tops still punch through the surface today, a forest that died standing up and refuses to fall. Former hilltops are now islands, some with tourist facilities. The town of Brokopondo sits on the west bank of the Suriname River about 3 kilometers below the dam, with a beach on the river and boat trips out to the lake's islands. It is a landscape where you can swim in what used to be a hilltop, sleep on what used to be a mountainside, and look across water toward trees whose roots are still in their original soil beneath the lake.

## Central Suriname Nature Reserve

The UNESCO World Heritage Site known as the Central Suriname Nature Reserve protects 1.6 million hectares of primary tropical forest - an area roughly the size of New Jersey. It links three older reserves - Raleighvallen in the north, Tafelberg in the center, and the Eilerts de Haan mountains in the south - into a single continuous corridor. About 80 percent of Suriname is covered in this kind of moist highland forest. The Raleighvallen reserve sits along the Coppename River, where the Raleigh Falls drop in a series of rapids near Fungo Island. Climb the Voltzberg, a granite dome that rises above the forest canopy, and you see monkeys, toucans, macaws, and the spectacular cock-of-the-rock bird. Jaguars live here but rarely show themselves to visitors. Surveys of the Lely and Nassau plateaus of eastern Suriname in 2005 and 2006 turned up 24 species believed new to science. The forest is still giving up its secrets because so few people have ever gone looking.

## Villages on the Upper Suriname

The Upper Suriname region begins south of Lake Brokopondo and extends along the Gran Rio and Pikin Rio rivers, which rise in the Eilerts de Haan Mountains. Here and around the lake live roughly 28,500 Saramaka Maroons (a 2013 estimate), descendants of Africans who escaped plantation slavery in the late 17th and early 18th centuries, fought for close to a century, and won a treaty recognizing their freedom in 1762. Their culture, built from many African traditions in long autonomy, is among the most distinctive in the Americas. Pokigron, also called Atjoni, is the last place a road reaches from Paramaribo - a ride of about 184 kilometers. From Pokigron you take a boat. River taxis run between some villages, and along the riverbank from Atjoni to Awarradam you can find many small lodges and stay in the homes and villages of Saramaka families for days or weeks. This is not a theme park of rainforest tourism. It is daily life in communities that negotiated their freedom centuries ago and have been maintaining it since.

## Kwamalasamutu and the Werehpai Grottoes

Deep in the far southwest, in Sipaliwini district, is the Indigenous village of Kwamalasamutu - home to about 1,000 people, mostly Tiriyó, with families from several other peoples. The location is extreme: visitors arrive by a two-hour flight from Paramaribo to the village airstrip, then continue by boat. What waits at the end is the Werehpai grottoes, a set of caves containing rock engravings estimated to be 5,000 years old. Modern science did not discover them until the year 2000. The carvings are still being studied. A small community-owned lodge, about 20 minutes by boat from the village, has hosted the few tourists who make the trip, usually on organised tours of several days that include canoe trips on the Sipaliwini River and the grottoes themselves. The high cost reflects the logistics, not the attraction. The attraction is the kind of experience that does not get inventoried at most prices.

## The Settlement That Time Buried

About 50 kilometers from Paramaribo sits Jodensavanne - the Jewish savanna - the ruined historic settlement of Sephardic Jews who began arriving in Suriname around 1650 and built a thriving plantation community, worked by enslaved Africans, around the synagogue Berachah Veshalom, completed in 1685. For over a century it was one of the most important Jewish communities in the Americas. As Paramaribo developed, residents drifted to the city, returning only for holidays. A 1832 fire gutted most of the buildings. The synagogue limped on until around 1860, when the community effectively ended. The jungle took the ruins back. Restoration began in the 1970s. Since 2023 the Jodensavanne Settlement and the nearby Cassipora Creek Cemetery have been inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The stone synagogue foundations, visible today, are what remains of two centuries of life in the settlement - built, flourished, dispersed, overgrown, and remembered in that order.

## Flight Context

The Surinamese Rainforest covers most of the country south of the coastal strip, centered around 3.67 degrees N, 55.75 degrees W. Recommended viewing altitude 6,000-12,000 feet gives a sense of the canopy's continuous extent. The Brokopondo Reservoir is a prominent feature around 4.8 degrees N, 55.1 degrees W with visible dead-tree canopies. Johan Adolf Pengel International (ICAO: SMJP) is the nearest major airport; flights to the interior leave from Paramaribo's Zorg en Hoop airport. Inland airstrips served by small carriers such as Blue Wing Airlines and Gum Air provide access to remote reserves. Julianatop (1,280 m) is in the southern mountain ranges.
