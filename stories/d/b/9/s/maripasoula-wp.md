# Maripasoula

The largest commune in France is a town you can only reach by plane or boat - 18,360 square kilometers of Amazon rainforest, gold panners, and Wayana villages.

**Location:** 3.6411, -54.0278

**Categories:** town, french-guiana, commune, remote, amazon

## Introduction

The largest commune in France has no road connecting it to the rest of France. It has no road connecting it to anything at all. To reach Maripasoula you take a small plane from Cayenne, or you pay for a seat on a motorized canoe coming upriver from Saint-Laurent-du-Maroni, bumping against rapids for a day or more before the town's airstrip and jetty appear out of the forest. The commune covers 18,360 square kilometers - larger than Kuwait, larger than a great many sovereign countries. Its population of fewer than 10,000 in the most recent official count is scattered across a handful of villages in territory that is almost entirely rainforest, and its western border is a river, the Lawa, separating it from Suriname, while its southern edge meets Brazil along the Tumuc-Humac highlands.

## Gold and the Far West

Maripasoula was founded as Upper Maroni at the end of the nineteenth century when prospectors discovered gold in the river gravel. The rush never fully ended. Today most of the gold-seekers working the Lawa and Maroni basins are Brazilian garimpeiros operating without French permits, their riverbank camps a constant headache for French gendarmes and military patrols. In the early 2000s, the crime associated with illegal mining earned Maripasoula the nickname "Far West" - a direct borrowing of American frontier mythology, which fit the place uncomfortably well. Crackdowns on illegal mining pushed supply settlements such as Antonio do Brinco onto the Surinamese bank of the Lawa, where shops, bars and restaurants draw prospectors and Maripasoula residents alike because prices are so much lower. The money is cash. The questions are few. The forest absorbs what it needs to absorb.

## The People of the River

Maripasoula's population is one of the most distinctive in all of France. The largest groups are the Aluku (also called Boni) - Maroon descendants of Africans who escaped slavery in Dutch Suriname and established free communities in the forest during the eighteenth century - and the Wayana, an Amerindian people whose language is Cariban and whose villages line the upper Maroni. Saint Lucian and Guadeloupean Creoles round out the mix along with metropolitan French administrators, teachers, and security personnel. In 1945 the town centre held just 45 people; the entire region counted 952. The population reached 5,545 by 2007 and 13,227 by 2017, before later census counts revised it down to about 9,600. Outside the administrative centre, most of the commune lives tribally in villages named Antécume-Pata, Élahé, Kayodé, Talhuwen, Kulumuli, Pilima, and several others - communities governed by their own structures alongside French civil law.

## Getting In, Getting Around

Maripasoula Airport handles the commercial flights - long served by a single airline - that constitute the primary link to the outside world. The Maroni River is the alternative: long pirogues with outboard motors making the journey upstream from Apatou or Saint-Laurent, a trip of many hours punctuated by rapids where passengers sometimes portage around the worst stretches. A road project launched in the summer of 2020 aimed to pave the dirt track between Maripasoula and Papaïchton by 2021, part of a larger plan to eventually extend the Route Nationale here from Saint-Laurent. That plan remains incomplete. The road between Maripasoula and Papaïchton runs about 34 kilometers, and its 32-million-euro budget works out to roughly a million euros per kilometer. Far-interior infrastructure is expensive.

## The Highest Mountain

The commune contains Bellevue de l'Inini, the highest point in French Guiana at 851 meters. It is not a tall mountain by global standards, and it stands deep in roadless forest, far from any village. Most of Maripasoula's southern territory falls within the Guiana Amazonian Park, a biodiversity reserve covering a substantial chunk of France's most ecologically rich territory. The forest is Amazonian. The biodiversity numbers - undocumented in many of their particulars - rank among the highest in the world. Near the Maroni, petroglyphs on riverside rocks depict animals and human figures, and the Roches gravées de la crique du Marouini, engraved rocks on a creek in the commune, have been a listed historic monument since 1995.

## The People Who Left

Cyrille Regis, the English footballer who played for West Bromwich Albion, Coventry City, and the England national team, was born in Maripasoula in 1958. Regis's father was a labourer from Saint Lucia, and the family followed him to London in 1963, when Cyrille was five. Regis became only the third Black player capped by England's senior team, winning five caps between 1982 and 1987. He endured racist abuse from opposing fans throughout the 1970s and 1980s and became, along with Laurie Cunningham and Brendon Batson, part of the West Bromwich "Three Degrees", three Black players who faced down racism in English top-flight football. He died in 2018. Jacobin Yoma, European super-featherweight boxing champion from 1993 to 1995, was also born in Maripasoula. A town this remote has produced two Europe-class athletes - proof that even the farthest commune in the Republic is not as cut off as its geography might suggest.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 3.641°N, 54.028°W. Recommended viewing altitude 8,000-12,000 feet for a sense of the commune's immense scale - it is larger than Kuwait. Landmarks: the Lawa River (western border with Suriname), the Maroni River downstream, Bellevue de l'Inini (highest point in French Guiana at 851 m), and the village pattern along the Maroni's upper reaches. Nearest airport: Maripasoula Airport (SOOA), single small airstrip. Weather: hot and humid, with rain year-round and a drier season from about August to November. Canopy cover dominant; rivers provide the main visible infrastructure from the air.
