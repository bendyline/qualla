# Benzdorp

A rainforest gold-mining outpost on the Lawa River, named for a Dutch bullion dealer and once arbitrated by a Russian tsar.

**Location:** 3.6739, -54.0850

**Categories:** gold mining, frontier towns, rainforest, history

## Introduction

In May 1891, a Russian tsar drew a line through a South American jungle he would never visit. Alexander III, asked to settle a colonial border dispute over a patch of rainforest between the Lawa and Tapanahony rivers, ruled in Suriname's favor. Six years earlier, around 1885, prospectors had found gold in those creeks. Decades later, a village named for a Dutch consul would stand on a riverbank that a Romanov pen had locked inside Dutch territory. Benzdorp still exists, still chases gold, and still watches the Lawa slide past toward French Guiana on the opposite shore.

## A Tsar's Pencil on the Map

The gold rush arrived before the border did. When prospectors struck gold near the Lawa around 1885, no one was quite sure whose colony they were staking. The Dutch held that the border followed the Lawa, the French that it followed the Tapanahony, and the land between the two rivers was in dispute; the case went to St. Petersburg for arbitration. Alexander III's May 1891 ruling made the Lawa the border and handed the contested land to Suriname, where Henry Albert Wilhelm Benz (1889-1962), a Dutch consul and bullion dealer, eventually gave his name to a landing on the riverbank. Governor Cornelis Lely bet on engineering: in 1902 he decided the government would build the Lawa Railway to carry gold some 350 kilometers to Paramaribo. Only about half the line was ever laid, south from Paramaribo; disappointing gold finds stopped it in the forest, far short of the Lawa.

## The Village That Moved

Around 1974, only about ten people remained at the original Benzdorp. The village seemed finished - a landing on the Lawa with nothing left to mine. Then, from the early 1990s, a new wave arrived: garimpeiros from Brazil and gowtuman, Surinamese miners, pushing inland through the forest. They founded a new settlement kilometers from the river and kept the old name. Today the original riverside site is called simply "the landing," while the working Benzdorp sits deeper in the trees, closer to the seams. Two villages, one name, split across a generation and a few kilometers of jungle. Upstream lies Anapaike; downstream, Cottica. Across the Lawa to the southeast is the French commune of Maripasoula - a foreign country reachable by dugout canoe.

## Fatu Switie and the Forest Floor

Southwest of the village, a ridge rises to about 375 meters above sea level. Fatu Switie is modest by Andean standards but dramatic here, where the forest is otherwise unbroken green stretching in every direction. Nearby, the Oemankrassiabra rapids churn the Lawa white. The river is the road. Boats loaded with fuel, rice, and mining supplies work their way up and down the river, negotiating shallows and the constant pull of the current. Along this stretch of the Lawa live Aluku Maroon and Wayana communities - Cottica downstream, Anapaike upstream - and the wider Tapanahony resort is home to Ndyuka villages whose ancestors escaped enslavement on coastal plantations and built free societies in the interior in the eighteenth century. The gold economy runs alongside - sometimes through, sometimes against - those older communities. Much of the small-scale mining is informal and uses mercury to bind the gold, and the runoff contaminates the river and the fish that riverside villages depend on; France and Suriname have struggled for years to curb illegal mining along the border.

## Small Airstrip, Long Memory

The fastest way to Benzdorp is not the river but the sky. Lawa Antino Airstrip, a small gravel strip about nine kilometers west of the village, connects the gold fields to Paramaribo by bush plane. It is also where, on April 3, 2008, a Blue Wing Airlines Antonov An-28 crashed while trying to land, killing all 19 people aboard; early reports said the pilot had aborted the landing and hit high ground on the go-around. Five years later, in May 2013, a private helicopter flying an unregistered gold-transport run out of French Guiana came down near a mining camp called Boewese, and the injured pilot was taken to Maripasoula with a broken leg. Flying in the gold country is not forgiving. In May 2006, the Lawa itself overflowed its banks, flooding Benzdorp along with other Surinamese villages. The river gives and the river takes.

## Flying the Border

From the air, Benzdorp is a pale scar in an ocean of forest - the bright stain of pit mining surrounded by uninterrupted canopy. The Lawa River traces a dark, meandering line beneath the aircraft, and somewhere in that water runs the border between Suriname and French Guiana, invisible but absolute.

## Flight Context

Located at 3.67°N, 54.09°W. Benzdorp sits on the Suriname side of the Lawa River, which forms the border with French Guiana. Lawa Antino Airstrip (SMAN; gravel, 1,155 m) lies about 9 km west-southwest; the French airport at Maripasoula (SOOA) is about 5 km east-southeast on the opposite bank. Paramaribo's Johan Adolf Pengel International (SMJP) is about 230 km north-northwest. Recommended viewing altitude: 3,000-6,000 feet AGL to appreciate the forest canopy and the scarred mining pits. Expect scattered cumulus and afternoon rain showers during the wet season.
