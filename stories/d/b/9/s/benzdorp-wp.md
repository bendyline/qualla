# Benzdorp

A rainforest gold-mining outpost on the Lawa River, named for a Dutch bullion dealer and once arbitrated by a Russian tsar.

**Location:** 3.6739, -54.0850

**Categories:** gold mining, frontier towns, rainforest, history

## Introduction

In May 1891, a Russian tsar drew a line through a South American jungle he would never visit. Alexander III, asked to settle a colonial border dispute over a patch of rainforest between the Lawa and Tapanahony rivers, ruled in Suriname's favor. Six years earlier, in 1885, prospectors had found gold in those creeks. Now a village with a Dutch consul's name sat on a riverbank that a Romanov pen had locked inside Dutch territory. Benzdorp still exists, still chases gold, and still watches the Lawa slide past toward French Guiana on the opposite shore.

## A Tsar's Pencil on the Map

The gold rush arrived before the border did. When prospectors struck the Lawa in 1885, no one was quite sure whose colony they were staking. France claimed the east bank, the Dutch claimed the west, and both sides sent the dispute to St. Petersburg for arbitration. Alexander III's May 1891 ruling handed the contested watershed to Suriname, where Henry Albert Wilhelm Benz, a Dutch consul and bullion dealer, eventually gave his name to a landing on the riverbank. The gold proved thinner than the promise. Governor Cornelis Lely tried to answer the shortfall with engineering: in 1902 he authorized the Lawa Railway to haul bullion 350 kilometers north to Paramaribo. Only half the line was ever laid. The rails ran into disappointing seams and stopped in the forest, a half-finished promise rusting in the green.

## The Village That Moved

By 1974, fewer than a dozen people remained at the original Benzdorp. The village seemed finished - a landing on the Lawa with nothing left to mine. Then the price of gold rose, and in the early 1990s a new wave arrived: garimpeiros from Brazil and gowtuman, Surinamese miners, pushing inland through the forest with pumps and sluices. They founded a new settlement kilometers from the river and kept the old name. Today the original riverside site is called simply "the landing," while the working Benzdorp sits deeper in the trees, closer to the seams. Two villages, one name, split across a generation and a mile of jungle. Upstream lies Anapaike; downstream, Cottica. Directly across the Lawa, visible through the mist, is the French commune of Maripasoula - a foreign country reachable by dugout canoe.

## Fatu Switie and the Forest Floor

Southwest of the village, a ridge rises to about 375 meters above the canopy. Fatu Switie is modest by Andean standards but dramatic here, where the forest is otherwise unbroken green stretching in every direction. Nearby, the Oemankrassiabra rapids churn the Lawa white. The river is the road. Dugouts loaded with fuel, rice, and mining supplies work their way between Benzdorp and the coast, negotiating shallows and the constant pull of the current. The surrounding Tapanahony resort is home to Ndyuka Maroon communities whose ancestors escaped enslavement on coastal plantations and built free societies in this interior three centuries ago. The gold economy runs alongside - sometimes through, sometimes against - those older communities.

## Small Airstrip, Long Memory

The fastest way to Benzdorp is not the river but the sky. Lawa Antino Airport, a small airstrip about nine kilometers west of the village, connects the gold fields to Paramaribo by bush plane. It is also where, on April 3, 2008, a Blue Wing Airlines Twin Otter crashed on approach, killing all 19 people aboard. Five years later, in May 2013, a private helicopter flying an unregistered gold-transport run out of French Guiana came down near a mining camp called Boewese, the pilot walking away with a broken leg. The accidents are part of a pattern familiar to any frontier gold economy: pressure to move fast, fly cheap, skip paperwork, and pray the weather holds. In May 2006, the Lawa itself overflowed its banks, flooding Benzdorp along with other Surinamese villages. The river gives and the river takes.

## Flying the Border

From the air, Benzdorp is a pale scar in an ocean of forest - the bright stain of pit mining surrounded by uninterrupted canopy. The Lawa River traces a dark, meandering line beneath the aircraft, and somewhere in that water runs the border between Suriname and French Guiana, invisible but absolute.

## Flight Context

Located at 3.67°N, 54.09°W. Benzdorp sits on the Suriname side of the Lawa River, which forms the border with French Guiana. Lawa Antino Airport (no ICAO code; small bush strip) lies about 9 km west; the French airstrip at Maripasoula (SOOA) is on the opposite bank. Paramaribo's Johan Adolf Pengel International (SMJP) is 340 km northwest. Recommended viewing altitude: 3,000-6,000 feet AGL to appreciate the forest canopy and the scarred mining pits. Expect scattered cumulus and afternoon rain showers during the wet season.
