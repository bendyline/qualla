# Guiana Amazonian Park

The largest national park in France and the European Union is not in the Alps or the Pyrenees - it is a slab of Amazon rainforest larger than Belgium, reachable only by plane or dugout canoe.

**Location:** 2.8383, -53.7722

**Categories:** National Parks, Amazon Rainforest, French Guiana, Protected Areas, Biodiversity

## Introduction

The largest national park in France is not in Europe. It sits on the northern shoulder of South America, covering 41 percent of the overseas department of French Guiana - 33,900 square kilometers of rainforest, core and surrounding zone together - more than the entire surface area of Belgium. You cannot drive there. You cannot sail there from the coast. To enter, you go by pirogue up the Maroni or the Oyapock, or you charter a small plane to one of the handful of airstrips carved into the green. And somewhere in that canopy, a single hectare of forest frequently holds more tree species than the whole of Metropolitan France.

## An Equatorial Paris

The park traces its beginnings to 1992, the year of the Rio Earth Summit. President François Mitterrand pushed the idea forward on June 4 of that year, and a Mission for the Creation of the French Guiana National Park opened for business in 1993. The project then took nearly fifteen years. A first proposal was rejected in December 1997. The Twenké agreement of June 21, 1998 led to recognition of the rights of the Amerindian peoples and the Businengue Maroons living inside the proposed boundaries. A 2006 decree gave the project its name, Parc amazonien de Guyane, and the decree creating the park was finally signed on February 27, 2007 and published the next day - over the reluctance of French Guiana's general and regional councils.

## The People Inside the Map

The central zone of 20,300 square kilometers enforces strict protection. Gold mining is banned there - though illegal gold mining persists in the park's territory, and soldiers and gendarmes based in places like Camopi run continuing operations against it. But the Aluku, Wayana, Wayampi, and Teko peoples who have lived in these rivers and ridgelines for centuries are not simply residents of a French park. Their villages in the communes of Camopi, Maripasoula, and Papaïchton sit outside the protected core, because the Teko objected to having their movements restricted and the Aluku refused to let sacred places become regulated sites. Any conservation regime written from Paris has to account for the people who were here first. The arrangement is imperfect, the boundaries are contested, and the park administrators in Rémire-Montjoly know it. What exists on the map is a compromise between French environmental law and the idea that no one has ever conquered this forest.

## Thousands of Species

Biologists estimate that 4,000 to 5,000 species of vascular plants grow within the park, including more than a thousand tree species. The tallest trees, sandbox trees and kapoks, reach 55 to 65 meters, their crowns visible from the air as scattered emergents above the green. Beneath them move at least six species of New World monkey, among them the red-faced spider monkey and the Guianan red howler, whose calls can carry more than a kilometer through humid air. The park also counts 90 amphibians, 133 reptiles, 520 birds, 182 mammals, and more than 200 species of freshwater fish. Giant river otters hunt in the blackwater creeks. Harpy eagles, rare across much of their range, still find room here.

## Together With Tumucumaque

Across the border, in Brazil, lies Tumucumaque Mountains National Park - another 38,800 square kilometers of protected Amazon. The two parks touch. Put together, they form one of the largest tropical rainforest preserves on Earth, a trans-border green block of some 72,700 square kilometers. Jaguars, tapirs, and harpy eagles do not read passports, and neither does the river. The arrangement is not a formal treaty so much as a geographic accident made meaningful by two governments choosing, around the same time, to draw the same line on their side of the map.

## Seeing It

Most visitors never get past Saül, a village of around 150 souls near the park's geographic center. Saül is a former gold-rush settlement reachable only by small plane from Cayenne, and 45 kilometers of signposted trails radiate out from its airstrip. Five of those trails are engineered for visitors with reduced mobility under France's Amazon for All program - a surreal thing to contemplate in the middle of the Guianan Shield. Other trails in the park lead up Mont Galbao, the inselberg Susu Bella, or to Gobaya Soula falls - or you can simply stand in a forest and listen to a soundscape that humans have barely altered in ten thousand years.

## Flight Context

The park is centered near 2.84°N, 53.77°W, covering much of southern French Guiana (about 41 percent of the territory). Saül's airstrip (SOOS), about 105 km northeast of this point, sits in the park's central sector; Maripasoula (SOOA, about 95 km north-northwest) serves the western side; Camopi's small airstrip, without scheduled service, the eastern. Recommended viewing altitude 8,000-10,000 ft for canopy detail, higher for scale. Principal gateway: Cayenne – Félix Eboué (SOCA / CAY) on the coast, about 270 km northeast. Afternoon convective buildups over the forest are routine; morning flights are calmer.
