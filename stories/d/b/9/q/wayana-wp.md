# Wayana

A Carib-speaking people who became blood brothers with escaped slaves, survived diseases that cut their number by two-thirds, and still speak their own language across three countries.

**Location:** 4.0267, -54.3328

**Categories:** indigenous, french-guiana, suriname, brazil, culture, amazon

## Introduction

In 1815, two peoples who had fought their way to the same stretch of river agreed to stop being strangers. The Wayana, Carib-speakers driven south across the Tumuk Humak Mountains by Tupi rivals, and the Aluku Maroons, descendants of people who had fled Dutch plantations and been chased upstream by Ndyuka allies of the colony, declared themselves blood brothers. They had been trading and sometimes sharing villages for decades. The formal declaration said what everyone had already decided: on the Litani River, survival was going to be a joint project.

## A People Made of Peoples

The Wayana as we know them today are what anthropologists call an amalgamation - a coming-together of older groups like the Upului, Opagwana, and Kukuyana, who combined under a shared name in the eighteenth century. In earlier times the ancestors of the Wayana lived farther east, along the Paru and Jari rivers in what is now Brazil and along the upper Oyapock, the river that today marks the border between French Guiana and Brazil. The first European mention of them comes from 1769. By then the Wayampi, a Tupi-speaking people pushing south and east, had forced them across the mountains. What might have been a disaster instead became the start of something new.

## The Aluku Alliance

The Aluku were Maroons - West Africans and their descendants who had escaped slavery on the Surinamese plantations and retreated deep into the interior. The Dutch colonial forces and their Ndyuka allies, who had settled for peace in exchange for hunting down later runaways, drove the Aluku up the Litani River. There they met the Wayana. A relationship built first on trade thickened into something more substantial - shared villages, shared rituals, and eventually the blood-brother declaration of 1815. Over the following decades the two peoples drifted together down the Litani and Lawa rivers. In 1865, the Ndyuka granman Alabi invited a Wayana group still living along the Paru in Brazil to join them along the Tapanahony in Suriname. That Wayana community is still there today, in villages along the Tapanahony and Palumeu rivers.

## The Diseases

The twentieth century brought the catastrophe that contact had mostly spared them. Imported diseases swept the villages. A population that was probably around 1,500 was reduced to an estimated 500 or 600 people - a loss of roughly two-thirds in a few decades. From 1962 onward, American missionaries from the West-Indies Mission, who had previously worked with the neighboring Tiriyó, arrived in Wayana country. They brought health care and schooling, and encouraged people to concentrate into larger, more accessible villages - which also made the work of conversion easier. The French side of the border, once the autonomous Territory of Inini, was absorbed in 1969 into the commune system of French Guiana, bringing with it state structures, taxes, and the pressure of francisation. Today the Wayana number perhaps 1,500 again, split roughly 1,000 in French Guiana, 400 in Suriname, and 150 in Brazil among the Apalai.

## Ëputop

For centuries, Wayana boys and girls became adults through a ritual called ëputop, or maraké: a woven wicker frame filled with stinging ants or wasps would be pressed against the initiate's body, and the pain they endured made them Wayana. Older members of the community still measure their Wayanahood partly by how many ëputop they have completed. Younger Wayana are increasingly skeptical - the ritual is not, they argue, a necessary part of being a valued member of the community. Today ëputop ceremonies are rare. One of the more recent took place in 2004 in the village of Talhuwen, organized by Aïmawale Opoya, grandson of the Wayana leader Janomalë, in collaboration with the French filmmaker Jean-Philippe Isel, who filmed it for a documentary. The French Ministry of Culture added ëputop to its inventory of intangible cultural heritage in 2011 - a bittersweet recognition for a practice that is being chosen less and less.

## Villages Without a King

Traditional Wayana politics did not recognize authority above the village level. Each village - often just an extended family gathered around a shaman or pïyai who mediates with spirits, and a community house called a tukusipan - ran itself through kinship, marriage, and shared ritual. When a leader died, the village often simply moved. The colonial states found this inconvenient. Suriname, France, and Brazil all installed head captains and granman above the village level, hoping to negotiate through single designated leaders. The authority of these appointees has always been limited, because the Wayana do not agree to paramount chiefs in principle. In Suriname, Kananu Apetina was head captain on the Tapanahony until his death in 1975; his successor was recognized as granman of all Surinamese Wayana until he died in 2023. In French Guiana, the current granman is Amaipotï, son of the first granman Twenkë, who resides in the village of Kulumuli. His authority depends on what each village chooses to grant him.
