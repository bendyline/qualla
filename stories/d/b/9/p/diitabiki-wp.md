# Diitabiki

Deep in Suriname's interior, a village of three islands is the seat of the Ndyuka Maroon people - descendants of those who walked out of slavery and into the rainforest, and never walked back.

**Location:** 4.1139, -54.6761

**Categories:** Maroon settlements, Suriname, Rivers, Indigenous peoples

## Introduction

Diitabiki means "three islands" in Ndyuka, and in Sranan Tongo, and in both languages the word *tabiki* means island. The Dutch, coming late and listening imperfectly, heard *drietabbetje* and wrote it that way on their maps - a word that sounds like their own but belongs to neither language. The village the name describes sits where the Tapanahoni River widens and splits, deep in the Sipaliwini forest of southern Suriname. It is, since 1950, the residence of the *gaanman* - the paramount chief - of the Ndyuka people, and the home of their oracle. Almost everything important about Diitabiki is in that one small fact.

## A People Who Walked Into the Forest

The Ndyuka are a Maroon people. Their ancestors were kidnapped in West and Central Africa in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, shipped across the Atlantic in chains, and sold to Dutch planters growing sugar and coffee along Suriname's coast. Some refused to live that way. They escaped into the rainforest singly and in groups, gathered there, built villages, fought off the expeditions sent after them, and founded a new people out of the pieces they could carry - West African languages reshaped into Ndyuka, spirit practices adapted to a new continent, a form of self-rule answerable to no European government. Clashes with the Dutch continued for decades. In 1760, exhausted on both sides, colonists and Ndyuka signed a peace treaty. It granted the Ndyuka something very few escaped-slave communities in the Americas ever obtained: autonomy, recognized on paper, holding until today.

## Settling the Tapanahoni

After 1761 the Ndyuka moved steadily southward, looking for ground the planters could not reach. They settled along the Tapanahoni River, founding a chain of villages of which Diitabiki is one. The land was already inhabited - the Tiriyó people had been there for generations - and the arrival of the Ndyuka displaced them further into the interior, a hard chapter in a story usually told as liberation. Near the confluence of the Tapanahoni and the Lawa, more recently escaped Africans from Armina and the Boven Commewijne plantations were stationed as guards against raids from the Aluku, another Maroon people. In the forest, borders between peoples were drawn in blood and river bends rather than on paper, and the lines kept shifting.

## The Seat of the Gaanman

Since 1950, Diitabiki has been home to the gaanman, the paramount chief who sits at the top of the Ndyuka political and religious order. The village also holds the oracle - a sacred institution that guides the people's decisions through rituals of consultation that outsiders rarely see and never fully understand. This makes Diitabiki more than a village; it is a capital, in a people's sense, for a nation whose authority is not mapped in any atlas. Everyday life runs alongside this larger role. Since the 1960s children have attended the Granman Akontoe Velantie Primary School, named for a former chief. As of 2015 it had 333 pupils, and when the building became too crowded, village volunteers built an extra classroom themselves. A Medische Zending clinic provides healthcare, part of the small network serving Suriname's interior.

## Power and its Cost

From 1985 to 1987 an overhead line brought electricity to Diitabiki from the Puketi hydroelectric plant. Then the Surinamese Interior War broke the power plant, and the lights went out. Proposals came and went. A university delegation surveyed the rivers in 2003 and 2004. The Ministry for Regional Development decided instead to build a new, larger hydroelectric plant at the Gran Olo rapids, which would initially power Puketi and Futupasi and eventually, someday, Diitabiki again. As of 2016 the Gran Olo plant was still under construction. In the meantime life at Diitabiki goes on the way life in the interior has always gone on - with kerosene and generators and the long patience of people who learned long ago not to wait on distant capitals.

## Getting There

There are no roads. Blue Wings and Gum Air fly small planes into the Drietabbetje Airstrip from Paramaribo. By river, the Granholo waterfalls block boat traffic, so a short rail line at Futupasi carries goods around them, and a ferry has run to Puketi and Godo Holo since February 2008. Ecotourism is being cautiously promoted. Visitors come to see the river, the forest, and a living culture that has lasted more than 250 years outside the plantations from which its founders fled. To arrive is to understand something the maps miss: that in this part of South America, some of the oldest and most continuous self-government belongs not to the colonial states but to the people who left them behind.

## Flight Context

Diitabiki sits at 4.11°N, 54.68°W on the Tapanahoni River in Suriname's Sipaliwini District, roughly 230 km south of Paramaribo. Drietabbetje Airstrip (SMDA) serves the village with scheduled light-aircraft flights. Nearest major airport is Johan Adolf Pengel International (SMJP / PBM) at Paramaribo-Zanderij. Visual landmarks: the three river islands that give the village its name, and the long braid of the Tapanahoni winding north through unbroken rainforest.
