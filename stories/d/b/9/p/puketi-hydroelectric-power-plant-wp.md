# Puketi hydroelectric power plant

Deep in the Surinamese interior, a 50-kilowatt cross-flow turbine lit up two Maroon villages for six years — then a civil war cut the line, and the jungle took back what it briefly loaned out.

**Location:** 4.1233, -54.6317

**Categories:** infrastructure, suriname, maroon-communities, energy-history

## Introduction

On 7 December 1981, President Henk Chin A Sen flew into a clearing deep in the Surinamese rainforest and threw a switch. A single cross-flow turbine — a clever little Michell-Bánki machine no bigger than a diesel generator — started pushing water-made electricity into the houses of Puketi and Futupasi. Lightbulbs came on in villages where lightbulbs had never come on before. Fifty kilowatts is a modest number. For the Ndyuka Maroon families whose forebears had built this place as a refuge from slavery, it was a new kind of daylight. The plant ran for six years. Then a civil war came through the interior, the technicians in Paramaribo couldn't reach the site anymore, and the turbine went quiet somewhere in 1987. It has not run since.

## A Different Kind of Infrastructure

Most of the big hydroelectric stories in the Americas involve flooded valleys, displaced communities, and concrete poured in geological quantities. The Puketi plant was the opposite of that — a micro-hydro facility designed for a specific forest, at a specific waterfall, serving a specific handful of villages. Construction began in 1980 and finished in 1981. The turbine ran off a hydraulic head of between 2.5 and 4.6 meters and a maximum flow of 1,390 liters per second. The original plan had been industrial: power a cassava mill and a sawmill. Those uses got abandoned. What came instead was more ordinary and more transformative — houses. Officers from the Dienst Elektriciteitsvoorziening in Paramaribo traveled periodically upriver to read the meters and collect the bills, a bureaucratic ritual that carried the capital's logic into the forest and, in the doing, made the forest part of the national grid for the first time.

## The Neighbors Wanted In

Electricity has a way of not staying put. The moment the lights came on in Puketi and Futupasi, villages along the upper Tapanahony River started pressing for connections of their own. In 1985, crews began running a seven-kilometer, 12-kilovolt overhead line toward Diitabiki, the Ndyuka capital — with planned extensions to Moitaki, Jawsa, and Mainsi. The dream was a forest grid. The engineering did not quite support the dream. The combined demand of the villages ran past the 33-kilowatt average the plant actually produced, so lights flickered and dimmed at peak hours. Worse, the overhead line was strung without lightning conductors, which in a rainforest with regular electrical storms amounted to an expensive invitation for outages. The system worked, in the sense that it delivered some electricity some of the time, but it was living on borrowed reliability.

## War in the Interior

Then came the Surinamese Interior War. Between 1986 and 1992, the interior — the forested heartland of the Maroon peoples — became the front line of a conflict between the government in Paramaribo and the Jungle Commando led by Ronnie Brunswijk. Villages emptied. Travel became dangerous. Technicians from Paramaribo could no longer reach sites in rebel territory, and the Puketi plant, dependent on periodic maintenance, simply stopped somewhere in 1987. Fuses blew, breakers tripped, bearings wore out, and nobody could get in to fix anything. When the war ended, the infrastructure for reaching the plant had rotted along with the plant itself. The Maroon villages that had briefly lit up returned to the older rhythms of candle and generator — and to rebuilding what the war had broken.

## Plans on Paper, Plans on Paper

In 1995, INGRO NV Engineering consultants proposed rehabilitating the Puketi plant. Nothing much came of it. In September 2003, a delegation from the Anton de Kom University of Suriname visited the site under Professor Sieuwnath Naipal, sent by the Ministry of Regional Development to assess the region's hydro potential. Naipal's team returned in February 2004 and concluded that nearby sites could generate more power. On their advice, the ministry shifted its attention to the planned Gran Olo hydroelectric plant a little further down the river. The old Puketi turbine sits where it was left — possibly rehabilitable, per hydrologist Rudi van Els, but not a priority. It is a common story in the history of rural electrification: the small thing that worked, the bigger thing that was supposed to replace it, the forest slowly taking both.

## What Six Years of Light Meant

It is easy, from the distance of an aviation chart, to see the Puketi plant as a footnote — fifty kilowatts, six years, a single Michell-Bánki cross-flow turbine running on a tropical stream in a country of 600,000 people. But the people in Puketi and Futupasi remember the ceremony of 7 December 1981. They remember reading at night. They remember the refrigerators and the small shops that opened with the lights. Six years of electricity arrived, then left, in a place where the rainforest still writes most of the rules. The plant's bones are still out there at 4.12°N, 54.63°W, where a small Surinamese river drops enough to make light, if anyone ever comes back to ask it.
