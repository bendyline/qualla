# Prison of St-Laurent-du-Maroni

For nearly a century, every man France condemned to the bagne passed through this sorting yard on the banks of the Maroni — a town built by prisoners, for prisoners, where even the guards had once worn the stripes.

**Location:** 5.5043, -54.0326

**Categories:** history, penal-colonies, french-guiana, colonial-history

## Introduction

The ships came in from the sea up the Maroni River and tied up at a dock that the prisoners themselves had built. There were usually several hundred men on board, chained in the hold for weeks out of Saint-Martin-de-Ré. The doors opened, and France dumped its condemned onto the bank of a jungle river in a colony most of them had never heard of. This was Saint-Laurent-du-Maroni, and from its founding in 1858 until its closure in 1946 it was the clearing house for the entire French Guiana bagne. Every story the world ever heard about Devil's Island — Alfred Dreyfus, Henri Charrière, René Belbenoit, the Dry Guillotine — started at this sorting yard on the river.

## Napoleon III's Economy Plan

On 22 November 1850, Napoleon III offered a cost-benefit analysis dressed as humanism. Six thousand condemned men, he said, weighed heavily on the French budget, becoming increasingly depraved and menacing society. Forced labour, redirected to colonial development, would be more effective, more moralising, less expensive and more humane. The first batch left Brest for the Salvation Islands on 31 March 1852. Six years later, on 21 February 1858, the bagne opened a sorting prison on the left bank of the Maroni River, just opposite Dutch Suriname. The river was the main road. The town of Saint-Laurent-du-Maroni proper was founded on 16 March 1880 — a town whose residents were almost entirely guards and freed prisoners called libérés, men who had finished their sentences but under French law could never go home.

## Sorting the Condemned

When La Loire or La Martinière docked, the first task was to separate the chevaux de retour — literally "returned horses," the men who had escaped before and been caught — along with recidivists. These went immediately to the Îles du Salut offshore, where the ocean made escape almost impossible. New arrivals stayed at Saint-Laurent for sorting. The men judged shifty and dangerous joined the escapees on the islands. The least threatening were offered jobs in the penitentiary administration, which sounds like mercy and was, sometimes. Special camps existed for prisoners from Indochina. Arab convicts often served as guards. The penal system's logic was not one of rehabilitation. It was one of sorting — finding who could be used, who could be controlled, and who was worth letting die.

## The Hospital and the Lucky

Saint-Laurent had a hospital, built in 1912. The prisoners figured out quickly that a hospital bed was the best real estate in the colony: better food, more space, time to think, and — crucially — time to plan. Henri Charrière, whose memoir Papillon became a worldwide book and film, faked illnesses to get inside. Many hospital workers were prisoners themselves, which made the faking easier. Men who drew the Saint-Laurent assignment rather than an outer camp were the lucky ones in a system where luck was measured in millimeters. Their work was simpler. They could move around the prison enclosure. They ate better rations. But the label "lucky" has to be used carefully here — it means you might survive. Elsewhere, survival was not on offer.

## Camp of Death

The worst of the outer camps was Camp Charvein, in the forest upriver, known to everyone who heard of it as Camp de la Mort — the camp of death. Men were sent there to be worked to extinction in the logging operations. They labored naked in the heat and the mosquitoes, with minimal rations, under guards who did not expect to see them alive at the end of the month. Most did not see the end of the month. The bagne was abolished in 1946, the same year the prison closed, and the last prisoners drifted out through the 1950s. Some of the buildings were restored in the early 1980s and now welcome visitors as a museum. You can walk into the Quartier Spécial, the condemned men's block, and stand on the patch of ground where the guillotine once waited.

## What the River Remembers

Saint-Laurent today is French Guiana's second-largest town, a riverside crossroads where Ndyuka Maroons cross to Albina in Suriname on long canoes, where Haitian and Brazilian migrants try their luck, and where the descendants of libérés still live. The old administrative buildings line the waterfront in a pale colonial formality that does not quite hide what they were. Every river town has a past, but few have one this bluntly preserved. The men who passed through here were condemned by a legal system that mostly catalogued them as numbers. The town that grew up around them has chosen, carefully and with some discomfort, to remember them as names.

## Flight Context
