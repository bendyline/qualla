# Prison of St-Laurent-du-Maroni

For nearly a century, almost every convict France transported to the bagne passed through this sorting yard on the banks of the Maroni — a town built by prisoners, for prisoners, where some of the guards were convicts themselves.

**Location:** 5.5043, -54.0326

**Categories:** history, penal-colonies, french-guiana, colonial-history

## Introduction

The ships came in from the sea up the Maroni River and tied up at a dock that the prisoners themselves had built. There were usually several hundred men on board, held below deck for weeks on the voyage from Saint-Martin-de-Ré. The doors opened, and France dumped its condemned onto the bank of a jungle river in a colony most of them had never heard of. This was Saint-Laurent-du-Maroni, and from its founding in 1858 until its closure in 1946 it was the clearing house for the entire French Guiana bagne. Most of the stories the world heard about the French Guiana bagne - Henri Charrière's Papillon, René Belbenoit's Dry Guillotine - started at this sorting yard on the river.

## Napoleon III's Economy Plan

On 22 November 1850, Louis-Napoléon Bonaparte, then President of the Republic and soon to be Emperor Napoleon III, offered a cost-benefit analysis dressed as humanism. Six thousand condemned men, he said, weighed heavily on the French budget, becoming increasingly depraved and menacing society. Forced labour, redirected to colonial development, would be more effective, more moralising, less expensive and more humane. The first batch left Brest for the Salvation Islands on 31 March 1852. Six years later, on 21 February 1858, the bagne opened a sorting prison on the right (east) bank of the Maroni River, just opposite Dutch Suriname. The river was the main road. In 1880 Saint-Laurent-du-Maroni became a special penitentiary commune - a town whose residents were almost entirely guards and freed prisoners called libérés, men who had finished their sentences but, under the bagne's doubling rule, had to stay on in the colony, many of them for life.

## Sorting the Condemned

When La Loire or La Martinière docked, the first task was to separate the chevaux de retour — literally "returned horses," the men who had escaped before and been caught — along with recidivists. These went immediately to the Îles du Salut offshore, where the ocean made escape almost impossible. New arrivals stayed at Saint-Laurent for sorting. The men judged most dangerous joined the escapees on the islands. Those convicted of lesser offences were offered jobs in the penitentiary administration, which sounds like mercy and was, sometimes. Special camps existed for prisoners from Indochina. Arab convicts often served as guards. The penal system's logic was not one of rehabilitation. It was one of sorting — finding who could be used, who could be controlled, and who was worth letting die.

## The Hospital and the Lucky

Saint-Laurent had a hospital, built in 1912. The prisoners figured out quickly that a hospital bed was the best real estate in the colony: better food, more space, time to think, and — crucially — time to plan. Henri Charrière, whose memoir Papillon became a worldwide book and film, described faking illness to get inside. Many hospital workers were prisoners themselves, which made the faking easier. Men who drew the Saint-Laurent assignment rather than an outer camp were the lucky ones in a system where luck was measured in millimeters. Their work was simpler. They could move around the prison enclosure. They ate better rations. But the label "lucky" has to be used carefully here — it means you might survive. Elsewhere, survival was far less certain.

## Camp of Death

The most feared of the outer camps was Camp Charvein, in the forest outside town, known to everyone who heard of it as Camp de la Mort - the camp of death. Men sent there cut timber in the heat and the mosquitoes on minimal rations, and many did not survive it. New transportation to the bagne ended under a 1938 decree-law, the bagne was closed in 1946, the same year the prison closed, and the last 132 convicts left French Guiana in August 1953. Some of the buildings were restored in the early 1980s and now welcome visitors as a museum. You can walk into the Quartier Spécial, the condemned men's block, and stand on the patch of ground where the guillotine once waited.

## What the River Remembers

Saint-Laurent today is French Guiana's second-largest town, a riverside crossroads where Ndyuka Maroons cross to Albina in Suriname on long canoes, where Haitian and Brazilian migrants try their luck, and where the descendants of libérés still live. The old administrative buildings line the waterfront in a pale colonial formality that does not quite hide what they were. Every river town has a past, but few have one this bluntly preserved. The men who passed through here were condemned by a legal system that mostly catalogued them as numbers. The town that grew up around them has chosen, carefully and with some discomfort, to remember them as names.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 5.504°N, 54.033°W, on the French-Guianese bank of the Maroni River opposite Albina, Suriname. Recommended viewing altitude 2,500–4,000 feet to follow the river's meander past the restored Camp de la Transportation on the waterfront. Nearest airport is Saint-Laurent-du-Maroni (SOOM), just south of town. Cayenne–Félix Eboué (SOCA) lies about 200 km east-southeast. Expect river haze at dawn; the light clears by mid-morning.
