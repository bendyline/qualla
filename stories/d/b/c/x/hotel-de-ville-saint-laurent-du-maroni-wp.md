# Hôtel de Ville, Saint-Laurent-du-Maroni

Saint-Laurent-du-Maroni's town hall occupies the former administrative headquarters of a penal colony - the building where France's convict system was managed until Gaston Monnerville finally shut it down.

**Location:** 5.5036, -54.0290

**Categories:** City Halls, Saint-Laurent-du-Maroni, French Guiana, Penal Colony History, Civic Buildings

## Introduction

Until 1946, the mayor of Saint-Laurent-du-Maroni was not elected. He was the commandant of the penal colony, appointed by Paris, and he ran the municipal commission from whatever room had been set aside for civic business that day. This was not a normal French town. For eighty-seven years Saint-Laurent was run as the administrative hub of the bagne de Guyane - the tropical prison camps that absorbed some 70,000 French convicts between 1852 and the liquidation of the system after the Second World War. When the town finally became a standard civil commune on 9 November 1949, its first elected mayor, Joseph Symphorien, took one of the old penal colony buildings and made it the new town hall. He chose the old administrative headquarters. It made a kind of grim sense.

## Admiral Baudin and the Commandant

In 1859, Rear-Admiral Auguste Baudin, then Governor of French Guiana, established the penal colony on the banks of the Maroni River, across the water from what was then Dutch Surinam. He appointed Nicolas Joseph Mélinon, usually called Eugène, as the first commandant. Mélinon ran the place for decades. When Saint-Laurent was designated a special penitentiary commune in 1880, he became its ex officio mayor, heading a commission rather than a council. The "officer quarter" emerged in the heart of town - a triangular zone of well-kept gardens and administrative buildings where former inmates of the colony were forbidden to enter. Everything about Saint-Laurent's civic life happened inside that triangle, within sight of the prison.

## The Bank and the Town Hall

The first municipal office occupied the first floor of the local branch of the Bank of Guyana on what is now Rue du Lieutenant-Colonel Chandon. The building was neoclassical and solid, built in ashlar stone, three bays wide with a forestair leading to a keystone-capped doorway and a balustraded balcony above it. Outside, an inscription at roof level read "Bank of Guyana." Inside, two traffic flows crossed each other daily. Local gold miners from the interior came down to exchange dust and nuggets for cash. And convicts who had completed their sentences - the ones the system called libérés - came through the same doors to collect the pitifully small amount of pocket money the administration issued them upon release. The free man leaving the colony and the prosperous prospector walking in passed each other on the front steps.

## Gaston Monnerville's Law

The penal colony endured far past its welcome. Newspapers, novels, and eventually photographs from the 1920s and 1930s made the bagne famous as a place of suffering and waste - malaria, tropical ulcers, escape attempts into the Surinamese forest, an administration that outlived the population it was meant to reform. The Radical politician Gaston Monnerville, born on Cayenne to mixed-race parents and later president of the French Senate, spent years pushing to close it. He won the argument in 1946. New convict shipments stopped. The last prisoners were either repatriated or left to fend for themselves. On 9 November 1949 Saint-Laurent-du-Maroni became a standard civil commune. In 1950, Joseph Symphorien took office as the first elected mayor - the first time in the town's history that the person running it had been chosen by its residents.

## The Building They Chose

Symphorien's council needed a town hall. The building they picked was the former administrative headquarters of the penal colony, sitting just northwest of the old bank. It had been completed in 1907 - brick under a stucco finish, a symmetrical frontage of seven bays separated by full-height columns, covered verandas on both floors, and a steeply pitched roof for the wet season. The files of the bagne had been moved out. Moving the mairie in was straightforward. There is something particular about a town choosing this specific building. It did not demolish the administration building and start fresh. It kept the bones of the prison's nervous system and remade them into a civic body. Today, in the same rooms where camp assignments were once issued and escape attempts documented, the municipal council of a border-river town meets to debate budgets, permits, and the slow business of governing a place that is still, in many ways, living with its own past.

## Flight Context

Located at 5.504°N, 54.029°W, on the east bank of the Maroni River facing Albina in Suriname across the water. The town is the administrative capital of western French Guiana. Saint-Laurent Aerodrome (SOOA) sits just south of town, handling light aircraft; the nearest international facility is Cayenne – Félix Eboué (SOCA / CAY) 250 km east. Recommended viewing altitude 2,000-3,000 ft. The old Camp de la Transportation - the main penal colony compound - sits immediately north of the town hall, its brick walls still visible from the air.
