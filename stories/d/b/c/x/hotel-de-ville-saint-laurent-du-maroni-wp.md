# Hôtel de Ville, Saint-Laurent-du-Maroni

Saint-Laurent-du-Maroni's town hall occupies the former administrative headquarters of a penal colony - the building where France's convict system was managed until the campaign led by Gaston Monnerville finally shut it down.

**Location:** 5.5036, -54.0290

**Categories:** City Halls, Saint-Laurent-du-Maroni, French Guiana, Penal Colony History, Civic Buildings

## Introduction

Until 1949, the mayor of Saint-Laurent-du-Maroni was not elected. He was the commandant of the penal colony, serving as mayor by virtue of his post, and the municipal commission he headed worked from an office above the local bank. This was not a normal French town. For eighty-seven years Saint-Laurent was run as the administrative hub of the bagne de Guyane - the tropical prison camps that absorbed some 70,000 convicts between 1852 and the liquidation of the system after the Second World War. The town finally became a standard civil commune on 9 November 1949, and its first elected mayor, Joseph Symphorien, took office in 1950. Its town hall today is one of the old penal colony buildings: the old administrative headquarters. It makes a kind of grim sense.

## Admiral Baudin and the Commandant

In 1858, Rear-Admiral Auguste Baudin, then Governor of French Guiana, established the penal colony on the banks of the Maroni River, across the water from what was then Dutch Surinam. He appointed Nicolas Joseph Mélinon, usually called Eugène, as the first commandant. Mélinon ran the place for years. When Saint-Laurent was designated a special penitentiary commune in 1880, he became its ex officio mayor, heading a commission rather than a council. The "officer quarter" emerged in the heart of town - a triangular zone of well-kept gardens and administrative buildings where former inmates of the colony were forbidden to enter. Everything about Saint-Laurent's civic life happened inside that triangle, within sight of the prison.

## The Bank and the Town Hall

The first municipal office occupied the first floor of the local branch of the Bank of Guyana on what is now Rue du Lieutenant-Colonel Chandon. The building was neoclassical and solid, built in ashlar stone, three bays wide, with a keystone-capped central doorway, a balustraded balcony and an elliptical upper window. Outside, an inscription at roof level read "Bank of Guyana." Inside, two traffic flows crossed each other daily. Local gold miners from the interior came down to exchange dust and nuggets for cash. And convicts who had completed their sentences - the ones the system called libérés - came through the same doors to collect the small amount of pocket money the administration issued them upon release - money for men who, under the rules of the bagne, were often not free to leave the colony.

## Gaston Monnerville's Law

The penal colony endured far past its welcome. Newspapers, novels, and eventually photographs from the 1920s and 1930s made the bagne famous as a place of suffering and waste - malaria, tropical ulcers, escape attempts into the Surinamese forest, an administration that outlived the population it was meant to reform. The Radical politician Gaston Monnerville, born in Cayenne and later president of the French Senate, spent years pushing to close it. As under-secretary for the colonies he secured the June 1938 decree-law that ended new transportation, and the last convoy sailed that November; the bagne itself was closed in 1946. The last prisoners were repatriated in stages, the final group in 1953, or left to fend for themselves. On 9 November 1949 Saint-Laurent-du-Maroni became a standard civil commune. In 1950, Joseph Symphorien took office as the first elected mayor - the first time in the town's history that the person running it had been chosen by its residents.

## The Building They Chose

The civil commune needed a town hall. The building that became the town hall was the former administrative headquarters of the penal colony, sitting just northwest of the old bank. It had been completed in 1907 - brick under a stucco finish, a symmetrical frontage of seven bays separated by full-height columns, covered verandas on both floors, and a sloping roof to shed the wet-season rain. There is something particular about a town choosing this specific building. It did not demolish the administration building and start fresh. It kept the bones of the prison's nervous system and remade them into a civic body. Today, in the building from which the bagne was once administered, the municipal council of a border-river town handles budgets, permits, and the slow business of governing a place that is still, in many ways, living with its own past.

## Flight Context

Located at 5.504°N, 54.029°W, on the east bank of the Maroni River facing Albina in Suriname across the water. The town is the administrative capital of western French Guiana. Saint-Laurent Aerodrome (SOOM) sits just south of town, handling light aircraft; the nearest international facility is Cayenne – Félix Eboué (SOCA / CAY), about 200 km east-southeast. Recommended viewing altitude 2,000-3,000 ft. The old Camp de la Transportation - the main penal colony compound - sits a few hundred meters to the west, near the riverbank, its walls still visible from the air.
