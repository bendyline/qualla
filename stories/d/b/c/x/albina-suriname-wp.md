# Albina, Suriname

A Surinamese river town founded by a homesick German and named for his wife, later rebuilt by its Ndyuka Maroon community after the civil war of the late 1980s.

**Location:** 5.5000, -54.0500

**Categories:** Suriname, Marowijne District, Border towns, Maroon communities, Atlantic coast

## Introduction

August Kappler named the town after his wife. The German had first come to Dutch Suriname as a soldier in 1836, and on the west bank of the Marowijne River - the wide tea-colored water that forms the border with French Guiana - he found an abandoned indigenous village and decided to stay. The year was 1846. The place needed a name. He chose Albina, after Albina Josefine Liezenmaier, his fiancée back in Germany, who later became his wife. Whether she was touched or bewildered by having a tropical river town named for her is not recorded. She lived until 1904. The town is still there, still called Albina, and still watches the sun rise across the river over French Guiana every morning.

## A River That Divides Two Countries

Stand on the Albina waterfront and Saint-Laurent-du-Maroni sits directly across the water - the French Guianese side, with baguette bakeries and euros and a post office that sends mail through Paris. A vehicle ferry links the two towns, and hundreds of motorized pirogues carry people and goods across every day. For decades officials on both sides have discussed a bridge across the Marowijne, but none has been built. So the river remains a working border: customs posts on each bank, small wooden korjaals carrying goods both legal and otherwise, and families with members on both sides crossing for weddings, funerals, and the festivals that don't respect borders. Paramaribo, Suriname's capital, is about 150 kilometers away by the East-West Link highway.

## 1986 and What It Destroyed

On the night of July 22, 1986, the Jungle Commando led by Ronnie Brunswijk opened fire on the army barracks at Albina. The fighting lasted three hours and wounded one soldier and two civilians. It was also the opening of what Surinamese historians call the Interior War - a brutal civil conflict between the military government of Dési Bouterse in Paramaribo and the mostly Maroon insurgency led by Brunswijk, who had been Bouterse's own former bodyguard. The army's response fell hard on civilians. Villages in the Marowijne district were destroyed, and much of Albina itself and the road to it were wrecked during the six years of fighting. On 29 November 1986, at Moiwana, Brunswijk's home village, soldiers of the National Army killed at least 39 villagers, many of them women and children, in what is now remembered as the Moiwana massacre; in 2005 the Inter-American Court of Human Rights held the state of Suriname responsible. Thousands of Maroons fled across the river into French Guiana as refugees. The war ended in 1992. The rebuilding of Albina has taken the three decades since.

## The Ndyuka Communities Along the River

The Marowijne district is Maroon country. The Ndyuka are one of six Maroon nations in Suriname - descendants of enslaved Africans who escaped Dutch plantations in the 17th and 18th centuries, fought for their freedom in the forest, and signed peace treaties with the colonial authorities guaranteeing their autonomy. They speak their own language, maintain their own spiritual traditions, and have governed their own communities along this river for more than 250 years. Moiwana is a Ndyuka village; Bigiston is a mixed community of Ndyuka Maroons and indigenous Kalina people. North of Albina, the indigenous Lokono villages of Alfonsdorp and Marijkedorp preserve their own languages and fishing traditions. The Interior War tore through this patchwork of peoples, and the slow rebuilding since has been as much a cultural and political project as a physical one.

## The Turtles of Galibi

An hour and a half downriver by korjaal, near where the Marowijne meets the Atlantic, lies Galibi - a small indigenous village with a beach that matters globally. Four species of sea turtle come here to lay their eggs: leatherback, green, olive ridley, and hawksbill. The leatherbacks are the largest, reaching nearly two meters long, and they travel staggering distances across the Atlantic between nesting seasons. Every year between February and August, volunteers and rangers patrol the sand at night, counting nests and guiding hatchlings safely to the water. The shop near the landing sells crafts made by the Kalina community. For many travelers, this short boat trip is why they come to Albina in the first place.

## Brunswijk, Chin A Sen, and a Singer

Albina has produced outsized political figures. Ronnie Brunswijk, born in 1961 in nearby Moiwana, rose from soldier to rebel commander to businessman and, from 2020 to 2025, Vice President of Suriname - one of the more striking political arcs of modern Caribbean history. Henk Chin A Sen, born in Albina in 1934, served briefly as President of Suriname in the early 1980s. Olton van Genderen served as Deputy Prime Minister. Oscar Harris, the singer born here in 1943, made his name in the Netherlands, first with his group the Twinkle Stars and then with a run of solo hits from 1974. For a town that has spent much of its recent history rebuilding from war, Albina has an uncanny record of sending its sons out to unexpected prominence.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 5.50°N, 54.05°W. Albina sits on the west bank of the Marowijne River at roughly 10 m elevation, directly across from Saint-Laurent-du-Maroni, French Guiana. Albina Airstrip (a 650 m asphalt runway in use since 1953) serves small aircraft. Main regional reference is Johan Adolf Pengel International (SMJP) at Zanderij, about 125 km west; Saint-Laurent-du-Maroni Airport (SOOM) is just across the river. The Marowijne River is an unmistakable navigation reference - a wide tea-colored waterway marking the Suriname-French Guiana border. Dense Amazonian rainforest covers both banks upriver.
