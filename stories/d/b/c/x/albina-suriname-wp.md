# Albina, Suriname

A Surinamese river town founded by a homesick German and named for his wife, later rebuilt by its Ndyuka Maroon community after the civil war of the late 1980s.

**Location:** 5.5000, -54.0500

**Categories:** Suriname, Marowijne District, Border towns, Maroon communities, Atlantic coast

## Introduction

August Kappler named the town after his wife. The German had left home in 1842 and worked his way across the Atlantic to Dutch Suriname, and on the west bank of the Marowijne River - the wide tea-colored water that forms the border with French Guiana - he found an abandoned indigenous village and decided to stay. The year was 1846. The place needed a name. He chose Albina, after Albina Josefine Liezenmaier, the woman he had left behind in Stuttgart. Whether she was touched or bewildered by having a tropical river town named for her is not recorded. She lived until 1904. The town is still there, still called Albina, and still watches the sun set across the river into French Guiana every evening.

## A River That Divides Two Countries

Stand on the Albina waterfront and Saint-Laurent-du-Maroni sits directly across the water - the French Guianese side, with baguette bakeries and euros and a post office that sends mail through Paris. A small ferry runs every hour or so between the two towns, carrying foot passengers, motorbikes, and the occasional car. For decades officials on both sides have discussed a bridge across the Marowijne, but as of 2020 no construction has begun. So the river remains a working border: customs posts on each bank, small wooden korjaals carrying goods both legal and otherwise, and families with members on both sides crossing for weddings, funerals, and the festivals that don't respect borders. Paramaribo, Suriname's capital, is about 150 kilometers west by the East-West Link highway.

## 1986 and What It Destroyed

On the night of July 22, 1986, the Jungle Commando led by Ronnie Brunswijk opened fire on the army barracks at Albina. The attack lasted three hours and killed two civilians. It was also the opening of what Surinamese historians call the Interior War - a brutal civil conflict between the military government of Dési Bouterse in Paramaribo and the mostly Maroon insurgency led by Brunswijk, who had been Bouterse's own former bodyguard. The army's response was indiscriminate. Villages across the Marowijne district were destroyed. Much of Albina itself was leveled during the six years of fighting. At Moiwana, Brunswijk's home village, soldiers killed 35 people - most of them women and children - in what is now remembered as the Moiwana massacre. Tens of thousands of Maroons fled across the river into French Guiana as refugees. The war ended in 1992. The rebuilding of Albina has taken the three decades since.

## The Ndyuka Communities Along the River

The Marowijne district is Maroon country. The Ndyuka are one of six Maroon nations in Suriname - descendants of enslaved Africans who escaped Dutch plantations in the 17th and 18th centuries, fought for their freedom in the forest, and signed peace treaties with the colonial authorities guaranteeing their autonomy. They speak their own language, maintain their own spiritual traditions, and have governed their own communities along this river for more than 250 years. Moiwana is a Ndyuka village; Bigiston is a mixed community of Ndyuka Maroons and indigenous Kalina people. North of Albina, the indigenous Lokono villages of Alfonsdorp and Marijkedorp preserve their own languages and fishing traditions. The Interior War tore through this patchwork of peoples, and the slow rebuilding since has been as much a cultural and political project as a physical one.

## The Turtles of Galibi

An hour and a half downriver by korjaal, near where the Marowijne meets the Atlantic, lies Galibi - a small indigenous village with a beach that matters globally. Four species of sea turtle come here to lay their eggs: leatherback, green, olive ridley, and hawksbill. The leatherbacks are the largest, reaching nearly two meters long, and they travel staggering distances - some tagged females at Galibi have been tracked crossing to Costa Rica and even as far as Australia. Every year between February and August, volunteers and rangers patrol the sand at night, counting nests and guiding hatchlings safely to the water. The shop near the landing sells crafts made by the Kalina community. For many travelers, this short boat trip is why they come to Albina in the first place.

## Brunswijk, Chin A Sen, and a Singer

Albina has produced outsized political figures. Ronnie Brunswijk, born here in 1961, rose from army private to rebel commander to businessman and, in 2020, Vice President of Suriname - one of the more striking political arcs of modern Caribbean history. Henk Chin A Sen, born in Albina in 1934, served briefly as President of Suriname in the early 1980s. Olton van Genderen served as Deputy Prime Minister. Oscar Harris, the singer born here in 1943, made his name in Europe with disco and soul hits in the 1970s. For a town that has spent much of its recent history rebuilding from war, Albina has an uncanny record of sending its sons out to unexpected prominence.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 5.50°N, 54.05°W. Albina sits on the west bank of the Marowijne River at roughly 10 m elevation, directly across from Saint-Laurent-du-Maroni, French Guiana. Albina Airstrip (a 650 m asphalt runway in use since 1953) serves small aircraft. Main regional reference is Johan Adolf Pengel International (SMJP) at Zanderij, 150 km west near Paramaribo; Saint-Laurent-du-Maroni Airport (SOOA) is directly across the river. The Marowijne River is an unmistakable navigation reference - a wide tea-colored waterway marking the Suriname-French Guiana border. Dense Amazonian rainforest covers both banks upriver.
