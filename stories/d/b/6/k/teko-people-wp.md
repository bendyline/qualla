# Teko people

A Tupi-Guarani-speaking people of French Guiana who survived centuries of raiding, epidemics, and assimilation pressure - and are still here.

**Location:** 2.2459, -52.8734

**Categories:** indigenous, french-guiana, amazon, tupi-guarani, culture, language

## Introduction

Call them Teko, or call them by the name Europeans insisted on - Emerillon - and the people themselves will recognize both. They live on the banks of the Camopi and Tampok rivers, in the interior of French Guiana, about four hundred and ten of them at the last count in 2010. For most of the twentieth century, many observers wrote about them as if they were fading. They are not. Their language is still spoken at home, and children still learn it first.

## Before the Ships

The Teko are one of six indigenous nations in French Guiana whose presence predates European contact. They were more mobile than their neighbors, which meant their villages shifted - sometimes because the soil needed rest, sometimes because a chief had died and custom required relocation, and sometimes because raiders were closing in. For this reason they built their settlements back from the rivers, where a canoe could not be paddled directly to the door. They lived on the Approuague, then on the Tampok and Maroni near the Surinamese border, and today also near the Camopi, close to Brazil. The rivers were roads, and the rivers were also the direction from which trouble usually came.

## Contact and Collapse

First recorded contact with Europeans came in 1767, when an expedition led by a man named Patris reached a Teko village. He estimated the population at 350 to 400. What followed was a familiar catastrophe written in small letters. Continuous raids by the Kalina weakened them. By the nineteenth century, intertribal warfare had pushed the Teko into a subordinate position - a kind of collective servitude - to the Wayampi. Only French colonial threat of military intervention brought the fighting to an end. Epidemics did the rest. By 1849 the naturalist Bagot counted roughly one hundred Teko left alive. A people of four hundred had been reduced by three-quarters in eighty years. That they exist today is a fact worth sitting with.

## Refusals

Recovery came slowly through the twentieth century, and with it a series of quiet refusals. In the 1960s the French government offered citizenship to all the indigenous nations of the interior. The Wayampi would eventually accept in 2000. The Teko, alongside them, said no. The French tried to concentrate scattered villages into larger, more administratively convenient settlements; the Teko declined that too. The state installed male captains to head each village, following the Maroon model - but the Teko are matrilocal, meaning a husband moves into his wife's community, and when a captain died the role often passed to his widow rather than the man the French had in mind. In 2012, when the Guiana Amazonian Park was created, the Teko resisted on grounds that should not surprise anyone who had read their history: their freedom to move across the land had been hard-won, and a park boundary was one more line they had not drawn.

## Language as Life

Their language, called Emerillon by linguists and Teko by its speakers, belongs to the Tupi-Guarani family - the same branch that produced the languages of the vast Amazonian interior. One of its distinctive features is nasal harmony, a pattern in which nasality spreads through a word. Children still learn it as a first language, which is why linguists mark it as endangered rather than dying. French, Portuguese, and Wayampi enter as second languages, mostly through schools and trade. The Teko lend vocabulary to them in return. It is a continuing conversation.

## The Forest They Kept

Teko life is tied to the forest in practical ways. They practice slash-and-burn horticulture, planting manioc and other crops in small clearings and letting each plot recover for years before returning to it. Hunting and multiple fishing techniques fill out the diet. Many families still live in wooden huts thatched with palm leaves, though concrete houses are becoming more common - a sign not of loss but of choice. The villages are small, often just extended family, and the rhythms of daily life still move with the river and the seasons. There are four hundred Teko in the world. Each one is the descendant of the hundred who survived 1849.

## Flight Context
