# Teko people

A Tupi-Guarani-speaking people of French Guiana who survived centuries of raiding, epidemics, and assimilation pressure - and are still here.

**Location:** 2.2459, -52.8734

**Categories:** indigenous, french-guiana, amazon, tupi-guarani, culture, language

## Introduction

They call themselves Teko; outsiders long called them Emerillon. They live on the banks of the Camopi and Tampok rivers, in the interior of French Guiana - about 410 of them in a 2010 count, and around 700 by a 2019 estimate. For most of the twentieth century, many observers wrote about them as if they were fading. They are not. Their language is still spoken at home, and children still learn it first.

## Before the Ships

The Teko are one of six indigenous nations in French Guiana whose presence predates European contact. They were more mobile than their neighbors, which meant their villages shifted - sometimes because the soil needed rest, sometimes because a chief had died and custom required relocation, and sometimes because raiders were closing in. For this reason they built their settlements back from the rivers, where a canoe could not be paddled directly to the door. They lived on the Approuague, then on the Tampok and Maroni near the Surinamese border, and today also near the Camopi, close to Brazil. The rivers were roads, and the rivers were also the direction from which trouble usually came.

## Contact and Collapse

First recorded contact with Europeans came in 1767, when an expedition led by a man named Patris reached a Teko village. He estimated the population at 350 to 400. What followed was a familiar catastrophe written in small letters. Continuous raids by the Kalina weakened them. By the nineteenth century, intertribal warfare had pushed the Teko into a subordinate position - a kind of collective servitude - to the Wayampi. Only French colonial threat of military intervention brought the fighting to an end. Epidemics did the rest. By 1849 an observer named Bagot estimated that roughly one hundred Teko were left. A people of three or four hundred had been reduced by about three-quarters in eighty years. That they exist today is a fact worth sitting with.

## Refusals

Recovery came slowly through the twentieth century, and with it a series of quiet refusals. In the 1960s the French government asked the Indigenous nations of the interior whether they wanted French citizenship. The Teko and the Wayampi were the only ones who said no; the Wayampi accepted in 2000. The French tried to concentrate scattered villages into larger, more administratively convenient settlements; the Teko declined that too. The state installed male captains to head each village, following the Maroon model - but the Teko are matrilocal, meaning a husband moves into his wife's community, and the role was often inherited by a captain's widow. As the Guiana Amazonian Park took shape - created in 2007, its charter approved in 2013 - the Teko resisted it on grounds that should not surprise anyone who had read their history: their freedom to move across the land had been hard-won, and a park boundary was one more line they had not drawn.

## Language as Life

Their language, called Emerillon by linguists and Teko by its speakers, belongs to the Tupi-Guarani family, which also includes Guarani and the Wayampi language spoken by their neighbors. One of its distinctive features is nasal harmony, a pattern in which nasality spreads through a word. Children still learn it as a first language, though linguists still class it as endangered. French, Portuguese, and Wayampi are becoming second languages, and Teko has borrowed vocabulary, mostly from the European languages. It is a continuing conversation.

## The Forest They Kept

Teko life is tied to the forest in practical ways. They practice slash-and-burn horticulture, planting manioc and other crops in small clearings and letting each plot recover for years before returning to it. Hunting and multiple fishing techniques fill out the diet. Many families still live in wooden huts thatched with palm leaves, though concrete houses are becoming more common - a sign not of loss but of choice. The villages are small, and the rhythms of daily life still move with the river and the seasons. There are perhaps seven hundred Teko today - a people rebuilt from the hundred or so counted in 1849.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 2.25°N, 52.87°W (upper Oyapock in the commune of Camopi, southeastern French Guiana, on the Brazilian border; the main Teko villages lie downstream around Camopi village, about 120 km to the north-northeast, and on the Tampok far to the west). Recommended viewing altitude 4,000-6,000 ft for the Camopi and Oyapock river systems. There is no scheduled air service; Camopi is reached by river from Saint-Georges-de-l'Oyapock. Cayenne's Félix Eboué International (SOCA) is roughly 290 km to the north-northeast. Dense equatorial rainforest with pronounced wet season (December-July). Respect that these are inhabited traditional territories - do not descend to low observation altitudes over villages.
