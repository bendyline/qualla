# Boni

An 18th-century Maroon leader in Suriname whose people built a forest fort, resisted the Dutch colonial army for decades, and whose descendants still carry his name.

**Location:** 2.5636, -54.0299

**Categories:** Maroon history, Suriname, resistance, 18th century

## Introduction

Bokilifu Boni was born in the forest, to a mother who refused to give birth on a plantation. Around 1730, somewhere in the Cottica region of Dutch Suriname, a pregnant enslaved African woman fled into the trees and found her way to the Maroons - communities of escaped enslaved people who had built free societies in the interior. Her child grew up among them. He would lead them for nearly three decades, build a fortified town in the wetlands, wage a war that Paramaribo could not win outright, and die betrayed at a camp beside a rapids in what is now the Marouini River. The people he led still bear his name: the Aluku, also called the Boni, living along the Lawa today.

## A Boy Raised Free

The story passed down says Boni's father was a Dutch colonist and his mother was enslaved - and that she was already pregnant when she ran. She reached the Cottica Maroons, a community descended from people who had been kidnapped from Africa, sold into the Surinamese plantation system, and chosen at terrible cost to risk the forest rather than stay in the cane fields. Boni was born into that choice. He grew up on its terms: hunting, navigating the waterways, learning the geography of freedom. In 1765 he succeeded Asikan Sylvester as leader of his people. He was about 35 years old. He would prove to be a freedom fighter, a diplomat, an engineer of defenses, and a man whose name became a people.

## Fort Boekoe

Boni's people built a town the colony could barely reach. Fort Boekoe, named after the Boekoe Creek, sat in the wetlands of Commewijne behind a four-meter wall. The Maroons armed themselves with rifles and a cannon and turned the marshland itself into a defense. For years the fort held, and from it Boni's fighters raided the plantations. The colony answered by reinforcing its militia in 1772 with the Zwarte Jagers, a corps of formerly enslaved men, and that year took Fort Boekoe after a secret path through the swamp was betrayed. Boni withdrew east across the Marowijne River and later settled his people along the Lawa River, the waterway that separates Suriname from what is now French Guiana. They would rebuild there.

## Treaties and Broken Peace

Their new neighbors were the Ndyuka, another Maroon people who had signed their own peace with the Dutch in 1760. In 1779, after negotiations, the two communities signed their own treaty, and Boni was offered a daughter of the Ndyuka granman as a wife. The peace held until 1788, when Boni's people attacked Plantation Clarenbeek and killed five soldiers. The Ndyuka ended their treaty with the Aluku the following year and joined the colonial side. In 1790 Fort Aloekoe fell. A year later, Lieutenant Colonel Beutler pushed Boni's people across the river into French Guiana.

## Akuba's Broken Gourd

On February 19, 1793, Boni's camp sat beside a set of rapids called Akuba Booko Goo - "Akuba's Broken Gourd" - on the Marouini River. That night he was killed by Bambi, an Ndyuka chief acting under pressure from a Dutch officer named Stoelman, who commanded the Redi Musus, a colonial corps of formerly enslaved Black men raised to fight the Maroons. Boni was about 63. His war had lasted decades. His community did not dissolve with him. The Aluku - Boni's people - continued to live along the Lawa on both sides of what would become an international border. They maintained their own leadership, their own spiritual practices, and their own memory of the man who had led them out of the wetlands and across the river. The broader Maroon struggle in Suriname continued until 1887, when the Kwinti became the last group to sign a peace treaty with the colonial government.

## What the Forest Remembers

The Lawa River today still carries dugouts between Aluku villages on the French side and Ndyuka and Aluku communities on the Surinamese side. The border Alexander III of Russia drew in 1891 - deciding a colonial dispute over gold - sliced through Maroon lands that had been free long before either European empire noticed them. Boni's name lives on in the collective identity of thousands of people whose ancestors chose the forest over the plantation. Fly above the Marouini and you cross a landscape that resisted a plantation colony built on slavery - not by military victory alone, but by the steady refusal of a community to accept the terms the colony offered.

## Flight Context

Boni's final camp is near the Marouini River rapids around 2.56°N, 54.03°W, in the Marouini tract near the Suriname border, administered by France and claimed by Suriname. The region is deep rainforest accessible primarily by small aircraft and dugout canoe. Maripasoula (SOOA) is the nearest airstrip on the French side; Lawa Antino is the nearest on the Suriname side. Recommended viewing altitude: 4,000-8,000 feet AGL. The Lawa and Marouini rivers are the clearest landmarks - dark serpentine lines through uninterrupted canopy.
