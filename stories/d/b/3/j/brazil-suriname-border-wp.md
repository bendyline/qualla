# Brazil-Suriname Border

A 593-kilometer international border drawn entirely along a ridge of rainforest where no rivers cross - the shortest border Brazil has, and one of the least visited places on the continent.

**Location:** 2.2861, -54.5333

**Categories:** international borders, Amazon, rainforest, geography

## Introduction

The Brazil-Suriname border is a line that follows water by refusing to touch it. For 593 kilometers it runs along a rainforest ridge, a drainage divide so precise that no river crosses from one country into the other. Rivers flowing south from this line drop into the Amazon. Rivers flowing north from it run to the Atlantic through the Guianas. The border is entirely dry - a geographical rarity - and the two commissions that spent years trying to mark it had to navigate seasonal flooding, unfamiliar watersheds, and dense rainforest where neither country had much interest in going. By April 30, 1938, they had placed 59 stone markers. On the map it looks simple. On the ground, almost no one lives there.

## The Shortest Border

This is the shortest border Brazil shares with any of its ten neighbors. The entire Surinamese side sits within Sipaliwini, the country's largest and most thinly populated district - the green southern four-fifths of a small nation. The Brazilian side runs almost entirely through Pará, with a final 52 kilometers in the east bumping against Amapá. On both sides the forest is unbroken. The border lies between two triple points: with Guyana in the west, and with French Guiana in the east. The western end is tangled in a separate dispute: Guyana administers the New River Triangle, or Tigri Area, which Suriname claims, because the two countries disagree over which river is the true source of the Courantyne - and so over exactly where their boundary meets Brazil's. In between, there are no border crossings, no customs posts, no highways. Kwamalasamutu, a village of the Tiriyó (Trio) people, is the nearest Surinamese town - still many kilometers from the line. On the Brazilian side, Tiriyó villages such as Missão Tiriyó and a small army border platoon at Tiriós sit near the headwaters of the West Paru, far from any road. A satellite image shows the border only by inference: it is the line where no rivers cross.

## A Border by Drainage Divide

The 1906 Treaty of Limits defined the line with a hydrologist's elegance: the border would follow "the watershed between the Amazon basin to the south, and the basins of the rivers flowing north to the Atlantic Ocean." This is a textbook example of a drainage divide border. Suriname gets no access to the Amazon basin. Brazil gets no access to the Corantyne or Maroni systems that drain north through the Guianas. Every drop of rain that falls south of the ridge eventually reaches the Amazon. Every drop that falls north reaches the Atlantic through the rivers of Suriname. The divide is not a dramatic mountain spine but a low, rolling crest through the forest - easy to miss at ground level, invisible from a boat, and, until the 20th century, unmapped.

## A Border the Colonizers Postponed

For centuries this border did not exist because no European power cared enough to draw it. French, English, Dutch, Spanish, and Portuguese outposts clung to the coast and the major rivers; the interior was simply the blank beyond. The Dutch, administering their Surinamese colony, had friendly relations with Brazil after Brazilian independence from Portugal in 1822 and specifically asked Brazil to wait - not out of hostility but because France and Britain also had designs on the unclaimed interior, and the Dutch did not want to provoke those larger neighbors. So Brazil settled its northern borders in pieces: with France in 1900 (after Swiss arbitration over French Guiana), with Britain in 1904 over British Guiana, and finally, on May 5, 1906, with the Netherlands over Suriname. The Hague ratified the treaty on September 15, 1908. Actually finding the line on the ground took thirty more years.

## The Commission in the Trees

On September 22, 1931, the two governments agreed to send joint commissions to physically mark the border. Field work did not start until 1934. The Dutch team, led by Vice Admiral Conrad Carel Kayser, and the Brazilian team faced what any rainforest surveyor would recognize: seasonal rains that swelled streams beyond their dry-season beds, watersheds that branched in confusing directions, and a near-total absence of maps of the exact ridge. On April 30, 1938, in Belem, they agreed their joint report: the frontier was 593 kilometers, marked by 59 border stones.

## Flying the Divide

From cruising altitude the border is invisible. There is no road, no clearing, no break in the canopy. What there is, if you know to look, is the subtle geometry of the watershed: streams curving away from a shared crest, rivers gathering into the Amazon basin to the south and into the Atlantic-bound basins of the north. The border is the ridge where those two drainages meet without exchanging a drop. It is perhaps the emptiest international line on the continent, and one of the most elegantly drawn.

## Flight Context

Centered around 2.29°N, 54.53°W. The border runs roughly east-west along a drainage divide through unbroken rainforest. No airports sit directly on the line. Paramaribo (SMJP) is north; Macapa, Amapa (SBMQ) is southeast; Boa Vista (SBBV) is far to the west. Recommended viewing altitude: 10,000-25,000 feet - you'll see uninterrupted green canopy and the serpentine upper reaches of rivers flowing north (toward Suriname) and south (toward the Amazon). No border crossings; expect only scattered clouds and high humidity.
