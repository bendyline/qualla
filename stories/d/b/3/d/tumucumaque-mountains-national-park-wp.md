# Tumucumaque Mountains National Park

The largest tropical forest national park on Earth - bigger than Belgium, bordering three countries, and home to animals that live nowhere else.

**Location:** 1.8333, -54.0000

**Categories:** national-park, brazil, amazon, rainforest, wildlife, conservation

## Introduction

Spread a map of Belgium on your table. Now imagine every inch of it as rainforest, with no cities, no roads, no farms - nothing but canopy and the rivers that slip beneath it. That map is still smaller than Tumucumaque Mountains National Park. At more than 38,800 square kilometers, Tumucumaque is the largest tropical forest national park in the world, and it sits in a quiet corner of Brazil most travelers have never heard of, in the states of Amapá and Pará, where the Guiana Shield meets the Amazon.

## A Park Declared in 2002

The Brazilian government declared Tumucumaque a national park on 23 August 2002, after years of collaboration with the World Wildlife Fund. The timing matters. The early 2000s were a period when the accelerating destruction of the Amazon had become impossible to ignore, and enormous protected areas were being stitched together in an attempt to get ahead of the bulldozers. Tumucumaque is a centerpiece of that stitching. Since 2003 it has been part of the Amapá Biodiversity Corridor, and it is supported by the Amazon Region Protected Areas Program. A formal management plan was published on 10 March 2010. These are bureaucratic dates that become less dry when you realize what they saved.

## The Corridor

Tumucumaque does not protect a forest in isolation. Across the northern border, in French Guiana, lies the Guiana Amazonian Park - another vast protected area that shares a boundary with Tumucumaque. Together they cover roughly 59,000 square kilometers of continuous protected rainforest. Add the neighboring Grão-Pará Ecological Station, the Maicuru Biological Reserve, and a patchwork of other reserves in northern Pará, and you have one of the largest and best-protected ecological corridors of tropical rainforest on Earth. Only the three-park system on the Brazil-Venezuela border - Parima-Tapirapecó, Serranía de la Neblina, and Pico da Neblina, totaling more than 73,000 square kilometers - beats it on combined area. The Guiana Shield, the ancient rock floor beneath all this, is one of the oldest geological formations on the continent, and it still does what old rocks tend to do: it hides things.

## What Lives Here

Tumucumaque is a habitat for jaguars, several species of primates, aquatic turtles, and harpy eagles - the enormous raptor with talons the size of a grizzly bear's claws that can pluck a monkey out of the canopy. Most remarkably, many of the park's species, especially its fish and aquatic birds, are found nowhere else in the world. The endemism rate is an artifact of the park's isolation: the streams that rise in the Tumucumaque range drain north into the Guianas and south into the Amazon, and the waterfalls between have kept fish populations genetically separate for long enough to become their own species. The highest point in the state of Amapá, at 701 meters, stands within the park. It is not a Himalayan figure, but the climb from near-sea-level forest to that ridge is steep, and the forest canopy holds entire unstudied ecosystems in between.

## A Climate Without Seasons

The climate is tropical monsoon - Köppen Am - which means a pattern typical of northern Brazil in the transition zone between the open savannas and the deep Amazon forest. The average temperature is 25°C (77°F). Rainfall ranges from 2,000 to 3,250 millimeters per year, enough to keep the forest drenched and the rivers swollen even through the drier months. The land here does not experience seasons the way temperate climates do. What changes is how much water is falling from the sky at any given moment, and whether the rivers are flooding the forest floor or retreating into their channels. Tourism is possible but limited, and the park is formally divided into an Amapari Sector (accessed via the city of Serra do Navio, or from Pedra Branca do Amapari in summer) and an Oiapoque Sector.

## Small Honors

Tumucumaque's fame spread in at least one unexpected direction. In 2010, Mozilla code-named the beta version of Firefox 4 'Tumucumaque' - a nod, from an open-source browser project, to one of the most remote and protected places in the world. The gesture was small, but the sentiment was fitting: the park, like the web at its best, is a place that exists because people decided to keep it open. Below the cruising altitude of an aircraft, there is no sign that humans reach in here at all. Just canopy, river, mountain, and the occasional white fleck of a harpy eagle rising above a kill.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 1.83°N, 54.00°W (northern Brazil, Amapá state, at the French Guiana / Suriname border). Recommended viewing altitude 6,000-9,000 ft to take in the canopy and the low ridgeline topping at 701 m. Nearest airstrip is Serra do Navio; Macapá (SBMQ) is the state airport 300 km to the south-southeast. Cayenne (SOCA) is roughly 200 km north in French Guiana. Expect solid tropical rainforest, significant afternoon convection, and unreliable VFR conditions much of the year.
