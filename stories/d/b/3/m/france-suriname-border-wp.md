# France–Suriname border

A 520-kilometer river line separates the European Union from South America - and two-thirds of the way down, it dissolves into an unresolved argument about which jungle stream really belongs on the map.

**Location:** 2.3376, -54.4346

**Categories:** International borders, France, Suriname, Rivers

## Introduction

Follow the Maroni River south from where it meets the Atlantic at Saint-Laurent, and for the first several hundred kilometers the border is unambiguous: one bank is Suriname, the other is an overseas department of France, which means an outer edge of the European Union. Keep going. The river thins. It splits into tributaries. And somewhere upstream, depending on which map you trust, the border stops being a line and becomes a disagreement. Two countries, three centuries, one Tsar, and 5,000 square miles of uncleared rainforest still waiting for someone to decide whose it is.

## The Shape of the Line

The France–Suriname border runs for roughly 520 kilometers, separating French Guiana from Suriname. The northern portion follows the Maroni River - a natural boundary agreed upon in principle in 1836 and finalized in 1915. Six French Guianese communes lie along it: Saint-Laurent-du-Maroni, Apatou, Grand-Santi, Papaichton, Maripasoula and, further upriver, the Wayana village of Antécume-Pata. Across the water stand the Surinamese settlements - Albina at the mouth, then Benzdorp, Kawemhakan, Stoelmanseiland, and Cottica Lawa in the interior. Where the Maroni ends, though, the river breaks into tributaries, and the governments of France and Suriname have never fully agreed on which tributary the line should follow. In addition, an approximately 200-nautical-mile maritime border was fully demarcated only in 2017.

## An Old Argument

The Dutch and the French - Suriname was a Dutch colony until 1975 - sat down together in 1836 and agreed, in principle, that the Maroni should mark the boundary between their colonies from the Caribbean Sea to the tripoint with Brazil. But the headwaters of the Maroni were, to them, unknown. Was the border to follow the Tapahoni or the Lawa upstream? Nobody knew yet. The question was left open, and then left alone, for thirty years. In 1861 another meeting was held, and after some exploration by colonists the Lawa was chosen. But this was a colonist's agreement, not a governmental one. Both nations' governments refused to ratify it. The line drifted.

## Gold and a Tsar

Thirteen years later, in 1874, prospectors found gold in the disputed region. The stakes changed. Gold gives every boundary dispute sharper teeth, and the French and the Dutch pressed harder. The Dutch asked for outside arbitration. The man they got was Tsar Alexander III of Russia, who ruled in May 1891 that the Lawa would be the official border - a decision favorable to the Dutch. But the Tsar's ruling solved one question and raised another. The Lawa itself is fed by tributaries. Which of its tributaries counts as the continuation of the border? France has since argued for the Itany River, the easternmost of the two candidates. Suriname has held out for the Marouini, further east again from the French perspective, further west from theirs. The area between the two rivers - approximately 5,000 square miles of rural and forested country, thinly populated but believed to hold mineral wealth neither government has formally surveyed - is called the Marouini River Tract. On most maps it is shaded, or striped, or quietly ignored.

## Life Inside the Question

People live in the disputed area. Wayana and Wayampi communities have been on these rivers far longer than either France or Suriname has existed as a nation. Gold miners, some legal, most not, have washed into the watersheds. French military patrols, including the Foreign Legion, sweep through periodically in Operation Harpie, chasing illegal *garimpeiros* who cross from Brazil and Suriname. For the people who live here, the line between nations is less a legal fact than a daily negotiation - where to fish, where to buy fuel, whose schoolhouse your children attend. The border exists; it simply does not stay in one place.

## Almost Finished, Nearly Forever

In 2021 France and Suriname reached a real breakthrough. The two governments agreed on two-thirds of the river boundary, from the mouth of the Maroni to the village of Antécume-Pata. It was the most significant progress in over a century, and it was received quietly in the capitals - no treaty signing televised in Paris, no parades in Paramaribo, just a working agreement between bureaucrats and geographers. As of 2025 the rest - most of the Lawa dispute, including the Marouini River Tract - remained officially unresolved. The river still flows. The forest still covers the question. And on any given day, somewhere in that 5,000 square miles of green, the Itany and the Marouini are indifferent to which of them the mapmakers eventually choose.

## Flight Context

The France–Suriname border centers on approximately 2.34°N, 54.43°W in its disputed southern section. The line runs roughly 520 km from the Atlantic coast at Saint-Laurent-du-Maroni / Albina south-southwest to the tripoint with Brazil. Nearest airports: Saint-Laurent-du-Maroni (SOOA) on the French side, Totness and Paramaribo-Zanderij (SMJP / PBM) on the Surinamese side. From cruising altitude, the Maroni River is the dominant landmark - a wide brown channel marking the outer edge of the European Union.
