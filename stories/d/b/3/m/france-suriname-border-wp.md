# France–Suriname border

A 520-kilometer river line marks the European Union's border with Suriname - and two-thirds of the way down, it dissolves into an unresolved argument about which jungle stream really belongs on the map.

**Location:** 2.3376, -54.4346

**Categories:** International borders, France, Suriname, Rivers

## Introduction

Follow the Maroni River south from where it meets the Atlantic at Saint-Laurent, and for the first several hundred kilometers the border is unambiguous: one bank is Suriname, the other is an overseas department of France, which means an outer edge of the European Union. Keep going. The river thins. It splits into tributaries. And somewhere upstream, depending on which map you trust, the border stops being a line and becomes a disagreement. Two countries, nearly two centuries of talks, one Tsar, and some 3,000 square kilometers of rainforest still waiting for someone to decide whose it is.

## The Shape of the Line

The France–Suriname border runs for roughly 520 kilometers, separating French Guiana from Suriname. The northern portion follows the Maroni River - a natural boundary agreed upon in principle in 1836 and finalized in 1915. Five French Guianese communes lie along it: Saint-Laurent-du-Maroni, Apatou, Grand-Santi, Papaichton and Maripasoula, and further upriver sits the Wayana village of Antécume-Pata. Across the water stand the Surinamese settlements - Albina at the mouth, then Benzdorp, Kawemhakan, Stoelmanseiland, and Cottica Lawa in the interior. Where the Maroni ends, though, the river breaks into tributaries, and the governments of France and Suriname have never fully agreed on which tributary the line should follow. In addition, an approximately 200-nautical-mile maritime border was fully demarcated only in 2017.

## An Old Argument

The Dutch and the French - Suriname was a Dutch colony until 1975 - sat down together in 1836 and agreed, in principle, that the Maroni should mark the boundary between their colonies from the Atlantic coast to the tripoint with Brazil. But the headwaters of the Maroni were, to them, unknown. Was the border to follow the Tapahoni or the Lawa upstream? Nobody knew yet. The question was left open, and then left alone, for a quarter century. In 1861 another meeting was held, and after some exploration by colonists the Lawa was chosen. But this was a colonist's agreement, not a governmental one. Both nations' governments refused to ratify it. The line drifted.

## Gold and a Tsar

Thirteen years later, in 1874, prospectors found gold in the disputed region. The stakes changed. Gold gives every boundary dispute sharper teeth, and the French and the Dutch pressed harder. The Dutch asked for outside arbitration. The man they got was Tsar Alexander III of Russia, who ruled in May 1891 that the Lawa would be the official border - a decision favorable to the Dutch. But the Tsar's ruling solved one question and raised another. The Lawa itself is fed by tributaries. Which of its tributaries counts as the continuation of the border? France has since argued for the Itany (Litani) River, the more westerly of the two candidates. Suriname has held out for the Marouini, further east. The area between the two rivers - about 3,000 square kilometers of thinly populated forest by a 2025 French Senate count, though older estimates run far higher - is called the Marouini River Tract. On most maps it is shaded, or striped, or quietly ignored.

## Life Inside the Question

People live along these rivers. Wayana communities have been on the upper Maroni and its tributaries far longer than either France or Suriname has existed as a nation. Gold miners, many of them working illegally, have washed into the watersheds. French military patrols, including the Foreign Legion, sweep through periodically in Operation Harpie, chasing illegal *garimpeiros* who cross from Brazil and Suriname. For the people who live here, the line between nations is less a legal fact than a daily negotiation. The border exists; it simply does not stay in one place.

## Almost Finished, Nearly Forever

In March 2021 France and Suriname signed a protocol settling two-thirds of the river boundary, from the mouth of the Maroni to the village of Antécume-Pata, and sorting out some 950 river islands that 1950s maps had assigned to the wrong country. As of mid-2025 Suriname had not ratified it, and Jennifer Simons, whose party had said it wanted to renegotiate the protocol, became Suriname's president that July. The rest - the stretch above Antécume-Pata, including the Marouini River Tract - remained officially unresolved. The river still flows. The forest still covers the question. And on any given day, somewhere in those 3,000 square kilometers of green, the Itany and the Marouini are indifferent to which of them the mapmakers eventually choose.

## Flight Context

The France–Suriname border centers on approximately 2.34°N, 54.43°W in its disputed southern section. The line runs roughly 520 km from the Atlantic coast at Saint-Laurent-du-Maroni / Albina south-southwest to the tripoint with Brazil. Nearest airports: Saint-Laurent-du-Maroni (SOOM) and Maripasoula (SOOA) on the French side, Paramaribo-Zanderij (SMJP / PBM) on the Surinamese side. From cruising altitude, the Maroni River is the dominant landmark - a wide brown channel marking the outer edge of the European Union.
