# Rio Curiaú Environmental Protection Area

A few kilometers from downtown Macapá, flooded cerrado fields ring a cluster of villages founded three centuries ago by people who chose freedom over the fortress walls — a protected area that guards a river, an ecosystem, and an African-Brazilian culture that refused to disappear.

**Location:** 0.1581, -51.0332

**Categories:** conservation, quilombo, brazil, amazon, afro-brazilian-heritage

## Introduction

About five kilometers from downtown Macapá — close enough that the city's traffic hums at the edges of the quiet — a river called the Curiaú winds through flooded fields and gallery forest on its way to the Amazon. The protected area around it is not one of Brazil's famous wildernesses. It is something rarer: a living piece of Amapá where the ecosystem and the culture protecting it grew up together over the course of three centuries. By one account, the villages here were founded by enslaved Africans who fled the construction of a Portuguese fortress. Their descendants still live here. The fish and the egrets still live here. The urban boundary of a state capital is now a few bulldozer-lengths away, and that is the problem.

## Where a River Meets the Amazon

The Rio Curiaú Environmental Protection Area — APA, in Brazilian shorthand — covers 21,676 hectares just north of Macapá. The Curiaú River rises inside the APA and flows southeast into the Amazon, which forms the eastern boundary. The Amapá railroad runs along the west; the EAP-070 highway cuts north through the middle. The climate is tropical humid, Köppen Af, with annual temperatures around 27°C, rainfall around 2,500 mm, and humidity averaging 85 percent. Vegetation shifts with the water table. Oxisols, 44 percent of the area, carry cerrado forest and savanna on gently rolling terrain. Gleysols, 43 percent, sit in the várzea — the seasonally flooded forest where the Curiaú spills across its banks for much of the year. Alluvial soils, just 6 percent, run along the watercourses and carry the most productive gallery forest. The river pulses with the tides from the Amazon, rises with the storms, and spreads out across the flood plain in sheets of shining water that look, from the air, like an inland sea.

## Founded in Flight

Two stories explain how the villages got here, and both are probably partly true. One says a free settler of African origin named Sr. Miranda arrived about three hundred years ago with his wife and seven enslaved brothers — and that he saw cattle country in the flooded fields and stayed. The other says the villages of the Vila do Curiaú were founded in the 18th century by enslaved people who revolted during the construction of the Fortress of São José de Macapá — the great star-shaped Portuguese fort still standing in the city — and fled. Both versions may hold some truth: escapees may well have found refuge with earlier settlers, and the community that coalesced became a quilombo — a self-governing settlement of African-descended people living on their own terms outside the reach of the colonial state. The descendants call themselves quilombolas. They have been here long enough that the landscape and the culture are no longer separable.

## A Protected Area with Two Missions

On 28 September 1992, Amapá state decree 1417 created the APA. A companion decree, 1418, recognized the Vila do Curiaú as Cultural Heritage of Amapá. The pairing was not accidental. The APA was designed to do two things at once: protect the Curiaú River basin from the encroaching edge of Macapá's urban sprawl, and protect the social and cultural life of the quilombo residents from being absorbed or displaced. Six years later, on 15 September 1998, law 0431 redrew the boundaries, shrinking the area by about 1,324 hectares — a reminder that protected status in Brazil is always a negotiation. A management board was created in 2001, with seats for public authorities and organized civil society. The APA became part of the Amapá Biodiversity Corridor in 2003, linking it to a statewide network of conservation units.

## Living in Between

As of 1999, about 1,500 people lived in the APA's five communities — Curiaú de Dentro, Curiaú de Fora, Casa Grande, Curralinho, and Mocambo. The main economic activities remain subsistence agriculture and the collection of plant and animal products from the flooded fields. Mocambo is itself a word worth slowing down for: in Brazilian Portuguese it means a settlement of people who had escaped slavery, a sister-term to quilombo. The place name carries the founding memory. And yet the economic pressures are unmistakable. Macapá is growing fast. The edge of the city is eating the edge of the APA. Residents are abandoning the traditional occupations — the fishing, the small-scale cattle raising, the river knowledge — for other ways of making a living. The land itself is contested: on 7 October 2025 a court-ordered repossession saw homes demolished and families evicted from part of the quilombo's territory, which was officially titled in 1999, and CONAQ, the national quilombola coordination, condemned the operation as violent. The protected area does what it can to slow the erasure, but it cannot stop the direction of things. It can only hold the space for the communities to decide what they want to keep.

## A Cultural Ecology

Flying over the Rio Curiaú in the wet season, you see water first — a mirror spread across kilometers of cerrado, with trees standing in it and cattle picking their way along the slightly higher ground. You see the houses of the Vila do Curiaú on the drier ground. What is harder to see from altitude is what makes this place distinctive: the batuque and marabaixo drumming that emerges from the community at festivals, the dances and foods and oral histories that connect these villages to their African origins and to three centuries of Brazilian history. The APA was created to protect a watershed. It protects something else too — a story of people who walked away from bondage, built a life in a flood plain, and have never stopped insisting that the life they built is worth keeping.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 0.158°N, 51.033°W, about 14 km north-northeast of downtown Macapá, Amapá, Brazil; the APA's southern edge begins about 5 km from the city centre. Recommended viewing altitude 1,500–3,000 feet to see the flooded-field mosaic of the Curiaú river basin, with the Amazon visible to the east. Nearest airport is Macapá International (SBMQ/MCP), about 13 km to the south-southwest. Expect strong tropical humidity year-round; the rainy season runs roughly January–July, with heavy afternoon storms. The Fortaleza de São José de Macapá lies on the city waterfront to the south.
