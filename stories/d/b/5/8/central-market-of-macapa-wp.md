# Central Market of Macapá

A 1953 public market at the feet of an 18th-century Portuguese fortress - where Japanese immigrants, Amazonian farmers, and a bar named for its founder all set up shop on the same day.

**Location:** 0.0309, -51.0537

**Categories:** public markets, Amazon, Brazilian history, Amapá

## Introduction

September 13, 1953, was the right day to open a market in Macapá. Ten years earlier, to the day, the Brazilian government had carved the Federal Territory of Amapá out of the state of Pará; so when Governor Janary Gentil Nunes and Mayor Claudomiro de Moraes cut the ribbon on the Mercado Central de Macapá, the act doubled as a birthday present for the territory itself. The building went up in the shadow of the Fortress of São José - an 18th-century Portuguese stone fort - and opened its doors to farmers bringing produce from the Eliezer Levy Pier, Japanese immigrant families setting up stalls, and a merchant from Rio Grande do Norte named Pedro who opened a bar next door that is still in business today.

## A Market for a New Territory

Macapá in 1953 was a small capital on the mouth of the Amazon, its commercial life squeezed onto Cândido Mendes Street and the dock at the old colonial fortress. When the federal government created the Amapá Territory in 1943, Governor Janary Nunes inherited the job of building a modern city around an old fort. The Central Market was part of that project. Designed in 1952 by a builder known as Master Júlio and constructed through the following year, it went up facing Cândido Mendes Street, directly opposite Theodoro Mendes Square - the square named for a former mayor who had run the city from 1896 to 1916. The building sat, and still sits, in the only historically protected quarter of Macapá: the zone around the Fortress of São José, where the colonial era still shapes the street grid.

## The Fortress at the Market's Back

The Fortress of São José de Macapá is not a backdrop; it is the reason the market could be placed here at all. Built by Portugal between 1764 and 1782 to defend the Amazon's mouth against Dutch, French, and British claims, it is a massive pentagonal stone fortress on the bank of the world's largest river - one of the most complete colonial fortifications in the Americas. The enslaved Africans and indigenous laborers who built it worked under brutal conditions, and the fort's walls are themselves a monument to that forced labor. When the market opened in 1953, the two buildings - 18th-century fortress and mid-20th-century commercial hall - began a slow shared life. Shoppers who finished at the market could walk across the street to the fortress. They still can.

## Produce Off the Eliezer Levy Pier

In 1953 the Eliezer Levy Pier was the main landing point in Macapá - the place where river boats from up and down the Amazon unloaded fish, vegetables, fruit, and goods from smaller communities. The Central Market was deliberately positioned to receive that produce and channel it into a formal retail space. Farmers who had brought cassava, açaí, tucupi, and tropical fruits down the river found buyers under a roof. Fishermen laid out pirarucu and tambaqui. Japanese immigrant families, who had begun arriving in Amapá in the early 1950s as part of Brazil's broader program of Japanese immigration, set up their own stalls alongside local vendors - a cross-cultural mix that still defines the market today. The logistics of Amazonian commerce flow through places like this: rivers into piers, piers into markets, markets into kitchens.

## Bar Du Pedro

Outside the market, on Antonio Coelho de Carvalho Avenue, stands Bar Du Pedro - the oldest continuously operating business associated with the market. The bar opened the same day the market did, belonging to a merchant named Pedro who had come from Rio Grande do Norte in the 1950s looking for opportunity in the new Amapá territory. He ran it for decades. When Pedro died in the 1990s, his son Luiz Gonzaga took over. The bar has survived seventy years of political change, renovation debates, shifts in the neighborhood, and the long stretches when the market itself sat half-empty. It is the kind of place where the history of a city condenses into a counter, a few tables, and a family story.

## Closed, Renovated, Reopened

By the 2010s the Central Market was tired. Its roof was failing, its electrical systems were dated, and the number of active stalls had dwindled. A major renovation was planned. On May 1, 2018, work resumed after previous starts and stops, using approximately 2.5 million reais from the federal Calha Norte program, which funds infrastructure in Brazil's Amazon frontier municipalities. The project replaced the old roof with a new metallic and thermoacoustic structure, added a mezzanine, installed a performance stage, rebuilt bathrooms (including one fully accessible for people with disabilities), and reorganized the interior. The market reopened on January 16, 2020, with 63 stalls. Today 16 internal boxes sell fresh and salted meat and vegetables, while 20 more stalls handle groceries, fish, and general foodstuffs. Outside, restaurants, snack bars, handicraft sellers, and occasional cultural events keep the sidewalks moving.

## Flying In to Macapá

From altitude, Macapá is a compact green-and-red grid on the northern bank of the Amazon, where the river is so wide you can mistake it for the sea. The market itself is a small rectangle at the city's historic center, flanked by the blocky star of the old fortress and the broad dark line of the river. The Equator crosses the city - there is a monument marking it - and from the sky the line is invisible but the geography is unmistakable: Brazil meets the Amazon meets the Atlantic, and people have been trading here for a very long time.

## Flight Context

Located at 0.03°N, 51.05°W - essentially on the Equator. The Central Market sits in historic downtown Macapá, next to the Fortress of São José. Main airport: Macapá International (SBMQ), about 6 km west of downtown. Recommended viewing altitude: 1,500-4,000 feet AGL to see the city, the fortress, the market, and the stunning width of the Amazon River as it reaches the Atlantic. Expect convective cloud buildups; the Equator's intertropical convergence zone delivers heavy afternoon rain year-round.
