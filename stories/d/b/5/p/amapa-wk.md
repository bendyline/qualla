# Amapá

Brazil's northernmost Atlantic state, where the equator runs through the capital, rainforest covers more than 70 percent of the land, and a pre-Columbian stone circle marks the December solstice.

**Location:** 1.3833, -51.8000

**Categories:** Brazil, Amapá, Amazon, States of Brazil, Equatorial regions

## Introduction

The equator passes right through Macapá. The capital of Amapá, on the Amazon delta, built a monument called Marco Zero on the zero-degree line - complete with a sundial - and locals call their city the capital of the middle of the world. You can stand with one foot in each hemisphere. North of here, the jungle thickens, the roads thin, and the rivers take over. More than 70 percent of Brazil's northernmost Atlantic state is largely untouched rainforest, bordered by Suriname and French Guiana. It holds one of South America's least-visited pieces of Amazonia and one of the continent's most surprising archaeological sites.

## The State Few Brazilians Know

Amapá is cut off from Brazil's road network. No highway connects it to the rest of the country. To arrive by land from the south you must ferry across the Amazon from Belém to Macapá - a 36 to 38-hour journey that skirts Ilha de Marajó, often called the largest river island in the world. From the west, a ferry runs from Santarém to Almeirim, and from there a dirt road reaches Laranjal do Jari via Monte Dourado, with a short river crossing. From the north, a paved bridge now crosses the Oiapoque River from French Guiana. Dense jungle blankets the interior, and the coastline offers lagoons and marshland rather than beaches. The state is one of Brazil's poorer ones, though its economy and population have been growing fast. All of which means Amapá has stayed genuinely wild - the rainforest here is not a postcard image of a rainforest, but an actual working ecosystem mostly left alone.

## Amazonian Stonehenge

Near the small town of Calçoene, 390 kilometers from Macapá, 127 granite blocks stand in a circle on a hilltop by the Rego Grande River. The tallest blocks reach about two and a half meters. The ring measures about 30 meters across. Radiocarbon dates from charcoal in its burial pits put the site's occupation about 1,000 years ago, well before European contact. The site is called Parque Arqueológico do Solstício - the Solstice Archaeological Park - because of the December solstice: on that day the shadow of one angled block disappears. The builders almost certainly used the site for astronomical observation, ceremonial purposes, burial, or all three. It is one of the most surprising pre-Columbian finds in Amazonia and predates the European arrival by many generations. It is nicknamed the Amazonian Stonehenge, and the name stuck.

## The Pororoca

The Araguari River once flowed east out of Amapá straight to the Atlantic. When the incoming tide met the outflow, something remarkable happened. A wave built - up to six meters tall, moving upstream at up to 30 kilometers per hour, audible as a rolling thunder before it arrived. This was the pororoca, a tidal bore that drew surfers from around the world; in 2006 the Brazilian surfer Serginho Laus rode it for nearly 12 kilometers. The Araguari's wave is gone now. Around 2014-2015 the river's mouth silted up - researchers blame buffalo ranching on the floodplain and the dams upstream - and the weakened river now drains into the Amazon through the Urucurituba Canal. Tidal bores still run on other rivers of the region, from the Cassiporé and Calçoene on Amapá's northern coast to the Guamá in Pará, and they can still strip trees from the banks. The name comes from the Indigenous Tupi language and evokes a great roar - which is exactly what it sounds like.

## Macapá's Colonial Core

The capital holds a few solid traces of the colonial period. The Fortaleza de São José de Macapá, begun in 1764 and finished in 1782, was built to assert Portuguese control over the mouth of the Amazon against French, Dutch, and English rivals. It is considered one of the most imposing military structures from colonial Brazil - a square star fort with a pentagonal bastion at each of its four corners, still largely intact. The Church of São José, inaugurated in 1761, is considered the oldest building in the city and still holds mass. The Marco Zero monument, on the equator itself, uses a sundial to trace the sun's annual path - during the March and September equinoxes, the city watches the sun pass directly overhead at noon and cast no shadow at all.

## Açaí and Pescada

Amazonian cuisine belongs to the forest. Local cooks learned from the Indigenous peoples who still live here - the Wajãpi, the Palikur, the Galibi-Marworno. Pescada da Gurijuba, prepared with a large regional catfish, is a signature dish. Tucunaré na Brasa grills the prized game fish over coals. Camarão ao Bafo steams local shrimp with minimal seasoning. And then there is açaí. The dark purple berry of Euterpe oleracea, the açaí palm, grows abundantly here and was a staple indigenous food long before it became a globalized health-food fad. In Amapá you still eat it the traditional way: served thick and cold, unsweetened, with farinha flour sprinkled over the top and often fried fish or shrimp on the side. It is a meal, not a smoothie - filling, slightly earthy, genuinely different from anything else in the world.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 1.38°N, 51.80°W (state center). Amapá state covers Brazil's northernmost Atlantic coast between the Amazon mouth and French Guiana. Macapá-Alberto Alcolumbre International Airport (SBMQ/MCP) is the main airfield, with domestic flights to Belém, Brasília, São Paulo and Rio de Janeiro. Regional airports include Oiapoque (SBOI) and Laranjal do Jari. From altitude, the Amazon delta appears as a vast distributary network to the south; the rainforest canopy is unbroken across most of the state; and the equator crosses through Macapá itself. The coastal plain is flat and often cloudy in the rainy season (January-May). The BR-156 is the main road, running Oiapoque-Macapá-Laranjal do Jari.
