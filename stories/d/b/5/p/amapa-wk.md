# Amapá

Brazil's northernmost Atlantic state, where the equator runs through the capital, 70 percent of the land is unexplored rainforest, and a pre-Columbian stone circle predates the Inca.

**Location:** 1.3833, -51.8000

**Categories:** Brazil, Amapá, Amazon, States of Brazil, Equatorial regions

## Introduction

The equator passes right through Macapá. The capital of Amapá, on the Amazon delta, built a monument called Marco Zero exactly on the zero-degree line - complete with a sundial on the pavement - and locals call their city the capital of the middle of the world. You can stand with one foot in each hemisphere. North of here, the jungle thickens, the roads thin, and the rivers take over. Brazil's northernmost Atlantic state is 70 percent unexplored rainforest, bordered by Suriname and French Guiana. It holds one of South America's least-visited pieces of Amazonia and one of the continent's most surprising archaeological sites.

## The State Few Brazilians Know

Amapá is cut off from Brazil's road network. No highway connects it to the rest of the country. To arrive by land from the south you must ferry across the Amazon from Belém to Macapá - a 36 to 38-hour journey that skirts Ilha de Marajó, the largest river island in the world. From the west, a dirt road runs from Santarém to Laranjal do Jari via a river ferry at Monte Dourado. From the north, a paved bridge now crosses the Oiapoque River from French Guiana. Dense jungle blankets the interior, and the coastline offers lagoons and marshland rather than beaches. The state is one of Brazil's poorest, though its economy is growing fast. All of which means Amapá has stayed genuinely wild - the rainforest here is not a postcard image of a rainforest, but an actual working ecosystem mostly left alone.

## Amazonian Stonehenge

Near the small town of Calçoene, 390 kilometers from Macapá, 127 granite blocks stand in a circle on a hilltop by the Rego Grande River. The blocks are up to four meters tall. The ring measures more than 30 meters across. Carbon dating places its construction at roughly 1,000 years ago, well before European contact. Archaeologists call the site Parque Arqueológico do Solstício - the Solstice Archaeological Park - because the alignments of the stones track the sun's path on the December solstice, when one stone casts a shadow exactly along the axis of the circle. The builders almost certainly used the site for astronomical observation, ceremonial purposes, burial, or all three. It is one of the most surprising pre-Columbian finds in Amazonia and predates the European arrival by many generations. Brazilian journalists nicknamed it the Amazonian Stonehenge, and the name stuck.

## The Pororoca

The Araguari River flows east out of Amapá toward the Atlantic. When the incoming tide meets the outflow, something remarkable happens. A wave builds - six meters tall, moving upstream at more than 20 kilometers per hour, audible as a rolling thunder several minutes before it arrives. This is the pororoca, a tidal bore that surfers travel from around the world to ride. The wave travels as far as 800 kilometers upriver on the Amazon and its tributaries, can strip trees from the banks, and continues until the tide turns again. The name is indigenous, roughly translating as 'great roar' - which is exactly what it sounds like. On certain days of the year, when the moon and tide align just right, the pororoca sweeps through jungle riverbanks and carries away whatever is not rooted deep enough.

## Macapá's Colonial Core

The capital holds a few solid traces of the colonial period. The Fortaleza de São José de Macapá, begun in 1764 and finished in 1782, was built to assert Portuguese control over the mouth of the Amazon against French, Dutch, and English rivals. It is considered one of the most imposing military structures from colonial Brazil - a five-pointed star fort of thick stone walls, still largely intact. The Church of São José, inaugurated in 1761, is the oldest building in the city and still holds mass. The Marco Zero monument, on the equator itself, uses a sundial to trace the sun's annual path - during the March and September equinoxes, the city watches the sun pass directly overhead at noon and cast no shadow at all.

## Açaí and Pescada

Amazonian cuisine belongs to the forest. Local cooks learned from the indigenous peoples who still live here - the Wayãpi, the Apalaí, the Galibi-Marworno. Pescada da Gurijuba, prepared with a large regional catfish, is a signature dish. Tucunaré na Brasa grills the prized game fish over coals. Camarão ao Bafo steams local shrimp with minimal seasoning. And then there is açaí. The dark purple berry of Euterpe oleracea, the açaí palm, grows abundantly here and was a staple indigenous food long before it became a globalized health-food fad. In Amapá you still eat it the traditional way: served thick and cold in a wooden bowl, unsweetened, with farinha flour sprinkled over the top and sometimes pieces of fried fish stirred in. It is a meal, not a smoothie - filling, slightly earthy, genuinely different from anything else in the world.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 1.38°N, 51.80°W (state center). Amapá state covers most of Brazil's northeast Atlantic coast between the Amazon mouth and French Guiana. Macapá-Alberto Alcolumbre International Airport (SBMQ) is the main airfield, with connections to Brasília, Rio, São Paulo, and Foz do Iguaçu. Regional airports include Oiapoque (SBOI) and Laranjal do Jari. From altitude, the Amazon delta appears as a vast distributary network to the south; the rainforest canopy is unbroken across most of the state; and the equator crosses through Macapá itself. The coastal plain is flat and often cloudy in the rainy season (January-May). The BR-156 is the only major road, running Oiapoque-Macapá-Laranjal do Jari.
