# Maicuru Biological Reserve

A 11,518 square kilometer Amazon fortress where no casual visitor is allowed and where 98.5 percent of the land shows no human footprint at all.

**Location:** 1.2780, -54.0778

**Categories:** protected-area, rainforest, brazil, amazon, biological-reserve

## Introduction

Imagine a rainforest the size of Jamaica in which 98.5 percent of the land bears no recorded sign of human presence. No roads cross it. No trails. No villages. The only way in is by small aircraft onto one of five airstrips cut for mining prospectors, and permission to set foot on the ground requires paperwork from a Brazilian state environmental authority. The Maicuru Biological Reserve in Pará state is designed specifically to keep people out - not as a wilderness area where visitors hike marked trails, but as a strict scientific reserve where Amazon ecology is protected from virtually all human interference.

## The Rivers That Define It

Maicuru sits between two rivers - the Maicuru to the west and the Jari to the east - on the border between Pará and Amapá states. The reserve covers 1,151,761 hectares, or 11,518 square kilometers. It touches a remarkable network of waterways. Almost all of the reserve drains toward the Paru-Jari basin through the Itapecuru, Ipitinga, Careparu, Paru, and Jarí rivers. The southern portion belongs to the Cuminapanema-Maicuru basin, fed by the Maicurú, Curuá, Mamiá, Cuminapanema, and Jauaru rivers. Altogether, approximately 3,700 kilometers of water courses braid through the forest. Most of these rivers carry the tea-black color of tannin-rich Amazonian waters. They cut past sandstone outcrops and under canopies that rarely open enough to show the sky.

## A Mosaic of Protection

The reserve doesn't stand alone. It forms part of the Calha Norte do rio Amazonas - a network of adjoining protected areas that together preserve one of the largest intact rainforest blocks left on Earth. To the north sits the Tumucumaque Indigenous Park. Along its edges the Rio Paru d'Este Indigenous Territory, the Tumucumaque Mountains National Park, and the Paru State Forest all adjoin. The reserve is part of the Center of Guianan Endemism, a biogeographic zone extending across Brazil, Venezuela, Guyana, French Guiana, and Suriname. Together these protected spaces form what conservationists call a mega-reserve - land vast enough that species populations have the space to perpetuate themselves across generations without outside intervention.

## The Decree

Brazil established the reserve through state decree 2610 on December 4, 2006. As a biological reserve under Brazilian conservation law, Maicuru provides full protection with human interference permitted only indirectly. Public access is prohibited except for specific educational purposes defined in the management plan, and any scientific research requires advance approval from the Pará Secretary of State for the Environment. Conservation International prepared the management plan with technical assistance from the Museu Paraense Emílio Goeldi and the Institute of Man and Environment in the Amazon. The plan took effect in 2011, and the management councils for Maicuru and the neighboring Grão Pará Ecological Station were formed in March 2013. Funding comes from the Amazon Region Protected Areas Program.

## Living Inventory

Scientists who have made it inside have documented 88 species of fish, 31 amphibians, 34 reptiles, 302 birds, and 33 mammals. Among the birds, the vulnerable crested eagle - Morphnus guianensis, a canopy raptor with a distinctive dark-and-white plumage - patrols the upper forest. Among the mammals, endangered jaguar, cougar, and giant anteater all range the reserve. Plant science has recorded 88 species of ferns and 306 species of flowering plants, numbers certain to grow as more research becomes possible. Nearly all of Maicuru - 98.24 percent - is classified as dense submontane rainforest. Small fractions hold transition forest (0.96 percent) and a sliver of Cerrado savanna (0.04 percent). Together the vegetation holds an almost unbroken canopy from horizon to horizon.

## What No Human Pressure Looks Like

The most astonishing number in the reserve's records is the one for human pressure: 98.5 percent of the reserve shows no recorded trace of human activity. The remaining 1.5 percent - hot spots scattered through the reserve - indicate medium pressure, largely concentrated in the mining areas that created the landing strips giving the reserve its only practical access. Climate data describe a tropical monsoon pattern, temperatures between about 18 and 32 degrees Celsius most of the year, annual rainfall of 2,300 millimeters or more, and a rainy season from December through June. Between July and November the rain drops to 50 millimeters a month. The numbers sound like any equatorial forest, but few of those forests remain this intact. Maicuru is one of the places where the Amazon still looks the way the Amazon used to.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 1.278°N, 54.078°W. Recommended viewing altitude 10,000-15,000 feet for a sense of scale - the reserve covers 11,518 square kilometers, roughly the size of Jamaica. Landmarks: the Maicuru River on the western edge, the Jari River on the eastern edge, the Tumucumaque Mountains to the north (which rise dramatically from the forest canopy), and the Paru-Jari river basin's dense drainage network. Nearest airstrips: the five interior strips in mining areas (private, not public), with nearest commercial airports at Almeirim and Monte Alegre to the south. Weather: tropical monsoon; very heavy rain December-June, drier July-November. Canopy cover is continuous; little ground visible.
