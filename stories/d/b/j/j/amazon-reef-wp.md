# Amazon Reef

A 9,500-square-kilometer coral and sponge reef hidden for centuries beneath the Amazon's muddy plume, discovered by modern science only in 2016.

**Location:** 1.0000, -49.0000

**Categories:** Brazil, French Guiana, Coral reefs, Atlantic Ocean, Marine ecosystems

## Introduction

No one thought a reef could possibly exist here. The Amazon pushes one-fifth of all the freshwater flowing into Earth's oceans into a single spot on the Atlantic coast of Brazil - a plume of brown, silt-heavy, low-salinity water that spreads far out to sea. Corals want clear tropical water and stable salinity; the Amazon mouth offers the opposite. So scientists long assumed: no reefs here. Then in 2012 a research vessel dropped equipment at the right depth, and in April 2016 the results came back. Beneath the plume, in deeper, saltier water, stretches a reef system 9,500 square kilometers in size and roughly 1,000 kilometers long.

## A Reef in the Wrong Place

The science is counterintuitive. Coral reefs thrive in warm, clear, stable saltwater - which is why the classic reef belts wrap tropical shorelines far from major river mouths. River mouths deliver fresh water, sediment, and shifting acidity, all lethal to coral. The Amazon delivers all three at the largest scale on Earth. But the Amazon Reef sits deep enough - 30 to 220 meters below the surface - that the freshwater plume passes above it without disturbing the clearer, saltier water below. The southern end of the reef sees the plume only three months a year; the northern end, further from the mouth, is shaded from sunlight by the plume for six or more months a year. Different sections have adapted to different light and chemical regimes, producing what researchers have described as a new kind of reef biome.

## Discovered, Almost Lost

The first hints came in the late 1950s, when a US survey ship dredged sponges from the seafloor off the Amazon. In 1977, scientists described reef fish living over sponge bottoms off the river mouth, found during a survey for shrimp-trawling grounds, but without precise locations. In 1999, Caribbean-type coral species were found near the Amazon delta. Decades later, a team led by Brazilian oceanographer Rodrigo Moura, with University of Georgia scientists aboard, used acoustic surveys and dredged samples to find the structure on cruises in 2010, 2012 and 2014. The 2016 publication in Science Advances stunned the marine biology community. A reef the size of a small country had been hiding in plain sight at the mouth of the world's largest river.

## What Lives in the Dark Reef

The Amazon Reef is not what most people picture when they hear the word 'reef.' In its shallowest zones, around 60 meters, sand dunes cover the bottom. Between 70 and 130 meters, sediment partly covers the living structure. At 130 meters, diverse reef communities open up - schools of creole fish, Paranthias furcifer, swim over bottoms carpeted in living crustose coralline algae and forests of black coral. Deeper still, at 220 meters, nearly 100 percent of the surface is covered by living benthic life: sponges, octocorals, and more black corals. Cleaning stations where shrimp pick parasites from larger fish operate at around 110 meters. Giant snowy groupers over 60 centimeters long patrol at 190 meters. Rhodolith beds - free-living balls of red calcareous algae that superficially resemble coral - dominate large stretches. It is a reef built more on sponge, algae, and deep cold-water coral than on the sun-loving brain corals of the Caribbean.

## Oil, Fishing, and the Future

The discovery came with a problem. By the time the reef was described, 80 oil exploration blocks had been acquired in the region over the previous decade, 20 of them already producing - with what the 2016 paper called an environmental baseline 'based on sparse museum specimens.' Drilling directly over a reef nobody had mapped raised immediate alarm. Greenpeace campaigned against drilling, and Brazil's environmental agency, IBAMA, refused Total a licence in 2018 and Petrobras one in 2023. The pressure never went away: IBAMA licensed Petrobras's Morpho exploratory well in the Foz do Amazonas basin in October 2025, the company reported finding oil there in 2026, and three more wells have since been authorized. Overfishing and coastal pollution add their own stresses. The reef is particularly vulnerable because so little of it is mapped, and because its deep, dim ecosystem would likely be slow to recover.

## Why It Matters

The Amazon Reef rewrites a chapter of what we thought we knew about oceans. The assumption that major river mouths always break up reef systems turned out to be wrong; the depth and current structure of this coast allow something remarkable to exist right where no one expected. Pockets of Caribbean coral species along the reef suggest it may function as a biological bridge between Caribbean and South Atlantic reef ecosystems - a possible pathway for species movement during past climate shifts. And as climate change warms and acidifies the surface ocean, deep reefs like this may serve as refuges where corals survive while their shallower cousins bleach. The Amazon Reef is young to science - a decade into its documented existence - and vast stretches remain unexplored. Expeditions have turned up species new to science.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 1°N, 49°W (approximate center). The Amazon Reef lies offshore along the continental shelf of northern Brazil and French Guiana, extending roughly 1,000 km along the shelf from off the French Guiana border to off Maranhão, on both sides of the Amazon mouth, in waters 30-220 m deep and up to 120 km offshore. Not visible from the surface. From altitude, the Amazon plume is a clear navigation marker - brown freshwater meeting blue Atlantic in a distinct color boundary that can stretch 100+ km offshore. Macapá International (SBMQ) and Belém-Val de Cans (SBBE) are the closest airfields along the Brazilian coast; Cayenne-Félix Eboué (SOCA) serves the French Guiana side.
