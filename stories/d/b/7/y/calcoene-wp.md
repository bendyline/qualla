# Calçoene

Often called Brazil's rainiest town and home to the Amazon Stonehenge - 127 granite megaliths aligned to the December solstice, raised by a society more complex than archaeologists once thought the Amazon held.

**Location:** 2.4978, -50.9489

**Categories:** archaeology, Amazon Stonehenge, Brazil, pre-Columbian history

## Introduction

Calçoene is often called the rainiest town in Brazil - an average of 4,165 millimeters every year. That is more than four meters of water falling on a single patch of coastal Amapá, a drowning of the calendar. And yet it is what that rain does not hide that has made this small municipality suddenly famous: on a hill outside town, 127 granite blocks stand upright, the tallest close to three meters, arranged with such deliberate geometry that the sun on the winter solstice aligns with one specific stone. Archaeologists announced the discovery in May 2006. They called it the Amazon Stonehenge. The builders, whoever they were, were active here roughly a thousand years ago, by radiocarbon dates from the site - long before Europeans believed the Amazon rainforest could produce such a thing.

## An Observatory in the Forest

The site sits on a low hill a few kilometers outside Calçoene. The stones are granite, the tallest close to three meters high, set firmly into the earth. Mariana Petry Cabral of the Amapá Institute of Scientific and Technological Research (IEPA), who led the archaeological work with João Saldanha, said the arrangement was designed to align with the winter solstice - a calibration that would have required careful observation of the sky. Radiocarbon dates on charcoal from the site's burial pits put its occupation about 1,000 years ago; early estimates based on pottery ranged from 500 to 2,000 years. For decades the prevailing archaeological view held that pre-Columbian Amazon cultures did not develop this kind of monumental complexity. The stones forced a rewrite. Whoever built them watched the sky with the seriousness of farmers who needed to know when the seasons would turn.

## The Wettest Place in Brazil

The 4,165 millimeters of annual rainfall is not evenly distributed - it arrives in torrents during the wet season, when coastal Amapá can feel as if the sky has opened permanently. That rain is why the forest around Calçoene is so dense, and a single wet season can leave unpaved stretches of road impassable. For visitors, the rain is the first thing you learn to plan around.

## The Republic That Almost Was

For most of the 19th century the borders between French Guiana and Brazil were unresolved, and the territory between the Amazon and the Oyapock River was treated as neutral ground. In 1885, a Frenchman named Paul Quartier met with the chiefs of two villages - Cunani and Carsewenne, the latter now called Calçoene - and in 1886 the result was proclaimed: the Republic of Counani, an unrecognized micro-state with Cunani as its capital. It was part real, part colonial daydream: a scheme by French adventurers and two local leaders to establish a country in the contested zone. The republic persisted in name until 1891. In 1900, Swiss arbitration awarded the territory to Brazil, and Counani dissolved back into Brazilian sovereignty. Calçoene itself did not become an official municipality until 1956.

## North Wedge

By one account, the name Calçoene is a corruption of Calço N - "North Wedge" - one of four mining zones the Brazilian government defined in the early 20th century, though the French already called the river and village Carsewenne. Today the municipality covers about 14,100 square kilometers and was home to 10,612 people at the 2022 census, most of them in or near the town. Calçoene is bordered on the north and east by the Atlantic Ocean, to the south by the municipalities of Amapá and Pracuúba, and to the west by Oiapoque and Serra do Navio. Macapá, the state capital, lies about 270 kilometers to the south as the crow flies - farther by road, a long drive along BR-156.

## Goiabal and the Wide Atlantic

Fourteen kilometers from the town of Calçoene, Goiabal beach opens onto the Atlantic. The water is brown, not turquoise - this is the edge of the Amazon's outflow, where continental rivers release their burden of silt into the ocean and color it the shade of strong tea. On a clear afternoon the beach stretches empty in both directions. To the north lies Cape Orange National Park. To the south, down the mangrove-laced coast, is the town of Amapá. The sea here has no tourist sheen. It has weather, distance, and salt - and a feeling, when the rain briefly stops, that you have reached somewhere you could disappear into.

## Flying the North Wedge

From altitude, Calçoene is a small clearing at the edge of an enormous coastal forest, with a brown river winding out to a browner sea. The Stonehenge site is a bare hilltop - just visible on a clear day as a pale spot above the canopy. In a country of famous places, Calçoene is still almost unknown. The stones have been waiting a thousand years or more. They are patient.

## Flight Context

Located at 2.50°N, 50.95°W, on the Calçoene River near the Atlantic coast of Amapá, Brazil. The nearest airport with scheduled flights is Macapá International (SBMQ), about 270 km to the south; the small Amapá airstrip (SBAM, no scheduled service) is about 48 km south-southeast. Oiapoque (SBOI) is about 180 km to the north-northwest. Recommended viewing altitude: 3,000-7,000 feet AGL to take in the coast, the rivers draining to the Atlantic, and the transition from mangrove to dense rainforest. Expect heavy rain and low ceilings, especially January to June; this is often called the wettest municipality in Brazil.
