# Calçoene

The rainiest place in Brazil and home to the Amazon Stonehenge - 127 granite megaliths aligned to the winter solstice, built up to 2,000 years ago by a civilization archaeologists didn't think existed.

**Location:** 2.4978, -50.9489

**Categories:** archaeology, Amazon Stonehenge, Brazil, pre-Columbian history

## Introduction

Calçoene catches more rain than anywhere else in Brazil - an average of 4,165 millimeters every year. That is more than four meters of water falling on a single patch of coastal Amapá, a drowning of the calendar. And yet it is what that rain does not hide that has made this small municipality suddenly famous: on a hill outside town, 127 granite blocks stand upright, each about three meters tall, arranged with such deliberate geometry that the sun on the winter solstice aligns with one specific stone. Archaeologists announced the discovery in May 2006. They called it the Amazon Stonehenge. The builders, whoever they were, finished their work between 500 and 2,000 years ago - long before anyone in Europe believed the Amazon rainforest could produce such a thing.

## An Observatory in the Forest

The site sits on a low hill above Calçoene. The stones are granite, each roughly three meters high, set firmly into the earth. Mariana Petry Cabral of the Amapá Institute of Scientific and Technological Research (IEPA), who led the archaeological work, determined that the arrangement aligns with the winter solstice - a precise astronomical calibration that required sustained observation across generations and careful surveying. Pottery sherds recovered from the site have been dated to about 2,000 years old, giving researchers a reasonable lower bound for when the observatory was in use. For decades the prevailing archaeological view held that pre-Columbian Amazon cultures did not develop this kind of monumental complexity. The stones forced a rewrite. Whoever built them watched the sky with the seriousness of farmers who needed to know when the seasons would turn.

## The Wettest Place in Brazil

The 4,165 millimeters of annual rainfall is not evenly distributed - it arrives in torrents during the wet season, when coastal Amapá can feel as if the sky has opened permanently. That rain is why the region is called the Amazon jungle basin near the Atlantic; it is why the forest around Calçoene is so dense; and it is, in part, why the municipality was so difficult for European powers to settle. A single Calçoene wet season dumps enough water on 14,269 square kilometers of municipality to make most roads impassable. For residents, umbrellas are architecture: houses are built with eaves that reach almost to the ground. For visitors, the rain is the first thing you learn to plan around.

## The Republic That Almost Was

For most of the 19th century the borders between French Guiana and Brazil were unresolved, and the territory between the Amazon and the Oyapock River was treated as neutral ground. In 1885, a Frenchman named Paul Quartier met with the chiefs of two villages - Cunani and Carsewenne, the latter now called Calçoene - and the result was the Republic of Counani, an unrecognized micro-state with Cunani as its capital. It was part real, part colonial daydream: a scheme by French adventurers and two local leaders to establish a country in the contested zone. The republic persisted in name until 1891. In 1900, Swiss arbitration awarded the territory to Brazil, and Counani dissolved back into Brazilian sovereignty. Calçoene itself did not become an official municipality until 1956.

## North Wedge

The name Calçoene is a corruption of Calço N - "North Wedge" - one of four mining zones the Brazilian government defined in the early 20th century. The name outlasted the mining plan. Today the municipality covers 14,269 square kilometers and is home to about 11,306 people, most of them in or near the coastal town. Calçoene is bordered on the north and east by the Atlantic Ocean, to the south by the municipalities of Amapá and Pracuúba, and to the west by Oiapoque and Serra do Navio. Macapá, the state capital, lies 272 kilometers to the southwest - a long drive through rainforest along BR-156.

## Goiabal and the Wide Atlantic

Fourteen kilometers from the town of Calçoene, Goiabal beach opens onto the Atlantic. The water is brown, not turquoise - this is the edge of the Amazon's outflow, where continental rivers release their burden of silt into the ocean and color it the shade of strong tea. On a clear afternoon the beach stretches empty in both directions, the forest meeting the sand almost without transition. Offshore, the Atlantic is shallow and muddy; ships stay far out. To the north lies Cape Orange National Park. To the south, down the mangrove-laced coast, is Amapá proper. The sea here has no tourist sheen. It has weather, distance, and salt - and a feeling, when the rain briefly stops, that you have reached somewhere you could disappear into.

## Flying the North Wedge

From altitude, Calçoene is a small clearing at the edge of an enormous coastal forest, with a brown river winding out to a browner sea. The Stonehenge site is a bare hilltop - just visible on a clear day as a pale spot above the canopy. In a country of famous places, Calçoene is still almost unknown. The stones have been waiting two thousand years. They are patient.

## Flight Context

Located at 2.50°N, 50.95°W, on the Atlantic coast of Amapá, Brazil. The nearest airport is Macapá International (SBMQ) about 272 km southwest; small airstrips serve the region locally. Oiapoque (SBOI) is about 150 km to the northwest. Recommended viewing altitude: 3,000-7,000 feet AGL to take in the coast, the rivers draining to the Atlantic, and the transition from mangrove to dense rainforest. Expect heavy rain and low ceilings year-round; this is the wettest municipality in Brazil.
