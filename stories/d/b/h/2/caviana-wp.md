# Caviana

An Amazon-mouth island that the Equator runs through - once the Aruã stronghold called Uyruma, now split in two by a tidal bore strong enough to reshape the land.

**Location:** 0.1667, -50.0000

**Categories:** Amazon Delta, islands, indigenous history, Marajó

## Introduction

Sometime between 1845 and 1850, a single tidal bore was powerful enough to cut a Brazilian island in half. The pororoca - the Amazon's famously violent tidal wave - has been reshaping the mouth of the world's largest river for as long as the river has had a mouth, but that one, or that decade of ones, finished a job the currents had been working on for years. Caviana is now two islands that share a name: Inner Caviana, 687 square kilometers to the north, and Outer Caviana, 2,257 square kilometers to the south. The Equator itself runs through Outer Caviana. In the 17th and 18th centuries, the Aruã people called this place Uyruma and made it their stronghold. The tides have been rearranging its coasts ever since.

## Third-Largest in the Delta

Caviana sits in the Marajó Archipelago, the cluster of islands at the mouth of the Amazon, and it is the third-largest of them all - bigger than almost every island in the Caribbean but ranked behind only Marajó and Ilha Grande de Gurupá. It belongs to the municipality of Chaves in Pará state. The 50th meridian west crosses Outer Caviana, and so does the Equator: on a long enough walk, you can theoretically be in the Northern Hemisphere at breakfast and the Southern Hemisphere by lunch without leaving the same island. Caviana is bordered by other Marajó islands on every side - Janaucu and Jarupari to the north, Mexiana to the east across the ominously named Canal Perigoso (Dangerous Channel), and the Jurupari Archipelago to the west. The channel earns its name: sandbanks and winds make low-tide crossings perilous.

## A Land Still Moving

Twelve thousand years ago, at the start of the Holocene, Caviana, Marajó, and Mexiana were connected to each other and to the South American mainland. Geologists can trace the evidence: the Barreiras Formation runs continuously under all three islands, and ancient paleochannels link them beneath the sediment. Then the seas rose during the Flandrian transgression and tectonic adjustment completed the separation, leaving the islands divided by water. That process has never really stopped. Wind, tides, and the Amazon's enormous sediment load rearrange the delta constantly. Residents of Caviana still distinguish parts of their island with names like Ilha Nova (new island) and Ilha da Prainha (island of the little beach) - remembering when those places were separate pieces of land before silt and current stitched them together.

## The Pororoca

Here the Amazon meets the Atlantic on its own terms. The river pushes water seaward with enormous force; the tide pushes back with equal stubbornness. Where they collide around Caviana, the result is extreme turbulence, erosion, landslides, and sedimentation - and the distinctive tidal bore called the pororoca, a wave that can roll upriver for miles, peeling bank sediment as it goes. Surfers have famously attempted to ride it, some succeeding, some pulled under by the logjam of floating debris the wave carries. For Caviana, the pororoca is both hazard and shaper. The wave that split the island between 1845 and 1850 was itself a pororoca; it broke through a stretch of already eroded coast, its floodwaters followed and widened a stream called the Igarapé Guajuru, and the two halves never rejoined.

## The Aruã Stronghold

The Aruã people called this place Uyruma and made it the center of their world. In the 17th and 18th centuries it was their stronghold, and they lived mostly along the eastern coast. Archaeological work at sites called tesos - raised ground whose natural or human origin is still debated - has revealed polychrome ceramics similar to those found in northern and southeastern Amapá, suggesting cultural connections across the wider Amazon mouth. A second phase of settlement, extending into colonial times, shows clear ties to the broader Arawak-speaking world: aligned stones, polished statuettes, nephrite beads and amulets, ceramic figures, plates, and roasters with distinctive dotted decoration. In the mid-17th century the Aruã chief was named Piyé. His community called their main settlement Aldeia de Piyé - the Village of Piyé - until a Portuguese missionary renamed it Rebordelo in 1760. In 1763 a fire struck the village. The Aruã's hold on the island did not survive the colonial century that followed.

## Marshes and Birds

Today Caviana is part of the Marajó várzea - the vast inundated biome around the Amazon's mouth - and the 59,985-square-kilometer Marajó Archipelago Environmental Protection Area, established in 1989. The island is flat, marshy, and frequently flooded, especially in the wet season. The east side is grassland that drowns seasonally; the west is rainforest dominated by palms. Water buffalo farms, owned mostly by people living in Belém, Chaves and Macapá, occupy the eastern fields, while riverside families fish and gather açaí and other forest products. For birdwatchers, Caviana is quietly legendary: as many as 145 species have been recorded here. The American ornithologist Joseph Beal Steere documented the birds of Caviana in 1871, and his specimens went to the University of Michigan. Few birders make the journey today, but those who do find an island still largely as the Aruã knew it - wet, warm, and full of wings.

## Flying the Delta

From cruising altitude, Caviana is a green wedge at the exact point where the Amazon stops being a river and becomes an ocean. The Equator crosses it. The water around it is brown for hundreds of miles in every direction. Somewhere beneath the wing, a pororoca is gathering itself.

## Flight Context

Centered at 0.17°N, 50.00°W - the Equator crosses Outer Caviana. The island is in the Amazon Delta, Pará state, Brazil. Nearest airport is Macapá (SBMQ), about 120 km to the west across the Amazon's northern channel; Belém (SBBE) is about 240 km to the southeast. Recommended viewing altitude: 5,000-10,000 feet AGL for the full delta geometry - the brown plume of the Amazon meeting Atlantic blue, islands braiding across the river's mouth. Expect convective cloud buildups, especially in late afternoon; the Equator's intertropical convergence zone runs through here.
