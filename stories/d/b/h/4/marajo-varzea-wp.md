# Marajó várzea

A forest flooded twice a day by Atlantic tides, where fish swim through the canopy eating fruit and where the Amazon empties into the sea.

**Location:** 0.4440, -50.3230

**Categories:** ecoregion, amazon, brazil, wetland, várzea

## Introduction

Twice a day, the ocean tides push the river's water back onto the forest floor, and the trees disappear up to their lower branches. Fish swim through what was forest an hour ago. Capybaras wade through waist-deep water toward fruit dropped by the tides. Then the tide retreats and the forest returns, damp and renewed. The Marajó várzea is a floodplain ecoregion at the mouth of the Amazon, where river meets ocean in a collision that reshapes the landscape every twelve hours.

## Where the Amazon Broadens

The várzea forest begins where the Xingu River joins the Amazon, and from there the landscape becomes something different - a labyrinth of islands and floodplains covering 8,857,759 hectares at the river's mouth. It spreads across coastal Pará and Amapá states, with a small extension into French Guiana. Marajó Island, some 40,100 square kilometers and often called the largest river-and-sea island in the world, sits at the heart of the region, flanked by smaller islands named Porcos, Pará, Mututí, and Uituquara. The estuary formed during the Holocene, the most recent geological epoch, as river sediments accumulated against Tertiary rock. The Amazon deposits its nutrient load here in annual floods, and the sediment builds. Slowly, the islands grow.

## A Forest Ruled by Palms

Unlike the terra firme rainforests farther inland, the várzea is shorter and less diverse - but it is dominated by palms in a way that gives the landscape its character. Açaí palms (Euterpe oleracea), now famous globally for the berries once eaten only in the Amazon basin, grow in vast stands. Buriti palms (Mauritia flexuosa) cluster along riverbanks, their fan-shaped fronds rising over clusters of scaly, orange-fleshed fruit that feeds everything from fish to capybaras to humans. Murumuru, raffia, bacaba, patauá, ubuçu, and cashapona palms thread through as well. The timber species - Virola surinamensis, Cedrelinga castanaeformis, Ceiba pentandra - reach up through the palm canopy. Along the banks, the shrub Machaerium lanatum forms dense thickets. Great lianas climb the older trees.

## Fish in the Canopy

The most remarkable biological fact about the várzea is that when the flood comes, fruit-eating fish leave the channels and swim into the flooded forest to feed. Pacus, tambaqui, and pirarucu - the last a truly giant fish reaching three meters long - move through trees pursuing the fallen fruit, and in the process they spread seeds to wherever they eventually defecate. Fish become gardeners here. They plant the next forest. 99 species of mammals have been recorded. Jaguars and cougars prowl the higher ground. Capybaras, the world's largest rodents, graze both land and water in herds. Gray brocket and red brocket deer move through the underbrush. At least 540 bird species use the region: scarlet ibis flash crimson against green, roseate spoonbills filter muddy water for invertebrates, whistling ducks call across the channels. Endangered white-cheeked spider monkeys and black bearded sakis still hold on.

## Twice a Day

The tides from the Atlantic reach deep into the Amazon's mouth. Twice daily the water rises two to three meters over the low-lying land. The soils - mottled clay with poor drainage, formed from countless centuries of sediment - hold the water and release it reluctantly. The constant action of tide and river forms a maze of channels that shift with each flood season. Köppen classifies this as an Am climate: equatorial with monsoon rainfall. Average temperatures hover around 28 degrees Celsius. Annual precipitation averages 2,600 millimeters. Monthly rainfall ranges dramatically - 33 millimeters in October, over 420 millimeters in March.

## Vulnerability

The ecoregion's conservation profile lists no protected areas; much of the archipelago now lies inside the sustainable-use Marajó Archipelago Environmental Protection Area, which holds Charapucu State Park and a few extractive reserves. That is thin cover for an ecoregion of global significance, and it reflects how long humans have used this landscape. Cattle and water-buffalo ranches have degraded natural grasslands and forests, cutting food supplies for capybaras, manatees, and the fruit-eating fish that hold the ecosystem together. Commercial logging on Marajó has nearly eliminated some valuable native tree species. Açaí monoculture - planted for the booming global market in the berries - has replaced stands of native buriti palms. Climate change compounds all of this. Low, flat ecoregions like the várzea are extraordinarily vulnerable to sea-level rise, and as tropical species try to migrate uphill to find suitable temperatures, the várzea's flat geography offers them nowhere to go. The endemic Marajó short-tailed opossum, so little studied that the IUCN lists it as Data Deficient, depends on this low, wet habitat.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 0.444°N, 50.323°W. Recommended viewing altitude 8,000-12,000 feet to comprehend the scale of the Amazon estuary - Marajó alone spans some 295 km. Landmarks: Marajó Island (about 40,100 km²), the confluence of the Xingu River with the Amazon, the many braided channels of the delta, and the transition zones to coastal mangroves on the ocean-facing edges. Nearest airports: Macapá International (SBMQ), about 95 km to the west-southwest, and Val-de-Cans International in Belém (SBBE), about 290 km to the southeast. Weather: equatorial monsoon; heavy afternoon rains March-May most dramatic; twice-daily tidal flooding visible from the air as the forest floor fills and empties.
