# Johan Adolf Pengel International Airport

Suriname's main airport - locally known as Zanderij - was Amelia Earhart's last stop in South America, a WWII Lend-Lease transit base, and the site of the deadliest aviation accident in Surinamese history.

**Location:** 5.4528, -55.1878

**Categories:** Airports, Suriname, Aviation History, World War II, International Airports

## Introduction

On the afternoon of 3 June 1937, at 2:38 P.M. local time, Amelia Earhart landed a Lockheed Model 10 Electra on a grass strip called Zanderij, forty-five kilometers south of Paramaribo. Her navigator was Fred Noonan, a retired Pan American aviator. They checked into the Palace Hotel in town, slept, and flew out for Fortaleza the next morning. One month later, on 2 July 1937, they vanished over the central Pacific somewhere near Howland Island. That grass strip is now the Johan Adolf Pengel International Airport, which locals still call Zanderij, and its runway - 3.5 kilometers long, one of the longest in the Caribbean - has since hosted everything from Air Force One to the Antonov An-225 Mriya, the largest cargo plane ever built. But few things on the ground here speak to the place's past more directly than the fact that Earhart's footprints are in its history.

## Mail, Then Celebrities

Pan American World Airways opened a mail route from Miami to Paramaribo in 1928, flying Sikorsky S-38 amphibians into the coastal waters. The Zanderij airstrip, carved out of sandy savanna south of the capital, became an official stop. What followed for a decade reads like a celebrity itinerary of interwar aviation. On 16 April 1934 the American aviator Laura Ingalls landed a Lockheed Air Express here during the first solo flight around South America in a land plane. The KLM tri-motor Fokker F.XVIII named Snip completed the first KLM transatlantic crossing to the Americas in December 1934, with Paramaribo as a stop. In January 1937 William Henry Vanderbilt III arrived in a baby Clipper with wife and friends. In May 1939 the Flying Hutchinsons, whose family round-the-world trip was sponsored by Pepsi Cola and broadcast on radio, landed in a twin-engine Lockheed Electra. Two pilots in March 1934 failed to find the strip at all and crashed their Lockheed so badly it had to be shipped back to the United States in pieces.

## The Bauxite War

The Netherlands fell to Germany in May 1940. Its government relocated to London, and from there negotiated with the United States. Suriname at that moment was the world's principal source of bauxite - the ore from which aluminum was refined for American aircraft - and it was effectively undefended. On 30 November 1941 the first American troops arrived at Zanderij. Within weeks the US Army Corps of Engineers was extending the runways and building a road from Onverwacht into the airport, completed in 1942. Zanderij became a major transport base on the South Atlantic air ferry route to Freetown, Sierra Leone, and onward to the European and African fronts. The 35th Bombardment Squadron flew B-25 Mitchells here from November 1941 to October 1943. The 99th Bombardment Squadron arrived on 3 December 1941 - four days before Pearl Harbor - with Douglas B-18 Bolos. On 2 October 1942 one of those B-18As, piloted by Captain Howard Burhanna Jr., depth-charged and sank the German submarine U-512 off Cayenne. The 22nd Fighter Squadron brought P-39 Airacobras, later replaced by P-47 Thunderbolts, to defend the field.

## A Princess and a President

On 2 November 1943 Princess Juliana of the Netherlands became the first member of the Dutch royal family ever to set foot in Suriname when she landed at Zanderij in a KLM Lockheed Super Electra - the same aircraft, PJ-AIM Meeuw, that had run the old Paramaribo-Curaçao mail route. Governor Kielstra met her on the tarmac with a guard of honor. On 3 March 1960 President Dwight D. Eisenhower landed at Zanderij on Air Force One, accompanied by Secretary of State Christian Herter. They left the same day. On 14 April 1967 it was President Lyndon B. Johnson's turn, arriving during a rainstorm on the same Boeing 707. On 7 April 1972 the first Boeing 747 wide-body jet ever to land in South America - a KLM aircraft - touched down here. In 2010 the Antonov An-225 Mriya, the world's largest cargo plane at the time, rolled down the same strip that had once been grass.

## Flight 764

On 7 June 1989 a Douglas DC-8 named "Anthony Nesty" - after the Surinamese swimmer who had just won Olympic gold in Seoul - crashed on approach to the airport. Surinam Airways Flight 764 was inbound from Amsterdam. The aircraft was leased from Arrow Air. Of the 187 people on board, 176 died. Fifteen of them were Surinamese-Dutch professional football players, an entire generation of the diaspora's sporting talent, returning home for what was meant to be a celebratory training camp. It remains the worst aviation disaster in Suriname's history. The airport carries the scar. The plaque inside the terminal that remembers the football players is as close as the building comes to a monument; the disaster is still spoken about in Paramaribo with the weight it deserves.

## The Name on the Terminal

The airport was renamed for Johan Adolf Pengel, the Surinamese politician who served as Prime Minister and whose career defined much of the country's late colonial and early independence period. Most Surinamers still call it Zanderij after the small village and sand savanna where it sits. Today it is Suriname's principal international gateway and the hub for Surinam Airways, with transatlantic service to Amsterdam by KLM, TUI fly Netherlands, and SLM, and regional routes to Belém, Georgetown, Cayenne, Panama City, Miami, and a handful of Caribbean islands. The Chinese-funded expansion begun in the late 2010s is meant to raise passenger throughput and finally connect the departures and arrivals halls with airbridges. For now, passengers still walk across the tarmac, same as they did when Earhart slept in town eighty-eight years ago.

## Flight Context

ICAO: SMJP. IATA: PBM. Located at 5.453°N, 55.188°W, in the town of Zanderij, 45 km south of Paramaribo. Single runway 11/29, approximately 3,480 m long - one of the longest in the Caribbean basin. Recommended viewing altitude on approach 1,500-3,000 ft; runway 11 approach crosses flat sand savanna, runway 29 approach crosses low-lying forest. Nearest alternate: Zorg en Hoop (SMZO) at Paramaribo for light aircraft, or Cayenne – Félix Eboué (SOCA / CAY) 320 km east for jet operations.
