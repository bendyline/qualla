# Johan Adolf Pengel International Airport

Suriname's main airport - locally known as Zanderij - was a stop on Amelia Earhart's final flight, a WWII Lend-Lease transit base, and the site of the deadliest aviation accident in Surinamese history.

**Location:** 5.4528, -55.1878

**Categories:** Airports, Suriname, Aviation History, World War II, International Airports

## Introduction

On the afternoon of 3 June 1937, at 2:38 P.M. local time, Amelia Earhart landed a Lockheed Model 10 Electra on a grass strip called Zanderij, forty-five kilometers south of Paramaribo. Her navigator was Fred Noonan, a former Pan American navigator. They checked into the Palace Hotel in Paramaribo, slept, and flew out for Fortaleza the next morning. One month later, on 2 July 1937, they vanished over the central Pacific somewhere near Howland Island. That grass strip is now the Johan Adolf Pengel International Airport, which locals still call Zanderij, and its runway - 3.5 kilometers long, one of the longest in the Caribbean - has since hosted everything from Air Force One to the Antonov An-225 Mriya, the largest cargo plane ever built. But few things on the ground here speak to the place's past more directly than the fact that Earhart's footprints are in its history.

## Mail, Then Celebrities

Pan American Airways extended its mail route from Miami to Paramaribo in 1929, with Charles Lindbergh flying Sikorsky S-38 amphibians on the opening run. The Zanderij airstrip, carved out of sandy savanna south of the capital, became an official stop. What followed for a decade reads like a celebrity itinerary of interwar aviation. On 16 April 1934 the American aviator Laura Ingalls landed a Lockheed Air Express here during the first solo flight around South America in a land plane. The KLM tri-motor Fokker F.XVIII named Snip completed the first KLM transatlantic crossing to the Americas in December 1934, with Paramaribo as a stop. In January 1937 William Henry Vanderbilt III arrived in a Sikorsky S-38 with his wife and the Flying Hutchinsons. In May 1939 the Flying Hutchinsons, whose family round-the-world trip was sponsored by Pepsi Cola and broadcast on radio, landed in a twin-engine Lockheed Electra. Two pilots in March 1934 failed to find the strip at all and made an emergency landing near Nieuwe Haven that damaged their Lockheed so badly it had to be shipped back to the United States.

## The Bauxite War

The Netherlands fell to Germany in May 1940. Its government relocated to London, and from there negotiated with the United States. Suriname at that moment was a principal source of the bauxite - the ore from which aluminum was refined - that went into American aircraft, and it was lightly defended. On 30 November 1941 the first American troops arrived at Zanderij. Within weeks the US Army Corps of Engineers was extending the runways and building a road from Onverwacht into the airport, completed in 1942. Zanderij became a major transport base on the South Atlantic air ferry route to Freetown, Sierra Leone, and onward to the European and African fronts. The 35th Bombardment Squadron flew B-25 Mitchells here from November 1941 to October 1943. The 99th Bombardment Squadron arrived on 3 December 1941 - four days before Pearl Harbor - with Douglas B-18 Bolos. On 2 October 1942 one of those B-18As, piloted by Captain Howard Burhanna Jr., depth-charged and sank the German submarine U-512 off Cayenne. The 22nd Fighter Squadron brought P-39 Airacobras, later replaced by P-47 Thunderbolts, to defend the field.

## A Princess and a President

On 2 November 1943 Princess Juliana of the Netherlands became the first member of the Dutch royal family ever to set foot in Suriname when she landed at Zanderij in a KLM Lockheed Super Electra - the same aircraft, PJ-AIM Meeuw, that had run the old Paramaribo-Curaçao mail route. Governor Kielstra met her on the tarmac with a guard of honor. On 3 March 1960 President Dwight D. Eisenhower landed at Zanderij on Air Force One, accompanied by Secretary of State Christian Herter. They left the same day. On 14 April 1967 it was President Lyndon B. Johnson's turn, arriving during a rainstorm aboard a Boeing 707 Air Force One. On 7 April 1972 the first Boeing 747 wide-body jet ever to land in South America - a KLM aircraft - touched down here. In 2010 the Antonov An-225 Mriya, the world's largest cargo plane at the time, rolled down the same strip that had once been grass.

## Flight 764

On 7 June 1989 a Douglas DC-8 named "Anthony Nesty" - after the Surinamese swimmer who had won Olympic gold in Seoul the year before - crashed on approach to the airport. Surinam Airways Flight 764 was inbound from Amsterdam. The aircraft was leased from Arrow Air. Of the 187 people on board, 176 were killed in the crash, and deaths among the injured raised the toll to 178. Fifteen of the dead came from the Colourful 11, a touring team of Dutch-Surinamese footballers, among them its coach; the team was flying in to play exhibition matches. It remains the worst aviation disaster in Suriname's history. The airport carries the scar. Memorials to the victims stand in Paramaribo and in Amsterdam.

## The Name on the Terminal

The airport was renamed for Johan Adolf Pengel, the Surinamese politician who served as Prime Minister from 1963 to 1969 and whose career defined much of the country's late colonial period. Most Surinamers still call it Zanderij after the small village and sand savanna where it sits. Today it is Suriname's principal international gateway and the hub for Surinam Airways, with transatlantic service to Amsterdam by KLM and SLM, and regional routes to Belém, Georgetown, Port of Spain, Panama City, Miami, and a handful of Caribbean islands. A long-running expansion plan, with Chinese investment cited for a new terminal, still lists airbridges connecting the departures and arrivals halls among the work to come. For now, passengers still walk across the tarmac, same as they did when Earhart slept in town nearly ninety years ago.

## Flight Context

ICAO: SMJP. IATA: PBM. Located at 5.453°N, 55.188°W, in the town of Zanderij, 45 km south of Paramaribo. Single runway 11/29, approximately 3,480 m long - one of the longest in the Caribbean basin. Recommended viewing altitude on approach 1,500-3,000 ft; runway 11 approach crosses flat sand savanna, runway 29 approach crosses low-lying forest. Nearest alternate: Zorg en Hoop (SMZO) at Paramaribo for light aircraft, or Cayenne – Félix Eboué (SOCA / CAY) 320 km east for jet operations.
