# Lawa Railway

A colonial railway that was supposed to reach Suriname's gold fields, built only about halfway before the gold ran thin.

**Location:** 5.5929, -55.1953

**Categories:** railway, historical, suriname, colonial-history

## Introduction

The first section opened in 1905, with an inauguration at Paramaribo's Vaillantsplein station that photographers recorded. The plan was grand: a line of more than 350 kilometers cutting south through the rainforest to the gold fields along the Lawa River, opening the Dutch colony's interior to industrial extraction. The reality was less grand. The track made it only halfway. The gold fields proved less productive than the prospectuses had promised. And the labor that carved the bed through tropical forest was hard, slow, and expensive.

## The Governor's Promise

Cornelis Lely, the Dutch engineer then serving as governor of Suriname, announced in 1902 that the state would build the line to help exploit the gold discoveries at the Lawa River along the French Guiana border. The plans had started with private businessmen, but the capital required was beyond their reach. The track would be metre gauge - narrow, cheap to lay through difficult terrain - and single-track the whole way. The intended distance was over 350 kilometers. The budget and the colony's patience proved thinner than the jungle. Construction began in 1903 with former Curaçao seamen laying track south from Paramaribo. The first section, as far as Republiek, opened to traffic in 1905 with an inauguration at Vaillantsplein.

## Built on Hard Hands

The work that followed was done by hand, by crews living in camps along the advancing line, who cleared the roadbed through swamp and rainforest and laid the rails mile by mile. Their names are largely absent from the archival photographs of ceremonies at Vaillantsplein. What survives are their results: the engineering work, the 173 kilometers that eventually reached Dam at the Sara Creek by 1912. The final cost totaled 8.5 million Surinamese guilders. A 300-meter aerial cable car crossed the Suriname River, since a bridge was deemed too expensive.

## Engines from Berlin

Borsig, the German locomotive manufacturer in Berlin, supplied the first six steam locomotives with serial numbers 5339 to 5344. Each weighed 16 tons, and their design was borrowed from tram engines that had worked successfully on the Semarang Railway in colonial Indonesia. Small, efficient, suited to light track - the Borsigs hauled passengers and freight south from the capital through forest towns and plantations. They ran for decades. In 1954, the colony replaced them with diesel railcars for passenger service, the steam era ending as it did almost everywhere else, quietly and without ceremony. By then the railway was already a commercial disappointment. The gold fields at the Lawa never matched the dreams that had justified the project, and the line never extended beyond Dam.

## Drowned by a Reservoir

The railway's physical ending came in pieces. In the 1960s, Suriname built the Afobaka Dam on the Suriname River, creating the Brokopondo Reservoir - a lake that displaced thousands of Maroon villagers and submerged enormous tracts of forest. The reservoir also swallowed the section of track between Brownsberg Nature Park and the aerial cable car crossing. The flooding was deliberate, planned, part of the engineering compromise that brought hydroelectric power to the bauxite industry. The rest of the line kept running, reduced and ghostlike, until the 1980s. The last train departed in 1987. What had begun as a promise to reach the far interior ended as a shortened line that, from 1961, started at Onverwacht rather than in the capital, then as nothing.

## What the Tracks Wanted to Become

Proposals to revive Surinamese rail travel surface periodically. In November 2014, the government announced plans for a new railway from Paramaribo to Onverwacht, with intermediate stations at Latour, Hannaslust, Welgedacht, Lelydorp, and Bernharddorp. The Dutch firm Strukton put construction at about 130 million euros, with a possible extension later to Johan Adolf Pengel International Airport. Nothing has been built yet. Dutch filmmaker Hans Hylkema made a documentary in 2002 called The Gold Line (De Goudlijn) for the broadcaster Humanistische Omroep, which assembled old black-and-white footage of the Lawa Railway. What stays most in the archive is the gap between ambition and arrival. A line meant to carry a colony toward gold stopped halfway into the forest, leaving behind drowned rails, a few old engines, and the memory of the crews who built it.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 5.593°N, 55.195°W, at Onverwacht, the line's northern terminus in its last decades. Recommended viewing altitude 4,000-8,000 feet to trace the line's trajectory from Paramaribo south to Dam at Sara Creek. Landmarks: the city of Paramaribo on the Suriname River estuary (northern terminus), Brokopondo Reservoir to the south (where part of the track was deliberately flooded in the 1960s), and Brownsberg Nature Park on the reservoir's western edge. Nearest airports: Johan Adolf Pengel International (SMJP), about 15 km south of these coordinates at Zanderij, and Zorg en Hoop Airport (SMZO) in Paramaribo. Weather: tropical rainforest, high humidity, two wet seasons; morning flights give best visibility over the old roadbed.
