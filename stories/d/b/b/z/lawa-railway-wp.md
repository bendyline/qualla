# Lawa Railway

A colonial railway that was supposed to reach Suriname's gold fields, built halfway by convict labor from penal camps and abandoned when the gold ran thin.

**Location:** 5.5929, -55.1953

**Categories:** railway, historical, suriname, colonial-history

## Introduction

The first train left Paramaribo's Vaillantsplein station in 1905 to photographs and crowds and a governor's speech about a new era for Suriname. The plan was grand: a 350-kilometer line cutting south through the rainforest to the gold fields along the Lawa River, opening the Dutch colony's interior to industrial extraction. The reality was less grand. The track made it only halfway. The gold fields proved less productive than the prospectuses had promised. And much of the labor that carved the bed through tropical forest was performed by men convicted in distant Dutch courtrooms, sent to Suriname to work out their sentences building an infrastructure meant to enrich the empire that had exiled them.

## The Governor's Promise

Cornelis Lely, the Surinamese governor who gave his name to the railway project, announced in 1902 that the state would build the line to help exploit the gold discoveries at the Lawa River along the French Guiana border. The plans had started with private businessmen, but the capital required was beyond their reach. The track would be metre gauge - narrow, cheap to lay through difficult terrain - and single-track the whole way. The intended distance was over 350 kilometers. The budget and the colony's patience proved thinner than the jungle. Construction began in 1903 with former Curaçao seamen laying track south from Paramaribo. The first section opened to traffic in 1905, and crowds gathered at Vaillantsplein to see steam locomotives inaugurated in a town that had never hosted one.

## Built on Hard Hands

Much of the work that followed depended on convict labor. Suriname's penal camps sent prisoners into the forest to clear the roadbed, lay ties, and haul rails through country where heat and malaria killed workers routinely. These were men condemned by Dutch colonial courts, often for offenses that today would draw fines or community service - desertion, petty theft, insubordination. They dug drainage ditches through swampland, felled hardwoods, and poured foundations for stations they would never ride to. Their names are largely absent from the archival photographs of ribbon cuttings and dignitaries at Vaillantsplein. What survives are their results: the engineering work, the 173 kilometers that eventually reached Dam at the Sara Creek by 1912. The final cost totaled 8.5 million Surinamese guilders. A 300-meter aerial cable car crossed the Suriname River, since a bridge was deemed too expensive.

## Engines from Berlin

Borsig, the German locomotive manufacturer in Berlin, supplied the first six steam locomotives with serial numbers 5339 to 5344. Each weighed 16 tons, and their design was borrowed from tram engines that had worked successfully on the Semarang Railway in colonial Indonesia. Small, efficient, suited to light track - the Borsigs hauled passengers and freight south from the capital through forest towns and plantations. They ran for decades. In 1954, the colony replaced them with diesel railcars for passenger service, the steam era ending as it did almost everywhere else, quietly and without ceremony. By then the railway was already a commercial disappointment. The gold fields at the Lawa never matched the dreams that had justified the project, and the line never extended beyond Dam.

## Drowned by a Reservoir

The railway's physical ending came in pieces. In the 1960s, Suriname built the Afobaka Dam on the Suriname River, creating the Brokopondo Reservoir - a lake that displaced thousands of Maroon villagers and submerged enormous tracts of forest. The reservoir also swallowed the section of track between Brownsberg Nature Park and the aerial cable car crossing. The flooding was deliberate, planned, part of the engineering compromise that brought hydroelectric power to the bauxite industry. The rest of the line kept running, reduced and ghostlike, until the 1980s. The last train departed in 1987. What had begun as a promise to reach the far interior ended as shortened commuter service to towns outside the capital, then as nothing.

## What the Tracks Wanted to Become

Proposals to revive Surinamese rail travel surface periodically. In November 2014, the government announced plans for a new railway from Paramaribo to Onverwacht, with intermediate stations at Latour, Hannaslust, Welgedacht, Lelydorp, and Bernharddorp. A Dutch company estimated construction at 130 million euros over twelve months, with a possible extension later to Johan Adolf Pengel International Airport. Nothing has been built yet. Dutch filmmaker Bram van Splunteren released a documentary in 2002 called The Gold Line for the broadcaster Humanistische Omroep, which assembled old black-and-white footage of the Lawa Railway and interviewed those who remembered it. What stays most in the archive is the gap between ambition and arrival. A line meant to carry a colony toward gold stopped halfway into the forest, leaving behind drowned rails, a few old engines, and the memory of convicts who built infrastructure they would never ride.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 5.593°N, 55.195°W, roughly midpoint of the former line. Recommended viewing altitude 4,000-8,000 feet to trace the line's trajectory from Paramaribo south to Dam at Sara Creek. Landmarks: the city of Paramaribo on the Suriname River estuary (northern terminus), Brokopondo Reservoir to the south (where part of the track was deliberately flooded in the 1960s), and Brownsberg Nature Park on the reservoir's western edge. Nearest airports: Johan Adolf Pengel International (SMJP) north of Paramaribo, Zorg en Hoop Airport (SMZO) in the capital. Weather: tropical rainforest, high humidity, two wet seasons; morning flights give best visibility over the old roadbed.
