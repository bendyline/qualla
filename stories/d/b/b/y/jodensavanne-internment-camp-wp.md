# Jodensavanne Internment Camp

A Dutch World War II internment camp, built in the Surinam jungle on the site of a long-abandoned Jewish plantation, held Dutch East Indies prisoners the Netherlands called 'irreconcilable' - and eight of them did not come out alive.

**Location:** 5.4292, -54.9843

**Categories:** Internment Camps, World War II, Suriname, Dutch Colonial History, Historical Sites

## Introduction

The camp took the name of the ruined Jewish plantation beside it, but no Jews were held there. Between 1942 and 1946 the Jodensavanne internment camp, built on a jungle site in wartime Surinam, held a different kind of prisoner: men shipped from the Dutch East Indies because the colonial government in Batavia had declared them too dangerous to keep on the same continent as a working port. Roughly three-quarters were sympathizers of the Dutch fascist NSB or the Nazi Party. The remaining quarter were not. Among them was Ernest Douwes Dekker, an Indonesian nationalist, and Lo Hartog van Banda, a Dutch conscientious objector. Eight men died inside the camp. Two of them were shot to death by marines while in handcuffs, which led to a Dutch government investigation years later - and to the slow, uncomfortable admission that the Netherlands had operated something very close to a concentration camp in its own South American territory.

## Why Here

The day Germany invaded the Netherlands, 10 May 1940, the colonial government of the Dutch East Indies declared martial law and rounded up anyone it considered a German sympathizer. The net scooped up mostly Europeans of German, Hungarian, Czech, and Italian origin, along with some Dutch citizens whose surnames sounded suspicious. Until that morning the Dutch NSB had been legal in the colony. Now its members were interned without charge, held on Onrust Island in Batavia Bay, in Ngawi, and elsewhere in East Java. When the Netherlands declared war on Japan in late 1941, the Indies no longer felt secure as a prison. Roughly two thousand German nationals were shipped to India and Siam. The Dutch citizens considered most irreconcilable were sent the other way - across the Pacific, through the Panama Canal, to Surinam. The Indies Governor-General Alidius Tjarda van Starkenborgh Stachouwer had made the arrangement directly with Surinam Governor Johannes Kielstra, who had already been interning local NSB members.

## The Site

Because the camp at Jodensavanne was not finished when the first prisoners arrived, the internees spent six months at Fort Nieuw-Amsterdam, an old Dutch coastal fortress at the mouth of the Suriname River. Then they were moved inland, about fifty kilometers south of Paramaribo, to a clearing next to the ruins of the centuries-old Jewish plantation community the name commemorated. The camp had three officers' names attached to it over time: the first commander, G. Gebuys; a lieutenant named Roos; and Troop Commander Colonel Johannes Kroese Meijer, who ran the operation during the darkest period and was later decorated for his wartime service. Meijer ended the war a major-general in the Royal Netherlands Indies Army. The men he had commanded never forgot him. Gebuys, the first commandant, was later quoted as telling prisoners they would only be sent to hospital if they died.

## What Happened There

The guards treated the prisoners as traitors, which in the strictest sense they were not - most had been interned without trial and without charge. Beatings with clubs were routine. Shackling of hands and feet together was routine. On the night of 4 November 1942, a group of internees escaped from the camp and fled to a nearby Indigenous village called Casipoera. The Wayana villagers there did not shelter them - the men were arrested within hours and held in custody for three days. The 1949 Dutch government investigation later established that they were tortured during those three days and then killed on Colonel Meijer's direct order. Two of them were shot while still in handcuffs. These were the deaths that finally forced the investigation, but the record suggests they were not isolated - eight people died in the camp total, and the conditions were such that Surinamese newspapers worried, not unreasonably, that the Netherlands was planning to turn the entire colony into a postwar penal dumping ground for unwanted Europeans.

## Release and Aftermath

The war ended in May 1945, but the camp did not close immediately. The generator broke down in March 1946 and the prisoners spent part of that month without electric lights. It was only on 15 July 1946 - more than a year after VE Day - that the last internees were finally released. Some of them went home to devastating postwar stigma. Civil servants who had been rounded up on 10 May 1940 found it nearly impossible to return to work; in 1950 the Dutch government allowed a group of them, including Dr. L. J. A. Schoonheyt, to have their records cleared and re-enter the Overseas Service. Ernest Douwes Dekker returned to Indonesia in 1947 in the middle of its war of independence against the Netherlands, and became an elder-statesman figure of the early independent republic. Lo Hartog van Banda went to The Hague and became a well-known cartoonist. The camp itself dissolved back into jungle. What is there now is bush, the occasional archaeological trace, and the adjacent ruins of the seventeenth-century synagogue whose name this place borrowed and never deserved.

## Flight Context

Located at 5.429°N, 54.984°W, in Para District of Suriname, roughly 50 km south of Paramaribo on the west bank of the Suriname River. The site lies within a few hundred meters of the Jodensavanne plantation ruins. Paramaribo-Zanderij International Airport (SMJP / PBM) is 20 km southwest. Recommended viewing altitude 2,000-4,000 ft. The clearing in the forest where the camp stood is still visible; the synagogue ruins beside it are on the UNESCO tentative list.
