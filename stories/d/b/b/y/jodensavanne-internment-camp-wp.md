# Jodensavanne Internment Camp

A Dutch World War II internment camp, built in the Surinam jungle near a long-abandoned Jewish plantation settlement, held Dutch East Indies prisoners the Netherlands called 'irreconcilable' - and eight of them did not come out alive.

**Location:** 5.4292, -54.9843

**Categories:** Internment Camps, World War II, Suriname, Dutch Colonial History, Historical Sites

## Introduction

The camp took the name of the ruined Jewish plantation settlement nearby, but no Jews were held there. Between 1942 and 1946 the Jodensavanne internment camp, built on a jungle site in wartime Surinam, held a different kind of prisoner: 146 men shipped from the Dutch East Indies because the colonial government in Batavia considered them "irreconcilable". Roughly three-quarters were sympathizers of the Dutch fascist NSB or the Nazi Party. The remaining quarter were not. Among them was Ernest Douwes Dekker, an Indonesian nationalist, and Lo Hartog van Banda, a cartoonist and former conscientious objector. Eight men died inside the camp. Two of them were shot to death by marines while in handcuffs, which led to a Dutch investigation in 1949 and, in 1994, to a Dutch minister's apology to the families of the dead.

## Why Here

The day Germany invaded the Netherlands, 10 May 1940, the colonial government of the Dutch East Indies declared martial law and rounded up anyone it considered a German sympathizer. The net scooped up mostly Europeans of German, Hungarian, Czech, and Italian origin, along with some Dutch citizens with German surnames. Until that morning the Dutch NSB had been legal in the colony. Now its members were interned without charge, held on Onrust Island in Batavia Bay, in Ngawi, and elsewhere in East Java. When the Netherlands declared war on Japan in late 1941, the Indies no longer felt secure as a prison. Roughly two thousand German nationals were shipped to India and Siam. The Dutch citizens considered most irreconcilable were sent the other way: 146 of them sailed from Surabaya on the Tjisedane in January 1942 and reached Paramaribo on 21 March. The Indies Governor-General Alidius Tjarda van Starkenborgh Stachouwer had asked Surinam Governor Johannes Kielstra to take them; Kielstra had already been interning local NSB members.

## The Site

Because the camp at Jodensavanne was not finished when the first prisoners arrived, the internees spent six months at Fort Nieuw-Amsterdam, an old Dutch coastal fortress at the mouth of the Suriname River. Then they were moved inland, about fifty kilometers south of Paramaribo, to a clearing near the ruins of the centuries-old Jewish plantation community the name commemorated. The camp had three officers' names attached to it over time: the first commander, G. Gebuys; a lieutenant named Roos; and the colony's troop commander, Colonel Johannes Kroese Meijer, who held authority over it during the darkest period and was later decorated with the Military Order of William. Meijer later rose to major-general in the Royal Netherlands Indies Army. Gebuys, the first commandant, was later quoted as telling prisoners they would only be sent to hospital if they died.

## What Happened There

The guards treated the prisoners as traitors, which in the strictest sense they were not - most had been interned without trial and without charge. Beatings with clubs were routine. Shackling of hands and feet together was routine. On 4 November 1942, four internees escaped from the camp and fled to a nearby Indigenous village called Casipoera. They were caught and held in custody for three days. According to a witness account given to the 1949 investigation, they were tortured, and on Colonel Meijer's direct order marines shot two of them, L. K. A. Raedt van Oldenbarnevelt and L. A. J. van Poelje, while they were still in handcuffs; the gun misfired when the other two were to be shot. The investigation concluded the two had not been shot while escaping but executed in custody. Meijer was never charged. These were the deaths that finally forced the investigation, but the record suggests they were not isolated - eight people died in the camp in total, two of them after hunger strikes, and after the war Surinamese newspapers worried that the Netherlands might keep the camp open as a penal colony for collaborators and other undesirables.

## Release and Aftermath

The war ended in May 1945, but the camp did not close immediately. The generator broke down in March 1946 and the prisoners spent part of that month without electric lights. It was only on 15 July 1946 - more than a year after VE Day - that the last internees were finally released. Some of them went home to devastating postwar stigma. Civil servants who had been rounded up on 10 May 1940 found it nearly impossible to return to work; in 1950 the Dutch government allowed a group of them, including Dr. L. J. A. Schoonheyt, to have their records cleared and re-enter the Overseas Service. Ernest Douwes Dekker returned to Indonesia in 1947 in the middle of its war of independence against the Netherlands, and became a member of parliament and a prominent intellectual in the young republic. Lo Hartog van Banda went to The Hague and became a well-known cartoonist. The camp itself dissolved back into jungle. What is there now is bush, the occasional archaeological trace, and, a few kilometers away, the ruins of the seventeenth-century Jewish settlement whose name this place borrowed.

## Flight Context

Located at 5.429°N, 54.984°W, near the village of Redi Doti in Para District of Suriname, roughly 50 km south of Paramaribo near the Suriname River. The pin lies about 4 km west-northwest of the Jodensavanne synagogue ruins. Paramaribo-Zanderij International Airport (SMJP / PBM) is about 23 km west. Recommended viewing altitude 2,000-4,000 ft. The nearby Jodensavanne settlement and Cassipora Creek Cemetery were inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2023.
