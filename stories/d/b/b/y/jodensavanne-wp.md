# Jodensavanne

In the 1600s, Sephardi Jews fleeing the Inquisition built a sugar plantation community on the Suriname River that became one of the most unlikely centers of Jewish life in the Americas - sustained by the labor of thousands of enslaved Africans.

**Location:** 5.4133, -54.9530

**Categories:** Jewish History, Suriname, Historical Sites, UNESCO World Heritage, Colonial History, Plantation Society

## Introduction

In 1694, Surinam's Jewish community - about 570 people, mostly Sephardi - owned some forty plantations worked by about 9,000 enslaved Africans. Its center was a settlement fifty kilometers up the Suriname River from Paramaribo, and the place on their maps was called Jodensavanne - Dutch for "Jewish Savanna." It was a rare self-governing Jewish community in the Americas. It had its own court. It had one of the oldest architecturally significant synagogues in the New World, the Beracha ve Shalom, whose centennial in October 1785 drew more than 1,500 attendees even though only about twenty Jewish families still lived on the spot. And it was built, like every other sugar estate in Surinam, on a foundation of slavery that kept collapsing under revolt, raid, and the slow exhaustion of its own soils.

## Refuge and Sugar

In the mid-seventeenth century the English, who then controlled Surinam, allowed Sephardi Jews from the Netherlands, Portugal, and Italy to settle on the colony's rivers. Many came first to Torarica, the old interior capital. A further group arrived in 1664 - Jews who had been living in Dutch Brazil under relatively tolerant Dutch rule until the Portuguese reconquered the colony in 1654 and brought the Inquisition with them. The English guaranteed the newcomers privileges that were almost unheard of elsewhere in the Americas: the right to operate their own court, freedom of religion, the right to keep their own Sabbath. Unlike the Christian planters who imagined getting rich and retiring to Europe, many of the Jewish colonists had little in Europe to return to. They settled for good, centered on a site they called Jodensavanne, and built a plantation economy around sugarcane.

## The Synagogue and the Centennial

The congregation's first synagogue was a wooden building put up between 1665 and 1671; the Beracha ve Shalom synagogue that replaced it in 1685 was built of brick imported from Europe, and was for its time a rare building in the western hemisphere. Its construction marked the shift of Jewish life in the colony from the older Cassipora settlement, founded in the 1650s, to Jodensavanne. Its centennial celebration, held in October 1785, was attended by more than 1,500 people, the majority of whom sailed upriver from Paramaribo for the occasion. By that year, perhaps twenty Jewish families remained permanent residents. The synagogue was the anchor; the population had drifted. The historian Natalie Zemon Davis, who died in 2023, spent years working on the eighteenth century of this place, centered on the figure of David Cohen Nassy, born in 1747, and her article "Regaining Jerusalem" describes a Passover celebration within the plantation community.

## The Enslaved

In 1694 the colony's roughly 570 Jewish colonists held about 9,000 enslaved Africans - some sixteen people in bondage for every free member of the community, a ratio that describes the entire economic basis of the community. The sugarcane that made the settlement wealthy was cut, boiled, and processed by people who had been kidnapped, transported, and forced into plantation labor in one of the cruelest systems the Atlantic world ever produced. The community's autonomy - its court, its synagogue, its privileges - did not extend to them. In 1690, enslaved people on a plantation owned by Immanuel Machado rose up, killed him, and fled into the forest to join one of the Maroon communities that were forming throughout the Surinamese interior. In 1712 French sailors from the Cassard expedition raided the settlement, aware of its wealth, and the colony paid a heavy tribute. In 1832 a fire during a revolt by enslaved people finally destroyed the settlement.

## The Drift to the Coast

The eighteenth century brought a cascade of troubles. A major Amsterdam sugarcane importer collapsed in 1773. Real-estate loans accumulated against aging plantations. European sugar beet cultivation began in the 1780s and would in time squeeze Caribbean cane prices. Paramaribo grew as the colony's exclusive trading port, and Jews migrated downriver toward commerce. The Maroon Wars made the interior increasingly unsafe for any isolated plantation. By 1790 the free population of Jodensavanne was about twenty-two. By the early nineteenth century it was under ten. Around seventy plantations still lined the Suriname River in 1750, many of them bearing Jewish names - among them the thousand-acre holdings of Solomon Meza - and they were mapped by Alexander de Lavaux, a Berlin-born engineer who had served in Prussian forces before carrying his draftsman's tools to the tropics. The map survived. The plantations, mostly, did not.

## What Remains

The site today holds the ruins of the Beracha ve Shalom synagogue and three cemeteries whose headstones carry inscriptions in Hebrew, Portuguese, and Dutch. The most heavily used, the Cassipora Cemetery, is expected to hold around 200 tombstones, the earliest from the seventeenth century and the most recent from about 1840. Several ohelim - small roof-like structures over rabbinical graves - stand among them. The site sits in the Carolina resort of Para District, near the Indigenous village of Redi Doti, reachable by the Carolinabrug bridge onto the Afobakaweg or by the Desiré Delano Bouterse Highway on the way to the international airport. A foundation maintains the grounds. In 2023 UNESCO inscribed Jodensavanne and the Cassipora Creek Cemetery on the World Heritage list - formal recognition of a place whose history contains, in the same geography, both refuge and cruelty, autonomy and enslavement, and a synagogue built in the middle of rainforest.

## Flight Context

Located at 5.413°N, 54.953°W, on the Suriname River in Para District, 50 km south of Paramaribo. Paramaribo-Zanderij International Airport (SMJP / PBM) lies 25 km west. The site is connected to the road network via the Carolinabrug bridge and the Afobakaweg. Recommended viewing altitude 1,500-3,000 ft. The clearing where the plantation stood is visible from the air, with the brick synagogue ruins at its center. The Jodensavanne WWII internment camp site lies a few kilometers to the west-northwest.
