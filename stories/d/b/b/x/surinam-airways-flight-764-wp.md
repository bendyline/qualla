# Surinam Airways Flight 764

On 7 June 1989, a plane named for Suriname's Olympic hero crashed near Paramaribo, killing 178 of the 187 people aboard, including most of a Dutch-Surinamese football team assembled to give their community hope.

**Location:** 5.4533, -55.2297

**Categories:** aviation-disasters, suriname, sports-tragedies, memorials

## Introduction

The plane was named Anthony Nesty, after the swimmer who had won Suriname its first Olympic gold in Seoul the year before. On board, flying to Suriname for a friendly football match, were eighteen members of the Colourful Eleven - Dutch-Surinamese footballers and their coach - who represented everything a small country's diaspora could hope to be. On 7 June 1989, at 04:27 local time, the plane crashed on approach to Paramaribo-Zanderij. Of the 187 people aboard, 176 were killed in the crash; with deaths among the injured in the days that followed, the toll rose to 178, and nine people survived. It remains the deadliest aviation disaster in Suriname's history.

## The Colourful Eleven

The team existed because a social worker named Sonny Hasnoe had noticed something in Amsterdam's Bijlmer district, where many Surinamese immigrants lived isolated from mainstream Dutch society. Hasnoe worked with underprivileged children and found that when young Surinamese boys saw professional footballers who looked like them - who came from where they came from - their prospects, their behavior, their sense of possibility changed. He organized a selection of top Dutch-Surinamese professionals to play SV Robinhood, the Surinamese champions, in 1986. The match was a celebration. More followed. A June 1989 match was planned in Suriname. But many of the top players were denied permission by their Dutch professional clubs, and eighteen second-stringers traveled instead. They were not famous names. They were young men who had broken into Dutch football from neighborhoods where that was supposed to be impossible. They boarded the plane as symbols of what their community could achieve, and they never played the match.

## The Approach

The weather at Zanderij had turned hostile between the forecast and the flight. The crew had been told to expect visibility of six kilometers. Instead they found dense fog: 900 meters of visibility, cloud base at 400 feet, calm winds. The aircraft was cleared for a VOR/DME approach - a procedure using a ground-based radio beacon. But the crew opted instead for an ILS approach - normally more precise, except that the ILS equipment at Zanderij was not operational for use. The crew knew this. The cockpit voice recorder captured First Officer Glyn Tobias saying, "I don't trust that ILS." The captain, 66-year-old Wilbert Rogers, chose to use it anyway. As the aircraft descended on the unreliable signal, it went below minimum altitudes without visual contact with the runway. Audible and visual warnings sounded in the cockpit. They were ignored. Low fuel may have driven the decision to press on. The DC-8 struck a tree short of the runway.

## The People Lost

One hundred and seventy-eight people died. Families waited at Zanderij for a flight that had already ended. Players who had been cheered in Amsterdam neighborhoods for being the proof that Surinamese kids could make it - were gone. Fifteen members of the team died, among them coach Nick Stienstra; only three players - Sigi Lens, Edu Nandlal, and Radjin de Haan - survived. The team had not been the best Dutch-Surinamese players available that week. It was the team that was willing to go when others were kept home. They were sons, brothers, fathers. They carried a community's pride on the same flight that carried its grief back days later. The survivors numbered nine. Whole families on the ground learned how small that number was.

## What the Investigation Found

The Surinamese investigating commission blamed the captain's "glaring carelessness and recklessness" in descending below published minimums, and faulted Surinam Airways for failing to check the qualifications of the contract crew supplied by Air Crews International. The NTSB in the United States found failures so systemic they generated formal safety recommendations to the FAA. Captain Rogers, at 66, was over the age-60 maximum for international commercial flights. His most recent proficiency check had been on a small twin-engine piston aircraft, not a DC-8 - the paperwork had been confused and the error overlooked. The first officer carried false identity papers. The NTSB recommended the FAA require air carriers to provide captain and first officer information to verify compliance with age limits, and to regulate US companies that supplied contract pilots to international airlines. The recommendations were aimed squarely at the gaps that had put this crew on this approach on this night of fog.

## Remembering

In Suriname, 7 June is not a national holiday. But it is remembered. The Dutch-Surinamese community in Amsterdam marks the anniversary. Anthony Nesty, the swimmer the plane was named for, went on to a long career - he still coaches. The survivors gave interviews, some of them, though not all of them spoke. Iwan Tol's 2005 book, Eindbestemming Zanderij - Final Destination Zanderij - set out to tell the team's forgotten story. They are not a footnote about an aviation disaster. They were Andro Knel and Steve van Dorpel and Lloyd Doesburg and Jerry Haatrecht - young men with real careers and real families, whose only crime was accepting a chance to play football in the country their parents came from. The plane came down short of the runway. Most of the team never came home.

## Flight Context

The crash site is at 5.45 degrees N, 55.23 degrees W, near the approach to Johan Adolf Pengel International Airport (formerly Zanderij, ICAO: SMJP), about 45 km south of Paramaribo. Recommended viewing altitude 3,000-6,000 feet. The airport remains Suriname's main international gateway. The crash occurred during a CFIT event on approach at 04:27 local time in dense fog conditions. The aircraft struck trees short of the runway.
