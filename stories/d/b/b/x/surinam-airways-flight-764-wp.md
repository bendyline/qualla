# Surinam Airways Flight 764

On 7 June 1989, a plane named for Suriname's Olympic hero crashed near Paramaribo, killing 176 people including most of a Dutch-Surinamese football team assembled to give their community hope.

**Location:** 5.4533, -55.2297

**Categories:** aviation-disasters, suriname, sports-tragedies, memorials

## Introduction

The plane was named Anthony Nesty, after the swimmer who had won Suriname its first Olympic gold in Seoul the summer before. On board, heading home for a friendly football match, were eighteen Dutch-Surinamese players - the Colourful Eleven and their coaches and friends - who represented everything a small country's diaspora could hope to be. On 7 June 1989, at 04:27 local time, the plane crashed on approach to Paramaribo-Zanderij. One hundred and seventy-six people died, nine survived. It remains the deadliest aviation disaster in Suriname's history.

## The Colourful Eleven

The team existed because a social worker named Sonny Hasnoe had noticed something in Amsterdam's Bijlmer district, where many Surinamese immigrants lived isolated from mainstream Dutch society. Hasnoe worked with underprivileged children and found that when young Surinamese boys saw professional footballers who looked like them - who came from where they came from - their prospects, their behavior, their sense of possibility changed. He organized a selection of top Dutch-Surinamese professionals to play SV Robinhood, the Surinamese champions, in 1986. The match was a celebration. More followed. A June 1989 match was planned in Suriname. But many of the top players were denied permission by their Dutch professional clubs, and eighteen second-stringers traveled instead. They were not famous names. They were young men who had broken into Dutch football from neighborhoods where that was supposed to be impossible. They boarded the plane as symbols of what their community could achieve, and they never played the match.

## The Approach

The weather at Zanderij had turned hostile between the forecast and the flight. The crew had been told to expect visibility of six kilometers. Instead they found dense fog: 900 meters of visibility, cloud base at 400 feet, calm winds. The aircraft was cleared for a VOR/DME approach - a procedure using a ground-based radio beacon. But the crew opted instead for an ILS approach - normally more precise, except that the ILS equipment at Zanderij was not operational for use. The crew knew this. The cockpit voice recorder captured First Officer Glyn Tobias saying, "I don't trust that ILS." The captain, 66-year-old Wilbert Rogers, chose to use it anyway. As the aircraft descended on the unreliable signal, it went below minimum altitudes without visual contact with the runway. Ground proximity warnings sounded in the cockpit. They were ignored. Low fuel may have driven the decision to press on. The DC-8 struck trees short of the runway.

## The People Lost

One hundred and seventy-six people died. Families waited at Zanderij for a flight that had already ended. Players who had been cheered in Amsterdam neighborhoods for being the proof that Surinamese kids could make it - were gone. Two generations of Surinamese sport, the Dutch journalist Iwan Tol later wrote in his 2005 book Eindbestemming Zanderij, were written off in one approach to one runway. The team had not been the best Dutch-Surinamese players available that week. It was the team that was willing to go when others were kept home. They were sons, brothers, fathers. They carried a community's pride on the same flight that carried its grief back days later. The survivors numbered nine. Whole families on the ground learned how small that number was.

## What the Investigation Found

The Surinamese commission and the NTSB in the United States uncovered failures so systemic they generated formal safety recommendations to the FAA. Captain Rogers, at 66, was over the age-60 maximum for international commercial flights. His most recent proficiency check had been on a small twin-engine piston aircraft, not a DC-8 - the paperwork had been confused and the error overlooked. The first officer carried false identity papers. The NTSB recommended the FAA require air carriers to provide captain and first officer information to verify compliance with age limits, and to regulate US companies that supplied contract pilots to international airlines. The recommendations were adopted. A generation of safer oversight descended, in part, from this one night of fog and poor choices on the final approach to Paramaribo.

## Remembering

In Suriname, 7 June is not a national holiday. But it is remembered. The Dutch-Surinamese community in Amsterdam marks the anniversary. Anthony Nesty, the swimmer the plane was named for, went on to a long career - he still coaches. The survivors gave interviews, some of them, though not all of them spoke. Iwan Tol's 2005 book, titled Eindbestemming Zanderij - Final Destination Zanderij - gave the lost team back its names. They are not a footnote about an aviation disaster. They were Sheridan Robinson and Andro Knel and Steve van Dorpel and Lloyd Doesburg - young men with real careers and real families, whose only crime was accepting a chance to play football in the country their parents came from. The plane came down short of the runway. The families never came home.

## Flight Context

The crash site is at 5.45 degrees N, 55.23 degrees W, near the approach to Johan Adolf Pengel International Airport (formerly Zanderij, ICAO: SMJP), about 45 km south of Paramaribo. Recommended viewing altitude 3,000-6,000 feet. The airport remains Suriname's main international gateway. The crash occurred during a CFIT event on approach at 04:27 local time in dense fog conditions. The aircraft struck trees short of the runway.
