# The Guianas

A stretch of coast the Europeans called the Wild Coast - two small nations, a French overseas region, and two quiet corners of larger countries, where the Amazon and the Orinoco meet the Atlantic.

**Location:** 4.3201, -56.0767

**Categories:** south-america, history, guyana, suriname, french-guiana, colonial

## Introduction

Walter Raleigh was looking for a city made of gold when he sailed up the Orinoco in 1595, and what he found instead was the coast Europeans would eventually call the Wild Coast. The name was not entirely a compliment. For three centuries, the stretch of South American shore between the Orinoco and the Amazon shrugged off one empire after another. The Spanish never quite settled it, the Portuguese were pushed east, the Dutch, English, and French swapped it like cards through a dozen wars. What was left when the dust settled was three of the most unusual small territories in the Americas - Guyana, Suriname, and French Guiana, still a part of France - plus two quiet edges of larger countries.

## Before the Europeans

The Lokono, an Arawak people, were here first, or at least first of those whose descendants remained. They had migrated up from the Orinoco and Essequibo river basins, fanning out across the Caribbean and eventually being followed into the islands by their rivals, the Carib. By the time Christopher Columbus sighted the coast in 1498, centuries of contact - sometimes peaceful trade, sometimes war and the taking of captives - had so blended Arawak and Carib culture in the Guianas that European observers could not easily tell the two apart. The region was also home to peoples who still live there today: the Tiriyó and Wayana of the interior highlands, the Wayampi of the southern forests, the Teko along the Camopi.

## The Empire That Did Not Come

The Spanish were supposed to rule this coast. The Treaty of Tordesillas had assigned it to them, and in 1613 Spanish troops from Venezuela destroyed the Dutch trading posts on the Essequibo and Corantijn rivers to make the point. But the Dutch returned in 1615, then the English settled modern Suriname in 1650 under Francis Willoughby, then the French dug in at Cayenne after several earlier failures. The Dutch West India Company, chartered in 1621 with capital that included foreign investors, had a different theory of empire from the Spanish - less about claiming maps and more about running trading posts that paid. The theory worked. By the end of the seventeenth century the Dutch held a string of sugar colonies along the coast, and all the competing powers were building the same economy on the same terrible foundation.

## The Sugar and the Swamp

The coast from which that sugar came is not like most coasts. It is flat, swampy, fever-ridden, and the inland forests are almost impenetrable. Early settlements were built miles upriver to escape the coastal marshes, which were not drained for plantation use until later. Every colony was built by the labor of enslaved West Africans, shipped across the Atlantic in numbers that turned small swampy river mouths into engines of global commerce. From those plantations, entire new peoples emerged. The Ndyuka, Saramaka, and Matawai Maroons fled into the interior and fought the Dutch to a standstill, winning peace treaties in 1760, 1762, and 1767; the Aluku fought on and crossed into French territory. They are still there, in villages along the Tapanahoni, the upper Suriname River, the Saramacca, and the Marowijne, speaking languages - Ndyuka, Saramaccan, Matawai, Kwinti - that linguists study closely as some of the world's younger creoles. Ndyuka is one of very few creole languages with its own writing system, the Afaka syllabary.

## The Peace of 1814

The colonial reshuffling of the Napoleonic era was settled in 1814 by the Convention of London, which heavily favored the British. Britain took Berbice, Essequibo, and Demerara and consolidated them in 1831 as British Guiana. France got its colony back after a Portuguese occupation that lasted until 1817, and would eventually use it for Devil's Island and other prisons whose names still chill. The Dutch kept Suriname. Venezuela and Brazil would absorb the thinner Spanish and Portuguese wings to west and east, becoming Guayana Region and Amapá State respectively. The populations stayed mostly coastal - in Georgetown, Paramaribo, Cayenne, and Macapá - because the interior was still what it had been for centuries: enormous, green, and largely in the hands of the people whose ancestors had lived there before anyone drew a treaty.

## A Different Kind of Diversity

The three Guianas proper - Guyana (about 800,000 people), Suriname (more than 600,000), and French Guiana (about 300,000) - make for one of the most linguistically and racially intricate regions on Earth. Abolition in the nineteenth century did not solve the plantation owners' labor problem, so they imported indentured workers: Bhojpuri-speaking labourers from British India, Javanese from Dutch-held Indonesia, Chinese from the ports, Portuguese from Madeira. In Guyana and Suriname, people of Indian and of African descent, Maroons among them, make up the two largest groups; Javanese and Chinese communities are substantial; and the Maroon peoples of the forest interior keep their own traditional authorities. Suriname is the only sovereign country outside the Netherlands where Dutch is the sole official language. Most of the interior is forest, still. Most of the population lives within sight of the Atlantic. And Raleigh's golden city was never there. The gold, it turned out, was in the bauxite - and, lately, in the oil off Guyana's coast.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 4.32°N, 56.08°W (central Suriname, representative of the three Guianas). Recommended viewing altitude 8,000-12,000 ft for coastal plain; 6,000-8,000 ft for the forested interior. Major airports: Georgetown's Cheddi Jagan International (SYCJ), Paramaribo's Johan Adolf Pengel International (SMJP), Cayenne's Félix Eboué International (SOCA). The coast is flat and humid; expect developed ITCZ thunderstorms much of the year. Inland, the forest canopy extends unbroken for hundreds of kilometers.
