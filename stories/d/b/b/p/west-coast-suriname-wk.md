# West Coast (Suriname)

Three coastal districts of Suriname where scarlet ibises burn against the mangroves, Dutch polders meet Atlantic surf, and most tourists never bother to come.

**Location:** 5.6105, -55.9286

**Categories:** suriname, travel, birds, nature, south-america, coastal

## Introduction

The scarlet ibis is too pink. That is the first thing everyone says. The bird burns against the mangroves at Bigi Pan like something that escaped from a painting - a pink that does not occur in any naturally selected animal you have seen before, until you see this one. It lives in a 135,000-hectare nature reserve just east of Nieuw Nickerie, in the quiet western half of Suriname that most travelers skip and most Surinamers love. Three districts make up this West Coast: Coronie, Nickerie, and Saramacca. Between them they hold coconut palms, rice paddies bigger than European countries, and the best sunsets in the country.

## Three Districts, Three Characters

Coronie is the middle district and the least populated in Suriname. Three thousand people live on 3,900 square kilometers, most of them stretched along a thin coastal strip bordered by the largest freshwater swamp in the country. The district is famous for its coconut palms. Drive the Oost-Westverbinding through Coronie and you will see people fishing from the shoulder of the road, lines cast into irrigation canals, patient as egrets. Nickerie sits at the far western edge, at the mouth of the Corentyne River that forms the border with Guyana. It is a rice district: 35,000 people, about 5,353 square kilometers, polders drained and diked by the Dutch and now planted end to end with paddies. Saramacca lies between the Saramacca and Coppename rivers, a quieter landscape of small family farms. The county seat of Nickerie, Nieuw Nickerie, has about 14,000 people - which makes it, remarkably, the third-largest settlement in Suriname.

## The Long Drive

From Paramaribo to Nieuw Nickerie by road is 237 kilometers on the Oost-Westverbinding, Suriname's main east-west highway, and it takes about four hours. The road is mostly well-paved and entirely unlined - there are no painted traffic lanes, just asphalt and the agreement of drivers. Slow trucks and agricultural vehicles take up much of the width. Watch for drempel - speed bumps, often installed as twins at the entrances and exits of small settlements, engineered to slow you nearly to a stop. There is a security checkpoint about two-thirds of the way. Be prepared to show your passport. A state bus makes the run multiple times a day from Paramaribo's Heiligenweg station; a single ticket, as of December 2025, costs SRD 150, and the ride stops once at a paid restroom. If you come by ferry from Guyana, you cross the Corentyne from Moleson Creek to South Drain - thirty minutes, US$20 one way, cash only, no ATMs on either side.

## The Sea Wall at Nieuw Nickerie

From Nieuw Nickerie, the A.K. Doergasawstraat runs eight kilometers out to De Zeedijk, the seven-and-a-half-kilometer sea wall that locals call Klein Scheveningen - little Scheveningen, after the Dutch dike. You do not need much imagination to see why. The wall holds back the Atlantic from the polders, and at its northern end there is a Hindu temple where, for years, a retired woman has changed the robes of the sculptures on a regular schedule. Further down the wall is the open-air cremation site where Hindu families from across Suriname perform final rites. It sounds somber, and it is, but it is also quiet and uncluttered in a way that feels right for the purpose. Locals say the sunsets at the sea wall are the best in the country, and when you stand there watching the sun go into the Atlantic from a dike engineered three hundred years ago, it is hard to argue.

## Bigi Pan

Bigi Pan - 'Big Pan' in Sranan Tongo - is the prize. A 135,000-hectare reserve of open water, mudflats, and mangrove forest, it is where you go if you are a birdwatcher or if you want to sleep in a stilt lodge over tidal water. The reserve hosts 72 breeding bird species plus dozens of visitors. The scarlet ibis can be seen most of the year, though it is harder to spot in the dry months from April to August. For the past decade or so, hundreds of American flamingos - a more muted pink than the ibis but still unmistakable - have wintered on the lagoon from roughly November to April. Herons, ospreys, storks, and kingfishers feast on the fish and crustaceans. Night tours can turn up tree boas and spectacled caimans, the small alligator-relative that glides through the mangrove darkness with eyes like red coals. Fish occasionally leap into boats. Monkeys were once common on the canal approach, but an illegal burn has converted part of that forest into savanna, and they have mostly moved on.

## The Statue at Wageningen

Wageningen is 50 kilometers east of Nieuw Nickerie, named for the Dutch agricultural university that once ran a mechanized rice project here. The project collapsed after independence in 1975, and the silo complex stands desolate on the outskirts of town. On a square near the closed rice-processing factory is a statue of a woman named Alida, who was enslaved and mutilated by a plantation owner's wife - a red mark indicates where her breast was cut off. The story is old and the statue is recent, and both are part of why Surinamers remember Wageningen. Further east, Groningen sits on the Saramacca River, forty kilometers west of Paramaribo. Its 'independence square' is crowded with monuments to everything at once: Javanese farmers, Boeroe Dutch settlers, the abolition of slavery, Surinam's 1975 independence. It is a monument to the habit of building monuments, and somehow, in the Surinamese sun, it works.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 5.61°N, 55.93°W (Nickerie district, western Suriname coastal plain). Recommended viewing altitude 3,000-5,000 ft for coastal mangroves and Bigi Pan lagoon; 2,000 ft for the Zeedijk sea wall. Nickerie has a small airstrip; Johan Adolf Pengel International (SMJP) near Paramaribo is the regional hub 237 km east. The coast is flat, humid, and prone to developed afternoon thunderstorms; expect low-level turbulence over the polder country. Cross-border ferry at South Drain / Moleson Creek connects to Guyana.
