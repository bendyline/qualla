# Brokopondo Reservoir

A 1,560-square-kilometer reservoir in Suriname, built to power an aluminum smelter, that drowned Saramaka Maroon villages and flooded 160,000 hectares of rainforest.

**Location:** 4.8406, -55.0731

**Categories:** reservoirs, hydropower, Suriname, displacement history

## Introduction

In 1964 a dam closed across the Suriname River and a slow green flood began. Over the next seven years the water rose through fifty-four meters of rainforest, climbing the trunks of trees still standing. It spread until it covered 1,560 square kilometers - nearly one percent of the entire country. By the time the reservoir reached its optimal level in 1971, it had swallowed the ancestral villages of about 5,000 Saramaka Maroon people, destroyed 160,000 hectares of tropical forest, and created a lake whose dead trees still spike through the surface like a drowned cathedral. The electricity flowed to an Alcoa aluminum smelter. The full official name, almost no one uses: Professor Doctor Ingenieur W. J. van Blommestein Meer.

## The Dam at Afobaka

Construction on the Afobaka Dam ran from 1961 to 1964. The dam is 54 meters tall and spans the Suriname River; with its secondary embankments along the reservoir's margins, the total length reaches about 12 kilometers. Its watershed covers 12,200 square kilometers. The reservoir was commissioned in 1965, but it took six more years for the water to reach its working level. The driving purpose was industrial: Suralco, a subsidiary of Alcoa, needed electricity to turn Surinamese bauxite into alumina and then into aluminum metal. Roughly 75% of the dam's output went to the smelter; the rest fed Paramaribo, the capital. The official name honors Willem Johan van Blommestein, the Dutch hydrological engineer born in Surakarta, Java. Locals shortened it almost immediately to Brokopondomeer, after the town of Brokopondo two miles downstream.

## The Villages That Were Drowned

The rising water did not flood empty forest. It flooded villages - most of them belonging to the Saramaka Maroon people, whose ancestors had escaped enslavement on coastal plantations in the 17th and 18th centuries and built free communities along the Suriname River. They had lived here for roughly 250 years when the dam came. The largest village to disappear was Ganzee, home to about 1,200 people. About 5,000 Saramaka in total were forced to leave their homes, their gardens, their ancestral burial grounds, and the river bends their oral histories had named for generations. Most were relocated to new villages downstream - transmigratiedorpen in Dutch - often given the same names as the drowned originals. Klaaskreek and Nieuw Ganzee still exist. The old Ganzee is underwater. Displacement on this scale does not end when the trucks stop arriving; the loss is carried through generations.

## Operation Gwamba

As the water rose, animals climbed. Some drowned. Others stranded on shrinking islands of canopy. Between 1964 and 1971, a government-led effort called Operation Gwamba - run largely by the Dutch zoologist and activist John Walsh of the World Society for the Protection of Animals - rescued wildlife from the shrinking hilltops. Boats moved through the half-drowned forest, pulling sloths out of submerging crowns and ferrying armadillos, tapirs, deer, monkeys, and large cats to dry ground. The operation evacuated about 10,000 animals before the water closed over the last of the high points. It was both a genuine rescue and, inescapably, a spectacle that drew international attention while the human displacement received far less notice.

## A Bad Dam, by the Numbers

The 2003 World Bank report Good Dams, Bad Dams reached a blunt conclusion: of every large hydropower project it analyzed, Brokopondo flooded the most land per megawatt of power generated. The arithmetic: 160,000 hectares of biologically rich rainforest drowned, 180 megawatts of capacity produced. That works out to 889 hectares per megawatt. By comparison, China's Three Gorges Dam flooded 6 hectares per megawatt; the global average for large hydro sits around 60. Brokopondo is an outlier by a factor of roughly fifteen. The decision to not harvest the forest before flooding it - considered too expensive at the time - meant the trees died standing, their trunks eventually snagging boats, disrupting shipping, and turning much of the lake into a navigational hazard.

## Wood From the Drowned Forest

Those dead trees found a second life. Orlando Lee On, a Paramaribo entrepreneur, read about underwater timber harvesting on Brazil's Tucurui reservoir during a flight to Miami, and the idea stayed with him. In 2002 his company Brokopondo Watra Wood International got permission from Suralco to try it at Brokopondo. Harvesting began in 2004. By June 2005 the first shipments of Brokopondo lake wood were reaching Europe and the United States - sold in Germany as Stauseeholz, "reservoir wood," prized for being dense, stable, and cut without taking a single living tree. A 2010 documentary by filmmaker Jens Hahne captured divers descending with chainsaws into the green dark, working among trunks that had been underwater for nearly half a century.

## Flying Over the Lake

From the air, Brokopondo is unmistakable: a vast, irregular body of water in the Surinamese interior, studded with islands that are really the tops of hills. Closer in, the surface shows the spectral pattern of dead trees still breaking the water in places - a drowned forest visible from a cockpit. The reservoir is beautiful and it is a scar. Both things are true.

## Flight Context

Located at 4.84°N, 55.07°W. The reservoir dominates central Suriname and is one of the clearest water features visible from cruising altitude over the Guianas. Nearest major airport is Johan Adolf Pengel International (SMJP), Paramaribo, about 75 km north. The Afobaka Dam sits at the reservoir's northern outflow. Recommended viewing altitude: 3,000-10,000 feet AGL to appreciate the irregular shoreline and island-studded surface. Expect scattered clouds and afternoon convective buildups during the wet season (April-August and November-February).
