# Sara Creek (river)

A Surinamese river that once ran past gold fields, a railway and ten Ndyuka villages before a 1964 dam drowned most of it.

**Location:** 4.4350, -54.9241

**Categories:** rivers, suriname, maroon-history, dams, gold-mining

## Introduction

In 1964, a river disappeared. The Sara Creek had been a major tributary of the Suriname River - a wide waterway of shoals and islands and rapids, home to ten Ndyuka villages, followed by a railway that once took prospectors toward the gold fields. On 10 April of that year, the railway along its banks closed for good. Then the water rose, and the lower creek, most of the villages, the tracks, the rapids all went under.

## The Ndyuka Along the Water

From 1793 onward, the Ndyuka people settled along the Sara Creek and built ten villages on its shores. The most important was the Federation of Koffiekamp - three Ndyuka villages clustered with a Moravian Church mission - at the confluence where Sara Creek met the Suriname River. The Ndyuka were Maroons, descendants of enslaved Africans who had escaped Dutch plantations and founded their own sovereign communities in the forest. Their presence on Sara Creek was contested by the neighboring Saramaka Maroons, whose territory lay upstream on the main Suriname River; the Ndyuka said they had come north from the Tapanahoni River. In 1809, the colonial government and the Ndyuka Granman signed a treaty to move the Ndyuka eastward to the Marowijne River. The treaty was ignored. The Ndyuka stayed.

## The Lawa Railway

Gold was discovered along the Sara Creek in 1876, and it turned out the same forest that had sheltered people escaping slavery also sheltered precious metal. By 1882 about 587,000 hectares of gold concessions had been handed out, and Governor van Sypesteyn, who had profited from them, resigned. A boat trip from Paramaribo to the gold fields on the Lawa River took 14 days under normal conditions. In 1903 construction began on the Lawa Railway, meant to shorten that journey dramatically. Completed to its end in 1912, the railway ran from Paramaribo parallel to the Sara Creek and ended at a station called Dam near the last rapids. The final 50 kilometers to the Lawa River itself were never built - the construction costs had already outrun what the gold fields were producing. The section beyond the Suriname River to Dam closed in 1930 and reopened during the Second World War, though without regular service. After the war, rail push trolleys - a kind of hand-powered flatcar - kept the line alive until that last day in April 1964.

## Water Over Everything

In 1958 the Suriname government decided to build the Afobaka Dam on the Suriname River to provide electricity for the country's aluminium industry. The plan required flooding a vast area upstream. Most people living along the Sara Creek would have to leave. The resettlement of the Ndyuka villages was traumatic. Communities with nearly two centuries of continuous settlement were moved to new transmigration villages on higher ground. Homes were abandoned to rising water. Ancestral burial grounds went under. The resulting Brokopondo Reservoir is one of the largest artificial lakes in the world, its surface still punctuated today by the bare tops of drowned trees - a forest that died standing up, its skeletons refusing to sink. The Sara Creek no longer flows into the Suriname River. It flows into a reservoir, and both waters arrived where they are by force.

## Gold Returns

The gold did not stay gone. Exploration resumed in the late twentieth century, when the Canadian company Canarc Resource Corp took an option on the Sara Creek gold concession, and in the decades since, small-scale mining has crept back along the creek's remaining stretches. In 2016 the village of Lediboti granted permits to eight skalians - floating gold dredges that churn riverbeds for deposits - in exchange for a monthly payment of 80,000 Surinamese dollars. The arrangement echoes the 19th-century concessions that once cost a governor his job. The dredges stir the creek's sediment and the mercury used in processing finds its way into the water and the fish. The Ndyuka communities who returned to parts of the watershed after resettlement now face questions about whether the extraction is worth what it costs their children to eat from the river.

## What Is Still There

Stand today on the shore of the Brokopondo Reservoir and you are standing above a lost landscape. The railway tracks are under water. The original Koffiekamp is under water. The rapids that gave the creek its character - wide, shallow, and full of stones - are gone. What remains is the memory carried by the descendants of the people who moved, the photographs in colonial archives, the occasional tree stump breaking the surface. The Sara Creek exists now as a tributary of something larger than a river: it feeds a reservoir built to power an industry that needed it more than the villages did.

## Flight Context

Sara Creek's former course flows into the Brokopondo Reservoir at 4.43 degrees N, 54.92 degrees W, in central Suriname. Recommended viewing altitude 3,000-8,000 feet for observing the reservoir's extensive surface and the dead tree canopies that mark drowned forest. Johan Adolf Pengel International Airport (ICAO: SMJP) lies about 115 km north-northwest. The Afobaka Dam is visible at the reservoir's northern end.
