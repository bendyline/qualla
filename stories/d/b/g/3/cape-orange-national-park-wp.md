# Cape Orange National Park

A Brazilian Amazon park that runs right down to the Atlantic coast - 657,000 hectares of mangroves, floodplains and forest where jaguars hunt, manatees drift and sea turtles come ashore.

**Location:** 4.4333, -51.5167

**Categories:** national parks, Amazon, mangroves, wildlife, Ramsar

## Introduction

Here a Brazilian national park carries the Amazon rainforest right down to the saltwater, and most people have never heard of it. Cape Orange National Park covers 657,318 hectares on the far north coast of Amapá, where the continent finally runs out of land and the jungle rolls straight into the Atlantic. Jaguars pad through the same park where West Indian manatees feed in the mangrove channels. Green sea turtles haul out on beaches that have no road access. It is the twelfth site in Brazil recognized as a Wetland of International Importance under the Ramsar Convention - and still, for most visitors, a place reachable only by boat.

## Where the Rainforest Meets the Sea

Most Amazon parks stop somewhere inland. Cape Orange goes all the way to the sea. The result is an unusual ecosystem mosaic. The park contains mangroves threading into the Atlantic, natural savanna fields, fluvial-marine forests where tides reach inland, seasonally flooded lowlands, and terra firma forest on higher ground. The biota reflects this variety: coastal species cannot survive deep in the interior, and interior species cannot handle the salt - but in a single day on the park's rivers you can move through both worlds. The park abuts the 2,369,400-hectare Amapá State Forest to the southwest, forming one of the largest continuous protected zones in the Brazilian Amazon.

## Four Rivers Into the Atlantic

Four rivers shape the park's interior access: the Cassiporé, the Cunani, the Uaçá, and the Oiapoque. Each winds through its own mix of mangrove and forest before reaching the sea, and each offers a different texture of the landscape. Boat and canoe are the usual means of transit - rafting is possible on some stretches, and the Oiapoque itself forms part of the international border with French Guiana. These waterways are roads for fishermen, for indigenous communities whose territories border the park, and for the small number of researchers and tourists who make it this far. You do not arrive at Cape Orange by accident. You arrive by river, after deliberate planning, and the journey itself becomes part of the experience.

## Who Lives Here

The species list reads like a naturalist's daydream. Giant armadillos plow the forest floor at night - the largest armadillo species on Earth, up to about 1.5 meters long with the tail. Giant anteaters sweep termite mounds with sticky tongues half a meter long. Jaguars, the apex predators of the Amazon, move through the entire park - the largest cats in the Americas. Oncillas, smaller spotted cats, hunt the understory. Bearded sakis swing through the canopy. In the water, green sea turtles nest on coastal beaches; the critically endangered smalltooth sawfish is on the park's list of protected species. Both West Indian manatees and the smaller Amazonian manatees use the park's waterways - one of the few places on Earth where both species overlap. The bird life is prolific, which is why Cape Orange draws the determined ornithologists who do manage to reach it.

## How the Park Came to Be

The Brazilian government created Cape Orange National Park by Decree 84,913 on July 15, 1980, to protect its flora, fauna and natural beauty. It is administered by the Chico Mendes Institute for Biodiversity Conservation (ICMBio), the federal agency that manages Brazil's national parks. A consultative council, created by IBAMA on March 9, 2006, helps guide policy. The formal management plan was approved on January 17, 2011 - more than thirty years after the park's creation. On March 22, 2012, ICMBio and the fishermen of Oiapoque reached a mediated agreement over fishing in the park's waters, balancing traditional livelihoods with conservation. In 2013 Brazil designated the park its 12th Ramsar wetland, recognizing it as globally important under the international wetlands convention.

## A Corner of the Corridor

Cape Orange is also one piece of a much larger plan. It belongs to the Amapá Biodiversity Corridor, created in 2003, which links protected areas across the state to allow wildlife - especially wide-ranging species like jaguars - to move across a landscape, not just between isolated reserves. The park is supported by the Amazon Region Protected Areas Program, one of the largest tropical forest conservation initiatives in the world. The category II designation under IUCN rules (national park) prioritizes ecosystem preservation, scientific research, environmental education, and ecotourism. Visitation is possible, but the park is primarily a place for the birds, the beaches, the manatees, and the mangroves - and for the idea that a coast can still belong to the wild.

## Flying the Coast

From altitude, the northern Amapá coast looks like green lace: mangroves fingering into brown water, sand flats and silt plumes curling into the Atlantic, and behind it all an unbroken carpet of rainforest running hundreds of kilometers inland. The river mouths are the clearest landmarks - dark ribbons reaching the sea. Somewhere in that green sits a jaguar watching a heron, and neither of them has any reason to know the park has a name.

## Flight Context

Located at 4.43°N, 51.52°W in Amapá state, Brazil, along the Atlantic coast near the French Guiana border. Nearest airport is Oiapoque (SBOI), about 70 km to the south-southwest; Macapá (SBMQ) is the primary regional hub about 490 km to the south. Recommended viewing altitude: 3,000-6,000 feet AGL to appreciate the mangrove-rainforest-ocean interface and the four rivers. Expect heavy cloud buildups and rain during the wet season; visibility best in the Sept-Nov shoulder months.
