# Inini

For sixteen years France ran the jungle interior of French Guiana as a separate colony called Inini, populated by Amerindians, Maroons, gold prospectors, and Vietnamese political prisoners shipped halfway around the world.

**Location:** 3.0000, -53.0000

**Categories:** Former Territories, French Guiana, Colonial History, World War II, Penal Colony

## Introduction

Five hundred and thirty-five men sailed from French Indochina to French Guiana in 1931. They were prisoners - Annamite revolutionaries who had risen against French colonial rule in what is now Vietnam - and they had been chosen to open up the interior jungle of South America. The French called this interior Inini, a territory carved out of French Guiana on 6 June 1930 to be run separately from the coast. Senegalese Tirailleurs were brought in as guards. The plan was to build roads, open gold mines, and prepare the rainforest for colonization. Sixteen years later, the plan had failed. The prisoners had outlasted Vichy France, outlasted the Nazis who had briefly been their distant patrons, outlasted the colonial theory that had sent them here in the first place - and three camps in the jungle stood empty, slowly being swallowed by the trees.

## A Second French Guiana

The territory of Inini came into existence by decree on 6 June 1930. Its purpose was administrative: French Guiana's coastal communes around Cayenne would continue as before, while the vast forested interior would be managed separately, with the goal of opening it to commercial development. The district took its name from the Inini River, a tributary of the Maroni that runs east to west across the interior - unusual in French Guiana, where most rivers flow south to north from the highlands to the sea. The population inside the new territory was thin: Amerindian peoples whose ancestors had lived there for millennia, Maroon communities descended from escaped enslaved Africans, and gold prospectors working placer claims on the creeks. Initially the colonial administration staffed Inini with a skeleton crew - three services (water management, forest management, mines), seven military officers, and nine gendarmes.

## The Annamite Prisoners

To make the interior accessible, the administration needed a labor force, and France had a convenient one available across the globe. On the other side of the Pacific, the colonial government of French Indochina had been dealing for years with nationalist uprisings, most notably the Yên Bái mutiny of February 1930. In 1931, 535 Annamite prisoners - men from the French protectorate of Annam, in what is now central Vietnam - were shipped to French Guiana to build roads into the Inini forest. Senegalese Tirailleurs guarded them. The conditions were harsh. In 1937 prisoners at Camp Crique Anguille revolted. The revolt was suppressed. The governor had written to Paris in 1936 that the project was a success and that the interior would soon be ready for settlement, but almost nothing that was promised ever materialized. Railroad construction in the interior proved impossibly difficult. The roads that were built were short. The mines that opened were small.

## Squeezed Between Two Wars

When France fell to Germany in June 1940, Inini technically became part of Vichy France - a neutral state bound to Nazi Germany by the Armistice of 22 June 1940. But Inini's neighbors were suddenly enemies. Suriname, to the west, was a Dutch colony under a government-in-exile fighting the Axis, and by 1941 American troops were stationed there to protect its bauxite mines. Brazil, to the east, declared war on Germany in 1942. Inini found itself a small Vichy island between two hostile powers, and its administration spent most of its energy guarding the borders rather than developing the interior. On 16 March 1943 the territory switched sides, joining Free France. On 6 December 1944 the prisoner camps were finally abandoned. The Annamites were transferred to regular prisons. The first group of them was released in July 1946. The last group, for reasons that have never been adequately explained, waited until August 1953.

## Brazzaville and Afterward

In 1944 Free French leaders met at the Brazzaville Conference and promised that, after the war, all citizens of France's colonies would hold equal rights with those in the metropole. On 19 March 1946, in partial fulfillment of that promise, French Guiana became a full department of France. Inini did not immediately dissolve - it continued as a special entity, governed apart from the rest of the department, for another twenty-three years. Only on 17 March 1969 did the territory vanish from the administrative maps, broken into regular communes. Today the ruins of the three prison camps are still reachable, more or less, by anyone willing to make the trip. They sit in the Guiana Amazonian Park now, swallowed by the forest they were supposed to conquer. The Annamite men who built them were, for the most part, never memorialized by either of the countries whose decisions put them in this forest. Their names exist in administrative files, in a few obscure histories, and in the Vietnamese memory of a revolution that eventually won.

## Flight Context

Inini covered roughly the southern two-thirds of French Guiana - centered near 3.00°N, 53.00°W. The boundaries followed the interior forest, excluding the coastal strip around Cayenne. No airports are located in the former territory; closest access today is Cayenne – Félix Eboué International (SOCA / CAY) on the coast, or the airstrip at Saül (SOOS) near the geographic center. Recommended viewing altitude 6,000-10,000 ft for terrain relief; the interior is nearly unbroken rainforest, with the Inini River visible as a lighter ribbon running east-west.
