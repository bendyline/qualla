# French Guiana

The only piece of mainland South America still governed by a European state - a department of France that is also one of the most biodiverse places on Earth, and the launchpad for Europe's rockets.

**Location:** 4.0000, -53.0000

**Categories:** Overseas departments of France, South America, History, Geography

## Introduction

French Guiana is a paradox with paperwork. It is part of the European Union but not part of the Schengen Area. It uses the euro but imports much of its food from mainland France. It sits on the continental mainland of the Americas but is governed from 7,000 kilometers away. It is 84,000 square kilometers of territory - roughly the size of the island of Ireland - but 41 percent of that is wrapped inside a single national park, the largest in the EU. And from a launchpad near the coast Europe fires Ariane rockets eastward into space, including the one that carried the James Webb Space Telescope into the sky in December 2021. Almost nothing about this place adds up neatly, which is part of why it is so interesting.

## Before and After the French

Long before any European ship drew the coast, people were here: the Kalina (also called Galibi), Lokono, Palikur, Teko, Wayampi and Wayana. The first French establishment is recorded in 1503; durable settlement did not come until Cayenne was founded in 1643. From the seventeenth century onward the colony developed as a slave society, with Africans kidnapped and transported to work sugar and other plantations along the coast. The French Revolution abolished slavery on paper in February 1794 and the decree was actually implemented in French Guiana, alongside Saint-Domingue and Guadeloupe - but not elsewhere. The colony was briefly occupied by a Portuguese-British naval squadron from 1809 to 1817, during the Napoleonic Wars, after which France recovered it. In 1852 France turned the colony into something darker: a penal system, eventually known by the name of one of its islands - Devil's Island - that would run until 1953 and consume the lives of tens of thousands of prisoners.

## Becoming a Department

During the Second World War French Guiana fell under Vichy control, then rallied to Free France on 16 March 1943. On 19 March 1946 the colony stopped being a colony and became a department - fully integrated into the French Republic, with the same formal rights as Seine-et-Marne or Bouches-du-Rhône. In 1964 Charles de Gaulle decided to build a space base here, replacing the earlier Sahara facility in newly independent Algeria. French Guiana was ideal: close to the equator, fronting the ocean, with vast undeveloped land as a buffer zone. The Guiana Space Centre, begun at Kourou, has grown into a major commercial spaceport. Ariane 4, Ariane 5, and the more recent Ariane 6 have lifted off from here; so did the James Webb Space Telescope on flight VA256 in 2021. In 2015 the old regional and departmental councils were dissolved and replaced by a single French Guiana Assembly under a Territorial Collectivity framework, led from 2021 by Gabriel Serville until his death in September 2026. Sporadic independence movements exist but have not found majority support; a 2010 referendum saw almost 70 percent of voters reject becoming an autonomous overseas collectivity.

## The Land and Its Weather

Geographically French Guiana is two regions pressed together: a narrow coastal strip where most of the nearly 300,000 inhabitants live, and an immense interior of near-inaccessible rainforest rising to the Tumuc-Humac mountains along the Brazilian frontier. The highest peak, Bellevue de l'Inini, reaches 851 meters in the commune of Maripasoula. Off the coast lie the three Salvation Islands - Royale, Saint-Joseph, and Devil's Island - whose prison ruins are now tourist destinations, and the Îles du Connétable bird sanctuary further east. The climate is equatorial: hot and humid year-round, with heavy rainfall from December to July (typically over 330 mm per month) driven by the Intertropical Convergence Zone. The eastern half of the country dries out between August and November, a tropical monsoon pattern.

## A Cosmopolitan Interior

For all its remoteness French Guiana is, demographically, one of the more layered places in the Americas. Creole culture is rooted in the history of slavery and has remained central. Maroon peoples - Ndyuka, Saramaka, Aluku, Paramaka - trace their ancestry to Africans who escaped Dutch plantations and built independent communities in the forests upstream. Indigenous peoples still live in the interior. Beginning in the 1970s and 80s, France resettled several hundred Hmong refugees from Laos here after the communist takeover; their villages now produce much of the region's fresh produce. In the late 1980s more than 10,000 Surinamese refugees - many Maroons - arrived fleeing the Surinamese Civil War. In 1936 Félix Éboué, from Cayenne, became governor of Guadeloupe, the first black man to serve as governor of a French colony. His name is on the international airport today.

## Open Questions

French Guiana is rich in biodiversity and, by some measures, rich in potential - gold is mined here, legally and illegally, and the Guianese Shield is enormous. It is also rich in difficulties. Unemployment runs at about 17 percent, GDP per capita is well under half that of mainland France, living costs are high, and illegal gold mining drives both deforestation and violence against indigenous and Maroon communities. In 2023 riots broke out in the wake of the killing of Nahel M. in a Paris suburb, and a mosquito-control worker standing on his balcony was killed. In March 2024 President Macron visited to commemorate Major Arnaud Blanc, a GIGN gendarme killed in 2023 during an operation against illegal gold mining near Maripasoula, then travelled on to Brazil, where he presented the Legion of Honor to the Brazilian Indigenous leader Raoni Metuktire for his rainforest-conservation work. The border with Brazil - France's longest land border with any country - is now crossed by the Franco-Brazilian Bridge over the Oyapock, open since 2017. The border with Suriname, along the Maroni, remains partially unresolved in its southern reaches. The territory keeps asking France, and itself, what kind of place it wants to be.

## Flight Context

French Guiana lies between 2° and 6° N, 51° and 55° W on the northern coast of South America. Main airport: Félix Eboué (SOCA / CAY) near Cayenne; regional fields at Saint-Laurent-du-Maroni (SOOM), Saint-Georges-de-l'Oyapock (SOOG), Maripasoula (SOOA), and Kourou (SOOK). Guiana Space Centre operations create temporary airspace restrictions during Ariane launches. Visual cues from altitude: a dense, dark-green canopy broken only by the silver ribbons of the Maroni (west border), the Oyapock (east border), the Approuague, and the Petit-Saut reservoir in the north.
