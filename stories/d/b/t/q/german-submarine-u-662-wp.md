# German submarine U-662

A Type VIIC U-boat lost in the South Atlantic in July 1943, whose captain was kept alive for seven days by a chief mate treading water with him in his arms.

**Location:** 3.9333, -48.7667

**Categories:** Shipwrecks, World War II, Submarines, Atlantic Ocean

## Introduction

Most U-boats that were lost went down with all hands. *U-662* went down with almost all hands. On 21 July 1943, in the South Atlantic off the Brazilian coast, a US Navy Catalina caught her on the surface and sank her with depth charges. Of a crew of some forty-four to sixty men, only the commander and two others survived. The commander, Oberleutnant zur See Heinz-Eberhard Müller, had both arms and one leg broken and suffered internal injuries in the attack. What follows next is the kind of story that makes you read the paragraph again. His chief mate, Horst Gaertner, kept him afloat by holding him, and swam around in the open ocean with Müller in his arms for seven days before a patrol craft finally found them.

## Built in Hamburg

*U-662* was a Type VIIC U-boat, the same workhorse class as hundreds of her sisters: 67.10 meters long, 769 tonnes surfaced, 871 submerged, maximum depth 230 meters, a crew of between 44 and 60 men. She was laid down at Deutsche Werft in Hamburg on 7 May 1941 as yard number 811, launched on 22 January 1942, and commissioned on 9 April 1942 under the command of Kapitänleutnant Wolfgang Hermann. Her career began with training at the 5th U-boat Flotilla and then a transfer, on 1 October 1942, to the 7th Flotilla for operational service. In four patrols she would sink three merchant ships and damage a fourth - a modest record by the standards of 1940 when U-boats hunted almost unopposed, but a real record by the standards of 1943 when they were being hunted themselves.

## A Restless Year of Wolfpacks

In the months between October 1942 and May 1943, *U-662* cycled through eleven different wolfpacks: Panther, Leopard, Südwärts, Delphin, Spitz, Jaguar, an unnamed group, Adler, Meise, Specht and Fink. The names read like a rain of carnivores dropped onto the Atlantic. The tactic was simple: spread across the convoy lanes, call the others at the first sighting, concentrate on a single target. The night of 26 December 1942, *U-662* reported sighting Convoy ONS 154 and later sank the crippled straggler *Ville de Rouen*, a freighter that had already been attacked earlier by another boat. By this point in the Battle of the Atlantic, though, the arithmetic had turned. Allied escorts were stronger. The convoy codes had been broken. Long-range aircraft - B-24 Liberators, PBY Catalinas - were roaming the gaps that had once been the U-boats' hunting grounds.

## Three Days in July

Between 19 and 21 July 1943 *U-662* was attacked three times in quick succession by American aircraft. On 19 July a B-24 Liberator dropped four depth charges; the bomber broke off after sustaining flak damage, and the U-boat escaped undamaged. The next day, 20 July, a Douglas B-18 Bolo attacked; again *U-662* survived. This was no longer hunting. This was being hunted. Every morning brought another aircraft, every watch brought the need to submerge, and the diesels charging the batteries on the surface were exactly when a boat was most vulnerable. A U-boat in July 1943 was a slow target that could not stay down forever and could not hide while running on the surface.

## The Last Attack

On 21 July 1943, off the Brazilian coast in the South Atlantic, two Catalinas from US Patrol Squadron VP-94 caught *U-662*. Lieutenant Stan Auslander in the first PBY attacked her for roughly an hour. A second Catalina piloted by Lieutenant (junior grade) R. H. Howland finished the work with a run of depth charges that sent the submarine to the bottom. Commander Müller, on the bridge when the attack hit, was horrifically wounded. Both arms and one leg were broken, and he had internal injuries that should by any honest calculation have killed him. Most of his crew were dead - drowned in the hull or killed in the water. Two other men survived. One was Horst Gaertner, his chief mate.

## Seven Days

What happened next is one of the strangest survival stories of the war. Gaertner kept Müller alive by holding him. Alone in the open Atlantic, with only a few survivors around them and no immediate rescue, he treaded water with his captain in his arms. Hours became a day. A day became several. Gaertner swam around with Müller in his arms for seven days before they were picked up by a patrol craft. Müller lived. He was so severely injured that he was judged no longer fit for combat, and in March 1944 he was repatriated to Germany. Gaertner, as far as the records say, survived too. The other sailors - dozens of young men from Hamburg, from the training schools, from occupied ports - were lost. The wreck of *U-662* lies at roughly 3.93°N, 48.77°W, near the mouth of the Amazon. Whatever else the story is, it is also a story about what one man will do for another when the sea has taken almost everything else.

## Flight Context

The wreck of U-662 lies near 3.93°N, 48.77°W in the South Atlantic off the northeastern coast of Brazil, near the mouth of the Amazon. Nearest coastal city: Belém (SBBE / BEL). From cruise altitude, look for the brown plume of the Amazon's discharge meeting the Atlantic - a visible signature on ocean color that reaches far out to sea in the area where this Catalina patrol intercepted the U-boat.
