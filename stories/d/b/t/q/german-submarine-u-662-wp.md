# German submarine U-662

A Type VIIC U-boat sunk off the mouth of the Amazon in July 1943, whose badly wounded captain and a few of his men survived sixteen days adrift in life rafts.

**Location:** 3.9333, -48.7667

**Categories:** Shipwrecks, World War II, Submarines, Atlantic Ocean

## Introduction

Most U-boats that were lost went down with all hands. *U-662* went down with almost all hands. On 21 July 1943, in the Atlantic just north of the equator off the Brazilian coast, a US Navy Catalina caught her on the surface and sank her with depth charges. Of the 47 men aboard, only the commander and two others survived. The commander, Kapitänleutnant Heinz-Eberhard Müller, was thrown into the sea by the explosion and badly injured. What follows next is the kind of story that makes you read the paragraph again. The Catalina dropped two life rafts, and Müller and a few of his men drifted in them for sixteen days, with no food and sharks following them, before an American patrol vessel finally found them.

## Built in Hamburg

*U-662* was a Type VIIC U-boat, the same workhorse class as hundreds of her sisters: 67.10 meters long, 769 tonnes surfaced, 871 submerged, maximum depth 230 meters, a crew of between 44 and 60 men. She was laid down at Howaldtswerke in Hamburg on 7 May 1941 as yard number 811, launched on 22 January 1942, and commissioned on 9 April 1942 under the command of Kapitänleutnant Wolfgang Hermann. Her career began with training at the 5th U-boat Flotilla and then a transfer, on 1 October 1942, to the 7th Flotilla for operational service. In four patrols she would sink three merchant ships and damage a fourth - a modest record by the standards of 1940 when U-boats hunted almost unopposed, but a real record by the standards of 1943 when they were being hunted themselves.

## A Restless Year of Wolfpacks

In the months between October 1942 and May 1943, *U-662* cycled through eleven different wolfpacks: Panther, Leopard, Südwärts, Delphin, Spitz, Jaguar, an unnamed group, Adler, Meise, Specht and Fink. The names read like a bestiary scattered across the Atlantic. The tactic was simple: spread across the convoy lanes, call the others at the first sighting, concentrate on a single target. The night of 26 December 1942, *U-662* reported sighting Convoy ONS 154 and on 29 December sank the crippled straggler *Ville de Rouen*, a freighter that had already been attacked earlier by another boat. By this point in the Battle of the Atlantic, though, the arithmetic had turned. Allied escorts were stronger. The convoy codes had been broken. Long-range aircraft - B-24 Liberators, PBY Catalinas - were roaming the gaps that had once been the U-boats' hunting grounds.

## Three Days in July

Between 19 and 21 July 1943 *U-662* was attacked three times in quick succession by American aircraft. On 19 July a B-24 Liberator dropped four depth charges; the bomber broke off after sustaining flak damage, and the U-boat escaped undamaged. The next day, 20 July, a VP-94 Catalina flown by Lieutenant (junior grade) Stan Auslander attacked; again *U-662* survived. This was no longer hunting. This was being hunted. Every morning brought another aircraft, every watch brought the need to submerge, and the diesels charging the batteries on the surface were exactly when a boat was most vulnerable. A U-boat in July 1943 was a slow target that could not stay down forever and could not hide while running on the surface.

## The Last Attack

On 21 July 1943, off the Brazilian coast just north of the equator, a Catalina of US Patrol Squadron VP-94 piloted by Lieutenant (junior grade) Rowland caught *U-662* on the surface. Its depth charges sent the submarine to the bottom. Commander Müller was thrown into the water by the explosion and badly wounded; one later account says both his arms and a leg were broken. Most of his crew were dead. The Catalina crew saw five men in the water and dropped two life rafts; one man died almost at once, and four, Müller among them, climbed aboard.

## Sixteen Days

What happened next is one of the harder survival stories of the war. The four men had no food in the rafts. Hours became a day. A day became many. For sixteen days they drifted in the open Atlantic, followed by sharks, until the patrol vessel USS *Siren*, escorting a convoy, picked them up on 6 August 1943. One of the four died aboard. Three lived: Müller and two young seamen, Hermann Grauff and Ferdinand Marx. A widely repeated account says a chief mate named Horst Gaertner kept Müller alive by swimming with him in his arms for seven days; the US Navy's interrogation report of the survivors names no such man. Müller was so severely injured that he was judged no longer fit for combat, and in March 1944 he was repatriated to Germany. The other 44 men - most of them young - were lost. The wreck of *U-662* lies at roughly 3.93°N, 48.77°W, near the mouth of the Amazon. Whatever else the story is, it is also a story about how little the sea leaves behind, and how much a raft can matter.

## Flight Context

The wreck of U-662 lies near 3.93°N, 48.77°W in the Atlantic off the northern coast of Brazil, north of the mouth of the Amazon. The nearest airfield is Oiapoque (SBOI), about 335 km to the west; Belém (SBBE / BEL) is about 590 km to the south. From cruise altitude, look for the brown plume of the Amazon's discharge meeting the Atlantic - a visible signature on ocean color that reaches far out to sea in the area where this Catalina patrol intercepted the U-boat.
