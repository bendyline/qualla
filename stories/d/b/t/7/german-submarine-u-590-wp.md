# German submarine U-590

Built in Hamburg, based in occupied France, sunk near the mouth of the Amazon - the service record of a Type VIIC U-boat that managed one kill in five patrols and lost all forty-five men aboard when a Catalina found her.

**Location:** 3.3667, -48.6333

**Categories:** Shipwrecks, World War II, Submarines, Atlantic Ocean

## Introduction

On 9 July 1943 a US Navy PBY Catalina flying patrol out of the northeast coast of Brazil caught *U-590* on the surface near the mouth of the Amazon. The aircraft, from Patrol Squadron VP-94, dropped depth charges. Forty-five men went down with the boat; there were no survivors. Five days earlier the same U-boat had torpedoed a Brazilian coastal ship called *Pelotaslóide* waiting for tugs before entering Salinas. That was *U-590*'s only confirmed sinking in her entire career. Everything else about her service reads like a long, uneventful journey ending abruptly in the wrong ocean.

## Hamburg to St. Nazaire

*U-590* was a Type VIIC U-boat, the workhorse design of the Atlantic battle: 67 meters long, displacing 769 tonnes on the surface and 871 submerged, driven by two Germaniawerft diesels and two Brown, Boveri & Cie electric motors, with a complement of between 44 and 60 men. She was laid down at Blohm & Voss in Hamburg on 31 October 1940 as yard number 566, launched on 6 August 1941, and commissioned on 2 October 1941 under the command of Heinrich Müller-Edzards. After training with the 6th U-boat Flotilla, she moved to the same flotilla for active operations on 1 April 1942. Her first war patrol ran from Kiel on 4 April 1942 around the north of Britain through the Faroe-Shetland gap and into the Atlantic, ending at St. Nazaire in occupied France on 17 April. From there she would sail for the rest of her career.

## Five Patrols, One Kill

The second patrol, into the mid-Atlantic, was uneventful. The third, beginning 11 August 1942, was *U-590*'s longest - 106 days. She did not leave the Bay of Biscay until the end of August. By mid-September she was off Western Sahara, by 9 October at the patrol's southernmost point off Sierra Leone and Liberia. On 1 November she was passing southeast of the Cape Verdes on the way home. She returned to St. Nazaire on 24 November with no sinkings. The fourth patrol left St. Nazaire on 31 January 1943. On 11 March, in the mid-Atlantic, she torpedoed the British refrigerated cargo ship *Jamaica Producer* - and believed she had sunk it. She had not; the *Jamaica Producer* reached port, was repaired, and returned to service in May 1943. She survived the war. Eleven days later a crew member on *U-590* broke his arm. That was the rest of the patrol.

## The Wolfpacks

*U-590* cycled through six wolfpacks during her career: Hecht in May-June 1942, Blücher in August, Iltis in September, then Neuland, Dränger and Seewolf in a compressed rush of early 1943. Wolfpacks worked on a simple, brutal principle: spread out across the convoy routes, sight a target, call the others, concentrate. The Allied North Atlantic convoys by 1943 were better escorted, better informed by Ultra code-breaking, and increasingly covered by long-range aircraft. For *U-590* the wolfpack tactic that had devastated Allied shipping in 1940 and 1941 was producing diminishing returns. The math that had once favored the U-boats was tilting hard against them.

## The South American Coast

The fifth patrol took *U-590* far from her normal grounds - south and west, across the equator, toward the shoulder of Brazil. On 4 July 1943 she sank the *Pelotaslóide* near the mouth of the Amazon. The Brazilian ship had been waiting for tugs before entering Salinas, and was essentially defenseless. It was *U-590*'s first and, as it turned out, last confirmed sinking. By this point in the war, American patrol aircraft were flying out of bases on the Brazilian coast - Brazil had declared war on the Axis in August 1942 - and they were hunting U-boats along this stretch of the South Atlantic in growing numbers.

## The End

Five days after the sinking, on 9 July 1943, a Catalina of VP-94 found *U-590* near the Amazon Estuary. The aircraft made its attack run. Depth charges straddled the boat. *U-590* went down, and forty-five men went with her - Müller-Edzards and his crew. There were no survivors. Her final resting place lies in warm, shallow water near the equator, a long way from the Hamburg shipyard where she was built and from the St. Nazaire pens she had sailed from five times. Like most U-boats of the Battle of the Atlantic, her record is mostly a record of wasted youth on all sides: one badly damaged freighter that limped home to sail again, one Brazilian coastal ship lost to an ambush, one young crew vanished in the closing phase of a war the boat had no chance of winning. Of roughly 40,000 men who served in German U-boats during the Second World War, about 30,000 never came home. *U-590*'s crew was forty-five of those numbers, and each of them had a name and a mother and a route he had meant to take back to Hamburg.

## Flight Context

The wreck of U-590 lies near 3.37°N, 48.63°W, off the Amazon Estuary on the northeastern coast of Brazil. Nearest coastal city is Belém (SBBE / BEL). The area is well offshore from the mouth of the Amazon; at cruise altitude, look for the enormous silt plume where the river meets the Atlantic - the largest freshwater outflow in the world - as a visible signature on the ocean surface.
