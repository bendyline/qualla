# Tiriyó language

A Cariban language of the Brazil-Suriname border whose speakers call themselves tarëno, spoken fluently across an entire people - and now listening warily to the hum of a new radar station.

**Location:** 2.5333, -55.7667

**Categories:** indigenous, language, suriname, brazil, cariban, linguistics

## Introduction

The people who speak Tiriyó do not, among themselves, use that name. They call themselves tarëno. Linguists and bureaucrats know them variously as Tiriyó, Trio, tarano, tirió - a small museum of near-spellings that tracks how many outsiders have tried to pin them down. What matters is that they all agree on one thing: the language is alive. Almost every tarëno speaks it, and the majority speak no other. That makes Tiriyó unusual among the Cariban languages of lowland South America, most of which are in steep decline.

## Along Both Sides of a Border

Tiriyó is spoken on both sides of the Brazil-Suriname border, in villages scattered across the forested uplands where rain and rivers define the map better than any line a colonial surveyor ever drew. The modern Tiriyó are, as oral history describes and outside ethnography confirms, an amalgamation of older groups - the Aramixó, for instance, mentioned in European records as early as 1609. Over centuries of war, disease, migration, and marriage, smaller nations fused into what is now one community with two main dialect areas. Jones (1972) called them Eastern, or Tapanahoni basin, and Western, or Sipaliwini basin. Sergio Meira, who has documented Tiriyó since 1997, labels them K-Tiriyó and H-Tiriyó.

## The Sound of a Glottal

The two dialects diverge most audibly on a single sound: a glottal fricative, the soft 'h' that appears in clusters like hp, ht, and hk. H-Tiriyó speakers pronounce it. K-Tiriyó speakers drop it, and where an H-speaker has 'hp,' a K-speaker has a long vowel in its place. It is the kind of difference a stranger might not notice, but that two speakers recognize instantly - the way a Londoner hears Scotland in a single vowel. About sixty percent of speakers use H-Tiriyó, which dominates the big village of Kwamalasamutu in Suriname and the villages along the Western Paru river in Brazil. K-Tiriyó is spoken in villages along the Eastern Paru, and at Tepoe and Paloemeu in Suriname.

## What Words Are Made Of

Tiriyó has seven vowels - including two central vowels, ï and ë, that have no neat English equivalents - and ten consonants. Its stress falls in iambic feet counted from the start of the word, meaning every second syllable gets weight except for the very last one, which is never stressed. When a word receives a prefix or a suffix, the stress reshuffles to match. There is also a process called syllable reduction that must delight anyone who loves historical linguistics: high vowels ï and u have been quietly weakening and dropping for centuries, turning old three-syllable stems into two-syllable ones with long vowels or consonant clusters left behind as the scaffolding of their former shape. Much of what is unusual about Tiriyó morphology is, in that sense, a fossil of how the language sounded a thousand years ago.

## A Grammar That Won't Be Pinned Down

Interrogatives in Tiriyó almost all begin with the letter a - akɨ for 'who,' atɨ for 'what' - a pattern that linguists note is unique within the Cariban family. Its pronouns distinguish by visibility, proximity, and animacy: whether what you are pointing at can be seen, is nearby, and is a living thing. Verbs distinguish not only past, present, and future, but also the speaker's level of certainty - different suffixes depending on whether you are sure that thing happened, or whether you are only guessing from the rainclouds. The case-marking system is one of the most complicated in South America. Meira described it simply as a 'split-participant' system, which in his hands is polite linguist-code for 'almost every combination of participant roles is instantiated somewhere in the grammar.'

## The Radar Station

For a language whose speakers number in the low thousands, Tiriyó looks remarkably secure. Children learn it first; adults use it in every setting; the community is geographically compact. Endangerment metrics rate it low. But one sentence in the linguistic literature carries a note of warning: a new radar station has been installed near the main village, staffed by a considerable number of non-indigenous personnel. That kind of presence tends not to stay outside a village's life. Languages that have survived centuries of disease and missionary pressure have sometimes tipped over quickly when a road, an airstrip, or a garrison arrives. The tarëno have watched outsiders come and go before. This time the newcomers have antennas.
