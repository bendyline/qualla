# Tiriyó language

A Cariban language of the Brazil-Suriname border whose speakers call themselves tarëno, spoken fluently across an entire people - though linguists have warned about a radar station staffed by outsiders near its main village.

**Location:** 2.5333, -55.7667

**Categories:** indigenous, language, suriname, brazil, cariban, linguistics

## Introduction

The people who speak Tiriyó do not, among themselves, use that name. They call themselves tarëno. Linguists and bureaucrats know them variously as Tiriyó, Trio, tarano, tirió - a small museum of near-spellings that tracks how many outsiders have tried to pin them down. What matters is that they all agree on one thing: the language is alive. Almost every tarëno speaks it, and the majority speak no other. That makes Tiriyó unusual among the Cariban languages of lowland South America, many of which are in decline.

## Along Both Sides of a Border

Tiriyó is spoken on both sides of the Brazil-Suriname border, in villages scattered across the forested uplands where rain and rivers define the map better than any line a colonial surveyor ever drew. The modern Tiriyó are, as oral history describes and outside ethnography confirms, an amalgamation of older groups - the Aramixó, for instance, mentioned in European records as early as 1609-1610. Over centuries of war, disease, migration, and marriage, smaller nations fused into what is now one community with two main dialect areas. Jones (1972) called them Eastern, or Tapanahoni basin, and Western, or Sipaliwini basin. Sérgio Meira, who began documenting Tiriyó in 1997, labels them K-Tiriyó and H-Tiriyó.

## The Sound of a Glottal

The two dialects diverge most audibly on a single sound: a glottal fricative, the soft 'h' that appears in clusters like hp, ht, and hk. H-Tiriyó speakers pronounce it. K-Tiriyó speakers drop it, and where an H-speaker has 'hp,' a K-speaker has a long vowel in its place. It is the kind of difference a stranger might not notice, but that two speakers recognize instantly - the way a Londoner hears Scotland in a single vowel. About sixty percent of speakers use H-Tiriyó, which dominates the big village of Kwamalasamutu in Suriname and the villages along the Western Paru and Marapi rivers in Brazil. K-Tiriyó is spoken in villages along the Eastern Paru in Brazil, and at Tepoe and Paloemeu in Suriname.

## What Words Are Made Of

Tiriyó has seven vowels - including two central vowels, ï and ë, that have no neat English equivalents - and ten consonants. Its stress falls in iambic feet counted from the start of the word, meaning every second syllable gets weight except for the very last one, which is never stressed. When a word receives a prefix or a suffix, the stress reshuffles to match. There is also a process called syllable reduction: the final syllable of many morphemes appears in full, shrinks to a single consonant or a lengthened vowel, or disappears entirely, depending on what follows it. Much of what is unusual about Tiriyó morphology traces back to that shape-shifting.

## A Grammar That Won't Be Pinned Down

Interrogatives in Tiriyó almost all begin with the letter a - akɨ for 'who,' atɨ for 'what' - a pattern that linguists note is unique within the Cariban family. Its third-person pronouns distinguish by visibility, proximity, and animacy: whether what you are pointing at can be seen, is nearby, and is a living thing. Verbs distinguish past, present, and future, and in the present and future they also mark the speaker's certainty - one suffix if you are sure, another if you are only guessing from the rainclouds. The case-marking system is notoriously complicated: a 'split-participant' system in which almost every possible combination of participants turns up in some construction.

## The Radar Station

For a language whose speakers number in the low thousands, Tiriyó looks remarkably secure. Children learn it first; adults use it in every area of life. Linguists rate its endangerment low, though UNESCO lists it as vulnerable. But one sentence in the linguistic literature carries a note of warning: a radar station was installed near the main village, staffed by a considerable number of non-indigenous personnel. That kind of presence tends not to stay outside a village's life. Languages that have survived centuries of disease and missionary pressure have sometimes tipped over quickly when a road, an airstrip, or a garrison arrives. The tarëno have watched outsiders come and go before. This time the newcomers have antennas.
