# Kanto Shrine

An imperial Shinto shrine built on Chinese soil to enshrine Emperor Meiji and the sun goddess Amaterasu lasted just seven years before Japan's defeat erased it from the landscape.

**Location:** 38.8153, 121.2277

**Categories:** Japanese diaspora in China, Shinto in China, 1938 establishments in the Japanese colonial empire, History of Dalian

## Introduction

There is nothing left to see. Where the Kanto Shrine once stood in Lushunkou -- a grand Shinto complex dedicated to the sun goddess Amaterasu and Emperor Meiji -- the landscape offers no trace of the sacred precinct that Japan erected on Chinese territory in 1938. Its absence tells a story more plainly than its architecture ever could: the shrine existed for precisely seven years, from its establishment on 1 June 1938 to Japan's surrender in August 1945, a monument to imperial ambition with an expiration date built into its foundations.

## Sacred Ground, Borrowed Soil

The Kanto Shrine held the rank of kanpeisha -- an imperial shrine of the first rank in Japan's modern system of ranked Shinto shrines, placing it among the most important religious sites in the empire. The kami enshrined there were Amaterasu, the sun goddess at the center of Shinto cosmology, and Emperor Meiji, the sovereign under whom Japan had transformed from a feudal society into an industrial and military power. Establishing such a shrine in the Kwantung Leased Territory was a statement of permanence: Japan intended Lushunkou to be Japanese in spirit as well as in administration. The choice of Emperor Meiji was particularly pointed, since it was under his reign that Japan had first seized Port Arthur in 1894 and then taken it again from Russia in 1905. The shrine consecrated the ground that Japanese soldiers had died to capture.

## An Empire of Shrines

The Kanto Shrine was part of a broader pattern of Shinto expansion across Japan's colonial territories. From Korea to Taiwan to Manchuria, the establishment of shrines served both religious and political purposes, embedding Japanese spiritual authority into conquered landscapes. These were not modest village shrines but substantial architectural projects, funded by the state and administered by the imperial household. The Kanto Shrine was established during a period of accelerating Japanese militarism -- 1938, a year into the Second Sino-Japanese War, when the empire was consolidating its hold on China's northeast and pushing deeper into the mainland. That the shrine received first-rank imperial status underscored Lushunkou's importance to Japan's self-image as a Pacific power. The Kwantung Leased Territory, carved from China after the Russo-Japanese War, was the physical proof of Japan's arrival among the great powers.

## Vanishing Without a Trace

When Soviet troops swept into Manchuria in August 1945, the institutions of Japanese colonial rule collapsed in days. The Kanto Shrine closed along with every other apparatus of the Kwantung Leased Territory's administration. Unlike some colonial-era buildings in Lushunkou that survived -- the Russian-built railway station, the Russo-Japanese Prison -- the shrine left no physical remnant. Its disappearance was swift and thorough, consistent with the broader dismantling of Shinto installations across Japan's former territories after the war. Today, the site in Lushunkou District, Dalian, carries no marker or memorial. The shrine exists only in archival records and commemorative stamps. In a district saturated with the physical legacy of foreign occupation -- Russian forts, Japanese war monuments, Soviet military installations -- the Kanto Shrine's total erasure makes it perhaps the most honest artifact of empire: a claim of eternal presence that lasted less than a decade.

## Flight Context

The former shrine site is located at approximately 38.82N, 121.23E in western Lushunkou District, Dalian. No visible structures remain at the site. Dalian Zhoushuizi International Airport (ZYTL) lies approximately 38 km to the northeast. The surrounding Lushunkou District is visible from altitude as a mix of residential development and green hillsides at the southern tip of the Liaodong Peninsula.
