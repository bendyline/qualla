# Trà Sư Cajuput Forest

A flooded melaleuca forest planted in 1983 to heal poisoned ground, now 845 hectares of green water, storks and darters on the An Giang border.

**Location:** 10.5853, 105.0581

**Categories:** Protected areas of Vietnam, An Giang province, Forests of Vietnam, Mekong Delta, Wetlands of Vietnam

## Introduction

The boat does not so much part the water as tear a seam in a lawn. Duckweed covers the channels at Trà Sư in a layer so dense and so uniformly green that the surface reads as solid ground until the prow opens it, and behind the stern the seam closes again within seconds. Above, the melaleuca trunks go up pale and papery and slightly crooked, and the light that reaches the water has been filtered through so much leaf that everything below arrives green. What makes this worth sitting still for is that none of it is old. Forty-odd years ago this was poisoned dirt that would not grow rice.

## Sour Ground

The problem was acid sulfate soil. Drain certain delta wetlands and the buried sulfides oxidise, the ground turns acid enough to kill a rice crop, and the resulting land does nothing for anybody. In 1983 planters went at 845 hectares of it in Văn Giáo, in what was then Tịnh Biên district, using the one tree that would tolerate the conditions: Melaleuca cajuputi, the cajuput or tràm. It is a tough, unglamorous species - grey-white bark that peels in papery sheets, cream-coloured flower spikes at the branch ends, and the ability to stand with its roots underwater indefinitely. Its leaves yield cajuput oil, up to sixty percent 1,8-cineole, sold across Southeast Asia as liniment, insect repellent and flavouring; its timber goes for fuel, charcoal and caulking boats. The planting was meant to green a wasteland and buffer floodwater coming down from upstream. What actually happened was that the birds moved in.

## A Census of the Swamp

Surveys have logged seventy bird species here, across thirteen orders and thirty-one families, two of them listed in Vietnam's Red Data Book. The painted stork, Mycteria leucocephala, is the one people photograph - white and pink and black, with a heavy yellow bill it sweeps through the shallows. The Oriental darter, Anhinga melanogaster, is the stranger sight: a snake-necked diving bird that swims submerged with only its head above the duckweed. There are eleven mammal species, mostly rodents and bats, including the fruit bat Cynopterus sphinx; twenty-five reptiles and amphibians; ten fish species year-round rising to thirteen when the floods come, among them the clown knifefish and the white featherback, both in trouble across their range. The plant list runs to 140 species in fifty-two families, seventy-eight of them medicinal, plus the duckweed, water cabbage, lotus and water lilies that make the surface what it is.

## Mùa Nước Nổi

The delta has two states, and Trà Sư is built for the wet one. From September into November the Mekong's flood pulse arrives, water levels climb, and the forest becomes what it was designed to imitate - a genuinely flooded forest, deeper, wider, harder to tell where the channels end. This is when the place is at its best and when it does its real work, holding back water that would otherwise arrive somewhere less able to absorb it. Fish that spend the dry months elsewhere move in to spawn. The birds concentrate. In 2003 the provincial government of An Giang recognised what the plantation had turned into, approving the Trà Sư Melaleuca Forest Landscape Protection Area on 27 May of that year, with conservation and research written in alongside tourism.

## Ten Thousand Steps

Tourism won the larger share. In 2018 An Giang Tourism Joint Stock Company leased 159 hectares for development, and the site opened commercially on 23 August that year. Its signature is the Ten Thousand Steps Bamboo Bridge, a raised walkway threaded through the trees and built from more than half a million bamboo stalks; the first stretch of nearly four kilometres opened on 1 January 2020 and two weeks later was recognised as the longest bamboo bridge in Vietnam, with further phases planned to take the total past ten kilometres. Motorised boats run the main channels, and small paddled canoes take over for the narrow ones, which is where the forest actually gets quiet. Trà Sư was named one of the country's most attractive destinations in 2024. Whether a wetland can carry that many visitors and still be a wetland is the open question here, as it is nearly everywhere.

## What a Plantation Becomes

It is worth being precise about what Trà Sư is, because the delta has very little primary forest left and it would be easy to mistake this for a survivor. It is not. Every tree here was put in the ground deliberately, on land that had been ruined, by people whose goal was flood control and reclamation rather than biodiversity. The storks arrived afterwards, uninvited, and stayed. Thirty kilometres away in Châu Đốc, pilgrims climb a hill to a goddess who is said to have grown out of the stone. Out here something less miraculous and more encouraging happened: somebody planted the right tree in the wrong soil, waited four decades, and got a functioning ecosystem out of it.

## Flight Context

Trà Sư lies at 10.585°N, 105.058°E in An Giang province, roughly 30 kilometres southwest of Châu Đốc and about 10 kilometres from the Cambodian border. From 2,000-4,000 feet it appears as a dark, sharply bounded rectangle of canopy in an otherwise open geometry of rice paddy and irrigation grid - one of the few solid green blocks between the Hậu River and the Seven Mountains, which rise to the south and are the best gross-navigation reference in the area. Cần Thơ International (VVCT) is about 49 nautical miles east-southeast, Rạch Giá (VVRG) about 38 nautical miles south, and Phnom Penh International (VDPP) about 59 nautical miles north-northwest. The forest photographs best in the September-November flood season; expect thick haze during the March-April burning season.
