# Tịnh Biên

A Vietnamese border crossing beneath the Seven Mountains, where the highway changes its number, the population is heavily Khmer, and the delta's only real hills carry both pilgrims and a very hard memory.

**Location:** 10.5583, 105.0083

**Categories:** An Giang province, Cambodia–Vietnam border crossings, Mekong Delta, Vietnam

## Introduction

Walk two hundred metres west of the barrier at Tịnh Biên and the road you are on stops being Vietnam's National Highway 91 and becomes Cambodia's National Highway 2, which will carry you the 125 kilometres to Phnom Penh. The gate on this side is the Tịnh Biên International Border Gate; the gate on that side, close enough to see, is the Phnom Den International Border Gate. Same tarmac, two names, two sets of paperwork, and a queue of motorbikes carrying cigarettes and electronics whose customs status nobody is keen to discuss. This is one of the busiest doorways between the two countries, and one of the least remarked upon.

## One Road, Two Numbers

Tịnh Biên earns its living from being adjacent to somewhere else. The frontier market here is the sort of place where three currencies circulate and the goods on the tables are Thai, Cambodian and Chinese as often as Vietnamese. Officially the crossing is a designated Vietnamese e-visa entry point, which makes it a legitimate route for foreign travellers rather than just a regional trade gate; unofficially, the proximity to Cambodia keeps a steady flow of smuggled tobacco and electronic goods moving through. Roads run in three useful directions from here: Highway 91 east to Cần Thơ and the delta's main artery, Highway 55 southwest to Hà Tiên on the Gulf coast some eighty kilometres away, and Highway 2 north into Cambodia. Everything about the place is defined by the line drawn beside it.

## Where the Delta Stops Being Flat

For hundreds of kilometres in every direction the Mekong Delta is a plane - rice, water, dyke, repeat, with the horizon at eye level. Then, right along the border, it isn't. The Bảy Núi, the Seven Mountains, break out of the paddy in a cluster of steep granite hills, and the tallest of them, Núi Cấm, reaches around 710 metres. That makes it the highest point in the whole delta region, which is a strange thing to say about a range you can drive past in twenty minutes. Height like this in country like this is inevitably sacred. Pilgrims climb Núi Cấm in their thousands through the fourth to seventh lunar months, up to around a thousand people a day in season, to temples set on slopes that look out over an entirely flat world. Millenarian faiths have come out of these hills repeatedly, and the range is known in that tradition as Bửu Sơn, the Precious Mountains.

## Khmer Ground

The Khmer presence here is not a minority footnote - it is the older layer. Communes such as An Cư, Tân Lợi, An Hảo, Giao Va and Vĩnh Trung have substantial Khmer populations, and their wats, with steep multi-tiered roofs and gilded finials, look nothing like the Vietnamese pagodas a few kilometres away. The Khmer Krom of the delta were here when this was Cambodian territory and remained when the frontier moved past them in the eighteenth century, which is the ordinary condition of borderland people everywhere: the line arrives, and you find yourself on a side of it. Theravada Buddhism, the Khmer calendar and the Khmer language persist in these villages alongside Vietnamese schooling and administration, in a district where the national boundary is younger than most of the temples.

## April 1978

The border has not always been a place of queues and cigarettes. During the Vietnam War a village in this district saw fighting hard enough that every member of a United States Army Special Forces detachment was wounded in a single engagement - a real battle that Robin Moore later worked into the opening chapter of his novel The Green Berets. Worse came after that war ended. Between 18 and 30 April 1978, Khmer Rouge troops crossed into Ba Chúc, a village barely fifteen kilometres from here in the same Seven Mountains, and killed 3,157 civilians. A handful of people survived, mostly by hiding in caves on the hillsides. The ossuary built at Ba Chúc in 1979 holds 1,159 sets of remains, including 1,017 skulls arranged by age and by sex, and families gather there every year on the fifteenth and sixteenth of the third lunar month. That massacre was among the events that pushed Vietnam to invade Cambodia in December 1978 and end Pol Pot's regime the following January. The people who died at Ba Chúc were farmers and their children, killed in their own hamlet, and the road that runs past the gate at Tịnh Biên goes to the country the killers came from and back again every day.

## A Name That Will Not Sit Still

Administratively, Tịnh Biên has been shuffled almost continuously. It was a district with a township at its centre from 2005, was promoted to a thị xã - a district-level town - in 2023, and had a population of 143,098 as of 2022. Then in June 2025 Vietnam abolished the district tier nationwide and folded Kiên Giang into An Giang, and the town became simply Tịnh Biên ward. Trà Sư, the flooded melaleuca forest that draws most of the area's visitors, sat within its boundaries throughout. The paperwork changes; the arrangement on the ground does not. The mountains stay where they are, the market opens at dawn, the wats keep their calendar, and the road keeps changing its number two hundred metres past the barrier.

## Flight Context

Tịnh Biên sits at 10.558°N, 105.008°E, directly on the Vietnam-Cambodia border in An Giang province. The unmistakable feature is the Seven Mountains: an isolated cluster of steep hills, Núi Cấm the highest at roughly 710 metres, standing out of an otherwise dead-flat delta and visible from a very long way off in clear air. The border itself often reads as a visible change in field pattern. Recommended altitude is 3,000-6,000 feet, high enough to see the range's full arc. Cần Thơ International (VVCT) lies about 53 nautical miles east, Rạch Giá (VVRG) about 36 nautical miles south-southeast, and Phnom Penh International (VDPP) about 60 nautical miles north. Watch for orographic cloud capping Núi Cấm during the May-October southwest monsoon and heavy agricultural haze in March and April.
