# Vĩnh Tế Canal

Eighty thousand Vietnamese and Khmer labourers cut eighty-seven kilometres of straight water across the frontier - and the canal carries the name of the woman who sat with their families.

**Location:** 10.5700, 104.9260

**Categories:** Canals in Vietnam, Nguyễn dynasty, Mekong Delta, Cambodia–Vietnam border, Forced labour, An Giang

## Introduction

A woman's name sits on eighty-seven kilometres of perfectly straight water. Emperor Minh Mạng bestowed it in the 1820s, honouring Châu Thị Tế, principal wife of the mandarin who ran the works - and the historian David Biggs has pointed out what she had been doing while her husband drove the project forward: arranging relief, and sitting with the families of men who did not come back. Roughly eighty thousand Vietnamese and Khmer labourers were put on the Vĩnh Tế Canal between 1819 and 1824. Disease and fighting took an unrecorded number of them. The trench they opened is still full, still moving boats, and still the reason the Cambodian-Vietnamese border runs where it does.

## The Cut Gia Long Ordered

Nguyễn Văn Thoại - Thoại Ngọc Hầu, born 1761 - had already proved he could move earth. In 1818 he opened the Thoại Hà, more than thirty kilometres of new canal, and the throne let him put his own name on both the waterway and a nearby mountain. Then Gia Long wanted more. Châu Đốc had no direct line to salt water, so cargo and troops bound for the Hà Tiên sea gate on the Gulf of Siam went the long way round. A canal would fix that, and it would do something else besides. Siamese and Vietnamese authority were both pressing on the Khmer country - Siem Reap, Battambang, the Cardamom Mountains, the southern coast, Hà Tiên itself - and a permanent Vietnamese waterway driven along that frontier was infrastructure and territorial claim in the same trench. Digging began at the end of 1819. Thoại held the Châu Đốc citadel, governed Hà Tiên, and from 1821 served as marshal of the Nguyễn protectorate over Cambodia.

## Five Dry Seasons

The work stopped and restarted four times before it was finished. Gia Long died in 1820, and Minh Mạng inherited a half-dug ditch that he pushed through to completion in 1824. There was nothing to dig with but baskets, hoes and human backs, and nothing to dig through but delta clay, sour acid-sulphate soil and standing water that bred fever. Levies came from the Vietnamese population and from Khmer villages across the surrounding country; nobody on the line had volunteered. Sickness did most of the killing, though the Nguyễn frontier was violent enough that fighting did the rest. The finished channel ran from Châu Đốc west-northwest to the coast near Hà Tiên, straight enough that from the air today it looks drawn rather than dug.

## The Monk Who Led the Diggers

Khmer accounts record what conditions on the works produced: rebellion, more than once. In the best-documented of these risings, several thousand Khmer labourers followed a Theravada monk into an ambush on a Vietnamese regiment - a unit that, like most on the frontier, was ethnically mixed. The Khmer soldiers inside it refused to fire on the workers. These were men who recognised the people on the other side, and who had very likely been carrying baskets themselves a season or two earlier. The refusal did not save the rising. The Nguyễn viceroy at Sài Gòn sent reinforcements, and the rebellion was crushed.

## What Cambodia Remembers

In Khmer the canal is ព្រែកយួន - plainly, the Vietnamese canal. Cambodian nationalist tradition carries a far darker account of the digging than any Vietnamese court record: that labourers who disobeyed were buried to their necks and their heads used to prop a fire for boiling tea. Historians have not been able to confirm it, and it is not the kind of atrocity that leaves paperwork. What is beyond dispute is what the story became. Passed down through generations, it hardened into the single most vivid emblem of Vietnamese treatment of the Khmer, and in the 1970s the Khmer Rouge picked it up and used it directly in anti-Vietnamese propaganda. A ditch dug in the 1820s was still being used to justify killing in the 1970s.

## Still Carrying Water

Two centuries on, the canal does the unglamorous work it was built for. It drains sour soils, feeds irrigation across some of the most productive rice country on earth, carries barge and sampan traffic, and marks a long stretch of the international boundary that the Nguyễn court was effectively drawing while it dug. Both ends changed status in the summer of 2025, when Vietnam's National Assembly collapsed sixty-three provinces into thirty-four: An Giang absorbed Kiên Giang, so Châu Đốc at one end and Hà Tiên at the other now sit inside a single province again, administered from Rạch Giá. The line between them has not moved since 1824.

## Flight Context

The canal runs roughly 87 km from Châu Đốc (10.70°N, 105.11°E) west-northwest to the coast near Hà Tiên (10.38°N, 104.49°E); the reference point here is 10.57°N, 104.93°E, near the midpoint. From 3,000-6,000 ft it is one of the most legible man-made features in the Mekong Delta: a dead-straight silver line across flat green paddy, running parallel to and just south of the Cambodian border, with a road and near-continuous ribbon of houses along its Vietnamese bank. The isolated peaks of the Bảy Núi range rise to the southeast of Châu Đốc and give a hard visual fix in otherwise featureless terrain that sits barely above sea level. Nearest fields: Rạch Giá (VVRG) about 35 nm south-southwest, Phú Quốc International (VVPQ) about 50 nm west over the Gulf of Thailand, and Cần Thơ International (VVCT) about 55 nm southeast. December through March gives the best visibility and the lowest water; the June-October monsoon brings convective cloud and flooding that spreads sheet water across the fields on both banks, which paradoxically makes the canal's raised embankments easier to trace.
