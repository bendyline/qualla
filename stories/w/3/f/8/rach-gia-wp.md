# Rạch Giá

The first place in Vietnam to manufacture its own land out of the sea, Rạch Giá became a provincial capital in the same year it stopped being a city.

**Location:** 10.0125, 105.0808

**Categories:** Vietnam, Mekong Delta, Cities, Gulf of Thailand, Land reclamation

## Introduction

Most cities grow by swallowing their suburbs. Rạch Giá grew by pouring fill into the Gulf of Thailand. It was the first place in Vietnam where the government attempted lấn biển — encroaching on the sea — dredging and filling the shallows off its western shore to manufacture ground where there had been water, and the new ground was substantial enough to become two entire wards. Look at the coastline on a satellite image and the seam is obvious: an older, ragged shore of river mouths and fishing quays, and then a clean geometric shelf pushed out into the gulf, streets laid across it in a grid that no river ever made.

## The Coast That Faces the Wrong Way

Almost all of Vietnam looks east. Rạch Giá does not. It sits on the eastern shore of the Gulf of Thailand, 250 km southwest of Ho Chi Minh City, on the far side of the Cà Mau peninsula from the South China Sea — which means the sun goes down over open water here, a thing most Vietnamese coastal towns never see. The climate is tropical savanna, Köppen Aw, though only just; it is wetter than most climates classified that way, and the rain arrives with real conviction. December through March is emphatically dry. The remaining eight months are consistently, heavily wet, and the water table does not so much rise as reassert itself across a landscape that is barely above sea level to begin with.

## Ground Made to Order

The reclamation project changed the city's shape and its ambitions at once. Two new wards appeared on land that had not existed, and Rạch Giá became one of the largest new urban areas in southwestern Vietnam. In 2005 it was formally upgraded from town to city by Government Decree 97/2005/NĐ-CP, signed on 26 July, and it settled into a structure of twelve administrative units — Vĩnh Thanh Vân, Vĩnh Thanh, Vĩnh Lạc, Vĩnh Lợi, Vĩnh Quang, An Hòa, An Bình, Rạch Sỏi, Vĩnh Thông, Vĩnh Hiệp and Phi Thông among them. Further growth was planned inland instead: an industrial zone at Rạch Vượt, a new urban district at Vĩnh Hiệp, both waiting on the completion of the Lạc Hồng Bridge to open the city's eastern flank.

## Boats, Buses, and a French Runway

Rạch Giá is a transfer point, and the infrastructure shows it. Two bus stations divide the work: Rạch Sỏi handles the local province and the highways 61 and 63, while Rạch Giá station sends coaches to Ho Chi Minh City and up the coast to Hà Tiên. Two ferry terminals divide the water. The Phú Quốc station runs fast boats out to Phú Quốc, Hòn Tre, Hòn Sơn and distant Thổ Chu; Rạch Mẽo serves the small towns of the Cà Mau peninsula, where the roads are an afterthought and the canals are the real network. And there is the airport — VVRG, built by the French in the 1950s to link Saigon to the southwest, still working off a single 1,500-metre asphalt strip barely two metres above the sea, with turboprops running to Ho Chi Minh City.

## Temples on Every Other Corner

For a city that keeps rebuilding itself, Rạch Giá is unusually crowded with the past. Eight sites here carry national historical designation, among them the Tam Bảo and Vĩnh Hoà temples, the Phật Lớn and Láng Cát pagodas, Quan Đế pagoda, the Kiên Giang museum, and the grave of the nineteenth-century poet Huỳnh Mẫn Đạt. Beyond those are the Bắc Đế and Thiên Hậu temples, the latter honouring the sea goddess who watches over Chinese sailors across all of maritime Asia — an unsurprising presence in a fishing port. The symbol of the city, though, is a gateway: the old tam quan, a three-entrance gate of the kind that fronts Vietnamese temples, which the city has adopted as its own emblem.

## Capital and Non-City, Same Year

On 12 June 2025 the National Assembly passed Resolution 202/2025/QH15, merging Kiên Giang province into An Giang and producing a single province of 9,888 square kilometres and nearly five million people. Rạch Giá became its capital. Weeks later, on 1 July, the nationwide elimination of district-level administration took effect and Rạch Giá ceased to exist as a municipal city — its status downgraded to a ward at the same moment its importance was upgraded to provincial seat. There is something fitting in it. A place that made its own land out of the gulf was never going to be defined by whatever the paperwork said it was.

## Flight Context

Rạch Giá lies at 10.013°N, 105.081°E on the Gulf of Thailand. Rạch Giá Airport (VVRG / VKG) sits just south of the centre with a single 1,500 m runway at an elevation of 7 ft — short, low and surrounded by rice, so expect a visual approach and pay attention to the sea breeze. From 3,000–5,000 ft the reclaimed western wards read clearly as a rectangular platform pushed into the gulf against the older organic shoreline. Phú Quốc International (VVPQ) is about 110 km west-northwest across the water. Best light is late afternoon over the gulf; the December–March dry season gives the cleanest visibility, while the long wet season brings afternoon convection and low ceilings.
