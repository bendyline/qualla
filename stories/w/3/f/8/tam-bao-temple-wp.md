# Tam Bảo Temple

A Rạch Giá pagoda where an orphanage, a free clinic and a hidden cache of resistance leaflets all shared the same courtyard.

**Location:** 10.0078, 105.0842

**Categories:** Buddhist temples, Buddhist temples in Vietnam, Religious sites, Colonial history, Vietnam, Mekong Delta

## Introduction

Thích Trí Thiền was fifty-nine when the French shipped him to Côn Đảo, the island penal colony where colonial Indochina sent the people it could not otherwise silence. For the previous decade he had been running an orphanage, a free clinic and literacy classes out of a Buddhist temple in Rạch Giá, on the flat shore of the Gulf of Thailand. He had also — as the colonial police correctly suspected — let that same temple hide documents, leaflets and weapons for the Vietnamese resistance. He died in prison in 1943, after a hunger strike. Tam Bảo Temple, the building he rebuilt and then gambled, is still standing: ten thousand square meters of courtyard, lotus pond and gilded woodwork, a few streets back from the water.

## The Widow's Hermitage

It began as almost nothing. In the late eighteenth century a woman named Dương Thị Oán raised a modest hermitage at Vĩnh Thanh Vân village, on a patch of ground high enough and airy enough to stay clear of the delta's floods. Then history walked in the gate. In 1789 the fugitive prince Nguyễn Ánh, running from the Tây Sơn armies that had swept his family out of southern Vietnam, sheltered here and left resupplied. Thirteen years later he was Emperor Gia Long, ruler of a reunified country, and he settled the debt in the only currency emperors carried. In 1802 the hermitage received an imperial title: Sắc Tứ Tam Bảo, Tam Bảo by decree. People in Rạch Giá also call it Chùa Ông Đồng. The building still faces east, and the sea wind still finds it.

## Two Dragons and a Moon

Trí Thiền took over as abbot in 1913 and spent the next four years rebuilding what he had inherited, giving the temple the shape a visitor sees today. The main hall uses the classic upper-floor, lower-veranda arrangement, so light comes in sideways and low. Its altars are carved and gilded with the motifs a Vietnamese woodcarver knows by heart — Lưỡng Long triều Nguyệt, two dragons turning toward the moon; Song Phụng Triều Châu, a pair of phoenixes facing a boat; the Bát Tiên, the Eight Immortals. Wooden figures of Di Lặc, Chuẩn Đề, Địa Tạng, Văn Thù, Phổ Hiền and Quan Âm stand among them, worked with a precision that survives close inspection. Out front there is a lotus pond and a rock garden, and on a hot afternoon the whole compound runs several degrees cooler than the street.

## Kiêm Tế

In the 1930s Vietnamese Buddhism went through a self-examination its adherents called chấn hưng — revival, or more literally, a shaking awake. Monks argued that a religion of ritual alone had nothing to offer a colonized, illiterate, hungry countryside. Trí Thiền agreed, and in 1936 he and the reform-minded monk Thiện Chiếu founded the Kiêm Tế Buddhist Studies Association at Tam Bảo; official approval followed in 1937. What they built was not a monastery in the retiring sense. The temple ran what is credited as Vietnam's first Western-style Buddhist orphanage, along with free clinics, literacy classes, care for the elderly and poverty relief, and from 1938 it published a magazine, Tiến Hóa, that pushed all of this into print. Charity and argument came out of the same doorway.

## The Raid

The line between social work and sedition was never going to hold, and both sides knew it. By the early 1940s the temple was hosting secret meetings of the Nam Kỳ Provincial Committee and storing weapons and printed material behind its altars. On 14 June 1941 the French raided it. Trí Thiền was arrested along with the monk Thiện Ân and sent to Côn Đảo, where he died two years later; Thiện Ân was executed. They were not soldiers, and their deaths bought no territory. What they had done was decide that a temple ought to feed and teach the people around it, and then refuse to stop when that became dangerous. In 1996 the Vietnamese state formally recognized both men as martyrs — a bureaucratic act, half a century late, for two men who had already made their decision without it.

## Return to the Source

On 22 March 1988 Tam Bảo was designated a national historical and cultural relic, and it remains a working temple rather than a museum: monastic retreats, chanting, the ordinary traffic of a neighborhood pagoda. It also houses the provincial Buddhist association's offices, though the province itself has changed underneath it — Kiên Giang was folded into An Giang in June 2025. Vietnamese travelers come here under the banner of du lịch về nguồn, return-to-origins tourism, which sounds like a slogan until you stand in the courtyard and realize the orphanage, the clinic and the weapons cache were all this same square of ground.

## Flight Context

Tam Bảo Temple sits at 10.008°N, 105.084°E in central Rạch Giá, on Vietnam's Gulf of Thailand coast. Rạch Giá Airport (VVRG/VKG) lies roughly 6 km west-northwest with a single 1,500 m runway; Cần Thơ International (VVCT) is about 100 km east and Phú Quốc International (VVPQ) about 115 km west across the gulf. From 2,000-3,000 ft the temple is hard to pick out individually — look instead for the distinctive reclaimed-land grid of Rạch Giá's western wards pushing out into the gulf, and follow the Cái Lớn and Rạch Giá-Long Xuyên canals inland. Haze from delta agriculture commonly cuts visibility in the March-April dry season; the clearest air usually comes just after rainy-season storms, from May through November.
