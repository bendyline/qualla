# Phnom Chisor

An eleventh-century Khmer temple called the Mountain of the Sun, reached by six hundred unshaded steps, looking out over the delta where Cambodian civilisation began.

**Location:** 11.1844, 104.8233

**Categories:** Hindu temples, Khmer Empire, Cambodia, Historical sites, Mountains

## Introduction

There are roughly 610 steps, they climb nearly 400 metres of hillside, and there is no shade on any of them. That is the first fact about Phnom Chisor and the one every visitor remembers. The hill is only 133 metres high — modest anywhere with mountains — but this is the Mekong lowland, where the land runs flat to the horizon in all directions, and 133 metres is enough to make a landmark visible for tens of kilometres. At the top sits a sandstone and laterite temple a thousand years old, still in use, with a view that explains exactly why a king put it here.

## Mountain of the Sun

The Khmer called it Sri Suryaparvata — Mountain of the Sun. Suryavarman I built it in the eleventh century, and the name is not modest: surya is the Sanskrit word for the sun and for the sun god, and it is also the first half of the king's own name. The temple runs about 60 metres by 50, oriented east, with two concentric galleries; the outer measures roughly 60 metres on a side, the inner is smaller, and the central gallery held the principal sanctuary. Around them stand brick towers, secondary shrines and a pair of libraries inside laterite walls. It was dedicated to Shiva, with a share given to Vishnu. Weather and a millennium have taken the roofs and softened the carving, but the plan is completely legible, and the doorways still frame the sky in the direction the builders chose.

## The King Who Fought His Way In

Suryavarman I ruled from 1002 to 1050, and he spent the first eight of those years securing a throne he had seized. He defeated Udayadityavarman I around 1002 and then contended with a rival named Jayaviravarman until 1010 before his hold was complete. What followed was one of the longer and steadier reigns in Khmer history. He pushed the empire west to Lopburi and the Menam basin, opened relations with the Cholas of southern India around 1012 and sent a chariot as a gift to the emperor Rajendra I, summoned some four thousand local officials to swear allegiance in person, and earned a reputation as a lawgiver. He built Preah Vihear, finished Phimeanakas, and began the great West Baray reservoir. And here is the wrinkle: the builder of this Shiva temple was himself a Mahayana Buddhist, given the posthumous name Nirvanapada, who commissioned Hindu sanctuaries for a largely Hindu population and tolerated the Theravada teaching then spreading through his kingdom. Kings of Angkor were rarely tidy about religion.

## Facing the Old Country

Stand at the eastern gate and the ground drops away toward the delta. That direction is not incidental. Below and east lies the country of Funan and of Chenla after it — Angkor Borei, Phnom Da, the canal-cut plain where the earliest Khmer states took shape five hundred years before Angkor existed. Suryavarman's own dynastic claims reached back through those older kingdoms, and historians read the placement of Phnom Chisor as a political statement as much as a religious one: a new king building high ground in sight of the ancestral heartland, announcing continuity to a population that had every reason to doubt it. A sacred pool called Tonle Om lies below on that side, still venerated. Whether or not the political reading is right, the effect from the top is unarguable. You can see what he was claiming.

## Broken and Rebuilt and Broken

The pagoda on the summit was rebuilt in 1917, under French Indochina, and destroyed again in the 1970s during the war that consumed Cambodia. It was rebuilt once more in 1979 — the year the Khmer Rouge regime fell, when a country that had just lost well over a million of its people began putting its religious life back together from almost nothing. Monks had been defrocked, murdered or driven into the fields; pagodas had been emptied, converted to warehouses or knocked down. That people who had just come through that chose, in that year, to carry materials up six hundred steps and rebuild a hilltop temple is not a small detail. It is arguably the most important thing about Phnom Chisor that is not a thousand years old.

## Who Comes Up the Hill Now

The site is Buddhist in daily practice today, though it was built for Shiva and the Brahman tradition has not entirely left it — Tonle Om below still matters to Brahman worshippers, and the two devotions coexist on the same hill without much fuss. On festival days the staircase fills with families, and children run the steps that visitors labour up. Cambodia's Ministry of Culture and Fine Arts has been preparing documentation to nominate Phnom Chisor for UNESCO World Heritage listing; it is not inscribed, and restoration work continues in the meantime. Go early. The light is best before eight, the stone has not yet started radiating heat, and from the top the paddy below is still holding its mist — the whole delta laid out flat and silver, exactly as it was for the king who chose the view.

## Flight Context

Phnom Chisor stands at 11.18°N, 104.82°E in Samraŏng District, Takéo province, Cambodia — a 133 m isolated hill rising abruptly from a plain that is essentially at sea level, making it a genuine visual landmark rather than terrain. It lies 42 km south of Phnom Penh and 27 km north of Doun Kaev by road, close to the line of National Highway 2. From 2,500–4,000 ft the summit temple, the long pale line of the staircase up the hillside, and the pool of Tonle Om on the eastern side are all resolvable; the hill throws a long shadow at low sun angles and is easiest to spot in early morning or late afternoon. Nearest airports are Techo International (VDTI), 19 km south of Phnom Penh and roughly 23 km north of the hill, and Phnom Penh International (VDPP). Dry season from December to March gives the cleanest air; during the May–October southwest monsoon expect afternoon build-ups, isolated hills obscured by cloud base, and widespread agricultural and cooking-fire haze that reduces slant visibility even on rain-free days.
