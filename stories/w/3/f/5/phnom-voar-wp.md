# Phnom Voar

A 284-metre range in southern Cambodia that was bombed by B-52s, used for Khmer Rouge prisons and killing fields, and held out as one of the regime's last strongholds until 1994.

**Location:** 10.4833, 104.3000

**Categories:** Geography of Kep province, Mountains of Cambodia, Cambodian Civil War

## Introduction

The train left Phnom Penh on the morning of 26 July 1994, bound for the coast. Where the line runs along the foot of Phnom Voar - a low range of hills in what is now Kep province, though in 1994 this was still Kampot - Khmer Rouge fighters stopped it. At least eight people died in the attack itself: three government soldiers, one civilian passenger, and at least four of the attackers, though accounts of the toll differ and some put the Cambodian dead higher. More than a hundred passengers were taken off the track and marched up into the hills. Three of them were Western backpackers, which is why the world heard about it. The rest were Cambodian and Vietnamese, which is largely why it did not.

## A Hill That Should Not Have Mattered

Phnom Voar is not much of a mountain. Its highest point reaches 284 metres, and the range sits just inland from the coast in Damnak Chang'aeur district, close to the Kampot border. What made it valuable was not height but texture. The main north-south railway between Phnom Penh and Sihanoukville runs directly along its base and National Road 33 passes nearby, which means that anyone holding these hills sits on top of the country's main southern artery. Inside the range, though, there is almost nothing - few roads, few paths, and only two permanent communities, the villages of Chamkar Bei and Rones. Broken ground, thick cover, and a supply line running past the front door: for four decades this was one of the most militarily useful pieces of terrain in southern Cambodia, and everyone who wanted it knew why.

## Bombed, Then Occupied

During the Cambodian Civil War the range served as a base for communist insurgents, and it was hit accordingly - B-52 strikes and other air attacks, part of the American bombing campaign that left large areas of eastern and southern Cambodia cratered and seeded with unexploded ordnance. The people of Chamkar Bei and Rones lived under that. Then, between 1975 and 1979, the Khmer Rouge held the area outright and made it worse: the hills around Phnom Voar became the site of several killing fields and two prisons run by the Santebal, the secret police of Democratic Kampuchea. The men and women brought here were not enemy soldiers. They were, as almost everywhere in Cambodia in those years, ordinary people the regime had decided it no longer needed.

## The Last Redoubt

When Vietnamese forces drove the Khmer Rouge from power in 1979, the regime did not disappear; it retreated into terrain like this. Phnom Voar became one of the southern rebel bases for the remaining Democratic Kampuchea forces, and it stayed one for fifteen years. That is the context for what happened on the railway line in 1994. This was not a random attack on a passenger train. It was a functioning insurgent stronghold, a few hours from Phnom Penh, still doing what it had always done, in a country the outside world had already decided was at peace.

## Three Months in the Hills

Of the hostages taken from the train, at least two Cambodians, three Vietnamese and three Westerners were killed. The three foreigners were David Wilson, an Australian of 29; Mark Slater, a Briton of 28; and Jean-Michel Braquet, a Frenchman of 27 - young men travelling through Southeast Asia who had bought tickets on a train. They were held in the hills for roughly three months while ransom demands went back and forth between the guerrillas, their embassies and the Cambodian government. When the negotiations failed, they were killed. The exact date is still unclear; some accounts put it as early as September 1994, others after 15 October. Their bodies were found on 2 November 1994. The Cambodian and Vietnamese hostages who died alongside them have never received the same attention, and in most retellings of this story they are a clause rather than a list of names - which is its own quiet injustice, given that they were the majority of the dead.

## Verdicts, and What Came After

Two commanders eventually answered for it. Sam Bith, a deputy to Ta Mok who led the ambush, defected to the Royal Cambodian Army in 1996 and was made a general before being arrested in 2002; convicted and sentenced to life, he died in prison in 2008 at 74. Chhouk Rin, the officer who commanded the unit that held and killed the three Westerners, was convicted of their murders, escaped custody in 2005, was recaptured, lost his appeal, and is serving life in Prey Sar prison. The last rebel forces in the range were captured or surrendered in 1994, and afterwards the aid organisations arrived - UNESCO from 1998 onward, working on health, schooling and livelihoods for villagers who had spent a generation cut off from the reconstruction happening everywhere else. Unexploded ordnance is still being surveyed in these hills. It is the bombing's last instalment, still being paid by people who were not born when it fell.

## Flight Context

Phnom Voar lies at 10.483°N, 104.30°E in Kep province, southern Cambodia, just inland from the Gulf of Thailand coast at Kep. The high point is 284 m (932 ft), so it reads as a compact, steep-sided forested range standing above flat rice country - distinctive from the air precisely because everything around it is flat. Maintain safe terrain clearance and expect mechanical turbulence and orographic cloud on the windward side during the southwest monsoon, May to October. Good visual references: the Phnom Penh-Sihanoukville railway running along the southern base of the range, National Road 33 between Kampot and Kampong Trach, and the Kep coast and archipelago to the south. Bokor plateau rises to the west. Nearest airports are Sihanouk International (VDSV) about 40 nm west, Phnom Penh International (VDPP) about 71 nm north, and Phú Quốc International (VVPQ) in Vietnam about 27 nm south. Kampot has a 579 m grass strip (KMT) 23 km northwest of Kampot town.
