# Kampot province

A province of pepper vines, salt pans and a mountain whose abandoned French resort cost nine hundred lives to build.

**Location:** 10.6000, 104.1667

**Categories:** Provinces of Cambodia, Kampot province, Gulf of Thailand, National parks, Agriculture

## Introduction

Nine hundred people died in nine months building a place for other people to be comfortable. That is the figure historians cite for the construction of Bokor Hill Station, the resort that colonial France raised in the early 1920s on a plateau above Kampot so that its officials and soldiers could escape the heat of the plain. The labourers who cut the road up the escarpment and hauled the materials to 1,000 metres are not named anywhere. The hotel they finished is still there, roofless in places, orange with lichen, one of the most photographed ruins in Cambodia — and almost nobody who photographs it knows the number.

## The Station in the Clouds

The centrepiece was the Bokor Palace Hotel, inaugurated in 1925, with the Résident Supérieur's villa, a post office and a small Catholic church around it. King Sisowath Monivong had a Buddhist temple built on the mountain in 1924 and died up there in 1941; the national park now carries his name. The French abandoned Bokor in the late 1940s when the Khmer Issarak insurgency made the road unsafe, then came back for a second act in 1962, opening a casino in new hotels near the lake. That lasted a decade. The Khmer Rouge took the plateau in 1972 and held it with remarkable stubbornness — they dug in against the Vietnamese invasion of 1979 and were still using Bokor as one of their last strongholds in the early 1990s. A luxury resort opened on the mountain in 2012, and a 29-metre statue of Lok Yeay Mao, guardian spirit of travellers, was erected in 2010. The old hotel stands empty beside them.

## Which Side of the Mountains

Geography does most of the work in Kampot. The Dâmrei — the Elephant Mountains — run along the province's western edge, catching the southwest monsoon head-on and wringing it out, which leaves the eastern districts in a comparatively dry rain shadow. Phnom Bokor, the high point at 1,081 metres, sits inside Preah Monivong Bokor National Park, a reserve of about 1,545 square kilometres established in 1993 and named an ASEAN Heritage Park in 2003. Asian elephants, gibbons, gaur, dholes and Asian black bears live in it, and green peafowl still call from the forest edges. It is not a settled peace: roughly nine per cent of the park's primary forest was lost between 2001 and 2019, and a government plan drawn up in 2019 zones nearly 19,000 hectares of it for urban and tourism development through 2035. South of the mountains, the province runs out into about 45 kilometres of coastline on the Gulf of Thailand.

## A Vine With a Long Memory

Pepper has been growing here for at least seven centuries — the Chinese envoy Zhou Daguan noted its cultivation during the Angkorian era. The French made it an industry from the 1870s, and by the early twentieth century Cambodia was harvesting something like 8,000 tonnes a year. In the 1960s a million pepper poles still stood, yielding around 3,000 tonnes. Then the vine's history and the country's became the same history. By the 1990s, national production had fallen to four tonnes a year. Four. Growers rebuilt from almost nothing through the 2000s, and Kampot pepper became the first Cambodian product to win geographical-indication protection, in 2010, with EU recognition following in 2016. Output reached 58 tonnes on 25 hectares in 2014 and about 80 tonnes by 2020, grown across seven districts of Kampot and Kep in two varieties, small-leaf kamchay and big-leaf lampong. Green, black, white and red pepper all come off the same plants; the colour is a matter of when you pick and how you dry.

## The Years Nobody Counted Properly

The collapse of the pepper harvest is the smallest measure of what happened here between 1975 and 1979. Across Kampot province, a reported 90,450 people were killed — farmers, teachers, monks, children, whole families emptied out of villages that still exist on the map. Ta Mok, the Khmer Rouge commander known for the thoroughness of his purges, is held responsible for 30,000 deaths in Angkor Chey district alone. These numbers are estimates assembled after the fact from mass graves and survivor testimony, and they are almost certainly incomplete. What they describe is not an abstraction: in a province that today holds fewer than 700,000 people, nearly everyone over fifty is a survivor, and nearly every family lost someone whose absence still sets the shape of the table.

## Counting Again

The province recorded 682,987 residents in 2024, spread across seven districts and two municipalities, 93 communes and 488 villages. Around 96.9 per cent are Theravada Buddhists. The Cham, a Muslim minority who have lived in Cambodia for centuries and were targeted with particular ferocity under the Khmer Rouge, make up about 2.8 per cent, and there is a small Christian population. Rice, durian, rubber and salt come off the lowlands; the forest covers a little over 227,000 hectares. It is an ordinary working province again. Given the century it came through, that is not a small achievement.

## Flight Context

Kampot province occupies southwestern Cambodia between roughly 10.4°N and 11.0°N, 103.9°E and 104.6°E, with the provincial centre at 10.60°N, 104.17°E. The navigational anchor is the Bokor plateau: a flat-topped massif reaching 1,081 m that ends in an abrupt south-facing escarpment above a coastal plain barely above sea level — from the air the contrast is stark, dark forest above and a pale patchwork of paddy and salt pans below. The Gulf of Thailand coastline runs about 45 km, with Kep and Rabbit Island (Koh Tonsay) to the east and the karst towers of Kampong Trach inland from them, rising like islands out of the rice. Best viewing altitude is 6,000-10,000 ft to hold the mountains and coast in a single frame. Nearest airports: Sihanouk International (VDSV) roughly 100 km west, Techo International (VDTI) south of Phnom Penh roughly 140 km north. The plateau generates its own weather — orographic cloud and heavy rain on the windward slopes from May to October, while the eastern districts stay comparatively clear.
