# Baladityapura

A seventh-century Khmer capital known almost entirely from a scholarly argument — the vanished city of Water Chenla, somewhere in the lower Mekong, between the fall of Funan and the founding of Angkor.

**Location:** 10.9950, 104.9747

**Categories:** Archaeology, Ancient cities, Cambodia, Khmer Empire, Historical sites

## Introduction

Some ruins you can walk around. This one you can only read about, and even then the reading is contested. Baladityapura — also called Aninditapura — appears in the historical record as a capital city of the lower Mekong, founded in the seventh century, argued over by the two greatest European scholars of ancient Cambodia, and never conclusively located on the ground. It is placed by convention in the flat, canal-cut country around Angkor Borei in Takéo province, where the plain floods every year and the archaeology sits under metres of silt. Whether that is where it stood, nobody can currently prove.

## A Name Before a Place

The sources give a founder, a patron and a general location, and then stop. A figure called Baladitya established the city during the reign of Isanavarman I, the Chenla king who ruled from 616 to 637 and built the great brick sanctuaries at Sambor Prei Kuk. Baladityapura is described as standing near Vyadhapura — the old Funan capital, 'city of the hunter king', usually placed at Ba Phnom in Prey Veng — but on the opposite bank of the Mekong. That single directional phrase is most of what survives about its geography. It carries a second name too, Aninditapura, and the two are generally treated as the same settlement, which is itself an inference rather than a certainty. Ancient Khmer cities were built of wood and thatch around a stone or brick core; when the core is a temple that later kings dismantled for materials, the city goes with it.

## Pelliot Against Coedès

In 707 CE, according to the Tang histories, the kingdom the Chinese called Zhēnlà split in two: Lùzhēnlà, Land Chenla, in the northern uplands, and Shuǐzhēnlà, Water Chenla, across the delta and the lower river. That much is agreed. Which cities ran them is not. Paul Pelliot, the formidable French sinologist, read the Chinese records as putting Sambhupura at the head of the northern realm and Vyadhapura at the head of the southern one. George Coedès, who did more than anyone to reconstruct the political history of Southeast Asia from Sanskrit inscriptions, read them differently: Baladityapura was the seat of Water Chenla, and Land Chenla answered to a polity the Chinese called Wen Dan. Both men were working from fragmentary Chinese court records and weathered stone inscriptions, and both were reasoning across a linguistic gap of a thousand years. The disagreement has never been settled, which is why Baladityapura's importance is stated twice in the literature, in incompatible forms.

## The Country It Would Have Governed

Whatever its exact site, the world Baladityapura belonged to is not in doubt. This delta was the engine room of the first Southeast Asian kingdom. Funan, running from roughly the first to the sixth century CE, grew rich on the sea route between India and China, and its people cut canals across the flat land the way later kingdoms cut roads. One of them runs sixty-eight kilometres from Angkor Borei southeast to the port site of Óc Eo in present-day An Giang province, Vietnam — a single engineered line across the floodplain, still legible from the air. At Óc Eo, French archaeologist Louis Malleret's excavations from 1942 turned up Roman medallions struck under Antoninus Pius. Goods from the Mediterranean, in the mud of the Mekong, in the second century. Chenla absorbed this world rather than replacing it, and its southern capital inherited both the wealth and the water.

## The Sea Takes an Interest

Water Chenla's location was its fortune and then its problem. Through the eighth century the maritime powers of the archipelago — the Shailendras of Java, and Srivijaya — pressed hard on the delta, and around 790 the last king of Water Chenla was, by most accounts, killed. What followed is the founding legend of Angkor. Jayavarman II, born around 770 and by several sources held in Java as a captive or exile in his youth, came back to the Mekong and took the country piece by piece: Vyadhapura in the southeast first, then north up the river to Sambhupura, where he married the reigning queen Jayendrabhā, then to Banteay Prei Nokor, and finally to the Kulen hills. In 802 he was consecrated on Mahendraparvata as universal monarch. The Khmer Empire dates from that ceremony. The lower delta, where Baladityapura had stood, became a province.

## The Missing Hinge

Cambodia's monumental history is told in stone, and stone survives where it was quarried and set — at Angkor, at Sambor Prei Kuk, on the sandstone ridges of the north. The delta had no quarries and no dry ground. Its cities were earth, brick and timber on mounds above the flood, and the flood has been depositing silt on them for twelve centuries. So the period that matters most for understanding how Funan became Angkor is precisely the period that left the least to dig. Baladityapura is the shape of that gap: a capital with a founder, a patron king, a rival theory and no address. Archaeologists working the Angkor Borei mounds since the 1990s keep turning up the outlines of a dense, organised society in exactly the right place at roughly the right time. Somewhere under the rice, a city is waiting to be argued about with better evidence.

## Flight Context

Baladityapura is conventionally plotted at 11.00°N, 104.97°E, in the Angkor Borei area of Takéo province, Cambodia — flat delta country only a few metres above sea level, roughly 21 km east of Doun Kaev and about 62 km due south of Phnom Penh. There is nothing standing to see from the air, but the landscape itself is the artefact: from 2,000–4,000 ft in dry-season light the ancient canal alignments, mounded settlement platforms and the ring of the Angkor Borei enclosure read as straight lines and low rectangles cutting across the organic curves of the modern paddy. The isolated granite outcrop of Phnom Da sits about 3 km southeast of Angkor Borei and is the best visual fix in the area; the Bassac River lies to the east and the Vietnamese border a short distance south. Nearest airports are Techo International (VDTI) and Phnom Penh International (VDPP) to the north. Fly it between January and March — the dry season both grounds the water and sharpens the crop marks; from June to October the whole plain is under sheet flood and haze cuts slant visibility badly.
