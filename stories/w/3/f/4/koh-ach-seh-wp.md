# Koh Ach Seh

A small island in the Kep Archipelago where an old Khmer Rouge outpost became the base for Cambodia's most stubborn experiment in marine protection.

**Location:** 10.3583, 104.3200

**Categories:** Islands of Cambodia, Marine conservation, Kep province

## Introduction

The strangest thing on the seabed around this island is not a wreck or a reef. It is a hexagon. Concrete, heavy, deliberately ugly, one of 150 blocks lowered into the shallows off Koh Ach Seh with a very specific job: catch the net of any trawler that drags across them and rip it open. Illegal trawling had been scraping the Kep Archipelago flat for years, and the people working from this island decided that argument had run its course. The blocks do not negotiate. They also, over time, grow bivalves, sponges and algae, so a device designed to destroy fishing gear has quietly become a reef.

## Bunkers Under the Fruit Trees

Koh Ach Seh - Khmer កោះអាចម៍សេះ, generally shortened by everyone to Koh Seh, Horse Island - sits in the Gulf of Thailand off Cambodia's southern coast, part of the Kep Archipelago and administered by Kep province. Its recent history is written into the vegetation. During the Cambodian-Vietnamese War the Khmer Rouge held the island as an outpost, and the military bunkers they dug are still there, along with the fruit trees they planted to feed the garrison. Someone stood on this rock four decades ago working out where an orchard should go and how long a small garrison could feed itself off it. Those calculations belonged to a regime that had emptied Cambodia's cities and killed something close to two million of its people, and the fishing families of this coast were among those who paid. The trees kept growing anyway. They are about the most benign thing the Khmer Rouge left behind anywhere in Cambodia, and they still fruit.

## A New Tenant

Since 2013 the island has been home to Marine Conservation Cambodia, the country's first permanent marine conservation organisation, founded in 2008 by the British conservationist Paul Ferber and originally based far to the west on Koh Rong Samloem. The Kep government lent MCC most of Koh Ach Seh, keeping only the southern tip, where a small marine police station and a cluster of fishermen's shelters share the shoreline. It is an odd, productive arrangement - researchers at one end of a former military island, the state's own patrol boats at the other, both facing the same problem out in the same water.

## Counting Seahorses

The work that put the island on the scientific map began with an animal almost nobody had bothered to count. MCC identified four seahorse species living in Cambodian waters and became the first organisation in the country to study them seriously, working with Project Seahorse and contributing to the case that saw the species written into CITES protection. Seahorses are a good indicator and a poor survivor: they hold station in seagrass, they do not flee well, and a trawl net takes everything. Protecting them means protecting the meadows they live in, which happen to be the nursery grounds for most of what Kep's fishermen actually catch. The argument for conservation here has never needed to be sentimental. It is arithmetic.

## Eleven Thousand Hectares

In April 2018 Cambodia approved the Kep Archipelago Marine Fisheries Management Area - 11,354 hectares of protected water spanning nine islands, with two no-take zones inside it. MCC helped build the case for it and now manages it. Two months later the concrete anti-trawling blocks won the organisation one of the first three Marine Protection Prizes ever awarded by the National Geographic Society. In April 2019 the archipelago was declared a Hope Spot by Mission Blue, Sylvia Earle's global network of marine sites judged critical to the ocean's recovery. For a country whose coastline spent decades as a war zone and then as an open-access free-for-all, that is a remarkable turn in the space of six years, driven from a single island most charts do not name.

## Not That Koh Seh

One caution for anyone navigating by name: there is another Koh Seh, sitting inside Ream National Park off Sihanoukville province, and the two are confused constantly. The Kep one is the island with the bunkers, the blocks and the seahorse counts. Look for it south of the Kep peninsula, low and green, sitting in shallow water not far from the Vietnamese maritime boundary - close enough that fishing disputes in this archipelago are never only about fish.

## Flight Context

Koh Ach Seh lies at 10.358°N, 104.320°E in the Gulf of Thailand, roughly 14 km south of the Kep peninsula in southern Cambodia. It reads well from 1,500-3,000 ft: a small low island, forested, with buildings and a jetty on the northern half and the marine police station at the southern tip. In calm, clear conditions the seagrass beds and the pale shallows of the surrounding fisheries management area are visible from the air as darker and lighter patches. Kep town and the White Horse Monument sit to the northwest; the Vietnamese maritime boundary and the Hà Tiên Islands lie to the east and southeast. Nearest airports are Sihanouk International (VDSV), about 42 nm west-northwest; Phú Quốc International (VVPQ) in Vietnam, about 22 nm southwest; and Phnom Penh International (VDPP), about 78 nm north. Kampot has a short grass strip (KMT) 23 km northwest of Kampot town. Expect haze, low cloud and afternoon convection during the southwest monsoon, May to October.
