# Côn Đảo Prison

For 113 years the French and then South Vietnamese governments used an island prison to hold people whose crime was opposition — until an American delegation walked off the official tour in July 1970.

**Location:** 8.6889, 106.6148

**Categories:** prison, vietnam, colonial-history, vietnam-war, human-rights

## Introduction

The guard heard voices in the corridor and opened the door himself. It was July 1970, and the American visitors standing outside were not where the itinerary said they should be. They had walked away from the official tour of Côn Sơn prison holding a hand-drawn map made by a man who had once been locked in this building, and the map had brought them here. On the far side of the door was a raised walkway, and below it, in stone pits, were people — shackled, some covered in sores, some mutilated by their treatment — who began calling up for water as soon as the strangers appeared. Tom Harkin, then a congressional aide, had a camera with him. His photographs ran in Life magazine on 17 July 1970, and a Vietnamese phrase most Americans had never heard, chuồng cọp, tiger cage, entered the vocabulary of the war.

## Built for the Distance

France took the Côn Đảo islands by force in 1861 and established the penal settlement the following year, choosing the site for precisely the reason travellers now praise it: nothing else is anywhere near. A prisoner sent here was removed not merely from society but from the possibility of contact with it. After the turn of the century the population tilted steadily from ordinary convicts toward political prisoners, and when France withdrew from Indochina in 1954 the Republic of Vietnam took the keys and carried on with the same intent, holding Việt Minh cadres, National Liberation Front fighters and civilians accused of sympathy. The camps were freed at midnight on 30 April 1975, in the hours between the fall of Saigon and the dawn of 1 May — 113 years without an interruption of purpose. Vietnam now ranks the site among its two dozen special national relics.

## The School Nobody Chose

An extraordinary share of twentieth-century Vietnam passed through these cells. Phan Châu Trinh, the reformer, was held from 1908 to 1911. Huỳnh Thúc Kháng arrived the same year and stayed more than a decade; released, he founded the newspaper Tiếng Dân in 1927 and served as acting president of the Democratic Republic of Vietnam for five months in 1946 while Hồ Chí Minh negotiated in France. Tôn Đức Thắng, a future president, and Phạm Văn Đồng and Lê Đức Thọ, both future architects of the North, were prisoners here in the 1930s. Inmates ran clandestine study circles, and the Vietnamese phrase for what happened — turning the prison into a school — carries a hard edge: the colonial government had gathered its opposition in one place and given it time to talk. Not everyone graduated. Nguyễn An Ninh, the Saigon journalist who founded La Cloche Fêlée in December 1923 and sold it himself in the street, died here on 14 August 1943 at forty-two; his jailers may have killed him rather than risk his becoming useful to the Japanese.

## Cages With a Catwalk

The structure the world came to know was completed in 1940 and is called Trại Phú Tường. It contains 120 solitary cells, arranged as two blocks of sixty facing each other across a warders' walkway, and its defining feature is what is not there: no ceilings, only iron grilles. Guards patrolled above the prisoners and looked down into them, which made every hour of every day an hour of being watched from overhead. Sixty adjoining pens have no cover at all. These are the so-called sunbathing cells, and the name is the jailers' euphemism — a prisoner put in one was left to the tropical sun and then to the rain. The whole compound occupies 5,475 square metres; the cells account for 1,408 of them, the roofless pens 1,873, and the remainder is the space in which guards moved. Nearby stands the Chuồng Bò, built in 1876 as a cattle enclosure and later repurposed to hold women.

## Seventeen July 1970

The delegation was small: Representatives Augustus Hawkins and William Anderson, Harkin as their aide, the translator Don Luce, and Frank Walton, who directed the USAID Office of Public Safety — the American agency underwriting South Vietnam's prison system. Luce and Harkin had the map. The route it described appeared on no schedule, and the officials escorting them had every reason to keep them away from it. What the photographs showed was not an aberration stumbled upon by accident but a design working as intended, and the reaction in the United States was immediate. Within a short time, 180 male and 300 female prisoners were moved off the island. Tiger cage stopped being a technical term and became an accusation.

## Three Hundred and Eighty-Four Replacements

Pressure produced a building programme rather than a reprieve. In 1971 a new block went up, constructed with American contracting — Camp 7, later renamed Trại Phú Bình, and known ever since as the American tiger cages. It holds 384 solitary cells in four lettered sections, AB, CD, EF and GH, each section running two rows of forty-eight. The arithmetic is worth pausing over: the response to the exposure of 120 isolation cells was the construction of more than three times as many. The concrete still stands, most doors still open, and prisoners' handwriting remains legible on some walls. Bats live in them now. Visitors walk the guards' catwalk, which is the museum's quietest and most effective decision — it puts you where the watching was done and leaves the rest to you.

## Flight Context

Côn Đảo Prison stands at 8.689°N, 106.615°E, on the eastern edge of Côn Sơn town on the largest island of the Côn Đảo archipelago, roughly 180 km south-southeast of Vũng Tàu. From the air the site reads as a set of low rectangular walled compounds on the flat coastal strip between the town's crescent valley and the beach, with Hàng Dương Cemetery visible as an open grid a few hundred metres to the north. Con Dao Airport (VVCS / VCS) lies about 12 km northeast at the island's tip, single runway 11/29, 1,830 m, elevation 6 m. Best viewed at 2,000–4,000 ft on a north-south pass along the eastern shore, where the prison compounds, the cemetery and the town all fall in one frame. Clearest air December to May.
