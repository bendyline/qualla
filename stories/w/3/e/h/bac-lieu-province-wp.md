# Bạc Liêu

A childless marriage ended here in 1916, and the song the husband wrote about it became the backbone of southern Vietnamese opera - one of several improbable legacies left by a coastal province that no longer exists.

**Location:** 9.3000, 105.5000

**Categories:** Cà Mau province, Former provinces of Vietnam, Mekong Delta, Music history, Renewable energy

## Introduction

In 1913 a young musician in Bạc Liêu married a woman named Trần Thị Tấn. Three childless years later, custom obliged him to return her to her family - a quiet domestic catastrophe of the sort that usually leaves no trace in any archive. Cao Văn Lầu turned it into a song. "Dạ cổ hoài lang" - hearing the night drum, longing for the absent husband - took shape around 1919, and he wrote it not in his own voice but in hers. The melody spread through the delta faster than anyone expected. By the late 1920s the cải lương opera troupes had built entire productions around it, and its skeleton hardened into vọng cổ: the mode that still anchors sung theatre across southern Vietnam. A flat coastal province of rice paddies and shrimp ponds had produced the region's national aria.

## The Song That Outlived the Marriage

Cao Văn Lầu was born in Long An on 22 December 1892 and carried south to Bạc Liêu at the age of four. He never left. Poverty pulled him out of French primary school in 1907; the following year he began studying under a local master, Lê Tài Khí, and by 1912 he was making his living as a musician. He died in Bạc Liêu on 13 August 1976, and he and his wife are buried together in a memorial park in the city devoted to đờn ca tài tử, the delta's chamber-music tradition. The word vọng cổ now carries three meanings at once - a musical mode, the specific composition of 1919, and any of the thousands of songs since written across its cadences. Few grief-stricken young men have been repaid quite so lavishly for one bad year.

## The Prince and the Fields Behind Him

Say Bạc Liêu anywhere in southern Vietnam and someone will mention Công Tử Bạc Liêu, the Prince of Bạc Liêu. Trần Trinh Huy, born 22 June 1900, inherited a fortune his father Trần Trinh Trạch had assembled into one of the four largest landholdings in the south - built on rice rents and on money lent to gamblers. The son spent it magnificently. He is widely credited as the first private citizen in Vietnam to own an aeroplane, which he once flew into Siamese airspace and had confiscated, his father reportedly settling the matter in shiploads of rice. The tale everyone repeats - that he burned banknotes to cook mung beans in a contest with a rival playboy - was denied by his own son, who said his father had cheerfully seeded such rumours himself. What rarely gets told is the arithmetic underneath: every extravagance was funded by tenant farmers working land they did not own. Land reform in the 1960s took the estates. Trần Trinh Huy died in Saigon on 13 January 1974, and his descendants sold the last of it and scattered.

## Salt Air and Sewing Machines

Geographically, Bạc Liêu is a slight outlier. It lies on the Mekong Delta but a little south of the river's main outflows, roughly 100 km below Cần Thơ, on a coast so low and level that the boundary between land and sea is a matter of the hour rather than the map. Rice farming and fishing carry the economy, joined more recently by food processing and garment manufacturing. The province is also known for its Teochew Chinese community, whose ancestors arrived by sea and whose kitchens, temples and business networks are woven through the towns. Drive the back roads and the pattern repeats: paddy, then embankment, then the flat grey rectangles of shrimp ponds running to the horizon.

## Turbines in the Tidal Flats

Off Hiệp Thành, the horizon does something no other stretch of the Vietnamese coast was doing in 2013. Ground was broken on 9 September 2010, and by August of 2013 the first turbines were selling power - machines standing on foundations driven into the intertidal mud, out where the water is only knee-deep at low tide. Three phases eventually put 133 General Electric turbines across some 1,300 hectares, for an installed capacity of 241.2 MW, financed in part by the U.S. Export-Import Bank. It is routinely described as Vietnam's first offshore wind farm, and by March 2020 it had passed a cumulative billion kilowatt-hours. It also, unexpectedly, became a tourist attraction: roughly 200,000 people a year now walk the causeway out among the towers to photograph them against the mudflats.

## Erased From the Map

Bạc Liêu has been abolished before. The French decreed the province on 20 December 1899, effective as an administrative unit on 1 January 1900. It was dissolved into Ba Xuyên on 22 October 1956, then re-established on 8 September 1964. After the fall of Saigon on 30 April 1975 it was folded together with Cà Mau into a single entity called Minh Hải, and the two were pulled apart again by a National Assembly vote on 6 November 1996. The most recent disappearance came on 12 June 2025, when Resolution 202/2025/QH15 merged Bạc Liêu into Cà Mau province and abolished the district tier nationwide. The combined province covers 7,942.39 km² with about 2.6 million residents, seated at Tân Thành ward. The name survives on the temple gates, the memorial park and the mouths of everyone who lives there.

## Flight Context

Centred near 9.30°N, 105.50°E on Vietnam's southern delta coast. Best viewed at 3,000-5,000 ft: below that the shrimp ponds lose their geometry, above it the whole province flattens into haze. The clearest visual anchor is the Bạc Liêu wind farm off Hiệp Thành - 133 turbines standing in the tidal shallows, unmistakable from 20 nm out. National Route 1A runs a straight line through the province, and the coastline is essentially a single unbroken arc facing the South China Sea. Cà Mau Airport (VVCM/CAH, elev. 7 ft, 1,500 m asphalt) lies about 20 nm west-southwest; Cần Thơ International (VVCT/VCA, elev. 10 ft, 3,000 m asphalt) is roughly 50 nm north-northeast. Expect excellent visibility December through March; from May to October the southwest monsoon brings afternoon convection and heavy haze over the coastal flats.
