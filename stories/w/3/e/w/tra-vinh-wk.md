# Tra Vinh

A small Mekong Delta city that grew up under old trees, where Khmer pagodas outnumber landmarks and a square pond keeps a 400-year-old argument alive.

**Location:** 9.5170, 106.3000

**Categories:** Cities, Mekong Delta, Vietnam, Buddhism, Khmer culture, Temples

## Introduction

The shade is the first thing you notice. Most Mekong Delta towns are built of concrete, corrugated steel and glare, and you spend the middle of the day looking for somewhere to stand. Trà Vinh is different. Its streets run under a canopy of trees old enough that their trunks have outgrown the pavement around them, and the light that reaches the ground arrives filtered and green. The city is small - 112,738 people in 2022, spread across just under 68 square kilometres - and its centre is walkable in an afternoon. What makes it worth the afternoon is not scale. It is the fact that roughly one resident in five is Khmer, another six percent are ethnic Hoa, and the architecture of daily life here reflects all three communities at once.

## The Square Pond and the Wager

Seven kilometres southwest of the centre, along Highway 53, sits Ao Bà Om - a rectangle of still water about 500 metres by 300, so deliberately shaped that its other name is simply Ao Vuông, the Square Pond. Sand ridges ring the shore, and the ancient trees growing on them have had their roots exposed by centuries of weather until they stand on knotted wooden stilts. The name carries a story. In the local telling, the community's men and women divided into two teams and raced each other to dig a reservoir of fresh water. The women's team, led by a woman called Om, released lanterns into the night sky. The men, taking the lights for the rising morning star, stopped work and rested. The women dug on and won, and their pond took Om's name. Vietnam's Ministry of Culture and Information recognised the site as a national relic on 20 July 1994.

## Angkorajaborey

Standing at the edge of Ao Bà Om is Chùa Âng, known in Khmer as Angkorajaborey, and tradition dates its founding to around the year 990 - which would make it the oldest Khmer pagoda in the district by several centuries. Whatever the true date, the building itself is unmistakably Theravada Khmer rather than Vietnamese Mahayana: a steep tiered roof, naga serpents running down the gable ends, and garuda figures shouldering the eaves. Elsewhere in the city, Chùa Ông Mẹt - known in Khmer as Bodhisálaràja, and also called Kom Pong pagoda - and the Watsamrongek pagoda in Ward 8 do the same work in the same idiom. These are not museum pieces. In Khmer Krom communities the pagoda has always been school, library, assembly hall and monastery at once, and the monks who walk the morning alms round through Trà Vinh's streets are the visible edge of an institution that holds the culture together.

## The Cave Gate at Kompông Chrây

The pagoda most visitors remember has a nickname rather than a name. Chùa Hang, the Cave Pagoda, is properly Kompông Chrây - mulberry wharf, after the ferry landing that once operated beneath a great tree at its entrance. Founded in 1637 and led by twenty-two abbots since, it earned its nickname from a side gateway built as a vaulted arch twelve metres deep, with walls so thick that passing through feels like walking a tunnel into the grounds. For generations bats hung in the trees above in enormous numbers. Bombing during the Tết Offensive of 1968 scattered them, damaged the pagoda badly, and killed many of the old trees; restoration did not begin until 1977. What came next is the part worth knowing. Rather than burn the war-killed timber, the pagoda opened a woodcarving workshop, and the master craftsman Thạch Buôl trained more than twenty apprentices to turn those trunks into Buddha figures and animals. Storks nest in the canopy now where the bats used to be.

## Arriving, and Getting About

Direct minibuses run down from Ho Chi Minh City, but the more interesting approach is by water - a private charter, or a place aboard a cargo boat out of Bến Tre, which puts you into the delta's actual circulatory system rather than alongside it. Once here, the centre needs nothing but shoe leather. A large covered market anchors the middle of town; the tourist office sells a fold-out map covering both the city and the surrounding countryside, and rents bicycles, which is the right speed for the flat lanes running out through the coconut groves and rice paddies. Motorbikes can be hired too, if the coast is on your list. Note that Trà Vinh's status changed on 1 July 2025: the city was dissolved as a municipal unit, its wards absorbed into an enlarged Vĩnh Long province. The signposts will catch up eventually. The trees will not notice.

## Flight Context

Trà Vinh sits at 9.517°N, 106.300°E in the Mekong Delta, on the strip of land between the Cổ Chiên and Hậu river branches. The town is best picked out from 3,000-6,000 ft, where the dark green mass of its street canopy stands out sharply against the pale geometry of surrounding paddy; Ao Bà Om reads as a distinct dark rectangle about 7 km southwest of the centre. The Cổ Chiên Bridge to the northeast and the broad Hậu River to the southwest are the two unmistakable navigation anchors, with the coast roughly 40 km east. There is no airport at Trà Vinh: nearest are Cần Thơ International (VVCT) about 60 km west and Tân Sơn Nhất, Ho Chi Minh City (VVTS) about 130 km north. Conditions are tropical monsoon - record high 37.7°C in May, record low 17.0°C in January, average humidity 84 percent, and 1,638.5 mm of annual rain falling almost entirely between May and October, when afternoon convection builds fast.
