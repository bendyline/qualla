# Cà Mau province

Vietnam's southernmost province takes its name from the Khmer for "dark water" — and the water is still dark, still rising, and still the reason anyone lives here.

**Location:** 9.0833, 105.0833

**Categories:** Cà Mau province, Mekong Delta, Provinces of Vietnam, Mangroves, Aquaculture

## Introduction

Dark water. That is what tưk kha-mau meant in Khmer, the name earlier inhabitants gave to rivers stained the colour of strong tea by rotting mangrove leaf and peat. Vietnamese settlers kept the sound and let the meaning drift, and two syllables became Cà Mau — the southernmost of Vietnam's thirty-four provincial-level administrative units, a wedge of silt and swamp where the country simply runs out of land. The water has not changed. Cut a fresh channel through the U Minh peat beds and what comes up is still the colour of tea left standing overnight.

## Five Kinds of Ground, None of Them Firm

Soil scientists working here sort the province into five groups, and the Vietnamese names give the game away: đất phèn, acid sulfate soil; đất than bùn, peat; đất bãi bồi, the raw alluvium still being laid down; đất mặn, saline; đất kênh rạch, the spoil thrown up along the canals. Not one of them is ordinary farmland. The land floods routinely, especially along its southern edge, and the whole province lies barely above the tide. What thrives instead is forest that likes salt: seventy-seven percent of the mangrove in the entire Mekong region grows in Cà Mau, alongside roughly 35,000 hectares of inland woodland. The climate is close to equatorial — an average of 26.5°C across the year, humidity near 86 percent, and about 2,360 millimetres of rain falling across some 165 days, most of it in the May-to-November wet season.

## Capital of Shrimp and Crab

Vietnamese newspapers call the province thủ phủ tôm cua — the capital of shrimp and crab — and the numbers earn the title. More than 435,000 hectares here are given over to shrimp, something close to forty percent of Vietnam's entire shrimp-farming area, and in 2025 the province's seafood exports were worth roughly 2.6 billion US dollars. Some of it is intensive pond aquaculture. A great deal of it is the older mangrove-shrimp arrangement, where the forest is left standing and the shrimp grow among the roots. Roads reached much of this landscape late and reluctantly; the canals came first and still do the work, carrying feed out and iced boxes back, so that the map of Cà Mau reads less like a road network than like a circulatory system.

## Who Came, and Who Was Already Here

For a long time nobody wanted this ground. Trịnh Hoài Đức's gazetteer Gia Định Thành Thông Chí records that under Emperor Gia Long, settlers had only begun scratching at the riverbanks — the Ông Đốc, the Gành Hào, the Bảy Háp — and by Tự Đức's reign the region was still mostly mangrove, thinly peopled because fresh water was scarce and the soil turned acid when disturbed. The administrative claim came before the population did: in the late seventeenth century Mạc Cửu and a group of Chinese settlers established themselves at Hà Tiên and later submitted the territory to the Nguyễn Lords, and by 1808 the area had been folded into Long Xuyên district. Today the province counts twenty-eight ethnic groups. Roughly 115,000 residents are Khmer and about 24,600 are Hoa, communities whose presence on this coast is older than the Vietnamese name for it.

## The Second of November, 1997

Typhoons rarely reach this far south, which is exactly why Linda was catastrophic. When the storm came ashore over Cà Mau on 2 November 1997, much of the fishing fleet was where it always was at that time of year — out on the water, working. It never came back. Across Vietnam the storm killed 3,111 people, wrecked or damaged 216,054 houses, and destroyed at least 3,122 boats, and southern Vietnam had seen nothing like it in at least a hundred years. The dead were overwhelmingly the men and boys who crewed those boats, and the province's coastal villages lost whole generations of them in a single night. Memorial lists in the fishing towns still run to columns of the same family names, repeated down the stone.

## One Province, Twice Over

Administratively, Cà Mau has spent fifty years being taken apart and put back together. In 1976 it was merged with neighbouring Bạc Liêu to form Minh Hải province; in November 1996 the National Assembly split the two apart again. Then on 12 June 2025, Resolution 202/2025/QH15 merged Bạc Liêu straight back into Cà Mau and abolished the district tier entirely, leaving wards and communes reporting directly to the province. The result covers 7,942.39 square kilometres and holds 2,606,672 people, with close to 310 kilometres of coast — about 163 kilometres facing the East Sea and 147 on the Gulf of Thailand. Old maps of Minh Hải, drawn before most of today's residents were born, are once again a fair guide to where the borders run.

## Flight Context

Centred near 9.08°N, 105.08°E, at the tapering southern end of the Mekong Delta. From 8,000–12,000 feet the province is unmistakable: an almost geometric grid of canals cut across brown-green mangrove, with the Gulf of Thailand to the west and the East Sea to the east, converging toward the point of the peninsula in the southwest. Cà Mau Airport (VVCM / CAH) sits at the provincial capital with a single 1,500-metre asphalt runway, 09/27, at an elevation of just 7 feet above sea level; Vietnam Airlines operates a daily ATR-72 service from Ho Chi Minh City's Tân Sơn Nhất. Expect haze and towering convection through the May-to-November wet season, and note that inland reference points are scarce — the canals and the two coastlines are the reliable navigation cues.
