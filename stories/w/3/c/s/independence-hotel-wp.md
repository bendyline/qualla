# Independence Hotel

Cambodians never called it the Independence — they called it "seven storeys," and the beach below still carries the nickname.

**Location:** 10.6173, 103.4950

**Categories:** Historic hotels, Modernist architecture, Cambodia, Khmer Rouge history, Coastal

## Introduction

Nobody in Sihanoukville called it the Independence. They called it bprahm-bpel jawn — seven storeys — because in 1964 that was the entire description a building needed. Seven floors made it by far the tallest thing in Cambodia, a white slab set on a headland above the Gulf of Thailand, and the name stuck so hard that the beach below it is still known locally as seven-storey beach. The hotel was designed to be a statement, and it has spent six decades being a different statement roughly every fifteen years.

## A Country Announcing Itself

The French architects Leroy and Mondet drew it as a deliberate landmark, the built expression of a country that had been independent for barely a decade and intended to look the part. King Norodom Sihanouk took personal responsibility for the interiors — not a ceremonial gesture but an actual design brief, in the same era when Cambodia was pouring concrete into modernist schools, theatres and stadiums at a pace that still startles architectural historians. The guest list did the rest of the talking. Catherine Deneuve stayed here. So did Jacqueline Kennedy. The hotel functioned as a show piece for the royal family, a place to bring visitors and let the view over the water finish the argument.

## Target Practice

Then the Khmer Rouge came, and the building's height became its liability. From seven storeys you can see the whole port town, so the regime used it as an observation post and camped in its stripped corridors. Soldiers also used the facade for target practice. The most disturbing account concerns the clover-leaf swimming pool: dissidents were reportedly held in it, the empty basin roofed over with bamboo to make a crude jail. These were people the regime had decided were enemies of it — Cambodians, in a pool built for Cambodians, in a hotel put up to celebrate their country's freedom. The building did not choose any of this. It simply had the wrong virtues at the wrong moment: tall, empty, and impossible to hide from.

## Half the Rooms Habitable

The next chapter arrived with the peacekeepers. In 1992, after the fall of Pol Pot's regime and the negotiated settlement that followed, the Independence reopened to house UNTAC personnel working on the reconstruction of the country — though by most accounts only about half the rooms were fit to sleep in. When the mission wound down the hotel emptied again, and for years it served an unofficial purpose, sheltering criminals and smugglers who appreciated a large abandoned building with a commanding view of the coast. Photographs from that period show a concrete carcass, stained and open to the weather, still unmistakably elegant in its proportions.

## The Beach That Borrowed the Name

Look at where the architects put it and the confidence of the thing becomes obvious. The hotel stands about five kilometres from the centre of Sihanoukville, on rising ground at the foot of Sihanoukville Mountain, with nothing between its west-facing rooms and the Gulf of Thailand. Below it curves a long beach — and that beach is now called Independence Beach, or, in the version locals actually use, seven-storey beach. The building named the shoreline. In a city that was itself only nine years old when the hotel opened, laid out from scratch in 1955 to serve the new deep-water port, a seven-floor slab of white concrete was not just the tallest structure in Cambodia. It was the most obvious landmark for miles, and it became the thing everything else was described in relation to.

## New Paint, Old Bones

Restoration came in 2007, financed by Canadia Bank, and the Independence reopened as a luxury hotel with its original lines intact. It runs today as a resort under the Dara Hotels group. Writing shortly before the doors opened, The Cambodia Daily caught the strangeness of it exactly: revamped and refurbished, its grisly memories buried under a new coat of paint, the historic building would once again represent a nation's optimism. That sentence is doing a lot of work. The optimism is real — the building is genuinely beautiful, the sea is genuinely that colour, and Sihanoukville has since been remade again by a construction boom the 1960s could not have imagined. But the paint is only paint. The clover-leaf pool is still the shape it always was.

## Flight Context

The Independence sits on a low coastal headland at 10.6173°N, 103.495°E, about 5 km south-west of central Sihanoukville and roughly 3 km down the coast from the port. From 2,500 to 4,000 feet it is easy to pick out: a white rectilinear block on green ground, directly above the arc of Independence Beach, with the wooded ridge of Sihanoukville Mountain behind it and Koh Puos island a short hop offshore to the north-west. Sihanouk International Airport (VDSV / KOS) is about 20 km east; Phnom Penh International (VDPP) about 200 km north-east. Late afternoon light coming off the Gulf of Thailand throws the west facade into full sun. Expect low cloud and rain showers along this coast from May through October.
