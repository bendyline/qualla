# Preah Sihanouk province

Cambodia's smallest coastal province carries two names — Port of the Moon and Holy Sihanouk — and twenty-two islands between them.

**Location:** 10.6333, 103.5000

**Categories:** Preah Sihanouk province, Provinces of Cambodia, Gulf of Thailand, Islands, Beaches, Coastal Cambodia

## Introduction

Kampong Som means Port of the Moon. The second word travels a long way to get there: Sanskrit saumya, the juice offered to the moon god in the Rig Veda, worn down through Pali into a word meaning moonlike, and also one of the names of Shiva. The first word is Malay — kampong, a village — stretched by centuries of coastal trade until it meant a landing place, a pier. That was this coast's name for as long as anyone recorded it. Then, in the 1950s, a king built a port here and the name changed.

## Lion Jaws

The province is now Preah Sihanouk: holy Sihanouk, for Norodom Sihanouk, who reigned from 1941 to 1955 and again from 1993 to 2004, and who is still spoken of as the father of the modern nation. His name is also Sanskrit by way of Pali — sīha, lion, and hanu, jaws. Sihanouk himself proposed the Western form, Sihanoukville. The administrative promotion came late: on 22 December 2008 King Norodom Sihamoni signed a decree converting the municipalities of Kep, Pailin and Sihanoukville into full provinces, and in January 2009 Kampong Seila District was transferred across from Koh Kong to fill out the map. At 1,938 square kilometres and a shade over 230,000 people, it remains one of Cambodia's smallest provinces — and, measured by human development index, among its better-off.

## Burned, Sold, Reclaimed

Long before the port, this was a coast of consequence to almost nobody. No settlement larger than a fishing or trading village stood on the peninsula. Cambodian royal chronicles mention Kompong Saom in passing, listed in 1479 among the provinces a king controlled during a nine-year civil war. Then the seventeenth century closed the Mekong route as Vietnamese power pushed into the delta, and this coast became a bargaining chip. A Siamese fleet burned the port of Kompong Som in 1717. In 1757 it was handed to Ha Tien, along with Kampot, as payment for a Chinese pirate lord's military support of the Cambodian king; until its destruction in 1771 it functioned as a duty-free entrepôt wired into Chinese trading networks. A colonial-era insurrection from 1885 to 1887 rolled through Veal Rinh and Kampong Seila under a pirate named Quan-Khiem, who held the north of the province until he — by then an old man — was arrested.

## Twenty-Two Islands

The province administers twenty-two islands, and several still carry the names French surveyors gave them: Ile des Paletuviers for mangrove-ringed Koh Kaong Kang, Ile de Cone for Koh Koun, Les Frères for the pair now called Koh Song Saa, the lovers' islands. Koh Rong is the giant — 78 square kilometres, hilly, forested almost throughout, rising to 316 metres, with roughly 43 kilometres of beach spread across more than twenty separate strands. About four thousand people lived there at the 2019 census, which for an island that size means the interior is essentially empty. American crews filmed two seasons of Survivor there between March and July 2015. In February 2018 Cambodia declared its first marine national park in the waters around it. Koh Rong Sanloem lies south, flatter and thinner, with better diving. Koh Kaong Kang has no permanent residents at all — it is too flat to hold fresh water.

## A Bay Worth Joining

The mainland beaches run in a chain and each has a different temperament. Ochheuteal stretches 3.3 kilometres under casuarina and tamarisk, lined with rental chairs and beach huts that turn into bars after dark; its western sixth was christened Serendipity in the 1990s by an American struck by how untouched it was, and the name took. Otres runs 4.6 kilometres and stayed scruffy and beloved by budget travellers until police cleared the on-beach bungalows in 2011. Independence Beach is 1.3 kilometres, named for the hotel on the rock at its northern end. Victory Beach is barely 300 metres with the deep-water port at its shoulder, and just south of it, at Lamherkay, is the exact patch of sand where French and Cambodian crews broke ground on that port in 1955. On 26 May 2011 the whole bay was admitted to Les Plus Belles Baies du Monde, the Paris club of the world's most beautiful bays.

## Grandmother Mao Watches the Road

Inland the province is ordinary and busy. Prey Nob farms; Stung Hav fishes, landing the largest catch in the province; the rice tonnage is too small to matter nationally, so the ledger is filled with fruit, industrial crops and roughly forty thousand tonnes of fish a year. National Highway 4 climbs from Phnom Penh over the Pich Nil pass to reach it, and at the pass drivers stop. Yeay Mao — Grandmother Mao — is a neak ta spirit venerated the length of this coast, protector of travellers, hunters and fishermen. In one telling she took command of an army after her husband's death; in another she was abandoned in the forest here and killed by a tiger. Drivers pray at her shrine and wash their vehicles with water from the stream before continuing down to the sea.

## Flight Context

Preah Sihanouk province occupies the Cambodian coast around 10.633°N, 103.500°E, on the Gulf of Thailand. The province is low — the peninsula tops out near 15 m and only the interior hills exceed that — so 4,000 to 6,000 ft AGL frames the whole coastal strip including the islands. Koh Rong is the unmistakable marker: a hilly, densely forested 78 km² island with a 316 m summit, about 25 km west of the mainland, with Koh Rong Sanloem immediately south. The port, the beach chain from Victory to Otres, and the mangrove line of the Ou Trojak Jet river give a clean visual run down the coast. Sihanouk International (KOS/VDSV, elev. 40 ft) sits 20 km east of the city in Ream commune. Tropical monsoon climate: wet season roughly April to November with heavy convective build-ups, dry and hazy December to March.
