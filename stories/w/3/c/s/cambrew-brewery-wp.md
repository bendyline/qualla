# Cambrew Brewery

Cambodia put its national brewery on the wrong side of the country on purpose, because the water here was right.

**Location:** 10.6329, 103.5345

**Categories:** Beer in Cambodia, Breweries, Food and drink companies of Cambodia, New Khmer Architecture, Buildings and structures in Preah Sihanouk province, Industrial heritage

## Introduction

The bottles were made outside Phnom Penh. The beer, somebody decided in the early 1960s, would be made clear across the country on the coast — which meant every empty bottle had to be trucked down the new highway before it could be filled, and every full one trucked back. The decision was argued about at the time. It was made anyway, and for one reason: the water at Sihanoukville was said to carry exactly the right mineral content for brewing. Sixty years on, the brewery is still there, and it still makes the beer Cambodians drink.

## SKD

The Cambodian government commissioned the plant in the early 1960s and had it built by a French contractor with French technical assistance. It was known as the SKD Brewery, and Prince Sihanouk opened it in 1965. It was a statement as much as a factory: an independent country, less than a decade out from the protectorate, producing its own lager under its own brand names in a city it had willed into existence. Two beers came off the line — Angkor and Bayon, both named for the temple complexes that Cambodians already treated as shorthand for the nation. The brewery went up during the same building spree that produced the port, the railway, the National Bank branch and the A-frame church on the hill. This was what the 1960s looked like here: concrete going up everywhere, all of it meaning something.

## Dong Raik

Vann Molyvann designed the buildings, and the main office block is one of his clearest ideas. Its upper floor overhangs the ground floor by several metres, and the overhang is not disguised but announced — a row of blunt triangular supports set into the facade, so the structural logic reads from the road. The form is called dong raik, after the carrying pole a farmer balances across the shoulders with a load hanging off each end. The same word names the Dângrêk mountains along the Thai border, which have the same profile. It is a characteristic move of New Khmer Architecture: solve the tropical problem — shade the walls below, keep the rain off, let air run underneath — with a shape already living in the language.

## Ten Years On, Fifteen Years Off

The brewery ran for barely a decade. Civil war disrupted operations in the early 1970s, and in 1975 it stopped entirely, along with everything else in the country. It sat idle for the rest of the decade and most of the next. In 1991 a Malaysian company, Cambrew Ltd, took control, spent nine months rebuilding the plant, and restarted production in 1992 — money arriving just as the Paris Peace Agreements took hold and the UN moved in to run an election. The same year, Cambrew struck a joint venture with PepsiCo to bottle and distribute soft drinks nationally. Beer and cola came back to Cambodia together, out of the same buildings.

## My Country, My Beer

Angkor is the point of the place. It is the most widely consumed beer in Cambodia and is generally accepted as the national beer, sold under a slogan that leaves no ambiguity about the claim: ប្រទេសខ្ញុំ ស្រាបៀរខ្ញុំ — My Country, My Beer. It is a pale lager at 5.0 percent, in cans and in the 640-millilitre bottles that arrive on restaurant tables in crates of ice. The range around it is broader than most visitors expect. Angkor Extra Stout runs at 8.0 percent and took a Grand Gold from Monde Selection in Belgium in 2011; Angkor Premium took a Gold there in 2013. Black Panther, another 8.0 percent stout with a heavy malt nose, arrived in 2000. Klang is a 6.0 percent lager for people in a hurry. Bayon, at 4.6 percent, is the mild one.

## Danish Now

The Carlsberg Group bought half the brewery in 2005, went to seventy-five percent in 2018, and on 1 November 2019 took the whole thing. So the plant that a newly independent Cambodia commissioned to prove it could brew its own beer, designed by its own state architect in its own new architectural language, is now wholly owned from Copenhagen — and still turns out the beer the country puts its own name on. The two facts sit together without much friction. Cambodians drinking Angkor are not drinking a Danish product in any sense that matters to them; they are drinking the beer from the brewery on the coast, the one built where the water was right.

## Flight Context

Cambrew Brewery lies at 10.6329°N, 103.5345°E, on the eastern side of Sihanoukville near the industrial approach to the Autonomous Port, at essentially sea level. It is an industrial complex rather than a landmark — best identified from 1,500 to 3,000 ft AGL by locating the port on the peninsula's north shore and following the highway corridor east. Sihanouk International (KOS/VDSV, elev. 40 ft, 3,300 m runway) is roughly 15 km east-southeast in Ream commune, and National Highway 4 runs northeast from here toward Phnom Penh. Coastal haze is common in the December-to-March dry season; the wet months from April to November bring heavy afternoon rain and low ceilings over the peninsula.
