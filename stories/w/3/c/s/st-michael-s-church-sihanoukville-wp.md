# St. Michael's Church, Sihanoukville

A concrete A-frame on a hill above the bay, spared by the Khmer Rouge because it made a serviceable animal shed.

**Location:** 10.6361, 103.5165

**Categories:** Roman Catholic church buildings in Cambodia, Architecture in Cambodia, New Khmer Architecture, Modernist architecture, Religious sites, Khmer Rouge history

## Introduction

It survived because somebody found a use for it. When the Khmer Rouge emptied Cambodia's churches in 1975, more than fifty Catholic buildings were pulled down outright. The one on the hill above Sihanoukville bay was not. Its interior was stripped bare, and for the next fifteen years the building served as an animal shed, a pantry and a storeroom — indignities that turn out to be an excellent preservation strategy. The concrete A-frame is still standing, still the only Catholic church in the city, and still the most quietly radical piece of architecture on this coast.

## A Roof Shaped Like Hands

There is almost no wall. Two great planes of reinforced concrete rise from the ground and meet at a ridge, and that is the building — roof and wall are the same surface, a shape a child would draw. Other missionaries at the time called it very original, which in the polite register of the period may not have been a compliment. The design is credited to Father Jean Ahadoberry, a Basque priest, working with advice from Vann Molyvann, and it belongs squarely to New Khmer Architecture: the modernist movement that ran from independence in 1953 until 1970, using reinforced concrete and assertive geometry while designing hard around a tropical climate. The church sits on high ground near Wat Leu, the upper pagoda, looking out over the bay. A Catholic A-frame a short walk from a Buddhist hilltop temple is not an accident of zoning; it is what the 1959 city plan intended.

## The Architect from Ream

Vann Molyvann was born on 23 November 1926 at Ream, on this same stretch of coast, a short drive from where the church would go up. He trained in Paris, returned in 1956, and was made head of public works and state architect almost immediately — he was the most qualified architect the newly independent country had. In fourteen years he put up more than a hundred structures. Two of them were rising in Sihanoukville at the same time as the church: the SKD brewery and the National Bank, both with their staff housing, both finished in 1968. He had been experimenting with the A-frame since the late 1950s, including a pavilion built in front of Phnom Penh's railway station for the 2,500-year Buddha celebration and, following Buddhist practice, dismantled once it had served its purpose. The church took that temporary form and cast it permanently.

## Three or Four Families

In 1957 a handful of French and Vietnamese Christians settled around the new port. Bishop Gustave Raballand sent Father Yves Ramousse down to say Easter Mass for them, and Ramousse kept coming back. Once he had succeeded Raballand as bishop, he wrote to King Sihanouk asking for land, and in 1961 the king granted it. The church was completed in 1965 to serve a congregation of three or four families — an act of considerable optimism. Ahadoberry worked on until 1967 and then returned to France. Under Lon Nol, between 1970 and 1975, the city swelled and the Christian community reached about twenty families, among them a Vietnamese nun who had gathered roughly ten orphaned children around the market and settled there with them.

## Shut

Then it stopped. From 1975 the church was closed, and the Christians of Sihanoukville were sent to labour camps like everyone else the regime distrusted, their faith treated as a crime and punished as far as death. Nobody knows how many came back. That is the honest end of the record — not an estimated death toll but a blank where a couple of dozen families used to be. Buddhist monasteries across the country fared no better. The building itself came through the years to 1990 structurally intact and completely gutted inside, its decoration torn out, its nave full of whatever the local authorities needed to store.

## Michael

The building got its name from soldiers. When UN peacekeepers arrived in the early 1990s to prepare the country's first postwar election, they took the church as a base. Some of them were Catholic; their commander, Colonel Leng Sound, passed on their request for a priest, and Bishop Ramousse came and said the first Mass here since the war. The soldiers credited the Archangel Michael with their protection and drew his icon on the front of the building, and the dedication stuck — the church had never formally had one. On 14 December 1993, after long negotiation, Ramousse got the property back from the army. Father Robert Venet took it on from 1993 to 2001, reaching Catholic communities as far as Koh Kong and Srae Ambel, opening a hospice for boys and funding scholarships. In 2012 Father Sun became the first Cambodian priest to serve as parish priest here. The congregation now includes a growing number of Chinese faithful, which is simply what happens when a parish sits in a port.

## Flight Context

St. Michael's Church stands at 10.6361°N, 103.5165°E on the hill above Sihanoukville bay, close to Wat Leu. The hill is the dominant high ground behind the city's northern shore and makes a useful visual fix on approach from the west. The church is small — best seen from 1,500 to 2,500 ft AGL, where the white A-frame reads clearly against the tree cover; the gilded roofs of Wat Leu immediately beside it are the easier target. Sihanouk International (KOS/VDSV, elev. 40 ft) is 20 km east in Ream commune. Morning light from the east puts the A-frame's seaward face in shadow; late afternoon is better. Wet season, April to November, brings low cloud over the hill.
