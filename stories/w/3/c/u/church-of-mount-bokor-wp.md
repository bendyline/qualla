# Church of Mount Bokor

A stone church built over nine years on a Cambodian mountaintop, occupied as a barracks by the Khmer Rouge, shot at by Vietnamese forces, and handed back to the Catholic Church on Christmas Eve 2017.

**Location:** 10.6270, 104.0226

**Categories:** Churches in Cambodia, French colonial architecture in Cambodia, Ruins in Cambodia, Buildings and structures in Kampot province, Buildings and structures completed in 1928

## Introduction

In March 1928 a bell was blessed on top of a mountain in southern Cambodia. Monsignor Jean-Claude Bouchut, the apostolic vicar, had come up from Phnom Penh with seven other priests to consecrate the church that morning, in front of the Resident-Superior and a congregation drawn from Phnom Penh and Kampot. The bell was blessed later the same day and given godparents in the usual way: Madame Le Fol, wife of the Resident General, stood as godmother, and a man named Richomme, who had opened a trail between Kratié and Saigon, as godfather. The building they were standing in had taken nearly ten years to finish. It is still there — walls up, belfry square against the sky, every window empty.

## Ten Years of Stone

Work began in 1919. The church was the project of Father Bernard, a missionary in Cambodia, financed with the support of Father Nicolas Couvreur, a former procurator of the Paris Foreign Missions Society. Construction was directed by Noël Salvarelli, a French cartographer whom the local authorities had already commissioned to build the access road up to the new mountain station — and whose son was baptised here once the work was done.

Nearly a decade for a building this size sounds absurd until you understand the site. Everything had to come up the mountain, along a road that was itself being hacked out of the slope. The stone, at least, came from the mountain: it was quarried from the sides of Bokor, which is why the walls read as a piece of the escarpment rather than something set down on top of it.

## Built for Wind

The design is neo-Romanesque and unfussy — a single barrel-vaulted nave, semicircular arched windows, a plain band running the length of the structure, and a squat belfry planted above the entrance at the head of a seven-step staircase crowned with a Calvary. The cross on the tower was made to withstand hurricanes, and it has. Until around 2010, visitors could still find fragments of glass brick clinging to the corners of the nave windows, and one side window holding the barest outline of a rusted crucifix.

It is the second-oldest Catholic church still standing in Cambodia. That is less a statement about its age than about what happened to the others.

## Barracks

When the hill station was abandoned, the church went with it. Then it acquired a garrison. The Khmer Rouge occupied the building from the 1970s and partitioned the interior; what survives inside is domestic wreckage — crates, improvised walls, the remains of a kitchen and toilets, the residue of men living inside a nave. Bokor was among the movement's last strongholds, and in the 1980s Vietnamese forces attacked the position. The exchanges left marks that travellers were still pointing out on the walls in 2008.

The occupation ended in 1993, when a French battalion of the United Nations Transitional Authority in Cambodia took over Bokor and set up in the old Palace Hotel nearby. The Khmer Rouge left the church. Across the rest of the country, churches and pagodas had been destroyed as a matter of policy. This one survived because it was useful.

## Given Back

On Christmas Eve 2017, the Royal Government of Cambodia invited Bishop Olivier Schmitthaeusler, the apostolic vicar, and other representatives of the Catholic Church up the mountain and formally returned the building and its land, describing the way the site had been used during the occupation as shameful. Fundraising for a restoration was launched in May 2021.

In the meantime the ruin has quietly gone back into service. Cambodia's Catholic community is small, and this is one of only a handful of places permitted to hold services, alongside Saint Joseph's in Phnom Penh, Saint Michael's in Sihanoukville, and the ground around the old cathedral at Battambang. Pilgrims come, many of them Vietnamese, and they leave things: statues of the Virgin and the Sacred Heart, rosaries, candles set into the corners of a building that was a machine-gun position within living memory.

## The Photograph Everyone Takes

Khmer couples climb up here for their pre-wedding photographs, which has made this one of the most reliably photographed ruins in the country — a bride in red against dark, lichen-blotched stone, with the Gulf of Thailand somewhere behind her when the cloud allows. It allows less often than you would like. Bokor is fogbound for much of the year, and the same mist that makes the church so photogenic has also turned it into a stock setting for horror fiction, which is a strange afterlife for a building that spent two decades sheltering soldiers.

Writing about ruins and what people want from them, the anthropologist Ann Laura Stoler listed Bokor's crumbling post office and empty Catholic church among the things travellers go looking for. The post office has since been demolished. The church is still standing, and it belongs to someone again.

## Flight Context

The Church of Mount Bokor stands at 10.627°N, 104.023°E, on the Bokor plateau roughly 1,050 m (about 3,450 ft) above sea level, a few hundred metres southeast of the Bokor Palace Hotel in Preah Monivong National Park, Kampot province. From the air it reads as a small, dark, cruciform stone mass with a square tower on otherwise open grassland at the plateau edge — a useful visual fix once the larger hotel and resort buildings have been identified. Recommended viewing altitude 4,000–6,000 ft AGL. Nearest airports: Sihanouk International (VDSV) about 75 km west-northwest, Phnom Penh International (VDPP) about 110 km north. Expect cloud: the plateau is under mist and low stratus for much of the year, heaviest in the May–October wet season.
