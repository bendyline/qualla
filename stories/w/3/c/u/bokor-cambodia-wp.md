# Bokor, Cambodia

Nine hundred people died building a mountain resort so the French could escape the heat; a century later the same plateau is becoming Cambodia's newest city.

**Location:** 10.6422, 104.0274

**Categories:** Hill stations, Cambodia, Colonial history, National parks, Ruins, Urban development

## Introduction

Nine hundred people died in nine months building it. That is the fact that sits underneath everything else about Bokor — underneath the fog, the ruined casino, the ghost-story photographs, the billion-dollar resort city now rising on the same plateau. Cambodian labourers cut a road and hauled materials up more than a thousand metres of jungle escarpment in the early 1920s so that French colonial officials would have somewhere cool to spend the hot season. The record preserves the number and not the names.

## What the Cool Air Cost

The logic of a hill station is simple and, from a colonial administrator's chair, unanswerable: the lowlands of Cambodia are punishing from March onward, and the top of Phnom Bokor is not. At around 1,050 metres the air is cool, damp and frequently full of cloud, and on a clear afternoon the escarpment gives a view straight out over the Gulf of Thailand. So the French built a town up there. The Bokor Palace hotel was inaugurated in 1925, joined by the Résident Supérieur's villa, a post office, and a Catholic church begun in 1919 and finally blessed in March 1928 — neo-Romanesque, squat-belfried, built from stone quarried out of the mountain it stands on. The buildings were for French officials and the Cambodian elite. The labour that put them there was neither.

## Wars, Plural

Bokor has been abandoned more times than it has been opened. The French left in the late 1940s as Khmer Issarak insurgents and the First Indochina War made the mountain untenable. It came back in 1962, when the Cité du Bokor reopened with new hotels near the lake and a casino, and the plateau was formally declared a tourist city. That lasted a decade. By 1972 the Khmer Rouge held the mountain, and they did not let go easily: when Vietnamese forces invaded in 1979, Khmer Rouge fighters entrenched on the plateau and held their positions for months. They were still there in the early 1990s. Inside the church, cadres partitioned the sanctuary and built barracks; Vietnamese forces shelled and shot at the building through the 1980s, and the gunfire is still legible in the walls. On Christmas Eve 2017 the Cambodian government formally returned the church to the Catholic Church.

## A Plateau in the Clouds

Strip away the human wreckage and the mountain is extraordinary in its own right. Phnom Bokor tops out at 1,081 metres, the high point of the Dâmrei — Elephant — range, and the climb from the coastal plain is steep and thickly forested before it opens onto grassland, evergreen stands and bare rock escarpment. Cloud sits on the plateau for much of the wet season from May to October, which is why arriving visitors so often find themselves walking through a colonial ruin in twenty metres of visibility. Popokvil Waterfalls drops in two tiers with a shallow pool between them, a few kilometres north-east of the old hill station. On a clear day the view reaches past the Cambodian coastline to Phú Quốc, out in Vietnamese waters. In 1993 the government made the whole massif Preah Monivong National Park — more than 1,500 square kilometres holding Asian elephants, pileated gibbons, gaur, banteng, dholes, Asian black bears and green peafowl.

## Three Kinds of Sacred

The plateau carries an unusually layered set of shrines for one mountaintop. Wat Sampov Pram, built in 1924 on royal orders, sits near the escarpment edge, one of the first structures the Cambodian monarchy put on this mountain. A short distance away stands the Yeay Mao monument — a 29-metre statue raised in 2010 to the protective spirit whom Cambodian travellers along this coast have long invoked for safe passage, and the tallest such figure in the country. And there is the Black Palace, Damnak Sla Khmao, the summer residence the Cambodian monarchy built here in the 1930s. Buddhist temple, Catholic church, royal villa and French casino, all within a few kilometres of each other, all of them at various points empty. Bokor collects the ambitions of whoever is currently in charge of Cambodia.

## The Newest City in Kampot

The current ambition is the largest yet. The Sokimex Group holds a 99-year lease on the site; it rebuilt the mountain road — National Road 32, 32 kilometres of switchbacks up from the Kampot lowlands, originally a French alignment — and finished the new sealed surface in December 2011, opening the Thansur Bokor Highland Resort the following year against a redevelopment budget reported at around a billion US dollars. Then came the administrative act. Three communes were carved out of Tuek Chhou district to create Bokor City, home to roughly 23,000 people in about 5,000 families as of 2021, and a government master plan set out to turn the plateau into a smart and green city by 2035. Residential estates are being laid out at a startling scale: Morakot Thansur alone plans 1,300 villas across 88 hectares, and Crown Estates covers 590. A 61-kilometre road is pushing toward National Highway 4 and the coastal corridor. Rights and environmental groups have objected loudly to the land clearing and to city boundaries drawn inside a national park — the massif lost roughly nine percent of its primary forest between 2001 and 2019. A century after the first road went up this mountain, the argument about what Bokor is for has never actually been settled.

## Flight Context

Bokor sits on a plateau at 10.642203°N, 104.027395°E, about 37-40 km west of Kampot in southern Cambodia, with the summit of Phnom Bokor at 1,081 m (3,547 ft). The terrain does the navigating for you: a long escarpment wall rises abruptly from the coastal plain and then flattens into open plateau, an unmistakable step in the landscape when approaching from the Gulf of Thailand. Look for the pale bulk of the Thansur Bokor resort and the smaller ruins of the old hill station near the western rim, with National Road 32 switchbacking up the southern face. Sihanouk International Airport (VDSV / KOS) lies about 60 km west; Phnom Penh International (VDPP) roughly 110 km north-north-east; Phu Quoc International (VVPQ) about 60 km south across the water. Terrain clearance matters here — plan on 5,000 feet or better. From May to October the plateau is often capped in cloud with visibility down to a few dozen metres on the ground, and the dry season from December to March offers the only reliable chance of seeing the coast and Phú Quốc from the rim.
