# Koh Seh

Horse Island: uninhabited, mangrove-ringed, the far southeastern corner of Ream National Park — and routinely confused with a more famous island sixty kilometres away.

**Location:** 10.4167, 103.8000

**Categories:** Islands of Cambodia, Islands of the Gulf of Thailand, Protected areas, Conservation, Wildlife

## Introduction

Some islands are famous by accident. Koh Seh is the opposite: it is anonymous by accident. Look the name up and you will mostly find a different island — Koh Ach Seh, some sixty kilometres east in the Kep Archipelago, where a conservation outfit drops concrete blocks onto the seabed to snag illegal trawl nets and collected one of the first National Geographic marine protection prizes for the idea in June 2018. The mix-up is common enough that reference works now carry a note warning against it. The real Koh Seh does nothing so newsworthy. It sits uninhabited at the far southeastern corner of Ream National Park, ringed almost entirely in mangrove, and its Khmer name means Horse Island.

## The End of the Park

Koh Seh covers 7.5 square kilometres — 4.4 kilometres long, between 1.8 and 4.4 wide — and carries fifteen kilometres of coastline for a landmass you could cross on foot in an afternoon. That ratio is the whole description: the shape is all inlets, points and tidal creeks rather than any clean outline. It lies a kilometre and a half south of Koh Thmei and about nine kilometres off the mainland at Sihanoukville's Ream commune, and it marks the southeastern limit of Ream National Park, established in 1993 across 210 square kilometres, 150 of them land and 60 sea. Less than four hundred metres to the southwest sits Koh Ky, an islet small enough that most charts give it a dot and leave it there. The French knew Koh Seh as Île à l'Eau, a name that now survives only on old maps.

## Four Point Three Kilometres

Southeast of the island the water opens into a sound 4.3 kilometres wide, and on the far side of it is Vietnam. Phú Quốc is one of the stranger outcomes of colonial cartography — an island lying far closer to Cambodia than to the Vietnamese mainland, and Vietnamese anyway, because in 1939 a French governor-general ruled a line across these waters and the line outlived the empire that drew it. The boundary stayed contested through independence and the wars that followed, and was not physically demarcated on the ground until 2012. None of that is history to the fishing crews working these shallows. It is a line you stay on the correct side of or explain afterwards, drawn through water that fish, turtles and dugongs cross without asking permission from either government.

## What the Mangrove Is Doing

Mangrove fringes nearly all of Koh Seh's shore, and that is the island's actual job. Stilt roots slow the water, drop sediment and build the murky shallows where juvenile fish, crab and shrimp can grow without being eaten in the first week; fisheries along this entire coast depend on nurseries like it. Ream National Park exists in large part to keep them intact. Within its boundaries live rhesus macaques in the forest, dugongs and dolphins offshore, marine turtles on the seagrass, and Sarus cranes in the wetlands — a list that holds together only as long as the mangrove does. Nearly thirty thousand people live in the five communes that overlap or border the park, across roughly 5,500 households, and that population climbs by about three percent a year. The park can support a limited level of use. The demand on it is not similarly limited.

## The Uses of Being Overlooked

So Koh Seh sits where it has always sat: no village, no pier worth the name, no recognition, quietly absorbing the credit and the blame owed to an island sixty kilometres east. Its neighbour Koh Thmei, a kilometre and a half north, has been sliced into development zones on paper and assigned to a casino company. Phú Quốc, across the sound, has an international airport, a safari park and a population past 150,000. Koh Seh has 7.5 square kilometres of mangrove, forest and coarse sand, and the singular advantage that nobody has yet drawn up a compelling reason to change it. On this coastline, in this decade, being mistaken for somewhere else may be the most effective protection an island can get.

## Flight Context

Koh Seh lies at 10.417°N, 103.800°E in the Gulf of Thailand, 1.5 km south of Koh Thmei and about 9 km south of the Cambodian mainland at Ream commune. It is low, forested and mangrove-fringed, roughly 4.4 km across with a heavily indented 15 km coastline, and marks the southeastern boundary of Ream National Park. The tiny islet of Koh Ky sits under 400 m to its southwest — a useful pair to identify from the air. There is no habitation, no lighting and no runway on the island, so it is a purely visual daytime waypoint. Immediately southeast, a 4.3 km sound separates Cambodian from Vietnamese waters; Phú Quốc's forested northern shore is the next land on that heading. Sihanouk International Airport (VDSV / KOS) is roughly 30 km northwest; Phu Quoc International (VVPQ / PQC) about 25 km southeast. Verify boundary clearances before transiting the sound. Fly 1,500–3,000 ft to pick out the mangrove creeks; visibility is best November through March, with the July–September monsoon bringing low cloud and rain squalls.
