# Presidential Office Building, Taipei

Designed by a Japanese architect, bombed by the Allies, restored by 81,000 workers, and renamed to honor a dictator's birthday, Taipei's Presidential Office Building is a Baroque monument to every power that has claimed the island.

**Location:** 25.0400, 121.5119

**Categories:** government, architecture, history, landmarks, Taiwan

## Introduction

The facade faces east, straight down the length of Ketagalan Boulevard, oriented toward the rising sun in the manner Japanese architects reserved for buildings of supreme importance. From the air, the Presidential Office Building's footprint describes two interlocking squares, and its 60-meter central tower -- once the tallest structure in the Taipei Basin -- still anchors the visual axis of downtown Taipei. Built to house a colonial governor-general, bombed nearly to rubble in the final months of World War II, and restored to serve the president of a government in exile, the building embodies every layer of Taiwan's contested modern history in a single Baroque shell.

## A Colonial Headquarters Rising

When Japan assumed control of Taiwan in 1895, the first governor-general set up temporary quarters in a former Qing dynasty secretariat. Plans for a permanent headquarters began almost immediately. A two-stage architectural design competition was held in 1906 and 1910, and the winning entry by architect Uheiji Nagano blended Renaissance, Baroque, and neoclassical European elements with the colonial tradition of east-facing facades. Tokyo authorities revised Nagano's plans before construction began, increasing the central tower from six stories to eleven and adding defensive improvements to the corner towers. Construction started on June 1, 1912, and finished on March 31, 1919, at a cost of 2.8 million Japanese yen. The finished building became one of the most recognizable structures in colonial Taiwan.

## Three Days of Fire

On May 31, 1945, during the Allied Raid on Taihoku, bombs struck the front left side, main lobby, and northern sections of the Governor-General's Office. The resulting fire burned for three days, gutting large portions of the building. Forty-five days later, Japan surrendered. The ruin sat untouched until 1947, when the Taiwan Provincial Government launched a restoration funded entirely through private donations. Approximately 81,000 workers participated in the reconstruction, which was completed by the end of 1948. The restored building looked only slightly different from the original -- most notably, the ornate Baroque entrance hall was rebuilt with simpler interior features. Because the restoration coincided with Chiang Kai-shek's 60th birthday, the building was renamed Chieh Shou Hall, a name it carried until 2006.

## Seat of a Government in Exile

In mid-1949, as the Chinese Civil War turned decisively against the Nationalists, the building began serving as a military affairs office. When the Republic of China government relocated its capital to Taipei, the structure became the Office of the President in 1950. The transition was more than administrative -- it transformed a colonial building designed to project Japanese imperial authority into the symbolic center of a Chinese government that no longer controlled the Chinese mainland. The Nationalists built a platform atop the central tower for martial flag-raising ceremonies, overlaying their own political rituals onto the Japanese architectural framework below. Former President Chen Shui-bian later revealed that a hidden tunnel connects the building to the Shilin Official Residence, adding a layer of Cold War intrigue to an already densely storied structure.

## The Building as Living Monument

Today the Presidential Office Building occupies the city block between Chongqing South Road and Bo'ai Road, its 130-meter-wide facade commanding the full width of Ketagalan Boulevard. The west gate features a grand marble staircase lined with Ionic and Corinthian columns. The office wings contain balconies and long corridors overlooking the sunlit North and South Gardens. Within walking distance stand the Judicial Yuan Building, 228 Memorial Park, the National Taiwan Museum, the National Theater and Concert Hall, and the historic Ximending shopping district. In 2019, the building opened its doors to international overnight guests through a program called "Spend A Night at Taiwan's Presidential Office Building," selected participants from eleven countries sleeping inside a structure that has sheltered colonial governors, wartime commanders, and democratically elected presidents. The program ran again in 2023, a gesture of openness from a building long associated with guarded power.
