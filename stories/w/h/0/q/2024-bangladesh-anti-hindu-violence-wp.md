# 2024 Bangladesh Anti-Hindu Violence

In the chaotic aftermath of Sheikh Hasina's resignation in August 2024, Bangladesh's Hindu minority faced a wave of targeted attacks - more than 2,000 incidents in 16 days by one Hindu advocacy group's count, including assaults on temples, homes, and businesses, though the true scale and how much was religious rather than political remain disputed.

## Introduction

On 5 August 2024, Sheikh Hasina resigned as Prime Minister of Bangladesh, ending the Awami League's years of rule after a fierce anti-government movement that had begun as student protests over job quotas. Within hours, mobs turned on a different target. Across the country, Hindu homes, businesses, and places of worship came under coordinated and opportunistic attack. The Bangladesh Hindu Buddhist Christian Unity Council documented 2,010 incidents against minorities between 4 and 20 August alone - a rate of more than 125 per day. Sixty-nine temples were attacked. Five Hindus were killed, at least two of whom were confirmed Awami League members, blurring the line between communal and political violence.

## When Political Upheaval Becomes Communal Violence

The July 2024 quota reform movement began with a specific demand: changing the system that reserved government jobs for descendants of 1971 freedom fighters, a policy that many young Bangladeshis saw as outdated patronage. But the movement rapidly evolved into a broader insurrection against what protesters described as Awami League authoritarianism, corruption, and democratic backsliding. When Hasina fell, the political vacuum created space for violence against perceived supporters of the old regime. Bangladesh's Hindu minority, roughly 8 percent of the population, had long been associated - fairly or not - with the Awami League. That association made them targets, though the violence was not exclusively political. Some attacks bore the hallmarks of opportunistic land grabbing and personal score-settling under cover of political chaos.

## A Geography of Attacks

The violence was nationwide and indiscriminate in its geography, striking from Barisal Division in the south to Rajshahi in the northwest. In Madhukhali Upazila of Faridpur District, attackers targeted Hindu homes and businesses, damaged a Kali temple, and assaulted a Hindu homeopathy doctor. In Kalapara Upazila of Patuakhali, a Hindu family was threatened with eviction and had their land confiscated - the victim alleged that a local BNP secretary was responsible. In Mohanpur Upazila of Rajshahi, the home of a Puja Celebration Committee secretary was attacked. The attacks followed a grim pattern: homes and shops looted or burned, temples vandalized, families threatened with expulsion from the country. The victims were shopkeepers, doctors, community organizers, and families who had lived in their neighborhoods for generations.

## Truth and Misinformation

The violence was real, but it also became fuel for a misinformation crisis. BBC News, Deutsche Welle, France 24, and multiple fact-checking organizations documented a flood of rumors circulating on X and other social media platforms, some exaggerating the violence and some misattributing it. In December 2024, locals in Sirajganj destroyed a Sufi shrine - an act of intra-Muslim sectarianism - that went viral on social media falsely labeled as the destruction of a Hindu temple. In January 2025, claims spread online that Hindus had been banned from government employment. The interim government's press wing refuted the claim, stating that "The Bangladesh government does not discriminate against any citizen based on religion." The misinformation made an already volatile situation harder to assess, weaponizing genuine suffering for political narratives on all sides.

## Minorities in the Crossfire

Bangladesh's Hindu population has declined from roughly 22 percent at the time of partition in 1947 to approximately 8 percent today - a trajectory shaped by decades of episodic violence, discriminatory legislation like the Vested Property Act, and steady emigration to India. Each cycle of political upheaval carries the risk of communal targeting: the logic is consistent even when the pretexts change. In 2024, the pretext was guilt by political association. The people who paid the price were those most vulnerable to it: families whose religion made them visible, whose property made them targets, and whose lack of political power in the new order left them with no protector. The 2,010 documented incidents in 16 days were not abstractions. They were individual moments of terror experienced by real people in their homes, shops, and places of worship - people whose belonging in their own country was suddenly, violently questioned.

## Flight Context

Centered on Dhaka at 23.69°N, 90.36°E, though the violence was nationwide across Bangladesh. Hazrat Shahjalal International Airport (VGHS/DAC) serves Dhaka. From altitude, Bangladesh's flat, river-crossed landscape stretches to the horizon in every direction. The affected areas span from Barisal Division in the Ganges delta south to Rajshahi in the northwest, covering much of the country's densely populated alluvial plain. The density of settlements visible from the air - the patchwork of villages, towns, and agricultural land - makes tangible the human scale of violence that touched communities across this landscape.
