# 2021 Nagaland Killings

On a December evening in 2021, an Indian Army special forces unit ambushed a pickup truck carrying coal miners home to their village in Nagaland, killing six unarmed civilians and setting off a crisis that reached the floor of Parliament.

**Location:** 26.8450, 94.9575

**Categories:** human-rights, military-incidents, massacres, india-politics

## Introduction

They were coal miners riding home in an open-bed pickup truck. Six men from Oting village in Nagaland's Mon District, returning from the mines at Tiru on the evening of December 4, 2021, sometime between 4:00 and 5:00 p.m. A unit of the Indian Army's 21st Para Special Forces was waiting in ambush. The soldiers opened fire on the truck. Six of the men died. Two survived with serious injuries. By the time the violence ended, eight more civilians and one soldier were dead, and a state already scarred by decades of military operations was confronting its latest wound.

## A Law That Shields the Gun

The killings did not occur in a vacuum. Since 1958, the Armed Forces Special Powers Act (AFSPA) has given Indian military personnel broad authority to use lethal force in designated "disturbed areas," with legal protections that make prosecution of soldiers for human rights violations effectively impossible without central government approval. Nagaland, long marked by secessionist movements and armed insurgency, has lived under AFSPA for most of its existence as a state. The Indian government had extended the act across Nagaland for six months just that June, a decision made within a day of the chief minister's meeting with the home minister. The act's critics describe it as a license for impunity. Its defenders call it a necessary tool in regions with active insurgencies. For the people of Oting, a village of 1,266 where 99% belonged to scheduled tribes and the literacy rate stood at 29%, the distinction was academic.

## The Evening of December 4

The army claimed its soldiers had received credible intelligence about insurgent activity in the area and that they opened fire only when the pickup truck failed to stop on their instructions. The two survivors rejected this account. So did Nagaland Police after a preliminary inquiry, finding that the troops fired without attempting to establish the identities of their targets. When neighboring villagers, alarmed by the gunfire and the failure of their relatives to return home, arrived at the scene, they encountered a military convoy. The troops claimed to be transporting "injured people" to hospital and professed ignorance about the bloodied pickup truck nearby. Instead, investigators found, the soldiers were moving the bodies to their base camp and had been changing the clothing of the dead, an apparent attempt to stage the scene as a legitimate encounter with insurgents.

## Grief, Fury, and a Festival Silenced

The aftermath moved swiftly. The Nagaland government imposed Section 144 in Mon District, banning public assembly. Mobile internet and bulk SMS services were suspended. The Eastern Nagaland People's Organisation, representing six tribes, withdrew from the Hornbill Festival, which had been underway at Kisama Heritage Village. Candlelight marches filled the streets of Kohima and Dimapur. By December 7, the government suspended all festival activities entirely. On December 6, all fifteen civilian casualties were buried in a mass funeral led by Chief Minister Neiphiu Rio. The local village council issued an informal ban on Indian Army personnel entering Oting. Even members of the state's own BJP unit rejected the central government's framing. State party president Temjen Imna Along described the killings as "summary execution as well as genocide."

## Justice Denied, Questions Enduring

Home Minister Amit Shah expressed regret before Parliament, which was in session, and established a Special Investigation Team to complete its inquiry within a month. But he repeated the army's account in full. The gap between the central government's narrative and the testimony of survivors, local police, and the state's own political leadership was stark and unresolved. The incident reignited demands to repeal AFSPA. On December 7, the Government of Nagaland announced it would formally seek the act's repeal. The demand joined a long queue of similar calls stretching back decades. On September 17, 2024, the Supreme Court of India quashed criminal proceedings against the accused soldiers, citing the lack of government approval required under AFSPA for prosecution. For the families of the dead miners from Oting, the legal avenue was closed. The law designed to protect the state had, once again, shielded those who wielded its power from accountability for how they used it.

## Flight Context

Located at approximately 26.85°N, 94.96°E near Oting village in Mon District, the easternmost district of Nagaland in northeast India. The terrain is mountainous and heavily forested, near the Myanmar border. Nearest airport is Dimapur (VEDZ), roughly 150 km to the southwest. Mon town, the district capital, has limited air access. The area sits in one of India's most remote corners, with roads as the primary transport link. Visibility can be limited during monsoon months (May-September). The village of Oting is small and not visible from high altitude, but Mon District's mountainous terrain is distinctive.
