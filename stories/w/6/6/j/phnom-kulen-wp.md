# Phnom Kulen

Angkor was quarried out of this mountain and the Khmer Empire was proclaimed on top of it, which is why Cambodians still come up Phnom Kulen in pilgrimage to a reclining Buddha carved from a single sandstone boulder.

**Location:** 13.6128, 104.1125

**Categories:** sacred-mountains, national-parks, khmer-empire, pilgrimage, cambodia, archaeology

## Introduction

Angkor Wat is made of this mountain. Nearly every sandstone block in the great temple, and in most of the monuments around it, was cut from quarries on the flanks of Phnom Kulen and hauled roughly 35 kilometres across the plain - probably by canal, along a network that Japanese researchers traced from satellite imagery and then walked on the ground. The Khmer did not build where the stone was. They built where the water was, and then went and fetched the stone. But this was never an ordinary quarry hill. Phnom Kulen is where Cambodia says the Khmer Empire began, and twelve hundred years later people are still coming up the mountain to say so.

## Mountain of the Lychees

Phnom Kulen is not really a mountain range in the ordinary sense. It is an isolated chain of small flat-topped plateaux lying south of the Dangrek Mountains, running about 40 kilometres from west-northwest to east-southeast, roughly 48 kilometres north of Siem Reap and 30 north of Angkor Wat. The tabletop holds a remarkably consistent elevation - averaging around 400 metres all along the range, with a high point near 500 - so from the plain it reads as a long green wall rather than a peak. Its name means mountain of the lychees. Geologically it is sandstone, and the major Angkorian quarries were worked in the southeastern angle of the massif. The Samre, who lived at the mountain's edge, cut that stone and moved it to the royal sites; the empire's architecture rests on their labour as surely as on its kings.

## The Year 802

In 802 CE, on this mountain, Jayavarman II was consecrated as chakravartin - universal monarch - and proclaimed his independence from the power the inscriptions call Java. The account comes from the Sdok Kak Thom inscription, which records a ritual performed by the brahmin Hiranyadama alongside the chief priest Sivakaivalya, and describes the founding of the devaraja cult, the linga cult of the god-king, dated to 804. Scholars have argued for a century about what "Java" meant here, since the Khmer word chvea was also used for Champa, and about the legend that the young Jayavarman had been held there as a hostage. The disputes have not dented the date. During the Angkorian period the massif itself was called Mahendraparvata, the mountain of the great Indra, and the city Jayavarman built on the plateau took the same name.

## The Buddha in the Boulder

At the top of the mountain sits Preah Ang Thom, a Buddhist monastery of the 16th century, and the reason most Cambodians come. Inside a modern shelter, reached by a staircase, an eight-metre reclining Buddha lies carved directly out of a single natural sandstone boulder - the largest reclining Buddha in the country. Two enormous champa trees stand nearby. This is the working religious heart of Phnom Kulen, not a ruin: Hindus and Buddhists alike come here in pilgrimage, the crowds thicken around Khmer New Year, and the incense smoke never really clears. The mountain carries an unusual double weight in Cambodian life. It is a sacred summit and it is a national origin story, and on a busy day it is difficult to tell which of the two has brought any given family up the hill.

## Carved Water

Water is the mountain's other language. Two waterfalls draw most visitors, a modest one of four or five metres dropping into a shallow pond and a bigger one of fifteen to twenty metres pouring into a pool people swim in; both run hard in the rains and thin in the dry months. Chup Preah threads its own valley. Elsewhere across the plateau the sandstone has been worked into things easy to walk past - Srah Damrei, a large elephant sculpted in the round with smaller companions, dated to the 8th or 9th century; the rock faces at Peung Tbal near Anlong Thom bearing Vishnu, Shiva, Brahma and Ganesh; Peung Aysey deeper in the jungle, remembered as a meditation place for rishis. On the southwest slopes of the same Kulen range, though reached by a separate road and usually treated as a site of its own, lies Kbal Spean, where more than a thousand small lingas are cut into the bed of the Stung Kbal Spean, a few centimetres under running water. The carving there began under Suryavarman I and was largely finished under Udayadityavarman II, who consecrated a golden linga at the site in 1059. The ethnologist Jean Boulbet identified it in 1969; war closed it again until 1989.

## A Difficult Modern Century

Phnom Kulen's remoteness, so useful to a ninth-century king, made it useful again to the Khmer Rouge, who held the mountain as a final stronghold as their regime collapsed in 1979. The legacy was mines, and the archaeologists who worked the plateau decades later still counted them among the hazards. Phnom Kulen National Park was established in 1993 across 373.76 square kilometres, and the site was placed on Cambodia's national Tentative List for World Heritage on 27 March 2020, having first been proclaimed on 1 December 1992, for nomination under criteria (iii), (iv) and (v). Heritage status has a cost that falls unevenly. In 2016, as part of that effort, more than 300 families were moved off crowded parts of the mountain while tourist facilities were upgraded - people who had made their lives on the plateau, relocated so that the plateau could be protected.

## Flight Context

Phnom Kulen lies at roughly 13.613 N, 104.113 E, about 48 km north of Siem Reap and 30 km north of Angkor Wat. It is the most legible terrain feature for a long way in any direction: an isolated 40 km chain of flat-topped plateaux oriented WNW-ESE, plateau surface near 400 m with a high point around 500 m, standing alone above an otherwise flat plain. Escarpment edges are steep and the top is heavily forested. Suggested viewing altitude 3,000-5,000 ft AGL, giving comfortable terrain separation and a good angle on the tabletop. Nearest major field is Siem Reap-Angkor International (VDSA); Phnom Penh International (VDPP) is the alternate. From May to October expect orographic cloud on the plateau and vigorous afternoon convection; the November-February dry season gives the clearest air and the sharpest view of the Angkor barays to the south.
