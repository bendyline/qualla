# Boeng Tonle Chhmar

For half the year this Ramsar wetland on the Tonlé Sap is a forest. For the other half you can paddle a boat through the tops of its trees.

**Location:** 12.8189, 104.2764

**Categories:** Wetlands, Ramsar sites, Protected areas, Cambodia, Birdwatching, Tonlé Sap

## Introduction

In October, if you take a boat out from Peam Bang on the northern rim of the Tonlé Sap, you can steer through the crowns of trees. The trunks are somewhere below the hull, invisible in water stained the colour of strong tea, and the branches you push aside were shading dry ground in April. This is Boeng Tonle Chhmar, a wetland of about 146 square kilometres where the annual Mekong flood does something no other landscape quite manages — it does not merely inundate a forest, it converts one into open water and then, six months later, hands it back.

## A Forest That Drowns on Purpose

The flooded forest is the Tonlé Sap's most important habitat and its least photogenic. The trees are short, tangled, and unremarkable to look at in the dry season, which is precisely why so much of it has been cut. What they do is structural. Their trunks and roots slow the incoming flood, dropping sediment and organic matter into a shallow, warm, oxygen-rich soup. Fish move in behind the water to spawn among the submerged branches, and their fry stay there through the high months, feeding and hidden. Nearly everything the lake produces — and it produces enough fish to supply most of the animal protein in the Cambodian diet — begins in drowned undergrowth like this. Boeng Chhmar's flooded forest is among the best-preserved stretches left.

## The Bird Cities

The colonies are the other reason this place is protected. Painted storks nest here in numbers, their pink-flushed wings unmistakable when a flock lifts off a shoal. Spot-billed pelicans work the shallows in ragged lines. Oriental darters — the snakebird, which swims with only its neck above water — hang in the trees with wings spread to dry. Indian cormorants, black-necked storks and brahminy kites all breed or forage in the area. Most striking are the adjutants: the lesser adjutant is threatened across its range, and the greater adjutant, a huge, bare-necked, faintly disreputable-looking stork, is one of the rarest in the world, clinging on in only a handful of places, of which Cambodia is one.

## Three Words on a Convention

Boeng Chhmar was designated a Wetland of International Importance under the Ramsar Convention on 23 June 1999, registered as site number 997 — one of Cambodia's first. That same year it was gazetted domestically as a multiple use management area of 145.6 square kilometres. It also serves as one of the core zones of the Tonlé Sap Biosphere Reserve, which UNESCO had established two years earlier in 1997. Three overlapping designations is a lot of paper for a place most Cambodians have never visited, and the layering reflects genuine difficulty: this is not a wilderness to be fenced off. People live inside it, and the water it protects belongs to a river system governed by six countries.

## Five Villages That Move

The protected area sits within Peam Bang commune, in Stoung district of Kampong Thom province, and the commune holds five floating villages — Pov Veuy, Pechakrei, Peam Bang, Ba Lat and Doun Sdaeng. Their houses are built on rafts or on stilts tall enough to clear a ten-metre rise, and some of them shift position with the shoreline as it advances and retreats each year. School, clinic, shop and monastery all float. The people here fish the same forest the designation exists to protect, which puts them permanently on the wrong side of enforcement conversations they rarely get to shape. Conservation on the Tonlé Sap has a long record of treating residents as the problem. The households in these five villages have a considerably longer record of living off a flood without exhausting it.

## What the Flood Still Has to Deliver

Everything about Boeng Chhmar depends on a pulse arriving on schedule and reaching a particular height. Dams upstream on the Mekong and its tributaries have begun flattening that pulse, holding water back through the wet season and releasing it in the dry, and in July 2020 the lake's level hit a sixty-year low for that point in the year. A smaller flood means less forest submerged, fewer spawning grounds, fewer fry, fewer fish. Meanwhile the forest itself keeps being cleared for farmland and fuelwood, with net losses recorded inside Tonlé Sap protected areas between 1992 and 2019. A wetland can be listed under an international convention, mapped, zoned and patrolled, and still fail — because the thing that made it is 500 kilometres upstream and answers to somebody else.

## Flight Context

Boeng Tonle Chhmar lies at 12.819°N, 104.276°E, on the northeastern shore of the Tonlé Sap in Kampong Thom province, close to the lake's centre of mass. Finding it depends entirely on season. From roughly December through April it appears as a distinct lake and creek system set behind the main shoreline, edged by darker, denser flooded forest that contrasts sharply against the pale, cracked mud of the drained floodplain. From August through November it disappears into the enlarged Tonlé Sap, and the only clue is texture — the submerged canopy scatters light differently from open water, producing a mottled, olive-toned patch where the forest is. Best viewed from 4,000 to 10,000 feet; lower passes reveal wading-bird colonies and the floating villages of Peam Bang commune as tight clusters of rafts. Terrain is flat and near sea level. Nearest airports are Siem Reap–Angkor International (VDSA) about 45 nm northwest, Phnom Penh International (VDPP) some 90 nm southeast, and Battambang (VDBG) to the west. Best visibility runs December to February; expect haze and afternoon convection during the southwest monsoon and smoke from burning in March and April.
