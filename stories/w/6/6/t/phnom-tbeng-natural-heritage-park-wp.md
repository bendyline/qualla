# Phnom Tbeng Natural Heritage Park

A 600-metre mountain in Preah Vihear province holds the headwaters of the largest river feeding the Tonlé Sap — and some of the last evergreen forest in northern Cambodia.

**Location:** 13.6979, 104.8960

**Categories:** Protected areas of Cambodia, Natural heritage sites of Cambodia, Protected areas established in 2016, Watersheds, Tonlé Sap

## Introduction

Rain that lands on the ridge of Phnom Tbeng has a long journey ahead of it. It drains off the mountain's flanks into the headwaters of the Stung Sen, a river that runs some 520 kilometres south and west through Preah Vihear and Kampong Thom before delivering its water to the Tonlé Sap. The mountain itself is unassuming — roughly 600 metres, rising roughly a dozen kilometres southwest of the provincial capital, the sort of low green massif that a pilot would note and forget. Its importance has nothing to do with its height and everything to do with where its water goes. In September 2016, Cambodia's government put that in writing.

## The Largest of Eleven

Eleven main tributaries feed the Tonlé Sap, and the Stung Sen basin is the biggest of them, draining 16,344 square kilometres that collect around 1,500 millimetres of rain a year. Downstream, that water joins the strangest hydrological arrangement in Southeast Asia. For six months the Tonlé Sap river carries water out of the lake toward the Mekong; for six months the Mekong's flood is strong enough to shove the river into reverse and push water back in. The lake swells from roughly 2,500 square kilometres to 16,000, deepening from about a metre to somewhere between nine and fourteen. Into that flooded forest comes one of the most productive inland fisheries on Earth — around 400,000 tonnes of fish a year, some sixty per cent of Cambodia's freshwater catch, and the livelihood of roughly 1.2 million people. Watersheds are abstractions until you follow one all the way down.

## Sub-Decree No. 189

On 13 September 2016, Sub-decree No. 189 established Phnom Tbeng as a natural heritage site — one of eight categories in Cambodia's protected-area framework, which also covers national parks, wildlife sanctuaries, protected landscapes, multiple-use management areas, Ramsar sites, biosphere reserves and marine parks. The designation covers 25,269 hectares, a little over 252 square kilometres. Inside that boundary sit some of the last remaining evergreen and semi-evergreen forest habitats in northern Cambodia: closed canopy, humid understorey, the kind of forest that holds water in the ground through the dry months and releases it slowly rather than all at once. Protect the mountain and you are not only protecting what lives on it. You are protecting the timing of a river.

## Four Days in December

In December 2017 a team spread out across the park with 24 camera traps and a map of pre-selected locations. The deployment took four days. "With good collaboration between stakeholders, we completed the camera trap deployment in PTNHP over four days," said Mao Khean, wildlife research project coordinator for the Wildlife Conservation Society, which ran the survey with the Ministry of Environment and local communities. Funding came from the Rainforest Trust, the Arcus Foundation and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service. The park was already known to hold pileated gibbons, bears and Indochinese silvered langurs; the cameras were there to find out what else remained, and in what numbers, among the small and medium-sized mammals nobody had ever counted. The researchers' conclusion was blunt. Excessive deforestation and poaching significantly endanger the survival of many mammals here.

## The Company It Keeps

Phnom Tbeng does not stand alone. It belongs to Cambodia's Northern Plains Landscape, more than half a million hectares of Ministry of Environment protected areas that also take in the Kulen Promtep, Prey Preah Roka and Chhep wildlife sanctuaries. Between them these reserves hold over 260 bird species — white-shouldered ibis and white-winged duck among the rarest — along with Asian elephant, banteng and gaur. The pressures scale up alongside the acreage, running from a villager's snare line to commercial mineral extraction, and the honest problem is arithmetic: the protected network is far larger than the number of people paid to patrol it.

## A Nomination and a Town

Cambodia has weighed Phnom Tbeng against four other candidates for World Heritage nomination — the Mekong's Prek Kampi dolphin area, Phnom Nam Lear Rock inside Phnom Nam Lyr Wildlife Sanctuary, Prek Prasab Wildlife Sanctuary, and Southern Cardamom National Park. The Ministry of Environment intends to put the park forward as a natural site paired with the Anlong Kampi Irrawaddy dolphin conservation area. Whether that ever happens, the mountain's more immediate audience is the town at its foot: Tbeng Meanchey, the provincial capital, home to 24,360 people at the 2019 census, who look up at a mountain that fills their skyline and holds the top of their river.

## Flight Context

Centred near 13.698°N, 104.896°E, about 75 km northeast of Siem Reap–Angkor International (VDSA/SAI) and roughly 13 km southwest of Tbeng Meanchey town. The massif tops out near 600 m, so 4,000–7,000 ft AGL gives the best read on the contrast between the dark closed canopy on the slopes and the more open country beyond the boundary. From the north the Dângrêk escarpment along the Thai border is visible on clear days; to the south the ground falls away toward the Tonlé Sap floodplain. The nearest strip, Thbeng Meanchey (OMY), is abandoned — treat this as overflight country, not a destination. Expect heavy convective build-ups over the ridge on monsoon afternoons from June through October, and dense agricultural haze in March and April.
