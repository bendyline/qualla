# Don Det

A farming island seven kilometres around that became Southeast Asia's most famous place to do nothing, where the boats leave when they are full and the electricity only recently stopped going off.

**Location:** 13.9731, 105.9228

**Categories:** islands, laos, rivers, travel, village-life

## Introduction

The boat leaves when the boat is full. That is the entire timetable. At the landing in Ban Nakasang a longtail sits low against the bank with a car engine bolted to a pole for a propeller, and the boatman waits, because a single passenger pays forty thousand kip and two passengers pay twenty thousand each. So you wait too. Nobody is rude about it and nobody is in a hurry, and after twenty minutes on the water — brown current, drowned trees, a heron lifting off a sandbar — you step onto Don Det at Ban Hua Det and discover that the waiting was the orientation session.

## Two Nights, or Possibly Years

Don Det is small: a walking path runs 7.2 kilometres right around it and takes about two hours at an unhurried pace. That has not stopped it from becoming one of the best-known backpacker islands in Southeast Asia. The visitors skew young, mostly twenties and thirties, and the arithmetic of their stays is famously unreliable. Some book two nights. Some are still here in week five. A handful never left at all and now run bars. The comparison everyone reaches for is Vang Vieng, the tubing town up north, but the resemblance is thin — Don Det has no adrenaline industry and no real nightlife, because bars shut around eleven under a nationwide curfew. What it has instead is a hammock, a river going past at walking speed, and the complete absence of any reason to get up.

## The Lights Came Late

For most of the island's life as a destination, the modern conveniences simply were not here, and their arrival has been staggered and incomplete. Don Det now has twenty-four-hour electricity — a genuinely recent development, and one the guidebooks still mention with a note of surprise — though the power does drop out from time to time. There is still no ATM anywhere on the island. Shops at the northern end will advance you dollars or kip against a Visa or Mastercard, at commissions running from six to ten percent, which is the sort of exchange rate that concentrates the mind. The actual banks are back across the water at Ban Nakasang, open half a day, and most guesthouses will run you over and wait while you queue. Wi-Fi, meanwhile, is everywhere and advertised as fast. The island got the internet before it got a cash machine.

## Sunrise Side, Sunset Side

Choosing where to sleep on Don Det means choosing a shore, and the two are not interchangeable. The sunrise side faces east; its road is paved, more or less footpath width, and the morning sun that hits those bungalows is gentler than the afternoon sun that bakes the other bank. The sunset side is unpaved and perfectly rideable, and the sunsets are worth the heat — the Mekong turns copper and the silhouetted islands stack up downstream like cutouts. Geography sorts the crowd too. The north end around Hua Det is dense with guesthouses and starting to feel generic. Head south along either shore and the buildings thin out into rice plots, banana stands and small farms, and the people you pass are going about a working day rather than a holiday. At night, in the unlit stretches, bioluminescent beetles drift through the palms.

## The Ride to the Bridge

Rent a bicycle for around ten thousand kip and the island opens up in an hour. The classic ride runs the length of Don Det to its southern tip, where a French colonial bridge — built for a railway that stopped running in 1940 — carries you across to Don Khon. It is a short, strange crossing on a piece of nineteenth-century infrastructure that has outlived its entire purpose, and it delivers you onto a quieter island with a waterfall two kilometres further south. Come back at dusk. On Don Det itself there is a Buddhist temple and two primary schools at the north end, and beyond that the sights are cattle in the lane, water buffalo standing chest-deep in a channel, and geckos ticking on every wall after dark.

## Please Don't Rush

The joke printed on menus across the island turns Lao PDR into "Please Don't Rush," and it is offered as a warning as much as a slogan. Food takes an hour. It is also, by common agreement, some of the weakest cooking in Laos, because the kitchens are aimed squarely at foreigners: nearly identical menus of burgers, pasta, fried rice and a couple of Lao dishes, priced so low it is hard to spend three dollars on dinner. Beerlao comes in big bottles for around ten thousand kip and is sold roughly every two hundred metres. Rice whisky, lao lao, arrives in a reused water bottle. It is worth remembering, between the shakes and the pancakes, that Don Det is a village first. The families running the guesthouses farm the land behind them, the boats on the landing are working boats, and the tourist economy is a layer laid over an older one that never stopped.

## Flight Context

Don Det sits at 13.9731°N, 105.9228°E, an island roughly 2 km long inside the Si Phan Don archipelago of Champasak Province, southern Laos. From 4,000-8,000 ft it reads as an elongated green teardrop paired with Don Khon immediately to its south, the two linked by a thin pale line — the old French railway bridge, the only man-made structure visible from altitude. The Khone Phapheng cataract shows as a white band about 6 km east. Nearest airport is Pakse International (VLPS/PKZ, elevation 351 ft) roughly 130 km north; Ubon Ratchathani (VTUU) in Thailand serves the wider region. Attapeu (VLAP) and Stung Treng (VDST) are both closed to scheduled traffic. Dry season, November to March, offers the clearest air and the sharpest island definition; during the June-October monsoon the surrounding low islands submerge and the archipelago pattern simplifies considerably.
