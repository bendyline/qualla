# Don Khon

A Mekong island carrying the trackbed of a seven-kilometre colonial portage railway, built so French gunboats could be taken apart, carried past the falls, and put back together on the other side.

**Location:** 13.9552, 105.9260

**Categories:** islands, railways, colonial-history, laos, engineering

## Introduction

Under a tin shed at Ban Khon, on a short piece of track lifted onto a concrete plinth to keep her wheels out of the floodwater, sits a rusting steam engine called Eloïse. Orenstein & Koppel built her in Germany in 1911. She is the only steam locomotive left anywhere in Laos, and she is parked beside a line that carried its last train in 1940 — seven kilometres of narrow-gauge track laid across two river islands for one reason: a waterfall that the French Empire could not get around, could not blast through, and would not accept.

## Seven Kilometres of Empire

The French administration of Indochina wanted the Mekong to be a highway. The governor described it as the point of connection binding Cochin-China, Cambodia, Laos and Tonkin together, and beyond that lay the real prize — a river route into southern China, and a way to check British expansion in Upper Burma. One obstacle stood in the way, and it stood exactly here. At the bottom of the Si Phan Don archipelago the river breaks apart across the Khone Phapheng ledges. Steamers tried to climb it in 1891, in 1892, and again in 1893, and every attempt failed. A British tidal expert living in Siam, Herbert Warington Smyth, suggested a canal with locks and noted acidly that it would cost roughly what the Manchester Ship Canal had cost while carrying perhaps a ten-thousandth of the traffic. The French chose the cheaper absurdity instead: a portage railway, on which boats would be dismantled, carried overland, and reassembled upstream.

## Laid, Lifted, Laid Again

The first four kilometres went down on Don Khon in 1893, running from Ban Hangkhon at the island's southeastern corner north-west toward Ban Khon. For its first four years the line was not really a line at all. Track was laid in segments, lifted once the train had passed over, and carried forward to be laid again ahead of it — the railway advancing across the island by consuming and rebuilding itself. There was no locomotive yet. The wagons carrying the sectioned hulls of the gun-sloops Lagrandière, Ham Luong and Massie, and later the Garcerie, Colombert and Trentinian, were hauled by hand. That work was done by labourers brought in from Vietnam, moved into southern Laos as French Indochina moved people wherever it needed them, and it was done in heat that visiting Europeans found close to unbearable while merely sitting down. In 1910 a French traveller named Marthe Bassenne crossed the island as a passenger and recorded the train "struggling and grating amid the clashing sound of steel," along with the sun, the mosquitoes and the exhaustion. Her worst afternoon was their working day.

## Paul Doumer, Seven Tonnes

A permanent railway was in place by 1897, laid to a 600-millimetre gauge and possibly widened later toward metre gauge; the equipment came from Decauville by way of Cochinchina. With it came steam, and manpower was retired from traction. The first locomotive weighed seven tonnes, burned wood, and was christened Paul Doumer after the Governor-General who held office from 1897 to 1902. A train ran to a maximum of twelve cars, and carrying a single vessel took roughly two of them. At the northern end passengers stepped off and boarded a steamship in the channel between the islands. Because the specially designed boats could only run when the river was high, the line was extended three kilometres onto Don Det during the 1910s, finishing at a pier near Ban Khon. The Second World War appears to have ended it. The last train reportedly ran in 1940.

## Walking the Trackbed

The rails are gone, but almost everything else survives, including a 170-metre concrete viaduct. What is left is a causeway of compacted ballast and sand that now functions as the island's main road, and the bridge at the northern end is how most people arrive on foot or by bicycle from Don Det. Follow the trackbed south and it delivers you to the Old French Port, where an elevated platform gives a long view down the Mekong toward Cambodia. Two kilometres south of the bridge, Li Phi Falls drop through a chaos of black rock and white water. Nearby, Khon Tai is a crescent of sand called a beach with more optimism than accuracy. A path circles the island in about thirteen kilometres, past two Buddhist temples, a primary school and a college, and past the boats that are the whole point of the river for the families living here.

## No Longer the Only One

For over a century, the line on these two islands could be described as the only railway ever built, opened and operated in Laos, and railway enthusiasts described it exactly that way. It is not true anymore. In 2009 a short link opened from Nong Khai in Thailand across to Thanaleng station near Vientiane, and on 3 December 2021 the country got something on an entirely different scale — the 422-kilometre standard-gauge Boten–Vientiane line running from the Chinese border to the capital. Don Khon's own revival never happened. In December 2005 the Vientiane Times reported plans to reopen the railway for tourists by 2007 at a cost of about 1.5 million US dollars; a South Korean construction company signed a memorandum of understanding and then walked away. Better roads and airports had already made river freight beside the point. So the trackbed stays a footpath, Eloïse stays under her shed, and the first railway in Laos is now firmly the oldest one.

## Flight Context

Don Khon lies at 13.9552°N, 105.9260°E in Champasak Province, southern Laos, among the southernmost inhabited islands of the Si Phan Don archipelago and only a few kilometres from the Cambodian border. From 4,000-8,000 ft, look for the paired islands of Don Det and Don Khon joined by a slim bridge; the old railway trackbed shows as a pale straight line cutting diagonally across Don Khon from northwest to southeast, the only ruler-straight feature in a landscape of curves. White water marks Li Phi Falls on the island's western flank and the Khone Phapheng complex about 7 km east. Nearest airport is Pakse International (VLPS/PKZ, elevation 351 ft), about 130 km north; Ubon Ratchathani (VTUU) in Thailand is the nearest major field. Clearest conditions run November to March; the wet season from April to October brings haze, afternoon convection and dramatically higher water in the channels.
