# Stung Treng Bridge

A 1,731-metre Chinese-financed span that ended the boat crossing at Stung Treng in 2015 and stitched two marooned districts back onto their own province.

**Location:** 13.5263, 105.9364

**Categories:** Buildings and structures in Stung Treng province, Road bridges in Cambodia, Bridges over the Mekong River, 2015 establishments in Cambodia, Infrastructure

## Introduction

Two of Stung Treng province's districts, Siem Bouk and Thala Barivat, sit on the wrong side of the Mekong. For most of the province's modern history that was a permanent condition rather than a temporary inconvenience: to reach the provincial town, its market, its hospital and its offices, you got in a boat. The river here is close to two kilometres across, wide enough that the far bank in the wet season is a smudge rather than a shoreline. On 1 April 2015 that changed in a single morning. Prime Minister Hun Sen and China's ambassador to Cambodia, Bu Jianguo, stood together at a ribbon and opened a 1,731-metre road bridge, and the far bank became somewhere you could simply drive to.

## The Sixth of a Series

The structure's official name is the Cambodia-China Friendship Bridge, and the number attached to it matters more than the name does. This was the sixth in a numbered sequence of Chinese-funded bridges built across Cambodia's rivers, a programme that has done more to reshape the country's road map in twenty years than anything since the French laid out the original highway network. The first in the series went up just east of the same town, over the Sekong River, opening in 2008. Seven years later the Mekong got its turn. Read the numbers in order and you can trace how a province that had been defined by its rivers was gradually redefined by the crossings over them.

## The Terms on Paper

The money came from the Export-Import Bank of China, structured as a concessional loan rather than a gift. Cambodia's own Council for the Development of Cambodia records the project's cost at just over 2.5 million US dollars, financed through Exim Bank of China on preferential terms. Shanghai Construction Group held the engineering and construction contract; Guangzhou Wanan Construction Supervision oversaw the work. It is worth pausing on what that structure means. A grant is a gift and a loan is an obligation, and this was the second kind - a bridge Cambodia is still repaying, on Chinese financing terms, years after the ribbon was cut.

## Building Across a Monsoon River

Work began in the spring of 2012, with a formal groundbreaking on 5 May. Building across the lower Mekong is a scheduling problem as much as an engineering one, because the river does not hold still. Between May and October the flood arrives, the channel deepens and widens, and the sandbar islands that dot this reach go under. Crews get a dry-season window and lose it again. The bridge came in ahead of its planned November 2015 completion date, opening that April, which for a project of this scale in this landscape counts as a genuinely good result.

## What a Bridge Actually Changes

The obvious gains are the ones the ceremonies talk about: cheaper freight, faster travel, better market access, a road link running west toward Preah Vihear province and north toward the Lao border 50 kilometres away. The less obvious ones show up in the small decisions of ordinary life. A ferry has a last sailing; a bridge does not. A woman in labour on the Thala Barivat side no longer waits for a boat. A student can attend school in the provincial town and sleep at home. Those changes do not photograph well, but they are the reason bridges get built. What they cost, in a wider sense, is still being counted upstream, where the dams and roads arriving alongside them are reordering a river system that tens of thousands of people fish.

## Flight Context

The Stung Treng Bridge crosses the Mekong at approximately 13.526 degrees north, 105.936 degrees east, immediately northwest of Stung Treng town in northeastern Cambodia. At 1,731 metres it is unmistakable from the air - a straight pale line laid across a river channel nearly two kilometres wide, with sandbar islands upstream and downstream of it. Pair it visually with the shorter Sekong Bridge a few kilometres east, and the two together frame the confluence where the Sekong and Sesan rivers enter the Mekong. Stung Treng Airport (VDST) lies just southeast of the crossing but has no scheduled service. Best viewed between 2,000 and 6,000 feet. Dry-season mornings from December to February give the cleanest light; the wet season brings low cloud, heavy convection and a river so swollen the bridge appears to run over open water.
