# Stung Treng Province

A northeastern Cambodian province that two French administrations swapped in 1904, where the Mekong gathers three great tributaries and the last dolphin of the border pools died in 2022.

**Location:** 13.5167, 105.9500

**Categories:** Stung Treng province, Provinces of Cambodia, Mekong River, Cambodia

## Introduction

In 1904, two French colonial administrations traded territory the way collectors trade cards. French Laos handed over Chiang Taeng - the place Khmer speakers call Stung Treng - to the French Protectorate of Cambodia, and took Champasak in return. Nobody consulted the villages along the riverbank. The swap stranded a small Lao minority on the Cambodian side of a freshly redrawn line, and it gave Cambodia a northeastern province built almost entirely out of forest, water and distance: 11,092 square kilometres bounded by Laos to the north, Ratanakiri to the east, Preah Vihear to the west, and Kratie and Kampong Thom to the south. The Mekong runs straight through the middle of it.

## Three Names for One Riverbank

Stung means river in Khmer, and it is one of the most common place-name elements in the country. Treng means reed. Together they give the name that appears on every modern map, though it is not the oldest name this stretch of water has carried. Before Stung Treng there was Tonle Ropov, which survives today only as the name of an area within the provincial town. Between the two came a name of Lao origin, Satung Teng, credited to a monk who travelled south from Vientiane, later worn by pronunciation into Xiang Taeng and then reshaped again by Khmer into the form used now. Three names in three languages, stacked on a single bend of the river. The political history underneath them is just as layered: the territory belonged first to the Khmer Empire, then to the Lao kingdom of Lan Xang, then to the kingdom of Champasak, before the Franco-Siamese crisis of 1893 put it under French Lower Laos and the 1904 trade moved it one last time.

## Where the 3S Rivers Come Home

Hydrologists have a shorthand for the three tributaries that make this province matter: the 3S rivers, meaning the Sekong, the Sesan and the Srepok. The Sekong rises near Hue in Vietnam and runs some 480 kilometres through southern Laos before crossing into Cambodia. The Sesan, known in Khmer as the Tonle San, drains Vietnam's Central Highlands, gathering the Srepok on its way down. All three deliver their water to the Mekong within a few kilometres of Stung Treng town, which sits on the Sesan just above the confluence, 376 kilometres from Phnom Penh and roughly 50 kilometres south of the Lao border. Scattered across that stretch of the Mekong are hundreds of small islands - sandbars, wooded islets, seasonal ground that appears in the dry months and vanishes under the monsoon. The provincial capital counted 29,665 people at the 2009 census. Beyond it, the province thins quickly into forest.

## A Corridor, and What It Cost

The border and the forested mountains of the northeast made this a corridor, and corridors get fought over. Vietnamese infiltration began in the 1950s; communist insurgency followed and lasted, in one form or another, until the late Khmer Rouge years. In between, American bombs fell here through the 1960s and into the 1970s. The province also produced the voice that Cambodia has never stopped listening to. Sinn Sisamouth was born in Stung Treng province around 1933, wrote and recorded more than a thousand songs, and moved across ballads, bossa nova, Latin jazz and psychedelic rock with a crooner's ease that earned him comparisons to Nat King Cole. When Phnom Penh fell on 17 April 1975, he was driven out of the city with everyone else. The most credible account has him executed at a jail in Prek Ta Duong village in November 1976. His remains were never recovered. His songs are still sung at weddings across the country.

## The Last of the Border Dolphins

For years, the deep pool at Anlong Cheuteal, in Borei O'Svay Sen Chey district near the Lao frontier, was one of the few places on Earth where you could watch Irrawaddy dolphins surface in the wild. They were a trans-boundary group, ranging between Cambodian and Lao water. In early 2022 the last of them, a 110-kilogram male, was found dead on Koh La Ngo island just across the line in Si Phan Don. Gillnets and explosive fishing had been grinding the population down for decades, and one dead animal ended a species' presence in an entire country. Downstream, the story is less final. Cambodia's remaining Mekong dolphins - around 105 animals as of 2024, along a 190-kilometre reach of river - are being watched closely enough that their numbers have begun to climb again.

## Timber, Rubber and a Seam of Copper

Around 85 percent of the province's people live rurally and make their living from the land. Timber and rubber plantations spread across the lowlands; animal breeding, fishing and silk weaving fill in the rest. The forest that defines the province is also under the heaviest pressure from it, with logging and fishing straining both the woodland and the fisheries. Two protected areas push back: the western portion of the vast Virachey National Park, reached from Siem Pang, and Veun Sai-Siem Pang National Park on the Ratanakiri border. Something else may be coming. In 2019, the Japan Oil, Gas and Metals National Corporation reported finding a mineralised belt of copper and zinc about 260 kilometres north of Phnom Penh, and signalled plans for deeper exploration with a Japanese partner. The climate will not make any of it easy: heavy rain from May through October, April averaging a maximum of 35 degrees Celsius, and January's cool nights bottoming out around 18.9.

## Flight Context

Stung Treng province is centred near 13.52 degrees north, 105.95 degrees east, in Cambodia's far northeast. The clearest visual signature from the air is the confluence complex just east of Stung Treng town, where the Sekong and Sesan rivers deliver into the Mekong within a few kilometres of each other - three distinct river threads braiding into one broad channel studded with sandbar islands. The Stung Treng Bridge and the Sekong Bridge are both visible as pale lines across the water. Stung Treng Airport (VDST) lies at the provincial capital but handles no scheduled commercial service; Ratanakiri Airport (VDRK) sits to the east. Cruise altitudes give the best sense of scale, but 5,000-8,000 feet reveals the island braiding. Expect haze and towering convection from May to October, and burning-season smoke in March and April.
