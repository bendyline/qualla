# Khlong Yai

A Thai town on a two-kilometre sliver of coast that takes nearly five metres of rain a year - and for seven years sheltered more Cambodian refugees than it had residents.

**Location:** 11.7763, 102.8858

**Categories:** thailand, trat-province, monsoon, refugees, border-towns

## Introduction

For seven years this stretch of coast held far more Cambodians than Thais. From 1979 until July 1986, a Thai Red Cross camp in Khlong Yai district took in people fleeing the fighting between the retreating Khmer Rouge and the Vietnamese army that had driven them from power, and at its height it sheltered more than 90,000 of them - in a district whose own population would be counted at fewer than 24,000 in 2000. The site is known today as the Ratchakarun Centre. Khlong Yai has gone back to being what it was before: a fishing town wedged onto a ribbon of Thailand two kilometres wide, catching more rain than almost anywhere else in the country.

## Two Kilometres of Thailand

Look at the map and the shape is startling. Thailand's territory here has been squeezed to a thin coastal shelf: the Gulf of Thailand on one side, and on the other, roughly two kilometres inland, the Cambodian border running parallel along the base of the Cardamom Mountains. The whole district covers just 50.2 square kilometres, packed at nearly 478 people to each of them. The hills immediately behind the town reach about 300 metres; a little further north the same range climbs past 1,000. One road serves the entire strip. Route 318 runs south to the border crossing at Hat Lek and north to Trat, where it joins Route 3 - the Sukhumvit Road - for the long run to Bangkok. There is no alternative route, no bypass, and nowhere much to spread out.

## Nearly Five Metres a Year

The mountains that pin the town against the sea also soak it. Khlong Yai has a tropical monsoon climate, and the Cardamom range standing directly behind it forces the southwest airflow upward and wrings it out on the way over: the town records about 4,845.8 millimetres of rain in an average year, spread across roughly 196 rainy days. No month is genuinely dry. December and January come closest, the rain builds through February to April, and from May to October the real monsoon settles in - more than 800 millimetres in each of June and July, and over 1,000 in August alone. In the 1961-1990 record, one August delivered 2,072.4 millimetres by itself. The temperature barely moves by comparison: 27.3°C on average, a March record high of 38.2°C, and a December morning in the archive that dropped to 15.8°C. That relentless water is why dense rainforest grows on a coastal shelf this narrow.

## Ceded, Then Returned

This coast has not always been Thai, and not for very long without interruption. Khlong Yai belonged to the old province of Patchan Khiri Khet, and in 1904 most of that province - Trat included - was handed to France in exchange for French troops withdrawing from Chanthaburi. Three years later the arrangement was unwound in part: France returned Trat and Khlong Yai to Siam, taking Siem Reap and Battambang in trade, and keeping Koh Kong just across the new line. The administrative machinery followed slowly. Khlong Yai became a minor district in 1912 and was not raised to a full district until 1959. Three subdistricts and twenty villages sit on the strip today, with townships at Khlong Yai itself and at Hat Lek on the border.

## Ninety Thousand

When Vietnamese forces pushed the Khmer Rouge out of Phnom Penh at the start of 1979, the war did not end; it moved west into the Cardamoms and along this border, and civilians moved with it. Many of the people who reached Khlong Yai had already lived through the years of the Khmer Rouge regime and then walked out through the same mountains you can see from the main road, carrying whatever they still had. The Thai Red Cross ran the camp that received them from 1979 until July 1986, and at its peak more than 90,000 people were living in it - nearly four times the district's own population, in a place with fifty square kilometres to its name and one road in or out. Some were resettled abroad. Many eventually went home to a country still at war with itself. The centre that stands on the site now carries a different name and a quieter purpose, and most people driving Route 318 to the border pass it without knowing what it held.

## Flight Context

Khlong Yai sits at 11.78°N, 102.89°E on the Gulf of Thailand coast in Trat province. It is one of the most distinctive pieces of coastline in Southeast Asia from the air: Thai territory narrows to a green ribbon barely two kilometres wide, with Route 318 threading down it between the sea and the abrupt forested wall of the Cardamom Mountains, which mark the Cambodian border. The hills immediately inland rise to about 300 m and exceed 1,000 m further north. Follow the road southeast about 15 km and it terminates at the Hat Lek border crossing; northwest it runs 60 km to Trat, with Ko Chang lying offshore. Trat Airport (VTBO, 1,800 m runway, elevation 105 ft) is the nearest field. Recommended viewing altitude is 3,000-6,000 ft. Plan carefully around weather: with almost 4,850 mm of rain a year and roughly 196 rain days, the May-to-October monsoon produces towering convection against the mountains and frequent low ceilings. December to February offers the most reliable visibility.
