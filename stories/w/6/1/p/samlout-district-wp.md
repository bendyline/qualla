# Samlout District

A remote Battambang district where a dispute over forced rice sales on 2 April 1967 became the opening act of the Cambodian civil war - and which now protects the last rainforest in the northwest.

**Location:** 12.5829, 102.8600

**Categories:** Districts of Battambang province, Cardamom Mountains, Protected areas of Cambodia, Cambodian Civil War

## Introduction

On the morning of 2 April 1967, soldiers came into the villages around Samlaut to collect the rice harvest, and the farmers refused. In the scuffle that followed two soldiers were killed and several rifles taken. By that afternoon around two hundred people were marching behind banners denouncing the government of Prime Minister Lon Nol, and they went for the most conspicuous symbol of the state within reach: the Royal Khmer Socialist Youth agricultural settlement at Stung Kranhoung. By nightfall they had overrun the guards at two more posts and executed a local mayor. Historians differ on almost everything about what happened next, but very few dispute where it started. Samlout - Samlaut in the older spelling - is a district of seven communes and forty-nine villages in the Cardamom foothills of Battambang Province, and the war that consumed Cambodia for the following decade opened here, over rice.

## Rice at Gunpoint

The immediate grievance was a scheme called the rammassage du paddy. Cambodia's economy leaned on taxing rice exports, and by the mid-1960s farmers were quietly selling their crop across the border where buyers paid better. In early 1967 Lon Nol had the army take over the buying, and Battambang province went first. Households saw their earnings collapse at the prices offered, and many were made to hand over their harvest at gunpoint. Layered underneath were older resentments. From 1965, thousands of Khmer Krom families displaced by the war in Vietnam had been resettled in Cambodia, with the largest settlement in Battambang, and local people - many of whom held no formal title to the land they farmed - found themselves pushed off it. Around Pailin, arable land was being bought up and converted into fruit plantations, leaving Khmer farmers as day labourers on ground their families had worked. The historian David Chandler summed up the revolt as springing from local grievances against injustice, corruption and ham-fisted government behaviour. Nobody in those villages was executing a plan from Phnom Penh.

## Four Thousand Into the Forest

Sihanouk was in France. Lon Nol answered with paratroopers and national police, who by some accounts were offered a bounty for every severed head of a rebel or leftist sent back to the capital. Seventy-three people were captured at a hideout in the first sweep; within three weeks nearly two hundred had been taken and nineteen killed. It did not work. By June more than four thousand villagers from southern Battambang had abandoned their homes and walked into the forest - families, not fighters, choosing the malaria and the wild animals over the soldiers. On 22 April Sihanouk publicly blamed five leftist deputies, among them Khieu Samphan, Hou Yuon and Hu Nim; two of them vanished into the maquis rather than answer. A second, far better organised rising followed in January 1968 and spread across multiple provinces. Ben Kiernan called Samlaut the baptism of fire for Cambodia's revolutionary movement. The men who fled into these hills in 1967 would be running the country eight years later.

## The District That Could Not Be Counted

War kept coming back here. When Cambodia conducted its national census in 1998, enumerators could not work in Samlout at all - the official record notes simply that conflict prevented it. This was one of the last corners of the country still contested, three decades after the shooting started, and the ordnance left behind has not gone anywhere; northwestern Battambang remains among the most heavily mined ground in a heavily mined country, and clearance teams are still at it. Today the district is administered as seven communes - Ta Taok, Kampong Lpou, Ou Samrel, Sung, Samlout, Mean Chey and Ta Sanh - and forty-nine villages, farming the same red soil their grandparents were arguing over in 1967.

## Elephants, Honey and a Peace Park

What survived all this, improbably, is the forest. In 1993 King Norodom Sihanouk designated the Samlaut Multiple Use Area, 599 square kilometres classed under IUCN Category VI for sustainable use - the last remaining tropical rainforest in northwestern Cambodia, holding Asian elephants and the headwaters of the Stung Sangker, whose water reaches around a million people downstream before it drains into the Tonlé Sap. From 2003 the Maddox Jolie-Pitt Foundation worked here in partnership with the Ministry of Environment, and in 2006 the reserve entered a sister-park agreement with Sequoia and Kings Canyon National Parks in California to trade technical knowledge. In 2009 talks opened on a transboundary Peace Park linking Samlaut with two Thai protected areas, roughly 110,000 hectares between them. Then in 2021 Angelina Jolie announced that the next chapter of the Women for Bees programme would come to Samlout - a scheme to train fifty women as beekeepers over five years and build 2,500 hives across twenty-five UNESCO biosphere reserves worldwide. It is a modest thing to set against this district's history. It is also, for once, something starting here that is not a war.

## Flight Context

Samlout District sits at approximately 12.583N, 102.860E in southwestern Battambang Province, hard against the Thai border in the northern Cardamom foothills. The terrain rises from cultivated red-soil plateau into forested hills; the visible boundary between cleared farmland and the dark canopy of the Samlaut Multiple Use Area is the best landmark. Recommended viewing altitude is 5,000-9,000 ft. Battambang Airport (VDBG) lies roughly 65 km northeast; Trat (VTBO) is about 90 km southwest across the Thai border, and Techo International at Phnom Penh (VDTI) some 300 km southeast. Do not plan to land or operate at low level away from established fields - large areas of this district remain mine-contaminated. Visibility is best November to February; expect monsoon buildups over the Cardamoms from May through October.
