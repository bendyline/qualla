# Areng Valley

A Cardamom valley where crocodiles are ancestors, the forest belongs to spirits, and six centuries of Chong belief have done the work of a protected area.

**Location:** 11.6314, 103.5311

**Categories:** Tourist attractions in Cambodia, Populated places in Koh Kong province, Cardamom Mountains, Valleys of Asia, Cardamom Mountains rain forests, Indigenous peoples of Southeast Asia, Environmental protests

## Introduction

In the Areng river there lives an animal that most of Asia has already lost, and the people of this valley do not hunt it. Not because a ranger told them so, and not because a law exists somewhere in Phnom Penh, but because the Siamese crocodile belongs to the category of things a person does not touch. The same holds for certain patches of forest. They have owners who are not people, and a household that clears one has taken something from a neighbour it cannot see. It is not a conservation policy. It is a way of accounting for the world, and it has kept this valley intact for longer than most conservation policies have existed.

## Spirit Forests and the Uncleared Ground

Anthropologists describe what the Chong practise as animism folded into Theravada Buddhism, which understates how practical it is. Certain stands of forest are spirit ground; certain animals, the crocodile foremost among them, carry a status that puts them beyond use. The effect on the landscape is direct - trespass carries a cost that no fence could impose, and the parts of the valley that go untouched tend to be the parts that matter. Across the Cardamoms this river system has been described as holding the second-largest wild population of Siamese crocodiles in the world, a species that has lost more than eighty percent of its global numbers since the 1940s and was assumed gone from Cambodia until surveyors found it here again in 2000. Belief got there first.

## Six Hundred Years, and a Clay Jar

The Chong have lived in the Areng for more than six centuries. Their ancestors appear in the record earlier still: in 1296 the Chinese envoy Zhou Daguan, writing about the countryside around Angkor, described mobile forest people who carried a clay jar on their heads, hunted, and cooked and ate together. Over time they settled, drawn into the Khmer state's system for cultivating cardamom - the spice that gave these mountains their name and the crown a monopoly worth guarding. They speak one of the Pearic languages, a branch of the Austroasiatic family so reduced now that fluent speakers can be counted, and they grow upland rice on cleared and rotated ground. Around fifteen hundred people live in the valley today, Chong and Sui together with families who arrived from elsewhere in Cambodia after 1979.

## Forty-Three Kinds of Fish

Surveys of the Areng watershed have documented forty-three fish species, among them the Asian arowana - the dragonfish, whose ornamental market value has emptied rivers across the region. On land the valley holds Asian elephants, clouded leopards and Asiatic black bears, and great hornbills cross overhead with the heavy mechanical wingbeat that announces them long before they appear. Twenty thousand hectares is not a large area by the standards of the Cardamom range, and much of what makes the valley biologically remarkable is simply that it is a broad, low, well-watered basin in the middle of steep country - the kind of terrain that concentrates animals, and the kind that engineers look at and see a reservoir.

## What Isolation Preserved

These mountains were the last place the Khmer Rouge held, retreating into them after Vietnamese forces took Phnom Penh in 1979 and holding out along the Thai border for years afterward. The war left this range mined, avoided and effectively sealed - a grim accident of preservation, since roads and logging arrived here decades later than almost anywhere else in Cambodia. Elsewhere in the Cardamoms, researchers have found ceramic jars and wooden coffins set on remote cliff ledges, burials from the fifteenth to seventeenth centuries containing glass trade beads that reached these mountains from the sea. The valley has since had its own encounter with development, in the form of a hydropower scheme that would have drowned it and was shelved in 2017. The forest is still standing. The Chong are still here. Neither outcome was ever guaranteed.

## Flight Context

The Areng Valley lies at approximately 11.63°N, 103.53°E in northern Koh Kong province, overlapping Central Cardamom Mountains National Park. From the air it is one of the few legible features in this part of the range: a broad, flat-floored basin roughly 20,000 hectares in extent, threaded by the Areng river and ringed by steep forested ridges. Best viewed from 6,000-10,000 ft, ideally in the early morning before convective cloud builds. There are no airstrips, no significant settlements and no emergency landing options across the surrounding park, which is roadless over most of its 4,011 square kilometres. Nearest airports: Dara Sakor International (VDDS/DSY) on the Koh Kong coast, Sihanouk International (VDSV/KOS), Phnom Penh International (VDPP/PNH), and Trat (VTBO/TDX) across the Thai border. Weather: southwest monsoon from roughly May to October delivers 3,800-5,000 mm annually on the windward slopes; expect dense dawn valley fog, afternoon thunderstorms and rapidly deteriorating visibility over the ridgelines.
