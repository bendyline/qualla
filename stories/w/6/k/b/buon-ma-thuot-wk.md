# Buôn Ma Thuột

A highland city built on red basalt and robusta, where a tank sits on the central roundabout, Ede longhouses have become suburbs, and almost nobody comes for the sights.

**Location:** 12.6667, 108.0500

**Categories:** Vietnam, Central Highlands, Cities, Coffee, Indigenous cultures

## Introduction

There is a tank on the roundabout. Not a replica gate-guard tucked into a park somewhere - an armoured vehicle raised on a plinth at Ngã Sáu Ban Mê, the six-way junction at the middle of town, marking the morning of 10 March 1975 when the war arrived here and, seven weeks later, ended in Saigon. Traffic circles it all day. And the air around it smells of roasting robusta, because in Buôn Ma Thuột it always does. Those two facts - the tank and the smell - are most of what you need to understand a city of 434,000 people that almost no one visits on purpose.

## Ama Y Thuột's Village

The name is Ê Đê, not Vietnamese, and it means the village of Ama Y Thuột - a respected local leader of the late nineteenth century, remembered in the highland fashion as the father of Thuột. The French made the village a provincial capital in 1904, folded it into Kon Tum province in 1913, then reversed themselves and restored it in 1923. Through the 1930s they built it into a genuine regional hub: roads, plantations, administration. It did not become a city in its own right until 21 August 1995. Today it is the largest urban centre in the Central Highlands, sitting at 536 metres on a plateau of weathered volcanic basalt, roughly 350 kilometres from Ho Chi Minh City and considerably further from anywhere else that matters.

## Red Basalt

That basalt is the whole economy. Coffee reached Vietnam with French missionaries in the nineteenth century, but it was the Central Highlands - and Đắk Lắk above all - that turned it into an industry, with serious planting from the 1930s and an explosive expansion after the Đổi Mới reforms. Vietnam is now the world's second-largest coffee producer after Brazil, exporting 1.62 million tonnes in 2023, and robusta accounts for about 97 percent of what it grows. Đắk Lắk alone farms well over 200,000 hectares of it. The consequence, at street level, is that cafés occupy nearly every corner and it is genuinely difficult to be served a bad cup. Trung Nguyên's flagship coffee garden east of town turns the crop into a pleasure park of artificial mountains and waterfalls. A biennial coffee festival, running since 2005, turns it into a trade fair. And when the coffee finally stops, there is rượu cần - rice wine drunk communally through long reed straws from a shared jar.

## Longhouse Suburbia

At the northern edge of the city, Akô Đhông is an Ê Đê community that the city has grown around rather than over. Wooden longhouses on stilts sit in yards full of flowers, close enough to the ring road that calling it a village no longer quite works. Ê Đê society is matrilineal: property passes through women, families take the mother's line, and a household expands physically as it expands socially - when a daughter marries, her husband moves in and the longhouse gains a compartment. Women's given names carry the prefix H', men's the prefix Y-. Their language belongs to the Chamic branch of Austronesian, closer to Malay than to Vietnamese, and the 2019 census counted 398,671 Ê Đê. Walking Akô Đhông, you are looking at a way of organising a family that predates every administration this plateau has had.

## The Last Royal Pagoda

Khải Đoan Pagoda was begun in 1951 at the initiative of Queen Mother Hoàng Thị Cúc and formally named by Emperor Bảo Đại in 1953 - making it the last pagoda in Vietnam ever to receive royal recognition, since there was no monarchy left to grant any more. Its four hectares are a hybrid: Huế imperial temple traditions grafted onto the long, low, stilted silhouette of an Ê Đê longhouse, which is either a diplomatic gesture or an architectural argument, depending on how you read it. Elsewhere in town, at the Đắk Lắk Cultural Centre, a kơnia tree is preserved deliberately - a species that highland communities treat as sacred and that Vietnamese song made into a national symbol of separation and longing.

## After Six O'Clock

Guidebooks have historically dismissed the food here, and they are wrong. Wander northwest of the main roundabout after six in the evening and the stalls come out: hủ tiếu, phở, bún, cơm, nem, and along Lý Thường Kiệt a run of places where you assemble your own spring rolls at the table. Across from the Co-op Mart, a shabby-fronted shop serves cơm hến and bún bò Huế and opens at the back into a courtyard with a garden, caged birds and an indoor waterfall. None of this exists for visitors. Buôn Ma Thuột has developed fast on internal investment rather than tourism, which is precisely why it is worth a stop - it is a Vietnamese city going about its business, with the highlands starting at the edge of the last coffee lot.

## Flight Context

Buôn Ma Thuột lies at 12.667°N, 108.050°E on the Đắk Lắk Plateau at about 536 m. Buon Ma Thuot Airport (BMV / VVBM) sits roughly 8 km southeast of the centre at 529 m elevation, with a single asphalt runway 09/27 of 3,000 m; it is domestic-only, served by Vietnam Airlines, VietJet Air, Pacific Airlines and Bamboo Airways to Hanoi, Ho Chi Minh City, Da Nang and Hai Phong. The nearest international gateway is Cam Ranh, about 226 km east. A good sightseeing altitude is 3,000-5,000 ft AGL: from there the city reads as a dense grid surrounded by the corduroy geometry of coffee lots on red soil, with National Highway 14 running north-south through it and Lak Lake visible about 55 km south. Terrain rises sharply southeast toward Chư Yang Sin at 2,442 m - plan escape routes accordingly. Best visibility is the November-April dry season; expect smoke haze in March and afternoon convection from May to October.
