# Operation Garfield

For three weeks in 1966 an American brigade searched the forests north of Ban Me Thuot and found almost nothing - until a point man picked up a wire and started following it.

**Location:** 12.6667, 108.0500

**Categories:** Vietnam War, Military history, Vietnam, Central Highlands

## Introduction

At the abandoned mortar position the patrol found unfired rounds, a booklet of firing tables, and a written history of the unit's operations over the previous year. For a brigade that had spent three weeks walking through empty forest, this was the first real intelligence of the campaign - somebody else's paperwork, left behind in a hurry. What the men of 3rd Platoon, Company A did not yet know was that the soldiers who had written it were still nearby, watching them read it. That was 15 March 1966, on the banks of a stream called the Ea Wy, some 65 kilometres north of Ban Me Thuot.

## Good Campaigning Weather

Operation Garfield ran from 25 February to 25 March 1966, conducted by the 3rd Brigade of the US 25th Infantry Division across Darlac province. The ground favoured the search: rolling terrain around 200 metres, a dry season of low humidity and northeasterly winds, the jungle and 2,500-metre mountains confined to the south and southeast. The brigade drew itself a box on the map - Highway 14 to the east, the Cambodian border west, Ban Me Thuot south, the Darlac-Pleiku line north - and set out to find People's Army of Vietnam units believed to be sheltering inside it. Belief was about all they had. The brigade intelligence officer thought there might be a PAVN battalion some 30 kilometres north of the town; beyond that, as the Army's own history conceded, intelligence was sketchy at best. From 25 February, C-130s flew round the clock for two days to lift the brigade from Pleiku down to Ban Me Thuot East Airfield, where Colonel Everette A. Stoutner set up his command post and an ARVN battalion took over the guarding, freeing every American rifleman for the hunt.

## Sterile Landing Zones

From 28 February to 7 March the brigade air-assaulted and patrolled its way toward the Mewal Plantation, a suspected infiltration way station about 20 kilometres north of Ban Me Thuot. The largest force it managed to encounter was a single squad. On 4 March, General Stanley R. Larsen ordered Stoutner to move his base 55 kilometres north to Buon Brieng, a closed Special Forces airstrip just west of Highway 14, and lent him Chinooks so he could shift troops and guns faster. Days blurred: an assault into an empty landing zone, patrols out into the trees, nothing, patrols back. The monotony was a kind of lie, because the people they could not find knew exactly where they were. At 03:20 on 11 March, forty to sixty mortar rounds came down on a company settled into its night position, wounding eleven men, and the tubes were gone before anyone could respond.

## The Wire on the Ea Wy

The night of 14 March, the 1st Battalion, 35th Infantry took mortar fire at a landing zone 13 kilometres northwest of Buon Brieng; the rounds fell short. Company A went looking for the gunners at first light, 3rd Platoon moving out at 06:30 along the Ea Wy. After finding the firing position and its abandoned records, the platoon spent three hours working upstream before its leader decided around midday to turn back. Then the point man saw a wire and began tracing it - and the PAVN soldiers who had been watching the whole time opened fire. Outnumbered by what was later judged as much as two reinforced companies of the 32nd Regiment, the platoon held. A forward air controller was overhead within fifteen minutes, the first of sixteen tactical air sorties that afternoon. At 13:15 the company commander air-assaulted in with a second platoon and was hit as he landed; the guns of the 2nd Battalion, 9th Artillery fired into the gaps between helicopter runs. A third platoon arrived half an hour later, and the counterattack overran the position. Eleven Americans were killed and twenty-seven wounded. Thirty-six PAVN dead were counted on the ground, with perhaps a hundred more claimed but never confirmed.

## The Trick That Nearly Worked

Stoutner tried patience next. On 18 March the 1st Battalion, 14th Infantry searched an area 30 kilometres northwest of Buon Brieng all day, then staged a departure - helicopters in, helicopters out, except that only the command group and the artillery were actually aboard. Three rifle companies stayed behind and slipped away along separate routes after dark, betting that the PAVN would relax once the Americans appeared to leave. The first night produced nothing. The companies went to ground through the daylight hours and moved again at dusk. Still nothing. Then at 07:45 on 20 March, Company C fought a PAVN force for about forty-five minutes, killing nineteen at the cost of one man wounded. Over the following five days, small firefights accounted for fifteen more PAVN soldiers and one more American life.

## The Arithmetic

Stoutner ended the operation on 25 March. The month's ledger read 122 PAVN dead counted against 21 Americans killed - a ratio that looked decisive on a briefing chart and explained very little about who controlled the forest. Stoutner's own claim was the more modest one: he believed he had kept the PAVN off balance and made it harder for them to prepare for the rainy season. Nine years later, PAVN divisions came out of these same hills and took Ban Me Thuot in three days, opening the campaign that ended the war. The men who died along the Ea Wy in March 1966, on both sides of that wire, were doing the same thing for the same stretch of ground.

## Flight Context

The operational area lay north and west of Buôn Ma Thuột (12.667°N, 108.050°E), a rough rectangle bounded east by National Highway 14, west by the Cambodian border and north by the old Darlac-Pleiku provincial line. Ground elevation across most of it runs 200-600 m, rising toward 2,500 m peaks well to the southeast. Key positions: Ban Me Thuot East Airfield, now Buon Ma Thuot Airport (BMV / VVBM, 3,000 m runway 09/27, elevation 529 m), about 8 km southeast of the city; the Mewal Plantation roughly 20 km north; and the former Special Forces strip at Buon Brieng about 55 km north, just west of Highway 14. Flying the route at 3,000-5,000 ft AGL, Highway 14 is the reliable north-south reference and the Ea Wy is one of many small streams draining the plateau - hard to pick out except in the wet season. February and March are the operation's own weather: dry, warm, low humidity, northeast winds, with smoke haze from burning off the visibility by late March.
