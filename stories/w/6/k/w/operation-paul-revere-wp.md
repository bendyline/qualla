# Operation Paul Revere

For eleven weeks in 1966 an American brigade hunted three North Vietnamese regiments across the plateau west of Pleiku, and again and again watched them step over a line on the map and disappear.

**Location:** 13.8050, 107.8720

**Categories:** Battles of the Vietnam War involving the United States, Battles and operations of the Vietnam War in 1966, History of Gia Lai province, Vietnam War

## Introduction

Twice in the summer of 1966, American soldiers fought their way up to entrenched North Vietnamese positions west of Pleiku, could not break them before dark, and watched the defenders walk away into Cambodia. Once, after eight days of searching a stretch of forest where a regiment had certainly been, they found nothing at all. That was the shape of Operation Paul Revere: eleven weeks of chasing an enemy who chose every fight, ended every fight, and had a border a short walk away that no pursuing American was permitted to cross.

## A Week of Empty Forest

It began with reports. In early May 1966 intelligence placed large People's Army of Vietnam formations west of the Special Forces camps at Đức Cơ and Plei Me, and on 10 May the 3rd Brigade of the 25th Infantry Division flew into Landing Zone Oasis, twenty-four kilometres southwest of Pleiku, to sweep the ground below it. Brigadier General Glenn D. Walker's brigade found nothing for a week. Walker shifted his weight westward instead, toward the Chu Pong Massif — the wooded high ground straddling the Cambodian frontier that had sheltered the regiments who fought the 1st Cavalry Division in the Ia Drang valley six months earlier. Somewhere in that country were the 32nd, 33rd and 66th Regiments. Finding them was the operation. Fixing them turned out to be a different problem entirely.

## The Patrol Out of Plei Djereng

The first blood was not American. On the morning of 24 May a Civilian Irregular Defense Group patrol working ten kilometres southwest of Plei Djereng Camp was ambushed by what was judged to be two PAVN battalions. Two more CIDG companies came up, the fight ran all day, and both sides dug in for the night. At first light the North Vietnamese attacked and the irregulars withdrew in a running firefight toward the camp, losing eighteen men and two American Special Forces advisers. Then the mortars found the camp itself, killing thirty more and wounding fifty-four. These were Montagnards — the French colonial word for mountain dweller, still the term of record in the American files — Jarai and Bahnar men recruited from villages within walking distance of the perimeter they were defending. Nearly fifty of them died in two days on ground their families had farmed for generations, and the operation they died in the opening hours of would take eleven more weeks to run its course.

## Sixty-Three Men on a Hot Landing Zone

Walker sent the 2nd Battalion, 35th Infantry to relieve Plei Djereng. On 27 May the battalion moved twenty kilometres south to Landing Zone Eleven Alfa, and the next day put Company B into LZ Ten Alfa, ten kilometres further west. The landing zone was hot. Fire closed it to helicopters after only sixty-three men were on the ground, and those sixty-three beat off several assaults before supporting fire opened the zone enough to land the rest of the company and a company of the 1/35th alongside them. After midnight the 66th Regiment came at Ten Alfa and the 33rd came at Eleven Alfa on the same night. Both attacks broke. At dawn the Americans counted eighty bodies in front of Ten Alfa's perimeter. When they pushed out beyond the wire they were driven back in, mortared, and assaulted twice more. By the end, 250 North Vietnamese dead were counted around the position — and when the brigade searched south and west for the next eight days, the regiment that had made those attacks had gone.

## The Line on the Map

Walker turned back toward Chu Pong on 17 June. Three days later a reconnaissance platoon of the 2/35th, working north of the massif, chased a group of North Vietnamese soldiers, was pinned by heavy fire, and had to be pulled out by its own battalion and a troop of the 3rd Squadron, 4th Cavalry; three Americans were killed. On the morning of 24 June another reconnaissance platoon came under fire near the border, called in air and artillery, and advanced — into well-prepared entrenchments that two more companies and an armored cavalry troop could not crack before nightfall. In the dark the defenders slipped across into Cambodia. On 3 July a patrol from Company B, 1/35th was ambushed close to the same frontier. A rescue force got them out under air and artillery cover and withdrew north, but seventeen Americans were dead and thirty-two wounded; twenty-three PAVN bodies were found the following day.

## LZ Orange, 1 August

At the end of July prisoner interrogations finally put pins in the map: the 66th Regiment north of Chu Pong, the 32nd about ten kilometres southwest of LZ Oasis. Walker was reinforced with the 2nd Battalion, 7th Cavalry and a battalion of the Republic of Korea Army's Capital Division. While the brigade and the Koreans deployed north and east of the massif, the 2/7th Cavalry was lifted on 31 July into LZ Orange, seven kilometres southwest of Oasis, directly on top of the 32nd. At dawn the North Vietnamese hit Orange with mortars and came in three times. Air support broke the last assault and the regiment withdrew to the northwest, leaving twenty-eight dead behind; five Americans were killed and forty wounded. Operation Paul Revere had formally ended at midnight, hours before the attack began. Its ledger read sixty-six Americans killed against a claimed 546 North Vietnamese dead and sixty-eight captured, and Paul Revere II began immediately, over the same ground, against the same regiments.

## Flight Context

The operation is geolocated at 13.805°N 107.872°E — the site of LZ Oasis, southwest of Pleiku — but its ground ran some forty kilometres west and southwest from there to the Cambodian border and the Chu Pong Massif. Fly it at 4,000-7,000 ft AGL to read the terrain that shaped the fighting: an open red-earth plateau around 500-800 m broken by forested ridges rising toward the frontier, with QL-19 the only hard-surface road. The Ia Drang valley and Chu Pong high ground lie to the southwest, Plei Djereng to the northwest. Nearest airport is Pleiku (VVPK), 24 km northeast of the geotag; Ratanakiri/Banlung (VDRK) sits across the border in Cambodia. Visibility is best from December to March; the southwest monsoon brings afternoon storms and low cloud on the border ridges from May onward — the same weather the operation was fought in.
