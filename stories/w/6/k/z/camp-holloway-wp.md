# Camp Holloway

A helicopter field three kilometers east of Pleiku, named for a warrant officer killed three days before Christmas 1962, that grew from a transport company's dirt strip into the aviation hub of Vietnam's Central Highlands.

**Location:** 13.9730, 108.0310

**Categories:** Installations of the United States Army in South Vietnam, Installations of the Army of the Republic of Vietnam, Pleiku, 1962 establishments in South Vietnam, Military installations established in 1962, History of Gia Lai province

## Introduction

Chief Warrant Officer Charles E. Holloway of De Leon Springs, Florida, was 31 years old when he was killed on 22 December 1962, and his name is cut into panel 1E of the Vietnam Veterans Memorial — near the very top of the wall, among the earliest deaths of a war most Americans had not yet noticed. He flew a Piasecki CH-21, the twin-rotor transport that crews called the flying banana, and he was the first aviator of the 81st Transportation Company to die in action. The following year the Army gave his name to the airfield his company operated outside Pleiku. It would keep that name for exactly ten years.

## Officially, Nobody Was Fighting

In 1962 the United States had no combat units in Vietnam. What it had were transportation companies — an accounting fiction that fooled nobody, least of all the men flying the aircraft. Their helicopters lifted South Vietnamese infantry into landing zones, took fire going in and going out, and came home with holes in them. The CH-21 was already obsolescent when it arrived: underpowered for the heat and altitude of the highlands, slow, and shaped like a banana bent in the middle, with a habit of settling into trees when the engine could not hold a hover. Holloway died in one that December. Camp Holloway was established that same year on the plateau three kilometers east of Pleiku, along Route 19 — the road that climbs from the coast into the highlands and runs west toward the Cambodian border. It began as very little: a strip, revetments, tents.

## The Machine That Ate the Highlands

What happened to Camp Holloway over the following decade is the history of the war in miniature. The 81st traded its CH-21s for Bell UH-1 Hueys in 1963 and was redesignated the 119th Assault Helicopter Company. In December 1965 the 170th Aviation Company joined it to form the 52nd Aviation Battalion, and the field kept swelling around that headquarters until it belonged to the 17th Combat Aviation Group under the 1st Aviation Brigade. At its peak the camp held two more Huey assault companies; the 179th Aviation Support Helicopter Company with its CH-47A Chinooks; a Sikorsky CH-54 Skycrane company, whose skeletal machines existed to pick broken helicopters up off mountainsides and carry them home; and the 219th Reconnaissance Airplane Company, the Headhunters, flying single-engine Cessna O-1 Bird Dogs low and slow over the border country. Add a signal battalion, an artillery battalion, an air cavalry troop. A dirt strip named for one dead pilot had become the aviation heart of the Central Highlands.

## Red Dust and Radio Calls

Pleiku sits high enough that the nights turn genuinely cold, and the red basalt dust of the highlands gets into everything — uniforms, rotor bearings, food, lungs. In the dry season it hangs behind every departing aircraft. In the monsoon the same soil becomes a red paste that swallows boots. Crews at Holloway lived on a rhythm set by weather and radios: pre-dawn briefings, ash-and-trash resupply runs to firebases and Special Forces camps scattered along the Cambodian border, medevac calls that came whenever they came. The Bird Dog pilots flew alone, or with an observer, at treetop height. The Huey crews flew into clearings they had never seen until the moment they committed to the approach. Almost nothing that happened in the western highlands between 1965 and 1972 happened without aircraft from this field being part of it.

## Two Nights

A base full of parked helicopters is, from the other side of the wire, a field of fragile and expensive targets. Twice the field learned that. At 1:50 on the morning of 7 February 1965, Viet Cong soldiers who had spent hours cutting through the perimeter opened fire on the airfield and the advisory compound: seven Americans were killed, 104 wounded, ten aircraft destroyed. That attack's consequences — retaliatory bombing of North Vietnam, and the escalation that followed — became a story much larger than the base itself. Nearly three years later, at 2:30 on 26 January 1968, two companies of the People's Army of Vietnam's 408th Sapper Battalion came through with mortars and demolition charges and destroyed five Hueys and an ammunition storage area. Sappers were specialists at exactly this: getting a small number of men inside a perimeter and doing damage out of all proportion to their numbers.

## The Name Does Not Survive the Handover

In 1973, as American forces withdrew under the Paris accords, Camp Holloway was turned over to the Army of the Republic of Vietnam. Two years later that army ceased to exist. The field is still in use today as a training base for the People's Army of Vietnam, which is a plainer ending than most: no monument, no preserved revetments, just an airfield outside Pleiku doing roughly what airfields do. The name went with the Americans. Charles Holloway remains where names remain — on panel 1E, line 15, in black granite in Washington, and in a small town in Volusia County, Florida, where somebody was expecting him home for Christmas in 1962.

## Flight Context

Located at 13.97°N, 108.03°E, approximately 3 km east of Pleiku along Route 19, on the Central Highlands plateau at roughly 800 meters elevation. The site remains an active People's Army of Vietnam training base — treat it as restricted airspace. Pleiku Airport (VVPK) lies just west; the Ia Drang valley and the Chu Pong Massif are about 45 km to the southwest, and the Cambodian border roughly 55 km west. Terrain is rolling red-earth plateau, easy flying in the November-April dry season; the wet season brings low ceilings and heavy afternoon convection over the highlands.
