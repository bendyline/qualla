# Cathay Pacific Flight 700Z

A bomb in the passenger cabin brought a Hong Kong-bound airliner down over the Central Highlands in June 1972, killing all 81 people aboard, in a case no court has ever resolved.

**Location:** 13.9833, 108.0000

**Categories:** Airliner bombings, Aviation accidents and incidents in 1972, Aviation accidents and incidents in Vietnam, Cathay Pacific accidents and incidents, Accidents and incidents involving the Convair 880, History of Gia Lai province

## Introduction

Norberto Fernandez was a Filipino civil servant travelling with his wife, their five children and a niece — eight seats on one aircraft, one family. Seven members of the Kenny family were aboard the same flight. On the flight deck sat Captain Neil Morison, a 43-year-old Australian with 14,343 hours in his logbook, and in the cabin were six young flight attendants from Hong Kong working under pursers William Yuen and Dicky Kong. Cathay Pacific Flight 700Z left Bangkok at midday on 15 June 1972 with 71 passengers and ten crew, bound for Kai Tak. Somewhere over the Central Highlands of South Vietnam, roughly halfway through the afternoon's routine, a bomb went off in the passenger cabin.

## Eighty-One People

The manifest was ordinary in the way that scheduled airline manifests are ordinary. The passengers were mostly Japanese, Thai and American nationals, travelling with families, on the Singapore-Bangkok-Hong Kong run that Cathay Pacific flew as a matter of course. Morison had 5,261 hours on this aircraft type alone. Beside him were First Officer Lachlan Mackenzie, 38, and second First Officer Leslie Boyer, 42; behind them Flight Engineer Ken Hickey, 36. Between the four of them they carried more than 32,000 flight hours. None of that mattered. This is worth stating plainly, because the case has since been retold mainly as a criminal puzzle: the people on 700Z were not participants in the war beneath them, or in any story about explosives and insurance policies. They were passengers on a commercial flight, most of them going home.

## The Convair

VR-HFZ was a Convair CV-880, built in 1961 — a handsome, very fast, very thirsty four-engine jet that Convair designed to outrun the Boeing 707 and then sold to almost nobody, losing the company a fortune. Cathay Pacific flew a fleet of them across Asia through the 1960s and early 1970s, and they are remembered fondly by the people who worked on them. Flight 700Z had come up from Singapore without incident and made its scheduled stop at Bangkok's Don Mueang airport. The crew kept up normal radio contact after departure. The flight data recorder ran cleanly until 05:59 GMT, produced about thirty seconds of nonsense, and stopped. Nothing in the recording suggested a mechanical fault, weather, or anything the crew had done or failed to do.

## A Crash Site Inside a War

The aircraft came down near Pleiku, in country where an active war was being fought — which turned the investigation into something close to a field operation. Two bodies were recovered almost immediately. After that, everything stalled: hostile forces were operating nearby, and South Vietnamese soldiers had to be brought in to search for wreckage and secure the ground. Before that happened, local villagers had gone through the debris looking for anything of value, which is what happens when an airliner falls into a poor and violently disrupted province. Investigators from the Swire Group, Cathay Pacific's parent company, and from the UK Civil Aviation Authority eventually reached the site alongside Hong Kong police. What they found was legible enough. The debris field showed the aircraft had broken into three large sections, with the break points falling almost exactly along the front and rear of the wingbox, and the sections lay close enough together to indicate the break-up had happened at low altitude. Two engines and the horizontal stabilizer were visible from helicopters but could not be reached on foot because of the fighting. The rear section was found impaled on a tree, with thirteen passengers and two cabin crew still inside it.

## The Finding

The inquiry concluded that the crash had been caused by an explosive device detonating inside the passenger cabin; subsequent reporting has placed it beneath row ten, and the blast destroyed the aircraft's structural integrity in a matter of seconds. It remains the deadliest accident involving a Convair 880 anywhere in the world. Everything after that becomes a criminal case rather than an aviation one, and criminal cases require evidence that survives a fire, a fall, a jungle and a war. Much of what an investigator would want was never recovered at all.

## Seats 10E and 10F

Somchai Chaiyasut, a lieutenant in the Thai police aviation division, had booked two of the seats directly above where the device was believed to have been placed: 10E and 10F, occupied by his fiancée, Somwang Prompin, and his seven-year-old daughter, Sonthaya. He had also taken out three travel accident insurance policies on them. He was arrested in August 1972 and charged with premeditated mass murder. At trial the prosecution called 67 witnesses. In May 1974 Thailand's Criminal Court found the evidence insufficient and acquitted him; an appeal court reached the same verdict in 1976. He maintained throughout that he was innocent and asked how anyone could imagine he would kill his own child. He returned to his police work, pursued the insurers through the courts and had collected 5.5 million baht by 1978, and he died of cancer in 1985 at the age of 43. Nobody has ever been convicted of destroying Flight 700Z. The Fernandez children, the Kenny family, the six young cabin crew from Hong Kong and the seventy-odd others died at the hands of a person the courts were never able to name — and the ground where they came down, a stretch of the Central Highlands that has long since gone back to farm and forest, carries no marker to say so.

## Flight Context

The crash site lies near 13.98°N, 108.00°E, in the Central Highlands close to Pleiku, Gia Lai province, Vietnam. Terrain is red-earth plateau and low forested hills at roughly 700-800 meters, now largely coffee, rubber and smallholder farmland; there is no memorial or preserved wreckage to look for. Pleiku Airport (VVPK) is a few kilometers northeast. The modern airway between Bangkok and Hong Kong still passes over this region. Dry season November to April gives the clearest view; the wet season brings heavy afternoon cloud over the highlands.
