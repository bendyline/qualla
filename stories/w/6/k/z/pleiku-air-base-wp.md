# Pleiku Air Base

A runway built before anyone had drawn it, abandoned in 1975 with sixty-four aircraft still on the ramp, and flying again today as Pleiku Airport.

**Location:** 14.0044, 108.0172

**Categories:** Installations of the United States Air Force in South Vietnam, Military installations of South Vietnam, Military airbases established in 1962, Pleiku, Buildings and structures in Gia Lai province, Vietnam War

## Introduction

The requirement arrived in January 1962 and it came with a deadline: a 6,000-foot runway at Pleiku, operational by the first of July. The design did not yet exist. Rather than wait for it, the U.S. Navy's construction office in Saigon told the contractor RMK-BRJ to start work on 19 January and sort the drawings out along the way. What went down was pierced steel plank — interlocking perforated panels bolted over graded earth — plus overruns, a parallel taxiway and aprons, for $2.7 million. It was the American war in the Central Highlands in miniature: enormous urgency, improvised answers, and steel where concrete should have been.

## Air Base 62

The Republic of Vietnam Air Force activated the field in December 1962 as Air Base 62, and in March 1964 it became the RVNAF 62nd Tactical Wing. The Vietnamese called the place Cù Hanh, and the name still turns up in veterans' accounts on both sides.

One clarification is worth making, because the histories blur constantly. Pleiku Air Base was not Camp Holloway. Holloway was a U.S. Army helicopter field about three kilometres east of the city, and it was Holloway that Viet Cong sappers hit in the early hours of 7 February 1965, killing seven Americans, wounding 104 and destroying ten aircraft. That attack pushed President Johnson into Operation Flaming Dart and the bombing of the North, which makes it one of the hinge moments of the war. A 1997 declassification revealed something stranger still: Hanoi had not authorised the raid, and did not know that senior American officials were in Saigon at the time.

## Skyraiders on the Plateau

Once the Americans took the base seriously it filled up fast. The 633d Combat Support Group stood up on 14 March 1966, taking over from a provisional squadron that had been running construction. The A-1-equipped 1st Air Commando Squadron moved up from Bien Hoa on 1 January 1966, bringing with it the lineage of Farm Gate, the earliest American air commitment to Vietnam. The 6th Special Operations Squadron arrived on 29 February 1968 with twenty A-1E and A-1H Skyraiders.

The Skyraider was a piston-engined relic in a jet war and precisely the right aircraft for this one. It could carry an extraordinary bomb load, loiter for hours above a firefight and absorb punishment that would have ended a fast jet's day. From Pleiku those aircraft flew close air support, day and night interdiction, armed reconnaissance and cover for downed-pilot rescues. AC-47 gunships worked from the field too, along with rescue and medevac Hueys.

## Handing Over the Keys

In March 1970 the USAF began transferring the base to the Vietnamese Air Force, and the handover was complete by the end of that year. The RVNAF built a serious presence on it: the 72d Tactical Wing, the 530th Fighter Squadron flying A-1 Skyraiders, the 229th and 235th helicopter squadrons with UH-1Hs, a medevac detachment from the 259th, and the 118th Liaison Squadron with Cessna O-2As and U-17s for forward air control.

After the Paris Peace Accords of January 1973, Pleiku became the eye that watched the highlands. The accords froze the lines but not the war, and from this field the RVNAF flew reconnaissance to track North Vietnamese movement and document violations of a treaty everybody understood to be provisional. By 1974 the base housed the headquarters of the 6th Air Division.

## Sixty-Four Aircraft

The end came quickly and from an unexpected direction. On the night of 9 March 1975 the People's Army struck Buôn Ma Thuột, the town controlling Highways 14 and 21 and with them the road to Saigon; Pleiku's airfield was rocketed and shelled the same night. On 11 March three A-37s were destroyed on the ground here by 122mm rockets. By 14 March Buôn Ma Thuột had fallen.

That day President Nguyễn Văn Thiệu convened an emergency meeting at Cam Ranh Bay and ordered an orderly withdrawal from the highlands to conserve forces for a counterattack. General Phạm Văn Phú, commanding the region, read the order as a call for immediate evacuation. Brigadier General Phạm Ngọc Sang was given forty-eight hours to empty the airfield. On 16 March shells began landing in the city and the army started down Highway 7B toward the coast, taking the civilian population with it. Sixty-four aircraft were left on the ramp at Pleiku with almost no attempt to destroy them, along with fuel and ordnance in usable condition.

What happened on that road is the part that matters. More than a hundred thousand people who fled Pleiku and Kon Tum were killed or stranded on the way to the sea — soldiers, their families, and civilians who had simply seen the army leaving and followed. Vietnamese accounts call it the Convoy of Tears.

## PXU

The base was abandoned after 1975 and stayed that way for a long time. It flies again now as Pleiku Airport — PXU, VVPK — 744 metres up, with a single asphalt runway 09/27 of 2,400 metres, and daily services to Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City.

The past is visible if you know the shape of it. The terminal stands on the pad of a former USAF hangar. The old control tower and the big fire station on the flightline are still up. The wartime parking ramp and the jet taxiway remain, mostly unused, far larger than a provincial airport needs — a set of dimensions built for a war and inherited by a departure lounge.

## Flight Context

Located at 14.004°N, 108.017°E, on the plateau just outside Pleiku at 744 metres (2,441 ft) elevation. Operating today as Pleiku Airport (VVPK/PXU) with a single asphalt runway 09/27, 2,400 m long. Visual landmarks: the oversized wartime parking ramp and disused jet taxiway alongside the modern apron, the surviving control tower and fire station on the old flightline, Biển Hồ crater lake to the north, and Núi Hàm Rồng at 1,028 metres about 12 km south — the highest terrain on the approach and the reference point for minimum safe altitude. Surrounding plateau runs 700–800 metres. Best viewing 3,000–6,000 feet AGL to make out the wartime footprint against the modern field. Clearest conditions November to April; the southwest monsoon from May to October brings low cloud, reduced visibility and rapid afternoon convection over the highlands.
