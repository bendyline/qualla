# Pleiku

A Jarai village name written down by French clerks in 1905 became the hinge of Vietnam's Central Highlands - and in 2025 stopped being a city at all.

**Location:** 13.9715, 108.0147

**Categories:** Pleiku, Populated places in Gia Lai province, Cities in Vietnam, Central Highlands, Vietnam War

## Introduction

On 1 July 2025, Pleiku stopped being a city. Nothing burned and nobody left. Vietnam abolished its district-level tier of government, and the provincial capital of Gia Lai was reorganised into five wards and two communes reporting straight to the province. Gia Lai then merged with coastal Binh Dinh, and the new administrative centre settled at Quy Nhon, down at the far end of Highway 19. For a town whose entire history has turned on who controlled the road to the sea, the last twist came from the road.

## The Name the Clerks Wrote Down

Long before any of that, there was a village on a basalt shelf about 740 metres up. The Jarai who farmed this plateau, and the Bahnar to the north and east, built settlement names out of ordinary words. Plơi means village. Kơdưr means upper, or north. Pleiku is the upper village, nothing grander.

The spelling is a colonial artefact. A decree of the Governor-General of Indochina dated 4 July 1905 carved an autonomous province out of the western mountains of Binh Dinh and named it Plei-Kou-Derr, the first appearance of the name in writing. French administrators called the highland peoples Montagnards - mountain people - a label that folded dozens of distinct languages, territories and lineages into a single filing category. Many of those communities call themselves Degar.

Their villages have not gone anywhere. Plei Op sits in Hoa Lu, Kep in Dong Da, Bruk Ngol in Yen The, now addresses inside a town where Kinh residents make up roughly seven of every eight people.

## Groupe Mobile 100

Dien Bien Phu had already fallen when, on 24 June 1954, Groupe Mobile 100 was ordered to abandon An Khe and fall back eighty kilometres up Route Coloniale 19 to Pleiku. The road ran through country the Viet Minh knew far better than the men driving it. Ambushed at Kilometre 15 and again days later, the column lost most of its vehicles and all of its artillery before survivors straggled into Pleiku - the Battle of Mang Yang Pass, among the last and bloodiest of the First Indochina War. What was left of the group was then sent back out to reopen Route Coloniale 14 toward Buon Ma Thuot.

Geography made this inevitable. Pleiku sits at the crossing of the two roads that matter: Highway 14 running the spine of the highlands from Kon Tum down to Buon Ma Thuot, and Highway 19 climbing west from the port at Quy Nhon. Hold Pleiku and you hold the plateau. Lose it and everything above and below it is cut in half.

## A Few Kilometres East

Camp Holloway lay a few kilometres east of town, a US Army helicopter field established early, before the American war was officially a war. In the small hours of 7 February 1965, Viet Cong sappers and mortars hit it, killing seven Americans, wounding 104 and destroying ten aircraft. Within hours President Johnson had ordered the retaliatory air strikes of Operation Flaming Dart, and the slide toward a ground war of half a million men was underway. A raid on a dirt airfield in the highlands is not usually where a decade turns, but this one was.

Seven years later the war reached Pleiku from an unexpected altitude. On 15 June 1972 a bomb hidden in a suitcase beneath a cabin seat destroyed Cathay Pacific Flight 700Z, a Convair 880 flying from Bangkok to Hong Kong at 29,000 feet. All 81 people aboard died, and the wreckage came down over Pleiku - passengers with no part in the war at all, falling into the middle of it.

## Route 7B

Buon Ma Thuot fell on 12 March 1975. Two days later President Nguyen Van Thieu ordered General Pham Van Phu to abandon Kon Tum and Pleiku and regroup on the coast. The withdrawal started on 16 March down Route 7B, a decrepit tertiary road through Ayun Pa to Tuy Hoa that had not carried serious traffic in years.

Soldiers went first. Then came everyone else - families who understood exactly what was being left behind, on foot, in trucks, on scooters, tangled into the military columns until neither could move or fight. By 18 March the People's Army was shelling the road. The head of the column reached Tuy Hoa on 25 March; of 60,000 troops who set out, roughly 20,000 arrived, and they were no longer fit to fight. Over 100,000 evacuees from Pleiku and Kon Tum were killed or stranded along the way. Vietnamese accounts call it the convoy of tears, and the name has never needed explaining.

## Red Earth, Thin Air

The plateau today runs between 700 and 800 metres, cool enough that visitors from Ho Chi Minh City arrive in jackets. At Ham Rong junction, where Highways 14 and 19 meet south of town, the road tops out at 785 metres. The soil is old basalt, weathered brick-red and deep enough to plough for generations, and across Gia Lai it carries more than a hundred thousand hectares of coffee, with rubber a close second and pepper, cashew and durian filling in behind.

The wartime airfield on the outskirts is a civil airport now, one 2,400-metre runway at 744 metres. On match days the crowds go to Hoang Anh Gia Lai, the club that made this highland town a name in Vietnamese football. Officially the city no longer exists. Every road sign, ticket and coffee sack still says Pleiku.

## Flight Context

Pleiku lies at 13.9715 N, 108.0147 E on the Gia Lai plateau, with terrain elevation between 700 and 800 metres (roughly 2,300-2,600 ft). Pleiku Airport (PXU / VVPK) sits on the near outskirts at 744 m / 2,441 ft field elevation with a single 2,400 m asphalt runway, 09/27. Approach from the east along Highway 19 and the ground climbs steadily out of Quy Nhon through the Mang Yang and An Khe passes; from the north, Highway 14 threads down from Kon Tum. Best viewing is 3,000-5,000 ft AGL: the red-earth plantations, the crater lake of Bien Ho about 7 km northwest, and the Ham Rong volcanic cone south of town all read clearly at that height. Dry-season months from around November to April give the cleanest air; the southwest monsoon from roughly May to October brings low cloud and strong afternoon buildups over the plateau. Nearest alternates are Buon Ma Thuot (BMV / VVBM) 181 km south and Da Nang (DAD / VVDN) 377 km northeast.
