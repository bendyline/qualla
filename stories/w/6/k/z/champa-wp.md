# Champa

For sixteen centuries an Austronesian, Indic, seafaring civilisation held the coast of what is now Vietnam — and its Cham descendants are still here.

**Location:** 14.0000, 108.0000

**Categories:** History, Vietnam, Cambodia, Kingdoms, Austronesian, Hinduism, Islam, Archaeology

## Introduction

The map pin for this story sits up on the plateau, and Champa's cities were down on the coast. The discrepancy is the point. There was never a Cham capital in the highlands; the Cham were seafarers who kept few settlements of any size away from the shore, and their temples faced the water. But the cargo that made their harbours worth sailing to came down out of forests like these — aloeswood above all, the dense resinous heartwood that Arab merchants bought by the shipload, and which does not grow on a beach. Champa's population was never only Cham, either. Jarai and Rade speakers, close cousins in language, lived in the uplands within its orbit, alongside Bahnaric- and Katuic-speaking peoples whose vocabularies still carry the loanwords that prove the contact. The wealth was coastal. The supply lines ran inland, through here.

## Out of the Sa Huỳnh

The Cham arrived on the mainland by sea. Their ancestors were the Sa Huỳnh, a seafaring Austronesian culture along the central Vietnamese coast between roughly 1000 BC and AD 200, and the closest living relative of the Cham language is Acehnese, spoken at the northern tip of Sumatra. The first polity Chinese records name here is Lâm Ấp — Linyi — which began in 192 AD when an official called Khu Liên revolted against Eastern Han rule; its precise relationship to Champa remains unclear. What is not in doubt is how early these people were writing. The Đông Yên Châu inscription, cut around 350 AD, is the oldest known text in any native Southeast Asian language, predating the first Khmer, Mon and Malay writing by centuries. Champa reached its height in the ninth and tenth centuries.

## Cities With Sanskrit Names

Fourth-century wars with Funan brought territory and, with it, a flood of Indian culture. Sanskrit became the language of learning and Shaivism the religion of state, and what followed was a thousand years of red brick. At Mỹ Sơn, king Bhadravarman — who reigned from about 380 to 413 — installed a linga called Bhadresvara, founding a sanctuary his successors kept extending for centuries. It is a UNESCO World Heritage Site today, as is the old Cham port of Hội An. Champa itself was less a single kingdom than a shifting set of principalities, and historians still argue over how unified it ever was. The names recur: Indrapura near modern Da Nang, capital from about 875; Amaravati in Quảng Ngãi; Vijaya in Bình Định; Kauthara at Nha Trang, where the towers of Po Nagar still stand; and Panduranga in the far south, always the most stubbornly independent.

## 1471

Pressure from Đại Việt, the Vietnamese polity centred on the Red River, had been building for centuries when Lê Thánh Tông marched south in 1471. Vijaya had been Champa's political and cultural heart since the 1150s, and it did not survive the year. Accounts put the number killed or captured at 120,000. Fifty members of the royal family and somewhere between twenty and thirty thousand others were taken north as prisoners, among them the king, Trà Toàn, who died in captivity on the road. Chinese records preserve the words of a Cham envoy at the imperial court: 'Annam destroyed our country.' Other reports describe forty to sixty thousand people slaughtered amid widespread burning and looting. What remained of Champa was an enclave around Nha Trang and Phan Rang, and a great many Cham left for Cambodia.

## The Long Ending

Kauthara was annexed in 1653. From 1799 the kings of Panduranga were no longer hereditary but appointed from Huế, and in August 1832 the emperor Minh Mạng, purging a rival faction, had the last Cham king Po Phaok The arrested. Panduranga was absorbed into Ninh Thuận and Bình Thuận under a policy of replacing local chieftains with rotating Vietnamese bureaucrats. Speaking Vietnamese and following Vietnamese custom became compulsory. Bani mosques were razed, temples burned, farmland seized, and Cham villages pushed inland toward arid ground. Two revolts followed between 1833 and 1835, the second led by Ja Thak Wa, a Bani cleric who briefly refounded a Cham state before Minh Mạng's forces crushed it. The emperor then declared the Cham Tân Dân — 'new people', a name that erased the distinction it acknowledged. His successor Thiệu Trị revoked most of the harshest measures.

## Who Is Still Here

Champa ended. The Cham did not. Vietnam's 2019 census counted 178,948, most living in Ninh Thuận and Bình Thuận — the old Panduranga. Roughly 64,500 are Balamon, also called Cham Ahiér: Hindus who held their faith through all of the above, and who with the Balinese and the Tengger of Java are among the very few indigenous Hindu communities anywhere outside India. The Bani practise a distinctly Cham form of Islam. In Cambodia the Cham number around 600,000, mostly Sunni of the Shafi'i school, descended in part from those who fled after 1471 — and that community came close to destruction a second time. Between 80,000 and 100,000 Cham died under the Khmer Rouge from 1975 to 1979, out of some 250,000 then in Cambodia, proportionally the heaviest loss of any targeted group. Three Cham scripts remain in use.

## Flight Context

This entry is anchored at 14.0°N, 108.0°E in Vietnam's Central Highlands — the inland flank of Champa's world rather than its heartland, which lay 60–90 nm east on the coastal plain. Flying the historical geography means tracking east from the plateau, over the escarpment, and down to the Quảng Nam–Bình Định littoral. The surviving landmarks are visible from low altitude: the brick towers of Mỹ Sơn in its ringed valley south-west of Da Nang, the Po Nagar towers on their hill above the Cái river mouth at Nha Trang, and the Po Klong Garai group outside Phan Rang–Tháp Chàm. Useful fields are Da Nang (VVDN), Phù Cát (VVPC) for the Vijaya area near Qui Nhơn, Cam Ranh (VVCR) for Kauthara, and Pleiku (VVPK) for the highland point itself. February to May gives the clearest coastal visibility; the September–December north-east monsoon brings heavy rain and low cloud against the mountains.
