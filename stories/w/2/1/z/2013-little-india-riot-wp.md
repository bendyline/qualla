# 2013 Little India Riot

A fatal traffic accident on a December evening in Singapore's Little India district triggered the city-state's first riot in over forty years, exposing deep tensions beneath its orderly surface.

**Location:** 1.3084, 103.8510

**Categories:** riots, singapore, social-history, migration, labor

## Introduction

Singapore prides itself on order. The city-state that bans chewing gum and fines jaywalkers had not seen a riot since the 1969 racial disturbances. Then, on the evening of 8 December 2013, a private bus struck and killed a 33-year-old construction worker named Sakthivel Kumaravelu at the junction of Race Course Road and Hampshire Road in Little India, and the carefully maintained calm shattered in a way no one expected.

## A Sunday Evening Unravels

Little India on a Sunday evening is one of the most densely packed places in Singapore. Thousands of migrant workers from South Asia gather on their weekly day off, filling the streets, shops, and restaurants along Serangoon Road and its side lanes. At 9:23 PM, at the corner where Race Course Road meets Hampshire Road, a private bus struck Sakthivel, a Tamil Nadu native who had come to Singapore for construction work. He died at the scene. What happened next stunned a nation accustomed to rigid public order. Around 300 migrant labourers, many of them witnesses or bystanders in the crowded streets, turned on the bus and then on the emergency vehicles arriving to respond. For approximately two hours, the mob overturned and set fire to vehicles, clashing with first responders in scenes that seemed impossible in Singapore's meticulously policed landscape.

## Two Hours of Chaos

The Singapore Police Force dispatched 300 riot officers, including the elite Special Operations Command and the Gurkha Contingent, a paramilitary unit originally recruited from Nepal that has served Singapore since the colonial era. By 11:45 PM, the rioters had dispersed into the surrounding streets and alleys. Behind them lay the wreckage: twenty-five emergency vehicles damaged, five of them burned. The speed and ferocity of the violence was without precedent in modern Singapore. In the days that followed, investigations revealed that the initial anger over Sakthivel's death had been compounded by alcohol and the sheer density of the crowd. The bus driver, a 55-year-old Singaporean, was ultimately acquitted of fault in the traffic accident itself.

## Reckoning and Response

The aftermath moved swiftly. Twenty-seven people were arrested in the immediate sweep, and the total eventually reached 33 charged in court. Sentences ranged from 15 weeks to 25 months in prison; one man received three strokes of the cane for flipping a police car. Fifty-three workers were deported for offences including obstructing police and failing to disperse, while another 200 received formal warnings. Yet the most unusual consequence came in the form of legislation. In 2015, Singapore enacted the Liquor Control (Supply and Consumption) Act, banning alcohol consumption in all public places between 10:30 PM and 7:00 AM. The law cast such a wide net that it initially prohibited the sale of rum raisin ice cream during curfew hours, a provision so absurd it was quietly lifted in 2019.

## Beneath the Surface

The government commissioned a Committee of Inquiry to investigate the riot, but the questions it raised extended far beyond a single night of violence. Singapore depends heavily on foreign labour. Hundreds of thousands of workers from India, Bangladesh, and other South Asian countries build the city-state's towers, lay its roads, and maintain its infrastructure, often living in crowded dormitories on the island's periphery. The riot forced a public conversation about working conditions, income inequality, and the social isolation of the migrant workforce. One moment from the night offered a counterpoint to the destruction: video footage captured a man stepping into the chaos and urging rioters to stop -- a reminder that courage and decency existed alongside the anger. The man was never identified; a worker who came forward after media appeals later acknowledged he was not the person in the footage.

## A Scar on the Street Corner

Little India today bears no visible trace of the riot. The junction of Race Course Road and Hampshire Road is once again an unremarkable crossing in one of Singapore's most vibrant ethnic neighborhoods, thick with the aromas of curry and incense. But the event left a lasting mark on the city-state's self-image. For a country that had built its identity on stability, efficiency, and the absence of disorder, the riot was a jarring reminder that rapid development carries social costs that statistics alone cannot capture. The Liquor Control Act remains in effect. The migrant dormitories are still there, on the edges of the island. And every Sunday evening, Little India fills again with thousands of workers on their one day of rest.
