# Depayin Massacre

On a rural road in Myanmar's Sagaing Region, a government-orchestrated mob ambushed Aung San Suu Kyi's convoy in 2003, killing at least 70 people in an attack later classified as a crime against humanity.

**Location:** 22.4667, 95.1500

**Categories:** Massacres in Myanmar, Aung San Suu Kyi, State-sponsored terrorism

## Introduction

The road to Depayin is an unremarkable stretch through rural Sagaing, the kind of place that appears on few maps and draws fewer visitors. On 30 May 2003, it became a killing ground. A convoy carrying Aung San Suu Kyi and supporters of the National League for Democracy was ambushed by a mob that investigators later determined had been organized, armed, and positioned by Myanmar's military government. At least 70 people died. The attack was not spontaneous rage or communal violence -- it was premeditated murder orchestrated at the highest levels of state power, designed to eliminate the country's most prominent opposition leader and terrorize anyone who stood with her.

## A Trap on a Country Road

The Ad Hoc Commission on the Depayin Massacre, which presented its preliminary findings on 25 June 2003, pieced together what happened. Up to 5,000 people had been transported to a remote rural location specifically to attack the NLD motorcade. The attackers were well-armed and positioned at two separate killing sites along the route, ensuring the convoy would face violence whether it pushed forward or tried to turn back. Before the motorcade arrived, local authorities went through nearby villages warning residents to stay indoors -- a detail that reveals how thoroughly the operation had been planned. This was not an ambush thrown together in haste. It was a coordinated military-style operation that used civilians as weapons against other civilians.

## The Target Who Survived

Aung San Suu Kyi, the Nobel Peace Prize laureate who had already spent years under house arrest for her opposition to military rule, was the convoy's most prominent member -- and almost certainly its primary target. In an April 2012 interview, Khin Nyunt, who had served as Myanmar's prime minister, claimed that he personally intervened to save her life during the chaos, mobilizing his men to extract her and bring her to a nearby army cantonment. Whether this account reflects genuine protection or simply one faction of the military shielding an asset from another remains debated. What is not debated is the outcome: Suu Kyi survived, but dozens of NLD supporters -- ordinary people who had joined the convoy to show solidarity with the democracy movement -- did not. Their names have never been fully accounted for by the government.

## Silence After the Violence

The aftermath of the Depayin massacre followed a grim pattern familiar in Myanmar's modern history. Authorities systematically searched for and arrested survivors of the attack, turning victims into criminals. The government offered no credible investigation, no accountability, and no acknowledgment of its role. The Asian Legal Resource Centre concluded that the massacre constituted a crime against humanity under Article 7.1 of the Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court, describing it as a "widespread or systematic attack directed against a civilian population, with a knowledge of the attack." Despite this assessment, no serious international action followed. A decade later, victims' families were still seeking justice. The police file on the incident, when it finally emerged, was widely described as a whitewash.

## What Depayin Represents

The massacre at Depayin was not an isolated event but a window into the methods Myanmar's military establishment was willing to use to maintain power. The scale of organization -- thousands of attackers transported to a remote location, multiple killing zones, preemptive threats to local villagers -- revealed a state apparatus that treated its own citizens as instruments and targets of political violence. For the democracy movement, Depayin became a touchstone: proof that the regime would not hesitate to kill civilians in broad daylight and then punish the survivors for having survived. The town of Tabayin, which the world briefly knew as Depayin, returned to its quiet anonymity. The road where 70 people died carries no memorial. The landscape holds no visible trace of what happened, which is perhaps the point -- in Myanmar's long struggle between military power and civilian aspiration, the erasure of memory has always been part of the strategy.

## Flight Context

Depayin (Tabayin) is located at 22.47N, 95.15E in Myanmar's Sagaing Region, in the agricultural lowlands between the Chindwin River and the Irrawaddy. The area appears as flat farmland from altitude. Nearest major airport is Mandalay International (VYMD), approximately 80 nautical miles to the southeast. The town itself is small and difficult to distinguish from the air without local knowledge.
