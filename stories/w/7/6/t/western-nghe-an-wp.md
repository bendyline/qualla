# Western Nghệ An Biosphere Reserve

Southeast Asia's largest biosphere reserve protects an animal that no one has photographed since 2013, and a community of a few hundred people whose language is nearly gone.

**Location:** 19.2400, 104.9200

**Categories:** Biosphere reserves of Vietnam, Geography of Nghệ An province, Protected areas, Annamite Range, Wildlife conservation

## Introduction

In 1992, a survey team working the forests of the Annamite Range came away with a skull. It had two long, straight, sharply pointed horns and belonged to nothing science had ever described — the first large mammal discovered in this part of the world in fifty years. They named it the saola. Since then almost nobody has seen one. The most recent confirmed record is a camera-trap image from 2013, and the total wild population may be somewhere between 50 and 300. Pù Mát National Park, in the mountains of western Nghệ An, is one of the very few places on the planet where it may still be moving through the trees. That is the character of this reserve: enormous, understudied, and holding things that could disappear before anyone properly documents them.

## A Reserve the Size of a Small Country

Western Nghệ An was designated a biosphere reserve in 2007 and covers 1,303,285 hectares — the largest such reserve in Southeast Asia. The architecture of it is deliberate. A strictly protected core of 191,922 hectares sits inside a buffer zone of 503,270, which is itself wrapped in a transition area of 608,093 where people farm, log and live under negotiated rules. Three protected areas form that core: Pù Mát National Park, Pù Huống Nature Reserve and Pù Hoạt Nature Reserve. The reserve spans nine mountainous districts of the old administrative map, including Con Cuông, Tương Dương, Quế Phong, Quỳ Châu, Quỳ Hợp, Anh Sơn, Thanh Chương and Tân Kỳ, and it presses 440.8 kilometres of forested frontier against Laos. Land rises from 57.8 metres to 1,800.

## Two Monsoons and a Wall

The Annamite Range does the climatic work here. Standing across the path of both the northeast and the southwest monsoons, the mountains force air to climb, cool and unload, and they do it differently on every slope and in every valley. The result is not one climate but dozens crowded into a single reserve — wet slopes and rain-shadowed ones, cold ridges and humid gorges, all within a day's walk. That variety is the reason the biodiversity here is so dense. Isolation compounds it: valleys separated by ridgelines have been effectively separate worlds for a very long time, which is how a range this close to two capital cities managed to keep a large mammal hidden until the 1990s.

## The Count So Far

The tally currently stands at 130 mammal species, 295 birds, 84 fish, 54 amphibians and reptiles and 39 bats — and the phrase to hold onto is 'so far'. Pù Mát alone, covering 94,804 hectares and formally established by decree in November 2001, has 2,461 confirmed plant species. Its mammal list reads like a catalogue of Annamite endemics found only in living memory: the saola, described in 1993; the Truong Son muntjac; the Annamite striped rabbit, described in 2000; the red-shanked douc, moved to critically endangered in 2020; and a substantial population of northern white-cheeked gibbons, a species already extinct in China. Several of these animals were unknown to science when the reserve's oldest residents were already adults.

## The People Inside the Lines

A biosphere reserve is not a wilderness — it is a place where people are counted as part of the ecosystem, and here that means primarily Thái communities, who make up around 69 per cent of the population, alongside Khơ Mú, Kinh, Tày and H'Mông. And it means the Ơ Đu, roughly six tenths of one per cent: the 2019 census recorded 428 in Vietnam, most of them in Tương Dương. Their Khmuic language has largely given way to Thái in daily speech. These are among the poorest districts in the country, which is the uncomfortable centre of the arrangement — the households asked to live lightly on the forest have the fewest alternatives to it, and the reserve's future depends on whether that asymmetry is ever addressed.

## Going In

Con Cuông is the usual way through the door. Homestays in Thái villages such as Nua hamlet in Yên Khê put visitors in stilt houses where the evening involves brocade weaving, rice wine and food cooked over a hearth rather than a tour itinerary. From there the forest opens up: the Khe Kèm waterfall dropping in white steps out of the canopy, the streams at Khe Bu and Khe Thơi, trails into Pù Mát where the realistic ambition is birdsong, leeches and a gibbon call at dawn rather than a sighting of anything famous. That is honest. The saola has been caught on camera in the wild only a handful of times since it was found. Coming here is less about seeing than about knowing what the trees are still capable of hiding.

## Flight Context

The reserve spans roughly 18.58°N–19.73°N and 103.87°E–105.50°E, centred near 19.24°N, 104.92°E, along Vietnam's border with Laos. Terrain climbs from about 58 m to 1,800 m, so terrain clearance matters: plan 8,000–12,000 ft for transits and expect significant orographic turbulence on the lee side of ridges. Vinh International (VVVH) is the nearest airport, roughly 120 km east-northeast of Pù Mát; Xiengkhouang (VLXK) lies across the border in Laos, with Thọ Xuân (VVTX) north in Thanh Hóa and Đồng Hới (VVDH) to the south. Navigate visually on National Route 7, which runs inland from the coast at Diễn Châu through Đô Lương, Con Cuông and Tương Dương to the Nậm Cắn crossing, cutting an unmistakable line through otherwise unbroken forest for much of its length inside the reserve. Valley fog and low stratus are routine on winter mornings, and the September–January wet season keeps the ridges in cloud; March and April give the clearest air, though agricultural burning can cut visibility sharply.
