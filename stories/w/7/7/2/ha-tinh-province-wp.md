# Hà Tĩnh province

A narrow strip of Vietnam between the Annamite range and the Gulf of Tonkin that held the national heat record, lost its sea to a steel plant, and sings its hardest work without instruments.

**Location:** 18.3333, 105.9000

**Categories:** Hà Tĩnh province, North Central Coast, Gulf of Tonkin, Provinces of Vietnam, Vietnam War sites, Intangible cultural heritage

## Introduction

In 2019, a weather station in Hà Tĩnh registered 43.4 degrees Celsius — 110 Fahrenheit — the hottest temperature ever recorded anywhere in Vietnam. The record stood for four years before a station in neighbouring Nghệ An broke it in the extraordinary May of 2023. That is roughly how Hà Tĩnh works: a province of about 1.6 million people, squeezed into a strip you can drive across in under an hour, that keeps turning up at the extreme end of the national story. Vietnam's hottest day. Vietnam's worst environmental disaster. Some of its most stubborn history.

## Between the Range and the Sea

Look at Hà Tĩnh on a map and the geography explains most of the temperament. Nearly 6,000 square kilometres are pressed between the Annamite mountains and the Gulf of Tonkin, with Nghệ An to the north, Quảng Bình to the south, and the Lao provinces of Bolikhamsai and Khammouane just over the ridge to the west. Hanoi is about 340 kilometres up the coast.

Highway 1A runs 130 kilometres through the province, from Bến Thủy Bridge at the Nghệ An line down to the Đèo Ngang pass in the south, and for decades that thread carried nearly everything. Road 8 climbs west from Hồng Lĩnh town toward Laos; the Hồ Chí Minh Route runs inland through the hills. In July 2025 the province's districts were abolished under Vietnam's move to a two-tier system, and Hà Tĩnh now administers 9 wards and 60 communes directly.

## Đồng Lộc, Noon

In Can Lộc district, a T-junction sits at the northern end of the Hồ Chí Minh trail, the point where traffic heading south had to funnel through. American bombers worked it relentlessly, and crews of young volunteers worked it back — filling craters, clearing unexploded ordnance, keeping the road passable for the next convoy.

On 24 July 1968, during the fifteenth raid of that day, a bomb landed near the mouth of a shelter tunnel around midday. Ten of those volunteers were inside. They were women between seventeen and twenty-two years old, and none of them survived. They had ordinary names and ordinary lives that had been rearranged around a road junction. Đồng Lộc is a shrine now, tended and visited constantly, and a 1997 feature film carried their story to a generation that had not been born when it happened.

## The Sea Turns Over

In the 2010s a US$10 billion iron and steel complex rose at Vũng Áng on the southern coast, part of an industrial park costing more than twice that, with a deep-water port and a 2,100-megawatt power station. Inland at Thạch Khê lies an iron deposit of 544 million tonnes, among the largest in Southeast Asia. For a province whose economy still leans a third of its output on farming, forestry and fishing, this was supposed to be the turn.

Then in April 2016, dead fish began washing up. They kept coming — 80 tonnes ashore by the end of the month, more than 100 tonnes collected by early May, along the coasts of Hà Tĩnh, Quảng Bình, Quảng Trị and Thừa Thiên–Huế. Roughly 450 hectares of coral were damaged. Formosa Hà Tĩnh Steel denied responsibility for months before admitting on 30 June 2016 that it had discharged toxic industrial waste illegally through its outfall pipes. Fishing families lost their livelihoods overnight; in Quảng Bình alone the losses ran to $5.2 million. Protests began on 1 May and continued into 2017.

## Songs Without Instruments

Hà Tĩnh and Nghệ An are spoken of together as Nghệ Tĩnh, and outsiders can identify a person from either the moment they open their mouth — the accent is one of the most distinctive in the country. That same voice carries the region's music.

Ví and giặm are sung with no instruments at all. They belong to the work: transplanting rice, rowing a boat, weaving conical hats, settling a child to sleep. People sing them to make hardship shorter, to put grief somewhere, and — a great deal of the repertoire — to flirt, question and answer between young men and women across a paddy field. The lyrics use Nghệ Tĩnh dialect and idiom so specifically that they do not fully translate even into other Vietnamese. UNESCO added them to the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2014.

## The Province of Scholars

For a place this poor and this often bombed, Hà Tĩnh has produced a startling roster. Nguyễn Du, whose Tale of Kiều is the central work of Vietnamese literature, came from Tiên Điền in Nghi Xuân. Lê Hữu Trác, the great physician of Vietnamese traditional medicine, practised here. Phan Đình Phùng led armed resistance to French rule from these mountains in the late nineteenth century. The poets Xuân Diệu and Huy Cận, the painter Nguyễn Phan Chánh, the mathematician Lê Văn Thiêm and the sculptor Điềm Phùng Thị all trace back to this strip of coast.

So does the politics. From 1930, Hà Tĩnh and Nghệ An together became the ground on which the rural Soviet movement rose against the colonial administration — a rebellion the French put down with aircraft and garrisons, and one the region has never stopped commemorating. Between the Hồng Lĩnh mountains in the north, the waterfalls at Vũ Môn, Kẻ Gỗ Lake, the hot springs at Sơn Kim and the beaches at Thiên Cầm and Xuân Thành, most of what visitors come for is strung along those same two roads.

## Flight Context

Hà Tĩnh centres near 18.3333°N, 105.9000°E and covers about 5,994 square kilometres in a northwest-southeast strip. The geography is exceptionally easy to read from altitude: the Annamite range forms a continuous forested wall along the Lao border to the west, the Gulf of Tonkin bounds the east, and the coastal plain between them narrows to a few tens of kilometres. Highway 1A and the North–South railway run the length of the plain from Bến Thủy Bridge in the north to the Đèo Ngang pass in the south. The Vũng Áng industrial complex at Kỳ Anh, with its port and power station, is the most conspicuous man-made landmark on the coast; the Hồng Lĩnh ridge marks the northern boundary. Vinh International Airport (VVVH) lies roughly 50 km north of the provincial centre. Expect hot westerly downslope flow and haze from April to July, and heavy tropical rain from September through November.
