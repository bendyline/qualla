# Nghi Sơn Refinery

Vietnam's second oil refinery — nine billion dollars, four owners on two continents, and a village levelled to make the ground flat enough to build on.

**Location:** 19.3422, 105.7978

**Categories:** Oil refineries in Vietnam, Industrial sites, Buildings and structures in Thanh Hóa province, Energy infrastructure, Ports and harbours

## Introduction

Before a single column of steel went up, 350 households — about 1,500 people — packed what they owned and left, and the ground where their homes had stood was scraped flat. That was 2008, and it was the least expensive part of a nine-billion-dollar project. Everything that has happened since on this stretch of the Thanh Hóa coast — the crude jetties, the storage farm, the flare stacks that mark the horizon at night — sits on land that was somebody's village first.

## Four Owners, One Barrel

The joint venture that owns Nghi Sơn was signed on 7 April 2008, and its shareholding says a great deal about how a refinery gets built in a country that cannot fund one alone. Idemitsu Kosan of Japan holds 35.1 percent. Kuwait Petroleum International holds an identical 35.1. Petrovietnam, the state company, holds 25.1, and Mitsui Chemicals takes the remaining 4.7. Vietnam is the minority partner in the refinery on its own coast. The crude comes from Kuwait, carried the length of the Indian Ocean and through the Strait of Malacca — which means the Kuwaiti stake and the Kuwaiti oil are the same arrangement seen from two ends. A supplier who owns a third of the refinery has a guaranteed customer, and a customer who owns none of the oilfield has a guaranteed barrel.

## Six Contractors and a Japanese Bank

Nobody builds something like this alone either. The engineering, procurement and construction work went to a syndicate assembled across four countries: JGC and Chiyoda from Japan, GS Engineering & Construction and SK Engineering & Construction from South Korea, Technip from France and Technip Geoproduction from Malaysia. Bloomberg reported JGC taking the lead EPC contract in 2011. Seventy percent of the construction cost was financed through the Japan Bank for International Cooperation, which is the quiet fact underneath the whole project — Japanese policy lending, not Vietnamese capital, paid for most of the steel. Ground was formally broken for construction on 23 October 2013, five years after the site clearing, with first operations targeted for 2017.

## What Comes Out of the Pipe

The design runs ten million tonnes of crude a year — 200,000 barrels a day — with headroom engineered in to double that to twenty million tonnes if the market ever asks. That capacity makes it slightly larger than Dung Quất, the refinery that came first and gave Vietnam its initial taste of refining its own fuel rather than importing it. The output list is deliberately broad: liquefied petroleum gas, unleaded gasoline, kerosene, jet fuel, diesel and fuel oil for the transport economy, then bitumen for roads and propylene and BTX as feedstock for the petrochemical plants built alongside. Power generation, pipelines and storage came with the package. A refinery of this design is less a factory than a small city with one product line and a very long supply chain.

## Ten Years to First Fuel

The 2017 target slipped, as such targets do. The first batch of MOGAS 92 gasoline came off the units in May 2018, and commercial operations began in mid-November of that year. Count it from the bulldozers rather than the ribbon-cutting and the project took ten years — a decade in which the households that moved in 2008 built new lives elsewhere while the site they left filled with pipe racks. Vietnam had gone from importing nearly all its refined fuel to running two large refineries of its own within that same window, and the second one is here, on a coast better known for fishing villages and salt flats.

## The Place Changed Its Name

The refinery reshaped its host as thoroughly as it reshaped the shoreline. Tĩnh Gia had been a rural district of Thanh Hóa province, a farming and fishing administration with its seat at Tĩnh Gia township. On 22 April 2020 the district was dissolved and reconstituted as Nghi Sơn, a district-level town — a change of category, not just of label, and the category that Vietnamese administration reserves for places that have stopped being rural. The 2019 count put 307,304 people across 455.61 square kilometres, organised into sixteen wards and fifteen communes, with the seat at Hải Hòa, the old township under a new name. The place is now named after the industrial zone rather than the other way round.

## Flight Context

The refinery stands at 19°20′32″N, 105°47′52″E on the southern Thanh Hóa coast, about 200 kilometres south of Hanoi. Thọ Xuân Airport (VVTX / THD) lies roughly 70 kilometres northwest; Vinh International (VVVH / VII) is about the same distance south. Cruise past between 5,000 and 10,000 feet — this is a target that reads better with some altitude, because the complex, its tank farm, its power plant and its marine terminal sprawl across several kilometres and only make sense as one object from above. The visual signatures are unmistakable at any hour: circular storage tanks in tight geometric ranks, a breakwater and crude jetties reaching into the Gulf of Tonkin, and flare stacks visible for a long way after dark. National Route 1 and the North–South railway run parallel just inland. Coastal haze and low stratus are common in the summer monsoon.
