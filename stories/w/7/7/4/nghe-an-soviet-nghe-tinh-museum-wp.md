# Nghệ An – Xô Viết Nghệ Tĩnh Museum

Built on the ground of the colonial prison that held its subject, this Vinh museum keeps the drums, presses and prisoner files of the 1930 peasant uprising that French aircraft bombed from the air.

**Location:** 18.6707, 105.6912

**Categories:** History museums in Vietnam, Buildings and structures in Nghệ An province, 1960 establishments in Vietnam, Vietnamese independence movement, Memorials

## Introduction

On 12 September 1930, several thousand people set out from Hưng Nguyên toward Vinh, three kilometres away. They were farmers, most of them, walking to present demands about taxes and land. French aircraft met the column and dropped six bombs on it. Somewhere between 140 and 200 people were killed and hundreds more wounded, on a road, in the middle of a working day. Thirty-three years later to the day, a museum opened in Vinh to hold what was left of them and of the movement they belonged to — and it opened on the site of the prison where the survivors had been locked up.

## Why They Walked

The conditions had been assembling for years. Roughly half the rural population of Nghệ An and Hà Tĩnh owned no land at all, working other people's fields for a share that shrank whenever the weather turned. The harvests of 1929 and 1930 failed. On top of that came the colonial revenue system: heavy taxation, and state monopolies on salt and alcohol that turned two household necessities into instruments of extraction. Local mandarins and village officials took their own cut on the way through.

What changed in 1930 was organisation. The newly formed Indochinese Communist Party did not manufacture the anger — it linked it, connecting protests that had previously flared and died in isolation into something with a shared timetable and shared demands. Five strikes broke out in Vinh and Bến Thủy within two months from March, and the unrest moved from the industrial riverfront out into the rice districts.

## Village Government

In the countryside, peasant committees began doing what the colonial administration and the village notables had done. They handled disputes, adjusted rents and taxes, ran their own local order. Outside observers called them soviets, and the name stuck to the whole episode: Xô Viết Nghệ Tĩnh, the Nghệ Tĩnh Soviets, after the two provinces where it went furthest.

The response was proportionate to how seriously the administration took it. The Foreign Legion was deployed alongside colonial infantry and indigenous police, and by early 1931 there were 68 military posts in Nghệ An and 54 in Hà Tĩnh — a garrison network laid over farmland. More than 1,300 men, women and children were killed in the suppression. Around a thousand more died of disease and malnutrition in the prisons and the concentration camp. Roughly 200 soldiers on the colonial side died as well. By the end of 1931 the soviets were gone.

## The Prison Underneath

Nhà lao Vinh — Vinh prison — held thousands of those arrested between 1929 and 1931. It was the administration's regional holding pen for political detainees, and it is the ground the museum stands on today, at 10 Đào Tấn Street in the middle of the city.

The choice was deliberate. Founded on 15 January 1960 as a branch of the Vietnam Museum of Revolution, the institution opened to the public on 12 September 1963, the thirty-third anniversary of the bombing at Hưng Nguyên. On 3 February 1964, the thirty-fourth anniversary of the Communist Party's founding, Hồ Chí Minh signed a preface for it. A marker on the grounds records where he had stood to speak to local officials and residents during a visit in 1957. It is a memorial to nearly 2,000 people from Nghệ An and Hà Tĩnh who died in the uprising, built on the spot where many of them were last held.

## Drums, Presses, Files

The collections run to tens of thousands of objects, photographs and documents, and the most affecting of them are the least dramatic. Drums — the instrument used to gather a village and start a march, ordinary festival equipment repurposed as a signal. Hand printing presses, small enough to hide, which produced the leaflets that made a scattered protest into a coordinated one. Seals. Weapons, such as they were. Household objects modified to conceal party organisers from searches, which tells you both how thorough the searches were and how many neighbours were willing to take the risk.

The museum also holds prisoner files from the colonial jails of both provinces covering 1930 to 1945, which means that for a great many families the record of what happened to a grandfather or great-aunt exists in one building in Vinh, catalogued and retrievable, when it exists at all.

## Still Being Rearranged

The movement's physical traces are protected as a special national relic made up of nine component sites, with 139 associated relics scattered across Nghệ An province — junctions, meeting grounds, execution sites, village halls. Preserving them and connecting them to tourism has become explicit provincial policy, and the museum has experimented with the tools that go with it, including 3D-mapping installations projected onto the buildings to stage the uprising for audiences born sixty years after it.

In May 2025 the institution changed shape again. It merged with the Nghệ An Museum to become the unified Nghệ An – Xô Viết Nghệ Tĩnh Museum, operating under the provincial Department of Culture, Sports and Tourism. The doors open at seven in the morning and close at five, every day of the year including holidays. The prison walls are long gone. The address has not moved.

## Flight Context

The museum stands at 18.6707°N, 105.6912°E in central Vinh, a few hundred metres from the surviving gates of the Nguyễn-dynasty citadel and close to Hồ Chí Minh Square. This is dense low-rise urban fabric on flat coastal plain, so the site reads better from the ground than the air; from altitude, use the citadel's surviving moat lines and the open plaza as the reference. Vinh International Airport (VVVH) lies about 5 km north, Dũng Quyết mountain about 5 km southeast, and the Lam River with the Bến Thủy bridges marks the city's southern edge. Hưng Nguyên, where the September 1930 column was bombed, is only 3 km away. Winter drizzle and low cloud reduce visibility over the city for weeks at a time; clearest conditions come in spring mornings before the heat haze sets in.
