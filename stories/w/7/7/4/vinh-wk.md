# Vinh

The guidebooks call Vinh drab, which is true and beside the point: this is a city rebuilt twice from rubble, on ground an emperor once chose for his capital.

**Location:** 18.6733, 105.6922

**Categories:** Cities in Vietnam, Nghệ An province, North Central Coast, Vietnam War sites, Historic citadels

## Introduction

Guidebooks are unusually blunt about Vinh. Drab. Rather uninteresting. A place to break a train journey. The verdict is fair as far as the streetscape goes — wide boulevards, low concrete blocks, very little older than sixty years — and it misses the reason entirely. Vinh looks like this because almost nothing was left standing. The French and the Việt Minh fought over it through the 1950s, and then American bombers spent years working over an industrial port city sitting on the only north-south road and rail line in the country. What you are walking through is not a city that never had character. It is a city that was rebuilt from the ground twice inside a generation.

## Rebuilt by Committee

The reconstruction was not improvised. Postwar Vinh was laid out according to Soviet and East German ideas about how a socialist city should function, with substantial East German assistance on the ground — planners, materials, methods. That is why the blocks sit in regular ranks, why the avenues are wider than the traffic of 1975 could possibly have needed, and why one of the main streets is still called Lenin Avenue.

Ho Chi Minh Square anchors the centre, a broad plaza of the kind the era liked to build. Whether the result is beautiful is a matter of taste. That it worked is not in doubt: a place levelled by bombing became a functioning regional capital of well over half a million, the largest city on the coast between Hanoi and Huế, within a few decades of the last raid.

## Three Gates and a Moat

The oldest thing you can put your hand on in Vinh is the citadel. Emperor Gia Long chose the site in 1803 and threw up an earthen enclosure; in 1831 Minh Mạng had it rebuilt in stone in the Vauban style then fashionable in East Asian fortification. It was a hexagon roughly 2,412 metres around, its walls 4.42 metres high, enclosing about 42 hectares, ringed by a moat 28 metres wide and more than three metres deep.

What survives is three gates and some lengths of that moat. Cửa Tiền faced south and was the ceremonial entrance, the one used by imperial processions and provincial governors; Cửa Tả stood to the east, Cửa Hữu to the west. Each is still marked in Chinese characters — front gate, left gate, right gate — and the interpretive plaques beneath them are in Vietnamese only. They stand among ordinary traffic now, arches with a city grown up around them, and the plan is eventually to make the ground between them a park.

## The Capital That Wasn't

Five kilometres southeast of the centre, Dũng Quyết mountain rises above the Lam River, and in 1788 the emperor Quang Trung — Nguyễn Huệ — decided this was where Vietnam should be governed from. The reasoning was geometric: Phú Xuân, his southern base, lay about 300 kilometres away, and Thăng Long about 300 in the other direction. He massed his army here before marching north to drive the Qing out of the capital.

He called it Phượng Hoàng Trung Đô, the Phoenix Central Capital, and building began in 1789 under the scholar Nguyễn Thiếp. Two concentric walls went up in a rough trapezoid, the outer circuit 2,820 metres around and enclosing 22 hectares. Quang Trung came to see it in May 1791 and again in January 1792, and died later that year. The capital never moved. Sections of the stone and brick walls survive, protected as a historic site since 1998, on a hillside now better known for its views.

## Eel Porridge and the 06:00 Bus

Vinh's pleasures are practical ones. Cháo lươn, eel porridge, is the local dish and belongs at roadside stalls rather than restaurants — thick, peppery, served early. Bánh mướt, soft rice rolls with fried shallot and Vietnamese pâté, comes from the same kind of pavement operation. Around the train station a cluster of khách sạn takes in the through traffic, and the Train Station Market handles everything else.

The transport is the point. Vinh sits on Highway 1A and the North–South railway, with Vinh International Airport five kilometres north — the fifth busiest in Vietnam. Trains run daily from Hanoi. The line south to Đồng Hới swings inland away from the highway and is worth taking for the scenery. And every second morning at six, a bus leaves for Phonsavan in Laos, over the Annamite range and onto the Plain of Jars — 850,000 dong, and no visa on arrival at that border.

## Two Sons and an Ending

Ten kilometres west of the city, in Kim Liên commune, is the village where Hồ Chí Minh was born — a thatched house, a garden, and a steady procession of Vietnamese visitors. Phan Bội Châu, the nationalist writer who shaped a generation of anti-colonial thinking before Hồ, came from the same corner of Nghệ An. For a provincial city that outsiders skip, Vinh has an outsized claim on the twentieth century.

It also, as of very recently, no longer exists. On 1 December 2024 the beach town of Cửa Lò was absorbed into the city, briefly making Vinh larger than it had ever been. Then on 1 July 2025, under the national restructuring that abolished district-level government across Vietnam, Vinh City was dissolved as an administrative unit, its territory redistributed into wards run directly by Nghệ An province. The buildings, the gates, the eel porridge and the 580,000 residents are all still there. Only the name on the paperwork has gone.

## Flight Context

Vinh lies at 18.6733°N, 105.6922°E on the north bank of the Lam (Cả) River, about 15 km inland from the Gulf of Tonkin. Vinh International Airport (VVVH) sits roughly 5 km north of the centre on flat coastal plain, with a single runway and no significant terrain on approach. The clearest visual references are the river itself and the pair of Bến Thủy bridges crossing it to the south, Dũng Quyết mountain rising just southeast of the city, and the Hồng Lĩnh ridge on the far bank about 15 km beyond. Cửa Lò beach marks the coast 15 km northeast. Terrain is low and flat, so 2,000–3,000 ft gives a good read on the grid layout and the citadel outline. Winter brings persistent low cloud and drizzle with poor visibility; tropical storms track through between August and October.
