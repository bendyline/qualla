# Hang En: The Swift Cave

Vietnamese named this cave after the birds that nest in it - a tunnel 1,645 meters straight through a mountain, with its own river, its own jungle, its own beach and its own weather.

**Location:** 17.4346, 106.3004

**Categories:** caves, karst, unesco-world-heritage, vietnam, natural-wonders

## Introduction

The birds got there first, and the name records it. Én is the Vietnamese word for swift, and the swifts nest here in a chamber roughly a hundred meters high, coming and going through gaps in the limestone that a person could not climb. Hang Én - swift cave - was known to local people for centuries before British and Vietnamese cavers put instruments to it in the 1990s and worked out what they were standing in. The mountain has a hole through it 1,645 meters long. Inside that hole there is a river, a beach, a patch of jungle and a climate that behaves differently from the one outside.

## A Mountain With a Tunnel Through It

The dimensions are the reason Hang Én is talked about at all. The passage runs 1,645 meters clean through the ridge, reaching a maximum height of around a hundred meters and a maximum width of about a hundred and seventy. That is enough to rank it the third largest cave in the world - behind only Hang Son Doong in the same national park and Deer Cave in Malaysia. Three entrances are known. A river runs through the length of it, and that river is also the principal hazard, because water level in a cave of this kind is not a fixed quantity. In the wet season it rises fast. The cave's scale is what makes it survivable and what makes it dangerous in the same breath: there is a great deal of room, and a great deal of water looking for somewhere to go.

## Weather of Its Own

What separates Hang Én from a merely large hole is that it has an interior world rather than an interior space. Sunlight reaches in through the entrances far enough to support vegetation, so there is jungle growing under rock. The river has deposited a genuine sand beach along one stretch, pale and flat, with a green pool beside it. Air moving between the entrances gives the cave its own temperature and humidity regime, distinct from the forest outside. Photographs of the place read oddly for exactly this reason: a beach, shafts of light, a wall of stone curving up and out of the frame, all of it arranged in a way that belongs outdoors and is not.

## Upstream of the Largest Cave on Earth

Hang Én is not an isolated curiosity. It is a feeder to Hang Son Doong, which lies about two kilometers away, and the two are part of the same drainage working its way through the Phong Nha karst - limestone that has been dissolving since roughly four hundred million years ago, the oldest major karst landscape in Asia. Getting to Hang Én is deliberately difficult. There are two ways in: helicopter, or a jungle trek of roughly four hours from the nearest road. The second is how everyone goes. The route drops off the highway into forest, crosses and recrosses a stream, and delivers walkers to an entrance that is difficult to read as an entrance at all until the ground stops being sky-lit.

## Neverland for a Season

In May 2015, footage of Hang Én and Son Doong aired on the American television program Good Morning America, an unusual amount of attention for a limestone passage in Quang Binh - the province that was merged into Quang Tri in June 2025. Viet Nam News later listed the broadcast among the top ten cultural events of the year in Vietnam. Around the same time, Warner Bros. selected Hang Én as a shooting location for Pan, its 2015 Peter Pan film, which is a fair indication of what the place looks like: producers looking for a convincing Neverland concluded that the real thing already existed in central Vietnam and simply needed a permit. The exposure raised an obvious question about how many visitors a cave like this can absorb. So far the four-hour walk has answered it better than any regulation could. The swifts, meanwhile, continue as they have.

## Flight Context

Hang En sits at 17.4346N, 106.3004E in Phong Nha-Ke Bang National Park, about two kilometers from Hang Son Doong. Like its neighbor it is invisible from the air - the entrances are shadowed notches in a limestone wall under dense canopy - so navigation is by valley and ridge rather than by the cave itself. Look for the Rao Thuong drainage cutting through steep conical tower karst with almost no standing surface water. Dong Hoi Airport (VVDH) is roughly 45 km east-northeast; Hue Phu Bai (VVPB) is about 200 km southeast. Best viewed at 6,000-9,000 feet. Valley fog is common until mid-morning, and the September-to-December monsoon brings sustained low cloud over the massif; February through June offers the best visibility.
