# Hang Son Doong: The Cave Nobody Could Find Twice

A woodcutter stumbled on the mouth of the world's largest cave in 1990, was frightened off by the wind roaring out of it, and then spent eighteen years failing to find it again.

**Location:** 17.4569, 106.2875

**Categories:** caves, karst, unesco-world-heritage, vietnam, natural-wonders

## Introduction

The wind gave it away, and the wind sent him home. On 10 December 1990, a man named Ho Khanh was working the jungle of Phong Nha in search of agarwood - the resinous heartwood that fetches serious money - when he came on an opening in the limestone. Cold air poured out of it hard enough to hear, and underneath that came the sound of fast water somewhere below. He decided it was nothing worth the risk and turned back. By the time he reached his village he could no longer place it on a map. What he had walked away from was the largest cave passage on Earth, and it would take him eighteen years to stand at that entrance again.

## Eighteen Years to Find It Again

Ho Khanh mentioned the hole in passing to two members of the British Cave Research Association who were working the area. They asked him to try to relocate it. He tried for years. In 2008 he finally managed it, and in April 2009 he led a BCRA team, under expedition leader Howard Limbert, back to the entrance. Between 10 and 14 April they surveyed what they had been let into. The numbers that came out of that week are still difficult to hold in the head: a cave running roughly nine kilometers, with a volume of about 38.5 million cubic meters, and a single passage more than five kilometers long, two hundred meters high and a hundred and fifty meters wide. Its cross-section is thought to be twice that of the next largest cave passage anywhere. The name it already had was local and modest - Hang Son Doong, the cave of the mountain behind Doong, Doong being a Bru-Van Kieu village nearby.

## Room for a Boeing 747

Scale in caves is famously hard to convey, so the comparisons reached for the familiar. An entire New York City block would fit inside the main passage, skyscrapers included. A Boeing 747 could fly down it without its wings touching either wall. Neither image is quite right, because both leave out what the space is actually full of. Stalagmites here reach eighty meters, among the tallest known anywhere. Behind a calcite barrier that the survey team named the Great Wall of Vietnam, they found cave pearls the size of baseballs - a size that is not supposed to happen, since cave pearls form grain by grain in shallow pools and rarely outgrow a marble. A fast subterranean river runs the length of it. The whole thing was dissolved out of soluble limestone somewhere between two and five million years ago, which by the standards of Phong Nha's four-hundred-million-year-old karst makes it a recent development.

## Where the Roof Fell In

Twice along its length, the ceiling of Son Doong has collapsed. These openings, called dolines, are what turn an enormous cave into something genuinely strange, because they let daylight down onto the cave floor. Rainforest grows in the shafts of light - real forest, with canopy trees, growing hundreds of meters below the mountain's surface. Mist forms and hangs. The cave generates weather of its own, and the ecology of those sunlit rooms differs from anything on the ridge above. Standing in one of the dolines is disorienting by design of the geology rather than of anything human: the sky is visible overhead, the walls curve away past the reach of a headlamp, and the ordinary distinction between inside and outside quietly stops applying.

## Still Getting Bigger

The survey was not the last word. By mid-2019, the underground river had been traced through to a neighboring cave called Hang Thung, adding more than 1.6 million cubic meters to the system's potential volume. The connection was not a new cave so much as the recognition that the boundary drawn in 2009 had been arbitrary - the water had always been going somewhere. Phong Nha-Ke Bang holds roughly three hundred known caves, of which about 126 kilometers of passage have been surveyed. The reasonable assumption is that the map is still wrong, just less wrong than it used to be.

## Three Thousand Dollars and No Cable Car

The first tourist group went in early in August 2013, paying US$3,000 each. Access is deliberately choked: permits are limited, the season runs January to August, and after that the rains raise the river and the cave becomes largely impassable. Since 2017 a single operator, Oxalis Adventure Tours, has held permission to bring visitors in. That restraint was tested. A proposal surfaced to run a 10.5-kilometer cable car through the cave, at a projected cost of between US$112 million and US$211 million, which would have converted a four-day expedition into an afternoon. Environmentalists opposed it and so did local residents, and the local government eventually cancelled the plan. The result is a world record that most people will only ever see photographed - which, given what a few hundred thousand visitors a year would do to a rainforest growing under a hole in a mountain, is probably the point.

## Flight Context

Hang Son Doong lies at 17.4569N, 106.2875E in Phong Nha-Ke Bang National Park, close to the Laotian border. There is nothing to see from the air but forest - the cave's entrances are narrow shafts in dense canopy, and the two dolines read at best as small dark irregularities in an unbroken green surface. What is visible is the setting: a field of steep conical karst towers with almost no surface drainage, cut by the Rao Thuong valley. Dong Hoi Airport (VVDH) is roughly 45 km east-northeast and is the practical base; Hue Phu Bai (VVPB) lies about 200 km southeast. Best viewed at 6,000-9,000 feet to appreciate the density of the tower karst. Morning fog sits in the valleys well past sunrise, and the September-to-December wet season brings persistent low cloud; February through June gives the clearest air.
