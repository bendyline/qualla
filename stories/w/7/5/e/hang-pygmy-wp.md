# Hang Pygmy

British cavers found the world's fourth-largest cave in 1997 and it took another twenty-one years before anyone was allowed to walk a paying visitor into it.

**Location:** 17.4265, 106.1965

**Categories:** Caves of Vietnam, Phong Nha-Kẻ Bàng, Karst, Landforms of Quảng Trị province, Tourist attractions in Quảng Trị province, Speleology

## Introduction

"We found this cave system in 1997, twenty years ago." Howard and Deb Limbert of the British Cave Research Association said that in 2017, standing at the edge of something they had located two decades earlier and mostly left alone since. The cave in question is Hang Pygmy, and the BCRA ranks it as the fourth largest on Earth. Twenty-one years passed between the survey and the moment the first commercial expedition was permitted to walk into it. Nothing was hiding. It simply sits in a part of the Annamite karst so awkward to reach that finding it and reaching it turned out to be two entirely different problems.

## Three of the Four

Rank the world's caves by the sheer size of the space inside them and the list produced by the British Cave Research Association reads like a regional inventory. First is Hang Sơn Đoòng. Second is Deer Cave in Malaysian Borneo. Third is Hang Én, and fourth is Hang Pygmy. Three of the four largest caves on the planet lie inside a single Vietnamese national park, within a few dozen kilometres of one another, in a limestone plateau that has been dissolving under monsoon rain since the Palaeozoic. Phong Nha–Kẻ Bàng holds more than three hundred surveyed caves and grottoes, and surveying is far from finished. The plateau is not unusually large. It is unusually hollow.

## An Arch You Cannot Photograph

The entrance is the thing people talk about: a limestone arch reaching around a hundred metres in both height and width, with the entrance section described at some 845 metres. Those numbers are hard to hold onto. A hundred metres of clear air overhead is roughly a thirty-storey building with the floors removed, and the practical consequence is that a camera at the mouth cannot get the whole of it — there is no vantage point far enough back. Beyond the arch the cave opens into large chambers and river passages, with stalactites and stalagmites in the formations you would expect and on a scale you would not. At the entrance itself the floor flattens into rock shelters, dry and level, and these are where expeditions camp.

## The Tiger's System

Hang Pygmy does not stand alone. It belongs to the Hang Hổ system — Tiger Cave — a network of connected and neighbouring passages running through the same block of limestone, and its neighbours are not modest either. Hang Over runs roughly 3.5 kilometres, a stretch of scrambling past stalactite formations and calcite pools that leads directly to Pygmy's back entrance. Kong Collapse lies in the same system. These are caves in the plural sense that the karst here demands: rainwater finding a joint in the stone, following it for a few hundred thousand years, and leaving behind a plumbing system large enough to walk through. What the surveyors mapped in the 1990s was not a cave but a district.

## Twenty Kilometres on Foot

Access is the whole story. There is no road, and reaching Hang Pygmy means a multi-day jungle trek of around twenty kilometres through terrain that is steep, wet, and thick enough that the route has to be maintained to remain a route at all. The standard expedition runs two days and one night: a trek from the roadhead, through Hang Over's stalactite-hung passages, to the back entrance of Pygmy, where a short abseil down a cliff face known as the Dinosaur Spine drops walkers into a campsite at the cave's main mouth. Groups are capped at thirty people. That figure is doing a lot of work — it is the difference between an expedition and a queue, and in a cave system with no hard floor of concrete and no fixed lighting, it is also the difference between a place that survives visitors and one that does not.

## Opened, Carefully

Quảng Bình province authorised exploration tours to Hang Pygmy in early 2018, twenty-one years after the cavers first reached it. The delay was not bureaucratic inertia so much as a deliberate policy that has shaped how the whole park is sold: the caves here are released slowly, one at a time, with tight permit numbers and licensed operators, on the reasoning that scarcity is worth more over thirty years than volume is over three. It is the same logic that keeps Sơn Đoòng permits tightly rationed. Whether it holds is an open question — Phong Nha's economy now depends on people arriving. For the moment, Hang Pygmy is reached the way it was found, on foot, carrying everything you need.

## Flight Context

17.427°N, 106.196°E, deep inside Phong Nha–Kẻ Bàng National Park in Quảng Trị province (formerly Quảng Bình, merged June 2025), roughly 25 km south-southwest of the Phong Nha cave entrances and about 55 km west-northwest of Đồng Hới. There is nothing built here to see — no road, no clearing, no structure — so treat this as terrain observation and fly it at 5,000–9,000 ft. Visual references: a dense unbroken field of grey-white conical karst towers and closed depressions, with the Hồ Chí Minh Highway's western branch running north–south some distance to the east and the Lao border about 25 km west. Large sinkholes and collapse dolines read as dark circular shadows in the canopy in low sun — the entrances themselves are effectively invisible from altitude. Nearest airport is Đồng Hới (VVDH), 55 km east-southeast; Huế/Phú Bài (VVPB) about 165 km south. Cloud sits on this karst most mornings year-round, the wet season from September to December brings sustained heavy rain and low overcast, and February to April gives the best visibility.
