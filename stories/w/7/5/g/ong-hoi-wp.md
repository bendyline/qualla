# Đồng Hới

A city on the narrowest waist of Vietnam that has been a frontier fort, a French airbase and a bombing target, and now lives off the largest caves on earth.

**Location:** 17.4833, 106.6000

**Categories:** Cities, Vietnam, Military history, Coastal, Caves

## Introduction

There is a lake near the middle of Đồng Hới called Bàu Tró, and the ground around it has been giving up stone tools and potsherds for a century. Human settlement here runs back some five thousand years. Walk out from the lake in any direction, though, and almost nothing you see above ground predates 1975. That gap — deep past, then a hard edit, then everything at once — is the shape of this city. Đồng Hới has been rebuilt so thoroughly that its oldest visible structure is a gate leading nowhere.

## The Gate That Leads Nowhere

Quảng Bình Quan stands beside National Route 1 with traffic streaming past both sides of it, a masonry arch attached to nothing. It was once the controlling gate of a serious fortress. The Nguyễn lords first put a garrison post here under Nguyễn Phúc Nguyên; in 1630 their strategist Đào Duy Từ proposed the wall system that Vietnamese still call Lũy Thầy, the Master's Rampart, and the post became one of its anchors. Gia Long enlarged the works in 1811. In 1824 Minh Mạng had the whole thing rebuilt in brick and stone on the Vauban model then fashionable across Asia — a perimeter of 469 trượng, about 1,860 metres, walls some four metres high, a moat roughly twenty-eight metres wide, and three gates. Of all that, the gate and one stretch of wall survive. The 2000s restoration was not universally admired.

## Where the Country Was Cut in Two

This stretch of coast spent centuries as somebody's frontier. It was contested ground between Champa and Đại Việt until 1069, when Lý Thường Kiệt's campaign settled the question, and it stopped being the far southern edge only after the Trần princess Huyền Trân was married to the Champa king Jaya Sinhavarman III and the border moved on past Huế. Then, from 1558, Vietnam split against itself. The Trịnh held the north, the Nguyễn the south, and the Gianh River became the line between Đàng Ngoài and Đàng Trong for more than two centuries. Đồng Hới sat just behind that line, and its walls existed for exactly one purpose: to stop an army coming down the coast. Geography made the job plausible. Vietnam is at its narrowest here, roughly forty kilometres from surf to mountains.

## Eleven February 1965

The French flew from Đồng Hới's airfield in the 1950s, striking at the Việt Minh and at the Pathet Lao across the Lao border. A decade later the same narrowness that made the town defensible made it a target: everything moving south toward the seventeenth parallel had to pass through a corridor a few dozen kilometres wide. On 11 February 1965, American bombing destroyed most of Đồng Hới. B-52 raids continued for years. The Catholic church at Tam Tòa was left as a shell, and its bell tower still stands near the centre of town, deliberately unrepaired. What is remarkable, walking the place now, is not the ruin that was kept. It is how completely the rest of it went, and how little fuss the city makes about that.

## Second City, Second Time

After the fall of Saigon in 1975 Quảng Bình vanished into a merged super-province called Bình Trị Thiên, and Đồng Hới became a provincial town in someone else's province. In 1990 the three provinces were separated again and Đồng Hới got its capital status back. The rebuild that followed is the city you see: a grid of low, bright buildings between the Nhật Lệ river and the sand, home to 133,672 people at the 2019 census across about 156 square kilometres. Trains stop here on the North–South line, 522 kilometres south of Hanoi. The airport opened on 18 May 2008 with a flight in from Nội Bài, and Ho Chi Minh City was connected the following July. The climate is tropical monsoon: hot, and in autumn spectacularly wet.

## The Caves Next Door

Đồng Hới's fortunes changed when the world worked out what was under the limestone an hour inland. Phong Nha-Kẻ Bàng became a World Heritage site, and on 10 December 1990 a local man named Hồ Khanh, out in the forest looking for agarwood, came across the entrance to a cave nobody had recorded. When British cavers surveyed it in April 2009, Sơn Đoòng turned out to hold the largest known cave passage on earth — over five kilometres long, around 200 metres high and 150 metres wide. The first tourist group went in during August 2013, on a permit costing three thousand dollars a head. Meanwhile the beaches at the Nhật Lệ mouth fill up every summer. On 12 June 2025 Quảng Bình was dissolved into an enlarged Quảng Trị province, and Đồng Hới, no longer a city in the administrative sense, became the seat of the whole thing.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 17.483°N, 106.600°E, on the central Vietnamese coast just north of the old seventeenth parallel. Đồng Hới Airport (VVDH) sits at the city's edge, elevation 59 ft, with a single 2,400 m runway. Approach cues from altitude: the Nhật Lệ river mouth and the long sandspit of Bảo Ninh, the white dune belt north and south of town, National Route 1 and the North–South railway threading the coastal plain, and the karst wall of Phong Nha-Kẻ Bàng rising 30–50 km inland to the northwest. Recommended viewing altitude 4,000–8,000 ft for the river-and-dune geometry; higher to appreciate how narrow Vietnam is here — roughly 40 km from the South China Sea to the Lao frontier. Best conditions March to August; September through November brings monsoon rain, low ceilings and flooding.
