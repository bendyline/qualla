# Nhật Lệ River

The short, contrary river that gave Đồng Hới its harbour, its frontier wars and its most famous citizen — a fifty-six-year-old woman who rowed the ferry through the bombing.

**Location:** 17.4831, 106.6289

**Categories:** Rivers, Vietnam, Coastal, Military history, Beaches

## Introduction

Nguyễn Thị Suốt was fifty-six when the bombing of Đồng Hới began, and she spent the next three years rowing a wooden boat back and forth across the Nhật Lệ with soldiers, ammunition and wounded men in it. Roughly fourteen hundred crossings a year, in open water, in daylight, on a river the Americans were actively trying to close. Vietnam knows her simply as Mẹ Suốt — Mother Suốt. Her statue now stands on the Đồng Hới bank looking out at the crossing she worked, and it is impossible to talk about this river without starting there.

## The Ferrywoman

She was born in 1908 in Trung Bính hamlet on Bảo Ninh, the long sandspit that shields the Nhật Lệ from the open sea, and she was already past fifty and working as a boatwoman when the war reached the coast. From 1964 to 1967 she ferried whoever needed ferrying. The poet Tố Hữu met her on 4 November 1965 and wrote a poem about her that same month; carried in the party newspaper and then recited on the radio in the Huế style by the singer Châu Loan, it made her a household name across North Vietnam. On 1 January 1967 she was formally named a hero of the transport sector in the war against the Americans. She stayed on the river, and in 1968 she was killed by a cluster bomb — her gravestone gives the date in the lunar calendar, the twenty-second day of the eighth month, which falls on 13 October.

## Two Rivers Make a Third

The Nhật Lệ does not really begin anywhere. It is the last stretch of a system that gathers in the Trường Sơn on the U Bò and Co Roi massifs and comes down in two arms: the Long Đại, seventy-seven kilometres through Quảng Ninh district, and the Kiến Giang, fifty-eight kilometres through Lệ Thủy. They meet near Trung Quán, and from that confluence the water travels under a single name for the short final run to the sea at Đồng Hới. Like its tributaries, it flows northeast — against the southeasterly grain of nearly every other river in Vietnam. The water is clear and carries little silt, so the mouth has stayed navigable and the beaches on either side of it have stayed white rather than grey.

## A Frontier Written in Water

For most of Vietnamese history this small river was a national boundary, and armies kept arriving at it. In 1069 Lý Thường Kiệt brought a Đại Việt fleet to the Nhật Lệ mouth and broke the Champa force defending it; the captured Champa king Chế Củ bought his freedom by ceding three prefectures, and the frontier of Đại Việt moved south past this point for good. Six centuries later the country divided against itself. Through the Trịnh–Nguyễn wars of the seventeenth century, the southern bank from the Gianh River down to the Nhật Lệ became the Nguyễn lords' fortified line, the water and the ramparts behind it doing the work that no army in the field could reliably do. Every one of those wars was fought over the same forty-kilometre-wide neck of land, because there is nowhere else to fight.

## The Mouth

The estuary is the reason people come now. Nhật Lệ Beach runs about two kilometres of fine white sand and is routinely listed among the most beautiful in the country; the season is April to September, before the monsoon shuts the coast down. Across the water lies Bảo Ninh, a long low spit of sand and casuarina where Mẹ Suốt was born, now given over largely to resorts and seafood restaurants. Small boats still work out of the river, and the fleet that ties up along the town bank in the evening is a working fleet rather than a picturesque one. Stand at the mouth at dusk and you get the whole geography in a single view: mountains behind, sand in front, and a narrow band of habitable ground squeezed between them.

## Where the Ferry Landed

There is a bridge now. The crossing that once required a woman in her fifties with a pair of oars takes a car about forty seconds, and the road that runs down to it on the town side is called Mẹ Suốt Street. Her statue was put up there in 2003, the work of the sculptor Phan Đình Tiến — an old woman leaning into an oar, facing the water. It is a curiously unheroic monument for a country that does not usually undersell its heroes: no flag, no rifle, no upward gaze. Just an old woman doing a hard job on a river she had known her whole life, through years in which nobody on either bank could count on surviving the week.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 17.483°N, 106.629°E, at the Nhật Lệ estuary on the central Vietnamese coast. Fly the mouth at 2,000–5,000 ft: the river reads as a bright band cutting the dune belt, with the long Bảo Ninh sandspit on the seaward side and the built-up grid of Đồng Hới on the west bank. Trace the water inland to the southwest to the Trung Quán confluence, where the Long Đại and Kiến Giang arms separate. Navigation cues are National Route 1 and the North–South railway crossing the coastal plain, the Quảng Bình Quan gate and Tam Tòa bell tower in the town, and the karst wall of Phong Nha-Kẻ Bàng 30–50 km inland to the northwest. Đồng Hới Airport (VVDH) is about 5 km north, elevation 59 ft. Best visibility March through August; September to November brings monsoon rain, low ceilings and flooding upstream.
