# Ban Karai Pass: The Gap They Could Not Close

For most of a decade American aircraft tried to seal a 418-meter notch in the Annamite Range, and for most of a decade the trucks kept coming through it.

**Location:** 17.3300, 106.2020

**Categories:** vietnam-war, mountain-passes, military-history, vietnam, laos

## Introduction

In March 1969, American UC-123 transports sprayed defoliant across four square miles of jungle in the Annamite Range. The target was not a base or a town. It was a single approach road feeding a saddle in the mountains 418 meters above sea level, a gap so ordinary that most maps of Vietnam never bothered to name it. The Ban Karai Pass mattered entirely because of what moved through it. Stripping the canopy was one attempt among many to make that traffic visible, and like nearly every other attempt, it did not work for long.

## A Gap in the Truong Son

The Annamite Range runs down the spine of Indochina like a wall laid on its side. Vietnamese call it the Truong Son, the Long Mountains, and the Ban Karai Pass cuts through it roughly 60 kilometers southwest of Dong Hoi and 115 kilometers northwest of Quang Tri. On the Vietnamese side the road is now National Road 565, marked Route 137 on wartime charts; on the Lao side it becomes Route 912, dropping into Khammouane Province. In peacetime this would be a minor border crossing serving a handful of upland villages. During the war it was one of two principal doorways, along with the Mu Gia Pass to the north, through which the Ho Chi Minh trail entered Laos. The people who built and ran that network called it Duong Truong Son, the Truong Son road. Southwest of Ban Karai, several highways converged in country that American planners nicknamed "the Chokes" - a name describing both the terrain and the hope invested in it.

## Turnpike, and Everything After

From 19 April to 24 June 1968, the U.S. Air Force ran Operation Turnpike, an intensive interdiction campaign aimed squarely at the Mu Gia and Ban Karai passes and heavy enough to include B-52 strikes. It was a prelude. That November, Commando Hunt began - seven numbered campaigns running until March 1972 - and Ban Karai became one of its prime targets. The CIA put roadwatch teams into the hills nearby to count what went past. Air Force intelligence eventually claimed some 51,000 trucks destroyed across the whole of Commando Hunt, a number later judged badly inflated by the people who had to defend it. The plainer measure is the one that mattered: despite constant bombing, neither the Air Force nor the Navy ever put the Ban Karai Pass out of operation for any sustained period.

## When the Guns Answered

In 1968, the defenses around the pass were machine guns and light anti-aircraft artillery in the 23mm and 37mm range - dangerous to a low-flying aircraft, survivable to most. That changed. In December 1970 the North Vietnamese moved SA-2 surface-to-air missiles onto their side of both the Mu Gia and Ban Karai passes. By 1971 the light guns had been joined by radar-guided 57mm, 85mm and 100mm batteries, and the arithmetic flipped. AC-119 gunships, which needed to loiter low and slow to be useful, stopped working near the pass. So did the B-52s. A place that three years of bombing was supposed to have closed had instead become airspace that American bombers went around.

## The Labor of Keeping a Road Open

None of this happened by itself. Group 559, formed in September 1959, was the unit responsible for the trail, and by 1967 it had completed 2,959 kilometers of road capable of taking vehicles. As many as 43,000 North Vietnamese and Laotians were working the system at a time - operating it, repairing it, pushing it further south. Most were not soldiers in any conventional sense. They filled craters, rebuilt bridges and cut bypasses under a bombing campaign that never really stopped, and they did it in a landscape of karst, leeches and malaria. In 1970 they finished something more ambitious still: a petroleum-oil-lubricants pipeline running straight through the Ban Karai Pass, so that fuel which had once ridden in drums on exposed truck beds could move where no gunship could see it.

## What Is Left in the Ground

By the campaign's own accounting, more than three million tons of ordnance fell on Laos during these years, roughly three times the tonnage dropped on North Vietnam itself. A great deal of it landed in the hills around Ban Karai and Mu Gia. Much of what missed is still there, and the clearance work in Khammouane and on the Vietnamese side has no end date. The pass itself is quiet now. Forest has grown back over the ground the defoliants stripped, National Road 565 carries ordinary traffic toward the Lao border, and the province has changed its name - Quang Binh was merged into Quang Tri in June 2025. The elevation has not changed. It is still 418 meters, and it never closed.

## Flight Context

Ban Karai Pass sits at 17.33N, 106.202E on the Vietnam-Laos border, 418 meters (1,371 feet) above sea level in the Annamite Range. The pass appears as a distinct low saddle in an otherwise continuous forested ridgeline, with National Road 565 threading through it toward Route 912 in Khammouane Province. Dong Hoi Airport (VVDH) lies about 60 km northeast; Thakhek (VLTK) is the nearest Lao field to the west, with Savannakhet (VLSK) further southwest. Best viewed at 5,000-8,000 feet AGL to read the saddle against the surrounding karst. The Annamites generate persistent low cloud and orographic rain on the eastern slopes, particularly from September through December; clear conditions are most likely in the March-to-June dry window. Terrain rises sharply on both flanks - treat published pass elevation as a minimum, not a clearance.
