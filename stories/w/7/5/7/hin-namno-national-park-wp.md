# Hin Namno National Park

Laos' first natural World Heritage site: a limestone wilderness in Khammouane where an entire river vanishes underground for seven kilometres and comes out the other side of a mountain.

**Location:** 17.4583, 105.9333

**Categories:** National parks of Laos, Geography of Khammouane province, Protected areas established in 2020, World Heritage Sites in Laos, Karst, Caves of Laos

## Introduction

The Xe Bang Fai does something rivers are not supposed to do. On the eastern side of the Khammouane limestone it runs straight into a mountain and does not reappear for seven kilometres. The passage it has carved reaches 200 metres across and 120 metres from waterline to ceiling — a corridor wide enough to fly a small aircraft down, in absolute darkness, with a river running beneath you. It is one of the largest river caves known anywhere on Earth, and for most of its existence it sat inside a protected area that almost nobody outside Laos had heard of.

## A Park by Decree

Hin Nam No — Hin Namno in some transliterations — lies in Boualapha District, Khammouane Province, in the narrow waist of Laos between the Mekong lowlands and the Vietnamese border. It covers 941 square kilometres. For years it was administered as a national biodiversity conservation area, a designation that carried rather less weight than its landscape deserved; in January 2020 a prime ministerial decree upgraded it to a national park outright, under the Ministry of Agriculture and Forestry. Its eastern boundary runs against Phong Nha–Kẻ Bàng in Vietnam, its northern one against Nakai–Nam Theun. The limestone Hin Nam No protects, in other words, is a piece of something much larger, sliced by borders that were drawn without any reference to the rock.

## Cliffs, Cones and Swallow Holes

Geologically the park sits at a seam, caught between the Khammouane limestone belt and the western flank of the Annamite Range. The result is a terrain of sheer escarpments, tower karst and conical hills, honeycombed with caves and drained almost entirely from below. Surface water here is temporary. Streams gather, run for a while, then find a swallow hole and go. What looks from above like an unbroken plateau of forest is in fact a lid over a plumbing system, and the great river caves — the Xe Bang Fai passage foremost among them — are simply the largest of its openings. In Lao the park is written ຫີນໜາມໜໍ່, and the first syllable, hin, is the word for stone. Everything else here follows from that.

## Forest Growing on Bare Rock

Around 722 square kilometres of the park, roughly 77.5 per cent, remains under forest, and it belongs to the Northern Annamites rain forests ecoregion. Evergreen, mixed deciduous and dipterocarp stands cover the softer ground. On the limestone itself grows something more particular: Central Indochina limestone karst forest, a plant community shared only with the Phou Hin Poun reserve to the north and with the Vietnamese karst across the border, and full of species that occur nowhere else. Trees here take root in cracks where a little soil has gathered, which makes recovery from any disturbance painfully slow. Surveys have counted 1,520 species of vascular plants and 536 vertebrate species. Seven species of diurnal primates live in the park, and every one of them is of conservation concern.

## The Case for Recognition

Recognition came slowly, and in stages. In early 2021 Hin Nam No was put forward for the IUCN Green List of Protected and Conserved Areas — a global standard that measures not what a park contains but how well it is actually run, which for a young park with a large resident population around it is a demanding test. Argument for the site had always rested on a simple observation: taken together with Phong Nha–Kẻ Bàng on the Vietnamese side, this would be one of the largest protected karst regions on the planet, and managing the two halves as though they were separate landscapes made no ecological sense.

## Laos' First

In 2025 the argument was accepted. Hin Nam No was inscribed on the World Heritage List under all three natural criteria — viii, ix and x — as an extension of Phong Nha–Kẻ Bàng, so that the two parks now form a single transboundary property. It is Laos' fourth World Heritage site and its first natural one, joining the town of Luang Prabang, inscribed in 1995, the temple complex of Vat Phou in 2001, and the megalithic Plain of Jars in 2019. Those three are monuments to what people built. Hin Nam No is a monument to what water did to stone over an unimaginable stretch of time, and to a decision — unusual anywhere — that a landscape should be governed by its own shape rather than by a line on a map.

## Flight Context

Centred near 17.46°N, 105.93°E in Khammouane Province, Laos, about 25 km west of the Vietnamese border. Best appreciated between 5,000 and 10,000 ft, where the limestone reads as a pale, deeply pitted plateau of towers and closed depressions, abruptly bounded by escarpments on its western side and blending into the darker Annamite ridges to the east. Visual references: the Xe Bang Fai river approaching from the northwest and vanishing at the karst front, and the continuous karst signature running unbroken across the border into Phong Nha–Kẻ Bàng. Nearest fields are Thakhek (VLTK) roughly 90 km northwest and Savannakhet (VLSK) to the south; Đồng Hới (VVDH) lies over the range in Vietnam. The wet southwest monsoon from May to October brings persistent build-ups and low cloud in the valleys; November to February offers the steadiest visibility, with dry-season burning haze increasing through March and April.
