# Sakon Nakhon

A lakeside Isan town where Thai and Lao blur into each other, and where every Christmas Eve the largest Catholic community in Thailand fills the roads with illuminated stars.

**Location:** 17.1564, 104.1456

**Categories:** Cities and towns in Thailand, Isan, Sakon Nakhon province

## Introduction

On the evening of 24 December, a corner of Buddhist northeast Thailand lights up with the Star of Bethlehem — roughly two hundred of them, built on frames, wired to generators and carried through the streets of Tha Rae on the northern shore of Nong Han lake. The stars are enormous. Some ride on trucks; some are shouldered by teams of parishioners; all of them converge on St Michael's Cathedral. Twenty-one kilometres away in Sakon Nakhon town, wax castles are offered at a Buddhist stupa in October and star parades roll in December, and nobody local finds the combination remarkable at all.

## The Star Makers of Tha Rae

Tha Rae began in 1884, when the French missionary Xavier Guego settled on the lakeshore with about ten Vietnamese Catholic households and ten local Isan families. Those Vietnamese families had crossed the Mekong to get here, and they stayed. What grew from those twenty families is now the largest Catholic community in Thailand, seat of the Archdiocese of Thare and Nonseng, with a cathedral, its own schools and a Christmas Eve procession that draws visitors from across the country. The star parade is not an import dropped into Isan. It is an Isan festival that happens to be Catholic: the same appetite for scale, light and competing neighbourhood floats that drives every other celebration on this plateau.

## A Town That Speaks Two Languages Without Noticing

Listen in the market and you will hear Thai and Lao braided together, which is what people here mean by Isan. Central Thai works everywhere, but it is the second language on the street, not the first. Foreign visitors are rare enough that English signage stops at the road markings, and shopkeepers will meet you with whatever fragments they have and a great deal of goodwill. The mix runs deeper than language. Provincial fairs here parade floats for the Phu Thai, So, Yo, Saek, Kaleung and Kula, alongside Vietnamese and Chinese communities — at the Ruam Nam Jai Thai Sakon and Red Cross Fair each new year, the procession leaves Ming Mueang Field at two in the afternoon and works its way down Sukkasem Road to the city hall.

## Six Hundred and Forty-Seven Kilometres From Bangkok

Sakon Nakhon is not on the way to anywhere, and it has never had a railway. Driving from Bangkok takes seven to nine hours if you respect the speed limits. The train gets you as far as Udon Thani — the sleeper leaves the capital around eight in the evening, is timetabled into Udon at six in the morning and frequently is not — and from there a hundred-baht air-conditioned bus covers the remaining 159 kilometres in three or four hours. Budget carriers fly into Sakon Nakhon Airport on the north side of town, which is by some distance the least romantic option and by some distance the most sensible. Once you arrive, transport means samlor, motorised tricycle and a negotiated fare, or a rented motorcycle at a hundred to two hundred baht a day.

## Clay, Cloth and Rockets

The province works in materials. At Ban Chiang Krua, fifteen kilometres out along Highway 22, farming families throw pots as a second income and have done for generations. At Ban Charn in Ban Muang district, a crafts centre teaches silk weaving, blacksmithing and wood carving. Out in Akat Amnuai district, the villages of Wa Yai and Ban Don Daeng are known for khit-patterned cotton and silk coloured entirely with natural dyes — a slow, unforgiving process that produces depths of blue and rust no chemical bath quite reproduces. Then there is the noise. In the first week of May, Phang Khon district holds Bun Bang Fai, the rocket festival, in which home-built rockets are fired into the sky to ask it for rain, and the district competes on altitude, noise and spectacle in roughly that order.

## The Town on the Lake

About 76,000 people live in Sakon Nakhon proper, on flat ground 175 metres above sea level along the southwest bank of Nong Han. April is brutal — the town has recorded 43.4°C — and the rains run from May to October. The place has a quieter history than its sleepiness suggests. During the Second World War it sheltered the Free Thai resistance, and in the late 1950s the Phu Phan hills behind it became a base for the Communist insurgency, which is why the mountains south of town were, for two decades, somewhere the Thai state did not go lightly. Today the same hills hold national parks and forest monasteries, and the road south to That Phanom and the Mekong is an easy afternoon.

## Flight Context

Sakon Nakhon town sits at 17.16°N, 104.15°E, on the southwest shore of Nong Han lake at about 175 m (574 ft) AMSL. Sakon Nakhon Airport (VTUI) lies on the north side of the city. From 3,000–5,000 ft AGL the lake dominates — a broad shallow sheet roughly 125 km² with more than thirty islands — and the gilded stupa of Wat Phra That Choeng Chum marks the old town centre. Tha Rae and its cathedral stand on the northern shore, 21 km from the city. The Phu Phan range walls the horizon to the south; the Mekong and the Lao border lie roughly 90 km east past Nakhon Phanom (VTUW). Udon Thani (VTUD) is 159 km west. Best visibility November to January; expect burning-season haze February to April and afternoon thunderstorms May to October.
