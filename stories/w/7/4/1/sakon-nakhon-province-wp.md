# Sakon Nakhon province

The province whose name means "city of cities" is better known in Thailand for what happens outside its cities: the forest monasteries of the Phu Phan hills.

**Location:** 17.1542, 104.1361

**Categories:** Sakon Nakhon province, Provinces of Thailand

## Introduction

On 11 November 1949, after eleven days of illness, a seventy-nine-year-old monk died at a forest monastery on the edge of Sakon Nakhon town. Ajahn Mun Bhuridatta had been born in a village in Ubon Ratchathani and had spent decades walking — through forest, along the Mekong, from one remote place to the next — refusing the settled monastic life almost entirely. His death here turned an ordinary provincial capital into a destination. For Thai Buddhists, Sakon Nakhon is not primarily a place on a map. It is where the forest tradition came to rest.

## The Teachers in the Trees

The Thai forest tradition was, in Ajahn Mun's hands, a deliberate rebuke to comfortable temple Buddhism: one meal a day, robes of discarded cloth, sleeping under a tree or in a cave, meditation as the whole of the work. He came back to Isan in 1939 to teach and eventually settled at Wat Pa Ban Nong Phue, in these hills. His students carried the method outward — Ajahn Maha Bua, Ajahn Khao Analayo, Ajahn Lee Dhammadharo, Ajahn Thate Desaransi, and, here in Sakon Nakhon, Luang Pu Fan Acaro. What Sakon Nakhon has that no other province quite matches is the density: forest meditation monasteries scattered through the Phu Phan uplands, most of them plain, quiet and entirely uninterested in visitors, which is exactly why people come.

## City of Cities

The name is Sanskrit twice over. Sakala means whole or entire; nagara means town or city. Put together, Sakon Nakhon reads as something like the city of cities — a grand title for a place that has worn several others. Under Khmer influence the settlement on the great lake was Nong Han Luang. When Lan Xang held this stretch of the Mekong's west bank, it became Chiang Mai Nong Han. Locals knew it as Sakon Thawapi. The current name dates to 1830, in the reign of King Rama III, and the modern province took its administrative shape under Rama V in 1892. Each renaming marks a change of allegiance rather than a change of population; the people stayed put while the maps around them were redrawn.

## The Ridge That Splits a Plateau

Sakon Nakhon sits on the Khorat Plateau within reach of the Mekong, and its southern boundary is drawn by the Phu Phan mountains. These are not tall — Phu Lang Ka, the range's highest point, reaches 641 metres over in Nakhon Phanom — but they matter out of all proportion to their height. Built of Early Cretaceous sandstone and siltstone, the range runs some 180 kilometres northwest to southeast and divides the plateau into two separate drainage basins. Everything north of the ridge, including Nong Han, drains toward the Mekong. Forest still covers 1,692 square kilometres of the province, about 17.7 percent of it, and Phu Phan National Park protects 665 of those — Thailand's seventh national park, gazetted in 1982, six years before its neighbour Phu Pha Yon.

## Who Lives Here

About 1,142,657 people were counted in the province in 2023, spread across 18 districts, 125 subdistricts and 1,323 villages, which places Sakon Nakhon seventeenth in Thailand by population. Calling them all Isan flattens something worth noticing. Phu Thai, So, Yo, Kaleung, Saek and Tai Dam communities each keep their own speech and their own ceremonies, and Vietnamese and Chinese families have been part of provincial life for well over a century. The economy runs on fish and rice, in that order of local pride. It also runs, as most of Isan does, on money sent home: the region holds roughly a third of Thailand's population and produces around a tenth of its GDP, and a great many Sakon Nakhon households are held together by a wage earned in Bangkok.

## Six Things Worth Boasting About

Thai provinces advertise themselves in rhyming mottoes, and Sakon Nakhon's is a decent index of what the place values. Phra That Choeng Chum, companion of the home. The Phu Phan royal residence, companion of the town. Nong Han, famed and lovely. The dazzling wax castles. The beautiful women of the Phu Thai. A land steadfast in the Buddha's dhamma. A stupa, a palace, a lake, a festival, a people and a faith — no mention of industry, no mention of Bangkok, and the dhamma given the last word, which in a province defined by its forest monks is not an accident.

## Flight Context

Sakon Nakhon province centres near 17.15°N, 104.14°E on the Khorat Plateau of northeastern Thailand, bounded clockwise from the north by Nong Khai, Bueng Kan, Nakhon Phanom, Mukdahan, Kalasin and Udon Thani. Terrain is low and flat over most of the province — the provincial capital sits at about 175 m (574 ft) AMSL — rising to the Phu Phan range along the southern boundary, where ridges reach roughly 500–650 m. From 6,000–10,000 ft the two defining features are unmistakable: Nong Han lake as a bright sheet northeast of the capital, and the long dark northwest-southeast wall of Phu Phan. Sakon Nakhon Airport (VTUI) serves the capital; Nakhon Phanom (VTUW) lies 91 km east near the Mekong and Lao border, Udon Thani (VTUD) 160 km west. Route 22 links them. Clearest conditions run November to January; expect dense agricultural-burning haze February to April and convective build-ups over the Phu Phan ridge on monsoon afternoons.
