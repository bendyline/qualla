# Phra That Dum

One brick Khmer tower survives out of three on a shared laterite platform just outside Sakon Nakhon, its northern lintel still carrying Vishnu asleep on the cosmic ocean.

**Location:** 17.1439, 104.1642

**Categories:** Buildings and structures in Sakon Nakhon province, Angkorian sites in Thailand, Tourist attractions in Sakon Nakhon province

## Introduction

The directions to Phra That Dum are the directions to a school. Three kilometres out of Sakon Nakhon, on the road toward Phatthana Suksa School, in the subdistrict of Ngew Don, and the pupils going past it every morning are walking beneath a piece of the Khmer Empire. There is not much of it left. A single brick tower stands on a broad laterite platform that was clearly cut to hold three, and the two empty places beside it are the most honest thing on the site. Angkorian sanctuaries in Isan tend to be presented as ruins that survived. This one is better understood as a ruin that mostly did not.

## Two Thirds Missing

The plan was conventional and it was ambitious: three towers in a row on one shared laterite base, all of them opening east toward the sunrise, which is where a Khmer sanctuary always faced. What went up on that base was brick. Laterite comes out of the ground soft enough to saw into blocks and then hardens in the air into something close to permanent, which is why the platform is still perfectly legible after a thousand years. Brick is a different proposition under a monsoon that hammers this plateau from May to October every year. Two of the towers went down and were carted off or ploughed under. The survivor is modest by the standards of the region's Khmer architecture, smaller and plainer than the sandstone stupa of Phra That Narai Cheng Weng on the far side of town, and it is the last witness to what the other two looked like.

## The Moat You Have to Be Told About

Walk the ground to the north and to the south and you can still pick out the trace of a moat, a shallow inflection in the field that reads as nothing at all until somebody points it out. Khmer builders enclosed their sanctuaries in water on purpose. The moat was drainage in a landscape that floods, it was a boundary between consecrated ground and everything else, and it was a model of the cosmic ocean that surrounds the world-mountain in Hindu cosmology. Here that last meaning is not abstract, because the carving over the north door shows exactly what the moat was standing in for. The building and the ditch were making the same argument in two different materials.

## Reading the Northern Arch

Above the northern opening lies Narayana, another name for Vishnu, stretched out asleep on the cosmic ocean between the destruction of one universe and the making of the next. It is the most serene image in the entire Hindu repertoire and it turns up all over Khmer Isan. The rest of the ornament is busier. Gods ride their animal mounts across the register, and beneath them sits the devouring face that Thai tradition names Rahu, the demon who swallows the moon and causes eclipses. Elephants and lions crowd the margins. Foliate scrollwork fills every gap the sculptors could find, because filling gaps was the aesthetic. The whole scheme belongs to roughly the same generation of work as the province's other Khmer monuments, the tenth to twelfth centuries in Western reckoning.

## An Ordinary Kind of Survival

Sakon Nakhon does not treat Phra That Dum as a headline. Nong Han glitters on the far side of the city, Phra That Choeng Chum carries the provincial identity, and the great pilgrimage stupa of That Phanom pulls the crowds away southeast to the Mekong. What Phra That Dum offers instead is proximity. It sits in a working subdistrict, on a road named for a school, close enough to town that people encounter it without planning to. That is a rarer kind of heritage than the ticketed sort. A monument you walk past on a Tuesday morning belongs to a place in a way that a monument you make a trip to never quite does.

## Flight Context

Phra That Dum lies at 17.144 N, 104.164 E, about three kilometres southeast of Sakon Nakhon city centre in Ngew Don subdistrict. The dominant visual reference from the air is Nong Han, the broad natural lake immediately northeast of the city; the site sits on the farmland between the lake's southern shore and the Phu Phan foothills. A single small brick tower will not resolve from cruise, so plan on 1,500 to 2,500 ft AGL. Sakon Nakhon Airport (VTUI) is essentially overhead the city, Nakhon Phanom (VTUW) is roughly 90 km east on the Mekong, and Udon Thani (VTUD) is about 150 km west. Expect towering cumulus over the Phu Phan ridge on monsoon afternoons between May and October; the November-to-February dry season offers the calmest air, with agricultural haze thickening toward March.
