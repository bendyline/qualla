# Wat Phra That Choeng Chum

Sakon Nakhon's white stupa is a Lao-style tower built straight over a Khmer sanctuary — one religion's architecture swallowed whole by the next, with the footprints of four Buddhas sealed underneath.

**Location:** 17.1647, 104.1532

**Categories:** Buddhist temples in Sakon Nakhon province, Angkorian sites in Thailand, Registered ancient monuments in Thailand

## Introduction

Cut this stupa open and you would find another building inside it. The white tower that dominates old Sakon Nakhon — twenty-four metres of brick and mortar rising from a square base to a tapering lotus-bud crown — is Lan Xang work, the architecture of the Lao kingdom that once held both banks of the Mekong. But it was not built on empty ground. Underneath the plaster stands a Khmer sanctuary of sandstone and laterite, older by centuries, and the four arched doorways at the tower's base are Khmer doorways, kept because there was no reason to destroy them.

## The Gathering of the Feet

Choeng chum means, roughly, the place where the feet come together, and the name is the whole point. The stupa is understood to seal the footprints of four Buddhas at a single spot: Kakusandha, Konagamana, Kassapa and Gotama, the historical Buddha of our era — with room reserved for Metteyya, the Buddha still to come. In Buddhist cosmology this is a big claim. It puts Sakon Nakhon on the itinerary of the entire cycle of world-teachers, not merely the current one. The legend supplies the mechanism: the Buddha, travelling the Mekong with his disciple Ananda to visit the places where earlier Buddhas had left their marks, stopped here, and a local ruler who came out to meet him built a monument over the spot afterwards. Lao chronicles record the shrine under another name entirely — Phra That Nong Han, the stupa of the great lake.

## Khmer Stone on the Upper Mekong

Set into the eastern doorframe is an inscription in old Khmer script, and it is the most honest thing on the site. Angkor's cultural reach ran a very long way north of Cambodia, and Sakon Nakhon province is scattered with the evidence — Phra That Narai Cheng Weng, Phra That Phu Pek on its hilltop, Phra That Dum, and a laterite causeway still called simply the Khmer Bridge. When Khmer power receded and Lan Xang moved in, the new arrivals did not clear the old sanctuaries away. They wrapped them. What stands at Choeng Chum today is less a replacement than an argument settled by absorption, and you can still read the losing side's handwriting on the door.

## The Compound Around It

The stupa is the reason people come, but it is not the only thing here. Beside it, a vihara houses Luang Pho Ong Saen, a Buddha image seated in the maravijaya posture — right hand reaching down to touch the earth, calling it to witness against Mara — and revered across the province in its own right. A well in the compound draws on an underground spring and is treated as sacred water. The ordination hall dates to 1827, from the years when the town was settling into its current name and identity. The three-tiered bell tower is far newer: it went up in 1960, built by Sakon Nakhon's Vietnamese residents, a community whose presence in this province long predates the temple's need for a belfry.

## A Working Temple, Not a Ruin

Choeng Chum holds the rank of second-class royal monastery, and it has never stopped functioning. Its own great festival falls on the eleventh to fifteenth days of the waxing moon of the second lunar month, when the compound fills, the stupa is circumambulated and the candles multiply. On ordinary days it is simply the temple in the middle of town: schoolchildren cutting through the grounds, dogs asleep in the shade of the wall, someone kneeling at the vihara with three sticks of incense. Sakon Nakhon province advertises itself in a rhyming motto, and this stupa opens it — Phra That Choeng Chum, companion of the home. Not companion of the province, or of the kingdom. Of the home.

## On the Coin

Thailand puts its temples on its money, one great monument to each denomination, and Choeng Chum's outline was given to the ten-satang piece. It is an oddly fitting placement. Ten satang is a tenth of a baht — a coin so small in value that it rarely reaches a shop till any more and mostly shuttles between banks, and yet it is the coin that carries this stupa. A monument said to hold the footprints of four Buddhas, minted onto the least valuable metal disc the country makes. The people of Sakon Nakhon, who have watched this place be Khmer and then Lao and then Siamese without ever ceasing to be theirs, would probably say that is exactly the right way round.

## Flight Context

Wat Phra That Choeng Chum stands at 17.165°N, 104.153°E in the centre of Sakon Nakhon town, northeastern Thailand, at about 175 m (574 ft) AMSL. The 24 m white-and-gold stupa is a distinct point target against the low urban grid — best identified from 1,500–3,000 ft AGL, with Nong Han lake immediately northeast of the town providing the approach reference. Sakon Nakhon Airport (VTUI) lies on the north side of the city, roughly 4 km away, so respect the circuit and any local overflight restrictions above an active religious site. Nakhon Phanom (VTUW) is 91 km east near the Mekong; Udon Thani (VTUD) 160 km west via Route 22. The Phu Phan range forms the southern skyline. Clearest air November to January; expect haze February to April and afternoon convection during the May-to-October monsoon.
