# Summer Isles

An archipelago in the mouth of Loch Broom where a conservationist lived alone in the 1930s and a fictional pagan island borrowed the name for cinema.

**Location:** 58.0303, -5.4534

**Categories:** Islands, Archipelago, Conservation, Scottish Highlands

## Introduction

In 1939, Frank Fraser Darling and his wife moved onto Tanera Mòr to spend two years alone with the seabirds. He was already known as a naturalist, but the experience would shape what became Scottish conservation. The book he wrote about it - 'Island Years', published in 1940 - described Priest Island, the most remote of the Summer Isles, as a place of staggering beauty. The Summer Isles are scattered like dropped coins in the mouth of Loch Broom, Scottish Highland coast, twenty-something islands and skerries that have spawned novels, stamps, and one famously misattributed horror film.

## An Archipelago of Names

Twenty-eight of the Summer Isles have names. Tanera Mòr - 'big Tanera' - is the largest, and the only one that has held a permanent population in recent times. Tanera Beag is its smaller sibling. Then come the islands the rest of the world has mostly never heard of: Bò Bhùiridh, Bottle Island, Càrn Deas, Càrn Iar, Eilean a' Chàr, Eilean Choinaid, Eilean Mullagrach, Glas-leac Beag and Glas-leac Mòr ('small' and 'big' grey-rock). Priest Island - Eilean a' Chlèirich - sits furthest out toward the Atlantic, owned now by the RSPB and important for storm petrels. Names tell you geology, ownership, and what someone once saw there: Horse Island, Bottle Island, the Sgeirs and the Stacs. They are skerries with biographies.

## The Conservationist's Years

Frank Fraser Darling lived on Tanera Mòr from 1939 to 1943 with his wife and son, growing what food they could and watching the seabirds work the cliffs. He was studying wildlife but he was also studying himself, and the long isolation became a turning point in his thinking about how humans should relate to land. 'Island Years' came out of those summers and winters - storms in February, light until midnight in June. Darling went on to become one of the most influential voices in twentieth-century British conservation. The islands he wrote about are now part of the Assynt-Coigach National Scenic Area, one of forty such designated landscapes in Scotland.

## The Wicker Man Confusion

Robin Hardy's 1973 cult horror film 'The Wicker Man' is set on a pagan Hebridean island called Summerisle. People have been confusing it with the Summer Isles ever since. The confusion is geographical wishful thinking - the film was actually shot around Newton Stewart in Dumfries and Galloway, on the opposite side of Scotland. The real Summer Isles never saw Christopher Lee. But the name has had other lives in fiction too: Ian R. MacLeod wrote a novella called 'Summer Isles', and Desmond Bagley's 1977 thriller 'The Enemy' features a fictional Summer Isle called Cladach Duillich, the 'Sad Shore'. Even the Children's Film Foundation got in on it: 'The Big Catch' (1968) sends a group of boys here to capture a wild pony, filmed in and around Ullapool and Lochinver.

## The Stamps

From 1970 onwards, the Tanera Mòr post office printed its own local stamps for tourists and collectors. The practice was permitted because the islands were so remote that a private postal service made sense: a visitor could write a postcard, affix a Summer Isles stamp and a Royal Mail stamp, drop it in the box, and the local boat would carry it to the mainland for onward forwarding. The stamps showed Highland wildlife, lighthouses, the islands themselves. They became collectors' items - philatelic bureaux around the world held them. The practice continued until around 2013 and ownership changed. Lundy off Devon and Caldey Island off Tenby had done the same. Whether it will resume under the current ownership is anyone's guess.

## Flight Context

The Summer Isles centre on 58.03°N, 5.45°W, in the mouth of Loch Broom. From the air the archipelago stands out as a tight scatter of islands south of Achiltibuie. Nearest airport is Inverness (EGPE), 75 nm southeast. Stornoway (EGPO) on Lewis lies roughly 50 nm to the west. Recommended viewing altitude 2,000-4,000 ft. Priest Island, the outermost, is RSPB-protected for storm petrels - low-level overflight not recommended in nesting season.
