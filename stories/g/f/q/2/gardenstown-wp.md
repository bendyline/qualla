# Gardenstown

Founded in 1720 by Alexander Garden, a fishing village 8.7 miles east of Banff - in the parish of Gamrie, with a 1513 ruined kirk above and the threat of the sea always close.

**Location:** 57.6703, -2.3370

**Categories:** village, fishing, Scotland, Aberdeenshire, coastal

## Introduction

The man's name was Alexander Garden. The year was 1720. The harbour came first. He chose the spot - a notch in the high Aberdeenshire cliffs east of Banff, in the civil parish of Gamrie - and built a fishing village around the water. Three centuries later Gardenstown is still here, the harbour still works, and the village is still called Gamrie by anyone who lives within twenty miles. The fishing has thinned. The houses have multiplied up the slope. The dolphins of the Moray Firth still come close enough to be watched from the seawall - the same seawall the village now anxiously keeps an eye on every winter.

## Battle of the Bloody Pits

Long before Alexander Garden picked the site, the cliffs above had a story of their own. In 1004 a force of Danes who had landed on this coast met a force of Scots in a battle Gamrie tradition calls the Battle of the Bloody Pits. The Scots won. The Church of St John the Evangelist, built nearby in 1513, was put up to commemorate that ancient victory - and is now itself a roofless ruin, abandoned in 1830 when the parish church moved down into the village. The site sits on the headland west of town: a scenic clifftop ruin with the Moray Firth at its feet and a view that has not changed in five centuries.

## Older Still

Human use of this coast runs much deeper than 1004 or 1513. Neolithic and Bronze Age peoples lived in the surrounding land, leaving the long barrow at Longman Hill and the cairn at Cairn Lee - earthworks that predate the village by more than three thousand years. The Aberdeenshire cliffs have always been a useful place: defensive, exposed to weather but also to fish, looking out over a firth rich in herring and haddock. Gardenstown is only the most recent of a long sequence of settlements that have used this stretch of coast for one purpose or another.

## Faith and Crisis

Between 2008 and 2010 the anthropologist Joseph Webster lived in Gardenstown studying its religious and economic life. He published the resulting ethnography in 2013 as The Anthropology of Protestantism: Faith and Crisis Among Scottish Fishermen. The book documents a community where evangelical Protestant faith and a declining fishing industry have wound around each other for generations. Gardenstown is unusual among Scottish villages for the strength and visibility of its religious life - the Gardenstown New Church and several smaller fellowships have congregations large for a village of 560. Webster's title carries the central idea: faith in this village has always been bound up with the boats and what the sea will or will not provide.

## Joseph Watt, V.C.

On 15 May 1917 a small Royal Navy drifter named Gowanlea was working an anti-submarine net in the Otranto Strait when she encountered the Austro-Hungarian cruiser SMS Helgoland. The cruiser ordered her crew to abandon ship. Her skipper - Joseph Watt, a fisherman born in Gardenstown - instead called his men to action stations, opened fire with the drifter's single small gun, and fought until the cruiser disengaged. Watt was awarded the Victoria Cross. He came home to Gardenstown after the war and lived the rest of his life as a herring fisherman. He is buried in Macduff. The village's quiet monument to him is mostly the memory itself.

## Sea Defences and the Future

Gardenstown's most pressing modern story is the sea. The road that runs along the seafront sits on top of the seawall that protects the village from the firth. Heavy floods in 1953 washed away two houses. In 2008 Aberdeenshire Council declined to fund road repairs on the grounds that this stretch was a private road. Residents pointed out that the road is the wall - and that the council does own the wall. The controversy continued. The village still has its meteorological station, its osteopathy clinic, its whale and dolphin rescue centre, its pub, and its church. Whether it keeps its seawall in working order is the question that runs under everything else.

## Flight Context

Gardenstown lies at 57.6703 N, 2.337 W on the north coast of Aberdeenshire about 8.7 miles east of Banff by road, on the south shore of the Moray Firth. From altitude the village reads as a cluster of houses stepping down a steep cliff to a small harbour, with the ruined Church of St John the Evangelist visible on the headland west of town. Nearest airport is Aberdeen (EGPD) about 35 nm to the south-east; RAF Lossiemouth (EGQS) lies west. The B9031 coast road threads east past Crovie and Pennan.
