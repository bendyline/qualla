# Fraserburgh

Once Scotland's herring capital and now Europe's biggest shellfish port, Fraserburgh - the Broch - has been remaking itself at the windswept corner of Aberdeenshire for five centuries.

**Location:** 57.6930, -2.0050

**Categories:** towns, fishing-port, scotland, aberdeenshire, coastal

## Introduction

The Fraser family bought the lands of Philorth in 1504, and within ninety years they had built a castle on Kinnaird Head, a port, a college, and a town with their name on it. The college was suppressed by James VI; the castle was converted into Scotland's first mainland lighthouse; and the port became the largest shellfish landing site in Scotland and one of the biggest in Europe. The Broch, as locals call it, has been remaking itself for five centuries on this exposed corner of Aberdeenshire, where the coast pivots from east to north and the wind never quite settles.

## A Burgh Built on Ambition

Sir Alexander Fraser built the port in 1579 and four years later petitioned the king to rename the village from Faithlie to Fraserburgh. In 1595 he secured a grant from the Parliament of Scotland to erect a college building, and in 1597 the Church of Scotland nominated the Rev. Charles Ferme as its first and only principal. The University of Fraserburgh did not last. Ferme was arrested on the orders of James VI for his role in the 1605 General Assembly of Aberdeen, and the college closed soon after, used briefly again in 1647 when King's College Aberdeen relocated to escape plague. Only a plaque at the Heritage Centre marks where Scotland's third university briefly stood. Sir Alexander Fraser had spent so much on his ambitions that he was forced to sell Philorth Castle, the family's ancestral home.

## The Light on the Castle

In 1787, Fraserburgh Castle stopped being a castle. The Trustees of the Northern Lights leased Sir Alexander's old tower on Kinnaird Head and converted it into the first lighthouse on mainland Scotland - and the first lit anywhere in Scotland by the Commissioners of Northern Lights. The lamp, designed by the engineer Thomas Smith with seventeen whale-oil burners backed by parabolic reflectors, was said to be the most powerful light of its day. It was first lit on 1 December 1787. Smith would go on to build ten more lighthouses around the Scottish coast and to apprentice his stepson, Robert Stevenson, founding the dynasty that would light the rest of the world's most dangerous shores. The Kinnaird Head light was rebuilt in the 1820s, superseded by a new tower in 1991, and now houses the Museum of Scottish Lighthouses.

## Silver and Loss

Fraserburgh's population grew from about 2,000 in 1780 to nearly 3,000 by 1831, driven by herring fishing that intensified after 1815. At the peak, the harbour held 220 boats of the herring fishery and another 1,200 seasonal workers crowded into the parish each season. Then came the lifeboat disasters that shaped the town's memory. The first RNLI station in Scotland opened here in 1858. Yet in 1919, the lifeboat Lady Rothes capsized while assisting HM drifter Eminent and two crew drowned. On 9 February 1953, six men died when the lifeboat capsized escorting fishing vessels home; Charles Tait was the only survivor. On 21 January 1970, the Duchess of Kent capsized on service to a Danish vessel and five of her crew of six were lost. Fourteen men in total. A monument to them was unveiled in 2010.

## The End of the Line

Fraserburgh railway station opened in 1865 and closed to passengers in 1965 as part of the Beeching cuts. Freight trains kept running until 1979, then the track was lifted. When the Borders Railway opened in September 2015, Fraserburgh quietly became the most distant town from the rail network in the entire United Kingdom. The nearest operating station is Inverurie, thirty-five miles south. This isolation shaped the modern Broch as much as the herring boom shaped the old one. The town never benefited from the Aberdeen oil money. It is third largest in Aberdeenshire, after Peterhead and Inverurie, with 13,180 residents at the 2011 census. After the 2004 EU expansion, around five percent of residents speak Polish as their first language. Sixty-three percent can speak Scots.

## The Unexpected Diaspora

For a town this remote, Fraserburgh has produced an extraordinary cast of people. Thomas Blake Glover, born here in 1838 to a coastguard's family, moved to Japan and helped industrialise the country, ending his life as a consultant to Mitsubishi and a legend in Tokyo. James Ramsay, born in Fraserburgh in 1733, became one of the earliest serious campaigners against the Atlantic slave trade. Bill Gibb, born nearby in 1943, became an international fashion designer. George Bruce, born here in 1909, was a major poet of the Scottish literary renaissance. And Dennis Nilsen, born on Academy Road in 1945, became one of Britain's most notorious serial killers, convicted in 1983 for murders committed in London. The list also includes a Victoria Cross recipient, a one-time president of Sun Life of Canada, and a Scotsman who defected to the Soviet Union. The wind here seems to blow in many directions.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 57.693N, 2.005W. Fraserburgh sits at the northeast corner of Aberdeenshire, the Buchan headland where Scotland's coast hinges east-to-north. From 3,000 ft the harbour basin, Kinnaird Head lighthouse, and the long Fraserburgh Beach are all clearly visible. Nearest major airport is Aberdeen (EGPD) about 35 nm south-southwest; Lossiemouth (EGQS) lies 55 nm west. The Broch is famously windy - the Met Office records this stretch among the UK's windiest. Cloud and low visibility are common; expect Scottish marine weather rather than calm cruising.
