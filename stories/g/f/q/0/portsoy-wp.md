# Portsoy

A burgh of barony since 1550, a Peaky Blinders filming location, and the place a herring fleet of fifty-seven boats once called home.

**Location:** 57.6830, -2.6880

**Categories:** scotland, aberdeenshire, moray-firth, fishing-village, burgh-of-barony, filming-location

## Introduction

Cillian Murphy walked through Portsoy in a peaked cap, and the Aberdeenshire fishing village pretended to be early twentieth century Birmingham. The Peaky Blinders production crew shot location footage here for season six in 2021, and locals watched as the small stone harbour was dressed for a different city and a different century. This is what Portsoy does now. The 17th century harbour and the 19th century street fronts have proven so resistant to obvious change that filmmakers keep finding excuses to come. Portsoy has been Tibet, the Hebrides, Birmingham, and itself, sometimes in the same year.

## Saithe Harbour, Burgh of Barony

The name probably comes from Port Saoithe, meaning saithe harbour in Gaelic. Saithe is the dark cousin of cod, a fish that runs in shoals along this coast and that built the original village. Portsoy became a burgh of barony in 1550, under Sir Walter Ogilvie of Boyne Castle, and the Scottish parliament confirmed the charter in 1581. The status gave Portsoy the right to hold markets and fairs and to handle its own civic affairs within the wider parish of Fordyce. From the sixteenth century until 1975 Portsoy was administered by its own Town Council and the Banffshire County Council, a small autonomy that lasted nearly four hundred years before the Local Government (Scotland) Act 1973 rolled the village into the Banff and Buchan District Council. In 1996 it became part of Aberdeenshire Council, where it remains.

## Two Harbours and Fifty-Seven Boats

The old harbour at Portsoy was built in the 17th century, making it the oldest on the Moray Firth coast. The new harbour beside it was built in 1825 for the growing herring fishery. At its peak the fleet from Portsoy reached fifty-seven boats, all working the same shoals of silver fish that ran along the Aberdeenshire coast every summer. The herring boom built the stone houses that line the streets above the harbour, the Old Town Hall completed in 1798, and a small commercial centre that supported its population through the nineteenth century. The fishery collapsed in the twentieth century the way most North Sea fisheries collapsed: too many boats, too few fish, factory ships taking what had been left for the local fleet. Portsoy's population at the 2011 census was 1,752, almost identical to what it had been a hundred years earlier.

## Whisky Galore and the Movie Trade

Portsoy played the fictional Hebridean island of Todday in Gillies MacKinnon's 2016 remake of Whisky Galore. The original 1949 Ealing comedy had been shot on Barra in the Hebrides, but seventy years of change had made Barra impractical for a period production. Portsoy looked the part with almost no dressing. The town has also featured in BBC period dramas The Camerons and The Shutter Falls, in season six of Peaky Blinders, and in a Tennent's Lager advert that parodied the original Whisky Galore. There is even a Portsoy-shot football scene from a forgotten BBC production. None of which the town makes much fuss about. The film crews come and go. The harbour stays where it is.

## The Green Stone Trade

Portsoy is known for jewellery made from Portsoy marble, the green and white stone that outcrops along the coast nearby. The name is misleading; the rock is serpentinite, a metamorphic mineral that polishes to a deep variegated green. Local craftspeople have worked the stone since at least the seventeenth century. Tradition holds that some Portsoy serpentinite was supplied to Versailles for the palace's chimneypieces in the reign of Louis XIV. The claim has the hallmarks of something locals want to be true and that historians cannot quite confirm or deny. What is certain is that the stone is still quarried and worked locally. The annual Scottish Traditional Boat Festival, founded in 1993 to mark the three-hundredth anniversary of the old harbour, fills the streets every June and the green jewellery is on every stall.

## Voices That Came From Here

Jimmy MacBeath, born here in 1894, became one of the great preservers of the bothy ballad tradition, recorded by Hamish Henderson in the 1950s and now a fixture in the Scottish folk archive. William Boyd became a Canadian pathologist whose textbooks taught generations of medical students. Eoin Jess played football for Aberdeen and Scotland, scoring goals for both. Jim Paterson is the trombone player of Dexys Midnight Runners, and the brass on Come On Eileen carries a small piece of Portsoy with it every time the song plays. For a village of fewer than two thousand people, this is more than a fair share of voices. Portsoy is a quiet place that has been quietly noisy for centuries.

## Flight Context

Portsoy stands on the Moray Firth coast at 57.68 degrees N, 2.69 degrees W, fifty miles northwest of Aberdeen and sixty-five miles east of Inverness. From the air the small stone village wraps around its 17th century harbour, with the new 1825 harbour immediately east. Cruise altitude three to six thousand feet gives a fine view of the relationship to Findlater Castle two miles west, Glenglassaugh distillery the same distance west, and Boyne Castle a mile and a half east. Nearest airports are Aberdeen (EGPD) about fifty nautical miles southeast, RAF Lossiemouth (EGQS) about thirty nautical miles west, and Inverness (EGPE) about forty-five nautical miles west.
