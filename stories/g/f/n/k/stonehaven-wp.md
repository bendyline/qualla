# Stonehaven

A town of 11,177 people on the northeast coast of Scotland — county town of the now-abolished Kincardineshire, birthplace of the BBC's first director and the pneumatic tyre, and home to a 420-million-year-old fossil and a still-burning Hogmanay tradition.

**Location:** 56.9600, -2.2100

**Categories:** towns, coastal, scotland, aberdeenshire, fishing ports

## Introduction

In the local Doric dialect they call it Steenhive — Stonehaven softened by centuries of north-east Scottish vowels. Locals just call it Stoney. It is fifteen miles south of Aberdeen, set in a deeply indented bay where two rivers, the Carron Water and the Cowie Water, run down off higher ground to meet the North Sea. The town has eleven thousand and a hundred and seventy-seven people, give or take what the 2022 Census managed to count. It was the county town of Kincardineshire until 1975, when that county was abolished into Aberdeenshire — a small administrative humiliation that locals have variously borne and ignored.

## Deep Time at Cowie Beach

Two layers of prehistory have surfaced on the ground around Stonehaven. The deeper one came to light in 2004, when an amateur palaeontologist found a fossil on Cowie Beach. National Museums Scotland and Yale University announced it was the earliest known fossil of a land-dwelling animal: a primitive millipede about 428 million years old, with breathing tubes that show it could function out of water. It was named Pneumodesmus newmani for Mike Newman, the bus driver who picked it up. The shallower layer came from the same year. Archaeologists working ahead of a gas pipeline found two short cist burials southwest of town containing cremated remains, dated by radiocarbon to the early Bronze Age — roughly two thousand years before Christ. The burials contained stone tools and shale beads. Two finds in the same year, separated by more than 424 million years, from soil within a few miles of each other.

## Castles, Causey, and Crown Jewels

Stonehaven sits at the southern end of the Causey Mounth, the ancient drovers' trackway built on high ground to keep travellers above the worst of the bogs between Aberdeen and the south. The route ran from the Bridge of Dee through Portlethen Moss past Muchalls Castle and into Stonehaven. In 1639, William Keith, 7th Earl Marischal, and the Marquess of Montrose led a Covenanter army of over nine thousand men along this road in the first major engagement of the Wars of the Three Kingdoms. The Covenanters captured by Royalists were imprisoned in nearby Dunnottar Castle, where many died. A memorial to them stands in Dunnottar Church. Dunnottar itself had been the home of the Keith family for centuries. The Scottish Crown Jewels — the Honours of Scotland — were hidden there during the Wars of Independence, lost briefly to Edward I in 1296, and reclaimed in 1297 when William Wallace burned the castle's church with the entire English garrison still inside. In 1650 Oliver Cromwell laid siege to the castle for eight months looking for the same Honours. Just before the castle fell, the jewels were smuggled out by two women, taken by boat down the coast to Kinneff Old Church, and hidden under the floor for eleven years.

## Jacobites, Tolbooth, and the Forty-Five

Stonehaven was an Episcopalian and Jacobite town in both the 1715 and 1745 risings, serving as a safe port where French money and equipment could be quietly landed for the rebel armies. In 1716, the retreating Jacobite forces stopped overnight in Stonehaven on 5–6 February. Earlier, in 1648–49, three Episcopal clergymen had been imprisoned in the 16th-century Tolbooth at the harbour for the crime of holding a religious service for more than nine people — an early Covenanting attempt at social-distancing the contagion of Episcopalianism, which was strongly associated with Jacobitism. The Tolbooth survived all of this. It is now a free museum at the working harbour, the oldest surviving building in town.

## Herring, Pneumatic Tyres, and the BBC

For most of the 19th century, Stonehaven ran on herring. The improved harbour by Robert Stevenson — grandfather of Robert Louis Stevenson — handled the peak catch of 1894, when local boats brought in about fifteen million fish a year and the industry employed 1,280 people. Overfishing destroyed the fleet by 1939. The town turned to other industries and to its own peculiar list of accidental celebrities. Robert William Thomson, the inventor of the pneumatic tyre, was born here. So was Lord Reith of Stonehaven — John Reith — the first Director-General of the BBC, whose name became shorthand for the BBC's high-minded mission of informing, educating, and entertaining. The novelist Lewis Grassic Gibbon (James Leslie Mitchell), whose Sunset Song remains one of Scotland's most beloved novels, attended what was then Mackie Academy. Visitors over the centuries have included William Wallace, Mary Queen of Scots, and the poet Robert Burns, who met friends in town in 1787.

## Fireballs and the Modern Town

On Hogmanay — New Year's Eve — Stonehaven still holds its annual fireballs ceremony. Volunteers walk down the High Street swinging burning balls of waste material on chains, finishing by throwing them into the harbour. Newspaper accounts go back to 1911; the tradition is older. It was cancelled during both world wars and in 2021 for COVID. The town also hosts a Folk Festival each summer, regularly attended by Glaswegian comedian Billy Connolly, and a Highland Games every July at which all heavy-event competitors — the hammer, the heavy stone, tossing the caber — must wear full Highland dress. Stonehaven has grown rapidly since the Aberdeen oil boom and now spreads across four new estates of middle-class housing. The town's seafront features a heated saltwater Olympic-sized open-air pool, a long beach below cliffs, and the famous Carron fish and chip shop where, in the 1990s, a deep-fried Mars Bar first appeared on a menu and entered the national mythology of Scotland's diet.

## Flight Context

Stonehaven sits at approximately 56.96°N, 2.21°W, on the northeast coast of Scotland about 15 nautical miles south of Aberdeen. Recommended viewing altitude is 2,500–5,000 feet AGL. The town is sheltered in Stonehaven Bay between Downie Point and Garron Point; Dunnottar Castle sits on its cliff peninsula about a mile south. EGPD (Aberdeen International) is the controlling airfield; the A90 trunk road and the Dundee–Aberdeen rail line both pass through the town. The Highland Boundary Fault crosses the coast just north of Stonehaven — the visible boundary between lowland farms and higher Grampian terrain.
