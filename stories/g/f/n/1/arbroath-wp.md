# Arbroath

A former royal burgh of 23,902 souls on the Angus coast, built around an abbey, a harbour, a smokehouse industry, and a fish-shaped sense of itself.

**Location:** 56.5640, -2.5882

**Categories:** town, scotland, history, fishing, industrial-heritage, coastal

## Introduction

The old name was Aberbrothock - the mouth of the Brothock - and you can still see the burn slipping out to sea beside the harbour wall. Iron Age people lived in souterrains here three thousand years ago. The Picts left carved stones at St Vigeans, just to the north, including the 9th-century Drosten Stone with one of the only Latin inscriptions in Pictish art. Vikings came and went. Then in 1178 King William the Lion founded an abbey, and a town grew around it as deliberately as a barnacle grows on a hull. By the 19th century Arbroath was making sailcloth for the Cutty Sark, lawnmowers for the Old Course at St Andrews, and shoes for everyone. Today the population is just under twenty-four thousand, and the town remembers all of it.

## Sails for the Cutty Sark

When the Industrial Revolution arrived, Arbroath caught it in full sail. Flax, then jute, drove the economy. By 1867 the mills employed 4,620 people. By 1875, 1,400 looms in 34 mills were producing over a million yards of osnaburg cloth and 450,000 yards of sailcloth annually. The town is believed to have supplied the sails for the Cutty Sark, the great tea clipper now preserved in dry dock at Greenwich. Alexander Shanks set up his lawnmower works at Dens Iron Works in 1853 and supplied mowers to the Old Course at St Andrews and the All England Lawn Tennis and Croquet Club at Wimbledon. The town also made shoes. By the late 20th century all of it was gone: Shanks merged away, Giddings and Lewis-Fraser closed, Alps Electric arrived in 1990 and left in 2001, taking 180 jobs.

## The Smokie and the Sea

Following major improvements to the harbour in 1839, the council recruited fishermen from Auchmithie and from as far away as Shetland. By 1980 some forty whitefish and pelagic vessels worked from Arbroath, employing hundreds at sea and hundreds more ashore to process the catch. Quota cuts and decommissioning from the 1980s hollowed the fleet out. One vessel now works regularly from Arbroath; three more Arbroath-owned boats work from Aberdeen and ports further north. The processing continues, but the fish now come from Aberdeen, Peterhead, Iceland, Norway and Ireland. What remains is the smokie. Made from haddock salted overnight, dried in pairs, then hung over a hardwood fire for forty-five minutes to an hour. Since 2004 it has been a Protected Geographical Indication: it can be made nowhere else.

## A Lifeboat and a Lighthouse

Arbroath established a lifeboat station in 1803, among the first in Scotland. It still operates today. Two boats are kept here, one of them the only remaining slipway-launched all-weather lifeboat in Scotland, the Mersey Class Inchcape. The Inchcape Rock, eleven miles offshore, gave its name to the lifeboat and to the famous lighthouse Robert Stevenson built on it between 1807 and 1810. Arbroath was the shore station for the entire construction effort. Stonemasons cut Cairngall granite here and sailed it out to the rock. The Bell Rock Signal Tower, finished in 1813, became the lifeline between keepers and shore. Today it houses the Signal Tower Museum, where you can see one of the original lights and a scale model of the impossible work that built it.

## Marines and Memorials

On the western edge of town sits RM Condor, home since 1971 to 45 Commando of the Royal Marines. The site was built in 1940 as RNAS Arbroath, a Royal Naval Air Station that trained Fleet Air Arm pilots during the war. When the Navy left, the Marines moved in. Around six hundred Arbroathians work for the Ministry of Defence today. The town's other heritage is gentler. Hospitalfield House, the baronial mansion to the west, was given to the nation by Patrick Allan-Fraser to support emerging artists. It still does. The Webster Memorial Theatre on High Street, opened in 1867, was renamed after Joseph Webster, a young man killed in the First World War. His family gave it to the town in 1919. The seafront, the abbey, the smokehouses, the lifeboat shed - the Marines training in the dunes - it is all here together, in roughly twenty-three thousand lives along a North Sea coast.

## Flight Context

Arbroath sits at 56.56°N, 2.59°W on the North Sea coast, 16 nm northeast of Dundee in the Angus region. From 2,000-4,000 ft AGL the town is identifiable by its harbour at the south end, the red sandstone abbey ruins at the head of High Street, the long sandy West Links beach to the southwest, and Seaton Cliffs running northeast toward Auchmithie. RM Condor airfield is on the western outskirts and still has a hard runway. Nearest civil airports: Dundee (EGPN) 14 nm southwest; Leuchars (EGQL) 18 nm southwest; Aberdeen (EGPD) 35 nm northeast. North Sea haar (sea fog) common in spring and early summer.
