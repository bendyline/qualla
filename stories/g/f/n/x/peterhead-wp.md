# Peterhead

Founded in 1593 on land that belonged to monks, bombed twenty-eight times by Luftwaffe pilots looking for Scotland, home to a notorious prison and the UK's busiest fishing port.

**Location:** 57.5091, -1.7832

**Categories:** towns, scotland, aberdeenshire, fishing, coastal, history

## Introduction

During the Second World War the Luftwaffe dropped bombs on Peterhead twenty-eight times. The town had a population then of around twelve thousand. London, with eight million people, was bombed more often. Nothing else in Britain was bombed as much. Aberdeen, the obvious target nearby, was bombed twenty-four times. Peterhead caught the unwanted attention not because of what it was, but because of where it was: the first built-up land that German bombers saw when they crossed the North Sea on raids against Scotland. Those crews were tired, or anxious, or merely glad to be over land. They dropped what they had. The town absorbed it, kept fishing, and after the war kept being Peterhead.

## From Monks to Marischals

Before 1560, the land where Peterhead now stands belonged to the Cistercian monks of Deer Abbey, who farmed it without much enterprise. After the Reformation, Mary, Queen of Scots, granted the property to Robert Keith, 1st Lord Altrie, of the powerful Earl Marischal family. Keith's nephew William, the 4th Earl, supervised the founding of a planned town along the Seagate shore in 1593. It was called Peterugie, or Inverugie of St Peter, before settling into Peterhead. The estate of the Earl Marischal was forfeited in 1716 after their support for the Jacobite rising, sold to the York Buildings Company, and dispersed. By 1738 the Leith shipmasters had recorded their official opinion that this was the best-situated harbour in Scotland for the northern seas. They were proven right several times over.

## Whales, Herring, Convicts

In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries Peterhead became one of the great whaling ports of Britain, with crews running to the Greenland Sea and Davis Strait for bowhead oil and baleen. When wars with revolutionary and Napoleonic France made the English Channel dangerous, Atlantic shipping took the long route around the north of Scotland, and Peterhead became a major port of call. Victorian-era herring booms followed. To handle the growing traffic the harbour had to be expanded, and the workforce came from a new institution opened in 1888: HM Convict Prison Peterhead. The prison earned a reputation as one of Scotland's toughest, holding hundreds of men at a time when Australia had stopped accepting transportees and Britain needed somewhere else to put long-sentence prisoners. Convict labour built the south breakwater between 1892 and 1912. The prison closed in 2013 and is now a museum where visitors can sit in the cells.

## Five Thousand Years of People Here

Archaeological work during a landfill expansion in 2002 and 2003 found that people had been living on this exact stretch of coast for over five thousand years. The dig turned up a clearance cairn with late Bronze Age stone tools, a burial cairn with late Neolithic and early Bronze Age implements, Beaker-culture pottery, and even Mesolithic and early Neolithic stone tools - the latter dating from the deep prehistory after the last glaciers retreated. The Earls Marischal who founded the town in 1593 were laying out a planned settlement on a coast that had already been inhabited for fifty centuries. Listed buildings now fan out from the harbour: 19th-century granite tenements on North Street, the 1877 fisher houses on Great Stuart Street (Burnie Street, locally) built for fishermen leaving the village of Burnhaven, the Peterhead Town House of 1788 on Broad Street, the 1833 Reform Monument.

## Oil, Decline, and a Port That Still Counts

The fishing industry collapsed in the late twentieth century through a combination of overfishing, European catch quotas, and competition from larger trawler fleets. The 1970s brought a new wave: North Sea oil. Peterhead never refined crude itself, but it became one of the major service ports for the offshore industry, supplying rigs and crews. The St Fergus gas terminal opened nearby. As the North Sea fields aged, that work moved north too. The town has weathered hard decades, but its port has refused to fade. In 2023, Peterhead landed over 193,000 tonnes of fish and shellfish, worth around 232 million pounds, employing roughly 700 fishermen. It remains the busiest fishing port in the United Kingdom by total landings. Twin-towned with Alesund in Norway. Hometown of Peterhead Football Club, the Buchanie newspaper since 1863, and of every Bloo Touner who has ever pulled a haddock out of the North Sea.

## Flight Context

Peterhead lies at 57.51 degrees north, 1.78 degrees west, at the easternmost point of mainland Scotland. From the air the town is a dense granite-grey urban patch hugging Peterhead Bay, with the two great breakwaters of the harbour clearly visible. The A90 runs along the western edge. The Buchan Ness Lighthouse stands south at Boddam. The former HM Prison Peterhead, now a museum, sits inland on the hill above the harbour. Aberdeen Dyce Airport, ICAO EGPD, is about twenty-five nautical miles south-south-west. Best viewed at 2000-4000 feet AGL. The wartime bombing pattern still shows in places where postwar rebuilding broke the Victorian street grid.
