# Benbecula

Twelve kilometres of low, loch-pocked machair between two larger islands, Benbecula is a Catholic Gaelic enclave whose history runs from Pictish missionaries to a community buyout.

**Location:** 57.4458, -7.3196

**Categories:** Benbecula, Outer Hebrides, Islands of Scotland, Community buyouts in Scotland

## Introduction

The first written record of the name appears in 1449, spelled Beanbeacla. Gaelic poets called it An t-Eilean Dorcha, the Dark Island, which has more to do with its rain-shadowed peat moors than any sinister history. From the air, Benbecula looks less like land than like a sieve: more than half of its surface is water, broken into hundreds of small lochs, with two slender causeways tethering it to North and South Uist like a child holding parents' hands.

## Twelve Kilometres of Lochs

The island runs roughly twelve kilometres east to west and a similar distance north to south, with almost every inch of it below 20 metres above sea level. Its geology was set by the Caledonian orogeny, which folded together what is now Europe and North America some 400 million years ago. The Outer Hebrides Thrust, a geological fault produced by that collision, runs along Benbecula's eastern coast, with one of the oldest exposed rocks on the planet, Lewisian gneiss, forming the bedrock. On top of all that ancient stone sits a thin skin of peat and blown shell sand, the machair, which produces an extraordinary summer wildflower show and feeds the corncrake. The highest point, Ruabhal, reaches just 124 metres.

## Town of the Monk

Balivanich, the main settlement, takes its name in Gaelic as Baile a' Mhanaich, Town of the Monk. Traditional accounts attribute the name to Saint Ternan, a late fifth- or early sixth-century Irish missionary who is said to have founded a chapel to convert the Picts. A Class 1 Pictish symbol stone, inscribed with two basic symbols, was found at the shore near Dun Buidhe and is one of the only firm pieces of evidence that the Picts ever reached the Outer Hebrides at all. From here Benbecula passed in the 9th century into the Norse-ruled Kingdom of the Isles. After Somerled's 12th-century coup it became MacRory land, then, by 1346, the inheritance of Amy of Garmoran, who built Borve Castle and is credited with the eastern wall of Teampul Chalumchille, the parish church of Saint Columba on the island.

## Bonnie Prince Charlie's Refuge

The most famous chapter of Benbecula's history is also one of its shortest. In late June 1746, Prince Charles Edward Stuart, on the run after Culloden, was driven onto the island by a storm. Benbecula's population were broadly sympathetic to the Jacobite cause and Catholic into the bargain. They sheltered the Prince, and arranged the famous escape with Flora MacDonald, disguised as her Irish maid Betty Burke, over the sea to Skye. The episode would not have been possible anywhere with a stronger Hanoverian presence. In the centuries since, much else has changed, but the religious geography has not. Benbecula remains predominantly Catholic, an unusual fact in a Scotland otherwise reformed.

## Cleared, Then Bought Back

The nineteenth century was hard. The island was given over to sheep and sporting estates, and many of its people were forced out in the Clearances. Twentieth-century military expansion brought the airfield, the missile range and a wave of English-speaking soldiers that pushed the Gaelic-speaking proportion of Balivanich down to thirty-seven percent. Then in 2006, after years of organising, the islanders bought the land back. The sporting syndicate that had owned 372 square kilometres covering Benbecula, South Uist and Eriskay sold the estate for £4.5 million to a community-owned organisation, Stòras Uibhist. The land is now managed in perpetuity for the people who live on it. The 2022 census recorded 1,255 residents.

## Flight Context

Located at 57.45 N, 7.32 W in the central Outer Hebrides. The island sits low to the sea (highest point Ruabhal, 124 m) and is connected by causeways to North Uist and South Uist. Benbecula Airport (EGPL/BEB) is in the northwest at Balivanich. The dense pattern of small lochs is distinctive from the air. Recommended viewing altitude 3000-5000 ft for the whole island and its causeway connections. Alternates: Stornoway (EGPO), Glasgow (EGPF).
