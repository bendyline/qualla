# Barra

An island of Vikings, pirates, and beach-landed aircraft, where a 2,500 BC pottery beaker turned up under a road and the MacNeil clan still leases its medieval castle to the nation for one pound and a bottle of whisky a year.

**Location:** 56.9833, -7.4667

**Categories:** islands, scotland, history, outer-hebrides, aviation

## Introduction

In the 1990s, road workers on Barra dug up a near-complete pottery beaker dated to roughly 2500 BC. That is the kind of island this is. The Neolithic platform nearby, the standing stones at Borve, the Bronze Age cemetery on Vatersay, the Iron Age broch on the east coast, the wheelhouses reused into the eighth century, the Norse name Barr-ey, the Gaelic-speaking Catholic population, the pirate clan in the castle on the rock, the Madonna on the mountain, the Twin Otter landing on the beach at low tide. Barra is layers, and the layers are all visible from a road that takes about an hour to drive.

## Vikings, Pirates, and a Pound a Year

Ninth-century Vikings gave the island the ey suffix that means island in Old Norse, and incorporated it into Sudreyjar, the southern isles, a crown dependency of Norway. Malcolm III of Scotland conceded in writing that the Hebrides were not his to claim. The 1266 Treaty of Perth changed that, transferring Sudreyjar to Scotland in exchange for a very large sum of money. In 1427 King James I declared the Lordship of Garmoran forfeit after the violence of its rival heirs got out of hand, and that same year the Lord of the Isles granted Barra to Giolla Adhamhnain Mac Neill. The MacNeils headquartered at Kisimul Castle on its rock in Castlebay and turned to piracy against English ships under Elizabeth I. Summoned by King James VI to explain himself, Roderick Mac­Neil argued he was avenging Mary, Queen of Scots, and James released him pleased. In 2000, MacNeil's American heir leased Kisimul to Historic Scotland for a thousand years for £1 and a bottle of whisky annually.

## The Beach With a Timetable

Traigh Mhor, the two-mile cockle-shell beach at the north end of the island, has been Barra Airport's runway since 1936. It is the only airport in the world running scheduled flights to a tidal beach. Three runways are marked across the sand for different wind directions, and the timetable shifts daily to match low tide. The Loganair Twin Otter from Glasgow lands on wet, compacted sand washed twice a day by the Atlantic. In 2011 the BBC pronounced it the world's most scenic landing, and that may actually undersell it. Land for the airport was first proposed in 1933 by John MacPherson, the local postmaster known as The Coddy, who reasoned that a beach this hard ought to take an aircraft.

## Whisky Galore!

In 1941 the SS Politician ran aground in the Sound of Eriskay with 22,000 cases of Scotch whisky and £3 million in Jamaican banknotes aboard. The islanders helped themselves with enthusiasm and considerable ingenuity, hiding bottles in rabbit holes, peat stacks, and creels weighted under the sea. Compton Mackenzie, who lived near the airport from 1933, turned the incident into his 1947 novel Whisky Galore, which Ealing Studios filmed on Barra in 1949. Mackenzie died in 1972 and is buried under a simple cross at Cille Bharra cemetery overlooking Eoligarry jetty. The film's sequel Rockets Galore! and a 2016 remake of the original were also shot here. A 2008 set of Royal Mail stamps featured Barra's RNLI lifeboat Edna Windsor in three-and-a-half-metre swell southwest of Barra Head.

## Gaelic, Heaval, and a New Distillery

About 1,209 people lived on Barra in 2022, an increase since 2011. Sixty-two percent of those at the 2011 census spoke Gaelic, down from 76 percent in 1991, but new investment (a £1 million Gaelic education and health hub in 2022) is pushing back. Heaval rises 383 metres above Castlebay, with the white marble Our Lady of the Sea statue near its summit, erected during the Marian year of 1954. Isle of Barra Distillers, founded in 2017, began with Barra Atlantic Gin in 2017; they are now building Scotland's most westerly whisky distillery at Castlebay, where construction in 2024 paused briefly when Iron Age pottery turned up at the site. On Barra the past has a way of interrupting the future.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 56.983N, 7.467W. Barra spans about ten miles north-south by six miles east-west, with Heaval (383 m / 1,257 ft) the prominent peak in the south. Barra Airport (ICAO: EGPR) operates on the tidal beach of Traigh Mhor at the north end; tide tables determine schedules. Kisimul Castle on its islet in Castlebay is unmistakable on approach. Recommended viewing altitude 3,000-5,000 ft. Common conditions: low cloud, strong southwesterlies, rapidly closing visibility. The causeway to Vatersay is visible at the south end.
