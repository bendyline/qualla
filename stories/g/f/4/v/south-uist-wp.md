# South Uist

An island built on 2.9-billion-year-old rock that holds Britain's only prehistoric mummies, the birthplace of Flora MacDonald, and a Gaelic-Catholic culture that survived clearances, conscription, and the Cold War.

**Location:** 57.3200, -7.3000

**Categories:** islands, Scotland, Outer Hebrides, geology, archaeology, Gaelic culture

## Introduction

The stone underfoot on South Uist is older than most things on Earth. Lewisian gneiss - one of the oldest rocks anywhere on the planet's surface - was forged here in the Archaean eon, 2.9 billion years ago, when most of the continents had not yet formed and the atmosphere had only just learned how to hold oxygen. Glaciation scraped it bare. The island sits on this ancient foundation like a thin skin: a wall of mountains on the east, machair grassland on the west, peat and loch in between. The people who live on it - 1,650 at the 2022 census, 60 percent Gaelic-speaking, 90 percent Catholic - are the most recent thin layer on top of a very long story.

## The Mummies of Cladh Hallan

At Cladh Hallan, on the southern part of the island, archaeologists excavating a row of Bronze Age roundhouses in the early 2000s made a discovery without parallel in Britain. Buried beneath the floors were two human bodies that had been intentionally mummified - the only known prehistoric mummies anywhere in the British Isles. The first was a male, mummified around 1600 BC and apparently displayed for centuries, with parts replaced as they decayed; the second was a female, mummified about three hundred years later. They were eventually buried under the roundhouses around 1100 BC, by which point each 'individual' had become a composite of pieces from different people. South Uist was practising preservation rituals contemporaneous with Egypt's pharaohs - using bog acids and entirely different techniques - while most of Europe was leaving its dead exposed.

## Vikings, Norse, and the Lordship of the Isles

In the ninth century the Vikings arrived and absorbed South Uist into the Kingdom of the Isles. A small Ogham inscription on a piece of animal bone from Bornish, dating from this era, suggests Norse settlers and Gaelic-speaking inhabitants were exchanging more than goods. A monastery was established at Howmore. After the Treaty of Perth in 1266, the Hebrides passed to Scotland, and over the following centuries South Uist became part of the Lordship of the Isles under various branches of Clan Donald. The MacDonalds of Clanranald held the central and northern thirds of the island into the eighteenth century; their seat moved between Ormaclete Castle on South Uist - which burned down in 1715 while its chief, Ailean Dearg, was being mortally wounded at the Battle of Sheriffmuir - and back to Benbecula. The clan story is one of long, complicated, often violent loyalty to a doomed Jacobite cause.

## Kelp, Clearance, and Departure

The Napoleonic Wars made South Uist briefly profitable. The wars restricted imports of mineral alkali, and burning Hebridean kelp to make soda ash became a highly profitable industry. The population worked the shore for the seaweed and the lairds banked the proceeds. When the wars ended in 1815, cheap Spanish barilla destroyed the kelp price, and by 1837 the Clanranald estate was bankrupt. The island was sold to Colonel John Gordon of Cluny, who proceeded to 'clear' the population in favour of Blackface sheep and Lowland farmers. The displaced - the majority of the original population - were largely shipped to Canada, often under coercion. The remaining population by 1851 was less than half what it had been a decade before. Eriskay, considered too poor to be worth clearing, became the dumping ground for evicted families from the southern Hebrides. Most of them were Catholic. The Catholic majority on Eriskay today is a direct inheritance from that displacement.

## Missiles, Buyout, and the Wind That Pays

In 1957-58 the Ministry of Defence built a missile range at the north end of the island to test the MGM-5 Corporal, the first guided nuclear weapon shared between Britain and America. Locals worried the influx of English-speaking soldiers would finish off the Gaelic language - a fear Compton Mackenzie turned into the novel Rockets Galore. The range still operates, now testing Rapier missiles and unmanned drones for QinetiQ. In November 2006, in Scotland's largest community land buyout to date, the 92,000-acre South Uist Estate - covering most of the island, much of Benbecula, and all of Eriskay - was bought from a sporting syndicate by Storas Uibhist, the community company. The Lochcarnan wind farm, three Enercon turbines totalling 6.9 megawatts, came online in 2013 and now generates income reinvested in housing, Gaelic culture, and infrastructure. The community owns the wind. The wind, finally, pays the rent.

## The Strongest Gaelic Place in the World

At the 2011 census, 60 percent of South Uist and Benbecula's combined population spoke Scottish Gaelic. In one township - the Middle District of South Uist - the proportion reached 82 percent, the highest concentration of Gaelic speakers anywhere in the world. The summer school Ceolas at Daliburgh, every July, draws students from across Scotland, Cape Breton, and Ireland. The Catholic parish structure, unbroken since pre-Reformation times, gave the language an institutional shelter that Protestant areas often lacked. Local folktales survive - including the story of a seal that came ashore at the boundary between Lochboisdale and Kilpheder to settle a land dispute by indicating where the line should run. The seal's verdict was accepted. There is nowhere else in Scotland where you could plausibly imagine such a story being told as anything other than fiction.

## Flight Context

South Uist extends from approximately 57.07 to 57.45 degrees north, centred near 57.32 degrees north, 7.30 degrees west. The island is the second-largest in the Outer Hebrides. The nearest airport is Benbecula (ICAO: EGPL), reached from the north end via causeway, with daily Loganair flights from Glasgow and weekday service from Stornoway. Barra Airport (EGPR) lies about 22 nautical miles south and lands on a tidal beach. From cruising altitude, look for the unbroken pale strip of west-coast beach, the dark eastern mountains topped by Beinn Mhor (620 m) and Hecla (606 m), and the distinctive missile-range buildings at the north end. Westerlies and frequent rain dominate the meteorology.
