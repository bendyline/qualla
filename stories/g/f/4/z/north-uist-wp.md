# North Uist

An island of Lewisian gneiss - the oldest rocks in Britain - whose history runs from Neolithic standing stones through Viking lords, MacDonald feuds, and the Highland Clearances.

**Location:** 57.6000, -7.3333

**Categories:** islands, scotland, outer-hebrides, north-uist, history, geology

## Introduction

The rocks underfoot here are nearly three billion years old. Lewisian gneiss, the oldest exposed rock in Britain, forms the foundation of North Uist - a stone older than complex life, older than oxygen in the atmosphere in any abundance, older by an order of magnitude than the dinosaurs. Everything that has happened on this island, from the Neolithic farmers who built Barpa Langass to the people forced from Sollas in 1849 to the laird who still lives at Callernish today, has happened on the same patient Archaean foundation.

## What the Name Might Mean

The Old Norse derivation Ivist - related to vist meaning "an abode, dwelling, domicile" - is one theory. A Gaelic derivation from I-fheirste, "crossings-island" or "fords-island," is another, sharing a root with place-names like Fersit and even Belfast. A 2018 paper by Clancy argued that Ivist itself is an Old Norse calque on an earlier Gaelic name, Ibuid or Ibdaig, which appears to correspond with Ptolemy's Eboudai from the second century. The geographer who never visited Britain wrote the island's name down before the Norse arrived. The Norse adapted it. The Gaels reabsorbed it. None of them, perhaps, knew exactly what it meant.

## The Oldest Rocks, The Oldest Buildings

North Uist is the tenth-largest Scottish island. The northern part lies within the South Lewis, Harris and North Uist National Scenic Area, one of forty in Scotland. The geology is heavily varied: metasediments and metavolcanics in a zone running west from Lochmaddy to Baleshare, banded metabasic rocks and Archaean granites in the northwest around Loch Phaibeil, a band of pseudotachylyte curving through the centre of the island, the Outer Hebrides Thrust Zone running along the east coast. On this ancient stone, prehistoric people built: the Barpa Langass chambered cairn, the Pobull Fhinn stone circle, Dun an Sticir broch, the Fir Bhreige standing stones, Eilean Domhnuill (possibly Scotland's earliest crannog site), and the Baile Sear roundhouses exposed by storms in 2005.

## Vikings, MacDonalds, and a Show Trial

In the 9th century Viking settlers established the Kingdom of the Isles - the Suoreyjar or "southern isles" to the Norse - throughout the Hebrides. The Treaty of Perth in 1266 transferred the islands to Scotland for 4,000 marks, but real power stayed with the descendants of Somerled. The MacRory branch ruled Uist along with Barra, Eigg, Rum, and others. The last MacRory heir was Amy of Garmoran, who married John of Islay, Lord of the Isles, and was divorced by him a decade later. Centuries of feuding followed. In 1427, frustrated by violence in the Highlands, King James I summoned the magnates to Inverness, imprisoned them on arrival, and had Alexander MacGorrie executed after a quick show trial.

## Kelp Boom, Kelp Bust

During the French Revolutionary Wars, the United Kingdom lost access to imported minerals, and burnt kelp - rich in soda ash needed for glass and soap - became enormously valuable. North Uist's main industry boomed. The population swelled. When the wars ended and foreign supplies returned, the kelp market collapsed almost overnight. The crofters could no longer afford their rents. Burning kelp had also exhausted the soil's fertility. By 1841 the population had fallen to 3,870. Sir Godfrey MacDonald, 4th Baron Macdonald, decided to put sheep on the land instead of people. The Highland Clearances came to North Uist with the systematic violence familiar across the Highlands and Islands.

## Voices That Survived

Iain Mac Fhearchair, known as John MacCodrum, was North Uist's family bard to Sir James MacDonald of Sleat in the eighteenth century. His song Smeorach Chlann Domhnaill, "The Mavis of Clan Donald," praises the isle of his birth and was recorded in 2014 by Julie Fowlis - born and raised on North Uist - on her album Gach sgeul. The war poet Domhnall Ruadh Choruna, born here in 1887, became one of the major figures of twentieth-century Scottish Gaelic literature for his vivid trench poetry from the First World War, earning the title "The Voice of the Trenches." The 6th Earl Granville, Fergus Leveson-Gower, is the current laird and lives on the island, sculpting driftwood when not administering the trust.

## Flight Context

Located at 57.60N, 7.33W in the Outer Hebrides. North Uist covers 117 square miles - a roughly rectangular island measuring 20 miles north-south and 25 miles east-west. From altitude the geological complexity is visible in subtle colour changes across the heath and the rough hilly terrain along the east coast where the Outer Hebrides Thrust Zone outcrops. Nearest airport is Benbecula (EGPL) about 12 nautical miles south. The white shell-sand beaches of the west and north coasts provide the most distinctive visual landmark. Atlantic weather systems crossing the island can produce dramatic shadows and squalls clearly visible from cruise altitude.
