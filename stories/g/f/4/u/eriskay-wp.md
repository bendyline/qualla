# Eriskay

From the Old Norse for Eric's Isle, a Hebridean island of 160 people, a 1745 royal landing, a 1941 whisky wreck, and a song that asks 'sad am I, without thee'.

**Location:** 57.0750, -7.2900

**Categories:** islands, scotland, outer-hebrides, history, jacobite

## Introduction

Three things made Eriskay famous. A prince stepped ashore here in 1745 and triggered a rebellion that would end at Culloden. A cargo ship dropped 264,000 bottles of whisky off the coast in 1941 and Ealing Studios turned the aftermath into a comedy. And a Gaelic song called the Eriskay Love Lilt, 'sad am I, without thee', escaped the island in the early twentieth century and is now sung anywhere two Hebrideans gather. The island has a population of 160. The Old Norse name means Eric's Isle, after some Eric whose name persisted longer than his story.

## Bonnie Prince Charlie's Beach

On 2 August 1745, by the Gregorian calendar, the French privateer Du Teillay put Prince Charles Edward Stuart and the Seven Men of Moidart ashore on a sandy beach on Eriskay's west coast. It is called Coilleag a' Phrionnsa, the Cockleshell Strand of the Prince, in his honour. He had arrived from France almost empty-handed, his transport ship and weapons turned back after a sea battle with HMS Lion. He continued to the mainland to raise the clans. The 1745 Jacobite rising would peak with the entry into Edinburgh and end at Culloden the following spring. In 1995 a memorial cairn was erected on the beach with bilingual verses from Alasdair Mac Mhaighstir Alasdair's iconic song-poem Oran Eile don Phrionnsa. Sea bindweed, a plant not native to the Hebrides, grows on the island; islanders say the prince dropped the seeds from a handkerchief in his pocket.

## Cleared Onto an Island

In the 1830s and 1840s, according to Napier Commission testimony from local resident John McCaskill, Eriskay held only three families, fewer than thirty people. Then it became a destination for the Highland Clearances. The estate factors of Colonel John Gordon, who owned most of the southern Outer Hebrides, considered Eriskay agriculturally worthless. They used it as a dumping ground for tenants evicted from neighbouring islands. The new arrivals were mostly Catholic families from South Uist. For decades after Catholic Emancipation in 1829 there was still no resident priest on Eriskay, and visiting priests had to walk to a point on the south coast called Priests' Point and light a bonfire as a signal to be ferried across the Sound. The first St Michael's Roman Catholic Church was a simple stone crofthouse with a thatched roof, built around 1852. The current St Michael's on Cnoc nan Sgrath was built later with stones and homemade mortar by the local population.

## Whisky Galore!

On 4 February 1941 the SS Politician, a 7,900-ton freighter en route to join an Atlantic convoy, ran aground off Eriskay with 22,000 cases of Scotch whisky and £3 million in Jamaican banknotes in her holds. Local islanders helped themselves with imagination, hiding bottles in peat stacks, rabbit holes, and underwater creels. The whisky was bonded for export, so no duty had been paid, and HM Customs and Excise pursued the islanders relentlessly. Nineteen men eventually served prison sentences. The writer Compton Mackenzie, who lived on Barra from 1933, turned the affair into his 1947 novel Whisky Galore; Ealing Studios filmed the 1949 adaptation locally. A 2016 remake followed. The Am Politician pub in Na Hann is named for the ship, and one of the surviving Jamaican banknotes is framed above the bar.

## Causeway, Buyout, and the Eriskay Pony

A causeway to South Uist opened in 2001, making Ardmhor on Barra the new ferry terminus instead of Lochboisdale. The 40-minute Caledonian MacBrayne crossing now defines the island's daily rhythm. In November 2006, after a long campaign, residents took control of the 372-square-kilometre estate including Benbecula, South Uist and Eriskay through Storas Uibhist, a community buyout. The Eriskay Pony, a critically endangered breed of around four hundred animals worldwide, still grazes the hill commons. They graze around the island depending on the weather, brought down for winter. Visitors are asked not to feed them. The 1934 silent documentary Eriskay: A Poem of Remote Lives, by Werner Kissling, paid for the island's first road; part of it is still called Rathad Kissling, Kissling Street.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 57.075N, 7.290W. Eriskay lies between South Uist (north, by causeway opened 2001) and Barra (south, by 40-minute CalMac ferry from Ardmhor). The island is about three miles north-south by two miles east-west, with low-lying settlement in the northwest corner and rugged grazing inland. Recommended viewing altitude 2,000-3,000 ft. Nearest airport is Barra (ICAO: EGPR) to the south on the Traigh Mhor tidal beach runway; Benbecula (ICAO: EGPL) is the larger field to the north on Benbecula. Common conditions: strong Atlantic westerlies, low cloud, rapid visibility changes.
