# Leverburgh

A South Harris village renamed in 1920 after the soap magnate whose ambitious herring empire collapsed with his death five years later.

**Location:** 57.7600, -7.0200

**Categories:** village, scotland, outer-hebrides, harris, industrial-history, fishing-port

## Introduction

In May 1918, William Hesketh Lever was 66 years old and one of the richest men in Britain. He had built Lever Brothers into a soap empire on the back of a single revolutionary product - Sunlight Soap, sold in cartoned bars to Victorian housewives across the Empire. That month he wrote a cheque for £167,000 and bought the entire Isle of Lewis. He had taken a boat trip through the Western Isles in his thirties and never quite gotten over it. Now he intended to remake them: to industrialise the herring fishery, distribute the catch through his Mac Fisheries shops, and turn the Hebrides into a working modern economy. Within five years he would be dead, his project abandoned, and the village called Obbe would have a new name nobody had thought to undo.

## The Lewis Falling-Out

Leverhulme's first plan went wrong fast. Servicemen demobilised from the First World War had been promised land of their own, and many of them - in Lewis especially - were starting to claim it. They occupied plots and began crofting again. Leverhulme called them squatters and went to court. The Scottish Office, weighing public sympathy and political reality, came down on the side of the ex-servicemen. By 1919 the Lewis investment plan was effectively dead. Leverhulme, undeterred, looked south. In late 1919 he bought the South Harris estate from the Earl of Dunmore for £36,000. At Obbe - the fishing hamlet on a sheltered bay - he saw what he needed: access to the waters of both the Minch and the Atlantic, sheltered anchorage in any weather, room to build.

## Why Not Tarbert?

Nigel Nicolson, writing in 1960, recorded that Leverhulme's advisers had quietly suggested a better site. Tarbert, further north, had deep sheltered water on both East and West Loch Tarbert, with only a narrow isthmus between them. A canal there - well within Leverhulme's engineering scope - would have given his boats access to both seas without rounding the headlands. Duncan Maciver urged this on Leverhulme early. Colonel Walter Lindsay, Leverhulme's personal representative in Lewis, raised it again in October 1920, writing that a canal at Tarbert would be a great advantage. But by that very month, work at Obbe had begun in earnest. Nicolson speculated on why: perhaps Leverhulme feared Tarbert's proximity to Stornoway, perhaps he did not wish to harm the Scalpay fishermen, or perhaps - and this seems most human - once he had renamed Obbe Leverburgh, abandoning it became unthinkable.

## Three Hundred Men and Fifty Trawlers

In 1920, with local consent, Obbe was renamed Leverburgh, and three hundred men began building. A new pier rose, then the shore infrastructure for fifty berthed trawlers: an accommodation block, curing sheds, smoke houses, a refrigeration building, store sheds, managers' houses, a twenty-car garage. A second phase was planned: the inner sea loch converted into a deeper harbour with a sea lock to maintain a constant 25-foot depth, capable of holding more than 200 trawlers. Leverhulme paid for upgraded roads to handle the freight. After purchasing the London butchers Wall's in 1920, the economic downturn of 1920-21 slowed momentum, and Mac Fisheries was incorporated into Lever Brothers Ltd in 1922. By 1924 Leverburgh was ready for production. Twelve Great Yarmouth drifters landed a herring catch so large that extra female workers had to be brought in from the mainland to gut, salt and barrel it.

## Africa and Pneumonia

Leverhulme made his last visit to Leverburgh in September 1924. Shortly afterwards he travelled to the Belgian Congo to inspect his palm-oil plantations - the source of much of the raw fat for his soap empire, and a business with its own dark colonial history. There he caught pneumonia. He died in May 1925, aged 74. His executors and the board of Lever Brothers - now run by professional managers with no romantic attachment to the Hebrides - had no interest in continuing the Leverburgh project. They wound it up. The village and production facilities sold for £5,000; the estate for £300. The buyer was the Campbell brothers, a local family. The whole adventure had cost Leverhulme an estimated £500,000 - a substantial fortune even by his standards. Mac Fisheries continued as a fishmonger chain on British high streets until 1979, but never again as the Hebridean herring empire he had imagined.

## What Remains

The Caledonian MacBrayne ferry MV Loch Portain links Leverburgh with Berneray, taking about an hour to cross the Sound of Harris four times a day. Berneray connects by causeway to North Uist and the long chain of islands beyond. The post office serving the village was established in 1873 - as the Obbe Post Office. It changed its name in December 1920, like the village itself. The beaches along the west-coast road from Leverburgh up to Tarbert are some of the most extraordinary in Europe - Luskentyre and Scarista the most famous, but every small bay has its own pale arc of sand. In 1971 the village had a population of 223. Today it stands at about 200. The pier Leverhulme built still works, just for ferries rather than fifty herring trawlers, and the curing sheds are long gone. But the bay he saw - the sheltered water that drew him - is still there, the same shape it has always been.

## Flight Context

Leverburgh sits at 57.76 degrees north, 7.02 degrees west, on the south coast of Harris. From the air the village reads as a small cluster around the ferry pier and harbour, with the A859 climbing north along the western shore toward Luskentyre and Tarbert. Visual landmarks include the harbour, the broken machair coast, and the long pale beaches a few miles north. Nearest airfields: Benbecula (EGPL) about 30 nm south through the Sound of Harris causeway chain, Stornoway (EGPO) on Lewis about 50 nm north. Recommended viewing altitude 2,000-4,000 ft AGL. The Sound of Harris is studded with islets and reefs - dramatic in low light. St Kilda lies 40 nm further west out into the Atlantic. Strong westerly winds and squalls are typical.
