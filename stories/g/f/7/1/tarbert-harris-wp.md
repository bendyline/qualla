# Tarbert, Harris

A Gaelic word for portage gave its name to this Hebridean village, where two sea lochs nearly meet and an earl built a hotel for fishermen.

**Location:** 57.8985, -6.8076

**Categories:** village, ferry-port, scotland, outer-hebrides, history

## Introduction

The name is a verb. Tarbert - in Gaelic, tairbeart - means an isthmus, a crossing place, the bit of dry ground over which a Viking might drag his boat between two stretches of water. There are several Tarberts in Scotland. This one is the main settlement on Harris, in the Outer Hebrides, and it sits at the slim throat where West Loch Tarbert and East Loch Tarbert reach toward each other and almost succeed. South Harris hangs from north Harris by this thread. The 1981 census counted 503 people here, give or take a Calmac crew.

## A Village Built on Crossings

Tarbert is, above everything else, a place you pass through. The car ferry from Uig on Skye docks at the pier on East Loch Tarbert. The short A868 connects the terminal to the A859, the main road that runs the length of Harris and Lewis from south to north. The village's parish church was built in 1862, within the established Church of Scotland parish of Harris. A Free Presbyterian church sits nearby - the Very Rev Mackintosh MacKay was its minister in the 1860s, a notable Gaelic scholar who would later edit the Highland Society's Gaelic dictionary. The village has long been bilingual; Gaelic is still spoken on the street, on the bus, in the shop.

## The Earl, the Hotel, and J.M. Barrie

The Harris Hotel began life in 1865 as the Tarbert Hotel, built by the 7th Earl of Dunmore as a 'sporting retreat' for anglers fishing the rivers and lochs of his Harris estate. It has been in the hands of the same family since the early 1900s. In 1912, the novelist J.M. Barrie - by then world-famous for Peter Pan - stayed at the hotel on his way to the Amhuinnsuidhe estate, where his hosts had taken the shooting and fishing for the season. Before leaving, Barrie scratched his signature into a dining-room window with a diamond. The signature is still there. A century later, the hotel still serves dinner under the same glass.

## A Distillery, A Tweed, A Future

The Harris Distillery, opened in 2015, is the first legal distillery ever built on Harris. It stands a few minutes' walk from the pier and has become the most visible symbol of an island trying to rebuild its working economy after a long century of emigration. Harris tweed is still woven in homes around the island, sold from a shop next to the distillery. The village has neither a hospital nor a secondary school - children go to Stornoway. But Tarbert is the place where the buses meet the ferry, where tourists fuel up, where the tweed gets loaded for export. It is small, and it knows what it is for: a hinge, a crossing place, a tairbeart between worlds.

## Flight Context

57.898°N, 6.808°W at the narrow neck between East and West Loch Tarbert. From the air, the isthmus is unmistakable - two sea lochs reaching inward, the village strung between them. Cruise 2,500-4,000 ft to see the pier, the distillery, and the Harris Hotel laid out along the shore. Stornoway (EGPO) lies about 30 nm north. The A859 runs north-south through the hills toward the Clisham (799 m). Expect Atlantic westerlies and lively turbulence over the high ground.
