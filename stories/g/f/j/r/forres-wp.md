# Forres

A former royal burgh on the Moray coast with Neolithic roots, Pictish stones, and a population that grew from 2,844 in 1841 to over 12,000 today.

**Location:** 57.6086, -3.6157

**Categories:** town, scotland, moray, royal-burgh, macbeth, whisky, pictish, historic-town

## Introduction

Between 2002 and 2013 archaeologists dug seventy hectares of land on the southern fringes of Forres, working ahead of a proposed housing development. What they found rearranged the town's chronology. Radiocarbon dates from the fourth to mid-third millennium BC put people on this site nearly five thousand years before the first written reference. Iron Age settlement with a souterrain - an underground passage, rare in Moray. Metalworking furnaces. Neolithic and Bronze Age ceramics. Cup-marked rock art. By the time Claudius Ptolemy mapped the place as Varar Estuary in the second century AD, Forres had already been inhabited for three millennia. By the time Shakespeare set the opening scenes of Macbeth here in 1606, it had been a royal burgh for nearly five hundred years. The town's tendency to be older than expected is one of its most consistent features.

## Sueno's Stone and the Battle Nobody Recorded

The most striking thing in Forres is the 6.5-metre (21-foot) sandstone slab now standing inside a tall glass case at the eastern edge of town. Sueno's Stone is the largest surviving Pictish stone of its type and dates from AD 850 to 950 - a period in which the Picts were fighting the Norse for control of northern Scotland. The carvings on the stone almost certainly commemorate a battle victory, but which battle remains unknown. The name Sueno translates to Sven, which suggests Norse involvement, though scholars argue about whether the stone celebrates a Pictish victory over Norsemen or something else entirely. The glass enclosure went up in the late twentieth century to protect the stone from acid rain and graffiti, and it has the side effect of making the carving harder to see and photograph - a fair trade for preserving what is one of the most important pieces of early medieval art surviving in Scotland.

## The Wolf and the Royal Burgh

A royal castle stood near Forres from at least AD 900, and around 1140 the town became a royal burgh under King David I - one of a series of urban charters that the 12th-century Kings of Scots issued to encourage trade and turn rural settlements into taxable, governable places. On 23 June 1496 King James IV reissued the charter, formally confirming rights the townspeople believed they had held for three centuries. Before that, in 1390, Alexander Stewart, Earl of Buchan - history remembers him as the Wolf of Badenoch - sacked the local abbey. The Wolf was a younger son of King Robert II who had been excommunicated for adultery, and his response was to burn Pluscarden Abbey, Elgin Cathedral, and Forres in revenge. The medieval church spent decades repairing what one bitter Stewart prince destroyed in a fortnight.

## The 1898 Boom and the Two Distilleries

Two distilleries opened just outside Forres in 1898, at the absolute peak of the late-Victorian whisky boom. Dallas Dhu, just south of town, ran until 1983 and has operated as a museum since 1992 - Historic Environment Scotland confirmed in July 2024 that it will reopen for production under Aceo Distillers Company. Benromach Distillery, just north of the bypass, is still actively producing whisky and runs a visitor centre. RAF Forres operated during the Second World War; its airfield is now a quiet remnant on the outskirts. The Glasgow School of Art established a campus in the town in January 2016 focused on design and innovation, partly drawing on the legacy of the nearby Altyre estate, where a 19th-century Italianate farm steading houses GSA's creative campus. Brodie Castle, the Brodie family seat since around 1160, sits three and a half miles to the west.

## The Notable Forresians

Forres has produced a striking number of people who became notable elsewhere. Sir Alexander Grant, 1st Baronet (1864-1937), the Scottish biscuit manufacturer who created the McVitie's Digestive. Donald Smith, 1st Baron Strathcona and Mount Royal (1820-1914), the Scottish-Canadian railway baron who drove the last spike in the Canadian Pacific. Charles Lumley (1824-1858), Victoria Cross recipient. Roy Williamson (1936-1990), member of The Corries, who wrote "Flower of Scotland" - the unofficial Scottish national anthem. James McIntyre (1828-1906), Forres-born cabinetmaker and undertaker who moved to Ontario in 1841 and became Canada's most famously bad poet, celebrated for his odes to cheese. Frank Fraser Darling (1903-1979), the ecologist whose name attaches to the Fraser Darling effect on bird colonies. The Findhorn Foundation, an intentional community established near Findhorn in 1962, has drawn its own set of cultural figures - including the actress Tilda Swinton, who has lived in nearby Nairn since 2007.

## Flight Context

Forres sits at 57.61N, 3.62W on the A96 corridor in Moray. Identifiable from low altitude by its position between the wooded slopes of Cluny and Sanquhar Hills (south) and the floodplain of the River Findhorn (west), with the bypass marking the northern town edge. Findhorn Bay (tidal basin) lies 4 miles north. Nelson's Tower stands prominently on Cluny Hill. Inverness Airport (EGPE) is 20 nm west; Kinloss Barracks (former RAF Kinloss) is 3 miles north. Best viewing 2,000 to 3,500 feet AGL. The Met Office records highest temperature 31.2C (Kinloss, 9 August 2003).
