# Braemar

A village of 808 people that shares the UK record for lowest temperature ever recorded, attended every September by the British royal family for the Highland Games.

**Location:** 57.0100, -3.4000

**Categories:** Villages in Aberdeenshire, Cairngorms National Park, Highland Games, Royal Deeside, Scottish weather records, Gaelic-speaking areas

## Introduction

Twice in 86 years, Braemar has set the same record. On 11 February 1895 the thermometer at the Met Office station dropped to -27.2C, and on 10 January 1982 it did so again - a tied UK low matched only by Altnaharra in the far north. On 11 February 2021 the village made national news once more when the temperature plunged to -23C, the coldest UK night since 1995. The village of 808 souls is the third-coldest low-lying place in the United Kingdom, behind only Dalwhinnie and Leadhills. And yet, for one Saturday every September, it is also the most photographed Highland village in the world.

## Bridge End, Castletown, Upland of Mar

The Gaelic name Braigh Mhar means 'Upland of Mar' - originally a regional term for the whole upper end of the historical Earldom of Mar, only applied to the village itself around 1870. Before then, this was Castleton of Braemar, or Baile a' Chaisteil. Before that, going back before the 11th century, there were two separate hamlets on opposite banks of the Clunie Water: Auchendryne on the west, meaning 'thorn field,' and Castleton on the east. The village name Cinn Drochaid means 'bridge end,' which is exactly what it was. Legend places King Malcolm III here around 1059, ordering a timber bridge across the Clunie and a wooden castle to guard it. The stone ruins of Kindrochit Castle that survive in the village today are largely 14th-century. The crossing they guarded sat on the Elsick Mounth, an ancient trackway used by Picts and Romans long before any king of Scots gave it a name.

## The Last Native Speaker

In the 1891 census, 59.2 percent of Braemar habitually spoke Gaelic. In the small crofting township of Inverey, just west, the figure was 86.3 percent. By the 2001 census, 5 of 839 residents in Crathie and Braemar parish - 0.6 percent - claimed any knowledge of the language. The last native speaker of the local Aberdeenshire Highland dialect died in 1984. The Gaelic that was lost here shared most of its features with the dialects of Strathspey and East Perthshire, both of which still have a few surviving native speakers. This was not a vague fading-away. It was a specific person, in a specific year, in living memory.

## The Games and the King

The Braemar Gathering is held on the first Saturday in September and is traditionally attended by the British royal family in residence at Balmoral, eight miles east. Local tradition traces it to the 11th century and Malcolm III. The modern form took shape after 1782, when the Act of Proscription - which had banned tartan and clan gatherings since 1746 in the wake of Culloden - was repealed. The current Chieftain is King Charles III. The day includes hill running, heavy events like the caber and hammer, light events, and Highland dancing of national calibre. The dancing boards sit in front of the patrons' pavilions, which means generations of children have danced for sovereigns - Queen Victoria, Edward VII, George V, Elizabeth II, and now Charles. Winners of the Aberdeenshire League, nicknamed the Shortbread League, receive a tin of Walker's shortbread and a commemorative vase.

## Cold, Snow, and Margot Robbie

Braemar has an annual mean temperature of 7.1C - which sounds mild until you account for 98 days of air frost a year. Snow can pile to 30 centimetres, and weather watcher Chris Booth measured 70 centimetres on 9 February 2021. In a single 24-hour stretch in September 2015, the village recorded both the warmest and coldest temperatures in the United Kingdom. Two months running. The local quirks extend to ownership: the Earldom of Mar runs deep here, the Farquharsons of Inverey produced the outlawed Jesuit John Farquharson, whose lost manuscript of Gaelic literature later fed the Ossianic controversy of the 18th century. And in 2025, the village made headlines of a different sort when Margot Robbie, the Australian-American actress, bought a house here - what the Daily Record cheerfully called her 'Scottish Barbie cottage.' Tabloid fame, then, joins the much older fame of cold.

## Flight Context

Coordinates 57.01N, 3.40W. Elevation 339 m (1,112 ft). The village sits where the Clunie Water joins the Dee, in the Cairngorms National Park. Recommended viewing altitude 5,000-7,500 ft AGL. Morrone rises to 859 m southwest, Carn na Drochaide to 818 m northwest, Carn na Sgliat to 690 m, and Creag Choinneach to 538 m northeast. From the air, look for the distinctive Y-shape of the upper Dee valley and the A93 climbing south toward the Cairnwell Pass (670 m), the highest public road in the UK. Nearest ICAO: Aberdeen (EGPD) 58 nm east; Inverness (EGPE) 60 nm northwest; Dundee (EGPN) 45 nm southeast. Mountain weather is volatile - this is one of the coldest low-lying places in the UK and frequently records the country's weather extremes. Brief the freezing level carefully in winter and watch for orographic cloud building over the Cairngorms.
