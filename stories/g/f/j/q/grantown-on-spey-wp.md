# Grantown-on-Spey

An 18th-century planned town founded by Sir James Grant in 1765 as part of post-Jacobite Highland economic improvement - now a Cairngorms regional centre with listed Georgian hotels, Victorian tourism heritage, and an unusual list of notable former residents.

**Location:** 57.3293, -3.6088

**Categories:** Grantown-on-Spey, Populated places in Badenoch and Strathspey, Populated places established in 1765, Towns in Highland (council area), Populated places on the River Spey

## Introduction

Sir James Grant decided in 1765 that the Highlands needed a town. The Jacobite rising of twenty years earlier had ended in massacre on Drumossie Moor a few miles away; the clan system was being deliberately dismantled by the Government; the old economy of cattle droving and seasonal raiding was being replaced by something the eighteenth-century landlords called 'improvement'. Grant laid out a grid pattern on a low plateau at Freuchie, beside the River Spey, and called it after himself. The plan was to encourage agricultural marketing and handicrafts, to raise land values, to reduce unemployment, and to keep people from emigrating. Grantown was one of dozens of planned settlements founded across late-18th-century Scotland in the same impulse - more rare in surviving with its grid largely intact, more interesting because of who has walked its streets in the centuries since. The town added 'on Spey' to its name in 1898 by act of the burgh council. By that date it was already a confirmed Victorian holiday resort.

## The Planned Burgh

Grantown was founded during the early stages of the Industrial Revolution, deliberately set up to encourage both agricultural marketing and handicrafts. It was part of a wider effort at social and economic improvement led by progressive Highland landlords in the decades of peace after 1745. The original plan was for a linen-manufacturing economy alongside the agricultural market. A church was built; a school was planned in 1765 but did not actually open until 1767, when there were finally enough children of school age in the new town to fill it. By 1841 the population had reached 1,000. The linen industry, which had been the economic point of the original foundation, declined through the 1860s. The town settled into being a market town for the surrounding agricultural district, then - as Victorians discovered the Highlands as a holiday destination, abetted by the new railway and Queen Victoria's well-publicised love of Deeside - reinvented itself as a tourist resort. By 1965 the population was just under 1,600, and the town could accommodate 800 visitors in tourist accommodation. The grid pattern Sir James Grant laid out in 1765 is still the bones of the place.

## The Hotels and the Square

Grantown's High Street and The Square contain a remarkable concentration of listed Georgian and Victorian buildings, most of them former hotels. The Garth Hotel on the Square dates back to 1769, originally built for James Grant - the clerk and factor to Sir James Grant - as a private residence, later converted to a hotel. Hastilow's Palace Hotel was constructed in 1894 on the site of the previous Black Bull Inn; the earlier 1765 building hosted Queen Victoria and Prince Albert on 4 September 1860. The Palace later became a care home. The former Strathspey Hotel (also called Dunbar's Hotel) is an early-19th-century category B listed building at 70-72 High Street, now converted into homes. The Ben Mhor at 57 High Street dates to the 1880s and was originally the Temperance Hotel - one of the temperance movement's signature inn-and-coffeehouse establishments. The Royal British Legion building on the Square is an Art Deco former cinema from the 1920s. The Speyside Orphanage, established 1795 with funds from Lady Grant of Monymusk, was rebuilt in 1824 after fire, closed in 1975, and converted to private flats in 1986; its category A listed clock tower still stands on the Square. The Speyside Orphanage clock from 1824 is now in the town museum; a 1975 electric public turret clock keeps time outside.

## Anagach Woods and the Capercaillie

On the eastern edge of town stretches Anagach Woods - a working pinewood with numerous waymarked trails. The woods are home to a secretive capercaillie population. Capercaillie are the great wood grouse of the Caledonian pinewood, almost extinct in Britain by the 1980s, the subject of intense conservation effort ever since, and so vulnerable to disturbance that local guides nowadays sometimes decline to direct visitors to known sites. The first capercaillie chicks fledged from Anagach in many years were reported on BBC News in the 2010s - a small but significant return. Inverallan Church, on the western edge of town, was rebuilt by the architect Alexander Smith Cullen in 1886 on the site of several earlier churches. The original stone bridge over the Spey at Grantown dates from 1754, built as part of the military road that General Wade and his successors drove from Grantown eastward to Corgaff - one segment of the wider road network that turned the Highlands into a country the Government could effectively police after 1745. The old bridge now carries only pedestrians and cyclists; modern traffic uses a newer crossing.

## Aung San Suu Kyi and Other Visitors

The list of notable former residents of Grantown is unexpectedly varied. Aung San Suu Kyi - the Burmese politician and Nobel Peace laureate, daughter of the Burmese independence leader General Aung San - lived in Grantown for most of 1975 with her young family, before history called her back to Burma in 1988. The town honoured her with a plaque in 2015. Bobby Cruickshank, the golfer who finished runner-up at the 1923 U.S. Open, was born here in 1894. Isabella Leitch, the pioneering nutritionist who established the Rowett Research Institute's reputation, was born in Grantown in 1890. James Ogilvie-Grant, 11th Earl of Seafield, lived here. So did the track cyclist Craig MacLean - whose name is on the leisure centre - and the Russian-born pianist Nikolai Orlov, the diplomat John Kerr, Baron Kerr of Kinlochard, the astronomer Robert Grant, and the Royal Navy captain W. N. T. Beckett. Grantown also briefly hosted Queen Victoria and Prince Albert at the Palace Hotel in 1860, and Queen Elizabeth II in modern times. For a market town of 2,500 souls, it has a list of distinguished alumni considerably longer than its population would suggest.

## Flight Context

Grantown-on-Spey sits at 57.33°N, 3.61°W on a low plateau beside the River Spey at the northern edge of the Cairngorms, about 12 nautical miles southeast of Inverness Airport (EGPE). The town lies on the A95 road, which runs broadly southwest-to-northeast through the Spey valley toward Moray. Recommended viewing altitude is 2,500-4,500 ft AGL. The 18th-century grid pattern of streets - laid out by Sir James Grant in 1765 - is unusually clear from the air, centred on The Square and with High Street running west. The Spey valley provides a clean visual handrail. The Cairngorm massif rises to 1,245 m about 12 nm south-southwest; the Monadhliath sit to the northwest. EGPE provides full instrument approaches. Aviemore lies 9 nm southwest along the valley; the proposed Strathspey Railway extension from Broomhill toward Grantown follows the old Highland Railway alignment along the south side of the valley.
