# Cairngorms National Park

Britain's largest national park — 4,528 square kilometres of granite plateau, sub-arctic wildlife, the last Caledonian pinewoods, and a continuing argument about wolves.

**Location:** 57.0833, -3.6667

**Categories:** national park, scotland, highlands, wildlife, mountains

## Introduction

Some enthusiasts want the wolves back. Lynx, too, and the bears that once roamed these hills before centuries of human pressure shut them out. The Cairngorms cover 4,528 square kilometres — more than 1,748 square miles — making this by far the largest national park in Britain, established in 2003 to protect a granite plateau that the ice age failed to erode. But even this much space, the campaigners are told, is not enough. The reintroduced predators would need huge ranges. And the park's official designation under the IUCN is Category 5: a landscape where farming, logging, hunting, and tourism are not just permitted but are considered an integral part of what makes the place itself. The family picnic, for now, remains safe. The wolves, for now, remain hypothetical.

## A Block of Granite the Ice Could Not Move

The Cairngorms are a single great block of granite, and what defines them geologically is what didn't happen. During the last ice age, the ice sheet sat across the top of the massif but barely flowed. In other British uplands — the Lake District, Snowdonia — the ice carved U-shaped valleys, dug corries, and gouged dramatic glacial relief. Here, with a few narrow exceptions like the Lairig Ghru pass, the ice merely polished a series of rounded tops to roughly equal height. What was left behind is a plateau averaging 1,000 to 1,200 metres in altitude, with four peaks rising above 1,290 metres: Ben Macdui at 1,309 metres, Braeriach at 1,296, Cairn Toul at 1,291, and Cairn Gorm at 1,244. The result is a sub-arctic island in northern Britain — too cold and stony for agriculture, where plants and animals more common in Lapland or northern Russia find their last British foothold. The remnant Caledonian pinewoods in the valleys are the last stand of the primeval forest that once covered Scotland.

## Beavers, Reindeer, and a Possible Wildcat

The Cairngorms now host wildlife you have to look hard to find anywhere else in Britain. Around 500 Eurasian beavers inhabit the Tay valley, with the best sightings between May and August at Loch of the Lowes above Dunkeld. A free-roaming herd of about 150 reindeer ranges between Cairngorm and Glenlivet — usually only a distant smudge on the moor, though Glenmore-based rangers run guided walks to their usual grazing grounds. The Scottish wildcat is the hardest to claim a sighting of: hybridisation with domestic cats has reduced the genuinely pure-bred population to perhaps a few dozen, and any large tabby could be mistaken. A formal breeding and release programme began in 2023 with the aim of releasing roughly twenty wildcats annually for three years. Other species — crossbills, ospreys, capercaillie, golden eagles, red deer, red squirrels, otters, mountain hare — are easier to encounter. Ospreys nest at Loch Garten near the Strathspey Railway; capercaillie, an enormous grouse on the edge of British extinction, still drum in the Abernethy Forest reserve.

## Drive In, Walk Far, Watch the Weather

The park has no gates and no entry fee. The A9 from Perth to Inverness enters from the south and follows along the western edge, with railway stations at Pitlochry, Blair Atholl, Dalwhinnie, Newtonmore, Kingussie, Aviemore and Carrbridge. Aviemore is the main service town for the western, larger side; Ballater near Balmoral and Braemar serve the eastern half via the A93 from Aberdeen. From Aviemore, the Lairig Ghru — a 31-kilometre boggy pass through the central plateau to Linn of Dee above Braemar — is the classic long traverse, with no bailout option once you commit. The Speyside Way runs 137 kilometres from Buckie on the Moray coast upriver through Grantown, Aviemore and Newtonmore. Loch an Eilein, a five-mile gentle stroll in Rothiemurchus Forest south of Aviemore, gives one of the easier days, with a ruined 14th-century castle perched on an islet. The mountain weather forecast is mandatory reading for anything serious — these hills sit on the same latitude as Labrador, and a navigation error in cloud, on slippery scree, in a cold wet wind, can escalate fast. The emergency number is 999 or 112: ask for police and mountain rescue. Provided the mobile signal holds.

## Castles, Whisky, and a Sad Mountain

Balmoral Castle, three miles west of Ballater, was completed in 1856 and remains a royal residence. Queen Elizabeth II spent August and September here every year, and died at Balmoral on 8 September 2022. Blair Castle in Blair Atholl is older — 15th and 16th-century — but was rebuilt in Victorian times to resemble its more famous neighbour. Ruthven Barracks near Kingussie, built in 1719 to garrison the Highlands against the Jacobites, is the most defiantly post-medieval ruin in the park. The Highland Folk Museum near Newtonmore reconstructs Highland rural life from 1700 to the 1950s in an open-air setting. Around Aviemore, Bronze Age stone circles cluster: the Aviemore Stone Circle sits incongruously inside a modern housing estate. The Strathspey Railway runs heritage steam excursions from Aviemore to Boat of Garten and Broomhill. Three ski areas — at Cairngorm, Glenshee, and the Lecht — operate within the park, though all suffer from limited terrain, unreliable snow, and weekend overcrowding. Cairngorm's funicular suffered a long shutdown and only reopened in 2023. When the snow holds, they are good. When it doesn't, the park has whisky distilleries — particularly around Kingussie, Carrbridge and Grantown-on-Spey — to fill the rest of the day.

## Flight Context

Cairngorms National Park: centred around 57.083°N, 3.667°W, covering 4,528 km² of upland between the Spey and Dee valleys. The granite plateau is unmistakable from the air — a high, gently rolling tableland 1,000–1,300 m elevation with four peaks above 1,290 m (Ben Macdui 1,309 m, Braeriach 1,296 m, Cairn Toul 1,291 m, Cairn Gorm 1,245 m). The Lairig Ghru pass cuts a sharp north-south notch through the centre. Best viewed at 6,000–8,000 ft AGL. Nearest airports are Inverness (EGPE) to the north, Aberdeen (EGPD) to the northeast, and Edinburgh (EGPH) to the south. Mountain weather changes rapidly; consult Mountain Weather Information Service forecasts before low-altitude operations.
