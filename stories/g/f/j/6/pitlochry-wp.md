# Pitlochry

A Victorian Highland resort built on Queen Victoria's enthusiasm, the arrival of the railway, and a stubborn local refusal to let the twentieth century spoil what the nineteenth had created.

**Location:** 56.7033, -3.7332

**Categories:** town, scotland, highlands, victorian, history

## Introduction

Queen Victoria came to Perthshire in 1842 and loved it so thoroughly that she bought a Highland estate at Balmoral, and the rest of Britain noticed. Twenty-one years later, the railway reached Pitlochry, a small settlement on the River Tummel that had grown up where the old military road north climbed out of the Lowlands. Within a generation it was a resort. Stone Victorian villas filled the hillsides. Hotels lined the high street under that distinctive cast iron canopy. And then - improbably - Pitlochry stopped. Later development largely passed it by. The town today is essentially the town the Victorians built, which is exactly why people still come.

## Two Older Villages, One Newer Town

Pitlochry is younger than it looks. The settlement we know dates from the nineteenth century, but Moulin to the north and Port-na-craig on the river are far older. Moulin Kirk was granted to Dunfermline Abbey in 1180, and Moulin became a burgh of barony in 1511. Port-na-craig was the site of the original ferry across the Tummel, which operated until a suspension footbridge replaced it in 1913. The two villages were stitched together by the construction of the eighteenth-century military road - the present main street - and then by the Victorian villas that filled in between them. Moulin's parish school produced Alexander MacKenzie, the second Prime Minister of Canada, who was born in 1822 and educated here before emigrating.

## The Writers, the Radar Man, and the Curler

Robert Louis Stevenson stayed at Fishers Hotel in June 1881 with his wife Fanny and his mother, then moved to Kinnaird Cottage in nearby Moulin. The Highland summer suited him. While in residence he wrote 'Thrawn Janet,' 'The Merry Men,' and 'The Body Snatcher' - three of his strangest and most enduring short stories. From the 1960s, Sir Robert Watson-Watt, one of the inventors of radar, lived here with his wife Dame Katherine Trefusis Forbes, who had directed the Women's Auxiliary Air Force during the Second World War. Both are buried in the churchyard of Holy Trinity Episcopal Church. The Olympic curler Eve Muirhead played at a club in Pitlochry before the curling rink was demolished. Small town, surprising company.

## The Dam and the Salmon Ladder

In 1947 Pitlochry became a burgh, and that same year construction began on the dam that would change the town. The Pitlochry Dam was one of the pioneering schemes of post-war Scottish hydroelectric power, part of the Tummel scheme of nine power stations and reservoirs. Harold Tarbolton designed the power station and died soon after, leaving the architect T. H. Eley to finish it. The dam was completed in 1951 and created Loch Faskally, flooding the old Recreation Park north of town. But the most remarkable feature was an early concession to ecology: the Pitlochry fish ladder, 310 metres of stepped pools that let spawning salmon climb past the dam to their upstream redds. Over 5,000 salmon pass through every year. Visitors watch them through underwater viewing windows.

## The Town That Stayed

Pitlochry's Victorian core survived because so little changed after the railway era. The high street kept its cast iron canopy, the villas kept their slate roofs, and the A9 was finally diverted onto a bypass in 1981, taking the through traffic with it. The Pitlochry Festival Theatre, founded in 1951 in a canvas tent, settled into a permanent glass-fronted building at Port-na-craig the same year the bypass opened. The Enchanted Forest sound and light show in Faskally Wood draws 70,000 visitors each October. The Highland Games on the second Saturday of September have run since 1852 - the last games of the Scottish season. Ben Vrackie at 841 metres dominates the skyline from the A9. The town was a Gold Medal winner in the 2009 Britain in Bloom competition. It is, in its quiet Victorian way, still doing what it was built to do.

## Flight Context

Pitlochry sits at 56.703 north, 3.733 west, in the valley of the River Tummel about 26 miles north of Perth in Perthshire. From the air the town appears as a compact Victorian settlement on the river, with Loch Faskally and its hydroelectric dam to the north, the 841-metre summit of Ben Vrackie immediately north, and Schiehallion's distinctive cone visible to the west. Best appreciated from 3,000 to 6,000 feet. Dundee Riverside (EGPN) lies roughly 36 nm east-southeast. Edinburgh (EGPH) is about 55 nm south, Inverness (EGPE) 75 nm north. The Highland Main Line railway is clearly visible from the air, threading north through the Pass of Killiecrankie.
