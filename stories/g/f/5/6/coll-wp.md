# Coll

An Inner Hebridean island of Lewisian gneiss and shifting sand, refuge to corncrakes and short-necked oil beetles, where local farming saved species the rest of Britain lost.

**Location:** 56.6286, -6.5614

**Categories:** inner hebrides, scotland, islands, wildlife, corncrake, dark sky, highland clearances

## Introduction

The corncrake is mostly extinct in Britain. The short-necked oil beetle was assumed to be entirely so for decades. Both turn out to be on Coll. The corncrake survives here because the crofting practices, late hay cuts and rough corners left ungrazed, are exactly what the bird needs to nest. The beetle, parasitic on ground-dwelling bees and unable to fly, was rediscovered on Coll in 2010 after being presumed extinct in the UK. Coll is one of those small islands that turns out to be quietly enormous in scientific terms, because the modern farming methods that erased species elsewhere never quite reached.

## Lewisian Gneiss and the Bays of Sand

Coll is thirteen miles long and three wide, formed largely from Lewisian gneiss of Archaean to early Proterozoic age, with normal faults running northwest to southeast through the eastern half of the island. Younger dolerite and camptonite dykes from the Permo-Carboniferous or Tertiary periods cut the older rock in places. The most striking surface feature is not the bedrock but what sits on it: vast areas of blown sand in the west and along the north coast, peat to the southwest of Arinagour, and the high sand dunes that ripple along the south and west beaches at Hogh, Feall, Crossapol and Grishipoll. The highest point on the island, Ben Hogh, is only 106 metres above sea level. In January 2024 a 3.3-magnitude earthquake shook the island. December 2013 brought a different distinction: Coll became Scotland's first officially designated dark-sky community, no street lights, the Northern Lights visible on winter nights.

## Cenel Loairn to Clan MacLean

An Irish invasion in the sixth century brought Coll into the Gaelic kingdom of Dal Riata, under the Cenel Loairn kin-group whose name eventually attached itself to the region of Lorn on the adjacent mainland. Coll spent the next thousand years sharing Lorn's history. The Norse Kingdom of the Isles absorbed it. The Treaty of Perth transferred it to Scotland in 1266. The MacDougalls, descendants of Somerled, held it briefly, lost it in the Wars of Scottish Independence by backing Balliol over Bruce, and saw it pass to the Lordship of the Isles via a 1354 quitclaim. Clan MacLean exercised baronial control until 1848, when the island was sold during the Highland Clearances. Local tradition describes three of Coll's dùns, Dùn Anlaimh, Dùn an Achaidh and Dùn Dubh, as Norse strongholds that survived until being attacked by the MacLeans. Dean Munro, writing in 1549, called Coll a fertile inhabited island with a castle and a parish kirk, gude for fishing and fowlers, and an utter fine Falcons nest in it.

## The Beetle, the Bird, the Lizard

Coll's wildlife inventory reads like a footnote to a different geography. The corncrake nests in late-cut hay meadows here and is now one of the largest single populations of the bird in Britain, the rest of its UK range having collapsed in the twentieth century. Skylarks also thrive. The short-necked oil beetle, Meloe brevicollis, was thought to be entirely extinct in Britain until a colony was discovered on Coll in 2010. Its only other known UK range is in southern England. It is flightless and parasitic on ground-dwelling bees, which raises the question of how it ever reached this far north. In the 1970s scientists released 39 sand lizards on the island as an experiment in northern survival. They are still there. The Maclean-Bristol family restored Breachacha Castle in the twentieth century. In September 2024 the island was reported to have one shop, one restaurant, one hotel, and a post office. Mairi Hedderwick lived here and turned Coll into the Isle of Struay for her Katie Morag children's books.

## Flight Context

Coll lies at 56.63 N, 6.56 W in the Inner Hebrides. Coll Airport (EGEL) sits between Uig and Arileod on the southwest, served by Hebridean Air Services from Oban about once a week using a BN2 Islander. Recommended viewing altitude 2,000-4,000 ft. Visual landmarks: Ben Hogh (106 m) the high point in the mid-west; Breachacha Castles, the old fifteenth-century and new eighteenth-century pair on the southwest coast; Arinagour at the head of Loch Eatharna on the east coast; the long Atlantic-facing beaches of Hogh and Feall in the southwest; the Sound of Gunna between Coll and Tiree; Gunna islet itself in the sound. The island's mottled landscape of bog, gneiss, peat and sand reads clearly from above.
