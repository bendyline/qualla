# Ardnamurchan

The remote 50-square-mile peninsula at the westernmost point of mainland Britain, accessed by single-track road and home to a Gaelic-speaking community, golden eagles, and an extinct volcano.

**Location:** 56.7333, -5.9833

**Categories:** Scottish Highlands, peninsulas, Gaelic culture, extinct volcanoes, Highland Clearances

## Introduction

There is a moment, on the single-track road into Ardnamurchan, where the traffic disappears. Passing places replace lay-bys. The trees thin. The mobile signal dies. The road narrows again, then again, and you realise you are heading somewhere mainland Britain barely remembers it has. Corrachadh Mòr, a small hummock on Ardnamurchan's western shore, is the most westerly point of mainland Great Britain - farther west than Land's End, farther west than the western tip of Wales. The peninsula has 2,000 residents spread across 50 square miles, one in five of them able to speak Scottish Gaelic, and one of the lowest road densities of any inhabited place in the United Kingdom.

## A Volcano Visible from Space

Ardnamurchan's geology is so striking that astronauts photograph it. The north-western corner of the peninsula is the eroded heart of an ancient volcano - more precisely a lopolith, a saucer-shaped intrusion of magma that solidified underground around 58 million years ago and was later exposed by erosion. The sub-concentric rings of the structure are visible from satellite imagery and on topographic maps, though they are surprisingly hard to spot on the ground. Geologists have long used Ardnamurchan as a teaching site; phenocrysts of plagioclase in the rock exposures show crystal alignments caused by magmatic flow within the lopolith. At least seven similar volcanic centres of the same age exist along the west coast of Britain - including Mull and Skye - relics of the great rifting episode that broke open the North Atlantic and separated Britain from Greenland.

## Columba, Vikings, and the Battle at Buarblaig

Adomnán of Iona, writing in the seventh century, records that St Columba visited Ardnamurchan in the sixth - the peninsula already settled by Irish Gaels. He describes three miracles Columba performed here: a prophecy about the death of two Irish kings, a baptism made possible when water sprang miraculously from a rock, and a curse against a wicked man named Ioan mac Conaill who attacked Columba's friend and was drowned along with his stolen goods after the saint waded into the sea to pray. Two centuries later the Picts fought the Dalriads at a place identified with Buarblaig, east of Kilchoan beneath Ben Hiant. The Annals of Tigernach record the battle for the year 731. In 2011, an even more dramatic discovery came to light: a fully intact Viking boat burial at Port an Eilean Mhòir, the first such grave ever found on the British mainland. The chieftain buried inside had been laid out with his sword, shield, spear, and pottery imported from Ireland and Norway.

## Wildcats, Gaelic, and the Clearances

Ardnamurchan is one of the few places in Britain where you might genuinely see a wildcat - the elusive Felis silvestris that ecologists fear is nearly extinct elsewhere through hybridisation with domestic cats. Pine martens hunt the woodlands; golden eagles and white-tailed eagles soar over the high ground. The northern half of the peninsula forms part of the Morar, Moidart and Ardnamurchan National Scenic Area, one of forty such protected zones in Scotland. The peninsula's Gaelic culture is unusually intact for the mainland: 19.3 percent of residents speak the language, and the area hosts its own annual Mòd, an eisteddfod-style festival of Gaelic song and recitation. That continuity is the more remarkable for being a survival rather than a default. Dr John MacLachlan, a nineteenth-century Ardnamurchan-born poet, wrote Dìreadh a-mach ri Beinn Shianta, an unusually direct attack on the Anglo-Scottish landlords behind the Ardnamurchan Clearances - and one of the few Gaelic Clearance poems whose anger went on record.

## Flight Context

Located at 56.73N, 5.98W on the west coast of the Scottish Highlands, bordered by Loch Sunart to the south, the Sound of Mull to the southwest, and the open Atlantic to the west. Most westerly mainland point: Corrachadh Mòr (56.7244N, 6.2255W). Nearest airport: Oban (EGEO), about 30 nm southeast. Tobermory on Mull lies just across the Sound to the south. Recommended viewing altitude 3,000-5,000 ft to see the volcanic ring structure of the northwest peninsula, the lighthouse at Ardnamurchan Point, and the long sea lochs of Sunart and Linnhe to the south and east.
