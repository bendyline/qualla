# Iona

The cradle of Celtic Christianity in Scotland, where Columba founded his monastery in 563, the Book of Kells may have been begun, and Scottish kings from Kenneth I to Macbeth are said to lie buried.

**Location:** 56.3306, -6.4119

**Categories:** inner hebrides, scotland, iona abbey, st columba, book of kells, celtic christianity, medieval scotland

## Introduction

Macbeth is buried somewhere here. So, by tradition, are Kenneth I and Donald II and Malcolm I and Duncan I and Donald III. Nobody can point to the graves. The inscriptions wore away by the end of the seventeenth century, the sixteenth-century inventory that listed forty-eight Scottish kings, eight Norwegian and four Irish has no corroborating archaeology, and the actual ground evidence the National Trust recovered in 2013 dated to the sixth to eighth centuries, then jumbled and reburied in the medieval period. The kings are here in some sense. Iona's claim on them is more spiritual than cartographic.

## The Founding and the Scriptorium

Columba came in 563 AD. The much later legend that he was exiled from Ireland after his part in the Battle of Cul Dreimhne dates from a thousand years after the fact and lacks any contemporary support. What is reliably known: the monastery he founded with twelve companions on this small island became the most consequential religious institution in the early medieval west. It may have helped convert the Picts and the Gaels in late sixth-century Scotland. It certainly drove the conversion of Anglo-Saxon Northumbria from 635 onwards. The scriptorium produced texts that lie beneath the early Irish annals. The Book of Kells, one of the supreme illuminated manuscripts of medieval Europe, may have been begun on Iona around the end of the eighth century before being moved to Ireland to escape the Vikings. The ringed Celtic cross may have been invented here. By the time the Norse had burned and resettled the island repeatedly, Iona was already too important to lose.

## Yew Place, Calum's Place

The name Iona is a transcription error. The place-name scholar William Watson showed that the oldest recorded forms of the island's name probably meant yew-place, related to the ogham element ivo-. By the medieval period the Gaelic name was Ì Chaluim Chille, Calum's Iona, meaning the island of the monastery of Calum, whose Latin form is Columba. The original short name Ì got confused with the unrelated Gaelic word ì meaning island borrowed from Old Norse ey, so people lengthened it. The Latin form Ioua, written in Insular Minuscule script, was misread as Iona around 1274 because n and u look almost identical in that hand. The mistake stuck. The island is also sometimes called Ì nam ban bòidheach, the isle of beautiful women. About 180 people live here today. Iona Abbey is now the headquarters of the Iona Community, an ecumenical Christian movement founded by George MacLeod in 1938 that runs three residential centres on Iona and Mull.

## Cradle, Quarry, Canvas

Half the island's bedrock is Scourian gneiss from the Lewisian complex, formed in the Archaean eon, making it some of the oldest rock in Europe. A small Silurian-age pink granite outcrops on the southeastern beaches. A separate quarry on the southeast coast worked a vein of green-streaked Iona marble in a small way for centuries, then intensively from around 1900 until 1919 when the First World War ended both demand and labour. A 1909 painting of the quarry by David Young Cameron now hangs in Cartwright Hall, Bradford. The island has a remarkable place in modern Scottish art. The Scottish Colourists F C B Cadell and S J Peploe painted here repeatedly across the 1910s and 1920s. So did George Houston, John Duncan, John Maclauchlan Milne. The reference book Iona Portrayed lists over 170 artists known to have worked on the island between 1760 and 1960. Samuel Johnson, who visited in 1773, wrote: That man is little to be envied whose patriotism would not gain force upon the plains of Marathon or whose piety would not grow warmer amid the ruins of Iona.

## Flight Context

Iona lies at 56.33 N, 6.41 W, west of the Ross of Mull across a narrow sound. No airport on Iona. Closest fields are Tiree (EGPU) some 22 nm northwest, Glenforsa (EGEH) on Mull 28 nm east, and Oban (EGEO) 38 nm east. Recommended viewing altitude 1,500-3,000 ft. Visual landmarks: Iona Abbey on the east coast, the roofed medieval complex; Iona Nunnery and Baile Mor village south of it; Dun I (101 m) at the north end; the white sand of Port Ban on the west coast and the beaches at the north end; Staffa with Fingal's Cave 6 mi northeast; Erraid 4 mi south; the Treshnish Isles north. The sound between Iona and Mull is narrow.
