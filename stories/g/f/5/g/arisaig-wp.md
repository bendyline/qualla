# Arisaig

A 300-person village whose Gaelic name means 'safe bay', whose Bronze Age burnt mounds defy modern interpretation, and whose remoteness made it the SOE's training ground for Czechoslovak agents during the Second World War.

**Location:** 56.9110, -5.8425

**Categories:** village, scotland, lochaber, highlands, soe, jacobite, bronze-age, clearances

## Introduction

In the autumn of 1943, somewhere in the hills behind Arisaig, a group of young Czechoslovak men were being taught how to kill. They had escaped occupied Czechoslovakia, made their way to Britain, and volunteered for the Special Operations Executive. The SOE's Scottish section was headquartered at Arisaig House, on the edge of the village, and from there it ran the most secret training programme in the British war effort. Hundreds of agents, French, Polish, Norwegian, Belgian, Yugoslav, Czechoslovak, learned to shoot, climb, sabotage, and parachute into Occupied Europe in this remote, dripping, mountainous corner of Lochaber. The Rough Bounds, the local name for this trackless region, looked enough like every other rugged landscape in Europe to serve as universal preparation. Many of those Czechoslovaks would not survive their missions. On 11 November 2009 a memorial to them was unveiled in the village.

## Safe Bay, Old Bones

Arisaig means 'safe bay' in Old Norse, and the bay has been used by humans for a remarkably long time. When the A830 was realigned in 2000-2001, archaeologists from the Centre for Field Archaeology and Edinburgh University excavated a Bronze Age kerb cairn, turf buildings, and the remains of shieling huts. The shielings, summer pastoral structures, had been reused continuously through medieval and post-medieval times, layered on top of much older Bronze Age remains. Peat-core analysis shows woodland declining slowly here since about 3200 BC, and continuous low-level human presence from roughly 2500 BC, with intensive grazing from 500 BC onwards. In 2005 another excavation revealed a burnt mound radiocarbon-dated to between 2550 and 1900 BC, the first such feature excavated in this part of the Highlands. Nobody knows what burnt mounds were for. The Arisaig mound did not solve the puzzle. The current best guesses are cooking pits, prehistoric saunas, or breweries. Possibly all three.

## From Norse to Clanranald

After Viking raids began in the 8th century, Arisaig became part of the Norwegian-controlled Kingdom of the Isles. In the late 11th century, Malcolm III of Scotland and Magnus Barelegs of Norway agreed in writing that the border would run along the coast: Arisaig thus became Scottish. In the early 12th century, the Norse-Gael warlord Somerled took control of the region. After his death in 1164, Norwegian sovereignty was nominally restored, but the lands containing Arisaig, known as Garmoran, were ruled in practice by his MacRory descendants. After the 1266 Treaty of Perth, Garmoran became a Scottish crown dependency. The final MacRory heir, Amy of Garmoran, married John of Islay, Lord of the Isles. John later divorced her in favour of a Stewart princess, but as compensation made their son Ranald the Lord of Garmoran. From Ranald descends Clanranald, who would dominate this coast for the next four centuries.

## Bonnie Prince Charlie's Last Boat

On 20 September 1746, six months after his army was destroyed at Culloden, Bonnie Prince Charlie boarded a French ship near Arisaig and sailed for France. The Prince's Cairn at Loch nan Uamh marks the spot. He had been on the run since April, hidden by tenants and lairds across the western Highlands at appalling personal risk. Among those who taught him Gaelic in the months before the rising was Alasdair Mac Mhaighstir Alasdair, also known as the Clanranald Bard, who would later become one of the two most important writers in Scottish Gaelic literature. Alasdair died at Arisaig in 1770. A gale prevented his body from being taken to Eilean Finnan for burial beside his parents. He was buried instead beside the ruins of Kil-Mael-Rubha Church in Arisaig, in an unmarked plot whose exact location is now lost. In 1927 a wall plaque was put up in the cemetery by Jacobite admirers in New Zealand.

## Cranstoun's Famine

In the 1820s, the financially extravagant 20th Chief of Clanranald, Ranald George, sold off almost all the traditional clan lands, including Arisaig. The new landlord, Lord Cranstoun, became notorious during the Highland Potato Famine for evicting more Arisaig tenants than any other local proprietor. He refused to bring relief to starving families. He refused even to pay his own servants: a Commons debate in February 1847 recorded that he had seventeen servants on the edge of starvation. When the government urged estate owners to provide road or drainage work to give destitute families employment, Cranstoun reluctantly accepted but refused to provide tools. Many Arisaig families emigrated to Nova Scotia, where in 1785 they founded their own Arisaig in Antigonish County. The remaining population stayed Gaelic-speaking and Roman Catholic, members of the once-illegal Catholic Church in Scotland. The Land, Sea and Islands Centre in the village today tells the SOE story but also remembers the famine and the Clearances that emptied the glens.

## Flight Context

Arisaig is on the west coast of Lochaber at 56.9110 degrees north, 5.8425 degrees west. From the air, the village appears as a small cluster of buildings overlooking a sheltered bay, with the A830 and West Highland Line running through it. Recommended viewing altitude 2,500-4,000 feet to see the village, the bay, Loch nan Uamh to the southeast, and the surrounding Rough Bounds. Visual landmarks include the Loch nan Uamh Viaduct, the ridge of Moidart to the south, and the Small Isles of Eigg and Muck to the west. Nearest airport is Oban (EGEO) about 40 nautical miles south. Glasgow (EGPF) is 90 nautical miles south. Arisaig House, the wartime SOE headquarters, sits on the edge of the village.
