# Plockton

A planned Highland village on Loch Carron where palm-like trees grow, Hamish Macbeth was filmed, and Gaelic remains stubbornly alive.

**Location:** 57.3380, -5.6523

**Categories:** village, scotland, highlands, wester-ross, gaelic, film-location

## Introduction

The cabbage trees on Harbour Street have no business being there. Cordyline australis is a New Zealand native, more at home in subtropical scrubland than on a Highland waterfront. But Plockton faces east, away from the prevailing westerly winds, and the North Atlantic Drift keeps the winters mild enough that the spiky fronds survive year after year. They have lined the village's main street since the 1960s, and they are part of why Plockton has been described - more than once, by writers who should perhaps know better - as having a subtropical appearance. At 57 degrees north. In the Scottish Highlands.

## Am Ploc

Old maritime charts spell it differently. MacKenzie's chart of 1776 marks the peninsula as Plack; Heather's of 1804 does the same. But the locally accepted root is Gaelic: the village stands on the Ploc of Lochalsh, ploc meaning a pimple or a bump. The same word turns up in other place-names along the coast - the Plock of Kyle, Plocrapool on Harris. Until the end of the eighteenth century the fishing hamlet here was simply Am Ploc, the blunt promontory. When the planned village rose around 1800, English speakers added -ton, and the awkward but accurate name we use today was born. A local laird funded the construction in the 1810s, turning displaced crofters into a fishing community on the edge of Loch Carron. Most of the houses date from the nineteenth and twentieth centuries; the bones of the place are nineteenth-century planning.

## Telford's Kirk

The village church was designed by Thomas Telford. The same Telford who built the Caledonian Canal and the Menai Suspension Bridge and so many of the roads, harbours and parliamentary kirks that knit Highland Scotland into one country in the nineteenth century. Plockton's kirk is small and white and modest by Telford's standards, but it is his work, and it still serves both the Church of Scotland and the Free Church of Scotland congregations. It is one of dozens of parliamentary churches Telford produced for remote Highland communities under the Church Building Act of 1824 - simple, cheap, beautiful in their austerity. Most are still standing. Most are still in use.

## Lochdubh on Screen

The BBC drama Hamish Macbeth ran from 1995 to 1997, starring Robert Carlyle as a Highland policeman in the fictional village of Lochdubh. Lochdubh did not exist; Plockton did, and Plockton was where the cameras went. Harbour Street, the moored boats, the cabbage trees, Duncraig Castle as backdrop - all became Lochdubh for three seasons. Plockton has been a film village longer than that, though. The 1973 cult horror The Wicker Man used local scenery, and so did several episodes of the Inspector Alleyn Mysteries. Long before any of them, painters were coming - Adam Bruce Thomson, David Macbeth Sutherland, and others from the Edinburgh School, drawn by the same light and the same shape of bay that the film crews would later find.

## Gaelic in the Schoolhouse

Plockton is home to Sgoil Chiùil na Gàidhealtachd - the National Centre of Excellence in Traditional Music - based at Plockton High School. Pupils come from across Scotland to learn fiddle, pipes, clarsach and Gaelic song under teachers who treat the tradition as living, not preserved. Since 1991 the primary school has run a Gaelic-medium education unit, where instruction is entirely through Gaelic. In the catchment area, 24.4 percent of the population report being able to speak the language - the highest incidence anywhere on mainland Scotland. Some of that owes a debt to one man: Sorley MacLean, the great Gaelic poet, was headmaster of Plockton High School between 1959 and 1972. He introduced Gaelic teaching to the school and championed shinty in the playground. His work is now considered among the most important Gaelic poetry of the twentieth century. He wrote much of it while looking out over Loch Carron.

## The Subtropical Highland

Climate statistics tell the story Plockton sells. The Met Office keeps a weather station here with thirty-year averages on record. The highest temperature ever recorded was 27.7 degrees C on 9 May 2016; the lowest, -9.8 degrees C. Cool summers, mild winters, the maritime climate of the British Isles slowed down and gentled by the North Atlantic Drift. Most of the land around the village belongs to the Balmacara Estate, owned by the National Trust for Scotland - which means the view of the village from across the loch is, more or less, the view our grandchildren will see. The cabbage trees may yet die in some hard winter we have not yet had. But until then, they will keep startling visitors who expected something less green.

## Flight Context

Plockton sits at 57.34 degrees north, 5.65 degrees west, on the north shore of Loch Carron near where it joins the Inner Sound. From the air the village reads as a tight crescent of white cottages along the bay, with The Plock headland to the west and Duncraig Castle visible on the east shore. Plockton Airfield, operated by Highland Council, handles light aircraft and microlights only - no fuel, very short runway. Larger options: Inverness (EGPE) 70 nm east-northeast, Stornoway (EGPO) on Lewis 75 nm northwest. Skye and the Cuillin are visible to the southwest. Recommended viewing altitude 1,500-3,000 ft AGL. Weather changes fast - sunshine to driving rain in twenty minutes is normal.
