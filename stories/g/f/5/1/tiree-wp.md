# Tiree

From an RAF weather station whose observations helped shift D-Day to a Lewisian gneiss bedrock two billion years old: the Hawaii of the North, on Britain's farthest sunlit edge.

**Location:** 56.5056, -6.8781

**Categories:** inner hebrides, scotland, islands, world war II, raf, windsurfing

## Introduction

On 4 June 1944, Group Captain James Stagg told Dwight Eisenhower to wait. The invasion of Normandy could not launch on the morning of 5 June. The Atlantic weather front would be too rough. Wait one day, Stagg said, and there would be a window. Some of the readings that built Stagg's case had come from 518 Squadron, flying meteorological missions out of an RAF station on a low Hebridean island so far west it sat closer to Labrador's latitude than to London. Tiree's weather went into the calculation. The invasion landed on 6 June 1944.

## The Hawaii of the North

Tiree is the most westerly island of the Inner Hebrides, low, fertile, and exposed to whatever the Atlantic brings. It is sometimes called the Hawaii of the North, which sounds absurd until you understand the call is about wind, not warmth. The island runs flat enough that storms cross it unobstructed, the highest point being Ben Hynish at 141 metres. That same flatness combined with reliable westerlies makes Tiree one of the great windsurfing venues in Europe. The Tiree Wave Classic has been held here most years since the 1980s, and the Corona Extra PWA World Cup Finals came to the island in 2007. Colonsay and Tiree share a quirk of climate: the highest spring and early-summer sunshine totals in the United Kingdom. The same exposed maritime moderation that keeps snow and frost almost unknown also keeps summer temperatures cool. The lowest reading on the modern record fell during the December 2010 cold spell.

## Two Billion Years of Bedrock

Walk the western beaches and you are walking on some of the oldest rock in Europe. Tiree's bedrock is Lewisian gneiss, metamorphosed from Archaean and early Proterozoic time, the same complex that floors much of the Outer Hebrides. Archaean granite outcrops locally. Younger dykes of dolerite, felsite, lamprophyre and diorite cut the older rock in places. Above all this lies the machair: a band of low-lying grassland enriched by shell sand blown inland from the beaches, sustaining a kind of crofting agriculture impossible on most Hebridean islands. Upper Palaeolithic stone tools of the Ahrensburgian culture, found here, push human presence on Tiree back to the end of the last Ice Age. The 1st-century Dùn Mòr broch survives as a tower ruin two metres high. Adomnán, who served as abbot of Iona from 679 to 704, recorded several stories of St Columba and Tiree, including a monastery on the island called Artchain. The island's name, Tìr Iodh, means land of corn. Tiree provisioned Iona with grain.

## RAF Tiree and the D-Day Decision

A large Royal Air Force station was built on Tiree during the Second World War. Three radar stations operated on the island, plus the airfield itself. 518 Squadron flew long meteorological reconnaissance missions far out into the Atlantic, recording pressure, temperature and wind at altitude in conditions that often killed the aircraft attempting it. The data fed into the forecasts that informed Stagg's recommendation to Eisenhower. The airfield became Tiree Airport in 1947, and Loganair now flies it daily to Glasgow. About 38 per cent of the population still spoke Scottish Gaelic in 2011, the highest proportion in the Inner Hebrides. The Tiree Music Festival has been held every summer since 2010 on the fields beside the community hall An Talla. Enya mentions the island in Orinoco Flow. Hamish Hawk recorded Calls to Tiree. And in January 2025 the Tyree Gin distillery released its first single-malt whisky, the first legal Tiree whisky in over 200 years.

## Flight Context

Tiree lies at 56.51 N, 6.88 W, 7,834 hectares low and flat in the open Atlantic. Tiree Airport (EGPU) at Crossapol, runway 11/29, served by Loganair from Glasgow daily. Recommended viewing altitude 2,000-4,000 ft to take in the whole island. Visual landmarks: Ben Hynish (141 m) with its civil-radar dome at the south end; Skerryvore lighthouse 12 mi southwest; Gott Bay on the east coast where the ferry lands; the windsurfing beaches at Balevullin and Crossapol. The 950 kW Tilley community wind turbine stands inland. Weather here is notorious; even calm days carry a steady breeze.
