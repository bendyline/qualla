# Portree

The capital of Skye, built around a Thomas Telford pier on a cliff-fringed harbour, with archaeological traces of human occupation reaching back 4,500 years.

**Location:** 57.4131, -6.1936

**Categories:** scottish-highlands, isle-of-skye, town, scotland, harbour

## Introduction

Long before there was a town here, there were people. CFA Archaeology dug into the ground at Portree in 2006 and 2007, ahead of a housing development, and found radiocarbon dates running from 2570 BC to 1400 AD. Stone tools suggest the area was used in the Late Mesolithic, perhaps 6,000 years ago. The bay, sheltered from the Atlantic by cliffs on three sides, has always been a good place for humans to put themselves. Today Portree is Skye's capital, population around 2,500, with a Thomas Telford-designed harbour pier and a long history of being whatever the wider world needed it to be: trading post, naval refuge, fishing harbour, Highland refuge for a hunted prince, and now the tourism engine of Skye.

## What's in a name

The name Portree is the Anglicized form of Port Rìgh, which translates from Gaelic as King's Port. The traditional explanation is that the name dates from a visit by James V of Scotland in 1540, when the king toured the Hebrides imposing royal authority on the often-rebellious clan chiefs. The trouble with that story is that James was bringing warships and threats rather than goodwill, and naming a harbour in his honour would have been an unusual response. Some scholars now think the older form was Port Ruighe(adh), meaning slope harbour, a straightforward description of the steep land around the bay. Whatever its origin, the modern name took hold; the settlement was Kiltaraglen, the church of St Talarican, before the 16th century, and that name is now used only for one of the streets.

## Telford's pier and the harbour

The pier at Portree was designed by Thomas Telford, the great Scottish civil engineer who built so much of the country's infrastructure in the early 19th century: roads, bridges, canals, the Caledonian Canal, harbour works the length of the coast. Portree's pier was part of his Highland improvement plan, an attempt to give the Highlands the infrastructure of fishing and trade that might lift the chronically poor west coast economy. The cliffs ringing the bay made it a natural shelter; Telford's pier made it functional. The Aros Centre, now repurposed as the Isle of Skye Candle Co. Visitor Centre, sits above the south side of the harbour, celebrating Gaelic heritage. Around 939 people in the parish, just over 37 per cent, can speak Scottish Gaelic; Portree High School and Portree Gaelic Primary School both teach in the language.

## Tourism, transformed

Portree was a fishing harbour and a regional service centre for most of its modern history. Tourism existed but stayed manageable until the Skye Bridge opened in 1995. After that, visitor numbers climbed steeply. In 2016, more than 150,000 people stopped at the VisitScotland centre alone, and a 2020 report estimated visitors added 211 million pounds a year to Skye's economy before the pandemic. Conde Nast Traveler named Portree one of the 20 most beautiful villages in the UK and Ireland. Rick Steves called it Skye's best home base. The result is a town that in July and August can feel besieged: police have, in some years, turned cars back on the A87 if travellers had not booked accommodation. The 19th-century Skye Woollen Mill on Mill Road closed in 2000 and the building has sat empty since. Some of the outbuildings have been converted to a bakery and an art gallery. The pre-tourism economy is gone; the post-tourism one has not yet been figured out.

## Mountains, shinty, and Portree shale

Portree sits at the foot of a steep wooded hill, sheltered from the Atlantic gales, with the Sound of Raasay opening east toward the long thin shape of Raasay itself. North of town the A855 leads past Achachork, Staffin and the Storr to the landslip terrain of the Quiraing, the most photographed landscape on Skye. The Cuillin mountains rise south. The town hosts Skye Camanachd, the island's shinty club, who play in the Mowi Premiership, the top tier of the sport; their ground at Pairc nan Laoch above the town is one of the world's most scenically situated playing fields. Two amateur football teams play in the local league. Geologically, Portree gave its name to Portree shale, a Jurassic formation associated with potential petroleum deposits, though no commercial drilling has ever taken place. The climate, in the Köppen classification, is Cfb: oceanic, mild, very wet, with around 1700 mm of rain a year and not many days without weather of some kind.

## Flight Context

Town position: 57.41 N, 6.19 W on Skye's east coast at the head of Loch Portree. Approximately 35 nm north of the Skye Bridge by road. Nearest airstrip is Broadford about 18 nm south; nearest commercial airport Inverness (EGPE) is about 75 nm east-southeast. Visual landmarks include the cliff-fringed harbour with its painted waterfront, The Lump and Apothecary Tower just south, the Cuillin Hills rising 7 nm southwest with peaks over 900 m, and the Trotternish landslip running 18 nm north past the Old Man of Storr to the Quiraing.
