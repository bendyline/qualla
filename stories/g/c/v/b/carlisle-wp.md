# Carlisle

The most besieged place in the British Isles - a Roman garrison that became a Norman castle town, a Reiver stronghold, a Jacobite trophy, and the largest cotton-mill city in Cumberland, all within walls that still half-stand on a slight rise above the Eden.

**Location:** 54.8900, -2.9300

**Categories:** Cities in Cumbria, Roman Britain, Border Reivers, Medieval cities of England, Anglo-Scottish frontier

## Introduction

Carlisle has been besieged at least ten times. That is not a tourist board's exaggeration; it is the count historians settle on when they total up the Scottish raids, English counter-raids, Civil War reductions and the brief Jacobite occupation of 1745. The Romans built the first fort here in the winter of AD 73, on the bluff where Carlisle Castle now stands, overlooking the confluence of the Caldew and Eden rivers. They called the place Luguvalium - from a Brittonic name meaning roughly 'stronghold of Lugus,' a Celtic god of strength. Two thousand years later, the city is England's most northerly cathedral seat, the only city in Cumbria, the home of about 74,000 people, and a place that has spent more of its life being fought over than at peace.

## Luguvalium and the Carvetii

The earliest recorded inhabitants of the area were the Carvetii, a Brittonic tribe whose territory covered ancient Cumbria and north Lancashire. Boethius and John of Fordun, writing centuries later, claimed Carlisle existed before the Roman arrival and was one of the strongest British towns of its day, supposedly burned down in the time of Nero. The hard archaeological evidence begins with the Roman timber fort excavated along Annetwell Street in the 1970s and dated to AD 73. The fort was rebuilt in oak in AD 83 and garrisoned by a cavalry regiment, the Ala Gallorum Sebosiana. By the late sixth century the place was the seat of a Brittonic kingdom of Rheged, ruled by King Urien and his son Owain - figures who later disappeared into Arthurian legend. Rheged passed under Northumbrian control before 730, probably by the marriage of Rienmelth, daughter of Royth, to Oswy of Northumbria. In 685 Saint Cuthbert visited the queen here and was taken to see the city walls and a 'marvellously constructed' Roman fountain still flowing in the streets.

## William Rufus and a Stone Castle

By the Norman Conquest in 1066 Carlisle was in Scottish hands and is not recorded in Domesday Book. That changed in 1092 when William the Conqueror's son William Rufus invaded and absorbed the region into England. Carlisle Castle was begun in 1092 on the Roman fort site and rebuilt in stone in 1112, with a keep and the line of city walls and three gates - the Irish or Caldew Gate, the English or Botcher Gate, and the Scotch or Ricker Gate. The gate names live on as street names. The cathedral was founded as an Augustinian priory shortly afterwards and raised to cathedral status in 1133 with the new Diocese of Carlisle. The county of Carliol was created in 1157 and renamed Cumberland in 1177. For the next four hundred years Carlisle changed hands repeatedly between Scotland and England, and the people on either side of the border lived under a particular kind of insecurity: armies came through in formal war, and between formal wars came the Border Reivers.

## Reivers and a 1,069-Word Curse

The Reivers were extended border families - some Scottish, some English, often related across the line - who lived by raid and counter-raid through the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries. They stole cattle, took hostages, burned barns and disappeared into the hills. They so plagued both governments that in 1525 the Archbishop of Glasgow, Gavin Dunbar, wrote a 1,069-word formal curse against them, calling down ruin on every part of every reiver's body 'from the top of their head to the soles of their feet, before and behind, within and without.' Henry VIII commissioned the engineer Stefan von Haschenperg in 1539 to modernise Carlisle's defences; von Haschenperg's castle towers were converted to artillery platforms and his Citadel - a Tudor fortification with two massive cannon towers - replaced the medieval Botcher Gate at the south of the city by 1541. He was sacked in 1543 for having 'spent great treasures to no purpose.' The reiving age finally faded after the union of crowns in 1603, when James VI and I could pursue cross-border bandits as a single sovereign. Many were hanged. Whole families were transported to Ireland. By 1681 the problem was deemed solved.

## Sieges, Jacobites, and a Cotton Boom

The castle should have been obsolete after 1603. It was not. In 1639, with war between the two kingdoms looming again, it was refortified using stone from the cathedral cloisters - the same cloisters the Scottish Presbyterian Army would later finish demolishing in 1646 to fortify the same castle. The tenth and last siege came in November 1745. Charles Edward Stuart, leading the second Jacobite Rising, took the city, garrisoned its castle with four hundred men, and pushed south as far as Derby before turning back. Ten days after the Jacobites left, Prince William, Duke of Cumberland recaptured the castle and executed over thirty Jacobite prisoners at Harraby Hill outside the city. The eighteenth and nineteenth centuries traded war for industry. Carr's of Carlisle, Kangol, Metal Box and the railway engineering firm Cowans Sheldon built large works in Denton Holme, Caldewgate and Wapping. Shaddon Mill in Denton Holme had the world's eighth-tallest chimney and was the largest cotton mill in England. By the early twentieth century the city had a population of over 45,000, two electricity power stations, electric trams and its first cinema.

## Floods, Football, and a Border Identity

The modern century has been quieter, but not without its catastrophes. In January 2005 Carlisle's worst floods since 1822 closed every road into town and killed three people. In December 2015 the floods were worse still - nearly 36 hours of rain breached defences and submerged Bitts Park, Hardwicke Circus and Warwick Road; David Cameron flew in to see the damage. Through it all Carlisle United have played at Brunton Park on Warwick Road since 1909, briefly topping the English league at the start of the 1974-75 season after promotion to the top flight, and escaping relegation from the Football League on the final day of 1999 when on-loan goalkeeper Jimmy Glass scored an injury-time winner against Plymouth Argyle. The Border Regiment, headquartered at Carlisle Castle, fought in everything from the French and Indian War to Arnhem before its lineage was absorbed into the Duke of Lancaster's Regiment. Tullie House Museum keeps the longer story: Romans, Rheged, Reivers, mills, and the slow transformation of a fortress town into Cumbria's only city - still on the border, still red-sandstone, still smaller than most of England has noticed.

## Flight Context

Carlisle sits at 54.89 degrees north, 2.94 degrees west, on a slight rise at the confluence of the Eden, Caldew and Petteril rivers, ten miles south of the Anglo-Scottish border. From altitude the cluster of cathedral, castle and railway station within the line of the medieval walls is unmistakable on the north-west side of the modern city; the M6 and West Coast Main Line run north-south through the urban area, with the A69 striking east toward Newcastle along Hadrian's Wall. Carlisle Lake District Airport (EGNC) lies about 6 nautical miles east near Brampton, with light aviation and air freight only. Newcastle (EGNT) is roughly 50 nm east, Glasgow Prestwick (EGPK) about 60 nm north-west. Best appreciated from low altitude on a clear winter morning when the red sandstone of the castle and cathedral catches low sun and the Eden's meander shines silver to the north of the city.
