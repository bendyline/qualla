# Leith Walk

A defensive earthwork against Cromwell became one of Edinburgh's longest streets, lined with tenements, tram history, and a public gallows whose name supposedly gave the area its drink.

**Location:** 55.9636, -3.1785

**Categories:** streets, edinburgh, leith, transport-history, urban-history

## Introduction

Cromwell's army was coming. In 1650 the Scots under Sir David Leslie threw up an earthen rampart between Calton Hill and Leith to defend Edinburgh's northern approach. The rampart didn't save Scotland; Leslie's army was crushed at Dunbar months later. But the rampart stayed, and the broad causeway that ran alongside it became a footway, then a coach route, then the high street of a working city. Locals still call it the Walk, because that is what it always was. Walk it from end to end today and you cross 900 years of pedestrian history, much of it buried just below the tarmac.

## From Rampart to Causeway

In a charter of 1456, James II granted land at Greenside in the valley between Calton Hill and the road to Leith. Archaeologists working ahead of the Trams to Newhaven project recently uncovered a cobble-lined road thought to be between 900 and 600 years old. Defoe walked this way in 1706 and remembered it as a broad causeway, paved with stones like a street, at least twenty feet wide, kept up at the public expense and forbidden to horses. From 1722 a regular coach ran the route at 3d in summer and 4d in winter. By 1779, when the historian Hugo Arnot was counting, 156 coaches a day made the journey, four passengers each. Two and a half miles slightly downhill from the city centre to the port; an hour each way at a steady walking pace; a corridor of human movement that has not stopped since Cromwell looked at it and turned back.

## Shrubhill and the Gallow Lee

Just south of Pilrig was a place called the Gallow Lee, literally the field with the gallows. Five Covenanters were executed here in 1681: Robert Garnock, Patrick Forman, David Farrie, James Stuart, Alexander Russel. In 1670, Major Thomas Weir, who confessed to sex crimes and stood accused of witchcraft, was garroted and burnt at the stake at nearby Greenside. His remains were brought to the Gallow Lee and hung on display. The bodies of the condemned were buried at the foot of the gallows, or their ashes scattered. The piles of sand and bones were sold to builders working on the New Town. The proceeds, the story goes, went to buy shrub, a mix of rum and lemon, and that is supposedly how Shrubhill got its name. A drink bought with the leavings of the executed. Edinburgh has rarely been delicate about its history.

## The Pilrig Muddle

Trams arrived early on Leith Walk and stayed late. Leith ran Scotland's first electric tram service in 1905, six years before Edinburgh's own corporation got around to it. There was just one problem: Leith's tram terminated at Pilrig Church, where the burghs met. Edinburgh's trams were still cable-drawn. Passengers had to disembark and change vehicles in the middle of the street. The locals called it the Pilrig muddle. Edinburgh took over Leith's trams in 1920 but did not unify the system until electrification in 1925. The whole network ran until November 1956, terminating at the Shrubhill depot on its last night. Then trams vanished from Edinburgh for almost seven decades. The line that was supposed to come back in 2014 stopped short at York Place after delays and cost overruns. Finally, on 7 June 2023, trams returned to Leith Walk after 67 years. A pair of Victorian-era cable wheels found during the excavations were installed at Iona Street as a monument to the original muddle.

## The Walk Today

Before the 1870s tenement boom, Leith Walk was largely rural, with mansion houses and plant nurseries lining its length. The east side had fewer mansions, which made the land cheaper to buy up for tenements, which is why the two sides of the street still feel different. McDonald Road Library, opened in 1902 in a Baronial Renaissance style by H Ramsay Taylor, is arguably the finest building on the Walk; it was originally a Nelson Hall funded by the publisher Thomas Nelson. At the Foot of the Walk, the old Leith Central Station building survives, its Central Bar decorated floor to ceiling with over 250,000 Staffordshire Potteries tiles, mostly by Minton, Hollins & Co. The Bier Hoose, formerly the Boundary Bar, sits on the old burgh line; before the 1920 merger of Leith and Edinburgh, drinkers used both doors because the magistrates set different closing times. Grace Cadell, one of Scotland's earliest female doctors and a leading suffragette, ran a refuge at 145 Leith Walk. The street keeps its layers.

## Flight Context

55.96 N, 3.18 W. Leith Walk runs roughly north-east from the eastern end of Princes Street down to the Foot of the Walk near the docks. From altitude, look for the green wedge of Calton Hill at the upper (south-west) end and the basin of Leith Harbour at the lower end; the Walk is the straight diagonal between them, about 2.5 km long. Edinburgh Airport (EGPH) is 9 km west of the city centre. Best viewing 2,000-3,000 ft. The Firth of Forth lies just beyond Leith; visibility often varies between landward Edinburgh and the seaward port.
