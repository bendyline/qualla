# Croydon

Once London's only commercial airport, once the summer palace of the Archbishops of Canterbury, now an 'edge city' of high-rise offices that has been quietly running south London for centuries.

**Location:** 51.3727, -0.1099

**Categories:** Areas of London, London Borough of Croydon, Towns in Surrey (historic), Aviation history of London

## Introduction

For four decades, every commercial airliner that flew into London landed at Croydon. Imperial Airways, KLM, Air France, Lufthansa — all of them, until the war, used a 1920s art deco terminal building on the southern edge of London as their gateway. Amy Johnson took off from here for Australia in 1930. The control tower at Croydon Airport was the first in the world to use the words 'Mayday, Mayday' as a distress call. The terminal still stands. It's surrounded now by a 1950s industrial estate. The runway is gone. Heathrow ate Croydon Airport in 1959 and the south London town that once welcomed the world's pilots became, for half a century, just another suburb.

## The Crocus and the Hub

The name probably comes from the Old English 'croh-denu', the crocus valley, suggesting the saffron crocus that grew here when the Romans were still walking down the road that became the A23. By the late Saxon period Croydon was the centre of a substantial estate held by the Archbishops of Canterbury — the church was probably a minster church, a base for clergy living in common, mentioned in a charter of King Coenwulf of Mercia and a will of around 960. Domesday Book recorded it in 1086 as Croindene, held by Archbishop Lanfranc himself: 16 hides and a virgate, a church, a mill worth five shillings, 38 plough-teams, eight acres of meadow, woodland for 200 hogs.

## The Archbishop's Palace

From the medieval period through to 1781, the archbishops kept Croydon Palace as their main summer residence — visited by monarchs, by ambassadors, by the cardinals and chancellors who shaped late-medieval England. The palace had grown by the sixteenth century into a substantial complex, but the slums and stagnant ponds around it eventually drove the archbishops out. They sold it in 1781 and moved to Addington Palace nearby. Old Palace School occupied the buildings from 1889 until 2025; many of the original palace fabric survives behind the school walls, including the great hall and the audience chamber where Elizabeth I once dined. In 1276 Archbishop Robert Kilwardby obtained a charter for a weekly market, and that decision turned Croydon from an estate village into one of the principal market towns of north-east Surrey.

## Whitgift's Almshouses

Archbishop John Whitgift petitioned Elizabeth I in the 1590s for permission to found a hospital and school in Croydon for what the document calls 'poor, needy and impotent people' from the parishes of Croydon and Lambeth. The Queen agreed. The foundation stone of the Hospital of the Holy Trinity went down in 1596 and the building opened in 1599: a courtyard of timber-framed lodgings around a central quadrangle, with a schoolhouse and a warden's house attached. Sixty-three almoners could live there. Four hundred and twenty-six years later the almshouses are still in use, still housing residents, still tucked into the corner of North End and George Street where the trams now cross beside what is the oldest functioning charitable institution in the borough.

## Croydon Airport

The land south of the town was open enough, by 1915, to be used as a wartime airfield. In 1920 it opened as London's main commercial airport, the country's only customs aerodrome handling international passenger flights. The art deco terminal, completed in 1928, was the first purpose-built airport terminal in the world. Imperial Airways' four-engined Handley Page biplanes carried passengers to Paris, to Brussels, to Cairo and Karachi. The Battle of Britain damaged the airfield badly in August 1940 — the first major Luftwaffe attack on a London airport killed 62 people on the ground. After the war, with the runway too short for the new generation of jets, Croydon could not compete. Heathrow took over. The last commercial flight, a De Havilland Heron, departed for Rotterdam on 30 September 1959. The terminal building survives as a Grade II listed museum, and from the air the rectangular footprint of the old aerodrome is still legible under the warehouses that filled it.

## Edge City

The post-war Croydon Corporation Act of 1956 turned a market town into something English planning had not really attempted before: a suburban office centre, the first 'edge city' in the country. Multi-storey blocks went up through the 1960s. Underpasses and flyovers cut the medieval street pattern apart. Tower cranes hung over what had been the archbishop's lands. By the 1990s Croydon held more office floor space than most British city centres, and a skyline that resembled, in miniature, Frankfurt or Houston — defiantly modern, controversial, and unloved. The trams returned in 2000 after a century's absence, the Westfield redevelopment was promised and stalled and promised again, and the borough remains what it has always been: a place that runs south London while London proper looks the other way.

## Pablo Fanque's Grand Fete

On 16 September 1834, the Royal Beulah Spa and Gardens hosted a 'Grand Scottish Fete' that included a tightrope performance by Pablo Fanque, the Black circus performer who would go on to dominate Victorian circus and, a century and a quarter later, be immortalised in the Beatles' 'Being for the Benefit of Mr. Kite!'. The spa, built in 1831 by Decimus Burton around a chalybeate spring on Beulah Hill, was a fashionable society venue until the Crystal Palace opened nearby in 1854 and the crowds moved on. Croydon was where you stopped being a market town and started being a leisure destination, then stopped being a leisure destination and started being a transport hub, then stopped being a transport hub and started being whatever it is now. Every layer is still there, if you know where to look.

## Flight Context

Croydon sits at 51.37N, 0.11W, about ten miles south of Charing Cross. The site of the old Croydon Airport at Roundshaw is identifiable from the air as the rectangular industrial estate on the south side of Purley Way; the Grade II listed terminal building stands at its northern corner. Biggin Hill (EGKB) lies about seven nautical miles east; Gatwick (EGKK) is twelve miles south. The Croydon skyline — taller than any other suburban centre in London — gives the town a distinctive silhouette from miles away. Recommended viewing altitude 2,000-3,000 ft AGL.
