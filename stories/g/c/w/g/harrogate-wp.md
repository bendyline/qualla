# Harrogate

An English spa town whose motto means 'a citadel famous for its springs' - and whose 88 mineral springs once made it the wellness capital of Britain.

**Location:** 53.9900, -1.5400

**Categories:** city, yorkshire, spa-town, victorian, english-history

## Introduction

Arx celebris fontibus. A citadel famous for its springs. Harrogate's town motto promises mineral water, and Harrogate delivered. By the Georgian era, this Yorkshire town was known across Europe as "The English Spa" - and the name was earned. Roughly 88 distinct springs were eventually identified within or near the town centre, each with its own subtly different mineral character. Iron-rich chalybeate water from one well. Sulphurous water from another. Saline waters elsewhere. The springs are mostly capped today, sealed beneath Victorian gardens, but the buildings remain - the Royal Pump Room, the Royal Baths, the Turkish Baths still steaming after 125 years. Harrogate's whole identity emerged from what bubbled up from the ground.

## Two Villages on a Royal Forest

Before Harrogate was Harrogate, there were two communities a mile apart: High Harrogate, which grew within the parish of Pannal, and Low Harrogate, which developed within the township of Bilton. Both lay inside the Royal Forest of Knaresborough, granted in 1372 by Edward III to his son John of Gaunt, Duke of Lancaster. The Duchy of Lancaster has been the principal landowner here ever since. In 1571, William Slingsby drank from the Tewit Well in High Harrogate and recognised what he had: chalybeate water like the springs at Spa in modern-day Belgium. Edmund Deane's 1626 book Spadacrene Anglica advertised the medicinal properties. By the 18th century, the two villages were drawing wealthy invalids from across Britain, and inns - the Queen's Head, the Granby, the Dragon, the World's End in High Harrogate; the Crown Hotel in Low Harrogate - had opened to receive them. The Forest of Knaresborough Inclosure Act of 1770 enclosed the medieval forest, but reserved 200 acres around the springs as a public common: The Stray, which still defines the town today.

## Samson Fox and the Captured Sunlight

In 1870, an engineering inventor named Samson Fox perfected a process for creating water gas - a fuel made by passing steam over heated coal - in the basement laboratory of Grove House. He built a trial plant at his home on Skipton Road, making it the first house in Yorkshire to have gas lighting and heating. Then he built a plant large enough to supply the whole town. When Harrogate's Parliament Street became the first thoroughfare in the world to be lit by water-gas, newspapers ran a memorable phrase: "Samson Fox has captured the sunlight for Harrogate." He donated the town's first fire engine, built the town's theatre, and served as mayor for three consecutive years from 1890 to 1892 - a record never matched. In 1893, a Harrogate doctor named George Oliver became the first person to observe the effect of adrenaline on the circulation, an experiment he conducted on himself and his young son. Harrogate's Victorian decades were extraordinarily productive.

## From Spa to Conference Capital

Like spa towns across Europe, Harrogate's popularity declined sharply after the First World War. The wealthy stopped "taking the waters"; the hotels stood empty. The Second World War rescued the town by accident - government offices evacuated from London filled the great Victorian hotels, demonstrating that Harrogate's combination of capacity, transport links, and pleasant surroundings made it ideal for business gatherings. After the war, the town rebuilt itself as a commercial, conference, and exhibition centre. Harrogate Convention Centre is now the third-largest fully integrated conference and exhibition complex in the UK and one of the largest in Europe. It brings in over £150 million annually and attracts more than 350,000 business visitors. The town also hosted the Eurovision Song Contest in 1982 and served as the finish line for Stage 1 of the 2014 Tour de France.

## The Town That Bottles Itself

Harrogate exports its identity. Yorkshire Tea - blended by Taylors of Harrogate - has become a national institution; the company also owns Betty's, the famous tearooms founded in 1919 by Frederick Belmont. Harrogate Spring Water bottles the local supply for international export. Farrah's makes Harrogate Toffee. Harrogate Blue cheese is produced nearby. RHS Harlow Carr - the Royal Horticultural Society's flagship northern garden - draws visitors year-round. The Mercer Art Gallery holds 2,000 works including pieces by William Powell Frith and Atkinson Grimshaw. The only Grade I listed building in town is St Wilfrid's on Duchy Road - designed by Temple Lushington Moore and often considered his masterpiece. And Fulwith Mill Lane is the most expensive residential street in Yorkshire, with average house prices of £1.9 million. The town that began as a place to drink unpleasant water has become, somehow, the centre of refined Yorkshire life.

## Flight Context

Harrogate stands at 53.99°N, 1.54°W in North Yorkshire, England, on the eastern edge of the Pennines. The nearest airport is Leeds Bradford (ICAO: EGNM), 10 miles south-west. From altitude, the town reads as a compact Victorian urban centre with The Stray - 200 acres of protected open parkland - wrapping the southern and western edges. Recommended cruising altitude 4,000-6,000 ft for clear ground identification. Look for the dome of the Royal Pump Room in central Harrogate and the distinctive Cenotaph on Prospect Square. Knaresborough lies 4 miles east, identifiable by its viaduct over the River Nidd. Leeds Bradford Airport sits 10 miles south-west; the runway runs east-west and is visible from cruising altitude.
