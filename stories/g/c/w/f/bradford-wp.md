# Bradford

The Woolopolis of the Industrial Revolution became the UK's first UNESCO City of Film and the 2025 City of Culture.

**Location:** 53.7940, -1.7510

**Categories:** city, industrial heritage, wool, culture, England

## Introduction

Saint Blaise, the patron saint of wool combers, still stands above the old Wool Exchange in Bradford, watching over a trade that is no longer there. He saw it all arrive in the 19th century, when worsted made this Pennine valley town the wool capital of the world. He saw it leave, mill by mill, as the 20th century wore on. Now he watches Bollywood premieres, mela festivals, and the cameras of the world's first UNESCO City of Film. Bradford has had to keep reinventing itself, and the saint above the exchange has had a front-row seat.

## The Broad Ford

The name is plain enough: brad and ford in Old English, the broad ford where travellers crossed Bradford Beck below the cathedral. In 1086 the Domesday clerks wrote it as Bradeford, and for centuries it stayed a small market town in the West Riding. Then came coal, soft water from the dales, and wool. By 1850 Bradford had 182,000 people, and over 200 factory chimneys poured black sulphurous smoke into a sky that rarely cleared. It was, by some measures, the most polluted town in England. Only 30 percent of children born to textile workers reached the age of fifteen, and life expectancy hovered just above eighteen years. The wool that clothed an empire was paid for in lungs and lives, mostly those of the very young.

## Worstedopolis

Bradford specialised in worsted, the smoother, finer cloth combed from long-staple wool. Titus Salt ran five mills here by 1850, then fled the squalor he had helped create, building his model village Saltaire down the valley in 1853. Henry Ripley's Bowling Dye Works employed over a thousand and was said to be the largest in Europe. Samuel Lister built Manningham Mills, whose chimney still rises above the city. German Jewish merchants set up textile export houses in a district they shaped into Little Germany, where Italianate warehouses still line the streets. Charles Semon became the first foreign and Jewish mayor of Bradford in 1864. Jacob Moser became the first Jewish Lord Mayor in 1910. They were merchants, philanthropists, civic builders, and they are part of why Bradford received its city charter from Queen Victoria in 1897.

## The Pals at the Somme

On 1 July 1916, the first day of the Battle of the Somme, the Bradford Pals climbed out of their trenches at Serre. Of the roughly 1,394 men from Bradford and District in the 16th and 18th Battalions of the West Yorkshire Regiment, 1,060 were killed or wounded in a single morning. The Pals battalions were a Kitchener-era idea: men who worked together, played football together, drank in the same pubs, would fight together. They also, it turned out, died together. Whole streets in Manningham, Bowling, and Wibsey received telegrams in the same week. The city has never fully looked away from that day. The memorials on Prince's Way and at the cathedral name the men, and the Telegraph and Argus still marks each anniversary.

## Fire at Valley Parade

On 11 May 1985, Bradford City was being promoted from the Third Division. The match against Lincoln was a celebration. Then smoke appeared under the wooden main stand at Valley Parade, and within minutes the whole roof was ablaze. Fifty-six people died: fathers, grandfathers, children, season-ticket holders who had come to cheer a championship. The fire reshaped British football: wooden stands disappeared, safety certificates tightened, the Popplewell Inquiry rewrote the rules. The Bradford disaster scarred a community already enduring the slow loss of its industrial heart, and the memorial in Centenary Square still draws fresh flowers each May. The city did not move on. It carried on, which is different.

## City of Film, City of Culture

Bradford has been making films since the Cieroscope was patented in Manningham in 1896. In June 2009 UNESCO designated Bradford the world's first City of Film. The National Science and Media Museum, the most visited museum outside London, sits in the centre with its IMAX, its Pictureville, and the Cottingley Fairies cameras downstairs. In 2022 the city won the UK City of Culture 2025 designation, beating Southampton, Wrexham and Durham, and £24.5 million was poured into City Park whose mirror pool throws up the tallest urban fountain in the UK. David Hockney grew up here. J. B. Priestley and Frederick Delius were born here. Zayn Malik went to school here. The Alhambra still stages West End musicals beneath its Edwardian dome. And the curry: six years running, Bradford has been crowned Curry Capital of Britain, a title earned not in restaurants alone but in food academies and charity work.

## Flight Context

Bradford lies at 53.79°N, 1.75°W in West Yorkshire, in the eastern foothills of the South Pennines. From the air, look for the Italianate clock tower of City Hall, the Lister Mills chimney rising above Manningham, and the green of Lister Park to the north of the centre. Leeds Bradford Airport (EGNM/LBA) sits 9 miles to the northeast at Yeadon, on the Leeds boundary. Leeds-Bradford is the West Yorkshire urban area's primary gateway. The M606 spur runs in from the south, joining the M62 motorway corridor.
