# Huddersfield

A West Yorkshire mill town that grew on Pennine water and Victorian wool money, became the birthplace of rugby league, and is the only English town to have outright bought itself from its aristocratic landlord.

**Location:** 53.6450, -1.7800

**Categories:** town, yorkshire, england, industrial-revolution, rugby-league-birthplace

## Introduction

In 1920, the people of Huddersfield bought their own town. For more than three centuries the Ramsden family had owned most of it, leasing land to the mills and merchants who grew rich on Pennine wool. When the Ramsdens decided to sell, the town corporation raised one and a third million pounds and took the freeholds for itself. The local press christened Huddersfield 'the town that bought itself,' and the name stuck. Few places in Britain can claim the same. Walk through St George's Square today and you are walking on land that belongs, in legal fact, to the people who walk on it.

## Iron Age Hill and Norman Field

Long before the wool money or the railway architects, there was a hill. Castle Hill, the great prominence southwest of the modern town centre, was the site of an Iron Age fort more than two thousand years ago. The Romans had a small fort to the west at Slack, near Outlane. The town's name first appears in the Domesday Book of 1086 as Oderesfelt, meaning Hudder's field, and it has carried that name in some form ever since. By Tudor times Huddersfield was a market town, with a market cross at its centre and a manor that passed by lease from the de Lacy family to the Crown and finally, in 1599, to William Ramsden. The Ramsden grip on the town would last 321 years.

## Luddites and the Mill Owner's Murder

When the Industrial Revolution mechanised the weaving trade, Huddersfield became a centre of resistance. Hand-loom weavers, watching their livelihoods disappear into the new mills, organised themselves into Luddites and began attacking the machines. One of the most notorious targets was Cartwright's Rawfolds Mill, owned by a man with a reputation for cruelty. The Crown stationed an army platoon in the town, at one point a thousand soldiers among ten thousand civilians. The campaign that broke the movement was triggered by a single act: in 1812 William Horsfall, a Huddersfield mill owner and aggressive prosecutor of Luddites, was shot dead. Parliament cracked down hard, but it also, eventually, began to introduce welfare provision for those put out of work and regulations to improve mill conditions. The dead millowner had inadvertently won his attackers a small measure of what they wanted.

## Prime Ministers and Founders' Bars

Two future Prime Ministers spent part of their childhoods in Huddersfield: Liberal H. H. Asquith and Labour's Harold Wilson, who was born in the town and attended Royds Hall School. A bronze Wilson stands at the front of the railway station, looking out toward Manchester. The station itself, completed in 1850 and designed by James Piggott Pritchett, has a colonnaded sandstone facade that Sir Nikolaus Pevsner called one of the best early railway stations in England, and that the poet John Betjeman declared the finest such facade in the country. It won a Europa Nostra award. But the most consequential building in the town's history may be a far quieter one. On 29 August 1895, a meeting in a room at the George Hotel on St George's Square broke the Rugby Football Union in two. The northern clubs that walked out, including Huddersfield's, founded what became rugby league. The room is still there, now the Founders Bar.

## The Town That Wool Built

Wool made Huddersfield. The soft Pennine water washed off mineral content that hard water carried, and the surrounding hills supplied the wool. By the late nineteenth century the town was a forest of mill chimneys, and the money funded everything else: Huddersfield Town Hall, the Choral Society founded in 1836, Beaumont Park opened by Prince Leopold in 1883, the great public library and art gallery with works by Bacon, Lowry and Henry Moore. Even after the textile economy contracted, the engineering and chemical industries that had grown up to support it stayed on. Cummins Turbo Technologies, founded as Holset in 1952, still makes turbochargers here. David Brown still makes gears. The University of Huddersfield is now the largest employer.

## Castle Hill and the View From Above

The Victoria Tower stands on Castle Hill, built in 1897 for Queen Victoria's Diamond Jubilee. From its summit on a clear day you can see the whole Colne and Holme valley system rolling away westward into the Peak District National Park, the green belt around the town shading into open moor. The Pennines were never neutral ground. They divided Lancashire from Yorkshire, separated mill economies that competed for the same markets, and shaped the dialect of the town below into something distinctly its own. Huddersfield's modern population pronounces its name without the initial H, a trait shared, oddly, with the Norman scribes who first wrote it down nine hundred years ago. The hill watches, as it has always watched, and the town gets on with whatever comes next.
