# Manchester

From Roman Mamucium to the world's first industrial city, Manchester's history reads as a sequence of accidents that became revolutions.

**Location:** 53.4667, -2.2333

**Categories:** Cities, Industrial Revolution, Northern England, Roman Britain

## Introduction

The Romans called it Mamucium, probably from a Brittonic word meaning 'breast', for the gentle hill above the confluence of the rivers Medlock and Irwell where they built their fort around AD 79. Recent scholarship suggests the word might instead come from mamma, 'mother', for a local river goddess. Either reading is appropriate for a city that has, for the better part of two thousand years, been generative. Manchester invented the industrial city. It then invented the inquiry into what the industrial city does to the people in it. It produced the Hacienda and Joy Division and Alan Turing's computers. Its population in 2024 stood at over 589,000, the centre of a 2.87 million-strong Greater Manchester Built-up Area, the second-largest metropolitan economy in the United Kingdom.

## Mamucium

The Roman fort at Mamucium guarded the road between the legionary fortresses at Chester and York, on a sandstone bluff above the Medlock-Irwell confluence. The civilian vicus that grew up around it had docks, workshops, and a small market. After the legions left, the settlement persisted but never grew significantly. Manchester remained a manorial township through the Middle Ages, holding a market charter from 1301 and a college from 1421 - now Chetham's School of Music and the Cathedral. Historically Manchester was a Lancashire town; the parts of the modern city south of the River Mersey were absorbed from Cheshire in the 20th century. The medieval town had a few thousand inhabitants. Then the steam engine arrived.

## Cottonopolis

By 1800, Manchester had become the world's first industrialised city. Cotton spinning, mechanised in Bolton in the 1770s, found in Manchester the unique combination it needed: damp climate that kept threads from breaking, soft water for processing, a navigable river system, the Bridgewater Canal bringing cheap coal from the Duke's mines at Worsley, and a labour force willing to flood the new mills. Within fifty years the city's population grew tenfold. Steam power, pioneered by Boulton and Watt, drove a factory built in Ancoats - the first steam-powered factory in the world. Trafford Park would later become the world's first industrial estate. The mills' damp, humid atmosphere produced 'Cottonopolis,' a nickname Manchester wore proudly until coal smoke literally blocked out the sun for days at a time. Friedrich Engels, working at his father's mill in Salford, walked these streets and wrote The Condition of the Working Class in England, the book Karl Marx would later cite by chapter and verse.

## Peterloo and the Politics

Manchester's political radicalism was already established before the Industrial Revolution amplified it. The city supported Parliament during the Civil War, hosted the Young Pretender's Jacobite forces in 1745, and by 1819 had become a centre for reform agitation. On 16 August of that year, cavalry charged a crowd of 60,000 gathered on St Peter's Fields to demand parliamentary reform; eighteen people died and hundreds were injured in what became known as the Peterloo Massacre, a name that mocked the British Army's recent victory at Waterloo. The shock changed British politics for decades. Manchester would later be the headquarters of the Anti-Corn Law League led by Richard Cobden and John Bright, the birthplace of the Cooperative movement just up the road in Rochdale, the home of the suffragettes (the Pankhursts lived on Nelson Street), and the city that hosted the 1868 founding of the Trades Union Congress. The Labour Party traces its lineage through these institutions.

## From Cotton to Code

By the early 20th century, the textile economy was already declining. Trafford Park diversified into cars (Ford built its first European plant here in 1911), engineering, and aerospace. During the Second World War, Avro Lancaster bombers and Rolls-Royce Merlin engines came out of Trafford Park, and the Manchester Blitz of December 1940 killed 684 people across two nights. After the war, the University of Manchester became the heart of British computing. In 1948, the 'Manchester Baby' became the world's first stored-program electronic computer. Alan Turing came to work here. Bernard Lovell built Jodrell Bank Observatory thirty miles south. The element vanadium was first isolated in a Manchester laboratory. The first commercial computer, the Ferranti Mark 1, was assembled in the city. None of this stopped Manchester's mid-century decline; the city centre population halved between 1931 and 1981. But the intellectual infrastructure that would power its later rebirth was being built.

## The Bomb and the Boom

On 15 June 1996, the Provisional IRA detonated a 1,500-kg truck bomb on Corporation Street. It was the largest IRA bomb ever set off on the British mainland. Phone warnings had emptied the immediate area; nobody died, but more than 200 were injured and roughly a quarter of the retail core was destroyed. Manchester used the rebuild to remake itself. Exchange Square, new public spaces, the rebuilt Marks & Spencer, the relocation of Selfridges, the Urbis building (now the National Football Museum), and the redevelopment of New Cathedral Street all date from this period. The 2002 Commonwealth Games accelerated the transformation of East Manchester around Sportcity. Salford Quays became MediaCityUK in 2011 when the BBC moved key programmes north. By the 2020s, Manchester was the second-largest economy in the UK and one of Europe's fastest-growing cities, attracting Hongkongers fleeing China's National Security Law, tech firms following the BBC, and tens of thousands of students each year.

## A City of Mancunians

The citizens have called themselves Mancunians since at least the 19th century, when the Latin Mancunium gave them a name. The accent leans more toward Liverpool's than to neighbouring Lancashire towns, a quirk of the Welsh and Irish population flows that built the city. Manchester is famously friendly; ask a stranger for directions and they will tell you. It is also famously proud, sometimes annoyingly so to outsiders. The city's musical output - Joy Division, the Smiths, Stone Roses, Happy Mondays, Oasis, Elbow, Doves, the entire Madchester scene - has outsized its population for forty years. So has its football: Manchester United at Old Trafford, Manchester City at the Etihad, both clubs with global followings that long ago left the geographical city behind. Manchester is, as the mosaic in the city centre puts it, what God created on the sixth day. It is also a city that has spent the seventh day arguing about it.

## Flight Context

Manchester sits at 53.4667°N, 2.2333°W in the basin where the Medlock joins the Irwell, hemmed by the Pennines to the east and the Cheshire Plain to the south. Recommended viewing altitude 2,000-4,000 ft AGL gives the best sense of the conurbation. From the air, look for the curving Manchester Ship Canal running east-west to Salford Quays, the Beetham Tower's needle silhouette on Deansgate, and the dense Victorian centre clustered around Albert Square. Nearest ICAO airports: Manchester (EGCC) 7 nm south-southwest, Manchester Barton (EGCB) 5 nm west, Liverpool John Lennon (EGGP) 25 nm west, Leeds Bradford (EGNM) 35 nm east-northeast. The Greater Manchester conurbation extends 15-20 nm in most directions. Weather can be marginal; high terrain to the east in the Peak District demands attention.
