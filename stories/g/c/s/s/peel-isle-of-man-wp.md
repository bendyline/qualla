# Peel

The Manx fishing port called the sunset city and the rose red city, built of red sandstone facing west across the Irish Sea, home to a Viking-age burial that produced grave goods unequalled in the British Isles.

**Location:** 54.2210, -4.6910

**Categories:** Coastal towns, Fishing ports, Norse heritage, Isle of Man, Medieval history

## Introduction

Peel has two nicknames, and both are accurate. The rose red city refers to the local sandstone, a reddish stone quarried from the cliffs around the harbour and built into nearly every old wall in town. The sunset city refers to the fact that Peel faces directly west across the Irish Sea, and there is nothing between its breakwater and County Down except open water. On a clear evening the Mourne Mountains of Northern Ireland appear in silhouette against the setting sun. The town is also where, in a Viking-age grave dug up in the 1980s, archaeologists found a necklace of beads traded from across Europe and the Mediterranean. No pagan Viking-age burial in the British Isles has produced grave goods of such high quality.

## Holmtown, Then Peeltown, Then Peel

The English name Peel means palisade or fortification, from Middle English peel or pele, ultimately from Latin palus, a stake. It is a shortened version of the earlier Peeltown, town of the fortification, in reference to Peel Castle. Before the English imposed that name, the settlement was Holmtown, from Old Norse holmr meaning island, a reference to St Patrick's Isle just offshore. The Manx name is Purt ny h-Inshey, harbour of the island. Peel is the island's second largest town after Ramsey, though it is the fourth largest settlement once Douglas (a city) and Onchan (a village) are counted. About 5,374 people live here. Until 2016 Peel had its own House of Keys constituency, electing one MHK; since September 2015 that seat has belonged to Ray Harmer, now part of a merged Glenfaba constituency.

## From Celtic Monks to Norse Kings

Peel's history is layered like the strata of its sandstone. There is evidence of Mesolithic settlers on both St Patrick's Isle and the nearby Peel Hill. Neolithic farmers are believed to have settled in the area. About AD 550 a Celtic monastery was founded on St Patrick's Isle. Excavations in the 1980s found a large early Christian burial ground with many burials dating from around the same time. Some later graves contained Norse burial goods, most famously the Pagan Lady, a woman buried in Christian style but with grave goods in the Norse tradition. Norsemen settled the harbour and used the Neb estuary to shelter their longships. In 1228, Olaf the Black, King of Mann and the Isles, beached his fleet here. His own half-brother Ragnald promptly attacked it and burned the ships. In 1266, after the Treaty of Perth, Norway's King Magnus VI ceded the Isle of Man to Scotland. English control followed in the 14th century.

## Fishing Town, Schooner Town

For most of the 19th century, Peel ran on fish. In the 1880s, fishing employed about 3,000 men and boys, with shipbuilding, kipper curing, and net-making providing hundreds of further jobs. Peel schooners traded across northwest Europe. Peel fishing boats followed herring down to the southern coast of Ireland and up to Shetland. Salted herring left by the cartload through the harbour, which was gradually improved, breakwater extended, lighthouse added. The numbers turned. Over-fishing thinned the catch. The 300 boats leaving Peel for Ireland in 1880 had shrunk to a handful by 1915. In 2005 a new floodgate was installed on the inner harbour to retain the waters of the Neb, allowing boats to float at low tide; before that they settled on the exposed seabed. Peel is still the most active fishing port on the Isle of Man, with a fish and shellfish processing industry and the traditional kipper-curing trade alongside fuel oil imports.

## The Pagan Lady, the Castle, and the Museum

Peel Castle is on St Patrick's Isle, a small island linked to Peel Hill by a causeway. Magnus Barelegs, King Magnus III of Norway, is thought to have started construction. William le Scrope, 1st Earl of Wiltshire, extended it in 1392. The Pagan Lady burial inside the castle grounds remains one of the most important Viking-age finds in the British Isles. Across the harbour the House of Manannan Museum, built in 1997 for £5.5 million partly in the old Peel railway station, covers the past and present of the island. It houses Odin's Raven, a two-thirds scale replica Viking longship built in Norway and sailed from there to Peel, arriving on 5 July 1979 to celebrate the millennium of the High Court of Tynwald. The Manx Transportation Museum, in the former brickworks building near the harbour, opened in 2002. The Leece Museum, in the Old Courthouse on East Quay since 2000, displays a large collection of TT and Manx Grand Prix bikes.

## Sport, Stars, and Sunset

Peel A.F.C., formed in 1888, is the most successful football club on the island: 31 league titles, 32 victories in the Manx FA Cup, and the first winners of the Isle of Man Football League in 1897. Peel Golf Club, founded in 1895, sits on Rheast Lane and has 18 holes totalling over 5,870 yards from competition tees. The Raad ny Foillan long-distance coastal footpath opened in 1986 and runs around the entire island, passing through Peel. Peel Castle and St Patrick's Isle together form a designated Dark Skies astronomy site, with low light pollution allowing the Milky Way and even the Northern Lights to be seen on rare clear nights. The seas off Peel are home to basking sharks in early summer, sometimes spotted from land but more often from boats. Seals favour the breakwater near the castle. And the sunsets, every clear evening, do their slow work over the Irish Sea.

## Flight Context

Peel sits at 54.221°N, 4.691°W on the west coast of the Isle of Man, at the mouth of the River Neb, in the historic parish of German. From 1,500 to 3,500 ft AGL the town is recognizable by its harbour, the rectangular bulk of Peel Castle on St Patrick's Isle to the immediate northwest, and the long sandy beach running south-southeast. Peel Hill rises immediately west of the river. The A1 road heads east-southeast toward Douglas (about 12 road miles), the A3 north toward Kirk Michael. Nearest airport is Isle of Man (EGNS) Ronaldsway, about 17 nm to the south-southeast. On clear days, Ireland is visible to the west, Scotland to the north.
