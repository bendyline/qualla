# Drummore

The southernmost village in Scotland, where a community is still trying to buy back the harbour their forebears built for lime ships.

**Location:** 54.6898, -4.8935

**Categories:** villages, harbours, scotland, ecclesiastical-history, extreme-points

## Introduction

Drummore translates from Gaelic as An Druim Mor - the great ridge. The hill it sits beneath is only 300 feet high, which is hardly grand by Scottish standards, and there are taller fells just west at Muntloch and Inshanks. So either the Gaelic was being generous with its adjectives, or the name refers to something else - perhaps the motte associated with the long-vanished castle of the Adairs of Kinhilt, granted these lands by King James VI in 1602. Either way, the village at the foot of the ridge has been here long enough to outlast both the castle and most of the families that owned it. Drummore is the southernmost village in Scotland, further south than the English cities of Durham and Carlisle, and it has been quietly minding its own business since long before there was a Scotland to be the southernmost of.

## Where Kildonan Meets the Sea

The village sits where the Kildonan Burn runs out into the bay, on the eastern shore of the Rhins of Galloway peninsula. Two satellite hamlets cling on - Kirkmaiden and Damnaglaur - each barely more than a clachan, the Gaelic word for a tiny cluster of houses with a stillness to it. Drummore proper lies in the parish of Kirkmaiden, named after St Maiden or Medan, one of the saints whose presence laced the Galloway coast in the centuries after Ninian arrived. The Mull of Galloway lies just 3 miles to the south, the actual end of mainland Scotland. Stranraer is 16 miles north, which is the nearest place a ferry can dock or a train can stop. In the 2011 census the population was 534, near enough to the village's rough average across the past century.

## A Gravestone for Ventidius

The southern Rhins was an area of early Christian activity, following the missionary work of Ninian across Luce Bay to the Machars in the late 4th century. Shortly before 1860, at Low Curghie just up the coast from Drummore - near an extant standing stone of indeterminate but considerable age - a gravestone surfaced from a field. The Latin inscription was weathered nearly to nothing, but two things were legible: the name Ventidius and a word that translated as 'sub-deacon'. A Christian, Latinate, presumably bilingual sub-deacon, dead and buried in the 5th or 6th century at the end of the Rhins, when Roman Britain was already memory. Place-names in the area testify to later Norse arrivals, which makes Drummore's Gaelic name - belonging to the long period of Gaelic Galloway that lasted until the Presbyterian 17th century - the third linguistic layer in the same patch of ground.

## The Disruption and Two Kirks

Parish life in Drummore has had its share of comings and goings. The original parish kirk of Kirkmaiden was some 5 miles south of the village - a long Sunday journey by any standard. In 1638 the parishioners successfully petitioned for a new church closer to home, and Kirk Covenant was built on Core Hill about a mile west of Drummore. Then came the Disruption of 1843, when more than 450 Church of Scotland ministers walked out over the question of who should appoint parish ministers, founding the Free Church of Scotland. A new Free Church kirk went up in Drummore itself, in the street now called Stair Street. By the early 20th century the two congregations had reunited. Worship now happens at the village church, with one service each summer month held at Kirk Covenant - a small acknowledgement that buildings carry their own kind of memory.

## The Harbour the Lime Built

Drummore's harbour faces north, sheltered by the Rhins from the prevailing southwesterly winds that batter the western side of the peninsula. A jetty went in during the early 19th century, built to serve a lime manufacturing industry that has long since vanished. For most of the 20th century the Ministry of Defence held the harbour, using it in connection with the bombing range and weapons-testing area still operating offshore in Luce Bay. In 2004 the MoD sold it for a nominal sum to the Drummore Harbour Trust Ltd, with the intention of developing it for pleasure boats. The transfer attracted attention in Parliament after disputes over fishermen's rights of access. A Court of Session judgement confirmed a Victorian precedent that no public right of harbour existed. The Trust's small membership and slow investment record caused concern. In 2015 it became liable for a £15,000 personal injury claim and dissolved itself, returning the harbour to the Crown Estate. The current effort is the Kirkmaiden Community Harbour Trust, trying to buy it back for the village.

## Three Bakers, Then None

Older residents recall Mill Street and Stair Street busy with shops - three separate bakers at one point, one of them on the corner. The bank is gone. The butcher's became wholesale-only after health regulations made the high-street trade impossible. The Harbour Stores, which once doubled as a fishing-supplies specialist, has closed. What remains is enough: the Mariners Coffee Shop, the Queen's Hotel, a post office and general shop on Mill Street, a volunteer tourist office, a garage for repairs, a doctor's surgery and pharmacy, the Ship Inn down on the shore. There is a bowling club and a children's play park. Two caravan sites sit near the sandy beaches. The Kirkmaiden Community Council meets monthly. After a landslip in the 1960s, the old Lower Road along the coast was barred to traffic and became a footpath - in summer 2014, somebody anonymously decorated it with fairy doors, a small Galloway grace note among the Japanese knotweed.

## Flight Context

Located at 54.69°N, 4.89°W on the eastern shore of the Rhins of Galloway peninsula, Scotland's southernmost village. Drummore harbour faces north and shelters from the prevailing southwesterly wind. The Mull of Galloway lighthouse is 3 miles south at the tip of the peninsula. Visible from cruising altitude in clear weather. Nearest airports: West Freugh (EGOY) about 12 nm north, with Prestwick (EGPK) on the Ayrshire coast for any longer leg. The MoD Luce Bay range sits immediately east of the village and is normally active - check NOTAMs carefully before flight planning. Cruise around 2,500-4,500 ft along the A716 corridor for the best view of Luce Bay, the Machars peninsula to the east, the Mull of Galloway south, and Northern Ireland west across the North Channel.
