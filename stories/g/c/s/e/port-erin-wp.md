# Port Erin

A south-west Manx seaside village with a sandy bay, a failed Victorian breakwater under its waters, and a long association with marine biology and motor racing.

**Location:** 54.0860, -4.7560

**Categories:** village, isle-of-man, seaside, history, marine-biology

## Introduction

At low tide in Port Erin Bay you can see the broken stones lying just under the surface like vertebrae. They are what is left of a breakwater the Victorians spent two decades trying to build, beginning in 1864, designed to turn a small Manx fishing harbour into a great Irish Sea port. Storms ate it. By the 1880s the project had been abandoned, the harbour entrance left unprotected, and the village had to find some other reason to exist. It found three: tourists, scientists, and the steam railway. Port Erin spent the next century becoming a seaside resort, hosting a famous marine biological station, and acquiring as a sometime resident the Formula One champion Nigel Mansell. Each layer is still visible. None of them is the whole story.

## The Name and the Bay

The English name Port Erin comes from Gaelic port Éireann, the Irish port, suggesting the bay's old role as the closest landfall on Mann for boats coming from Ireland. The Manx version Purt Çhiarn, Lord's Port, comes from a sound-change in the Manx Gaelic that confused the original Éireann with the existing word çhiarn, lord. Both names are still used; the bilingual signs at the railway station read Purt Çhiarn / Port Erin. The bay is bounded by two headlands which (on a windy day) funnel the prevailing south-westerlies straight into the village, and (on a calm one) trap the sun and make the village feel ten degrees warmer than the rest of the island. Bradda Glen, one of the Manx National Glens, runs down to the bay from the north, with the iconic Milner's Tower on top of Bradda Head, a memorial in the shape of a lock and key for the Victorian safe-maker William Milner, who funded much of the village's nineteenth-century building.

## The Breakwater That Failed

Begun in 1864 to turn Port Erin into a refuge harbour for the Irish Sea trade, the breakwater was an engineering ambition typical of the age: a great stone arm thrown across the mouth of the bay. By 1876 it was largely complete. By 1884 the sea had broken it apart. The submerged remains are still there, a hazard to fishing boats and a perpetual reminder that the bay was perhaps not meant to be a port. Without a sheltered harbour, Port Erin's commercial fishing never grew the way Port St Mary's did. The village shifted its energies into tourism and into science. The disused fish hatchery building on the outer harbour later became home to the University of Liverpool's Marine Biological Station, founded as the Port Erin Marine Biological Station, which spent over a century studying the ecology of the Irish Sea before closing in 2006. The building still stands, its scientific equipment removed, the laboratory windows looking out at the same water where the breakwater stones disappear at high tide.

## Hotels, Resort, Decline

From Victorian times to the 1960s, Port Erin was a holiday resort. The promenade is lined with grand hotels built in the 1880s and 1890s for visitors from Lancashire and the North-West, who came over by steamer from Liverpool. The hotels still stand, though many have now been quietly converted into flats as the British seaside holiday gave way to the package tour. The village was twinned with the small wine-growing commune of Latour-de-France, in the Pyrenees, an unlikely partnership that owed something to a mid-twentieth-century cultural exchange and quite a lot to local fondness for red wine. At the 2021 census the population stood at 3,730, making Port Erin the largest settlement by population in the south of the island, larger than Castletown, though Castletown holds the formal status of a town.

## Famous Residents

Nigel Mansell, the 1992 Formula One world champion, lived on the headland north of the bay through most of his racing career, leaving in 1995. The photographer Leonard McCombe (1923-2015), who took some of Life magazine's defining American portraits in the 1940s and 1950s, grew up in Port Erin. The travel writer and big-game hunter Agnes Herbert (1873-1960), one of the more startling Edwardian women, also grew up here. Brianne West, the New Zealand environmentalist and founder of plastic-free toiletries company Ethique, was born here. Ruth Keggin, the contemporary singer-songwriter who has done more than anyone of her generation to put Manx Gaelic song back on the cultural map, lives and works in the village. The film industry has come here too: scenes from the 2006 film Stormbreaker, featuring Ewan McGregor, were shot on the Isle of Man, and BBC's The Ginger Tree was filmed at the railway station in 1989.

## The Village Today

Port Erin remains a small village of one square mile, governed by its own board of Commissioners. Recent residential expansion has pushed it into contiguity with Port St Mary, two miles south-east, and in July 2018 Tynwald authorised a public enquiry into expanding the district boundary to take in some of the new building. The promenade still works as a promenade: people walk it morning and evening, the sun sets over the bay (the spectacular sunsets over Bradda Head are a local boast), and on the clearest days the Mourne Mountains of Northern Ireland are visible on the western horizon. The Lifeboat Station on the south side of the bay, the steam railway in the centre of the village, and the seven churches scattered across the small district between them tell most of the village's story. The rest is in the broken stones under the bay.

## Flight Context

Port Erin lies at 54.086 degrees north, 4.756 degrees west, on the south-west coast of the Isle of Man in the historic parish of Rushen. Best viewed at 1,500 to 2,500 feet on a coastal pass from the south-west, with the white sand of Port Erin Bay enclosed by Bradda Head (382 feet, Milner's Tower visible on top) to the north and Mull Hill to the south. The submerged remnants of the failed 1860s breakwater are visible at low tide. Nearest airport is Ronaldsway (ICAO: EGNS), about six miles to the east. The Mourne Mountains of Northern Ireland often appear on the western horizon on clear days.
