# Calf of Man

A 618-acre island off the southwest tip of the Isle of Man, where Manx shearwaters take their scientific name from these waters - and where a generation of conservationists has fought to bring them back.

**Location:** 54.0528, -4.8203

**Categories:** Islands of the Isle of Man, Bird sanctuaries, Lighthouses in the Isle of Man, Manx National Heritage sites

## Introduction

The Manx shearwater is named for these cliffs. In the eighteenth century, the seabird the world calls Puffinus puffinus was so abundant on the Calf of Man that more than thirty thousand pairs nested in burrows across the island's slopes - the largest colony in the world. Then, sometime around 1781, a shipwreck dumped brown rats onto the island. By 1800, the colony was effectively gone. The shearwaters held on in tiny numbers in the most defensible burrows, and for two centuries waited. The story of the Calf of Man in the modern era is, in significant measure, the story of getting the rats back off the island and watching what the birds do next.

## A Calf Beside Its Mother

The name comes from Old Norse. Kalfr meant an islet lying just off a larger isle - the calf following the cow - and the Calf of Man sits exactly there, separated from the southwest tip of the Isle of Man by the narrow turbulent gap of the Calf Sound. The island is 618 acres, roughly a mile across in any direction, part of the historic parish of Rushen. It rises to an unnamed peak in the west. Two short streams cross it. Two minor unfenced roads connect Cow Harbour on the north with South Harbour. Two seasonal inhabitants live here - the bird wardens who staff the observatory from spring to autumn. Around the Calf cluster smaller pieces of geography: Kitterland, the bifurcated islet in the Calf Sound; The Burroo and The Stack just offshore; The Puddle, a small bay on the southern coast; and a mile to the southwest, Chicken Rock - the most southerly territory of the Isle of Man.

## How It Became a Reserve

Until 1939 the Calf was privately owned by the Keig family - they grazed sheep on it, ran fishing operations from its harbours, and left the bird life largely to its own devices. That year Mr F. J. Dickens of Silverdale in Lancashire bought the island and immediately gave it to the National Trust, on the condition that it become a bird sanctuary. In 1951 the Manx Museum & National Trust - later renamed Manx National Heritage - was founded, and rented the Calf from the National Trust for one pound a year. In 1986 ownership was formally transferred to Manx National Heritage, which retains it today. Since 2006 the day-to-day wardenship has been contracted to the Manx Wildlife Trust, whose summer staff monitor breeding birds, ring migrants, count grey seals, tally butterflies and moths, and run the small observatory accommodation.

## The Bird Observatory

Since 1959 the Calf has been a recognised bird observatory - one of the official ringing and migration-monitoring stations in the British and Irish network. The observatory is housed in a modest building with self-catering accommodation for up to eight visitors, bookable through Manx National Heritage. Volunteers and ornithologists come for days or weeks to help with the work: trapping and ringing birds, monitoring breeding success, watching for migrant rarities passing through on their way along the Irish Sea flyway. The observatory's records form one of the longer continuous migration datasets in the British Isles, and have been crucial to understanding the population dynamics of the Manx shearwater colony and the impact of the rat eradication programme.

## Lighthouses and Lighthouse Engineers

The Calf and its offshore rocks have hosted no fewer than four lighthouses across two centuries of engineering. In 1818, Robert Stevenson - founder of the famous Stevenson lighthouse dynasty and grandfather of the novelist Robert Louis Stevenson - built two lighthouses on the Calf itself, the High Light and the Lower Light, designed to work together as a transit pair warning ships off the murderous Chicken Rock. Mist defeated them often enough that in 1875 his sons David and Thomas built a lighthouse on Chicken Rock itself - a feat of marine engineering on a wave-washed islet that is only above water for approximately five hours a day. After a major fire in December 1960 destroyed the Chicken Rock light, a temporary 'New Lighthouse' was built in 1968 between the two Stevenson originals; it was discontinued in 2007 when the rebuilt Chicken Rock returned to full service.

## Bringing the Shearwaters Back

The Manx shearwater is the bird the Calf's history orbits. These extraordinary seabirds spend most of their lives at sea, return to land only to breed in burrows under cover of darkness, and migrate to South American waters in winter. The Calf colony, devastated by the rats from the 1781 shipwreck, dropped to almost nothing by 1900. Localised rat baiting from 1979 onwards began a slow recovery - 127 occupied breeding burrows by 2011. Then a particularly cold winter in 2011-2012 killed off much of the rat population, and Manx National Heritage launched an intensive eradication programme through 2012-2013. By 2014 the survey counted 424 occupied burrows. The numbers have continued to climb. Alongside the shearwaters, the Calf supports a major grey seal colony that breeds on the rocky coastline, a small population of chough, and large numbers of migratory songbirds passing through each spring and autumn. The conservation work continues, year by year, on an island where almost no one lives.

## Flight Context

Located at 54.053°N, 4.820°W, a 618-acre island off the southwest tip of the Isle of Man, separated by the Calf Sound. Three lighthouses are visible on the island itself (the disused 1818 Stevenson pair plus the disused 1968 New Lighthouse); Chicken Rock lighthouse sits 1 mile southwest, still active. Ronaldsway Airport (EGNS) is 17km northeast. The Calf Sound contains strong tidal currents - the islet of Kitterland sits in the middle. Best viewed at 2,000-5,000 ft from the south or west in clear weather. Grey seals often visible hauled out on the rocky shore; Manx shearwaters fly at and after dusk, returning to burrows under cover of darkness.
