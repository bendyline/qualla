# Port St Mary

A herring port that became a Victorian resort, a lifeboat station with 130 years of saves, and a memorial to a ship that went down in two minutes off Dunkirk.

**Location:** 54.0749, -4.7389

**Categories:** coastal-villages, isle-of-man, maritime-history, lifeboat-stations

## Introduction

Purt le Moirrey - harbour by St Mary's church - took its name from a chapel that no longer exists. The Keeill Moirrey stood on a small bluff above Chapel Bay long enough to plant the saint's name into the geography, then vanished so completely that the town hall now sits on what is thought to be its foundations. The village that grew up beside it, however, had a different sort of staying power: a herring fleet, a lifeboat station running since 1896, and a sandstone harbour where most of the working memory of the place still gets handed across the same quay walls.

## From Chapel to Harbour

Linguistically the name traveled an odd route. The original Manx was purt kill Moirrey - harbour of Mary's church - and that kill, the Gaelic word for church, was either reshaped by sound-change into le or replaced outright by a French borrowing that meant simply the. The 19th century sometimes wrote it Port-le-Murray. The chapel itself disappeared early enough that no village memory pinpoints exactly when, leaving only the name to mark the site. The village proper grew up around the small inner harbour built in 1812 - a tidal basin that dried out from half-tide down and made unloading boats a matter of careful scheduling. The Alfred Pier of 1882 finally gave Port St Mary an outer harbour usable at every state of the tide, the only such anchorage among the Manx ports, and the herring boats came in numbers.

## The Lifeboat Years

The Royal National Lifeboat Institution established a station at Port St Mary in 1896 and has not closed it since. The current boat, the Gough Ritchie II, lies on a permanent floating mooring inside the outer breakwater - ready for the kind of weather the Irish Sea reserves for fishing vessels in the wrong place at the wrong time. The most quoted Port St Mary rescue happened earlier, in 1858, when the crew of a stricken schooner were pinned by stormy seas against the Thousla rocks west of the Sound. The memorial at The Sound, the southwestern tip of the island, commemorates that rescue alongside Sir William Percy Cowley (1886-1958) - founder of the Manx National Trust - whose lifespan of 71 years spans much of the modern history of the village itself.

## The Pepperpot and the Storm

Port St Mary inherited a small cast-iron lighthouse from the 1860s, perched at the end of the Alfred Pier. The locals called it the pepperpot, and it served for nearly 150 years before the night of 11-12 January 2009, when a winter storm broke it from its foundations and dropped it into the harbour. A temporary beacon stood in for nine years. The permanent replacement constructed in 2018 attracted controversy over whether it could withstand the kind of weather that took the first one, and the village - already given to public arguments about its commissioners - duly added the new lighthouse to its repertoire of disagreements. The 2018 statement in Tynwald that the Port St Mary Commissioners "lurch inelegantly from one crisis to another" has been quoted on the island ever since.

## Mona's Queen and the Memorial at Kallow Point

On 29 May 1940, during Operation Dynamo, the Isle of Man Steam Packet Company vessel Mona's Queen approached Dunkirk harbour and struck a sea mine outside the entrance. She went down in two minutes. Twenty-four crew were lost. Many came from Port St Mary and the surrounding villages - this was the closest the war ever came to the harbour, and it came in the form of letters arriving at houses where the boat had been familiar for years. Seventy years later, on 29 May 2010, the starboard anchor of Mona's Queen was raised from the seabed and brought back to the island. On 29 May 2012 a memorial was opened at Kallow Point, with that restored anchor at its centre, commemorating Mona's Queen alongside two other Steam Packet vessels lost in 1940: King Orry and Fenella. The Lieutenant Governor attended. So did representatives of the French Navy.

## Who Came From Here

Two thousand people produce a long list. Colonel John Dale Lace (1859-1937), born in Port St Mary, made his fortune in South African gold and diamonds and married a Johannesburg socialite who reportedly rode about in a carriage drawn by zebras. Mollie Sugden, the comedy actress, lived in the village for years before returning to England. The blues guitarist Davy Knowles was born here in 1987 and is now based in Chicago but comes home regularly. Juan Watterson, current Speaker of the House of Keys, lives in the village. John Gell (1899-1983) taught Manx Gaelic from his home here and wrote textbooks that kept the language alive through the decades when fluent speakers could be counted in dozens. A place this small does not stop producing arguments, characters, or unlikely fortunes - it just keeps doing it from the same houses on the same harbour, generation after generation.

## Flight Context

Port St Mary sits at 54.075N, 4.739W on the southwest coast of the Isle of Man, with the inner and outer harbours forming a recognisable L-shape protected by the Alfred Pier. From the air, look for the Victorian Promenade, the Town Hall's pediment at the north end, and the lifeboat station inside the outer breakwater. Cruising altitude 2,000-3,000 ft gives a good view of the whole village. Nearest airport: Isle of Man Airport (Ronaldsway, EGNS) approximately 4 nm northeast. Irish Sea storms can develop with little warning; the same gales that took the pepperpot lighthouse in January 2009 remain a regular feature of winter weather here.
