# Laxey

A Norse-named Manx village built around the world's largest working waterwheel and three vintage railways climbing the glen.

**Location:** 54.2278, -4.4019

**Categories:** Villages in the Isle of Man, Industrial heritage, Manx culture, Coastal villages

## Introduction

The Vikings named this glen for what swam up the river. Laxa, in Old Norse, meant Salmon River, and the name stuck through a thousand years of Manx settlement, mining booms, and tourist invasions. Today the salmon are mostly memory, but something almost as improbable spins in the upper glen: a red and white waterwheel seventy-two feet across, taller than a six-story building, still revolving at three calm rotations per minute. Locals call her Lady Isabella. The rest of the world calls her the largest working waterwheel anywhere on Earth.

## Lady Isabella

Designed by Manx engineer Robert Casement and finished in 1854, the wheel was named for the wife of the lieutenant governor, Charles Hope. She was built for a single, urgent purpose: pumping water from mineshafts that plunged more than 2,200 feet beneath the glen. Without her, the Great Laxey Mine would have drowned. With her, Manx miners pulled silver, lead, zinc, copper pyrites, and hematite iron out of the hills for nearly a century. The wheel turned all day in any weather, harnessing a careful network of streams diverted from the moorland above. When mining ceased in the 1920s, she nearly went for scrap. A local man named Edwin Kneale bought her in 1939 and ran her as a tourist attraction, then handed her to the Manx nation in 1965 on the condition that she keep turning. She does.

## Three Railways, One Village

Laxey is one of the very few villages on Earth where three vintage railways still converge. The Manx Electric Railway, opened through here in the 1890s, rolls in from Douglas on roadside tracks, its boxy original tramcars from 1893 still in service. From Laxey station, the Snaefell Mountain Railway climbs five miles to the summit of the island's highest peak, hauling passengers above the heather on its own narrow gauge. And down in the glen, the restored Great Laxey Mine Railway, reopened in 2004, runs two small steam engines along the old miners' route. Trams cross the village square in summer, sea air mixes with the soft hiss of compressed steam, and the whole arrangement feels less like a museum than a working town that simply never stopped using what worked.

## Mining Cottages and Winding Streets

Walk down from the station toward the harbour and the streets begin to twist. Many of Laxey's stone cottages were built in the 1800s for miners, in the traditional Manx style with thick walls and small windows facing inward against the wind. The neighbourhood called Minorca climbs the southeast bank of the river. Old Laxey clusters around the harbour at the river mouth, where a small stone quay built in the 1850s once served the mines and now shelters fishing boats and pleasure craft. A local-born resident, asked where they are headed for an afternoon walk, will say they are going over the kern, meaning the cairn on the cliffs above the beach. From there the view opens south across Laxey Bay, now a Marine Nature Reserve, toward Clay Head.

## Wool, Beer, and Quiet Industries

Down by the river, the Laxey Woollen Mills have been weaving since 1881, when Egbert Rydings opened them with the encouragement of John Ruskin himself. The mills still produce Manx tartan on traditional looms, one of them powered by bicycle. Loaghtan wool, sheared from the island's distinctive four-horned sheep, runs warm and brown through hats, scarves, capes, and kilts. A few streets away, The Old Laxey Brewing Company keeps the village in cask ales. There are five pubs in town, a primary school on Quarry Road, a Methodist chapel with its own complicated geneaology of mergers and closures, and a Church of England parish church built in 1856 by the mining company itself, at a cost of nine hundred and fifty pounds. The village raised cyclist Mark Cavendish, born in Douglas in 1985 and brought up here, and singer-actress Samantha Barks, born here in 1990.

## King Orry and the Older Story

Above the village, hidden in a residential garden, sit the scattered stones of King Orry's Grave: a Neolithic chambered cairn that predates everything else in the glen by perhaps five thousand years. The Norsemen who named the river never knew its real story. The miners who tunneled beneath the hills never paused to wonder. The Linux Bier Wanderung, which held its annual hike-and-beer gathering here in 2016, certainly didn't. The grave is named after a Manx folk-king who has nothing to do with the people actually buried inside. That kind of layering, of history piled on history with the older parts forgotten, is the Manx mode. Laxey wears it cheerfully.

## Flight Context

Located at 54.228°N, 4.402°W on the east coast of the Isle of Man. Nearest airport is Ronaldsway (EGNS) about 14 miles south at Castletown. From cruising altitude the village is visible as a wooded glen running down to the bay, with the white circle of the Laxey Wheel marking the upper end. The Snaefell summit (2,037 ft) rises immediately west and is often used as a visual reference along the east coast.
