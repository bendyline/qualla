# Strangford

Vikings named it for the strong current that still runs past its front door - and a string of monks, saints and tower-house builders have all left marks within walking distance.

**Location:** 54.3707, -5.5555

**Categories:** village, northern-ireland, viking-history, saint-patrick, castles

## Introduction

Step off the ferry at Strangford and you are standing where Vikings stood. Not metaphorically - the name they gave this place, Strangr fjorthr, the strong sea-inlet, is the same name written on the road sign now. Norsemen sailed up this channel in the ninth century to raid the monasteries that ringed the lough, and the current they wrote down in their sagas still pulls past the harbour wall. Before them, St Patrick is said to have come this way too. Tradition places his entry into Ireland in the year 432 through these same Narrows and up the Quoile Estuary that opens to the west. A village of fewer than 500 people, Strangford has carried the names of saints and raiders side by side for fifteen centuries.

## Patrick and the Monasteries

The early Christian history of this corner of Ireland is dense beyond reasonable expectation. Patrick is believed to have sailed through the Strangford Narrows in 432 and up the Quoile Estuary, beginning the mission that would eventually convert most of the island. By the mid-sixth century, monastic centres had sprung up around the lough at Nendrum, Downpatrick, Comber and Kilclief - religious settlements that combined prayer, agriculture, scholarship and, eventually, accumulated wealth. The Nendrum monastery on Mahee Island, just inside the lough, would later contain the world's earliest known tide mills: dendrochronology has dated the older of two excavated mills to 619 CE, and a later phase to 787 CE. The wealth that built these places was the same wealth that drew the Vikings.

## The Norsemen's Visits

Viking raids on Ireland began in 795 with the burning of the monastery on Rathlin Island, off the Antrim coast. For the next three centuries, Norsemen returned to Strangford Lough regularly - drawn by the riches stored in its monasteries, but also, archaeological evidence suggests, choosing to trade with and settle near places like Nendrum. The lough had been called Loch Cuan in Irish, meaning the lough of the harbours, or more poetically the quiet lough. The Norse name Strangr fjorthr - strong fjord - was originally just the name of the channel by the village, where the tides accelerate to nearly four metres per second. By the seventeenth century the channel name had spread to the whole body of water, and Loch Cuan had faded from common use. A linguistic exchange, conducted across eight hundred years.

## Castles Within Walking Distance

Strangford itself has barely 500 residents - the 2001 census recorded 475 - but the cluster of medieval and post-medieval structures within easy walking distance is unusual for any village this size. Strangford Castle stands near the harbour, a 16th-century tower house complete with a defensive drop-hole at roof level above the door. One mile northeast, Audley's Castle rises above the lough's shore, a 15th-century tower whose silhouette dominates the surrounding fields. A mile and a half away lies Castle Ward, a National Trust property where a 16th-century tower house keeps company with an eccentric 18th-century mansion - built half in Classical style and half in Gothic because the husband and wife who commissioned it could not agree on architecture. Two miles northwest of the village, the Audleystown Court Tomb represents a much earlier period: a Neolithic dual court cairn whose stone chambers were already five thousand years old when the Vikings rowed past.

## The Living Village

The harbour at Strangford is small, lined with rows of nineteenth-century cottages and a fine Georgian terrace - all the more striking for being preserved in such a tightly contained space. The ferry to Portaferry, which has run for almost four centuries, pulls in and out every thirty minutes, the route across the Narrows taking eight minutes when the road journey around the lough takes ninety. Game of Thrones used Castle Ward's farmyard as the Winterfell set, most prominently in Season 1, and the estate has been associated with Winterfell throughout the series. Tourism now mixes with the older economy of farming, fishing and ferry traffic. On any given morning, the same harbour wall might support a National Trust visitor with a camera, a Dubliner waiting for the boat, and a fisherman checking nets - all standing on stones that were quarried before any of the structures behind them existed.

## Flight Context

Strangford sits at 54.37N, 5.55W on the west bank of the Strangford Narrows in County Down, Northern Ireland. From altitude, look for the small harbour and ferry slip at the southernmost reach of Strangford Lough, where the lough drains into the Irish Sea. Castle Ward parkland is visible immediately to the southwest as a wooded area. Audley's Castle is the prominent tower visible to the northeast on a rise above the lough. Nearest airport is Belfast City (EGAC), about 23 nautical miles north-northwest. Recommended viewing altitude 2,000-3,500 feet. The Portaferry ferry crosses the Narrows every thirty minutes through daylight hours.
