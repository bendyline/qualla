# Sunderland

The Wearside city that once built a quarter of all British wartime merchant tonnage, lost its shipyards and coal mines in a single generation, and is rebuilding itself on the cleared ground.

**Location:** 54.9078, -1.3819

**Categories:** Cities, Industrial heritage, Shipbuilding, Coal mining, North East England

## Introduction

Between 1939 and 1945, the shipyards along the River Wear launched 245 merchant ships totalling 1.5 million tons - a quarter of all the merchant tonnage produced in the United Kingdom during the war. Sunderland was once hailed as the largest shipbuilding town in the world. The last shipyard closed on 7 December 1988. The last coal mine - Wearmouth Colliery - closed in 1994. What replaced them, in stages and with some difficulty, was a city working out what comes after heavy industry. The 49,000-seat Stadium of Light now stands on the colliery site, with a miner's Davy lamp monument outside.

## What The Numbers Hide

At the 2021 census, the built-up area of Sunderland had a population of 168,315 and the wider borough 274,200. The figures are stable, but they conceal a generation of upheaval. Unemployment ran high through the 1980s and 1990s; in 2016 the city voted 61 percent in favour of Brexit, one of the early signals on referendum night that a different result was coming. Ethnic diversity is modest by national standards - the main BAME populations live in Hendon and Millfield in the east, where Bangladeshi, Indian and Chinese communities cluster, while Sunderland West and North have smaller but distinct South Asian and East Asian populations. The University of Sunderland brings 147 nationalities through its doors, which complicates any neat snapshot.

## The Industries That Left

Coal had been mined in the Durham coalfield for centuries; at the 1923 peak, 170,000 miners worked the county. After the Second World War the pits began to close one by one, with mass unemployment following the closures northward and eastward. Wearmouth Colliery was the last to go in 1994. Shipbuilding had been on the Wear since at least 1346. Joseph Conrad sailed on the clipper Torrens, built at a Sunderland yard, and began his first novel on her deck. The decline came from overseas competition: cheaper ships built in cheaper yards. Glass-making - the industry that the abbot of Monkwearmouth had encouraged in AD 674 - lasted until 2007, when Corning's Sunderland works and the Pyrex plant both closed within months of each other. Vaux Breweries, in the town centre since the 1880s, closed in July 1999.

## Nissan, The Stadium And The Spire

Regeneration arrived in stages. Nissan opened its Sunderland plant in 1986, the first Bluebird rolling off the line that same year; today the factory is one of the most productive in Europe. The Stadium of Light opened in 1997 on the cleared Monkwearmouth Colliery site, with the Sunderland Aquatic Centre - the only Olympic-size pool between Leeds and Edinburgh - opening alongside it in 2008. The Northern Spire Bridge, a 180-metre cable-stayed crossing put to a public vote on its design, opened upstream of the city centre in 2018. The Vaux brewery site, a 26-acre brownfield wound at the heart of the city, sat dormant through a tangled dispute with Tesco that lasted until 2011. Phase one of its redevelopment started in 2016.

## The Mackem Dialect And The Minster

Sunderland's accent is Mackem, related to but distinct from Geordie. The dialect carries Anglo-Saxon roots and a small vocabulary worth knowing: nee for no, marra for mate, ha'way for come on (not to be confused with Newcastle's howay), git for very. In 1998, following the city's award of city status, the parish church of Bishopwearmouth - St Michael's - was redesignated Sunderland Minster with a city-wide role. It was believed to be the first new minster church created in England since the Reformation, a claim that quietly stakes Sunderland's pride in a region where Newcastle usually takes the bigger headlines. The Sunderland Polytechnic founded in 1969 became the University of Sunderland in 1992. Its St Peter's Riverside Campus sits next to the National Glass Centre, the school of glass-making facing the river the industry once depended on.

## Flight Context

Located at 54.90°N, 1.38°W on the North Sea coast of North East England. The city sprawls along both banks of the River Wear near its mouth, with Roker and Seaburn beaches to the north and the A19/A1018 corridor running through. Nearest airport: Newcastle (ICAO EGNT), 13 nautical miles north-northwest. Best viewed from 3,000-5,000 feet; the river mouth, the Stadium of Light and the long beaches are easy navigation landmarks. North Sea coastal weather: variable cloud, occasional haar in summer.
