# Chester

The Roman fortress city of Deva Victrix that became England's only complete walled city, almost the capital of Roman Britain, and the place where Cheshire keeps two thousand years of its history.

**Location:** 53.1933, -2.8931

**Categories:** city, roman, medieval, chester, england, cheshire, history

## Introduction

Chester was almost the capital of Roman Britain. The legionary fortress here was twenty percent larger than the contemporary fortresses at York and Caerleon - large enough that some historians have argued the Romans were planning to make Deva the centre of their northern province, not London. They never quite did. The empire collapsed first. But the ambition is still legible in the ground. The four main streets of modern Chester - Eastgate, Northgate, Watergate, and Bridge Street - follow exactly the routes laid out by Roman surveyors in the 70s AD. The walls trace the line of the Roman fortress. The cathedral sits where the Roman granaries probably stood. Below the basements of the modern shops, the foundations of a city that was meant to be a capital still wait in the sandstone bedrock.

## The Fortress That Almost Was Capital

In AD 79, during the reign of the Emperor Vespasian, Legio II Adiutrix established a castrum on the territory of the Celtic Cornovii at the strategic point where the River Dee swung in a wide bend. They called it Deva Victrix - Deva either from the British name for the Dee or after the river goddess, and Victrix from the later resident legion, Legio XX Valeria Victrix, the Twentieth Legion Valeria Victorious. The fortress that grew here was unusually large. While the comparable fortresses at York (Eboracum) and Caerleon (Isca Augusta) covered around 50 acres each, Deva covered 60. The civilian amphitheatre, also unusual in its scale, could seat between 8,000 and 10,000 spectators - the largest known military amphitheatre in Britain. The Minerva Shrine carved into the Roman quarry on the south bank of the Dee is the only rock-cut Roman shrine still in situ in Britain. The legion stayed here until at least the late fourth century. When they finally withdrew around 410, the Romano-British civilian population stayed on, sheltering inside the fortress walls against raiders from across the Irish Sea.

## Saxons, Werburgh, and Edgar's Eight Kings

In 615 or thereabouts, Aethelfrith of Northumbria fought and won the Battle of Chester nearby, slaughtering twelve hundred unarmed monks from Bangor-is-y-Coed before turning his army on the Welsh defenders. The Anglo-Saxons called the place Legacaestir - Old English for City of the Legion - and the second half of that name eventually became simply Chester. In 689 King Aethelred of Mercia founded a minster church on the site of what is now St John the Baptist. In the ninth century the body of his niece Saint Werburgh was brought here from Hanbury in Staffordshire to save it from Danish raiders, and it was reburied in what became the abbey church - the building that is now Chester Cathedral. The Anglo-Saxons strengthened the walls against the Danes. In 907 Aethelflaed, Lady of the Mercians and daughter of Alfred the Great, refounded Chester as a fortified burh. In 973 King Edgar of England came here after his coronation at Bath and held court in a palace near the old Dee bridge. Edgar's Field still bears his name. According to the monk Henry Bradshaw, Edgar was rowed up the Dee from his palace to the great minster church of St John by eight tributary kings working the oars - an extraordinary piece of medieval political theatre by an English king who wanted his rivals to be seen carrying him.

## Cathedral, Castle, and a Long Trade in Lead

After the Norman Conquest, William the Conqueror made Hugh d'Avranches Earl of Chester in 1071, and Hugh built Chester Castle. The walls were extended west and south. The four medieval gates - Northgate, Eastgate, Bridgegate, and Watergate - took their final shape. From the fourteenth to the eighteenth centuries the city was sometimes called Westchester to distinguish it from other Chesters. The abbey church became the cathedral in 1541, when Henry VIII created the Diocese of Chester during the Dissolution of the Monasteries. Through the medieval and early-modern centuries Chester was a working port - lead came down the Dee from the mines of north-east Wales and went out into the Irish Sea trade, slowly building the city's mercantile wealth. The Rows - the two-level shopping galleries unique to Chester - took their basic form in the thirteenth century after a catastrophic 1278 fire. The city walls became permanent. The cathedral grew.

## Civil War, Industry, and the Victorian Black-and-White Revival

When the English Civil War broke out, Chester sided with King Charles I. The city was besieged for nearly two years and the King himself watched the Battle of Rowton Heath from the tower on the east wall that now bears his name. Chester finally fell to the Parliamentarians on 3 February 1646. The walls, no longer needed for defence, were converted into a public promenade by the early eighteenth century. Then the Industrial Revolution arrived in the late 1700s - first the Chester Canal, then the railways. The Walkers, Parker shot tower of 1799 still stands beside the canal. The two great railway stations of Chester General and Chester Northgate brought workers and trade. By 1841 the population had reached 23,000. The Victorian era saw a remarkable architectural revival when the architect John Douglas, employed by the Duke of Westminster as his principal architect, designed a generation of new buildings in the Jacobean half-timbered style. Most of the black-and-white facades that tourists photograph today are Victorian, not medieval - what Pevsner called the black-and-white revival, pioneered by Douglas and T.M. Lockwood, replacing or reskinning older buildings in a deliberately archaic style. Douglas's trademark twisted chimney stacks rise from many city-centre rooftops.

## What Chester Saved

By the late 1960s, Chester faced the same threat that destroyed much of historic Britain: post-war traffic planning. The bypass of 1964, the Inner Ring Road, the spread of suburbs across former farmland - all of it cut into the historic city. In 1968, the architect Donald Insall produced a report for the government recommending that Chester's historic buildings be preserved and adapted rather than demolished. The City Conservation Area was designated in 1969 and the principle held. Buildings like The Falcon Inn, the Dutch Houses, and the Kings Buildings survived because someone decided they were worth saving. On 13 January 2002, Chester became the first city in the United Kingdom to be granted Fairtrade City status by the Fairtrade Foundation. The 2021 census put the built-up area population at 92,760, with the wider Cheshire West and Chester unitary authority covering 138,875 people. The city's economy is now mostly service-sector - tourism, retail, public administration, and financial services - with the Airbus wing factory at Broughton just over the Welsh border employing around 6,000 people making aircraft wings. But the historic core, inside the walls, still holds most of what makes Chester Chester. Two thousand years of continuous occupation, layered on top of itself, walkable in a single afternoon.

## Flight Context

Located at 53.20 degrees north, 2.88 degrees west, on the River Dee in Cheshire, England, immediately adjacent to the English-Welsh border. Best viewed from 2,500 to 4,500 feet, where the nearly-complete rectangular circuit of the city walls is unmistakable, enclosing the medieval and Roman core. The Dee bends around the south and west of the historic centre; the Roodee racecourse forms a green oval inside that bend. Chester Cathedral and the Eastgate Clock dominate the centre. Hawarden Airport (EGNR) is 4 nautical miles southwest, Liverpool John Lennon (EGGP) is 12 nautical miles north, RAF Shawbury (EGOS) is to the south-southeast. Estuary fog can build over the Dee on still mornings; afternoon visibility is generally excellent in stable westerly conditions.
