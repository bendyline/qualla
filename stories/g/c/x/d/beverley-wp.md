# Beverley

The county town of East Yorkshire, founded in the 7th century around the church of St John of Beverley, was once England's eleventh-largest settlement and now lends its name to a Hollywood neighbourhood.

**Location:** 53.8425, -0.4316

**Categories:** england, yorkshire, market-town, medieval, anglo-saxon, county-town

## Introduction

John of Beverley founded a church here some time around 700 in a stretch of woodland called Inderawuda, the wood of the men of Deira. The Anglo-Saxon kingdom of Northumbria was still standing. Vikings would arrive within 150 years. John would die on 7 May 721, be canonised in 1037, and become the engine of a pilgrimage economy that pulled a town up around his shrine. By the high Middle Ages, Beverley was the eleventh-largest settlement in England. The Reformation cut it back. The Industrial Revolution mostly went around it. What remains is an unusually intact medieval market town that quietly named a piece of Los Angeles after itself in a chain of coincidence three centuries long.

## From Inderawuda to wool town

John of Beverley was Bishop of York from around 706 to 714 and founded the church that became the seed of the town. He was credited with miracles during his lifetime and venerated as a saint after his death. The settlement grew slowly under Northumbria, came under Viking control in the 850s, then joined the kingdom of England. After John's canonisation in 1037, pilgrims arrived in numbers and the town stretched out along the road from the North Bar to the Beck in the elongated pattern still visible today. Archbishop Thurstan granted borough status in 1122. By the 12th century Beverley was exporting wool to the cloth-making towns of the Low Countries and growing rich on it. Dominican and Franciscan friars established houses in the 13th century. By the time the antiquary John Leland passed through in the Tudor period, he estimated the population at 5,000.

## Pilgrimage of Grace, plague and decline

In October 1536, 500 men gathered on Westwood under the leadership of a local lawyer, William Stapleton, and joined what became the Pilgrimage of Grace, the great northern rising against Henry VIII's religious reforms. Some 30,000 rebels eventually marched against the new laws; Henry crushed the rebellion with a mixture of false promises and executions. Beverley was hit twice. The cult of St John was suppressed and the pilgrimage trade that had sustained generations of working people vanished. The Reformation widened the gap between Beverley's rich and poor, and the town's population fell. During the Civil War, after being refused entry to Hull at the Beverley Gate in April 1642, Charles I retreated to Beverley itself and spent three weeks there as a guest at a house by the North Bar, greeted with the ringing of St Mary's bells. The Parliamentarians eventually won. The Puritans shut the alehouses on Sundays and stripped what they considered idolatrous from the churches.

## Tanneries and shipyards

From the 19th century the town did its share of industrial work, but on a manageable scale. Hodgsons Tannery operated from 1816 to 1979, turning animal hides into leather, employing hundreds, and finally closing with 750 redundancies. Beverley Shipyard opened in 1884 on the River Hull and built ships sideways into the water because the channel was too narrow to launch them straight on. In 1954 it employed 650 people. The yard built trawlers for the Hull deep-sea fishing fleet and minesweepers for the Royal Navy in both World Wars. The deep-sea trawler Arctic Corsair, now preserved as a museum ship in Hull, was built there in 1960. Construction ended in 1977. Victoria Barracks, completed in 1878, gave the town a permanent army presence until it closed the same year; the Defence School of Transport at nearby Leconfield carries on the military thread.

## The Blitz that missed

Hull, eight miles south, suffered some of the heaviest aerial bombardment of the Second World War. Beverley, separated only by a few miles of farmland, was largely untouched. Survivors of the Hull raids could see the bombing from the chalk wolds. After the war Beverley settled into the role it has held ever since: a quiet county town with one of the finest parish churches in England, a famous racecourse and an annual round of festivals. In 2007 the Royal Bank of Scotland's Affordable Affluence study named Beverley the best place to live in the United Kingdom. The Flemingate shopping development opened in late 2015 on the site of the old Hodgsons Tannery. The Beverley Treasure House holds the town's library, archives, museum and art gallery. The medieval pattern of the streets, the North Bar arch through which buses still pass in single file, and the Minster's twin Perpendicular towers visible across the wolds, all remain. Beverley is twinned with Nogent-sur-Oise in France and Lemgo in Germany.

## The Beverly Hills connection

The naming chain that ties this Yorkshire town to Rodeo Drive begins in 1668, when English settlers founded a town in Massachusetts and named it Beverly after their old market home. The Beverly Farms district of the Massachusetts town gave its name in turn, in 1907, to the new Los Angeles subdivision called Beverly Hills, developed by Burton Green. The Beverly Hills Hotel opened in 1912. The name traveled three thousand miles, lost an e in the journey, and ended up on the most famous postcode in California. Back in Beverley itself, Beverley Grammar School, founded by Saint John in around 700 AD, is the oldest state school in England. Its alumni include the chemist Smithson Tennant, who discovered iridium and osmium in 1803, and Thomas Percy, executed in 1605 for his role in the Gunpowder Plot. Quiet town, long reach.

## Flight Context

Beverley sits at 53.8425 degrees north, 0.4316 degrees west, eight miles north-northwest of Hull on the line where the chalk wolds meet the flat low ground falling south to the Humber. From the air the Minster's twin Perpendicular west towers at the south end of the town and St Mary's spire at the north are the two unmistakable landmarks, with the medieval North Bar gatehouse between them on the A164. The Westwood common and Beverley Racecourse spread to the west. Humberside (EGNJ) is 18 nautical miles south across the estuary; Leeds Bradford (EGNM) is 47 nautical miles west; RAF Leconfield (now DST Leconfield) lies two miles north. Recommended altitude 2,500 to 4,500 feet AGL frames the town, the wolds, and the route south to Hull.
