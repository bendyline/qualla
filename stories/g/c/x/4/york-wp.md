# York

The cathedral city at the confluence of the Ouse and Foss, founded by Roman legions in AD 71 and continuously inhabited ever since.

**Location:** 53.9500, -1.0833

**Categories:** city, history, roman, viking, medieval, yorkshire, england

## Introduction

Two rivers, marshland on three sides, high ground in the middle: in AD 71, the Ninth Legion looked at the spot and saw a fortress waiting to be built. The Brigantes, the Iron Age people who had lived in the region, were duly conquered. The legion's wooden palisades, raised on flat ground above the Ouse near its confluence with the Foss, enclosed fifty acres and 6,000 soldiers. Stone walls followed under the Sixth Legion. The principia, the headquarters at the fortress's heart, sat where the foundations of York Minster now stand. Excavations in the Minster's undercroft have laid bare Roman columns still bearing the weight of the Gothic cathedral above. The city has not been empty since.

## Emperors, Bishops, Saints

Roman Eboracum became capital of the province of Britannia Inferior in 211. Hadrian, Septimius Severus, and Constantius I all stayed in the city during military campaigns to the north against the Picts and Britons. Constantius died here in 306, and his son Constantine, later called the Great, was first proclaimed emperor by his soldiers at York. The Constantine who would legalize Christianity across the empire took his first imperial step at the edge of the Roman world, in a damp northern fortress, in a city whose later cathedral would house a Christian relic culture spanning a thousand years. Around AD 314 a bishop from York attended the Council at Arles. By the seventh century, York Minster had been founded as a wooden church for the baptism of King Edwin of Northumbria. Bede's history records the moment.

## Jorvik

On All Saints' Day 866, the Vikings struck. Ivar the Boneless and Halfdan led the Great Heathen Army into York while most of its leaders were attending Mass. The city fell quickly, was renamed Jorvik, and became the capital of Viking-held northern England. At its peak, more than 10,000 people lived inside the walls, second only to London. The Coppergate excavations of the 1970s and 1980s uncovered the workshops of Viking artisans: textiles, metalwork, glass-blowing, jewellery, and goods traded across networks that reached the Persian Gulf. Eric Bloodaxe ruled the last independent Jórvík until 954, when Eadred of Wessex drove him out and unified England. But the Norse population stayed. Old Norse and Old English mingled in the streets and shaped the language and place names of northern England in ways still visible today.

## Conquest and Massacre

In 1068, two years after Hastings, York rebelled against William the Conqueror. He suppressed it. They rebelled again in 1069. He responded with the Harrying of the North, a campaign of organized destruction that left villages from York to Durham burned and starving. The Normans rebuilt York under their iron grip, raising castles, monasteries, and the first stone phase of what would become the Minster. In 1190, the Jewish community of York was massacred at Clifford's Tower, with at least 150 people killed and possibly as many as 500. They had taken refuge in the keep from a mob whipped up by crusade fervor and crusader debts. The pogrom remains one of the darkest moments in English medieval history, an act of mass violence remembered each year at the tower where it happened.

## Rebellion, Siege, Reinvention

York became one of medieval England's great trading centres, the city's first charter granted by King John in 1212. The Wars of the Roses took their name partly from the Yorkist white rose. Henry VIII's dissolution of the monasteries shut down St Mary's Abbey in 1539, and the Pilgrimage of Grace rose against him in protest. During the English Civil War, Royalist York was besieged for ten weeks in 1644 before Prince Rupert briefly broke the siege; his army was then crushed at Marston Moor, and York surrendered. Guy Fawkes was born here in 1570 and educated at St Peter's School; his attempt to blow up Parliament in 1605 made his birthplace forever a footnote in his story. In April 1942, the Luftwaffe bombed York in the Baedeker Blitz, killing 92 people. The Guildhall lay in ruins until 1960.

## Rails, Chocolate, Tourism

George Hudson, the so-called Railway King, brought the railway to York in 1839, and the city became a major hub on the East Coast Main Line. The Rowntree's chocolate factory, founded 1862, employed thousands and gave the world Kit-Kat, Smarties, Aero, and Fruit Pastilles. Terry's of York produced the Chocolate Orange. The National Railway Museum opened in 1975, the Jorvik Viking Centre in 1984, and the University of York in 1963. In 2018, the Sunday Times named York the best place to live in Britain, citing the perfect mix of heritage and modern infrastructure. The 2021 census recorded 141,685 people in the built-up area. They float through 1,950 years of stratified urban history without much trouble, popping into pubs older than most countries.

## Flight Context

York sits at 53.95°N, 1.08°W in the flat Vale of York, a former glacial outwash plain on the terminal moraine of the last ice age. Recommended viewing altitude 3,000-5,000 feet for the city centre and surrounding river floodplain. Nearest airports: Leeds Bradford (EGNM) 22nm southwest, Humberside (EGNJ) 35nm southeast. The Ouse winds south through the city; the Foss joins it from the northeast. Linton-on-Ouse aerodrome (now closed) lies 10nm northwest. Climate is mild and damp; fog forms easily on the river meadows in autumn and winter.
