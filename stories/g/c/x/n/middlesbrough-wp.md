# Middlesbrough

Once the steel capital that built the Sydney Harbour Bridge, Middlesbrough is a Tees-side town learning to live without the industries that made it.

**Location:** 54.5800, -1.2300

**Categories:** cities, industrial-heritage, steel-towns, england, north-east-england

## Introduction

Look at the south end of the Sydney Harbour Bridge. The steel was made in Middlesbrough. Look at the Tyne Bridge in Newcastle: same firm, Dorman Long, same town. For a stretch of decades on either side of 1900, the iron and steel that held up the British Empire's most ambitious engineering came from a place that almost nobody outside Britain has heard of. Middlesbrough's population in 1801 was 25 people. By 1881, it was over 50,000. By 1914, Dorman Long employed more than 20,000 workers and was the largest company in the country. The bridge across Sydney Harbour was their swan song.

## Iron Ore in the Eston Hills

The acceleration began in 1850, when prospectors found ironstone in the Eston Hills just to the southeast. Blast furnaces appeared along the south bank of the Tees, lining the river all the way to the sea. In 1875, Bolckow Vaughan opened the Cleveland Steelworks, the moment iron production tipped into steel production at industrial scale. By 1900, Bolckow Vaughan was the largest steel producer in Great Britain. Several large shipyards lined the Tees as well, including Sir Raylton Dixon, which built hundreds of steam freighters. One of them, the SS Mont-Blanc, would cause the 1917 Halifax Explosion in Canada, the largest accidental explosion in human history until the atomic age. That ship was launched from Middlesbrough.

## The Transporter Bridge

Sir William Arrol & Co. of Glasgow built the Tees Transporter Bridge in 1911 to a design that crossed the river with a hanging gondola instead of a roadway. It is a Grade II* listed building and Middlesbrough's defining silhouette. The Newport Bridge opened further upstream in 1934, originally a vertical-lift design. The Transporter has been closed for long stretches in recent years over safety concerns; in August 2022, the Mayor announced that one of its legs is sinking and that repair estimates keep rising. The town's other monumental structure is Anish Kapoor and Cecil Balmond's Temenos sculpture, unveiled in 2010 by the Riverside Stadium. It is roughly 110 metres long and 50 metres high, a tensioned ring of steel cables forming a 3D net. Near the Town Hall stands the Bottle of Notes by Claes Oldenburg, the only public Oldenburg in the UK, its surface inscribed with text from Captain Cook's journals.

## Captain Cook and the Town That Made Him Possible

James Cook was born in Marton, now part of southern Middlesbrough, on 27 October 1728. The Captain Cook Birthplace Museum in Stewart Park opened in 1978 on the 250th anniversary of his birth. The Tees gave Cook his first experience of the sea, and the colliers of the northeast coast taught him seamanship: HMS Endeavour, the ship he sailed to Australia and New Zealand, was a converted Whitby collier of exactly the type that worked the Tees. The town has not stopped claiming him. He is on the statue near the museum, on the Oldenburg sculpture downtown, in the museum's permanent exhibitions, and in the name of the city's largest hospital. James Cook University Hospital, on the south side of town, is one of the biggest employers in the region.

## Decline and Reinvention

The mid-twentieth century undid almost everything heavy industry had built. Shipyards closed. Steelworks closed. The ICI plant at Wilton fragmented, and the last of ICI itself left the area in 2006. Unemployment climbed and stayed. What replaced industry was patchy: a port that still moves 27 million tonnes of cargo a year through Teesport, a university now serving roughly 20,000 students, a digital cluster in the Boho Zone, and a long-running effort to make MIMA, the Middlesbrough Institute of Modern Art, into a national contemporary gallery on the scale the town deserves. Albert Park, donated by ironmaster Henry Bolckow in 1866 and opened by Prince Arthur in 1868, still draws walkers. Stewart Park hosted BBC Radio 1's Big Weekend in 2019. The town has not stopped working out what comes next.

## Flight Context

Middlesbrough sits at 54.58°N, 1.23°W in a tight bend of the River Tees as it widens toward the North Sea. The Transporter Bridge, the Newport Bridge, and the steel curve of the Temenos sculpture by the Riverside Stadium are the visual anchors from above. Teesport's container cranes and the Wilton industrial complex line the coast to the east. Nearest airports: Teesside International (EGNV) 12 miles west, Newcastle (EGNT) about 40 miles north. The Cleveland Hills and Roseberry Topping rise to the south, marking the edge of the North York Moors National Park.
