# Berwick-upon-Tweed

The northernmost town in England sits on the wrong side of its own river, in a county named after a town in another country, with Elizabethan walls that are among the finest of their kind in Europe.

**Location:** 55.7692, -2.0025

**Categories:** town, border, history, northumberland, elizabethan

## Introduction

Berwick-upon-Tweed is the only town in Britain that gave its name to a county in a different country. Berwickshire, Scotland, takes its name from a town that has been in England since 1482, in an arrangement medieval cartographers would have considered deranged and modern bureaucrats simply file under "historical." The town is also the northernmost in England, slightly further north than Copenhagen, and faces a North Sea that is closer to Denmark than to London. Berwick's identity has been the slow accretion of all this strangeness.

## Royal Burgh

Berwick was founded as an Anglo-Saxon settlement in the Kingdom of Northumbria, which entered the English realm in the 10th century under King Eadred. After the Battle of Carham in 1018 the area passed to Scotland, and David I made the town a royal burgh in the 12th century. He also established the surrounding shires - and Berwick became the county town of Berwickshire, a largely rural area extending north-west into what is now the Scottish Borders. A mint operated by 1153. By the mid-13th century the chancellor William Edington wrote that Berwick was "so populous and of such commercial importance that it might rightly be called another Alexandria, whose riches were the sea and the water its walls." The duty on Berwick's wool trade was the Scottish Crown's largest single source of revenue.

## Disputed Territory

Edward I of England came to Berwick in 1291-92 to arbitrate the disputed Scottish succession between John Balliol and Robert Bruce, fifth Lord of Annandale. He pronounced for Balliol in the Great Hall of Berwick Castle on 17 November 1292. Four years later he came back to take the town by force, beginning the First War of Scottish Independence with a massacre of the burgesses and merchants. The wars went on for over four hundred years. The town walls were built between 1296 and 1318, and were rebuilt under Scottish rule. An arm of William Wallace was displayed at Berwick after his execution and quartering in August 1305. Edward II mustered 25,000 men here in 1314 before the disaster at Bannockburn. Scotland recovered the town in 1318. The English took it back the day after Halidon Hill in 1333. Scotland regained it briefly in 1355, then lost it again in 1356. In 1461 Margaret of Anjou ceded Berwick back to Scotland in exchange for help against the Yorkists, then in 1482 Richard, Duke of Gloucester - soon to be Richard III - took it for the final time.

## Elizabeth's Investment

The Elizabethan ramparts cost £128,648, the most expensive public works project of Elizabeth I's reign. They were built in the new Italian style, the trace italienne, with angled bastions designed to deflect cannon fire and to mount their own artillery for cross-fire along the curtain walls. The Italian military engineer Giovanni Portinari was involved in the design. Most English cities of the period were tearing down their medieval walls. Berwick was wrapping itself in the most modern military architecture of its time, against the fear of a Scottish or French attack that never came. The walls have been described as "the only surviving walls of their kind" in Britain - a freeze-frame of late 16th-century military engineering, preserved because they were never tested in anger and never needed to be replaced.

## Britain's Earliest Barracks

After the Union of Crowns in 1603 the border fortress town no longer needed a frontier garrison. The Crown ordered the dissolution of most of the garrison in December 1603, reducing it to a hundred men and pensioners. In 1639 the armies of Charles I and General Alexander Leslie of the Covenanters faced each other across the Tweed during the Bishops' Wars - the only time English and Scottish armies confronted each other at Berwick after the Union - and negotiated the Pacification of Berwick without fighting. Holy Trinity Parish Church was built 1648-1652, the most northerly Anglican parish church in England, constructed under special licence from Oliver Cromwell with stones quarried from the decaying castle. From 1717-1721 Nicholas Hawksmoor designed Berwick Barracks for the Board of Ordnance - among the earliest purpose-built barracks anywhere in Britain. The town's military identity outlasted its military purpose by more than a century.

## Bridges and Identities

Three bridges cross the Tweed at Berwick, and each one is a chapter of British engineering history. The Old Bridge of 1610-1624 was commissioned by James VI and I to bind his two kingdoms together after the Union of Crowns; fifteen sandstone arches, still carrying one-way traffic. The Royal Tweed Bridge of 1928 had what was then the longest single concrete span in the UK. The Royal Border Bridge of 1847-1850, Robert Stephenson's railway viaduct of 28 arches and 720 yards length, was opened by Queen Victoria and carries the East Coast Main Line 126 feet above the river. Today Berwick's population sits around 12,000-13,000, the local football club plays in the Scottish league system, and the residents will tell you - older voices more often than younger - that they feel Scottish, or English, or independent. Lucy Bronze, the England footballer who has played for Lyon and Chelsea, was born here. So was Henry Travers, who grew up in Berwick before going to Hollywood and playing Clarence the guardian angel in It's a Wonderful Life. So was Eric Lomax, whose memoir The Railway Man told the story of his captivity on the Thai-Burma railway during the Second World War, and his decades-long path to confronting his torturer. A border town breeds people who understand more than one side of a story.

## Flight Context

Berwick-upon-Tweed at 55.77°N, 2.00°W, on the East Coast Main Line midway between Edinburgh and Newcastle. Recommended viewing altitude 1,500-3,000 ft AGL. The Elizabethan trace italienne ramparts form a distinctive star shape around the old town on the north bank; the Royal Border Bridge railway viaduct of 28 arches is the dominant landmark. Nearest ICAO airport: EGNT (Newcastle) 55 nm south; EGPH (Edinburgh) 45 nm north-west. Holy Island visible 8 nm south, Eyemouth 9 nm north, the Cheviots rise 20 nm south-west. The Anglo-Scottish border is 2 nm north at Marshall Meadows; the actual border crosses the railway line just south of the Lamberton signposts.
