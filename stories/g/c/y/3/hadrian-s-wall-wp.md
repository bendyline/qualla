# Hadrian's Wall

The Roman Empire's most ambitious frontier work in Britain: 73 miles of stone, turf, ditches, and forts built to control movement between the provinces and the unconquered north.

**Location:** 55.0240, -2.2590

**Categories:** Roman Britain, UNESCO World Heritage Sites, Fortifications, Ancient engineering, Northumberland

## Introduction

There is evidence that Hadrian's Wall was originally plastered and whitewashed. If so, the entire seventy-three-mile barrier would have gleamed in the northern light, visible for miles -- a shining line drawn across the landscape announcing that everything south of it belonged to Rome. The Wall was never just a defensive structure. It was a statement of power, a customs barrier, and a system for controlling the movement of people across the frontier between Roman Britain and the territories beyond.

## Hadrian's Grand Design

Construction began in AD 122, following a visit to Britain by the Emperor Hadrian. The original plan called for a continuous stone wall from the Tyne to the Solway, punctuated by small fortified gateways called milecastles at every Roman mile and two observation turrets between each pair. No full-scale forts were part of the first design. But plans changed during construction -- the Wall's overall width was reduced, forts were added directly onto the Wall line, and a massive earthwork known as the Vallum was dug to the south, creating a controlled military zone. The western third was initially built of turf rather than stone, a decision that was later reversed when the turf section was rebuilt in stone in two phases. Where the ground was boggy, wooden piles were driven in as foundations. The resulting structure stretched 73 miles from Wallsend on the Tyne to Bowness-on-Solway.

## More Than a Wall

The Wall was part of a larger defensive system. In front of it, a wide ditch was dug wherever the terrain permitted, with the excavated earth piled into a glacis on the north side. On the flat ground in front of the Wall, pits called cippi held entangled sharpened branches -- the Roman equivalent of barbed wire. Behind the Wall, the Vallum consisted of a flat-bottomed ditch flanked by two mounds, running parallel to the Wall for much of its length. This rear earthwork had controlled crossing points and seems to have defined the southern boundary of the military zone, preventing unauthorised access to the Wall from the Roman side. Along the Wall's length, an alternating series of forts each housed roughly six hundred men, while the milecastles between them were operated by between twelve and twenty soldiers.

## Life on the Frontier

The garrisons were not Roman legionaries but auxiliary troops recruited from across the empire -- Tungrians from Belgium, Frisians from the Netherlands, Batavians from the Rhine delta, Hamian archers from Syria. They brought their religions, languages, and customs to this remote posting. Civilian settlements called vici grew up outside the forts, providing services that the military could not: taverns, shops, bathhouses, and the company of families. Evidence from Vindolanda's famous writing tablets shows soldiers ordering socks and underpants, complaining about the weather, and receiving birthday party invitations. The frontier was not a line of constant warfare but a managed border where trade, diplomacy, and daily routine occupied far more time than combat.

## Abandonment and Afterlife

The Wall was temporarily abandoned when the Antonine Wall was built further north in Scotland around AD 142, only to be reoccupied when that frontier was given up roughly twenty years later. It continued to function as a military frontier until the end of Roman Britain around AD 410. After the Romans left, the Wall became a quarry. Its dressed stone was carted away to build churches, farmhouses, and field walls across Northumberland and Cumbria. Substantial sections disappeared entirely, particularly in the east where Newcastle and other towns expanded across the line. The military road built by General Wade after the 1745 Jacobite rising destroyed further stretches. What survives today -- primarily in the central section where the Whin Sill made the stone harder to reach -- represents only a fraction of the original structure.

## The Wall in the Landscape

Hadrian's Wall was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1987, later extended to form part of the transnational Frontiers of the Roman Empire site. The most dramatic surviving sections follow the Whin Sill, a ridge of volcanic dolerite that provides both the Wall's most spectacular scenery and its best preservation. The crags rise and fall sharply, and the Wall follows their line with an engineer's precision, exploiting every natural advantage. Walking the full length of the Hadrian's Wall Path reveals how completely the barrier was integrated into the landscape -- not imposed on it but shaped by it, rising where the ground rises, dipping where it dips, and disappearing where the terrain itself provided the defence that stone could not.

## Flight Context

Hadrian's Wall runs 73 miles across northern England from Wallsend (55.01N, 1.53W) to Bowness-on-Solway (54.95N, 3.22W). The most visible aerial section follows the Whin Sill crags in the central stretch between approximately 54.99N 2.20W and 55.02N 2.50W, where the Wall, ditch, and Vallum earthwork run parallel and are clearly distinguishable. Best viewed at 2,000-5,000 ft AGL. The rectangular outlines of forts (Housesteads, Chesters, Birdoswald) are prominent landmarks. Nearest ICAO: EGNT (Newcastle), EGNC (Carlisle).
