# Gateshead

A town of 196,151 on the Tyne's south bank, historically part of County Durham, whose industrial decline gave way to the most ambitious post-millennial cultural transformation in the English North.

**Location:** 54.9500, -1.6000

**Categories:** city, north-east-england, industrial-heritage, tyne, regeneration

## Introduction

Joseph Swan flipped a switch in his Low Fell house in 1881 and the world's first home wired for domestic electric light came on. Two years before, Edison had filed his patent in America. The argument over priority went on for years, eventually settling into a joint company. But for those two years, the only domestic electric lamp glowing anywhere on Earth burned at Underhill, Gateshead, where Swan lived from 1869 to 1883 and ran the experiments that produced the carbon-filament incandescent bulb. The town that gave the world electric light spent most of the following century in the shadow of its much larger neighbour across the Tyne. Then, in the late 1990s, it decided to stop apologising for itself.

## The Goat's Head

The name is older than English. A Brittonic predecessor - probably containing the element gabro, meaning goat (cognate with the Welsh gafr) - may underlie 'Gateshead.' Some historians have argued the site was the Roman-British fort of Gabrosentum. Christianity arrived early; Bede mentions a monastery here in the 7th century. A church burned down in 1080 with the Bishop of Durham inside. St Mary's was built near the site of that destruction and remained the only church in town until the 1820s. Historically Gateshead was part of County Durham, becoming a municipal borough in 1835 and a county borough in 1889. Under the 1974 local government reorganisation, it joined the new metropolis of Tyne and Wear. The 2021 census recorded a town population of 196,151.

## Industry and Inferno

The Industrial Revolution roughly doubled, then quadrupled, Gateshead's population - growth of over 100,000 between 1801 and 1901. The town spread southward, with Hawks, Crawshay and Sons becoming one of the largest iron concerns in Britain, employing over 2,000 across 44 acres. William Hawks had started as a blacksmith in 1747, working iron brought to the Tyne as ballast in colliers. His descendants built an industrial dynasty. In October 1854 came catastrophe: a burning worsted mill ignited stored sulphur in a quayside warehouse, the explosion levelling much of the riverfront on both sides of the Tyne and killing many; thousands of curious onlookers travelled to Gateshead by special train to view the aftermath. Most of the town's medieval architecture was lost. Hawks Crawshay closed in 1889; unemployment became a chronic burden, deepening through the 1920s and 30s and prompting the construction of the Team Valley Trading Estate in the mid-1930s as a job-creation scheme.

## Brutalism and Bensham

Post-war Gateshead acquired the Trinity Centre Car Park, a concrete megastructure designed by Owen Luder that became internationally famous as the setting for the climax of Mike Hodges's 1971 film Get Carter, in which Michael Caine's character throws a man off it. Sylvester Stallone, who took the role in the 2000 remake, backed an unsuccessful campaign to have the car park listed. It was demolished in 2010 to make way for a Tesco-anchored development. In the Bensham district, meanwhile, a community of hundreds of Orthodox Jewish families has earned the nickname 'the Oxbridge of British Jewry' - home to the Gateshead Yeshiva, founded in 1929, and several seminaries with international enrolments. Two mosques in the same district serve a sizeable Muslim community. Both populations are growing where the heavy industry left.

## The Quayside Reborn

Late-2000s council planning aimed to transform Gateshead into a city. The riverside changed almost completely - the Baltic Centre for Contemporary Art opening in a converted flour mill in 2002, the Gateshead Millennium Bridge opening in 2001 and winning the Stirling Prize in 2002, and the Sage Gateshead (now the Glasshouse International Centre for Music) following. The Angel of the North had already gone up in 1998, just south of the town. In 2000, Gateshead and Newcastle Councils teamed up to create the unified marketing brand NewcastleGateshead, recognising that the world increasingly saw the two as a single conurbation. The £150 million Trinity Square development opened in May 2013, replacing the old brutalist car park; it was nominated for the Carbuncle Cup the following year (it lost to a Tesco-led development in Woolwich).

## Brian Johnson Sings

The list of people Gateshead has sent into the world is improbably broad. Joseph Swan, the light-bulb pioneer. Catherine Booth, the 'Mother of the Salvation Army.' William Booth, its founder. Steve Cram and Jonathan Edwards, world-class athletes. Brian Johnson, who replaced Bon Scott as the lead singer of AC/DC. Paul Gascoigne, the most gifted English footballer of his generation. William Shield, Master of the King's Musick. The triple-jumper Jonathan Edwards is now the patron of the Great North Museum's fundraising. Twin towns abroad: Saint-Etienne-du-Rouvray near Rouen, and Komatsu in Japan. Gateshead International Stadium hosts athletics meetings on summer weekends - a working town hosting the long throws and high jumps. The town that lost its medieval heritage to fire and its industry to globalisation has slowly become, by reinvention, something else: a place worth crossing a bridge for.
