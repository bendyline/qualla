# Milton Keynes

The most ambitious experiment in British urban planning - a city designed from scratch on twenty-two thousand acres of Buckinghamshire farmland, built grid square by grid square, and still arguing about whether it worked.

## Introduction

In January 1967, a designated area of 21,883 acres of Buckinghamshire farmland was handed to a development corporation with an instruction that has not been issued in Britain before or since: build us a city. Not a town, not an extension of an existing settlement, but a planned city for 250,000 people, drawn fresh on the map, with a target population larger than any of the postwar new towns that had come before. The designation was a gamble. The planners who designed Milton Keynes deliberately rejected the centralized British model in favour of an American-influenced grid - a non-hierarchical layout inspired by the theorist Melvin M. Webber, who believed that cities of the future would prize movement over proximity. The result has been argued about for sixty years. Whatever you think of the answer, the question itself is still one of the most interesting things ever asked about how Britain should be lived in.

## The Brief and the Boundary

The pressure was housing. London in the 1960s could not absorb its own growth, and the postwar new towns - Stevenage, Harlow, Crawley - were filling up. Whitehall studied north Buckinghamshire and decided that an area straddling the existing towns of Bletchley, Fenny Stratford, Wolverton and Stony Stratford could become something larger. Bletchley had already been receiving London overspill housing since the 1950s. The four towns and fifteen villages already inside the designated boundary held about 40,000 people. The plan was to add 210,000 more, on a scale and to a design that would make the result self-sustaining: not a dormitory satellite of London but a regional capital in its own right. The name 'Milton Keynes' was lifted from one of the small historic villages caught inside the boundary - a quiet bit of camouflage to give a brand-new city an old-sounding name.

## Grid Squares and a Father of the City

Derek Walker, the founding chief architect, called Melvin Webber 'the father of the city.' Webber, working at Berkeley in the early 1960s, had argued that telecommunications made the old concentric model of a city obsolete. People needed to move freely, not cluster around a single centre. Milton Keynes Development Corporation translated the theory into hardware: a grid of main roads running roughly every kilometre, with the resulting kilometre squares - known as grid squares - each containing a self-contained district with its own local centre. The H roads and V roads run between the squares; they do not run through them. Within each square is a neighbourhood of streets, schools, shops and houses that residents can navigate on foot or via the redway cycle network. Central Milton Keynes was designed not as a traditional town centre but as a central business and shopping district to complement, not replace, the local centres. The intention was equality - no neighbourhood feeling like a backwater of someplace else.

## The Forest City

The planners called it the forest city concept, and they meant it. Twenty-two million trees and shrubs have been planted in Milton Keynes's public spaces, grown initially in the development corporation's own nursery at Newlands. Approximately twenty-five per cent of the urban area is parkland or woodland. Two Sites of Special Scientific Interest - Howe Park Wood and Oxley Mead - lie inside the city limits. The Ouzel river valley and the flood plains of the Great Ouse were protected as linear parks from the outset, partly for landscape value and partly because the clay soils made flood management urgent. The total park area runs to 1,650 hectares, ten times the size of London's Hyde Park. The landscape architects, recognizing they could not manage that scale on the Royal Parks model, divided the linear parks into 'strings, beads and settings' - circulation routes, activity hubs, and self-managed zones like woods, paddocks, golf course and farm. Most of the parks are owned not by the council but by a charitable trust, deliberately isolated from political budget cycles.

## The Tale of Two Cities

The criticism was loud from the start. Britain's first 'American city,' jeered the architectural press. Soulless. Anonymous. The mocked icon was Liz Leyh's Concrete Cows - a 1978 sculpture in a field by Bancroft, intended affectionately and adopted by the national imagination as proof that Milton Keynes had no real cows, only fake ones. Sixty years in, the criticism has softened but not vanished. Architectural historians now describe Milton Keynes as having 'stood the test of time far better than most' of the British new towns, calling it flexible and adaptable. The town competed unsuccessfully for formal city status in 2000, 2002 and 2012; in 2022, the borough finally won the title in the Queen's Platinum Jubilee Civic Honours competition. The 2021 census recorded 264,349 people in the urban area - within striking distance of the original 250,000 target. The city also lives with the same problems as everywhere else. A 2016 'Vital Signs' report from the Community Foundation described affluence and deprivation living next to each other and called it 'a Tale of Two Cities.' In 2018, rough sleeping in tents around the central area became a national story, the apex of a national failure rather than a local one.

## The Layered Landscape

What the planners never tried to hide is that they were building on top of a deeply lived-in landscape. Before construction began, every part of the designated area was archaeologically surveyed. The results provided one of the largest coordinated insights into the prehistory and history of south-central England ever undertaken. Stone Age artefacts, late Bronze Age and early Iron Age sites, Romano-British settlements, Anglo-Saxon and Anglo-Norman villages - all sit beneath the new grid. A hoard of Iron Age gold torcs and bracelets, now in the British Museum, was found within the boundary. Bletchley Park, where Allied codebreakers read the German Enigma during the Second World War, lies at the southern end of the urban area; the National Museum of Computing next to it preserves a working rebuild of Colossus, the first programmable electronic digital computer. The grid squares of Milton Keynes have absorbed villages whose churches predate the Norman Conquest. The future, here, is built on a very long past.

## Flight Context

Milton Keynes lies at 52.033°N, 0.767°W in north Buckinghamshire, about fifty miles northwest of London. From altitude the planned grid is unmistakable - a regular pattern of main roads at one-kilometre intervals, with green linear parks following the Ouzel and Great Ouse rivers. The northern boundary is marked by the Great Ouse and the Wolverton Iron Trunk aqueduct of the Grand Union Canal. Cranfield (EGTC) sits ten miles east; London Luton (EGGW) is twenty-five miles south, and London Heathrow (EGLL) is fifty-five miles south via the M1 and M25. The West Coast Main Line runs north-south through the urban area, a useful visual reference.
