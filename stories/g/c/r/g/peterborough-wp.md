# Peterborough

A Fenland cathedral city built on Anglo-Saxon foundations, Roman pottery, and a 20th-century empire of bricks.

**Location:** 52.5695, -0.2405

**Categories:** Cities in England, Cathedrals, Industrial heritage, Fenland, New Towns

## Introduction

The town's first name was Medeshamstede, an Anglian word that no modern tongue uses with confidence. By the late tenth century it had been replaced by Burgh, after Abbot Kenulf wrapped his monastery in a defensive wall and dedicated it to Saint Peter. Burgh became Peter's burgh became Peterborough. The story of this Fenland city is the story of names that stuck to walls and walls that outlasted kingdoms.

## A Monastery on Drained Ground

The Romans found the place first. They built Durobrivae five miles west on Ermine Street and a fort at Longthorpe large enough for half a legion, and they made the Nene Valley one of the great ceramic workshops of Roman Britain. Pots fired here travelled as far as Cornwall and the Antonine Wall. When the empire withdrew, the kilns went cold and the land returned to marsh. Around AD 655 a Mercian abbot named Sexwulf founded a monastery on a strip of permanently drained ground that the River Nene had built up at the Fen edge. The Mercian king Peada had granted him the land, then Peada's brother Wulfhere finished the buildings as atonement for murdering his own sons. The monastery would burn, be sacked by Hereward the Wake in 1069 or 1070, and rise again. Between 1118 and 1238 the abbey church was rebuilt to the form that still stands - one of the most complete Norman cathedrals in England, even though it was not yet a cathedral.

## Of Catherines and Old Scarlett

When Henry VIII dissolved the abbey in 1539, the building survived because the king gave it a second life: in 1541 it became Peterborough Cathedral, and the last abbot was made the first bishop. Henry had buried his discarded first wife, Catherine of Aragon, in the south aisle two years earlier. Mary, Queen of Scots was buried here too, after her execution at nearby Fotheringhay in 1587, though her son James later moved her bones to Westminster. The gravedigger who handled both queens was an actual man named Old Scarlett. His portrait hangs above the cathedral's west door. He died in 1594 aged 98, and the lines beneath his portrait give him a Shakespearean credit he probably never earned - some have wondered whether he inspired the gravedigger in Hamlet. Whether or not, the wooden Peterborough Chronicle written by monks here in the 12th century gave English something rarer: the only known prose history of England between the Conquest and the late 14th century, kept alive in a tongue the Normans were busy displacing.

## The Town the Bricks Built

Railways made Peterborough modern. The Great Northern's London line opened in 1850 and turned a market town into a hub between London and Doncaster. Then engineers at Fletton, in the 1890s, worked out how to fire the harder Oxford Clay from a lower seam, and the brick industry that followed was extraordinary. The London Brick Company, founded by the Scottish builder John Cathles Hill, made Peterborough the leading producer of bricks in the United Kingdom for much of the twentieth century. Fletton bricks were cheap, regular, and almost everywhere - in interwar suburbs, in council estates, in factories that shipped their own goods. Perkins Engines arrived in 1932; by 1962 it employed more than a tenth of the city. Baker Perkins and Peter Brotherhood made industrial machinery here. The town that had bricked Britain was now also building the diesel engines and bakery ovens that ran inside its other buildings.

## New Town, Italian Town

In 1967 Whitehall designated Peterborough a New Town. London's overspill was to be settled in townships at Bretton, Orton, Paston, and a fourth at Castor that never happened. Population jumped 45 percent between 1971 and 1991. The car parkways laid out across the new boroughs still give Peterborough the fastest peak travel times of any British city its size. Thomas Cook moved here; Pearl Assurance came; financial services replaced the brick fields. Quietly, another transformation was underway. The London Brick Company had been recruiting in southern Italy through the 1950s, and by 1960 around three thousand men from Apulia and Campania were working at the Fletton kilns. In 1962 the Scalabrini Fathers bought an old school and made it San Giuseppe, the mission church to the patron saint of workers. By 1996 the Italian community here numbered roughly seven thousand, the third largest in the UK after London and Bedford.

## Gateway to the Fens

Peterborough sits where drained land meets the marsh, and parts of its borough lie below sea level - at Holme Fen, just south of the city, the ground is 2.75 metres below mean sea level, the lowest point on the British mainland. East Anglia is dry by British standards and slightly more continental, and from the air the geometry of the Fens is unmistakable: arrow-straight drainage cuts, fields stitched to the horizon, the cathedral's three west-front gables a single landmark above flat ground. Flag Fen, discovered in 1982, gave back a Bronze Age wooden causeway and what is thought to be the oldest wheel in Britain. The Nene viaduct - cast iron, 1850, by Sir William and Joseph Cubitt - remains the oldest surviving cast-iron railway bridge in the country. Names accumulate here the way silt does. Medeshamstede became Burgh; Burgh became Peterborough; the New Town added neighbourhoods that themselves now feel old.

## Flight Context

Peterborough lies at 52.57°N, 0.24°W, on the western edge of the Fens. From cruise on the London-Edinburgh airway the cathedral's three west gables stand out against arrow-straight Fenland drainage. The nearest major airports are London Stansted (EGSS, 50 nm south) and East Midlands (EGNX, 45 nm west-northwest); RAF Wittering (EGXT) is 7 nm west. Visibility from cruise is excellent in clear East Anglian winters; expect haze in summer.
