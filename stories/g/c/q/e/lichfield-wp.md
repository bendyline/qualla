# Lichfield

A red-sandstone city where Bishop Chad of Mercia died in 672, the largest Anglo-Saxon gold hoard ever found was unearthed in a nearby field, and the last man burned at the stake for heresy in England met his end in the market place.

**Location:** 52.6815, -1.8300

**Categories:** Cathedral cities, Staffordshire, Anglo-Saxon archaeology, Mercia

## Introduction

In July 2009 a metal detectorist named Terry Herbert was working a field at Hammerwich, four miles southwest of Lichfield, when his coil started screaming. By the time the archaeologists finished, more than 4,600 items had been pulled out of the Staffordshire ground: gold sword fittings, helmet cheek-pieces, ornamental crosses, garnet inlays the colour of pomegranate seeds. The Staffordshire Hoard, weighing more than five kilograms of gold and over a kilogram of silver, was the largest Anglo-Saxon precious-metal find in history. It had probably been buried in the 7th century - the same century when, three miles up the road, a missionary bishop named Chad arrived from Northumbria in 669 and set up his see at a place called Licidfelth. Lichfield's history starts there. The fact that someone hid a king's ransom in gold within a day's walk of the cathedral is one of the great unexplained intimacies of the early English centuries.

## Greywood and a Saint

The Romans were here first. At Wall, three and a half kilometres south of the modern city, the legionaries built a fort called Letocetum - a Brittonic name meaning Greywood - at the crossroads of Watling Street and Ryknild Street, the two great Roman roads that still survive as the A5 and the A38. By the second century AD Letocetum had a bath house, a mansio for travellers, and a small town. When the Romans left, the name passed into Old Welsh as Luitcoyt and then into Old English as Licid, eventually combined with the Anglo-Saxon word feld - open country - to become Lichfield. Chad of Mercia arrived in 669 from York, took possession of the place from Bishop Wilfrid, and built his cathedral on the sandstone ridge above what would become Minster Pool. He died on 2 March 672. His shrine made Lichfield one of the great pilgrimage sites of medieval England.

## Three Spires, Two Sieges

The Gothic cathedral that stands today was begun in 1195 and largely finished by 1330. It is the only medieval cathedral in the British Isles with three spires: a central tower spire and two western towers. During the English Civil War, the Cathedral Close was fortified and besieged three times. Parliamentary commander Lord Brooke led the first assault in March 1643 and was killed by a sniper - shot in the eye - on St Chad's Day, a date local royalists treated as divine vengeance. The close yielded shortly after, was retaken by Prince Rupert later that year, and finally surrendered to Parliament in 1646. The central spire was destroyed; the building was wrecked. Bishop John Hacket led the restoration after 1660, with Charles II contributing both money and political support. The cathedral has been continuously repaired and conserved ever since. The Herkenrode glass in the Lady Chapel, brought from a dissolved Belgian monastery in 1801, is among the finest 16th-century stained glass anywhere.

## Wightman Burned

On 11 April 1612, Edward Wightman was burned to death in Lichfield's Market Place. He was a draper from Burton-upon-Trent who had embraced an extreme Anabaptist theology and declared himself the Paraclete - the Holy Spirit - and Saviour of the world. King James I, fresh from his work on the new Bible translation, had personally intervened in the case. Wightman became the last person ever burned alive for heresy in England. The execution had not gone smoothly: an earlier attempt to burn him a few weeks before had ended when Wightman, scorched but alive, recanted and was pulled out of the flames. He recanted again afterwards. On the second attempt no rescue came. The square where it happened is now full of cafes and Johnson statues and the seventeenth-century half-timbered front of Three Spires House. The plaque commemorating Wightman is quiet, easy to miss, and worth standing in front of for a moment. Three burnings at the stake for heresy under Mary I had happened in Lichfield before him; Wightman's was the last anywhere in the country.

## The City of Philosophers

Lichfield's golden age was the 18th century. The town sat on the coaching route between London and Chester, with traffic peaking between 1800 and 1840, and inns and stables and corn-merchants thrived. Samuel Johnson was born on Breadmarket Street in 1709, the son of a struggling bookseller. David Garrick, the actor who reinvented English performance, was born in 1717 and grew up in the city. Erasmus Darwin - physician, poet, evolutionary thinker, grandfather of Charles - lived on Beacon Street and hosted the meetings of the Lunar Society, drawing in Joseph Priestley, Matthew Boulton, James Watt and Josiah Wedgwood. Anna Seward, the poet known as the Swan of Lichfield, wrote here. Johnson's remark that Lichfield was 'a city of philosophers' was not hyperbole - it was demographic fact. Defoe in the 1720s called Lichfield 'a fine, neat, well-built, and indifferent large city', the principal town of the region after Chester. The Georgian red-brick houses lining the streets today date from this prosperity.

## What Lasted

The railway killed coaching, and Lichfield's main-route importance went with it. The Industrial Revolution that transformed Birmingham fifteen miles south barely touched the place. The market gardens around the city kept feeding the Black Country into the early 20th century. Brewing took over from coaching. Today Lichfield holds around 35,000 people and over 230 listed buildings, an unusually high concentration for a small English city. Armitage Shanks bathroom fittings and Arthur Price of England cutlery and silver remain local manufacturers. The Staffordshire Hoard, now displayed across Birmingham Museum and the Potteries Museum in Stoke-on-Trent, draws visitors who would never have come for the cathedral alone. Pilgrims still come too - the Diocese of Lichfield is one of the oldest in England, and although St Chad's shrine was destroyed in 1538, the bones the medieval monks rescued are still venerated, in their casket above the altar at the Catholic cathedral in Birmingham. Even the body parts of Lichfield's saint have travelled, and come back, and travelled again.

## Flight Context

Lichfield sits at 52.681 degrees north, 1.829 degrees west, in southeast Staffordshire at roughly 100 metres elevation - lower in the centre, higher on the ridge where the cathedral stands. Best viewed from 2,500 to 4,500 feet AGL, the city shows as a compact urban centre with two distinct lakes (Minster Pool and Stowe Pool) and the three spires of the cathedral on the sandstone shelf between them. Birmingham Airport (EGBB) is about 16 nautical miles south-southeast. East Midlands Airport (EGNX) lies 24 nautical miles to the northeast. Look for Cannock Chase rising green to the northwest and the line of the West Coast Main Line passing on the east side of the city near Trent Valley station.
