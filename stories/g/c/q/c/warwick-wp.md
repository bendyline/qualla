# Warwick

A Warwickshire county town whose history reaches from Neolithic flint pits through a Roman barn, Anglo-Saxon mint, Norman castle, medieval guild hall, Great Fire, Georgian rebuild, and the 1571 charitable foundation that still houses retired soldiers in dark-blue Elizabethan gowns.

## Introduction

Beneath the cricket pitch of Warwick School, archaeologists in 2017 found the footings of a Roman barn from the second century AD. It was not the first thing under the soil. Deeper still, the same site yielded thirty shallow pits with early Neolithic flints and pottery, and the post holes of rectangular buildings raised by Early European Farmers somewhere between 4000 and 2351 BC. A Bell Beaker arrowhead lay among them, dated 2500 to 1700 BC. Six thousand years of human habitation in one hillside above the River Avon. The reasons people kept choosing the spot are still readable in the geography: a defensible outcrop of sandstone, a river crossing, fresh water, building material to hand.

## Aethelflaed's Burh

The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle records that in 914 Aethelflaed, Lady of the Mercians and daughter of Alfred the Great, built a burh at Warwick - a fortified town, one of ten she raised to defend Mercia against Viking incursion. The choice of site was strategic: a hilltop overlooking the earlier riverside settlement, with sandstone for walls and the Avon's river crossing to control. Within a generation Warwick had become a county town with its own shire and, during the reign of Aethelstan in the 920s and 930s, a royal mint. The mint continued striking coins until the mid-twelfth century. The Danes burned much of Warwick including a nunnery in 1016, but the settlement was rebuilt. There has probably been a grammar school in Warwick continuously since before the Norman Conquest; Warwick School, claiming foundation in 914, may well be the oldest boys' school in England, though the actual founding date is unclear and Henry VIII formally re-founded the institution in 1545.

## What the Fire Did Not Take

The Great Fire of Warwick on 5 September 1694 destroyed 460 buildings in five hours, but two medieval survivors deserve attention because they are visible today. The Lord Leycester Hospital on the High Street, against the medieval West Gate, occupies timber-framed guild buildings dating from 1383 - among the most complete surviving medieval guildhalls in England. Robert Dudley, Earl of Leicester, refounded the complex in 1571 as a charitable home for retired or disabled servicemen, calling them the Brethren and dressing them in dark-blue gowns marked with his bear-and-ragged-staff badge. The institution has not closed since. Brethren still live there today, still wear the gowns, still occupy rooms whose oak beams have been in place for more than six centuries. The other great survivor is the Beauchamp Chapel of the Collegiate Church of St Mary - built between 1443 and 1464 to house the tomb of Richard Beauchamp, Earl of Warwick, who died in Rouen in 1439. His full-size copper-gilt effigy lies on a tomb of Purbeck marble, cast in 1459 in what is widely considered the finest piece of medieval metalwork to survive in England.

## The Castle, the Tourists, the Trebuchet

Warwick Castle was begun by William the Conqueror in 1068 on his way north to deal with rebellion in Yorkshire. It stood inside the larger Anglo-Saxon burh and gave the town a continuous Norman military presence at its southern edge. The Beauchamp earls of the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries built much of what visitors see today - the great curtain walls along the river, the towers, Guy's Tower and Caesar's Tower. The Greville family bought it in 1604 after the Beauchamp and Dudley lines had ended. They held it as a family seat until 1978, when Madame Tussauds (now part of Merlin Entertainments) acquired the castle and reopened it as a tourist attraction. It remains the most-visited castle in the United Kingdom, drawing more than a million visitors a year to its towers, dungeons, and the riverside lawns where stunt performers stage daily medieval spectacles.

## Tolkien, Tramways, Anthony Eden

J.R.R. Tolkien married Edith Bratt at the Catholic Church of Saint Mary Immaculate in Warwick in 1916; a blue plaque marks the spot. The Tolkien scholar Lynn Forest-Hill has argued in detail that Warwick - the Saxon hilltop town under the Norman castle - influenced Tolkien's Edoras and Minas Tirith. Christopher Tolkien wrote in The Book of Lost Tales that Kortirion, capital of Tol Eressea in his father's invented mythology, would in after days become Warwick. The town gave up its tram service in 1930 after fifty years of running a line over Portobello Bridge to Leamington Spa. Politically it returned a Conservative MP for the Warwick and Leamington constituency continuously from 1923 to 1997 - including Sir Anthony Eden, Prime Minister 1955-57, who held the seat for thirty-four years. Labour took the seat in 1997, lost it in 2010, and won it back in 2017.

## Tudor and Stuart, Mostly Stuart

The town that emerged from the 1694 fire is what most visitors come to see now: a coherent late-Stuart and Georgian centre laid out around the surviving medieval church and the rebuilt Court House on Jury Street, completed in 1731. The Shire Hall, built 1758-76, still houses Warwickshire County Council. Castle Bridge, the elegant single sandstone arch over the Avon at the castle's eastern flank, opened in 1793 and replaced the medieval Old Castle Bridge whose ruined arches can still be seen downstream. Warwick declined a Platinum Jubilee bid for city status in 2022 - it ranked among 38 contenders, was not one of the eight successful applicants, and remains a market town and civil parish of about 36,000 inhabitants. The University of Warwick, despite the name, sits four miles to the north on the southern outskirts of Coventry. The town that has been here for six thousand years continues, with some equanimity, to be a town.

## Flight Context

Located at 52.28N, 1.59W, the historic county town of Warwickshire on a sandstone outcrop above the River Avon. Warwick Castle and its riverside walls are visible in the southeast of the town centre, with the tower of St Mary's Collegiate Church marking the centre. The M40 motorway crosses to the south, joining Warwick to Leamington Spa and Whitnash in a continuous conurbation. Nearest airports: EGBE (Coventry, 7nm NE), EGBB (Birmingham, 18nm NW).
