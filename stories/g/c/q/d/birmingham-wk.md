# Birmingham

Britain's second-largest city, the Workshop of the World, where Black Sabbath and HP Sauce and the Spitfire were all invented, and where the canals outnumber Venice's by a wide margin.

**Location:** 52.4800, -1.9025

**Categories:** Cities in England, West Midlands (county), Wikivoyage

## Introduction

The h is silent. Birm-ing-um, not Birm-ing-ham, and locals will tolerate the wrong pronunciation only briefly. They call the place Brum. They call themselves Brummies. Britain's second-largest city sits at the centre of the Midlands like a hub on a wheel -- motorways radiating outward, canals threading through the centre, a vast and somewhat baffling rail station underneath everything. The Victorian era christened Birmingham the City of a Thousand Trades and the Workshop of the World, and the city has spent the years since the war first being demolished by bombs, then being smothered in concrete, and finally -- since the 1990s -- rebuilding itself into a place worth a long weekend. The skyline keeps changing. The accent does not.

## Bombs, Brutalism, and the Comeback

The Birmingham Blitz of 1940-43 destroyed about a fifth of the city centre. The postwar reconstruction filled the holes with concrete that aged badly. By the 1980s the centre was choked by the Inner Ring Road and its underpasses, a city wrapped in its own bypass. The 1990s and 2000s reversed the trend: chunks of the ring road came down, central New Street became fully pedestrianised, and the canal towpaths at Brindleyplace and Gas Street Basin were cleaned up into walkable, cafe-lined corridors. The Bullring shopping centre was demolished and replaced in 2003 with the spectacular Future Systems Selfridges building -- a curving, scale-clad blob that looks like a sea creature dropped onto a Victorian church. New Street station was rebuilt as Grand Central in 2015. The Library of Birmingham opened in 2013, its golden filigree facade lifting Centenary Square. Each new building has been a quiet apology for whatever the previous decade installed.

## More Canals Than Venice

The Birmingham Canal Navigations -- the dense network of cuts built between 1772 and the 1840s -- gave the city more miles of canal than any other place in Europe, more than Venice itself. They are different sorts of canals, of course: not lined with palazzi but with brick warehouses and former forges, narrow and made for slow narrowboats rather than gondolas. About 100 miles remain navigable. The central showpieces are Gas Street Basin, where the Worcester and Birmingham Canal meets the BCN Main Line at a famous historical division called the Worcester Bar, and Brindleyplace just to the north -- an entire reconfigured wharf district of bars, restaurants, the International Convention Centre and the National Sea Life Centre. You can walk from the ICC to The Mailbox along the canal in about ten minutes, never touching a road, never sharing the path with anything faster than a cyclist.

## The Districts Locals Argue About

Birmingham proper is a sprawling city of 1,144,900 people at the 2021 census, taking in dozens of former towns and villages that were absorbed over the past century and a half. Locals divide it into wards and districts that mean nothing to outsiders. The Jewellery Quarter, just north-west of the city centre, still produces roughly 40 percent of the UK's jewellery and houses the country's largest Assay Office; its nineteenth-century brick streets are a designated conservation area. Aston, to the north, gave the world Black Sabbath and is home to Villa Park. Edgbaston, to the south-west, contains the University of Birmingham and the county cricket ground. Bournville, further south-west, is the Cadbury chocolate village -- a model settlement where alcohol is still not sold within the historic estate boundaries. Digbeth, just east of the Bullring, has become the creative quarter. Moseley remains the Brummie idea of bohemian. Sutton Coldfield, once a market town in its own right, sits at the northern edge of the city next to Sutton Park -- the largest urban national nature reserve in Europe.

## Balti, Bands and Tolkien

Birmingham's food scene is shaped by its post-war South Asian migration. The Balti Triangle in Sparkbrook, Balsall Heath and Sparkhill perfected the Birmingham balti -- a particular curry served in a thin pressed-steel two-handled bowl, eaten with naan. The dish spread from these streets to almost every British high street. The city's music history is similarly disproportionate: Black Sabbath, Judas Priest, UB40, Duran Duran, The Beat, Napalm Death, Steel Pulse, and an extraordinary roster of jazz musicians who passed through the Birmingham Conservatoire. The BBC drama Peaky Blinders took the city's real Edwardian gang heritage and ran it as a global hit. The author J. R. R. Tolkien grew up in Sarehole and based much of the Shire on Worcestershire countryside south of the city. Washington Irving wrote Rip Van Winkle while lodging with his sister's family in Birmingham. Conan Doyle bought his first violin on Sherlock Street while a medical student here. The associations pile up the longer you look.

## Getting Around

New Street station is the rail hub: 36.6 million annual passenger movements, services radiating to London, Manchester, Bristol, Edinburgh, Cardiff. Snow Hill and Moor Street -- both within ten minutes' walk of New Street -- handle most local services and the Chiltern Railways route to London Marylebone, which is slower than Avanti's New Street trains but cheaper and far more scenic. Birmingham Airport (EGBB) sits 7 miles east; an automatic Air-Rail Link people-mover connects it to Birmingham International station in under two minutes. The West Midlands Metro is a single 22 km tram line running between Wolverhampton and Edgbaston via central Birmingham, with extensions planned. The city centre is mostly pedestrianised and walkable; Birmingham's bus network is operated chiefly by National Express West Midlands, with the Outer Circle service 11A/11C providing a famously useful 26-mile loop around the suburbs that ranks among the longest urban bus routes in Europe. The City Council operates car parks throughout the centre; a congestion charge applies inside the A4540 Middleway ring road, though low-emission vehicles are exempt. Most visitors do not need a car. Most visitors should also not assume that an address with "Birmingham" on it is anywhere near the centre -- the city limits are very large.

## Flight Context

Located at 52.48N, 1.90W in the West Midlands of England, in the centre of the Birmingham conurbation. Visible landmarks from the air include the silver-disc Selfridges building, the curving Library of Birmingham, the Rotunda tower, Villa Park stadium to the north, and the Spaghetti Junction motorway interchange (Gravelly Hill, M6/A38(M)). Nearest airports: EGBB (Birmingham, 5nm ESE), EGBE (Coventry, 22nm ESE), EGOC (RAF Cosford, 14nm W). Best viewed at 2,000-4,000 ft AGL.
