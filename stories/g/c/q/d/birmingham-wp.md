# Birmingham

Britain's second city, with a population of 1.14 million and more listed buildings than anywhere outside London -- a thousand trades, hundreds of canals, and one of Europe's most diverse migrant populations.

**Location:** 52.4800, -1.9025

**Categories:** Birmingham, West Midlands, Cities in the West Midlands (county), Local government districts of the West Midlands (county)

## Introduction

Birmingham has more listed buildings than any English city outside London, more parks than any other city in Europe, and more miles of canal than Venice. The superlatives stack up faster than the city can advertise them. Behind every one is the same story: this is a place that grew by making things, which gave it a vast inventory of buildings dedicated to making, and a population pulled together from across the world to do the making. The 2021 census recorded 1,144,900 people inside the city boundary -- an increase of 6.7 percent in a decade -- making Birmingham the 27th largest city in Europe by population within municipal limits. The wider Larger Urban Zone holds well over two million. Around 305,688 residents were foreign-born, one of the largest migrant populations of any European city.

## The City of a Thousand Trades

The Victorian nickname was not metaphor. Birmingham's industrial profile, unlike Manchester's cotton or Sheffield's steel, was a deliberate refusal to specialise. The city's prosperity came from thousands of small workshops, each making something different and trading parts with the workshop next door. Buttons, pen nibs, jewellery, brass fittings, screws, gun parts, locks, lamps, electroplated cutlery, custard powder, cocoa, HP Sauce, bicycle bells, automobile components -- the city made any of it and sometimes all of it. The Soho Manufactory of Matthew Boulton and James Watt produced steam engines that ran the Industrial Revolution. The Lunar Society, which met in Birmingham from the 1760s, brought together Boulton, Watt, Joseph Priestley, Erasmus Darwin and Josiah Wedgwood -- a cluster of inventors and natural philosophers whose work would change manufacturing, chemistry and biology across the world.

## Parks, Canals, and Green Belt

Birmingham has 571 parks, more than any other European city, totalling over 3,500 hectares of public open space. Sutton Park, in the north of the city, is the largest urban park in Europe and a designated national nature reserve. Cannon Hill Park, Lickey Hills Country Park, Handsworth Park, Kings Heath Park and Woodgate Valley Country Park provide further green lungs across the city. The Winterbourne Botanic Garden in Edgbaston reflects the more informal Arts and Crafts horticultural ideas of its Edwardian origins. Birmingham sits inside the West Midlands Green Belt, the planning policy that prevents the city sprawling outward into the Worcestershire and Staffordshire countryside. Within the green belt the city retains room for wildlife corridors -- Project Kingfisher along the River Cole, Woodgate Valley, Pype Hayes Park -- and the Birmingham Wildlife Conservation Park at Cannon Hill houses a small zoo focused on conservation rather than spectacle.

## The Migrant City

Birmingham's population in 2021 was 48.7 percent White (42.9 percent White British, 1.5 percent White Irish, 4.0 percent Other White, plus small Roma and Irish Traveller populations); 31 percent Asian (17.0 percent Pakistani, 5.8 percent Indian, 4.2 percent Bangladeshi, 1.1 percent Chinese, 2.9 percent Other Asian); 10.9 percent Black (5.8 percent African, 3.9 percent Caribbean, 1.2 percent Other Black); 4.8 percent Mixed; and 1.7 percent Arab. No single ethnic group makes up an outright majority. Around 26.7 percent of residents in 2021 were foreign-born. The city's mosques, gurdwaras, mandirs, synagogues and churches are spread across the wards: the Birmingham Central Mosque on Belgrave Middleway, the Guru Nanak Nishkam Sewak Jatha gurdwara at Soho Road, the BAPS Shri Swaminarayan Mandir in Hodge Hill. South Asian migration shaped the food scene; Caribbean migration shaped the music and the Carnival in Handsworth Park; Yemeni and East African migration shaped distinct neighbourhoods. The city's name for itself, Brum, originally a working-class shortening, is now a label of pride.

## What Was Built and Rebuilt

The Birmingham Blitz of 1940-43 destroyed roughly a fifth of the central business district. The post-war reconstruction filled the gaps with concrete in the New Brutalist style; the Inner Ring Road, completed in 1971, isolated the city centre inside a concrete necklace that took decades to undo. Since the 1980s, much of that work has been deliberately reversed. Sections of the Inner Ring Road have been demolished and replaced with new public squares -- Centenary Square, Chamberlain Square, Victoria Square. The Bullring shopping centre was demolished and rebuilt in 2003 around the curving, scale-clad Selfridges building by Future Systems. The Library of Birmingham opened in 2013 in Centenary Square, its delicate aluminium filigree facade replacing the brutalist Central Library demolished the same year. The Mailbox, formerly the Royal Mail sorting office, became upscale shopping and restaurants. New Street station was rebuilt as Grand Central in 2015. Through it all the Birmingham Cathedral of St Philip, consecrated in 1715, has kept its distinct Baroque silhouette intact above the redeveloping skyline.

## Inventions and Exports

Birmingham's contribution to the world's inventory of things is wildly disproportionate to its size. The flushing toilet (Thomas Crapper actually came from Yorkshire, but Birmingham's George Jennings popularised the design through Crystal Palace exhibitions). The pneumatic tyre (John Boyd Dunlop developed it in Belfast but his company Dunlop Rubber was Birmingham-based for most of the twentieth century). The Mini car. The Spitfire fighter (assembled at Castle Bromwich during WWII). Cadbury chocolate. HP Sauce. Custard powder. The board game Cluedo, invented in 1944 by Anthony Pratt of Balsall Heath. The cavity magnetron that made wartime radar practical was developed at the University of Birmingham by John Randall and Harry Boot in 1940; its design was secretly carried to America in the Tizard Mission to enable mass production. The Frisbee-throwing of Birmingham's musical exports -- Black Sabbath inventing heavy metal in Aston, UB40 inventing British reggae in Balsall Heath, Duran Duran inventing the New Romantic in city-centre clubs -- belongs in the same column. The Workshop of the World still does its work.

## Flight Context

Located at 52.48N, 1.90W in the West Midlands of England, at the centre of the Birmingham conurbation that extends from Wolverhampton in the west to Coventry in the east. The Birmingham Larger Urban Zone covers roughly 600 square miles and includes the Metropolitan Boroughs of Dudley, Sandwell, Solihull and Walsall. From the air, the Selfridges sphere, the Library of Birmingham, the Rotunda, Villa Park stadium, and the Spaghetti Junction motorway interchange (Gravelly Hill, M6/A38(M)) all provide navigational landmarks. Nearest airports: EGBB (Birmingham, 5nm ESE), EGBE (Coventry, 22nm ESE), EGOC (RAF Cosford, 14nm W). Best viewed at 3,000-5,000 ft AGL to take in the wider conurbation.
