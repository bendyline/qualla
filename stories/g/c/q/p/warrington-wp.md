# Warrington

A Roman crossing on the Mersey, a Saxon ford, a wire-making town that built half of Britain's chemical industry, and a 1968 New Town that doubled its population in fifty years.

**Location:** 53.3873, -2.6029

**Categories:** Towns in Cheshire, Warrington, New towns in England, History of Cheshire

## Introduction

The Romans were here first, at Wilderspool just south of the modern town, drawn to a place where the Mersey could be crossed without too much trouble. The Saxons came next, and gave the settlement the name it still carries: Weringtun, the enclosure of the guardians of the ford. There has been a bridge at Warrington since at least 1285, and there has almost always been someone trying to charge a toll to cross it. The river has organised everything else. Industry pressed up against it. The Manchester Ship Canal cut alongside it. Motorways were eventually laid around it. The town that grew out of all of this now has a population of about 211,000 in the wider borough, more than double what it had in 1968 when central government declared Warrington a New Town and started moving Liverpool families out to slum-clearance estates in Birchwood and Westy.

## Romans, Saxons, and a Civil War Crossing

The Roman settlement at Wilderspool sat at a strategic point on the Mersey, with road links north into Lancashire and south into Cheshire. Local archaeology has also turned up Bronze Age finds, suggesting the crossing was important well before the Romans formalised it. The medieval town clustered around St Elphin's Church, where the river was forded about a kilometre upstream of the present Warrington Bridge. By 1285 a bridge had been built; in 1321, Warrington became the first paved town in Lancashire. The English Civil War caught the town up in 1648 and 1651. The last Royalist victory of the war came on 13 August 1651 at the Battle of Warrington Bridge, when Scottish troops under Charles II and David Leslie pushed back Parliamentarian forces under John Lambert. The cannonball pocks rumoured to mar the walls of St Elphin's date to those fights, or so local tradition has it.

## The Industrial Revolution Arrives by Water

Warrington's growth into a serious manufacturing town tracks the rivers and the canals. The Mersey was made navigable in the eighteenth century. Brewing, tanning, soap-making and chemicals followed; so did textiles, then steel wire, the trade that earned the rugby league club the nickname The Wire. The Manchester Ship Canal opened in 1894, allowing seagoing freighters to thread forty miles inland from the Mersey estuary, and Warrington became a working canal town as well as a riverside one. The Crosfield soap and chemical works on Bank Quay grew so large that its two halves on opposite banks of the Mersey were eventually linked by a transporter bridge, built in 1915 and still standing today, the last railway transporter bridge in the world. The Warrington Academy, where Joseph Priestley taught between 1761 and 1767, briefly earned the town the nickname the Athens of the North; Priestley discovered oxygen during his time in England, though not in Warrington itself.

## Burtonwood and the American Decades

During the Second World War, RAF Burtonwood on the northern edge of Warrington became the largest United States Army Air Force base outside the continental United States. The American presence transformed the local economy and the social fabric in ways that lasted long after the war. Bob Hope and Humphrey Bogart came through to entertain the GIs. The base remained American through the Berlin airlift and into the Cold War, finally closing in 1991. Risley to the east hosted a Royal Ordnance munitions factory whose former site is now the Birchwood new-town development. The American influence on Warrington's twentieth century is one reason IKEA chose the town for its first British store, which opened on the old Burtonwood land in October 1987 and brought more than 200 jobs.

## Made a New Town in 1968

The 1968 New Town designation reshaped Warrington more than any single event since the Industrial Revolution. Liverpool was overcrowded; slum clearances needed somewhere to put displaced families; and Warrington had rail, motorway and canal connections plus land to spare. The town's population, around 80,000 in 1968, has since climbed past 174,000 in the built-up area and 210,000 across the wider borough. Heavy industry contracted through the 1970s and 1980s, replaced by retail and light industry, distribution centres around the M62, and the technology cluster at Birchwood. Unilever still makes washing powder at the old Crosfield works, though that plant has been under review since 2020. Warrington Borough Council became a unitary authority in 1998, and the town has applied unsuccessfully more than once for city status, most recently after the Peace Centre opened, branding the bid the City for Peace.

## What the Town Has Made Famous

The list of Warringtonians is genuinely peculiar. John Harrison, who solved the longitude problem with the marine chronometer, lived here. Lewis Carroll, who was vicar's son nearby at Daresbury, is not local enough to count, but Ian Brown of the Stone Roses lives in Lymm within the borough. Burt Kwouk of the Pink Panther films was born in Warrington. So was Pete McCarthy, whose books on Ireland made him a household name in the 2000s. Owen Cooper, the young actor of Netflix's Adolescence, is from Warrington. Rugby league dominates the sporting culture: Warrington Wolves play at the Halliwell Jones Stadium, the only club to have spent every season in the top flight of English rugby league since the sport's foundation in 1895. The Walking Day each summer, a Sunday-school festival that has run for nearly two centuries, still closes the town centre on the closest Friday to the last day of June while church congregations parade through the streets in their best. It is not the future. But it is the kind of past that a town remembers about itself.

## Flight Context

Warrington sits at 53.39N, 2.60W on the north bank of the Mersey in Cheshire, sixteen miles east of Liverpool and sixteen miles west of Manchester. From the air, the M6 motorway cuts north-south on the east side of town, the M62 runs east-west along the north, and the Manchester Ship Canal threads west-east through the southern suburbs. The West Coast Main Line passes north-south through Bank Quay station; the Liverpool-to-Manchester line crosses east-west through Central. The Warrington Transporter Bridge, the last working example of its kind, is visible just west of Bank Quay. Recommended viewing altitude 3,000-5,000 ft for the canal, river and motorway relationship. The former RAF Burtonwood site is northwest of town, now mostly the Omega business park. Nearest airports: Manchester (EGCC) 16 nm east, Liverpool John Lennon (EGGP) 16 nm west, Hawarden (EGNR) 18 nm southwest.
