# Hereford

A small cathedral city on the Wye that has been a Mercian capital, an SAS regimental town, the home of the Hereford cow, and the unofficial capital of English cider.

**Location:** 52.0562, -2.7156

**Categories:** cities, cathedrals, cider-country, saxon, welsh-marches, england

## Introduction

Richard the Lionheart granted Hereford its first town charter in 1189, and the charter describes it - astonishingly - as Hereford in Wales. The name itself is contested. One reading derives it from the Anglo-Saxon here, meaning an army, and ford, meaning a river crossing: a place where armed men crossed the Wye. The Welsh call it Henffordd, the old road, after a Roman route that ran past nearby Stretton Sugwas. Both etymologies point to the same idea: this has always been a place between things. England and Wales. Roman and Saxon. Past and whatever comes next.

## Saxon Capital, Welsh Border

Putta became the first Bishop of Hereford somewhere between 676 and 688 AD, and the small Saxon settlement on the Wye grew quickly because of where it stood - on the frontier between Mercia and the Welsh kingdoms beyond. By the early 8th century Hereford was the Saxon capital of West Mercia. In 760 the Welsh and the Anglo-Saxons fought the Battle of Hereford here, and the Britons briefly freed themselves from English influence. In 1056, during the reign of Edward the Confessor, the Welsh king Gruffydd ap Llywelyn marched on the town with Viking allies, put it to the torch, and returned home in triumph. Under King Athelstan, Hereford housed the only mint west of the Severn. It was to Hereford that Athelstan summoned the Welsh princes to swear allegiance. The city has never quite stopped being a frontier.

## Castle, Cathedral, and Conflict

Hereford Castle, now vanished, once rivalled Windsor in scale - a base for English kings campaigning against Welsh rebels like Owain Glyndwr. The present cathedral dates from the early 12th century, as does the first bridge across the Wye. After the Battle of Mortimer's Cross in 1461, during the Wars of the Roses, the defeated Lancastrian leader Owen Tudor - grandfather of the future Henry VII - was brought here and executed in High Town. A plaque now marks the spot. During the English Civil War the city changed hands four times in three years. In 1645 a Scottish Covenanter army of 14,000 under the Earl of Leven besieged it for a month and failed to take it. King Charles I was so grateful for the city's defence that he augmented its coat of arms with three lions, ten Scottish saltires for the defeated regiments, and a peer's gold-barred helm found on only one other municipal authority in England - the City of London.

## The Cider Capital

Herefordshire is England's cider county, and Hereford is its capital. Bulmer's started here in 1887 when Henry Percival Bulmer set up a small operation on the outskirts of the city. The brand still belongs to Hereford - now owned by Heineken, but still produced here, still employing local people - and the names it owns include Strongbow, Woodpecker, and Bulmers itself. The orchards stretch for miles in every direction; the Museum of Cider lives in a former Bulmer factory at Pomona Place. Other Hereford employers reveal the city's odd specialisations: Special Metals Wiggin makes nickel alloys, Avara Foods makes poultry products, Painter Brothers makes galvanised steel towers including the Skylon at the 1951 Festival of Britain. And the Hereford cow - red-bodied, white-faced, hardy and docile - was bred here in the 18th century and is now one of the most farmed cattle breeds in the world.

## The Quiet Regiment

From 1960 until 1999 Hereford was the home of the British Army's Special Air Service, based at Stirling Lines in the city. The regiment relocated to nearby Credenhill in 1999, but its presence has shaped the city in lasting ways. Inside Hereford Cathedral, dedicated in October 2017, hangs Ascension, a sculpture and stained-glass window honouring the SAS. In the churchyard of St Martin's, an area is set aside as a regimental memorial; more than twenty SAS soldiers are buried there. A Wall of Remembrance lists those who could not be buried - including the eighteen lost when a Sea King helicopter crashed during the Falklands campaign on 19 May 1982. Hereford honours its soldiers without parade or display. The men who train here disappear into the world and sometimes do not come back.

## Famous Daughters and Sons

Nell Gwynn - actress, orange-seller, and mistress to King Charles II - was said to have been born in Hereford in 1650. Other cities claim her too, but Gwynn Street in central Hereford keeps the local case alive. David Garrick, the actor who transformed 18th-century English theatre, was born here in 1717. Sir Edward Elgar - the composer of Pomp and Circumstance and the Enigma Variations - lived here for years and is commemorated by a bronze statue of him leaning on his bicycle outside the cathedral. Hereford United Football Club had its moment of national fame in February 1972 when, as a non-league side, they beat top-flight Newcastle 2-1 in an FA Cup third-round replay - one of the great giant-killings in English football. The original club folded in 2014, but its successor, Hereford FC, plays at Edgar Street and the goal celebrations are just as loud.

## Flight Context

Hereford sits at 52.06 N, 2.72 W on the north bank of the River Wye, 16 miles east of the Welsh border. The cathedral tower (165 ft) dominates the skyline from the air; the wide bend of the Wye south of the city is a clear navigation feature. Best viewed from 2,000-4,000 ft AGL. Nearest airports: Gloucestershire (EGBJ) about 30 nm east-south-east, Shawbury (EGOS) about 35 nm north, Wolverhampton/Halfpenny Green (EGBO) about 30 nm north-east. No civil airport in Hereford itself. Maritime climate; low cloud and rain in winter, often haze over the Severn-Wye valleys in summer.
