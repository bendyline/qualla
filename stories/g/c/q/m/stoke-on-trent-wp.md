# Stoke-on-Trent

Six towns refused to merge their identities even after they were forced into a single city, and the result is one of the strangest urban shapes in England.

**Location:** 53.0000, -2.1833

**Categories:** Cities in England, Stoke-on-Trent, Staffordshire, Pottery industry, Industrial history

## Introduction

Arnold Bennett, who grew up there, called it the Five Towns. He left Fenton out because, he said, Six Towns was less euphonious, which is the kind of literary slight that a place never quite forgets. Fenton is still occasionally called the forgotten town. Stoke-on-Trent has always been an awkward shape: six older settlements - Tunstall, Burslem, Hanley, Stoke, Fenton, and Longton - federated into a single county borough in 1910, granted city status in 1925, and still refusing to behave like one city. The only polycentric city in the United Kingdom is also the only one whose name does not match its commercial centre.

## How Six Became One

An early federation proposal failed in 1888, when an amendment to the Local Government Bill tried to make the six towns into districts of a Staffordshire Potteries county. The 1910 federation that did go through was driven by industrial logic: the coal seams ran under all six towns, the canal connected them, and the pottery factories formed a single economic network. The Home Office initially refused city status in 1925 because the population sat below 300,000, but the council went over their heads to King George V, who arrived in person on 5 June 1925 to announce that Stoke-on-Trent was now a city. The motto chosen for the new entity reads Vis Unita Fortior, usually translated as United Strength is Stronger - a piece of civic optimism that the six towns have only intermittently honoured. Cecil Wedgwood of the Wedgwood pottery dynasty served as the first mayor, and the family connection to the new city's identity ran straight back to the man who, in 1766, had cut the first sod for the Trent and Mersey Canal.

## Coal, Clay, and the Genius of Wedgwood

The pottery industry that defined this region did not emerge by accident. North Staffordshire sat on abundant coal for firing kilns, on clays suited to earthenware, and after 1777, on a canal that could carry Cornish china clay in and finished wares out. Josiah Wedgwood, more than any other single figure, raised local potting from craft to industry. He systematised experimentation - thousands of recorded trials - and trained generations of skilled artists who in turn made Staffordshire pottery a global brand. Josiah Spode introduced bone china at his Trent works in 1796. Thomas Minton opened his manufactory. By the early twentieth century, ceramic artists from Stoke - Clarice Cliff, Susie Cooper, Charlotte Rhead, Frederick Hurten Rhead - were shaping how the world thought a teapot should look. North Staffordshire was also a coal mining centre; striking miners in Hanley and Longton helped ignite the nationwide 1842 general strike and the Pottery Riots that followed.

## The Long Contraction

By 1947, when coal mining was nationalised, around 20,000 men still worked in Stoke pits. Wolstanton Colliery briefly held the deepest mining shafts in Europe at 3,197 feet. In 1992 the combined Trentham Superpit produced 2.5 million saleable tonnes, the first European mine to do so. Two years later, in 1994, the last pit closed. The pottery industry contracted on a parallel curve. Wedgwood shifted most production to Indonesia. Royal Doulton commissioned a Jakarta factory. Shelton Steelworks ceased crude steel production in 1978 and finally closed its rolling mill in 2002. Michelin once employed nearly 9,000 in its Stoke tyre plant; by 2006 the figure had fallen to about 1,200. The factories that built the city's prosperity left more slowly than they had arrived, but they left, and the people who had worked in them were not easily redeployed. Unemployment in the skilled-but-low-paid workforce climbed sharply through the 1980s and 1990s.

## A City Still Reinventing Itself

What remains is a city of paradoxes. Stoke had the second-largest Primark in the UK, the third-biggest hospital, and Bet365 - the gambling firm co-founded by Denise Coates and her father Peter Coates, the club's chairman - operating one of the largest online betting platforms in the world. The 1986 National Garden Festival reseeded tourism in the area, and the pottery factory shops continue to draw visitors who want to see how a teacup is born. Stoke City, the second-oldest professional football club in England, plays at the bet365 Stadium; the club's most celebrated player, Sir Stanley Matthews, was born here and ended his career here. The mining scars have softened into local nature reserves; Chatterley Whitfield, once the first British colliery to mine a million tonnes in a year, is now a scheduled monument. The slag heaps on the skyline have greened over. The bottle kilns, those upturned-jar shapes that once defined the Potteries' silhouette, mostly came down in the twentieth century; the surviving ones are protected, photographed, and quietly admired.

## Flight Context

Stoke-on-Trent sits at 53.00 N, 2.18 W on the upper valley of the River Trent, between the Peak District uplands to the north-east and the Cheshire Plain to the south-west. Elevation ranges from 92 to 250 metres (301 to 820 feet). The polycentric layout is best seen from 4,000-5,000 feet, where the six towns appear strung along the A50/A500 spine. The M6 motorway runs to the west. Nearest major airport: Manchester (EGCC), 40 miles north. East Midlands (EGNX) lies 40 miles south-east, Birmingham (EGBB) 45 miles south.
