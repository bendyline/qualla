# Glasgow

Once called the Second City of the Empire, Glasgow built more than half of Britain's ships, a quarter of the world's locomotives, and now writes its next act on the same riverbank.

**Location:** 55.8651, -4.2576

**Categories:** Cities, History, Scotland, Glasgow, Shipbuilding, Industrial Heritage

## Introduction

Saint Mungo is said to have preached a sermon containing the phrase "Let Glasgow flourish by the preaching of the word and the praising of thy name." The civic authorities, with practical Scottish thrift, trimmed that down to "Let Glasgow Flourish" and stamped it on coats of arms, lampposts, and manhole covers. For most of the 19th century the motto seemed prophetic. By the time Queen Victoria died, the city on the Clyde was producing more than half of Britain's tonnage of shipping and a quarter of every locomotive on Earth, calling itself the Second City of the British Empire and meaning it.

## A Bishop's Town Becomes a Tobacco Town

Glasgow began as something far smaller and quieter than what it became. The Romans built outposts in the area and stretched the Antonine Wall across central Scotland to keep the Caledonians out, but the real foundation came in the 6th century, when the Christian missionary Saint Mungo established a church beside the Molendinar Burn where Glasgow Cathedral now stands. The settlement grew slowly around the bishopric, picked up a university in 1451 (the fourth oldest in the English-speaking world), and remained modest enough that Daniel Defoe could call it "the cleanest and beautifullest, and best built city in Britain, London excepted." The population was perhaps 12,000. Then came 1707 and the Act of Union, which gave Scotland access to England's overseas markets. Within a generation, more than half of all British tobacco imports were flowing through the Clyde, much of it bound for re-export to France. The wealth was real, and so was its source: Glasgow's tobacco lords built their fortunes on cash crops produced by enslaved Africans, with Scottish slave ships sailing out of Greenock and Port Glasgow.

## The City That Built Ships

What tobacco started, iron and steel finished. In 1801 ironstone was discovered in the Lanarkshire coalfields east of the city. The 1828 invention of the hot-blast furnace transformed Scottish iron-making, and steel-making followed. Engineers like Robert Napier, John Elder, and Sir William Pearce turned the deepened upper Clyde into the undisputed world centre of shipbuilding. By 1900 the port had twelve miles of quays and 500 miles of railway. Cunard, Allan, British India, Union-Castle, City Line, and Clan Line all flew out of Clydeside. The chemical industry achieved world status. The North British Locomotive Company in Springburn pushed locomotives down to ports for export across the Empire. At its peak in 1938, the city held 1,127,825 people, a density crammed into tall sandstone tenements where life expectancy was short and tuberculosis was a household word.

## Minds Worth the City

It was not all furnaces and forecastles. The Scottish Enlightenment had a Glasgow chapter as serious as Edinburgh's. Adam Smith taught moral philosophy at the University of Glasgow, where he wrote much of The Wealth of Nations. James Watt worked as an instrument maker on the same campus when, in 1765, he had the insight that led to the separate condenser and a working steam engine - the spark that lit the Industrial Revolution. A century later, Charles Rennie Mackintosh emerged from the Glasgow School of Art with a unified design language that did for Glasgow what Antoni Gaudi did for Barcelona. His Willow Tearooms, his Hill House, and the school of art itself (twice ravaged by fire) made the city a pilgrimage site for architects.

## After the Yards

The fall came hard and fast. Japanese and German shipyards undercut Clydeside in the 1960s. Industry collapsed, employment dried up, and an ill-judged programme of mass demolition replaced infamous tenement slums with tower blocks and peripheral housing estates that became their own kind of trap. By 2005, Glasgow held the lowest life expectancy of any city in the UK, with a male life expectancy in Calton of just 54 years compared to 82 in nearby Lenzie. The Bruce Report of 1945 had imagined wholesale rebuilding, and much of it happened - sometimes brutally. The Glasgow Garden Festival of 1988 marked a turning point. European Capital of Culture in 1990 marked another. UK City of Architecture and Design in 1999 was a third. Today the Clyde Waterfront Regeneration draws affluent residents back to the inner city, and the population, long in decline, has begun growing again.

## Mungo's City Still Flourishing

Walk through Glasgow now and you walk through layers. The Necropolis on its hill above the cathedral is full of Victorian merchants and their indispensable monuments. Provand's Lordship and the cathedral are the last fragments of medieval Glasgow. The Merchant City and Blythswood Hill carry the grand grid of Georgian and Victorian expansion. The West End around Kelvingrove and the University holds the 19th-century professional class. Pacific Quay, the SEC Hydro, the Riverside Museum, and the Clyde Arc trace the 21st-century waterfront. Glasgow hosted COP26 in 2021, will co-host UEFA Euro 2028, and welcomes the pared-back 2026 Commonwealth Games. The city that built the Empire's ships now builds songs - Franz Ferdinand, Belle and Sebastian, Mogwai, Travis, Primal Scream - and was named a UNESCO City of Music in 2008. Let Glasgow flourish, the seal says. Against everything that should have stopped it, it does.

## Flight Context

Glasgow is centred near 55.86 N, 4.25 W on the banks of the River Clyde in west central Scotland. The city sits within the Central Belt, the narrow corridor between the Forth and Clyde estuaries. From altitude, the M8 motorway cuts a clear diagonal through the urban core. The deep arc of the Clyde, the SEC Armadillo on its north bank, and Glasgow Tower opposite at Pacific Quay are reliable visual cues. Glasgow International Airport (EGPF) sits 6 nautical miles west at Paisley, with Glasgow Prestwick (EGPK) 28 nautical miles southwest on the Ayrshire coast. Edinburgh Airport (EGPH) is 35 nautical miles east. Recommended viewing altitude: 3,000-6,000 feet for clear identification of the river, motorway, and university spire on Gilmorehill.
