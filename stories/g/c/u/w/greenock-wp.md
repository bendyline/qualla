# Greenock

An Inverclyde port whose deep-water harbour built sugar empires and Cunard liners, then absorbed two devastating nights of bombing in May 1941 that the town has never forgotten.

**Location:** 55.9500, -4.7667

**Categories:** scotland, port, wwii, industrial-heritage, firth-of-clyde

## Introduction

On the nights of 6 and 7 May 1941, around 350 Luftwaffe aircraft came down the Clyde with Greenock as their target. Two hundred and seventy-one people died. The Municipal Buildings lost a tower and two pediments. A drapery business at the corner was so badly damaged that demolition was the only option, leaving a blank brick face that locals still call Cowan's Corner. Eight decades later, the scar remains part of the streetscape — a deliberate, quiet reminder that this working town on the Firth of Clyde was once a strategic target worth two consecutive nights of bombs.

## The Schaws and the Charter Town

Before the bombs, before the sugar warehouses, before the Cunard liners moored at the Tail of the Bank, there was a fishing village and a family. Hugh de Grenock was created a Scottish Baron in 1296. Around 1400 his successor Malcolm Galbraith died without sons, and his estate was split between two daughters. The eldest got Easter Greenock and married a Crawfurd. The younger inherited Wester Greenock and married a Schaw of Sauchie. By 1635 Sir John Schaw had a jetty built into the bay and obtained a charter raising Greenock to a Burgh of Barony with the right to a weekly market. In 1589 the same family had secured a charter from James VI to build a kirk for the poor fishermen on their lands — the Old West Kirk, said to be the first Protestant church built in Scotland after the Reformation.

## Sugar, Tobacco, and Tate & Lyle

Sugar refining began in Greenock in 1765. By the end of the 19th century, around 400 ships a year were arriving from Caribbean plantations carrying raw sugar to be processed here. Fourteen refineries operated at one point. The most successful was founded by Abram Lyle, a local cooper and ship-owner, who in 1865 bought into the Glebe Sugar Refinery with four partners. In 1921 his firm merged with Henry Tate's London business to form Tate & Lyle, a name that still appears on supermarket shelves. The James Watt Dock, opened in 1886, included a massive Sugar Shed — a Category A listed building with a colonnade of cast-iron columns forming a sheltered unloading area. Tate & Lyle finally closed its Greenock refinery in 1997, ending a 150-year connection. A fire damaged the Sugar Shed in 2006, but the shell still stands.

## The Custom House and the Steamboat Age

Europe's first commercial steamboat service began on the Clyde in 1812, with the paddle steamer Comet sailing between Glasgow, Greenock, and Helensburgh. The pier where she tied up became known as Steamboat Quay. In May 1817 the foundation stone was laid there for a new Custom House, designed by William Burn. Completed in 1818, the building's Grecian Doric portico still looks out over what is now Custom House Quay. By 1828 it was being praised as a grand National Structure in the highest style of elegance. By then steamers were running scheduled services from Greenock to Belfast, Derry, Liverpool, Inverness, Campbeltown, the Hebrides, and all the principal places in the Highlands. The building closed in 2010 after a long campaign to save it failed; it has since been refurbished and let to private tenants.

## The Blitz

Greenock's anchorage at the Tail of the Bank became the base for the Home Fleet during the Second World War and the main assembly point for Atlantic convoys carrying men and material from North America. That strategic importance made the town a target. On 30 April 1940, the French destroyer Maillé Brézé blew up off Greenock with heavy loss of life after an accident involving two of her own torpedoes — 37 men died, trapped below as she was flooded to prevent her magazine exploding. A year later, on 6 and 7 May 1941, came the Greenock Blitz itself. Around 350 Luftwaffe aircraft attacked across the two nights. RAF Greenock, a flying-boat maintenance base, was hit on the second night. The Municipal Buildings lost their southern tower. Across the town, 271 people died — fathers, mothers, children, neighbours — most of them civilians who happened to live near a working port that mattered to the war.

## Shipyards and What Came After

Scotts Shipbuilding and Engineering, founded in 1711, gained Royal Navy contracts from 1806 and kept building for more than a century and a half. In 1967 it merged with Lithgows — itself the largest privately owned yard in the world at one point — to form Scott Lithgow. The Inchgreen Drydock, opened in 1964 at 305 metres long, was used to refit Cunard's Queen Elizabeth and to fit out the QE2. Then the orders thinned. South Korea and Japan undercut the prices, the 1970s and 1980s shrank the workforce, and by the end of the century almost nothing was left. The Sugar Shed sits empty. The Inchgreen cranes were demolished in 2017. Greenock has reinvented itself as a cruise-ship terminal and call-centre town with mixed success, but the bones of the working port are still everywhere — in the place names, the empty docks, the war memorial on Lyle Hill.

## Flight Context

Greenock lies at approximately 55.95°N, 4.77°W on the south bank of the Firth of Clyde, with Helensburgh visible across the water 3 nm to the north. Recommended viewing altitude 2,500-4,000 ft for the full sweep of the Tail of the Bank and Ocean Terminal. Nearest airport: Glasgow International (EGPF) 15 nm east on the south side of the M8 corridor. Prestwick (EGPK) is 30 nm south on the Ayrshire coast. The Cross of Lorraine on Lyle Hill west of town is a distinctive marker.
