# Glendalough

In a glacial valley where two lakes meet, a sixth-century monk founded a monastery that survived Viking raids, supplied timber for longships, and drew pilgrims whose gatherings became so riotous the authorities tried to ban them.

**Location:** 53.0103, -6.3275

**Categories:** Former populated places in Ireland, Climbing areas of Ireland, Glens of Ireland, National monuments in County Wicklow, Roman Catholic pilgrimage sites in Ireland

## Introduction

The name means "valley of two lakes," and that is exactly what you find: two dark bodies of water lying end to end in a steep-sided glacial trench, surrounded by mountains that rise to nearly 700 metres. Glendalough in County Wicklow is the kind of landscape that makes hermits of reasonable people. In the sixth century, a man named Kevin took that impulse to its logical conclusion. Born into one of the ruling families of Leinster, he chose a cave above the Upper Lake over any throne or manor, and from that unlikely beginning grew one of the most important monastic settlements in medieval Ireland.

## Kevin's Cave and What Grew From It

St Kevin's Bed, as the cave is known, is a narrow ledge cut into the cliff above the Upper Lake. Reaching it requires a scramble that deters most visitors, which may have been exactly the point. Kevin came here as a young man, having studied under three holy men -- Eoghan, Lochan, and Eanna -- and wanting to go further into solitude. His writings describe fighting "knights" at Glendalough, which scholars now interpret as a metaphor for inner spiritual struggle. But solitude attracts company. Kevin's reputation for holiness drew followers, and he eventually founded a monastery where the two rivers form a confluence. He died around 618, traditionally on 3 June, and for the next six centuries Glendalough thrived as a centre of learning and worship.

## Vikings, Longships, and a Book

The monastery's wealth made it a target. The Irish Annals record the deaths of abbots and raids on the settlement across several centuries. But Glendalough's relationship with the Norse world was not purely adversarial. Around 1042, oak timber from Glendalough was used to build the second-longest Viking longship on record, a vessel of approximately 30 metres. A modern replica of that ship was constructed in 2004 and now sits in Roskilde, Denmark. At the Synod of Rath Breasail in 1111, Glendalough was designated as one of the two dioceses of North Leinster. The Book of Glendalough, written around 1131, is among its scholarly achievements. St Laurence O'Toole, who became abbot in the twelfth century, was so attached to the place that even after his appointment as Archbishop of Dublin in 1162, he returned periodically to the solitude of Kevin's cave.

## Stone and Sanctuary

The monastic ruins that survive are among the most complete early medieval complexes in Ireland. The Gateway, with its two granite arches, is unique in the country. A cross-inscribed stone in its west wall marked the boundary of sanctuary -- anyone who crossed that threshold was under the monastery's protection. The cathedral, built in phases from the earliest nave to the late twelfth-century chancel, reused massive mica-schist stones from an even older church. Reefert Church, set in a grove of trees near the Upper Lake, takes its name from Righ Fearta, "burial place of the kings." Its simple granite doorway with sloping jambs dates to around 1100. St Kevin's Cross, a granite cross with an unpierced ring, stands a few metres from the cathedral.

## The Miners Who Followed the Monks

Glendalough's second life began underground. In 1825, the Glendalough Mining Company began extracting galena lead ore from veins running through the granite. By 1859, miners had connected the Glendasan and Glendalough workings through a series of tunnels called adits, bored straight through Camaderry mountain, allowing ore to be drained and transported more efficiently. The rusted remains of ore crushers still stand at the Miner's Village near the Upper Lake. The mines operated until 1957, more than a century of industrial activity hidden in a valley best known for prayer. This earlier chapter of extraction had its own roots: the Wicklow Gold Rush of 1795 first drew geologists to map these mountains, work that would inform mining operations for decades.

## Riotous Assemblies and Quiet Trails

Descriptions of Glendalough from the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries include references to "riotous assembly" on the feast of St Kevin, 3 June, when pilgrimages to the valley became celebrations so boisterous that authorities tried repeatedly to suppress them. Today the valley is quieter, though hardly empty. Nine waymarked trails thread through the national park, from easy lakeside walks to the 9-kilometre White Route loop along boarded railway sleepers. Rock climbers have worked the south-facing granite cliffs above the Miner's Village since 1948, with routes reaching over 100 metres. The 30-kilometre Saint Kevin's Way, a medieval pilgrimage trail starting in Hollywood village, ends here. And in the semi-natural oak woodland surrounding the lakes, spring carpets the forest floor with bluebells, while goosanders and great spotted woodpeckers -- two of Ireland's newest breeding species -- hunt along the water.

## Flight Context

Located at 53.01N, 6.33W in a deep glacial valley in the Wicklow Mountains. From the air, the two elongated lakes are the dominant feature, aligned in the valley between Camaderry (699m) and Lugduff (652m). The round tower and monastic ruins cluster near the Lower Lake.
