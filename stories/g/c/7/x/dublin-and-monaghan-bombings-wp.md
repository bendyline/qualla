# Dublin and Monaghan Bombings

On a Friday rush hour in May 1974, four car bombs killed 33 people and a full-term unborn child across Dublin and Monaghan -- the deadliest single day of the Troubles, and a crime for which no one has ever been charged.

## Introduction

The bus drivers were on strike that day, so the footpaths were packed. It was a Friday in May, payday for many, the start of the long weekend. At 17:28 on Parnell Street, a metallic green Hillman Avenger that had been hijacked in Belfast that morning detonated outside the Welcome Inn pub. Two minutes later a Ford Escort tore apart Talbot Street outside Guiney's department store. Four minutes after that a third car bomb shattered South Leinster Street beside the railings of Trinity College, three hundred metres from Leinster House. Almost ninety minutes later, in the border town of Monaghan, a fourth car bomb exploded outside Greacen's pub on North Road. Thirty-three people died, and a full-term unborn child died with its mother. Almost three hundred more were injured. It was the deadliest day of the Troubles. No one has ever been charged.

## The Forgotten

The dead were not soldiers or politicians. They were a French Jewish woman named Simone Chetrit whose family had survived the Holocaust. An Italian man named Antonio Magliocco. A World War I veteran. Two infant girls -- Jacqueline and Anne-Marie O'Brien, aged 17 months and 5 months -- killed alongside their parents John and Anna in a single moment on Parnell Street. Many of the women who died were young civil servants from rural towns, in Dublin to earn a living. The youngest victim, Baby Doherty, was the full-term child of Colette Doherty; both mother and unborn baby were killed on Talbot Street. Colette's 22-month-old daughter survived, found wandering near the blast site relatively unharmed. The oldest victim was 80. In Monaghan, seven died, including the elderly mother of one of the dead, who is said to have died six weeks later of the shock. They were doing the most ordinary things a person can do: going home from work, catching a train, looking at records, buying their groceries.

## The Strike

The bombings did not happen in a vacuum. They happened during the Ulster Workers' Council Strike, a fourteen-day shutdown of Northern Ireland by loyalists and unionists who opposed the Sunningdale Agreement -- a power-sharing arrangement that for the first time gave the Republic a consultative role in the governance of Northern Ireland. The strike crippled the North. The bombings, eleven days in, were a message to the Republic about the price of involvement. Eleven days after the explosions, the Northern Ireland Executive collapsed and Sunningdale fell with it. The British government had de-proscribed the Ulster Volunteer Force the month before the attacks, briefly removing it from the list of illegal organisations. Sammy Smyth, press officer for the Ulster Defence Association and the Strike Committee, was asked for his reaction. 'I am very happy about the bombings in Dublin,' he said. 'There is a war with the Free State and now we are laughing at them.' The UVF itself did not claim responsibility until 1993, nineteen years later.

## Justice Withheld

The specially-appointed Garda investigation team was disbanded in July 1974, two months after the bombings. Department of Justice files on the Dublin attacks went missing in their entirety. When the Royal Ulster Constabulary told the Gardai it had arrested some of the suspected bombers, the Gardai apparently did not ask their names. Mr Justice Henry Barron's 2003 report -- commissioned only after decades of campaigning by victims' families through their group Justice for the Forgotten -- found that the bombings were carried out by two groups of loyalist paramilitaries from Belfast and the Portadown-Lurgan area, mostly UVF members. It found it likely that Ulster Defence Regiment soldiers and RUC officers helped prepare the attack. It found that the British authorities had obstructed the Inquiry, refusing to share intelligence files. Subsequent Barron reports linked the same Glenanne gang -- a fusion of UVF members and security force personnel operating out of an RUC officer's farm -- to the Miami Showband massacre and dozens of other killings. The Irish Dail has passed motions in 2008 and 2011, unanimously, asking Britain to release the files. The files remain sealed.

## The Child of Our Time

Two days after the bombings, the Irish poet Eavan Boland saw a photograph on the front of a national newspaper. A fireman was carrying a small body from the rubble. 'The most arresting feature,' Boland later wrote, 'was the expression on the face of the fireman who lifted that child, an expression of tenderness as if he were lifting his own child from its cradle to its mother's breast.' She wrote 'Child of Our Time', an elegy for the two infants and the unborn baby. The poem was published in her 1975 collection The War Horse. Down the road from Talbot Street, an 11-year-old boy named Andy Rowen -- a friend of a Mount Temple schoolboy named Paul Hewson, later better known as Bono -- witnessed the aftermath of the Parnell Street bomb. The scene never left him. Decades later, U2 wrote 'Raised by Wolves' about that day. When Bono called Rowen for permission, Rowen told him he had kept a piece of shrapnel from the bomb all those years, 'to remind him of how it took a little piece of him.'

## Memorials

On Talbot Street there is a bronze plaque set into the pavement, listing every name. On Parnell Street there is another. On South Leinster Street, where two young women died instantly against the railings of Trinity College, the railings still stand. In Monaghan town a memorial garden marks the site of the fourth bomb. Every 17 May, families lay flowers and read out the names. The Justice for the Forgotten campaign continues, fifty years on, asking for documents Britain has never released and a prosecution that has never come. The dead are not statistics. They were a French woman whose family had outlived Auschwitz; two baby girls from inner-city Dublin; an Italian who worked in a chip shop; a teenager pumping petrol; civil servants riding home to their bedsits. The Irish word 'forgotten' means something specific in this context: not that the country has forgotten them, but that the state which might have brought their killers to court did not, would not, and still has not. The names remain. The files do not.

## Flight Context

The three Dublin bomb sites form a tight triangle in the city centre. Parnell Street runs east-west through the north inner city at 53.353N, 6.262W. Talbot Street lies a few blocks south, running east toward Connolly Station at 53.350N, 6.252W. South Leinster Street is just south of the Liffey at 53.343N, 6.253W, beside the eastern wall of Trinity College and 300 metres from Leinster House. Monaghan town lies 130 km north-west at 54.249N, 6.969W. From altitude, the Dublin sites cluster around the dense Georgian core; Monaghan shows as a small market town near the Northern Irish border. Nearest airport for Dublin: EIDW (8 km north). For Monaghan: Belfast International (EGAA) 70 km north-east.
