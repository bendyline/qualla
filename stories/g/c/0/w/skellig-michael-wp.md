# Skellig Michael

A twin-pinnacled Atlantic crag where sixth-century monks built a monastery so remote that it survived intact for over a millennium -- and later became a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

**Location:** 51.7700, -10.5400

**Categories:** world-heritage, archaeology, monastery, islands, medieval-history

## Introduction

The word sceilig means "splinter" in Irish, and you can see why. Skellig Michael is a shard of Devonian sandstone thrust 218 metres out of the Atlantic, 11.6 kilometres west of the Iveragh Peninsula in County Kerry. It has two peaks, a central depression called Christ's Saddle, and almost no flat ground. Sometime between the sixth and eighth centuries, monks looked at this place and decided it was exactly where they wanted to spend the rest of their lives. The monastery they built -- six beehive huts, two oratories, a cemetery, and hundreds of hand-carved stone steps -- survives in a condition so remarkable that UNESCO designated the island a World Heritage Site in 1996, calling it a "unique example of an early religious settlement."

## Geology of Extremes

Skellig Michael and its smaller twin, Little Skellig, were formed between 360 and 374 million years ago, during the Devonian period, when Ireland sat south of the equator on a larger continental landmass. The same geological upheaval created the MacGillycuddy's Reeks and Caha Mountains on the mainland. When Atlantic waters rose, they carved deep marine inlets like Bantry Bay and left the Skelligs stranded offshore. Erosion along a major north-south fault line split Skellig Michael's centre, creating Christ's Saddle between the two peaks. The rock is Old Red Sandstone and compressed slate, deeply fractured, and relentlessly eroded by wind and water. The only flat, fertile ground on the island is a strip of grassland at Christ's Saddle -- traces of medieval crop farming have been found there.

## The Monastery in the Sky

Celtic Christian monks founded the settlement sometime between the sixth and eighth centuries. The first definite historical reference comes from the eighth century, with the death of "Suibhini of Skelig." The Annals of the Four Masters record a Viking raid in 823 AD, when the monastery was plundered and its abbot, Eitgal, was carried off and died of hunger. The Vikings attacked again in 838. Despite these assaults, the community endured and expanded, eventually affiliating with the Augustinian order. At its peak, no more than twelve monks and an abbot occupied the site. They grew vegetables in terraced gardens, ate fish and seabird eggs, prayed in their corbelled stone cells, and endured a climate that grew steadily harsher. By the late 12th or early 13th century, the combination of worsening weather, continued raids, and changes in the Irish Church's structure drove the monks to abandon the island for Ballinskelligs Abbey on the mainland.

## Stone Without Mortar

The monastery sits on a terraced shelf on the northeast peak, roughly 185 metres above sea level. Its six clochans -- domed beehive cells built entirely of dry-stacked corbelled stone -- remain structurally sound after more than a thousand years. The largest, Cell A, has a floor area of 14.5 by 3.8 metres and stands 5 metres high. Protruding stones on the interior walls served as pegs, possibly supporting an upper sleeping loft, while those on the exterior likely anchored thatch roofing. The main oratory is boat-shaped, measuring 3.6 by 4.3 metres, with a small altar and an eastern window. Saint Michael's Church, added in the 10th or 11th century, was the monastery's only mortared structure, built with imported sandstone from Valentia Island. Over a hundred individual stone crosses have been found scattered across the island.

## After the Monks

The island remained a pilgrimage site from the early 16th century onward, even after the dissolution of Ballinskelligs Abbey in 1578 under Elizabeth I. Ownership passed through the Butler family until the 1820s, when the Corporation for Preserving and Improving the Port of Dublin purchased it for 500 pounds and built two lighthouses on its Atlantic face. Lord Dunraven's 1871 archaeological survey was the first comprehensive study of the monastery. He described the island with reverence -- "so solemn and so sad that none should enter here but the pilgrim and the penitent" -- while excoriating the lighthouse builders for their "objectionable modern walls." The Office of Public Works took the monastery remains into guardianship in 1880. The lighthouses were automated in the 1980s, ending the last permanent habitation.

## A Living Sanctuary

Skellig Michael's isolation has made it a refuge for wildlife. The island hosts breeding populations of peregrine falcons, fulmars, Manx shearwaters, storm petrels, gannets, kittiwakes, guillemots, and Atlantic puffins. Grey seals haul out on the island's ledges. Rabbits and house mice -- probably introduced by lighthouse keepers in the 19th century -- are the only land mammals. The Skellig Islands were classified as a Special Protection Area in 1986 for their extraordinary avian diversity. It is this double identity -- sacred ruin and nature sanctuary -- that gives Skellig Michael its power. The International Council on Monuments and Sites called it an illustration of "the extremes of a Christian monasticism characterising much of North Africa, the Near East and Europe," preserved not by human effort but by the sheer difficulty of reaching it.

## Flight Context

Skellig Michael is located at approximately 51.77N, 10.54W, 11.6 km west of the Iveragh Peninsula in County Kerry. The island's twin peaks (218m and 185m) are visible from the air as sharp pyramidal rocks. Little Skellig, 1 km to the east-northeast and white with gannets, serves as a useful visual reference. Kerry Airport (EIKY) is about 60 km to the northeast. Cork Airport (EICK) is approximately 130 km east. The monastery is visible on the northeast peak terrace. Best observed at lower altitudes in clear conditions.
