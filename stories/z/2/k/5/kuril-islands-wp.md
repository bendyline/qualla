# Kuril Islands

A volcanic archipelago of 56 islands stretching between Japan and Russia, where tectonic forces, imperial ambitions, and some of the richest marine ecosystems on Earth converge.

**Location:** 47.0000, 152.1000

**Categories:** islands, volcanoes, nature, history, military, disputed-territories

## Introduction

The Russian word kurit means 'to smoke,' and the name fits. From the air, the Kuril Islands look like a 1,300-kilometer chain of chimneys strung between Hokkaido and the Kamchatka Peninsula, their volcanic summits trailing plumes into the subarctic wind. Around 100 volcanoes line the chain, roughly 40 of them active. A magnitude 8.5 earthquake struck in 1963. A magnitude 8.3 followed in 2006, sending five-foot tsunami waves all the way to the California coast. The Kurils sit on the Ring of Fire, and the Ring does not let them forget it.

## Fifty-Six Islands, Two Claimants

The archipelago consists of the Greater Kuril Chain and the parallel Lesser Kuril Chain at its southwestern end, together comprising 56 islands and many minor islets with a total area of about 10,503 square kilometers. The islands are summits of stratovolcanoes formed by the subduction of the Pacific Plate beneath the Okhotsk Plate, a process that digs the Kuril Trench some 200 kilometers to the east. Russia has administered the entire chain since Soviet forces invaded in August 1945, near the end of World War II. Japan claims the four southernmost islands — Iturup, Kunashir, Shikotan, and the Habomai islets — as its Northern Territories. This dispute has prevented the two countries from signing a formal peace treaty for eight decades. As of 2017, Russia named five previously unnamed islands in the chain, a gesture not designed to reassure Tokyo.

## The Ainu and the Empires

The indigenous Ainu inhabited the Kurils from early times, and the name Kuril itself may derive from the Ainu autonym kur, meaning 'man.' The Matsumae clan claimed the islands during the Edo period, and a 1644 map produced by the Tokugawa shogunate shows 39 islands northeast of Hokkaido. A Dutch expedition under Maarten Gerritsz Vries explored the chain in 1643. Russian Cossacks landed on Shumshu in 1711. By the late 19th century, the Japanese administration had begun forced assimilation of the Ainu, granting them citizenship while requiring Japanese names and forbidding traditional practices like tattooing and animal sacrifice. In 1868, about 100 Ainu reportedly lived on the islands. American whaleships hunted right whales in Kuril waters between 1847 and 1892, and three of those vessels wrecked on the islands.

## The Waters That Feed the World

The Kurils sit at the confluence of the Okhotsk Sea gyre and the southward Oyashio Current, making the surrounding waters among the most productive in the North Pacific. Extensive kelp beds support sea urchins, mollusks, and countless invertebrates. Walleye pollock, Pacific cod, and flatfish sustain the commercial fishing industry. Millions of seabirds colonize the islands each summer — northern fulmars, tufted puffins, murres, kittiwakes, auklets, and petrels nest on virtually every available hummock and cliff niche. Steller sea lions maintain rookeries on several islands. Northern fur seals, once numbering in the tens of thousands, were hunted nearly to extinction in the 19th century. Sea otters suffered the same fate. Brown bears, foxes, and martens roam the larger islands, and the Kurilian Bobtail cat — a domestic breed with a naturally short tail caused by a dominant gene mutation — claims these islands as its ancestral home.

## Pearl Harbor's Staging Ground

In 1941, Admiral Isoroku Yamamoto ordered the Imperial Japanese Navy strike force to assemble in Hitokappu Bay on Iturup Island — chosen for its sparse population, lack of foreigners, and constant fog coverage. On the morning of November 26, he ordered the fleet to sail for Hawaii. The Kurils later became a target of American bombing raids: on July 10, 1943, eight B-25 Mitchells struck Paramushir and Shumshu, and subsequent missions hit the northern islands repeatedly. The Americans' strategic feint, Operation Wedlock, was so convincing that Japan increased its Kuril garrison from 8,000 troops in 1943 to 41,000 in 1944 and kept more than 400 aircraft in the area. The Soviet invasion came between August 18 and 31, 1945. The entire Japanese civilian population — roughly 17,000 people — was expelled by 1946.

## An Archipelago in Waiting

Roughly 20,000 people live on the Kurils today, most of them on the four disputed southern islands or on Paramushir at the chain's northern end. They are ethnic Russians, Ukrainians, Belarusians, Tatars, and a scattering of others, mostly employed in fishing, fish processing, or the military. The 18th Machine Gun Artillery Division is headquartered on Iturup, and Russia has deployed anti-aircraft missiles, coastal defense systems, and Kamov Ka-52 combat helicopters to the islands. A geothermal power plant on Mount Baransky taps the volcanic steam for electricity. In 2022, Russia established a special economic zone with tax exemptions lasting 20 years, hoping to attract investment to one of the most isolated corners of its territory. Whether the gesture will bring new residents or simply new paperwork remains to be seen.

## Flight Context

The Kuril Islands extend approximately 1,300 km from Hokkaido (44°N) to the Kamchatka Peninsula (51°N), between 146°E and 157°E. The chain separates the Sea of Okhotsk (west) from the North Pacific (east). Key airports include Iturup Airport (UHSI) and Mendeleyevo Airport (UHSM) on Kunashir. Gateway hub is Yuzhno-Sakhalinsk (UHSS). Atlasov Island at the north end (highest point in Sakhalin Oblast) and Tyatya volcano on Kunashir at the south end are prominent visual landmarks. The Kuril Trench lies approximately 200 km to the east. Expect subarctic conditions, persistent fog, and frequent volcanic activity. Active volcano Raikoke erupted in June 2019 with emissions reaching into the stratosphere.
