# Pei Plan

I. M. Pei's 1960s blueprint for downtown Oklahoma City delivered office towers, parking and a convention center, but the shops and homes it promised never came, and the historic buildings it cleared left a resentment that shaped the city for decades.

**Location:** 35.4689, -97.5206

**Categories:** Urban planning in the United States, History of Oklahoma City, City plans, Urban renewal, Historic preservation

## Introduction

The deciding vote came from a historian. In 1965 George H. Shirk was a local civic leader and historian known for his work in historic preservation, and the city council had chosen him as mayor of Oklahoma City. When the council voted on an ambitious plan to rebuild downtown, Shirk cast the deciding vote in favor. The plan was the work of the architect I. M. Pei. Over the next fifteen years it transformed the center of the city. Hundreds of buildings came down, several historic landmarks among them. One of the buildings that went up during those years would later be the target of the 1995 Oklahoma City bombing.

## A Downtown Squeezed Dry

Like most American cities after World War II, Oklahoma City spread outward during the 1940s and 1950s. Downtown struggled to keep up. The Oklahoma City Railway was dismantled after the war, and the growing number of cars quickly used up the limited parking in the center of town. Even the land itself worked against business. Downtown lots dated back to the Land Run of 1889, and they were too small for companies that wanted to expand. By 1962, 53 downtown retailers had closed or moved to the suburbs. By then the city council had authorized an Urban Renewal Authority. Local business leaders put up its first funding and hired I. M. Pei, who had already won international recognition for his urban redesign plans for Cleveland, Philadelphia and other cities, to draw up a complete redevelopment proposal.

## Pei's Blueprint

Pei worked on his plan for the 528-acre central business district through 1964, sharing it only with the leaders of the Urban Renewal Authority and of the privately financed Urban Action Foundation. The "Pei Plan" is the informal name for two commissions, a general neighborhood renewal plan finished in 1964 and a development plan for a first project, known as Project I-A, finished in 1966. It called for demolishing hundreds of antiquated buildings. It also proposed keeping several landmarks, among them the Biltmore and Huckins hotels, building new facilities for the John A. Brown department store, and turning Main Street into an interior shopping arcade. Before the city could adopt the plan, Mayor Jack Wilkes resigned to become president of Centenary College in Shreveport, Louisiana. The council chose Shirk to replace him. Despite his preservationist leanings, Shirk became an enthusiastic supporter of the plan, and his vote carried its adoption in 1965.

## Towers, Tunnels and Parking

Large-scale demolition began in 1967, paid for with federal redevelopment grants under the Johnson administration. Some landowners sold willingly, while others fought eminent domain hard, and the project drifted away from Pei's original design. After a special election in July 1968 guaranteed its funding, a new convention center rose in southeast downtown, in an area then widely called "skid row." Broadway Avenue, now E.K. Gaylord Avenue, was widened and rerouted into a six-lane commuter road that included the huge Santa Fe Parking Garage. The redevelopment authority also provided sites for new headquarters for Kerr-McGee, Liberty Bank and Fidelity Bank, and it built a pedestrian tunnel meant to connect to the Main Street arcade. The Nixon administration then scaled back federal support. Retailers chose shopping malls such as Penn Square, Shepherd and Crossroads over downtown locations, and the hotel and retail projects in the plan fell apart. On January 30, 1974, John A. Brown, which had merged with the Dayton Hudson Corporation in 1971, announced that it would leave downtown. The arcade site, already largely cleared, ended up as public parking. Without shops downtown, the planned housing never came either.

## The Criterion and Other Casualties

In 1977 the last major demolition cleared the way for the Myriad Botanical Gardens, and it took the Biltmore Hotel, which Pei himself had meant to keep. That same year the Alfred P. Murrah Federal Building opened. On April 19, 1995, it was the target of the Oklahoma City bombing, which killed 168 people, including 19 children. The ruins were demolished, and the Oklahoma City National Memorial now stands on the site. By 1980 there was little redevelopment work left. The losses stayed with the city. The Criterion Theater, a French-style stage theater, was torn down in 1973 for the Century Center, an enclosed shopping center that stood largely empty until The Oklahoman moved in in 2015. A preservation group took the name Criterion Group in its memory. The Venetian-inspired Baum Building fell even though Shirk tried to save it. The owners of the Hales Building fought condemnation for nearly a decade before it came down.

## A Model in an Atrium

The Pei Plan did deliver office buildings and parking, but it failed to bring the retail and residential life it had promised. As activists mourned the lost buildings, public opinion turned against the plan. The backlash was so strong that city leaders avoided large-scale downtown planning through the 1980s and early 1990s, until the Metropolitan Area Projects initiative, known as MAPS, passed in 1993. The ideas that did take shape are still visible. The former Kerr-McGee headquarters later housed SandRidge Energy and is now the State of Oklahoma's Strata Tower, Liberty Bank's tower has become the BancFirst Tower, and the Fidelity Bank building is now the Park Harvey Center. The Oklahoma City/County Historical Society keeps a large conceptual model of the early plan in the second-floor atrium of the Hart Building, at Sheridan and Shartel avenues. It shows the downtown Pei imagined, while the one that was actually built lies just outside.
