# Oklahoma City National Memorial

The chairs that remember - where 168 people died when Timothy McVeigh bombed the federal building on April 19, 1995, in the deadliest act of domestic terrorism in American history until that time.

**Location:** 35.4731, -97.5170

**Categories:** oklahoma, terrorism, memorial, 1995, domestic-terrorism

## Introduction

At 9:02 AM on April 19, 1995, a Ryder truck packed with nearly 5,000 pounds of ammonium nitrate fertilizer and racing fuel exploded outside the Alfred P. Murrah Federal Building in downtown Oklahoma City. The blast killed 168 people, including 19 children in the building's daycare center. It injured over 680 others and damaged or destroyed 324 buildings within a sixteen-block radius. Timothy McVeigh, a decorated Gulf War veteran turned anti-government extremist, had deliberately chosen April 19 - the second anniversary of the Waco siege's fiery end - to strike against what he saw as federal tyranny. The Oklahoma City bombing was the deadliest terrorist attack on American soil until September 11, 2001. Today, 168 empty chairs stand in rows on the former building's footprint - one for each victim, smaller chairs for the children - a memorial to lives ended in an instant of rage.

## The Attack

Timothy McVeigh drove the rented Ryder truck into Oklahoma City early on April 19, 1995. He parked in a drop-off zone directly beneath the Alfred P. Murrah Federal Building's America's Kids daycare center. At 9:02 AM, he detonated the bomb. The explosion sheared off the building's north face, collapsing nine floors into rubble. The blast wave shattered windows for miles. Downtown workers heard what sounded like thunder and emerged to find smoke rising from a crater. First responders arrived within minutes, beginning a rescue operation that would continue for weeks. Some survivors were pulled from the rubble; many were not. McVeigh, driving his getaway car without a license plate, was stopped by a state trooper 90 minutes later and arrested for carrying an illegal firearm. He had no idea he was already a suspect.

## The Bomber

McVeigh was a paradox: a soldier who had served with distinction in Iraq, earning a Bronze Star, but who returned home alienated and angry. He became immersed in the militia movement and its conspiracy theories about federal overreach. The sieges at Ruby Ridge and Waco radicalized him completely. He saw the Oklahoma City bombing as revenge for Waco and as a strike against the 'tyrannical federal government.' His accomplice, Terry Nichols, helped build the bomb. McVeigh was convicted of murder and executed by lethal injection on June 11, 2001 - exactly three months before the attacks that would eclipse his atrocity. He never expressed remorse, calling the children 'collateral damage.'

## The Response

The bombing stunned America. In 1995, terrorism meant foreign enemies - the initial assumption was Middle Eastern terrorists. When McVeigh's arrest revealed an American perpetrator, the nation confronted the violence within its own borders. The investigation was massive: within days, the FBI had traced the truck's axle to the rental company and identified McVeigh. Nichols surrendered days later. Oklahoma City itself responded with remarkable solidarity. Rescue workers came from across the country. Blood donations overwhelmed capacity. Strangers comforted survivors. The 'Oklahoma Standard' - the community's response to tragedy - became a model for how cities could heal.

## The Memorial

The Oklahoma City National Memorial opened in 2000 on the former Murrah Building site. The design is powerful in its simplicity. 168 bronze and glass chairs stand in rows on the building's footprint - 19 smaller chairs for the children. The chairs are arranged by floor, showing where each victim worked or, in the case of the daycare children, played. At either end of the memorial, massive bronze gates mark the moments before and after the bombing: 9:01 and 9:03. A reflecting pool fills the space where the street ran. The 'Survivor Tree,' an American elm that withstood the blast, still grows on the site. The memorial museum tells the story through artifacts, oral histories, and an experience designed to convey both the horror and the hope.

## Visiting the Memorial

The Oklahoma City National Memorial & Museum is located at 620 N. Harvey Avenue in downtown Oklahoma City. The outdoor memorial, including the Field of Empty Chairs, Gates of Time, and Survivor Tree, is open 24 hours and free. The indoor museum, which tells the story through exhibits and survivor testimonies, requires admission and typically takes two to three hours. The memorial is particularly moving at night, when the chairs glow from internal lighting. Anniversary observances on April 19 include a 168-second moment of silence beginning at 9:02 AM. Will Rogers World Airport (OKC) is 8 miles southwest. The memorial is one of Oklahoma City's most visited sites, drawing those who remember the day and those born after who seek to understand.

## Flight Context
