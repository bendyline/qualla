# 1948 Tinker Air Force Base tornadoes

Two tornadoes struck Tinker Air Force Base five days apart in March 1948; the first came without warning and wrecked dozens of aircraft, and the second arrived exactly as two Air Force meteorologists had predicted in what NOAA calls the nation's first official tornado forecast.

## Introduction

At 9:52 on the night of Saturday, March 20, 1948, a tornado was sighted near Will Rogers Airport in Oklahoma City. It was seven miles from Tinker Air Force Base, and it was headed that way. No one had predicted thunderstorms that evening, let alone a tornado. Minutes later it was on the base, lit up by nearly constant lightning as it cut across the airfield, tossing around aircraft parked in the open. Five days later, another tornado struck Tinker. This time, the base knew it was coming. Between those two storms lies a turning point in the history of weather forecasting.

## No Warning

Around 10 p.m., the first tornado reached the southwest corner of the base. It bisected Tinker from corner to corner, tossing around the planes parked out in the open. The control tower reported a 78 mph gust before the windows shattered, and several personnel were injured by flying glass. The tornado finally dissipated at the northeast corner of the base. When the losses were counted, they came to around $10 million, the costliest tornado in Oklahoma history up to that time. According to the National Weather Service, 54 aircraft were destroyed, among them 17 C-54 transports valued at $500,000 apiece. The storm is estimated to have been equivalent to an F3 on the Fujita scale, which was not devised until 1971, though in 2025 tornado historian Thomas P. Grazulis described it as "near F5" in intensity.

## Not Forecastable

An official inquiry followed. Air Force investigators concluded that "due to the nature of the storm, it was not forecastable given the present state of the art." They recommended that the meteorological community develop a tornado warning system for the public, along with a protocol for protecting lives and property on military bases. Meanwhile, two of the base's meteorologists, Major Ernest J. Fawbush and Captain Robert C. Miller, went looking for patterns. In the days after the March 20 storm, they studied weather data from past tornado outbreaks and began to notice how much the overall patterns had in common. They were, in effect, trying to learn the signature of a tornado day. They did not have long to wait for a test.

## Lightning Strikes Twice

On Thursday, March 25, the weather began to look familiar. That morning, a forecast went out for heavy thunderstorms, effective from 5 to 6 p.m. As the day went on, Fawbush and Miller saw conditions developing that were strikingly similar to those of five days earlier, and base meteorologists met in the afternoon and composed and issued a tornado forecast, what NOAA calls the nation's first official one. Tinker took it seriously. Equipment that could be moved was placed in bomb-proof shelters, and personnel were moved to safer areas. At around 6 p.m., a tornado struck the base, just as predicted. It caused about $6 million in damage, but thanks to the precautions, injuries were kept to a minimum. The forecast had not stopped the storm, but it had changed what the storm could do to the people in its path.

## The Forecast That Stayed

The March 25 forecast is what NOAA calls the nation's first official tornado forecast, and it was a successful one. Because of the lives and money it had saved, Fawbush and Miller kept issuing tornado forecasts, and over the next three years their predictions verified at a high rate. The Weather Bureau began using the pair's forecasts to predict what it called severe local storms, and in February 1951 the Air Force established its Severe Weather Warning Center. The work begun in a base weather office after a destructive Saturday night marked the beginning of tornado forecasting as a practice rather than a hope. Tinker would be touched by tornadoes again, including the 1999 Bridge Creek-Moore tornado, but March 1948 remains the moment when the sky over Oklahoma first became something that could be anticipated.

## Flight Context

Tinker Air Force Base lies at approximately 35.4147° N, 97.3867° W on the southeast side of Oklahoma City, with Interstate 40 running along its northern edge. Tinker (KTIK) is an active military installation, and Will Rogers World Airport (KOKC), near which the March 20 tornado was first sighted, lies about 12 miles to the west; the area sits within busy Oklahoma City controlled airspace, so coordinate with air traffic control and remain clear of military operations. From 3,000 to 4,000 feet above ground level, the base's long runways and large maintenance hangars are unmistakable against the surrounding suburbs. Central Oklahoma sits in the heart of Tornado Alley: spring brings rapidly developing severe thunderstorms, and pilots should check convective outlooks and SIGMETs before flying in the region.
