# Overholser Mansion

Built among farm fields in 1903 by the man many call the father of Oklahoma City, the turreted Overholser Mansion still keeps its French stained glass, Italian light fixtures and a third-floor ballroom reached only by the back stairs.

**Location:** 35.4853, -97.5194

**Categories:** Historic house museums in Oklahoma, Museums in Oklahoma City, Houses on the National Register of Historic Places in Oklahoma, Châteauesque architecture, Reportedly haunted locations in Oklahoma

## Introduction

In 1903 Henry and Anna Ione Overholser finished a house on land far from the center of Oklahoma City, with farmland all around it. The city has long since grown past it. Today the house stands in the Heritage Hills neighborhood, and it is considered Oklahoma City's first mansion. It has 11,700 square feet of buff brick and red sandstone, an octagonal tower with a pointed roof, and a ballroom tucked under the third-floor dormers that can be reached only by the back stairs. The house has come through more than a century largely intact, and according to local ghost stories, so has its first mistress.

## The Man Who Built a City

Many people consider Henry Overholser the father of Oklahoma City. He had already made a fortune by the time he arrived in Oklahoma Territory in 1889, and he began buying lots right away. He put up business buildings and paid for capital improvements with his own money in the town that would become Oklahoma City. On October 23, 1889, he married Anna Ione Murphy, who became one of the city's socialites. In 1901 Overholser bought land for a home, and he hired W. S. Matthews, a London-trained architect, to design it. The house cost $38,000. In 1903, the year the house was finished, Overholser also built an opera house on Grand Avenue at a cost of $108,000. He was building for the city and for his own family at the same time.

## A Château on the Prairie

Matthews designed the house in the Châteauesque style. At the southeast corner a three-story octagonal tower rises to an eight-sided pyramidal roof topped with a finial. On the south side a porte-cochère sheltered arriving carriages, facing the neighboring Hales Mansion across the way. The walls are buff-colored brick trimmed with red sandstone. West of the house, built at the same time and in the same brick, stands a two-story carriage house of about 4,000 square feet. It has a pyramid roof with a dormer on each side and a bell-shaped cupola at the peak. Horses once lived on the ground floor, with hay stored in the loft above. Later the stables became a garage and the loft became servants' quarters. Today the building holds offices and event space.

## Painted Ceilings and Stained Glass

The house has two main entrances, on North Hudson Avenue and NW 15th Street, joined inside by an L-shaped hall. The walls and ceilings were painted by hand and are lit by fixtures imported from Italy. The original French stained-glass windows are still in place, along with the original English carpets and Belgian woodwork. On the ground floor, a colonnade leads from the drawing room into the music room, and the library, dining room and butler's pantry lie nearby. The main staircase climbs past two large stained-glass windows showing Greek women making music, one playing a violin and the other a tambourine. Upstairs are the family bedrooms, a sewing room and a nursery. The back staircase is the only way up to the third floor, where a ballroom sits among dormer nooks next to a spacious maid's room.

## One Family, Then Everyone

Henry Overholser died in 1915. Anna stayed in the house with their daughter, Henry Ione, who was born in 1905 and married David Perry in 1926. Mrs. Overholser transferred ownership to her daughter in 1937 and lived there until her death in 1940. The Perry Bedroom was updated and redecorated in the 1950s. After Henry Ione died in 1959, David Perry kept the house until April 14, 1972, when he sold the mansion and its contents to the Oklahoma Historical Society, with the purchase funded by the Oklahoma chapter of the American Institute of Architects. The house had been listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1970. Preservation Oklahoma, a private nonprofit, has managed it for the Society since 2003, and restoration work since 2008 has repaired its original windows, roof and several chimneys. The house is currently closed to visitors for further restoration work, and the Oklahoma Historical Society expects to welcome them back in 2027. According to The Oklahoman, local ghost stories claim that Anna has never left, and that her ghost still haunts the mansion.

## Flight Context

The Overholser Mansion is in the Heritage Hills historic district just north of downtown Oklahoma City, at about 35.4853°N, 97.5194°W. The downtown skyline, a little over a mile to the south-southeast, makes an easy reference. The mansion sits among the houses of an older residential district and is hard to pick out from the air, so use downtown to orient yourself. Will Rogers World Airport (KOKC) is about 7 nm to the southwest, Wiley Post Airport (KPWA) about 7 nm to the west-northwest, and Tinker Air Force Base (KTIK) about 8 nm to the southeast. With busy terminal airspace all around, fly at about 3,000–3,500 ft AGL and get ATC clearance where required. Visibility is usually good, but spring and early summer bring strong thunderstorms.
