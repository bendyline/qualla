# Centennial Land Run Monument

Paul Moore spent some twenty years casting 45 oversized bronze riders and wagons racing across Bricktown in memory of the 1889 Land Run, and within months of its completion the monument became the site of a debate over whose history it tells.

## Introduction

The last piece to join the stampede was a horse carrying two children. A sister holds on to her brother as the animal gallops forward, and the sculptor, Paul Moore, modeled the pair on his own grandchildren. It was installed in December 2019, completing a bronze charge that had taken some twenty years to build at the southern point of the Bricktown Canal in Oklahoma City. Seven months later, protesters gathered at the monument for a sit-in. The Centennial Land Run Monument commemorates the Land Run of 1889 in the Unassigned Lands, and it has become a place where Oklahomans argue about what that event meant.

## Twenty Years of Bronze

Work on the monument began in 2000, with a further contract signed in 2003, and the result has been described in local news coverage as among the world's largest bronze sculptures. Moore's installation features 45 bronze figures, each one and a half times life size, depicting horsemen and wagons racing across the Oklahoma landscape. The scene spreads over an area slightly larger than a football field, so that it reads less like a statue than a whole scene. The project cost about $6.2 million, with state and federal dollars helping to cover the costs. The National Sculpture Society has honored Moore with its Special Medal of Honor, praising the quality of his sculpture. The effect is of a single instant frozen in motion, horses and wagons straining forward toward land their riders hope to claim.

## A Sit-In Among the Horses

On July 11, 2020, the organization SPIRIT, the Society to Protect Indigenous Rights and Indigenous Treaties, held a sit-in at the monument. The timing mattered. Just two days earlier, the U.S. Supreme Court had issued its decision in McGirt v. Oklahoma, a landmark case concerning the Muscogee Nation's reservation. The protesters argued that the monument erased Native American history, particularly that of the Muscogee Nation. They also spoke about the harm that land-run reenactments in schools had done to Native American students. Armed counter-protesters arrived, and they heckled the speakers, but no violence occurred. The afternoon made visible a disagreement that the bronze figures, frozen in their single moment of hope and hurry, could not express on their own.

## Adding to the Story

In the years after the 2020 sit-in, SPIRIT has gathered opinions from local residents about the monument and its future. Some of the responses are blunt. One resident wrote that the statue "feels like a celebration of colonization, of the stealing of Indigenous land and disenfranchisement of [Native] people, and like a whitewashing of history that overlooks how that land came to be open for white settlers." Another suggested adding "a space with Native art that celebrates our past, present, and future," with "QR codes that provide more historical context of the removal treaties." SPIRIT's own stated vision stops short of removal. The group has described a process in which Indigenous historians, authors and artists would collaborate on a new public monument "that would add to the story already being told, not eliminate or replace it."

## When the Earth Remembers

That vision has begun to take shape. In July 2025, the Oklahoma City council approved an Indigenous art installation to be placed near the Land Run Monument, the work of artist Addison Karl, which grew out of a collaboration between SPIRIT and the city after the 2020 protest. Titled When the Earth Remembers, it features cast-concrete bison bones and cast bronze bison skulls arranged in a counterclockwise spiral, a work meant to symbolize both Indigenous land loss and cultural resilience. The city allocated $50,000 toward the project. If it is completed as planned, visitors to the southern end of the canal will find two works in conversation: one showing the rush toward the land, and one remembering what the land held before the rush began. That is exactly what SPIRIT proposed: not erasing one story, but adding another beside it.

## Flight Context

The Centennial Land Run Monument lies at approximately 35.4611° N, 97.5052° W at the southern end of the Bricktown Canal, just east of downtown Oklahoma City. The bronze figures are too small to pick out except at low altitude, so navigate by the downtown skyline, dominated by the Devon Energy Center tower, and by the canal winding south through the Bricktown entertainment district; Interstate 40 runs east-west just to the south. Will Rogers World Airport (KOKC) is about 7 miles to the southwest, and Wiley Post Airport (KPWA) about 9 miles to the northwest. Downtown lies within busy Oklahoma City controlled airspace, and minimum-altitude rules over congested areas apply. Central Oklahoma sees strong spring thunderstorms and frequent gusty winds.
