# First Americans Museum

Three decades in the making, Oklahoma City's First Americans Museum tells the stories of the state's 39 tribal nations in a building where every wall, column and mound carries meaning.

**Location:** 35.4578, -97.4812

**Categories:** Museums, Native American history, Native American culture, Architecture, Oklahoma City

## Introduction

Ten columns of stone hold up the glass semi-dome at the heart of the First Americans Museum in Oklahoma City, and they are there to be counted. Each one represents the ten miles Indigenous people traveled each day during their forced removal to Indian Territory. The differences in their stone stand for the distinct tribal groups who came to this land. Opened on September 18, 2021, the museum tells the histories of the 39 federally recognized tribes of Oklahoma, in a building meant to be read as closely as anything in its galleries.

## Three Decades to Open the Doors

The idea for a museum celebrating Oklahoma's Indigenous peoples grew out of the state's 1989 celebration of the land run. Work on what was first called the American Indian Cultural Center and Museum began in the 1990s. Construction started in 2006, then stopped in 2012 when state funding ran out, and for seven years the project waited. It resumed in 2019 after responsibility moved from the State of Oklahoma to Oklahoma City, with financial help from a Chickasaw Nation subsidiary, the American Indian Cultural Center Foundation and numerous donors. It was not the city's first Native museum. The Center of the American Indian, a Native-led intertribal museum, operated in Oklahoma City from 1978 to 1992. When the new doors opened in 2021, the institution carried a new name: the First Americans Museum, or FAM.

## A Building That Speaks

Johnson Fain Architects and Hornbeek Blatt Architects of Edmond designed the museum as a sequence of statements. Visitors first pass through the Remembrance Gate, two inclining 40-foot walls of Mesquabuck stone. Inscribed in the stone is the name of the Potawatomi Chief Mes'kwah-buk, "whose name signifies the color of the sky at sunrise and sunset." Next comes the Hall of the People, inspired by the traditional grass houses of the Wichita, the area's original inhabitants, with its glass semi-dome rising on those ten columns of varied stone. Behind the museum curves a semi-circular earthen mound, a tribute to the great mound-builder civilizations. The sunken space inside the mound serves as an outdoor Festival Grounds. The project's redevelopment of the site also won the EPA Region 6 Award and a National Phoenix Award for Best Brownfield Project in 2022.

## Thirty-Nine Nations, Many Voices

The museum's mission is "to serve as a dynamic center promoting awareness and educating the broader public about the unique cultures, diversity, history, contributions, and resilience of the First American Nations in Oklahoma today." On the first floor, the OKLA HOMMA gallery chronicles the histories of the 39 tribes. Its title echoes the Choctaw words okla, "people," and humma, "red," which Choctaw delegate Allen Wright put forward as a name during the treaty negotiations that followed the Civil War, the origin of the word Oklahoma. Native professionals shape the institution from the top. In 2024, Dr. Kelli Mosteller (Citizen Potawatomi Nation) became executive director and CEO, succeeding James Pepper Henry (Kaw/Muscogee). Curator heather ahtone is Choctaw and Chickasaw, and Greg Wadley (Choctaw Nation) was board chair when Mosteller was appointed. In the on-site 39 Restaurant, Loretta Barrett Oden (Citizen Potawatomi) helped establish the menu's Indigenous cuisine, and in 2026 Kickapoo chef Crystal Wahpepah became consulting chef.

## Objects Coming Home

Upstairs, the exhibit WINIKO: Life of an Object gathers more than 100 objects collected from Oklahoma tribes in the early 1900s, many of them by archaeologist Mark R. Harrington, on loan from the National Museum of the American Indian, alongside newly commissioned works of art. Co-curated by heather ahtone and Welana Queton, it was developed in consultation with representatives from the 39 tribes and Knowledge Keepers. In 2024 the National Endowment for the Arts awarded the museum a grant for this reunification work. Recognition has come quickly for so young an institution: an Engineering News-Record Award of Excellence for Best Public Facility in the United States and Oklahoma's RedBud Award for Outstanding New Attraction in 2022, an Urban Land Institute national award for Best Public Facility in 2023, and a 2024 American Alliance of Museums award for advancing diversity, equity, accessibility and inclusion in museums.

## Flight Context

The First Americans Museum is at 35.4578°N, 97.4812°W in Oklahoma City. From 2,000–3,000 feet AGL, look for the glass semi-dome of the Hall of the People and the large semi-circular earthen mound behind the museum, the most distinctive shapes on the site. Nearby airports: Tinker Air Force Base (KTIK, military), about 5 nm ESE; Oklahoma City's Will Rogers airport (KOKC), about 7 nm SW; and Wiley Post Airport (KPWA), about 9 nm WNW. This is busy metropolitan airspace near both KOKC and Tinker, so stay in contact with approach control. Spring and summer thunderstorms are common in central Oklahoma.
