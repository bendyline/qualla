# Oklahoma City National Memorial

On the footprint of the Alfred P. Murrah Federal Building stand 168 bronze and glass chairs - one for every person killed on April 19, 1995, and nineteen of them small, because nineteen of the dead were children. The memorial does what memorials are supposed to do: it makes the absence visible.

## Introduction

The chairs are arranged in nine rows, one for each floor of the building that used to stand here. They are made of bronze and glass, the base inscribed with a name, the seat lit from within so that after dark the field of them glows quietly above the grass. There are one hundred and sixty-eight chairs. Nineteen of them are smaller than the others, sized for children, because nineteen of the people killed on the morning of April 19, 1995, were children - most of them in the America's Kids daycare center on the second floor of the Alfred P. Murrah Federal Building. The chairs are arranged by floor. The daycare is on the chairs' second row. Visitors come and find a chair by name. They leave flowers in the grass, or photographs, or letters, or stones. The grounds are open twenty-four hours a day. Surviving family members come early in the morning, and again in the evening, and they have been coming for thirty years.

## The Gates of Time

Two enormous bronze gates frame the long reflecting pool that runs through the center of the memorial. The east gate is inscribed with the time 9:01. The west gate reads 9:03. Between them is the moment that changed Oklahoma City: 9:02 on a Wednesday morning, when a rented Ryder truck packed with nearly five thousand pounds of ammonium nitrate detonated at the building's north curb. The reflecting pool occupies what used to be Fifth Street. Standing on either gate and looking through, you see your own face on the water before you see anything else - which is the design's intention. The minute before. The minute after. The memorial does not commemorate the explosion. It commemorates the minute that came before, when one hundred sixty-eight people were going about an ordinary morning, and the minute that came after, when the entire country had to learn how to grieve a kind of violence it had not seen at home before.

## The Survivor Tree

An American elm stood in the parking lot across the street from the Murrah Building. The blast stripped its branches, set the cars beneath it on fire, embedded glass and metal in the trunk. Arborists who examined it that summer assumed it would not survive. The next spring, it leafed out. The Survivor Tree, as it became known, anchors the memorial's high terrace, still bearing the scars in its bark beneath the new growth. Seeds and cuttings are propagated each year and distributed to other communities marked by tragedy: Columbine, Sandy Hook, Las Vegas, the Pulse nightclub in Orlando, Charleston's Mother Emanuel A.M.E. Church. The seedlings travel, take root, grow. The metaphor is unsubtle. The metaphor is also accurate. A living tree continues to do the work.

## What the Museum Holds

Across the street from the chairs, in the old Journal Record Building that was itself damaged in the blast, the Oklahoma City National Memorial Museum walks visitors through April 19, 1995, in chronological order. The exhibit begins with the ordinary - the radio broadcasts of that Wednesday morning, the breakfast plates, the children being dropped at daycare. In one early room, a recording of a hearing taking place that morning at the Oklahoma Water Resources Board, in the building next door, plays for visitors. You hear the routine business of the meeting; then you hear the blast. The recording is not edited. After the explosion, the exhibit turns to the rescue: the firefighters, the nurses, the construction workers who pulled neighbors out of rubble. The image you may remember - firefighter Chris Fields carrying one-year-old Baylee Almon - was taken by an amateur photographer named Charles Porter who happened to be a block away. Baylee did not survive. The photograph won the Pulitzer Prize. Her mother, Aren, asked for it to be used to remind people that the children were real.

## What the Memorial Asks of Visitors

There is a mission statement carved into the gates: 'We come here to remember those who were killed, those who survived, and those changed forever. May all who leave here know the impact of violence.' It is direct. It asks something of you. The Field of Empty Chairs occupies an actual building footprint, not a symbolic one - the chairs stand where the floors stood. Walking the grass between them, the absence of the building becomes physical. You can read the names: Tevin D'Aundrae Garrett, age sixteen months. Antonio Ansara Cooper Jr., age six months. P.J. Allen and Brandon Denny, two children who survived the blast and grew up. The memorial does not ask visitors to forgive. It does not ask anything political. It asks only that the people who died here be remembered as people - not as the data point a domestic terror attack produces, but as the lives those one hundred sixty-eight names belonged to. Families still come. Survivors still come. The chairs still light up at night.

## Getting There

The Oklahoma City National Memorial is at 620 North Harvey Avenue, in the heart of downtown Oklahoma City between Fifth Street and Sixth Street. The outdoor memorial - the chairs, the gates, the reflecting pool, the Survivor Tree - is open twenty-four hours a day and is always free. The indoor museum across the street charges admission and is open daily. Evening is when the chairs are at their most powerful: each glass base glows from within, and the long pool reflects the lit chairs back. Will Rogers World Airport (KOKC) is about ten miles southwest. The memorial sits two blocks from the Myriad Botanical Gardens and a short walk from the Bricktown entertainment district, but visitors who have spent an hour here often need a quieter rest of the day. The memorial earns its quiet.
