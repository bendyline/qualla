# Gold Dome

Oklahoma City's Gold Dome, the fifth geodesic dome ever built and the first used as a bank, has spent decades dodging the wrecking ball on Route 66 while one owner after another tried to decide what it should be.

**Location:** 35.4928, -97.5308

**Categories:** Architecture, Route 66, Historic preservation, Geodesic domes, Oklahoma City, National Register of Historic Places in Oklahoma

## Introduction

In 2001, one of the strangest buildings on Route 66 was on a deadline. Bank One wanted to sell its corner at NW 23rd Street and North Classen Boulevard to Walgreens, and the geodesic dome standing there was in the way. That dome was the fifth of its kind ever constructed and the first ever used as a bank. The Gold Dome survived that winter, and several close calls after it. Its story since then has been one long, public argument over what a landmark is worth and who should pay to keep it.

## Banking Under a Geodesic Sky

Citizens State Bank began construction here in 1958. The building uses the geodesic dome design created by the futurist and architect Buckminster Fuller, and the architects on the project were Bailey, Bozalis, Dickinson and Roloff. The dome measures 145 feet across and is made of 625 panels, ranging from 7.5 to 11.5 feet in length and weighing 60 to 70 pounds apiece. Only four geodesic domes had been constructed anywhere in the world before this one, and none of them had been used as a bank. Standing beside Route 66 in Oklahoma City, the Gold Dome became a landmark of the road.

## The Drugstore Deadline

Its neighborhood gave it allies. Twenty-third Street runs between the urban conservation districts of the Paseo and Jefferson Park to the north and the historic preservation districts of Mesta Park and Heritage Hills to the south, and a new zoning area would require owners to get a design review board's permission before demolishing or modifying buildings. Bank One, however, said the dome was too large to serve as a bank and that refurbishing it would cost roughly $1.7 million. It intended to sell to Walgreens, which would have put a new pharmacy across the street from its competitor, Eckerd. In August 2001 the Bank One president offered alternatives to demolition, including a smaller bank building on part of the property. A Bethany company called Blue Stuff, which had outgrown its own location, seemed to be the only viable hope for saving the dome. December came and the deadline expired, yet the bank agreed once more to postpone demolition until January.

## Saved, and Saved Again

An ownership group, Gold Dome LLC, formed to preserve the building. The dome was declared eligible for the National Register of Historic Places in 2002 and was listed in 2003. Keeping it proved harder than saving it. A later owner fell behind on loans the Oklahoma City council had secured for the building through the Department of Housing and Urban Development, and on April 12, 2011, the council voted to take over responsibility for repaying the HUD loan. Unpaid property taxes followed. Bank 7 in Oklahoma City foreclosed, and on September 13, 2012, the Gold Dome went up for public auction. A local developer bought it for $800,000. In September 2012 he said he had no plans to tear it down, but in March 2013 he applied for a demolition permit. The city refused to issue it.

## Fish Tanks, Groceries and a Possible Encore

New plans kept arriving. In June 2013, the Edmond environmental engineering firm TEEMCO announced it would renovate the dome and move its 65-person operation inside, with large saltwater and freshwater fish tanks in the lobby and a digital touchscreen wall for guests. Then oil prices fell, TEEMCO fell into financial distress, and by March 2015 the property was back on the market. That May, Land Run Commercial Properties bought it for $1.1 million, and in August 2016 the developers filed for a $2.5 million building permit to convert it into a Natural Grocers store while preserving the dome. In 2021 they entered negotiations with TempleLive, which operates live music venues in historic Masonic temples in Ohio, Arkansas and Kansas and began examining whether the Gold Dome could become a concert venue. That sale did not close. The city later backed a Tulsa promoter's concert-venue plan with a $3 million tax-increment loan, but the dome sat vacant, and on September 1, 2026, after a foreclosure, it sold at a sheriff's sale for $1.1 million to a buyer who had not been publicly identified. With the sale still awaiting court confirmation, the dome's next act was still being written.
