# Wild Mary Sudik

In the spring of 1930 an oil well on a Czech immigrant family's dairy farm south of Oklahoma City blew out, fountained oil 400 feet high for eleven days, and became a national radio sensation that helped make blowout preventers standard equipment.

**Location:** 35.3850, -97.4539

**Categories:** 1930 in the environment, History of Oklahoma City, Oil spills in the United States, Oil wells in Oklahoma

## Introduction

Mary Sudik never went looking for fame. She had simply been the first to sign the lease. Because her signature came first, the wells drilled on the Sudik farm south of Oklahoma City carried her name, and on March 26, 1930, the first of them tore loose. For eleven days the gusher the country came to know as Wild Mary Sudik roared out of the ground, while NBC radio listeners and movie theater audiences watching the newsreels followed every turn of the fight to tame it.

## A Dairy Farm Above the Wilcox

The Sudiks were Czech immigrants who came to Oklahoma from Nebraska in 1903 or 1904 to buy a 160-acre dairy farm. Over the years they added two more quarter sections, and the one they bought in 1924 became the site of the wild well. The Indian Territory Illuminating Oil Company drilled Mary Sudik No. 1 there, about 2,000 feet southeast of what is now the intersection of Interstate 240 and Bryant Avenue, south of downtown Oklahoma City. The target was the newly developed Wilcox formation, and the drill went down 6,471 feet. Nobody on the crew fully appreciated what waited at the bottom.

## Running Wild

The crew underestimated the pressure in the Wilcox, and they made it worse. They had not kept enough drilling mud in the hole, and contrary to the accepted practice of the time, they had not installed a safety head. In the oilfield phrase, they were running the well wild. When it blew, gas came first. After about a day the flow turned to oil, and the well began throwing a fountain as high as 400 feet into the Oklahoma sky, producing some 20,000 barrels of oil and 200 million cubic feet of gas every day. The wind picked up the oil vapor and carried it as far as Norman, eleven miles to the south. Around the well, a safety zone was established to keep away anything that might ignite the gas and oil pouring into the air.

## Eleven Days on the Air

The drama played out before a national audience. NBC's Floyd Gibbons broadcast daily reports from the scene, and newsreels carried the towering black plume into movie theaters. The job of capping the well fell to the American Iron and Machine Company, with superintendent H. M. Myracle in charge. The first attempt failed after twelve hours. A second restricted the flow enough that the oil could be diverted into a pit while the crews prepared a final seal. The third try succeeded, and on April 6, 1930, Gibbons told his listeners that the roaring well had finally been closed with a two-ton "overshot" cap. Crews recovered more than 211,000 barrels of oil from around the site, but as many as 800,000 barrels were believed to have been wasted.

## A Hard Lesson in Pressure

Once under control, Mary Sudik No. 1 was reported to be the most productive well in the world in 1930. In a test run recorded in its completion report, it produced 15,441 barrels in 13 hours and 15 minutes. Its more lasting legacy, though, was a change in how wells were drilled. The Sudik blowout, together with a similar gas well blowout in Oklahoma City the day after the Sudik well was brought under control, spurred the development and use of blowout preventers as standard equipment in petroleum drilling, and it led to greater regulation of well drilling in Oklahoma City. More than four decades after the blowout, its operator, Cities Service Oil Company, finally plugged Mary Sudik No. 1 on June 22, 1974.

## The Woman Behind the Name

Mary Valish Sudik was a modest woman. Offers came for a vaudeville tour and a movie role, and she turned them down. Instead, she and Vincent left the farm, moved into town, and lived quietly on the royalties from the thirteen wells on their land. Vincent died in 1940. When Mary died two years later, in 1942, her obituary ran on the front page of the Daily Oklahoman, a last reminder of the spring when her name had filled the national airwaves. The oil field that had brought the family its good fortune also brought loss: in 1945 their son Orie was killed while working on a well in the Moore oil field.

## Flight Context

The site of Mary Sudik No. 1 lies at approximately 35.385°N, 97.4539°W, about 2,000 feet southeast of the Interstate 240 and Bryant Avenue interchange in south Oklahoma City. Nothing of the gusher remains visible today, so use I-240 as the reference line and view from roughly 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL. The site sits close to busy airspace: Tinker Air Force Base (KTIK) is only about 4 nm east-northeast, Will Rogers World Airport (KOKC) about 7 nm west, and University of Oklahoma Westheimer Airport (KOUN) in Norman about 8 nm south. Coordinate with Oklahoma City approach, and expect the city grid to make navigation straightforward in clear weather.
