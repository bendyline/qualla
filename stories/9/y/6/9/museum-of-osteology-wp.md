# Museum of Osteology

Born from a dog skull a seven-year-old found in the woods, the Museum of Osteology on the southern edge of Oklahoma City displays hundreds of skeletons, from a humpback whale hung overhead to a rare Javan rhino, all cleaned by beetles next door.

**Location:** 35.3647, -97.4419

**Categories:** Museums in Oklahoma City, Natural history museums in Oklahoma, Osteology, 2010 establishments in Oklahoma, Museums established in 2010

## Introduction

The first one was a dog skull. Jay Villemarette found it in the woods when he was seven years old, and his father encouraged him to bring it home. Most childhood fascinations fade. His did not. In 1986 he founded Skulls Unlimited, a business built around cleaning and preparing skeletons, and in October 2010 he opened the Museum of Osteology on the southern edge of Oklahoma City, a private museum devoted entirely to the study of bones. Today more than 450 skeletons of animal species from around the world are on display, and the dog skull's legacy fills a building.

## A Collection That Outgrew Its Owner

Villemarette began construction on a 12,000-square-foot building in mid-2004, with the museum itself taking about 7,000 square feet, and it took years to bring the project to its October 2010 opening. His goal was twofold: to put his collection on public display and to make osteology, the study of bones and skeletons, more accessible to ordinary people. The collection had grown far beyond anything a single building could show. Beyond the skeletons on display, some 7,000 more specimens are held in the collection, and the museum bills the whole as the largest privately held collection of osteological specimens in the world. The museum is an entity of its parent company, Skulls Unlimited International. For a few years the collection even stretched across two states: a second location opened in Orlando, Florida, in 2015, but it closed in 2020, and its collections were combined with those in Oklahoma.

## The Beetles Next Door

Every skeleton on display begins its second life in the Skulls Unlimited building next door. There, specimens are cleaned by dermestid beetles, which eat away the remaining tissue and leave the bones behind. Only then are the skeletons articulated, each bone fitted back into place, before going on display. It is patient, exacting work, and it is what allows a visitor to see a whole animal's frame rather than a pile of parts. The museum organizes its displays around the form and function of the skeletal system, with educational and taxonomic exhibits representing all five classes of vertebrates, exploring locomotion, adaptation, and forensic osteology and pathology.

## A Whale Overhead

Look up, and the scale of the place becomes clear. Whale skeletons hang from the ceiling, part of what the museum describes as the largest private collection of cetacean skeletons, covering whales, dolphins and porpoises, with 46 different species represented. The centerpiece is a humpback whale that the museum counts among only twelve fully articulated humpback skeletons in North America. It washed ashore in 2003, was buried for two years and was then cleaned by Skulls Unlimited International before being assembled. Elsewhere in the building is what the museum calls the rarest specimen in the entire collection: a Javan rhinoceros. Between the giant overhead and the rarity on the floor, visitors get a vivid sense of the range of the living world, told entirely in bone.

## Bones You Can Touch

This is not a museum of glass cases alone. Guests can touch bones and take pictures with fully articulated skeletons, and the museum hosts educational events aimed at every age group, along with scavenger hunts and activities for children and parents. The collection also includes human skeletons, donated to science and purchased by the museum, displayed alongside the animals as part of the same story of structure and adaptation. Displaying remains that were donated to science has drawn criticism, including from a 2023 episode of HBO's Last Week Tonight, on the grounds that donors may not have expected such a display. The human skeletons are also a quiet reminder that the subject here is not only other species but ourselves. For a collection that began with one dog skull carried home from the woods, the Museum of Osteology has kept faith with that first moment of curiosity, inviting everyone who walks through the door to look closely at what lies beneath the skin.

## Flight Context

The Museum of Osteology lies at approximately 35.3647° N, 97.4419° W on South Sunnylane Road, on the southern edge of Oklahoma City near Moore. The building itself is modest from the air, so navigate by larger features: Tinker Air Force Base (KTIK), with its long runways and huge hangars, lies about 5 miles to the northeast, and Lake Stanley Draper spreads out a few miles to the east. Will Rogers World Airport (KOKC) is about 9 miles to the west-northwest, and the University of Oklahoma's Max Westheimer Airport (KOUN) in Norman lies about 8 miles to the south. The area sits within busy Oklahoma City controlled airspace close to Tinker's military operations, so coordinate with air traffic control. Central Oklahoma sees frequent spring thunderstorms.
