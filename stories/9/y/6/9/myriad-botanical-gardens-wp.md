# Myriad Botanical Gardens

In downtown Oklahoma City, a 224-foot translucent conservatory holds two tropical climates inside 15 acres of free-to-roam gardens, lakes and playful public art.

**Location:** 35.4650, -97.5179

**Categories:** Botanical gardens, Parks, Public art, Oklahoma City, Architecture

## Introduction

In February 1993, Southern Living ran a story with a title that sounds like a contradiction: "In the tropics of Oklahoma." Its subject sits in the middle of downtown Oklahoma City. Myriad Botanical Gardens spreads across 15 acres of paths, lawns, water and sculpture, and at its center lies the Inasmuch Foundation Crystal Bridge Conservatory, a long, glowing tube where tropical plants thrive in the middle of Oklahoma. Outside the conservatory, the grounds are free to roam.

## A Downtown Dream, Built Slowly

The gardens began with the Pei Plan, a downtown urban renewal project that city leaders and the architect I.M. Pei dreamed up in the late 1960s. The first design took Copenhagen's Tivoli Gardens as its model, and city leaders even traveled to Denmark to study it. In 1971, the New York firm Conklin + Rossant was chosen to design the gardens, which took their name from "the Myriad," the convention center later known as the Cox Convention Center. Dean A. McGee, the oil executive who led Kerr-McGee, championed building a garden at the center of the city. Ground was broken on November 17, 1977, and the work went ahead as money became available. In 1981, leaders formed the Myriad Gardens Foundation to raise private funds for the conservatory. It was built from 1983 to 1985, yet it did not open to the public until March 25, 1988.

## A Tube of Captured Light

The Crystal Bridge is 224 feet long and 70 feet in diameter, and its skin is made of 3,028 sections of translucent, double-layered acrylic panels. Inside, 13,000 square feet of plant displays are split between two distinct climates: the Tropical Wet Zone and, at the north end, the Tropical Dry Zone. The design has earned attention and praise in the architectural community. Earlier funds from Project 180 had already brought new landscaping, a children's garden, a restaurant space and reglazed acrylic panels on the Crystal Bridge. Then, after more than 30 years, the conservatory received its first complete renovation, an $11 million project that ran from April 2021 through November 2022. It brought new path layouts, educational exhibits, improved accessibility and a new gift shop, along with a more diverse plant collection, rotating exhibits and new and enhanced water elements.

## Sculpture Around Every Bend

Art waits all over the grounds. Gateway, by Hans Van de Bovenkamp, stands on a raised berm at the northwest corner, and Childhood is Everlasting, a 1992 work by Robin Orbach, occupies the southwest quadrant. On the northwest side of the lake, the Philodendron Dome rises from an 8-by-9-foot base of iron and bronze. Iron vines hold up a canopy of bronze leaves, and visitors can step inside to look up through them. Kenny McCage's kinetic sculpture Flying Fish sits in the east lake, while on the west side Land of the Brave and the Free, a brightly colored wind sculpture of archetypal shapes, was donated to the Festival of the Arts in 2002 by California artist Susan Pascal Beran. Near the north fountain plaza stand the Spirit Poles, a gift from the City of Tulsa marking Oklahoma's statehood centennial in 2007.

## A Calendar Written in Blooms

The gardens keep a year of rituals. On a Saturday in early March, the Oklahoma Gardening School brings an all-day seminar of speakers on Oklahoma gardening topics. Orchids in October has traditionally been a ceremony and luncheon honoring the recipient of the James R. Tolbert III Crystal Orchid Award, and each winter the Crystal Bridge hosts an orchid show. In late November, the gardens join Downtown Oklahoma City's "Downtown in December" celebration, strung with twinkling lights. Between events there is plenty to find: an entrance waterfall at Reno Avenue and Ron Norick Boulevard, an amphitheater, a bandshell on the Devon Lawn and a carousel near the Children's Garden. Getting here is easy, too, since the Oklahoma City Streetcar stops at its own Myriad Gardens station.
