# American Banjo Museum

In downtown Oklahoma City, more than 400 banjos trace the instrument's journey from the Atlantic slave trade through parlor fashion, the Jazz Age and bluegrass to the Muppets and Steve Martin.

**Location:** 35.4667, -97.5115

**Categories:** Musical instrument museums in the United States, Music museums in Oklahoma, Museums in Oklahoma City, Banjo, American music history

## Introduction

For years, whenever a Muppet picked up a banjo, the sound came from a five-string Maya banjo that Martin Kershaw owned and played. The same instrument appears on roughly 7,000 recordings, backing performers such as Frank Sinatra, Bing Crosby, Peggy Lee and Sammy Davis Jr. That banjo was shown in Oklahoma City at the American Banjo Museum during an exhibit on Jim Henson. Its neighbors there include a fretless banjo from 1854, an instrument that Les Paul electrified, and a banjo Steve Martin received with the Mark Twain Prize for American Humor. The banjo has mattered in several very different eras of American music, and this museum sets out to show all of them.

## Tightening the Skin

The museum's exhibits follow the banjo from its arrival in North America through the Atlantic slave trade to the present day. On the ground floor the instruments are arranged in chronological order. By the 1840s, at the start of the minstrel era, banjos had moved away from homemade folk instruments toward a more modern style. One five-string from that decade has a peghead shaped like a lyre, and no one knows who made it. A. B. Bullock of Rhode Island built a fretless banjo in 1854 with a metal body and bolts for tightening the skin head. In the decades after the Civil War, makers kept working on the same problem of how to hold a skin head at the right tension. An 1866 banjo by William B. Tilton does it with hoops and bolts inside the head, and an 1880s instrument uses a wooden hoop tacked to the outside of the body.

## Parlors, Clubs and the Jazz Age

By the 1880s there was a push to make the banjo "new and sophisticated." Upper-class Americans looked down on it as a "musically feeble gadget of the lower classes," but they were fascinated by it all the same. Teachers moved students away from traditional clawhammer playing, and banjo clubs sprang up at colleges and universities. The collection from this period includes an 1894 Washburn presentation banjo with a rim of polished spun silver. Changing musical tastes then brought in the four-string banjo, and the museum's Jazz Age holdings include a 1929 Gibson bass banjo, a 1930 Ludwig Big Chief with wood inlay on the back, and a 1938 Gibson RB-7. A 1955 Gibson PB-250 that Les Paul electrified is displayed opened up, so visitors can see how he modified it.

## The Long Revival

After the Jazz Age the banjo largely disappeared, except in regions such as Appalachia and the Ozarks. Pete Seeger and Earl Scruggs helped bring it back. Scruggs played a Gibson Granada as his main banjo, even after Vega built a special instrument for him, and that Granada is now at the Country Music Hall of Fame in Nashville. The museum itself changed along the way. It was founded in Guthrie, Oklahoma, and originally focused on four-string banjos. By 2013 it had widened its scope to include the five-string, and its hall of fame began honoring five-string players. In 2007 it acquired a European collection of 182 Jazz Age instruments, and in 2009 it moved to Oklahoma City. By 2018 it owned more than 400 instruments and could show more than 300 of them on two floors covering 21,000 square feet.

## Kermit, Steve Martin and the Hall of Fame

The hall of fame has honored a wide range of people. The 2018 class included Béla Fleck and puppeteer Jim Henson, who was inducted for promotion of the banjo. His programs had presented the instrument in a positive way. Beginning in September 2018, the museum ran "Jim Henson – Life and Legacy," curated by Karen Falk of The Jim Henson Company. Heather Henson opened the exhibit, and the Henson family allowed the museum to display one of the Kermit the Frog puppets. Executive director Johnny Baier has said that when many people today think of the banjo, they think of Steve Martin. In a donation announced in December 2019, Martin gave the museum a one-of-a-kind banjo that Gibson made for him as part of the 2005 Mark Twain Prize. It is engraved and gold-plated, with an image of Mark Twain on the back and the Kennedy Center inlaid on the fingerboard. He also donated a Jazz Age Gibson Florentine.

Will Rogers World Airport (KOKC) is about 6 nm southwest, Tinker Air Force Base (KTIK) about 7 nm southeast, and Wiley Post Airport (KPWA) about 8 nm northwest.
