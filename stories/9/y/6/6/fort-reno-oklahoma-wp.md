# Fort Reno (Oklahoma)

A plains cavalry post that watched over a reservation, policed a land rush, raised Army mounts and held World War II prisoners, on ground the Cheyenne and Arapaho have spent decades trying to reclaim.

**Location:** 35.5617, -98.0350

**Categories:** Military history, Indian Territory, Forts, Native American history, National Register of Historic Places in Oklahoma

## Introduction

Around 1897, audiences at Pawnee Bill's Wild West Show could watch "Madeline of Fort Reno," billed as a "great western military romantic play." The real Fort Reno, a former Army cavalry post on the plains west of El Reno, Oklahoma, was never that simple. Named for General Jesse L. Reno, who died at the Battle of South Mountain during the Civil War, the post has watched over a reservation, policed a land rush, supplied the Army with mounts and held enemy prisoners from across the Atlantic. Its ground has also been the subject of a long effort by the Cheyenne and Arapaho to take it back.

## A Camp Beside the Agency

Fort Reno began as a temporary camp in July 1874, set up near the Darlington Agency amid unrest that eventually led to the Red River War. When that conflict ended, the soldiers stayed. The post remained to control and protect the Southern Cheyenne and Southern Arapaho reservation, and it became a permanent fort. Both verbs matter: the garrison was there to keep watch over the people on the reservation as much as to shield them. Among the units stationed here was the famed Ninth Cavalry, one of the regiments of Buffalo Soldiers. The fort's name traveled, too. The city of El Reno took it, and so did Reno City, a town that was abandoned before Oklahoma became a state.

## Holding the Line on the Rush

As settlers pressed to open the Unassigned Lands, soldiers from Fort Reno were sent to control Boomer and Sooner activity, trying to keep order during a rush for land. On April 12, 1892, President Benjamin Harrison signed a proclamation that extinguished all Cheyenne-Arapaho claims to their reserve except for individual allotments. It made no mention of any claims to Fort Reno, an omission that would matter a century later. Statehood came in 1907, and on February 24, 1908, the Army abandoned Fort Reno as a post. The place did not empty out. It carried on as a quartermaster remount depot, part of the Army's system for supplying its mounts, long after its days as a frontier garrison were over.

## Prisoners on the Prairie

World War II brought residents no one on the frontier could have imagined. German and Italian prisoners of war were housed on the grounds, and members of the Afrika Korps built the fort's chapel. Some prisoners never went home. The fort's cemetery holds the remains of German and Italian prisoners of war who died in captivity in Oklahoma and Texas, alongside residents of the fort, pioneer settlers and military personnel, a quiet gathering of people whose paths crossed on this stretch of Oklahoma plain for very different reasons. In the late 1940s the Army let the fort go for good and transferred it to the U.S. Department of Agriculture. The USDA uses it as its Grazinglands Research Laboratory, where the work now centers on managing agricultural and environmental systems rather than soldiers and horses.

## Whose Ground Is It?

For decades, the combined Cheyenne-Arapaho Tribes have been trying to reacquire the lands the fort occupied. In 1996 they asked the Clinton administration to get an opinion from the Department of the Interior on their claims. The opinion came in 1999, and it said the tribes had a credible argument that they never ceded the lands used by the military. Several lawmakers tried to help, most notably Eni Faleomavaega of American Samoa in 1997 and Senator Daniel Inouye of Hawaii in 2000. In 2005, Oklahoma Senator Jim Inhofe, with Senator Tom Coburn as co-sponsor, offered a different idea: a bill to let the Secretary of Agriculture lease the oil and gas beneath the fort to pay for preserving the historic site and its buildings. It received a committee hearing and went no further. The old post remains what it has been since 1874, a place where different people hold very different ideas about who it belongs to.

## Flight Context

Fort Reno lies at 35.5617°N, 98.0350°W on open, flat plains just west of El Reno in Canadian County, Oklahoma. A viewing altitude of 2,500–3,500 feet AGL shows the fort grounds with the town of El Reno to the east. Nearest airports: El Reno Regional (KRQO), about 6 nm SSE; Clarence E. Page Municipal (KRCE), about 11 nm ESE; and Oklahoma City's Will Rogers airport (KOKC), about 24 nm ESE. Visibility over the level terrain is usually good in clear weather, but plan around spring and summer thunderstorms in central Oklahoma.
