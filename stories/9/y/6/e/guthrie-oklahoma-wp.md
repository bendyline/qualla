# Guthrie, Oklahoma

Born in six hours during the Land Run of 1889 and stripped of its capital status in 1910, Guthrie lost its political crown but kept something rarer: Oklahoma's largest intact Victorian downtown.

**Location:** 35.8564, -97.4358

**Categories:** Former state capitals in the United States, Cities in Logan County, Oklahoma, Historic districts, Land Run of 1889, Victorian architecture

## Introduction

On the day of the 1910 capital election, Governor Charles N. Haskell was in Tulsa and the state seal of Oklahoma was in Guthrie. It did not stay there long. Haskell ordered his secretary, W. B. Anthony, to have Secretary of State Bill Cross get the seal to Oklahoma City, even though Logan County Sheriff John Mahoney had served a restraining order blocking the transfer. Anthony obtained written authorization from Cross, retrieved the seal from the Logan County courthouse and delivered it to Oklahoma City. Losing the capital was a heavy blow for Guthrie. It also turned out to be the reason the town still looks the way it did when the seal left.

## Six Hours in April

Guthrie began in 1887 as a railroad station called Deer Creek on the Southern Kansas Railway, later renamed for John Guthrie, a jurist from Topeka, Kansas. Then came the Land Run. Some 50,000 would-be settlers massed along the edges of the Unassigned Lands, a section of Indian Territory covering about two million acres at the center of the lands the Muscogee (Creek) and Seminole nations had been forced to cede to the United States after the Civil War. At noon on April 22, 1889, cannons boomed to launch President Benjamin Harrison's "Hoss Race," and people ran for farmland and town lots alike. Over the next six hours, about 10,000 people settled at Guthrie, which became the capital of the new Territory of Oklahoma. What followed happened astonishingly fast. Within months, Guthrie had become a brick-and-stone "Queen of the Prairie," complete with municipal water, electricity, a mass-transit system and underground parking garages for horses and carriages.

## Promoters and Gunfighters

Territorial Guthrie drew ambitious people of every stripe. Hobart Johnstone Whitley, later known as the Father of Hollywood, was the first president of the Guthrie Chamber of Commerce and built the territory's first brick block building for his National Loan and Trust Company. Local people asked him to be Oklahoma's first governor. Whitley traveled to Washington, D.C., and persuaded Congress to let Guthrie be the capital of the future state, a provision written into the 1906 Oklahoma Enabling Act. The frontier had not entirely left town, though. In April 1898, the outlaw Richard "Little Dick" West, a former member of the Wild Bunch gang, was tracked to Guthrie by a posse under Deputy U.S. Marshal Chris Madsen. West refused to surrender and was killed in the gunfight that followed. He is buried in Guthrie's Summit View Cemetery, the same cemetery as Bill Doolin, the outlaw who founded the Wild Bunch.

## The Capital That Got Away

By 1907, when Guthrie became the first capital of the new state, it looked like a well-established Eastern city. Oklahoma City, however, was pulling ahead. It had become a major junction for several railroads and had drawn a major meatpacking industry, and its business leaders began campaigning for the capital soon after statehood. The 1910 special election was not close: 96,488 votes for Oklahoma City, 31,031 for Guthrie and 8,382 for Shawnee. Then the seal made its hurried trip. Guthrie remained the seat of Logan County, but economic influence had moved on, and the town settled into a quieter life about 32 miles north of its old rival. That quiet proved to be a kind of protection. Growth and inattentive urban planning led Oklahoma City and other towns to destroy much of their early downtown architecture, while much of Guthrie's central business and residential district survived intact.

## A Victorian Town Preserved

Walk Guthrie's streets today and the payoff is everywhere. It is the largest urban historic district in Oklahoma, with 2,169 buildings across 1,400 acres and 400 city blocks, and in 1999 the National Park Service designated its center district a National Historic Landmark. The Victorian storefronts now frame carriage tours, replica trolley cars, specialty shops, art galleries and Wild West entertainment, and the town claims to be the "Bed and Breakfast" capital of Oklahoma. Filmmakers have noticed. Guthrie's main street appears briefly in Rain Man, the town supplied the drive-in scene in Twister, and The Killer Inside Me was filmed here. The Oklahoma Territorial Museum keeps the old capital's story, and the Oklahoma International Bluegrass Festival draws about 15,000 visitors a year.

## Pavarotti in the Temple

For a city of about 10,750 people at the 2020 census, Guthrie has an unusually rich stage. The Guthrie Scottish Rite Masonic Temple, one of the world's largest Masonic centers, has brought Henry Mancini, Lena Horne, Victor Borge and Luciano Pavarotti to local audiences over the years. The Pollard Theatre Company, Oklahoma's oldest year-round professional theatre company, produces six or more plays and musicals annually with artists from across the United States, and its holiday favorite, A Territorial Christmas Carol, returns every season. The National Finals Steer Roping Rodeo is held here as well. A town that was settled in a single afternoon and then passed over for something bigger has made a lasting virtue of staying put.

## Flight Context

Guthrie lies at approximately 35.86°N, 97.44°W along Interstate 35, about 32 miles north of Oklahoma City in the Cross Timbers of central Oklahoma. From 2,500-4,000 feet AGL, the compact grid of the historic downtown stands out against surrounding farmland, with Liberty Lake and Guthrie Lake to the south. Guthrie-Edmond Regional Airport (KGOK) sits about 2 nautical miles south of town, and Will Rogers World Airport (KOKC) is roughly 33 miles south-southwest. Spring thunderstorms are common in central Oklahoma; prevailing summer winds are from the south.
