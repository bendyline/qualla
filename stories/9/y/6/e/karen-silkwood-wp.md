# Karen Silkwood

A 28-year-old lab technician and union activist who documented safety problems at an Oklahoma plutonium plant, Karen Silkwood died on a dark stretch of highway while driving to meet a reporter, reportedly with her evidence, in a crash that was never entirely explained.

**Location:** 35.8500, -97.5800

**Categories:** American whistleblowers, American women trade unionists, Kerr-McGee, Nuclear safety, Unsolved deaths

## Introduction

The health physics team that followed Karen Silkwood home on November 7, 1974, found plutonium where no one should ever find it: traces on several surfaces, especially in the bathroom and the refrigerator. Her house would later be stripped and some of her belongings destroyed. Silkwood was a 28-year-old laboratory technician at the Kerr-McGee Cimarron plant near Crescent, Oklahoma, where she made plutonium pellets, and she had spent the autumn documenting what she believed were serious safety failures. Six days after that search of her home, she died on State Highway 74 while driving to meet a New York Times reporter. The circumstances have never been entirely explained.

## Chemistry Was Her Best Subject

Karen Gay Silkwood was born in 1946 in Longview, Texas, and grew up in Nederland, a straight-A student and National Honor Society member whose best subject was chemistry. In 1964 she enrolled at Lamar State College of Technology in Beaumont on a scholarship but left the next year to elope with William Meadows, an oil pipeline worker. They had three children. After she left the marriage in 1972, she moved to Oklahoma City and worked briefly as a hospital clerk. That August, Kerr-McGee hired her as a metallography laboratory technician at its Cimarron River plutonium plant near Crescent. She soon joined the Oil, Chemical & Atomic Workers Union local, and in November 1972 took part in a strike over poor working conditions. The company broke the strike by hiring people from the surrounding area to cross the picket line.

## Taking Notes on the Plant

In August 1974, Silkwood was elected to the local's three-person bargaining committee, the first woman to hold such a position at Kerr-McGee. It was a critical moment: a decertification drive had forced a union election for October 16. Her duties included health and safety, and she found what she considered numerous violations: workers exposed to contamination, faulty respiratory equipment, improperly stored samples and too few showers. She also found evidence of missing or misplaced plutonium. On September 26, the committee met in Washington with Tony Mazzocchi, the union's legislative director. "When I explained the connection between plutonium exposure and cancer," he later wrote, "it took Karen by surprise." Mazzocchi and his staffer Steve Wodka advised the workers to take their concerns to the Atomic Energy Commission and the national press. The next day, Silkwood told the AEC she had been contaminated with plutonium and alleged that safety standards had been relaxed to increase production.

## Three Days in November

Between November 5 and 7, Silkwood repeatedly tested positive for plutonium contamination. On the morning of the 7th she arrived at the plant severely contaminated, even exhaling contaminated air, and the search of her home followed. She, her boyfriend Drew Stephens and her roommate Sherri Ellis were sent to Los Alamos National Laboratory to measure the plutonium in their bodies. How it happened became a bitter dispute. Silkwood said the bathroom contamination may have come from a urine sample she spilled that morning, which fit the extremely high levels found in the samples collected at home. She concluded that someone working for Kerr-McGee had deliberately contaminated her; the company's management alleged she had contaminated herself to damage its reputation. Author Richard Rashke later wrote that the plutonium in her home came from pellet lot 29, a production area where she did not work, whose samples were kept in a vault she could not access.

## Seven Miles on Highway 74

On November 13, Silkwood attended a union meeting at the Hub Cafe in Crescent. A fellow member, Wanda Jean Jung, later stated in a sworn affidavit that Silkwood cried quietly during a break, afraid she would get cancer from the plutonium in her lungs, but said she was glad she had "all the proof concerning falsification of records." At 7:10 p.m. she drove off alone in her Honda Civic toward Oklahoma City, about 30 miles away, to meet reporter David Burnham and Steve Wodka. Less than 30 minutes later she was found dead, 7.3 miles from the cafe. Her car had run off the left side of the highway and struck the wall of a concrete culvert. None of the documents were in it. The Highway Patrol concluded she fell asleep at the wheel; the coroner found the sedative methaqualone in her blood. A. O. Pipkin Jr., a crash investigator hired by the union, found fresh dents on the left rear fender and bumper, damage her friends said had not been there before, and a microscopic analysis turned up paint chips that, he concluded, could only have come from impact with another vehicle.

## What the Questions Left Behind

Some friends and journalists came to believe her car had been rammed from behind, though speculation about foul play was never substantiated. Mazzocchi and Wodka doubted a premeditated killing on such a flat stretch of road, suspecting instead an attempt to frighten her that went tragically wrong. Silkwood was buried in Kilgore, Texas, on November 18. Her contamination and death became a national news story and prompted a federal investigation of security at the Cimarron plant, which, National Public Radio reported, determined that plutonium had been misplaced at the plant. Kerr-McGee shut down its Cimarron nuclear fuel operations in the mid-1970s, and a 1994 Department of Energy report described the Cimarron plant as decontaminated and decommissioned. Her family sued over her contamination; the company settled out of court for $1.38 million without admitting liability. In 1983, Mike Nichols's film Silkwood carried her story to movie audiences and earned Meryl Streep an Academy Award nomination for playing her.
