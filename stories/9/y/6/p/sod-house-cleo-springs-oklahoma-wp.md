# Sod House (Cleo Springs, Oklahoma)

Built from blocks of buffalo-grass sod in 1894 by land-run settler Marshal McCully, this two-room soddy near Cleo Springs is the only remaining sod house in Oklahoma built by a homesteader.

**Location:** 36.4714, -98.4231

**Categories:** Houses on the National Register of Historic Places in Oklahoma, Houses completed in 1894, Sod houses, Oklahoma Historical Society, Museums in Alfalfa County, Oklahoma, Historic house museums in Oklahoma, Houses in Alfalfa County, Oklahoma, National Register of Historic Places in Alfalfa County, Oklahoma

## Introduction

Marshal McCully built his house out of the prairie itself. He ran a sod plow through the buffalo grass that blanketed northwestern Oklahoma, chopped the matted turf into blocks, and stacked them like bricks until he had walls. That was 1894. The house he raised about four miles north of Cleo Springs, in what is now Alfalfa County, still stands on the spot where he built it, and it carries a distinction no other building in the state can claim. According to the Oklahoma Historical Society, it is the only remaining sod house in Oklahoma that was built by a homesteader. Every other one is gone.

## From Land Run to Dugout

McCully came with the rush. When the Cherokee Outlet opened to settlement on September 16, 1893, he took part in the land run, staking his future on a stretch of open prairie with very few trees. A claim, though, is not a home. For his first stretch on the land, McCully lived in a dugout, a shelter cut down into the earth rather than raised above it. Only in 1894 did he build something that stood on its own: a two-room soddy with upright walls and a roof overhead. Moving from dugout to sod house was a real step up in the world, even if both were made of the same ground. On a prairie where timber was so hard to come by, the soil underfoot was the one building material in endless supply, and McCully put it to work.

## Bricks Cut From Buffalo Grass

The method was as practical as it was clever. McCully used a 14-inch sod plow to slice the thick buffalo grass into long rows, then chopped those rows into 18-inch sections. Each block was a dense mat of roots and soil, and he laid them like bricks, course upon course, until the walls rose. The roof called for wood, the one thing the land barely offered. McCully cut poles from the few trees growing in the area and set them as rafters, then covered them with twelve inches of sod. Inside, he plastered the walls with alkali clay. The result was a house made almost entirely from what lay within reach of his plow. Across Oklahoma, sod houses of that era typically measured about fourteen by sixteen feet, with walls roughly seven and a half feet high stacked two or three blocks deep, which put two to three feet of earth between a family and the weather.

## Fifteen Years Beneath the Turf

The McCully family made the soddy their home from 1894 until 1909. That is fifteen years of prairie summers and winters lived behind earthen walls, under a ceiling of poles and turf. During those years the territory around them changed status entirely, and Oklahoma became a state in 1907. In 1909 the family finally moved into a large two-story frame house, a very different kind of statement about permanence on the plains. What matters most to history is what happened to the old house afterward: it survived. The sod house stayed standing on the claim after the family moved out, and that survival is the only reason anyone can see it today. A building meant to get a family through its first hard years ended up outlasting its original purpose by more than a century.

## A Roof Over the Roof

On December 31, 1963, the Oklahoma Historical Society acquired the sod house, and in 1970 it was listed on the National Register of Historic Places. The soddy remains in its original location, but it no longer faces the elements alone. A protective museum building now encloses it, so the old earthen walls sit within a modern shell like an artifact in a display case. Today the site operates as the Sod House Museum. Alongside the house itself, visitors find exhibits, artifacts and photographs, a root cellar, and a building displaying horse-drawn equipment and period farm implements. It is a modest place by any measure. Yet standing beside those thick walls of sod, it is easy to grasp what the first years on this prairie demanded of the people who settled it: a home made from nothing but the ground beneath their feet.

## Flight Context

The Sod House sits at 36.4714°N, 98.4231°W in Alfalfa County, about four miles north of Cleo Springs in northwestern Oklahoma. The soddy itself is small and enclosed within a protective museum building, so look for a compact cluster of buildings set in the section-line grid of wheat and farm fields rather than for the house itself. An altitude of 2,000–3,000 feet AGL shows the surrounding homestead landscape well. Great Salt Plains Lake and its white salt flats lie roughly 25 miles to the northeast and make a strong visual reference. Vance Air Force Base (KEND) and Enid Woodring Regional Airport (KWDG) are about 30–35 miles to the east near Enid, and Alva Regional Airport (KAVK) is about 25 miles to the northwest. Expect gusty prairie winds and summer thunderstorms; visibility is often excellent on clear winter days.
