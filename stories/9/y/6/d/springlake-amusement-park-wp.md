# Springlake Amusement Park

A spring-fed pond in northeast Oklahoma City grew into Springlake, home of the Big Dipper and a stage for Johnny Cash and the Beach Boys, but a whites-only past, a 1971 riot, neglect and fire ended it in a 1981 garage sale and a burial on site.

## Introduction

Somewhere beneath the campus of the Metro Technology Center in northeast Oklahoma City lie the leftovers of a vanished amusement park: huge electric motors from the merry-go-round and other rides, old bumper cars and coaster cars, boxes of paper cups, rolls of tickets and piles of advertising. They were buried on site in 1981, after everything that could be sold had been sold. For nearly sixty years before that, this was Springlake, a place where Oklahoma City went to swim, picnic, ride the Big Dipper and hear some of the biggest names in American music.

## A Pond Becomes a Park

It began with water. Roy Staton's spring-fed pond near NE 38th and Eastern, now Martin Luther King Boulevard, was open to swimmers and picnickers for about six years before he turned it into something bigger, and by 1924 he had built a swimming pool there. Sources differ on whether the amusement park itself dates from 1922 or 1924. Staton kept expanding, acquiring many rides from the defunct Belle Isle Park and building a ballroom. In 1929 he added the Big Dipper roller coaster, which would remain a fixture of the park for the next fifty years. Admission was free, and the rides and pool were pay-as-you-go, so families could picnic beside the lake without spending a cent. That changed only in the 1960s, when the park switched to a single pay-one-price admission.

## Stars by the Lake

Springlake's heyday came in the 1950s and 1960s, when the park was at its most popular and attracted some of the top entertainers of the era. Johnny Cash performed there, and so did Jerry Lee Lewis, the Righteous Brothers, Roy Acuff, Conway Twitty and the Beach Boys. For a park that had started as a swimming hole, it was quite a lineup, spanning country, rock and roll and the surf sound of the West Coast. The combination of a roller coaster, a lake, a ballroom and big-name music made Springlake a favorite Oklahoma City gathering place. Yet the fond memories that many residents carry of those decades are not shared by everyone, because in those same glory years the park was not open to everyone.

## Not Open to All

Oklahoma City's Black community remembers Springlake's golden era as a time when the park restricted its attractions to whites only. Only with the passage of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 did Black residents begin coming to the park without hesitation. Integration came, but the swimming pool did not last long afterward. Springlake's pool, open since 1924, closed for good after the 1967 season and was turned into a Sea-Aquarium, with dolphins swimming where people once had. In 1971, a large riot broke out in the park between white and Black visitors. News of it frightened away potential customers and hastened the park's decline. Springlake never fully recovered.

## Everything Must Go

The final years brought one blow after another. Ownership changed hands to Thomas Traveling Shows, maintenance slipped, and fires struck both the arcade and the owner's nearby home. In the spring of 1981, a large, unadvertised garage sale began the end. The rides, buildings and memorabilia were all offered for sale, and some traveled as far away as Lima, Peru. What no one bought went into the ground. In mid-1981 the Oklahoma City Vo-Tech Board purchased the property, and the Metro Technology Center was built on the site. Of the original park, only the amphitheater still stands. Inside Metro Tech, a car from the Big Dipper and many photographs of the park remain on display, a small museum of a place that meant very different things to different people in Oklahoma City.

## Flight Context

The former Springlake Amusement Park site lies at approximately 35.5131° N, 97.4786° W, near NE 38th Street and Martin Luther King Boulevard in northeast Oklahoma City, now occupied by the Metro Technology Center campus. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet above ground level, the Oklahoma State Capitol, about two miles to the southwest, and the Oklahoma City Zoo and Remington Park area just to the north make good visual references; Interstate 35 runs north-south a short distance to the east. Wiley Post Airport (KPWA) lies about 9 miles to the west, and Will Rogers World Airport (KOKC) about 11 miles to the southwest. The area is within Oklahoma City's busy controlled airspace, and spring thunderstorms are common in central Oklahoma.
