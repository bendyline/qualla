# Elmer McCurdy

Killed after a botched 1911 train robbery, Elmer McCurdy spent 65 years as an embalmed sideshow exhibit before a TV crew discovered he was real, and he was finally laid to rest in Guthrie beside Bill Doolin.

**Location:** 35.8958, -97.4033

**Categories:** Outlaws of the American Old West, Train robbers, Mummies, Sideshow attractions, Guthrie, Oklahoma

## Introduction

On December 8, 1976, the crew of The Six Million Dollar Man was filming at The Pike, an amusement zone in Long Beach, California. A prop man went to move a mannequin hanging from a gallows, and its arm broke off. Inside were human bone and muscle tissue. The figure had never been made of wax. It was the body of Elmer McCurdy, a small-time outlaw killed in Oklahoma in 1911, who had spent the intervening 65 years as a carnival attraction, a movie prop and a curiosity passed from owner to owner. He was finally buried in April 1977 in Guthrie, Oklahoma.

## A Boy from Maine

McCurdy was born on January 1, 1880, in Washington, Maine, to Sadie McCurdy, a 17-year-old who was not married. His father's identity is unknown. One possibility is Sadie's cousin, Charles Smith, and McCurdy later used "Charles Smith" as an alias. To spare Sadie the stigma of the time, her brother George and his wife, Helen, adopted the boy. After George died of tuberculosis in 1890, the family moved to Bangor. When Sadie eventually told Elmer the truth about his parents, he became "unruly and rebellious," and as a teenager he began drinking heavily, a habit he never broke. He trained as a plumber and did good work until an economic downturn in 1898 cost him his job. In 1900 his mother and grandfather died within two months of each other, and McCurdy left Maine. He drifted as a lead miner and plumber, lost job after job to alcohol, and was arrested for public drunkenness in Iola, Kansas, in 1905.

## Forty-Six Dollars

McCurdy turned to robbing banks and trains with nitroglycerin, which he had been trained to use, but he often misjudged the amount, and his jobs were usually bungled. His last robbery came on October 4, 1911, near Okesa, Oklahoma. He and two accomplices meant to stop a Katy train carrying $400,000 in cash, a royalty payment for the Osage Nation. They stopped a passenger train instead and came away with $46 from the mail clerk, two demijohns of whiskey, a revolver, a coat and the conductor's watch. One newspaper called it "one of the smallest in the history of train robbery." McCurdy went back to a friend's ranch near Bartlesville and started on the whiskey, unaware that a $2,000 reward had been posted for him. On October 7 a posse of three deputy sheriffs, Bob and Stringer Fenton and Dick Wallace, confronted him. After a gunfight of about an hour, McCurdy was dead from a single gunshot wound to the chest.

## A Nickel a Look

His body went to Joseph L. Johnson's funeral home in Pawhuska, where it was embalmed with an arsenic-based preservative common at the time. No one paid for the embalming, so Johnson refused to bury or release the body. Instead he put it on display in street clothes with a rifle in its hands, and visitors paid a nickel to see "The Bandit Who Wouldn't Give Up." In October 1916 two men who falsely claimed to be McCurdy's brothers persuaded Johnson to release the body for burial in San Francisco. They shipped it to Arkansas City, Kansas, for the Great Patterson Carnival Shows instead. In 1922 James Patterson sold the operation to showman Louis Sonney, who exhibited McCurdy in his Museum of Crime alongside wax figures of famous outlaws, Bill Doolin and Jesse James among them. In 1928 the body toured with the official sideshow of the Trans-American Footrace. In 1933 director Dwain Esper put it in theater lobbies to promote his film Narcotic!, falsely presenting it as a "dead dope fiend."

## The Long Road to Guthrie

After Louis Sonney died in 1949, the body went into a Los Angeles warehouse, and it later made a brief appearance in the 1967 film She Freak. In 1968 Sonney's son Dan sold it with other wax figures for $10,000 to Spoony Singh of the Hollywood Wax Museum. A windstorm damaged the body during a stop near Mount Rushmore, and Singh decided it looked "too gruesome" to exhibit and sold it to a part owner of The Pike. After the discovery in 1976, tests found tuberculosis in the lungs along with bunions and scars that matched McCurdy's records, and Dan Sonney confirmed the identification. Several funeral homes offered free burial, but Fred Olds of the Indian Territory Posse of Oklahoma Westerns persuaded Los Angeles County coroner Thomas Noguchi to let him bring McCurdy back to Oklahoma. On April 22, 1977, about 300 people gathered at Summit View Cemetery in Guthrie as he was buried next to Bill Doolin, whose wax likeness had once shared a museum with him. Two feet of concrete were poured over the casket to make sure the body could never be stolen. His story has since become the musical Dead Outlaw, which reached Broadway in 2025.

## Flight Context

Elmer McCurdy's grave is in Summit View Cemetery in Guthrie, Oklahoma, at about 35.8958°N, 97.4033°W, beside Bill Doolin. Guthrie lies about 25 nm north of downtown Oklahoma City, and from 2,500–3,000 ft AGL its street grid and downtown are easy to find. Guthrie–Edmond Regional Airport (KGOK) is about 3 nm south of the cemetery. Stillwater Regional (KSWO) is about 22 nm northeast. Oklahoma City's busier airspace, including Tinker Air Force Base (KTIK) and Will Rogers World Airport (KOKC), lies about 30 nm south. Pawhuska, where McCurdy's body was first put on display, is roughly 65 nm to the northeast. Visibility is usually good, but watch for spring and summer thunderstorms.
