# 2013 El Reno tornado

On May 31, 2013, the widest tornado ever recorded in the United States swept across the fields near El Reno, Oklahoma, with radar-measured winds far beyond its official EF3 rating, killing eight people, among them three veteran storm researchers.

**Location:** 35.4792, -97.9000

**Categories:** Tornadoes in Oklahoma, Tornadoes of 2013, 2013 natural disasters in the United States, Storm chasing, Canadian County, Oklahoma

## Introduction

At its widest, the tornado that crossed the farmland southeast of El Reno, Oklahoma, on May 31, 2013, measured 2.6 miles across. Days later, the National Weather Service office in Norman announced that it was the widest tornado ever recorded in the United States. A mobile radar clocked winds of 296 miles per hour about 500 feet above the ground. And yet, because the storm spent most of its forty minutes on the ground over open country, its official rating ended up as EF3. The numbers only hint at what made El Reno so dangerous. It changed shape, speed and direction with terrifying suddenness, and it caught even professional storm chasers off guard.

## The Storm Turns

At 6:28 p.m., the storm began moving into more densely populated parts of Canadian County without losing strength. The Norman forecast office issued a tornado emergency for Yukon, Richland, Wiley Post Airport, Bethany, The Village and eastern El Reno, because the tornado was projected to track into the western Oklahoma City metro area. Within minutes it turned northeast, and at about 6:42 p.m. it crossed Interstate 40, killing two people. Shortly afterward it lifted near Banner Road, having traveled 16.2 miles. What made those forty minutes so treacherous was the tornado's behavior. It abruptly changed direction, swelled dramatically in width in about thirty seconds and sharply accelerated. Multiple vortices spun within and around it, and a vast, translucent outer circulation, wrapped in rain, hid much of it from view.

## Winds Beyond the Rating

El Reno's intensity became a subject of internal debate within the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration. Mobile radar found the strongest winds in small subvortices along the south side of the main funnel, with the two most intense north and east of the intersection of 10th Street and Radio Road, about three miles southeast of El Reno. The weather service first announced an EF5 rating. On August 30, 2013, however, the Norman office revised it to EF3, because the Enhanced Fujita Scale rates tornadoes by the damage they leave, and the survey team found nothing to support a higher rating. The EF5-strength subvortices had simply not struck any structures. William Hooke of the American Meteorological Society put it bluntly: Oklahoma City "dodged a bullet... You lay that path over Oklahoma City, and you have devastation of biblical proportions."

## Into the Bear's Cage

Many chasers were positioned northeast of the tornado, in the area known as the bear's cage, which offers a clear view but little time to react if the storm veers. El Reno veered. Tim Samaras, a respected researcher and National Geographic Explorer, his son Paul Samaras and Carl Young of the TWISTEX team were killed. They were traveling that day in an unmodified Chevrolet Cobalt carrying heavy instrument probes, a car poorly suited to high winds and slick dirt roads. They were the first known tornado deaths among storm chasers or scientific researchers. The Weather Channel's Mike Bettes survived after his vehicle was thrown about 200 yards, though his driver was seriously injured. The hood of Reed Timmer's Dominator 2, a vehicle built for intercepting tornadoes, was torn off. A later Doppler on Wheels analysis found that the Weather Channel and TWISTEX vehicles had been struck by an intense internal subvortex, with radar-indicated winds approaching 200 mph, moving erratically and at times faster than 100 mph.

## Running for Their Lives

Eight people died, all of them in vehicles, and injury counts vary by source, from 26 in the National Weather Service's tornado table to as many as 151 across the storm. The highways became a scene of chaos. "People were going southbound in the northbound lanes," one fleeing resident said. "Everybody was running for their lives." Weather Underground's Jeff Masters said that had the tornado tracked directly over one of those congested highways, the death toll could easily have exceeded 500. Where it did hit buildings, it left heavy damage. The Oklahoma City West Livestock Market was described as a war zone. At the Canadian Valley Technology Center, large brick buildings were heavily damaged or destroyed and a metal wind turbine blade was hurled 100 yards into a daycare building, with damage there alone estimated at up to $40 million. The storm even stripped gravel off rural roads down to bare dirt. Because the EF5 tornado that devastated Moore had struck just eleven days earlier, a state of emergency was already in place, which helped residents obtain assistance quickly.

## What El Reno Taught

Scientists and veteran chasers turned the tragedy into a research effort. They crowd-sourced video, photographs and GPS logs from chasers, assembling the largest visual data set ever collected on a tornado and combining it with radar and lightning data. In 2015 they unveiled software that synchronizes maps, radar and chaser footage, named the Tornado Environment Display, or TED, after tornado scientist Ted Fujita. One of the tool's architects, Anton Seimon, said that although it had so far been used only for El Reno, it could be applied to future tornadoes with enough footage, and the project aimed to grow into a standardized, open-access database. Tim Samaras was remembered, too: in November 2013, National Geographic, which had helped fund his work, devoted its cover story to him and to a detailed scientific analysis of the storm that took his life.

## Flight Context

The tornado's most intense stretch centered near 35.4792 N, 97.9 W, about three miles southeast of El Reno in Canadian County, Oklahoma, with the path crossing Interstate 40 before lifting near Banner Road. From 3,000-5,000 feet AGL, Interstate 40 and the El Reno street grid are the best references. Nearest airports: El Reno Regional Airport (KRQO), five miles southwest of town, and Clarence E. Page Municipal Airport (KRCE) to the east near Yukon, with Wiley Post (KPWA) and Will Rogers World (KOKC) farther east in the Oklahoma City area. Late spring is peak severe-weather season here; never fly near supercell thunderstorms.
