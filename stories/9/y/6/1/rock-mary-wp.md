# Rock Mary

A twin-towered red sandstone butte in Caddo County, Oklahoma, named in 1849 by two army lieutenants for a seventeen-year-old emigrant, became a landmark on the California Road for travelers bound west.

**Location:** 35.4597, -98.4256

**Categories:** Geography of Caddo County, Oklahoma, Geography of Oklahoma, Tourist attractions in Caddo County, Oklahoma, Natural features on the National Register of Historic Places, National Register of Historic Places in Caddo County, Oklahoma

## Introduction

On May 23, 1849, two army lieutenants climbed a red sandstone butte on the prairie of what is now western Oklahoma, unfurled the flag of the United States on its crest and gave the rock a name. They chose to honor Mary Conway, a seventeen-year-old traveling west with her family among the emigrants their expedition was escorting. The name held. Rock Mary became a landmark on the California Road, and more than 175 years later the butte near Hinton still carries the name of a young woman who passed this way in 1849 on her way to a new life on the far side of the continent.

## Mary of the Emigrant Train

Mary Conway came from a prominent Arkansas family. She was the niece of James Sevier Conway, then the Governor of Arkansas, and the eldest daughter of Dr. John R. Conway, a physician and surveyor. In 1849 her father set out for California with his wife and ten or more children, and Mary, the eldest daughter, made the long journey across the plains with them. Among the soldiers of the escort were Lieutenants James H. Simpson and M. P. Harrison. It was Simpson's detachment that first spied the butte with its twin projections, and the two officers named it for her. Accounts of the naming remember her as a seventeen-year-old belle, but she was also the eldest daughter of a very large family, moving her whole life halfway across a continent. The rock is her only monument here.

## Captain Marcy's Road

The emigrants were traveling with Captain Randolph B. Marcy, who led a party across present-day Oklahoma in the spring of 1849. His expedition was the first to record the butte, and the route it followed came to be known, for a short time, as the California Road. Marcy picked Rock Mary as a landmark on this southern trail to California because of its prominence. Just as important was what lay at its foot. A spring flowed just southeast of the hill, since gone dry, and nearby was a good campground, with wood available in the creek breaks. On the open prairie, water, fuel and a recognizable marker in the same place made a natural stopping point. The Oklahoma Historical Society marker erected in 1960 describes Rock Mary as a famous landmark relied on by countless thousands of western emigrants.

## Two Towers of Sandstone

The butte itself is a large outcrop of red sandstone, rising approximately sixty feet high and about two hundred feet across. It is made largely of Permian-age Rush Springs sandstone capped with Weatherford dolomite, and two tower-like projections loom up from its top, giving it the distinctive profile that caught the lieutenants' eyes. Travelers who came after them recorded it too. In 1853 the artist Heinrich Balduin Möllhausen sketched the formation, and the Oklahoma Historical Society considers his drawing the most accurate picture of Rock Mary in the 1850s. Today the rock can seem less commanding than those accounts suggest. Seen from some approaches, the formation sits low relative to the surrounding land, tucked into the breaks of the creek, and cannot be spotted from far away. It is a reminder that a landmark depends partly on where a traveler happens to be standing.

## Behind a Private Gate

Rock Mary was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1970. It stands on private property, where trespassing is not allowed, approximately 4.75 miles west of the town of Hinton in Caddo County. That does not put it entirely out of reach. The Hinton Historical Museum, located in Hinton about five miles east of the rock, has been granted management of access to the property and can arrange for visitors to enter with advance notice. Those who make the trip will find the spring long dry, but the red butte and its twin towers still stand. What remains is the name, given on a spring day in 1849 by two soldiers to a girl who kept going west.

## Flight Context

Rock Mary lies at approximately 35.4597° N, 98.4256° W in Caddo County, western Oklahoma, about 4.75 miles west of Hinton and roughly five miles south of Interstate 40. From 1,500 to 2,500 feet above ground level, look for a small red sandstone butte with two tower-like projections on top, set among creek breaks in rolling pasture; because the formation sits low in the terrain, it is easiest to spot by first locating Hinton and then following the section roads west. Interstate 40, running east-west to the north, is the best large-scale reference. Thomas P. Stafford Airport (KOJA) at Weatherford is roughly 15 miles to the west-northwest, and Clinton Regional Airport (KCLK) about 29 miles to the west. Western Oklahoma is often windy, with strong spring thunderstorms; low morning or evening sun brings out the red of the sandstone.
