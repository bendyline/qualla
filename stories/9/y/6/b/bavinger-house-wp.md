# Bavinger House

Two Norman artists spent five years building Bruce Goff's spiraling stone house by hand, drew paying crowds for decades, and lost it all to a broken spire, a stalled rescue and finally the bulldozer.

**Location:** 35.2278, -97.3528

**Categories:** Architecture, Organic architecture, Bruce Goff, Lost buildings, Norman, Oklahoma

## Introduction

Eugene Bavinger got his walls with dynamite. On farmland he had bought near Robin Hill School, a few miles from home, he blasted loose 200 tons of local "ironrock" sandstone and hauled it back on his 1948 Chevy flatbed truck. With that stone and a design by the architect Bruce Goff, Eugene and his wife Nancy built a house in Norman, Oklahoma, that the American Institute of Architects would honor with its Twenty-five Year Award in 1987. Today the site is a vacant lot.

## Five Years of Their Own Labor

Nancy and Eugene Bavinger were artists, and they built the house themselves. Their architect worked close by: Bruce Goff had joined the University of Oklahoma's School of Architecture in 1947 and became its chairman within a year, and he was known for organic, eclectic and often flamboyant designs made for a specific client and site. Construction stretched over five years, with help from a few of Eugene's art students, volunteers and local businesses. The couple moved in during February 1955, and that September 19, Life magazine featured the house in its pages. It came to be considered a significant example of organic architecture, and its materials told part of that story: walls quarried by its owner from ground a few miles away, and a mast salvaged from the oil fields.

## A Spiral With No Rooms

The house wound around itself. Its wall was a 96-foot-long logarithmic spiral of that dynamited sandstone, curling inward like a shell. At the center rose a mast more than 55 feet high, made from a recycled oil field drill stem, and the whole structure was anchored to it. There were no interior walls at all. Instead, a series of platforms hung at different heights, some fitted with curtains that could be drawn for privacy. The ground floor was given over to pools and planted areas, so water and plants filled the lowest level. Living here meant moving up and down through the spiral rather than from room to room.

## A Dollar at the Door

Despite its remote location, the house built by two artists became a destination. Strangers kept arriving, and the Bavingers first tried to slow the flow by charging a dollar per guest. It did not work the way they hoped. Life reported that the tours had brought in over $4,000, and according to Goff, the couple eventually raised more than $50,000 before deciding they no longer wanted to be disturbed by the constant stream of tourists. Formal recognition followed. The AIA's Twenty-five Year Award arrived in 1987, and in 2001 the house was added to the National Register of Historic Places.

## The Spire Falls

Behind the honors, the house was slipping away. It stood vacant for more than a decade and fell into disrepair. In 2008 came reports that it would be renovated and reopened for tours, but fundraising ran into difficulties. In June 2011, early reports blamed a windstorm for damaging the house and leaving its central spire broken at a 45-degree angle, while the Bavingers' son Bob, who then owned it, told The Norman Transcript that he had torn it down, calling it "the only solution that we had." The house's website soon announced that it would not be able to reopen, then changed its message to "Closed Permanently," and in August 2012 to "The House will never return under its current political situation." In April 2016, The Norman Transcript reported that the house had been demolished and completely removed, leaving only a vacant lot. The president of Friends of Kebyar, a preservation group devoted to Goff's work, confirmed it. The spiral that two artists raised from their own quarried stone survives now only in photographs and in the pages of Life.

## Flight Context

The former Bavinger House site is at 35.2278°N, 97.3528°W in a once-remote spot east of central Norman, Oklahoma. The house was demolished around 2016, so there is no structure to see; a pass at 2,000–3,000 feet AGL shows only the setting where it stood. Nearby airports: University of Oklahoma Westheimer Airport (KOUN) in Norman, about 6 nm W; Tinker Air Force Base (KTIK, military), about 11 nm N; and Oklahoma City's Will Rogers airport (KOKC), about 16 nm NW. Stay clear of Tinker's airspace to the north. Spring and summer bring frequent thunderstorms to central Oklahoma.
