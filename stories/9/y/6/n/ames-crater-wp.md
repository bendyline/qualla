# Ames crater

A roughly ten-mile meteorite crater buried beneath Major County, Oklahoma, lay hidden for hundreds of millions of years until a seismic map revealed "a cow track in the mud" and a remarkable store of oil and gas.

**Location:** 36.2844, -98.1939

**Categories:** Impact craters, Geology, Oil and gas, Ordovician, Major County, Oklahoma

## Introduction

It looked like "a cow track in the mud." In 1991, Rex Olson, an exploration manager for Continental Resources, was studying a map generated from seismic test data when he noticed a pattern shaped like a hoof print. He showed it to Harold Hamm, the company's owner and chief executive, and pointed out what seemed to be an anomaly, a "glitch" in the data. The two agreed it resembled an astrobleme, a term meaning "star wound." Beneath Major County, Oklahoma, near the small town of Ames, lay a buried meteorite crater roughly ten miles across. Drilling would show it held a large amount of oil and gas.

## A Blow Beneath the Sea

The crater's age has long been estimated at 470 million years, give or take 30 million, placing it in the Ordovician period, although the American Oil & Gas Historical Society reports that a 2026 study of zircon crystals found inside the crater put the impact nearer 370 million years ago. When the object struck, this part of the Earth lay under a shallow sea. Traveling at an estimated 70,000 miles per hour, it punched a crater into the Earth's crust and created enormous pressures below the point of impact. Those pressures produced a slight recoil that created an uplift within the structure. Then the sea came back. Over eons it laid down layer after layer of sediment, other geological movements tilted the formation slightly, and eventually the sea disappeared altogether, leaving the crater buried and invisible from the surface. Ames, Oklahoma, sits near the center of the structure today. The town was founded at the opening of the Cherokee Outlet on September 16, 1893, first called Hoyle after a nearby creek, and renamed in 1902 for a railroad official, Henry S. Ames. For almost a century, nobody living there knew what lay below.

## Reading a Star Wound

Because the crater is not exposed at the surface, its story has to be read from seismic data and drilling. Several origins have been proposed for the structure, including meteorite impact, volcanic activity and dissolution collapse. Evidence from its geomorphology, rock textures, mineralogy and stratigraphy has been used to support the impact explanation. Under the older age estimate, Ames may also have had company. It may be one of several Middle Ordovician meteors that fell at roughly the same time, about 469 million years ago, as part of a proposed Ordovician meteor event. The other candidates are scattered across the continent: the Decorah crater in Iowa, the Slate Islands crater in Lake Superior and the Rock Elm crater in Wisconsin. If the idea holds, a single episode of bombardment left scars from Oklahoma to Lake Superior.

## Treasure in the Wound

Buried impact sites can hide more than history. An unknown number of concealed impact sites have been discovered, and some have produced uranium, gold or diamonds. At Ames, the prize was hydrocarbons. Drilling within the crater found a large amount of oil and gas, and about 30 of the original wells were still producing in 2007. According to an article published by the American Oil & Gas Historical Society, Ames had produced 17.4 million barrels of oil and 79.5 billion cubic feet of natural gas by 2007, making it one of only six producing astroblemes in the United States. A shallow sea, a violent impact and hundreds of millions of years of patient sediment had built an oil field, and it took a seismic map and a sharp eye to find it. The town above it now tells the story itself. Ames, home to fewer than 200 people at the 2020 census, opened the Ames Astrobleme Museum on August 18, 2007, with image panels and a video showing how the crater formed and how it was discovered.

## Flight Context

The center of the Ames structure is near 36.2844°N, 98.1939°W, close to the town of Ames in Major County, Oklahoma, southwest of Enid. The crater is completely buried and cannot be seen from the air; flying at 5,000–7,000 feet AGL gives a sense of its roughly ten-mile diameter across the countryside around Ames. Nearby airports: Vance Air Force Base (KEND, military), about 14 nm ENE; Enid Woodring Regional (KWDG), about 20 nm ENE; and Fairview Municipal (FAA identifier 6K4), about 14 nm W. Expect military traffic around Vance to the east. The open plains of northwest Oklahoma usually offer long visibility, but watch for spring and summer thunderstorms and strong surface winds.
