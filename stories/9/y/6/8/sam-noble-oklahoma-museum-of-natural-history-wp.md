# Sam Noble Oklahoma Museum of Natural History

Oklahoma's natural history collection spent most of a century scattered through barns, barracks and a horse stable before Norman's voters helped build it one of the world's largest university-based natural history museums.

**Location:** 35.1945, -97.4493

**Categories:** Natural history museums in Oklahoma, University of Oklahoma campus, Dinosaur museums in the United States, Native American language revitalization, Paleontology in Oklahoma

## Introduction

In 1980, part of the State of Oklahoma's natural history collection was kept in a horse stable. Other pieces sat in a wooden barn, in two wooden barracks built during World War II, in assorted attics and basements, and in a 1930s ROTC armory that served as the museum's main building: ten separate buildings in all, many of them substandard for preserving specimens. Two decades later, those same collections moved into one of the world's largest university-based natural history museums. The Sam Noble Oklahoma Museum of Natural History, on the University of Oklahoma campus in Norman, now holds more than 10 million objects and specimens across 12 collections.

## A Museum Without a Home

The museum is older than the state. In 1899, nearly a decade after the University of Oklahoma was founded, the Oklahoma Territorial Legislature passed a law creating the post of territorial geologist and a Department of Geology and Natural History, charged with a scientific survey of the territory's flora, fauna and minerals. Specimens piled up; a building never came. The closest call arrived in 1920, when university leaders funded an expedition to Alaska to collect grizzly bears, caribou, mountain goats and other North American megafauna, hoping the animals would excite Oklahomans and their legislators into paying for a museum. The specimens survived, and scientists still study them today. The funding bill did not: the governor vetoed it. For decades afterward the collection kept growing in whatever corners of campus could hold it, renamed the Museum of the University of Oklahoma in 1943 and the Stovall Museum of Science and History in 1953.

## Shovels of the New Deal

The Great Depression, of all eras, turned the collection into something formidable. Through the Works Progress Administration, about 50 workers were assigned to museum paleontologist J. Willis Stovall to excavate dinosaur fossils across Oklahoma. They uncovered large, unique specimens of dinosaurs and mammals, and the vertebrate fossil collection quickly grew in prominence, along with its hunger for storage space. At the same time, university archaeologists supervised WPA crews in eastern Oklahoma, most notably at Spiro Mounds, an important center occupied primarily from AD 1000 to 1400. That work began as an effort to salvage Craig Mound, which had been extensively looted in the mid-1930s, and it deepened understanding of Native American history in Oklahoma before European contact. Stovall spent years chasing funds for a proper building and died in 1953 without one. He did, however, unite the scattered holdings under a single administrative unit, the foundation for everything that followed.

## A Town Votes Yes

The turnaround began in 1983, when director Michael Mares launched a campaign to show Oklahomans how fragile their natural and cultural treasures were and how poorly they were being stored. Mares and his staff carried that message to state lawmakers, and in 1987 a new law designated the Stovall Museum as the official Oklahoma Museum of Natural History. Then Norman's residents took up the cause. A citizens' group secured a special election in 1992, and voters pledged a $5 million bond for a new building, contingent on the state and private donors raising $30 million. A statewide bond election supplied $15 million, and a $10 million gift from the Samuel Roberts Noble Foundation and affiliates covered most of the rest and gave the museum its name. Ground was broken in 1996, and the 198,000-square-foot building opened to the public in May 2000. In 2014, First Lady Michelle Obama presented the museum with the National Medal for Museum and Library Service at the White House.

## Giants and a Painted Skull

Visitors meet a giant before they meet anything else. A representation of Sauroposeidon proteles, which holds the Guinness World Record for the tallest dinosaur, is immediately visible on entering the exhibit hall. Deeper inside, the Hall of Ancient Life stages its largest scene: Saurophaganax maximus, a large carnivore and Oklahoma's state fossil, challenging a 92-foot Apatosaurus, reconstructed by scaling up smaller fossils to match the largest pieces in the museum's own collection. Nearby stands an original, nearly complete Pentaceratops skeleton, which some classify as Titanoceratops ouranos, whose skull holds the Guinness record for the largest of any known land animal. The quietest object may be the most moving. The Cooper Skull, from an extinct species of bison, carries a red zigzag painted roughly 10,000 years ago, making it one of the oldest painted objects in North America. It anchors a hall that traces 30,000 years of Native history in Oklahoma and North America, up to what it means to be Native American in Oklahoma today.

## Voices Worth Keeping

Not everything the museum preserves fits in a display case. Its Native American Languages collection, founded in 2002, holds more than 8,500 objects, among them audio and video recordings, manuscripts, books and teaching materials, from more than 175 Native languages, and it is especially strong in the severely to critically endangered languages of the central United States. It also houses the world's largest collection of Dhegiha language materials. Through the museum's annual Oklahoma Native American Youth Language Fair, that work reaches well beyond the archive, and in 2017 the program earned the museum the International Council of Museums' University Museums and Collections Award, presented in Helsinki. For an institution that began as a territorial survey of rocks, plants and animals, it marks a remarkable evolution. The museum that once could not find a proper roof for its fossils now keeps living voices as carefully as ancient bones.

## Flight Context

The museum sits on the University of Oklahoma campus in Norman at approximately 35.19°N, 97.45°W, roughly 20 miles south of downtown Oklahoma City. From 2,500-4,000 feet AGL, the OU campus and its large football stadium are easy to pick out in Norman's street grid, with Lake Thunderbird visible to the east. Max Westheimer Airport (KOUN) is about 4 miles north-northwest, and Will Rogers World Airport (KOKC) about 16 miles north-northwest. Central Oklahoma sees frequent spring thunderstorms; clear fall and winter days offer the best visibility.
