# National Severe Storms Laboratory

In Norman, Oklahoma, a radar that could clock a tornado's winds but not find the tornado set off six decades of storm science, from pulse-Doppler radar and NEXRAD to balloons launched straight into thunderstorms.

**Location:** 35.1814, -97.4403

**Categories:** Weather forecasting, Storm chasing, Norman, Oklahoma, Science, Government agencies established in 1964

## Introduction

The radar in Norman had a strange blind spot. Installed in 1956 by the Cornell Aeronautical Laboratory, the 3-centimeter continuous-wave Doppler set was designed to detect the very high wind speeds inside tornadoes, and it could. What it could not do was tell anyone how far away those winds were. Solving that puzzle helped turn a university town in central Oklahoma into a national workshop for dangerous weather. Today the National Severe Storms Laboratory, one of ten research laboratories run by the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration, studies tornadoes, hail, lightning, flash floods, damaging winds and winter weather from Norman, and research on its radar grew into the Doppler network the National Weather Service relies on today.

## Listening Between the Pulses

In 1962 a research team from the U.S. Weather Bureau's National Severe Storms Project moved from Kansas City, Missouri, to Norman, joining the Weather Radar Laboratory there. In 1964 NSSL's engineers modified the old radar to transmit in pulses instead of a continuous beam. The change sounds modest, but it was decisive. A pulse-Doppler radar can receive data in between each transmitted pulse, so a single antenna could now do the work of two, and the distance problem that had hobbled the original set was solved. That same year the rest of the National Severe Storms Project moved to Norman, merged with the radar lab, and took a new name: the National Severe Storms Laboratory. Dr. Edwin Kessler became its first director. NSSL meteorologists went on to develop the first Doppler radar built for meteorological observation, and research with the Norman Doppler during the 1970s led to the National Weather Service's NEXRAD network of WSR-88D radars.

## Minivans in the Hail

NSSL's scientists have never been content to watch storms from a distance. The lab's "probes" are modified minivans with a suite of weather instruments mounted on a roof rack and a tangle of computer and communication equipment inside; researchers drive them through storms and storm environments, measuring temperature, pressure, humidity and wind. Two mobile laboratories, NSSL6 and NSSL7, were custom built by an ambulance company and carry balloon-launching gear so they can deploy on short notice. In 1994, NSSL and University of Oklahoma researchers began developing their first mobile Doppler weather radar, which was operating in the field by 1995. Its successors, such as NSSL's NOXP, can be driven into position very close to a storm, catching details that stay out of sight of the beams of more distant WSR-88D radars. Mobile radars have since been aimed at tornadoes, hurricanes, dust storms, winter storms, mountain rainfall and even biological phenomena. One image from the lab's history shows a tornado in its early stage of formation near Union City, Oklahoma, captured by the NSSL Doppler radar and by NSSL chase personnel on the ground.

## Where Aircraft Dare Not Fly

Some of the lab's boldest instruments go up rather than out. NSSL launches special research balloons directly into thunderstorms, carrying sensor packages to places where it has often proved too dangerous for research aircraft to fly. One payload, the Particle Size Image and Velocity Probe, or PASIV, photographs water and ice particles as it rises through a storm, flying in a "train" of instruments strung one after another beneath the balloon. Another, the Electric Field Meter, measures the strength and direction of the electric fields that build up before lightning strikes. On the ground, a two-dimensional video distrometer films anything falling through its viewing area, whether raindrops, hail or snow, from two angles at high speed, recording drop shape and size. Across the state, the Oklahoma Lightning Mapping Array, which NSSL installed and operates, can map thousands of points within a single lightning flash, revealing where it struck and how its structure developed.

## Rehearsing the Next Warning

Much of NSSL's work now happens inside the National Weather Center on the University of Oklahoma campus, where instruments on the roof measure the lowest one to two kilometers of the atmosphere. There, the Hazardous Weather Testbed, managed jointly by NSSL, the Storm Prediction Center and the National Weather Service's Norman forecast office, exists to speed promising science into real forecasts and warnings. The lab's computer models look both forward and back. The NSSL WRF generates daily experimental forecasts at 4-kilometer resolution, while the 3D cloud model COMMAS can rebuild past storms from radar and lightning data; researchers have used it to simulate the early tornadic phase of the supercell that destroyed much of Greensburg, Kansas, in 2007. The FLASH project, launched in early 2012, forecasts flash floods at 1-kilometer, 5-minute resolution. In Norman, the Advanced Technology Demonstrator, a dual-polarization phased array radar installed in 2018 at the National Weather Radar Testbed site where an earlier Navy-derived phased array radar ran until 2016, is being used to test whether a radar that steers its beam electronically can update storm scans faster than today's rotating dishes.

## Eyes on the Ground

Not every observation needs a balloon or a radar truck. The mostly student-run Severe Hazards Analysis and Verification Experiment, known as SHAVE, collects reports of hail, wind damage and flash flooding through phone surveys. Combined with the voluntary reports gathered by the National Weather Service, those calls build a database of severe and non-severe weather events alike. Through the mPING project, volunteers use a mobile app to report the precipitation reaching the ground where they stand, and researchers compare those reports with dual-polarized radar data to refine the algorithms that identify what is falling. It makes a fitting loop for a lab that began with a radar unable to say where a storm was. More than six decades later, some of its data comes from people standing directly beneath the clouds.

## Flight Context

The National Severe Storms Laboratory is housed in the National Weather Center on the University of Oklahoma campus in Norman, at approximately 35.18°N, 97.44°W, roughly 20 miles south of downtown Oklahoma City. From 3,000-5,000 feet AGL, look for the Norman street grid, the OU campus and its large football stadium to the north, and Lake Thunderbird to the east. Max Westheimer Airport (KOUN) lies about 5 miles north-northwest in Norman, and Will Rogers World Airport (KOKC) about 17 miles north-northwest. Spring brings the thunderstorms NSSL studies; give convective weather a wide berth and expect the best visibility on clear days after frontal passages.
