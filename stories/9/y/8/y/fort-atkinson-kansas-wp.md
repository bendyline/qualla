# Fort Atkinson (Kansas)

Built of sod on a treeless stretch of the Arkansas River to guard the Santa Fe Trail, Fort Atkinson lived two brief lives in the early 1850s and is best remembered for the treaty signed there in 1853.

**Location:** 37.7530, -100.0833

**Categories:** Santa Fe Trail, Forts in Kansas, Ford County, Kansas, Native American history, Treaties of the United States

## Introduction

Some of the troops called it Fort Sod. Others called it Fort Sodom. On an open stretch of prairie beside the Arkansas River, about two miles west of present-day Dodge City, Kansas, the U.S. Army established a post on August 8, 1850, in an attempt to stop attacks on travelers along the Santa Fe Trail. With the nearest trees, by one visitor's count, fifteen miles away, the soldiers built with what the plains offered: sod walls under canvas roofs. The post, named Fort Atkinson in 1851, lived two short lives, and its most lasting legacy was not a battle but a treaty.

## Without Timber, Even on the River

Lieutenant Colonel Edwin Vose Sumner, who would later serve as a Union major general in the Civil War, had occupied a nearby site in July 1850, known as Camp Mackay or Fort Mackay, before the camp moved to its permanent location. A railroad survey party described the setting bluntly: "The bluffs are low, and the country on all sides unbroken prairie, without timber even on the river bottom." Gwinn H. Heap, who visited in 1853, wrote that the nearest trees stood fifteen miles off. Water, at least, was never a problem, with the Arkansas River running along the south side of the post. According to the Kansas Historical Society, the fort was named in honor of the late General Henry Atkinson. It was abandoned in September 1853, and its buildings were torn down so no one could hide inside them and surprise travelers on the trail.

## Words on the Arkansas

The most important event at Fort Atkinson came on July 27, 1853, when the United States government concluded a major treaty with the Comanche, Kiowa and Apache peoples of the region. By then the plains tribes were feeling the encroachment of whites onto their lands. The treaty allowed roads to be used, and military posts and similar settlements to be built, on Indian lands. The tribes agreed to refrain from raids into Mexico, to take no property or captives from Mexican provinces, to make restitution for wrongs and to free any captives. In return they were to receive annuities for a period of time, which could be reduced for breaches. Both sides violated the agreement at times, and by the late 1850s it had become unenforceable, as whites flooded through the region on their way to the gold fields of Colorado and, later, Montana.

## The Fort That Came Back

Almost as soon as the post was gone, people wanted it back. In February 1854, the New Mexico territorial legislature asked Congress to reestablish Fort Atkinson. Secretary of War Jefferson Davis, the future Confederate president, wrote on May 20, 1854, that he considered reviving the fort too costly and impractical. It was reestablished anyway in the late spring of 1854 (one account says May 27, another June 13), when eight officers and 110 enlisted men from Fort Riley set up camp beside the original site under Major Albemarle Cady. This time no buildings went up; the troops lived in tents, protected a mail station and aided travelers, though the roughly 140 men were unable to do much to help. In July a great gathering of Comanches and Kiowas arrived to receive their annuities, and Indian agent J. W. Whitfield distributed at least some of them. The post did not outlast the season. Cady's final field return recorded its end in five words: "This post abandoned Oct. 2/54."

## Flight Context

The Fort Atkinson site is at approximately 37.75°N, 100.08°W on the north bank of the Arkansas River, about two miles west of Dodge City, Kansas. From 1,500-2,500 feet AGL, follow the Arkansas River valley west from Dodge City, the same valley 1850s travelers described as unbroken prairie. Dodge City Regional Airport (KDDC) is about 6 miles to the east. Expect strong winds typical of the High Plains and summer thunderstorms.
