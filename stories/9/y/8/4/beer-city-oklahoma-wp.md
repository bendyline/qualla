# Beer City, Oklahoma

A tent town of saloons and gambling houses that sprang up in lawless No Man's Land just south of dry Kansas in 1888, Beer City vanished after 1890 and is now nothing but a wheat field.

**Location:** 36.9640, -100.9280

**Categories:** Towns in Beaver County, Oklahoma, Towns in Oklahoma, Populated places established in 1888, 1888 establishments in the United States

## Introduction

Go looking for Beer City today and you will find a wheat field. There are no foundations, no collapsing saloon, no weathered sign. A few photographs and newspaper clippings are the only proof it ever existed. Yet for a brief, riotous stretch at the end of the 1880s, this patch of prairie just south of the Kansas line was a town with a reputation that has outlived every board and tent it was built from. It thrived for one simple reason: no state or territory claimed the ground beneath it.

## The Strip Nobody Owned

Beer City's story begins with slavery and a line on a map. When Texas sought to enter the Union in 1845, it came in as a slave state, but federal law under the Missouri Compromise prohibited slavery north of the 36°30' parallel. Under the Compromise of 1850, Texas surrendered its lands north of that line. That left a strip of land about 170 miles long belonging to no state and no territory. People called it the Neutral Strip, or more memorably, No Man's Land. From 1850 until 1890, it sat in legal limbo, outside the reach of any state or territorial government. For most people that was a problem. For anyone looking to escape a neighbor's laws, it was an opportunity.

## White Tents, Cold Beer

The neighbor in question was Kansas, whose strict prohibition laws made a drink hard to come by for the cowboys and cattlemen working the region. In 1888, Kansans crossed into No Man's Land and started a town where no such laws applied. It began as a cluster of white tents, which gave it its first name: White City. That name did not last. The townsfolk's rowdiness soon earned the place a more honest title, and White City became Beer City. At its peak it held a handful of saloons, gambling houses and brothels. With no rules to enforce and no government to enforce them, the town drew outlaws, and by local tradition they made up much of its population. It was a boomtown built for vice, and its prosperity depended entirely on the law staying away.

## Seventy-Four Bullets

The most notorious figure in Beer City was a woman known as "Pussy Cat Nell," who ran the Yellow Snake Saloon. The town's most famous event centered on her and on a man who had given himself the title "Sheriff Bush." Bush held no legitimate office, and the townsfolk believed he was unfairly extorting them. As the story has come down, Nell and a group of fourteen men settled the matter themselves. They shot and killed Bush, and his body was found with seventy-four gunshot wounds. Accounts of the episode differ in their details, and a contemporary 1889 newspaper report said an inquest found eight bullets and 23 shot in Bush's body, a far smaller tally. Much of what is told about Beer City has the polish of frontier legend. But the killing captures the essential truth of the place. In a town with no law, justice meant whatever the people holding the guns decided it meant.

## Swallowed by the Law

Beer City's end came not from a shootout but from an act of Congress. In 1890, No Man's Land was folded into the new Oklahoma Territory, and with territorial authority came the end of the strip's lawless era. Beer City ceased to exist. Its residents moved on, and the town that had sprung up out of tents simply faded away. Even its county has been redrawn: the whole strip became a single Beaver County in 1890, that county was divided into three at statehood in 1907, and the site now lies in the smaller Beaver County, in the Oklahoma Panhandle. Nothing physical remains. Where saloons and gambling houses once stood, wheat now grows in orderly rows. It is hard to imagine a quieter afterlife for a town that burned so brightly, or a better illustration of how completely the prairie can erase the people who pass across it.

## Flight Context

The site of Beer City lies at about 36.964°N, 100.928°W in Beaver County in the Oklahoma Panhandle, roughly 3 to 5 miles south of Liberal, Kansas (accounts and map coordinates differ, and the exact site is unmarked), and east of the small town of Tyrone. There is nothing left to see on the ground except farmland, so fly it for the landscape and the history: from 3,000–5,000 feet AGL, the grid of wheat fields and section roads stretches to the horizon, with Liberal's streets clearly visible to the north and the Kansas-Oklahoma state line running just north of the site. Liberal Mid-America Regional Airport (KLBL) is about 6 miles to the north, and Guymon Municipal Airport (KGUY) lies roughly 37 miles to the southwest. Expect strong, gusty winds typical of the High Plains, and watch for fast-building thunderstorms in late spring and summer.
