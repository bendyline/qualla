# Dalton Gang Hideout and Museum

In Meade, Kansas, visitors walk through a secret tunnel to reach the house of the outlaw Daltons' sister, though the tunnel they use was built by the WPA in 1941 and the legend behind it has never been proven.

**Location:** 37.2800, -100.3370

**Categories:** Meade County, Kansas, American outlaws, Works Progress Administration in Kansas, National Register of Historic Places in Meade County, Kansas, Museums in Kansas

## Introduction

The secret tunnel is real. Visitors to the Dalton Gang Hideout in Meade, Kansas, still reach the little four-room house by walking through it: 96 feet long, just 32 inches wide and 80 inches high, running underground from the barn. But this tunnel was built in 1941 by the Works Progress Administration and the National Youth Administration. Whether the notorious Dalton brothers ever slipped through an earlier passage on this spot is another question entirely, and the answer reveals as much about how the West remembers its outlaws as about the outlaws themselves.

## A Sister in Meade

Eva Dalton, sister of the Dalton Gang brothers, married John N. Whipple in Meade on October 25, 1887. Whipple ran a general store under his own name, selling groceries, clothing and all manner of merchandise, and he paid $400 for the house and property. Eva's brothers were known to have been in Meade in the years before 1890, when their spree of bank and train robberies began (sources date the start to 1890 or 1891). That spree ended on October 5, 1892, in Coffeyville, Kansas, where the gang tried to rob two banks at once. Townspeople fought back and killed every member of the gang except Emmett Dalton; the dead included his brothers Bob and Grat, and four residents of Coffeyville were killed as well. A little over six weeks later, on November 19, 1892, Whipple lost the Meade house at a sheriff's sale.

## Strangers in the Stairwell

The next occupants, H. G. Marshall and his family, found something odd. A small hidden tunnel led from a closet door beneath the stairwell, through the basement and out to the barn. Their daughter, later Mrs. Roy Talbott, recalled strangers entering the house through the stairwell closet after leaving their horses in the barn. In time the tunnel entrance was sealed off and plastered over. In the 20th century a local legend grew that the Dalton brothers had used their sister's home as a refuge from the law. No documentation of their presence in Meade after 1890 has surfaced so far.

## Friends and Neighbors

Historian Richard White has argued that the Daltons were "social bandits," outlaws enabled by supporters who actively helped them evade capture, kept quiet about what they knew or otherwise abetted them. Such bandits, White wrote, lived openly in communities that accepted them as friends and neighbors. Read in that light, it is easy to see why a tale of outlaw brothers slipping into their sister's house through a closet door would take root in a small Kansas town. Proven or not, the legend became Meade's calling card.

## The New Deal Hideout

The Meade Chamber of Commerce purchased the Whipple property and opened it to the public in July 1940. A WPA guide published the year before described Meade, then home to 1,552 people, as a town that "spreads out pleasantly on the prairie," unusual for western Kansas in its trees, shrubs and green lawns, all made possible by an abundant supply of artesian water. In 1941 the WPA reconstructed the two-story barn, with gypsum hauled in from Clark County for the basement walls, and the WPA and the National Youth Administration, a jobs program for people aged 16 to 25, built the present tunnel. The barn's second floor became the museum, which Ruth and Walter Dingess had first operated out of the house. The original front door and its small porch survive, but no one enters that way now. The complex was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on January 7, 2015.
