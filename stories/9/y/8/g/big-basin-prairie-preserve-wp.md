# Big Basin Prairie Preserve

In the Red Hills of southwestern Kansas, the ground collapsed into a mile-wide bowl and a sinkhole pool that has never been known to go dry, a landmark for Cheyenne travelers and Texas cattle drives long before it became a bison preserve.

**Location:** 37.2403, -99.9975

**Categories:** Nature reserves in Kansas, National Natural Landmarks in Kansas, Sinkholes of the United States, Grasslands of Kansas, Clark County, Kansas

## Introduction

Beneath the Red Hills of southwestern Kansas, groundwater dissolved underground layers of salt and gypsum, and in places the land above simply collapsed into the void. The result, in Clark County near Ashland, is the Big Basin: a lush, crater-like depression about a mile across, with no outlet for the water that drains into it. A short distance to the east, in a smaller bowl called Little Basin, lies St. Jacob's Well, a water-filled sinkhole that has never been known to go dry. Together they anchor the 1,818-acre Big Basin Prairie Preserve, owned and managed by the Kansas Department of Wildlife and Parks, where bison roam and the public is welcome.

## The Well That Never Runs Dry

Willard D. Johnson of the United States Geological Survey visited around the turn of the 20th century and published his account in 1901. He described Big Basin as approximately circular, about a mile in diameter and 100 feet deep, with no drainage out; water running in frequently pools in shallow ponds across its floor. A smaller basin, also without an outlet, notches its southwestern rim. On the floor of Little Basin, Johnson noticed shallow depressions that looked like "the nearly healed scars of other and earlier sink holes," and he judged that St. Jacob's Well had "every appearance of having been rather recently formed." According to the Kansas Geological Survey, Little Basin measures about 280 yards across and 35 feet from rim to floor, and St. Jacob's Well is a pool in its bottom that the Kansas Sampler Foundation puts at 84 feet wide and 58 feet deep (the survey describes it as roughly 50 feet wide).

## Water in a Dry Country

Reliable water made this corner of the plains matter to people passing through. In the fall of 1878, Northern Cheyenne families camped and rested here during their exodus, some 350 people who had fled the reservation in Indian Territory, led by Dull Knife and Little Wolf, in an attempt to return to their homeland on the northern plains. At the time, settlers had not occupied the area and perhaps did not even generally know of it. Big Basin also served as a landmark and watering site for cattle drives heading north from Texas to the railroads in Kansas, a fixed point in a sea of grass where herds and riders could drink.

## Janie Stein's Prairie

Saving the basin began with one determined woman. In 1972, Mrs. C. G. "Janie" Stein formed the Big Basin Foundation and spent several years working with agencies, congressional representatives, local landowners and The Nature Conservancy to set the land aside for bison and pronghorn. In 1974 the state acquired the property from The Nature Conservancy, which made its operation as a nature preserve a condition of the sale, and within a few years (sources give December 1978 or 1979) the preserve was designated a National Natural Landmark. Her son, John Paul Stein, later wrote a song about his mother's part in the effort. Today U.S. Routes 283 and 160, running together for a short stretch, cut straight across the Big Basin; the portion west of the highway remains privately owned.

## Flight Context

Big Basin lies at approximately 37.24°N, 100.00°W, almost exactly on the 100th meridian, in Clark County, Kansas, roughly 13 miles west-northwest of Ashland. Ground elevations on the preserve range from about 2,200 to 2,360 feet MSL, so 1,500-2,500 feet AGL (roughly 4,000-5,000 feet MSL) shows the mile-wide circular depression well, with U.S. Routes 283 and 160 running through it and the small, water-filled St. Jacob's Well to the east. Dodge City Regional Airport (KDDC) is about 36 miles north. Expect strong winds typical of the High Plains and summer thunderstorms.
