# Red Crown Tourist Court

In July 1933 Bonnie and Clyde's gang holed up in two brick cabins behind a Platte County tavern, where a woman in riding breeches paying in small change set off the gun battle that doomed Buck Barrow.

**Location:** 39.3100, -94.6849

**Categories:** Bonnie and Clyde, Crime in Kansas City, Missouri, 1933 crimes in the United States, History of Kansas City, Missouri, Buildings and structures in Platte County, Missouri

## Introduction

She paid for the two cabins with a fistful of small change. It was July 1933, and the young woman in tight riding breeches, an outfit unheard of in Platte City, Missouri, laid $4 in coins on the counter of the Red Crown Tavern. Later she bought five chicken dinners and five beers for a party she had registered as three, and paid in coins again. Manager Neal Houser noticed. So did the lawmen who ate at his restaurant. The woman was Blanche Barrow, and in the cabins out back were Bonnie Parker, Clyde Barrow, his brother Buck and 17-year-old W.D. Jones.

## A Brick Refuge

Parkville banker and developer Emmett Breen built the Red Crown in 1931 at the junction of US 71 and Missouri Route 35: a red brick and tile tavern with a popular restaurant and ballroom, and behind it a tourist court of two small cabins joined by two garages. Clyde usually kept moving, never staying more than a night, but the gang was worn down. On June 10 a car wreck at Wellington, Texas, had left Bonnie critically burned, and Buck and Jones had killed the town marshal of Alma, Arkansas, in a bungled robbery. On July 18 the gang raided three gas stations in Fort Dodge, Iowa, in fifteen minutes, even scooping the change out of gumball and vending machines, then drove to Kansas City. Buck objected; the Kansas City Massacre weeks earlier had filled the area with police. But the Red Crown's sturdy brick made Clyde feel secure. "This is where we stay the rest of the night," he said, "even if we all get killed before morning."

## Newspapers on the Windows

Clyde had picked badly. The Red Crown was a favorite meeting place of the Missouri Highway Patrol, whose officers, in the days before two-way radios, gathered at mealtimes to exchange messages and orders. With only two units, the gang had no other guests to blend in with, and their windows curtained with newspapers drew notice. When Houser followed Blanche back to record her license number, Clyde would not let him into the cabin, so he peered into the garage and found Oklahoma plates. Platte County Sheriff Holt Coffey watched Blanche pay for another round of meals, and Houser shared his suspicions with Captain William Baxter of the state patrol. Then Louis Bernstein, druggist at Platte City Drugs, called the sheriff about a stranger in a riding habit who had just bought bandages and atropine sulfate. Pharmacies had been told to watch for exactly such purchases. To Coffey and Baxter, burn supplies pointed to the badly burned Bonnie Parker.

## Squirrel Rifles and an Armored Car

Coffey's small county force had no protective gear and no radios, and its weapons were little more than squirrel rifles and a few pistols. He went to Kansas City to ask Jackson County Sheriff Tom Bash for help and was at first rebuffed: "I'm getting pretty damn tired of every hick sheriff in the country coming in here and telling me they have a bunch of desperadoes holed up." Coffey persisted, and Bash eventually supplied men, weapons, bulletproof shields and a bulletproof car. That night about a dozen officers from Platte County, Jackson County and the state patrol were ready, and curious locals gathered at the tavern and at Slim's Castle, the service station across the road. At about 10:30 p.m. Jones crossed to Slim's Castle for five sandwiches and five sodas, and the clerk noticed how nervously he watched the crowd in the Red Crown parking lot.

## One in the Morning

By 1 a.m. most of the revellers had left, and Coffey advanced on the cabins, possibly with others carrying shields of thick boilerplate. When he asked Blanche where the men were, she answered as loudly as she could, so Clyde would hear. Clyde and Jones opened fire with Browning Automatic Rifles through the windows and doors, and Buck joined them. The armored car proved not so bulletproof: rounds wounded driver George Highfill in both knees and jammed its horn into a steady wail, which the posse took as a cease-fire signal, and many fell back. Clyde hustled Bonnie and Jones into the car through an internal door. Buck and Blanche had to cross open ground. A bullet fired by Baxter struck Buck in the left temple and exited through his forehead, and a final volley shattered the car's rear window, driving glass into both of Blanche's eyes. Then the car was gone.

## Five Uneaten Sandwiches

Officers' injuries were light; Coffey's two buckshot scratches came, unknown to him, from a fellow officer's gun. In the cabin, the five sandwiches sat uneaten beside five empty soda bottles. The gang left behind as many as six BARs, 47 Colt .45 pistols from a July 7 burglary of the Enid armory, and the sidearm taken from the Alma marshal's body. Four days later, at Dexfield Park near Dexter, Iowa, the gang was cornered again. Buck was shot and died five days afterward in Perry, Iowa. Blanche, blinded in her left eye, was captured; though she had not fired at the Red Crown, she pleaded guilty to assault with intent to kill Sheriff Coffey, served until her sentence was commuted in 1939, and died in 1988. A kitchen fire destroyed most of the tavern in 1967, the year Arthur Penn's film Bonnie and Clyde restaged the shootout, and in 2011 a marker honoring the lawmen went up near the site.

## Flight Context

The Red Crown site lies at 39.3100°N, 94.6849°W in Platte County, now within Kansas City, Missouri, just northeast of the main Kansas City International Airport exit on Interstate 29; an interstate entrance ramp runs almost squarely through the old property. Nothing of the tavern or cabins survives. Kansas City International Airport (KMCI) is about 1.5 nm west-southwest, so the site sits inside busy Class B airspace and low-level sightseeing requires ATC coordination. Charles B. Wheeler Downtown Airport (KMKC) is about 12 nm south-southeast. From the air, the I-29 interchange at the airport entrance is the reference point.
