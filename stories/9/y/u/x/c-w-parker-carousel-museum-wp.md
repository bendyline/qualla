# C.W. Parker Carousel Museum

In Leavenworth, Kansas, a volunteer-run museum keeps C.W. Parker's carousel legacy turning, from a hand-cranked ride of the 1850s to a 1913 Carry-Us-All that still spins faster than modern carousels.

**Location:** 39.3172, -94.9096

**Categories:** Carousels, Amusement museums in the United States, Museums in Leavenworth County, Kansas, Buildings and structures in Leavenworth, Kansas, Museums established in 2005

## Introduction

Climb aboard the 1913 carousel in Leavenworth, Kansas, and be ready: this Carry-Us-All moves much faster than a modern-day merry-go-round. Visitors of all ages can ride it, choosing among 31 hand-carved wooden figures and seats that include two racing rabbits, a chariot and a spinning tub. The ride is the centerpiece of the C.W. Parker Carousel Museum, also known as the Leavenworth Carousel Museum, which opened in 2005 on the eastern side of downtown Leavenworth, with the Missouri River just to the east.

## The Carry-Us-All

Charles Wallace Parker bought a used carousel in 1892 and by the late 1890s was manufacturing his own Carry-Us-All amusement rides in Abilene. The invention was such a success that his quickly growing company, based in Abilene, Kansas, moved in 1911 to a bigger factory in Leavenworth. Parker, who styled himself the "Amusement King," built an enterprise, the C. W. Parker Amusement Company, that produced approximately 1,000 carousels. The museum, named for Parker, has one of his machines as its star attraction: a 1913 Carry-Us-All, the 118th his company produced. When the museum acquired it, the carousel and its figures needed thousands of hours of restoration before they could meet safety requirements. Today it is fully operational, and an Artizan band organ, the kind typically played alongside carousel rides at amusement parks, supplies the music while it turns. The collection also holds a Wurlitzer 153 band organ and a C.W. Parker cylinder piano from around 1900, which at first glance looks like a small upright piano but is hand-cranked and plays ten different tunes.

## From Muscle to Aluminum

The oldest ride in the building has no known maker and no exact date. The primitive carousel has been dated to around 1850 to 1860, and when it was in operation, two men turned it by hand. Its 12 paired horses and two benches all hang from the top frame, and its horses, assembled from several pieces of wood, are far less ornate than the 1913 carousel's. It is too old to ride, but if asked, museum volunteers will demonstrate how it worked. A century on, the Parker family was building something very different. In a separate room stands the Liberty Carousel, built in the 1950s by Paul Parker, C.W. Parker's son. Smaller than the 1913 ride, it carries 20 aluminum horses and two benches, with a maximum capacity of 24 riders. Made of aluminum and mounted on wheels, it was designed to be portable.

## Carved Back to Life

Every piece in the museum has been donated, lent, or paid for through memorial funds and donations, and each needed significant restoration. Many of the artifacts were close to 100 years old, despite an original life expectancy of only about five years. Wood rot meant that many of the horses had to be completely re-carved, a job done by hand with the same kinds of primitive tools used in their original making. In keeping with historical design, each horse is held together with wooden dowels instead of nails. Volunteers are estimated to have given thousands of hours of labor to the work. The nonprofit museum, sponsored by the Leavenworth Historical Museum Association, still runs on community donations and volunteer effort, and it has been listed as one of the "Eight Wonders of Kansas."
