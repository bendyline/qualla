# Longview Farm

Lumber baron Robert A. Long built a red-roofed country estate of more than 50 buildings near Lee's Summit in just 18 months, and his daughter Loula made it a champion show-horse farm known as the World's Most Beautiful Farm.

**Location:** 38.9056, -94.4469

**Categories:** Farms on the National Register of Historic Places in Missouri, Horse farms in the United States, Buildings and structures in Lee's Summit, Missouri, Houses completed in 1913, Rural culture in the United States

## Introduction

Revelation, Realization, Hesitation, Consternation. Loula Long had a habit with names. Many of the horses bought or born at her family's farm got titles ending in "-ion," and those four made up what she called "the park four." The names are a small clue to the scale of the place that raised them. Longview Farm, on 1,780 acres near Lee's Summit, Missouri, came to be known as the World's Most Beautiful Farm, and for decades its horses carried ribbons home from Madison Square Garden to the Kentucky State Fair.

## Eighteen Months, Two Thousand Workers

Robert A. Long made his fortune in lumber with the Long-Bell Lumber Company, an empire that by the 1900s spanned several states, up to 110 lumber yards and more than 250,000 acres of timberland. He already owned Corinthian Hall, a mansion in Kansas City, but it was too small for his animals, so by 1912 he had chosen a site in the country. He hired Henry F. Hoit of Hoit, Price and Barnes, who had designed Corinthian Hall and the R.A. Long Building, with George Kessler as landscape architect. Construction started in 1913 and took just 18 months. Among the roughly 2,000 workers were 50 Belgian craftsmen and 200 Sicilian stonemasons, and Italian laborers lived in tents while they built the foundations and sewer system. By 1914 a small town had appeared.

## A Village in Stucco and Red Tile

Longview had more than 50 buildings, and apart from the log grandstand and clubhouse, all were stucco with red tile roofs. There were barns for draft horses, saddle horses and broodmares, a hospital barn, a hog barn and a large show-horse barn with its own arena. There was a power house, an ice plant, a milk house with a bottling room, a blacksmith shop, a post office, a firehouse, a general store, a chapel and a school room. The farm employed 175 people. Married workers got houses for their families, and single workers and guests stayed at a hotel with a tennis court. Electricity ran underground, a 100,000-gallon water tower stood over the grounds, and 25 miles of white wooden fence were built without nails or bolts. The 22,000-square-foot mansion had 48 rooms, 14 bedrooms and 10 baths, and it claimed the first central vacuum system west of the Mississippi. Long refused antiques because they were used, so replica antique furniture came new from New York. The chapel, opened in 1915, still holds services.

## Jerseys by the Hundred

Longview was meant to be scenic and self-sufficient. Hogs, chickens, greenhouses and fields for food all had a place, along with a manicured main yard of 225 acres. Its modern dairy, stocked with prize Jersey cows, produced enough milk for the farm, the surrounding community and charities. By 1916 the herd numbered more than 500 Jerseys, 232 of them milked every day in two dairy barns. The same count listed 375 Duroc Jersey hogs, 29 mules, 16 Percheron draft horses, 61 saddle horses, 44 harness horses and 29 colts. By 1941 there were more than 800 cows, and more dairy barns had been added to handle the 500 being milked.

## Loula's Ribbons

Robert and Ella Long lost a son shortly after birth and raised two daughters. On a world tour, their daughter Sally met a naval officer, Hayne Ellis, who proposed nine days later; they married in 1904, and she traveled with him as a military wife, returning to the farm only after his death in 1961. Loula stayed. She shared her father's love of horses and ruled the stables. She married Pryor Combs, reportedly only after he agreed to live at Longview, and she lived there from its opening until her death in 1971, after 65 years of showing horses. Her prize, Revelation, bought in 1909 for $1,500, lies buried before the arena beneath a memorial. With the black mare Aspiration, she was reportedly the first woman to drive a winner in the Sporting Novice Roadster Class at Madison Square Garden in 1913. In 1920 alone she won 31 championships in 16 shows. Chief of Longview, bred at the farm, became a multiple champion stallion and, by one account, the only horse to win the Kentucky State Fair's $10,000 stake two years running, in 1928 and 1929. Loula also held benefit shows for Children's Mercy Hospital, the Red Cross and others.

## Schoolchildren in the Show Ring

Longview Farm was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on October 24, 1985. By then the land had begun to take new shapes. The U.S. Army Corps of Engineers bought part of it for a flood-protection project on the Little Blue River that created Longview Lake, and another portion was donated to become Metropolitan Community College-Longview. The remaining acreage went to a developer who built New Longview, a community planned with historic preservation in mind. Its most striking reuse came in 2005, when the old show-horse arena, where Loula's champions once performed, was converted into Longview Farm Elementary School.

## Flight Context

Longview Farm lies on the southeast side of the Kansas City metro area in Lee's Summit, Missouri, at about 38.9056°N, 94.4469°W. From 2,500 to 3,000 feet AGL, Longview Lake, created from part of the farm's land, is the best landmark; the New Longview community borders it, and the farm's red tile roofs stand out. Kansas City - Lee's Summit Regional Airport (FAA identifier LXT, GPS code KLXT) is about 5 nm to the northeast. Charles B. Wheeler Downtown Airport (KMKC) is about 15 nm to the north-northwest, and Johnson County Executive Airport (KOJC) is about 14 nm to the west-southwest. Kansas City's busy airspace lies to the north and west, so plan accordingly.
