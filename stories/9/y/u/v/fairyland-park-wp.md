# Fairyland Park

For more than half a century a Sicilian immigrant's family ran Fairyland Park on Kansas City's Prospect Avenue, until a new theme park, a winter storm and dwindling crowds closed its gates, and its star roller coaster left for Oklahoma.

**Location:** 38.9906, -94.5570

**Categories:** Defunct amusement parks in Missouri, Amusement parks opened in 1923, Amusement parks closed in 1977, Buildings and structures in Kansas City, Missouri

## Introduction

Fifty cents. That was the price of a day at Fairyland Park in its final seasons, and the owners made sure everyone knew it. "Where 'Fun' is Still Affordable," the advertising promised, a pointed jab at the newer Worlds of Fun nearby, where admission ran five dollars plus a parking fee. The Brancato family had been running their amusement park at 7501 Prospect Avenue in Kansas City since 1923. They had roller coasters, a towering Ferris wheel and summer concerts by big-name bands. What they could not buy was time. Within a few years the fifty-cent park was gone, and its biggest ride would eventually end up in another state.

## The Blacksmith's Park

Fairyland began with Salvatore "Sam" Brancato, a Sicilian immigrant and blacksmith by trade who came to the United States in 1896. After settling in Kansas City he went into the grocery business, then began buying real estate. In 1923 he opened Fairyland Park on a tract of about 80 acres bordered by Prospect Avenue on the west, 75th Street on the north, 77th Street on the south and Indiana Avenue on the east. For more than five decades it would be a Kansas City fixture. Along the way the Brancatos also bought the Kiddeland park at 85th and Wornall and moved its rides to Fairyland.

## Coasters, a Giant Wheel and a Double-Olympic Pool

Fairyland hit its stride in the 1950s and 1960s. At one time or another in those years it boasted three roller coasters, an eight-story Ferris wheel, bumper cars, a shooting range, a petting zoo and a swimming pool twice the size of an Olympic pool, which closed in the late 1960s. Not every chapter was sunny. Until 1964 the park admitted Black visitors only in private groups and as employees, and protests that began in 1961, including mass arrests of demonstrators, helped force its gates open to everyone. In 1967 an arsonist tried and failed to burn the park's wooden roller coaster, and that same year the family commissioned a new coaster, The Wildcat. It became the park's biggest ride and helped keep Fairyland competitive. The Ferris wheel had a rougher fate: a tornado bent it in half.

## Rock and Roll Against the Odds

Worlds of Fun opened in 1973, newer and more elaborate, and Fairyland spent its later years trying to keep up. The owners tried one gimmick after another. Cheap admission was one. Music was another: in the final summers, "Summer Jams" brought acts including REO, Dr. Hook, Blue Öyster Cult and Charlie Daniels to the old park. It was not enough. With attendance dwindling, a storm in the winter of 1977 to 1978, described in some accounts as a windstorm and in others as a tornado, caused extensive damage. Combined with the competition from Worlds of Fun, it was the end. Fairyland never reopened.

## Where the Wildcat Went

For years the Brancato family tried to find a new use for the land; a swap meet and a zoo were among the ideas floated, but none came to pass. The park sat closed, its ruins still standing. In 1990 The Wildcat was finally dismantled and moved, reopening in 1991 at Frontier City in Oklahoma City, where it remains, with a small plaque in the waiting area telling riders where it came from. In the 1990s Bruce R. Watkins Drive, U.S. Route 71, was cut through the old park grounds, and the last crumpled remains of Fairyland were hauled away in 2004. Not quite everything left. In the woods just west of U.S. 71, many footers and some structural steel bents from The Wildcat still sit on the ground, most accessible in the cooler months when the vegetation is more forgiving. Much of the rest of the land has found new purpose. Alphapointe, an association for the blind, built its Life Skills Campus headquarters at 7501 Prospect Avenue, the park's old address. Kansas City bought 7.5 of the original 80 acres for a new police station, and Satchel Paige Elementary School stands on former park land east of the highway. Children still gather where Fairyland once stood, just for a different reason.

## Flight Context

The former Fairyland Park site is at approximately 38.9906 N, 94.5570 W, around 75th Street and Prospect Avenue in south Kansas City, Missouri. From 2,000-3,000 feet AGL, follow Bruce R. Watkins Drive (U.S. Route 71), which cuts north-south through the old park grounds; the Alphapointe campus lies along Prospect on the west side, and the large wooded expanse of Swope Park, a short distance to the northeast, makes a good reference.
