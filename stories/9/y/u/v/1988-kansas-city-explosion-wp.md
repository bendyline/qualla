# 1988 Kansas City explosion

Before dawn on November 29, 1988, a trailer of explosives at a south Kansas City highway project blew up and killed six firefighters, leaving a city in mourning and an arson case whose verdicts are still disputed.

**Location:** 38.9719, -94.5433

## Introduction

The first call came in at 3:40 in the morning on November 29, 1988: a pickup truck was burning at a highway construction site in south Kansas City. The caller also raised a warning that would haunt the city for decades - explosives on the site might catch fire. Six firefighters answered that night. Less than half an hour later, all six were dead, killed by a blast that shattered windows across a ten-mile area and left behind an arson case whose verdicts are still questioned.

## Explosives Beside the Highway

In 1988, U.S. Route 71 was being expanded near 87th Street in south Kansas City. The project called for blasting, and so two trailers holding tens of thousands of pounds of ANFO (published figures range from 25,000 to 50,000 pounds in all), a blasting agent made from ammonium nitrate and fuel oil, were parked near the work. Stored in one place, that much blasting agent carried the potential for catastrophe - exactly the danger the 3:40 a.m. caller had flagged. The Kansas City Fire Department dispatched Pumper 41, a captain and two firefighters, which reached the site at 3:46. Instead of one fire, the crew found two. Something about the scene was wrong, and the night was about to get far more dangerous.

## Twenty-Seven Minutes

Pumper 41 asked for backup, and Pumper 30, with its own captain and two firefighters, pulled in at 3:52. Suspecting that someone had set the fires deliberately, that crew requested police. Radio traffic with dispatch showed that both companies were wary of the trailers, which by then had caught fire themselves. At around 4:07 a.m., one of the trailers exploded. Thomas Fry, Gerald Halloran, Luther Hurd, James Kilventon Jr., Robert McKarnin and Michael Oldham were killed instantly. Twenty-seven minutes had passed since the first call. They had gone toward the danger because that was the job, and six families lost someone they loved before dawn.

## Heard Forty Miles Away

The first explosion was not the last. Roughly forty minutes later, the second trailer detonated, but by then all fire crews had been pulled back, and no one else was lost. The force of the two blasts was staggering. The first left a crater about 80 feet wide and 8 feet deep, and the second tore open a crater some 100 feet wide. Windows shattered across a ten-mile area, and the explosions could be heard 40 miles away. For the Kansas City Fire Department, which had lost two full crews in an instant, the morning was a devastating blow.

## A Verdict Still Questioned

Investigators concluded the fires had been set. For the next nine years, the ATF and Kansas City police worked to identify who was responsible, with little luck. Eventually they built a case against five suspects, who were indicted by a grand jury in June 1996. All five went to trial, and in 1997 they were convicted of arson causing death and sentenced to life in prison without parole. Their guilt has been disputed ever since. Bryan Sheppard, who was 17 at the time of the explosion, was released in 2017 after U.S. Supreme Court rulings on juvenile life sentences, including Miller v. Alabama and Montgomery v. Louisiana, made his sentence unconstitutional. After he sued the Department of Justice, he obtained records of a federal review suggesting that two security guards may have been involved, though that review concluded the findings did not undermine the defendants' guilt. In early 2026 a federal judge also vacated the life sentence of another of the five and ordered his release. The questions remain open. What does not waver is the memory of the six men who drove toward the fire.
