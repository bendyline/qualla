# Ruskin Heights, Kansas City

Ruskin Heights was a brand-new postwar suburb on Kansas City's southeastern edge when an F5 tornado tore through it on the evening of May 20, 1957, and the neighborhood's story since has been one of rebuilding and change.

**Location:** 38.9214, -94.5052

**Categories:** Neighborhoods in Kansas City, Missouri, Tornadoes in Missouri, 1957 natural disasters, Postwar suburbs

## Introduction

The houses were barely a few years old. Ruskin Heights had risen in the early 1950s on former farmland at the southeastern edge of Kansas City, a postwar suburb of new houses and new streets. Then, on the evening of May 20, 1957, a tornado that had touched down near Williamsburg, Kansas, crossed into Missouri and came through Martin City, Hickman Mills and Ruskin Heights. It was rated F5 on the Fujita scale. By the time it lifted, it had killed forty-four people along its path and injured more than five hundred, and whole blocks of the young suburb were gone.

## A Suburb on the Farm Fields

Ruskin Heights was built for the postwar boom. Laid out in the early 1950s in southeast Kansas City, in Jackson County, the neighborhood covers a little under a square mile of mostly flat ground, slightly elevated in places. Its ZIP Code is 64134. People moved in at record speed, and over the building boom of the 1950s and 1960s, 3,106 houses were constructed here. Among the new buildings was Ruskin Heights High School, built of brick and reinforced with steel. The neighborhood was still filling in when the storm arrived, and people kept coming even after it passed.

## The Evening of May 20

The storm was a large, long-tracked tornado, part of the May 1957 Central Plains tornado outbreak sequence, and it touched down in eastern Kansas around 7:15 that evening. Moving northeast, it entered Missouri and tore through the southern suburbs of Kansas City: Martin City, Hickman Mills, Ruskin Heights and the northeastern side of Grandview. Near Hickman Mills the tornado scoured the ground and snapped large trees. In Ruskin Heights, homes took F5 damage, and so did some of the businesses at the neighborhood shopping center. Of the forty-four people who died, thirty-seven lost their lives in Missouri. Each number was a life, lost in a community that had barely finished being built.

## Swept Clean

What the tornado left behind was stark. Entire blocks of homes were leveled, and many houses were swept away entirely, their debris flung long distances into nearby fields. In some places the subflooring itself was ripped off, leaving only bare basements. Some areas were reportedly "swept clean." A grocery store, the shopping center and restaurants were destroyed. Cars were thrown through the air. Even the new high school, steel-reinforced as it was, was badly damaged. Across the tornado's path, more than 800 homes and businesses were damaged or destroyed, and in nearby Martin City, 85 percent of the town was left uninhabitable.

## Rebuilding, and a Changing Neighborhood

Ruskin Heights rebuilt, a process that took about ten years, and people kept moving in even in the tornado's aftermath. In the 1970s, as Kansas City's African American population grew, the first Black families moved into the neighborhood. The decades that followed brought larger shifts. Since 1990 the population has fallen by about nine percent, a decline that began as many white families moved away. White residents made up 86 percent of the population in 1990, 53 percent by 2000 and 38 percent by 2010, when 59 percent of residents were African American. The area's Bannister Mall opened in the 1980s and peaked that same decade, then closed for good in 2007 amid falling demand and rising operating costs. By the 2020 census, 63.5 percent of residents were Black. The streets the tornado swept clean in 1957 are lined with homes again.
