# John Wornall House Museum

Built in 1858 of bricks fired on the family farm, the Wornall house sheltered wounded soldiers of both armies after the Battle of Westport and now tells a fuller story of antebellum Kansas City, including the enslaved people whose labor that prosperity relied on.

**Location:** 39.0186, -94.5922

## Introduction

In October 1843, five dollars bought an acre of farmland outside Westport, Missouri. Richard Wornall paid that price for 500 acres stretching between what are now 59th and 67th streets, from State Line to Main Street - ground that today lies deep inside Kansas City. Fifteen years later his son John raised a brick house on that land, and it still stands at 6115 Wornall Road in the Brookside area. Furnished to evoke the daily life of a prosperous family before the Civil War, the John Wornall House Museum preserves both the comfort of that life and the harder truths beneath it.

## Debts in Kentucky

The Wornalls came west to start over. In Shelbyville, Kentucky, Richard Wornall had run a mule- and horse-trading business that fell into financial trouble. In 1843, to settle debts totaling almost $25,000, he sold 640 acres of Shelby County land, most of his livestock and possessions, and thirteen enslaved people, whose lives were upended to pay a trader's creditors. With what money remained, Richard, his wife Judith and their sons George Thomas and John Bristow moved to Westport. On arrival that October, Richard bought his farm from John Calvin McCoy, the town's founder, and he also purchased five enslaved people. The family's new beginning, in other words, rested on the same system that had just broken other families apart.

## A House of Hand-Fired Brick

Richard and Judith's second son, John B. Wornall, eventually inherited the property. In 1858 he built the present house for his second wife, Eliza S. Johnson Wornall, and he built it to last. The bricks were hand-fired right on the Wornall property, and the design, whose architect is uncertain (the museum says unknown, though Asa Beebe Cross is sometimes credited), followed the Greek Revival style, with the classical symmetry that style prizes. The home announced that the Wornalls had arrived, prosperous landholders on the edge of a growing frontier town. Few of its contemporaries survived the decades of growth that followed. Today the Wornall house is one of the few surviving pre-Civil War houses in Kansas City.

## Two Armies, One Hospital

Within six years of its completion, the war arrived at the door. In 1864 the Battle of Westport was fought in the fields and woods around Westport, and the sturdy brick farmhouse was pressed into service as a field hospital. Remarkably, it served both sides. Union and Confederate forces alike used the Wornall home to treat their wounded, and the rooms built for a family's parlor life became places of pain and waiting. It is one of the most human details of the house's history. The divisions that tore Missouri apart during the war, setting neighbor against neighbor, briefly gave way here to the simpler business of keeping injured men alive, whatever uniform they wore.

## Telling the Whole Story

The house outlasted the farm around it as Kansas City grew south and swallowed the Wornall acres. The Jackson County Historical Society acquired the property, restored it to its antebellum appearance, and saw it listed on the National Register of Historic Places on May 21, 1969. In 1972 it opened to the public, which its operators describe as the first house museum in Kansas City. Today Wornall/Majors House Museums preserves the home as a setting for conversations about Kansas City history, enslavement in Jackson County, and the Civil War on the western border. That framing matters. The polished furnishings show how a prosperous family lived; the museum's broader mission asks visitors to remember the people whose labor that prosperity relied on.

## Flight Context

The John Wornall House stands at 39.019°N, 94.592°W on Wornall Road in Kansas City's Brookside neighborhood, south of the Country Club Plaza. From 2,500-3,500 feet AGL, find the green expanse of Loose Park about a mile to the north and the Plaza's Spanish-style towers beyond it along Brush Creek; the house sits among tree-lined residential blocks just south. The State Line between Missouri and Kansas runs less than a mile to the west. Charles B. Wheeler Downtown Airport (KMKC) lies about 6 nm north, Kansas City International (KMCI) roughly 18 nm north-northwest, and Johnson County Executive Airport (KOJC) about 12 nm south-southwest. This is busy metropolitan airspace near Kansas City's Class B and KMKC's Class D, so plan altitudes and communications accordingly. Autumn offers the clearest views, when the neighborhood's mature trees turn color.
