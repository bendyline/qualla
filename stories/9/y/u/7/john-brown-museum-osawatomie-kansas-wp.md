# John Brown Museum (Osawatomie, Kansas)

In Osawatomie, a rough frontier log cabin that sheltered John Brown, his half-sister Florella and her minister husband now stands under a stone pavilion, preserved as the museum of a family caught up in Bleeding Kansas.

**Location:** 38.4989, -94.9594

**Categories:** History museums in Kansas, Kansas state historic sites, Houses on the Underground Railroad, Bleeding Kansas, Biographical museums in Kansas

## Introduction

Florella Brown Adair had graduated from Oberlin College, and on the Kansas frontier that counted for very little. The log cabin she shared with her husband, the Reverend Samuel Adair, demanded a different education: how to do without the comforts she had known, how to keep a household running from a single fireplace used for both warmth and cooking. Florella was also the half-sister of the abolitionist John Brown. During Brown's time in Kansas, her cabin at Osawatomie was where he stayed from time to time and where he conducted many of his abolitionist activities. Today that same cabin stands sheltered inside a stone pavilion and preserved by the Kansas Historical Society as the John Brown Museum State Historic Site.

## Two Oberlin Graduates Head West

When Kansas Territory was established in 1854, newspapers in the North urged settlers to go there and make sure the future state would be free. The land offered families something more personal too: fertile soil, peaceful countryside and a chance to stop the spread of slavery. Samuel and Florella Adair shared that dream. Florella's father, Owen Brown, was a strict Calvinist and a trustee of Oberlin College; after the death of his first wife, Ruth Mills Brown, in 1808, he married Sally Root in 1809, and Florella was among their children. That made her John Brown's half-sister. Both Adairs graduated from Oberlin, a progressive Ohio college that admitted women and Black students alongside white men. After Samuel finished his theology studies, the couple married and moved west, where he sought a position in Osawatomie, Kansas Territory.

## Sons, Fever and Fire

Five of John Brown's sons followed the Adairs to Kansas, and there they met both severe illness and the violence of Bleeding Kansas. Brown came to help them, with no plan to stay permanently. Instead he found a sympathetic circle ready to act on his ideas. The abolitionists rebelled against the pro-slavery government that controlled the territory and often fought the Missourians who crossed the border to push slavery into Kansas. Osawatomie, close to that border, paid for it. On August 30, 1856, pro-slavery forces attacked and burned the town. Brown's son Frederick died nearby, the first to fall in the Battle of Osawatomie, and his father's activities in the area earned him the name "Old Osawatomie Brown." The Adair cabin, which the family had bought in the mid-1850s, survived. Its back room, it is believed, was used to hide people escaping slavery.

## A Minister's Long Labor

Reverend Adair struggled for years to build a congregation. His church, the first in Osawatomie and the third Congregational church in Kansas, rose from walnut lumber and native stone that he supplied himself, and it was dedicated on July 14, 1861. It still stands. The Civil War pulled the family apart. Samuel was sent to Fort Leavenworth as a military chaplain while Florella carried on his duties at home, until she fell ill and joined him there. She died at Leavenworth in 1865. Samuel returned to Osawatomie alone and helped establish the first mental institution in Kansas, the future Osawatomie State Hospital, where he volunteered as chaplain for eleven years. When he died in 1898, he left the cabin to his son, Charles Storrs Adair.

## A House Within a House

The old battleground became John Brown Memorial Park, where Theodore Roosevelt dedicated a memorial on August 30, 1910, and in 1912 the cabin was dismantled and moved to the site where it stands now. A stone pavilion went up around it in 1928, so that visitors today walk around the rough logs under its protection. In 1935 a statue of Brown by George Fite Waters joined the park, sponsored by the Women's Relief Corps, Department of Kansas, and in 1963 the Kansas Historical Society took over the site. The displays tell the story of John Brown, the Adairs and the local abolitionists around them. Alongside the original cabin are Adair family furnishings and belongings and Civil War artifacts, the ordinary possessions of a family that lived through an extraordinary time.
