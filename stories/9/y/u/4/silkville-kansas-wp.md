# Silkville, Kansas

An exiled French aristocrat planted thousands of mulberry trees on the Kansas plains in 1870 to build a utopian silk colony, and its silk won praise in Philadelphia before the dream came apart.

**Location:** 38.4500, -95.4892

**Categories:** Ghost towns in Kansas, Utopian communities in the United States, Sericulture, Franklin County, Kansas, National Register of Historic Places in Franklin County, Kansas

## Introduction

In 1876, silk made in Franklin County, Kansas, won praise at the Centennial Exposition in Philadelphia. It came from Silkville, a utopian colony founded by a French aristocrat who had fled an emperor, left the American South to escape his neighbors' hostility and settled on the plains to build a society around silkworms. Today Silkville is a ghost town about two miles southwest of Williamsburg, where U.S. 50 meets Arkansas Road, and only three stone buildings remain to tell its story.

## An Aristocrat in Exile

Ernest de Boissière was born in 1810 into an ancient French aristocratic family, but his politics were anything but aristocratic. He embraced the utopian socialism of the philosopher Charles Fourier, and those radical views put him in danger once Napoleon III came to power in 1851 and made himself emperor of the Second French Empire. Boissière was soon forced into exile. He came to America and settled first in New Orleans, where he set up a shipping line. There he spent part of his fortune supporting orphaned Black children, and by one account the heavy criticism that followed from wealthy white neighbors pushed him to leave the South altogether. He turned instead to Franklin County, Kansas, a place he believed was well suited to a utopian community.

## Mulberries on the Plains

In 1869 Boissière bought between 3,000 and 3,500 acres from the Kansas Educational Association of the Methodist Episcopal Church, and in 1870 Silkville was established. Dozens of French emigrants (forty, by one account) came to settle there at its founding, each paying a fee to join the commune. The economy rested on sericulture, the raising of silkworms for silk, and each settler was paid according to what he or she could produce. Boissière built a stone cocoonery and planted thousands of mulberry trees to feed the silkworms. Hundreds of peach, apple and ailanthus trees followed, along with more than a thousand grape vines.

## Sixty Rooms and 2,500 Books

At the heart of the colony stood Boissière's manor. For a time it was said to be the largest in the state, with sixty rooms that could comfortably hold between 50 and 100 people, and it held a library of 2,500 books and other volumes. The colony's children were not forgotten. Boissière founded a school that, according to author and historian Daniel Fitzgerald, was "the first in Kansas in which the instructors attempted to teach the contemporary world literature of the day." Manor, library, school and mulberry groves together made up an ambitious experiment in communal life, all resting on a single luxury crop.

## Unraveling

The praise in Philadelphia did not save Silkville. Settlers drifted away over the years, and the silk proved hard to sell. To compensate, Boissière shifted production toward butter, cheese and livestock, which kept the settlement afloat for a few more years before it finally collapsed and its members dispersed. Boissière returned to France in 1892 and died two years later, and his property was given to the Independent Order of Odd Fellows for use as an orphanage. Today only three stone structures survive: the school house and two barns. One of the barns was once the colony's cocoonery, though a tornado damaged its top floor and reduced it to a single level. In 1972 the buildings were added to the National Register of Historic Places, recognized for their place in Kansas history as an intentional community and as a home of sericulture.

## Flight Context

Silkville lies at 38.4500°N, 95.4892°W, about two miles southwest of Williamsburg, Kansas, at the intersection of U.S. Highway 50 and Arkansas Road, at an elevation of about 1,161 feet. From 1,500–2,500 ft AGL, follow U.S. 50 and look for the small cluster of stone buildings among the surrounding farm fields. Ottawa Municipal Airport (FAA identifier OWI; no ICAO code listed) is about 12 nm to the east-northeast, Garnett Municipal Airport (FAA identifier K68) is about 17 nm to the southeast, and Emporia Municipal Airport (KEMP) is about 34 nm to the west-southwest. Kansas weather can change quickly; watch for spring thunderstorms and strong winds.
