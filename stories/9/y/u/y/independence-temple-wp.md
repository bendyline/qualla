# Independence Temple

A stainless steel spiral modeled on the nautilus shell, the Community of Christ's temple in Independence stands on land set apart for a temple in 1831 and opens its doors to the public for a weekly prayer for peace.

**Location:** 39.0908, -94.4264

**Categories:** Churches in Independence, Missouri, Religious buildings and structures of the Community of Christ, Latter Day Saint movement in Missouri, Temple Lot, Peace symbols, Architecture

## Introduction

Every Wednesday at 1:00 p.m. Central Time, a Prayer for Peace is held inside a spiral of stone and steel in Independence, Missouri, part of a cycle of prayers that upholds a different nation of the world each day. The service, once held daily, is open to the public, which suits a building dedicated, in its own words, "to the pursuit of peace". This is the Temple of the Community of Christ, the denomination formerly known as the Reorganized Church of Jesus Christ of Latter Day Saints. Its stainless steel spire rises 300 feet, dominating the Independence skyline and anchoring the church's world headquarters.

## A Revelation Given Shape

The temple exists because of a revelation presented at the church's 1984 World Conference by its prophet-president, Wallace B. Smith. The Community of Christ regards that revelation as the culmination of instructions shared over more than 150 years by earlier prophet-presidents it recognizes. Ground was broken on April 6, 1990. The International Women's Conference in June 1993 became the first major event held inside, and the finished structure was dedicated on April 17, 1994. Architect Gyo Obata shaped the building to evoke the spiral shell of the nautilus, so that it seems to coil upward from the ground before resolving into that 300-foot spire. The form is more than decoration. The temple is regarded as a symbol of the church's mission and is closely tied to its developing theology of Zion, the peaceable Kingdom of God.

## Walking the Spiral

The way into the sanctuary passes through an etched glass archway depicting the Sacred Grove, where Joseph Smith had his initial revelatory experience. From there the worshipper's path begins, a gently ascending walkway of textured stone that spirals around the building's periphery. Artworks line the way, drawn from a collection of modern and traditional religious art from around the world, along with a fountain symbolizing the "living water" of John 4:10, placed for meditation. The path opens into a main sanctuary with seating for approximately 1,600 people and a Casavant pipe organ of 60 stops, 102 ranks and 5,685 pipes. In the exit foyer, at the mouth of the spiral, stands a large stained glass wall titled "The Field is White, Already to Harvest", a field of rice and wheat that earned an American Institute of Architects award for religious art.

## Lion, Lamb and Child

The massive bronze exit doors carry the church seal, showing the peaceful lion, lamb and child of Isaiah 11:6 together with a single word: PEACE. Beyond them, wide steps descend to a world plaza inlaid with a global map in brick. The temple holds no private ceremonies. It is open to everyone, and the sacraments performed here are communion, administration to the sick through the laying on of hands, and priesthood ordination. A meditation chapel, hung with Jack Garnier's paintings of sacraments being performed around the world, opens onto a Japanese-style garden for individuals and small groups. The building also holds the church archives, the Temple School, headquarters offices, theaters, a museum and a gift shop. About 60,000 people have been reported to visit each year, many on guided tours. Ken Robinson, a former member of the First Presidency, described the temple as being "at the center of meaning and identity" for the church's members.

## Ground Set Apart in 1831

The site carries a much older promise. In 1831, Joseph Smith, founder of the Latter Day Saint movement and great-grandfather of Wallace B. Smith, visited Jackson County and prophesied that a temple to the Lord would be built there. The early Latter Day Saints bought a 63-acre parcel known as the "greater temple lot", dedicated part of it as a temple site and laid cornerstones. They never got to build. Church members were driven from the county before construction began, and the original temple site proper is now owned by a different denomination, the Church of Christ (Temple Lot). More than a century and a half later, the Community of Christ raised its spiral on the greater temple lot, which it shares with the Auditorium, the Church of Christ (Temple Lot) headquarters chapel and a visitor center of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints.

## Flight Context

The Independence Temple stands at approximately 39.0908° N, 94.4264° W, near the historic center of Independence, about 13 km east of downtown Kansas City. Its 300-foot spiraling stainless steel spire dominates the Independence skyline and catches the sun well from 2,000–3,000 feet AGL; the Auditorium sits close by on the same temple lot. Charles B.
