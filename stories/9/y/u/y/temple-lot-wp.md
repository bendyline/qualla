# Temple Lot

In 1831 Joseph Smith dedicated a hilltop in Independence, Missouri, as the site of a temple at the center of Zion; nearly two centuries of expulsion, lawsuits, a failed dig and arson later, the grassy lot is still waiting.

**Location:** 39.0910, -94.4262

**Categories:** Latter Day Saint movement in Missouri, Temple Lot, Churches in Independence, Missouri, Unbuilt buildings and structures in the United States, National Register of Historic Places in Jackson County, Missouri

## Introduction

"The temple shall be reared in this generation." Joseph Smith is recorded as saying so in 1832, as his followers were buying land in Independence, Missouri, for the temple at the heart of a new City of Zion. Nearly two centuries later, the spot is a grass-covered field of about two and a half acres, with a few trees and a modest church headquarters in one corner. No temple has ever stood on the Temple Lot. Its emptiness is the story: a promise that has outlived the people who made it and divided the churches that inherited it.

## Not Far From the Court House

Independence became important to the Latter Day Saint movement in the autumn of 1830, only months after the church was organized in New York. In June 1831 Smith said a revelation placed the New Jerusalem on Missouri's western border, "on the borders by the Lamanites," the church's term for Native Americans; Independence lay about ten miles east of Kaw Point, on the line west of which tribes were to be removed under the Indian Removal Act of 1830. On July 20, 1831, Smith presented a more precise revelation naming Independence the "center place" and describing "a spot for the temple" lying westward "upon a lot which is not far from the court house." On August 3, Smith and seven companions laid a stone as the northeast cornerstone, and on December 19 Bishop Edward Partridge bought 63 acres, including the Temple Lot. Some early believers even held that the Garden of Eden had been here, though Smith never made that official.

## Plans for a House of the Lord

In June 1833 Smith set out the Plat of Zion, a city plan with 24 temples at its center. Its grid would echo in many later Latter Day Saint communities, including Salt Lake City and Mesa, Arizona. The temple planned for this lot, called the House of the Lord for the Presidency, was described in exacting detail: 87 feet long and 61 feet wide, two stories of 14 feet each under elliptical arches, a stone foundation with brickwork above, Gothic-topped doors and windows, venetian blinds, curtains that could divide the hall into four parts, and a belfry at the east end holding "a bell of very large size." None of it was built.

## Driven From Zion

Tension with Jackson County's other settlers ran high, and Smith's vision of acquiring every tract between Independence and the Kansas border alarmed them. In July 1833 church leader W. W. Phelps printed a Missouri law setting requirements for free Black people entering the state in The Evening and the Morning Star. Enraged residents, slaveholders foremost among them, took it as a signal to Black Missourians that there was an alternative to slavery; they burned the newspaper's plant and tarred and feathered Partridge and elder Charles Allen. On November 5, 1833, Partridge's home, the first meetinghouse on the Temple Lot, was burned along with an estimated 200 homes of Latter Day Saint families. The community fled to Clay County, then Caldwell County, before being expelled from Missouri altogether after the 1838 Mormon War and Governor Lilburn Boggs's "extermination order." In 1842 Boggs, living three blocks east of the Temple Lot, was shot in the head at home and survived; Smith's associate Porter Rockwell was arrested, but no one was ever convicted.

## Custodians of an Empty Field

After Smith's assassination in 1844, the movement splintered, and the Temple Lot passed in 1867 to the small Church of Christ (Temple Lot). In 1891 the Reorganized Church, led by Smith's son Joseph Smith III, sued for the land and won in federal district court, but lost on appeal, and the U.S. Supreme Court declined to review the case. The Temple Lot church tried once to build. After apostle Otto Fetting said John the Baptist had visited him in 1927, ground was broken on April 6, 1929, with a seven-year deadline, but a doctrinal dispute and a lack of money stalled the work at an excavated foundation, which the city filled in 1946. The church's headquarters has twice been damaged by arson, in 1898 and again on January 1, 1990, after which it was rebuilt. Since about 1976, the church has said it will neither sell the lot nor cooperate with other denominations to build a temple, and a small museum inside keeps some of the corner stones placed in Smith's time.

## The Greater Temple Lot

Around the little field, the rest of Partridge's 63 acres tells a different story. The Community of Christ, formerly the Reorganized Church and the second-largest body in the modern Latter Day Saint movement, owns most of the surrounding land. It opened its Auditorium just south of the lot in 1958 and dedicated its Independence Temple just to the east in 1994, a building whose stainless steel spiral spire rises 300 feet. The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints runs a visitors' center nearby. Three churches, each tracing its roots to Joseph Smith, now surround the ground that Joseph Smith dedicated, and the temple he promised remains unbuilt.

## Flight Context

The Temple Lot lies at 39.0910°N, 94.4262°W in Independence, Missouri, not far from the Independence courthouse square and east of downtown Kansas City. From 2,000–3,000 feet AGL, the easiest landmark is the Community of Christ Temple's 300-foot stainless steel spiral spire just east of the lot; the Auditorium sits just to the south, and the grassy Temple Lot lies between them.
