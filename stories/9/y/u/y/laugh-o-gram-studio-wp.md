# Laugh-O-Gram Studio

On the second floor of a Kansas City building, a 20-year-old Walt Disney ran his first animation studio into bankruptcy, and left for Hollywood carrying the unfinished reel that launched everything after.

**Location:** 39.0704, -94.5699

**Categories:** Walt Disney, American animation studios, Film history, 1920s in Kansas City, Missouri, Historic buildings

## Introduction

By the end of 1922, Walt Disney was sleeping at work. His animation studio on the second floor of the McConahay Building in Kansas City, Missouri, was so short of money that he lived in the office and went to Union Station once a week to bathe. The company was Laugh-O-Gram Films, and it would not survive. Yet in its cramped rooms Disney worked alongside a group of future animation pioneers, tested the idea that would carry him to California, and, by his own later account, kept a tame mouse at his desk.

## Twelve Cartoons and a Fairy Tale

It began with a commission. In 1921, Milton Feld contracted Disney to animate twelve cartoons, which Disney called Newman's Laugh-O-Grams. Their local popularity at the theater encouraged him, and on May 23, 1922, at 20 years old, he incorporated Laugh-O-Gram Films with money from local investors and the remaining assets of his earlier, defunct venture, Iwerks-Disney Commercial Artists. The studio delivered nine of the twelve requested films while earning little from them. Disney wanted more than contract work. He set out to make his own modernized animated fairy tales, spending six months on his first attempt, Little Red Riding Hood. The staff he gathered reads like an early roll call of American animation: Ub Iwerks, Hugh Harman, Rudolf Ising, Friz Freleng and Carman Maxwell all worked for the little Kansas City company.

## One Hundred Dollars Down

Salvation seemed to arrive in September 1922. The studio's sales manager, Leslie Mace, was in New York hunting for distributors when he signed a contract for six animated shorts with Pictorial Clubs, Inc. of Tennessee, intended for schools and other non-theatrical venues. Pictorial paid only $100 up front, with the balance due on January 1, 1924, once every short had been delivered. That day never came for Laugh-O-Gram. Pictorial went bankrupt only a few months later, the studio never saw the rest of the money, and its already serious financial troubles deepened until the staff drifted away. This was the season when Disney was living in the office, an ambitious young producer with a studio full of work and no way to be paid for it.

## A Toothbrush and a Girl Named Alice

A local dentist, Thomas B. McCrum of the Deener Dental Institute, offered a lifeline: a short film on dental hygiene for Missouri schools. Disney reassembled some of his staff and made Tommy Tucker's Tooth, which earned the studio $500. Rather than pay his creditors, he gambled the money on a new idea. Disney had noticed the popularity of the Fleischer Studios' Out of the Inkwell series, in which animated characters stepped into the real world. He decided to reverse the trick and place a real girl inside a cartoon universe. The result was Alice's Wonderland, starring the young Virginia Davis, whose parents signed her contract on April 23, 1923, granting her 5% of the film's receipts. On May 14, still looking for a distributor, Disney wrote to Margaret Winkler, a New York film distributor.

## A One-Way Ticket West

Once the raw edits of Alice's Wonderland were done, the studio filed for bankruptcy in July 1923. Disney scraped together his fare by filming a 6-month-old girl named Kathalee Viley and selling his movie camera, earning just enough for a one-way train ticket to Hollywood. He carried an unfinished reel of Alice's Wonderland with him. The gamble paid off. He secured a distribution deal with Winkler that led to the Alice Comedies series and the founding of the Disney Brothers Cartoon Studio, the predecessor to The Walt Disney Company. Kansas City left another mark as well. Disney told interviewers that Mickey Mouse was inspired by that tame mouse at his Laugh-O-Gram desk. On a 1928 train ride from New York back to Los Angeles, the story goes, he proposed calling his character Mortimer Mouse, and his wife, Lillian, found the name too pompous and suggested Mickey instead.

## Saving the Second Floor

The McConahay Building fell into disrepair after the studio's collapse, and a nonprofit called Thank You, Walt Disney has worked to restore it. The Disney family promised $450,000 in matching funds toward plans that include telling the story of Walt Disney's Kansas City years, a movie house for original and restored Laugh-O-Grams, and an education center for animation workshops. Setbacks have come in unexpected forms. In the early hours of a Saturday at the end of July 2021, a car struck the building and badly damaged its exterior, and Butch Rigby, who launched the campaign to save it, shrugged it off as "a bump in the road". The studio's own films have had their rescues too. In October 2010, animation historian David Gerstein reported that copies of three long-missing 1922 fairy tales, Jack and the Beanstalk, Jack the Giant Killer and Goldie Locks and the Three Bears, had been found.

## Flight Context

The Laugh-O-Gram Studio's McConahay Building stands at approximately 39.0704° N, 94.5699° W, on East 31st Street in midtown Kansas City, south of downtown Kansas City. The building itself is small; from 2,000–3,000 feet AGL, Union Station and the Liberty Memorial tower, about 2 km to the northwest, are the easiest nearby references, with the downtown skyline beyond them. Charles B. Wheeler Downtown Airport (KMKC) lies about 6 km to the north-northwest across the Missouri River, Kansas City International (KMCI) about 28 km to the north-northwest, and Johnson County Executive Airport (KOJC) in Olathe about 29 km to the south-southwest. The downtown area sits under busy Kansas City terminal airspace; expect summer haze.
