# City workhouse castle

Built in 1897 as a model workhouse jail of yellow limestone near 18th and Vine, Kansas City's castle-like workhouse outlived its purpose, a dozen reuses and decades of abandonment, and still stands as an open stone shell.

**Location:** 39.0880, -94.5631

**Categories:** Buildings and structures in Kansas City, Missouri, Buildings and structures completed in 1897, Poverty in Missouri, Unused buildings in Missouri, Prisons

## Introduction

In April 1901, Carrie A. Nation, the temperance crusader, walked into M. A. Flynn's saloon at 117 East 12th Street in Kansas City, chopped up liquor bottles with her hatchet, and climbed onto a table to lecture the drinkers on their moral depravity. She was arrested, given a suspended $500 fine, and ordered out of town within the hour. The alternative was the workhouse castle, the city's limestone jail on Vine Street.

## A Disgrace, Then a Castle

Kansas City grew explosively in the late 1800s, from 700 people in 1846 to 32,260 in 1870 and 132,716 in 1890, and petty crime grew with it. After Kansas enacted prohibition in 1881, drinkers simply crossed to the hundreds of saloons in Kansas City, Missouri. Offenders who refused the city's help or its orders to leave could be sentenced to the workhouse, and the old one was a horror. By 1897 almost 200 inmates were packed into a rotten frame shack, some of them for most of the past decade. The Kansas City Star called the cell room "a place to turn the stomach of a sewer rat" and the whole facility "a disgrace to any city in a civilized country." Its replacement, designed by architects James Oliver Hogg and A. Wallace Love in the Romanesque Revival style, cost $25,700. It rose beside a natural deposit of yellow limestone that had been quarried by inmates of the old workhouse, which stood across Vine Street.

## The Best Building in Town

The castle opened on December 20, 1897, with castellated towers of the kind then fashionable among Kansas City's upper class. Its first superintendent, Major Alfred Brant, proudly declared it "the best building Kansas City has," and it was conceived as a model of humane housing and rehabilitation. Judges attached fines to workhouse sentences for prisoners to work off at a daily rate. Women sewed prison uniforms, and men labored for the city's public works department. The ideals faded fast. Within a few years life inside was uniformly hard, with reports of an "overcrowded, vermin-ridden dungeon" and of prisoners caught stealing bread being chained, standing, to a dungeon wall. One prisoner summed up breakfast as "bad, very bad": a rusty pan of gravy, a rusty cup of coffee, half a brick of moldy bread and a small piece of meat.

## A Farm Instead of a Cell

In early 1906, with crime at a peak, Police Chief Hayes ran campaigns to clear vagrants from the city's North End, sometimes catching dozens a week, and a deal with Judge Brady sent those who defied orders to leave town to the workhouse for a year. The castle overflowed. Park planner George Kessler consulted the warden of the Leavenworth federal prison, who recommended a 115-acre tract southeast of Leeds, and Kessler helped design a workhouse farm for training in horticulture, gardening, quarrying and stonecutting. On June 23, 1911, the male inmates moved there for good, leaving only women in the castle. The farm's superintendent, Jacob Billikopf, later an internationally respected authority in social services, believed that "the best way to reform a man is to do it in the open, at some useful and healthful work."

## A Dozen Lives and a Long Silence

The castle's days as a jail ended in 1924. Over the next five decades it was repurposed more than a dozen times, serving as city storage, a Marine training camp and a dog euthanasia center. In 1969, remodeled, it held sewer department offices, and the old basement dungeon became a neatly painted supply room. In 1972 it was abandoned. As the Vine Street district around it sank into blight, the wooden interior collapsed until only the open limestone walls remained, filling with trash, trees and graffiti. A series of owners and investors came and went, and grand plans to redevelop the district repeatedly fell through; at least one developer proposed tearing the castle down. Its listing on the Kansas City Register of Historic Places on November 29, 2007, protected it from demolition.

## A Wedding in the Ruins

In 2014 the castle had a brief, joyful revival. Daniel and Ebony Edwards and their nonprofit, 2orMore, led hundreds of volunteers in hauling out 62 tons of trash, then held their own wedding and a run of community events inside the stone walls. The Kansas City Star called it "Daniel and Ebony's Modern Fairy Tale," the castle's first use in 42 years. Their hope of turning it into a permanent community center went unrealized, and since 2016 the site has stood vacant again, gathering graffiti. It sits just two blocks south of 18th and Vine, a street that, thanks to the legacy of Kansas City jazz, has been called America's third most recognized, after Broadway and Hollywood Boulevard.
