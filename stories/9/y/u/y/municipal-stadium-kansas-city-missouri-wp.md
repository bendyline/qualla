# Municipal Stadium (Kansas City, Missouri)

Near 18th and Vine, a Kansas City ballpark hosted the Negro league Monarchs and Jackie Robinson, Charlie Finley's goats and mechanical rabbit, the Beatles, two Super Bowl-era Chiefs teams and the longest game in NFL history.

**Location:** 39.0858, -94.5569

**Categories:** Defunct Major League Baseball venues, Defunct NFL venues, Negro league baseball venues, Former buildings and structures in Kansas City, Missouri, Sports venues demolished in 1976

## Introduction

When a home run sailed over the right-field fence at Municipal Stadium in the Charlie Finley years, goats and sheep scampered up the hill beyond it, residents of a small zoo behind the wall. At home plate, a mechanical rabbit named Harvey rose from the ground to hand the umpire fresh baseballs, and an air jet nicknamed "Little Blowhard" dusted the plate. Those gimmicks belong to one short, colorful chapter. The ballpark at Brooklyn Avenue and East 22nd Street in Kansas City holds a far deeper history, one that begins with the Kansas City Monarchs.

## Monarchs and Blues

The stadium opened in 1923 as Muehlebach Field, built for $400,000 by George E. Muehlebach, owner of the minor-league Kansas City Blues as well as Muehlebach Beer and the Muehlebach Hotel. In the inner-city neighborhood near 18th and Vine, it became home both to the Blues, an all-white team, and to the Kansas City Monarchs of the Negro leagues, from 1923 to 1955. The first Negro World Series game was played here in 1924, and in 1945 Jackie Robinson played here for the Monarchs until the Brooklyn Dodgers signed him. When the New York Yankees bought the Blues as a top farm team in 1937, the park became Ruppert Stadium, then Blues Stadium in 1943. About 580 Blues players went on to the majors, among them a young Mickey Mantle, who hit .361 for the Blues in 1951.

## Rebuilt in Ninety Days

Muehlebach had expected Kansas City to land a major league team someday and built footings meant to carry a future upper deck. When Chicago real estate magnate Arnold Johnson bought the Philadelphia Athletics in 1954 and moved them west, workers discovered that three decades of harsh Midwestern winters had weakened those footings too much. The city demolished the stadium and rebuilt it faithfully from scratch, running three shifts to finish in 90 days. Overtime costs forced capacity down from a planned 38,000 to just over 30,000, and the old Braves Field scoreboard was bought from Boston for $100,000. On opening day in 1955, former President Harry S. Truman, from nearby Independence, threw out the first pitch, with Connie Mack and Jimmie Foxx in attendance, and the A's beat the Detroit Tigers 6–2.

## Charlie Finley's Circus

Finley bought the A's in 1960 and treated the ballpark as a stage. In April 1964 he built a 296-foot "Pennant Porch" in right field to mock Yankee Stadium's short right-field fence; when the league vetoed it, he pulled the fence back to the legal minimum of 325 feet and repainted it "One-Half Pennant Porch." He had the announcer call out balls that "would have been a home run at Yankee Stadium," a gag that reportedly ended when he noticed most of them came off opponents' bats. He swapped the team's elephant mascot for a live mule named Charlie-O. On September 17, 1964, he paid the Beatles $150,000 to play here on one of the band's few days off, and they added "Kansas City" and "Hey, Hey, Hey, Hey" to their set. None of it lifted the team, which finished in or near last place nearly every year before Finley moved it to Oakland after 1967.

## Eighty-Two Minutes and Forty Seconds

Football arrived in 1963, when Lamar Hunt moved the AFL's Dallas Texans to Kansas City as the Chiefs. The field ran east–west along the first-base line, and temporary stands in left field went up each fall. The Chiefs of the Municipal Stadium years included Hall of Famers Len Dawson, Buck Buchanan, Bobby Bell, Willie Lanier and Jan Stenerud, played in the very first Super Bowl, and won Super Bowl IV. Their final game here, on Christmas Day 1971, became history. Ed Podolak piled up 350 total yards, still an NFL playoff record, but Miami's Garo Yepremian kicked the winning field goal in double overtime for a 27–24 Dolphins victory. At 82 minutes and 40 seconds, it remains the longest game in NFL history.

## A Plaque on 22nd Street

The expansion Kansas City Royals played here from 1969 to 1972, a season longer than planned because labor strikes delayed their new ballpark. Led by 1969 Rookie of the Year Lou Piniella, they drew nearly a million fans in their first year. On October 4, 1972, they won the stadium's final game, 4–0 over the Texas Rangers, the last game Ted Williams ever managed. The stadium came down in 1976 and gave way to a municipal garden; the site is now being redeveloped with single-family homes. A plaque at 22nd and Brooklyn marks the spot. Arthur Bryant's Barbecue, a favorite of fans and players, still serves at 18th and Brooklyn, and within a mile stands the Negro Leagues Baseball Museum, founded in 1990 with help from former Monarch Buck O'Neil.

## Flight Context

The former Municipal Stadium site lies at 39.0858°N, 94.5569°W, at Brooklyn Avenue and East 22nd Street on Kansas City's east side, near the historic 18th and Vine District and a mile or two southeast of downtown. Nothing of the stadium remains; from 2,000–3,000 feet AGL, use the downtown skyline and the Missouri River bend to the northwest as references. Charles B. Wheeler Downtown Airport (KMKC) is only about 3 nm northwest, so expect busy airspace and coordinate with ATC before any low-level sightseeing. Kansas City International (KMCI) is about 15 nm north-northwest.
