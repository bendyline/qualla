# Arthur Bryant's

A plain counter-service "grease house" on Brooklyn Avenue that a humorist once crowned the best restaurant in the world, and the place credited with turning brisket scraps into burnt ends.

**Location:** 39.0920, -94.5562

**Categories:** Barbecue restaurants in the United States, Restaurants in Kansas City, Missouri, Culture of Kansas City, Missouri, Food and drink, Restaurants established in 1908

## Introduction

"It has long been acknowledged," Calvin Trillin wrote in Playboy in 1972, "that the single best restaurant in the world is Arthur Bryant's." Then you walk in and see the Formica tables. The fluorescent lights hum overhead, there is a service counter instead of waiters, the meat arrives on butcher paper, and the sauce goes on with a paintbrush. Arthur Bryant himself called the place a "grease house." This unpretentious room at 1727 Brooklyn Avenue in Kansas City, Missouri, is sometimes called the most famous barbecue restaurant in the United States, and it is credited with originating the barbecue dish known as burnt ends.

## An Alley Stand and a Trolley Barn

The story starts before anyone named Bryant was involved. In 1908, Henry Perry, remembered as the "father of Kansas City barbecue", began serving smoked meats from an alley stand to workers in the Garment District of downtown Kansas City. He later moved to the 18th and Vine neighborhood, selling barbecue for 25 cents a slab out of a trolley barn at 19th and Highland. One of his employees was Charlie Bryant, who was soon joined by his brother Arthur. When Perry died in 1940, Charlie took over the business. In 1946, Arthur took it over in turn, running it for a time at 18th and Euclid. The lineage matters: Arthur Bryant's traces its roots to Perry's first stand in 1908, a direct line back to the man called the father of Kansas City barbecue.

## Five Blocks From the Ballpark

In 1958, Bryant moved to Brooklyn Avenue and put his own name on the door. The timing was fortunate. Municipal Stadium stood just five blocks to the south, home to the Kansas City Athletics from 1955 to 1967, the Kansas City Chiefs from 1963 to 1971 and the Kansas City Royals from 1969 to 1972. From the 1950s into the early 1970s, fans and players alike found their way to Bryant's counter. The stadium was demolished in 1976, but by then the restaurant's reputation had outgrown the neighborhood crowd. President Harry S. Truman ate here, and after Trillin, a Kansas City native, sang its praises in Playboy in 1972, the restaurant drew international attention. The visitors who followed included area resident Tom Watson, Steven Spielberg, Harrison Ford, Jack Nicholson and presidents Jimmy Carter, Bill Clinton and Barack Obama, who stopped in during 2014.

## What Was Once Given Away

Burnt ends are the flavorful end pieces of smoked beef brisket, and at Bryant's they began as scraps. Bryant handed them out for free as a side to other meals. As their popularity grew, the restaurant added open-faced burnt end sandwiches to the menu, and the former leftovers earned a place of their own on the menu. The rest of the menu reads like a roll call of the smoker: beef brisket, pulled pork, sausage, chicken, turkey, ham and ribs. Meat is ordered by weight or piled onto white bread as an open-faced sandwich with a side of fries. The sauces are openly available to diners, and bottles of them are sold at the restaurant, in grocery stores around the Kansas City area, and online.

## After Arthur

Arthur Bryant died in 1982, and the restaurant was sold to an ownership group that included Bill Rauschelbach and Gary Berbiglia. The new owners tried to grow the brand, opening a location inside Ameristar Casino and another near the Kansas Speedway in Kansas City, Kansas. Neither lasted. The casino outpost closed in January 2014 and the Kansas location in 2019, leaving the original Brooklyn Avenue restaurant as the one that endured. In December 2022, the building and the business were sold to MMD Acquisitions LLC for an undisclosed amount, with local food industry professional Andrew Miller and Chris Stuewe as the principals. The paintbrush, the butcher paper and the counter remain part of the ritual, much as they were when Trillin made his extravagant claim.
