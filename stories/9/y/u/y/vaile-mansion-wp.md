# Vaile Mansion

Harvey Vaile's 1881 Second Empire showplace in Independence survived a fraud scandal, family tragedy, an inheritance fight and decades as a sanatorium and nursing home before its restoration as a museum.

**Location:** 39.1081, -94.4172

## Introduction

Two of the chandeliers in the Vaile Mansion were meant for the White House. Some flaw kept them from going there, and Harvey Merrick Vaile, who was in Washington, D.C., at the time, bought them for $800 and carried them home to Independence, Missouri. It was a fitting purchase for the house he built at 1500 North Liberty Street. Vaile, as one account puts it, "desired a magnificent residence as an outward expression of his wealth," and the Second Empire mansion he finished in 1881 became the showplace of Jackson County. Over the following century it would also become the prize in an inheritance fight, a sanatorium, a nursing home and very nearly a demolition site.

## A Vermonter on the Santa Fe Road

Vaile was born in Vermont in 1831, earned a law degree from the University of Louisville, and moved to Kansas City in 1859 before settling in Independence in 1870. He was a strong supporter of abolition and loved politics, and he was among the founders of the Republican Party in Jackson County. His money came partly from the mail: he was a part-owner and operator of Star mail routes, including the rights to the route to Santa Fe. Construction on the house began around 1871 and took roughly a decade, at a cost of $150,000. Once it was finished, the Vailes offered what was described as "lavish hospitality to notables of the 1880s and 1890s including many U.S. Senators and congressional representatives."

## Thirty-One Rooms and Running Water

Kansas City architect Asa Beebe Cross designed the mansion, reportedly drawing on a large house the Vailes had visited in Normandy. The hand-pressed red brick alone cost $50,000. Inside are thirty-one rooms with fourteen-foot ceilings, painted by French, German and Italian artists, and nine marble fireplaces, one of which cost $1,500. The house was also built for comfort. It had speaking tubes, gasoliers, hot and cold running water and flush toilets, fed by a built-in 6,000-gallon water tank, and it is said to have been the first house in Jackson County with indoor plumbing. Around it spread a 630-acre estate with a vineyard and an apple orchard. Vaile ran a wine processing plant on the property, with a cellar that could hold 48,000 gallons.

## Trials and Losses

The mail contracts that helped pay for the house also brought trouble. In the early 1880s Vaile was caught up in the Star route scandal and charged with defrauding the government. He stood trial twice, in 1882 and again in 1883; the retrial ended in acquittal, but the defense cost him more than $100,000. Tragedy came during the same stretch. In February 1883, while Vaile was in Washington, his wife Sophia, who had been diagnosed with stomach cancer, was found dead at home from a morphine overdose, a suspected suicide. Vaile went on living in the mansion until his own death in 1894. His heirs then spent five years in court fighting over the estate.

## Nearly Lost, Slowly Restored

The house changed hands many times after that. In 1908 it became a sanatorium. Later, attorney Carey May Carroll turned it into a nursing home and the base of the Vaile Pure Water Co., which bottled spring water. After Carroll's death the mansion faced demolition, until Roger and Mary Mildred DeWitt bought it and began repairs. In 1983 Mrs. DeWitt donated it to the City of Independence, and a long restoration followed. The original furniture had been auctioned off when the estate left the Vaile family, so the Vaile Victorian Society, a nonprofit founded by local residents in 1983, refurnished the rooms. The original paintwork, the marble fireplaces and two of the three original chandeliers remain. Listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1969, the house now sits on 5.6 acres and is open to the public as a museum.

## Flight Context

The Vaile Mansion stands at 39.108N, 94.417W on North Liberty Street in Independence, Missouri, east of Kansas City. The house sits on only 5.6 acres, so from 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL it is best found by reference: downtown Independence lies just to the south along Liberty Street, and the two stadiums of the Truman Sports Complex are about five miles to the southwest beside Interstate 70.
