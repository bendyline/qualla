# 18th and Vine

In a few blocks of Kansas City's East Side, riff-driven jam sessions forged a style of jazz the world still recognizes, and the neighborhood that hosted them is still fighting to keep its footing.

**Location:** 39.0911, -94.5625

**Categories:** Neighborhoods in Kansas City, Missouri, Jazz, African-American history, Historic districts on the National Register of Historic Places in Missouri, Music history

## Introduction

Past midnight on a Friday, the music at 1823 Highland Avenue is only getting started. Since 1930, musicians have gathered at this building, the former home of the Colored Musicians Local 627, on Friday and Saturday nights after midnight to jam into the early morning hours. That stubborn after-hours ritual says a great deal about 18th and Vine. This Kansas City neighborhood is internationally recognized as a historical point of origin of jazz, and for generations it was a hub of African-American business as well. U.S. Representative Emanuel Cleaver once called it America's third most recognized street, after only Broadway and Hollywood Boulevard.

## Riffs in Crowded Rooms

Kansas City jazz was not imported. It was worked out here, in jam sessions inside the neighborhood's crowded clubs, where the sound grew riff-based and deeply influenced by the blues. That style earns 18th and Vine a place alongside the other great streets of American jazz: Basin Street in New Orleans, Beale Street in Memphis, 52nd Street in New York City and Central Avenue in Los Angeles. Many jazz musicians of the 1930s and 1940s lived in the neighborhood or got their start in it, and Charlie Parker was among them. The building on Highland Avenue that hosts those late sessions is now the Mutual Musicians Foundation. Its union, Local 627, merged with Local 34 in 1970, but the jamming carried on. The building appeared in Bruce Ricker's 1979 film The Last of the Blue Devils, joined the National Register of Historic Places that same year, and became a National Historic Landmark in 1981.

## Main Street for Black Kansas City

Music was only half the story. The district was a center of Black commercial and civic life, and its institutions still line the blocks. The African-American newspaper The Call kept its offices here for many years. The Historic Lincoln Building, restored in the early 1980s by the Black Economic Union of Kansas City, serves as a hub of professional and business activity in the Black community. It also served as an incubator. When the Negro Leagues Baseball Museum was founded in 1990, it began in a single-room office inside the Lincoln Building. By 1997 it had moved into a 10,000-square-foot, purpose-built structure, which it shares with the American Jazz Museum. Walk the district today and you pass the Gem Theater, the Blue Room jazz club, restaurants, and a growing number of apartments and condos, all within a few steps of the corner that gave the neighborhood its name.

## The Corner That Vanished

Six blocks to the north lies a more famous intersection that no longer exists. In 1952, Leiber & Stoller wrote the song "Kansas City" about 12th Street and Vine. Little Willie Littlefield recorded it as "K.C. Loving", and it was later taken up by Little Richard, Wilbert Harrison and the Beatles, carrying the name of a Kansas City street corner around the world. The corner itself did not survive. A housing project was built over the spot, and Vine Street no longer meets 12th Street at all. The city has since put up a street sign in a nearby park to mark where 12th and Vine used to be, a small monument to a place that lives on mainly in song.

## Façades and Second Chances

On September 9, 1991, the historic district, about 9 acres encompassing 35 contributing buildings, was listed on the National Register of Historic Places. Recognition did not bring easy recovery. In the 1990s, parts of the film Kansas City were shot here, and façades left from the movie remained on most of the dilapidated buildings until the end of the decade, a movie-set neighborhood layered over a real one in decline. Blight has long troubled the area. By 2006, more than $30 million of civic investment had flowed into the district since the late 1980s, yet redevelopment had struggled, with large portions passing between owners whose rehabilitation plans went unrealized. Still, the jazz museum, the baseball museum, the Gem Theater and the musicians' foundation remain, and every weekend the after-midnight sessions keep the neighborhood's oldest promise.

## Flight Context

18th and Vine sits at approximately 39.0911° N, 94.5625° W, on Kansas City's East Side a short distance southeast of the downtown skyline. From about 2,500–3,500 feet AGL, look for the downtown towers to the northwest and the street grid of the East Side spreading beyond them. The district itself is only a few blocks across, so it is best identified relative to downtown rather than picked out on its own. Charles B. Wheeler Downtown Airport (KMKC) lies across the Missouri River about 4 km to the northwest, Kansas City International (KMCI) is roughly 27 km to the northwest, and Kansas City - Lee's Summit Regional Airport (KLXT, formerly Lee's Summit Municipal) is about 22 km to the southeast. Mind the busy Kansas City airspace. Summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms can reduce visibility over the metro area.
