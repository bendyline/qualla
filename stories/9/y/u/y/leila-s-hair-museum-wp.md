# Leila's Hair Museum

For more than three decades, a retired cosmetology teacher's museum in Independence filled room after room with wreaths and jewelry woven from human hair, keepsakes of love and grief from an age before photography.

## Introduction

The flowers on the walls were made of hair. Petal by petal, strand by strand, generations of hands, many of them Victorian, had twisted and looped the hair of relatives, friends and the dead into wreaths, then sealed them in shadow boxes to hang at home. In a museum on Noland Road in Independence, Missouri, those wreaths covered the walls from top to bottom, hundreds of them, alongside rings, lockets and bracelets worked with human hair. Leila's Hair Museum was the life's work of one woman who found hair endlessly fascinating, and until it closed in September 2025, it offered a strange and moving glimpse of how people once held on to those they loved.

## Keepsakes Before the Camera

Hairwork is an old art, dating to the sixteenth century or earlier, and it flourished in the Victorian era. The idea was simple and deeply personal. Hair gathered from family, from friends, or from everyone at a gathering such as a wedding was shaped into flowers and assembled into a wreath, which was then set inside a shadow box frame. Some wreaths served as memorials, made with hair from someone who had died. Before photography, a lock of hair was one of the few ways to keep a tangible piece of a loved one, and people wove it into necklaces, bracelets, rings, lockets, paintings and medallions. What looks peculiar to modern eyes was, for those who made it, an act of devotion and remembrance.

## A Cosmetology Teacher's Life Work

Leila Cohoon was a retired cosmetology teacher living in Independence. She had a lifelong fascination with hair and considered hair one of the most interesting parts of the human body. In 1956 she began collecting hairwork, and over the decades that followed she came to regard the pursuit as her life's work. The museum that bore her name opened between 1986 and 1990, depending on the account, at first in a small room at the cosmetology school she founded. In its later location, multiple rooms were covered from top to bottom with hairwork. It was a display both odd and oddly tender, and in assembling it Cohoon had built something close to an archive of intimate history, one wreath at a time.

## Locks of the Famous

The numbers were staggering. By the end the collection numbered about 3,000 pieces, roughly 1,000 of them wreaths and the rest jewelry such as watch fobs, bracelets, rings and brooches, many made before 1900. Mixed among the anonymous keepsakes were locks attributed to some very famous heads: George Washington, Abraham Lincoln, Daniel Webster, Aaron Burr, the singer Jenny Lind, the abolitionist John Brown, Marilyn Monroe and Elvis Presley. Presidents, a duelist, a Swedish nightingale and the King of Rock and Roll shared the same walls with the anonymous keepsakes of ordinary families. The collection's sheer oddity earned it wide attention, including an appearance in the first episode of Mike Rowe's CNN series Somebody's Gotta Do It and a clue on Jeopardy! in 2016.

## The Last Strands

The museum's final years were hard. It closed during the COVID-19 pandemic and reopened in autumn 2023 on an appointment-only basis, with visitors required to schedule by email. Leila Cohoon died in November 2024 at the age of 92, and the museum struggled on without her. In September 2025, it closed permanently. The collection she spent nearly seven decades assembling did not simply vanish, however. Her granddaughter, Lindsay Evans, set to work finding new homes for the pieces in museums across the United States, among them the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York and the National Museum of Women in the Arts in Washington, D.C. The wreaths that once crowded one small museum in Independence are finding new audiences for their quiet stories of love and loss.
