# Jackson County Jail and Marshal's House

Independence's 1859 limestone jail crammed up to twenty Civil War prisoners into cells built for three, briefly held William Quantrill, and later gave Frank James a carpeted cell where he hosted card games.

**Location:** 39.0933, -94.4153

## Introduction

Frank James did his time on a Brussels carpet. When the older brother of the outlaw Jesse James spent several months in the Jackson County jail in the 1880s, his cell was furnished with fine furniture and paintings, he had the free run of the building, and at night he hosted card games. Just two decades earlier, the same six-by-nine-foot cells had held as many as twenty Civil War prisoners each. The 1859 jail in Independence, Missouri, holds both of those stories behind walls of limestone two feet thick, and today it is a museum where visitors can step inside them.

## Limestone, Iron and One Lamp

Jackson County built the jail in 1859, pairing the cell block with a residence for the marshal. The jailer's office formed part of that house but had its own separate entrance. Behind it, the jail held twelve cells, six upstairs and six down, enclosed by two-foot-thick walls of limestone blocks. Each cell had two doors, one of grated iron and one of solid iron, and a window covered with grated iron that let the wind blow straight in. At night, a single kerosene lamp in the hallway supplied the only light. Each cell measured six by nine feet and was intended for three prisoners. In a Missouri winter, with the wind coming through the grate and only a distant flame for light, it would have been a bleak place to wait for justice.

## Crimes of a Frontier Town

Before the Civil War, the charges recorded against the jail's prisoners read like a portrait of a lively, unruly frontier town. People were locked up for horse racing on public streets and for firing guns in town. Others were charged with operating a gaming house, assault and battery, disturbing the peace, or disturbing a religious meeting. And at least one offense still makes modern readers smile: building a privy "not over a pit." Independence was a growing community trying to impose order on its streets, its gaming houses and its sanitation, and the county jail was where those rules found their teeth.

## Twenty to a Cell

The war changed everything. During the Civil War, the building held both military and civilian prisoners and served as the office of the U.S. Provost Marshal. Cells designed for three people were packed with as many as twenty, crowded together behind the limestone with a single hallway lamp for light. William Quantrill, later the Confederate guerrilla leader, had spent a single night here in December 1860, months before the war began. During the war the jail also held people whose offense was refusing to take a pro-Union loyalty oath - a reminder of how deeply the conflict split Missouri, where political allegiance itself could land a person behind bars. For some of those confined here, imprisonment had less to do with any crime than with which side they stood on in a divided state at war.

## An Outlaw's Parlor

After the war, the jail's most famous inmate arrived. Frank James, older brother of Jesse, spent several months here in the 1880s, and his stay could hardly have been more different from the wartime crowding. His cell was fitted out with a Brussels carpet, fine furniture and paintings. He was allowed the free run of the jail, and in the evenings he hosted card games in his cell. That cell is preserved as it was. The jail itself stayed in service until 1933, when it was decommissioned and replaced. Restored as a public museum, it was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1970.
