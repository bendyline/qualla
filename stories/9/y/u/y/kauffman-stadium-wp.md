# Kauffman Stadium

Built for baseball alone in the age of cookie-cutter stadiums, the Royals' home in Kansas City pairs modernist concrete with the world's largest privately funded fountain and a red seat kept for Buck O'Neil.

**Location:** 39.0514, -94.4806

**Categories:** Major League Baseball venues, Kansas City Royals stadiums, Sports venues in Kansas City, Missouri, Sports venues completed in 1973, Modernist architecture

## Introduction

Among the rows of blue seats behind home plate, one is red. Since 2007 the Kansas City Royals have kept it for Buck O'Neil, who played for and then managed the Kansas City Monarchs of the Negro leagues between 1938 and 1955 and once sat in that spot himself. Every game, someone from the community who embodies his spirit is chosen to fill it. It is a quiet gesture in a ballpark known for louder ones, a stadium with a 322-foot fountain in right field and a scoreboard topped by a crown. Kauffman Stadium, "The K," has been the Royals' home since 1973.

## A Team Won by Ultimatum

The ballpark was planned for another team. In 1967 Jackson County voters approved bonds for the Truman Sports Complex, with separate stadiums for football's Chiefs and baseball's Athletics, an unusual idea when conventional wisdom held that separate stadiums were not commercially viable. Then, before the 1968 season, A's owner Charles O. Finley moved his team to Oakland. Missouri Senator Stuart Symington threatened to press for revoking baseball's antitrust exemption unless Kansas City got a new team, and Major League Baseball hastily granted four expansion franchises, including one owned by local pharmaceutical magnate Ewing Kauffman. Symington also forced the league to start the new teams sooner. The accelerated schedule had a casualty: the Seattle Pilots, lacking a suitable stadium, went bankrupt after a single season and moved to Milwaukee as the Brewers.

## One-Third of a Cookie Cutter

Royals Stadium broke ground on July 11, 1968. In an era of multipurpose "cookie-cutter" stadiums, it was built for baseball alone, and it was the only baseball-only park built in the majors from 1966 to 1991. It is often held up, along with Los Angeles's Dodger Stadium, as one of the best examples of modernist stadium design. Its smooth, uncovered concrete wraps the infield and stops at the foul poles; one baseball travel book described it as essentially one-third of a cookie-cutter stadium, keeping only the seats with the best views of baseball. Its signature is the Water Spectacular behind the right-field fence, a fountain and waterfall display that, at 322 feet, is the largest privately funded fountain in the world. For two decades the field was artificial turf, kept going by groundskeeper George Toma, famous for tending the first 57 Super Bowl fields, before grass arrived for the 1995 season.

## Nights to Remember

The stadium opened on April 10, 1973, with a 12–1 win over the Texas Rangers before 39,464 fans. Five weeks later, Nolan Ryan of the California Angels threw the first of his seven no-hitters here, blanking the Royals 3–0. The park hosted All-Star Games in 1973 and 2012, and Royals World Series games in 1980, 1985, 2014 and 2015. In Game 3 of the 1985 American League Championship Series, George Brett hit two home runs, made a backhanded stop at third to throw out a runner at home and recorded the final out of a 6–5 win. In 2014, fittingly, the city's old team, the Athletics, came to town for the Wild Card Game; trailing 7–3 in the eighth, Kansas City rallied to win 9–8. A year later the Royals beat the New York Mets in five games to win the 2015 World Series.

## Under the Crown

On July 2, 1993, the stadium was renamed for Ewing Kauffman, the franchise's founder and first owner, and it remains the only American League park named for a person. A $250 million renovation between 2007 and 2009 cut capacity to 37,903 and added a 360-degree outfield concourse, fountain-view terraces and a Royals Hall of Fame. Its centerpiece, the Crown Vision scoreboard, was 85 feet wide and 105 feet tall and, when it entered service in 2008, the largest high-definition LED display in the world. Assembled in 55 segments and topped by a crown, it flashes strobe lights after every Royals home run. Statues of George Brett, Dick Howser and Frank White stand in right field, and Ewing Kauffman and his wife Muriel are honored in left. Since 2014 the Royals have added a "W" to the Hall of Fame wall for every home win.

## Extra Innings for The K

The fifth-oldest stadium in the majors may not host the Royals much longer. In 2022 the team announced it intended to build a new stadium before its lease with Jackson County expires after the 2030 season. In April 2024 county voters rejected a sales-tax extension to fund a downtown ballpark, with more than 58 percent voting no, and after weighing sites on both sides of the state line, including in Kansas, the team announced on April 22, 2026 that its new ballpark and entertainment district will rise at Crown Center near downtown Kansas City, just south of the Crossroads. Whatever comes next, the fountains, the crown and the red seat behind the plate have given The K an identity all its own.

## Flight Context

Kauffman Stadium sits at 39.0514°N, 94.4806°W in the Truman Sports Complex on Kansas City's eastern edge, right beside Arrowhead Stadium and near the interchange of Interstate 70 and Interstate 435. From 2,500–3,500 feet AGL, look for the baseball diamond and the long fountain display beyond the outfield next to the larger football bowl, both surrounded by broad parking lots. Charles B. Wheeler Downtown Airport (KMKC) is about 7 nm northwest, Kansas City - Lee's Summit Regional Airport (KLXT, formerly Lee's Summit Municipal) about 7 nm southeast, and Kansas City International (KMCI) about 18 nm northwest; check Kansas City Class B boundaries before descending.
