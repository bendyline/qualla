# SubTropolis

Inside a limestone mine in the bluffs north of the Missouri River, Lamar Hunt turned the rock that built Kansas City into an underground business complex with lighted streets, rail lines and a steady 65 to 70 degrees all year.

**Location:** 39.1612, -94.4762

## Introduction

Roller coasters rattle over the surface at the north edge of the complex, where Worlds of Fun and Oceans of Fun draw their crowds. Below, as deep as roughly 150 to 160 feet under the ground, the weather never changes. Paved roads run under electric light through solid rock, and the air holds between 65 and 70 degrees Fahrenheit in January and July alike. This is SubTropolis, a business complex built inside a limestone mine in the bluffs north of the Missouri River in Kansas City, Missouri. Its developer marketed it under a phrase he trademarked: the World's Largest Underground Business Complex.

## The Rock That Built a City

The story begins with stone. In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, limestone mines in these bluffs supplied material for Kansas City's construction, and every ton hauled out left a room behind. As mining activity declined, the site was repurposed by Lamar Hunt, the owner of the Kansas City Chiefs, working through Hunt Midwest Real Estate Development. A spent mine, it turned out, was space with a roof already on it. The first of the underground complexes Hunt's company developed opened for commercial use in 1964, putting tunnels and rooms to work for storage of goods and equipment. Some of the first tenants were familiar names: Pillsbury, and Russell Stover Candies. In the early 1970s the Ford Motor Company leased 25 acres of the underground to store vehicle inventory, filling caverns that had once yielded building stone with automobiles.

## A Grid Cut in Stone

The complex is dug into the Bethany Falls limestone, and its layout follows the logic of the room and pillar method of hard rock mining. Miners removed rock in a regular grid and left the rest standing as support. The result is a pattern of tunnels 16 feet high and 40 feet wide, separated by limestone pillars 25 feet square, repeated across a mine of some 55 million square feet, or about 1,260 acres. Nearly 10.5 miles of illuminated, paved roads thread through it, along with several miles of railroad track. The earth itself does the work of a thermostat. Insulated by all that rock, the mine naturally holds its air between 65 and 70 degrees, whatever the season is doing overhead.

## Stamps, Records and a Federal Presence

That steady, sheltered environment has drawn some unexpected tenants. The United States Postal Service has leased space here for its collectible stamp operations, so stamps meant for collectors are handled inside a limestone mine. The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency has run its Region 7 Training and Logistics Center underground. And the National Archives and Records Administration has leased space for a Federal Records Center, keeping government files deep inside the bluffs. It is an odd combination to imagine: stamps for collectors, federal records and an environmental agency's logistics hub, all sharing a grid of rock tunnels north of the Missouri, reached by lighted roads that never see daylight.

## Above Ground, and Beyond

Hunt's ambitions did not stop at the mine. On the surface at the north edge of the complex he developed the Worlds of Fun and Oceans of Fun amusement parks, so one end of his underground real estate has thrill rides overhead. His extensive business dealings in Clay County also contributed to the Chiefs setting up a training camp at William Jewell College in nearby Liberty, where the team trained from 1963 until 1991. SubTropolis is not the only mine to find a second life, though others work on a smaller scale. An abandoned mine in Butler, Pennsylvania, has been used by Corbis and the federal government for secure storage, and the Mega Cavern in Louisville, Kentucky, is a similar but smaller space. Because room and pillar mining extracts limestone throughout the Midwest, hundreds of millions of square feet of such space exist, and companies have looked at uses ranging from mushroom farming to stockpiling crude oil.

## Flight Context

SubTropolis lies at 39.1612°N, 94.4762°W in Clay County, in the bluffs on the north side of the Missouri River in Kansas City, Missouri. The complex itself is entirely underground and cannot be seen from the air, so navigate by what sits around it: the Missouri River's northern bluffs and the Worlds of Fun and Oceans of Fun amusement parks on the surface at the complex's north edge. From 2,500–3,500 feet AGL on a clear day, the river, the parks and the dense Kansas City street grid to the south and west make the area easy to place. Charles B. Wheeler Downtown Airport (KMKC) lies about 6 nm west-southwest beside the river, and Kansas City International (KMCI) about 14 nm northwest; the area sits beneath Kansas City's Class B airspace, so coordinate with approach control. Summer haze can soften the view of the bluffs.
