# Mine Creek Battlefield State Historic Site

On the banks of a small creek in Linn County, a Union cavalry charge shattered Sterling Price's retreating army in 1864, in one of the largest cavalry battles of the Civil War and the largest Civil War battle fought in Kansas.

**Location:** 38.1408, -94.7311

**Categories:** American Civil War battlefields, Kansas state historic sites, Military and war museums in Kansas, Protected areas of Linn County, Kansas, Conflict sites on the National Register of Historic Places in Kansas

## Introduction

"Both lines seemed like walls of adamant," wrote Colonel Charles W. Blair of the 14th Kansas Cavalry. "One could not advance; the other would not recede." For a time, he remembered, the fire was "incessant and terrific." Then the wall broke. On the morning of October 25, 1864, beside a modest stream about two and a half miles southwest of present-day Pleasanton, Union horsemen smashed into a Confederate line nearly three times their number and sent it reeling south in what General Sterling Price himself called "utter and indescribable confusion." The Battle of Mine Creek was one of the largest cavalry battles of the Civil War and the largest Civil War battle fought in Kansas. Today the ground where it happened is a state historic site.

## Price's Great Gamble

The road to Mine Creek began on September 19, 1864, when Price led a Confederate army of about 12,000 men across Missouri's southern border, hoping to capture the state for the South. His orders were to "rally the loyal men of Missouri" and fill his ranks with fresh recruits. If "compelled to withdraw from the state," he was to retreat through Kansas, "sweeping that country of its mules, horses, cattle, and military supplies of all kinds." Price's three divisions moved toward St. Louis and on September 27 defeated a much smaller Federal force at Pilot Knob, but their losses were heavy and St. Louis had been reinforced. Price turned west without attacking the city, marching along the south bank of the Missouri River, tearing up railroad track and capturing small towns on his way toward the Kansas border.

## Kansas Braces

As word of the advance spread, Kansans prepared for invasion. Governor Thomas Carney called out the state militia on October 8, and General Samuel R. Curtis gathered every force at hand into the Army of the Border. The fighting opened for Kansas troops at Lexington, Missouri, on October 19. The Confederates won several fights around Kansas City, including the Battle of the Big Blue on October 22, but at Westport the next day they were thrown back and forced to retreat south along the state line. Curtis released most of the militia and reorganized, giving General James G. Blunt the First Division and General Alfred Pleasonton the Second. About 10,000 Union cavalry took up the pursuit. The nearby town of Pleasanton would later take its name, spelled slightly differently, from that general.

## Less Than an Hour

On October 24, Price's army crossed into Linn County, Kansas, and camped near Trading Post. Before dawn the Federals caught up, and a running fight began that would last all day. The decisive clash came late in the morning. General John S. Marmaduke was forced to turn and fight a rear-guard action on the north bank of Mine Creek to protect Price's fleeing wagon train, backed by General James Fleming Fagan's division, which had already crossed. The two Confederate divisions held about 7,000 men. Pleasonton's advance, led by the brigades of Colonels Frederick W. Benteen and John Finis Philips, numbered fewer than 2,500. They charged anyway. Benteen described a "fierce hand-to-hand fight, one that surpassed anything for the time it lasted" that he had ever witnessed. In under an hour it was over, and Price's army narrowly escaped total destruction.

## From Retreat to Remembrance

After Mine Creek, Price had to abandon his plan to attack Fort Scott, and his men deliberately destroyed most of the wagon train that carried their supplies and plunder. Curtis and Blunt kept up the chase, and on October 28 they handed Price his final defeat at Newtonia, Missouri. The battered army recrossed the Arkansas River on November 8. For all practical purposes, the Civil War in the West was over. Preserving the battlefield took far longer than winning it. In 1970 the Kansas legislature approved buying 160 acres of the battlefield, which was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1973. Another 120 acres followed in 1974. A visitor center opened on October 24, 1998, the 134th anniversary of the eve of the battle. The American Battlefield Trust and its members have saved more than 326 acres of the field where the lines once stood like walls.

## Flight Context

Mine Creek Battlefield State Historic Site lies about 2.5 miles southwest of Pleasanton in Linn County, Kansas, at approximately 38.1408 N, 94.7311 W From 1,500-2,500 feet AGL, pick up Pleasanton first, then follow the line of Mine Creek to the southwest; the historic site has a visitor center that opened in 1998. Fort Scott Municipal Airport (KFSK) lies about 21 nm to the south and Butler Memorial Airport (KBUM) in Butler, Missouri, about 21 nm to the east-northeast; Garnett Municipal Airport (FAA identifier K68) is about 24 nm west-northwest. Expect spring thunderstorms and summer haze; late autumn, near the October anniversary of the battle, often brings crisp visibility.
