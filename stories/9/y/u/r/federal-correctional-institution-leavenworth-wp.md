# Federal Correctional Institution, Leavenworth

Built over two and a half decades by the prisoners it would hold, Leavenworth's domed "Big Top" was for a century the country's largest maximum-security federal prison, and its history runs from a locomotive jailbreak to a fugitive who became a Canadian mayor.

**Location:** 39.3308, -94.9358

**Categories:** Prisons in Kansas, United States Penitentiaries, Buildings and structures in Leavenworth, Kansas, Execution sites in the United States, 1903 establishments in Kansas

## Introduction

On April 21, 1910, six prisoners smashed through the gates of the still-unfinished federal penitentiary at Leavenworth, Kansas, aboard a hijacked supply locomotive. Five were soon recaptured. The sixth, Frank Grigware, who had been imprisoned for train robbery, made it to Canada, and in 1916 he became mayor of Spirit River, Alberta. The Royal Canadian Mounted Police and the FBI finally identified him in the early 1930s, but serious doubts about his original conviction led the United States to drop its extradition request in 1934. Grigware never returned, and he died in Alberta in 1977. His escape is only one of the stories held inside the walls of what is now the Federal Correctional Institution, Leavenworth, a prison that for a century stood as the largest maximum-security federal prison in the United States.

## Built by Its Own Prisoners

The story starts with the Army. In 1827 Colonel Henry Leavenworth chose the site for a new fort, and in 1875 that fort was picked as the site of a military prison; within a year, more than 300 prisoners were held in a remodeled supply depot. Congress transferred the prison to the Department of Justice on July 1, 1895, creating the United States Penitentiary, and in 1896 authorized a new federal penitentiary to replace it. Beginning in March 1897, Warden James W. French marched prisoners two and a half miles every morning from Fort Leavenworth to the new site, where they built the prison that would hold them. Work went on for two and a half decades. By 1903 enough was under roof for the first 418 prisoners to move in, and by February 1, 1906, everyone had been transferred, allowing the War Department to take back its old prison.

## The Big Top

Leavenworth was one of the first-generation federal prisons, alongside Atlanta and McNeil Island. Before them, federal prisoners had been held in state prisons. The St. Louis architecture firm Eames and Young designed both Leavenworth and the Atlanta penitentiary, following the format popularized at New York's Auburn prison: cell blocks inside a large rectangular building, built around indoor group labor with staff constantly on patrol. The cells sit back to back in the middle of the structure, facing the walls. Those walls rise 40 feet, reach 40 feet below the surface and run 3,030 feet, enclosing 22.8 acres. The domed main building earned the nicknames "Big Top" and "Big House." To the north, the domed Disciplinary Barracks on the Army post was known as the "Little Top" until it was torn down in 2004.

## Doors That Would Not Hold

Grigware's locomotive breakout came while the prison was still under construction, but finished walls did not end the attempts. Basil Banghart escaped from Leavenworth three times, and escaped federal custody a fourth time while waiting to be sent back. On December 11, 1931, seven inmates took Warden Thomas B. White hostage and broke out, reportedly aided by the well-known gangsters Frank Nash, George "Machine Gun" Kelly and Thomas James Holden. Perhaps the boldest exit came on March 12, 1989, when a bank robber with a long record of escapes posed as a federal safety inspector and simply walked out the front doors.

## Grim Chapters

The prison's records hold darker entries too. On November 10, 1901, Joseph Waldrupe became the first correctional officer at Leavenworth known to have been killed in the line of duty. On September 5, 1930, the serial killer Carl Panzram, sentenced to death for murder, was hanged, the first execution at Leavenworth in records dating back to 1927. On August 12, 1938, Robert Suhay and Glenn Applegate, both sentenced to death for murder, were hanged in the prison's first recorded double execution. From 1944 to 1947 the prison also held Japanese American conscientious objectors who had refused military service in protest of the wartime incarceration of themselves and their families. Outside the walls, the penitentiary keeps a cemetery for prisoners who died in its custody.

## A Name to Match

In the 1980s and 1990s, three of the four cellhouses, A, B and C, were heavily renovated, leaving D-Cellhouse as the only cellblock true to its original design. In 2005 the Bureau of Prisons lowered the security level from high to medium while keeping the old USP name for historical reasons. In December 2021 the institution began taking in pre-trial inmates of all custody levels alongside its medium-security general population. Finally, in 2024, it was renamed the Federal Correctional Institution, Leavenworth, so the name would match its security level. A satellite camp next to the main prison houses minimum-security men. It remains separate from, though often confused with, the military's Disciplinary Barracks a short distance north at Fort Leavenworth. A $532 million replacement prison and camp, begun in 2023 on Bureau of Prisons land just east of the old walls, was scheduled for completion in 2026. At the groundbreaking, training space or a historical museum were floated as uses for the old penitentiary once it empties, but no final decision had been made.

## Flight Context

FCI Leavenworth sits at approximately 39.3308 N, 94.9358 W, on the edge of Leavenworth, Kansas, about 25 miles northwest of Kansas City, Kansas, near the west bank of the Missouri River. From 2,500-3,500 feet AGL, look for the large walled compound with its domed central building; the Army's Fort Leavenworth and the military Disciplinary Barracks lie a short distance to the north. Sherman Army Airfield (KFLV), a public-use military field at Fort Leavenworth, is about 2.5 nm north-northeast, so expect military traffic. Kansas City International Airport (KMCI) is about 10.5 nm east, so mind its busy airspace; Rosecrans Memorial Airport (KSTJ) in St. Joseph is about 27 nm north. Summer haze and spring storms are common along the Missouri River valley.
