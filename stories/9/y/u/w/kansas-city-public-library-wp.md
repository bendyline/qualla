# Kansas City Public Library

Founded in 1873 with money raised from six public lectures, the Kansas City Public Library is the metro area's oldest library system - and the owner of a parking garage disguised as a giant bookshelf.

**Location:** 39.1027, -94.5839

## Introduction

Along 10th Street in downtown Kansas City, a parking garage wears the spines of 22 enormous books. Catch-22, Charlotte's Web, Fahrenheit 451, The Lord of the Rings and a volume called Kansas City Stories are all up there. Each spine measures roughly 25 feet by 9 feet. The Community Bookshelf, built in 2004 along the south wall of the Central Library's garage, is the most visible face of the Kansas City Public Library, a system that began in 1873 with a lecture series, a superintendent's reading list and a city that wanted books.

## A Shelf the Size of a Block

The bookshelf runs along 10th Street between Wyandotte Street and Baltimore Avenue, and it grew out of a community effort to give character to a structure the library needed anyway. The spines are made of signboard mylar laid over concrete panels and an aluminum substructure. Kansas City readers suggested the titles, and the library's Board of Trustees made the final selection. The result is a deliberately wide-ranging shelf: Plato's Republic and the Tao Te Ching, Zora Neale Hurston's Their Eyes Were Watching God and Ralph Ellison's Invisible Man, Gabriel García Márquez's One Hundred Years of Solitude in its original Spanish title, and Black Elk Speaks. American history appears in the journals of Lewis and Clark, Stephen Ambrose's Undaunted Courage and David McCullough's Truman. One spine gathers a set of children's favorites, from Goodnight Moon and Green Eggs and Ham to The Wonderful Wizard of Oz.

## Six Lectures and a Stack of Books

The library's origins were modest and resourceful. In November 1873, the Kansas City Board of Education arranged a course of six popular lectures to raise money for books, and the superintendent of schools, James M. Greenwood, selected the first volumes himself. The library was founded on December 5, 1873. Carrie Westlake Whitney took over in 1881 as its first full-time librarian. A decade later, on November 19, 1891, the board adopted a resolution from Major L. K. Thacher asking for a record of the library's origins, and Greenwood prepared a pamphlet, A history of the Kansas City Public Library from 1873 to 1893, dated December 1, 1892. From those beginnings grew what is now the oldest and third-largest public library system in the Kansas City metropolitan area.

## The City's Memory

Inside the Central Library, the Missouri Valley Room holds the library's special collections on Kansas City local history: original and published materials, news articles, postcards, photographs, maps and city directories reaching back to the community's earliest days. The Ramos Collection gathers books, pamphlets, journal articles and other materials on African-American history and culture. The library also turns outward with its knowledge, periodically hosting edit-a-thons on topics relevant to the region, with recent sessions devoted to Kansas City Black history, Kansas City jazz and Kansas City philanthropists.

## Open Doors, Across the City

Beyond the Central Library at 14 West 10th Street, the system runs neighborhood branches across Kansas City, Independence and Sugar Creek, among them the L.H. Bluford branch on Prospect Avenue, the I.H. Ruiz branch, and the Plaza, Waldo and Westport branches. The library keeps an open-door policy for patrons, and its programs reflect it. Beginning in 2016, its AmeriCorps VISTAs and outreach team launched Coffee & Conversations, modeled on a Dallas Public Library program, to address homelessness in the community, sharing information on homelessness and other social issues and inviting open dialogue. Through its Refugee and Immigrant Services & Empowerment program, known as RISE, the library connects immigrants with resources to help them pursue citizenship.

## Flight Context

From 2,000-3,000 ft MSL, look for the downtown grid on the south side of the Missouri River; the Art Deco Power and Light Building tower rises a few blocks to the south. Charles B.
