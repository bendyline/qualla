# Airline History Museum

Born in 1986 as a volunteer crusade called Save-A-Connie, Kansas City's Airline History Museum gathered TWA-era airliners at the downtown airport, then fought to keep the hangar that sheltered them.

**Location:** 39.1187, -94.6003

**Categories:** Aerospace museums in Missouri, Museums in Kansas City, Missouri, Trans World Airlines, Aviation history, Museums established in 1986

## Introduction

The first name said everything about the mission. In 1986, aviation enthusiasts Larry A. Brown and Dick McMahon founded an organization called Save-A-Connie, and the Connie in question was a Lockheed Super "G" Constellation that Paul Pristo donated to the group that same year. Current and former TWA employees soon joined them. What grew from that effort became the Airline History Museum at Kansas City's Downtown Airport, devoted to the history of America's airlines - and, for nearly four decades, a stubborn experiment in keeping old airliners alive in the city that had once been TWA's hometown.

## Hangar 9 and the Star of America

For a time the group weighed building a hangar of its own for the Constellation. In 2000 it settled instead on a lease of Hangar 9 at Kansas City Downtown Airport from Executive Beechcraft. The airplane inside carried a remarkable billing: the museum called it the first Constellation ever fully restored to flying condition. Nicknamed "Star of America," the Super-Connie also built a second career in front of cameras. It appeared in the A&E documentary First Flights, narrated by astronaut Neil Armstrong, and in the film Voyager, released in the United States in 1992. Its interior served as a set for Ace Ventura: When Nature Calls in 1995, and in 2004 it turned up in Martin Scorsese's The Aviator, a portrait of the young Howard Hughes, the aviator who once owned TWA. Between roles, it completed several successful four-engine test runs as volunteers chased the goal of getting it back into the air.

## A Fleet with Kansas City Roots

The collection leaned hard on local ties. The museum's Douglas DC-3, registration NC1945, was built in Santa Monica, California, in February 1941 and delivered to Transcontinental and Western Airlines at Kansas City on March 4, 1941, so its arrival at the museum was a homecoming. Volunteers overhauled one of its two engines and installed restored seats and new carpeting as they worked toward airworthiness. Its Martin 4-0-4 represented a rarer breed. Only 103 were built; TWA flew 40 of them, while rival Eastern Airlines ordered the most, with 60. An inspection in August 2011 found that rumors of extensive corrosion on the museum's example were untrue.

The biggest arrival came on January 30, 2010, shortly after 3 p.m., when a Lockheed L-1011 TriStar landed after an FAA-approved ferry flight from Roswell, New Mexico. First flown in 1972 in TWA's red and white, it was one of the last six operational TriStars in the United States. Its engines had been sold before the purchase, and it sat on the apron with its enormous tail hanging beyond the fence line.

## Coffee, Rockets and Big Ambitions

In January 2011, marking its 25th anniversary, the museum set out to meet the American Alliance of Museums' "Characteristics of Excellence" standards, launching a rebrand that touched everything from its membership structure to its logo. That March it became the National Airline History Museum, a name meant to position it for federal grants, and Kansas City's Roasterie became "The Official Coffee of the National Airline History Museum," printing the museum's DC-3 on its products. Out front stood a borrowed piece of pop-culture history: the TWA Moonliner II, a scale-model rocket that had once topped TWA's downtown headquarters, restored by a Columbia, Missouri, lawyer who collected Disney memorabilia and lent to the museum. The ambitions kept coming. In 2013 the museum announced plans to return the Constellation to flight, and in 2020 it acquired a Ryan PT-22 Recruit that had belonged to actor Harrison Ford.

## Airplanes Without a Home

Not every acquisition found a place to land. A Douglas DC-8 bought in 2014 could not stay, because the property owner rejected the plan to store it on the ramp outside the hangar. A Boeing 727 acquired in 2016 while in storage at Seattle's Boeing Field never made the trip; unable to move it, the museum saw it scrapped in 2021. By 2016 the name had reverted to the Airline History Museum.

The larger threat concerned ground, not airplanes. In 2019 the airport began requiring Signature Flight Support, owner of the hangar, to pay rent, and eviction loomed. A notice arrived in 2022, and in 2023 the museum was locked out of its hangar. It filed a complaint with the Federal Aviation Administration in 2024, a complaint dismissed in April 2026. Later in 2024 the Star of America was sold to John Travolta. The Moonliner II moved across the airport to the TWA Museum on September 27, 2025, and the TriStar that had arrived to such fanfare was reportedly scrapped in May 2026.

## Flight Context

The museum's former home, Hangar 9, is on the field at Charles B. Wheeler Downtown Airport (KMKC / MKC, field elevation about 757 ft), at approximately 39.1187 N, 94.6003 W - roughly half a mile southwest of the airport reference point. The airport sits inside a bend of the Missouri River directly across from the downtown Kansas City skyline, which makes it easy to pick out from 2,500-3,500 ft MSL on a clear day. Kansas City International (KMCI) lies about 12 nm to the north-northwest; expect busy controlled airspace throughout the metro area. Summer haze and thunderstorms can reduce visibility over the river valley.
