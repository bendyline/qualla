# Country Club Plaza

Mocked as "Nichols' Folly" when it was planned on pig-farming land, Kansas City's Seville-inspired Country Club Plaza became what is considered the first planned large outdoor suburban shopping center in the United States.

**Location:** 39.0413, -94.5918

## Introduction

When J.C. Nichols began buying land in Brush Creek Valley in 1907, the valley was better known for pig farming than for shopping. The only developed property there belonged to the Country Day School, and when Nichols announced what he intended to build, people called the project "Nichols' Folly." The joke aged badly. The district that opened in 1923 is considered the first planned large outdoor suburban shopping center in the United States, and among the first regional centers built for shoppers arriving by car. A century later its towers and fountains still rise about four miles south of downtown Kansas City, a stretch of Seville set down beside a Missouri creek.

## Seville on Brush Creek

Nichols planned the Plaza in 1922 and hired architect Edward Buehler Delk to design it, and the result borrowed heavily from Spain. Its Baroque Revival and Moorish Revival buildings echo the architecture of Seville, spread across a 14-block area with 40 statues, fountains and sculptures and 12 towers. The tallest building is a half-sized reproduction of Seville's Giralda Tower. Reproductions of San Francisco's Path of Gold streetlights line the streets, and a fountain designed by Henri-Léon Gréber shows four horses rearing on their hind legs. Other works celebrate the classics, nature and American themes such as westward expansion. For all its Spanish airs, there is one thing the Plaza lacks: despite the name, it does not include a traditional open plaza. It has nonetheless earned a place on the Project for Public Spaces' list of 60 of the World's Great Places.

## Designed Around the Automobile

Nichols named the Plaza for his Country Club District, the neighborhood he developed around the Kansas City Country Club, now Loose Park. He set it at the northern end of Ward Parkway, the boulevard whose wide, manicured median is lined with fountains and statuary, so residents had a direct route to the shops. The innovations ran deeper than scenery. The Plaza was the first regional shopping center designed with parking for automobile access, the first suburban center with common management and tenant selection, and the first with unified architecture. Its cars, however, were kept out of sight, tucked into multilevel garages beneath and behind the shops or onto rooftops rather than spread across open lots.

## A Legacy in Two Colors

One of Nichols's ideas became an industry standard. The Plaza was the first to use the percentage lease, with rents based on a share of tenants' gross receipts - a novelty when Nichols invented it, and standard practice today. New Urbanist developer Andres Duany has observed that the Plaza has had the longest life of any planned shopping center in history. His legacy has a darker side as well. Nichols is held responsible for redlining and for residential covenants that kept Black residents, Jewish residents and other marginalized people from buying homes and living in the more desirable areas of Kansas City. An honest account of the Plaza has to hold both truths at once: an influential piece of American urban design, and a developer whose influence shut many of his fellow citizens out.

## Flood, Fire and Reinvention

For its first four decades the Plaza mixed higher-end shops such as Harzfeld's with Sears and Woolworth's, along with a bowling alley, a movie theater and a grocery store for the neighborhood's daily needs. Around 1970, competition from newer suburban malls pushed management upmarket, bringing in luxury hotels such as the Ritz-Carlton and retailers like Gucci and Saks Fifth Avenue. On September 12, 1977, Brush Creek flooded, causing severe damage and a number of deaths; the vast renovation that followed helped the district thrive. Tragedy struck again on February 19, 2013, when a contractor doing underground work struck a gas line and an explosion destroyed JJ's Restaurant, leaving one person dead and more than a dozen injured. Ownership changed hands too: the J.C. Nichols Company merged with Highwoods Properties in 1998, Highwoods agreed in 2016 to sell to a joint venture of Taubman Centers and Macerich for $660 million, and HP Village Management completed its purchase of the Plaza in late June 2024.

## Sixteen Bulbs Over a Doorway

The Plaza's most beloved ritual began modestly. In 1925, a single strand of 16 colored lights went up above a doorway to celebrate Christmas. The display grew every year, and in 1930 it inspired an official lighting ceremony. Now, on Thanksgiving night, tens of thousands of people gather for performances and to watch a special guest flip the switch, a moment carried live on local television station KMBC. The lights then shine overnight through mid-January - the "Season of Lights," one of the city's oldest traditions.

## Flight Context

Country Club Plaza is centered near 39.0413 N, 94.5918 W, about four miles south of the downtown Kansas City skyline. From 2,500-3,500 ft MSL, look for Brush Creek along the Plaza's southern edge and the half-scale Giralda Tower, the district's tallest building; Ward Parkway's landscaped median runs off from its southern side. The Kansas state line is about one mile west. Charles B. Wheeler Downtown Airport (KMKC) is about 5 nm north; Kansas City International (KMCI) is about 16 nm north-northwest, and Johnson County Executive Airport (KOJC) is about 13.5 nm south-southwest. Busy controlled airspace covers the metro area. From Thanksgiving night through mid-January, the holiday lights make the district unusually easy to spot after dark.
