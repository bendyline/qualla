# Hannibal Bridge

Opened on July 3, 1869, as the first permanent rail crossing of the Missouri River, the Hannibal Bridge turned a river town called the City of Kansas into a rail center - and was built by an engineer who would later help launch the age of flight.

**Location:** 39.1127, -94.5886

## Introduction

On July 3, 1869, a crowd of some 40,000 people gathered on the banks of the Missouri to celebrate a bridge. The Hannibal Bridge had just become the first permanent rail crossing of the Missouri River, and the town that won it - then known as the City of Kansas - would never be the same. Trains could now roll straight across the river that had long been a barrier to the west. This single span helped establish the river town as a major city and rail center.

## The Prize on the River

Rival river towns wanted the bridge, and the fight to land it was short but consequential. The Hannibal & St. Joseph Railroad needed a crossing of the Missouri, and Leavenworth, Kansas, competed with the City of Kansas for the honor. When the City of Kansas prevailed, Leavenworth found itself on the wrong side of the tracks, so to speak, while its rival was suddenly on the map. The City of Kansas was renamed Kansas City, Missouri, in 1889, but by then its future had already been set in motion. In its early days, the new crossing was simply called the Kansas City Bridge. The name Hannibal came from the railroad that commissioned it, and it stuck long after the original structure was gone.

## Taming the Missouri

Construction began in 1867, shortly after the end of the Civil War, and the Keystone Bridge Company built the crossing for the Hannibal & St. Joseph Railroad. The river fought back. Its shifting bottom and strong current posed a daunting challenge to Octave Chanute, the engineer in charge, who designed and built the bridge with Joseph Tomlinson and George S. Morison. The finished bridge stretched nearly a quarter mile across the Missouri on seven deep-sunk piers. Near the middle sat a swing span with an arched truss, which could pivot open in under two minutes to let steamboats pass. For Morison, the project was an apprenticeship under Tomlinson and Chanute, and he went on to become one of the leading bridge designers in North America.

## Rails, Depot and a Future in the Air

The bridge worked exactly as its backers hoped. Train traffic through the area increased, and all those passengers and all that freight demanded a proper station. The result was Union Depot, the predecessor to today's Kansas City Union Station. The bridge had become the hinge on which the city's early growth turned. Its chief engineer carried an even longer legacy. Octave Chanute, the man who had bridged the Missouri, later turned his attention from bridges to flight, becoming one of the early pioneers of aviation and a supporter of the Wright brothers. For pilots tracing the river today, there is something fitting in that: the bridge that opened Kansas City to the West was built by a man who would help open the sky.

## Wind, Rebuilding and a Successor

In 1886, a tornado struck the bridge and collapsed one of its middle spans. The Hannibal Bridge was rebuilt, but not quite as it had been; its arched truss gave way to a more traditional truss design. It carried on for three more decades before being replaced by the Second Hannibal Bridge, built in virtually the same location. The new crossing sat about 200 feet upstream on the northern bank, but on the southern bank it met the same spot as its predecessor, where the tracks enter the gooseneck cut into the bluff. That shared landing in the bluff tied the successor to 1869, the year the Missouri River stopped being a wall and became a gateway.

## Flight Context

The Hannibal Bridge crossed the Missouri River at 39.113°N, 94.589°W, linking downtown Kansas City, Missouri, on the south bank with the Clay County bottoms on the north. Its successor, the Second Hannibal Bridge, crosses at nearly the same spot near the Broadway Bridge, just north of the River Market district and the bluffs below downtown. Charles B. Wheeler Downtown Airport (KMKC) lies less than a mile north across the river, so the bridge sits under its Class D airspace and near its approach paths; coordinate with the tower before loitering. From 1,500-2,500 feet AGL on a legal path, look for the rail bridge where tracks curve up into a cut in the south-bank bluff. Kansas City International (KMCI) is about 12 nm north-northwest. The river valley often holds morning fog in autumn; midday light gives the clearest view of the bridges.
