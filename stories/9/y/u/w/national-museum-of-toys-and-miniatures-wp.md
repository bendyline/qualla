# National Museum of Toys and Miniatures

Born from the collections of two lifelong friends, Kansas City's National Museum of Toys and Miniatures bills itself as holding the world's largest collection of fine-scale miniatures, inside a doctor's early-1900s home on the UMKC campus.

## Introduction

At one-twelfth scale, a foot shrinks to an inch, and an entire drawing room can sit comfortably on a tabletop. On the campus of the University of Missouri-Kansas City, in a museum anchored by an early-twentieth-century house, that tiny arithmetic plays out in room after room. The National Museum of Toys and Miniatures describes its holdings as the world's largest collection of fine-scale miniatures and one of the nation's largest collections of antique toys on public display - more than 100,000 objects in all, and the whole enterprise began with the friendship of two women who loved small things.

## Two Friends, Two Passions

Mary Harris Francis collected toys. Barbara Hall Marshall collected fine miniatures. The two were avid collectors and lifelong friends, and when the museum opened in 1982, it brought their collections together under one roof. The pairing gave the institution its dual identity from the start. On one side sat the playthings of childhood, objects made to be handled, loved and sometimes worn out. On the other sat miniatures made with painstaking precision, made as small works of art rather than playthings. Together they turned a private enthusiasm shared between friends into a public collection, open to anyone curious enough to lean in close.

## A Doctor's House on Campus

The museum's home has a history of its own. The house was designed in 1906 by the noted architect John McKecknie for a physician, Herbert Tureman, and it was completed by 1911. In the 1960s it was bequeathed to the University of Missouri-Kansas City, and it later became the setting for the museum. There is a pleasing symmetry in the arrangement. A substantial family residence, built for real rooms and real furniture, now anchors a museum of rooms and furniture reduced to a fraction of their size. The contrast between the two scales is part of the pleasure. Today the museum shares the university's campus, and the house has belonged to the university since that bequest more than half a century ago.

## A World at One-Twelfth

Fine-scale miniatures are a discipline, not a novelty. The museum's collection spans architectural works and room settings, fine and decorative art, tools, equipment and figures, all made to scale - most commonly 1:12, where one inch represents one foot. At that ratio, every detail has to be shrunk faithfully, and the best pieces reward long, close looking. The museum bills its holdings in this field as the largest in the world. Alongside them, the antique toy collection ranks among the nation's largest on public display. Spread across about 33,000 square feet, the collections invite visitors to lean in, squint and marvel at how much can fit into so little.

## Going National

The museum has not stood still. Once known as the Toy and Miniature Museum of Kansas City, it added the word "National" to its name, a nod to ambitions that reach well beyond the city. It also underwent an $8 million renovation and reopened on August 1, 2015. Today it welcomes about 30,000 visitors a year. Some visitors find the toys of their own childhoods behind glass; others linger over the craftsmanship of the miniatures. Either way, the experience rests on the same foundation laid in 1982: two friends who believed that small things deserved serious attention. In a museum devoted to the very small, that idea has proved to be anything but.
