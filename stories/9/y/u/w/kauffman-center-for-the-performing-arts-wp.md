# Kauffman Center for the Performing Arts

Sketched on a dinner napkin by Moshe Safdie and built without taxpayer funds, Kansas City's Kauffman Center pairs an opera house and a concert hall inside two shells of arches behind a wall of glass.

**Location:** 39.0937, -94.5868

**Categories:** Performing arts centers in Missouri, Moshe Safdie buildings, Culture of Kansas City, Missouri, Music venues completed in 2011, Crossroads, Kansas City

## Introduction

The first drawing of Kansas City's great performing arts center was made on a napkin. Architect Moshe Safdie had just come to town to see the site, and over dinner with Julia Irene Kauffman he sketched an idea for the building. That scribble grew into the Kauffman Center for the Performing Arts, two stages wrapped in shells of concentric arches at 16th and Broadway, opened in 2011 and now home to the city's ballet, opera and symphony.

## A Mother's Idea, a Daughter's Project

The vision belonged to Muriel Kauffman, who first talked about a performing arts center with her family and the community in 1994. She died the following year, and her daughter Julia Irene Kauffman, chairman of the Muriel McBrien Kauffman Foundation, took up the cause. A feasibility study in 1997 gave the board a practical footing, and in 1999 the foundation bought an 18.5-acre plot just south of the central business district. By 2000 the search for an architect had narrowed to four candidates, and the board chose Safdie, the modernist behind Habitat 67 in Montreal, Marina Bay Sands in Singapore and Crystal Bridges Museum of American Art in Arkansas. He presented his plan in May 2002, and ground was broken on October 6, 2006. Unlike some civic projects, the center itself was built without taxpayer funds, though the city financed the adjacent garage, and it still operates as a non-profit. Until it opened, the three resident companies had performed at the Lyric Theatre, eight blocks to the north.

## Arches and a Wall of Glass

From outside, the building reads as two symmetrical half shells made of vertical, concentric arches, both opening toward the south. Each shell holds its own acoustically independent hall, though they share a backstage. The south façade is made entirely of glass, which Safdie describes as "an expansive glazed porch contained by a glass tent-like structure." Inside it is Brandmeyer Great Hall, the white lobby that links the two venues through a series of stacked, open balconies. On performance nights the effect runs both ways. People inside look out on the city, while people outside see a kind of terrarium, thousands of concertgoers backlit against the white interior and visible to one another across the open balconies.

## Red Seats and Gold Light

The 1,800-seat Muriel Kauffman Theatre takes its cues from the great European opera houses. Multiple balconies and boxes line the sides, putting audiences closer to the stage than in most auditoriums, and the balustrades glimmer with gold lighting that dims as the performance begins. Seats come in various shades of red, and the undulating walls carry a brightly colored mural designed and painted by students at the Kansas City Art Institute under Safdie's guidance. The theater has a 5,000-square-foot stage, an orchestra pit for up to 90 musicians and a 74-foot fly tower, all at the service of the Kansas City Ballet and the Lyric Opera of Kansas City. Opera-goers do not have to crane their necks for translations, either: a seatback system displays subtitles in various languages on the backs of the chairs.

## A Hall Built Like an Embrace

Across the lobby, the Kansas City Symphony plays in Helzberg Hall, a 1,600-seat oval. The stage reaches about a third of the way into the room, and vineyard-style seating surrounds it on all four sides, so even the farthest seat is only about 100 feet away. "From the outset," Safdie has explained, "we wanted a hall that was intimate and in which the public is engaged with the musicians in a feeling of embrace." Motorized risers in the stage lie flat or rise into tiers as a performance requires. The hall also houses a pipe organ by Casavant Frères of Saint-Hyacinthe, Quebec, with 79 stops, 102 ranks and 5,548 pipes, fewer than a tenth of them visible. The largest pipe is 32 feet tall and weighs about 960 pounds. After two months of installation and two more of tuning, the organ was dedicated on March 10, 2012, with a concert by James David Christie.

## A Lawn on a Parking Garage

Even the lawn has a story. The city-owned Arts District garage on the center's south side, a $47 million project paid for with bonds, doubles as the building's landscaping. Its green roof took 300,000 pieces of high-density foam, 3,000 tons of sand mixture and 100,000 square feet of sod, planted with fescue and a low-water-use grass called Reveille by local landscape architects Jeffrey L. Bruce & Company. Down in the garage, the city's One Percent for Art ordinance produced "Terpsichore for Kansas City," named for the Muse of choral song and dance: original music plays from ceiling speakers while a four-story "light organ" of seven acrylic tubes glows with LEDs in time to the sound.

## Flight Context

The Kauffman Center stands at 16th and Broadway on the south edge of downtown Kansas City, Missouri, at about 39.0937°N, 94.5868°W, near the Crossroads Arts District and the Power & Light District. From 2,500 to 3,000 feet AGL, look for the two ribbed shells and the green lawn beside them against the downtown skyline. Charles B. Wheeler Downtown Airport (KMKC) is under 2 nm to the north, so expect close-in traffic. Kansas City International Airport (KMCI), with scheduled airline service, is about 14 nm to the north-northwest.
