# West Bottoms

At the meeting of the Missouri and Kansas rivers, Kansas City's West Bottoms grew from a French trading post into a stockyard empire, a political machine's birthplace and a wartime shipyard, and now fills its brick warehouses with antiques and haunted houses.

**Location:** 39.1028, -94.6034

**Categories:** Neighborhoods in Kansas City, Missouri, Economy of Kansas City, Missouri, Pendergast era, Stockyards, Industrial history

## Introduction

During World War II, a steel plant on the edge of Kansas City turned out landing craft for amphibious invasions at the rate of one a day. Darby Steel Corporation then floated its landing craft tanks more than 1,000 miles down the Missouri and Mississippi rivers to New Orleans, and the vessels earned a fitting nickname: "Prairie Ships." Its plant at the mouth of the Kansas River could hold eight LCTs and 16 LCMs in various stages of construction. It was one more improbable chapter for the West Bottoms, the low-lying district where the Kansas River meets the Missouri.

## Where the Rivers Meet

The West Bottoms lies immediately west of downtown Kansas City, straddling the line between Kansas City, Missouri, and Kansas City, Kansas. Across the water is Kaw Point, an early campsite of the Lewis and Clark Expedition. Native tribes settled this ground first. In the early 1800s François Chouteau permanently settled the spot as part of the French Bottoms, trading with the tribes and with early American pioneers, which makes the district, along with Westport, one of the oldest parts of the metro. Next door, the Quality Hill neighborhood was a historical center of the pioneer Town of Kansas, which grew into Kansas City, Missouri.

## Cattle, Rails and Red Lights

The district was founded as a livestock and meatpacking center in 1871. It was home to the Kansas City Live Stock Exchange, the Kansas City Stockyards and the city's first Union railway depot. The stockyards alone covered more than two hundred acres, ringed by hotels, offices, shops and banks serving cattle buyers and cowboys. The work drew unskilled laborers - recent European immigrants and native-born white and African American workers - who settled in tenements and boarding houses. With so many railway passengers passing through each day, the area also became a red-light district of saloons, gambling dens and brothels. In the late 1800s and early 1900s, immigrants from Eastern and Central Europe were recruited to work in the meatpacking plants because of strikes by local workers. They first settled in the "Strawberry Patch" near the plants, and after the flood of 1903 founded the Strawberry Hill neighborhood uphill. Serbian immigrants founded St. George Serbian Orthodox Church on April 18, 1906, converting one of two houses on North 1st Street into a church and the other into a parish home; the parish stayed in the West Bottoms until 1925.

## The Saloon That Built a Machine

In 1876, James "Jim" Pendergast, the son of Irish immigrants who had settled in St. Joseph, Missouri, came to the West Bottoms looking for work. He lived in boarding houses and labored in meatpacking and then in several of the neighborhood's iron foundries. In 1881 he bought the American House saloon and hotel on St. Louis Avenue. It became a gambling den, an informal bank and a headquarters for political organizing by Jim and his brother Tom Pendergast, the architects of the Pendergast political machine that would control Kansas City for the next four decades.

## High Water

Low-lying and close to the Missouri River, the West Bottoms has always been prone to flooding. In 1903 a major flood damaged its businesses, shut down water and power in the city, and persuaded developers to choose a new location for the Union Station railway depot. In 1946 construction began on a flood wall to protect the meatpacking and livestock business, part of the Pick-Sloan Missouri Basin Program. It was not enough. The Great Flood of 1951 severely damaged the stockyards, and Kansas City's meatpacking industry never fully recovered. The district's arena, Kemper Arena, later hosted the 1976 Republican National Convention.

## Brick, Antiques and Ghosts

What the floods and the decline of meatpacking left behind was a district of brick high-rise industrial buildings, built in the early 1900s for stockyards, train yards and factories. Most have since been converted into art galleries, restaurants, shops, apartments and corporate offices. The Kansas City Livestock Exchange Building still stands among them, near the arena now known as Hy-Vee Arena. Today the West Bottoms draws crowds for its antique shops and its haunted house attractions, which have become very popular - a lively afterlife for a neighborhood built on livestock.

## Flight Context

The West Bottoms centers near 39.1028 N, 94.6034 W, on the floodplain at the confluence of the Kansas and Missouri rivers, immediately west of downtown Kansas City, Missouri, and below the Quality Hill bluff. From 2,000-3,000 ft MSL, the river junction and Kaw Point make an unmistakable reference, with the warehouse district and Hy-Vee Arena spread across the flat land between the rivers and the bluff. Charles B. Wheeler Downtown Airport (KMKC) is only about 1.3 nm north-northeast, just across the Missouri River, so expect busy controlled airspace; Kansas City International (KMCI) is about 13 nm north-northwest. River fog and haze can reduce visibility over the low ground.
