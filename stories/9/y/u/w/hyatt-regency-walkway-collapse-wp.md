# Hyatt Regency walkway collapse

On July 17, 1981, two suspended walkways fell into a crowded tea dance at Kansas City's Hyatt Regency, killing 114 people - a disaster traced to a single, unchecked change in how the walkways were hung.

**Location:** 39.0850, -94.5800

**Categories:** 1981 disasters in the United States, Building and structure collapses in the United States, Engineering failures, 1980s in Kansas City, Missouri, Crown Center

## Introduction

On the evening of Friday, July 17, 1981, about 1,600 people filled the soaring atrium of Kansas City's new Hyatt Regency for a tea dance. Above them, suspended from the ceiling, walkways of steel, glass and concrete crossed the lobby at the second, third and fourth floors. Around 7:05 p.m., about 40 people stood on the second-level walkway, more on the third, and another 16 to 20 on the fourth, which hung directly above the second. Then the fourth-floor walkway gave way and fell onto the second, and both crashed into the crowd below. One hundred and fourteen people were killed and more than 200 were injured.

## A Hotel Built in a Hurry

Construction on the hotel began in May 1978, in what The Kansas City Star later described as a climate of "high unemployment, inflation and double-digit interest rates" that pressured builders to win contracts and finish quickly. The newspaper called the project fast-tracked. There were numerous delays and setbacks, including the collapse of 2,700 square feet of the roof during construction. Nor was the city a stranger to structural failure: the Star noted that "notable structures around the country were failing at an alarming rate," among them the 1979 roof collapse at Kansas City's own Kemper Arena. The lobby was the hotel's defining feature: a multi-story atrium spanned by walkways about 120 feet long, linking the north and south wings.

## Fourteen Hours in the Lobby

The rescue lasted 14 hours, directed by Kansas City emergency medical director Joseph Waeckerle. Survivors lay trapped beneath many tons of steel, concrete and glass that the fire department's jacks could not move. Answering an appeal, volunteers arrived from construction companies and suppliers with jacks, compressors, jackhammers, concrete saws and generators, and they forced crane booms through the lobby windows to lift debris. "They said 'take what you want'," Deputy Fire Chief Arnett Williams recalled. "No one has ever asked for an accounting and no one has ever submitted a bill." The hotel's driveway and lawn became a triage area, and hundreds of people lined up at blood centers. Water from the ruptured sprinkler system flooded the lobby, threatening to drown people still trapped. Twenty-nine people were rescued from the rubble. The last, Mark Williams, spent more than nine hours pinned beneath the lower walkway with both legs dislocated, and nearly drowned before the water was shut off.

## One Rod Became Two

The Kansas City Star hired architectural engineer Wayne G. Lischka and the engineering firm Simpson, Gumpertz and Heger to investigate, and Lischka discovered that the walkways had not been built as originally designed. The design by Jack D. Gillum and Associates called for continuous rods running from the ceiling, through the fourth-floor walkway's beams, down to the second-floor walkway - a design that itself supported only 60 percent of the minimum load required by Kansas City's building codes. Havens Steel Company, which made the rods, objected that the whole rod below the fourth floor would have to be threaded, and proposed two separate, offset sets of rods instead: one hanging the fourth-floor walkway from the ceiling, the other hanging the second-floor walkway from the fourth. That change doubled the load on the fourth-floor beams. Those box beams split along their welds, the supporting nuts slipped through, and the walkways fell in a cascading failure. Investigators concluded that the structure would have failed under one-third of the weight it held that night.

## Who Answered for It

Investigators traced the failure to poor communication between the two firms. Gillum's drawings were preliminary sketches that Havens treated as final; Gillum's firm failed to review the design thoroughly, and its engineer accepted the change in a phone call without the calculations that would have revealed the flaw. Edward Pfrang, lead investigator for the National Bureau of Standards, described the project's culture as "everyone wanting to walk away from responsibility." The Missouri Board of Architects, Professional Engineers and Land Surveyors found the engineers who had approved the final drawings culpable of gross negligence, misconduct and unprofessional conduct; the firm lost its engineering licenses in Missouri, Kansas and Texas and its membership in the American Society of Civil Engineers. Gillum later claimed full responsibility and became a lecturer on engineering disasters. The Star and the Kansas City Times won a 1982 Pulitzer Prize for their 16 months of investigative coverage.

## What Kansas City Carries

Every defendant, including Hallmark Cards and Crown Center Corporation, denied legal liability. One lawsuit yielded $10 million, including $6.5 million for charitable and civic causes that Hallmark called a "healing gesture." In 1983, local authorities reported that a $5 million reconstruction had made the hotel "possibly the safest in the country." The collapse was the deadliest accidental structural failure since the Pemberton Mill collapse more than 120 years earlier. It remains a case study for engineering ethics students and for emergency responders learning the "all-hazards approach." On November 12, 2015, the Skywalk Memorial Foundation, established for the victims, dedicated a memorial in Hospital Hill Park.

## Flight Context

The former Hyatt Regency, now operating as the Sheraton Kansas City Hotel at Crown Center, stands at approximately 39.085 N, 94.58 W, south of downtown Kansas City. From 2,500-3,500 ft MSL, the hotel tower is a prominent landmark rising south of the downtown skyline. Charles B. Wheeler Downtown Airport (KMKC) is about 2.4 nm north-northwest across the Missouri River; Kansas City International (KMCI) is about 14 nm north-northwest. The city lies beneath busy controlled airspace, and summer haze and thunderstorms can limit visibility.
