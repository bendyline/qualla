# Fort de Cavagnial

France's westernmost Missouri River outpost traded with the Kansa for two decades, was ordered torn down in 1764, and still eludes every archaeologist who has gone looking for it.

**Location:** 39.1067, -94.6764

**Categories:** Kansas City metropolitan area, Forts in Missouri, Forts in Kansas, Fort Leavenworth, French forts in the United States, Colonial forts in Missouri, Colonial forts in Kansas, 1744 establishments in the French colonial empire

## Introduction

In July 1804, the account of Lewis and Clark's passage noted that the site could still be recognized: the remains of chimneys, the rough outline of a fortification, and a fine spring that had once supplied it with water. Today nobody can find it at all. Fort de Cavagnial, the French outpost that traded with the Kansa on the Missouri River for twenty years, has been searched for by helicopter, by aerial photograph and on foot, and its exact location remains one of the stubborn historical and archaeological mysteries of Kansas. What survives is a paper trail of commandants, traders and orders - and the tantalizing sense that the fort is still out there somewhere.

## A Governor's Name on the Missouri

The French established the post in 1744 on the western cliffs of the Missouri River, north of present-day Kansas City, and named it for the governor of Louisiana, the Marquis de Vaudreuil-Cavagnial. Its first commandant was François Coulon de Villiers, a member of a prominent New France military family. The fort was both a military outpost and a place of business. From January 1, 1745, to May 20, 1750, Joseph Athanase Trottier dit Desruisseaux held a monopoly on trade along the Missouri, and the site was chosen with commerce in mind. The Kansa people, who were described as a dependable source of high-grade furs, lived nearby, and most accounts place the fort close to a major Kansa village. For the French, that relationship sat at the heart of the post's purpose.

## Stockade and Bastions

The Kansas Historical Society describes the fort as small but substantial, enclosed by a stockade of stout piles with a bastion at each of its four corners. Inside stood a commandant's house, a guardhouse, a powder house, a trader's house and quarters for employees, built mostly of logs and mud. It was a frontier compound rather than a stone citadel. A succession of officers took command after Coulon de Villiers: Augustin-Antoine de la Barre from 1750 to 1751, Louis Robineau de Portneuf from 1751 until about 1754, and Jean Francois Tisserant de Moncharvaux from about 1754 until around 1758. Each inherited the same task of holding a remote post on the far edge of France's colonial claims.

## Orders to Throw It Down

The fort's fortunes turned with the French and Indian War, fought from 1754 to 1763. As the war ended, the region passed under Spanish control, though French soldiers lingered at the post until 1764. That year Pierre-Joseph Neyon de Villiers, acting on behalf of Jean-Jacques Blaise d'Abbadie, the director-general of the Colony of Louisiana, ordered the troops to "throw down" the outpost and retreat to New Orleans. With that order, the French garrison's time on this stretch of the Missouri came to an end. The buildings were not wiped away all at once, however. Traces of the fort lingered long enough to catch the eye of the next generation of explorers pushing up the river.

## Chimneys and a Fine Spring

On July 2, 1804, Lewis and Clark passed the site and wrote that "the French formerly had a fort at this place, to protect the trade of this nation." Clark added that the situation appeared to be "a verry elligable one for a Town," and the narrative later published from the expedition's journals noted that the spot could be recognized by some remains of chimneys, the general outline of the fortification and the fine spring that supplied it with water. Fifteen years later, in 1819, members of Stephen Harriman Long's Yellowstone expedition described the ruins as lying "a few miles below Isle au Vache." Such glimpses did not last. By the middle of the nineteenth century, every visible trace had disappeared.

## The Fort Nobody Can Find

Even the basic question of where the fort stood has never been settled. British reports placed it below the confluence of the Kansas and Missouri rivers, in what became Kansas City, Missouri. Most sources, however, put it on the bluffs above the meeting of Salt Creek and the Missouri, just north of Fort Leavenworth. For decades, searchers have flown over that area in a helicopter, pored over aerial photographs, driven across it and walked practically every square foot without finding a definite trace. A 1987 account suggested that possible foundations had been located through mapping work, but no archaeological confirmation has followed. In 2004, the Kansas Historical Society summed it up: the fort's exact location "remains as one of the active historical and archeological mysteries in Kansas."

## Flight Context

The mapped point for Fort de Cavagnial is 39.107°N, 94.676°W in Kansas City, Kansas, a few miles west of where the Kansas River meets the Missouri, but most accounts place the fort roughly 20 miles to the northwest, on the bluffs above the Salt Creek-Missouri River confluence just north of Fort Leavenworth. From 3,000-4,000 feet AGL, follow the winding Missouri River northwest from downtown Kansas City toward Leavenworth; the wooded bluffs on the Kansas side mark the zone where the fort most likely stood. Charles B. Wheeler Downtown Airport (KMKC) is about 4 nm east-northeast of the mapped point, Kansas City International (KMCI) roughly 12 nm north, and Sherman Army Airfield (KFLV) at Fort Leavenworth lies near the likely site. Clear winter days, when the trees are bare, give the best look at the bluff lines.
