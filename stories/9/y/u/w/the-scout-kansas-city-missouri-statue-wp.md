# The Scout (Kansas City, Missouri statue)

Cyrus Dallin's gold-medal bronze of a Sioux rider was only passing through Kansas City in 1915, until the city's schoolchildren joined a drive to raise its $15,000 price, in part with nickels and dimes.

## Introduction

Kansas City's schoolchildren helped buy a statue with their pocket change. The Scout, Cyrus E. Dallin's bronze of a Sioux man on horseback, was only supposed to be passing through town, a temporary guest in Penn Valley Park on its way back east from a world's fair. But the city fell for it. Through a citywide drive that included a children's campaign called "The Kids of Kansas City," residents raised the $15,000 asking price, part of it in schoolchildren's nickels and dimes, to make it stay. More than a century later, the rider still sits astride his horse south of downtown, gazing out over a landscape transformed.

## Gold in San Francisco

Dallin conceived The Scout in 1910. The finished sculpture stands well over 10 feet tall and depicts a Sioux rider pausing on horseback to survey the land before him - a moment of stillness and watchfulness captured in bronze. In 1915, the statue went on display at the Panama-Pacific International Exposition in San Francisco, the great fair of that year, where it won a gold medal. Its journey afterward is what brought it to Missouri. On the way back east, the statue was installed on a temporary basis in Kansas City's Penn Valley Park. It was meant to be a brief stop. Instead, the city that hosted it decided it could not let the rider go.

## Nickels, Dimes and The Kids of Kansas City

The statue proved so popular that residents set out to buy it for its $15,000 asking price. Seventeen area organizations led the drive, and under the banner "The Kids of Kansas City," local teachers worked with area schoolchildren to collect small donations, nickel by nickel and dime by dime. The goal was met after a donor, Ida Nelson, offered $1,000 if the rest could be raised within two months. It was a civic effort in which the smallest contributions mattered. In 1922 The Scout was dedicated as a permanent memorial to local Native American tribes. There is a complexity in that dedication worth noticing: the memorial to local tribes takes the form of a Sioux rider, from a nation whose homeland lay far to the north. Still, for a century the statue has stood as the city's tribute to the Native peoples of the region.

## A Name the City Borrowed

The city has borrowed the statue's name more than once. Kansas City Scout, the metropolitan area's electronic traffic alert system, takes its name from the statue, and so did the National Hockey League's Kansas City Scouts, whose logo featured the famous rider. The statue has traveled, too, in a sense. In 1992, Kansas City gave a half-size replica to its sister city, Seville, Spain, sending a small echo of the Penn Valley rider across the Atlantic. At home, the original remains in Penn Valley Park, east of Southwest Trafficway and south of downtown, where it has stood since the city decided it was worth every coin. The Scout still surveys the land, and the land he surveys has become a skyline.

## Flight Context

The Scout stands at 39.076°N, 94.593°W in Penn Valley Park, just east of Southwest Trafficway and south of downtown Kansas City, Missouri. From 1,500-2,500 feet AGL, find the green expanse of Penn Valley Park south of downtown's towers and west of the Crown Center district; the tall Liberty Memorial tower of the National WWI Museum, also in the park, stands only about half a mile to the northeast and makes an easy reference. Union Station lies just north of the memorial. Charles B. Wheeler Downtown Airport (KMKC) is about 3 nm north across the Missouri River, so this area sits close to its Class D airspace; Kansas City International (KMCI) is roughly 14 nm north-northwest. Clear spring and autumn days give the sharpest views of the park and skyline.
