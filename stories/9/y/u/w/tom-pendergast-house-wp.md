# Tom Pendergast House

Behind the French Provincial facade at 5650 Ward Parkway lived Tom Pendergast, the boss whose machine kept Kansas City wide open, built its skyline and ended with a federal prison sentence.

**Location:** 39.0251, -94.6033

**Categories:** Houses in Kansas City, Missouri

## Introduction

From the curb, 5650 Ward Parkway looks like the home of a prosperous banker, a gracious French Provincial house on one of Kansas City's most elegant boulevards. It belonged instead to the boss. Tom Pendergast ran the political machine that controlled Kansas City through the 1920s and 1930s, a machine that bent laws, bought loyalty and reshaped the city's skyline. His family moved into this house when it was completed in 1927, and it remained home until his death in 1945. In between, it became a symbol of both the power of machine politics and its spectacular fall.

## A Boss in the Country Club District

The house sits in the Country Club District, and its design came from within the very company that shaped that neighborhood: Edward Tanner, an architect with the J.C. Nichols Company, drew up a modified version of the French Provincial style. Completed in 1927, it gave the Pendergasts a residence of polish and restraint, a world away in tone from the back-room business of machine politics. Members of the family lived there from its completion until Tom Pendergast died in 1945. The address became one of the best known in Kansas City, and the reason was never the architecture. It was the man who lived there.

## Vice and Concrete

Pendergast's machine is remembered above all for corruption. During the 1920s and 1930s, it bribed police and city leaders to look the other way on alcohol and gambling laws, and the result was wide-open access to drink and gambling. That openness had an unexpected cultural legacy: it played a major role in the birth of Kansas City jazz. The machine also backed a young politician named Harry S. Truman. Yet the Pendergast era was not only about vice. It also brought large-scale development projects that still stand in downtown Kansas City. Civic buildings such as the Jackson County Courthouse rose under a Depression-era bond program of about $40 million, and the machine is credited with having a hand in Municipal Auditorium, Kansas City City Hall and private landmarks such as the Fidelity Bank and Trust Building and the Kansas City Power and Light Building. These buildings gave the city a skyline of real ambition. They also complicate the story. The same machine that paid off the police is tied to some of Kansas City's landmark buildings, and both legacies are still visible downtown.

## The Fall of the Boss

The machine's enemies eventually closed in. Missouri governor Lloyd C. Stark and President Franklin D. Roosevelt chipped away at Pendergast's power and investigated his dealings. In May 1939, Pendergast pleaded guilty to income tax evasion and was sentenced to fifteen months in the federal penitentiary at Leavenworth, along with a $10,000 fine. For a man who had held a city in his grip, it was a stunning collapse. After serving his sentence, he returned to Ward Parkway and lived quietly in the house until his death in 1945. That is why the home carries such weight today. More than a handsome residence, it came to represent the corruption and downfall of one of the best-known political machines in U.S. history.

## Flight Context

The Tom Pendergast House stands at 39.025°N, 94.603°W at 5650 Ward Parkway in Kansas City's Country Club District, just east of the Missouri-Kansas state line. From 2,000-3,000 feet AGL, trace Ward Parkway, the broad boulevard with a landscaped median lined by large homes, south from Brush Creek and the Country Club Plaza, which lies about a mile and a quarter to the north-northeast. Loose Park's open green is a short distance to the east. Charles B. Wheeler Downtown Airport (KMKC) is about 6 nm north, Johnson County Executive (KOJC) roughly 12 nm south-southwest, and Kansas City International (KMCI) about 17 nm north-northwest. This is busy airspace near the Kansas City Class B and KMKC's Class D. Spring and autumn bring the clearest views of the tree-lined parkway.
