# Huron Cemetery

Begun by Wyandot families burying their dead after removal from Ohio, this ridge-top cemetery in downtown Kansas City, Kansas, survived a century of development pressure thanks to three sisters, a senator and the descendants of those buried there.

**Location:** 39.1147, -94.6261

**Categories:** Native American cemeteries, Wyandotte Nation, Native American history of Kansas, National Historic Landmarks in Kansas, Geography of Kansas City, Kansas

## Introduction

In 1907, the Conley sisters marched to a small burial ground on a ridge in Kansas City, Kansas, threw up a one-room shack measuring six feet by eight, and moved in. Lyda, Helena and Ida Conley were Wyandot descendants who would lead a years-long fight for this ground, and at the cemetery they took turns standing guard with their father's musket beside "No trespassing" signs. The graves they defended held their own people. Today the site is formally called the Wyandot National Burying Ground, better known as the Huron Indian Cemetery, a National Historic Landmark in a downtown where developers eyed it for more than a century.

## Graves on the Ridge

The Wyandot, whom French explorers called the Huron, arrived here after the United States forced them from their homes in Ohio. Their migration began in July 1843, and they first reached the place where the Kaw River meets the Missouri. They stayed near Westport until the Delaware sold them thirty-six sections of land and gave them three more in memory of friendship, in what is now Wyandotte County. The new arrivals were living in a low-lying area, and epidemic illness swept through them. The survivors buried their dead on a ridge overlooking the Missouri River. That hillside became the Huron Cemetery, and when the United States granted the Wyandot land in Kansas, the ridge was part of it.

## Refuge, Citizenship and a Second Removal

In 1855 many Wyandot accepted allotments of communal land by household and became United States citizens, and the tribal government was dissolved. The cemetery stayed in communal hands, and in 1859 it ended up inside the newly incorporated Town of Wyandot, later annexed by Kansas City, Kansas. In the mid-1850s the Wyandot also approved the sale of land for Quindaro, founded in late 1856 by abolitionists as a free port on the Missouri River and named for the Wyandot wife of one of its founders. At Quindaro and on their own land, the Wyandot gave important refuge to people escaping slavery on the Underground Railroad, helping those who crossed the river from Missouri travel farther north. After the Civil War, in 1867, the federal government removed most tribal members who had not become citizens again, this time to 20,000 acres in Oklahoma. Because that Oklahoma nation kept its tribal institutions, its leaders held legal authority over the cemetery in Kansas.

## Three Sisters and a Senator

By the 1890s the ridge was prime land in a growing city, and developers negotiated with the Wyandotte Nation's leaders in Indian Territory. In 1906, at the Wyandotte Nation's request, Congress instructed the Secretary of the Interior to sell the site, with the proceeds going to the Wyandotte Nation and the remains to be moved to nearby Quindaro Cemetery. The Conley sisters fought back with a public campaign and a legal defense, joined by other local Wyandot descendants and by women's clubs. Lyda, who had graduated from the Kansas City School of Law in 1902, carried the case to the U.S. Supreme Court and argued it herself in January 1910, becoming the first Native American woman to argue a case before the Court. Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes wrote that he had weighed her arguments with care, but the Court ruled against her. Rescue came from Congress instead. In 1916, with the help of Kansas Senator Charles Curtis, a multiracial member of the Kaw tribe who would later become Vice President, legislation protected the cemetery as a park.

## A Century of Tug-of-War

Protection did not end the pressure. New development proposals surfaced roughly every decade, and the dispute increasingly ran between two groups of Wyandot: the federally recognized Wyandotte Nation, based in Oklahoma, which held legal authority over the site and wanted to sell it for redevelopment, and the much smaller Wyandot Nation of Kansas, incorporated in 1959, which wanted the burying ground preserved. The cemetery joined the National Register of Historic Places in 1971. The 1990 Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act gave lineal descendants a voice in the fate of graves, and when a Wyandotte Nation chief evaluated the site as a possible casino location in 1994, the Bureau of Indian Affairs noted that nothing could happen without the consent of descendants of those buried there. In 1998, after more than 100 years of disagreement, the two groups signed an agreement limiting the ground to religious, cultural and other activities compatible with a burial site.

## Four Hundred Graves, Few Marked

Scholars believe more than 400 and perhaps as many as 800 people are buried here, few of them under marked stones, and burials continued into the late 20th century. In 1991 the city installed more than 70 new grave markers, working with the tribe and archaeologists. Among those at rest are Silas Armstrong, chief of the Wyandotte Nation from 1858 until his death in 1865, and Hiram Northup, a banker who worked closely with the Wyandot. Lyda Conley is here too. She died on May 28, 1946, and was buried near her family in the cemetery she had spent decades defending. In December 2016 the ground she guarded became a National Historic Landmark.

## Flight Context

The cemetery sits in downtown Kansas City, Kansas, at North 7th Street Trafficway and Minnesota Avenue, about 39.1147°N, 94.6261°W, on a ridge overlooking the Missouri River. From 2,000 to 2,500 feet AGL, the Missouri River and the downtown Kansas City skyline are the landmarks. Charles B. Wheeler Downtown Airport (KMKC) is less than 2 nm to the east-northeast, so expect traffic close by. Kansas City International Airport (KMCI), with scheduled airline service, is about 12 nm to the north-northwest.
