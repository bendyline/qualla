# Kansas City Power and Light Building

Raised by an electric utility in 1931 to create jobs downtown, the Art Deco Power and Light Building was Missouri's tallest habitable structure for 45 years and still lights the Kansas City skyline - now as an apartment tower.

**Location:** 39.0975, -94.5847

**Categories:** Art Deco skyscrapers, Skyscrapers in Kansas City, Missouri, Residential skyscrapers in Missouri, National Register of Historic Places in Kansas City, Missouri, Downtown Kansas City

## Introduction

It seems only right that the tower an electric company built should be remembered for its light. Joseph F. Porter, president of Kansas City Power and Light and an Edison Pioneer, put up the Power and Light Building in downtown Kansas City in 1931 as a way to promote new jobs. The Art Deco skyscraper has anchored the skyline ever since, and its crowning lantern became a civic emblem, appearing on promotional materials and signage for the entertainment district that bears the building's name and even for Kansas City as a whole. After dark today, LED floodlights wash the tower in a rotating spectrum of colors.

## Porter's Tower

The building was designed by the Kansas City firm of Hoit, Price and Barnes, the same architects behind Municipal Auditorium and 909 Walnut. The tower rises 34 stories, with two more below grade, to a roof roughly 480 feet above the street. At the top sits an ornate Art Deco lantern whose prismatic glass panels conceal red-orange lights, and each recessed setback of the building also held multicolored flickering floodlights - a fitting display for a power company. Its height set a long-standing record. From 1931 until 1976, when One U.S. Bank Plaza was completed in St. Louis, the Power and Light Building was the tallest habitable structure in Missouri - a run of 45 years. Its east facade faces the Power & Light District, the entertainment neighborhood that borrowed the building's name.

## The Twin That Never Was

For years, Kansas City told itself a story about the building's blank west side. The original plans, the rumor went, had called for a twin tower on the lot immediately to the west, and only the Great Depression's effect on local real estate prices had kept the second tower from being built. In 2013 local architect Dan Hicks put the legend to the test. He reviewed the plans and interviewed Clarence Kivett, a well-known architect who had worked for Hoit, Price and Barnes when the building was designed, and the twin turned out never to have existed. The real explanation was practical. The west wall has no windows because it was meant to act as a firewall if another building were ever built right beside it, and because the elevator shafts run along that side.

## Emptying Out

Kansas City Power & Light itself left the building in 1991, though other tenants stayed on. In 2010 the city picked the area beside it as a potential site for a hotel and convention center, but only two developers submitted proposals, and in 2011 city leaders judged both lackluster and considered reopening the bidding for a different downtown location. The last tenant, the Kansas City architecture and planning firm BNIM, moved out on September 2, 2014, leaving the landmark, listed on the National Register of Historic Places since January 2002, without a single occupant.

## Lights On at Home

The emptiness did not last. In October 2014, work began to convert the tower into apartments, a project led by NorthPoint Development of Riverside. The completed conversion put 210 apartments inside the historic tower, with another 81 units wrapped around and built above a new 500-stall parking garage serving the building. The old lobby became an event space holding up to 500 guests. Where the power company once ran its business, people now live, and after dark the floodlights still cycle through their colors above the Power & Light District below.

## Flight Context

The Power and Light Building stands at approximately 39.0975 N, 94.5847 W in downtown Kansas City, Missouri. Its Art Deco tower and illuminated lantern top make it one of the easiest skyline landmarks to identify, especially at night when colored floodlights rotate across the crown; the Power & Light District lies along its east side. A good viewing altitude is 2,500-3,500 ft MSL; expect busy controlled airspace close to the downtown airport. Charles B. Wheeler Downtown Airport (KMKC) is about 1.6 nm north-northwest, just across the Missouri River, and Kansas City International (KMCI) is about 13 nm north-northwest. Summer haze and thunderstorms can obscure the skyline.
