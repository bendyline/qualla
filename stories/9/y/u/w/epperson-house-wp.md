# Epperson House

A 54-room Tudor-Gothic mansion on the UMKC campus, the Epperson House is wrapped in ghost stories - and in one murder legend that never happened.

**Location:** 39.0311, -94.5813

**Categories:** Houses in Kansas City, Missouri, Reportedly haunted locations in Missouri, University of Missouri campus, Houses completed in 1923, 1923 establishments in Missouri

## Introduction

The story most often told about the Epperson House is a murder. Grief-stricken after the death of a woman the family considered a daughter, the legend goes, Uriah Epperson suffered a breakdown, killed his wife and then himself inside the mansion, binding the whole family's souls to its halls. None of it happened. Epperson did not murder his wife or take his own life, and Mary Epperson survived him by twelve years. The real history of the house at 5200 Cherry Street, now part of the University of Missouri-Kansas City, is quieter and sadder, and it turns on an organ that the woman who designed it did not live to hear.

## Fifty-Four Rooms and a Tunnel

Uriah Spray Epperson was born in Indiana on December 22, 1861, and came to Kansas City at the age of six. He grew into a banker, industrialist and philanthropist, building a fortune in the insurance and meat-packing industries. For his home he hired Horace LaPierre, an eccentric French architect, and the result was monumental. Construction began in 1919 and finished in 1923 at a cost of $450,000. The four-story Tudor-Gothic structure held 54 rooms, including six bathrooms, elevators, a swimming pool, a billiard room and even a barbershop. A tunnel linked the east and west wings. At its heart was the house's most personal feature: a custom organ built for a loft above the living room.

## The Organist Who Never Played It

That organ belonged, in spirit, to Harriet Evelyn Barse. She was an organ student at the Kansas City Conservatory of Music, and Uriah and Mary Elizabeth Weaver Epperson brought her with them when they moved into the house. They called her their adopted daughter, though no legal adoption ever took place. Barse designed the custom organ herself. She never saw it finished. On December 20, 1922, soon after the move, she died of a perforated gall bladder at the age of 47. The instrument, built by the Reuter Organ Company of Lawrence, Kansas, was dedicated only after a respectful period of mourning, on Sunday, November 29, 1925. Local organist Powell McCullough Weaver - no relation to Mrs. Epperson - organized the dedication recital, joined by soprano Winifrede Repp Railey, heiress of the Duff & Repp Furniture Company.

## From Mansion to Dormitory

Uriah Epperson died in 1927, only four years after his great house was completed. In 1942 his widow donated the building to the university, which used it as a men's dormitory until 1956. The halls that had been built for an organ loft and a private barbershop filled for a time with students, then emptied again. Today the Epperson House stands vacant; in January 2025 UMKC announced a development team to turn it into a 14-room boutique hotel. An empty mansion full of other people's history is fertile ground for stories, and this one has gathered more than its share.

## What the Night Patrols Reported

The stories began in earnest in the 1970s, when UMKC students reported seeing Barse in an evening gown, as though dressed for a recital. Campus security guards reported strange lights and the sound of organ music at night. In 1978, weekend guards began hearing footsteps in the empty building. That same year, two officers on a regularly scheduled 2 a.m. patrol were turning lights on and off as they moved through the house when one light stayed on. One patrolman then reported seeing an arm in a blue suit coat reach out and claw at the switch before the arm and hand vanished into the darkness as the light went out. Some people took it for Epperson himself.

The best-known account came in May 1979. A patrol officer parked near the house felt another vehicle strike his car from behind and heard glass shatter. He got out and found no other car, no damage and no broken glass. His car, however, had moved eight inches, as a pair of skid marks showed.
