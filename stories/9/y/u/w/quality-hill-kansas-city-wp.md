# Quality Hill, Kansas City

On a 200-foot bluff where Lewis and Clark once stopped, Quality Hill rose as Kansas City's most fashionable address, fell into disrepair, and came back as one of the city's most celebrated urban renewals.

**Location:** 39.1049, -94.5921

**Categories:** Neighborhoods in Kansas City, Missouri, Historic districts on the National Register of Historic Places in Missouri, Works Progress Administration in Missouri, Lewis and Clark Expedition, 1857 establishments in Missouri

## Introduction

On September 15, 1806, heading home from the Pacific, the Lewis and Clark Expedition stopped on a high bluff above the meeting of the Kansas and Missouri rivers. Meriwether Lewis and William Clark climbed it, and Clark wrote in his journal that the hill appeared to have a "Commanding Situation for a fort." The bluff became Quality Hill, a neighborhood on the west side of downtown Kansas City, Missouri, that has been the city's most fashionable address, one of its most neglected, and finally one of its great comebacks.

## A Commanding Situation

Quality Hill sits on a 200-foot-high bluff overlooking the confluence of the rivers and the West Bottoms below, with the Kansas state line just half a mile away across the Bottoms. The Lewis and Clark stop is marked today at the north end of Case Park, at 8th and Jefferson streets, with signposts, a lookout point and a large bronze sculpture by Eugene Daub. Among its figures is York, the enslaved man who traveled with the expedition. It has been billed as the only monument in Missouri to include an enslaved person. Cyclists pass the spot too: the Kansas City Riverfront Bicycle Trail, a 20-mile route along the Missouri River around downtown, runs through the park and along the edge of the bluff.

## Before There Was a City

The hill's roots reach back to French Kansas City. At the northwest corner of 11th and Washington streets, a bilingual signpost put up by the Chouteau Society says the corner was the site of an early-18th-century French missionary church, the oldest structure known to have been built by Europeans in the region, from the era when French fur trappers traveled these rivers. The first recorded church on the spot was a log church built in 1835 under Father Benedict Roux; it became known as Chouteau's Church because so many of its parishioners were family and employees of François Chouteau, credited as Kansas City's founder and first permanent Euro-American settler. Today the Cathedral of the Immaculate Conception, built in 1882-1883 and known for its gold-covered dome, stands on the site and is among the oldest standing buildings on Quality Hill. Two blocks down Washington Street rises Grace and Holy Trinity Cathedral, whose parish dates to 1870. Quality Hill is often described as the oldest established residential area in the metro area to have been continuously inhabited.

## Goats and Rabbits

From its formal start under Kersey Coates in 1857 until shortly after the First World War, Quality Hill was the most fashionable and expensive neighborhood in Kansas City. The city's leaders of power and industry built large houses on its limestone bluffs, looking down on the West Bottoms, home to the industrial heart, the rail center and the famous stockyards. Politics took its imagery from that same geography. Jim Pendergast, a West Bottoms saloon owner, built a Democratic machine that came to hold nearly total power over the region's politics, and his brother Tom later succeeded him as boss. Their rival Joe Shannon's faction was nicknamed "the rabbits," tied to the old ways of the river lowlands in a town once called "Rabbitville." The Pendergast faction were "the goats," for their urge to climb out of the Bottoms.

## Planes Below the Bluff

The edge of the bluffs is parkland, today's West Terrace Park and Case Park, overlooking the Kansas City Downtown Airport. A statue of Jim Pendergast, erected after his death at Tom's behest and moved here in 1990, still stands there. Until Kansas City International Airport opened in 1972, the park was a popular place to watch planes take off, and the view explained a problem. Departing aircraft had to climb steeply to clear the 200-foot bluffs and Quality Hill's buildings, a difficulty that was one of the considerations behind building the new airport. In 1953 the American Hereford Association dedicated its new headquarters on the hill, with President Dwight D. Eisenhower presiding, and in October 1954 the hill gained another landmark when a fiberglass bull was raised on a 90-foot pylon at the building. Locals called it "Bob," for "bull on building." Some loved it and some thought it kitsch. A 2002 agreement moved it across I-35 to Mulkey Square.

## The Long Way Back

From the 1960s, downtown Kansas City lost population as the city's center shifted south. Quality Hill's elite moved to areas such as the Country Club Plaza and Sunset Hill, big houses were divided among several families, and the neighborhood fell into extreme disrepair. In the 1970s a developer began assembling parcels and promoting a mix of new building and historic renovation; his plan never went ahead, but it spurred investors to renovate loft buildings. In 1983 Kansas City's LCRA redevelopment agency made Quality Hill part of its downtown plan and chose McCormack Baron Salazar of St. Louis as master developer, and a consortium including the Hall Family Foundation, DST Systems and J.E. Dunn helped acquire nearly all the land. New buildings rose and old ones were restored. Listed as a historic district on the National Register since 1978, the hill is, in the words of developer Tony Salazar, widely regarded as one of the best urban renewal successes in the United States.

## Flight Context

Quality Hill occupies a 200-foot bluff on the west side of downtown Kansas City, Missouri, at about 39.1049°N, 94.5921°W, overlooking the West Bottoms and the confluence of the Kansas and Missouri rivers. From 2,000 to 2,500 feet AGL, the river confluence, the bluff edge of Case Park and the downtown skyline mark the spot. Charles B. Wheeler Downtown Airport (KMKC) lies only about 1 nm to the north, in full view of the Case Park overlook, so this is busy, close-in airspace. Kansas City International Airport (KMCI), with scheduled airline service, is about 13 nm to the north-northwest.
