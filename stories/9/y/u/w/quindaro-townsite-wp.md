# Quindaro Townsite

Founded on Wyandot land in 1857 as a Free-State port on the Missouri River, Quindaro sheltered freedom seekers, gave rise to the first Black school west of the Mississippi, and survives today as a national commemorative site.

**Location:** 39.1539, -94.6617

**Categories:** Abolitionism in the United States, African-American history of Kansas, Underground Railroad, Archaeological sites on the National Register of Historic Places in Kansas, Bleeding Kansas, Native American history of Kansas

## Introduction

Fourteen slave hunters, her enslaver among them, were camped at the edge of town, looking for a woman named Caroline. In Quindaro, Kansas Territory, Caroline had been brought to the house of Clarina Nichols - a writer for the local Free-Soil paper, a friend of Susan B. Anthony, and an important conductor and "Station Master" of the Underground Railroad. Caroline was hidden overnight in an empty, elaborately disguised cistern, then sent north on the road as soon as it was safe. Nichols later described the night in a letter. Today the town where it happened is an archaeological site around North 27th Street in Kansas City, Kansas.

## Strength Through Numbers

Quindaro was born of the Kansas-Nebraska Act, which opened Kansas Territory to settlement and left settlers to vote on whether it would enter the Union as a slave state or a free one. The New England Emigrant Aid Company, which had already helped more than 1,200 settlers migrate, hoped to secure Kansas as free territory, and it needed a port. In 1855 the Wyandot Reservation had been divided among its families, and the Quindaro Town Company bought the allotment of Nancy Quindaro Brown for a townsite. The company's leaders reflected that partnership: Joel Walker, brother of Wyandot chief William Walker, served as president; Abelard Guthrie, a Wyandot by marriage to Nancy Quindaro Brown, as vice-president; and the Emigrant Aid Company's Charles Robinson, a future governor of Kansas, as treasurer. Quindaro, a common Wyandot name for women, means "bundle of sticks" or "strength through numbers" in the Wyandot language.

## A Free-State Port Under the Bluffs

The planners chose well. About six miles above the mouth of the Kansas River, on Wyandot land, they found a natural rock ledge where the Missouri ran along the bank six to twelve feet deep, making a convenient landing, with plenty of wood and stone for building and fertile land close by. Construction began in January 1857, and the town soon held numerous stone houses and the beginnings of several businesses; its sawmill was the largest in Kansas. Wyandot people living there were not dispossessed and became part of the town. John Morgan Walden founded a Free-Soil newspaper, the Quindaro Chindowan, taking its name from a Wyandot word for "leader." The boomtown's population peaked at about 600, and its people aided freedom seekers escaping slavery in Missouri as a station on the Underground Railroad, part of the town's founding purpose of resisting the westward spread of slavery.

## Boom, Bust and a School

The boom ended quickly. Once Kansas was established as a free state, there was less need for the port. A nationwide economic depression and a failed campaign to attract a railroad hurt the commercial district, and during the Civil War the Union Army recruited many of the young men away, leaving only a few farming families. The lower town along the river was largely abandoned and overgrown. Yet Quindaro's most lasting institution was just beginning. In 1862, classes started for the children of formerly enslaved people, and in 1865 a group of men chartered the Quindaro Freedman's School, later Western University, probably the earliest school for Black students west of the Mississippi River. Freedpeople continued to gather in the residential community, which was mostly African American by the late 19th century. In the early 1900s the university added a full industrial curriculum, with buildings for livestock, a laundry and, later, auto mechanics.

## What the Ground Remembers

Western University closed in 1943. Its buildings were lost to fire or demolition, and the last three faculty houses came down near the end of the 20th century. Aside from cornerstones of some early buildings, only the university's statue of abolitionist John Brown remains; the John Brown Memorial Plaza was dedicated in 1978. Archaeological study in the late 1980s rediscovered many parts of the 1850s town beneath the overgrowth. Quindaro Townsite was placed on the National Register of Historic Places on May 22, 2002. In 2018, community stakeholders, including historians, archaeologists, scholars and activists, began resolving decades of struggle over how to manage the site, and in 2019 the John D. Dingell Jr. Conservation, Management, and Recreation Act designated it the Quindaro Townsite National Commemorative Site, allowing the National Park Service to assist with preservation and education. In April 2026 Representatives Emanuel Cleaver, Derek Schmidt and Sharice Davids introduced a bill to make it a National Historic Landmark, after a federal review panel's recommendation stalled.

## Flight Context

Quindaro Townsite lies at approximately 39.1539 N, 94.6617 W, on the narrow bank of the Missouri River beneath the bluffs of northern Kansas City, Kansas, near North 27th Street and the Missouri Pacific rail line. From 2,000-3,000 ft MSL, follow the Missouri River upstream (northwest) from its confluence with the Kansas River at Kaw Point; the townsite sits on the riverbank roughly six miles above the Kansas River's mouth. Charles B. Wheeler Downtown Airport (KMKC) is about 3.7 nm to the east-southeast, and Kansas City International (KMCI) is about 9 nm north-northwest across the river. Expect busy controlled airspace; haze and river fog can reduce visibility.
