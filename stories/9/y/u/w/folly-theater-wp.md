# Folly Theater

Built in 1900 as the Standard, Kansas City's Folly Theater hosted Sarah Bernhardt, the Marx Brothers and Gypsy Rose Lee before preservationists saved it from becoming a parking lot.

**Location:** 39.1019, -94.5869

**Categories:** Theatres in Kansas City, Missouri, Vaudeville theaters, Theatres on the National Register of Historic Places in Missouri, Theatres completed in 1900, Downtown Kansas City

## Introduction

In 1974, the owners of a shuttered downtown Kansas City theater issued an ultimatum: if no buyer came forward by the end of the year, the building would be demolished to make way for a parking lot. By then the old house had already lived several lives. It had opened as a vaudeville hall, hosted a legendary actress, a Marx Brothers run and a long burlesque era, and had most recently been known simply as the Folly. What happened next turned a doomed building into what its nonprofit operators call Kansas City's oldest historic theater.

## Colonel Butler's Standard

The theater opened as the Standard on September 23, 1900, with 2,400 seats and a bill of burlesque and vaudeville. Colonel Edward Butler of St. Louis built it for $250,000 so that his son could present shows on the Empire vaudeville circuit. Kansas City architect Louis S. Curtiss designed it, along with the adjoining Edward Hotel, later known as the Hotel Missouri and demolished in 1965. Fate soon raised the Standard's profile. In 1901, a fire at the nearby Coates Opera House sent opera and comic opera to the Standard's stage, bringing performers such as Sarah Bernhardt, Richard Mansfield and Maude Adams to a house built for lighter fare.

## A Theater of Many Names

In 1902 the Standard became the Century. Empire burlesque acts including Al Jolson and Eddie Foy played there, and the building doubled as a venue for prizefighting and wrestling, with appearances by Jack Johnson and Jack Dempsey. Johnson stayed in the manager's apartment because hotels refused to rent him a room. In 1923 the Shubert brothers bought the Century and, after extensive renovations by Shubert architect Herbert J. Krapp, renamed it Shubert's Missouri, booking Shakespeare and O'Neill. The Marx Brothers played I'll Say She Is for three weeks in 1923 and 1924, and that long run convinced the Shuberts to keep the theater going. After a tax sale, it reopened in 1941 as the Folly, featuring striptease; performers such as Gypsy Rose Lee and Tempest Storm appeared before the Folly closed in January 1974.

## The Race Against the Wrecking Ball

With the demolition deadline looming, the theater's owners, Annbar Associates and Elk Realty of New York, offered it for sale for $950,000. A group of local preservation activists, including Joan Dillon and William N. Deramus III, formed a nonprofit called the Performing Arts Foundation to raise the money to buy and restore it. They gathered $350,000 in contributions. Then, through negotiation, they persuaded Annbar Associates to donate the remaining $600,000 of the price. That same year, 1974, the theater was listed on the National Register of Historic Places. A full renovation was completed in 1981, including an annex built on the site of the former Edward Hotel.

## Limestone, Brick and an Encore

Outside, the Folly still wears Curtiss's Neo-Palladian face of Carthage limestone and red pressed brick. An adapted Palladian window dominates the center, its lower portion divided into three arched windows. At street level, heavily rusticated limestone frames three arched entrances, and on the east wall a 19-foot-high opening admits stage scenery. The building measures 125 feet deep, 70 feet wide and 65 feet high. Care has continued since the rescue. For its centennial in 2000 the theater gained a new lobby, restrooms and a second-floor Shareholders' Room, and in 2005 the auditorium's original color scheme was restored.

Today the Performing Arts Foundation, doing business as the Folly Theater, presents a Jazz Series, a Kids Series and an Americana Series. It hosts the Harriman-Jewell Series of classical performers, the Friends of Chamber Music and the Heartland Men's Chorus. KC Burlesque's Kansas City Burlesque Festival marked the first burlesque performance revival at the Folly, a genre returning to the house it once defined.
