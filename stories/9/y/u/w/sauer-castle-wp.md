# Sauer Castle

Built in the early 1870s for German immigrant Anton Sauer, Kansas City, Kansas's towered Italianate "castle" sheltered five generations of one family, then decades of ghost stories, trespassers and a long wait for restoration.

**Location:** 39.0685, -94.6340

**Categories:** Houses on the National Register of Historic Places in Kansas, Italianate architecture in Kansas, Buildings and structures in Kansas City, Kansas, Reportedly haunted locations, Houses completed in 1871

## Introduction

Anton Sauer came to Kansas City carrying grief and a failing body. Born in Germany in 1823, he had moved to New York City in 1858 to join his mother and sisters, but after his wife, Francesca, died in 1868 and his tuberculosis worsened, he brought his family west. Kansas City gave him a second life: a successful business, a new marriage and, between 1871 and 1873, a towered Italianate mansion at 935 Shawnee Road in Kansas City, Kansas. A local library historian would later call it, "Without a doubt, the most famous residence in Kansas City, Kansas."

## A Villa on the Shawnee Trail

Sauer hired Asa Beebe Cross, one of the most prominent Kansas City architects, to design the house in the Italianate Villa style, a mode known for classical proportions, decorative brackets and tall, narrow windows. Its most distinctive feature is reportedly its four-story tower, which offers panoramic views. The mansion stood on the Shawnee trail that led to the historic Santa Fe Trail. It filled with a large, blended family. Anton and Francesca had five children: Gustave, Anthony Philip Jr., Julius, Emil and Johanna. In 1869 he married a 28-year-old widow, Mary (Maria) Einhellig Messerschmidt, who brought two daughters of her own, Anna and Maria. Together they had five more daughters, four of whom survived to adulthood - Eva Marie, Antoinette, Josephine and Clara. Their daughter Helen died in infancy at 14 months.

## Five Generations Under One Tower

Anton died in the second-floor master bedroom on August 16, 1879. Mary and the children stayed on, and she lived in the house until her death from a heart attack on November 29, 1919. The family's joys and sorrows kept unfolding within its walls. Daughter Eva Marie married William C. Van Fossen in the house; they had one child, Helen, before the marriage ended after 18 months. She then married John S. Perkins, a Wyandotte County businessman and landowner and a widower with six children, and they had three more. The marriage lasted until Perkins, suffering from declining health, took his own life at 73. Tragedy came again when an infant daughter of their son, John Harrison Perkins, drowned in the swimming pool on the west side of the house. Eva Marie lived on in the family home with her son and two daughters until her death in 1955.

## Ghost Stories and Trespassers

After Eva Marie's death, the house passed out of the family for the first time in five generations. Paul Berry, owner of a home heating oil company, bought it and lived there until his own death in December 1986. The castle had a reputation. Ghost stories about the house dating back to 1930 drew a steady stream of trespassers and vandals, and Berry and his dog fought them off. In January 1987 new buyers arrived hoping to turn the mansion into a bed and breakfast, though no one lived in the house at the time. That same month, on January 29, 1987, it was added to the Kansas City, Kansas, Historic Landmarks. The house is also listed on the National Register of Historic Places.

## Back in the Family, Then a Rescue

In 1988, Carl Lopp, Anton Sauer's great-great-grandson, bought the house, intending to fix it up and live there to keep it in the family. The work proved difficult, yielding only modest improvements such as repaired balconies and a large fence around the property. On August 15, 1996, a caretaker hired for the house was charged with felony theft for allegedly stealing $30,000 worth of artifacts, including a chandelier, wall sconces, an antique dress, copper from the furnace and a garden tractor. In January 2022 the property went on the market for $10 million. The next chapter began in 2023, when an enthusiast bought the site at a Wyandotte County tax sale and hired teams of historical restoration specialists. Their goals were structural stability and the recovery of the original architectural features, using modern techniques such as foundation stabilization and roof repair alongside period-appropriate materials and methods, especially in the stonework. Recent reports describe the work as still under way, with the owner expecting the house to host weddings and community events again, though its final use has not been settled.
