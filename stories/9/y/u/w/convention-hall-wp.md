# Convention Hall

Kansas City's Convention Hall burned three months before the 1900 Democratic National Convention and was rebuilt in 90 days, then spent three decades as the city's great stage, shelter and meeting place before becoming a parking lot.

## Introduction

Kansas City had ninety days. On April 4, 1900, fire destroyed Convention Hall, the grand arena at 13th and Central Streets, just three months before the city was due to host the Democratic National Convention. Surrendering the convention might have seemed the sensible choice. Instead, the city rebuilt the entire hall in 90 days, an effort so defiant it earned a name of its own: the "Kansas City Spirit." For nearly four decades, the building that rose from those ashes served as the city's great common room, the place where Kansas City gathered to cheer, to shelter, to argue and to be seen.

## Sousa Opens the Doors

The first Convention Hall, designed by Frederick E. Hill, opened on February 22, 1899, with a performance by John Philip Sousa's band. It was a fitting debut for a building meant to do a little of everything. As a major indoor arena and convention center, the hall quickly became a central hub of civic life in Kansas City. Landing the 1900 Democratic National Convention promised to put the city in front of the whole country. Then, just over thirteen months after Sousa's opening night, the building went up in flames, and it looked for a moment as though the prize might be lost along with it.

## The Kansas City Spirit

Rather than surrender the convention, the city went to work. Frederick Hill redesigned the hall, this time to be fireproof, and the new building was completed in only 90 days. The deadline was unforgiving: the convention was scheduled to open over the Fourth of July. The hall was ready. Democrats gathered inside to nominate William Jennings Bryan for president, and among the pages running errands on the convention floor was a 16-year-old local Democrat named Harry S. Truman. Nobody watching the teenager could have guessed that he would one day sit in the White House himself. For Kansas City, the achievement became a point of civic pride, a story about a town that refused to be beaten by bad luck, retold under that same banner of the Kansas City Spirit.

## Shelter from the Flood

Three years later the hall served a far humbler purpose. When the Great Flood of 1903 drove families from their homes, Convention Hall became a refuge for several thousand people. The arena built for cheering crowds and political speeches filled instead with the displaced, and it held them until they could go elsewhere. The last 110 refugees were moved to tent camps at 31st and Summit, and after their departure on June 12, 1903, the hall had to be fumigated. The episode revealed another side of the building's civic role. In a crisis, it was not a showplace but a lifeline. The hall also came close to a spectacular prize. The world's largest pipe organ, the centerpiece of Festival Hall at the 1904 Louisiana Purchase Exposition in St. Louis, was planned for the hall's north end once the fair was over, but the operators backed out before installation when they discovered the contract had never been legally ratified. That organ went on to become the nucleus of Philadelphia's famous Wanamaker Organ.

## A Stage for Better and Worse

Over the years, the hall's calendar mirrored the full range of American life. Sarah Bernhardt performed Camille there, bringing one of the most celebrated actresses of her age to the Missouri River city. National politics returned in 1928, when the Republican Party held its national convention in the hall and nominated Herbert Hoover. Not every gathering deserves celebration. Between 1922 and 1924, Convention Hall hosted a series of Ku Klux Klan rallies, its most controversial use - a reminder that the same public room that sheltered flood victims also gave a platform to a movement built on hatred and intimidation. The end came quietly. In 1936, Kansas City demolished Convention Hall so the site could serve as parking for the new Municipal Auditorium nearby. A hall rebuilt in ninety heroic days ended its life as a parking lot.
