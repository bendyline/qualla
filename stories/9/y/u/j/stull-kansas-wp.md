# Stull, Kansas

A German-speaking farm hamlet that never grew past about fifty people, Stull became famous for a 1974 student-newspaper legend that cast its little cemetery as a gateway to Hell.

**Location:** 38.9711, -95.4561

## Introduction

Christ Kraft, who lived in Stull during the twentieth century, remembered life there as "quiet and easy, sometimes even boring." That verdict is hard to square with the town's reputation. For half a century, rumor has cast this speck of Douglas County farmland as one of the seven gateways to Hell, a place the Devil supposedly visits twice a year. Today the cemetery gates are locked at night and law enforcement patrols the grounds. Between those two versions of Stull - the sleepy German farm settlement and the haunted legend - lies the story of a hamlet that never held more than about fifty people, yet became known far beyond Kansas.

## Deer Creek and Its Stone Chapel

The settlement first appeared on territorial maps in 1857 under another name: Deer Creek. Nobody is quite sure where that came from. Martha Parker and Betty Laird have suggested it might translate an indigenous place name, or that it might simply record a deer seen beside the water. The first European settlers were Pennsylvania Dutch and German-speaking immigrants, and faith bound them together. Their congregation met in members' homes until 1867, when they raised a stone church, the Evangelical Emmanuel and Deer Creek Mission, and chartered a cemetery beside it that same year. Sermons in the little chapel stayed in German until 1908. The name Stull arrived almost by accident. When a post office opened in 1899, Sylvester Stull served as its only postmaster, and according to Parker and Laird, the Post Office simply named the place after him. The office closed in 1903. The name never left.

## Rabbits, Rail Dreams and a Bank Charter

Stull never came close to being a boomtown. In 1912 only 31 people lived in the area, and even at its height the settlement counted about fifty. Farm life set the rhythm, and it was not unusual for the community to haul some 300 freshly killed rabbits to butchers in Topeka. The Louk & Kraft grocery, opened in the early 1900s, served its neighbors until 1955. For one brief season, though, Stull dared to imagine something bigger. The Roaring Twenties brought early talk of an interurban railroad between Kansas City and Emporia that would have run right through town. Expecting growth, residents began discussing a Farmers State Bank, and J. W. Kreider, a banker from nearby Lecompton, went so far as to secure an official charter. In 1922 the congregation raised $20,000 for a new wooden church across the road, leaving the old stone chapel to the weather. The boom everyone anticipated never arrived.

## Two Sorrows

In a settlement of a few dozen people, loss lands hard, and Stull suffered two tragedies during the twentieth century. A young boy named Oliver Bahnmaier wandered into a field his father was burning and died there. Later, a man who had gone missing was found hanging from a tree. These were real griefs, borne by real families. In time, though, rumor attached itself to them. Whispers spread that anyone who stepped on Oliver's tombstone would go to Hell, turning a child's grave into a dare. It was an early sign of how easily Stull's plain history could be bent into something darker - and of how the people who actually lived here would one day have to share their dead with strangers looking for a thrill.

## The Devil's Calendar

Unlike most legends, this one has a traceable birthday. In November 1974, The University Daily Kansan, the student newspaper at the University of Kansas in nearby Lawrence, ran an article titled "Legend of Devil Haunts Tiny Town." It claimed the Devil appeared in Stull twice a year: once on Halloween and once on the spring equinox. The tale grew with every retelling. The cemetery became one of seven gateways to Hell, and the abandoned stone church was said to be possessed. Later embellishments added witches hanged in the cemetery, a staircase inside the old church descending to the underworld, and a rumor that Pope John Paul II had his flight path changed to avoid the place. None of it holds up. Historians and local residents agree the legend has no basis in historical fact and was created and spread by students. Knowing that has never kept the curious away.

## Locked Gates and Lightning

The attention has cost Stull dearly. Vandalism forced the cemetery to be locked at night, law enforcement patrols the area, especially around Halloween, and trespassers face up to six months in jail or a fine of up to $1,000. The old stone church, which deteriorated badly over the twentieth century, was demolished in 2002; some residents had hoped to rebuild it, but those efforts never materialized. Meanwhile the legend escaped into popular culture. Urge Overkill released an EP called Stull in 1992, and the 2013 horror film Nothing Left to Fear drew on the story. The television series Supernatural staged its season five finale, "Swan Song," at Stull Cemetery, and creator Eric Kripke set his heroes' hometown in Lawrence because of Stull's proximity. Then, on July 25, 2022, the newer Stull church caught fire in the early afternoon. No one was hurt, though damage was substantial. Officials suspected lightning - an ordinary cause in an extraordinary place.

## Flight Context

Stull sits at 38.971°N, 95.456°W, about 940 feet (286 m) above sea level in the rolling farmland of western Douglas County, roughly midway between Lawrence and Topeka. From 2,500-3,500 feet AGL, look for a small rural crossroads with a church and a fenced cemetery amid square-mile fields. Clinton Lake spreads a few miles to the southeast, while the Kansas River and the Kansas Turnpike (I-70) run a few miles to the north. Lawrence Regional Airport (KLWC) is about 11 nm east-northeast; Topeka's Philip Billard Municipal (KTOP) lies about 10 nm northwest and Topeka Regional Airport at Forbes Field (KFOE) about 10 nm west. Kansas City International (KMCI) is roughly 40 nm northeast. Autumn usually brings crisp, clear visibility; spring and summer thunderstorms can build quickly over the eastern Kansas prairie.
