# Fort Titus

Near Lecompton in Kansas Territory, the fortified log home of pro-slavery militia colonel Henry T. Titus fell to free-state fighters on August 16, 1856, when a much-captured brass cannon firing balls made from newspaper type ended the battle in about half an hour.

**Location:** 39.0114, -95.3940

**Categories:** Forts in Kansas, Buildings and structures in Douglas County, Kansas, 1856 establishments in Kansas Territory

## Introduction

The cannonballs were made from printer's type. Metal letters from a Lawrence newspaper, the stuff that had once spelled out the news, had been turned into ammunition, and on August 16, 1856, they were fired from a brass cannon called Old Sacramento into the log walls of Fort Titus. The walls were no match. The balls passed entirely through the fortified cabin, and within about half an hour the battle was over. Out of it came a burned homestead, a wounded colonel dragged off to Lawrence, and one more grim chapter in the struggle over whether Kansas would enter the Union as a free state or a slave state.

## A Cabin Built for War

Henry T. Titus was a colonel in the militia of Kansas Territory's southern-oriented government and a pro-slavery advocate. About April 1856, he built the home that became Fort Titus, and by one account he did so by squatting on the claim of a free-state settler while that settler was away. It was a log cabin designed for a fight. Gun loopholes cut into its walls allowed defenders to shoot from inside, and a small log addition on the north side served as the kitchen. The fortification made sense in its moment. Kansas Territory was split by a bitter political and ideological fight over the legality of slavery, and the violence between pro-slavery forces and free-state settlers would become known as Bleeding Kansas. The people with the most at stake, those who would be held in bondage if slavery took root there, had no voice in that contest.

## Two Days in August

In mid-August 1856, the fighting around Lecompton came in quick succession. On August 15, free-state partisans destroyed Fort Saunders, some distance to the south. At 2 a.m. the next morning, southern partisans including Titus attacked the fortified home of Judge Wakefield but failed to take it. Later that same day, free-state men under Samuel Walker came for Fort Titus. Inside, Titus had at least twenty-one men, among them thirteen German stonemasons from nearby Lecompton. The first assault went in before the free-staters' cannon had arrived, and it failed; its leader, Captain Henry Shombre, was mortally wounded. Some of the attackers positioned themselves between Lecompton and a U.S. Army post, Camp Sacket, about a mile from the fort, so no messages could pass between territorial governor Wilson Shannon and the troops.

## Old Sacramento Speaks

Then the cannon arrived, and the battle ended quickly. Old Sacramento had a history of its own, having changed hands between northern and southern partisans three times before this fight. Its type-metal balls smashed straight through the cabin, and the defenders could not hold. The whole engagement probably lasted no more than thirty minutes. Major John Sedgwick, commanding at Camp Sacket, moved his troops toward Fort Titus to stop the fighting, but by the time they arrived it was already over. Titus himself was wounded, in the head and shoulder by one newspaper correspondent's account, and taken prisoner.

## Ashes and Aftermath

As soon as the battle was finished, the prisoners were taken to Lawrence and Fort Titus was burned, never to be rebuilt. Titus was later exchanged for free-state prisoners, and he eventually left Kansas behind, going on to found Titusville, Florida, a town that still carries his name. The fort was gone, but its story has not disappeared. Historic Lecompton, the nearby town that served as the pro-slavery territorial capital, tells the tale of the battle and has featured a replica of the fort. Near Lecompton today, it is hard to imagine loopholed walls, a brass cannon and the smoke of a burning cabin on an August afternoon.

## Flight Context

The site of Fort Titus lies at about 39.011°N, 95.394°W in Douglas County, Kansas, a couple of miles south of Lecompton between Lawrence and Topeka. From 2,500-3,500 feet MSL, use the Kansas River valley and the small town of Lecompton as your references; nothing of the fort remains to be seen from the air. Lawrence Regional Airport (KLWC, formerly Lawrence Municipal) is about 10 miles to the east, and Topeka's Philip Billard Municipal Airport (KTOP) lies roughly 13 miles to the west-northwest. The river valley is broad and easy to follow, though summer thunderstorms can build quickly over the Kansas plains.
