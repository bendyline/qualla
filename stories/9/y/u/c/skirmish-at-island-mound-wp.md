# Skirmish at Island Mound

In October 1862, on the open prairie of Bates County, Missouri, men who had escaped slavery held a fence-rail fort they called "Fort Africa" and became the first known African-American regiment to fight Confederate forces in the Civil War.

**Location:** 38.2353, -94.4399

**Categories:** Battles of the American Civil War in Missouri, African Americans in the American Civil War, Union victories of the American Civil War, Bates County, Missouri, 1862 in the American Civil War

## Introduction

While the men ate, smoke began rising south of camp. The guerrillas surrounding the Toothman homestead had set the prairie on fire, and the flames drove the Union skirmishers back toward the fence-rail breastworks the soldiers called "Fort Africa." It was October 29, 1862, in Bates County, Missouri, and the men behind those rails belonged to a regiment made up mostly of people who had escaped slavery in Missouri and Arkansas. The United States had not yet agreed to accept Black soldiers into its army. What happened over the next few hours on this open grassland became the first known combat between an African-American regiment and Confederate forces in the Civil War.

## Ready Before the Nation Was

The regiment had been raised in Kansas by Captain, soon Colonel, James M. Williams, and mustered into Kansas militia service in August 1862 as the 1st Kansas Colored Volunteers. Federal service would not come until January 13, 1863, after the Emancipation Proclamation. Kansas did not wait. Uncertain as the regiment's future was, the state armed its men with good Austrian and Prussian muskets fitted with bayonets. That autumn, Major B. S. Henning sent Captain Richard G. Ward's 170-man battalion and Captain Henry C. Seaman's 70-man battalion into Bates County, with scouts from the 5th Kansas Cavalry, among them Cherokee and Black men. Their target was a guerrilla force based on Hog Island in the Osage River, near the farm of Enoch Toothman, about nine miles across the state line. Toothman was a suspected Confederate sympathizer.

## Fence Rails on the Prairie

The country away from the river was open tall-grass prairie and farmland, with few trees to hide behind. As the Kansans approached on Monday, October 27, their scouts spotted a large mounted party ahead: local Confederate guerrillas under Bill Truman and Dick Hancock, joined by Missouri State Guard recruits under Colonel Jeremiah "Vard" Cockrell. Finding the enemy stronger than expected, the two battalions, some 240 men in all, took over the Toothman homestead and stacked its fence rails into breastworks. Tuesday passed in occasional skirmishing. The Austrian muskets outranged the guerrillas' weapons, and that advantage kept the horsemen at bay.

## Fire, Smoke and a Volley

On Wednesday the guerrillas tried fire instead. Seaman answered with a back burn to keep the flames from the camp and sent out a scouting party, which was drawn into skirmishing out of sight. Lieutenant Joseph Gardner led a detachment to bring them back and was soon engaged as well. Then the mounted guerrillas appeared in force, riding to cut Gardner off from Ward's approaching men. Unable to reach camp, Gardner's soldiers formed a line and fired a volley into the charging cavalry. They were badly outnumbered, and in the general melee that followed, most of the Union losses occurred. But riders who swept past Gardner found themselves hemmed in by volleys from the rest of the Kansans advancing across the prairie, and the guerrillas finally withdrew.

## The Men Who Fell

Eight Union soldiers died and eleven were wounded. Among the dead were Captain A. G. Crew of Company A; Corporal Joseph Talbot and Privates Samuel Davis, Thomas Lane, Marlon Barber, Allen Rhodes and Henry Gash, all of Company F; and John Six-Killer, a Cherokee, of Seaman's battalion. Guerrilla losses are unknown, though some Kansans at the time claimed up to 40 killed. A New York Times correspondent traveling with the regiment praised the men's "desperate bravery," and Bill Truman himself reportedly told listeners in Butler that the Black soldiers had fought "like tigers." At a time when some observers openly doubted whether formerly enslaved men would make good soldiers, the fight drew national attention.

## Remembered at Last

The United States Colored Troops were established in 1863, and on December 13, 1864, the 1st Kansas Colored Volunteers became the 79th United States Colored Troops. The prairie where they first fought waited much longer for recognition. Missouri acquired the land in 2008 and, in October 2012, on the 150th anniversary of the fight, dedicated the Battle of Island Mound State Historic Site, a 40-acre tract that takes in much of the Toothman homestead. It honors all the soldiers who fought here, including the eight men of the Kansas force who died in action and were buried near the farm. Their story reached a new audience in 2014, when Missouri State Parks and St. Louis filmmaker Brant Hadfield made a documentary, The Battle of Island Mound; it won two Mid-America Emmy Awards in 2015.

## Flight Context

The story's map pin, 38.235 N, 94.440 W, is the Battle of Island Mound State Historic Site, about six to seven miles west-southwest of Butler, Missouri, at the junction of County Roads 1002 and 5001. From 1,500-2,500 feet AGL, look for open farmland and prairie southwest of Butler, with the Osage River bottoms nearby, where the guerrillas kept their base on Hog Island. Butler Memorial Airport (KBUM), about 6 nm to the northeast, makes an easy reference point; Lawrence Smith Memorial Airport (KLRY) at Harrisonville is about 23 nm north-northeast and Nevada Municipal Airport (KNVD) about 24 nm south-southeast. Spring thunderstorms and summer haze are common; late October, the anniversary of the fight, often brings clear, cool air.
