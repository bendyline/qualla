# Lecompton, Kansas

A bluff-top town of under 600 people on the Kansas River, Lecompton was the pro-slavery territorial capital of Bleeding Kansas, where a constitution drafted in 1857 helped push the nation toward civil war.

**Location:** 39.0431, -95.3950

**Categories:** Cities in Douglas County, Kansas, Former colonial and territorial capitals in the United States, Capitals of Kansas, Bleeding Kansas, 1854 establishments in Kansas Territory

## Introduction

The stone building on the Lecompton bluff sits on the foundation of a capitol that was never finished. A capitol was started here and never completed, and years later a college built on its footings instead. That unfinished foundation tells much of Lecompton's story. For more than five years, from 1855 to 1861, a town that counted 588 residents in 2020 stood at the center of the fight over whether Kansas would enter the Union free or slave.

## Bald Eagle Becomes a Capital

Lecompton was founded in 1854 on a bluff above the south bank of the Kansas River. It started out as Bald Eagle and was soon renamed for Samuel Dexter Lecompte, chief justice of the territorial Supreme Court. In August 1855 it became the capital of Kansas Territory, and it was also the Douglas County seat until 1858. The town quickly became a stronghold of pro-slavery politics and Southern sympathy. That set it against nearby Lawrence, founded by Free-Staters from Massachusetts. This was the era remembered as Bleeding Kansas, named for the violence between pro-slavery and anti-slavery factions in the eastern part of the territory, and Lecompton was one of its hotbeds.

## The Constitution That Divided a Nation

In late 1857 a convention met in Constitution Hall and drafted the Lecompton Constitution, which would have brought Kansas into the Union as a slave state. The document set off an intense national debate. Congress rejected it, and it became one of the prime topics of the Lincoln-Douglas debates, feeding the dispute that would soon erupt into civil war. It failed partly because the antislavery party won the territorial legislature in the election of 1857. The new lawmakers met in the same Constitution Hall and immediately began abolishing the pro-slavery laws passed since July 1855 by what they called the Bogus Legislature. Free-staters even voted to move the capital to Minneola, but Governor James W. Denver vetoed the bill and U.S. Attorney General Jeremiah S. Black ruled it void. Lecompton remained the legal capital until Kansas became a state on January 29, 1861, and the victorious free-state leaders chose Topeka instead. Less than three months later, on April 12, the Civil War began.

## A College on a Capitol's Footings

Four years after statehood, the town found a second act. In 1865 the United Brethren Church opened a university in Lecompton, first occupying the Rowena hotel, which had been built for the Territorial Legislature and its visitors. In 1882 the school put up a stone building on the foundation of the unfinished capitol. It was called Lane University, after the free-stater James H. Lane, an ironic name for a school in the old pro-slavery capital. The university thrived until 1902, when it moved to Holton and merged into Campbell College, which later merged with the now-defunct Kansas City University. Its bell went along and can still be found on the campus of Holton High School. Back in Lecompton, the building served as the town's high school until 1926, then fell into disrepair. In the 1970s residents raised money to rescue it, and it is now the Territorial Capital Museum, run by the Lecompton Historical Society. Two blocks away, Constitution Hall is a museum operated by the Kansas Historical Society.

## Schisms, Fires and Limestone

Lecompton's later history is written in stone and smoke. In the 1880s a dispute over secret organizations split the United Brethren congregation, and one faction built its own Radical United Brethren Church on adjoining land. That church burned around 1902, and a limestone church replaced it. It served as city hall until about 2006, and today it is a community building and a Douglas County sheriff's substation. When it joined the Kansas Register of Historic Places in 2016, Lecompton could count four buildings on state or national registers, along with Constitution Hall, the Lane University building and the Democratic Headquarters of the Bleeding Kansas era. The Historical Society began restoring that native limestone headquarters in 1998, and it now stands in Riverview Park, along the bluff overlooking the Kaw Valley. In 1916 fire destroyed the frame business buildings on the east side of Elmore, the main street, and brick replaced them. A mural in the post office shows the town as it looked before the flames.

## A Small Town Minding Its Past

Today Lecompton is part of the Lawrence metropolitan area, a town of 1.78 square miles with a humid subtropical climate of hot, humid summers. School consolidation in 1970 closed Lecompton High School, home of the Owls, and joined the town with Perry in USD 343, whose teams are the Kaws. The city later bought the old high school building to remodel as a community space. Among the notable people linked to Lecompton is Chuck Wright, who served from 1965 to 1969 as mayor of Topeka, the city that ended up with the capital. It is a quiet setting for a place whose name once echoed through Congress.

## Flight Context

Lecompton sits on a bluff above the south bank of the Kansas River in Douglas County, at about 39.0431°N, 95.395°W, roughly midway between Lawrence and Topeka. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL, follow the Kansas River (the Kaw) and look for the small town on its south bank. Lawrence Regional Airport (KLWC) is about 8.5 nm to the east-southeast, Topeka's Philip Billard Municipal Airport (KTOP) is about 11 nm to the west, and Topeka Regional Airport (KFOE) is about 14 nm to the west-southwest. Hot, humid summers often bring haze and afternoon thunderstorms.
