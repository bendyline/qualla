# Allen Fieldhouse

Built under the guise of an armory to get around a steel shortage, the University of Kansas's Allen Fieldhouse became one of college basketball's most storied and loudest arenas, played on a court named for the game's inventor.

**Location:** 38.9543, -95.2524

**Categories:** College basketball venues in Kansas, Kansas Jayhawks men's basketball, University of Kansas campus, Sports venues completed in 1955, Basketball history

## Introduction

To finish the building, the university had to call it something else. Construction began in 1952, but a federal mandate restricting steel use, imposed after the Second World War and during the Korean War, soon brought the work to a halt. University officials found a loophole. By adding some rooms for storing guns and weapons, they could carry on under the guise of an "armory." What rose on the University of Kansas campus in Lawrence was no armory. It was Allen Fieldhouse, home of the Jayhawks and one of the most historically significant buildings in college basketball.

## Two Names Above the Floor

The arena carries the name of Phog Allen, a former Jayhawks player and a head coach whose tenure lasted 39 years; its nickname, "The Phog," honors him too. The floor itself belongs to someone older still. It is James Naismith Court, named for basketball's inventor, who founded the KU program and served as the Jayhawks' first coach from 1898 to 1907. The building was dedicated on March 1, 1955, and 17,228 people packed in for a basketball game that day. That remains the largest crowd in the building's history, and it will likely never be broken. The arena was built for 17,000, then trimmed to 15,200 during Ted Owens' coaching years to improve fire-code exit routes. It later rose to 16,300, and 2024 renovations adding corner video boards and more chair-back seats brought it to 15,300.

## Beware of the Phog

ESPN The Magazine once named Allen Fieldhouse the loudest college basketball arena in the country, and on February 13, 2017, against West Virginia, the crowd proved it by breaking the Guinness World Record for loudest roar at 130.4 decibels. The previous record, 126.4 decibels at Kentucky's Rupp Arena, had lasted less than three weeks, and many Kansas fans had been in that building too as the Jayhawks beat the fourth-ranked Wildcats 79-73. Above the championship banners on the north wall hangs the building's warning: "Pay Heed, All Who Enter: Beware of the Phog." The original was made from dormitory shower curtains by a group of KU students before a late-season game against Duke in 1988, inspired partly by advertisements for the horror movie The Fog. By 1999 it was falling apart. Its tidier replacement drew public complaints, so the current banner was redesigned to look more like the students' handiwork, and the original now sits in the Booth Family Hall of Athletics museum.

## Where Visitors Rarely Win

Allen Fieldhouse is often counted among the best home-court advantages in men's college basketball, and the numbers back that up. Entering the 2023-24 season, after almost 70 years in the building, the Kansas men had lost only 118 games there, just 20 of them under head coach Bill Self. They had also sold out 352 games in a row, a streak stretching back to the 2001-02 season, and twenty times the Jayhawks have finished a season unbeaten at home. About 4,000 seats are set aside for KU students. Most of the rest belong to season-ticket holders in the Williams Educational Fund, the athletics fundraising arm named for Lawrence banker Dick Williams and his sons, Skipper and Odd.

## Banners, a Voice and Thirteen Rules

Kansas does not retire numbers. Instead, banners on the south wall honor players such as Wilt Chamberlain, Clyde Lovellette, Jo Jo White, Danny Manning, Lynette Woodard and Paul Pierce, while the numbers themselves go back into circulation. One banner honors the only non-athlete to receive the tribute. Max Falkenstien called every home game in the building from its opening until he retired in 2006, 51 years later, and he received the "number" 60 for his 60 years of service to the university. The north wall carries banners for NCAA titles in 1952, 1988, 2008 and 2022, along with Helms Foundation championships for 1922 and 1923. In 2022 a banner reading "Just Load the Wagon" went up for Bill Self's late father, borrowing the phrase he used to encourage his son throughout his life. An addition to the fieldhouse called the DeBruce Center houses the founding document of the sport: Naismith's original 13 Rules of Basketball, bought by the Booth family at a Sotheby's auction in December 2010 after fending off a rival bidder who hoped to send it to Duke.

## Rock Concerts and a Nuclear Nightmare

The fieldhouse has never been only about basketball. Its concourse began as an indoor track, and over the years the building has hosted track and field, volleyball, wrestling and football and softball practices. The Beach Boys, Elton John, Tina Turner and the band Kansas played here. In March 1968, Senator Robert F. Kennedy drew more than 20,000 people, and Abbie Hoffman spoke in 1970. The arena even appeared on television as a place of catastrophe, serving as the filming location for a climactic hospital scene in the 1983 ABC movie The Day After, one of the most-watched made-for-TV films of all time.
