# Franklin's Fort

Twice in the summer of 1856, free-state fighters from Lawrence raided the pro-slavery blockhouse at Franklin in search of a cannon called Old Sacramento, and the second time they found it disguised as a dressmaker's mannequin.

**Location:** 38.9338, -95.1761

**Categories:** Forts in Kansas, Bleeding Kansas, Buildings and structures in Douglas County, Kansas, 1854 establishments in Kansas Territory

## Introduction

When the free-staters finally forced their way into the blockhouse at Franklin, they found the prize they had come for twice that summer hidden in the unlikeliest way: the "Old Sacramento" cannon had been disguised as a dressmaker's mannequin. It was August 12, 1856, and the capture capped two nighttime battles over a small blockhouse in a small pro-slavery town a few miles from Lawrence, Kansas Territory. Neither fight was large. Together, they show how personal and close-quartered the violence of Bleeding Kansas could be, with neighbors firing at each other in the dark over a single gun.

## A Southern Town Next Door

Franklin was founded in 1853 in what became Douglas County by settlers who came mostly from the South and wanted Kansas admitted to the Union as a slave state. By late 1855 it had about a dozen homes and businesses and a post office, and it had become a center of the southern cause in the territory. Its neighbor, Lawrence, was the opposite: a free-state stronghold. By May 1856 Franklin's residents had built a blockhouse inside the town, meant to be defended against an attack from Lawrence and used to store arms. It also held Old Sacramento, a cannon seized in a raid on the arsenal at Liberty, Missouri, and fired at Lawrence's Free State Hotel when pro-slavery forces sacked that town in May 1856. In that attack, Lawrence lost its only cannon.

## A Shot in the Dark

Having lost their only cannon, Lawrence's free-staters hoped to take Old Sacramento for themselves. On June 4, about fifteen members of the Stubbs, a free-state militia from Lawrence, slipped into Franklin after dark to look for it. Their noise woke some of the sleeping defenders, and what became known as the First Battle of Franklin broke out. The cannon fired once without hitting anyone. Residents shot at the intruders from their houses and forced them to retreat. One Franklin man was killed and a few people were wounded, and some Franklin men may have been taken prisoner by the raiders.

## The Burning Hay Wagon

The townspeople strengthened the fort, but on August 12 the free-staters came back in greater force. Free-state leader James H. Lane led the march; possibly two companies, at least 75 men, set out from Lawrence after dark. Apparently only about 20 men were defending Franklin. For hours the two sides shot at each other in the darkness, doing little damage, though one free-stater was killed and several were wounded, along with one of the fort's defenders. A Franklin man rode for help to a company of U.S. cavalry camped about five miles away. The attackers finally broke the deadlock by setting a hay wagon on fire and pushing it against the fort's front door. Fearing they would be burned alive, the defenders fled into the night. When the cavalry arrived just before dawn, the battle was over.

## Muskets, Prisoners and a Mannequin

The fire damaged the post office next door, but the fort itself was scarred only by bullets. Inside, the free-staters took fifty to eighty muskets and ammunition, carried off the food stored there, and led away 14 prisoners, having lost one man killed and several wounded. They also took Old Sacramento, which they found disguised as a dressmaker's mannequin. Afterward, a free-stater named Thomas Bickerton scavenged lead printing type from the river and used it to make cannonballs. The attackers left the building standing, but the fight ended its days as a town defense. It became a home instead, occupied by Dr. R. L. Williams until probably the 1870s.

## Flight Context

The site of Franklin's Fort is at approximately 38.9338 N, 95.1761 W, in Douglas County, Kansas, roughly four miles east-southeast of downtown Lawrence. Navigate by Lawrence itself, which sits astride Interstate 70 between the Kansas and Wakarusa rivers, then look southeast of the city from 2,000-3,000 feet AGL. Lawrence Regional Airport (KLWC) is about 5 nm to the north-northwest; Gardner Municipal Airport (FAA identifier K34) is about 13 nm southeast and New Century AirCenter (KIXD) about 15 nm east-southeast. Expect spring thunderstorms and summer haze in northeast Kansas.
