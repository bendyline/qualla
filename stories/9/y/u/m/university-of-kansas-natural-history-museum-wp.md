# University of Kansas Natural History Museum

Built like a French church to house Lewis Lindsay Dyche's famous wildlife panorama, KU's Dyche Hall also keeps Comanche, the horse that survived the Little Bighorn, alongside marine reptiles from the Kansas chalk.

## Introduction

Dyche Hall was built like a church. The limestone building on the University of Kansas campus in Lawrence was modeled after a church in France (its main entrance echoes St. Trophime in Arles), and its designers even gave the entrance gallery an "apse," borrowing the language of sacred architecture. But the place of honor inside was never meant for an altar. It was designed to hold animals: the Panorama of North American Wildlife, a sweeping display so admired that it was the reason the whole building went up in the first place.

## A Panorama That Built a Museum

The panorama was created by naturalist Lewis Lindsay Dyche, who lived from 1857 to 1915. Part of it represented Kansas at the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago, where it was featured in the Kansas Pavilion. The panorama then became the impetus for funding and constructing a proper home for it, and between 1901 and 1903 the university raised Dyche Hall and its Natural History Museum, with the panorama set into the apse of the entrance gallery. Dyche's taxidermy is still housed here. When Dyche Hall was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on July 14, 1974, it was recognized both for its connection with Dyche and for its distinctive Romanesque architecture.

## Carved from Kansas Stone

The building wears its home state on its walls. Its exterior is local Oread Limestone, while the window facings, columns, arches, and grotesques were carved from Cottonwood Limestone, so even the grotesque figures on the facade are made of Kansas rock. Outside stands another quiet curiosity. Dyche Hall is home to one of only three Victory Eagle statues in Kansas, eagles that once served as markers along the Victory Highway. Inside, the museum has grown to more than 350 separate exhibits, and the panorama remains the one it is most famous for.

## The Horse Called Comanche

One of the museum's most famous residents is a horse. Comanche, a mixed-breed horse, survived the Battle of the Little Bighorn on June 25, 1876, in what is now Montana, the clash remembered as Custer's Last Stand, in which Lakota, Northern Cheyenne and Arapaho warriors killed Custer and the five companies of 7th Cavalry soldiers under his command. The museum is renowned for Comanche, long billed as the battle's sole U.S. survivor. The better-supported claim is that he was the only living thing found on Custer's battlefield, since other cavalry horses and the troopers of Reno's and Benteen's commands survived. When the horse's life was over, the Army asked Dyche to preserve him for display, and Comanche remains at KU today, a direct physical link to that June day on the northern plains.

## Monsters of the Kansas Chalk

Long before cavalry or panoramas, Kansas lay under water. During the Late Cretaceous, a vast inland sea spread across what is now the Great Plains, and the chalk beds it left behind in western Kansas are famous for exceptionally well-preserved marine reptiles. The museum is renowned for its extensive exhibits of plesiosaurs, mosasaurs, pterosaurs, and other fossils from the Kansas Chalk, a gallery of marine reptiles and the flying reptiles that shared their world. Newer displays have pushed the collection in unexpected directions, with exhibits on mammalian skulls, the parasites of sharks and rays, and the pre-Columbian archaeology of Costa Rica.

## Millions of Specimens Behind the Scenes

The galleries are only the public face of something far larger. The museum belongs to the University of Kansas Biodiversity Institute, a designated research center devoted to the study of life on the planet. Its collections hold about 11 million plant, animal, and fossil specimens plus more than 2 million archaeological artifacts, and the institute counts among the world's leaders in collection-based research into systematics, evolution, paleobiology, and past cultures. It is also a leader in biodiversity informatics, making collection data digitally accessible and putting that data to work forecasting environmental change. Collections, curators, staff, and students now fill eight buildings across campus. In 2006 and 2007, the Division of Entomology, along with parts of the bird and mammal collections, moved to a new facility on West Campus.

## Flight Context

Dyche Hall stands at 38.9586°N, 95.2439°W on the University of Kansas main campus in Lawrence, in northeastern Kansas. From 2,000 to 2,500 feet AGL, the large university campus makes an easy visual target within the city. Lawrence Regional Airport (KLWC) is only about 3.5 nm north-northeast, making this a short local sightseeing loop. Topeka's Philip Billard Municipal Airport (KTOP) lies about 19 nm west-northwest, and Kansas City International Airport (KMCI) about 32 nm northeast. Clear days offer the best view across the campus.
