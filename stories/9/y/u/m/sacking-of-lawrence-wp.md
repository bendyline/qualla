# Sacking of Lawrence

On May 21, 1856, a pro-slavery posse rode into free-state Lawrence, smashed its newspapers and burned its hotel, as townspeople jeered each failed cannon shot in one of the defining episodes of Bleeding Kansas.

**Location:** 38.9600, -95.2533

**Categories:** Bleeding Kansas, Lawrence, Kansas, 1856 in Kansas Territory, Riots and civil disorder in Kansas, History of racism in Kansas

## Introduction

The first cannonball sailed clean over the roof, and the people of Lawrence cheered. It was May 21, 1856, and a pro-slavery posse had turned a cannon on the Free State Hotel in Lawrence, a town founded two years earlier by anti-slavery settlers from Massachusetts. The next shot struck the walls and did little harm. A keg of powder produced, as one account put it, "a big noise and a big smoke." Every miss drew another shout from the spectators lining the street. The laughter could not last. Before the day was over the hotel was ash, the town's newspapers were wrecked, and the conflict that would become known as Bleeding Kansas had fresh fuel.

## A Posse Gathers

The trouble had been building for weeks. On April 23, Douglas County Sheriff Samuel J. Jones was shot and wounded in Lawrence while trying to arrest free-state settlers, and residents drove him out of town. On May 11, U.S. Marshal Israel B. Donalson issued a proclamation commanding the territory's "law-abiding citizens" to appear at Lecompton in numbers sufficient to execute the law. A grand jury added fuel, declaring that the Free State Hotel had been built for "military occupation and defense, regularly parapeted and portholed, for the use of cannon and small arms." Armed with those words, Jones and Donalson assembled a force of roughly 800 Southern settlers. Their plan was plain: enter Lawrence, disarm its citizens, destroy its antislavery newspapers and level the hotel.

## Banners on Hogback Ridge

On May 21 they closed in. A large force took the high ground at Hogback Ridge, the hill now called Mount Oread, and a cannon was placed to command the area. Jones made his headquarters in the house of Charles L. Robinson, who would later become the first governor of Kansas, and his men guarded every road, and the far side of the river, so no one could slip away. Their flags announced their cause: the state banners of Alabama and South Carolina, a flag of black and white stripes, and inscriptions reading "Southern Rights" and "Supremacy of the White Race." Shalor Eldridge, the hotel's proprietor, went out to meet them and was told the posse would attack only if the town resisted. According to The New York Times, Eldridge then laid on "an elegant dinner" with "costly wines" in hopes of softening the marshal and his men.

## The Price of Surrender

Jones asked to speak with someone for the town. Samuel C. Pomeroy, who with Robinson had led the second group of settlers to the Lawrence site in 1854, agreed to meet him. Jones wanted every weapon in Lawrence surrendered. Pomeroy replied that he had no power to order that, since each citizen would have to give up their own arms, but hoping to send the sheriff away peacefully, he handed over the town's only artillery piece. It did not satisfy Jones. As the Lawrence minister Richard Cordley later told it, the sheriff gave the hotel's occupants until five o'clock to remove their belongings. The cannon he turned on the building was "Old Sacramento," a gun that had been kept at the Liberty Arsenal in Missouri.

## The Happiest Moment

When shot and powder failed, Cordley wrote, Jones "applied the vulgar torch" and burned the hotel to the ground. As its walls fell he shouted, "This is the happiest moment of my life." Meanwhile, the offices of the town's two free-state newspapers, the Kansas Free State and the Herald of Freedom, were wrecked: machinery smashed, type thrown into the river, libraries pitched out the windows, loose copies tossed to the wind or used to help set the hotel ablaze. Jones's men looted the half-deserted town and burned Robinson's home as they left. Yet the human toll was strikingly low. Only one person died, a member of the pro-slavery force, and his death was accidental. For months Lawrence had no free-state newspaper. Josiah Miller chose not to revive the Kansas Free State, and it was November before George Brown restarted the Herald of Freedom.

## A Cannon's Long Road Home

Losing the town's only cannon had consequences. It was at least one reason free-staters attacked the pro-slavery blockhouse at nearby Franklin in June and again in August 1856, hoping to take Old Sacramento for themselves. On August 12, in the Second Battle of Franklin, they succeeded, finding the gun inside Franklin's blockhouse disguised as a dressmaker's mannequin. The gun that had been turned on the Free State Hotel now belonged to the town it had shelled. Afterward a free-stater named Thomas Bickerton scavenged the newspapers' lead type from the river and used it to make cannonballs. The letters Jones's men had thrown away came back as ammunition.

## Flight Context

Lawrence, Kansas, lies at approximately 38.96 N, 95.25 W, astride Interstate 70 between the Kansas and Wakarusa rivers. From 2,500-3,500 feet AGL, look for downtown Lawrence just south of the Kansas River and the University of Kansas campus on Mount Oread, the hill the 1856 posse knew as Hogback Ridge. Lawrence Regional Airport (KLWC) is only about 3.5 nm north-northeast of downtown, so stay alert for local traffic. Philip Billard Municipal Airport (KTOP) in Topeka lies about 18 nm west, and New Century AirCenter (KIXD) about 19 nm east-southeast. Spring thunderstorms and summer haze are common; fall usually brings the clearest days over the Kansas River valley.
