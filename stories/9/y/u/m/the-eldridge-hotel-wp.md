# The Eldridge Hotel

Burned by a pro-slavery posse in 1856 and again by Quantrill's raiders in 1863, the hotel on Massachusetts Street kept rising, and it still carries the name of the abolitionist who refused to quit.

**Location:** 38.9710, -95.2363

## Introduction

Twice in seven years, a hotel at the heart of Lawrence was burned by men who hated what it stood for. The first went down in 1856 under a pro-slavery sheriff's torch. The second fell in 1863 to Confederate guerrillas who left the town in flames. Each time, a hotel rose again. Today the Eldridge Hotel stands at 701 Massachusetts Street in downtown Lawrence, Kansas, and it carries the name of the anti-slavery builder who would not let fire have the last word: Shalor Eldridge.

## A Way Station for Free-Staters

The story begins not in Kansas but in Boston. After Congress passed the Kansas-Nebraska Act, the New England Emigrant Aid Company formed to carry anti-slavery settlers into the new Kansas Territory, where the question of slavery was still up for grabs. In Lawrence, the company put up a temporary way station for these newcomers and called it the Free State Hotel. The name was a banner, and pro-slavery leaders saw the building as a fortress rather than an inn. On May 21, 1856, Douglas County sheriff Samuel J. Jones arrived in Lawrence with a large group of pro-slavery men, and in what became known as the Sack of Lawrence, they burned the Free State Hotel to the ground. For settlers who had come west to make Kansas free, the ruin was a warning. For one of them, it was a challenge.

## Built in Defiance, Burned in Terror

Shalor Eldridge answered by building a new hotel and naming it after himself. The Eldridge House grew into a large brick hotel, among the most prominent buildings in town - which made it a target once more. Before dawn on August 21, 1863, Confederate irregular leader William Quantrill led his raiders into Lawrence, and the Eldridge House was among their first objectives. Guests were still asleep when gunfire and the clatter of hooves filled the streets. A white sheet soon hung from a hotel window, and Quantrill agreed to spare the people inside, though his men marched them out of the building as prisoners before looting and burning it. Across the rest of Lawrence, the raiders showed far less mercy: more than 150 men and boys were killed that morning. Then the torch came out again, and the hotel burned along with the town around it. Seven years after the Free State Hotel fell, Eldridge's building lay in ashes.

## Rising for the Third Time

Eldridge began again. The new hotel, completed in 1866 under the supervision of George W. Deitzler, gave Lawrence back its landmark on Massachusetts Street. It would not stay frozen in time. In 1925 the building underwent a major reconstruction. Its design is credited to the architects Shepard & Wiser and Mont John Green, working in the revival styles popular in the early twentieth century. Decades later, the grand hotel slipped into a quieter life: beginning on July 1, 1970, it served as apartments. The community eventually pushed to bring it back, and in 1985 it was reconverted to a hotel, financed with $1 million from investors and $2 million in city industrial revenue bonds. It joined the National Register of Historic Places on December 1, 1986. A comprehensive renovation beginning in 2004 aimed to restore "its original 1925 grandeur," and the Eldridge reopened in 2005.

## The Guest Who Never Checked Out

A building that has burned, risen and been reborn so many times seems bound to collect ghost stories, and the Eldridge has plenty. Popular legend holds that Shalor Eldridge himself still walks its halls. Most of the tales gather around room 506, which is said to contain the original cornerstone, though some guests insist the elevator is haunted too. A photograph taken in the 1980s is claimed to show the ghost. The legend reached television in 2011, when "The Demon Shadow," the first episode of the third season of the Biography Channel series My Ghost Story, featured the hotel and its supposed spirit, and several books on haunted places have devoted chapters to it. Whatever one makes of the stories, they carry a certain poetry. A man who rebuilt his hotel from ashes might understandably be reluctant to leave it.
