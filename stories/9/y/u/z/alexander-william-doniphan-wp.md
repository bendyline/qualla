# Alexander William Doniphan

A Liberty, Missouri, defense lawyer who refused an order to execute Joseph Smith in 1838, Alexander Doniphan went on to lead a march of nearly 5,500 miles through the Mexican-American War and write the legal code behind New Mexico's Bill of Rights.

**Location:** 39.2428, -94.4239

**Categories:** People from Liberty, Missouri, 1838 Mormon War, American military personnel of the Mexican–American War, Kentucky lawyers, Missouri history

## Introduction

"It is cold blooded murder. I will not obey your order." Alexander William Doniphan, a brigadier general in the Missouri militia, spoke those words in 1838 when his superior ordered him to execute Joseph Smith, founder of the Latter-day Saint movement, along with other church leaders. A drumhead court martial had just declared the prisoners guilty of treason. Doniphan went further than refusing: if the men were executed, he warned, he would hold the officer responsible before an earthly tribunal. The executions never happened, and a lawyer from Liberty, Missouri, became a lasting hero to the Latter-day Saints.

## A Defense Lawyer by Nature

Doniphan was born on July 9, 1808, near Maysville, Kentucky, the youngest of ten children of Joseph and Anne Fowke Doniphan, both Virginians. His father had been a friend of Daniel Boone, and both of his grandfathers fought in the American Revolution. After graduating from Augusta College in 1824, he was admitted to the bar in 1830 and started practicing in Lexington, Missouri, before settling in Liberty. He always worked as a defense attorney, never as a prosecutor, and he was known as a gifted orator. He served in the state legislature as a Whig in 1836, 1840 and 1854. Years later he would take on another famous client, Porter Rockwell, a controversial Latter-day Saint accused of trying to assassinate former governor Lilburn Boggs. Doniphan got the attempted-murder charge dismissed for lack of evidence and arranged a five-minute jail sentence for a jailbreak attempt.

## The Order He Would Not Carry Out

By 1833 about 1,200 Latter-day Saints lived in Jackson County, where their religious beliefs, and their neighbors' fear that they were abolitionists, stirred the anger of earlier settlers. Late that year they were forcibly expelled. Joseph Smith's appeals to the governor went largely unheeded, so church leaders hired Doniphan, Atchison and other lawyers to defend their rights in court. Doniphan helped create a special county in northwestern Missouri for them, but friction continued and led to the Mormon War of 1838. After a clash at Crooked River, Governor Boggs issued his infamous order that the Mormons be "exterminated, or driven from the state." Doniphan, who had worked to avoid the conflict and believed the Saints were largely acting in self-defense, was ordered into the field anyway. After Far West surrendered came General Samuel Lucas's order and Doniphan's refusal. Smith and the others spent the winter in Liberty Jail, and Doniphan defended them in 1839. Joseph and Emma Smith's son Alexander Hale Smith, born in June 1838, was named in Doniphan's honor.

## Nearly Five Thousand Five Hundred Miles

When the Mexican-American War began in 1846, Doniphan became colonel of the 1st Regiment of Missouri Mounted Volunteers. He served in General Stephen W. Kearny's capture of Santa Fe and was left in charge of New Mexico when Kearny departed for California. His men then pushed south, winning the Battle of El Brazito outside modern El Paso and the Battle of the Sacramento River, which opened the way to the city of Chihuahua. At Sacramento they were outnumbered more than four to one yet lost one man killed and eleven wounded, against Mexican losses of 320 dead, 560 wounded and 72 prisoners. The volunteers finally sailed home by way of New Orleans to a hero's welcome, after a march of nearly 5,500 miles that was considered the longest military campaign since the days of Alexander the Great. Kearny also appointed Doniphan to write a code of civil laws, in English and Spanish, for the annexed lands. It still forms the basis of New Mexico's Bill of Rights.

## A Moderate as the Nation Split

Back in Liberty, Doniphan helped found William Jewell College, sitting on its board alongside Rev. Robert James, father of Frank and Jesse James, and he became Clay County's first superintendent of schools. As the Civil War approached, he opposed secession and wanted Missouri neutral. He was himself a slaveholder, and he favored a gradual end to slavery over the immediate emancipation Republicans proposed. After a frustrating peace conference in Washington in February 1861, he declined command of a Missouri State Guard division, turned down high rank in the Union Army, and in 1863 moved to St. Louis for the rest of the war. Lincoln allegedly told him he was the only man whose appearance matched his expectations. Private grief marked those years too. Neither of his sons lived to eighteen, and one, John Thornton, died after mistakenly taking mercury chloride for Epsom salts while seeking relief from a toothache. Doniphan died on August 8, 1887.

## A Hero's Welcome in Salt Lake City

Decades after 1838, an aged Doniphan visited Salt Lake City, by then the center of the main body of Latter-day Saints, and was welcomed and thanked as the man who had saved their prophet's life. His story is still told in church literature. Missouri remembers him too: he was inducted into the Hall of Famous Missourians in 2008, a bronze bust sits in the State Capitol rotunda, and a large bronze statue stands at the Ray County courthouse in Richmond. Missouri Highway 152 between Liberty and Leavenworth is the Alexander Doniphan Memorial Highway, and Liberty named an elementary school for him.
