# Liberty Jail

In the stone-and-oak dungeon of Clay County's jail in Liberty, Missouri, Joseph Smith and his companions spent four winter months after the 1838 Mormon War, and the letters written there became scripture.

**Location:** 39.2485, -94.4208

**Categories:** History of the Latter Day Saint movement, Latter Day Saint movement in Missouri, Defunct prisons in Missouri, Prison museums in the United States, Museums in Clay County, Missouri

## Introduction

A crowd gathered in Liberty, Missouri, to watch a large, heavy wagon arrive from Richmond in late 1838. Some of the onlookers were disappointed at how ordinary the prisoners looked. At the top of the jail's outside stairway, Joseph Smith, founder of the Latter Day Saint movement, paused on the small platform, turned to the crowd, raised his hat and said, "Good afternoon, gentlemen." Then he went inside. He and his companions would spend the coldest part of the Missouri winter in the lower room of the Clay County jail, and that cramped dungeon has held a lasting place in the faith's memory.

## Zion on the Frontier

Smith's followers from Kirtland, Ohio, were among the first settlers in the Kansas City area, arriving in 1831 at Independence, about 15 miles south of Liberty. Smith proclaimed that Independence was the location of the biblical Garden of Eden and that the City of Zion should rise there, and older settlers feared losing political control of the county to the growing number of Mormon arrivals. Tensions turned violent, and in November 1833 the Latter-day Saints were driven from Independence and resettled for a time in Clay County. In 1836 they moved on to Far West, in Caldwell County, created by the state especially for them. Some pushed north into Daviess County, where Lyman Wight ran a ferry at Adam-ondi-Ahman. After Smith proclaimed in May 1838 that this was where Adam had gone after leaving Eden, the county's population exploded to 1,500 within three months.

## A War and an Order

Fearing they would lose control of Daviess County, non-Mormon settlers tried to keep Mormons from voting at Gallatin on August 6, 1838, the first skirmish of the Mormon War. Men were killed and property destroyed on both sides, and vigilantes burned farms, homes and the towns of Gallatin and Millport. In October, Mormon militia fought a state militia unit at Crooked River, and on October 27 Governor Lilburn W. Boggs issued Executive Order 44, declaring that the Mormons "must be treated as enemies, and must be exterminated or driven from the State." General Samuel D. Lucas arrived at Far West with 2,500 militiamen, and on November 1 Smith and several other leaders surrendered. A secret, illegal court martial ordered them shot the next morning. General Alexander Doniphan refused to carry it out, calling it "cold-blooded murder," and the prisoners lived. After a preliminary hearing in Richmond, where they were not permitted to call witnesses, Joseph and Hyrum Smith, Lyman Wight, Alexander McRae, Caleb Baldwin and Sidney Rigdon were committed to Liberty Jail.

## Fourteen Feet Square

The jail was built to hold people fast. Its outer walls were stone, two feet thick. Inside them stood a second wall of hewn oak logs about a foot square, and the foot-wide gap between the two, along with the space above the upper ceiling, was packed with loose rock to discourage digging out. Inside, the rooms measured fourteen and a half feet by fourteen. The guards used the upper room, while the prisoners lived below, under a ceiling six and a half feet high, reached only through an opening in the floor above. Besides that hatch, the room's only openings were two iron-barred windows, two feet wide and just six inches high. For about four months the men slept on the stone floor with a little loose straw and no bedding. Food was scanty and poor, and friends outside were only occasionally able to bring them something wholesome.

## A Baby's Blessing and a Letter to the Church

Visitors eased the confinement. Brigham Young, Heber C. Kimball and Porter Rockwell were among those who came. In February, Mary Smith arrived with her three-month-old son, Joseph F. Smith, and Hyrum named and blessed the baby while still in custody. Emma Smith visited Joseph several times with their children. Rigdon won release in January 1839 after an eloquent self-defense, though threats kept him at the jail until early February. On March 20, 1839, Joseph Smith dictated a letter to Edward Partridge and the church, written down by Caleb Baldwin and Alexander McRae. It pleads with God for help amid the Saints' suffering and speaks of righteous and unrighteous dominion. Parts of it were canonized and are known today in the Latter-day Saint canon as Sections 121, 122 and 123 of the Doctrine and Covenants.

## Whiskey, Horses and a Reconstruction

In April 1839 the prisoners were moved to Gallatin, where a grand jury was preparing charges, and then sent toward Boone County on a change of venue. On April 16, along the way, they were allowed to escape: the guards drank the whiskey the prisoners had bought them, and the sheriff and another guard helped them to horses. They reached Quincy, Illinois, within a week. The jail, which served Clay County for about two decades, was eventually torn down, though the dungeon walls were still visible when a house was built over them. The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints bought the property in 1939, and in 1963 it opened a partial reconstruction inside a museum at 216 North Main, cut away so visitors can look into both rooms. In 2024 the church also acquired the jail's original door, which had belonged to the Community of Christ since after 1888.
