# Excelsior Springs, Missouri

Founded after a farmer's sick daughter recovered on spring water in 1880, Excelsior Springs became a nationally advertised spa town with some of the rarest mineral waters in America, and the place where Harry Truman woke up to learn he had won the 1948 election.

**Location:** 39.3414, -94.2308

**Categories:** Cities in Clay County, Missouri, Cities in Ray County, Missouri, Spa towns in the United States, Mineral springs, 1880 establishments in Missouri

## Introduction

On Election Day 1948, Harry S. Truman spent the night at the Elms Hotel in Excelsior Springs, Missouri, while the returns seemed to show him losing to Thomas E. Dewey. In the early morning his aides woke him with the news that he had, in fact, won. The hotel where Truman learned of his upset stood in a town that owed its own existence to a recovery. Excelsior Springs, about 30 miles northeast of central Kansas City, began with a sick child and a spring bubbling out of a riverbank.

## A Cure From the Riverbank

The valley had already seen one dramatic turn of weather. In 1834, when the Latter-day Saint march called Zion's Camp came through, a fight was about to break out near two forks of the Fishing River when a severe thunderstorm rolled in and put the mob to flight. The town itself came later. In 1880 Opal, the daughter of a Black farmer named Travis Mellion, fell ill with scrofula, a form of tuberculosis. Mellion asked nearby campers for advice, and they pointed him to a spring oozing from the bank of the Fishing River. Water was brought to Opal, and within a few weeks she was noticeably better; she eventually recovered. A farmer named Frederick Kugler began using the water on his rheumatic knees and an old Civil War wound, and he too recovered. Word spread to Rev. John Van Buren Flack, who urged landowner Anthony W. Wyman to plat the land, have the water analyzed and advertise its cures. The spring was named Excelsior, after a popular Longfellow poem, and later renamed Siloam.

## Tents, Trains and a Hotel That Kept Burning

Within a year, nearly two hundred households had sprung up in the valley and on the hillsides, and many newcomers camped in tents or covered wagons. The settlement became a village on February 7, 1881, and a fourth-class city that July, soon filled with hotels, boarding houses, an opera house and livery stables. In 1887 the Chicago, Milwaukee and St. Paul railroad ran its Chicago-to-Kansas City line through town, and the visitors multiplied. A land company bought 1,000 acres around the city, a 1,320-seat Music Hall went up, and at Siloam Spring a pavilion offered benches and hooks for cups, the mugs soon stained by the water's heavy iron. With the railroad as a partner, the city advertised itself nationwide as a resort. The Elms Hotel had a rough start: the second Elms, finished in 1909, was soon destroyed by fire, and the third and present hotel was built in 1912.

## Twenty Springs Under One Town

New springs kept turning up, and W.P. Mason of the Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute analyzed them. He reported that the Siloam and Regent springs carried bicarbonates of iron and manganese, a combination found in only four springs in all of Europe, and that these two were the only ones known in the United States. With 20 separate mineral springs in the area, including saline-sulphur, soda-bicarbonate and lithia waters, Excelsior Springs earned a reputation for having the world's greatest collection of mineral waters. In the 1930s the town gave its waters a grand new home. Between 1936 and 1937, architects Keene & Simpson raised the Hall of Waters over the Siloam and Sulpho-Saline springs, and CBS broadcast its cornerstone ceremony nationally on May 27, 1936. It held a women's bath department, hydrotherapy rooms and a bottling department, and it now serves as city hall.

## Life After the Spa Era

The late 1950s and early 1960s were hard on the spa town as the resort business declined sharply. By 1967 the bottling operation had lost $25,000, and the city decided to put its mineral water past behind it. The history did not vanish, though. The Elms Hotel, the Hall of Waters and several historic districts are listed on the National Register of Historic Places, and the city later licensed the rights to its mineral water to the Excelsior Springs Bottling Company for commercial bottling. In 1970 Michael Douglas filmed his second starring movie, Adam at Six A.M., partly in town, and in 1974 the National Civic League named Excelsior Springs an All-America City.

## A Small City on the Fishing River

Today Excelsior Springs is a city of 10,553 people, counted in the 2020 census, straddling Clay and Ray counties along the East Fork Fishing River. Its downtown, once lined with empty buildings, has been renewed with a quilt shop, an art store and small bistros alongside the antique stores, and two theaters stage everything from professional productions to community shows. The town's notable natives include the artist Donald Judd and the actress Brenda Joyce, who played Jane in five Tarzan films. Lyle Waggoner, a cast member of The Carol Burnett Show and Wonder Woman, spent part of his childhood here, and James Benton Grant, the third governor of Colorado, died here in 1911.
