# Mammoth Spring

Rain that falls on southern Missouri travels an underground river to surface in Arkansas's largest spring, a cold, restless pool whose mouth lies more than 70 feet below the surface.

**Location:** 36.4975, -91.5356

**Categories:** Springs of Arkansas, State parks of Arkansas, National Natural Landmarks in Arkansas, Hydroelectric power plants in Arkansas, Protected areas of Fulton County, Arkansas

## Introduction

You can stand at the edge of Arkansas's largest spring and never actually see it. Its mouth lies more than 70 feet down, at the bottom of a 10-acre pool, and the only evidence of what happens there is the water itself: a harmonic mean flow of about 322 cubic feet per second, welling up at a constant 58°F and spilling over a high stone dam. Mammoth Spring rises in the town that shares its name, in extreme north-central Arkansas, just 500 feet south of the Missouri line. It sits so close to US Highway 63 that drivers can see the pool from the road.

## Rain From Another State

In a sense, this water belongs to Missouri. Precipitation falling across the southern part of that state seeps into the water table and moves through a vast system of passages and cavities in the rock. Together those channels form an underground river, and this pool is where it finally breaks the surface. Nine miles to the northwest, at Grand Gulf State Park in Missouri, a stretch of that hidden river lies open to the sky. A cave there collapsed, leaving a chasm 130 feet deep with a natural bridge arching over it. Dye tests have settled the question of where the water goes: what flows through the bottom of Grand Gulf continues through the surviving cave and emerges here, at Mammoth Spring.

## Among the Giants

Mammoth Spring is a first-magnitude karst spring, and its scale is hard to overstate. Within the Ozark Plateau region, only Big Spring and Greer Spring are comparable in size, and it is often ranked among the largest natural springs in the world. Its water does not linger. From the pool it rushes a few hundred feet down an outlet channel, then merges with the Warm Fork of the Spring River, and the Spring River begins at that meeting. The cold outflow gives the river excellent trout fishing, along with enough current for water sports downstream. In June 1972 the Department of the Interior declared Mammoth Spring a National Natural Landmark.

## Harnessing the Flow

People put the spring to work almost as soon as European settlers arrived, using its steady power to turn a gristmill. The Mammoth Spring Milling Company built a dam and a water-powered mill just downstream of the spring lake's outlet. In 1925 the Arkansas-Missouri Power Company bought the dam and built a hydroelectric plant there, and for nearly half a century the spring's discharge powered the surrounding area. The plant kept supplying electricity until 1972. The dam and hydro plant still stand within the park today, and visitors can tour them.

## From Sale Barn to State Park

Mammoth Spring was approved as a state park in 1957, but the first land was not acquired until late 1966, and most purchases were not complete until 1975. Before the state took that land, roughly where the Welcome Center stands today, local entrepreneur Bert Kenneth Bishop and an associate ran the Mammoth Spring Cattle Sales Barn. Farmers routinely brought livestock and other wares to sell, and one of them, Howard Green, sold home-made walking sticks. Eventually the state legislature voted to condemn the land and join it to the park. The original Frisco Depot, restored in 1971, now holds artifacts and memorabilia from the railroad and the spring area. The park offers a visitor center, picnic areas and walking trails, and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service maintains a fish hatchery nearby.

## Flight Context

Mammoth Spring State Park sits at 36.4975°N, 91.5356°W on the Arkansas–Missouri border, directly beside US Highway 63 and about 500 feet south of the state line. From 2,500–3,500 feet AGL, look for the spring pool and its dam beside US 63 in the town of Mammoth Spring, with the short outlet channel running to the Spring River. Grand Gulf State Park, the collapsed-cave chasm linked to the spring, lies about nine miles northwest in Missouri. Nearby airports include Sharp County Regional (KCVK) near Ash Flat, about 14 nm south, and West Plains Regional (KUNO) in Missouri, about 29 nm northwest. Clear days give the best view of the pool and the Spring River below it.
