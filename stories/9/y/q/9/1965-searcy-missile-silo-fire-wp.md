# 1965 Searcy missile silo fire

On August 9, 1965, fire broke out inside a fueled Titan II silo north of Searcy, Arkansas, during an upgrade project, and 53 civilian workers died underground.

**Location:** 35.3722, -91.8047

**Categories:** Fires in Arkansas, 1965 fires in the United States, 1965 in Arkansas, Titan (rocket family)

## Introduction

Lunch was over. On the afternoon of August 9, 1965, civilian contractors at missile silo 373-4, eleven miles north of Searcy, Arkansas, went back down into a shaft sunk some 150 feet into the White County countryside. Standing in that shaft was a Titan II intercontinental ballistic missile, installed and fueled, though its nuclear warhead had been taken out while the work went on. Soon after the crews returned, fire broke out on Level 2. Smoke poured through the silo, the electrical power failed, and the men below were left with little but the ladders to find their way out. Of the 55 workers in the launch duct that afternoon, only two came out alive.

## A Missile Beneath the Farmland

Silo 373-4 was one of 18 Titan II launch sites scattered across Arkansas, part of the Cold War nuclear arsenal. Completed on July 31, 1962, it was brought online on May 16, 1963, under the 308th Strategic Missile Squadron at Little Rock Air Force Base. By the summer of 1965 the Air Force wanted its silos tougher. Under a program called Project Yard Fence, non-military contractors were strengthening the complex against the possibility of a nuclear attack, and as part of the job the missile's warhead had been disarmed and removed. The rocket itself stayed where it was, fueled and upright in the launch duct, while painters, welders and laborers worked on the levels around it. It was construction work, in the end, and the people doing it were tradesmen rather than soldiers.

## Smoke on Level 2

The fire began on Level 2, and it spread with terrible speed. The Air Force's final findings traced it to a welder whose rod struck a high-pressure hydraulic line, igniting the fluid, though survivor Gary Lay testified that no one was welding there that day. The launch duct filled with smoke almost at once. With visibility gone and the electrical power out, the workers turned to the one escape they knew: the vertical ladders running up the silo toward the surface. Men crowded onto the emergency exit ladders until they jammed, and the smoke overtook the climbers before they could get clear. Most of the victims died from asphyxiation as the fire consumed the air in the enclosed duct. Fifty-three men were killed, the largest loss of life ever suffered at a U.S. nuclear weapons facility.

## The Two Who Took the Passageway

The survivors made a different choice. Hubert Saunders, 59, and Gary Lay, a 17-year-old on his very first day on the job, headed not for the ladders but for a passageway and cable tunnel, which led them toward the silo's command center. Saunders came out with smoke inhalation; Lay suffered second- and third-degree burns. Four Air Force personnel in the control center were unharmed, as were workers on the surface. Above ground, the rescue turned into an afternoon-long struggle. Firefighters battled the blaze, but attempts to open the main silo door to vent the smoke failed, and at first rescuers could not get any deeper than Level 2. The men they were trying to reach had come to work that morning to make a weapons site safer.

## Lessons Written in Granite

Investigators looked beyond the moment of ignition. They cited inadequate ventilation in the launch duct, the lack of an independent power source for the silo's elevator, and the absence of alternative exits as contributing factors. Silo 373-4 itself was refurbished and returned to service, remaining in operation until February 1987. Arkansas would see another Titan II disaster in 1980, an explosion at a site near Damascus. In 1986, a granite monument to the victims of the Searcy fire was dedicated at Little Rock Air Force Base in Jacksonville; it now stands at the Jacksonville Museum of Military History. Closer to the site, a smaller marker and historical plaque stand at the corner of Dewey and Snowden Road, near Arkansas Highway 16 between Pangburn and Searcy, on the country roads that lead back toward the site.

## Flight Context

The silo site lies at approximately 35.372°N, 91.805°W, about 11 miles north of Searcy in White County, Arkansas. A cruise altitude of 3,000-4,500 feet MSL gives a good view of the surrounding farmland and wooded ridges. Use the town of Searcy to the south and Arkansas Highway 16 running north toward Pangburn as your references; the memorial marker stands near Highway 16 between the two towns. Greers Ferry Lake spreads out to the northwest, and the Little Red River winds through the country north of Searcy. Nearby airports include Searcy Municipal (KSRC) in Searcy, about 11 miles south, Little Rock Air Force Base (KLRF, military) near Jacksonville, and Bill and Hillary Clinton National Airport (KLIT) roughly 50 miles to the south-southwest. Summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms are common in central Arkansas.
