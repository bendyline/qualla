# Rush Historic District

Deep in the Arkansas Ozarks along the Buffalo River, the ghost town of Rush preserves the last visible remains of the state's zinc-mining boom: mine openings, waste piles, a smelter and a general store left standing in the hollows.

**Location:** 36.1325, -92.5711

## Introduction

In 1893, visitors to the World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago could stand beside a six-ton lump of zinc they called "Jumbo." It had come out of the ground at Rush, a mining settlement tucked into the valleys where Rush Creek and Clabber Creek run down to the Buffalo River in the Arkansas Ozarks. Today the fairgrounds are long gone, and so are most of the people who once worked these hills. What remains is a ghost town: mine openings and waste piles on the bluffs, and a scatter of weathered buildings that make Rush the last place in Arkansas where the zinc era can still be seen whole.

## Ore Beneath the Hollows

Zinc was found here in 1880, and the discovery set off decades of digging. In 1885 the Morning Star Mine opened, and it would grow into the largest mining operation in the district. A year later came the Rush Smelter, built to separate silver from the zinc ore. The silver never materialized in useful amounts, and the smelter quickly became redundant, an early lesson in how quickly fortunes could turn in these hills. Still, the mines kept working. Rush became an important part of what came to be known as the North Arkansas Lead and Zinc District, a mineral belt that once stretched across the northern part of the state. The district also played a role in bringing railroads and modern infrastructure to a region that had long been remote.

## Wartime Zinc

World War I turned Rush into the center of Arkansas's zinc industry. Military demand for the metal soared, and the mines along Rush Creek answered. Ten mining companies operated 13 developed mines within the district, more than in any other part of the North Arkansas District. At the height of operations, somewhere between 2,000 and 3,000 people lived in the district, enough to support hotels and general stores, among them the Morning Star Hotel and the Hicks Hotel. Mills processed the ore dug from the hillsides. For a few years, this isolated corner of Marion County mattered to a nation at war, and its hollows were busier than they would ever be again.

## The Emptying

The boom did not outlast the war by much. After 1919, the demand that had sustained Rush fell away, and the people went with it. By 1920 the town counted only 344 residents. The Morning Star, long the district's largest operation, held on, but in 1931 the last Morning Star mine closed. The ruins left behind span the years from 1880 to 1940, six decades of work that ended with buildings emptied and mine openings left dark in the hillsides. When the land became part of Buffalo National River, the ghost town came under federal protection, and in 1987 the Rush Historic District was listed on the National Register of Historic Places.

## What the Bluffs Still Hold

Across northern Arkansas, the other old mining districts have mostly vanished, marked now by little more than limited debris. Rush is different. It is the only place left that retains not just its mines but also the buildings, structures and ruins of mining and community life, all standing in the same relationships they held during the boom. The Taylor-Medley General Store still stands, along with the Rush Blacksmith Shop, built in 1925, and the old smelter. Along House Row, wooden residences dating from about 1899 line the way. Ruins mark the sites of the Morning Star Hotel and mill, and of the Hicks Hotel and general store. Spread across about 1,316 acres, the district remains nearly as isolated as it was in its working years, little touched by time and still surrounded by the same Ozark woods.

## Flight Context

The Rush Historic District lies at about 36.133°N, 92.571°W in Marion County, Arkansas, in the lower Buffalo River country southeast of Yellville. From 3,500-5,000 feet MSL, follow the Buffalo River's winding green corridor and pale gravel bars through the forested Ozark hills; Rush sits in the side valleys of Rush Creek and Clabber Creek. The ghost town itself is small and tree-shaded, so the river bends and bluffs are your best references. Nearby airports include Baxter County Airport (KBPK) at Mountain Home, about 15 nm to the north-northeast, and Boone County Airport (KHRO) at Harrison, about 29 nm to the west-northwest. Terrain is rugged and valleys can hold fog on cool mornings.
