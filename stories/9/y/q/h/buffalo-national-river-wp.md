# Buffalo National River

Saved from the dam builders in 1972, the Buffalo became America's first National River, an Ozark stream of about 150 miles of towering bluffs, hidden waterfalls, gravel-bar campsites and Arkansas's only elk herd.

**Location:** 35.9809, -92.8120

**Categories:** Buffalo National River, Rivers of Arkansas, National Park Service areas in Arkansas, National Park Service Wild and Scenic Rivers, Bodies of water of the Ozarks

## Introduction

At Gilbert, a tiny river village in Searcy County, and at Lone Rock farther downstream, the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers once planned to build dams across the Buffalo River. Had they been built, long stretches of the river would have become reservoirs. Instead, on March 1, 1972, Congress made the Buffalo the first National River in the United States, ending the Corps' recurring plans for good. The river still runs free for about 150 miles across northern Arkansas, cutting through the Ozarks beneath towering bluffs, and it remains a favorite of paddlers, campers, hikers and anglers.

## The Fight for a Free River

The river owes its freedom to people who refused to let it go quietly. In the early 1960s Dr. Neil Compton, an avid hiker and canoeist, joined others in organizing against the proposed dams, and in 1962 he became president of the newly formed Ozark Society. Through a vigorous campaign lasting a decade, the society helped stop both dams. The National River designation that followed protects the Buffalo from impoundments, industrial uses and other obstructions that would change its natural character. Congress designated the Buffalo National River Wilderness in 1978, and the park now has nearly 36,000 acres of designated Wilderness. The fight did not end there. In 2012 C&H Hog Farms won a permit to operate on a major tributary, with more than 6,500 hogs and storage for their manure. In 2019 the company reached a deal with the state to shut down, and it completed its closure in January 2020. A 2020 attempt to make a moratorium on such farms permanent was rejected by a legislative panel, but in June 2025 the Arkansas Legislative Council approved rules making the ban on large-scale hog operations in the Buffalo River watershed permanent.

## From the Boston Mountains to the White

The Buffalo begins as the Main Prong of Big Buffalo Creek, high in the Boston Mountains of Newton County. It runs north through Boxley to Ponca, then turns east on a long journey through Newton, Searcy, Marion and Baxter counties to meet the White River. Along the way it descends from the Boston Mountains onto the Springfield Plateau near the historic community of Erbie, then crosses part of the Salem Plateau before its confluence. The lower 135 miles fall within the national river managed by the National Park Service, while the upper reaches in the Ozark National Forest are designated wild and scenic. Experienced paddlers call the 15-mile stretch above Boxley the Hailstone River, a demanding whitewater run floatable only at high water. The upper river is steep and fast, holding most of the whitewater and dramatic karst country of sinkholes, caves, springs and waterfalls beneath sandstone and limestone bluffs more than 500 feet tall. Downstream, the gradient eases and the current slows.

## Hemmed-In Hollow and the Elk of Boxley

The river's most famous hidden treasure lies up a side canyon. A hike of about two-thirds of a mile from the river, up a narrow, boxed-in hollow, ends at Hemmed-In-Hollow Falls, which drops 209 feet and is considered the highest waterfall of its kind between the southern Appalachians and the Rockies. The river itself supports well over 300 species of fish, insects, freshwater mussels and aquatic plants. Larger residents roam the meadows. The park is home to Arkansas's only elk herd, descended from 112 elk that the Arkansas Game and Fish Commission brought from Colorado and Nebraska and released near the river between 1981 and 1985. Today elk grazing the fields of the Boxley Valley, where hunting them is off limits, draw wildlife watchers to the valley.

## Life on the Gravel Bars

For most visitors, the Buffalo is a river to float. Paddlers bring their own canoes or rent from local outfitters, and campers can pitch tents on the gravel bars and sandbars along the water, as well as in developed campgrounds. At Ponca, one of the old low-water bridges still spans the river, forcing every boater to portage around it, and the boyhood home of Beaver Jim Villines stands nearby. Downstream, access points carry names that trace the river's history: Grinders Ferry at the U.S. 65 bridge, Gilbert, Dillards Ferry at the Highway 14 bridge, Buffalo Point and Rushland. The last take-out comes at Buffalo City, outside the park where the Buffalo finally meets the White River. The National Park Service runs the river from headquarters in Harrison.

## Flight Context

This reference point, about 35.98°N, 92.81°W, sits on the middle Buffalo, a few miles upstream of the village of Gilbert. From the air, trace the river's tight meanders through forested Ozark hills; pale gravel bars and sheer bluff faces make its course easy to follow. U.S. 65, which bridges the river at Grinders Ferry, is a good checkpoint. The Buffalo is a unit of the National Park Service, and FAA guidance asks pilots to remain at least 2,000 feet above the surface over national park lands, which also keeps you well clear of the steep ridges and 500-foot bluffs. Boone County Airport (KHRO) at Harrison lies to the northwest, and Baxter County Airport (KBPK) at Mountain Home is to the northeast. Valley fog is common on cool mornings; fall foliage makes for spectacular views.
