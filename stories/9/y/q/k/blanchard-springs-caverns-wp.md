# Blanchard Springs Caverns

Beneath Arkansas's Ozark National Forest, Blanchard Springs Caverns is a still-growing, three-level limestone cave and the only show cave run by the U.S. Forest Service.

**Location:** 35.9637, -92.1793

**Categories:** Caves of Arkansas, Show caves in the United States, Limestone caves, Ozark–St. Francis National Forest, Landforms of Stone County, Arkansas

## Introduction

The water that rises from the cave at Blanchard Springs is 58 degrees Fahrenheit in the heat of an Arkansas July and 58 degrees in the dead of winter. It carries the cave's own temperature with it, because it has come straight out of the cave. Behind the spring, under the hills of the Ozark–St. Francis National Forest in Stone County, lies a three-level cave system still being shaped by that water. Blanchard Springs Caverns is the only tourist cave owned by the U.S. Forest Service, and the only one owned by the federal government outside the National Park System.

## Half-Mile Cave

Local residents knew about the cave by the 1930s and called it Half-Mile Cave. The name undersold it. Systematic exploration began in the 1950s and continued in fits and starts through the 1960s. In 1955 explorers found an incomplete skeleton in the cave; the cause of death could never be determined.

After ten years of development on its first trail, the cave opened to the public in 1973. Surveyors have since mapped about 8 miles of passage, a figure the state now puts at 8.5 miles, making Blanchard the second-longest cave in Arkansas and the largest in volume. All three of its levels can be seen on guided tours.

The cave's management is changing too. In December 2025 Arkansas and the Forest Service signed an agreement to begin designating the complex as Arkansas's 53rd state park, Blanchard Springs State Park, and for the 2026 season Arkansas State Parks has helped run the guided tours, which began May 1, with the Forest Service still overseeing the facilities.

## Two Trails Down

The Dripstone Trail, opened in 1973, winds for about half a mile through the uppermost level. The Discovery Trail followed in 1977, looping through a 1.2-mile section and descending to the lower level, deep underground. It also reaches the Natural Entrance, about 100 feet below the surface at that point, and follows the bed of the stream that carved the cavern, past the Rimstone Dams, natural ridges that hold pools of water along the streambed. In the uppermost level, the small Ghost Room is densely decorated and dominated by huge white flowstone.

For visitors who want more, a "Wild Cave" tour leads into undeveloped passages upstream, beyond where the Discovery Trail ends, letting people see all three levels the way the original explorers did.

## An Ancient Sea, Still at Work

The limestone here was laid down in an ancient sea more than 350 million years ago, and the cave cuts through six rock formations, from the middle Ordovician to the lower Mississippian. Most of the Discovery route runs through Plattin limestone roughly 100 feet thick, while the Dripstone route crosses the Boone Chert, the Cason Shale and the Fernvale Limestone.

The cave formed largely below the water table, which is why its passages have the elliptical cross-sections typical of such caves. Over time its streams dropped from one level to the next. The cave shows five levels of passage development, but the top two have been lost as surface valleys deepened and cut into them.

Blanchard remains a living cave, thanks in part to careful visitors and Forest Service management. Seeping, dripping water still deposits calcite, and the formations keep growing. The Giant Flowstone, one of the largest in the United States, measures 164 feet long, 33 feet wide and 30 feet thick.

## Flight Context

Blanchard Springs Caverns lies at 35.964°N, 92.179°W in the Ozark–St. Francis National Forest of Stone County, about 2 miles off Arkansas Highway 14, a short distance north of Mountain View. The cave itself is underground, so look for the forested hills and hollows of the national forest near Highway 14; from 3,000-4,500 ft AGL the rugged Ozark terrain rolls unbroken in every direction. Mountain View Wilcox Memorial Field (FAA identifier 7M2; no ICAO code) is about 7.5 nm southeast, and Calico Rock Izard County Airport (FAA identifier 37T; no ICAO code) is about 12 nm north. Baxter County Airport at Mountain Home (KBPK) is about 28 nm north-northwest, and Batesville Regional Airport (KBVX) is about 30 nm east-southeast. Mind terrain clearance over the hills; valley fog is common on cool mornings.
