# Emmett Kelly Museum

Housed in the historic Sedan Opera House, the museum honoring hobo clown Emmett Kelly in his Kansas hometown holds a few Kelly mementos, local memorabilia and some 1,500 commemorative Jim Beam bottles.

**Location:** 37.1269, -96.1853

**Categories:** Biographical museums in Kansas, Museums in Chautauqua County, Kansas, Circus museums in the United States, History museums in Kansas

## Introduction

The museum is named for a clown, but its star attraction is whiskey bottles. In Sedan, Kansas, the Emmett Kelly Museum honors the town's native son, the famed hobo clown whose sad-faced "Weary Willie" made him a major attraction of the American circus. Step inside the historic Sedan Opera House, though, and you will find only a few Kelly-related items among the local memorabilia, alongside some 1,500 commemorative Jim Beam bottles, described as the world's largest such collection. Even the museum's founding came with a punchline, and Kelly himself delivered it.

## A Railroad Worker's Son

Emmett Kelly was born in Sedan on December 9, 1898, the son of an Irish railroad worker. His path to the big top began with a pencil. At an early age, his mother enrolled him in a correspondence school for cartooning, and drawing became his first craft. Kelly worked a series of jobs before signing on with a carnival as a painter, and it was there, among the tents and midway lights, that circus life took hold of him. Nobody in the small southeastern Kansas town where he started out could have guessed where that would lead: to the ring of Ringling Brothers, with a character born on paper.

## The Birth of Weary Willie

That character first appeared on Kelly's drawing board in the early 1920s, while he was working for a commercial film company. He was Weary Willie, a down-on-his-luck hobo, and he broke sharply with the traditional look of a circus clown. Instead of a painted grin and slapstick cheer, Willie wore a look of weary defeat, the very picture of a man for whom nothing ever quite worked out. Kelly worked with many circuses over the years but settled at Ringling Brothers and Barnum & Bailey in 1942, remaining there until 1956. He also appeared in the 1952 film The Greatest Show on Earth. Kelly died on March 28, 1979, at his home in Sarasota, Florida, having turned a cartoon sketch into a lasting face of American clowning.

## The Wrong House

Sedan opened its museum honoring Kelly in 1967, and the town's famous son came home for the dedication. What happened next comes down to us in a story told by Larry Runyon. During the ceremony, Kelly leaned over to the mayor and quietly informed him that the town had purchased the wrong house. He had actually been born in the house across the street. The mayor, red-faced, replied, "Don't tell anyone!" It is hard to imagine a more fitting beginning for a museum devoted to a clown whose whole act turned on things going gently, comically wrong. In 1980 the museum moved out of the house entirely and into the historic Sedan Opera House, where it remains today.

## Fifteen Hundred Bottles

Visitors who arrive expecting a shrine to Weary Willie are in for a surprise. The museum houses a mix of local memorabilia along with a few items connected to Kelly, but its main attraction is something else entirely: roughly 1,500 commemorative Jim Beam bottles. The collection was donated by a couple who once took the bottles on tour, and it is described as the largest of its kind in the world. The combination sounds odd until you remember what small-town museums often are, a gathering place for whatever the community has chosen to keep. In Sedan, that means a famous clown, the town's own history and a remarkable array of decanters, all under the roof of an old opera house. The museum keeps seasonal hours, with off-season visits arranged by phone.
