# Whizbang, Oklahoma

Officially named Denoya because the post office found its real name undignified, the Osage County oil town of Whizbang boomed to 10,000 people in the 1920s, earned a reputation as Oklahoma's rowdiest, and faded to foundations and sidewalks.

**Location:** 36.7676, -96.7076

**Categories:** Ghost towns in Oklahoma, Unincorporated communities in Osage County, Oklahoma, Populated places within the Osage Nation reservation, Oil boom towns, History of Oklahoma

## Introduction

The post office would not call it Whizbang. The name was not dignified enough, so on paper the town became Denoya, after a prominent French and Osage family. Everyone else called it Whizbang, and the undignified name suited the place. Born almost overnight in 1921 in the oil fields of Osage County, about a mile and a half north and a mile and a half west of present-day Shidler, the Whizbang area grew at its peak to some 10,000 people and 300 businesses. Among all of Oklahoma's oil field towns, it was considered the rowdiest.

## Six Hundred Barrels a Day

Whizbang sits within the former Osage Indian Reservation, whose boundaries matched those of Osage County. In 1921, E.W. Marland drilled a well here that produced 600 barrels a day, and the strike set off an oil rush. The crude was superb in both quality and quantity, and people poured in to get their share. Some of those who lived and worked near Whizbang in its heyday would become famous. Local accounts place Clark Gable, not yet an actor, in the oil fields as a roustabout. The oilmen E.W. Marland and Frank Phillips were active here too, and Ben Johnson Jr., a ranching family's son born in nearby Foraker, would become a rodeo star and an Oscar-winning actor. Nobody knows for certain where the name came from. One theory points to Captain Billy's Whiz Bang, a bawdy cartoon and joke magazine popular at the time. Another holds that Osage residents imitated the sound of the oil pumps and engines by saying "whizbang." A third, the one that best matches the town's reputation, claims people "whizzed all day and banged all night." Whichever is true, the name captured something the official Denoya never could.

## The Law at Pistol Hill

Whizbang was a violent place. The bank was robbed twice, and by one account "it wasn't safe for a woman to be on the streets after dark." Highway robbers regularly held up travelers on the road to Shidler at a spot called Pistol Hill. To keep the peace, the oil companies hired a Texan who called himself Jose Alvarado; his real name was Bert Bryant, and he reportedly had ridden with Pancho Villa in Mexico. Opinions of him ranged from "cold-blooded killer" to "Robin Hood." By local lore, he was once arrested for stealing $2,500 from a brothel owner, but he returned the money and was acquitted. In another story told about him, he got into a gunfight with a lawman from a neighboring town over a woman. The other lawman killed her and shot Alvarado in the chest; her name does not survive in this telling. In the melee that followed, Alvarado shot the man four times and had both of his own legs broken by bullets. Both men survived, ended up in the same hospital and became friends.

## Sidewalks to Nowhere

The boom was brief. When the petroleum reserves ran out in the late 1920s, Whizbang declined, and the post office that had opened in 1921 closed in 1942. Today the rubble and remains of the town can easily be seen from the road. Sidewalks still run parallel to it, leading past building foundations where businesses once stood, and a few occupied houses remain nearby. For a town that once held 10,000 people, it is a quiet ending.
