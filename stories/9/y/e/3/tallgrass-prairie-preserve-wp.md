# Tallgrass Prairie Preserve

In the thinly peopled Osage country that pilots call the Black Hole, The Nature Conservancy's Tallgrass Prairie Preserve protects the world's largest tract of tallgrass prairie, kept alive by fire and grazed by a herd of more than 2,500 bison.

**Location:** 36.8420, -96.4190

**Categories:** Nature Conservancy preserves, Grasslands of the North American Great Plains, Protected areas of Osage County, Oklahoma, Prairies, Osage Nation, Bison

## Introduction

Pilots crossing northeastern Oklahoma after dark have a name for The Osage: the Black Hole. So few people live here that the ground below them simply goes dark. By daylight that emptiness turns out to be a gift. Near Foraker in Osage County, the Joseph H. Williams Tallgrass Prairie Preserve spreads across 39,650 acres owned by The Nature Conservancy, plus another 6,000 acres leased. It is the largest protected tract of tallgrass prairie in the world, a surviving piece of the grassland that once stretched across the Great Plains from Texas to Manitoba, and it is still grazed by bison.

## Too Rocky to Plow

The preserve lies at the southern end of the Flint Hills, also known as the Osage Hills, a band of rocky, rolling prairie that runs from northern Kansas into Oklahoma. Limestone lies exposed across much of it, and that stone is the reason the prairie survived. It made cultivation difficult, so the Flint Hills stayed much as they were when the Wichita, Osage and Kaw hunted here. Oklahomans call the region "The Osage," after the Native nation that occupies this territory and the county named for it. The landscape is not all grass. The well-timbered Salt Creek and its tributaries cut through the preserve, and its eastern portion reaches into the Cross Timbers, a north-south belt of tangled oak forest that was a major obstacle to early travelers heading west.

## Grass That Needs Fire

The tallgrass prairie owes its existence to fire, whether sparked by lightning or set by people. Without it, the prairie quickly turns to brushland. Native Americans understood this and burned the prairie regularly, nurturing new growth of succulent grasses and killing the trees and shrubs that would otherwise invade. The Nature Conservancy has continued the practice through what it calls patch burning, setting fire to about one-third of the prairie each year. The results show up in the animals. Bison graze the new green grass in areas burned the previous fall, cattle benefit from the fresh forage, and the burning has improved habitat for the threatened greater prairie-chicken, which lives on the preserve in small numbers.

## The Herd Comes Home

Before The Nature Conservancy bought it in 1989, much of the preserve lay within the Barnard Ranch. In 1993, the Oklahoma oilman Kenneth Adams donated 300 bison, and the herd has since grown to more than 2,500 animals grazing 21,000 acres. They are the preserve's most prominent attraction. Each fall the bison are rounded up and the surplus sold, while cattle graze another 11,000 acres. Around them grows a remarkable diversity: 755 plant species, many unique to the tallgrass prairie, and more than 300 species of birds. Its forest trees include several species of oak, along with cottonwoods, ash, red cedar, elm and sycamore.

## What Lies Beneath the Grass

The preserve sits on land that was once the Osage Indian Reservation. When the Osage were forced to accept the allotment of their communal land to individual households, they kept the sub-surface mineral rights to all of their former lands. Members of the nation hold what are known as Osage headrights, entitling them to a share of the revenue from mineral leases. In the early 20th century, oil was discovered, and by the 1920s and 1930s the Osage were among the richest people per capita in the world. That wealth made them targets. In what became known as the Reign of Terror, Osage people were harassed, manipulated, defrauded and murdered as white people tried to seize control of their family members' headrights. Oil production has since declined, but more than 100 wells still pump on the preserve, and the bison graze among them. The land also has a Hollywood thread. Ben Johnson Sr., foreman of the Chapman-Barnard ranch, was a rodeo champion, and so was his son, Ben Johnson Jr., who appeared in dozens of movies and won an Oscar for The Last Picture Show.

## Flight Context

The preserve centers on approximately 36.84°N, 96.42°W in Osage County, north of Pawhuska, Oklahoma. From 2,000-3,000 ft AGL, the open, rolling grassland is broken by the wooded line of Salt Creek and its tributaries, with the Cross Timbers' oak woodland toward the east; in seasons after the annual patch burns, the burned blocks may show as darker patches in the grass, and bison herds can sometimes be picked out. At night the region lives up to its nickname, the Black Hole, with very few ground lights, so plan night flights here with care. Nearest airports: Pawhuska Municipal (FAA identifier H76, no ICAO code), about 10 nm south, and Bartlesville Municipal (KBVO), about 20 nm east-southeast.
