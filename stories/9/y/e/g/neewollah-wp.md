# Neewollah

Independence, Kansas, spelled Halloween backward in 1919 to steer its children away from pranks, and the result became a nine-day festival that swells a town of under 8,600 people to about 75,000.

**Location:** 37.2259, -95.7244

**Categories:** Festivals in Kansas, Halloween, Independence, Kansas, Parades, Tourist attractions in Montgomery County, Kansas

## Introduction

Spell Halloween backward and you get Neewollah, a word that sounds like a lost kingdom and began, in fact, as a plan. In 1919 the people of Independence, Kansas, looked at the pranks that arrived every October 31 and decided their children deserved something better to do with the night. More than a century later, that idea has become one of the largest annual celebrations in Kansas, a nine-day festival that swells a town of just under 8,600 residents to about 75,000 people in its closing days.

## A Queen Chosen by the Penny

The first Neewollah was a day of parades, held morning, afternoon and night on October 31. Decorated cars and carriages rolled through town, carrying queens and princesses from other area festivals. Alongside them rode Queen Neelah, whose crown was a matter of small change: votes were bought at a penny each. The celebration did not run unbroken. The Great Depression and then World War II interrupted it. In 1958 four local businessmen revived Neewollah, elaborated on its theme and brought it back as a three-day celebration. It kept growing from there. Today the festival stretches across nine days at the end of October, and it rests on the efforts of more than 500 community volunteers who carry it through to the last night of music on the bandstand.

## Five Deep by Eleven O'Clock

The Grand Parade is the centerpiece, and the town treats it that way. In the wee hours of parade day, spectators are already setting out chairs along the front row of the route. The parade runs about two hours. It starts at the corner of Penn and Chestnut, moves from Memorial Hall south on Penn Avenue and east to Sixth, drops south a block to Maple, turns west to Ninth, and makes its final run north on Ninth Street, ending just short of Independence Middle School. By the time the "great fly over" arrives at 11 a.m., the crowd stands five deep in many places along the way. The show spills beyond the route itself. Free street acts work the crowd, the carnival hums, and food concessions line Penn Avenue both before and after the procession passes.

## Floats, Drill Teams and Bearded Beauties

Neewollah's founders wanted to give children a better October, and the Kiddie Parade keeps that promise most directly. Organized years ago for the children of Independence and the surrounding area, the Parade of the Children has grown to more than 1,000 young participants. In recent years as many as 14 middle school bands from across eastern Kansas have marched in it, and families and organizations have built more than thirty floats in past parades. Then the grown-ups get their turn. The Doo Dah Parade is Neewollah's adults-only event, a Mardi Gras-style procession running from Memorial Hall to just south of Penn and Main. Some years bring an overall theme; other years it is a free-for-all. A King and Queen Doo Dah, chosen each year, preside over a parade of rowdies liberally sprinkled with odd drill teams, bearded beauties and unusual creatures.

## Brass on the Last Saturday

On the last Saturday of October, the music moves to the football field used by Independence Community College and the high school. The Neewollah Marching Festival is one of the longest-running festivals in the state or area, and its entry rule ties it to the rest of the celebration: a band that wants to compete must first march in the Grand Parade. Under regulations approved by KSHSAA, any band can compete for top honors in a Best of Class category, split into four groups by the size of the band rather than the size of its district. Trophies go to the outstanding drum major, color guard and percussion line in each class, and the grand champion earns one of its own. Around town, the calendar fills with a chili cook-off, a hometown musical, a queen's pageant, an arts and crafts show, a Great Pumpkin contest and a fun run, while more than 30 food vendors, a large carnival and a nightly bandstand crowd the downtown.

## Madge's Crown

The festival has even found its way into American drama. William Inge, born in Independence on May 3, 1913, received the Pulitzer Prize in 1953 for his play Picnic, and in it the character Madge is said to have been voted Queen of Neewollah. It is a quiet nod from a playwright to his hometown's backward holiday, tucked into a work that carried his name far beyond Kansas. The honor suits a celebration that began as a way to keep children out of mischief and ended up defining a town's autumn. Every October the parade chairs go out before dawn, the bands tune up, and Independence turns its Halloween inside out on purpose.
