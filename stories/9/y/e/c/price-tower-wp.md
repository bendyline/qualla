# Price Tower

A Manhattan skyscraper design shelved by the Depression finally rose in 1956 on the Oklahoma prairie, where Frank Lloyd Wright talked a pipeline builder into a 19-story copper-trimmed tower.

**Location:** 36.7475, -95.9761

**Categories:** Frank Lloyd Wright buildings, National Historic Landmarks in Oklahoma, Modernist architecture in Oklahoma, Bartlesville, Oklahoma, 1956 establishments in Oklahoma

## Introduction

Harold C. Price wanted a modest headquarters: two or three stories, with room to park ten trucks. Frank Lloyd Wright wanted twenty-five stories. Wright allegedly told him, "I'm going to give you the building I've been trying to build for 35 years," and by Price's own account, "We finally compromised on nineteen floors." The result, the Price Tower in Bartlesville, Oklahoma, stands 221 feet to the tip of its spire, is the only skyscraper Frank Lloyd Wright ever built, and rises from a small city about 47 miles north of Tulsa.

## A Tower from the Bowery

The design began in New York. In October 1927 William Norman Guthrie, rector of St. Mark's Church in-the-Bowery and Wright's friend since at least 1908, wrote about building a high-rise to ease the church's financial troubles. After a year of haggling over fees, with Guthrie asking Wright to waive all but $150 of a $7,500 fee, Wright sketched the towers in December 1928. Unlike Manhattan's stepped skyscrapers, his buildings resembled inverted cones, with floors divided into quadrants around a pinwheel-shaped core and laid out on a grid of 30-60-90-degree geometry. The project was canceled during the Great Depression. Wright kept refining the idea for two decades, and echoes of it appeared in the research tower of the Johnson Wax Headquarters in Racine, Wisconsin, completed in 1950.

## The Tree That Escaped the Forest

Sources disagree on how the Prices found Wright. Some credit the architect Bruce Goff, then chair of the University of Oklahoma's architecture school, whom Price's son Joe had asked for advice; others credit Harold's wife, Mary Lou, or say his sons heard Wright lecture at the university. Harold Sr. doubted Wright would care about his pipeline-construction company, but his sons argued that Wright would cost no more than any architect drawing a generic box. Joe later recalled it took Wright two hours just to talk his father up to twelve stories, and the bargaining went on from there. Wright believed skyscrapers belonged in rural areas, where they would stand out from the surrounding landscape, and he titled his book about the building The Story of the Tower: The Tree That Escaped the Crowded Forest.

## Copper, Concrete and a Pinwheel

Groundbreaking came on November 13, 1953. Wright's son-in-law and apprentice, William Wesley Peters, served as his representative on site, and the work pushed through material shortages, a labor strike and subcontractor bids so high that one firm offered to install the exterior copper for $450,000. Reinforced-concrete floors were cantilevered outward from a pinwheel core of four piers, and by late 1954 crews were completing one story every 12 days. The finished tower wears embossed copper spandrels and louvers, tinted glass and poured stucco. Inside, the rentable space was split into one residential quadrant and three office quadrants, originally reached by separate lobbies and elevators, and the top three stories held a penthouse apartment and office for the Price family. Wright, who added the apartments at the Prices' request, saw the building as a model for mixed-use towers in smaller American towns.

## Thirteen Thousand Sightseers

The tower opened in February 1956, with Bartlesville residents given the first look before the public came that weekend; 13,000 sightseers turned out. Nobody could agree on the price. The Price family cited $6.5 million, while estimates ranged from $2.4 million to $13 million. Harold Price, who had said "it was not our intent to build a monument," still put the building on the cover of his company newsletter. As a business, it was less of a triumph. Two years after opening, two of the eight apartments stood empty, and Joe Price said that even fully occupied the tower would earn only $24,000 a year. Bruce Goff moved into an apartment spanning the ninth and tenth floors and later recalled times when he was the only resident. A sundial went up beside the southwest corner in 1961, and by 1972 every apartment except the Price penthouse had been turned into offices.

## An Icon Changing Hands

Phillips Petroleum bought the tower in 1981, reportedly for $2.5 million, preserved its interior decorations and moved out by the mid-1980s. In 1983 the building received the American Institute of Architects' Twenty-five Year Award, and it has been designated a National Historic Landmark. Phillips donated it in 2001 to the Price Tower Arts Center, which opened a museum inside and a boutique hotel and restaurant on the upper stories. Recent years have been rockier. The tower was sold in 2023 and closed in 2024 amid financial problems and legal disputes, then resold in 2025 to McFarlin Building LLC, which is renovating it into a hotel and residences, with a reopening planned for 2027. Through every owner, the copper-trimmed exterior has remained intact, still standing alone above the low rooftops of Bartlesville as Wright intended.

## Flight Context

The Price Tower stands at 36.748°N, 95.976°W at 510 South Dewey Avenue in downtown Bartlesville, Oklahoma, about 47 miles north of Tulsa. At 221 feet to the tip of its spire, with its copper trim, it towers over the surrounding low-rise downtown and is easy to spot from 2,000-4,000 feet above ground. Bartlesville Municipal Airport (KBVO) serves the city, and Tulsa International Airport (KTUL) lies to the south. Northeastern Oklahoma generally has good visibility, but spring thunderstorms can be severe.
