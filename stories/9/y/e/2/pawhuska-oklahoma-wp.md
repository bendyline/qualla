# Pawhuska, Oklahoma

Named for the Osage chief White Hair and seat of the Osage Nation since 1872, Pawhuska saw oil leases auctioned for fortunes under a single elm, and today draws visitors for its history, its films and its famous Mercantile.

**Location:** 36.6692, -96.3331

**Categories:** Cities in Oklahoma, County seats in Oklahoma, Cities in Osage County, Oklahoma, Osage Nation, Oil boom towns

## Introduction

Beside the Osage Council House in Pawhuska stood a tree the town called the Million Dollar Elm. During the Osage oil boom of the 1910s and 1920s, bidders gathered in its shade for big-money public auctions of oil and gas leases, and the fortunes won there reshaped the town, the tribe and, in time, the nation's memory of what that wealth cost. Pawhuska has been the home of the Osage tribal government since 1872, and the story of the Osage people runs through every chapter of the town's life.

## White Hair's Town

The town takes its name from Paw-Hiu-Skah, a 19th-century Osage chief whose name means White Hair; in Osage it is written hpahúska. It began in 1872 as a place called Deep Ford, established along with the reservation for the Osage Nation in Indian Territory. The Osage Indian Agency stood beside Bird Creek, one of the tribe's three main bands settled here, and the tribal government opened its offices in the new town, where it remains today. Traders followed, building stores in 1872 and 1873. In 1875 George Edward Tinker, an Osage, founded Pawhuska's first newspaper, the Indian Herald, also known as the Wah-Sha-She News. His son, Clarence L. Tinker, became the first Native American major general in U.S. Army history and the namesake of Tinker Air Force Base. A post office opened in 1876.

## Boom Years on Bird Creek

Oil transformed Pawhuska. During the boom the population grew to 6,414 by 1920, and in 1923 the Atchison, Topeka and Santa Fe Railroad extended its line to town from Owen, in Washington County. Minor league baseball arrived briefly too: the Pawhuska Huskers played from 1920 to 1921, and the Pawhuska Osages lasted part of the 1922 season before folding. The good times did not hold. As the oil boom faded and the Great Depression set in, the population began a long decline that has continued ever since, reaching 2,984 at the 2020 census.

## A Fortune That Drew Killers

Osage wealth also attracted terrible greed. Oil royalties were paid to Osage people through headrights, which could not be bought or sold, only inherited, and outsiders schemed to obtain them by marrying into Osage families or becoming their legal guardians. Between 1918 and 1931, Osage County saw a series of murders of Osage people aimed at seizing that oil money, with the worst of it from 1921 to 1926, the period remembered as the Reign of Terror. The killings became one of the first major investigations of the Bureau of Investigation, later renamed the FBI. Nearly a century later, Martin Scorsese's 2023 film Killers of the Flower Moon told the story. It was filmed in and around Pawhuska, with downtown Kihekah Avenue dressed to stand in for 1920s Fairfax.

## Scouts, Schools and a Museum

Pawhuska claims an unusual first. In May 1909, the Rev. John F. Mitchell, an English missionary priest at St. Thomas Episcopal Church, organized a Boy Scout troop, months before the Boy Scouts of America was founded in 1910. Whether it was the first troop in the country is debated, but the troop celebrated its centennial in 2009 with a mini-jamboree that drew more than 300 Scouts from across the United States. Osage culture is kept alive here in deliberate ways. The Osage Nation Museum, founded in 1938, is the oldest tribally owned museum in the United States, and Daposka Ahnkodapi, a private Osage language immersion school for preschool through seventh grade, opened in 2015 and earned accreditation in 2021.

## Prairie Town, Screen Town

Pawhuska sits 57 miles northwest of Tulsa, with the Tallgrass Prairie Preserve stretching away to the north, and filmmakers have long found it useful. In 1930, prairie and oilfield scenes for Cimarron were filmed here, and Tracy Letts' Pulitzer Prize-winning play August: Osage County is set in a country house near the town, with the 2013 film version partly shot in Pawhuska. The actor Ben Johnson and the Osage historian and author John Joseph Mathews count among the town's notable people. Lately the crowds come for food as well. On October 31, 2016, Ree and Ladd Drummond opened The Pioneer Woman Mercantile, a shop, deli and bakery in a historic downtown building, and fans of Ree Drummond's television show have been finding their way to this small prairie town ever since.

## Flight Context

Pawhuska lies at 36.669°N, 96.333°W in Osage County, northeastern Oklahoma, along Bird Creek about 57 miles northwest of Tulsa. From 2,500-4,500 feet the town sits among the rolling, wooded hills of the Osage country; to the north, the open grassland of the Tallgrass Prairie Preserve makes a striking contrast with surrounding ranchland. Bartlesville Municipal Airport (KBVO) lies to the northeast, and Tulsa International Airport (KTUL) is the major airport to the southeast. Visibility is generally good, but spring brings frequent severe thunderstorms to northeastern Oklahoma.
