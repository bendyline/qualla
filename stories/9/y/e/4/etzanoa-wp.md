# Etzanoa

Beneath Arkansas City, Kansas, lie the remains of Etzanoa, a Wichita city of grass houses and granaries that Spanish explorers called the Great Settlement and that may once have been home to 20,000 people.

**Location:** 37.0675, -97.0111

**Categories:** Archaeological sites in Kansas, Wichita tribe, Native American history of Kansas, Plains Village period, Spanish exploration

## Introduction

In 2017, a teenager with a metal detector found a cannonball near Arkansas City, Kansas. It had been fired in 1601, during a clash between Spanish soldiers and Native warriors, and its discovery helped pin down the location of a city the Spanish had described and history had largely forgotten. An Escanjaque captive named Miguel, drawing a map of the region for the Spanish, called it Etzanoa; the Spanish called it the Great Settlement. It was a city of the Wichita people. Between about 1450 and 1700 it flourished near the Arkansas River, and it may have been home to as many as 20,000 people.

## Beyond Quivira

Etzanoa belongs to the larger story of Quivira, the name Francisco Vásquez de Coronado gave to the Wichita settlements he visited in central Kansas in 1541. The people of Etzanoa were Wichita as well, a group the Spanish later called the Rayados. They are believed to have spoken a Caddoan language, and their grass houses, dispersed settlement, chief's title and granaries all match what is known of the Wichita, and archaeologists and historians generally regard them as a Wichita sub-tribe, related to the people Coronado met sixty years before. Not every scholar agrees; the linguist Nancy Parrott Hickerson identified them instead as "Jumanos," which seems to have been a generic term for Plains peoples with painted or tattooed faces.

## The Lone Survivor's Report

The first Spanish description apparently came from the sole survivor of an ill-fated expedition. In 1594 or 1595, Antonio Gutierrez de Umana and Francisco Leyba de Bonilla led the first known expedition onto the plains of Oklahoma and Kansas in more than fifty years. Its lone survivor was Jusepe Gutierrez, an Indigenous man from Mexico. After traveling east and north from New Mexico for more than a month, Jusepe said, the party found a "very large settlement" stretching more than ten leagues, about 26 miles, along a river and two leagues wide. Its straw-roofed houses stood close together, with fields of maize, squash and beans between the clusters. The people were numerous, he reported, yet they received the Spanish peacefully and supplied them with abundant food, while a "multitude" of bison grazed the surrounding country.

## Oñate Among the Striped People

In 1601 Juan de Oñate, founder and governor of New Mexico, followed that trail with Jusepe as his guide, more than 70 Spanish soldiers and priests, an unknown number of Indigenous soldiers and servants, and 700 horses and mules. On the plains he met the Escanjaques, enemies of the people Oñate called Rayados, meaning "striped," for their painted or tattooed faces. Near a river, probably the Arkansas, a few hundred Rayados appeared on a hill, throwing dirt into the air as a sign they were ready for war. Oñate made peace, and he found them "united, peaceful, and settled" and generous. They deferred to their chief, Catarax, whom Oñate nonetheless detained as a guide and hostage. Catarax led the Spanish across the Arkansas to the settlement, and they found it deserted: more than 1,200 round, grass-thatched houses, surrounded by large granaries for corn, beans and squash.

## Two Hours of Battle

Oñate restrained the Escanjaques from looting the town and sent them home. The next day his army marched eight miles through the settlement without seeing many Rayados, but word came that they were gathering an army. Catarax, who had been chained, was rescued by the Rayados in a bold raid. Oñate estimated that he would need 300 soldiers to confront them, and he turned back toward New Mexico. The attack, when it came, was apparently from the Escanjaques. Oñate claimed many of them were killed, but many of his own soldiers were wounded, and after two hours he broke off the fight and retreated. The Rayados' flight from their homes on Oñate's arrival may suggest they had already had unfavorable dealings with the Spanish.

## A City Rediscovered

Wichita State University archaeologist Donald Blakeslee has led the recent research. In 2013, historians at the University of California, Berkeley, retranslated the early Spanish accounts, and the clearer text let Blakeslee match descriptions to sites, including the 1601 battle ground in Arkansas City. Archaeologists have identified more than a dozen large settlements along six miles of the Walnut River, and a 2020 aerial survey found a probable ceremonial site marked by a ditch about two meters wide and 50 meters across. The Rayados likely left in the early 18th century, much reduced, possibly by European diseases, warfare and the trade in enslaved Native people. Their descendants were absorbed into the Wichita tribe. Tours are arranged through the Cherokee Strip Land Rush Museum in Arkansas City, and in June 2026 the Etzanoa Conservancy opened an Etzanoa Immersion Center there, with displays, virtual reality tours and nature trails.

## Flight Context

Etzanoa's remains lie in and around Arkansas City, Kansas, near 37.068°N, 97.011°W, where the Walnut River meets the Arkansas River just north of the Oklahoma line. There is little to see of the ancient settlement from the air, but from 2,000-4,000 feet the wooded bends of the Walnut River east of town trace the six-mile stretch where more than a dozen large Wichita settlements once stood. Arkansas City Regional Airport (KARK) serves the city, and Strother Field (KWLD) lies to the north between Arkansas City and Winfield. Visibility is typically good, with strong south winds and spring thunderstorms common.
