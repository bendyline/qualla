# Free Will Baptist Church of Pennytown

Built block by block by a congregation that could afford only a few tiles at a time, this 1925 church is all that remains of Pennytown, a Missouri hamlet founded by a formerly enslaved man who insisted his neighbors hold clear title to their land.

**Location:** 39.0164, -93.1797

**Categories:** African-American churches, African-American history, Freedmen's towns, Baptist churches in Missouri, Churches on the National Register of Historic Places in Missouri, Buildings and structures in Saline County, Missouri

## Introduction

The tile blocks came a few at a time. After fire destroyed their wood-frame church in 1924, the members of Pennytown's Free Will Baptist congregation had no money to build a new one all at once, so over a period of months they bought blocks as money allowed and stacked them on the empty site. In the fall of 1925 the foundation finally went in, and over the months that followed, the men of the community and the church did most of the building themselves. That patient little building, eight miles south of Marshall in Saline County, Missouri, is today all that remains of the town that built it.

## Eight Acres and a Deed

Pennytown began with one man's purchase. Joseph Penny, a formerly enslaved man, moved from Kentucky to Saline County, Missouri, shortly after the Civil War. Around 1871 he bought an eight-acre tract for $160, intending it as a place of refuge and new beginnings for people newly freed from slavery. He divided the land into building lots and sold them to arriving families at affordable prices. Other freedpeople bought small tracts around the village, and by 1879 the community had grown to about forty families on sixty-four acres. By 1880 roughly 200 African Americans lived in Pennytown, which had a school, two churches, a blacksmith shop and other businesses. Penny's most important contribution may have been a rule. He insisted that residents hold clear legal title to their property, and as a result Pennytown largely escaped the land speculators and unscrupulous neighbors who preyed on other freedmen's settlements.

## A Village That Worked Together

Owning their land meant Pennytown's families could avoid the pitfalls and abuses of sharecropping that befell so many formerly enslaved people after the war. The men earned wages as laborers on area farms and in Marshall, and most of the women worked too, as domestic help. With the working-age adults gone most of the day, the older residents cared for the community's children together, a shared arrangement that held the village's days in place. Faith had its own improvised geography. Until 1886, services were held in residents' homes or, when the weather allowed, under brush arbors nearby. That year a white neighbor donated a half-acre plot for a house of worship, and a simple wood-frame church went up on the spot where the current building stands. A natural pond beside it served for baptisms during the church's active years.

## Carbide Light and Empty Lots

When the tile-block church was finished, a large carbide lamp hung from the ceiling to light services; later, kerosene lamps were added in holders along the walls. But the village around it was already beginning to thin. In the 1920s more farms turned to machinery, and the demand for hired labor that had sustained Pennytown's families shrank. The Great Depression of the 1930s wiped out much of the remaining work in and around the rural community. The last family moved away in 1943, leaving only a few elderly residents, who stayed until their deaths. By the 1980s the church was in serious disrepair. Vandals had broken its few windows, and hard Missouri weather had badly damaged the roof and foundation.

## Coming Home in August

Pennytown's people had scattered across the Midwest and the nation, but they had not let go. Shortly after the end of World War II, former residents began gathering for an annual Homecoming, a tradition that continues on the first weekend of August for former residents and their descendants. The church was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1988, largely through the efforts of historian and former Pennytown resident Josephine Robinson Lawrence. By 1996 its restoration was largely complete: a new foundation, new windows and doors, the original tile blocks reconstructed, a new roof, and an interior refurbished with paint, drywall and a new wood plank floor. The volunteer Friends of Pennytown Historic Site maintains the building today, and a large sign on the property tells the story of the community and its church, and of the man who made sure his neighbors owned the ground beneath their homes.

## Flight Context

The church stands at approximately 39.02°N, 93.18°W in rural Saline County, Missouri, about 8 miles south of Marshall. It is a small single building amid farmland and will be very hard to spot; from 1,500–2,500 feet AGL, use Marshall to the north as your reference and look for an isolated structure beside a small pond. Nearby airports: Marshall Memorial Municipal (KMHL), about 5 nm north-northwest; Sedalia Regional (KDMO), about 19 nm south. Best viewed in clear weather with good ground contrast.
