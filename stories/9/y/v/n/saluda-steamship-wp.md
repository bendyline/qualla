# Saluda (steamship)

On Good Friday 1852, the paddle steamer Saluda, carrying Mormon emigrants from England and Wales up the Missouri, exploded at Lexington, Missouri, killing at least 75 people (some accounts say more than 100), and the town took in the survivors and raised the orphans as its own.

## Introduction

The paddlewheel never finished its second turn. On Good Friday morning, April 9, 1852, the steamer Saluda pushed away from the landing at Lexington, Missouri, and before the wheel had come around twice, her boilers exploded. The blast could be heard for miles. People watching from the bluffs above the river saw pieces of the boat and bodies thrown into the air. Estimates of the dead run from about 75 to more than 100, making it one of the worst disasters in the history of the Missouri River, and many of the dead had come a very long way to be there.

## Far From Home

Saluda was a paddle steamer working the Missouri River in the early 1850s. In March 1852 she left St. Louis bound for Council Bluffs, Iowa, and many of the people aboard were Mormon emigrants from England and Wales. They had already crossed an ocean. Now they were heading upriver toward the staging grounds for the long journey west, travelers bound for a new life. The river they traveled was a hard one that spring, muddy, icy and running high. When Saluda reached Lexington, she stopped to take on supplies before pressing on. Just beyond the town lay the obstacle that would decide everyone's fate.

## The Bend That Would Not Yield

Past Lexington, the Missouri squeezed into a narrow channel where very strong currents made it hard for boats to get around a sharp turn in the river. Saluda's captain, Francis T. Belt, tried to make the bend and failed. He tried again, and kept trying, for two days. The current held the steamer back each time. By the morning of April 9, Belt was frustrated by the lack of progress, and he ordered an increase in steam pressure, hoping more power would carry the boat through. Saluda pushed off from the landing. It was a fatal decision.

## Ten Minutes

The explosion tore the steamer apart. Wreckage and victims came down in the river, on the wharf and even on the nearby bluff. Estimates of the dead run from about 75 to more than 100, among them Captain Belt, many of the passengers, and two men standing on shore who were struck by flying debris. Because the current was so strong, many of the bodies were never recovered, carried off by the same river the emigrants had been trying to climb. By some accounts only 40 to 50 people survived. A passing boat heading down the Missouri pulled some of the survivors from the water. Within ten minutes of the blast, Saluda had sunk, and the families who had crossed an ocean together had been torn apart in a single moment.

## A Town Opens Its Doors

Lexington did not look away. The community rushed to help, and doctors worked on the injured while survivors were taken into local homes to be cared for. Townspeople raised a fund to bury the dead in Machpelah Cemetery and to help the survivors with their financial needs. Most remarkable of all, families in Lexington took some of the children orphaned by the explosion into their own homes and raised them as their own. Strangers from across the Atlantic became sons and daughters of a Missouri river town. The kindness has not been forgotten. On April 9, 2002, the 150th anniversary of the disaster, Lexington unveiled a monument to the Saluda, and a headstone naming known victims stands at Machpelah Cemetery, while the monument sits in the town's Heritage Park.

## Flight Context

The Saluda disaster site is at 39.1858°N, 93.8978°W on the Missouri River at Lexington, Missouri, roughly 40 miles east of downtown Kansas City. From 2,000–3,000 feet AGL, follow the Missouri River east from the Kansas City area; Lexington sits on the high bluffs on the south bank, where the river bends past the town. Midwest National Air Center (KGPH) lies about 21 nm west-northwest, and Charles B. Wheeler Downtown Airport (KMKC) about 32 nm west. The river corridor is easy to follow in good visibility, but morning fog often settles in the Missouri River valley, especially in spring and fall.
