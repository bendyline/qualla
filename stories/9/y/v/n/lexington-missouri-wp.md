# Lexington, Missouri

A Missouri River town that outfitted the westward trails, helped launch the Pony Express, and still keeps a Civil War cannonball lodged in its courthouse.

**Location:** 39.1831, -93.8750

**Categories:** Cities in Lafayette County, Missouri, County seats in Missouri, Missouri populated places on the Missouri River, American Civil War sites, Santa Fe Trail, Historic towns

## Introduction

Look up at the columns of the Lafayette County Courthouse and you will find a cannonball still stuck in one of the upper pillars. It has become the symbol of Lexington, a town of about 4,650 people at the 2020 census, and it is a fitting one. The Greek Revival courthouse went up on Main Street in 1847 and is the oldest courthouse in continuous use west of the Mississippi, so it has stood through nearly everything this river town has lived: frontier boom, slavery, siege, guerrilla war and a long, dignified decline. Lexington sits on the south bank of the Missouri River about 40 miles east of Kansas City, and for a time it mattered far more than Kansas City did.

## Outfitters to a Continent

The town took shape in 1822 near a Missouri River ferry, and it was named in commemoration of the Battle of Lexington, the opening clash of the American Revolution. In 1823 it became the county seat. John Aull had already opened a mercantile store in 1822; his brothers James and Robert soon joined him, and the Aull Brothers built a frontier chain with stores in Independence, Westport and Liberty. Through the 1830s and 1840s Lexington was the largest city west of St. Louis, the place where trappers, traders and emigrants bought what they needed before heading out on the Santa Fe, California, Oregon and Mormon trails. Goods sent west from Lexington were valued at $450,000 in 1843. In the 1850s Russell, Majors and Waddell, the largest freighting firm in western Missouri, handled its bookkeeping and finances from Lexington, where partner William Waddell was a store owner. At their peak the partners are said to have run 3,500 wagons hauling goods to Sacramento, Denver and beyond, and in 1860 they launched the Pony Express.

## Hemp, Steam and Human Cost

The prosperity rested on hemp, tobacco and cattle, and on the labor of enslaved people. Lexington and Lafayette County held one of the largest enslaved populations in Missouri, and many homes in town still have the old slave quarters standing behind them, a quiet reminder of who did the work. Down at the wharf, steamboats made fortunes, burning coal from some of the first mines in the state, dug straight into the river bluffs. The river also brought tragedy. In 1852 the side-wheeler Saluda was carrying about 175 passengers, among them Latter-day Saint emigrants bound for Salt Lake City, when its boilers exploded at Lexington, killing at least 75 people (some accounts say more than 100) in one of the worst steamboat accidents in Missouri history. Some of the children left orphaned were taken in and adopted by Lexington families. Four years later, in March 1856, pro-slavery Border Ruffians stopped and searched the steamboat Arabia near town, seizing 100 rifles and two cannons that the Massachusetts Aid Society was sending to the free-soil settlers of Kansas Territory.

## The Battle of the Hemp Bales

The crop that built Lexington also shaped its most famous fight. On September 12, 1861, an estimated 10,000 or more soldiers of the Missouri State Guard under Major General Sterling Price laid siege to the Federal post in the old Masonic College, held by Colonel James A. Mulligan. The college had operated only from 1847 to 1857; now it was a military post. On September 18 Price's army attacked. On the third day, September 20, some of his men pushed hemp bales ahead of them as moving breastworks, working their way up the river bluffs toward Mulligan's headquarters behind walls of the county's own crop. Later that day Mulligan, who was wounded, surrendered his troops. Combined casualties came to roughly 64 dead and 195 wounded, not counting the thousands of Union soldiers taken prisoner. The battlefield on the bluffs is now a state historic site, and three years later, on October 19, 1864, a Second Battle of Lexington was fought during Price's Missouri Expedition.

## A Sniper in the Courthouse

Lexington was known as a center for Quantrill's Raiders during the war, and the violence did not end with the war. Two months after the fighting stopped, guerrillas who had refused the cease-fire rode into Lexington to surrender under a special federal amnesty. Reportedly under a white flag, they were fired on by Union soldiers of the 2nd Wisconsin Cavalry, and Jesse James was severely wounded in the right lung. Some credit that moment as a major push toward his postwar career as an outlaw. In October 1866 a gang of former guerrillas linked to Archie Clement robbed Lexington's Alexander Mitchell bank of about $2,000, a holdup sometimes counted among the first daylight bank robberies in United States history. In December 1866 Archie Clement, a James accomplice and perhaps the most notorious of all the guerrillas, terrorized the town until a sniper posted on the courthouse's second floor shot him from his horse.

## Athens of the West

Lexington never regained its prewar standing. The railroad took the trade the river once carried, and Kansas City became the great city of western Missouri. The town turned instead to learning and billed itself as the "Athens of the West," its most significant institutions being three schools for women: the Elizabeth Aull Seminary, Lexington Ladies College and Central College for Women, the last housed in the old Masonic College. Wentworth Military Academy, founded in 1880, drew students from around the world until it closed in 2017. What the decline preserved is remarkable: more than 150 homes and public buildings older than the Civil War, including the 1848 Gothic Revival Christ Episcopal Church with its walnut interior. Carl Stalling, who scored Looney Tunes cartoons for decades, and Wal-Mart co-founder Bud Walton are among the town's notable names, and the old courthouse, cannonball and all, still presides over Main Street.

## Flight Context

Lexington lies at approximately 39.18°N, 93.88°W on the south bank of the Missouri River, where Missouri Route 13 meets US Route 24. From 2,500–4,000 feet AGL the town's street grid stands out atop the river bluffs, with the Missouri River running along its northern edge and the battlefield state historic site on the bluffs above the water. Kansas City lies about 40 miles to the west. Nearest airport is Higginsville Industrial Municipal (KHIG), roughly 13 miles to the southeast; Kansas City International (KMCI) is the nearest major airport, to the west. River valleys here can hold morning fog in spring and fall; expect summer haze and afternoon thunderstorms.
