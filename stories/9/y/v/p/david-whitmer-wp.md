# David Whitmer

One of the Three Witnesses to the Book of Mormon, David Whitmer broke with Joseph Smith, spent fifty years in Richmond, Missouri, and never stopped affirming what he said he saw.

**Location:** 39.2790, -93.9860

**Categories:** American Latter Day Saint leaders, Book of Mormon witnesses, Founders of new religious movements, People from Ray County, Missouri, Religious history

## Introduction

In June 1878 a tornado tore through Richmond, Missouri, and smashed the home of an old livery-stable owner and former mayor named David Whitmer. According to later accounts, one room came through intact: the room where he kept the printer's manuscript of the Book of Mormon, the handwritten copy used to set the type of the first edition. Whitmer believed God had protected it. He had reason to feel a personal stake. Half a century earlier he had been one of the Three Witnesses who testified to seeing the golden plates, and though he had long since broken with Joseph Smith, he never took that testimony back.

## Guests at the Whitmer Farm

Whitmer was born near Harrisburg, Pennsylvania, on January 7, 1805, the fourth of nine children of Peter Whitmer Sr. and Mary Musselman. The family's roots were German on both sides, they spoke with a German accent, and David grew up attending a Presbyterian church. In 1828, on a business trip to Palmyra, New York, he talked with his friend Oliver Cowdery, who thought there "must be some truth" to the stories of golden plates. Cowdery kept writing, and Whitmer shared the letters with his family. When Cowdery asked whether the Whitmers could give the Smiths lodging and a place to finish translating the Book of Mormon, they agreed. On June 1, 1829, Joseph Smith, his wife Emma and Cowdery arrived at the Whitmer farm, and the translation was completed about a month later. Decades afterward, in an 1878 testimony, Whitmer described seeing a light "not like the light of the sun, nor like that of a fire, but more glorious and beautiful."

## From Founder to Dissenter

When Smith organized the Church of Christ on April 6, 1830, Whitmer was one of its six original members. He went on early missionary journeys, baptizing converts, and in 1835 he helped Cowdery and Martin Harris select and ordain the first Quorum of the Twelve Apostles. Then the movement fractured. After the Kirtland Safety Society bank collapsed, Smith and his counselor Sidney Rigdon, pursued by creditors, moved to Far West, Missouri, and a leadership struggle dissolved the Missouri church presidency that Whitmer headed with his counselors, W. W. Phelps and his brother John Whitmer. He resigned, and in 1838 he was excommunicated on charges that included breaking the Word of Wisdom and circulating unfavorable information about Smith. Rigdon's "Salt Sermon" called for the dissenters' expulsion. A secret society called the Danites formed to remove them, and eighty-three Mormons signed a letter warning them to "depart, depart, or a more fatal calamity shall befall you." Whitmer and his family fled to nearby Richmond.

## A Stable Keeper in Richmond

Richmond became his home for the rest of his life. The violence he escaped soon engulfed the whole Mormon community in Missouri: the 1838 Mormon War led Governor Boggs to issue his extermination order that October, authorizing deadly force, and most Latter Day Saints were driven from Missouri by early 1839. Whitmer stayed. He ran a livery stable and served as the town's mayor from 1867 to 1868. Shortly before Oliver Cowdery's death in 1850, his old friend had given him the printer's manuscript, and Whitmer guarded it for the rest of his life. In January 1876 he revived a small Church of Christ of his own, ordaining his nephew John C. Whitmer as its "First Elder." That church, known as the Whitmerites, lasted until the 1960s.

## The Witness Who Would Not Recant

In 1887 Whitmer published "An Address to All Believers in Christ." It is a striking document. He affirmed his testimony of the Book of Mormon while denouncing the other branches of the movement; polygamy had been chief among the reasons he separated. That same year he turned his standing as a witness into an argument: if readers believed God had spoken to the three witnesses, he wrote, they should believe that in June 1838 "God spake to me again by his own voice from the heavens" and told him to separate from the Latter Day Saints. He became the most interviewed of all the Book of Mormon witnesses, and he died in Richmond on January 25, 1888. The manuscript outlived him: his grandson George Schweich sold it in 1903 to the church now called the Community of Christ, and in 2017 the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints bought it for a reported $35 million.

## Flight Context

Richmond, Missouri, where Whitmer lived from 1838 until his death, sits at approximately 39.28°N, 93.99°W, a few miles north of the Missouri River bottomlands. From 2,500–3,500 feet AGL, look for the small county-seat grid of Richmond on the uplands north of the river, with the historic river town of Lexington visible on the bluffs to the southeast. The closest public field is Excelsior Springs Memorial Airport (FAA identifier 3EX), about 10 nm west-northwest, with Midwest National Air Center (KGPH) about 15 nm to the west; Higginsville Industrial Municipal (KHIG) lies across the river about 19 nm to the southeast, and Kansas City International (KMCI) is the nearest major airport, to the west. Expect summer haze and convective storms; river-valley fog is common on calm mornings.
