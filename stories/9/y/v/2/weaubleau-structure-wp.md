# Weaubleau structure

Beneath the farm country around Osceola, Missouri, lies a 12-mile ring that geologists believe was punched into the Earth by a 1,200-foot meteoroid roughly 320 to 340 million years ago.

**Location:** 37.9833, -93.6333

**Categories:** Impact craters of the United States, Carboniferous impact craters, Possible impact craters on Earth, Geology of Missouri, Landforms of St. Clair County, Missouri

## Introduction

Around Osceola, Missouri, people have long known a peculiar kind of stone, so nearly perfectly round that locals call them "round rocks," "Missouri rock balls" or, most charmingly, "Weaubleau eggs." For years they were taken for leftovers of the ice ages. They may instead be the offspring of a single violent moment in Missouri's deep past. Roughly 320 to 340 million years ago, a meteoroid believed to have been about 1,200 feet across appears to have struck here, leaving a scar roughly 12 miles wide that nobody recognized for what it was until a geologist looked at the land from above.

## A Circle in the Creeks

Geologists knew for a long time that something strange lay under this corner of western Missouri, near the small towns of Gerster, Iconium, Osceola and Vista. The rock here shows severe structural deformation and extensive brecciation, bedrock broken into angular fragments. The usual explanations were thrusting over a dome or a "cryptoexplosive" event, and the deformation remained poorly understood. The breakthrough came when geologist Kevin R. Evans examined digital elevation data and found a circular structure about 12 miles (19 km) across, though some work also describes a smaller inner ring about 8 km across. On a shaded-relief map the ring can be traced in the drainage patterns themselves, the creeks bending around a shape it took elevation data to reveal. It was first named the Weaubleau-Osceola structure, after Weaubleau Creek and the town of Osceola, and is now known simply as the Weaubleau structure. The Impact Field Studies Group lists it as a "probable" impact site.

## Sealed for Safekeeping

What makes Weaubleau unusual is how well it kept. After the impact the site was buried under sediments of the later Pennsylvanian Period, and only relatively recently has erosion partly exposed it again. That blanket preserved the structure and allows its age to be bracketed within the Mississippian Period, most likely around the boundary between the Osagean and Meramecian stages. A team led by Evans presented work on the site in 2003 at the Third International Conference on Large Meteorite Impacts in Nördlingen, Germany, describing its deformation, event stratification and shock metamorphism. A 2004 abstract by Evans and colleagues carried the title "Oblique Impact at Weaubleau-Osceola Structure," suggesting the impactor may have come in at an angle rather than straight down.

## Among the Largest, First in Its Class

By size, Weaubleau ranks among the largest impact structures in the United States. Three of the bigger American craters each come with a catch. The Manson crater has been glaciated and buried. The Chesapeake Bay crater lies under water. The Beaverhead impact structure has been twisted by mountain building. Weaubleau has suffered none of those fates, which makes it, by one account, the largest exposed, untectonized impact structure in the country. No larger American impact structure is so open to study, its broken rocks partly exposed at the surface rather than locked under ice-age debris, seawater or folded mountains.

## Lined Up on the 38th Parallel

Weaubleau does not stand alone. It is one of a series of known or suspected impact sites strung along the 38th parallel across Illinois, Missouri and Kansas. One possibility is that they formed together in a serial impact, a chain of strikes arriving one after another, similar to the one comet Shoemaker-Levy 9 delivered to Jupiter. On Earth such an event would be extremely unlikely, and the idea is unproven. It would grow much stronger if the ages of the other 38th parallel structures could be narrowed to the same window as Weaubleau's. For now, the idea remains an intriguing possibility waiting on better dates.

## The Weaubleau Eggs

Which brings the story back to those round stones. The conglomerate balls found in the area were long thought to be glacial remnants, and some have suggested they are chert concretions or nodules. One proposed explanation ties them to the impact itself: the collision may have flung shale fragments of the Northview Formation outward from the center of the structure, after which silica-saturated waters precipitated silica around each fragment, building a sphere around it. How they actually formed is still poorly understood and needs more research. Until it is settled, anyone turning a Weaubleau egg in the hand may be holding a small, patient souvenir of a very bad day for western Missouri.

## Flight Context

The center of the structure lies at approximately 37.98°N, 93.63°W in St. Clair County, Missouri, near the towns of Osceola, Vista, Iconium and Gerster. The 12-mile ring is subtle from the air; from 8,000–10,000 feet it is best read in the curving drainage patterns of the creeks rather than as a visible bowl. Nearby airports: Clinton Regional (KGLY), about 22 nm north; Stockton Lake (FAA identifier MO3), about 21 nm south-southwest; El Dorado Springs Memorial (FAA identifier 87K, no ICAO code), about 19 nm west-southwest; Warsaw Municipal (KRAW), about 26 nm north-northeast. Low-angle morning or evening light helps bring out the terrain.
