# Sacking of Osceola

On September 22–23, 1861, Jim Lane's Kansas Jayhawkers looted and burned Osceola, Missouri, reportedly executing nine local men and freeing about 200 enslaved people, in a raid that fed a cycle of border revenge ending at Lawrence and General Order No. 11.

## Introduction

According to the reports that came out of Osceola, Missouri, James Henry Lane's personal share of the plunder included a piano and a quantity of silk dresses. Many of his Kansans were so drunk by the time they left that they could not march, and had to ride out in wagons and carriages. Behind them, the town was burning. It was September 22–23, 1861, and an informal band of anti-slavery Kansas "Jayhawkers" had just sacked the seat of St. Clair County. No Union military authority had ordered it.

## A Raid Behind the Lines

In the summer of 1861, momentum in western Missouri belonged to Sterling Price. At the Battle of Wilson's Creek, Sterling Price's Missouri State Guard defeated Nathaniel Lyon's Union army, and Price then pushed north through the western part of the state. At Dry Wood Creek, near the Kansas border, his men forced back Lane's own Kansas volunteers, and Price pressed on toward the Siege of Lexington. Lane answered by striking at the country Price had left behind. On September 10 he crossed the border at Trading Post, Kansas, and began an offensive moving east through Butler, Harrisonville, Osceola and Clinton. His men were not acting under Union military orders. They were Jayhawkers, an informal group of anti-slavery Kansans whose aim was to push pro-slavery Southerners out.

## The Burning of a County Seat

The campaign reached its climax at Osceola. Lane's forces drove off a small Southern force defending the town, and then the looting began. An artillery battery under Captain Thomas Moonlight shelled the St. Clair County courthouse. By most accounts, nine local men were rounded up, given a quick drumhead court-martial, and executed, their fates settled by a summary trial held in the field. Then the town was set alight. When the fires were done, all but three of Osceola's buildings had burned. Families lost their homes and businesses in a single day, and the town never fully recovered.

## Plunder and Freedom

The raid had two faces, and both are part of its history. For the enslaved people of Osceola, Lane's arrival meant liberation. His men freed about 200 of them, and Lane led them across the border to Kansas and freedom. At the same time, the Kansans stripped the town bare. They took 350 horses, 400 cattle and 3,000 bags of flour, along with supplies from all the town's shops and stores, and carriages and wagons to haul it away. It was a tremendous load of plunder. From Osceola the troops moved northwest, reaching Kansas City, Missouri, on September 29 to pursue Price, who by then was retreating south through the state.

## A Chain of Revenge

Osceola did not stay a local tragedy. Lane's raid stirred a hatred along the Missouri–Kansas border that fed directly into what came next. William Quantrill's raid on Lawrence, Kansas, followed in the chain of retaliation, and the Lawrence raid in turn led to General Order No. 11, which depopulated four counties of western Missouri. Each act of violence became the justification for the next, and civilians on both sides of the line bore the cost. The sack of Osceola is remembered today as a massacre, and the Kansas senator behind it echoes through popular culture as well: a Senator Jim Lane appears as a character in the film The Outlaw Josey Wales. For the residents who watched their town go up in smoke, it was simply the day Osceola was lost.
