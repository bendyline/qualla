# Osage Village State Historic Site

On a high, open hilltop near the Osage River valley, some 2,000 to 3,000 Osage people lived in about 200 lodges until 1775, and the archaeological site of their village is now a National Register-listed Missouri state historic site.

## Introduction

For decades in the early and middle 1700s, until 1775, a large group of the Osage made their home on a high, open hilltop near the Osage River valley in what is now Vernon County, Missouri. At its peak the village held about 200 lodges and between 2,000 and 3,000 people. The lodges are gone now, and what survives lies in the ground. That buried record, and the importance of the people who left it, earned the hilltop a place on the National Register of Historic Places. Today it is preserved as the Osage Village State Historic Site, maintained by the Missouri Department of Natural Resources.

## A Nation That Spanned Four States

The Osage first appear in written European records in 1673, when the explorers Louis Joliet and Jacques Marquette noted them. At the height of their influence, the Osage claimed a vast territory covering what is now southern Missouri, Arkansas, eastern Kansas and Oklahoma, and the river that runs through the middle of this country still carries their name. The village on this hilltop was one of their major settlements. Its size is a large part of why the ground is protected today: the importance of the Osage in this region and the sheer scale of the community that once lived here, a town of thousands on a single rise above the valley, are what the site preserves.

## Life Inside the Lodges

Archaeologists have recovered much of how people lived here. Excavations showed that the dwellings were rectangular, and the pottery, weapons and tools pulled from the soil have filled in the rhythms of daily life. Work was divided. Men hunted game and traded with Europeans, exchanging furs for guns and manufactured goods. Women cultivated the crops and processed the harvest, and they also processed the game and hides the hunters brought in, turning them into food, clothing and tools. Every clay pot and stone tool recovered from the hilltop is a trace of someone's household: a meal cooked, a hide scraped, a hunt prepared for. Two to three thousand people made their lives in these lodges, and the ground has kept a record of their work.

## Masters of the Fur Trade

The trade that passed through villages like this one grew into something enormous. Throughout the late 18th and early 19th centuries, the Osage supplied a very large share of the European-American fur trade along the Missouri River, by one account more than half of it. It is a striking figure. The furs that moved down the river to European-American merchants came, in large part, from Osage hunters and from the work of the Osage women who processed the hides. The exchange ran both ways, bringing guns and trade goods into Osage communities. For the traders of the Missouri, the Osage were not a sideshow to the business. By that measure, they were more than half of it.

## Treaties and Loss

That position did not survive the arrival of a new power. After the United States took control of the Louisiana Purchase territory in 1804, its settlement policies encouraged European Americans to move into the area. The government then made treaties with the Osage so that they would cede their territory to the United States. Piece by piece, the homeland that had once stretched across four future states slipped out of Osage hands, and the Osage were gradually forced to retreat to a reservation in Kansas and later to one in Indian Territory, now Oklahoma. A people who had once filled this hilltop with a town of thousands were pushed far from the land that had sustained them.

## Walking the Hilltop

The Osage Village State Historic Site was established in 1984, and it is maintained today by the Missouri Department of Natural Resources. Visitors can follow an interpretive trail across the hilltop, where signs describe the history of the site and the lives of the people who lived there. What you walk over is not a ruin in the usual sense. It is an archaeological site, a place where the evidence lies mostly underground, and imagination has to do some of the work. Standing on the rise, it takes little effort to picture the 200 lodges and the thousands of Osage people who made this high, open ground their home for most of the eighteenth century.

## Flight Context

Osage Village State Historic Site is at 37.9811°N, 94.2097°W in Vernon County, western Missouri, near the small town of Walker and close to the Osage River valley. The site is an open hilltop with little built structure, so from 1,500–2,500 feet AGL navigate by Walker and the river valley rather than expecting a visible landmark. Nevada Municipal Airport (KNVD) is about 9 nm to the south-southwest. The surrounding country is a mix of farmland and low hills with good visibility in stable weather; spring and summer thunderstorms are the main hazard.
