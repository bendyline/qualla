# Fort Carondelet

A Spanish-sanctioned fur fort built by the Chouteau family to secure peace with the Osage Nation, Fort Carondelet stood on a bluff above the Osage River for barely seven years before the forest swallowed it.

**Location:** 38.0370, -94.2112

**Categories:** Fur trade, Forts in Missouri, Pre-statehood history of Missouri, Trading posts in the United States, Osage Nation, Buildings and structures in Vernon County, Missouri

## Introduction

"Not a vestige of which was remaining." That was all Zebulon Pike could find to say in 1806 as he passed the place on the Osage River where Fort Carondelet had stood. The spot, he wrote, was "only marked by the superior growth of vegetation." Eleven years earlier, the Chouteau family had raised a fortified trading post here in what was then Spanish Louisiana, a building meant to secure two things at once: the loyalty of the Osage Nation and the wealth of the fur trade. It lasted barely seven years.

## A Governor's Worry

Starting in the mid-1700s, the Osage Nation traded furs with French and Spanish settlers, and no traders mattered more to them than two French brothers from St. Louis, Pierre and Auguste Chouteau. Licensed by Spain's colonial government, the Chouteaus had built a temporary post on the Osage River by 1787, in what is now western Missouri. The idea of something permanent came from Governor General Esteban Rodríguez Miró, who proposed a fort in 1791 to strengthen Spanish allies living among the Osage. Before it could be built, Miró was replaced by Francisco Luis Héctor de Carondelet, and the new governor arrived with a worry of his own: he feared the Osage might ally themselves with the French. In mid-1794 he called six Osage chiefs to a peace meeting. They agreed, in exchange for peace, to let a fort rise beside their river.

## Thirty-Two Feet Square

Auguste Chouteau offered to pay for the fort as a replacement for his temporary post, and he described it for Carondelet in careful detail. The stronghold would have two parts, the first of brick or stone and the second of logs ten inches square "laid horizontally one upon the other, as the Americans practice." It would form "a perfect square each side of thirty-two feet," with two small embrasures in each facade of the first story for artillery and "a very thick door as on the vessels." Because peace with the Osage mattered most to him, Carondelet accepted. The terms were generous. Chouteau would receive $2,000 a year to support twenty soldiers at the fort, along with a six-year monopoly on trade with the Osage. If Spain supplied the soldiers itself, he would keep the monopoly but get nothing more. In 1795, Fort Carondelet went up.

## Sons Among the Osage

Pierre Chouteau ran the post, and he brought his sons to live among the neighboring Osage. The boys grew up learning Osage culture and the ways of Indigenous life more broadly, while the Osage, for their part, accepted Chouteau and his family. That closeness carried a price. Other nations, among them the Miami, grew jealous of the favoritism they saw shown to the Osage. The monopoly also bred rivals closer to home. By 1800, competing traders were demanding that the government break the Chouteau contract, and by 1802 the Spanish government had handed the trade rights to one of those rivals, Manuel Lisa. In 1802, Pierre Chouteau gave up the fight and sold Fort Carondelet to Lisa, who withdrew the garrison and abandoned it the same year. Within four years, the vegetation had closed over the buildings.

## Furnaces on Halley's Bluff

After Pike, few travelers mentioned the site at all. The last report of it comes from an 1874 Missouri travel guidebook describing Vernon County. The book never names the fort, but it notes that the top of Halley's Bluff held "the foundations of three furnaces" and "the remains of works—both earth and stone, covering the approaches to the furnaces and the descent to the excavations below, as if thrown up for fortifications." Then the bluff passed into very different hands. In 1941 a Latter Day Saint congregation that had split from the Church of Christ (Temple Lot) relocated to northeast Vernon County and built on Halley's Bluff, calling its 441-acre tract Zion's Retreat. A leadership fight in 1972 divided that church, and the breakaway faction led by Dan Gayman became the Church of Israel, which began as a Christian Identity church and later distanced itself from that movement. In 1984 Eric Robert Rudolph, who carried out the Centennial Olympic Park bombing in 1996, spent several months with his mother at a Church of Israel property in the area.

## A Fort Made of Paper

What survives of Fort Carondelet today is mostly a paper trail: a trader's letter specifying the thickness of a door, a contract pricing twenty soldiers at $2,000 a year, and an explorer's single sentence about unusually lush growth on a riverbank. Those documents trace a brief, calculated alliance between a European empire, a St. Louis trading family and the Osage Nation, whose chiefs agreed to the fort in exchange for peace. Spain granted the Chouteaus six years of exclusive trade; rivals pried it loose before Spanish rule ended. When the monopoly changed hands, the alliance's physical anchor was simply left to the woods.

## Flight Context

The fort site lies at approximately 38.04°N, 94.21°W, on Halley's Bluff above the Osage River in Vernon County, western Missouri. Nothing of the fort is visible, and the bluff is private church property. From 2,500–3,500 feet AGL, follow the wooded meanders of the Osage River across the surrounding farmland. Nearby airports: Nevada Municipal (KNVD), about 12 nm south-southwest; Butler Memorial (KBUM), about 16 nm north-northwest; El Dorado Springs Memorial (FAA identifier 87K, no ICAO code), about 15 nm southeast. Best viewed in clear weather, when the river's tree lines stand out against the fields.
