# Capture of Sedalia

In the pre-dawn dark of October 15, 1864, Confederate raiders in captured Union blue rode onto the open prairie at Sedalia, scattered its militia, and left with about 2,000 mules and cattle.

**Location:** 38.7031, -93.2311

**Categories:** Battles of the American Civil War in Missouri, Price's Missouri Expedition, 1864 in Missouri, Sedalia, Missouri, Military history

## Introduction

The riders approaching Sedalia's pickets before daylight on October 15, 1864, wore Union blue. They were not Union soldiers. Benjamin F. Elliott's Confederate cavalry regiment, largely dressed in captured uniforms, got within pistol shot of the guards before anyone realized the mistake, and by then the capture of Sedalia was already under way. It was a small action in a large and doomed campaign, a raid for mules and cattle on a railroad town in the middle of the Missouri prairie. But it was fast, it was chaotic, and it ended with Confederates riding out of town barefoot, some of them carrying their boots filled with stolen whiskey.

## Price's Last Gamble

The raid belonged to Price's Missouri Expedition. By September 1864 the war was turning against the Confederacy, and General Edmund Kirby Smith, unable to move his infantry across a Mississippi River controlled by the Union Navy, approved an invasion of Missouri instead. Major General Sterling Price, a former governor of the state, hoped to spark a popular uprising, pull Union troops away from the main theaters and help George B. McClellan defeat Abraham Lincoln in the November election. On September 19 his column of about 12,000 to 13,000 men crossed into Missouri. At Fort Davidson near Pilot Knob, on September 27, the Confederates were repulsed with heavy losses, at least 800 casualties, and Price abandoned his plan to take St. Louis. He turned away from Jefferson City too, misled by intelligence that overstated its defenders, and marched into the pro-Confederate Little Dixie region. Recruits joined him there, and they needed weapons and supplies.

## A Rumor of Mules and Cattle

Price sent two side raids to find them, one to Glasgow and one to Sedalia. The lure at Sedalia was a rumor that the Union army kept thousands of mules and cattle in town, animals that would help feed and remount Price's force. Brigadier General M. Jeff Thompson of the Missouri State Guard led Shelby's Iron Brigade toward it with around 1,200 to 1,500 men; the brigade was nominally about 2,000 strong, but many of its soldiers were away visiting their homes in the Boonslick country. Near Georgetown the column picked up two Union stragglers who warned that Union forces were gathering at Jefferson City and Kansas City and that infantry at California was preparing to move. Thompson called off the attack. Then he changed his mind, reasoning that the infantry was not headed his way and that the Union cavalry under Brigadier General John B. Sanborn was too far off to interfere.

## Ten Minutes on the Open Prairie

Sedalia had little to hide behind. It sat on an expansive prairie with few trees, defended by two redoubts and some rifle pits and held by almost 800 home guard and Enrolled Missouri Militia under Colonel John D. Crawford. Also in town were 33 men of the 7th Missouri State Militia Cavalry under Captain Oscar B. Queen, sent to meet an ammunition train. When Elliott's disguised riders struck, the pickets scattered, and Crawford and many of his militia went with them; Queen later claimed Crawford had ordered a retreat as soon as the enemy appeared. Queen's men stood their ground and formed a point of resistance, and Elliott, who had outrun the rest of the brigade, fell back. Within ten minutes Thompson's full force and artillery arrived. The guns scattered the remaining militia and the 7th surrendered. The defenders had left their flags flying over the fortifications, so the attackers did not at first realize most of them had already gone.

## Boots Full of Whiskey

What followed depends on which historian you read. Paul B. Jenkins, writing in 1906, said Thompson's men looted the town. Kyle Sinisi, a modern historian, describes Thompson trying to keep things orderly, even shooting a mule out from under one soldier and spanking others with the flat of his sword, before events slipped out of his control; the looting, Sinisi says, fell mainly on businesses rather than private homes. The post office was plundered, and eyewitnesses reported those barefoot riders with whiskey sloshing in their boots. Thompson insisted "no outrage or murder was committed." Union losses were reported as one man killed and 23 wounded, though the Confederates who chased fleeing militiamen across the prairie inflicted an unknown number of further casualties. Unable to hold several hundred prisoners, Thompson released a few hundred home guards and gave 122 militiamen irregular verbal paroles laced with threats. Taking some 2,000 mules and cattle, the raiders left the same day.

## The Road to Westport

Thompson rejoined Price at the Salt Fork River near Waverly on October 18, and the whole army pushed west. The next day came the Second Battle of Lexington, then a victory at the Little Blue River. On October 23, near Kansas City, Price's roughly 8,500 men met some 22,000 Union soldiers at the Battle of Westport and were soundly defeated. At Mine Creek in Kansas two days later, about 600 Confederates were captured, and Price ordered most of his cumbersome wagon train destroyed. After a final defeat at the Second Battle of Newtonia on October 28, his army was disintegrating, and it did not stop retreating until it was back in Arkansas in early December. The campaign failed to sway the election, and the Union troops it drew into Missouri were diverted only temporarily. Whatever Thompson's raiders carried out of Sedalia, it could not change that outcome.

## Flight Context

The action took place in and around Sedalia at approximately 38.70°N, 93.23°W. From 3,000–4,500 feet AGL the setting still reads clearly: Sedalia's street grid spreads across open, gently rolling prairie with long sightlines in every direction, the same terrain that left the 1864 defenders so exposed. Sedalia Regional Airport (KDMO) lies about two miles east of town, and Whiteman Air Force Base (KSZL) is to the west near Knob Noster (military field; do not plan to land there). Expect gusty prairie winds, summer haze and spring and summer thunderstorms.
