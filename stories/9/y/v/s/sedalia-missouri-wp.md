# Sedalia, Missouri

A prairie railroad town that shipped Texas cattle, gave Scott Joplin a home and the Maple Leaf Rag its name, and hosts the Missouri State Fair every August.

**Location:** 38.7098, -93.2296

**Categories:** Cities in Pettis County, Missouri, County seats in Missouri, Railroad towns, Ragtime, Missouri State Fair

## Introduction

According to local lore, the town council dropped the name Sedville because "towns that end in -ville don't amount to anything." Whether or not that is the whole story, the town that became Sedalia went on to prove the point. General George Rappeen Smith, who had already founded nearby Smithton, filed plans for Sedville on November 30, 1857, and the place existed only on paper until it was incorporated as Sedalia in 1860. It had about 300 people that year. Within a few decades it would become a railroad hub, a cattle railhead, a notorious center of vice, the adopted home of Scott Joplin and, since 1901, the site of the Missouri State Fair.

## Where the Rails Ended

Long before Smith's plat, the Osage occupied this country, and bands of Shawnee who had migrated from east of the Mississippi were living nearby when European Americans arrived. What changed everything was a route decision. Backers of a "ridge route" for the railway won out over those who favored following the river, and in January 1861 the tracks reached Sedalia. For three years it was the end of the line. A small Union garrison and the merchants who followed it gave the town a boomtown energy, and after the Civil War many of the thousands of soldiers who had been stationed there came back to stay. From 1866 to 1874 Sedalia was a railhead terminus for cattle drives, its stockyards covering a large area; it received cattle from the massive Texas drive of 1866. Decades later the television western Rawhide would make Sedalia the destination its trail crews were driving toward.

## The KATY and Its Shops

For nearly a century the town's fortunes ran on steel rails. The Missouri Pacific and the Missouri-Kansas-Texas Railroad, widely known as the "KATY" from its K-T stock exchange code, both ran large machine shops here, and track crews and shop hands filled the payrolls. By the end of the 19th century the KATY owned shops, stockyards, a roundhouse and a hospital for workers of its Sedalia Division. The town grew with them. By 1900 more than 15,000 people lived in Sedalia, making it the sixth-largest city in Missouri. When the KATY cut back its operations in the 20th century, its right-of-way across much of the state became the 240-mile KATY Trail, now shared by cyclists, walkers and riders on horseback. It has been described as the largest new trail developed in the nation among the late 20th-century Rails to Trails projects.

## Pianos Above West Main

A town full of railroad men and traveling salesmen had another economy too. In the late 19th century Sedalia was known as a center of vice, especially prostitution, and as early as 1877 it was being called the "Sodom and Gomorrah of Missouri" in the St. Louis press. Respectable businessmen profited as landlords, and a town reluctant to raise taxes funded its services from fines charged to the women who worked in the trade. In the 1890s, as the middle class tried to isolate them, the brothels were concentrated above businesses on West Main Street (a former brothel at 217 West Main is now on the National Register of Historic Places). They employed musicians, especially piano players. The result was a thriving musical culture that helped foster the ragtime composer Scott Joplin, who made Sedalia his adopted home. His famous Maple Leaf Rag is thought to be named for the Maple Leaf Club, one of the Black clubs in Sedalia where he played, though no direct evidence proves the link, and the city now hosts the Scott Joplin International Ragtime Festival.

## Fair Weather and Crowds

Since its first run in September 1901 the Missouri State Fair has come to Sedalia, now every August, skipping only 1943 and 1944 during World War II. Presidents including Ronald Reagan and George W. Bush have spoken on the fairgrounds, though not during the fair itself. In 1974 those same grounds hosted the Ozark Music Festival, planned for about 50,000 ticket holders. Estimates of the crowd that showed up instead run from about 160,000 to 350,000, straining the capacity of the fairgrounds and the town. Hosted by Wolfman Jack, it counts as one of the largest rock festivals in history, yet it is one of the least remembered of the 1970s. Sedalia also keeps a quieter cultural life: a symphony orchestra founded in 1935 during the Depression, and the Daum Museum of Contemporary Art, named for radiologist and collector Harold Daum, with works by Andy Warhol, Dale Chihuly and Helen Frankenthaler.

## Silos Beneath the Prairie

In 1983 ABC's television movie The Day After told of Sedalia destroyed in a Soviet attack on the missile silos around it. The premise was grounded in fact. Starting in early 1964, Minuteman missiles under the 351st Missile Wing at nearby Whiteman Air Force Base were placed in underground silos across the area, and when the film aired, 150 of them stood in silos around Sedalia. The broadcast led to a significant, if belated, rise in local concern, which stayed high until the missiles were dismantled in the 1990s under the Strategic Arms Reduction Treaty. Today the city of 21,725 people, as counted in 2020, sits where U.S. Routes 50 and 65 cross, about 30 miles south of the Missouri River, with a copper "Little Sister of Liberty" statue at the county courthouse.

## Flight Context

Sedalia sits at approximately 38.71°N, 93.23°W on open, gently rolling prairie about 30 miles south of the Missouri River. From 3,000–4,500 feet AGL look for the city grid at the crossing of U.S. Routes 50 (east–west) and 65 (north–south); the broad Missouri State Fairgrounds complex stands out within the city, and the rail lines that built Sedalia still cut through it. Sedalia Regional Airport (KDMO) lies about two miles east of the city. Whiteman Air Force Base (KSZL), home of B-2 operations, is roughly 17 miles to the west near Knob Noster; it is a military field, so avoid its airspace unless cleared. Spring brings frequent thunderstorms and occasional tornadoes; summers are hot and hazy.
