# Arrow Rock, Missouri

A Missouri River village of a few dozen residents, once a ferry crossing on the road to Santa Fe, now preserved whole as a National Historic Landmark and home to one of Missouri's oldest professional theaters.

**Location:** 39.0700, -92.9472

**Categories:** Villages in Saline County, Missouri, Missouri populated places on the Missouri River, Santa Fe Trail, National Historic Landmarks, Historic preservation, Pre-statehood history of Missouri

## Introduction

At the 2020 census Arrow Rock had 60 residents. More than 100,000 people visit it every year, and over 33,000 of them are patrons of a professional theater that began in an abandoned Baptist church. The whole village, all 0.13 square miles of it, lies inside the Arrow Rock Historic District, designated a National Historic Landmark in 1963. It earned that status the hard way, as a ferry landing on the road west, a river port built on hemp and enslaved labor, and the home of George Caleb Bingham, whom Missouri State Parks calls the state's preeminent artist of the 1800s. Almost everything that happened here left a building behind.

## The Rock of Arrows

The name is older than the town. Missouri State Parks explains that Native Americans came to the bluff above the river for the stone used to point their arrows, and a French map of 1732 marked it as "pierre à flèche," the rock of arrows. The Missouria and the Osage were the two dominant nations French traders encountered in the area early in the 18th century. Lewis and Clark passed on June 9, 1804, noting a "Cliff of rocks called the arrow rock" and a "Prairie of Arrows." In 1808 William Clark came by again on his way to build Fort Osage, recorded a "Big Arrow Rock" and a "Little Arrow Rock," and declared that the place would "make a handsome spot for a town." For more than 150 years the bluff served explorers and travelers as a landmark on the river.

## Salt, Forts and the Ferry West

In the early 1800s this part of central Missouri was called the Boonslick Country, after the salt lick about four miles east where Nathan and Daniel Morgan Boone, sons of Daniel Boone, boiled salt from 1805 to 1812 and shipped it to St. Louis. Settlers arrived in 1810, and during the War of 1812 they built small forts against the Ioway and the Sac and Fox, who were allied with Britain. When Fort Osage closed in 1813, trader George C. Sibley moved his post to the Arrow Rock bluff that winter to keep trading with the Osage. By 1815 a ferry was running. From 1819 to 1833 it belonged to Judge David Todd, uncle of Mary Todd Lincoln, and in 1821 Santa Fe trading caravans out of Franklin began crossing here. In 1849 and 1850 the covered wagons of emigrants bound for California "were hardly ever out of sight at Arrow Rock."

## Tavern, Painter and Physician

The town was laid out in June 1829 as Philadelphia, but the locale was better known as Arrow Rock, and in 1833 the legislature changed the name. The next year town commissioner Joseph Huston put up a two-story brick building. Travelers on the river and the Santa Fe Trail surely asked him for lodging, and he began adding frame and log wings; and by 1840 he was widely known as a hotel keeper. His J. Huston Tavern, with a store, ballroom and meeting hall, now houses the oldest continuously operating restaurant west of the Mississippi. In 1837 Bingham built a house here; he traveled extensively and was seldom home for long, but his family lived in it, and it is now a National Historic Landmark in its own right. Arrow Rock was also home to Dr. John Sappington, whom Missouri State Parks calls a pioneer in the use of quinine to fight malaria, and to gunsmith John P. Sites, whose 1866 shop is the only known working restoration of a 19th-century gunsmith's shop still in its original location in the United States.

## The Block on the Bluff

Most settlers came from Virginia, Kentucky and Tennessee, and they brought enslaved people and Southern customs with them. Arrow Rock grew into a river port shipping tobacco and hemp, the most important cash crop in Saline County, along with wheat, corn, pork and mules for the cotton districts downriver. All of it depended on enslaved labor. In its early decades the town held a slave market, and the auction block stood on the bluff in front of the tavern for many decades after emancipation. The river bend below town was even known by a racial slur because of that market. After freedom, Black families moved into town, bought homes and built their own institutions. Brown's Chapel Free Will Baptist Church, built in 1869, served as the first school for African Americans and had 66 students in 1870. In 1881 Zack Bush moved the church to its present site with a team of mules, and the Brown Lodge, a Masonic hall, rose the same year.

## A Village That Chose to Stay

Preservation began early. The restoration of Huston's tavern in 1923 marked the start of historic preservation in Missouri, and Arrow Rock State Historic Site, established that year, was one of the first three units of the state park system. In 1961 the Arrow Rock Lyceum Theatre opened its first season in the empty Baptist church. It is considered one of Missouri's oldest professional regional theaters, staging musicals and plays each summer, with actors arriving from New York and California. In the early 1970s Hollywood came too, filming a musical Tom Sawyer here with Jodie Foster, in her third film role, as Becky Thatcher, and many of the town's buildings are recognizable on screen. Since 2004 architectural ordinances have guided new construction and restorations alike, and on the second full weekend of each October the village fills with craftspeople and musicians for its Fall Heritage Festival.

## Flight Context

Arrow Rock sits at approximately 39.07°N, 92.95°W on a bluff above the south bank of the Missouri River in Saline County. From 2,000–3,500 feet AGL, look for the tiny village grid on the high ground above a broad bend of the river, with the state historic site and its four-acre fishing lake at the village edge and the wetlands of the Jameson Island Unit of the Big Muddy National Fish and Wildlife Refuge in the bottomland alongside. Marshall Memorial Municipal Airport (KMHL) lies about 14 miles to the west; Columbia Regional (KCOU) is the nearest airport with airline service, to the east-southeast. River fog is common on calm mornings, and spring and summer bring thunderstorms.
