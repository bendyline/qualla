# George Caleb Bingham House

In a two-room brick house in Arrow Rock, a newly married George Caleb Bingham developed the style that made him the painter of the Missouri frontier, and the house itself has since been enlarged, stripped back and carefully reimagined.

**Location:** 39.0708, -92.9431

## Introduction

The small brick house at the corner of 1st and High Streets in Arrow Rock, Missouri, looks settled and certain, but part of what you see is an educated guess. Nobody left a detailed record of how it looked when George Caleb Bingham lived here, so when restorers set out in the 1960s to return it to its early appearance, they aimed for a Federal style appearance instead. What is not in doubt is who built it. In 1837, Bingham, newly married and just starting his career, put up this modest home, and in its rooms he became the painter of the Missouri frontier.

## Two Rooms and a Start

Arrow Rock is laid out as a rectangular grid of streets, and the Bingham house stands near the village's eastern end, at the southeast corner of 1st and High. It is a small, single-story brick building with a three-bay front, a side gable roof and chimneys built into the end walls. The main block holds just two rooms, with a wood-frame addition at the back. For a young artist and his first wife, Sarah, it was a first home rather than a grand one. Bingham lived here intermittently from 1837 until 1845, and through those years, even with his comings and goings, it remained his principal residence.

## Where the Frontier Became Art

Bingham was a portraitist and a landscape painter, and in this house he developed his signature style: paintings that captured the landscapes and the people of the Missouri frontier. His years here ended in 1845, around the time he painted Fur Traders Descending the Missouri, the luminous river scene that now hangs in the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York. With its calm water and glowing light, it idealized the American frontier for an Eastern audience. It is a long way from a two-room brick house in a river village to one of the country's great museums, and Bingham made that journey without leaving his Missouri subject behind.

## Added To, Stripped Down, Reimagined

Houses rarely stand still, and this one changed a great deal after Bingham moved out. By 1870 it had reportedly been enlarged, with a second story added on top. In 1926 a preservationist bought the property, and in 1934 it passed to the state of Missouri. The first restoration was blunt: it removed every addition and reduced the house to its original brick core. A more thoughtful restoration followed in 1964 and 1965. With no documentary evidence of exactly how the house looked in Bingham's day, the restorers aimed for a Federal style appearance, bringing it back to a form true to its era if not provably to its first owner. In 1965 the house was declared a National Historic Landmark.

## A Piece of Arrow Rock

Today the house belongs to Arrow Rock State Historic Site, and it forms part of that site's northern boundary. Visitors walking the village's orderly grid of streets can find it near the eastern edge of town, a plain brick cottage that is easy to pass by. That modesty is part of its appeal. Here a young painter with a new wife and an unfinished career set up house, and here he found the way of seeing that made his name.

## Flight Context

The George Caleb Bingham House is at 39.0708°N, 92.9431°W in the village of Arrow Rock, Saline County, central Missouri, near the Missouri River. From 1,500–2,500 feet AGL, look for the small, tidy grid of Arrow Rock's streets close to the river; the house itself is too small to pick out, but the village is compact and distinct from the surrounding farmland.
