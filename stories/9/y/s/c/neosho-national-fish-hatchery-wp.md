# Neosho National Fish Hatchery

Bought partly for a one-dollar spring in 1888, the hatchery at Neosho is the oldest federal fish hatchery still in operation, and it still runs on cold Ozark spring water.

**Location:** 36.8650, -94.3606

**Categories:** National Fish Hatcheries of the United States, Neosho, Missouri, Tourist attractions in Newton County, Missouri, Conservation, Ozarks

## Introduction

For the land, the government paid Lemuel B. and Mary A. Hearrell $2,472. For the spring on it, the price was one dollar. That lopsided bargain, struck in the Ozark hills of southwest Missouri, bought the water that has sustained the Neosho National Fish Hatchery since 1888, and it makes the place the oldest federal fish hatchery still in operation. It is one of about 70 hatcheries run by the US Fish and Wildlife Service, and more than a century on, it still depends on the springs.

## Water and Rails

Neosho won the hatchery for two practical reasons: it had spring water, and it had the railroad. The first supply came from Hearrell Spring, the one the Hearrells sold for a dollar, and it delivered about 300 gallons a minute. That was enough to get a working fish station going quickly. By 1890, just two years after its founding, the hatchery was already productive, raising seven species of warm and cold water fish. An 1896 image of the station shows its bass and trout ponds. Growth meant more water, and in 1907 McMahon Spring was added to the system, lifting the supply to 1,000 gallons a minute, more than three times what the station had started with.

## Raceways and Army Springs

By the middle of the twentieth century the old station needed rebuilding. A rehabilitation program began in 1961, and a modern tile-and-brick hatchery building replaced the hatching house that had stood since 1890. The trout rearing facilities were torn out, and twelve concrete raceways took their place. The same program reached beyond the hatchery grounds. The government obtained title to 244 acres on the Fort Crowder Military Reservation near Neosho, land that held Elm and Bartholic Springs. Those two springs added another 500 gallons a minute. Add it all up and the arithmetic of the place becomes clear: 300 gallons at the start, 1,000 after 1907, and 1,500 once the Fort Crowder springs came in.

## Four Springs Underground

Today the hatchery draws on four gravity-flow underground springs, the farthest of them up to four miles (6 km) away. The water arrives at 54 to 64 degrees and high in quality, about 1,500 gallons of it every minute. That flow lets the staff produce up to 90,000 pounds of fish a year while also rearing several species of imperiled fish and other aquatic animals. Over the hatchery's long life, more than 130 species of cold, cool and warm water fish have been raised here. The springs are the thread that ties those eras together, from the trout ponds of the 1890s to the sturgeon of today.

## Sturgeon, Mussels and a Cavefish

Much of the work now is about rescue as well as supply. Federal hatcheries release rare, endangered and other fish back into the nation's lakes and rivers, and some also make up for fishing lost to the large federal dams of the last century. At Neosho the current work centers on restoring lake sturgeon and the Topeka shiner, recovering the endangered pallid sturgeon, and propagating native mussels, several of them threatened or endangered. Rainbow trout are still raised too, under a mandated mitigation program for Lake Taneycomo, Missouri. One resident was never stocked at all. In January 1990 the threatened Ozark cavefish was observed living in one of the springs that feeds the hatchery, and in 2002 steps were taken to protect the surrounding area. Visitors can see the work up close on guided hatchery tours.

## Flight Context

The hatchery sits in the town of Neosho, Missouri, at 36.865°N, 94.3606°W, in the Ozark country of the state's southwest corner. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL, look for the town of Neosho and the hatchery's ponds and raceways. The nearest field is Neosho Hugh Robinson Airport (KEOS), about 4 nm to the south-southwest, field elevation 1,255 feet. Joplin Regional Airport (KJLN) lies about 18 nm to the north-northwest and has scheduled service, and Monett Regional Airport (FAA identifier HFJ, GPS code KHFJ) is about 17 nm to the east. Summer haze is common over the Ozarks, so a clear morning gives the best view of the water features.
