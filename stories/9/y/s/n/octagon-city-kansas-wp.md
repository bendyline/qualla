# Octagon City, Kansas

In 1856 about 100 settlers came to the Neosho River expecting an eight-sided utopia with mills and farms, and found one log cabin, one plow and a summer that emptied the town.

**Location:** 37.7241, -95.4284

**Categories:** Utopian communities in the United States, Ghost towns, Former populated places in Allen County, Kansas, Vegetarian communities in the United States, 1856 establishments in Kansas Territory, Architecture related to utopias

## Introduction

The settlers were promised a grist mill and a sawmill. By May 1856 about 100 of them had reached the Neosho River in Kansas Territory, where they found one log cabin, one plow, and dozens of tents sheltering families. That was Octagon City on its best day. Planned as a model community with eight roads radiating from an eight-sided square, it lasted barely a year, and today it is a ghost town in Allen County whose only surviving trace is the name of a nearby stream: Vegetarian Creek.

## A Town Drawn in Eight Sides

The geometry came from Orson Squire Fowler, a leading advocate of octagon house architecture. On paper, Octagon City would center on an octagonal town square, and eight roads would run out from it like spokes. Between those roads, across four square miles, 64 families were to build octagonal farmhouses with octagonal barns. The venture belonged to the Vegetarian Kansas Emigration Company, headed by the prominent vegetarian Henry S. Clubb along with entrepreneurs Charles DeWolfe and John McLaurin. Their first idea was a utopian vegetarian commune on the south side of the Neosho River. Investors, it turned out, were far more interested in a moral community that did not require giving up meat, so to make the whole project sustainable the company chose to build Octagon City on the north bank. Members still swore an oath to educate their children and uphold a stable life.

## One Cabin, One Plow

The site, about six miles (10 km) south of today's Humboldt, was very remote. The nearest place to buy goods was Fort Scott, roughly 50 miles away, so the newcomers had to adapt and improvise with what they had carried in. The land fought back. Mosquitoes were relentless, and a flu-like epidemic spread through the camp, blamed variously on malnutrition, exhaustion or malaria. Strong thunderstorms rolled over the tents. And over everything hung the threat of border ruffians. Instead of a blossoming town with its mills turning, the families of Octagon City got hardship, sickness and fear.

## The Summer the Springs Went Dry

By early July the springs that fed the community had dried up. Some residents moved to an Osage settlement a few miles away, where a spring still flowed, but they fled back to Octagon City when they learned the Osage were about to return from their annual buffalo hunt. Through the summer months settlers kept leaving, and the population thinned quickly. By August, crop theft by outsiders had become a major problem. Few of those who stayed past summer meant to make permanent homes in Kansas, and they soon left as well. By the spring of 1857, only four of the original residents remained, and as the weather warmed again, they reported more illness.

## What the Dreamers Left Behind

The country around the old site stayed remote until 1873, when the railroad arrived and the town of Chanute sprang up four miles (6 km) to the south. Nothing of Octagon City survives on the ground. A marker was placed near the site in 1986, but repeated vandalism led to its removal in 2001, and it was donated to the St. Paul/Neosho County Museum. The utopia's founder had a long life after it. Born in Colchester, England, in 1827, Henry Clubb served in the Union Army as a quartermaster during the Civil War and took part in the Siege of Vicksburg. He sat in the Michigan state senate in 1873 and 1874, founded the Vegetarian Society of America in 1886 as its first president, and died in Philadelphia in 1921 at the age of 94. The eight-sided town he helped launch is remembered mainly through Vegetarian Creek.

## Flight Context

The site of Octagon City lies in the Neosho River valley of southeastern Kansas, at approximately 37.7241°N, 95.4284°W, an area-level point near the mouth of Vegetarian Creek, between the towns of Humboldt to the north and Chanute to the south; nothing remains on the ground to mark it. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL, follow the Neosho River between the two towns. Chanute Martin Johnson Airport (KCNU) is about 4 nm to the southwest, and Allen County Airport near Iola (FAA identifier K88; no ICAO code) is about 9 nm to the north-northeast. Afternoon thunderstorms are common here in spring and summer.
