# Splitlog Church

A limestone mission church near Grove, Oklahoma, built around a Belgian bell and signed in its own windows, has outlasted the town, the parish and the family that raised it.

**Location:** 36.6335, -94.6797

**Categories:** Churches in Oklahoma, Churches on the National Register of Historic Places in Oklahoma, Churches completed in 1893, Buildings and structures in Delaware County, Oklahoma, Native American history

## Introduction

Read the windows in order and they spell a name. Above each arched window of this limestone church sits a single carved letter. Start at the east end of the altar, run down the right side of the nave, cross the back and come up the left, and the letters form "Splitlog." Over the front doors, a stone arch carries fifteen more carvings, described as Native American symbols, and their meaning is unknown. Splitlog Church, also called Cayuga Mission Church, stands in the unincorporated community of Cayuga near Grove, Oklahoma, and more than 130 years after construction began, it still stands as a church, open for tours by appointment.

## A Fortune From the Railroad

Mathias Splitlog's road to Cayuga began in Kansas City. In 1863 the Union Pacific Railroad wanted to cross his land near the river and build machine shops there, and Splitlog later said he was paid a fabulous sum for the property. By 1874 he had sold most of his holdings and journeyed to Indian Territory, though he kept his Kansas City home and the 12 acres around it until 1888, when he sold them to the Orchard Place Company. In the territory he found a tract between the Grand River and the Cowskin River, near the present-day city of Grove. It included a large spring, which he named Cayuga in honor of his original tribe. The community he and his wife Eliza founded there became an industrial center in the late 1880s.

## A Lapsed Catholic and a Devout Quaker

Faith in the Splitlog household came in more than one form. Mathias had been baptized as an infant in the Catholic Church but had lapsed; Eliza was a devout Quaker, though she and eleven family members were later baptized as Catholics. The couple often welcomed missionaries of different denominations to use the meeting hall on the upper floor of their general store, and later built a large arbor where Methodist and Quaker missionaries could hold services. Mathias, though, attended only the Catholic ones. In 1893 he set out simply to buy a large church bell. Father Ketcham suggested he build an entire mission church to house it instead, and Splitlog agreed. Several of the Splitlog grandchildren later attended the Catholic Sacred Heart Academy in Vinita, Oklahoma, which Father Versavel established in 1897 while serving as pastor there.

## A Bell From Belgium

The church measures roughly 50 feet long by 30 feet wide, built of native limestone from the nearby Boone Formation beneath a high, steeply pitched roof. Its tall front steeple held the reason for the whole project: a 1,600-pound bronze bell cast in Belgium and shipped to Neosho, Missouri, from where Mathias's son Henry Splitlog hauled it the last 24 miles to Cayuga. A smaller rear belfry held a second, smaller bell. Inside, imported wood was carved in ornate detail, the arched windows held stained glass, and an organ ordered for the choir loft arrived only when the church was nearly complete. At the dedication to St. Matthias, Bishop Meerschaert, Father Ketcham and Father Versavel gave blessings before more than 100 Catholics and 400 non-Catholics. The great bell was blessed too. Its first toll, in memory of Eliza Splitlog, was heard 12 miles away.

## Many Congregations, One Building

After Mathias died, the three acres holding the church and cemetery were deeded to the Catholic Church. Cayuga's residents moved away, membership declined, and the Church found it very difficult to supply priests for the mission, so the parish was dissolved. The altar, the confessional and other Catholic furnishings went to other churches, and the big bell was lowered and sent to St. Catherine's church in Nowata, Oklahoma. The Methodists who acquired the building next installed a pulpit. In 1953 they sold the church and cemetery land to the Sellers family, who had known the Splitlogs and did not want to see the property decay. The building joined the National Register of Historic Places in 1972, and non-denominational services have been held beneath the carved letters of its founder's name.

## Flight Context

Splitlog Church stands at 36.6335°N, 94.6797°W in Cayuga, just outside Grove in northeastern Oklahoma, between the Grand River and the Cowskin River near the shores of Grand Lake o' the Cherokees. From 2,000–3,000 feet AGL, Grove and the lake's long arms are the obvious references; the small stone church with its tall steeple is best sought from low altitude. Grove Regional Airport (KGMJ) is only about 3 nm west-southwest. Neosho Hugh Robinson Airport (KEOS), in the Missouri town where the church's Belgian bell was shipped, is about 17 nm northeast, and Miami Regional (KMIO) about 19 nm north-northwest. Northwest Arkansas National (KXNA) is about 28 nm southeast.
