# Battle of Baxter Springs

On October 6, 1863, Quantrill's Raiders, some wearing Federal uniforms, struck a small Kansas fort and then a Union general's escort on the open prairie, killing roughly 100 men in what is still more commonly called the Baxter Springs Massacre.

**Location:** 37.0308, -94.7350

**Categories:** Battles of the American Civil War in Kansas, Massacres in 1863, African Americans in the American Civil War, Guerrilla warfare in the American Civil War, Cherokee County, Kansas

## Introduction

The horsemen who rode toward the Union column on the Kansas prairie on October 6, 1863, were dressed in Federal uniforms, and the disguise took the soldiers completely by surprise. The riders belonged to Quantrill's Raiders, a large band of pro-Confederate bushwhackers heading south to spend the winter in Texas. The fighting that day near the present-day town of Baxter Springs is filed in Civil War histories as a minor battle. It is more commonly remembered by another name: the Baxter Springs Massacre.

## A Road South, and a Small Fort

By the fall of 1863, William Quantrill's guerrillas had already carried out many raids in Kansas, including the massacre at Lawrence. Now, numbering about 400, they were traveling south along the Texas Road toward their winter quarters. Along the way they captured two Union teamsters who had come from a small Federal post called Fort Baxter, often referred to as Fort Blair, and killed them both. The fort lay within reach, and Quantrill decided to attack it. He split his force into two columns, keeping one for himself and giving the other to a subordinate, David Poole. That decision sent the two halves of the band into two very different fights.

## One Lieutenant and a Howitzer

Fort Baxter was an earth-and-log post, and its garrison was small: about 25 cavalrymen of Company C, 3rd Wisconsin Cavalry, and 65 to 70 infantrymen of the United States Colored Troops from the 2nd Kansas Colored Infantry. First Lieutenant James Burton Pond led the defense. Poole's men came down the Texas Road, ran into Union soldiers on the road, and chased them, killing some before they could reach the fort. Then the garrison made its stand. Pond went outside the walls and, alone and unaided, fired a howitzer three times. The Black infantrymen and Wisconsin troopers fought Poole's column off, and the fort held. Pond later received the Medal of Honor for leading the defense.

## Blue Uniforms on the Prairie

Quantrill's own column found a different target. Maj. Gen. James G. Blunt was moving his command headquarters south from Fort Scott, Kansas, to Fort Smith, Arkansas, and out on the open prairie the guerrillas met the detachment escorting him. Wearing Federal uniforms, they took the Union troops by surprise, and they greatly outnumbered them. Quantrill's men killed most of the detachment, including many who tried to surrender. The dead included the men of a military band; Maj. Henry Z. Curtis, son of Maj. Gen. Samuel R. Curtis; Johnny Fry, remembered as the first official westbound rider of the Pony Express; and James R. O'Neill, an artist-correspondent for Leslie's Weekly. By the most widely cited count, 103 men were killed. A few men escaped, Blunt among them, and soldiers from Fort Baxter went out to search for other survivors. They found few.

## A Refusal and a Rift

Quantrill then sent a demand to the fort: surrender. Pond refused. William T. Anderson, the subordinate known as "Bloody Bill," wanted to attack the fort again, but Quantrill said no, and the guerrillas moved on toward Texas. The disagreement did not end there. Quantrill and Anderson kept clashing over how to wage war along the Kansas–Missouri border, and in 1864 the two split their forces, confining the bushwhackers' fighting to Missouri. On the Union side, Blunt was removed from command for failing to protect his column, though he was soon restored. Fort Baxter was reinforced for a time, but by the end of 1863 the army pulled its troops back to the better-fortified Fort Scott. Before leaving, soldiers demolished the post and carried off everything usable so the enemy could not use it.

## From Killing Ground to Cow Town

The numbers show how one-sided the day was: one widely cited tally counts 103 Union dead against 3 Confederate, though accounts vary. Union supporters called the killings a massacre, and the violence at Baxter Springs was characteristic of the vicious border warfare between Kansas and Missouri. Yet the place did not stay a battlefield. Baxter Springs grew into what is often called the first "cow town" in Kansas, a way station for cattle drives heading to markets and railroads farther north. By 1875, one county history estimated its population at 5,000. The town moved on, but the older name for October 6, 1863, has stuck.

## Flight Context

The battlefield lies near the town of Baxter Springs in the far southeastern corner of Kansas, at about 37.0308°N, 94.735°W, close to where Kansas meets Missouri and Oklahoma. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL, the town itself is the landmark, set in open prairie country. Miami Regional Airport (KMIO) in Oklahoma is about 10 nm to the southwest, and Joplin Regional Airport (KJLN), with scheduled service, is about 14 nm to the northeast.
