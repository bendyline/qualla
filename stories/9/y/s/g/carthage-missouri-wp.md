# Carthage, Missouri

Burned in the Civil War and rebuilt in limestone polished like marble, Carthage became a Route 66 crossroads, a Victorian showplace and, since 1975, home to a Vietnamese Catholic congregation whose Marian Days draw tens of thousands.

**Location:** 37.1678, -94.3150

**Categories:** Cities in Jasper County, Missouri, County seats in Missouri, Missouri in the American Civil War, U.S. Route 66, Vietnamese-American culture, Carthage, Missouri

## Introduction

The limestone quarried around Carthage is hard enough to take a polish, and when it does, people call it marble. "Carthage marble" went into the interior and exterior of Missouri's state capitol in Jefferson City, and it gave this town its look: a stone county courthouse, a Carnegie library, blocks of stone buildings and ornate Victorian houses. That solidity was hard-won. During the Civil War, guerrillas burned most of Carthage. The town that rose afterward built to last, and it has kept reinventing itself ever since, as a Route 66 crossroads and as an unexpected center of Vietnamese American Catholic life.

## Named for an Ancient Rival

Jasper County was formed in 1841, and the next year the site chosen as its seat was cleared and platted. Its founders named it for Carthage, the ancient city-state in what is now Tunisia that was one of the Roman Republic's main rivals. By the eve of the Civil War, it had grown to more than 500 residents, with a brick and stone courthouse and several businesses. The area was divided over slavery, and almost all of the African Americans living in the county at the time were enslaved. In the decades to come, the roll of people tied to Carthage would stretch from the Wild West outlaw Belle Starr to Baseball Hall of Fame pitcher Carl Hubbell and Marlin Perkins, the naturalist host of Mutual of Omaha's Wild Kingdom.

## The War Comes Early

The war arrived early. On July 5, 1861, the Battle of Carthage, one of the earliest battles of the American Civil War, pitted Union troops from St. Louis against Confederate forces led by Missouri's pro-Southern governor, Claiborne Fox Jackson. In October 1863 a second clash came when Union troops confronted Confederates north of town and forced them back into Arkansas. Skirmishes and raids continued throughout the war, and in September 1864 pro-Confederate guerrillas burned most of the city, the courthouse included. Today local groups stage reenactments near the State Historic Site that commemorates the 1861 battle.

## A City Built of Stone

Carthage grew rapidly after the war. The Missouri and Western Railway arrived in 1872, and residents opened a foundry, a furniture factory, woolen and grain mills, a plow works and numerous liveries. Leggett & Platt, the bedspring maker long based here, was founded in 1883; Somnigroup acquired it in August 2026 and plans to keep its Carthage offices. Nearby lead mines and limestone quarries added wealth, and Carthage became one of the most prosperous towns in the area. Residents poured money into ornate Victorian homes, many now in the Carthage South District, listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1982. The Jasper County Courthouse, also on the Register, was built of Carthage stone in 1894–95, and a mural inside depicts the county's history. The Carthage Public Library, opened in 1905 with a $25,000 grant from Andrew Carnegie after voters approved a supporting tax 831 to 94, was built of Carthage marble too. Ragtime composer James Scott, regarded as one of the three most important composers of classic ragtime, lived here from 1901 to 1906 and published "A Summer Breeze" through local music-store owner Charles L. Dumars in 1903.

## Breakfast at the Crossroads

In the 1920s U.S. Highways 66 and 71 came through town, and for a time Carthage watched a steady stream of cross-country traffic. The two routes met at what is now the intersection of Central and Garrison Avenues, and the Boots Court motel there promoted a drive-in restaurant with a KDMO radio broadcast called "Breakfast at the Crossroads of America." Route 66 was eventually rerouted, then replaced in the 1960s by Interstate 44, running south of town. In the late 20th century Carthage began courting tourists with its history: the battle, the Victorian architecture, and the original Route 66 alignment, which is still marked around town. The Precious Moments Park and Chapel, with paintings and oversized versions of the porcelain figurines, draws visitors as well.

## Marian Days

In 1925 Ozark Wesleyan College merged three Methodist colleges and built a campus in the center of town, though it closed after only a few years. From 1944 to 1971 the campus housed Our Lady of the Ozarks College. Then, in 1975, immediately after the Vietnam War, a Vietnamese order of priests and brothers, the Congregation of the Mother Co-Redemptrix, arrived and made the campus its home. Since 1978 the congregation has hosted Marian Days, an annual celebration for Vietnamese American Catholics that draws tens of thousands of people to its 28-acre grounds each August, with recent estimates ranging from about 60,000 to 100,000. Each October Carthage throws a different kind of party, the week-long Maple Leaf Festival, held since 1966 when the town's many maples turn red, orange and yellow and live up to its nickname, "America's Maple Leaf City."

## Flight Context

Carthage lies at 37.1678°N, 94.3150°W in Jasper County, southwest Missouri, just south of the Spring River along I-49/US 71, with Interstate 44 passing south of town. Joplin is about 12 miles to the southwest and Neosho about 21 miles south. From 2,500–3,500 feet AGL, look for the stone Jasper County Courthouse downtown. Joplin Regional Airport (KJLN) is about 9 nm west, Lamar Municipal (KLLU) about 19 nm north, and Neosho Hugh Robinson Airport (KEOS) about 22 nm south. Expect good visibility on most days outside spring storm season.
