# Battle of Dry Wood Creek

In September 1861 a Kansas force laid an ambush in the woods along Dry Wood Creek, held off Sterling Price's far larger army for about two hours, and then fled so fast it left its mules behind, giving the fight its nickname: the Battle of the Mules.

**Location:** 37.8197, -94.4862

**Categories:** Battles of the American Civil War in Missouri, Operations to control Missouri (American Civil War), Confederate victories of the American Civil War, Vernon County, Missouri, 1861 in Missouri

## Introduction

Most battles take their names from a creek, a town or a ridge. This one earned a second name from what the losing side left behind. On September 2, 1861, Kansas troops retreating from Dry Wood Creek in Vernon County, Missouri, abandoned their supplies and their mules to the enemy, and the capture of those animals is why the fight is sometimes called the Battle of the Mules. Behind the comic nickname sits a sharp little engagement in the struggle for a divided state, one that cleared the road north for a Confederate-aligned army.

## A State Pulled Two Ways

When the Civil War broke out in April 1861, Missouri was a border state: slavery was legal there, but it did not vote to secede. Governor Claiborne F. Jackson sided with the Confederacy and began using the militia to support its cause, marching toward the arsenal in St. Louis. Brigadier General Nathaniel Lyon, commanding Union forces in the city, moved fast and forced the militia to surrender in what became known as the Camp Jackson affair. Jackson answered by forming a new body, the Missouri State Guard, and put Sterling Price in command. Lyon beat it at Boonville and pushed Price back toward Springfield, where Confederate troops under Brigadier General Benjamin McCulloch joined him. On August 10, Lyon attacked their camps along Wilsons Creek despite being outnumbered by more than two to one. The Union army lost, and Lyon was killed.

## Price Turns North

After Wilson's Creek, the Union army fell back to Rolla, McCulloch withdrew to Arkansas, and Price's Missouri State Guard occupied Springfield. John C. Fremont arrived to take over Union forces in the state, and James H. Lane took command of several Kansas regiments stationed at Fort Scott, Kansas. Then Price began to march north, with a force estimated at either 6,000 or 10,000 men. Fremont responded to the incursion by declaring Missouri subject to martial law. Lane decided to try to intercept them before they got far.

## Two Hours in the Woods

Lane sent a force from Fort Scott, estimated at either 600 or 1,200 men, to intercept Price's army. They set their ambush along Dry Wood Creek, where the ground was wooded and gave the Kansans the element of surprise. The leading elements of the Missouri State Guard walked into the trap. For about two hours, Lane's men held Price off. But the numbers were against them. Even at the low estimate for Price and the high one for Lane, the Missourians held a wide advantage, and they forced the Kansans back to Fort Scott. By Lane's own report, his side lost five men killed and six wounded. Price put his losses at two killed and twenty-three wounded, noting that most of the wounds were not serious.

## Mules, Hemp Bales and Markers

The retreat was costly in a different way. Lane's troops abandoned their supplies and mules to the Missouri State Guard, and the animals gave the battle its enduring nickname. More important, the victory let Price keep moving north. After reaching Johnson County, he pushed on to Lexington, where a Union garrison held the town, and surrounded it, beginning a siege that ran from September 13 to 20. At one point his men advanced uphill using hemp bales as cover. The garrison surrendered, and the State Guard stayed in the Lexington area for two weeks before Price ordered it back south. Today the battle is remembered with a series of markers near Deerfield, Missouri, not far from the creek. In nearby Nevada, Missouri, a museum covers both the Battle of Dry Wood Creek and the wider story of the Civil War in Missouri, a border state pulled in two directions from the war's first months.

## Flight Context

The battlefield lies in Vernon County, Missouri, near the small town of Deerfield, at about 37.8197°N, 94.4862°W, a short distance east of the Kansas line. From 2,000 to 3,000 feet AGL, follow the course of Dry Wood Creek near Deerfield.
