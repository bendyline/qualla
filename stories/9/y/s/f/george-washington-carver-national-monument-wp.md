# George Washington Carver National Monument

On 240 acres of rolling farmland, woods and prairie near Diamond, Missouri, the first national monument dedicated to an African American preserves the boyhood home of George Washington Carver, who was born into slavery here and grew up with an "inordinate desire for knowledge."

## Introduction

"From a child I had an inordinate desire for knowledge," George Washington Carver wrote in a letter around 1897, looking back on his boyhood in southwest Missouri. He loved music, painting, flowers and the sciences, and he collected plants so devotedly that people began bringing him their ailing ones. He became known, he recalled, as the "plant doctor." The farm where that curiosity first took root lies in the rolling hills near Diamond, in Newton County. In 1943 it became George Washington Carver National Monument, the first national monument dedicated to an African American, preserving the woods, prairie and fields where a child born into slavery first learned to look closely at the living world.

## Born at the War's End

Carver was born enslaved on this farm near the end of the Civil War. In his own account, he was "about 2 weeks old when the war closed," and his father was killed shortly after his birth. His mother, Mary, was enslaved by Moses and Susan Carver, who raised livestock, tended orchards and grew corn on the farmstead. Then came a devastating loss. As an infant, George was carried off along with his mother and a sister, and taken to be sold in Arkansas. Only George was brought back, nearly dead with whooping cough. His mother and sister were reported dead, though, as Carver wrote, some said they had seen them afterward going north. Moses and Susan Carver raised the orphaned boy and his brother Jim on the farm.

## The Plant Doctor

The woods and fields around the Carver farm became George's classroom long before he had a school. He gathered plants, studied them and nursed them, and his reputation spread until, by his own telling, plants from all over the country were brought to him for treatment. Formal learning was harder to come by. As a boy, somewhere between about nine and eleven, he left to attend a school in the town of Neosho, roughly eight miles away, and from there he moved on through schools in Kansas, pursuing an education wherever he could find one. That persistence carried him far from this farm. Carver went on to become a celebrated agricultural scientist and educator at Tuskegee Institute in Alabama, but the curiosity that drove his life's work first showed itself here, among the Missouri hills.

## A Monument to a Scientist

Carver died in January 1943. Just months later, on July 14, 1943, Congress authorized the national monument and President Franklin Delano Roosevelt signed the bill, dedicating $30,000 to the project; the site was formally dedicated in 1953. The site was later listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1966. Today the monument covers 240 acres of historic farmland about two miles west of Diamond. It preserves the landscape of Carver's boyhood, rolling hills, woodlands and prairies, along with the 1881 Moses Carver house, built years after George had left for school, and the Carver cemetery. Together they hold the farm's whole complicated story, from slavery to the boyhood that followed.

## Walking the Carver Trail

The best way to understand this place is on foot. A nature trail of roughly a mile winds through the grounds, passing the cemetery and leading visitors through the kind of woods and open ground where young George once searched for plants. Along the way stands a row of walnut trees, planted by the National Park Service in the 1950s to recreate a fence row thought to have been planted by Moses Carver himself. The details are a reminder that this monument is less about buildings than about a landscape and a boy's relationship to it. It is quiet country, and it rewards the same patient attention Carver brought to it more than 150 years ago.

## Flight Context

George Washington Carver National Monument lies at about 36.986°N, 94.354°W, roughly two miles west of Diamond in Newton County, Missouri, and about ten miles southeast of Joplin. From 2,500-3,500 feet MSL, look for a patch of woodland and open prairie set among the surrounding farm fields, with the small town of Diamond just to the east. Joplin Regional Airport (KJLN) is about 14 miles to the northwest, and Springfield-Branson National Airport (KSGF) lies roughly 55 miles to the east-northeast. The terrain here is gentle Ozark-edge farmland; visibility is usually good, with spring and summer thunderstorms the main weather concern.
