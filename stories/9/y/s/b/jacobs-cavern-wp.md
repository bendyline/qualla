# Jacobs Cavern

A shallow limestone shelter near Pineville, Missouri, Jacobs Cavern held deep beds of ancient ash, tools, animal bones and human burials, excavated square meter by square meter in 1903.

**Location:** 36.5900, -94.3400

**Categories:** Caves of Missouri, Archaeological sites in Missouri, Landforms of McDonald County, Missouri, Rock shelters

## Introduction

Under a single flat ledge of limestone east of Pineville, Missouri, the floor was made of ashes. In places the ash lay a meter and a half deep, and water dripping from the rock had slowly spread over it, binding ash, charcoal, bones, flint flakes and tools into a mostly firm, solid crust. When excavators opened that crust in 1903, they found the long record of people who had lived beneath the ledge, and the people who had been buried there. Jacobs Cavern is hardly more than a rock shelter, but its 1903 excavation was recorded in careful detail.

## A Room Beneath One Stone

The cavern lies entirely within the St. Joe Formation, limestone of the Mississippian subperiod. Its roof is one flat stratum of rock, and its walls are plainly lined with layers of stratification. The opening runs more than 21 meters long and reaches 14 meters deep, and the roof stood between 1.2 and 2.6 meters above the undisturbed ash. At the back, a low recess where bones were found rose only 50 to 80 centimeters.

Dripstone partly covers the walls, fills a deep fissure at the end of the cave and spreads across the floor. Beneath the ash lies the cave's true floor, a meter of homogeneous yellow clay scattered with limestone fragments, itself the product of the limestone breaking down over time.

## Digging by the Square Meter

The shelter is named for E. H. Jacobs of Bentonville, Arkansas, who brought it to the archaeologists' attention and explored it in 1903 with Charles Peabody and Warren K. Moorehead. Their method was careful for its day. They staked off the ash surface in square meters and removed it in order. Every stalactite, stalagmite and pilaster was measured, numbered and taken out in sections.

The findings reached print quickly: in Jacobs's own account in the Benton County Sun, in a report by C. N. Gould in Science on July 31, 1903, in articles by Peabody, and in 1904 as the first bulletin of the Department of Archaeology at Phillips Academy in Andover, Massachusetts, whose museum has held exhibits, maps and photographs from the site.

## What the Ashes Held

The excavators recorded six burials in the ashes, though the Robert S. Peabody Institute of Archaeology's modern inventory identifies the remains of at least 15 individuals from the cavern. Around them lay seven-tenths of a cubic meter of animal bones, from deer, bear, wolf, raccoon, beaver, buffalo, elk, turkey and tortoise, among others, all dating to the time the shelter was occupied. The excavators also recovered three stone metates, a stone axe, a celt and fifteen hammerstones. The cavern was especially rich in flint knives and projectile points: 419 objects in all, besides hundreds of fragments, cores and flakes kept for study. Bone or horn awls turned up in quantity, along with pieces of pottery. There were no ceremonial objects.

## Reading Time in Stone

The early investigators tried to read the site's age from its contents. To them the rough tools, the lack of fine pottery and features of the human remains pointed to occupants older than the people they called the "mound-builders." The lowest tool found lay 50 centimeters beneath the stalagmitic surface. Horace Carter Hovey, whose 1911 encyclopedia entry on the site survives, had measured stalagmite growth at Wyandotte Cave in Indiana at 0.0254 centimeters a year; if the same rate held here, he reckoned, sealing in that tool would have taken 1,968 years.

That "if" matters, and the language of races and mound-builders belongs to its era. Polished rocks outside the cavern and pictographs nearby were read the same way, as the work of people who came before the Osage, the historic owners of this land before Europeans arrived. A federal notice published in December 2023 by the Peabody Institute, which then had custody of the remains, tells a different story: it affiliates the ancestors taken from Jacob's Cavern with the Osage Nation and opens the way for their repatriation.

## Flight Context

Jacobs Cavern lies near 36.59°N, 94.34°W, east of Pineville in McDonald County, in the far southwestern corner of Missouri close to the Arkansas and Oklahoma lines. The shelter itself is small and hard to spot in the Ozark hills, so from 2,500-3,500 ft AGL use Pineville and the surrounding valleys as reference points. Neosho Hugh Robinson Airport (KEOS) is about 13.5 nm north. To the south, Bentonville Municipal Airport/Louise M. Thaden Field (KVBT) is about 16 nm away and Northwest Arkansas National Airport (KXNA) about 18.5 nm away. Watch terrain clearance over the ridges; morning valley fog is common in cool months.
